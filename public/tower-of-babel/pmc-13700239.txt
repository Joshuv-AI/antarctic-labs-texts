Nurses’ role in the management of patients living with hypertension in a district hospital in Ghana: a qualitative study
Authors: Bridget Amoako, Cecilia Eliason, Menford Owusu Ampomah, Luke Laari, Alhassan Hassan, Euphemia Tumpi, Joy Georgette La Bulley, Paul Kolbugri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension is a chronic condition characterised by persistently high blood pressure, increasing the risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke, and kidney failure. Its management remains challenging in low- and middle-income countries due to limited awareness, lifestyle-related risk factors, and restricted access to healthcare. In Ghana, rapid urbanisation and dietary transitions toward processed foods have intensified this burden. This study aims to explore the role of nurses in managing patients with hypertension in Ghana.MethodsThis qualitative, exploratory and descriptive study was conducted between January and September 2025 with 15 participants at Ga West Municipal Hospital in the Greater Accra Region of Ghana. The participants were selected by a purposive sampling method. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews. Data analysis was done using thematic analysis following a systematic process involving data familiarisation, generation of initial codes, categorisation of codes into subthemes, development of overarching themes, and validation of findings to ensure credibility and consistency.ResultsData analysis yielded three main themes and 10 subthemes. The themes included nurses’ knowledge of hypertension management, nurses’ experiences in managing hypertension and community and follow-up support. Under nurses’ knowledge of hypertension management, the sub-themes identified were understanding of hypertension, initial assessment and blood pressure monitoring, pharmacological interventions, and counselling and discharge planning. For nurses’ experiences in managing hypertension, the sub-themes comprised the use of clinical guidelines and protocols and challenges encountered in practice. Under community and follow-up support systems, the sub-themes included peer support groups, follow-up via telephone calls, community pharmacies and clinics, and telehealth services. Nurses play a critical role in hypertension management; however, their practice is hindered by limited knowledge, systemic constraints and weak community linkages. Enhancing nurses’ competencies through regular professional training, access to updated evidence-based guidelines, adequate staffing and resources, and the integration of digital health tools can strengthen care delivery.ConclusionsThese findings provide valuable insights into the roles and challenges of nurses in hypertension management within a municipal hospital setting. While the results may not be generalisable, they highlight areas for potential improvement, including strengthening professional training, improving access to evidence-based guidelines, and enhancing community follow-up systems. These considerations may inform future research, policy discussions, and practice improvements in similar resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05121-w.

Background

Hypertension is one of the leading non-communicable diseases worldwide, representing a major risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1]. It is defined as persistently elevated blood pressure, diagnosed when systolic pressure exceeds ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic pressure is ≥ 90 mmHg [1]. Globally, an estimated 1.28 billion adults live with hypertension, yet only one in five achieves adequate control [1, 2]. The burden falls disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries, where health systems face limited resources, low public awareness, and inadequate access to regular screening [3]. The World Health Organisation (WHO) has recognised hypertension as a major driver of premature mortality, urging countries to prioritise prevention and control strategies [1].

In sub-Saharan Africa, prevalence ranges between 19 and 48% [4]. Rapid urbanisation, dietary shifts toward processed foods, and socioeconomic factors are key to this rise [4, 5]. In Ghana, it is estimated that 34% of adults aged 30–70 years are hypertensive [6]. Urbanisation and changing food environments, including increased fast-food consumption, also contribute to this trend [7].

Effective hypertension care requires a comprehensive approach encompassing lifestyle modification, medical therapy, and regular monitoring [8]. Dietary interventions, particularly the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, emphasise reduced sodium intake, potassium-rich fruits and vegetables, and calcium-rich low-fat dairy products to maintain blood pressure control [9]. Physical activity is also essential, with at least 150 min of moderate-intensity exercise (e.g., walking, cycling) or 75 min of vigorous activity (e.g., running, swimming) per week recommended to improve cardiovascular function [10]. Adherence to antihypertensive medications remains critical to prevent complications such as heart failure, kidney disease, and myocardial infarction [11, 12]. Finally, routine blood pressure monitoring facilitates early detection and allows timely adjustment of treatment plans. Sociocultural factors also shape outcomes. Reliance on traditional remedies, strong dietary preferences, and economic hardship often undermine adherence to prescribed treatment [13, 14].

However, nurses play a pivotal role in hypertension management. They integrate self-care support, clinical decision-making, and interprofessional collaboration to ensure long-term blood pressure control [15]. Their responsibilities extend beyond clinical management as their contribution is particularly essential in resource-constrained settings, where they often serve as the primary link between patients and the health system [15]. Furthermore, understanding hypertension management begins with accurate knowledge of the condition and its underlying mechanisms. Nurses are expected to assess patient history comprehensively, classify severity, and guide treatment planning through evidence-based practices [16].

Tabangcora [12] emphasised the importance of nurses’ competence, noting that sound knowledge of antihypertensive medications, including dosages, side effects, and interactions, supports safe administration and improves patient adherence. Similarly, patients who received medication counselling from knowledgeable nurses demonstrated improved compliance and reduced hospital readmissions [17]. Beyond clinical knowledge, nurses provide self-management support, and this refers to empowering patients with the knowledge and confidence to take charge of their health, thereby improving adherence to lifestyle changes and treatment plans [18]. Studies highlight that nurses who provide self-management education significantly improve patients’ understanding of hypertension and its complications [19, 20]. For instance, hospitalised patients who received nurse-led self-management support showed improved monitoring practices and follow-up adherence [19].

In Ghana, however, patients frequently lack home blood pressure monitors and rely on irregular hospital checks, which limits continuous monitoring [20]. Nurses could bridge this gap by encouraging patients to use pharmacies and community facilities for regular checks, thus improving continuity of care [21]. Nevertheless, high workloads and limited time often restrict nurses’ ability to deliver detailed self-management education [22]. The use of clinical guidelines is another critical component of effective hypertension management. Guidelines provide evidence-based frameworks that promote consistency in diagnosis and treatment [23]. Internationally, the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) and the European Society of Cardiology/European Society of Hypertension (ESC/ESH) guidelines are widely applied [24]. In Ghana, the 2017 national cardiovascular guidelines, adapted from ESC/ESH, recommend diagnosing hypertension based on the average of two or more blood pressure readings taken on two or more occasions, with pre hypertension defined as a systolic blood pressure of 130–139 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of 80–89 mmHg, and grade 1 hypertension defined as a systolic blood pressure 140–159 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure 90–99 mmHg, grade 2 hypertension defined as a systolic blood pressure 160–179 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure100-109 mmHg, grade 3 hypertension defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 180 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 100–109 mmHg [25]. However, many nurses may lack formal training in these guidelines, often leading to errors in blood pressure measurement and misclassification of patients [26]. Similarly, patients discontinued treatment due to unclear instructions and inadequate counselling, reflecting the consequences of poor adherence to standardised protocols [27]. Therefore, continuous professional training and reinforcement of guideline use are essential to improving hypertension outcomes in Ghana [28].

Nurses also play a vital role in leveraging community resources for improved hypertension management. Community-based interventions such as mobile clinics, local screenings, and educational campaigns have proven effective in reaching underserved populations [29]. The WHO [30] emphasises that community linkages reduce readmissions and enhance adherence through continuity of care. Structured nurse-led discharge planning, supported by community nurses, significantly improved follow-up and reduced complications [31]. In Ghana, mobile health tools such as SMS reminders and telehealth consultations are emerging as practical solutions to overcome geographical barriers [32]. Similarly, nurse-led telehealth follow-ups improved adherence and blood pressure outcomes in Iran [33]. However, the limited awareness of community resources, poor coordination between hospitals and community health centers, and low digital literacy among patients hinder the full potential of community-based nursing interventions [34]. Taken together, nurses’ roles in hypertension management encompass a broad scope, including accurate assessment, patient education, application of guidelines, and integration of community resources. Studies across diverse contexts, from Ghana to the United States, Europe, and Asia, demonstrate both progress and persistent gaps in practice. While the global evidence underscores the effectiveness of nurse-led interventions in improving hypertension outcomes, Ghanaian studies highlight barriers such as inadequate training, resource constraints, and weak community linkages [28].

However, the organisation and delivery of hypertension care in district hospitals differ from tertiary hospitals, as district hospitals often operate with limited specialist support, fewer diagnostic resources, higher patient-to-nurse ratios, and greater reliance on nurse-led approach for both clinical and follow-up care [35]. These contextual constraints shape how nurses perform their roles and influence patient outcomes in ways that may not be fully captured in studies conducted in tertiary hospitals. Although the nurses’ role in the management of patients with hypertension has been investigated, this has not been extensively explored qualitatively in the Ghanaian district-hospital context with under-resourced healthcare systems. Hence, a need to explore and understand how nurses understand their role, and how they can be supported to optimise such roles in hypertension management.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a qualitative, exploratory and descriptive design, in which 15 registered nurses from Ga West Municipal Hospital in the Greater Accra Region of Ghana participated. The hospital was established in 1984 and upgraded to a municipal hospital in 2008 [36]. It serves as a major referral centre within a network of community-based health planning and services (CHPS) compounds and health centres. The hospital records approximately 60,000 outpatient visits annually and 7,500–8,000 inpatient admissions and provides a range of services including hypertension screening, diagnosis, and management through a specialised hypertensive clinic, with linkages to community-level facilities for follow-up care. This context makes it a relevant setting for examining nurses’ roles in hypertension management in resource-constrained district hospitals, with findings offering transferable insights to similar settings. The study was conducted between January and September 2025. A descriptive exploratory qualitative design was chosen to gain an in-depth understanding of nurses’ roles and experiences in managing patients with hypertension [37]. Participants were selected using purposive sampling, a technique commonly used in qualitative research to identify individuals with relevant knowledge and experience [38]. Participants were recruited through the hospital’s administration and unit heads using predefined inclusion criteria that required they be registered nurses with experience managing patients with hypertension and working in relevant clinical units.

Nevertheless, an initial sample of 24 nurses, comprising both male and female participants, was obtained. However, all male participants subsequently withdrew their consent without providing specific reasons. Due to time constraints, it was not feasible to recruit additional male participants to replace those who withdrew, leaving 15 female registered nurses to continue in the study, none of whom withdrew their consent. Because the male nurses who withdrew their consent to participate in this study were the entire cohort on the selected wards meeting the inclusion criteria, recruiting more male nurses would require identifying a similar study setting and undergoing another ethical approval process. However, this was not feasible within the time limits of the study. Therefore, this should not invalidate the findings but needs to be acknowledged when interpreting them, and future studies should aim to include both male and female nurses to provide a more comprehensive understanding of hypertension management practices. Data saturation was determined when the researchers noticed that no new information was being obtained from the study participants after the eleventh interview, as evidenced by transcribing data after each interview concurrently, and this was used to determine that no information was obtained. Therefore, four more interviews were carried out, and the researchers concluded that no new information was being elicited. Maximum variation was sought in terms of age, years of professional experience, and department of practice to ensure diverse perspectives. Inclusion criteria were as follows:

Reflexivity

In this study, the researchers’ prior familiarity in hypertension management facilitated rapport building and promoted effective engagement with participants during interviews. However, to minimise potential bias, reflexivity was rigorously maintained throughout the research process as preconceptions were identified and discussed prior to data collection, and emphasis was placed on privileging participants’ perspectives during data collection and analysis. A reflective journal was maintained, and regular peer debriefing with co-authors was conducted to critically examine and validate emerging interpretations.

Data collection

After participants were recruited based on the inclusion criteria, the study’s objectives, interview procedures, and details regarding the time and location of the sessions were thoroughly explained to them. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection, in accordance with established ethical research standards. Individual, in-depth, semi-structured interviews served as the main data collection method, providing flexibility to explore participants’ experiences and understanding of their role, while ensuring consistency through the use of the interview guide [39] (see supplementary file). This interview guide was developed by the researchers and pre-tested with three registered nurses, working on a medical ward and caring for patients with hypertension. The pilot study was done at a similar facility to the study site, the Government District Hospital in Nsawam, Eastern Region of Ghana. Modifications involving how some questions were reframed and the introduction of probes to prompt additional responses on each question in the interview guide were made to ensure in-depth data would be obtained and be consistent with the objective of the study. However, the interviews from the pilot study were not included in the final data for analysis.

All interviews were conducted face-to-face in quiet rooms within Ga West Municipal Hospital to maintain privacy and minimise distractions. Sample questions from the interview guide included: “What is your understanding of nurses’ roles in managing patients with hypertension”? “Can you describe the strategies you use to support patients in managing their hypertension”? “How do you stay updated on the latest evidence-based practices”?

Each interview lasted between 30 and 55 min, depending on participants’ availability and engagement. Field notes were taken to capture contextual and non-verbal cues, and all interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ permission to ensure data accuracy. Interview enhancement techniques such as probing, summarising responses, and active listening were used to promote depth and clarity [40]. The interviewer, who happens to be the first author, employed bracketing to minimise bias and avoid imposing personal interpretations on participants’ statements. Each session concluded with a summary, confirmation of the participant’s satisfaction with the discussion, and appreciation for their contribution. All interviews were transcribed verbatim on the same day to preserve detail and reliability.

Data analysis

All interviews and field notes were transcribed verbatim and analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis approach [41]. The researchers (MOA and LL) initially immersed themselves independently in the data by reviewing notes, listening to audio recordings, and repeatedly reading the transcripts to achieve familiarisation with the data. They then generated initial codes manually, examining each segment of text to capture its meaning. These codes were organised into potential categories and subsequently refined into broader themes that reflected patterns across the dataset. The themes were reviewed and compared with the original transcripts to ensure accuracy, consistency, and relevance. The analysis was iterative, involving repeated cycles of checking and refinement until the researchers were confident that the themes accurately captured the participants’ perspectives. Regular peer debriefing among researchers was held for in-depth discussions on coding decisions, refining themes and ensuring shared understanding. In cases of disagreement on themes and subthemes, consensus was reached through discussion. Coding revisions and analytic decisions were documented throughout the process. The process was conducted in close consultation with the research team, and final agreement was reached on the coding framework and theme structure.

Rigor

To establish rigor in this qualitative study, the four criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba [42]; credibility, transferability, confirmability, and dependability were applied [42]. Credibility was enhanced through member checking, prolonged engagement with participants, peer debriefing, and triangulation. Participants were allowed to verify the information provided after the interviews to ensure that the findings accurately reflected their experiences. The researcher also spent sufficient time in the field to develop a deep understanding of the study context. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the participant characteristics, study setting, sampling strategy, and methodological procedures. These descriptions are to enable readers and other researchers to assess the applicability of the findings to different contexts.

Confirmability was strengthened through good audio recordings, verbatim transcription, and independent researchers’ review. In addition, the researchers held regular peer debriefing sessions to review the research process and analytical decisions by critically examining emerging codes, themes, and interpretations. Also, the maintenance of reflexive notes to minimise researcher bias. Dependability was ensured by using an audit trail that documented the research procedures, decision-making processes, and analytical steps. These strategies ensured that the findings were grounded in the data and that the research process was traceable and transparent.

Knowledge of hypertension management

From the participants’ point of view, knowledge of hypertension management was an important element in ensuring effective patient care. The subthemes included understanding of hypertension, initial assessment and BP monitoring, pharmacological interventions, counselling and discharge planning.

Understanding of hypertension

A clear understanding of hypertension allows the recognition of its causes, identifies risk factors, and guides patients toward proper management to prevent complications. However, participants demonstrated a basic understanding of what hypertension entails as they could generally perceive hypertension as elevated systolic and diastolic pressures with emphasis on its recognition as a chronic illness requiring lifelong management.High blood pressure, also known as hypertension, is a chronic health condition requiring long-term management. It’s also when the heart pumps blood into the arteries, resulting in high systolic and diastolic pressures of 140/90 mmHg, whereas normal blood pressure is 120/80 mmHg. (P14)

On the awareness of underlying causes and risk factors, this is what some participants had to say:Hypertension is when a person’s blood pressure becomes high above the normal level. Some people can get it because of stress or thinking too much, and we usually advise patients to take their medications and avoid too much salt. (P9)High blood pressure is where the blood pressure remains high over time, although I do not have full knowledge of its specific causes and risk factors and can only mention a few general factors such as stress, excessive salt intake and lack of physical activity. However, patients are encouraged to adhere strictly to prescribed medications. (P12)

Overall, the level of understanding was adequate in terms of identifying hypertension as a chronic condition, but not all participants had a clear understanding of its causes, risk factors, and preventive management.

Initial assessment and blood pressure monitoring

Initial assessment and blood pressure (BP) monitoring are critical for establishing a baseline and guiding individualised care. A few participants reported conducting some evaluations in their practice. A participant explained:When patients come to the ward and are diagnosed with hypertension, I first ask them whether they know they are hypertensive. I try to find out about the foods they eat, the work they do, and the medications they are taking. (P8)

When patients come to the ward and are diagnosed with hypertension, I first ask them whether they know they are hypertensive. I try to find out about the foods they eat, the work they do, and the medications they are taking. (P8)

However, most participants focused primarily on recording the BP reading without engaging in a detailed preliminary assessment. A participant shared:Usually, we just take the blood pressure and document it for the doctor to review. When a patient presents with hypertension, our attention is mostly on the readings they bring. We begin by checking the vital signs, and if the blood pressure is elevated, we initiate antihypertensive treatment. After that, we continue to monitor the blood pressure at regular intervals, sometimes every four hours, two hours, or even hourly, depending on the values obtained. (P1)

Pharmacological interventions

From the participants’ perspective, the use of medications was the primary strategy for lowering and controlling high blood pressure. They frequently mentioned drugs such as nifedipine and amlodipine, yet their explanations revealed a limited understanding of how these medications work or the pharmacological classes to which they belong. Participants described their use of drugs largely in terms of blood pressure figures and protocol-driven administration. A participant expressed:When patients come with very high blood pressure, I give amlodipine or nifedipine, depending on what is prescribed and monitor. Honestly, I know it lowers the pressure, but I don’t know in detail what it does or the group of drugs it belongs to. (P8)

When patients come with very high blood pressure, I give amlodipine or nifedipine, depending on what is prescribed and monitor. Honestly, I know it lowers the pressure, but I don’t know in detail what it does or the group of drugs it belongs to. (P8)

Counselling and discharge planning

Counselling and discharge planning were important components of hypertension management, as this helped patients to better understand their condition and take responsibility for their own care. A few participants explained that before patients were discharged, they were educated on the correct timing of taking their medication, dietary restrictions, and the need for rest.A participant remarked:We counsel them on adherence to medication, teaching them the exact times they should take their antihypertensives. We also advise on diet, reducing salt and fat and encourage rest, while informing them of the need to check their BP if they feel unwell. (P9)

A participant remarked:

We counsel them on adherence to medication, teaching them the exact times they should take their antihypertensives. We also advise on diet, reducing salt and fat and encourage rest, while informing them of the need to check their BP if they feel unwell. (P9)

Participants also emphasised the importance of patients actively managing their condition at home through blood pressure monitoring, follow-up visits, and lifestyle modifications.We let them know that it’s their responsibility too, to watch what they eat, take the medicine, and check their BP so they don’t come back with the same problem. We encourage them to take charge of their health because if they don’t, their condition can worsen and lead to readmission. (P12)

Nurses’ experiences in managing hypertension

From the participants’ point of view, nurses’ experiences in managing hypertension were shaped by several systemic factors. The subthemes included the use of clinical guidelines and protocols, and the challenges in managing patients with hypertension.

Clinical guidelines and protocols

Participants had limited awareness and poor adherence to any official or up-to-date protocols. Hypertension management on the ward was largely based on personal knowledge, past training, and doctors’ instructions rather than on written or accessible protocols. Many participants expressed uncertainty about whether such guidelines existed in the facility, and if they did, they had not been disseminated to the staff. A participant noted:We haven’t been given any written protocol here. If guidelines exist, they haven’t been shared with us, so we just manage patients with what we already know, rely on the doctors’ instructions, and use what we were taught back in school. (P12)

We haven’t been given any written protocol here. If guidelines exist, they haven’t been shared with us, so we just manage patients with what we already know, rely on the doctors’ instructions, and use what we were taught back in school. (P12)

Challenges in Managing Hypertension in the Hospital

Many participants regarded the challenges they faced in the hospital as a major barrier to effective hypertension management. The participants attested that heavy patient loads and inadequate staffing made it difficult to give individualised attention, while shortages of functioning equipment further delayed care. They also explained that personal financial sacrifices and excessive documentation reduced the time and energy they could dedicate to patient education and follow-up.I think managing hypertension here is very stressful… Sometimes, we are only two or three nurses for many patients, so we simply check their vitals and administer medicines without explaining much. At times, the BP machines are faulty or only one is available, so we all have to share and wait. We even use our own phone credit to call patients for reviews, but it is not refunded, and it becomes too expensive. The documentation is also too much. After each patient, you must record everything, and even when the shift is over, you cannot leave until all the records are completed. All these challenges, including the workload, lack of resources, financial pressure, and paperwork, make hypertension care very difficult. (P11)

Community and follow-up support

Community and follow-up support serve as ways to engage other systems outside the hospital in providing ongoing care and encouragement for people living with hypertension. Most participants explained that while they advised patients to continue care at the nearest clinic or hospital after discharge, there was no formal system for linking patients to community health workers or ensuring continuity of care. Participant shared:After discharge, we mostly just advise patients to continue care at a nearby clinic or health centre, but we don’t contact community health nurses or follow up directly. There is no formal system linking patients to providers outside the hospital, so they manage on their own until they return when unwell. (P5)

After discharge, we mostly just advise patients to continue care at a nearby clinic or health centre, but we don’t contact community health nurses or follow up directly. There is no formal system linking patients to providers outside the hospital, so they manage on their own until they return when unwell. (P5)

Peer support groups in the community

Peer support groups, which bring together individuals with similar experiences to share advice and encouragement, offer valuable psychosocial support for individuals living with hypertension. Yet, from participants’ perspectives, there is limited awareness of such initiatives, and they rarely inquired about patients’ involvement in them. Participant indicated:We don’t really ask about peer support groups, and most of us are not aware of patients being involved in them. Some have heard that churches or NGOs organise meetings, but generally we just advise patients to take care of themselves at home without linking them to any external support. (P9)

We don’t really ask about peer support groups, and most of us are not aware of patients being involved in them. Some have heard that churches or NGOs organise meetings, but generally we just advise patients to take care of themselves at home without linking them to any external support. (P9)

Follow-up via telephone calls

Telephone follow-ups were not consistently practised and were largely dependent on individual initiative rather than a structured system. Participants also attested that follow-up phone calls are not consistently practised, occurring only occasionally, with an estimated 10% of patients being contacted, and some patients voluntarily return to the ward during review appointments to provide updates on their condition. A participant said:Patients’ numbers are taken during admission, but this calling of patients is done once in a blue moon. We call occasionally, not all the time, on average, we would say only about 10%. Some patients also come to us on the ward during reviews to tell us how they are doing. (P14)

Patients’ numbers are taken during admission, but this calling of patients is done once in a blue moon. We call occasionally, not all the time, on average, we would say only about 10%. Some patients also come to us on the ward during reviews to tell us how they are doing. (P14)

Community pharmacies and clinics

Participants explained that they often encouraged patients to visit pharmacies or clinics to check their BP, especially if they could not travel to the hospital, and to record these values for review during follow-up visits.

A participant explained:I mostly tell patients to go to any pharmacy nearby to check their BP if they can’t come all the way here, and when their review date is up, come to the hospital. (P4)

I mostly tell patients to go to any pharmacy nearby to check their BP if they can’t come all the way here, and when their review date is up, come to the hospital. (P4)

Telehealth

Most participants had limited knowledge of telehealth and noted that such services were not currently available in their hospital. Some participants recommended its introduction as a way of supporting lifestyle changes, reinforcing medication adherence, and providing continuous education remotely.

A participant shared:I don’t really know much about telehealth, and here in this hospital, we don’t have such a system. But I think if it is introduced, it will really help remind patients about their medications, guide them with lifestyle changes, and educate them from home without always coming to the hospital.”(P14)

I don’t really know much about telehealth, and here in this hospital, we don’t have such a system. But I think if it is introduced, it will really help remind patients about their medications, guide them with lifestyle changes, and educate them from home without always coming to the hospital.”(P14)

Discussion

Adequate knowledge of hypertension management is essential for delivering high-quality care. However, the findings showed that participants’ understanding of hypertension was largely superficial. Most defined the condition simply as persistently elevated blood pressure, with limited awareness of underlying causes, risk factors, or evidence-based management strategies. This reflects the work of Adzitey et al. [43], who similarly observed that providers often recognise hypertension at a basic level without deeper clinical insight. Many participants focused almost exclusively on taking and recording blood pressure readings, with little emphasis on comprehensive assessments that could guide individualised care planning. These practices reflect the routine responsibilities of nurses, particularly those in outpatient departments (OPD), where initial screening and monitoring are prioritised, compared to ward nurses who are more involved in ongoing management and stabilisation of admitted patients. This finding aligns with Boonyasai et al. [44], who emphasised that inadequate assessment undermines both patient-centred and guideline-consistent hypertension care.

Knowledge of pharmacological management was similarly limited. While participants could identify commonly used antihypertensive medications, they showed only partial understanding of medication mechanisms and the rationale for specific drug choices. Many prioritised rapid blood pressure reductions over long-term management, a trend also reported by Azad [45]. Some participants expressed concerns about patient dependence on multiple medications without addressing underlying lifestyle or socioeconomic factors, echoing Arshed [46], who caution that overreliance on pharmacotherapy restricts sustainable hypertension control.

Nurses’ experiences in managing hypertension are essential, as their ability to provide effective and comprehensive care is strongly influenced by the availability of supportive health system structures, such as adequate staffing levels, functional equipment and resources, and clear access to clinical guidelines and protocols [47]. Nurses form an integral part of the hospital care system, with responsibilities varying by unit. OPD nurses primarily conduct initial assessments, monitor vitals, provide patient education, and coordinate referrals, while ward nurses provide ongoing monitoring, administer medications, and support adherence to treatment plans [48]. However, high patient loads, staff shortages, and administrative demands reduced the time available for comprehensive assessments and patient education. Similar constraints have been documented by Vedanthan et al. [49], who noted that heavy workloads hinder effective hypertension management. Participants also reported shortages of functional BP devices and educational materials, consistent with Hinneh et al. [47], who identified a lack of counselling tools as a barrier to effective communication.

Participants also demonstrated limited awareness and use of updated clinical guidelines. Many relied on personal experience or initial training rather than evidence-based protocols, leading to inconsistencies in treatment thresholds, BP targets, and lifestyle recommendations. This corresponds with Zuin et al. [50], who found that inadequate knowledge of clinical protocols contributes to variability in hypertension care. Both OPD and ward nurses rely on individual judgement rather than standardised role-guided practice. Participants attributed this gap to the absence of refresher courses, restricted access to continuous professional development, and a lack of up-to-date facility guidelines, consistent with Gyamfi et al. [26].

These challenges extended beyond the facility level to community follow-up. Participants reported weak coordination with community health workers (CHWs), often advising patients to seek follow-up care at local clinics without engaging CHWs to support continuity of care. This highlights gaps in the system of care, particularly in the expected role of OPD nurses in coordinating follow-up and ensuring continuity between facility and community care, while ward nurses have limited involvement after patient discharge. Similarly, Asambo et al. [51] identified persistent disconnects between facility-based providers and CHWs due to unclear referral pathways. Participants were also unaware of peer-support groups for hypertensive clients, limiting opportunities for shared learning and emotional support, an approach shown by Isiguzo et al. [52] to improve adherence and BP control.

Follow-up communication was similarly inconsistent. Participants acknowledged that telephone outreach was unstructured and infrequent, despite evidence from Taghinejad et al. [53] that structured follow-up strengthens continuity of care. A few participants encouraged patients to monitor their BP at community pharmacies or nearby clinics, which supports the findings of Marfo et al. [54] that community-based monitoring enhances adherence and BP control. This further underscores the role of nurses at both the OPD and the medical ward within the care system in supporting continuity of care.

Although understanding of telehealth was limited, participants expressed optimism about its potential for virtual consultations, SMS reminders, and remote BP monitoring. This aligns with Byiringiro et al. [28], who highlight telehealth as an effective strategy for enhancing hypertension management in resource-limited settings. The coexistence of nurses’ limited knowledge of telehealth and their encouragement of its use in this study highlights a significant rational tension. This finding likely reflects an acknowledgement of gaps in current care, rather than indicating informed approval of a specific digital approach, particularly for medication adherence, follow-up, and lifestyle support. The nurses’ views may hence be understood as aspirational, communicating ingenuousness towards innovations perceived to improve access and continuity of care for individuals living with Hypertension. Similarly, this has been reported in low- and middle-income countries [55, 56], where the motivation to engage with digital health solutions often precedes a detailed understanding of such interventions.

Nevertheless, operationalising telehealth demands context-sensitive approaches within the Ghanaian health system. Prioritising low-cost, widely accessible technologies such as voice calls, mobile phone–based SMS reminders, or the WhatsApp platform should be a considerable, feasible model. These could be incorporated into existing service delivery structures, including primary care and Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS). In sub-Saharan Africa, the evidence suggests that such advances can enhance adherence and follow-up when adapted to local contexts [57]. Nonetheless, Significant limitations, including limited digital literacy, limited health personnel capabilities, unpredictable network connectivity, and high mobile data costs, must be tackled to guarantee reasonable realisation. Furthermore, provider training, patient education, and supportive policy frameworks are fundamental for viable integration [56, 58].

Overall, the findings demonstrate that participants’ knowledge of hypertension management was limited, inconsistently applied, and further constrained by systemic and organisational barriers. Clinical decision-making relied heavily on routine and individual judgement rather than standardised guidelines, underscoring the need for regular training, updated protocols, and strengthened community linkages to improve hypertension outcomes. Efforts should focus on realistic, resource-appropriate telehealth approaches that support user capabilities and system realities to sustain informed uptake and engagement in District hospitals and related communities in the Ghanaian context.

Implications for nursing education and practice

Strengthening hypertension management among nurses requires policies that mandate regular professional training and certification for nurses and enforce standardised clinical guidelines across all healthcare levels. For clinical practice, nurses’ roles in hypertension management can be boosted by improving their knowledge and skills in understanding the condition, identifying risks, and creating personalised care plans beyond routine BP checks. Care should include clear, structured self-management education on monitoring blood pressure, recognising symptoms, making healthy lifestyle changes, and taking medicines correctly. Ensure adequate staffing to allow quality counselling, follow-up, and consistent access to essential resources such as patient education materials. Training in digital tools like mobile apps and telehealth can help extend patient education and follow-up outside the hospital. Regular use of updated hypertension guidelines will ensure consistent and effective care, and building stronger connections with community health workers, peer groups, and pharmacies can improve follow-up and prevent patients from being lost to care.

Limitations of the study

The study sample included only female registered nurses who were working on the medical wards and the outpatient department of a district hospital. However, this does not undermine the data but needs to be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. Future studies could look at comparing the experiences and perspectives of both female and male nursing staff populations in a similar facility, in their role in managing patients with hypertension.

The nurses’ caring role in this study was not categorised into patient-aged groups. Different patient-aged populations may have different care needs, and therefore, the nurse management role may be different. Further studies can explore the nurses’ role in the management of patients with hypertension in different age groups.

Again, the sample size and single setting that were used for the study might limit the generalizability of findings to other facilities in Ghana. Nonetheless, the findings support the development of policies that are relevant to nursing practices in the role of the nurse in caring for patients with hypertension in settings with limited healthcare resources.

Additionally, because the researchers were nurse educators involved in the training of nurses, there was a possibility of power dynamics between them and the participants. This could have influenced the data collection process. However, the researchers bracketed themselves and tried to lessen this situation during participant selection and data collection by emphasising the need for voluntary participation, and the need to maintain privacy in the reporting of the study findings and adhering strictly to ethical measures throughout the study.

Conclusion

This study explored nurses’ roles in hypertension management and identified some key domains: knowledge of hypertension management, nurses’ experiences in managing hypertension, and community and follow-up support. The findings highlight that while nurses play a central role in hypertension control, their efforts are constrained by limited knowledge, systemic constraints and weak community linkages. Strengthening continuous professional training, ensuring access to clinical resources, ensuring adequate staffing, and promoting structured community engagement are essential for improving care quality. Policymakers and healthcare managers should prioritise capacity building and supportive systems that empower nurses to deliver evidence-based, patient-centred care for hypertension. By advancing these measures, nurses can play an even greater role in reducing hypertension-related complications and improving cardiovascular outcomes in Ghana.
