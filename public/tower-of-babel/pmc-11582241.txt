Heartfelt Impact: A Descriptive Analysis of Ceftaroline-Containing Regimens in Endocarditis due to Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Kaylee E. Caniff, Chloe Judd, Kristen Lucas, Sandra Goro, Caroline Orzol, Mirna Eshaya, Mohammed Al Musawa, Michael P. Veve, Michael J. Rybak
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionInfective endocarditis (IE) due to methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is characterized by frequent treatment failure to first-line agents and high mortality, necessitating use of alternative management strategies. Ceftaroline fosamil (CPT) is a cephalosporin antibiotic with activity against MRSA but without regulatory approval for the indication of IE. This study describes clinical experience with CPT-based regimens utilized in MRSA-IE.MethodsThis is a retrospective, observational, descriptive analysis of patients from two major urban medical centers in Detroit, Michigan from 2011 to 2023. Included adult patients (≥ 18 years) had ≥ 1 positive blood culture for MRSA, met definitive clinical criteria for IE, and received CPT for ≥ 72 h. The primary outcome was treatment failure, defined as a composite of 30-day all-cause mortality from index culture or failure to improve or resolve infectious signs/symptoms after CPT initiation.ResultsSeventy patients were included. The median (interquartile range [IQR]) age was 51 (34–63) years and 45.7% were male. Persons with injection drug use (PWID) made up 55.7% of the cohort and right-sided IE was the most prevalent subtype (50.0%). CPT was frequently employed second-line or later, often in combination with vancomycin (10.0%) or daptomycin (72.9%). Overall, 31.4% experienced treatment failure and 30-day all-cause mortality occurred in 15.7%.ConclusionsThese findings illustrate the challenges posed by MRSA-IE, including frequent treatment failures, and highlight the utilization of CPT as salvage therapy. Comparative studies are needed to more clearly define its role in MRSA-IE.

Introduction

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a predominant etiology for infective endocarditis (IE) due to its propensity to invade the endothelium [1–4]. MRSA-IE is characterized by high mortality, especially if the left side of the heart is affected or a prosthetic valve is present [1, 2, 5, 6]. Although vancomycin is the current standard of care for serious MRSA infections, its treatment failure is well described, particularly in complicated infections such as IE [1, 7, 8]. Daptomycin is an alternative therapeutic option, but there is concern regarding cross-resistance when daptomycin follows vancomycin failure [9, 10]. Given these challenges, investigating alternative treatment regimens for MRSA-IE is warranted.

Ceftaroline fosamil (CPT) is an advanced-generation cephalosporin antibiotic with broad activity, including against MRSA [11]. It is approved in the USA for the treatment of community-acquired pneumonia and skin/soft tissue infections [12]. Although it lacks a regulatory indication for the treatment of MRSA bacteremia and IE, its off-label use for these diagnoses has been described primarily through observational studies [13–20]. The most extensive analysis describing clinical experience with CPT in IE was reported in CAPTURE, a phase IV retrospective registry study, which included 55 patients treated with CPT for IE caused by any Gram-positive pathogen [19]. However, it was not exclusively focused on MRSA and provided limited details on prior and concurrent therapies administered with CPT. Consequently, there remains a gap in the literature regarding the real-world use of CPT in MRSA-IE. Our study aims to fill this gap by further describing CPT-containing regimens among patients with confirmed MRSA-IE.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

This is a retrospective, observational, descriptive analysis of patients who received CPT for the treatment of MRSA-IE at Henry Ford Health and the Detroit Medical Center, two urban healthcare systems located in Detroit, Michigan. Included patients came from one of five acute care hospitals at Henry Ford Health, equating to approximately 2400 acute care beds total, or one of four acute care hospitals from Detroit Medical Center, equating to approximately 1200 beds total. Both healthcare systems include level 1 trauma centers and possess academic affiliations. At both healthcare systems, CPT is available for prescribing in consultation with the infectious disease (ID) service and considered for use in patients with persistently positive blood cultures (> 3–5 days) or patients demonstrating lack of improvement on first-line regimens (vancomycin or daptomycin alone or in combination with other therapies).

Adult patients (≥ 18 years of age) were eligible for inclusion if they had ≥ 1 positive blood culture for MRSA between December 2011 and November 2023, met the 2023 Duke clinical criteria for definitive endocarditis, and received ≥ 72 h of CPT [21]. Excluded patients were those with a polymicrobial index culture, those who were pregnant/nursing, or a prisoner.

Ethical Approval

This study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments. Approval for this study was obtained from the Henry Ford Healthcare Institutional Review Board (#16481-01) and the Wayne State University Institutional Review Board (#22-11-5172) with Detroit Medical Center research authorization. This study received a waiver for the requirement of patient consent given the retrospective design.

Data Collection and Study Definitions

Patient data was collected from the electronic medical record and entered into the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap, Vanderbilt University) tool, hosted by Wayne State University [22]. Extracted data included demographics, comorbidities, antibiotic therapy administration details, relevant laboratory parameters, diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, microbiologic information, and ID consult information. Microbiologic data included organism identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing that was performed by Microscan (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Malvern, PA, USA), Vitek-2 (bioMérieux, Durham, NC, USA), Phoenix (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), and/or Etest (bioMérieux) depending on the admission date and institution. Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) definitions were utilized to characterize susceptibility testing results [23]. Severity of illness was characterized using the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score based upon the worst physiologic parameters within 24 h of the index blood culture [24]. Baseline comorbidity burden was described with the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [25]. The duration of bacteremia was calculated from the time of index culture draw until the time of the first available negative blood culture. Combination therapy was defined as co-administration of ≥ 1 anti-MRSA agents for ≥ 24 h.

The primary outcome was treatment failure, defined as a composite of mortality from any cause within 30 days of the index blood culture collection or the failure to resolve or improve infectious signs and/or symptoms following the initiation of CPT until the end of therapy. This includes persistent infection-related leukocytosis, abnormal temperature, or the need for an antibiotic change due to lack of clinical improvement as documented by the treating medical team in the electronic medical record. Secondary outcomes included 30-day all-cause mortality, 90-day all-cause mortality, and 90-day recurrence from the end of treatment, defined as blood culture positivity for MRSA up to 90 days after the treatment end. Safety outcomes assessed the occurrence of possible adverse effect temporally associated with CPT administration, defined as occurring after CPT initiation and up to 72 h following its discontinuation. Adverse effects potentially attributed to CPT included nephrotoxicity, rash/dermatologic reaction, allergic reaction, hematologic reactions, electrolyte disturbance, Clostridioides difficile infection, central nervous system disturbances including altered mental status or seizure onset, gastrointestinal disturbances (nausea, vomiting, and/or diarrhea) or hepatoxicity. Nephrotoxicity was defined as a serum creatinine increase ≥ 0.5 mg/dL or 50% from baseline; thrombocytopenia was defined as a decrease in platelets to < 150,000/μL from baseline if baseline was > 150,000/μL or a > 50% decrease from baseline if baseline ≤ 150,000/μL; neutropenia was defined as an absolute neutrophile count (ANC) to < 1500 cells/mm3 or a 50% decrease in ANC if ANC < 1500 cells/mm3; C. difficile infection was defined as a positive C. difficile test accompanied by clinical signs/symptoms; electrolyte disturbance was defined as an electrolyte value (i.e., sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium) above or below normal limits that was documented as potentially related to CPT; and hepatotoxicity was defined as elevations in alanine aminotransaminase (ALT) and/or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) ≥ 3 times the upper limit of normal.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the baseline demographics, clinical characteristics, treatment variables, and outcomes. Categorical variables were described with count and frequency. Normally distributed continuous variables were described with mean (standard deviation) and non-normally distributed variables were described with median (interquartile range [IQR]). Clinical outcomes were assessed for the overall cohort and subgroups of interest. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest report to date describing the use of CPT-containing regimens among patients meeting clinical criteria for definitive MRSA-IE. This cohort predominantly comprised younger patients, many of whom were PWID. As a well-known risk factor for MRSA-IE reported in the literature, injection drug use likely contributed to the high incidence of right-sided IE observed in this group [1]. These findings also mirror global epidemiological data as the incidence of MRSA-IE attributable to injection drug use is increasing and nearly two-thirds of IE cases overall occur in PWID internationally [2].

We observed that CPT was likely utilized as part of salvage therapy regimens in most patients as it was frequently administered as second-line therapy or later following an initial vancomycin- or daptomycin-based regimen. The necessity for salvage therapy may have been exacerbated by the low incidence of cardiac surgical interventions in this cohort. Despite reports indicating that surgical intervention rates have increased over past decades and typically approach 50%, only 14.3% of patients underwent a cardiac-related surgery in this study [1, 26, 27]. Many of the included patients were likely to have been indicated for surgical intervention given that prolonged bacteremia was common and there was a high frequency of vascular events [1]. It is unclear if the low surgical rate reflects a general practice pattern at the study sites, if patients were deemed to be poor surgical candidates, or if surgical intervention was avoided because of the high incidence of PWID. Nonetheless, these findings demonstrate the importance of investigating optimal regimens for medical management of MRSA-IE in patients who are unsuitable for surgical intervention. In such patients who are not surgical candidates, CPT, especially when used in combination regimens, may represent a viable therapeutic option.

We found that CPT was frequently administered in combination with an additional anti-MRSA antibiotic, most frequently daptomycin. Prolonged combination therapy was common, as the median duration of combination treatment was 9 days when CPT was administered with either vancomycin or daptomycin. In vitro studies have previously demonstrated synergy between vancomycin or daptomycin with CPT, even after the emergence of daptomycin resistance [28, 29]. Clinical studies of patients with MRSA bacteremia have also reported successful use of this combination; however, robust comparative studies with standard of care therapeutic regimens are lacking [30–33]. Retrospective, observational studies attempting to compare these treatments may be difficult to perform. Those receiving CPT in combination with vancomycin or daptomycin are likely at higher risk of poor outcomes at baseline given CPT regimens are often administered late in the disease course after failure of previous regimens in real-world practice, as we also observed in the present study [30, 31, 33]. Notably, a previous clinical trial among patients with MRSA bacteremia was halted early because of the unexpected finding of 0% mortality among patients receiving daptomycin with CPT compared to vancomycin or daptomycin monotherapy [34].

The clinical failure rate observed herein is slightly higher than observed among MRSA-IE cases included in CAPTURE, where presumably 22.7% of cases experienced clinical failure utilizing a similar outcome definition [19]. However, a direct comparison is challenging because the CAPTURE study did not report illness severity scores, surgical interventions, or detailed information regarding combination therapy and prior regimens. Regarding mortality, we found that rates were similar or better than rates reported within the literature for MRSA-IE, even considering rates among right- and left-sided IE subgroups [2]. This is notable, given that patients in our cohort likely failed one or more therapeutic regimens prior to receiving CPT. The potential synergy between CPT and co-administered daptomycin or vancomycin may have contributed to this outcome [28, 29].

CPT demonstrated a favorable safety profile. Although nephrotoxicity occurred in 12.9% of patients during CPT administration, a causal relationship with this possible adverse drug effect cannot be established. This rate is higher than what was observed in CPT clinical trials and likely reflects the severity of illness and comorbidities of the population, rather than nephrotoxicity induced by CPT administration [35, 36]. Moreover, IE due to Staphylococcus spp. has itself independently been identified as a risk factor for acute kidney injury [37]. Overall, CPT appears to be safe when utilized for extended durations observed in this study.

There are several limitations to this study. Firstly, this is a retrospective analysis with a small sample size lacking a comparator group. Furthermore, this study is limited to two healthcare centers in Detroit, MI; thus, epidemiologic trends, CPT prescribing patterns, and clinical outcomes may not be broadly generalizable. Moreover, CPT susceptibility data was limited in this cohort. However, the 29 isolates with available susceptibility information were all susceptible to CPT, aligning with epidemiologic data that has demonstrated that CPT has high in vitro activity against MRSA isolates collected from blood [38]. Finally, there were a variety of CPT dosage regimens prescribed with substantial deviation from the manufacturer’s package insert recommendations [12]. Rationale for CPT regimen selection was not collected as part of this study; however, it was likely influenced by the severity of infection and renal function. As a result of the small sample size, definitive conclusions about the optimal CPT regimen cannot be made. Further studies are necessary to determine the most effective dosing strategy for CPT in IE.

Conclusion

In summary, this study offers valuable insights into the use of CPT for treatment of MRSA-IE, particularly be detailing combination regimens utilized in this cohort. The findings also highlight the need for prospective, comparative studies to further elucidate the efficacy and safety of CPT in MRSA-IE. Key unanswered clinical questions are related to defining optimal dosing, duration of combination therapy, and potential synergistic effects with other antibiotics. Research efforts to optimize and define CPT’s role in care are essential for reducing the burden of MRSA-IE-associated morbidity and mortality, which remains a significant challenge despite advances in management strategies.
