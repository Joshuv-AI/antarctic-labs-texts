ABSTRACT
On 18 December 2023, a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\textrm{s}}$$\end{document} 6.2 earthquake struck Jishishan, Gansu Province, northwestern China. The resulting aftershock sequence provides an important opportunity to investigate the seismogenic structure and postseismic activity in the source region. In this study, we analyzed continuous waveform data recorded by 31 seismic stations within 250 km of the epicenter from 19 to 31 December 2023 to construct a high-resolution earthquake catalog. Seismic phases were automatically detected using the deep-learning phase picker PhaseNet, followed by phase association and preliminary location with the REAL algorithm. Absolute location were then refined using VELEST, and relative relocations were performed using HypoDD. The resulting catalog contains more than 3200 earthquakes, more than three times the number reported in the routine catalog of the China Earthquake Networks Center (CENC), substantially improving the completeness of the aftershock sequence. The relocated events are mainly concentrated at depths of 5–16 km within an aftershock zone approximately 18 km in length and 13 km in width, defining two major aftershock clusters. Fault-structure analysis indicates that the aftershocks exhibit a clear NW–SE alignment and are primarily concentrated between the northern and southern marginal faults of the Lajishan fault zone. These observations suggest that the mainshock likely ruptured a secondary or previously unmapped fault located between the two major mapped faults, whereas the subsequent sequence reflects a complex damage zone driven by stress redistribution. The high-resolution earthquake catalog presented here provides new constraints on the detailed structure of the aftershock sequence and contributes to a better understanding of the seismogenic setting of the region.

Introduction

High-resolution earthquake catalogs provide a fundamental basis for investigating the evolution of seismic sequences and for delineating the geometry of causative faults1–5. The completeness and location accuracy of a catalog directly influence interpretations of the spatiotemporal distribution of seismicity and the underlying physical processes within the source region. Compared with focal mechanism inversion or geodetic observations6, precise earthquake location can delineate fault geometry and segmentation more directly through the spatial distribution of hypocenters7–9. Therefore, the construction of a high-resolution earthquake catalog is a prerequisite for detailed structural analysis of the source region.

The development of such catalogs relies critically on robust earthquake detection and phase picking. With the continuous expansion of regional seismic networks and the progressive implementation of earthquake early warning systems, the volume of continuous waveform data has increased substantially. For example, a relatively dense network with an average station spacing of approximately 30 km has been established in Gansu Province, generating large volumes of continuous records. Traditional manual phase picking and association are no longer sufficient to meet the demands of rapid processing and real-time monitoring, and they may also limit catalog completeness. In this context, machine-learning-based approaches10, particularly deep-learning methods11–14, have emerged as efficient tools for automatic phase identification. These approaches enable the detection of seismic events and the identification of P- and S-wave arrivals directly from continuous waveform data15, which can then be combined with phase association and high-precision location algorithms to produce reliable earthquake catalogs in an automated manner11,16.

In recent years, such workflows have been successfully applied to several well-recorded earthquake sequences10,17–20. Reference21 employed the PhaseNet model for phase picking, together with phase association and both absolute and relative relocation procedures, to construct a high-resolution catalog of the 2019 Ridgecrest earthquake sequence. Reference22 applied a similar strategy to the 2016–2017 central Italy seismic sequence and constructed a high-precision catalog containing nearly 900,000 earthquakes, about an order of magnitude more than the routine catalog. The resulting aftershock distribution clearly delineated complex fault structures activated during the sequence. Reference23 used PhaseNet to automatically pick P- and S-wave arrivals recorded by a dense array along the Anninghe–Xiaojiang fault zone. Their catalog contained approximately three times as many events as the routine catalog of the China Earthquake Networks Center (CENC) and was subsequently used in double-difference tomography to derive a high-resolution crustal velocity model. In this study, we adopt the general processing framework of Loc-Flow24 to analyze continuous waveform data recorded during the 13 days following the 2023 Jishishan earthquake. Phase arrivals were first automatically picked using PhaseNet25, followed by phase association and preliminary location with the REAL algorithm26. Absolute location was then refined using VELEST27, and relative relocations were performed with HypoDD28 to obtain a high-precision earthquake catalog. We analyze the spatiotemporal characteristics of the resulting catalog and use depth cross-sections, together with the results from the E2F program, to investigate the seismogenic fault geometry. Finally, we compare our catalog with that the reported by Zhang et al.29 to evaluate the consistency and differences between the two catalogs. This comparison highlights that our catalog achieves a high recovery rate while relying on a relatively limited amount of waveform data.

Data and methods

Data

The waveform data analyzed in this study were recorded between 19 and 31 December 2023 following the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\textrm{s}}$$\end{document} 6.2 Jishishan earthquake. A total of 31 seismic stations located within 250 km of the epicenter were used, including nine permanent seismic stations (light-yellow triangles in Fig. 1), 12 early-warning stations (pink triangles in Fig. 1), and 10 temporary stations (orange triangles in Fig. 1). All stations were equipped with broadband sensors sampling at 100 Hz. The total data volume was approximately 40 GB. The analysis period was limited to the first 13 days following the mainshock. This window was selected because it captures the most active phase of the sequence, including the largest aftershock (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_L$$\end{document} 4.6) and all events with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_L \ge$$\end{document} 4.0, after which seismicity decreased markedly. Methods

The high-resolution earthquake catalog in this study was constructed following the general Loc-Flow workflow24, a step-wise relocation strategy designed to progressively improve earthquake locations (Fig. 2). First, seismic phases were automatically detected and picked from continuous waveform data using the deep-learning phase picker PhaseNet25. Second, these phase picks were associated into earthquake events and assigned preliminary hypocenters using the Rapid Earthquake Association and Location (REAL) algorithm26. After the initial association, local magnitudes were estimated for the detected events. The event locations were then further refined through a sequence of absolute and relative relocation procedures. Specifically, the REAL-associated events were first relocated using VELEST27 to obtain improved absolute location. The VELEST-derived hypocenters were then used as the input catalog for HypoDD_dtct relocation, and the resulting catalog was subsequently employed as the initial catalog for the final HypoDD_dtcc relocation28. The resulting HypoDD_dtcc catalog was regarded as the final high-resolution earthquake catalog in this study.

During the PhaseNet phase-picking stage, the probability threshold was set to 0.5 based on empirical testing. An example of the picking results is shown in Fig. 3. The REAL algorithm associates seismic phases by analyzing the number of P- and S-wave picks and performs preliminary earthquake location using a grid-search strategy that minimizes travel-time residuals. The preliminary hypocentre location was determined from the grid cell containing the largest number of associated picks. In this study, the horizontal search range in REAL was set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.5^{\circ }$$\end{document} with a grid spacing of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.03^{\circ }$$\end{document}, while the depth search range was set to 30 km with a grid interval of 3 km. The search grid was centered on the station that first recorded the P-wave arrival. Only events associated with a sufficient number of phase picks were retained. Specifically, we required a minimum of 4 P-wave picks, 2 S-wave picks, and at least 6 total picks per event. Furthermore, both P- and S-wave arrivals must be recorded by at least 2 stations. Using these criteria, 27,066 P-wave arrivals and 28,889 S-wave arrivals (Fig. 4) were successfully associated to 3577 events. The REAL catalog was subsequently refined using VELEST to obtain improved absolute earthquake location. During this step, only seismic observations recorded within 250 km of the epicenter and having travel-time residuals smaller than 0.5 s were retained, resulting in 3561 events with reliable absolute location.

Relative relocation was then performed using the HypoDD algorithm. Two relocation strategies were applied: the differential travel-time method based on catalog picks (HypoDD_dtct) and the waveform cross-correlation method (HypoDD_dtcc) implemented with FDTCC31,32.

During the phase-pairing stage using the ph2dt program, the maximum distance between event pairs and stations was set to 120 km, while the maximum distance between event pairs was set to 10 km. The maximum number of neighboring events for each event was set to 50. The minimum number of links required both to define a neighbor and to retain an event pair was set to eight, and the maximum number of links between event pairs was limited to 100. Using the HypoDD_dtct method, 3268 events were successfully relocated.

For waveform cross-correlation using FDTCC, the maximum time-shift window was set to 0.5–1.0 s for P waves and 0.3–1.0 s for S waves. The maximum sliding window length for both phases was set to 0.5 s. No additional band-pass filtering was applied during the FDTCC calculation, because the filter parameter was set to B=-1/-1. The cross-correlation coefficient threshold was set to 0.7 and the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) threshold was set to 1, and the maximum absolute time difference between the two picks was set to 2 s. In the ph2dt stage, both MINLNK and MINOBS were set to 8, meaning that event pairs were retained only when they were supported by at least eight differential-time links. In the subsequent HypoDD_dtcc relocation, the clustering parameter OBSCC was also set to 8 to impose a consistent minimum constraint on the cross-correlation differential-time data used for cluster construction. Therefore, an event pair was retained only when at least eight valid cross-correlation differential-time observations were available. Using the HypoDD_dtcc approach, 3238 events were finally relocated.

Before the HypoDD_dtcc relocation, the phase file was updated using the HypoDD_dtct relocation results following the Loc_Flow procedure. In this step, the relocated hypocentral coordinates and depths from HypoDD_dtct were used as the initial locations for HypoDD_dtcc, while the original event origin times and observed P/S pick information were retained. This preserves the absolute observed arrival times used by FDTCC to extract waveform windows for cross-correlation.

We also tested a lower minimum-link threshold of 6. Although this setting increased the number of relocated events by slightly more than 100, the number of CENC-matched events increased by only one. The mean origin-time difference remained nearly unchanged, while the mean epicentral difference slightly increased. Moreover, the epicentral distribution and depth profiles remained essentially unchanged. Therefore, the original threshold of 8 was retained in the final relocation.

Results

Analysis and evaluation of the catalogs

After automatic phase picking and absolute location using VELEST, a total of 3561 earthquakes were obtained. Subsequent relative relocation using HypoDD_dtct resulted in 3261 events, and the final relocation using HypoDD_dtcc produced 3238 events (Fig. 5). The increased number of detected earthquakes provides a much denser image of aftershock activity in the source region.

From the perspective of spatial distribution, the relocated aftershock epicenters are markedly more clustered than those from the initial location. The aftershock activity delineates two distinct seismic clusters within the source region. This dual-cluster pattern is clearly expressed in both the HypoDD_dtct and HypoDD_dtcc relocation results and is highlighted by elliptical outlines in Fig. 5c,d.

To comprehensively evaluate the reliability of our earthquake catalog and address potential structural artifacts caused by depth uncertainties, we analyzed the location errors for both the initial absolute location and the final relative relocation.

For the initial absolute location derived from VELEST (Fig. 6), the results demonstrate a well-constrained catalog. The vast majority of events have an azimuthal station gap of less than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$150^{\circ }$$\end{document}, with a dominant peak below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$50^{\circ }$$\end{document} (Fig. 6a), indicating excellent spatial coverage by the seismic network. The root-mean-square (RMS) travel-time residuals are predominantly distributed between 0.1 s and 0.4 s, peaking at approximately 0.3 s (Fig. 6b). These values are well within the acceptable threshold of 0.5 s for regional 1D velocity model inversions, providing a robust starting point for subsequent analyses.

Furthermore, the inclusion of waveform cross-correlation data in the HypoDD inversion (HypoDD_dtcc) significantly improved the relative location precision (Fig. 7). The relative horizontal and depth errors are tightly constrained, mostly ranging from 2 to 6 meters (Fig. 7a,b). The RMS of the double-difference residuals is sharply peaked at approximately 5 to 7 ms (Fig. 7c), achieving sub-sample precision. Such minimal relative depth uncertainties (on the order of a few meters) strongly confirm that the sharp geometric features observed in the cross-sections, such as the vertically distributed seismicity on the NW part of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{AA}^\prime$$\end{document} profile represent genuine seismogenic fault structures rather than artificial vertical smearing caused by poor depth estimates.

In addition, we compared the HypoDD_dtcc catalog constructed in this study with the CENC catalog, which contains 903 events during the study period. The HypoDD_dtcc catalog includes more than three times as many events as the CENC catalog. Overall, 94.8% of the events in the CENC catalog can be matched to those in the HypoDD_dtcc catalog. E2F-inferred fault geometry

To further constrain the fault structures associated with the aftershock sequence, the relocated seismicity was analyzed using the E2F program34. Figure 11a shows the planar distribution of candidate fault structures identified by the Hough transform. At this stage, a total of 12 candidate faults were detected, and 3101 earthquakes were used in the fault-identification process. The aftershock distribution shows a clear NW–SE alignment, which is consistent with the regional tectonic trend.

During the subsequent fault-structure modeling stage, clustering analysis was applied to merge and refine the candidate faults, resulting in four major fault structures (Fig. 11b). In three-dimensional space, the aftershocks form a well-defined linear zone that extends downward along the dip direction.

Combining the E2F results with the regional fault framework indicates that most aftershocks are concentrated between the LJS-NF and LJS-SF faults. The seismicity defines a continuous active zone between these two mapped faults. This pattern suggests that rather than occurring strictly along either of the two major mapped faults or a single continuous secondary fault, the Jishishan earthquake sequence likely occurred within a structurally complex damage zone between the LJS-NF and LJS-SF faults. This seismicity distribution may reflect the activation of a network of pre-existing micro-fractures, driven by the redistribution of stress (e.g., Coulomb stress changes) following the mainshock.

Furthermore, the three-dimensional modeling results reveal two relatively compact clusters of seismicity along the fault trend, which is consistent with the clustering pattern revealed by the HypoDD relocation results. Discussion

As a further benchmark, we compared our catalog with that of Zhang et al.29, who also constructed an enhanced catalog for the 2023 Jishishan \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\textrm{s}}$$\end{document} 6.2 earthquake sequence using the Loc-Flow. To enable a consistent comparison, we extracted from Zhang et al.’s catalog the events within the same 13-day time window considered in this study.

Although Zhang et al. utilized additional temporary nodal stations near the epicentral area, the study region covered by the two catalogs is essentially the same. The additional stations mainly increased the station density around the source region rather than extending the spatial coverage of the catalog.

A CENC event was considered to be matched if at least one event in the enhanced catalog occurred within 5 s in origin time and within 10 km in epicentral location. When multiple candidate events satisfied these criteria, only the closest event was retained to ensure a one-to-one match.

The resulting subcatalog extracted from Zhang et al. contains 6724 events and shows a matching rate of 96.8% (875/903) relative to the CENC catalog, which contains 903 events. In comparison, our 13-day catalog contains 3238 events and has a matching rate of 94.8% (856/903). Although our catalog contains substantially fewer events, the difference in matching rate is only about 2 percentage points, indicating that our workflow recovers most of the routine catalog events.

It should be noted that both studies adopted the Loc-Flow workflow with PhaseNet phase picking. Moreover, the REAL association thresholds used in Zhang et al.29 were stricter than those adopted in this study. Therefore, the larger number of events in Zhang et al.’s catalog is unlikely to result from looser association parameters, and is more likely related to the denser temporary nodal stations deployed near the epicentral area. Accordingly, the purpose of this comparison is not to rank the catalogs solely by the total number of detections, but rather to show that our workflow can recover most routine catalog events while producing a comparatively smaller event set.

It should be noted that we did not apply a common magnitude threshold in this comparison. Although both this study and the CENC catalog use the Chinese local magnitude formula, the specific amplitude measurements are not fully identical because the waveform processing and amplitude-selection procedures differ. Consequently, the reported magnitudes are not strictly one-to-one comparable between catalogs.

In addition to the catalog comparison discussed above, some uncertainty remains in the absolute event location because the 1D velocity model cannot fully account for 3D crustal heterogeneities. Future studies incorporating source-specific station terms (SSST)37 or 3D velocity models may further reduce these travel-time errors and better constrain the absolute geometry of the seismogenic structures.

Conclusions

Seismic phases were automatically detected using PhaseNet, followed by phase association with REAL, absolute relocation using VELEST, and two different HypoDD-based relocation methods.
