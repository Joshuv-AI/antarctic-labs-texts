Numerical approximation of the first p p -Laplace eigenpair
Hannah Potgieter, Razvan C. Fetecau, Steven J. Ruuth

Abstract
We approximate the first Dirichlet eigenpair of the p p -Laplace operator for 2 ≤ p < ∞ 2\leq p<\infty on both Euclidean and surface domains. We emphasize large p p values and discuss how the p → ∞ p\to\infty limit connects to the underlying geometry of our domain. Working with large p p values introduces significant numerical challenges. We present a surface finite element numerical scheme that combines a Newton inverse-power iteration with a new domain rescaling strategy, which enables stable computations for large p p . Numerical experiments in 1 1 D, planar domains, and surfaces embedded in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} demonstrate the accuracy and robustness of our approach and show convergence towards the p → ∞ p\to\infty limiting behavior. Keywords: p p -Laplacian, infinity Laplacian, nonlinear eigenvalue problems, surface finite element method, Dirichlet eigenvalue problems, p → ∞ p\to\infty limit



1
Introduction

The
p
-Laplacian is a nonlinear generalization of the classical Laplacian operator, given in Euclidean domains by

\Delta_{p}u=\text{div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u),

where
2\leq p<\infty
is the considered range of
p
values. Note that
p=2
reduces to the linear Laplace operator. For surface (and more generally, manifold) domains, the differential operators are intrinsic and
p=2
reduces to the Laplace-Beltrami operator. In this work, we are concerned with the numerical approximation of the first Dirichlet
p
-Laplace eigenpair on both Euclidean and surface domains, and in particular we investigate large
p
values.

As
p
becomes large, numerically handling the
p
-Laplace operator presents challenges and has significant geometric implications associated with the bounded domain
\Omega
under consideration. This motivates our numerical study. Approximating the first eigenpair of the
p
-Laplacian for large
p
introduces several difficulties. The
p
-Laplacian is highly nonlinear and becomes increasingly degenerate as
p
increases
[
37
]
.
For the nonlinear case (
p>2
) we have eigenfunctions lying in
C^{1,\sigma}(\overline{\Omega})
where
0<\sigma<1

[
4
,
5
,
25
]
, which means the numerical approximation method needs to be robust against reduced smoothness. Several numerical methods have been employed to approximate the first eigenpair of the
p
-Laplacian
[
8
,
21
,
35
,
6
]
. We build on these methods and employ a surface finite element method (SFEM) discretization combined with a Newton inverse-power iteration scheme.

The study of the first eigenpair of the
p
-Laplace operator has attracted attention, both theoretically and numerically. The
p\to\infty
limit has connections with the geometry of the underlying domain. Juutinen et al.
[
22
]
established that the
p
th root of the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{p}
converges to the inverse of the maximum distance-to-boundary, i.e.,
\lambda_{p}^{1/p}\to\|\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)\|_{\infty}^{-1}

as
p\to\infty
, and as noted in
[
20
]
, this also holds for surface domains.
Further, the first eigenfunction, suitably normalized, converges to the distance-to-boundary function under a certain symmetry assumption on the domain, which we discuss in Section
3
.
Also, Barles
[
4
]
and Kawohl & Lindqvist
[
26
]
proved in the Euclidean setting that the first eigenpair is unique (modulo scaling the eigenfunction) and that the first eigenfunction is positive. There are some works which extend some of these results beyond Euclidean space. Grosjean
[
20
]
extended the eigenvalue limit as
p\to\infty
to Riemannian manifolds, and Belloni et al.
[
5
]
examined the entire limiting eigenvalue problem on domains equipped with Finsler metrics.

In the one-dimensional case, we can characterize the first eigenpair of the
p
-Laplacian explicitly. This is the only case for which we know the explicit eigenpairs for any
p\in[2,\infty]
, as derived by Lindqvist
[
28
]
and Edmunds & Lang
[
18
]
using generalized trigonometry. For our numerical purposes, these explicit expressions of the eigenpairs allow us to test the performance of our numerical schemes on exact solutions.

Numerically, we may use the inverse-power method to update the eigenvalue and eigenpair alongside one another. In Euclidean domains, Bozorgnia
[
11
]
proved convergence to the unique first eigenpair by alternately updating
\lambda_{p}
and
u_{p}
. However, existing numerical approaches are not robust against large
p
and numerical literature which considers surface domains is presented for specialized cases. El Soufi et al.
[
19
]
numerically approximate the first eigenpair for low
p
values on the unit sphere by discretizing the radially symmetric ordinary differential equation. Lanza et al.
[
27
]
consider surfaces represented as graphs also for low
p
values, primarily
p\in(1,2)
. Finite element, constrained descent, and spline-based formulations
[
8
,
21
,
35
,
6
]
have all been implemented for Euclidean domains, but report results only for small values of
p
(up to about
p=10
).
Recently, Bozorgnia et al.
[
10
]
introduced a monotone scheme specifically for the
p=\infty
case and report results on planar domains. To our knowledge, no works have yet bridged the gap between (small) finite
p
and the
p\to\infty
limit.

Studying the first Dirichlet
p
-Laplace eigenpair for large
p
values is important both computationally and in applications such as shape optimization
[
1
,
33
]
. From a numerical standpoint, an accurate approximation of the first eigenpair is a prerequisite for algorithms that compute higher modes, since these typically rely on the principal mode as an initialization or reference
[
21
,
39
,
27
]
. In addition to its algorithmic role, the first eigenpair itself carries meaningful geometric information: as
p
increases, the eigenfunction develops a distance-like structure in the large
p
limit that reflects the shape of the domain.

Beyond the first eigenpair, several works in the Euclidean setting have focused on the computation of the second Dirichlet
p
-Laplacian eigenpair through its characterization as an optimal bi-partition problem. In symmetric domains, Bozorgnia and Arakelyan
[
9
]
exploit this structure to construct an algorithm specifically targeting the second eigenpair, while also emphasizing the associated optimal bi-partitioning problem as an application in its own right. More generally, Bobkov and Galimov
[
7
]
propose an iterative nodal-balancing framework that is motivated by the second eigenvalue problem but whose convergence behavior depends on the choice of initialization, and may therefore lead to higher eigenpairs in practice. The use of
p
-Laplace eigenpairs has recently attracted attention in modern data-driven and geometric applications. In particular, Lanza et al.
[
27
]
proposed a variational Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) framework for computing multiple graph
p
-Laplacian eigenpairs, demonstrating promising results for clustering and dimensionality reduction tasks. Their formulation, however, is theoretically restricted to the range
1<p\leq 2
, where convexity of the subproblems is preserved.

1.1
Contributions

This work investigates numerical approximation of the first Dirichlet

p
-Laplace eigenpair for
2\leq p<\infty
on both Euclidean and surface
domains, with particular emphasis on the large
p
regime. We introduce a
Newton inverse-power iteration scheme combined with a surface finite element
(SFEM) discretization, extending prior FEM-based approaches to the surface
setting. A continuation method in
p
, together with a domain rescaling
strategy, enables stable computation of the first eigenpair for large
p
values. We provide what appears to be the first systematic treatment of the surface
p
-Laplacian eigenvalue problem across a wide range of
p
.

Previous numerical studies, to our knowledge, have reported results only up
to
p=10
, whereas we show computations for
p
as large as
100
. This extended
p
range allows us to explore the limiting relationship between the first eigenpair and the distance-to-boundary function, bridging the gap between finite
p
computations and the asymptotic
p\to\infty
regime. A key feature of our approach is the new domain rescaling strategy, which mitigates eigenvalue growth or decay with respect to
p
, thereby improving the numerical conditioning of the problem.

We validate the method through one-dimensional convergence tests against known
analytical solutions and through self-convergence studies in higher dimensions
where exact solutions are unavailable. To support the numerical findings, we include in Appendix
A
a derivation establishing the regularity class
C^{1,1/(p-1)}
of the one-dimensional eigenfunction which explains the observed loss of smoothness for large
p
. Finally, we discuss the large
p
behavior of the eigenpairs on surfaces, and our computational observations are consistent
with the expected asymptotic properties of the
p
-Laplacian.

2
Dirichlet
p
-Laplace eigenvalue problem

For a given surface
S
lying in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we consider the
p
-Laplace operator on the surface
S
, given by

\Delta_{p}^{S}u=\text{div}^{S}\Bigl(|\nabla^{S}u|^{p-2}\nabla^{S}u\Bigr),

where the superscript
S
denotes a differential operator intrinsic to the surface and
|\cdot|
indicates the Euclidean norm.

For surfaces of codimension
1
, the intrinsic gradient and divergence are given in terms of the corresponding Euclidean operators by

\nabla^{S}u=\nabla\tilde{u}-\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla\tilde{u}),\qquad\text{div}^{S}(\mathbf{v})=\text{div}(\mathbf{\tilde{v}})-\mathbf{n}^{\top}\nabla\mathbf{\tilde{v}}\>\mathbf{n},

where
\mathbf{n}
is the unit vector normal to the surface
S

[
17
]
. Here
\tilde{u}
and
\mathbf{\tilde{v}}
are smooth extensions of
u
and
\mathbf{v}
to a tubular neighborhood of
S
.

Let
\Omega
be an open, bounded subset of
S
, with
p\geq 2
fixed. The Dirichlet
p
-Laplace eigenvalue problem on
\Omega\subset S
(cf.,
[
31
]
) is given by

\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}^{S}u_{p}=\lambda_{p}|u_{p}|^{p-2}u_{p},

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ in }\Omega,

(1a)

\displaystyle u_{p}=0,

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

(1b)

\displaystyle\|u_{p}\|_{\infty}=1

.

(1c)

Note that we impose the normalization condition (
1c
) to ensure uniqueness, as otherwise solutions of (
1a
) are only unique up to rescaling. Another common normalization is
\|u_{p}\|_{p}=1

[
20
]
; however for numerical convenience we choose the infinity norm.

We interpret the Dirichlet
p
-Laplace eigenvalue problem in the classical weak sense as in
[
28
]
, which considers Euclidean domains. Note the weak form generalizes directly to the surface setting
[
17
]
. Moreover, the weak formulation lends itself naturally to the
SFEM discretization employed in our computations. Specifically, the pair
(\lambda_{p},u_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
,
with
u_{p}\not\equiv 0
, is called an eigenpair if

\int_{\Omega}\bigl|\nabla^{S}u_{p}\bigr|^{p-2}\,\nabla^{S}u_{p}\cdot\nabla^{S}\varphi\,dA=\lambda_{p}\int_{\Omega}|u_{p}|^{p-2}u_{p}\,\varphi\,dA,\qquad\forall\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

The first (principal) eigenpair admits a variational characterization as the minimizer of a nonlinear Rayleigh quotient, which we will introduce in Section
3
. In contrast, higher eigenpairs do not enjoy such a simple minimization property because the operator is nonlinear and the space
W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
is not a Hilbert space for
p>2
.
Consequently, orthogonality relations between eigenfunctions are lost, and higher modes must be defined through more delicate minimax principles
[
21
,
27
,
28
]
.
There remain many open problems concerning higher eigenpairs. While the variational spectrum of the Dirichlet
p
-Laplacian on bounded domains is known to form a discrete sequence of eigenvalues tending to
+\infty
, it is not known in general whether this sequence exhausts all possible eigenvalues of the nonlinear operator; see
[
28
]
and references therein.
For these reasons, we will restrict our attention to the principal eigenpair, which is central to understanding the nonlinear behavior of the operator.

For
p>2
, it is known that the first eigenfunction
u_{p}
is a positive function lying in
C^{1,\sigma}(\overline{\Omega})
with
0<\sigma<1

[
4
,
5
,
25
]
.
In Appendix
A
we show that
\sigma\to 0
as
p\to\infty
for the one-dimensional case. Our numerical results in Section
5
suggest that in higher dimensions, similarly to
1
D, the smoothness reduces with increasing
p
.

Of particular interest in our study is the large
p
behavior and the limiting behavior of both eigenvalues and eigenfunctions as
p\to\infty
. In this limit, the divergence structure of the
p
-Laplacian is lost, and solutions must be interpreted in a viscosity sense. We will discuss the asymptotic behavior of the first eigenpair and its associated geometric properties in the next section.

3
The first eigenpair

The first
p
-Laplace eigenvalue is given by

\lambda_{p}:=\inf_{u\in W_{0}^{1,p},u\not\equiv 0}R_{p}(u;\Omega),

where
R_{p}(u;\Omega)
is the nonlinear Rayleigh quotient

R_{p}(u;\Omega)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla^{S}u|^{p}\>dA}{\int_{\Omega}|u|^{p}\>dA}=\frac{\|\nabla^{S}u\|_{p}^{p}}{\|u\|_{p}^{p}}.

(2)

Minimizing the Rayleigh quotient gives rise to the Euler-Lagrange equation (
1a
)
[
20
]
. The nonlinear term on the right-hand side of (
1a
) arises naturally from minimizing the Rayleigh quotient, subject to a normalization constraint. The minimizer
u_{p}
represents the first (or principal) eigenfunction of the
p
-Laplacian and is unique up to rescaling
[
4
]
.

The stability of the principal frequency
\lambda_{p}
as
p
varies is discussed by Lindqvist
[
29
]
, where it is proven that

p\left(\lambda_{p}\right)^{1/p}<s\left(\lambda_{s}\right)^{1/s}

(3)

when
1<p<s<\infty
. The proof of this monotonicity property given in
[
29
]
extends directly to surfaces, with primary ingredients being a strategic choice of test function and application of the Hölder inequality.
We confirm that our numerical approximations of the eigenvalues adhere to (
3
) – see Figure
13
in Section
5
.

The first eigenfunction possesses several noteworthy properties. In particular, the first eigenfunction is simple and does not change sign (see
[
28
]
for a proof in the Euclidean setting).
Literature for the principal
p
-Laplace eigenpair on manifolds has largely focused on analytical properties and inequalities
[
20
,
25
,
31
]
. Numerical works appear limited to specialized cases such as low
p
values on the unit sphere
[
19
]
. In the following subsection we elaborate on the limiting behavior of the principal eigenpair as
p\to\infty
.

3.1
Asymptotic behavior

The first eigenpair tells us geometric information about the underlying domain. In particular, as
p\to\infty
we find a connection to the distance function. Section
5
demonstrates this connection through our numerical experiments. Moreover, knowing the asymptotic behavior of the eigenvalues guides the development of our domain rescaling strategy which allows computations for large
p
(see Section
4.1
).

Formally, taking a limit
p\to\infty
involving the
p
-Laplacian Rayleigh quotient leads to the infinity eigenvalue and associated eigenvalue problem. Raising the quotient (
2
) to the power
1/p
and taking the infimum, gives

\Lambda_{p}:=\lambda_{p}^{1/p}=\inf_{u\in W_{0}^{1,p},u\not\equiv 0}\frac{\|\nabla^{S}u\|_{p}}{\|u\|_{p}}.

Then, formally taking the
p\to\infty
limit we obtain the infinity eigenvalue

\Lambda_{\infty}:=\inf_{u\in W_{0}^{1,\infty},u\not\equiv 0}\frac{\|\nabla^{S}u\|_{\infty}}{\|u\|_{\infty}}.

The limiting PDE for the first eigenfunction of (
1
) as
p\to\infty
, is given by

\displaystyle\min\left\{|\nabla^{S}u_{\infty}|-\Lambda_{\infty}u_{\infty},-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}u_{\infty}\right\}=0,

\displaystyle\qquad\text{in }\Omega,

(4a)

\displaystyle u_{\infty}=0,

\displaystyle\qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(4b)

\displaystyle\|u_{\infty}\|_{\infty}=1

,

(4c)

where
\Delta_{\infty}^{S}u=\left<\nabla^{S}u,\mathcal{H}^{S}\>u\nabla^{S}u\right>
and
\mathcal{H}^{S}
denotes the (intrinsic) Hessian on
S
. The limiting equations are derived in the Euclidean settings in
[
22
]
and generalized to non-Euclidean spaces in
[
5
]
. The derivation of (
4
) follows the same arguments used in
[
22
]
and extends to compact Riemannian manifolds almost directly, with minor notational changes and a standard application of Arzelà–Ascoli for compactness. We refer the reader to
[
5
]
for the non-Euclidean setup.

Juutinen et al.
[
22
]
also show that

\lim_{p\to\infty}\Lambda_{p}=\Lambda_{\infty}=\frac{1}{\underset{{\bf x}\in\Omega}{\max}\>\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)}=\frac{1}{R},

(5)

where
R
is the radius of the largest ball which may be inscribed in
\Omega
. As noted in
[
20
]
and shown in
[
5
]
, this holds beyond the Euclidean case and in particular for compact Riemannian manifolds. This limit can be used to ensure that our computed eigenvalues have the correct limiting behavior as
p\to\infty
.

For some simple symmetric domains,
the limiting first eigenfunction is given by the normalized distance-to-boundary function given by

u_{\infty}({\bf x})=\frac{\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)}{\|\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\partial\Omega)\|_{\infty}}.

(6)

Note that the distance function
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot,\partial\Omega)
is not differentiable everywhere in
\Omega
, so
u_{\infty}
needs to be interpreted as a solution of (
4
) in the viscosity sense.

More precisely, as in
[
23
]
we define the ridge set as

\mathcal{R}:=\{{\bf x}\in\Omega:\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\partial\Omega)\text{ is not differentiable at }{\bf x}\},

(7)

and the maximal distance-to-boundary set (which is a subset of
\mathcal{R}
) as

\mathcal{M}:=\{{\bf x}\in\Omega:\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)=\|\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\partial\Omega)\|_{\infty}\}.

(8)

For instance, on a geodesic ball both sets consist of the center point. On the other hand, in a square (or cube) the ridge set
\mathcal{R}
comprises the diagonals connecting opposite vertices, and only their intersection at the center forms
\mathcal{M}
, so in this case
\mathcal{M}
is a strict subset of
\mathcal{R}
.

In the special case when
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{M}
, it can be shown that the normalized geodesic distance-to-boundary function indeed satisfies (
4
) in the viscosity sense – see Appendix
B
. This assumption is satisfied by domains such as geodesic balls, stadiums, and annuli
[
12
]
. On such domains, we may check that numerically computed solutions are indeed converging to the normalized distance-to-boundary function as
p\to\infty
.

3.2
Analytic solution in
1
D

In one dimension, the eigenvalue problem (
1
) on
\Omega=(a,b)
is given by

\displaystyle-(|u_{p}^{\prime}|^{p-2}u_{p}^{\prime})^{\prime}=\lambda_{p}|u_{p}|^{p-2}u_{p}

\displaystyle,\quad a<x<b,

\displaystyle u_{p}(a)=u_{p}(b)=0

,

\displaystyle\|u_{p}\|_{\infty}=1

.

It is only in
1
D that the exact eigenpairs are known
[
28
]
.
Thus, it is crucial to make use of the exact
1
D eigenpairs in assessing performance of numerical schemes. In Section
5.1
, we perform
1
D numerical tests.

Lindqvist
[
28
]
shows that the first eigenpair is given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{p}

\displaystyle=(p-1)\left(\frac{\pi_{p}}{b-a}\right)^{p},

(10a)

\displaystyle u_{p}(x)

\displaystyle=\sin_{p}\left(\pi_{p}\frac{x-a}{b-a}\right).

(10b)

This eigenpair involves
\pi_{p}
and
\sin_{p}
which come from a generalized trigonometry. Edmunds & Lang
[
18
]
discuss details about the generalized trigonometric functions. They define
F_{p}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
by

F_{p}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{(1-t^{p})^{1/p}}dt,

for
1<p<\infty
which is a strictly increasing one-to-one function with range
[0,\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}]
. We generalize
\pi
via

\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}=F_{p}(1)=\frac{\pi}{p\sin(\pi/p)},

and for
x\in[0,\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}]
, we define the generalized sine function

\sin_{p}(x):=F_{p}^{-1}(x).

Note that for
p=2
we obtain
\sin_{2}(x)=\sin(x)
and
\pi_{2}=\pi
.

Since we have the exact eigenvalues, we can expand both
\lambda_{p}
and
\lambda_{p}^{1/p}
about
\epsilon=\frac{1}{p}=0
to show that

\lambda_{p}=\left(\frac{2}{b-a}\right)^{p}\left(p+\frac{1}{6}(\pi^{2}-6)+\frac{1}{72}\pi^{2}(\pi^{2}-12)\frac{1}{p}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\right),

(11)

and

\lambda_{p}^{1/p}=\left(\frac{2}{b-a}\right)\left(1+\frac{\log p}{p}+\frac{1}{6}\left(\pi^{2}-6+3(\log p)^{2}\right)\frac{1}{p^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(\log p)^{3}}{p^{3}}\right)\right).

(12)

Taking the
p\to\infty
limit in (
12
), we find

\lim_{p\to\infty}\left(\lambda_{p}\right)^{1/p}=\frac{2}{b-a},

and indeed
\tfrac{2}{b-a}=\tfrac{1}{R}
where
R
is the radius of the largest
1
D ball lying within the domain
(a,b)
. Moreover, we find that
\lambda_{p}^{1/p}
approaches
\Lambda_{\infty}
at the rate
\tfrac{\log p}{p}
for large
p
. Also, we see that when
b-a=2
,
\lambda_{p}
grows linearly in
p
for large
p
.

We obtain high–accuracy reference values of
\sin_{p}(x)
by solving the initial–value problem

\displaystyle v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=|w|^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\mathrm{sign}(w),

\displaystyle v(0)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle w^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-(p-1)|v|^{p-1}\mathrm{sign}(v),

\displaystyle w(0)

\displaystyle=1,

using the
Matlab
code from
[
38
]
with the

ode45
solver. Both absolute and relative tolerances are set to

.

4
Numerical scheme

The continuous domain
\Omega
is replaced by a discrete computational domain which we call
\Omega_{h}
. We will use a finite element space

V_{h}=\{\phi_{h}\in C(\Omega_{h}):\phi_{h}|_{T}\text{ is in }Q^{1}\text{ for each }T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

where
Q^{1}
is the space of bilinear polynomials on each quadrilateral cell in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, the collection of faces on the surface mesh. Here, the mesh size is defined as
h:=\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\mathrm{diam}(T)
. Numerically approximated eigenvalues will be denoted by
\lambda_{p,h}
and eigenfunctions by
u_{p,h}
. We use polynomial degree
1
Lagrange elements since
p
-Laplace eigenfunctions are not
C^{2}
for
p>2
.

We note that the finite element method extends to surface domains. For the classical linear surface Poisson problem,
\Delta^{S}u=f
, Dziuk & Elliott
[
17
]
show that under the standard regularity assumption
\|f-f_{h}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq c_{f}h^{2}
, SFEM achieves optimal convergence rates analogous with those for Euclidean domains:

\|u-u_{h}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq ch^{2},\quad\|\nabla^{S}(u-u_{h})\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leq ch.

Although these rates pertain to a linear problem which is only directly applicable when
p=2
, it shows how the geometric and interpolation errors behave when extending to a surface framework. Dziuk & Elliott
[
17
]
further discuss how SFEM is applicable for nonlinear diffusion equations including the parabolic surface
p
-Laplace equation. Here, we consider the stationary
p
-Laplace eigenvalue problem.

We use a Newton inverse-power iteration in order to solve the nonlinear problem for
p\in[2,\infty)
, similarly to
[
35
]
. This means we alternate updating the eigenvalue and eigenfunction approximations. The eigenvalue approximation is obtained by computing the discrete nonlinear Rayleigh quotient (
2
). For the eigenfunction updates, we seek solutions of

F(u;\>\lambda):=\nabla^{S}\cdot\left(\gamma(u;\eta)\nabla^{S}u\right)+\lambda|u|^{p-2}u=0,

where the coefficient with small regularization constant
\eta>0
is given by

\gamma(u;\eta):=(\eta^{2}+|\nabla^{S}u|^{2})^{\frac{p-2}{2}}>0.

(13)

We solve for the eigenfunction update at Newton iteration
n+1
(denoted by superscripts) via

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(u^{n};\lambda)[\delta u^{n}]

\displaystyle=-F(u^{n};\>\lambda),

\displaystyle u^{n+1}

\displaystyle=u^{n}+\beta^{n}\delta u^{n},

where
F^{\prime}(u;\lambda)[\delta u]
is the derivative of
F
in direction of
\delta u
. Here,
\eta
is a small regularization constant which ensures positive-definiteness of the generalized stiffness and mass matrices appearing in the resulting linearized problem, allowing a conjugate gradient (CG) solver to be used.
In our experiments we take
\eta=10^{-5}
. The damping parameter,
0<\beta^{n}\leq 1
, is chosen at each iteration via a quadratic interpolation line search with a sufficient decrease condition
[
34
]
.
That is, we find
0<\beta^{n}\leq 1
such that

R_{p}(u^{n}+\alpha_{n}\delta u^{n};\Omega_{h})\leq R_{p}(u^{n};\Omega_{h})+c_{1}\beta^{n}R_{p}^{\prime}(u^{n};\Omega_{h})[\delta u^{n}],

(14)

where
c_{1}=10^{-3}
is the prescribed tolerance,
R_{p}
is given by (
2
), and
R_{p}^{\prime}(u)[\delta u]
is the derivative of
R_{p}
in the direction of
\delta u
.

This gives the Newton iteration equations as

\displaystyle\nabla^{S}\cdot\bigg(\gamma(u^{n};\eta)\nabla^{S}\delta u^{n}+(p-2)\gamma(u^{n};\eta)

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla^{S}u^{n}\cdot\nabla^{S}\delta u^{n}}{\eta^{2}+|\nabla^{S}u^{n}|^{2}}\nabla^{S}u^{n}\bigg)+\lambda^{n}(p-1)|u^{n}|^{p-2}\delta u^{n}

(15a)

\displaystyle=-\nabla^{S}\cdot\bigg(\gamma(u^{n};\eta)\nabla^{S}u\bigg)-\lambda^{n}|u^{n}|^{p-2}u^{n},

\displaystyle u^{n+1}

\displaystyle=u^{n}+\beta^{n}\delta u^{n}.

(15b)

The weak formulation of (
15a
) is obtained by multiplying with a test function
\phi
and integrating by parts on both sides. Reducing to a finite dimensional space with basis
\{\phi_{h}^{0},...,\phi_{h}^{N-1}\}\in V_{h}
, we can write the update
\delta u^{n}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, as

\delta u^{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\delta U^{n}_{j}\phi_{h}^{j}.

Similarly to
[
35
]
, we find the resulting linear system for the update coefficients
\delta U^{n}
vector at each iteration to be

\left(K^{n}-\lambda^{n}M^{n}\right)\delta U^{n}=b^{n},

(16)

with the entries of the generalized stiffness matrix
K^{n}
given by

K^{n}_{ij}:=\int_{\Omega_{h}}\nabla^{S}\phi_{h}^{i}\cdot\gamma(u^{n};\eta)\left(I+\frac{(p-2)}{\eta^{2}+|\nabla^{S}u^{n}|^{2}}[\nabla^{S}u^{n}]\otimes[\nabla^{S}u^{n}]\right)\nabla^{S}\phi_{h}^{j}dA_{h},

(17)

the entries of the weighted mass matrix
M^{n}
given by

\qquad M^{n}_{ij}:=(p-1)\int_{\Omega_{h}}|u^{n}|^{p-2}\phi_{h}^{i}\phi_{h}^{j}dA_{h},

(18)

and the entries of the right hand side
b^{n}
given by

b^{n}_{i}:=\int_{\Omega_{h}}\left(\lambda^{n}|u^{n}|^{p-2}u^{n}\phi_{h}^{i}-\nabla^{S}\phi_{h}^{i}\cdot\left(\gamma(u^{n};\eta)\nabla^{S}u^{n}\right)\right)dA_{h}.

(19)

In practice, following
[
35
]
, we instead solve the shifted linear system

(K^{n}+\lambda^{n}M^{n})\,\delta U^{n}=b^{n}
, rather than (
16
),
as the latter is not necessarily positive definite.
The shifted matrix is positive definite, since both
K^{n}
and
M^{n}
are
positive definite and
\lambda^{n}>0
. This shift preserves the Newton update direction and therefore the convergence to the first eigenpair.

Our code is written in C++ using the
deal.II

[
2
]
finite element library. In our implementation, the first Dirichlet
p
-Laplacian eigenpair is computed using a nonlinear inverse-power iteration
[
11
]
, in which the eigenfunction and eigenvalue are updated in an alternating fashion. The linear system which arises at each iteration becomes ill-conditioned when the gradient is small and when
p
is large.
We apply algebraic multigrid (AMG) preconditioning and use a CG solver at each iteration. The AMG preconditioner is implemented through the
TrilinosWrappers::PreconditionAMG
interface in
deal.II
. It is configured for symmetric elliptic operators by setting
elliptic = true
and
higher_order_elements = false
, with two smoother sweeps and an aggregation threshold of
0.02
. Symmetric successive over-relaxation (SSOR) preconditioning was also tested but proved less effective, typically requiring more than
100
CG iterations even for moderate
p
values. By comparison, AMG maintained a smaller (
<50
) and more stable iteration count across different values of
p
and mesh refinement.
We initialize with the linear
p=2
problem using the
SLEPc
Krylov-Schur eigensolver from the
deal.II
library. For
p>2
, we need an initial guess very close to the exact solution in order to see convergence. Accordingly, we iterate in
p
following Algorithm
1
.

For the examples under our consideration, we take
\mathrm{tol}_{\mathrm{Newton}}=10^{-7}
,
\mathrm{tol}_{\mathrm{CG}}=10^{-6}
, and
\delta p=1
unless the target
p
value is not an integer or otherwise specified. We continue incrementing
p
until the algorithm fails to converge usually due to failure to find a suitable descent direction according to the decrease condition (
14
).

4.1
Handling large
p
values

Iterating in
p
when applying the Newton scheme is one measure we take in order to handle larger
p
values. Additionally, we rescale the domain in order to prevent numerical blowup or underflow problems. From (
5
) we see that
\lambda_{p}\sim(\max_{{\bf x}\in\Omega}\>\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega))^{-p}
for large
p
so if
\max_{{\bf x}\in\Omega}\>\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)
is not close to
1
then
\lambda_{p}
may become very large or small as
p
increases.

We apply the transformation

T_{\alpha}:\Omega\to\alpha\Omega:=\{\alpha{\bf x}\mid x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\},

(20)

with dilation parameter
\alpha
chosen such that
\underset{{\bf x}\in\Omega}{\max}\;\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)
is close to
1
. Figure
2
visualizes this transformation on a rectangular domain. Applying transformation (
20
) impacts the eigenvalues. In particular,

R_{p}(u_{p};\alpha\Omega)=\alpha^{-p}R_{p}(u_{p};\Omega),

where
R_{p}
is given by (
2
). This can be seen by applying a change of variables. Thus, we can readily recover the eigenvalues on the original domain
\Omega
.

Fig. 2
:
Illustration of the rescaling transformation

T_{\alpha}

R=1/\alpha

T_{\alpha}

so that the maximal distance to the boundary becomes
R=1
.

For general domains and in particular for surfaces, choosing a suitable
\alpha
value is not trivial. We take
\alpha=d_{m}^{-1}
where
d_{m}
is an approximation of
\max_{{\bf x}\in\Omega}\>\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)
. This can be found via a handful of alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) iterations for the
p
-Poisson problem
[
36
]
. This helps because the
\lambda_{p}^{1/p}
values then approach a value close to
1
. Figure
3
shows an example on a hand mesh where the maximal geodesic distance
d_{m}
is obtained. This approximation of
\max_{{\bf x}\in\Omega}\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)

is then used to set the scaling parameter
\alpha=d_{m}^{-1}
.

Fig. 3
:
Illustration of the maximal distance approximation

on a hand surface mesh.
The marked point (orange) corresponds to the location where the distance to the boundary

\partial\Omega
(blue) is maximized, giving

.
This value is used in the scaling
\alpha=d_{m}^{-1}
.

In practice, we may employ an adaptive thresholding strategy to keep the computed eigenvalues within a stable range. Let
\tau_{-}>0
and
\tau_{+}>0
with
\tau_{-}<\tau_{+}
denote prescribed lower and upper thresholds, respectively. Typically, we use
\tau_{-}=2
and
\tau_{+}=20
in our computations. At each step of the continuation in
p
, once an approximation of
\lambda_{p,h}
is obtained, we check:

\displaystyle\text{if }\lambda_{p,h}>\tau_{+},\quad\text{then }\alpha\;\leftarrow\;\alpha\cdot 2^{1/p},

\displaystyle\text{if }\lambda_{p,h}<\tau_{-},\quad\text{then }\alpha\;\leftarrow\;\alpha\cdot 2^{-1/p}.

Here
\alpha
denotes the cumulative scaling factor applied to the domain, so the current working domain is always
\alpha\Omega
. When
\lambda_{p,h}
is outside the range
[\tau_{-},\tau_{+}]
,
\lambda_{p,h}
is shifted by a factor of two back into range.
This adaptive strategy is particularly useful when the maximal distance to the boundary
is not known a priori. For planar domains
\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)
can often be determined explicitly or computed with reasonable effort
[
15
]
. However, for surface domains the maximum geodesic distance is generally more difficult to obtain. In such cases, adaptive thresholding avoids the need for exact geometric information while still preventing blowup or underflow in the eigenvalues. The modification of the
p
continuation Algorithm
1
with adaptive thresholding is presented in Algorithm
2
.

5
Computational results

This section presents a series of numerical experiments that illustrate the accuracy and convergence properties of our SFEM Newton inverse-power scheme for computing the first
p
–Laplace eigenpair.
We begin with the one–dimensional interval, which is the only case where an analytical solution is known. This allows a clean verification of the predicted convergence rates, which to our knowledge have not previously been reported in the literature. We then move to planar Euclidean domains (disks and squares) and explore the limiting behavior as
p\to\infty
. Finally, we report computations on some two–dimensional surfaces embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to demonstrate that the method extends
naturally to curved manifolds. These surface experiments include both simple surfaces (hemisphere and half torus) and more complicated geometries from Crane’s
[
16
]
3D Model Repository and the Chen et al.
[
13
]
benchmark set for segmentation. All downloaded meshes are converted to quadrilateral meshes using the open‐source tool
tethex

[
32
]

and in the case of the hand model the raw mesh has been preprocessed in
MeshLab

[
14
]
by truncating at the wrist and applying smoothing to obtain a natural boundary.

All experiments shown are carried out with the
deal.II

[
2
]
finite element library in C++. We use
piecewise bilinear elements for the spatial discretization and the computational quadrilateral meshes are either generated using the built-in
GridGenerator
utilities or read in from a surface mesh file. We use quadrilateral surface meshes as they provide comparable geometric fidelity to triangle surface meshes with fewer elements, and are natively supported in the
deal.II
framework. For visualization of the eigenfunctions, we export to
ParaView

[
3
]
.

5.3
Surface domains

In this subsection, we consider
p
-Laplace eigenvalue problems on curved surfaces of codimension
1
lying in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
We first study two simple surfaces, the hemisphere and the half torus, for which
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{M}
, and therefore, from Section
3.1
, we know the first eigenfunction should converge toward the (geodesic) distance-to-boundary function as
p\to\infty
, a result detailed in Appendix
B
.
To illustrate the versatility of the method, we also consider several “fun” examples by reading in surface meshes including a duck, a hand, a mug, and a pig. For these domains, the symmetry assumption is generally not satisfied, and the location of the ridge set is less predictable, so the limiting eigenfunction is unknown. We use Algorithm
1
for the hemisphere and half torus, where the maximum geodesic distance-to-boundary is known, and Algorithm
2
for the “fun” examples, where this value is not known exactly.

5.3.1
Hemisphere
\Omega=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}=R,\;z>0
}

For the hemisphere, the condition
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{M}
discussed in Section
3.1
is satisfied.
Therefore, as
p\to\infty
, the first eigenfunction approaches

u_{\infty}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)}{\|\textrm{dist}(\cdot,\partial\Omega)\|_{\infty}}=\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\arccos\left(\frac{z}{R}\right)\right),

where
\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)
denotes the geodesic distance along the hemisphere from the point
\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)
to the boundary at the equator. This expression follows directly from the spherical law of cosines.

In our computations, we scale the domain so that the maximal geodesic distance from the boundary is
1
. For a hemisphere of radius
R
, the maximal geodesic distance occurs at the pole
(0,0,R)
and is given by
R\pi/2
, so the scaling factor
\alpha=2/\pi
ensures
\max_{{\bf x}\in\Omega}\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)=1
. As with the Euclidean examples, results may be recovered for other scalings.

Table
4
reports the self-convergence results for various
p
, showing
L^{2}
errors of the eigenfunction, relative errors of the first eigenvalue, and computed values of
\lambda_{p,h}
. For small to moderate (
p=3,10
), the observed convergence rates are close to
2
for the eigenvalue relative errors and mostly well above
1
for the eigenfunction
L^{2}
errors. For larger values (
p=50,100
), the convergence rates on coarser meshes are irregular, but they appear to become more consistent on finer meshes. Overall, the convergence behavior is similar to that of the Euclidean disk considered above, and better than that of the square.

p

p=10
and
p=\infty
.

5.3.3
Geometrically complex surfaces

In Figure
11
, we plot the computed eigenvalues
\lambda_{p,h}
along with their
p
th roots for the following examples: (i) duck mesh with
\partial\Omega
taken along the central axis which divides the surface into two symmetric halves, (ii) hand mesh with
\partial\Omega
corresponding to the natural wrist boundary, (iii) mug mesh with
\partial\Omega
defined as a single point (or small
\epsilon
-ball) on the outer surface, and (iv) pig mesh with
\partial\Omega
defined as a single point (or small
\epsilon
-ball) on the left ear. From a PDE analysis point of view, prescribing Dirichlet conditions on isolated points is degenerate. In this case, one can consider
\partial\Omega
to be the boundary of an arbitrarily small geodesic ball centered around the single point.

For the hand and mug geometries, the original mesh resolutions exhibited an uptick in
\lambda_{p,h}
around
p\sim 40
, which flattened out after one level of mesh refinement in
deal.II
and aligned with the trend observed for lower

p
. Insufficient mesh resolution can lead to an artificial increase in the nonlinear Rayleigh quotient (
2
), as seen in prior examples, in particular the square (see Table
3
). The refined results are therefore presented in Figure
11
, as they provide a more accurate representation of the true eigenvalue behavior for
p>40
.

Visualizations of these surfaces are shown in Figure
12
. Since the maximum geodesic distance to the boundary is not known exactly for these domains, the spectrum is reported under approximately optimal scaling by taking
\alpha=d_{m}^{-1}
, where
d_{m}
is an approximate value of
\max_{{\bf x}\in\Omega}\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)
, as described in Section
4.1
. Consequently, the limiting value
\Lambda_{\infty}
is not known exactly, although it is expected to be close to
1
for all examples. In Figure
11
on the right we observe non-monotonicity in
\lambda_{p,h}^{1/p}
, particularly pronounced for the mug and pig examples, which both have Dirichlet regions defined at points (or small
\epsilon
-balls).
This behavior aligns with the rapid changes in the eigenfunctions for small
p
observed in Figure
12
for these two cases.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we have studied the first
p
-Laplacian eigenpair on both Euclidean and surface (non-Euclidean) domains, with the extension to surface domains representing a novel contribution to the numerical literature. A key component of our approach is a new domain rescaling strategy that enables accurate computations for large
p
values. We carried out numerical convergence studies, including comparisons with exact one-dimensional solutions, to assess the accuracy of our method. Additionally, by combining a Newton inverse-power iteration with
p
continuation, we were able to efficiently compute eigenpairs across a wide range of
p
(typically up to
p\sim 100
). For domains where the ridge set and maximal set of the distance-to-boundary function align exactly, our results numerically illustrate the connection between the eigenfunctions and the limiting (geodesic) distance-to-boundary function. Overall, we have demonstrated the effectiveness of our approach in handling complex geometries and provide a foundation for future work on higher eigenpairs, alternative boundary conditions, and potential applications of
p
-Laplacian eigenpairs such as shape optimization problems.

Appendix A
Regularity class of the
\sin_{p}
function

We show that on a one-dimensional interval
\Omega=(a,b)
,
u_{p}\in C^{1,1/(p-1)}(\overline{\Omega})
for finite
p>2
, by local asymptotic expansion for
\sin_{p}
near the local maximum
\pi_{p}/2
, where a cusp forms. Note that for
p=2
, we have the ordinary sine function, and so
u_{2}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
.

The first eigenfunction is

u_{p}(x)=\sin_{p}\left(\pi_{p}\frac{x-a}{b-a}\right),

and since
\sin_{p}
attains a maximum at
\pi_{p}/2
,
u_{p}
attains its maximum at
x_{0}=(a+b)/2
.

Recall from Section
3.2
that
\sin_{p}
is defined through the inverse of the integral

F_{p}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}(1-t^{p})^{-1/p}dt,\quad F_{p}(1)=\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}.

Therefore, to investigate local behavior near the maximum, we consider asymptotic behavior of
F_{p}
near
1
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be small. Then

\displaystyle F_{p}(1)-F_{p}(1-\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{1-\epsilon}^{1}(1-t^{p})^{-1/p}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\epsilon}(1-(1-\tilde{t})^{p})^{-1/p}d\tilde{t},

where we have applied the change of variable
\tilde{t}=1-t
so that for convenience we may expand about
\tilde{t}=0
. We have

1-(1-\tilde{t})^{p}=p\tilde{t}-\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\tilde{t}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\tilde{t}^{3}),

so, to leading order, our integrand becomes

(1-(1-\tilde{t})^{p})^{-1/p}=(p\tilde{t})^{-1/p}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).

Then

F_{p}(1)-F_{p}(1-\epsilon)\sim\int_{0}^{\epsilon}(p\tilde{t})^{-1/p}d\tilde{t}=\frac{p^{(p-1)/p}}{p-1}\epsilon^{(p-1)/p}.

Thus, for
\epsilon
small we have

F_{p}(1)-F_{p}(1-\epsilon)=\frac{p^{(p-1)/p}}{p-1}\;\epsilon^{(p-1)/p}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),

Since
\sin_{p}(x)=F_{p}^{-1}(x)
, for
t
near
\pi_{p}/2
, we have

\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}-t=F_{p}(1)-F_{p}(\sin_{p}(t))\sim\frac{p^{(p-1)/p}}{p-1}\left(1-\sin_{p}(t)\right)^{(p-1)/p}.

Taking the
p/(p-1)
th root we obtain

1-\sin_{p}(t)\sim p^{-1}{(p-1)^{p/(p-1)}}\left|t-\frac{\pi_{p}}{2}\right|^{p/(p-1)}.

Hence, for
x
near
x_{0}
,

u_{p}(x)=\sin_{p}\left(\pi_{p}\frac{x-a}{b-a}\right)\sim 1-K_{p}\left|x-x_{0}\right|^{p/(p-1)},

(21)

where
K_{p}:=p^{-1}{(p-1)^{p/(p-1)}}\left(\frac{\pi_{p}}{b-a}\right)^{p/(p-1)}
.

The local approximation (
21
) shows the formation of a cusp at
x_{0}
for
p>2
. Indeed, differentiating (
21
) yields

u_{p}^{\prime}(x)\sim-\frac{p}{p-1}K_{p}\left|x-x_{0}\right|^{1/(p-1)}\mathrm{sign}(x-x_{0}),

(22)

for
x
near
x_{0}
. Therefore,
u_{p}^{\prime}
is Hölder continuous at
x_{0}
, with exponent
1/(p-1)
. Also,
u_{p}
is smooth away from the maximum at
x_{0}
, as
F_{p}(x)
is smooth for
x<1
. We conclude that
u_{p}^{\prime}\in C^{0,1/(p-1)}(\overline{\Omega})
, and hence
u_{p}\in C^{1,1/(p-1)}(\overline{\Omega})
. Furthermore, for any
p>2
,
u_{p}
is not
C^{2}
, and the Hölder exponent of
u_{p}^{\prime}
decreases to
0
as
p\to\infty
.

Finally, note that for
p=2
the local expansion near
x_{0}
reduces to

u_{p}(x)\sim 1-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{b-a}\right)^{2}(x-x_{0})^{2},

corresponding to a smooth maximum, as consistent with the Taylor expansion of the regular sine function.

Appendix B
Geodesic distance function as the limiting
p\to\infty
eigenfunction

In this appendix we show that, when the set of maximal distance points coincides with the ridge of the distance function,
the limiting
p\to\infty
eigenfunction equals the normalized distance-to-boundary function.

The definition of a viscosity solution as in
[
24
]
for the limiting
p\to\infty
PDE (
4a
) is provided below and will be used in the proof of the proposition to follow.

Definition B.1

(Viscosity solution)
.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of a surface
S
and denote by
B_{g}({\bf x}_{0},r)
a geodesic ball centered at
{\bf x}_{0}
of radius
r
. A continuous function
u:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be a

viscosity subsolution
(respectively,
supersolution
) of

\min\left\{|\nabla^{S}u|-\Lambda_{\infty}u,-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}u\right\}=0,\qquad\text{in }\Omega,

if for every
C^{2}
smooth
\psi:B_{g}({\bf x}_{0},r)\subseteq\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that
u-\psi
has a local maximum (respectively, minimum) at
{\bf x}_{0}
with
\psi({\bf x}_{0})=u({\bf x}_{0})
, the following holds:

\min\left\{|\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})|-\Lambda_{\infty}\psi({\bf x}_{0}),-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})\right\}\leq 0\qquad\text{(respectively, $\geq 0$)}.

This is equivalent to

(subsolution)

\displaystyle|\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})|-\Lambda_{\infty}\psi({\bf x}_{0})\leq 0\quad\;\>\text{or}\;\quad-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})\leq 0,

(supersolution)

\displaystyle|\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})|-\Lambda_{\infty}\psi({\bf x}_{0})\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})\geq 0.

If
u
is both a viscosity subsolution and supersolution,
it is called a
viscosity solution
.

Proposition B.2
.

Let
\Omega
be an open and bounded subset of a surface
S
, and assume that the set
\mathcal{M}
of maximal distance-to-boundary points coincides exactly with the ridge set
\mathcal{R}
(see (
7
) and (
8
)).
Then the function
d({\bf x})=\mathrm{dist}({\bf x},\partial\Omega)
is a positive viscosity solution of

\displaystyle\min\left\{|\nabla^{S}u|-\Lambda_{\infty}u,-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}u\right\}=0,

\displaystyle\qquad\text{in }\Omega,

(23a)

\displaystyle u=0,

\displaystyle\qquad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(23b)

where
\Lambda_{\infty}=\|d\|_{\infty}^{-1}
.

Proof.

Away from the ridge, a standard direct calculation may be carried out to show that the equation is satisfied in the classical sense. We present this calculation first; the calculation is a direct extension from the Euclidean case. We will then show that the equation is satisfied in the viscosity sense at the ridge.

Case
{\bf x}_{0}\in\Omega\setminus\mathcal{R}
.

Away from the ridge set, the distance-to-boundary function
d(\cdot)
is smooth. At any point
{\bf x}_{0}\in\Omega\setminus\mathcal{R}
, let
{\bf y}_{0}\in\partial\Omega
denote the unique closest boundary point, and let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s)
be the unit-speed geodesic on
S
connecting
{\bf x}_{0}
to
{\bf y}_{0}
.
By taking the second derivative of
d(\cdot)
along the geodesic
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s)
we find

\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}d(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s))\Big|_{s=0}=\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(0)^{\top}\mathcal{H}^{S}d({\bf x}_{0})\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(0).

(24)

On the other hand, from
d(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s))=d({\bf x}_{0})-s
we infer that the second derivative of
d(\cdot)
along the geodesic vanishes:

\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}d(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s))\Big|_{s=0}=0,

which combined with (
24
) gives

\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(0)^{\top}\mathcal{H}^{S}d({\bf x}_{0})\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(0)=0.

(25)

By definition of the distance function,
d(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s))
decreases at the maximal rate in the direction of the gradient and hence,
\nabla^{S}d(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s))
is tangent to
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s)
. In particular, we have

\nabla^{S}d({\bf x}_{0})=-\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(0),

and by combining with (
25
) we find

\nabla^{S}d({\bf x}_{0})^{\top}\mathcal{H}^{S}d({\bf x}_{0})\,\nabla^{S}d({\bf x}_{0})=0.

Then, by the definition of the surface infinity Laplacian, we get

-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}d({\bf x}_{0})=0.

(26)

Finally, note that
|\nabla^{S}d({\bf x}_{0})|=1
, and by definition of
\mathcal{M}
and
\Lambda_{\infty}
, we have

d({\bf x}_{0})<\|d\|_{\infty}=\Lambda_{\infty}^{-1}.

Hence,

|\nabla^{S}d({\bf x}_{0})|-\Lambda_{\infty}d({\bf x}_{0})>0,

which together with (
26
) shows (
23a
).

Case
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathcal{R}
.

The geodesic distance-to-boundary function
d(\cdot)
satisfies the Eikonal equation,
|\nabla^{S}d|=1
, in the viscosity sense at the ridge
[
30
]
. Therefore for any
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{M}
, as
d({\bf x}_{0})=\Lambda_{\infty}^{-1}
, we have

|\nabla^{S}d({\bf x}_{0})|-\Lambda_{\infty}d({\bf x}_{0})=0,

(27)

in the viscosity sense, meaning
d(\cdot)
is both a viscosity supersolution and viscosity subsolution of (
27
)
[
24
]
.

(i) Viscosity subsolution for
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathcal{R}
.

We will show that
d(\cdot)
is a viscosity subsolution of (
23a
) according to Definition
B.1
. We already know that
d(\cdot)
is a viscosity subsolution of (
27
). Thus,
|\nabla^{S}d({\bf x}_{0})|-\Lambda_{\infty}d({\bf x}_{0})\leq 0
in the viscosity sense, which is enough as it implies
\min\left\{|\nabla^{S}d|-\Lambda_{\infty}d,-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}d\right\}\leq 0
in the viscosity sense at the ridge.

(ii) Viscosity supersolution for
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathcal{R}
.

Now we will show that
d(\cdot)
is a viscosity supersolution of (
23a
) according to Definition
B.1
.
Suppose
r>0
,
{\bf x}_{0}\in\Omega
, and
\psi:B_{g}({\bf x}_{0},r)\subseteq\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function such that
d-\psi
has a local minimum at
{\bf x}_{0}
with
\psi({\bf x}_{0})=d({\bf x}_{0})
. As
d(\cdot)
is a viscosity supersolution of the Eikonal equation, it holds that

|\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})|-\Lambda_{\infty}\psi({\bf x}_{0})\geq 0.

It remains to show that
-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})\geq 0
.

Without loss of generality, rescale so that
|\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})|=1
. Note if
\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})=0
, we have
-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})\geq 0
trivially since
-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})=-\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})^{\top}\mathcal{H}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})=0
. For convenience, now let
{\bf v}=\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})
and consider the unit-speed geodesic that starts from
{\bf x}_{0}
in the direction
\bf v
, that is,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)=\exp_{{\bf x}_{0}}(t{\bf v})
. Here,
\exp
denotes the Riemannian exponential map. For
0<h<r
, let
{\bf y}_{h}\in\partial\Omega
be a point such that
\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(h),{\bf y}_{h})=d(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(h))
, and similarly, let
{\bf y}_{0}\in\partial\Omega
satisfy
\mathrm{dist}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf y}_{0})=d({\bf x}_{0})
.

Then,

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(h))

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(h),{\bf y}_{0})

(28)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(h),{\bf x}_{0})+\mathrm{dist}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf y}_{0})

\displaystyle=h+d({\bf x}_{0}),

where for the second line we used the triangle inequality, and for the third we used that the geodesic
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
has unit speed, and hence,
\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(h),{\bf x}_{0})=h
.

Since
d-\psi
has a local minimum at
{\bf x}_{0}
,

\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(h))-\psi({\bf x}_{0})\leq d(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(h))-d({\bf x}_{0}),

which combined with (
28
) leads to

\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(h))-\psi({\bf x}_{0})\leq h.

(29)

Taylor expand
\psi
along
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
(use
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}(0)={\bf v}
) to get

\psi(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(h))=\psi({\bf x}_{0})+h\langle\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0}),{\bf v}\rangle+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\langle\mathcal{H}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0}){\bf v},{\bf v}\rangle+o(h^{2}).

Since
{\bf v}=\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})
has unit norm,
\langle\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0}),{\bf v}\rangle=|{\bf v}|^{2}=1
, and hence, by combining the Taylor expansion with (
29
) we find

h+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\langle\mathcal{H}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0}){\bf v},{\bf v}\rangle+o(h^{2})\leq h.

Now subtract
h
on both sides of the inequality above, and divide by
h^{2}
on both sides to get

\frac{1}{2}{\bf v}^{\top}\mathcal{H}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0}){\bf v}+\frac{o(h^{2})}{h^{2}}\leq 0.

Taking
h\to 0^{+}
yields

{\bf v}^{\top}\mathcal{H}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0}){\bf v}\leq 0.

Since
{\bf v}=\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})
, this is equivalent to

-\Delta_{\infty}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})=-\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})^{\top}\mathcal{H}^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})\nabla^{S}\psi({\bf x}_{0})\geq 0.

The boundary condition (
23b
) is trivially satisfied. Thus,
d
indeed satisfies (
23
) in the viscosity sense at the ridge.
