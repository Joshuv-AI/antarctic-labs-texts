Abstract

We present a novel candidate for cold dark matter consisting of condensed Cooper pairs in a theory of interacting fermions with broken chiral symmetry. Establishing the thermal history from the early radiation era to the present, the fermions are shown to behave like standard radiation at high temperatures, but then experience a critical era decaying faster than radiation, akin to freeze-out that sets the relic abundance. Through a second-order phase transition, fermion - antifermion pairs condense and the system asymptotes towards zero temperature and pressure. By the present era, the non-relativistic, massive condensate decays slightly faster than in the standard scenario – a unique prediction that may be tested by combined measurements of the cosmic microwave background and large scale structure. We also show that in the case of massive fermions, the phase transition is frustrated, and freeze-out instead leaves a residual, long-lived source of dark energy.

In brief, we present a scenario in which the relativistic NJL fermions decouple from the SM at early times, evolving like radiation with separate temperature from the thermal bath of SM particles. When the temperature drops past the axial chemical potential, the interacting fermion system begins to stiffen with an effective equation of state
\displaystyle w\equiv p/\rho=1
, defined as the ratio of the homogeneous pressure to energy density. The duration of this stage sets the relic dark matter abundance, similar to the role of freeze out for thermal dark matter. Below a critical temperature, the system undergoes a phase transition to the true vacuum, and the fermions coalesce into a cold, massive condensate as CDM. The standard Big Bang cosmology with dark matter proceeds as expected, from approximately GeV scales on down in the example we present.

In a second scenario, massive fermions are shown to spoil the phase transition, and the condensate is stuck in a long-lived, metastable vacuum as dark energy. The thermal history dictates that the equation of state proceeds in stages as
\displaystyle w=1/3\,\rightarrow\,1\,\rightarrow\,-1

(
Zlatev et al. 1999
)

Chapter 2
Particle Model

2.1
NJL Theory

The proposed NJL theory of CDM (and dark energy) is inspired by analogy with BCS superconductivity, consisting of fermion pairs
\displaystyle\bar{\psi}\psi
each with Dirac mass
\displaystyle m
and a quartic, scalar, attractive self-interaction with coupling
\displaystyle M>0
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\bar{\psi}\left(i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m\right)\psi-\kappa\bar{\psi}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5}\psi+\frac{\left(\bar{\psi}\psi\right)^{2}}{M^{2}}.

(2.1)

Here
\displaystyle\kappa
is the axial chemical potential associated with an asymmetry between left- and right-handed fermions:
\displaystyle n_{5}=n_{L}-n_{R}=\langle\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}\psi\rangle\neq 0
. Its origin can be prescribed to a baryogenesis scenario that takes place above the valid energy scale of this model. Possible scenarios include an electroweak phase transition with handedness, primordial chiral magnetic fields, and chiral gravitational waves. Since chemical potential
\displaystyle\mu
is zero, the
\displaystyle U(1)
vector charge
\displaystyle\langle\bar{\psi}\gamma^{0}\psi\rangle=n-\bar{n}=0
is neutral by construction. The Cooper pairs in this theory are fermion-antifermion pairs of like helicity, equal and opposite momenta, which are drawn together by the self-interaction. Since the Hubble expansion rate is negligible compared to the energy scales and parameters of the theory, we are justified to work in flat space, using temperature as our clock.

Without axial asymmetry, the theory is effectively the low-momentum limit (
\displaystyle p^{2}\ll M^{2}
) of the Yukawa interaction, where
\displaystyle M
is interpreted as the mediating boson mass. We note similarities and differences between our NJL theory (scalar-exchanging) and the four-Fermi interaction (with V-A structure). The latter is reproduced in the low-momentum limit (
\displaystyle p^{2}\ll M_{W}^{2}
, the W-boson mass) of the weak interaction. Both are contact interactions with a scattering rate of
\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm scatter}=\langle n\sigma v\rangle\sim G_{F}^{2}T^{5}
, where
\displaystyle G_{F}=\frac{1}{M_{W}^{2}}
is the weak coupling constant. Below, we illustrate a boson mediated scattering process (left) and low-momentum contact scattering (right).

The nonzero
\displaystyle\kappa
construction is justified as follows. The chirality flipping rate
\displaystyle\Gamma_{f}
defines the rate at which chiral asymmetry is erased. There are two potential sources of chirality flipping in the Lagrangian,
\displaystyle m\bar{\psi}\psi
and
\displaystyle\frac{(\bar{\psi}\psi)^{2}}{M^{2}}
, because they contain chiral mixing terms
\displaystyle\bar{\psi}\psi=\bar{\psi}_{L}\psi_{R}+\psi_{L}\bar{\psi}_{R}
. Chirality flipping from
\displaystyle m\bar{\psi}\psi
is negligible due to the small mass that concerns us. By analogy with weak interaction, the scattering cross section is
\displaystyle\sigma=\frac{T^{2}}{M^{4}}
and the scattering rate
\displaystyle\Gamma=\langle n\sigma v\rangle
. In the context of cosmology, since
\displaystyle n\propto a^{-3}\propto T^{3}
and
\displaystyle v=c=1
,
\displaystyle\Gamma=\frac{T^{5}}{M^{4}}
coincides with the helicity flipping rate. Intuitively, since chirality is equivalent to the time-invariant (conserved) fermion helicity in the
\displaystyle m\rightarrow 0
limit, a mass factor relates a definite helicity state with a definite chirality state. (In Appendix C, we prove helicity is conserved in our scalar-exchanging interaction.) For this reason, the quartic interaction has
\displaystyle\Gamma_{f}=\left(\frac{m}{3T}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{T^{5}}{M^{4}}\right)

(
Pavlović et al. 2016
;
Boyarsky et al. 2021
)
. For subsequent considerations of CDM and dark energy, the mass is either zero or small and suppresses chirality flipping. Therefore,
\displaystyle\Gamma_{f}
is either zero or falls below the Hubble expansion rate
\displaystyle H
sufficiently early, and the axial asymmetry persists.

To better understand this theory, we write down the fermionic path integral
\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}
:

\mathcal{Z}=\int D\bar{\psi}D\psi e^{i\int d^{4}x\mathcal{L}}

(2.2)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
is from Eq.(
2.1
). Since it is beneficial to work with scalar fields in cosmology, we now introduce an auxiliary scalar field, the order parameter
\displaystyle\Delta
, which allows us to integrate out the fermions and rewrite Eq.(
2.1
) as a scalar theory. Physically,
\displaystyle\Delta
is interpreted as the energy gap. With the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation
(
Hubbard 1959
)
, we rewrite the identity operator:

1=\int D\Delta\exp\left[-\int d^{4}x\left(\frac{1}{2}{M\Delta}-\frac{1}{M}{\bar{\psi}\psi}\right)^{2}\right]

(2.3)

Thus, we arrive at

\int D\Delta\exp{i\int d^4x\left(\frac{\bar{\psi}\psi}{M}\right)^2}=\int D\Delta\exp{i\int d^4x \left(\bar{\psi}\psi\Delta-\frac{M^2\Delta^2}{4}\right)}.

(2.4)

The fermion theory can be rewritten with a Grassman spinor property

\int D\bar{\psi}D\psi\exp(i\int d^4x\bar\psi\mathcal{G}^{-1}\psi)=\det(\mathcal{G}^{-1}).

(2.5)

Here, the inverse Green’s function is the Fourier space equation of motion:

\mathcal{G}^{-1}=-\gamma^{\mu}k_{\mu}+\Delta-m-\kappa\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5}

(2.6)

Working in Fourier space
\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\rightarrow ik_{\mu}
:

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{4}M^{2}\Delta^{2}+\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\ln\det\mathcal{G}^{-1},

(2.7)

Redefining
\displaystyle\Delta-m\rightarrow\Delta
, the tree-level contribution with one-loop corrections are

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{4}M^{2}(\Delta+m)^{2}+\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\ln U_{+}U_{-},

(2.8)

where
\displaystyle U_{\pm}=\Delta^{2}-\omega^{2}+(k\pm\kappa)^{2}
. The corresponding path integral is now:

\mathcal{Z}=\int D\Delta\exp{i \int d^4x \left[-\frac{1}{4} M^2 \Delta^2 -i\ln Z_f(\Delta)\right]},

(2.9)

where
\displaystyle\ln Z_{f}(\Delta)=\int d^{4}x\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}(\ln U_{+}+\ln U_{-})
.

2.2
Zeta Regularization

The integral in Eq.(
2.8
) is now explicitly evaluated, first over energy and then momentum. We treat the energy integration by Wick rotating
\displaystyle\omega\rightarrow i\omega_{E}
:

\epsilon=i\int\frac{d\omega_{E}}{2\pi}\left[\ln\left(\Delta^{2}+\omega_{E}^{2}+(k-\kappa)^{2}\right)+(\kappa\to-\kappa)\right].

(2.10)

This integral is formally divergent, but we can Zeta regularize it with a mathematical device:

\epsilon=-i\left(\frac{d}{ds}\int\frac{d\omega_{E}}{2\pi}\left[\Delta^{2}+\omega_{E}^{2}+(k-\kappa)^{2}\right]^{-s}+(\kappa\to-\kappa)\right)_{s=0}.

(2.11)

Recall the integral definition of the
\displaystyle\Gamma(s)
function for Re
\displaystyle(s)>0

(
Sebah & Gourdon 2002
)
:

\Gamma(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{s-1}{\rm e}^{-t}.

(2.12)

We observe that under transformation of variables
\displaystyle t=ux

\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{s-1}{\rm e}^{-tx}=x^{-s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,du\,u^{s-1}\,{\rm e}^{-u}\,\rightarrow x^{-s}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{s-1}\,{\rm e}^{-tx}.

(2.13)

Therefore, the energy integral becomes:

\epsilon=-i\left(\frac{d}{ds}\,\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\,\int\frac{d\omega_{E}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{s-1}\left[{\rm e}^{-t(\Delta^{2}+\omega_{E}^{2}+(k-\kappa)^{2})}+(\kappa\to-\kappa)\right]\right)_{s=0}.

(2.14)

The Gaussian integration result

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega_{E}}{2\pi}{\rm e}^{-t\omega_{E}^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}

(2.15)

enables us to simplify this integral:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-i\left(\frac{d}{ds}\,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(s)}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{s-3/2}\left[{\rm e}^{-t(\Delta^{2}+(k-\kappa)^{2})}+(\kappa\to-\kappa)\right]\right)_{s=0}

(2.16)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{d}{ds}\frac{\Gamma(s-\tfrac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(s)}\left[\left(\Delta^{2}+(k-\kappa)^{2}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}-s}+(\kappa\to-\kappa)\right]\right)_{s=0},

(2.17)

where in the second equality, we used the identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{s-3/2}{\rm e}^{-tx}=\Gamma(s-\tfrac{1}{2})\,x^{-(s-1/2)}.

(2.18)

Approximating
\displaystyle\epsilon
at leading order for small values of
\displaystyle s>0
,

\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\Gamma(s-\tfrac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(s)}x^{\tfrac{1}{2}-s}\right)=\frac{d}{ds}\left(-2\sqrt{\pi}s+{\cal O}(s^{2})\right),

(2.19)

we find

\displaystyle\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle i\left(\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+(k-\kappa)^{2}}+\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+(k+\kappa)^{2}}\right)

(2.20)

\displaystyle\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{i}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}k^{2}\,dk\,\left(\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+(k-\kappa)^{2}}+\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+(k+\kappa)^{2}}\right)

(2.21)

\displaystyle\displaystyle L_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}M^{2}\Delta^{2}-iA.

(2.22)

2.3
Dimensional Regularization

\displaystyle A
is another formally divergent loop integral. Let us define

I_{\pm}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,k^{2}\,\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+(k\pm\kappa)^{2}}

(2.23)

and change variables
\displaystyle u_{\pm}=k\pm\kappa
. We get
\displaystyle I_{+}+I_{-}=2\left(J_{2}+\kappa^{2}J_{0}\right)
, where

\displaystyle\displaystyle J_{n}=\int_{0}^{\infty}duu^{n}\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+u^{2}}.

(2.24)

Per dimensional regularization, we let the number of spacetime dimensions
\displaystyle D

\displaystyle 4
from below. Using Eq.(11.A.2) of Weinberg’s Quantum Field Theory Volume 1
(
Weinberg 1995

\displaystyle\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu^{4-D}\int dkk^{D-2}\left.\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+k^{2}}\right|_{D=4-\epsilon}

(2.25)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu^{4-D}\Delta^{D}\left.\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{D-1}{2})\Gamma(-\tfrac{D}{2})}{2\Gamma(-\tfrac{1}{2})}\right|_{D=4-\epsilon}

(2.26)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta^{4}\left(\frac{\mu}{\Delta}\right)^{\epsilon}\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{3-\epsilon}{2})\Gamma(-2+\tfrac{\epsilon}{2})}{4\sqrt{\pi}}=-\frac{\Delta^{4}}{8}\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\frac{\Delta^{4}}{32}\left(1+4\ln\frac{\Delta}{2\mu}\right)+{\cal O}(\epsilon),

(2.27)

By a similar procedure:

J_{0}=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2}\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{4}\Delta^{2}\left(1-2\ln\frac{\Delta}{2\mu}\right)+{\cal O}(\epsilon).

(2.28)

Since this is a low-momentum effective field theory, we can absorb
\displaystyle\epsilon
and
\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\Lambda_{UV}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\ln 2\mu=\ln\Lambda

V_{\rm eff}\left(\Delta\right)=\frac{1}{4}M^{2}(\Delta+m)^{2}+\frac{\Delta^{4}}{32\pi^{2}}\left(1+4\frac{\ln{\Delta}}{\Lambda_{UV}}\right)-\frac{\kappa^{2}\Delta^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\left(1-2\frac{\ln\Delta}{\Lambda_{UV}}\right).

(2.29)

where
\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(\Delta)=-L_{\rm eff}(\Delta)+\mathcal{O}(\partial_{\mu}\Delta)^{2}
is not quite the effective potential.

2.4
Effective Potential

In cosmology where negative pressure drives the expansion, we lift the effective potential by
\displaystyle V_{0}
so that the non-thermal
\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}
is zero at the minimum:

V_{\rm eff}\left(\Delta\right)=\frac{1}{4}M^{2}(\Delta+m)^{2}+\frac{\Delta^{4}}{32\pi^{2}}\left(1+4\frac{\ln{\Delta}}{\Lambda_{UV}}\right)-\frac{\kappa^{2}\Delta^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\left(1-2\frac{\ln\Delta}{\Lambda_{UV}}\right)+V_{0}.

(2.30)

Eq.(
2.30
) is one of the main results of this thesis. Self-consistency of this effective theory requires
\displaystyle M\gtrsim\Lambda_{UV}\gtrsim\kappa
, since
\displaystyle M
is the tree-level mass scale. A striking feature of this model is the emergence of an exponentially-suppressed energy scale. For sufficiently small mass
\displaystyle m\ll\Delta_{0}
, there is an exponentially suppressed local minimum, the gap, at approximately

\Delta_{0}=\Lambda_{UV}\exp(-\frac{\pi^2M^2}{2\kappa^2}).

(2.31)

Since
\displaystyle M>\kappa
,
\displaystyle\Delta_{0}
is small with respect to
\displaystyle\Lambda_{UV}
.

2.6
Vector Charge

The theory’s lack of chemical potential
\displaystyle\mu
ensures the U(1) vector charge density is zero. This is now verified by averaging the zeroth component of the vector current
\displaystyle\rho_{V}=\langle\bar{\psi}\gamma^{0}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle b\bar{\psi}\gamma^{0}\psi

\mathcal{L}=\bar{\psi}\left(i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m\right)\psi-\kappa\bar{\psi}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5}\psi+\frac{\left(\bar{\psi}\psi\right)^{2}}{M^{2}}+b\bar{\psi}\gamma^{0}\psi.

(2.34)

The equation of motion is now:

{\cal G}^{-1}=i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-\kappa\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5}+\Delta+b\gamma^{0}

(2.35)

It thus follows that the determinant of
\displaystyle{\cal G}^{-1}
is

\ln{\rm det}{\cal G}^{-1}=\ln\left(\Delta^{2}-(b+\omega)^{2}+(k-\kappa)^{2}\right)+(\kappa\to-\kappa).

(2.36)

Now, define
\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}=\omega+b

\displaystyle\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle-\infty

\displaystyle\infty

Defining

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}=\frac{1}{4}M^{2}(\Delta^{\prime 2}-m^{2})+\frac{\Delta^{\prime 4}}{32\pi^{2}}\left(1+4\ln\frac{\Delta^{\prime}}{\Lambda}\right)-\frac{\kappa^{2}\Delta^{\prime 2}}{4\pi^{2}}\left(1-2\ln\frac{\Delta^{\prime}}{\Lambda}\right).

Pseudoscalar + Scalar Interaction

It is natural to consider a pseudoscalar interaction in addition to the scalar interaction:

L=\bar{\psi}(i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m)\psi-\kappa\bar{\psi}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5}\psi+\frac{(\bar{\psi}\psi)^{2}}{M^{2}}+\frac{(\bar{\psi}i\gamma^{5}\psi)^{2}}{M_{5}^{2}}.

(2.39)

In parallel with the single-field scenario, we define:

\displaystyle\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int D\Sigma\exp\left[-\int d^{4}x\left(\frac{1}{2}{M\Sigma}-\frac{1}{M}{\bar{\psi}\psi}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}M_{5}^{2}\langle\Pi\rangle=\langle\bar{\psi}i\gamma^{5}\psi\rangle

\ln\det\mathcal{G}^{-1}=\ln\left[\Sigma^{2}+\Pi^{2}-2\Sigma m+m^{2}-\omega^{2}+(k-\kappa)^{2}\right]\left[\Sigma^{2}+\Pi^{2}-2\Sigma m+m^{2}-\omega^{2}+(k+\kappa)^{2}\right].

We now change variables to
\displaystyle\Delta=\sqrt{\Sigma^{2}+\Pi^{2}}

\displaystyle\Theta=\arctan(\Pi/\Sigma)

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}-2m\Delta\cos\Theta+m^{2}\geq m^{2}\sin\Theta^{2}\geq 0

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{M^{2}\cos^{2}\Theta+M_{5}^{2}\sin^{2}\Theta}{4}\left(m^{2}\cos 2\Theta+\Delta^{2}_{5}+2m\cos\Theta\sqrt{\Delta^{2}_{5}-m^{2}\sin^{2}\Theta}\right)

(2.41)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\Delta^{4}_{5}}{32\pi^{2}}\left(1+4\log\frac{\Delta_{5}}{\Lambda}\right)-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\left(1-2\log\frac{\Delta_{5}}{\Lambda}\right)\Delta^{2}_{5}

(2.42)

For
\displaystyle m=0
,
\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}
changes at the tree-level but not at one-loop. If
\displaystyle M=M_{5}

\displaystyle\Theta

We further note the existence of a Nambu-Goldstone boson in this theory, because the pseudoscalar interaction spontaneously breaks the chiral U(1) symmetry. Although this generalized case is interesting from the perspective of particle physics, we will focus on the single-field scenario in the subsequent discussion.

Chapter 3
Finite Temperature Field Theory

3.1
Formalism

In the previous section, we have implicitly assumed the effective field theory is at zero temperature. To understand its unique thermal behavior, we calculate its corresponding finite temperature field theory. This is achieved through the Matsubara formalism, where we impose periodic (antiperiodic) boundary conditions on bosonic (fermionic) quantum fields over imaginary, Wick-rotated time. Their periodicity is equal to the inverse temperature
\displaystyle\beta=\frac{1}{T}
, which diverges to infinity at the
\displaystyle T=0
limit. Therefore, in the Fourier transform to momentum space, we replace the
\displaystyle\omega
integral of the zero temperature theory with a Matsubara summation over discrete fermionic modes,
\displaystyle\omega_{n}=\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{\beta}
,
\displaystyle\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\rightarrow\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{-1}\rightarrow\beta\mathcal{G}^{-1}
. The resulting one loop correction is thus

\mathcal{L}_{1}(\Delta,T)=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\ln\beta^{4}U_{n+}U_{n-}

where
\displaystyle U_{n\pm}=\Delta^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}+(k\pm\kappa)^{2}
.
By the complex identity
(
Kapusta & Gale 2007
)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{i\omega_{n}\delta}\ln(\beta s - i \omega_n \beta)=\ln(1 + {\rm e}^{-\beta s}),

(3.1)

we sum over the fermionic frequencies:

\mathcal{L}_{1}(\Delta,T)=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\beta}\left[\ln(1+e^{\beta Q_-})+\ln(1+e^{-\beta Q_-})+(Q_{-}\rightarrow Q_{+})\right]

where
\displaystyle Q_{\pm}=\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+(k\pm\kappa)^{2}}
. Notice that:

\frac{1}{\beta}\ln(1 + {\rm e}^{\beta Q_{\pm}})=\frac{1}{\beta}\ln({\rm e}^{\beta Q_{\pm}}(1 + {\rm e}^{-\beta Q_{\pm}}))=Q_{\pm}+\frac{1}{\beta}\ln(1 + {\rm e}^{-\beta Q_{\pm}}).

(3.2)

The theory can be separated into a zero temperature contribution as well as finite temperature corrections:

V_{\rm eff}(\Delta,\,T)=V_{\rm eff}(T=0)-\frac{2}{\pi^{2}\beta^{4}}[I_{2}(\beta\Delta)+\kappa^{2}\beta^{2}I_{0}(\beta\Delta)]

(3.3)

where

I_{n}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,s^{n}\ln\left[1+e^{-\sqrt{s^{2}+x^{2}}}\right].

(3.4)

Eq.(
3.3
) is important because it contains the unique thermal history of the system.

In Eq.(
3.3
),
\displaystyle I_{0}(0)=\pi^{2}/12
and
\displaystyle I_{2}(0)=7\pi^{4}/360
. We can obtain high and low temperature approximations for the thermal contributions. At
\displaystyle T\gg\kappa,\,m,\,\Delta
, when the minimum of
\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}
is at
\displaystyle\Delta=0
:

V_{\rm eff}=-\frac{7}{8}\frac{2\pi^{2}}{45}T^{4}.

(3.5)

where
\displaystyle T^{4}
is characteristic of cosmological radiation. At low temperatures, we approximate
\displaystyle\ln(1+e^{-\beta\Delta})\simeq e^{-\beta\Delta}
and
\displaystyle I_{n}(x)
can be analytically integrated and written in terms of the modified-Bessel and Gamma functions.
\displaystyle I_{0}(x)=xK_{1}(x)
and
\displaystyle I_{2}(x)=x^{2}K_{2}(x)
, both of which decay to zero as
\displaystyle x\rightarrow\infty
. The leading terms in the finite-temperature potential are given by

I_{n}(x\gg 1)\approx\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}x^{\frac{n+1}{2}}{\rm e}^{-x},

(3.6)

meaning finite temperature corrections to the potential are exponentially suppressed for
\displaystyle\Delta\gg T
. In the case
\displaystyle\kappa\gg\Delta\gg T
, the finite-temperature contribution is approximately

-\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi T}{2\Delta}}{\rm e}^{-\Delta/T}\Delta\kappa^{2}T,

(3.7)

where the leading term depends on the asymmetry through
\displaystyle\kappa
and contributes negligibly to the potential.

3.2
Second Order Phase Transition

3.2.1
Gap Equation

The thermal history is now clear from a numerical standpoint.
Above a critical temperature,
\displaystyle T>T_{c}
, the gap field
\displaystyle\Delta

sits at zero. For
\displaystyle T<T_{c}
in the massless case the
field undergoes a second-order phase transition, where
the local minimum smoothly transfers from
\displaystyle\Delta=0
to
\displaystyle\Delta_{0}
. Fig.(
3.1
) illustrates this change in
\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}
as temperature drops below
\displaystyle T_{c}
, given an example of CDM parameters at TeV scale. The gap equation is defined by
\displaystyle\frac{\partial V_{\rm eff}}{\partial\Delta}=0
. Thus, we take the partial derivative of
\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}M^{2}\Delta^{2}-\mathcal{L}_{1}(\Delta,T)
:

\frac{1}{4}M^{2}=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left[\frac{\tanh(\frac{1}{2}\beta Q_k^-)}{2Q_{k}^{-}}+\frac{\tanh(\frac{1}{2}\beta Q_k^+)}{2Q_{k}^{+}}\right]=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left[\frac{1-2f(Q_{k}^{-})}{2Q_{k}^{-}}+\frac{1-2f(Q_{k}^{+})}{2Q_{k}^{+}}\right]

(3.8)

where
\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta x}+1}
.

3.2.2
Critical Temperature

By definition, since the gap at the critical temperature gives
\displaystyle\Delta(T_{c})=0
, the critical gap equation is:

\frac{1}{4}M^{2}=T_{c}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left[\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+(k-\kappa)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+(k+\kappa)^{2}}\right]

(3.9)

After substituting variables and similar dimensional regularization as in Eq.(

):

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}M^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{c}\sum_{n}\left[\kappa^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\xi^{2}d\xi}{\xi^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}}\right]

(3.10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{c}\sum_{n}\left[\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\frac{\pi}{\absolutevalue{\omega_n}}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{(2\pi)^{(3-\epsilon)/2}}{\Gamma(3/2-\epsilon/2)}\Gamma\left(\frac{3-\epsilon}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\epsilon-1}{2}\right)\frac{\omega_{n}^{1-\epsilon}}{2\Gamma(1)}\right]

(3.11)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\pi T_{c}\sum_{n}\left[\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\omega_n}}-\frac{\omega_{n}}{\sqrt{2}}\right]+O(\epsilon)

(3.12)

at leading order. We notice that positive and negative contributions to the sum over
\displaystyle\omega_{n}
cancel out. Plugging in
\displaystyle\omega_{n}=T_{c}(2n+1)\pi
and a summation cutoff
\displaystyle N=\frac{\Lambda_{UV}}{4\pi T_{c}}
, we arrive at:

\frac{\pi^{2}M^{2}}{4}=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}\ln(\frac{e^{\gamma_e}\Lambda_{UV}}{\pi T_c}).

(3.13)

Therefore,
\displaystyle T_{c}=\frac{e^{\gamma_{e}}\Delta_{0}}{\pi}
, where
\displaystyle\gamma_{e}
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. In the Appendix, we verify the
\displaystyle T_{c}
formula with an alternative method. The termination of this phase transition as
\displaystyle T\rightarrow 0
is less well-defined, because temperature contributions only relax but do not completely vanish. The following analytical fit has been compared to the numerical solution and proved useful
(
Tinkham 2004

\Delta\simeq\Delta_{0}\tanh(\pi e^{-\gamma_e}\sqrt{\frac{T_c}{T}-1}).

(3.14)

This indicates, for example, that
\displaystyle\Delta\simeq 0.9\Delta_{0}
at
\displaystyle T\simeq 0.59T_{c}
.

Before embedding this model in a cosmological context, we compute kinetic contributions to the effective field theory at
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\partial_{\mu}\Delta\right)^{2}

3.3
Kinetic Dispersion

To compute first-order kinetic fluctuations to the theory, we consider the self-energy diagram below. Since we work with a contact interaction without mediating bosons, we will set
\displaystyle k=0
in the final step.

At finite temperature, the diagram corresponds to an amplitude

\mathcal{M}=-\text{Tr}\left[i\mathcal{G}(p)i\mathcal{G}(p+k)\right]=-\sum_{i\omega_{n}}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\text{Tr}\left[i\mathcal{G}(p)i\mathcal{G}(p+k)\right].

(3.15)

Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{-1}(p)=-(\not{p}-\Delta+\kappa\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5})
for our given equation of motion, the fermion propagator is the Green’s function
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(p)=-i(\not{p}-\Delta+\kappa\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5})^{-1}

\mathcal{G}(p)=-i\frac{(\not{p}+\Delta+\kappa\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5})(p^{2}+\Delta^{2}+\kappa^{2}+\kappa\{\not{p},\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5}\})}{4\kappa^{2}(p_{0}^{2}+p^{2})-(p^{2}+\Delta^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}}.

(3.16)

Defining
\displaystyle\xi(p)=-4\kappa^{2}(p_{0}^{2}+p^{2})+(p^{2}+\Delta^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
takes the form:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

(3.17)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

(3.18)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-

(3.19)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{4\kappa^{2}\left[q^{2}p_{0}q_{0}+2\absolutevalue{p}^{2}\left(\Delta^{2}+q^{2}-2\absolutevalue{q}^{2}\right)\right]}{\xi(p)\xi(q)}

(3.20)

where
\displaystyle p=q+k
,

\displaystyle\xi^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\delta\Delta\partial_{\nu}\delta\Delta

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle k^{2}-
derivatives of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle d^{4}p
in the zero-temperature case. Thus:

\xi^{00}=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{12\pi^{2}\Delta^{2}}\left[1-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\left(4+3\ln(\frac{\Delta}{\Lambda_p})\right)\right]

where

This gives a negligible LSS group velocity of:

v_{g}=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=\frac{1/\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{1+m_{\rm eff}^{2}/c_{p}^{2}k^{2}}}\simeq\frac{k}{m_{\rm eff}}\sim\mathcal{O}(10^{-36})

\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{2\left(\Delta^{4}-(p-\kappa)^{4}+\omega_{n}^{4}-6\Delta^{2}\omega_{n}^{2}\right)}{\left(\Delta^{2}+(p-\kappa)^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}\right)^{4}}+\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\kappa\rightarrow-\kappa\right).

3.4
Size of Cooper Pairs

Fermion pairs are spaced apart at the correlation length scale. The fermion-antifermion correlation function
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x,t;y,t^{\prime})=\langle\bar{\psi}(x,t)\psi(y,t^{\prime})\rangle
describes the probability for a particle created at
\displaystyle(x,t)
to be annihilated with its antiparticle at
\displaystyle(y,t^{\prime})
. Thus, the length scale associated with the Green’s function captures the characteristic size of fermionic Cooper pairs.
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x,t;y,t^{\prime})
is obtained via a Fourier transform of the Green’s function
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\vec{p},\omega)
:

\mathcal{G}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t^{\prime}))=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\mathcal{G}(\vec{p},\omega_{n})e^{i\vec{p}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{y})-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}

(3.29)

where we assumed finite temperature. First recall that

\mathcal{G}(\vec{p},\omega)=-i\frac{(\not{p}+\Delta+\kappa\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5})(p^{2}+\Delta^{2}+\kappa^{2}+\kappa\{\not{p},\gamma^{0}\gamma^{5}\})}{4\kappa^{2}(\omega^{2}+p^{2})-(p^{2}+\Delta^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}}.

(3.30)

Taking its trace, we get

\Tr(i\mathcal{G}(\vec{p},\omega))=\frac{4\Delta(p^{2}+\Delta^{2}+\kappa^{2})}{-(p^{2}+\Delta^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}+4\kappa^{2}(p^{2}+\omega^{2})}.

(3.31)

Here, we are interested in the spatial correlation function with
\displaystyle r=\absolutevalue{\vec{x}-\vec{y}}
and same time
\displaystyle t=t^{\prime}
. Therefore,

\xi(r)=\Tr(i\mathcal{G}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t))=\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\Tr(i\mathcal{G}(\vec{p},\omega_n))e^{i\vec{p}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{y})}.

(3.32)

In evaluating the Matsubara sum over fermionic frequencies, we used:

\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{(i\omega_{n})^{2}-a^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2a}\left(1-\frac{2}{1+e^{\beta a}}\right)

(3.33)

for
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{R}
. We arrive at the integral

\xi(r)=-\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\vec{p}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{y})}\frac{\Delta}{\kappa}\frac{p^{2}+\Sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{(p^{2}+\Sigma^{2})^{2}-4\kappa^{2}p^{2}}}\tanh\left[\frac{\beta}{4\kappa}\sqrt{(p^{2}+\Sigma^{2})^{2}-4\kappa^{2}p^{2}}\right]

(3.34)

where
\displaystyle\Sigma^{2}=\kappa^{2}+\Delta^{2}
. Here, the argument of
\displaystyle\tanh
is positive, with a minimum value
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\beta\Delta
. For
\displaystyle\Delta/T\gg 1
, as is the case for our CDM model, the
\displaystyle\tanh
function is effectively one. Thus,

\xi(r)\simeq-\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\vec{p}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{y})}\frac{\Delta}{\kappa}\frac{p^{2}+\Sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{(p^{2}+\Sigma^{2})^{2}-4\kappa^{2}p^{2}}}.

(3.35)

We now define
\displaystyle f(p)=(p^{2}+\Sigma^{2})/\sqrt{(p^{2}+\Sigma^{2})^{2}-4\kappa^{2}p^{2}}
and observe that
\displaystyle f\to 1
at
\displaystyle p\to 0,\,\infty
. However,
\displaystyle f
is sharply peaked at
\displaystyle p=\kappa
with amplitude
\displaystyle\kappa/\Delta
. Numerical simulations reveal its proximity to a Gaussian function with narrow width
\displaystyle\sigma=\sqrt{3}\Delta
:

f\simeq 1+\frac{\kappa}{\Delta}\exp\left({-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(p-\kappa)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(3.36)

Using the above approximation for
\displaystyle f
, the correlation function is now

\xi(r)\simeq-\frac{\Delta}{\kappa}\delta(\vec{x}-\vec{y})-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dp\,p^{2}\,\frac{\sin(p r)}{pr}\,\exp\left({-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(p-\kappa)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(3.37)

Since
\displaystyle\sigma\ll\kappa
, the Gaussian behaves as a delta function,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left({-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(p-\kappa)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\right)\simeq\delta(p-\kappa).

(3.38)

In this case, the correlation function evaluates to

\xi(r)\simeq-\frac{\Delta}{\kappa}\delta(\vec{x}-\vec{y})-\frac{\sqrt{6\pi}}{2\pi^{2}}\Delta\kappa^{2}\frac{\sin(\kappa r)}{\kappa r}.

(3.39)

For the spatially separated fermions, the Dirac delta function is zero. We observe that the characteristic length scale on which the correlation function decays is
\displaystyle r_{c}\sim\kappa^{-1}
. In our example of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\kappa)
at TeV-scale, the correlation length is microscopic. This shows that on astrophysical scales concerned with dark matter, condensed fermion pairs behave effectively as one particle.

Chapter 4
Cosmological Implications

4.1
Thermal History

In this chapter, we follow the standard definitions of pressure and energy density,
\displaystyle p=-V
and
\displaystyle\rho=T\frac{dp}{dT}-p

(
Kolb & Turner 1981
)
. This definition takes into account all fermions in the grand canonical ensemble with interaction specified by Eq.(
2.1
). As a result of the fermion thermal field theory, there are three distinct epochs in their cosmic evolution.

4.1.1
Radiation Epoch

At high temperatures,
\displaystyle T\gg\kappa,m
, the gap field lies at the potential minimum
\displaystyle\Delta=0
. Then by definition above and Eq.(
3.5
):

\rho=\frac{7}{8}\frac{g\pi^{2}}{30}T^{4}=3p.

(4.1)

This agrees exactly with the energy density and pressure of a relativistic gas of fermions with degeneracy factor
\displaystyle g=4
, which includes both particle and antiparticle and two helicities. Furthermore, the fermion equation of state
\displaystyle w=\frac{p}{\rho}=\frac{1}{3}
behaves like standard cosmological radiation. Thus, we assume these fermions were once in thermal equilibrium with the SM, sharing the same temperature
\displaystyle T_{\Delta}=T_{\gamma}
as photons. We further assume scattering processes maintaining equilibrium later became inefficient and the fermion system decoupled, so that
\displaystyle T_{\Delta}
evolved separately, adiabatically thereafter:
\displaystyle s_{\Delta}=(\rho_{\Delta}+p_{\Delta})/T_{\Delta}\propto a^{-3}
, where
\displaystyle a
is the cosmic expansion scale factor. The subsequent dropout of SM species from equilibrium heats the photons so that
\displaystyle T_{\gamma}>T_{\Delta}
. The presence of this additional, relic radiation contributes to the rate of cosmic expansion, which in turn is tightly constrained through observations of the light element abundances predicted by Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN), and through observations of the cosmic microwave background (CMB). These constraints can be satisfied by enforcing a sufficiently early time for decoupling. For example, decoupling prior to the QCD phase transition would be adequate to lower
\displaystyle T_{\Delta}
relative to
\displaystyle T_{\gamma}
to satisfy the Planck 2018 bound
\displaystyle\Delta N_{\rm eff}<0.3
(95% CL, one-tailed Planck TT,TE,EE+lowE+lensing+BAO
(
Aghanim et al. 2020
)
) on the effective number of additional, relativistic particle species, as customarily measured in units of massless neutrinos. This constraint is easily satisfied by, for example, the TeV parameters in Fig.(
3.1
).

4.1.2
”Rapid-Decay” Epoch

As the temperature drops below
\displaystyle\kappa
, and the gap remains at
\displaystyle\Delta=0

\displaystyle I_{0}
term in Eq. (
3.3

\displaystyle I_{0}(0)=\pi^{2}/12

\displaystyle p_{\Delta}=\rho_{\Delta}=\kappa^{2}T^{2}/6

\displaystyle w_{\Delta}=1

\displaystyle\rho_{\Delta}\propto(1+z)^{6}

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\Delta
field decays faster than both radiation and non-relativistic matter. This
\displaystyle\kappa

4.5
Conclusions

In this thesis, we present a novel candidate for CDM and dark energy. In analogy with superconductivity, the dark matter particles are non-relativistic Cooper pairs of massless fermions. For dark energy, the potential energy is associated with pairs of massive fermions. We highlight the important role of the thermal history, in particular the mechanism of the chemical potential, in setting the relic abundance. The scenario further benefits from the exponentially suppressed physical scales, well below the cutoff of this low energy theory, that are generated by the gap field solution. The exponential suppression may help explain two small numbers in cosmology: the smallness of dark energy density
\displaystyle\left(2\times 10^{-3}\text{eV}\right)^{4}
, and the axial asymmetry
\displaystyle\eta\sim 10^{-10}
which may link dark matter to the baryon asymmetry. A unique prediction of the model is a non-zero, time-evolving equation of state,
\displaystyle w_{\Delta}
. Data from CMB and LSS surveys may be able to constrain
\displaystyle w_{\Delta}
down to the percent level across certain redshift bands
(
Kopp et al. 2018
;
Ilić et al. 2021
)
, and thereby test the energy scales and interactions of the fermion system. More precise constraints on
\displaystyle\Delta N_{\rm eff}
expected from future CMB missions such as the Simons Observatory
(
Ade et al. 2019
)
and CMB-S4
(
Abazajian et al. 2016
)
will further inform the energy scales and decoupling of these species. The slightly faster decay of CDM will also shift the geometry of the CMB acoustic oscillations, and may help relieve another outstanding problem of cosmology, the Hubble Tension
(
Freedman 2017
)
. We reserve for future work to investigate the imprint on the CMB and LSS, to consider the collisional nature of this species of dark matter, and embed this low energy theory in a more complete model of particle physics.
