Clinical evaluation of restorations made of ELS extra low shrinkage versus APT advanced polymer technology resin composite: a prospective randomized controlled clinical trial up to 3 years
Authors: Alev Özsoy-Kaner, Mahmut Kuşdemir, Funda Öztürk-Bozkurt, Tugba Toz-Akalin, Mutlu Özcan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe objective of this randomized clinical trial was to evaluate the clinical performance of restorative material extra low shrinkage (ELS) versus advanced polymer technology (APT) based resin composite for Class I and Class II preparations in permanent teeth.MethodsBetween 12.12.2017–26.10.2018, 30 patients aged between 18 and 44 years old (22 females, 8 males, mean age: 25,2) received randomly 30 pairs of restorations restored using either ELS (Saremco, Switzerland) or APT composite (Saremco, Switzerland) in Class I and Class II cavities. Two operators performed all restorations using ELS and APT resin composites according to manufacturer’s instructions. Two independent calibrated operators evaluated the restorations 2 weeks after placement (baseline), at 6 months, 1 year, 2 and 3 years using FDI criteria (Scores 1–5) for surface staining, marginal staining, marginal gap, marginal fracture, marginal irregularities, secondary caries, marginal tooth integrity, surface luster, color match and translucency, fracture of material and retention, occlusal wear, approximal contact point, patient view, tooth integrity and post operative sensitivity.ResultsSixty restorations were placed of which 37 (ELS: 18, APT:19) were in the maxilla and 23 (ELS: 12, APT:11) in the mandible. From the occluso-cervical (OC) perspective, the dimension of the preparations ranged between 5 mm and 2.7 mm and from buccolingual (BL) dimension between 4 mm and 2 mm. 32 (53.3%) restorations completed their 2-year follow up, 50 restorations (83.3%) completed their 3 years. In the 2-year follow ups 1 endodontic complication was recorded in the APT group. With the 3 year results a total of 4 failures (2 restorations at 1 year, 2 restorations at 2 year) were recorded up to now. Both resin composite restorations were clinically acceptable.ConclusionsThis study provided promising evidence for application of ELS and APT materials applied in Class I and II cavities.Trial registrationThe study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov with registration No. NCT03306576 at 2017-10-11 and approved by İstanbul Medipol University Ethics Committee with protocol No:10840098-604.01.01-E.21,426 in accordance with the Decleration of Helsinki.Keywords: Clinical trial, Dental materials, Restorative dentistry, Survival, Low shrinkage

Introduction

The search for the ideal restoration material resulted in the development of new restorative materials to meet the clinical requirements and expectations of the patients.

The expectation for esthetic restorations has brought about the widespread use of adhesive restorative materials and these materials are routinely used in operative dentistry to improve tooth tissues with minimal preparation, achieve more esthetic and long-term restorations. Esthetics, good surface texture, longevity, and low cost are important parameters from the patient’s perspective. Despite the evolution of resin composites and the improvement of the adhesive systems, composite restorations still present some drawbacks. The detrimental impact of resin composite polymerization shrinkage on restoration interface quality and stability has been recognized since the early use of this material is still one of the major drawbacks in adhesive dentistry, as it frequently leads to loss of marginal integrity or enamel fracture [1]. The main compounds of methacrylate-based resin composites are inorganic filler particles (glass, quartz), organic matrix (base monomers, pigments, photo-initiators, stabilizers), and the coupling agent (silane) to bond the inorganic filler particles to the organic matrix [2]. When the resin based composites are photo-polymerized, the van der Waals spaces between methacrylate monomers convert to shorter covalent bonds. The volume of resin-based composites shrinks, and stress arises at the bonding interface [3, 4]. These internal stresses are transferred to the tooth/restoration interface as tensile forces, because the resin composite shrinks towards the bonded surface [5], but is constrained by the rest of its mass, which is bonded to the opposing surface [6]. According to clinical studies, drawbacks such as cuspal deflection [7], enamel cracking [8], postoperative sensitivity, marginal discoloration and possibly secondary caries are often associated with loss of marginal integrity in composite restorations occurred as a result of polymerization shrinkage [9]. Many efforts have been made in order to reduce the volumetric shrinkage of composite resins, with different compensating procedures such as incremental methods [10], the use of ceramic inserts [11, 12], the application of base/liners [13], or indirect techniques. Various technological solutions were then investigated, including improved filler technology; improved, novel matrix structure with reduced shrinkage; use of stress-decreasing compounds within the resin matrix; changes in light-initiation technology to increase curing depth; and use of sonic vibrations and energy to favor flow and adaptation of highly filled resin composite [14]. To achieve optimal long term performance, the requirements will be first to manage polymerization stress buildup following restoration. Low-shrinking composites should help to avoid clinical problems that are commonly associated with composite restorations, such as post-operative sensitivity, enamel cracks, rapid discoloration and deterioration of restoration margins, early development of caries recurrence [15], with a new formula and improved sculptability. When using new simplified restorative systems featuring distinctive physicochemical characteristics, the potential impact of different parameters such as fatigue behavior and/or elastic modulus on restoration quality and behavior is unknown and justifies additional investigations. Instead of in vitro evaluations, consideration of a rather well-established clinical trials suggesting medium to long term observation periods to discriminately appraise the clinical performance of various operative protocols [14].

The objective of this controlled clinical trial was to evaluate the clinical performance of restorative material ELS versus APT resin composite for Class I and Class II cavities that needs to be restored in permanent teeth. The null hypothesis of the current study was that there would be no significant differences observed on the clinical performance between the tested materials.

Treatment protocol

The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement was used to design this clinical trial. Between 12.12.2017–26.10.2018, 30 patients aged between 18 and 44 years old (22 females, 8 males, mean age: 25.2) referred to the Department of Restorative Dentistry, Medipol University, Dental School, Istanbul, Turkey, received 60 Class I and II restorations by two operators. Before enrolment in the trial, all patients were provided with a written informed consent form approved by the ethics committee of the university institutional review board (Vote number of the Local Ethics Committee no: 10840098-604.01.01-E.21426; Clinical Trial Registration Number: NCT03306576), the study was performed in accordance with the Decleration of Helsinki. Information was given to each patient regarding the alternative treatment options.

Clinical procedures

Two operators applied all the restorations who have experience in restorative dentistry more than 15 and 22 years since graduation. Before the restorative procedures, cold pulp test was performed by spraying ice crystals (Nexcare, 3 M Oral Care, USA) on cotton pellet and applying this on the buccal surface of tooth. Authors randomly assigned one half of the restorations to receive ELS resin composite (Saremco, St Gallen, Switzerland) and the other half to APT (Saremco, St Gallen, Switzerland) resin composite restorations. 60 (33 molars and 27 premolars) were prepared and restored with ELS resin composite (30) or APT resin composite (30) restorations randomly by using randomization software program (https://www.random.org) one of each being in the same patient. In total, Class I and II fillings (n = 60) were placed in adjacent vital premolars or molars, of which were in the (18) maxilla and (12) in the mandible for ELS resin composite and (19) in maxilla and (11) in the mandible for APT resin composite.

Operative procedures were performed under local anesthesia if necessary and caries or existing restorations were removed under constant water-cooling. The preparation walls were finished with extra fine burs to achieve smooth surfaces. The operative field was cleaned with an air/water spray, gently air-dried, and then carefully isolated with cotton rolls and suction. Cavity dimensions were measured using periodontal probe. A metal matrix band (Adapt SuperCap Matrices, Kerr) and wooden wedge were placed for separation. The self-etch adhesive, (ELS Unibond, Saremco, Switzerland), was applied to the cavity walls and photo-polymerized for 10s with a polymerization device (Bluephase Style Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein). Then, ELS resin composite was inserted to the cavities in layers (2 mm) and photo-polymerized for 40s with the light intensity of 1200 mW/cm2. APT resin composite was inserted to the cavities after the application of the self-etch adhesive ELS Unibond as mentioned above.

After the polymerization of restorations, premature contacts were controlled using carbon paper and interproximal contacts were checked using dental floss and adjustments were made where needed. The restorations were finished under water-cooling with finishing burs and finally polishing was performed using discs and rubber points (Compo Master, Shofu Inc., Japan).

Evaluation

The evaluation protocol was performed in consideration of the FDI criteria [16]. For some parameters Semiquantitative Clinical Evaluation (Squace) was carried out by two independent calibrated observers (M.K., A.Ö.) [17]. Calibration was performed using Ecalib system prior to the study. When observers decided on different scores, inter-examiner agreement was reached by consensus after re-evaluation.

After baseline recordings (2 weeks after treatment), patients were followed at 6 months, 1, 2 and 3 years. Patients were instructed to report any restoration failure (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with statistical software (SPSS Statistics, v22.0IBM, NY, USA). Qualitative data were expressed by count and percentage. Comparisons of scores between materials were performed using McNemar, McNemar Bowker symmetry and Friedman tests. Kaplan-Meier Survival curves will be calculated in case of failures. In all analysis, two-tailed significance level was considered as 0.05. According to GPower, sample size is calculated 18 regarding to error margin 5% and power size %80.

Twenty-seven patients came at 3-year recall, so we were able to evaluate 50 restorations. In total, 50 restorations (83.3%) were evaluated at the 3-year recall. After finishing the preparation no enamel or dentin crack was observed. After 12 months. 2 restorations from ELS group showed edge chipping and scored as 4 (Functional properties-FDI 5) and accepted as failure. Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical performance of ELS and APT restorative materials in class I and class II cavities. After three years of follow up, no significant changes in functional, biological and esthetic properties were observed. The null hypothesis was therefore not rejected. Although composite resins were introduced as aesthetic materials for anterior restorations, their use was quickly extended to posterior teeth. Many efforts have been made to reduce the volumetric shrinkage of composite resins, with different approaches being used. New materials are being introduced claiming to present low polymerization shrinkage and being suitable for use posterior teeth, where setting stresses can be critical for the survival of the restoration [18]. ELS resin composite introduced to dentistry with a new formula and improved sculpt ability is based on BisGMA and BisEMA to reduce the shrinkage. BisGMA has a very high molecular weight, which provides lower polymerization shrinkage than other monomers and superior mechanical qualities, what explain its high usage as the main monomer for composite. The other monomer including ELS is BisEMA that differs from BisGMA because it does not present the pendant hydroxyl groups that form the hydrogen bonds among molecules and has significantly lower viscosity. Aiming to develop composite resins with low shrinkage, manufacturers replaced monomers such as TEGDMA with monomers with increased molecular weight, for instance, BisEMA. A previous report showed that composites containing BisEMA have less shrinkage than those based on TEGDMA as a diluent monomer [19, 20]. APT resin composite is a new material which consists of Bis-EMA and UDMA as inorganic fillers. Bis GMA and UDMA are usually combined with a low-viscosity TEGDMA monomer, whose addition improves the degree of conversion, filler loadings and handling characteristics. However, it also decreases the general mechanical properties and increases polymerization shrinkage and water sorption [21]. Bis-EMA can be added by the manufacturers to improve clinical consistency and overcome the high viscosity of Bis-GMA or UDMA [22]. A study evaluated the clinical performance of a low shrinkage TEGDMA/HEMA-free resin composite system in Class II restorations and concluded after 6 years period that Class II restorations performed with this resin composite showed good durability like hybrid or nanofiller resin composite systems containing HEMA, TEGDMA or other low molecular weight monomers, with high effectiveness [23]. Similar to this clinical observation, in our project ELS and APT resin composite showed good and similar clinical performances after 3 years. Although in vitro studies might be accepted as useful data regarding the performance of resin composites, the findings need to be evaluated and verified by clinical trials [24]. In this study, the clinical performance of extra low shrinkage ELS was compared with APT.

The pandemic conditions of the world had affected the percentage of the recall rate for the second year. Only 18 patients were convinced to attend the recall appointment. One of the teeth with a deep caries lesions restored with APT showed endodontic problems after 2 years and had a root canal treatment. At 3-year recall 27 patients came to the appointment in which 50 restorations were checked and scored like 2 years recall. In one of the studies, it is concluded that the low-shrinking composite ‘ELS’ in combination with the three step etch-and-rinse adhesive ‘CMF’ (Saremco, Switzerland) performed best in high C-factor Class-I cavities [25]. It has been suggested for many years that stress generation at tooth/resin composite interfaces and the resulting interfacial deficiencies remain one of the most important reasons for clinical failure [26]. The claim that minimizing the shrinkage stresses may lead to improvements in the success rate and survival of restorations can be found in many scientific articles [27, 28]. Both low shrinkage composite restorations in Class I and II cavities performed acceptable results showing no marginal fracture or tooth fracture in our study, and this finding is in agreement with the statement. In this study, the clinical performance of ELS was compared with APT in conjunction with self–etch adhesive agent free from HEMA and TEGDMA. Longevity results of restorations placed in Class V non-carious lesions showed also that the clinical retention of the studied TEGDMA/HEMA free adhesive in low stress bearing localizations was highly acceptable [29]. The main reason for failure was material fracture followed by recurrent caries. This is in accordance with recent findings in most of the clinical follow up [30, 31]2 restorations from ELS group, after 12 months showed edge chipping and accepted as failure (FDI 5.4.1). Marginal discoloration and defects usually result from defects present between the composite restoration and the cavity margins. These defects may be caused by inadequate restoration placement and finishing procedures, by unsatisfactory bonding and by subsequent stress fatigue [32, 33] and this may be due to the adhesive strategy applied and the lack of selective enamel etching. Only 3 restorations were scored as 3 which is defined moderate surface staining that may also present on other teeth, esthetically acceptable. Also, polymerization shrinkage can generate high stresses at bonded surfaces in confined cavity preparations [34, 35]. Low shrinkage resin composites were placed by using incremental technique in our study. Caution should be adopted when restoring such situations and the use of incremental placement techniques encouraged where reasonably possible [36]. The surface texture and color stability of resin composites can be accepted as important factors affecting the clinical prognosis of adhesive restorative materials. In the present clinical study, regarding surface and marginal staining, two restorative material groups received similar scores after three years. Most of the restorations in both ELS and APT groups showed score one which defines restorations showed no surface staining. Color match and translucency parameters in both groups were also similar and 47 of 50 restorations showed minor deviation after three years.

The surface lusture of all restorations was satisfactory and mostly obtained score 2 at all recall periods. The two tested restorative composite materials exhibited relatively smooth surfaces after polishing. Except two restorations (in ELS group) all of the restorations were scored 1 or 2 at 3-year recall which is surprising as the extent of the cavities was not small to moderate. The preparation size has significant effect on restoration survival [34]. In this clinical investigation, restorations in both material groups presented with predominantly slightly dull surfaces. There were no significant differences observed between the two materials regarding surface texture criteria after 3 years. In our study, a small localized secondary caries lesion was observed in two fillings in the same patient (one ELS, one APT) located at the interface between enamel wall and restorative material. These small lesions did not require any intervention in 3 years follow-up. Based on baseline and recall radiographs, the caries lesions observed in this study were defined as secondary caries lesions; however, longer observation time is needed for the changes. Wear was not observed to be a clinical problem, and all the restorations were scored 1 which defines wear equivalent to enamel, despite suggestions in the literature that wear may be a significant mode of failure in larger restorations, especially in patients with bruxing and clenching habits [37]. There were not any parafunctional habits in the selected patients in our study. After 3-year follow up 50 restorations were clinically satisfactory with esthetic and function which are similar with the two years follow up results. Postoperative sensitivity and tooth vitality were scored as 1 which defines normal without any symptoms in three years period. The vitality of restored teeth was unchanged at all recall periods at 3 years recall except 1 patient’s spontaneous pain. This result is in agreement with previous studies [38, 39]. Low-shrinkage composites, ELS and APT, showed similar success in both Class I and Class II restorations. However, clinical observations are still limited on the performance of low-shrinkage ELS and APT resin composites. Although the study was a randomized controlled clinical trial, short time observation period of 3 years is the limitation of the study and the lack of selective etching, non-application of rubber dam are considered as the other limitations. While it is recognized that the duration of this study is insufficient to confirm the long-term suitability of the tested materials, Neverthelesares, these findings provide an indication of their clinical performance. Long-term analysis may show critical alterations that will affect restoration longevity.
