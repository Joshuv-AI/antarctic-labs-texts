Multiscale quantum-to-device simulation framework for Ca3AsBr3 perovskite solar cells: engineering efficient electron transport layers
Authors: Md Ferdous Rahman, Md Azizur Rahman, Mst Marufa Sabrin, Md Faruk Hossain, Noureddine Elboughdiri, Nacer Badi, Aijaz Rasool Chaudhry, Ahmad Irfan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of stable, environmentally benign, and high-performance perovskite solar cells (PSCs) has increasingly focused on innovative inorganic absorber materials. In this study, we conduct a detailed evaluation of the optoelectronic and mechanical properties of Ca3AsBr3, a promising non-toxic halide perovskite, using density functional theory (DFT) alongside SCAPS-1D simulations. The DFT results indicate that Ca3AsBr3 possesses a direct bandgap of 1.66 eV, along with good mechanical stability and strong optical absorption, making it well-suited for photovoltaic applications. To further investigate device performance, four electron transport layers (ETLs)—WS2, SnS2, CdS, and TiO2 were incorporated into HTL-free FTO/ETL/Ca3AsBr3/Au architecture, allowing analysis of energy band alignment, defect tolerance, and overall efficiency. Among these configurations, the WS₂-based device demonstrated superior performance, achieving a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 20.50%, with an open-circuit voltage (Voc) of 1.165 V, a short-circuit current density (Jsc) of 20.55 mA/cm², and a fill factor (FF) of 85.64%. Further simulation results highlight that an optimal absorber thickness of 1200 nm, along with reduced bulk and interface defect densities (≤ 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ and ≤ 10¹³ cm⁻²), plays a crucial role in minimizing non-radiative recombination losses and improving charge carrier collection. Overall, this work identifies Ca3AsBr3 as a viable eco-friendly absorber material and emphasizes the importance of ETL optimization in achieving efficient, stable, and scalable PSC devices.Keywords: DFT, SCAPS-1D, Ca3AsBr3, Perovskite, ETLs, Next-generation photovoltaicsSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

A major transition is underway in the global energy domain, necessitated by the increasing urgency to mitigate climate change, lessen dependence on fossil fuels, and ensure long-term energy sustainability1, 2, 3. In this context, photovoltaic (PV) technologies have emerged as pivotal solutions, offering direct conversion of sunlight into electricity with minimal environmental impact. Among these, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have received widespread recognition, on account of their rapidly increasing power conversion efficiencies (PCEs), ease of fabrication, and material versatility. While hybrid organic–inorganic lead halide perovskites have achieved efficiencies increasing from 3.9% to over 20%, with predictions suggesting they could reach up to 31%, their widespread adoption is hindered by intrinsic instability, toxicity (primarily due to lead content), and scalability limitations4, 5.

To overcome these challenges, the research community is intensively investigating lead-free, fully inorganic perovskite materials that can combine stability, environmental safety, and competitive efficiency6, 7, 8. One such promising candidate is Ca3AsBr3, an emerging inorganic halide perovskite that has not yet been extensively explored in the photovoltaic domain. Ca3AsBr3 corresponds to the A3MX3 group of inorganic perovskites, where ‘A’ represents alkaline earth metals like Ba, Sr, Ca, and Mg; ‘M’ includes elements such as Sb, As, N, and P; and ‘X’ consists of halogens like I, Br, Cl, and F. Its non-toxic elemental composition, structural robustness, and potentially favorable optoelectronic properties position it as an attractive alternative to conventional lead-based perovskites9. Moreover, these perovskites have a tunable band gap from 0.8 to 2.0 eV, making them suitable for high-performance solar cells9. Simultaneously, these perovskites structure demonstrates superior stability, higher efficiency, and enhanced structural, mechanical, and optical properties compared to those of other inorganic perovskites within the more common ABX3 group. However, to validate and exploit its photovoltaic potential, a comprehensive investigation into its fundamental material characteristics and device-level performance is essential10, 11, 12. Effective optical management is crucial for achieving the high efficiencies predicted in simulations under real conditions by reducing reflection losses at device interfaces. Anti-reflection coatings (ARC), such as single-layer MgF₂, improve refractive index matching and enhance light transmission. Multilayer dielectric stacks (e.g., SiO₂/TiO₂) provide broadband anti-reflection through interference effects. Additional strategies like surface texturing, light trapping, and graded refractive index layers further enhance photon absorption, improving light coupling and helping maintain high device performance13. Recent advances in SCAPS-based modeling and inorganic absorber engineering have significantly accelerated the development of high-efficiency photovoltaic devices. For instance, DFT and SCAPS-1D guided optimization of GaInP solar cells has demonstrated notable improvements in device performance14. Similarly, temperature-resistant and highly doped Ag2BeSnSe4 kesterite solar cells have shown promising efficiency and stability for sustainable energy harvesting15. Moreover, the integration of machine learning with SCAPS-1D simulations has enabled efficient optimization of N719/BEHP-co-MEH-PPV bilayer dye-sensitized solar cells, highlighting the growing importance of multi-scale and data-driven approaches in photovoltaic research16. Recent studies have also explored advanced absorber and device configurations, such as Cs2SnBr6/Cs2TeI6 bilayer absorbers17, SWCNT-based carbon solar cells18, and radiation-tolerant perovskite devices19, demonstrating enhanced performance through combined DFT, SCAPS, and machine learning approaches. Despite these advancements, many previously reported systems either rely on complex multilayer architectures, expensive materials, or exhibit limited environmental stability, even when higher power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) are achieved. In this context, there remains a strong need for simple, stable, and lead-free absorber materials with efficient device configurations. To address this gap, the present work focuses on Ca3AsBr3, a relatively unexplored inorganic perovskite, and investigates its potential as an efficient and stable absorber for next-generation photovoltaic applications. On the other hand, ML further accelerates optimization by identifying critical parameters such as defect density, thickness, and mobility. The approach enables efficient, low-power, high-performance device design, making it well-suited for IoT applications and advancing scalable, lead-free perovskite technologies20.

Following the material-level analysis, we design and simulate solar cell architectures incorporating Ca3AsBr3 using the SCAPS-1D (Solar Cell Capacitance Simulator) software within the PSC architecture FTO/ETL(WS2, SnS2, TiO2, and CdS)/Ca3AsBr3/Au. The effectiveness of PSCs heavily depends on electron transport layers (ETLs), which enable rapid charge collection and reduce carrier recombination21, 22. WS2, SnS2, TiO2, and CdS were selected as electron transporting layers (ETLs) because of their demonstrated effectiveness in other perovskite solar cells and their suitable band alignment with Ca3AsBr3, which facilitates efficient electron transport23, 24. These materials are noted for their strong chemical and thermal stability, which enhances the overall durability of the device25, 26. Each ETL presents distinct characteristics, such as high electron mobility or appropriate band gaps, enabling a thorough examination of their impact on device performance24, 27, 28, 29. Their compatibility with Ca3AsBr3 improves electron extraction and reduces recombination losses, making them excellent choices for this study28, 30, 31, 32, 33. Selecting an appropriate ETL is therefore critical to boost both efficiency and long-term stability of emerging PSC technologies34. Additionally, our paper presents a novel structure, specifically an HTL-free design based on Ca3ASBr3 (FTO/ETL(WS2, SnS2, CdS and TiO2)/Ca3AsBr3/Au). Traditional HTLs often involve expensive materials or complex production techniques, making large-scale production challenging. This new configuration could particularly benefit such large-scale production. Moreover, the susceptibility of certain HTL materials to degradation over time due to environmental exposure poses a risk to the prolonged operational stability and performance of solar cells. Considering the potential to enhance device stability, adopting an HTL-free configuration becomes a viable option. On the other hand, ML further accelerates optimization by identifying critical parameters such as defect density, thickness, and mobility. The approach enables efficient, low-power, high-performance device design, making it well-suited for IoT applications and advancing scalable, lead-free perovskite technologies20.

This study integrates first-principles DFT calculations with device-level SCAPS-1D simulations to systematically evaluate the structural, electronic, optical, and photovoltaic performance of Ca3AsBr3-based PSCs. The results highlight the critical role of ETL engineering and identify WS2 as the most promising electron transport layer. This work not only establishes Ca3AsBr3 as a viable lead-free absorber but also clearly differentiates itself from previous studies by combining material design, device optimization, and simplified HTL-free architecture within a unified framework, thereby providing a robust foundation for future experimental and technological development.

Methodology

This study employs a dual approach combining first-principles density functional theory (DFT) with numerical simulations using SCAPS-1D to investigate the performance of a new inorganic halide PSC incorporating Ca3AsBr3 as the absorber layer. The photovoltaic device structure under investigation follows the configuration: FTO/ETL (WS2, SnS2, CdS, TiO2)/Ca3AsBr3/Au.

First-principles calculations

To explore the fundamental behaviors of Ca3AsBr3, DFT-based simulations were performed with the aid of the Quantum ESPRESSO package. The electronic and structural characteristics were computed using the plane-wave pseudopotential (PWPP) formalism35, applying the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional36 within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). Ultrasoft norm-conserving pseudopotentials were utilized to analyze core-valence interactions37, 38. The kinetic and charge density cutoffs were set to 225 and 30 Rydbergs, respectively to ensure convergence of total energies and forces. Brillouin zone integrations were accomplished by means of an 8 × 8 × 8 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh to ensure precise sampling of the electronic structure. Geometry optimization continued until the atomic forces remained under 0.01 eV/Å, with the electronic SCF convergence threshold assigned a value of 10⁻⁶ atomic units. Band configuration and partial density of states (PDOS) analyses were conducted to confirm the direct band gap and assess orbital contributions near the Fermi level.

Device configuration and simulation

Following material characterization, the optoelectronic behavior of the Ca₃AsBr₃-based device was simulated using SCAPS-1D. It solves the coupled Poisson, continuity, and drift-diffusion equations under steady-state conditions, enabling precise modeling of charge carrier dynamics, recombination processes, and current-voltage characteristics39. The layered device architecture incorporates a p-type Ca₃AsBr₃ absorber (band gap: 1.66 eV; thickness: 1.2 μm), overlaid by different n-type ETLs: WS₂, SnS₂, CdS, and TiO₂. The front contact is composed of fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) with a thickness of 100 nm and a band gap of ~ 3.60 eV, while gold (Au) serves as the rear electrode.

The ETLs were chosen for their varied electronic and optical characteristics. WS₂ (1.80 eV) offers excellent band alignment and high electron mobility; SnS₂ (2.24 eV) is chemically stable with favorable transparency; CdS (2.40 eV) is widely used but has parasitic absorption concerns; TiO₂ (3.20 eV) is historically dominant but limited by low carrier mobility and high interfacial recombination. Each ETL’s suitability was assessed based on its energy band alignment with the Ca₃AsBr₃ absorber and its role in governing charge extraction processes and recombination mechanisms. Band diagrams were constructed to visualize heterojunction alignments.

Material parameters, such as dielectric constants, carrier mobilities, band gaps, and electron affinities, were sourced from experimental literature and validated theoretical databases (Table 1). The thermal velocities of the carriers were standardized at 10⁷ cm/s across all layers. The simulation also considered interface defect densities, absorber doping concentrations, and variations in layer thickness. Neutral defect states were introduced at interfaces to model Shockley–Read–Hall recombination realistically (Table 2)40. Series and shunt resistances were kept at 1 Ω·cm² and 10⁶ Ω·cm², respectively. The high shunt resistance value was adopted to represent near-ideal device conditions with minimal leakage current41, which is a commonly used assumption in SCAPS-1D simulations of high-performance perovskite solar cells. All computational analyses assumed AM 1.5G solar spectrum conditions (100 mW/cm²) and an ambient temperature of 300 K.

Electronic properties of Ca3AsBr3

The characteristics of a compound’s electrons, known as its electronic properties are crucial in estimating the material’s optical, magnetic and electrical behaviors. These attributes are of great importance across diverse fields such as condensed matter physics, electronics, and materials science. This discussion covers key terms like band structure, TDOS, and PDOS, which are instrumental in identifying the nature of a substance, whether it is metallic, insulating, or semiconducting57, 58.The investigation focused on the optical characteristics of an inorganic Ca3AsBr3 perovskite by examining its electronic band structures (EBS).

Figure 3(a) demonstrates the electronic band structure of Ca3AsBr3 along high-symmetry directions (Γ–X–M–R–Γ) in the first Brillouin zone, extending across an energy spanning from − 3 eV to 3 eV. At 0 eV, the dashed line specifies the Fermi level, which is located between the conduction band (CB) and valence band (VB). The Fermi level indicates where the VB and CB intersect, in accordance with the band structures. Figure 3(a) also highlights the valence band maximum (VBM) as well as conduction band minimum (CBM) close to the Γ-point. The computed direct band gaps are obtained to be 2.45 eV and 1.66 eV at the HSE and PBE function, respectively, as indicated in Fig. 3(a). It is noteworthy that the widely recognized drawback of GGA-based methods (such as PBE and LDA) is their tendency to underestimate the bandgap value during evaluation57, 59, 60, 61. The use of the GGA-PBE functional in this study is motivated by its computational efficiency and its well-established reliability in capturing structural and electronic trends in perovskite materials. Although it is well known that GGA-PBE tends to underestimate the bandgap compared to hybrid functionals such as HSE, it provides a consistent framework for comparative analysis. Moreover, hybrid functionals like HSE require significantly higher computational cost, particularly for evaluating optical and mechanical properties, making them less feasible for large-scale simulations. Since the present work primarily focuses on understanding relative electronic behavior and device-level performance through SCAPS-1D simulations, the use of GGA-PBE is considered appropriate and widely adopted in similar studies.

(a) Electronic band structure of Ca3AsBr3 calculated utilizing both PBE and HSE functionals, and (b) PDOS highlighting the orbital contributions to the electronic structure of the Ca3AsBr3 perovskite.

The study’s results clearly indicate that structures based on Ca3AsBr3 feature a broader bandgap, along with larger atomic sizes and an increased number of nucleons. A decreased electrical interaction between the atomic nucleus and surrounding electrons in the highest occupied energy level (HOEL) is the cause of this bandgap widening. The space between the CB and VB gets wider as the cohesive energy drops and the electrical force of attraction becomes less strong. The measured energy band gap values at the attained level propose that Ca3AsBr3 is a potential material for PSC use because of its larger band gap49, 57, 58, 62. The DOS signifies the count of unique electron states possible at a specified energy level. Figure 3(b) illustrates the calculated TDOS and PDOS for Ca3AsBr3. The energy states of PDOS for various Ca3AsBr3 configuration are detailed in Fig. 3(b) across the energy spectrum ranging from − 3 eV to 3 eV.

The TDOS for Ca3AsBr3 have a Fermi level (EF) of about 27 states/eV in Fig. 3(b). It is important to highlight that the As 2p orbital is the main contributor to the CB, particularly around ~ 0.7 eV (about 13 states per eV), whereas the Br-2p orbital contributes to the VB around ~ − 0.5 and − 3 eV (about 26 as well as 24 states per eV). Due to Ca3AsBr3’s significant influence on both bands, there is minimal overlap between the CB as well as VB near the Fermi level. This significant contribution establishes Ca3AsBr3 as a semiconductor.

Optical properties of Ca3AsBr3

Investigating a compounds optical characteristic is essential to comprehend how it interacts with light. A thorough comprehension of the diverse optical features of Ca3AsBr3 material is vital for maximizing its utilization in optoelectronic devices. The dielectric function is expressed as ε(ω), with two parts: the real part (signified as ε1 (ω)) and the imaginary portion (symbolized as ε2(ω)).

The ε1 (ω) is obtained utilizing the Kramers-Kronig transformation68, whereas the ε2(ω) is calculated utilizing the momentum matrix’s 69.

Analyzing the ε1 (ω) contributes significantly to understanding the compound’s polarization response and dispersion characteristics. For the cubic phase of Ca3AsBr3, the static dielectric constant ε₁(0) is calculated to be around 4.80. As exemplified in Fig. 4 (a), the ε₁(ω) of the dielectric function shows a characteristic trend: it initially rises from ε₁(0), reaches several pronounced peaks—most notably around photon energies of 2.2 eV and 6.0 eV—before gradually declining. This behavior reflects the energy-dependent polarizability of the material. These variations are fundamentally linked to interband electronic transitions.

The dielectric function (a) real parts, (b) imaginary part, (c) the absorption coefficient, and (d) loss function of Ca3AsBr3.

In contrast, the ε₂(ω), which is directly linked to the electronic band configuration, reflects the compound’s light absorption capabilities. Figure 4 (b) reveals a prominent absorption peak in ε₂(ω) at a light energy of almost 1.5 eV, with the highest optical response reaching 4.6 eV. These peaks in the imaginary spectrum stem from transitions of electrons from the VB to the CB, highlighting the strong light-matter interaction within the visible to near-infrared range. The observed absorption characteristics can be ascribed to the relatively narrow bandgap of Ca3AsBr3. Furthermore, the shifting of absorption peaks correlates with modifications in the bandgap and lattice parameters, indicating a sensitive dependence of optical properties on structural variations. Notably, beyond a photon energy of 9.9 eV, the ε₂(ω) reaches zero, revealing enhanced optical transparency and minimal absorption at higher energies.

The ability of a compound to capture incoming photon serve a key function in finding its effectiveness for high solar PCE, with the optical absorption coefficient serving as an extremely important parameter in this assessment52. Throughout every configuration studied, a strong association emerges between the ε₂(ω) and the shape of the optical absorption spectra, indicating a consistent optical response behavior. For Ca3AsBr3, the absorption spectrum exposes a pronounced peak within the visible region, underscoring its strong interaction with photons in this range. As described in Fig. 4(c), the absorption coefficient attains the highest value near 7.8 eV, with significant absorption occurring over a broad spectral range spanning approximately 3.8 to 9 eV. The absorption onset begins at 2.0 eV and gradually diminishes, approaching zero near 10 eV. This wide absorption bandwidth suggests that Ca3AsBr3 is capable of efficiently capturing high-energy photons, a behavior attributed to its relatively extended optical bandgap. The relationship between the photon energy (eV) and the loss function, L(ω), is illustrated in Fig. 4(d). This figure depicts Ca3AsBr3 as a semiconductor and shows how the material responds energetically to electromagnetic radiation. Less energy is available for electronic transitions at low photon energies, as indicated by the L(ω) being almost zero. As photon energy increases, the semiconductor absorbs incident light, with a sudden increase after 9 eV and a return to zero after 10.4 eV. signifying a rapid decline absorption coefficient in Ca3AsBr3. These optical characteristics suggest that Ca3AsBr3 is a favorable choice for applications involving optoelectronic and photovoltaic devices. In addition to photovoltaic applications, the favorable optoelectronic properties of Ca3AsBr3, including its direct bandgap and strong optical absorption, indicate its potential for multifunctional applications such as photodetectors and hybrid optoelectronic devices. These properties enable efficient light detection and carrier generation, making the material suitable for broadband photodetection and advanced optoelectronic integration.

Analysis of SCAPS-1D simulation results

Band alignment of Ca3AsBr3-based solar cells with different ETL

Figure 5 illustrates the band diagrams of Ca3AsBr3-based PSCs integrated with different electron transport layers (ETLs). Amongst the four structures, the WS₂-based device demonstrates the most satisfactory band orientation with the Ca3AsBr3 active layer, facilitating actual electron collection and lessening the interfacial recombination due to its suitable CB offset (~ 0.14 eV) and high electron mobility. This optimal alignment directly contributes to its superior PCE exceeding 20%. SnS2, with a moderate band offset (~ 0.28 eV) and good chemical stability, offers relatively efficient charge transfer but with slightly higher recombination losses compared to WS2. CdS, despite its historical usage, shows a less ideal band offset (~ 0.34 eV), which results in parasitic absorption and increased interfacial resistance, thus limiting the performance. TiO2, although widely used in conventional PSCs, suffers from a relatively large CB mismatch (~ 0.46 eV) and lower electron mobility, leading to enhanced charge recombination and the lowest overall performance among the ETLs studied. These variations in energy band alignment, carrier mobility, and optical losses fundamentally explain the hierarchy in device efficiency observed across the four ETLs as exhibited in Fig. 5.

Effects of active layer thickness and shallow acceptor concentration

The output parameters of a PSC is largely influenced by the active layer thickness and shallow acceptor concentration32, 70. Figure 6 reveals the role of changing thickness of active layer (ranging from 100 nm to 2500 nm) as well as shallow acceptor concentration (spanning from 1014 to 1021 cm− 3) on the output of our four simulated (FTO/ETL(WS2, SnS2, TiO2, CdS) /Ca3AsBr3/Au) PSCs structures. Under conditions where the active layer is highly thin (0.1–2.5 μm) at a shallow acceptor concentration of 1017 cm− 3, light absorption is limited, as revealed in Fig. 6(a). Although the Voc remains nearly constant with elevating absorber thickness, the JSC, FF, and PCE display a substantial enhancement as the thickness of the Ca₃AsBr₃ layer enhances in the PSC design incorporating an ETL layer28. Figure 6(b) shows how changes in a shallow acceptor density affect the situation. The reciprocal correlation between the carrier density and reverse saturation current results in a notable is rised in VOC when the shallow acceptor density is raised from 1014 to 1021 cm− 3. Higher carrier concentrations cause the JSC to drastically drop, which raises the VOC71. In the construction of solar cells (SCs) incorporating an electron transport layer (ETL), a higher shallow acceptor density typically results in an enhanced Voc, while concurrently causing a reduction in the Jsc.

Role of (a) thickness and (b) shallow acceptor density of Ca3AsBr3 active layer on the output parameters of the PSCs with several ETLs.

Concurrently, both the FF and PCE tend to improve. However, this trend does not apply when using TiO₂ as the ETL, where different behavior is observed. Keeping a constant shallow acceptor density of 1017 cm− 3 with an abctive layer thickness of 1200 nm, the PSC shows optimized PCE. It should be noted that very high absorber thickness (1200 nm) may introduce practical limitations such as increased series resistance and reduced charge transport efficiency in real devices. For the efficiency of the designated structural device, a doping concentration of 1017 cm− 3 is thought to be ideal.

Role of acceptor density along with defect density variation of Ca3AsBr3 active layer on performance

The PSC performance markedly drops owing to severe carrier recombination attributed to increased levels of defect density (Nt) and acceptor density (NA)72, 73, 74. Increased recombination of photogenerated charge carriers significantly lowers the output of the cell75, 76, 77. This work examines the impacts of varying Nt and NA values in Ca3AsBr3 absorber within definite span of 1010–1017 cm− 3 and 1014–1020 cm− 3, respectively, affect the output parameters. The objective is to assess PSC output utilizing accurate and dependable data to reflect accurate behavior.

Influence of acceptor and defect densities on VOC

Figure 7 presents the influence of NA and Nt on the VOC, with NA varying from 10¹⁴ to 10²⁰ cm⁻³ and Nt spanning from 10¹⁰ to 10¹⁷ cm⁻³. The VOC response varies across the different ETL materials used in the FTO/ETL (WS₂, SnS₂, CdS, TiO₂)/Ca₃AsBr₃/Au solar cell configurations. Specifically, VOC increases from 0.918 V to a top of 1.35 V for WS₂ (Fig. 7a), from 0.910 V to 1.34 V for SnS₂ (Fig. 7b), decreases from 1.14 V to 0.867 V for TiO₂ (Fig. 7c), and declines from 1.35 V to 0.912 V for CdS (Fig. 7d). However, a significant decline in VOC to approximately 0.8095 V is observed when NA falls below 10¹⁸ cm⁻³. Analogous tendencies are evident for SnS₂ and TiO₂ ETLs, as showed in Fig. 7b and c, respectively. In contrast, the CdS-based ETL demonstrates its highest VOC of 1.14 V when NA is below 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, with minimal sensitivity to Nt (Fig. 7d). Raised defect densities in the absorber layer intensify SRH recombination, adversely affecting device performance76, 77. Based on these findings, the optimal parameters for the Ca₃AsBr₃ absorber are identified as an NA of 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ and Nt of 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, yielding a topmost VOC of 1.165 V in the proposed device configurations.

Role of acceptor and defect densities on Jsc

Figure 8 depicts the change in JSC as a function of NA (10¹⁴–10²⁰ cm⁻³) and Nt (10¹⁰–10¹⁷ cm⁻³) across Ca₃AsBr₃-based solar cells with different ETLs. A general trend is observed where JSC declines with increasing NA and Nt, mainly due to intensified non-radiative SRH recombination, which limits the collection of photogenerated carriers. For the WS₂ ETL configuration (Fig. 8a), JSC peaks at 22.90 mA/cm2 under NA (≤ 1017 cm− 3) and Nt (≤ 1015 cm− 3), but drops significantly to 14.28 mA/cm2, once Nt surpasses 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ owing to increased trap-assisted recombination. A similar pattern is evident in the SnS₂-based device (Fig. 8b), where JSC decreases from 22.90 to 16.76 mA/cm2 under is the same condition, highlighting SnS2 is slightly improved tolerance to defects compared to WS2.

In the TiO2 ETL structure (Fig. 8c), the JSC drops more drastically from 22.90 to 9.47 mA/cm2 as Nt rises, which can be ascribed to the higher CB offset between TiO2 and Ca3AsBr3 that hinders effective charge extraction and exacerbates recombination losses. For the CdS-based device (Fig. 8d), JSC is declines from 19.85 to 6.65 mA/cm2 under elevated defect densities, again due to intensified SRH recombination within the absorber layer. Based on these results, the optimal doping and defect parameters for Ca₃AsBr₃ are identified as NA≈ 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ and Nt≈ 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, yielding maximum photocurrent and overall device output.

Effect of acceptor and defect densities on FF

The FF varies notably across different ETLs: from 87.35% to 69.15% for WS₂ (Fig. 9a), 90.60% to 64.50% for SnS₂ (Fig. 9b), 82.30% to 55.90% for TiO₂ (Fig. 9c), and 81.00% to 31.00% for CdS (Fig. 9d). A consistently high FF is observed when NA surpasses 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ and Nt remains under 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, indicating minimized SRH recombination under these conditions. For instance, the peak FF of 87.35% in the WS₂-based device (Fig. 9a) is attained under optimal doping and low defect conditions, while increased Nt beyond 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ triggers a substantial drop to 69.15%, due to enhanced trap-assisted recombination. A similar pattern is observed in SnS₂-based cells (Fig. 9b), where FF declines from 90.60% to 64.50% with rising defect densities. In TiO₂ ETL systems (Fig. 9c), FF falls from 82.30% to 55.90% as Nₜ surpasses 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, and for CdS (Fig. 9d), a sharp reduction from 81.00% to 31.00% is observed beyond Nₜ = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³. These reductions are primarily ascribed to the accumulation of defect states that enable non-radiative recombination, hence impeding charge carrier extraction and reducing photovoltaic efficiency. Collectively, the analysis identifies Nₐ ≈ 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ and Nₜ ≈ 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ as optimal parameters for maintaining high FF, approaching 86% in Ca₃AsBr₃-based heterojunction devices.

Role of defect density and thickness on VOC

Boosting the thickness of the Ca₃AsBr₃ active layer generally enhances the VOC by improving light-harvesting efficiency. However, beyond a certain threshold, thicker absorber layers can intensify bulk recombination, particularly in the presence of moderate-to-high defect densities, which introduce trap-assisted recombination pathways and reduce carrier lifetimes, thereby diminishing VOC. As depicted in Fig. 11(a–d), VOC declines sharply with increasing defect density (10¹⁰ to 10¹⁹ cm⁻³) 29 and varies with absorber thickness (100 to 2500 nm). VOC goes from 1.19 V to 0.867 V for WS₂ (Fig. 11a), 1.18 V to 0.872 V for SnS₂ (Fig. 11b), 1.158 V to 0.9815 V for CdS (Fig. 11c), and 1.19 V to 0.998 V for TiO₂ (Fig. 11d) ETL as defect concentrations increase. Optimal VOC values are achieved when defect densities are maintained at or below ~ 1015 cm− 3—representing a moderate defect level consistent with previously reported experimental and simulation ranges for perovskite solar cells—and the absorber thickness exceeds 1200 nm, yielding 1.165 V (WS₂), 1.1599 V (SnS₂), 1.1424 V (CdS), and 1.1657 V (TiO₂), confirming the serious role of defect suppression and active layer thickness in rising device voltage output.

Effect of defect density and thickness on JSC

The JSC the highest current produced when the solar cell operates under zero voltage is governed by the efficient collection of photogenerated carriers. It is controlled by multiple contributing factors, including the device’s active area, incident photon flux, spectral distribution of the light, and the absorption characteristics of the active compound83. Enhancing the thickness of the Ca₃AsBr₃ absorber generally improves light absorption, thereby increasing JSC. However, excessively thick layers may hinder carrier extraction due to recombination losses before collection, resulting in a decline in JSC. Furthermore, elevated defect densities inside the absorber layer act as non-radiative recombination centers, dropping carrier lifetime and overall collection efficiency, which also degrades JSC84.

As demonstrated in Fig. 12(a–d), the JSC of the Ca₃AsBr₃-based PSC varies with absorber defect density and thickness. Specifically, JSC declines from 22.30 to 12.70 mA/cm² for WS₂ (Fig. 12a), from 21.80 to 8.95 mA/cm² for SnS₂ (Fig. 12b), from 21.75 to 7.55 mA/cm² for TiO₂ (Fig. 12c), and from 21.70 to 6.90 mA/cm² for CdS (Fig. 12d) as the defect density upsurges from 10¹⁰ to 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ and thickness varies from 100 to 2500 nm. The highest JSC values are observed once the active layer thickness exceeds 1500 nm and the defect concentration remains below 10¹⁵ cm⁻³. Under optimized conditions at a thickness of 1200 nm and a defect concentration of 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, the peak JSC values attained are 20.55 mA/cm² for WS₂, 19.993 mA/cm² for SnS₂, 19.783 mA/cm² for CdS, and 19.73 mA/cm² for TiO₂.

Roll of defect density and thickness on FF

The most effective thickness for the Ca₃AsBr₃ absorber represents a harmony between efficient photocarrier collection and minimized recombination losses. Greater thickness can contribute to a rise in series resistance and lessened carrier extraction efficiency, thereby lowering the FF. Additionally, elevated defect densities in the active layer enhance non-radiative recombination and hinder charge transport, further degrading FF85,86. As displayed in Fig. 13, the FF exhibits a trend analogous to that of the VOC46, declining as defect density increases and thickness deviates from the optimal range. The FF of FTO/ETL(WS₂, SnS₂, CdS, and TiO₂)/Ca₃AsBr₃/Au architectures decreases across different ETLs: from 86.80% to 69.56% for WS₂ (Fig. 13a), 86.95% to 68.60% for SnS₂ (Fig. 13b), 79.60% to 36.00% for TiO₂ (Fig. 13c), and 82.85% to 64.30% for CdS (Fig. 13d). Maximum FF values were attained at a thickness of 1200 nm and a defect concentration of 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, specifically: 85.64% for WS₂, 86.34% for SnS₂, 82.26% for CdS, and 77.84% for TiO₂.

Role of defect density and thickness on PCE

The interaction of VOC, JSC, and FF influences the PSC’s performance as measured by PCE. Therefore, adjusting the active layer’s thickness is necessary to get the best PCE, which calls for balancing elements like recombination, carrier collection, and light absorption. Increased defect densities have an adverse effect on each of the separate output parameters, which lowers PCE87. The PCE of FTO/ETL(WS2, SnS2, CdS, and TiO2)/Ca3AsBr3/Au configurations exhibits a decreasing trend across various ETLs: from 23.05% to 7.650% for WS2 (Fig. 14a), 22.35% to 5.40% for SnS2 (Fig. 14b), 20.50% to 4.850% for TiO2 (Fig. 14c), and 20.50% to 2.450% for CdS (Fig. 14d). Figure 14 shows the optimal PCE attained at a thickness of 1200 nm and a defect density of 1015 cm⁻³: 20.50% for WS₂, 20.02% for SnS2, 18.59% for CdS, and 17.80% for TiO2.

Optimization of interface defect densities

Interfacial trap states act as recombination centers, limiting photogenerated carrier collection and thereby reducing device performance88. Figures 15, 16, 17 and 18 illustrate how variations in Ca₃AsBr₃ absorber thickness (100–2500 nm) and interfacial defect concentration (10¹⁰–10¹⁹ cm⁻²) influence the output parameters of the PSC. The interface quality between Ca₃AsBr₃ and ETLs (WS₂, SnS₂, CdS, TiO₂) critically governs PV performance70, 89, 90. In the present study, the interface defect states were modeled in SCAPS-1D using a simplified approach by assuming a uniform distribution of trap states at the ETL/Ca3AsBr6 interface. While this method effectively captures the general trends of interfacial recombination, it does not fully represent the complex nature of real interfaces, where defect energetics, spatial distribution, and interface chemistry may vary significantly. In practical devices, interfacial recombination is one of the dominant loss mechanisms, strongly influencing carrier lifetime and extraction efficiency. Recent device-level optimization studies have emphasized the importance of advanced interface engineering strategies, including defect passivation, band alignment tuning, and interface modification techniques to suppress recombination losses and enhance device performance91. Therefore, more realistic interface modeling and experimental validation are required for accurate prediction of device behavior. Once interfacial defect densities exceed 10¹³ cm⁻², sharp declines in efficiency occur due to enhanced carrier recombination from high-density interfacial states80, 92.

Impact of interface defect densities and thickness on VOC

The VOC of Ca₃AsBr₃-based PSC exhibits a substantial reduction with raising interfacial defect density, mainly owing to elevated non-radiative recombination at the ETL/ Ca₃AsBr₃ interface. As evidenced by Fig. 15, VOC declines for various ETLs: from 1.18 V to 0.998 V for WS₂ (Fig. 15a), 1.189 V to 0.65 V for SnS₂ (Fig. 15b), 1.20 V to 0.551 V for CdS (Fig. 15c), and 1.186 V to 0.8815 V for TiO₂ (Fig. 15d), as the interface defect density rises from 10¹⁰ to 10¹⁹ cm⁻². This output deterioration becomes significantly more pronounced beyond 1013 cm− 2 due to enhanced SRH recombination and the consequent reduction in carrier lifetime. It is important to note that an interfacial defect density of 1013 cm− 2 falls within the moderate range rather than being considered low. Such values are commonly reported for experimentally fabricated thin-film interfaces, where imperfections arising from lattice mismatch, surface roughness, and fabrication-induced defects are unavoidable. Optimized device performance is achieved at a Ca3AsBr3 absorber thickness of approximately 1200 nm combined with an interfacial defect density of 1013 cm− 2, resulting in VOC values of 1.165 V for WS2, 1.1599 V for SnS2, 1.1424 V for CdS, and 1.1659 V for TiO2. These results are consistent with realistic interfacial defect density ranges (Nit ≈ 1010–1013 cm− 2), indicating that the selected values represent practically achievable moderate defect conditions rather than idealized low-defect scenarios88. Notably, the WS₂-based device maintains the uppermost VOC under these conditions, underscoring its favorable band alignment and superior carrier-selective interface properties.

Effect of interface defect densities and thickness on JSC

Figure 16(a–d) illustrates the variation in PV output as a function of both interfacial defect density (ranging from 10¹⁰ to 10¹⁸ cm⁻²) and active layer thickness (0.1 to 3.0 μm) at the WS₂/Ca₃AsBr₃, SnS₂/Ca₃AsBr₃, CdS/Ca₃AsBr₃, and TiO₂/Ca₃AsBr₃ interfaces. As the interfacial defect density is increases, JSC drops significantly from 22 mA·cm⁻² to 6 mA·cm⁻² for WS₂ and SnS₂, 22 mA·cm⁻² to 5.5 mA·cm⁻² for CdS, and 22 mA·cm⁻² to 6.5 mA·cm⁻² for TiO₂ because of enhanced trap-assisted recombination at the defective interfaces. The reduction in carrier collection efficiency is further compounded at lower absorber thicknesses. Optimal JSC are attained at a thickness of 1200 nm and an interfacial defect density of 1013 cm− 2: 20.55 mA/cm2 for WS2, 19.976 mA/cm2 for SnS2, 19.78 mA/cm2 for CdS, and 19.71 mA/cm2 for TiO2. Among all configurations, the WS2-based ETL yields the highest JSC, ascribed to its better interface quality and favorable band alignment facilitating efficient carrier extraction.

Influence of interface defect densities and thickness on FF

The FF of the Ca₃AsBr₃-based PSC exhibits a significant decline across different ETLs as the interfacial defect density increases. Specifically, FF decreases from 86% to 62.16% for WS2 (Fig. 17a), 88% to 66.28% for SnS2 (Fig. 17b), 88% to 62.10% for CdS (Fig. 17c), and 80% to as low as 37% for TiO2 (Fig. 17d).

This degradation is largely caused by elevated trap-assisted recombination and impaired charge extraction at the ETL/absorber interface, which is exacerbated by elevated defect densities and suboptimal carrier transport pathways. Interfacial traps act as non-radiative recombination centers, meaningfully reducing the quality of the charge transport junction and suppressing the power output of the device. Figure 17 highlights that the optimal FF values are achieved at an active layer thickness of 1200 nm and an interfacial defect concentration of 10¹³ cm⁻². Within these operational settings, the FF reaches 85.64% for WS₂, 86.34% for SnS₂, 82.26% for CdS, and 77.84% for TiO₂. Among these, the WS₂ ETL demonstrate superior FF performance because of their advantageous energy level configuration and lower interfacial recombination rates, promoting more efficient charge extraction and transport.

Influence of interface defect densities and thickness on PCE

The PCE of Ca₃AsBr₃-based PSC exhibits significant dependence on the choice of ETL and the interface quality. As illustrated in Fig. 18, rising the interfacial defect density from 10¹⁰ to 10¹⁹ cm⁻² leads to a substantial decay in PCE due to intensified trap-assisted recombination and hindered carrier extraction at the ETL/absorber junction. Specifically, PCE decreases from 22% to 4% for WS₂ (Fig. 18a), 22% to 3.5% for SnS₂ (Fig. 18b), 22% to 3% for CdS (Fig. 18c), and 20.5% to 3.21% for TiO₂ (Fig. 18d). The optimal performance is reached at an interface defect concentration of 1013 cm− 2 and active thickness of 1200 nm, yielding peak PCEs of 20.55% for WS2, 20.02% for SnS2, 18.59% for CdS, and 17.80% for TiO2.

Comparative performance analysis

To further validate the effectiveness of the proposed device, a comparative performance analysis with recently reported SCAPS-based photovoltaic systems is presented in Table 5. The Ca3AsBr3-based HTL-free device exhibits a maximum PCE of 20.50%, along with a high FF of 85.64% and an VOC of 1.165 V. These values indicate efficient charge transport, reduced recombination losses, and favorable band alignment, particularly for the WS2-based configuration. In comparison, previously reported devices such as FTO/TiO2/RbGeI3/CuI demonstrate a comparable PCE of 18.97%, but with lower VOC and FF values, indicating relatively higher recombination losses. In contrast, the present work emphasizes a simplified HTL-free architecture using a lead-free Ca3AsBr3 absorber, which offers a balanced combination of efficiency, structural simplicity, and potential stability. The elimination of the hole transport layer not only reduces fabrication complexity but also minimizes degradation pathways associated with commonly used HTL materials. Therefore, despite not achieving the highest reported PCE, the proposed device demonstrates competitive performance with practical advantages, making it a promising candidate for scalable and stable next-generation photovoltaic applications.

Limitation

This study considers absorber thicknesses up to 1200 nm to explore the theoretical performance limits of the device. However, such thicknesses may not be practically feasible for perovskite solar cells, as they can lead to increased series resistance and inefficient charge transport. In realistic device fabrication, thinner absorber layers (typically 300–900 nm) are preferred to ensure efficient carrier extraction and minimize resistive losses. Future work should focus on optimizing device architectures, improving carrier mobility, and engineering interfaces to achieve high efficiency at reduced thicknesses.

Conclusions

This work introduces an integrated design-to-performance approach for Ca₃AsBr₃-based perovskite solar cells by combining DFT-driven material analysis with SCAPS-1D device simulations. The results confirm that Ca₃AsBr₃ exhibits promising mechanical, optical, and electronic properties, such as a direct bandgap of 1.66 eV, strong dielectric behavior, and dynamic structural stability. At the device level, the study reveals that effective passivation of bulk and interfacial defects, along with the careful selection of electron transport layers (ETLs), plays a vital role in reducing recombination losses and enhancing overall efficiency. Among the ETLs considered, WS₂ provides the most favorable band alignment and improved charge transport, leading to superior device performance. The optimized HTL-free architecture (FTO/WS₂/Ca₃AsBr₃/Au) delivers high power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 20.50%, highlighting Ca₃AsBr₃ as a promising candidate for next-generation, lead-free perovskite solar cells. This study not only offers key insights into designing efficient and environmentally friendly PSCs but also establishes a theoretical foundation to guide future experimental synthesis and device development.
