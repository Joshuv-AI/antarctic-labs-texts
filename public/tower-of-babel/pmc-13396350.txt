Patient-friendly simplification and translation of neuroradiology impressions using artificial intelligence
Authors: Wendy Sun, Advait Patil, Maria A. Vicent, Basile Montagnese, John Zielke, Gerald M. Hefferman, Ian Pan, Gracie Cleaver, Mark C. Eldaief, Rajesh Patel, Daniela L. Mendoza-Millan, Julia Landry, Oscar Moreno-Lizarazo, O. Angel Odukoya, Benjamin R. Johnston, Kevin T. Huang, Omar Arnaout
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Healthcare systems in the United States are now mandated to provide patients with immediate access to medical results. Access to complex results prior to appropriate clinical follow-up may cause confusion and fear, especially in populations with limited health literacy and English language proficiency. In this study, we demonstrate a use case of large language models (LLMs) to simplify and translate complex medical information for patients. We collected a consecutive series of brain MRI (N = 200), head CT (N = 100), and spine MRI (N = 200) reports, prompting a GPT-4o model to simplify these reports to a middle-school reading level and translate a subset of them to Spanish. Critically, two independent neuroradiology raters compared the simplified report impressions to the original report impressions, and found low rates of hallucinations, omissions, and imprecisions. The reading grade of the report impressions decreased from approximately college to approximately middle-school level, and readability improved across several text complexity metrics. Additionally, simplified impressions were classified as more neutral and less fearful. Machine generated translations of simplified impressions were statistically indistinguishable from manual translations as assessed by certified interpreters. In sum, we present a scalable, patient-friendly approach to promote healthcare literacy and engagement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48030-3.

Introduction

The 21st Century Cures Act’s implementation has fundamentally changed how patients receive their medical information, mandating immediate electronic access to test results such as radiology findings1. This policy dramatically reduced the lag between report completion and patient access; nearly half of radiology reports are now viewed by patients before their clinician has reviewed them2. While this transparency empowers patients, it also presents a new challenge: many patients encounter complex, jargon-heavy terminology without guidance. Radiology reports are often written at a college reading level, far above the average American’s 7th–8th grade literacy level3. Not surprisingly, patients frequently report confusion, anxiety, and misinterpretation when reading these reports on their own. In one multi-site survey, nearly half of patients accessing test results online experienced negative emotions or misunderstandings, underscoring the gap between information access and comprehension4, highlighting the need for strategies to make medical findings more understandable.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) offer a potential solution. Researchers have begun exploring large language models (LLMs) to translate technical medical text into plain language. Early studies indicate that LLMs can simplify radiology documentation while preserving essential content. For example, Schmidt et al. showed that ChatGPT could rephrase radiology reports at a lower reading level and improve patient understanding, albeit without fully eliminating all confusion5. Similarly, Park et al. found that non-clinicians had significantly better comprehension of AI-generated “patient-friendly” report summaries, though a small fraction of these AI outputs contained hallucinated or misleading details6. Beyond radiology, LLMs have demonstrated value in other domains: Ali et al.7 used GPT-4 to simplify surgical consent forms to an 8th-grade reading level without losing legal or clinical content. Others have used GPT to draft neurosurgical operative notes comparable in accuracy to expert-written notes8. These successes illustrate the promise of LLMs to bridge communication gaps, but they also reinforce the importance of human oversight to identify errors.

As the healthcare system embraces AI, there is growing recognition that effectiveness must be measured not only by factual accuracy but also by qualities like clarity, empathy, and emotional tone. Abbasian et al.9 argue for “foundation metrics” in AI-generated health communications, emphasizing that truly patient-friendly reports should inform without alarming, engage without overwhelming, and be inclusive of diverse language needs. In this context, we developed a scalable framework leveraging a ChatGPT-based LLM (GPT-4o) to simplify and translate neuroradiology reports for patients. Our approach was designed to preserve all critical clinical information while substantially improving readability and expanding accessibility through translation. By focusing on how patients interpret imaging results, including the emotional impact of wording, our aim is to promote health literacy and empower patients to better understand their medical information.

Methods

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Mass General Brigham (MGB) waived the need of informed consent. Primary sites of study were Brigham and Women’s Hospital (BWH) and Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH).

Data collection and processing

Utilizing the MGB Research Patient Data Registry (RPDR), we systematically collected and parsed radiology reports from BWH and MGH. In this study, we focused on brain MRI, head CT, and spine MRI reports. Specifically, we queried the MGB RPDR for a consecutive series of radiology reports in a specified 1-month time window (August 2024; see Supplement for details). Report headings were programmatically parsed and filtered using key-word identifiers for brain and spine MRIs as well as head CTs. For subsequent analyses, we selected the first 200, 100, and 200 brain MRI, head CT, and spine MRI reports, respectively. Prior to any downstream processing, we removed report footer content associated with electronic signatures and teaching faculty attestations. For each report, the text was programmatically truncated at the first occurrence of “Electronically Signed by” and the first occurrence of “ATTESTATION.” This approach excluded signature/attestation blocks and any trailing text (including addenda appearing after one or both markers). The resulting cleaned report text was then used as input to the LLM for simplification.

Report structure

We used programmatic string parsing to characterize LLM input report structure. Across the cohort (N = 500), reports contained the following labeled sections: indication (“Referring clinician’s provided indication for this examination in Epic”; 96.2%), technique (“TECHNIQUE”; 99.0%), comparison (“COMPARISON”; 100%), findings (“FINDINGS” or “Findings”; 99.6%), and impression (“IMPRESSION”; 100%).

Two head CT reports lacked a labeled FINDINGS header but contained appropriately structured findings content (e.g., “HEAD: Brain Parenchyma: …”), and one head CT report contained a combined FINDINGS/IMPRESSION header. Eighteen reports (3.6%) contained a standalone recommendation section (“RECOMMENDATION(S)” or “Recommendation”), which, when present, immediately followed the IMPRESSION section and was treated as part of the IMPRESSION.

The unit of analysis was the final signed radiology report. Individual reports could include information from multiple imaging modalities ordered for the same clinical indication, but all reports contained a unified “IMPRESSION” section. Ten spine MRI reports were also present within the brain MRI report category (predominantly as part of the multiple sclerosis imaging protocol). Because these reports were indexed as distinct entities in RPDR, we retained them as separate records in modality-specific analyses. For formal statistical analyses combining all reports, these ten reports were counted once to avoid duplication.

Report simplification and subsequent extraction of impressions

Full input report texts were processed using an internal instance of the Azure AI Foundry platform (Microsoft Co., Seattle, WA) utilizing the native version of GPT-4o (model version: 2024-05-13; Open AI, San Francisco, CA). Requests were made via the Azure OpenAI Chat Completions API (API version: 2024-05-01-preview). No fine-tuning of the model was implemented. Each report was processed independently in a stateless, single-turn manner without conversational memory. Additional deployment and generation parameters are provided in the Supplement.

The system-level instruction provided to the model was:

“You are an AI assistant that simplifies radiology reports for patients to understand at an 8th grade reading level. Preserve clinical accuracy while simplifying the text.”

For each report, the following user-level instruction was supplied, followed by the full report text:

“Simplify the following radiology report for the patient to understand at an 8th grade reading level. Avoid using jargon, and avoid prognosticating for the patient. Make sure to have the ‘FINDINGS’ and ‘IMPRESSION’ sections at the end (case sensitive).”

After LLM simplification, the “IMPRESSION” section of each original and simplified report was extracted for further analysis and underwent manual quality control (see Supplement for details). We made the decision to only retain the IMPRESSION section with the radiology team to minimize inaccuracies and preserve the essential information needed by clinicians, billing coders, and legal professionals. In addition, for deployment in clinical practice, this targeted approach is more feasible, as radiologists can quickly verify brief, patient-friendly summaries as needed.

Report translation

The simplified impression sections of a consecutive subset of brain MRI (N = 20), head CT (N = 20), and spine MRI (N = 20) reports were selected for translation to Spanish. Simplified report impressions were processed using an internal instance of the Azure AI Foundry platform (Microsoft Co., Seattle, WA) utilizing the native version of GPT-4o (model version: 2024-08-06; Open AI, San Francisco, CA). Requests were made via the Azure OpenAI Chat Completions API (API version: 2024-12-01-preview). No fine-tuning of the model was implemented. Each report was processed independently in a stateless, single-turn manner without conversational memory. Additional deployment and generation parameters are provided in the Supplement.

The system-level instruction provided to the model was:

Evaluation of clinically relevant model errors

The original and simplified report impressions were given to two radiology MDs (authors GMH and IP) for independent validity checks. These categories were not mutually exclusive; that is, one report could be flagged for multiple categories if it contained multiple mistakes.

For each of the categories in Table 1, we calculated the Prevalence-Adjusted, Bias-Adjusted Kappa as a measure of inter-rater reliability (PABAK = 2*P0 − 1; P0 via the accuracy_score function in the Sklearn Python package, version 1.6.1;10).

Text complexity and readability analysis

We used the Textstat Python package (version 0.7.10;11), see also12 for analysis of textual complexity and readability before and after LLM-driven simplification. Specifically, we examined the Flesch-Kincaid Grade (corresponding to an American school grade;13, Gunning Fog Index (the years of formal education needed to understand a piece of text), and Automated Readability Index and Flesch Reading Ease (measures of how easy a text is to understand). Lower numbers indicate greater readability and lesser complexity for Flesch-Kincaid Grade, Gunning Fog Index, and Automated Readability Index, while higher scores indicate greater readability for Flesch Reading Ease. All 500 reports were processed for readability and complexity metrics. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check for normality. For data that met normality assumptions, paired t-tests (two-tailed) were used to compare differences in means for the complexity and readability metrics, pre- vs. post-simplification, and Cohen’s d was used to measure effect size. In non-normally distributed cases, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare differences in medians, and nonparametric r was computed to measure effect size.

Sentiment analysis

We used “Emotion English DistilRoBERTa-base,” an open source, pre-trained sentiment analysis model14 to characterize the emotional tone of the original and simplified text. This model has been validated and previously used in health/medical contexts15,16. Given text as input, it outputs probability scores (0 to 1) for Ekman’s 6 basic emotions (fear, joy, surprise, sadness, anger, disgust), as well as a neutral category. The model was run on all original and simplified radiology impressions, yielding 7 emotion probability scores per report. One spine MRI report could not be run through the sentiment analysis model due to token limits and was thus excluded from the sentiment analysis. All other reports (N = 499) were successfully processed for sentiment analysis. As with the text complexity metrics, based on results of the Shapiro-Wilk test, either paired t-tests (two-tailed) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to compare differences in emotion scores between pre- and post-simplification, and either Cohen’s d or nonparametric r was calculated to determine effect size.

Translation accuracy analysis

Machine generated translations were compared to manual translations using the Metrics for Evaluation of Translation with Explicit ORdering (METEOR) algorithm17,18. METEOR is an automated metric to evaluate machine translation output that allows for synonymity and shows improved correlation with human judgement relative to other metrics. We used the NLTK Python package (version 3.9.1;19) to calculate METEOR scores with two reference translations (highest score reported). All subsequent analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1;20) with the rstatix package (version 0.7.2;21). The data passed normality assumptions using the Shapiro-Wilk test. One way ANOVA was used to compare differences in mean METEOR scores across report types, with Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons.

Additionally, simplified impression sections were randomized and provided to two certified English-Spanish medical interpreters (authors JL and OM) for blinded, independent ratings of translation accuracy. The original English version, both Spanish manual translations, and the LLM translated output were provided. The interpreters were given the following instructions: “Please score these translations from 1–5, with increasing values meaning increasing quality of translation. In your score, consider translation accuracy, information loss/gain, gross grammatical mistakes, readability, and tone match between the source and the translation. Comment on your score if you believe it is necessary.” Statistical analyses were performed in R. The data did not pass normality assumptions using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Separate Kruskal-Wallis tests per report type and pairwise Wilcoxon tests with Bonferroni correction were used to compare differences in rating scores for manual vs. LLM translations.

Results

Simplified report impressions yield low rates of clinically relevant error

Clinical validity checks were performed by two independent radiology MDs, using the definitions in Table 1. Results are shown in Table 2. In general, model inaccuracies were infrequent. Brain MRIs tended to have the lowest rates of errors. Major omissions occurred in 0.5% of brain MRI reports, 2.0% of head CT reports, and 5.5% of spine MRI reports. Malignant hallucinations were rare, occurring in 0.5%, 0%, and 1.0% of reports, respectively. Major imprecisions occurred in 1.5%, 1.0%, and 6.0% of reports across the three modalities. Inter-rater reliability, as defined here by the PABAK metric, was high across report types and error categories, ranging from 0.86 to 1.00 (perfect match).

Some examples of model inaccuracies are included as follows. A major omission flagged by both raters is the removal of a sentence integrating findings from multiple scans in the same report and differential diagnoses: “Constellation of left facial nerve enhancement and subtle enhancement of the cauda equina raises concern for Guillain-Barre syndrome or neurosarcoidosis…” In addition, a malignant hallucination flagged by R1 was the simplification of “developmental venous anomaly” to “vein problem.” R2 flagged this as a benign hallucination. Lastly, a major imprecision flagged by both raters is the simplification of “Nonenhancing infiltrative, mildly expansile FLAIR/T2 hyperintense lesions involving the left greater than right basal ganglia and internal and external capsules” to “There are mild changes in the basal ganglia and capsules on both sides, more on the left.”

Simplified report impressions are less complex and more readable

Illustrative examples of original versus simplified impressions are available in Table 3 (one example per report type). Qualitatively, the simplified report impressions have fewer complex terms (e.g., “parenchymal edema”, “occipito parietal distribution”, “lipomatosis”) and are easier to read for non-medical professionals. Figure 1 quantifies this effect, showing the distribution of text complexity and readability metrics for original and simplified impressions, with embedded boxplots containing the median (white line) and interquartile range (IQR; thick gray lines). In pooled readability analyses combining report types (N = 490), the median [IQR: Q1,Q3] for original vs. simplified impressions, respectively, was 13.4 [IQR: 11.3,15.4] vs. 7.0 [IQR: 5.2,8.6] for Flesch-Kincaid Grade (W = 688, p < 0.001, r = 0.9); 16.3 [IQR: 13.8,18.6] vs. 10.0 [IQR: 8.0,11.9] for Gunning Fog Index (W = 1738, p < 0.001, r = 0.8); 12.3 [IQR: 9.6,14.6] vs. 6.9 [IQR: 4.4,8.9] for Automated Readability Index (W = 1394, p < 0.001, r = 0.8); 23.3 [IQR: 10.3,34.3] vs. 72.9 [IQR: 64.6,81.2] for Flesch Reading Ease (W = 7, p < 0.001, r = 0.9). On average, LLM-driven simplification brought reports from a college reading level to that of a middle schooler. We also found statistically significant improvements across all four metrics between pre- and post-simplification in analyses examining brain MRI, head CT, and spine MRI reads separately (ps < 0.001).

Simplified report impressions are more neutral and less fearful

Figure 2 illustrates changes in emotional tone between original and simplified impressions. The violin plots show the distribution of probability scores for each of Ekman’s six basic emotions, plus a neutral category. Qualitatively, the most prominent changes are an increase in neutrality and decrease in fear following LLM-driven simplification. Machine generated translations are consistent with expert translations

Translation accuracy was assessed in two ways: by a previously developed algorithm (METEOR) and by blinded scoring from certified medical interpreters. Both metrics demonstrate high accuracy of machine generated translations compared to two references (manually generated translations). Figure 3A shows the distribution of METEOR scores for each report type, with inner boxplots containing the median (white line) and IQR (thick gray lines). Each dot represents the highest METEOR score obtained (compared to both available references) for each of the 60 reports translated. On average, all report types exhibit high accuracy (mean METEOR score 0.86, 0.88, and 0.75 for brain MRI, head CT, and spine MRI, respectively). METEOR scores were significantly lower for spine MRI reports compared to brain MRI and head CT reports (p < 0.001). Figure 3B is a schematic of the evaluation by certified interpreters. Discussion

Our findings demonstrate the potential of LLMs to simplify and translate medical results with low rates of clinically relevant error. In 500 neuroradiology reports across brain MRI, head CT, and spine MRI, the GPT-4o model consistently reduced the reading grade level from roughly college level to middle-school level, and readability indices improved markedly. Simplified “impression” sections were checked by two radiology MDs, who independently found low rates of clinically relevant error. Notably, the emotional tone of reports shifted as well – after simplification, language was significantly more neutral and less fearful than in the original reports. The GPT-4o model also demonstrated high accuracy in translating simplified reports, making them indistinguishable from manual human translations assessed by blinded ratings from certified medical interpreters.

These findings are timely, particularly in the context of evolving patient care paradigms influenced by the 21st Century Cures Act and the rapid exploration (and adaptation) of AI-based tools in medicine. Our work complements earlier studies such as Schmidt et al.5, which focused on LLM-generated summaries of knee MRI findings, and Park et al.6, which evaluated GPT-generated spine MRI reports and found significant improvements in layperson comprehension.

Building on prior findings, our work evaluated a broader corpus of radiology reports (brain MRI, head CT, spine MRI). We also assessed outputs using dual-rater review for three types of model inaccuracies: omissions, hallucinations, and imprecisions. We propose this evaluation method as an option to assess clinical validity in future LLM-based efforts. In our sample, malignant hallucinations were rare (0–1.0% across imaging modalities), and major omissions (0.5–5.5%) / imprecisions (1.0–6.0%) were infrequent—suggesting that GPT-4o, when applied with guardrails, is viable for producing patient-facing radiology summaries.

Not all healthcare systems have ready access to certified medical interpreters, and even those that do, encounter limitations in their availability. Moreover, medical interpreters do not typically translate or interpret written results immediately upon patient receipt, if at all. This, in addition to the heavy workload of manually translating text, makes automated translation via LLMs a promising tool for addressing barriers to care related to language proficiency.

Limitations and future directions

This work has several limitations. First, even low-frequency mistakes can pose significant clinical risks, particularly in scenarios where patients make decisions or assumptions based on AI-generated summaries. The presence of major/malignant mistakes necessitates human oversight, at least in the current implementation strategy. Towards this end, one option is to implement an automated pipeline into existing radiology workflows, whereby a simplified impression is generated as the MD is about to sign the report. The MD can then decide to accept or reject the model’s simplification as an addendum to the report. Another option is to deploy a monitored, automated pipeline that retrieves new reports from the EHR-linked data warehouse, performs scheduled simplification, and routes outputs for secure review by physicians and interpreters and eventual patient delivery. In any implementation, it will be critical to include a patient-facing disclaimer that the AI-generated simplification is not equivalent to clinical communication.

Second, the findings of this study reflect the behavior of GPT-4o under the specific prompting protocol evaluated here, and future investigations should examine more formally operationalized prompting strategies to strengthen claims regarding clinical accuracy and safety. Our prompting strategy specified high-level objectives (e.g., target reading level, avoidance of jargon and prognostication, preservation of clinical meaning) but did not operationalize the specific linguistic mechanisms by which simplification should occur (e.g., lexical substitution versus structural compression versus reinterpretation). While our prompt contains similar levels of operational detail compared to those from prior studies5–7,22, different mechanisms of simplification have distinct implications for fidelity, and greater formalization of such constraints is an important direction for future research, particularly as LLM-based tools move towards integration into clinical workflows. In particular, our prompt did not explicitly require preservation of epistemic qualifiers or diagnostic uncertainty expressions (e.g., “may represent,” “cannot exclude,” “likely”), which are central to the semantics of radiology reporting. Representative examples of how our prompt handled such cases are available in Table S1. Although expert review was conducted here to guard against clinically harmful drift, future work should incorporate explicit structural constraints and uncertainty preservation to ensure that simplification occurs in a clinically accurate and safe manner. Our error analysis revealed that minor omissions and hallucinations were more common in spine MRI reports, where descriptions of degenerative disease or artifact can be lengthy and nuanced. Integration of structured reporting templates for simplification or prompt engineering from clinician feedback may improve performance.

Third, a relatively small subset (60 out of 500) of the reports was assessed for translation, and more expansive validation is needed. Relatedly, while our current system supports Spanish translation, additional languages must be incorporated to address the needs of increasingly diverse patient populations. Additionally, in our subset of translated reports, even though all report types received overall high METEOR scores, spine MRI reports received statistically significantly lower METEOR scores compared to brain MRI and head CT. These reports also tended to receive lower scores from certified interpreters, regardless of the method of translation (manual vs. machine generated). This is likely due to increased presence of jargon in these reports referencing spinal levels and nerve root pathways/openings and is consistent with higher error rates in the original simplification of these reports. There were also comments from the scoring interpreters about synonym use and terminology translations. Both issues are easily addressed by including pre-specified, preferred translations for predictable terminology in the LLM prompt, as was done here to avoid confusion with date formatting differences between English (MM/DD/YYYY) and Spanish (DD/MM/YYYY).

Fourth, we are working on real-world evaluation of patient understanding and response to LLM-simplified medical information. While we used sentiment analysis to infer emotional tone in this study, direct surveys and qualitative interviews with patients will provide richer data on how AI-simplified reports affect their understanding, emotions, and trust in their providers. Encouragingly, a recent randomized trial in oncology found that cancer patients who received LLM-simplified radiology reports demonstrated significantly better understanding of their disease and higher confidence in their care, compared to those who received the original reports22.

Finally, it is important to note that radiology reports are primarily designed for communication among professionals, where the emphasis is on technical precision and medicolegal clarity1. Thus, the linguistic complexity observed here may reflect the inherent requirements of documentation. However, as reporting formats and stylistic conventions evolve, initiatives such as a patient-friendly addendum6 may also improve readability at the point of creation. We therefore view LLM-based simplification not as a replacement for upstream reporting reform, but as a complementary strategy that can coexist with ongoing efforts to enhance readability during report authoring.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that large language models can be leveraged to simplify and translate complex radiology reports with low rates of model error, improved readability, and more patient-friendly emotional tone. This scalable framework addresses a critical need in modern healthcare, bridging the gap between increasing patient access to medical data and the difficulty of interpreting that data. With appropriate safeguards, clinician oversight, and ongoing validation, AI-based simplification tools are poised to become essential components of health communication.
