Implementation of a colorimetry-based urinary biomarker screening system for early UTI risk detection
Authors: H. M. Monisha, R. Rashmi, Nethra Sureshkumar, Rida Rabeea Fatima, Shama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs) are a significant health concern, particularly among infants, elderly individuals, and bedridden patients, where delayed detection can lead to severe complications. This paper presents a colorimetry-based smart diaper system for the early, noninvasive screening of UTIs using Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) principles. The system integrates TCS34725 color sensors with urinalysis test strips to capture RGB data corresponding to key biomarkers such as leukocytes, nitrites, and pH. An ESP32 microcontroller processes the data using a Euclidean distance-based classification algorithm, and the results are transmitted to a cloud-based backend for periodic monitoring and alert generation. The system successfully classified 14 out of 15 simulated test cases, indicating the feasibility of the proposed screening approach under controlled testing conditions. The results demonstrate the feasibility of automated, periodic screening with minimal human intervention. Compared to conventional urinalysis methods, the proposed system offers advantages in automation, accessibility, and scalability. This work establishes a foundation for low-cost, wearable screening systems and highlights the potential for further enhancement through machine learning and clinical validation.

Introduction

The increased attention given to preventive health care, along with continual monitoring of patients, has accelerated the entrance of Internet of Things (IoT) systems into everyday clinical practice1. Most hygiene products, such as diapers, do the job but do not provide health information, potentially leading to delayed identification of urinary tract infections (UTIs)2,3. If undiagnosed, UTIs can become serious, costly and chronic conditions. This situation can seriously impact the quality of life of infants, elderly individuals, and bed-bound patients4,5. The monitoring of symptoms in these groups can be very challenging. As part of the LifeSync project, smart diapers are being developed that contain sensors that use TCS34725 color sensors and urinalysis test strips to sense and track urine biochemical color changes to facilitate the noninvasive monitoring of UTIs6.

A cloud data acquisition system using the ESP32 microcontroller has been designed. This microcontroller is the central data acquisition and communication hub. The color sensors capture RGB data from urinalysis test strips that react to biomarkers of leukocytes, nitrites, and pH associated with urinary tract infections (UTIs)6,7. A threshold screening algorithm processes and analyzes the data.

Existing smart diaper systems are primarily designed to detect moisture levels or monitor urination events, with limited focus on biochemical analysis of urine. Similarly, conventional urinalysis methods often require manual testing and laboratory infrastructure, making continuous monitoring difficult. The proposed system addresses this gap by combining colorimetric urine analysis, embedded sensor-based classification, and cloud-enabled remote monitoring in a single platform for non-invasive UTI screening. By integrating biomarker screening for leukocytes, nitrites, and pH with automated classification and alert generation, the system offers a practical approach for periodic monitoring of infants, elderly individuals, and bedridden patients.

Background and significance of the problem

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the primary bacterial infections affecting the world as well as people of all ages and stages of life8. Delayed detection of infection can lead to more complex issues concerning patients, such as sepsis, prolonged hospitalization, and renal failure4. Those who are more compromised and at increased risk, such as infants, bed-bound patients,

elderly patients and older patients, may not be able to sense changes in their body. Sepsis and renal failure are just some of the complications that may be endured in these patients. Consequently, olfactory and visual assessments must be performed, and these assessments are subjective and can lead to incorrect diagnoses, which can greatly postpone treatment. Although urinalysis is one of the most respected tests in laboratories concerning UTIs, it is still invasive, time-consuming and not quantitative for use in all medical situations7. As demand for personalized and preventive health care continues to increase, there is now a clear and urgent need for solutions that offer periodic and noninvasive health status monitoring1. Recent advancements in the Internet of Things (IoT) and biosensor technologies enable us to combine the collection of physiological data automatically and seamlessly with the transmission of that data to digital environments for near-real-time interpretation9,1. While there are many commercial products in the smart hygiene space, most are limited to providing basic wetness screening functionality and do not analyze the chemistry of urine or offer any insights into an individual’s underlying health status, including urinary tract infections (UTIs), dehydration, or electrolyte status2,10,11.This represents a significant opportunity for health monitoring wearables that integrate biochemical screening into IoT-enabled devices.12,13. The system designed for the LifeSync concept is a platform that provides the amalgamation of biochemical sensing, cloud connectivity, and intelligence to interpret suggestive urinalysis at the point of care in a compact device. By employing colorimetric urine analysis, the system enables non-invasive screening of infection biomarkers such as leukocytes, nitrites, and pH, extending the functionality of conventional hygiene products beyond basic monitoring into automated health screening.6,14.

Discussion

The objective of the present work was to demonstrate the engineering feasibility of integrating colorimetric sensing, embedded processing, and IoMT connectivity into a wearable screening platform. Consequently, the experimental evaluation focused on controlled laboratory evaluation using simulated urinalysis strip responses. Comprehensive clinical validation and deployment under practical diaper-use conditions remain subjects of future investigation.

The experimental findings support the engineering feasibility of the proposed screening framework. However, several limitations remain in the current implementation.

Limited sample size

The system was evaluated using only 15 simulated test cases, which limits the statistical significance of the reported performance. Larger datasets are required for more comprehensive validation.

Simulated testing environment

The current evaluation was conducted using simulated biomarker conditions rather than real clinical urine samples. Further validation using real-world samples is necessary to assess clinical applicability.

Sensor calibration dependency

The classification performance depends on accurate calibration of RGB reference values.

Variations in sensor drift over time may affect classification consistency.

Environmental sensitivity

Although measurements were acquired under controlled laboratory lighting conditions, variations in ambient illumination, strip positioning, temperature, and humidity may affect sensor readings. In addition, potential interferents such as blood or hemoglobin, glucose, protein, hydration status, medications, fecal contamination, and cleaning agents may influence the colorimetric responses of urinalysis strips, as reported in previous studies. These factors were not evaluated in the present proof-of-concept study and should be investigated during future clinical validation to assess their impact on classification performance under real-world conditions.

Limited biomarker scope

The current system evaluates only leukocytes, nitrites, and pH levels. Additional biomarkers could improve screening reliability and broaden screening capabilities.

Privacy and security considerations

As the proposed system supports cloud-based storage and transmission of health-related information, privacy and security remain important considerations for future deployment. Secure communication protocols, authenticated user access, encrypted data storage, and compliance with applicable healthcare data regulations should be incorporated into future versions of the system. These measures will help ensure patient confidentiality while supporting remote monitoring and caregiver access.

Future research should also investigate long-term wearable deployment, user comfort, sensor durability, environmental robustness, and regulatory considerations associated with healthcare monitoring devices. Additional studies involving larger datasets and clinical samples will be required to further validate the system and assess its practical applicability in real-world healthcare environments.

Methods

System design of the proposed model

The LifeSync UTI Screening System offers an innovative blend of hardware and software to facilitate the automated screening of urinary tract infections through the analysis of test strip colors6. This architecture is displayed in Fig. 1 and consists of three main layers: the sensing layer, the control and processing layer, and the display interface.

Sensing layer

During this stage of sensing, a chemical urine test strip is used. The test strip shows a response to the urine sample, changing color in three diagnostic areas that reflect primarily on the pH level, leukocytes, and nitrites. These components indicate a possible urinary tract infection6,7. To capture and quantify the variations in color, three TCS34725 color sensors are used. There is one for each parameter.

Each sensor captures the color changes on the test strip and converts them into digital RGB values for further analysis. The selection of the TCS34725 module is grounded in its industry standards for precision and the computation, IR blocking filter, and I²C communication making it ideal for accurate and noise-free color screening14.

Control and processing layer

Since all three TCS34725 sensors share identical I²C addresses, a PCA9548A I²C multiplexer is employed to manage communication. The multiplexer dynamically switches between sensors to allow the ESP32 microcontroller to connect with each one in turn. The ESP32 serves as the central processing unit of the system. Gaps addressed by the proposed model

The LifeSync initiative was tailored to address a number of the limitations described in existing systems:

Lack of biochemical sensing in commercial products

Most of the smart diapers only inform the end user when they are wet2,10,11. They often do not provide any information about the risk of infection or urine chemistry. LifeSync enables multi-sensor colorimetric analysis to monitor UTI-associated biomarkers—specifically, pH, nitrites, and leukocytes—to obtain enhanced screening capability6.

Absence of cloud integration and historical analysis

Existing systems can gather some data locally, however, there are no common indicators that data would also accumulate over time18,19. Life Sync has a cloud-based backend for data logging, data visualization, and data analytics in order that the caregiver may make decisions regarding trending over time.

Manual intervention and human error

Historical urinalysis is mainly predicated on visual inspection, resulting in significant inconsistency7. The LifeSync approach is an automated data capture with colorimetric analysis of calibrated RGB readings that involves less human error while ensuring an objective, repeatable measurement process.

Limited accessibility and alerting

In most of existing systems, alerting was not either real-time accessible–communication was not equipped with even an alert process3,11. The LifeSync adopted both cloud and Twilio technologies to alert the users either by email, SMS, and/or mobile alert.

Scalability and affordability issues

High-cost medical sensors or hospital-based systems are impracticable for ongoing monitoring9,20. LifeSync uses low-cost, off-the-shelf components (ESP32, TCS34725, PCA multiplexer) to offer scalable and affordable health technology that can be used in homes and in the clinic.

As it addresses these gaps, LifeSync becomes an intelligent screening platform and allows caregivers to access and take action on data-driven insights beyond basic monitoring of hygiene.

Implementation overview

The implementation phase involved developing hardware and software modules and then integrating them. The system consists of biosensing strips embedded within the diaper, followed by processing to identify colorimetric changes produced when urine comes into contact with the reagent strips6,8.

Hardware design

The system consists of three TCS34725 color sensors interfaced with an ESP32 microcontroller through a PCA9548A multiplexer. The sensors detect the intensity of the colorimetric change produced by the pH, leukocyte and nitrite pads6. The microcontroller then reads the data, processes it, calculates the closest color match via the Euclidean distance and displays the results onto the serial monitor while also transmitting them to the backend to render onto the dashboard. Each sensor is powered by the 3.3 V supply of ESP32 (Fig. 2).

Calibration methodology

The TCS34725 sensors were configured using an integration time of 600 ms and a gain setting of 1× throughout calibration and testing. Before testing, the sensors were calibrated using standard urine test strips for leukocytes, nitrites, and pH. Each strip was placed in front of the corresponding TCS34725 sensor, and RGB values were recorded for different color variations.

Multiple readings were taken for each strip and the average RGB values were stored as reference values to reduce minor variations in sensor readings. During calibration, the strip position was kept fixed to maintain consistency.

To minimize external lighting effects, calibration was performed under consistent laboratory lighting conditions while maintaining a fixed sensor-to-strip distance and strip orientation.

Calibration repeatability

To evaluate calibration consistency, five repeated RGB measurements were recorded for each biomarker calibration state under identical laboratory conditions. The arithmetic mean of the recorded RGB values was used as the reference value for subsequent Euclidean distance classification, while the standard deviation was calculated to quantify measurement variability. As shown in Table 6, most calibration states exhibited low standard deviation values, indicating good repeatability of the sensing system. During calibration, the test strips were manually positioned at a fixed distance above the corresponding TCS34725 sensor under consistent laboratory lighting conditions to minimize measurement variability.

Embedded firmware

ESP32 is programmed in C via the Arduino IDE. It does the following.

Sensor initialization

Three sensors are used for three parameters. Channel 0 represents leukocytes, channel 3 represents nitrite, and channel 4 represents pH.

Color measurement

Each sensor reads the RGB values produced due to the colorimetric reaction on the test strips6,14.

Color classification

A matchColor() function calculates the Euclidean distance between the measured RGB values and predefined reference RGB values. The measured values are then classified into labels such as ’Positive’ and ’Negative’ for leukocytes and nitrite and float values such as 7.5, 8.0, and 8.5 for pH.

Data transmission

The system compiles the readings and labels into a JSON object via the ArduinoJSON library and sends them to the cloud server via HTTP POST every 2 min. This 2-minute interval was used to avoid server overload and optimize the bandwidth.

Backend software

A cloud-based backend was developed to receive sensor readings from the embedded device, store historical records, and support alert generation. The backend enables remote monitoring by maintaining patient records and processing incoming classification results.

Frontend web application

A web-based user interface was developed to display uploaded screening results, historical records, notifications, and user guidance for caregivers.

Functional flow

When a test strip is placed against the sensor module, the color change produced as a result of contact with urine is detected by the three sensors6,7. ESP32 reads these values, compares them against the calibrated reference values and transmits them to the cloud-based server. The screening function analyzes these values and evaluates potential UTI risk levels. These values are also updated on the dashboard after each acquisition cycle. If a test is flagged as positive, the cloud-based server triggers an automated alert via email to the caregiver5 (Fig. 3).

The colorimetric readings obtained from the TCS34725 sensors have been classified using Euclidean Distance6. The firmware computes the Euclidean distance between the measured RGB values and a set of predefined calibration values.

The Euclidean distance-based classification approach was selected due to its low computational complexity and suitability for embedded systems such as ESP32. Unlike machine learning models, which require large datasets and higher processing power, this method enables fast and efficient on-device classification with minimal resource consumption1. This makes it ideal for wearable healthcare applications where power and latency constraints are critical8,17.

Alternative machine learning approaches such as support vector machines, decision trees, and neural networks may offer improved classification performance; however, they typically require larger training datasets and higher computational resources than those available on low-power embedded platforms.

Decision rule

The classification decision is based on a minimum-distance criterion. The measured RGB vector is assigned to the class whose reference RGB vector yields the smallest Euclidean distance. This nearest-neighbor approach ensures efficient classification while maintaining low computational complexity.

No human participants or clinical urine samples were involved in this study. All evaluations were conducted using simulated urinalysis strip conditions under controlled laboratory settings.

Threshold selection and justification

The classification thresholds used in this study were derived from manufacturer-provided color scales associated with commercial urinalysis strips and validated through repeated calibration measurements. Leukocyte and nitrite classifications were determined using nearest-reference RGB matching based on Euclidean distance. For pH classification, reference values corresponding to pH levels of 5.0, 6.5, 7.5, and 8.5 were established during calibration. Elevated urinary pH values greater than 7 are commonly associated with increased UTI risk and were therefore incorporated into the screening decision rules. This threshold-based approach enables efficient implementation on resource-constrained embedded hardware while maintaining consistent classification performance.

Experimental setup

Reference RGB values for each biomarker class were obtained through controlled calibration using simulated urinalysis color samples. During calibration, the test strips were manually positioned at a fixed distance above the corresponding TCS34725 sensor while maintaining consistent strip orientation and laboratory lighting conditions. Five repeated RGB measurements were recorded for each calibration state. The mean RGB values were used as calibration references, while the standard deviation was computed to evaluate measurement repeatability.

To evaluate the performance of the proposed system, a controlled experimental setup was designed using simulated urinalysis strip color responses representing different urinary biomarker conditions under controlled laboratory settings.6,7.

A total of 15 test cases (T1–T15) were created, each corresponding to a specific combination of biomarker levels. These simulated strips were used to generate controlled and repeatable color variations for testing the system. The color changes were captured using TCS34725 sensors under consistent lighting conditions and fixed sensor positioning to ensure measurement reliability.
