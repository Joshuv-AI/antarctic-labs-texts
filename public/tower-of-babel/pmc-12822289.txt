Informal care and cognitive frailty among Chinese older adults: the mediating role of social participation
Authors: Tianyu Gao, Dan Zhao, Shujun Chai, Xiaohan Qi, Yang Liu, Yihong Sun, Yuxun Zhang, Zihan Lin, Jie Zhou, Chengchao Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesWith the intensification of ageing, cognitive frailty has become a major public health issue. The underlying mechanisms of the association between informal care and cognitive frailty were poorly understood. This study aimed to examine the mediating effect of social participation in the relationship between informal care and cognitive frailty among Chinese older adults.Research Design and MethodsThis research included 2,066 participants aged 60 and above from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). We conducted the logistic regression model and Karlson-Holm-Breen (KHB) analyses to test the mediating role of social participation in the relationship between informal care and cognitive frailty.ResultsAfter adjusting the confounders, three dimensions of informal care were negatively associated with cognitive frailty (financial support: β = -0.124, P = 0.043; emotional support: β = -0.149, P = 0.042; life care:β = -0.282, P = 0.015). Diversity and frequency of social participation were all mediated the association between financial support and cognitive frailty (diversity: P = 0.019, frequency: P = 0.013), as well as life care and cognitive frailty (diversity: P = 0.022, frequency: P = 0.018). However, the mediating effect of social participation on the association between emotional support and cognitive frailty was insignificant (diversity: P = 0.619, frequency: P = 0.957).Discussion and ImplicationsInformal care was associated with cognitive frailty among Chinese older adults. Diversity and frequency of social participation were all mediated the association between financial support and cognitive frailty, as well as life care and cognitive frailty. More attention should be devoted to promoting social participation among Chinese older adults with cognitive frailty, especially those who receive life care and financial support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06874-w.Keywords: Mediation analysis, Social participation, Cognitive frailty, Old adults

Introduction

China’s aging process can no longer be underestimated. By 2020, the population aged 60 years and above has accounted for 18.7% [1]. In recent years, cognitive frailty has attracted widespread concern due to its increasing negative influence on older adults. Cognitive frailty is a new definition first proposed by the International Academy on Nutrition and Aging (I.A.N.A) and the International Association of Gerontology and Geriatrics (I.A.G.G) in 2013 [2]. That is, cognitive frailty denotes the coexistence of physical frailty and cognitive impairment in the absence of neurodegenerative disease (Alzheimer’s disease or other types of dementia). Compared to single cognitive impairment or physical frailty, increasing studies have proved that cognitive frailty powerfully predicts all-cause mortality, disability, depression and other adverse health outcomes among older adults [3–7]. Cognitive frailty not only brings care pressure and disease financial stress to the family, but also places a heavy burden on society and makes it difficult to achieve the allocation of health resources [8]. However, cognitive frailty is reversible and interventions such as emotional support and social participation have significantly improved cognitive frailty [7]. Consequently, exploring risk factors and interventions for cognitive frailty among Chinese older adults is essential.

Informal care, which involves unpaid attention primarily centered around family and community, is extended to old adults by spouses, offspring, other blood relatives, neighbors, and friends [9]. Generally, informal care includes financial support, emotional support and life care [10, 11]. Nowadays, with the increase in population, changes in population structure, and decrease in public expenditure, informal care has become the mainstream way of home care and a hot topic of discussion today [12, 13]. An increasing number of countries are emphasizing the “family-community” model of long-term care, with informal care as the main form of practice. Positive reforms in informal care have been realized, and the status of informal care is constantly being raised [14]. Some studies indicate that as a primary source of social support for older adults, informal care can promote healthy behaviors [10]. In addition, previous studies have looked at each of the three factors of informal care to demonstrate the protective effect of the factor on cognitive frailty [15, 16]. However, studies have yet to comprehensively explored the relationship between informal care and cognitive frailty among Chinese older adults.

Social participation, a potential modifiable protective factor for cognitive frailty, has captured extensive attention due to its low cost and high feasibility among older adults [16–18]. Based on social exchange theory [19], older people who receive informal care use their free time to improve quality of life, for example by using free time to participate in various group activities. Previous studies suggested that positive emotional support, adequate financial support from adult children and life care encouraged older people to engage in the community actively [20, 21]. A study conducted in Chinese communities found that older adults participating in more and more frequent social activities were less likely to experience cognitive decline [22].

The goal of social support is to shield individuals from the adverse effects of life stressors and to reduce the incidence of negative health outcomes [23, 24]. It plays a critical role in shaping health trajectories, with particularly pronounced benefits observed among older adults [25]. Informal care constitutes a vital component of social support, focusing on enhancing the condition and wellbeing of care recipients [26], and significantly impacts health outcomes among older adults. Meanwhile, cognitive frailty has been shown to be one of the major health threats for older adults [5]. Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 1: Informal care may be a key determinant of cognitive frailty, exhibiting a significant inverse relationship. Providing high-quality informal care may help lower the risk of cognitive frailty in older adults. Methods

Date source

The data used in this study came from the 4th wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) in 2018. CHARLS is a large-scale national survey investigating health status and medical and health services utilization for Chinese adults aged 45 years and above. It was launched in 2011 and was followed up every two years. The CHARLS 2018 collected the information of 19,816 respondents. The study employed the following exclusion criteria: (1) participants below 60 years of age (n = 9,203); (2) individuals unable to answer Mini-Mental State Examination independently (n = 1,371); (3) individuals with incomplete data required for frailty index (n = 4,273); and (4) respondents lacking complete demographic records or essential study variables including health insurance status, educational attainment, financial support frequency from adult children, and emotional support reception frequency (n = 2,903). Finally, 2,066 respondents were included in our study. A detailed flowchart of the inclusion exclusion process can be found in the supplementary document (see Fig. S1).

Measures

Informal care

Informal care includes three dimensions: financial support, emotional support and life care. Financial support is measured by the amount of money provided by adult children [28]. Emotional support is assessed by the frequency of contact between older adults and adult children [11, 29]. Life care is evaluated by whether older adults receive care [10]. Detailed questions and assignment rules are in the supplementary material (see Table S1-S6).

Cognitive frailty

To measure the cognitive frailty [30], we adopted the frailty index and Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) [31, 32].Respondents who tested positive on both instruments were classified as exhibiting cognitive frailty and assigned a code of “1”, while those who did not meet this criterion were coded as “0”. The frailty index was composed of 5 dimensions and 32 items. The Frailty Index consists of 5 dimensions and 32 items, including the respondent’s self-assessment of health, the respondent’s mobility test, depression measurements, memory test, intelligence and orientation test. In this study, we assigned a value to each item, culminating in a comprehensive frailty score. We divided the final total score by 32 to determine the individual frailty score. Individuals were categorized based on their scores as follows: those ranging from 0 to less than 0.25 were considered non-frail, while individuals with scores from 0.25 up to 1 were classified as frail [33]. Detailed questions and assignment rules are in the supplementary material.

The MMSE was applied to evaluate an individual’s cognitive function. The MMSE score ranges from 0 to 30, with a high score indicating better cognitive function. The cut-off values for cognitive impairment on the MMSE vary based on educational attainment: illiterate (≤ 17), primary school (≤ 20), junior school (≤ 22), and university or higher education (≤ 24) [34].

Social participation

The diversity of social participation was assessed by the following question: “Have you participated in one of the following social activities in the past month?” in the questionnaire, with a total of 12 options, such as “went to a sport, social, or another kind of club”, “done voluntary or charity work” and so on. The frequency of social participation was assessed by the following question: “How often in the last month (did/have) you do these activities?” in the questionnaire, with 3 options: “Almost daily, almost every week and not regularly” Detailed questions and assignment rules are in the supplementary material. When measuring diversity in social participation, the scores ranged from 0 to 11, with higher scores indicating higher levels of social participation. When measuring frequency in social participation, we combined the frequency of social participation score with the number of activities participated in, with scores ranging from 0 to 33, with higher scores being associated with higher frequency of social participation [35–37].

Other covariates

Sociodemographic characteristics included gender (male/female), age (60–70, 70–80, ≥ 80), education (illiteracy/primary school/middle school/high school and above), marital status (single/married), residence (rural/urban). Living condition (Live alone/residence with spouse only/living with spouse and children/living with children only). Medical insurance was divided into five categories (urban employee medical insurance/urban and rural resident medical insurance/urban resident medical insurance/new rural cooperative medical insurance/others) [38]. Living habit is defined by two variables: smoking status (yes/no) and drinking status (yes/no). The above measures of the variables, as well as the scales involved in the measurements, have been proven in the relevant literature to have good reliability and validity.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA 17.0. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Firstly, descriptive statistics were used to describe the characteristics of participants. Second, the Student’s t-test and the Chi-square test were used to evaluate the association among the main variables. Third, logistic regression analysis was use to explore the association between informal care and cognitive frailty, while the mediating role of social participation in this relationship was estimated by the Karlson-Holm-Breen (KHB) method [39].

Sensitivity analyses

After testing (2=3529.06, P < 0.001), we found that the main variable was not missing completely at random. Therefore, imputation was not appropriate, and all samples with missing values for the main variable were excluded from the analysis. Since CHARLS is a longitudinal database and some of the responses to the questions will be shown in the previous wave of follow-up, we filled in time-invariant variables such as gender and age, which are filled in this study through previous periods. In addition, we make supplement for other covariates, resulting in a final sample size of 2066. In Table S7-S8, the results of the analyses before supplement of the variables are shown.

The largest proportion of older adults lived with their spouses only, and the proportion of cognitive frailty older adults living with their children only was higher than the proportion of older adults living with their children only in the healthy population. The prevalence of cognitive frailty was 22.94%. The mean scores for diversity and frequency of social participation between respondents with and without cognitive frailty were significantly different (P<0.001). Emotional support was not related to the diversity and frequency of social participation (diversity: β = −0.008, P = 0.877; frequency: β = −0.009, P = 0.813). Sensitivity analyses

After adjusting for control variables, both the diversity and frequency of social participation significantly mediated the relationships between financial support and cognitive frailty, as well as between life care and cognitive frailty. However, neither the diversity nor the frequency of social participation significantly mediated the association between emotional support and cognitive frailty. This finding remained consistent after applying the imputation method to address missing data. In Table S9, we conducted a comparative analysis between the included and excluded samples and found that, although the included participants were younger, there were no significant differences in other characteristics. such as education, gender, marital status, place of residence, residential status, smoking status, drinking status, diversity of social participation, and frequency, which again ensures the robustness of our findings. Similarly, the characteristics of samples with and without missing data were reported in Table S10.

Discussion

This study revealed two main findings. Firstly, all dimensions of informal care were negatively correlated with cognitive frailty. Secondly, the diversity and frequency of social participation mediated the relationship between financial support and cognitive frailty, as well as the relationship between life care and cognitive frailty, whereas no significant mediating effect was observed in the relationship between emotional support and cognitive frailty.

In this study, we found that the prevalence of cognitive frailty among older adults was 22.94%, which is high than in another study (15%) [40]. This difference may be partly attributable to the characteristics of our study sample and analytical methods. Specifically, 68% of participants in our sample lived in rural areas, where older adults typically face higher risks of body frailty and cognitive impairment due to socioeconomic disadvantages and more limited access to health and social resources [41].

This study found that financial support from adult children of informal care was associated with cognitive frailty in older adults, but when the mediating factor of social participation was added, the relationship between financial support from adult children and cognitive frailty was no longer significant. Financial support from adult children can significantly reduce the possibility of cognitive frailty in older adults, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [8, 42]. However, as a means of material incentives, financial support does not have a direct impact on cognition and physical frailty in older adults in the same way that emotional support and life care do. Meanwhile, our study found that social participation mediated the relationship between financial support and cognitive frailty. Previous studies have confirmed that when older adults obtain financial support, they will have more abilities to get the corresponding social support and related medical resources [42], thereby improving their cognitive frailty [43]. For older people over 60 years of age, social participation is the main way for them to feel social support [15, 27], and social participation has become the first target of transformation for older people after they have received financial support. In the process of participating in social activities, with the increase of physical activity and team collaboration, the physical frailty of older adults will be improved [44]. Individual depression and social fear among older adults will also be reduced due to communication barriers being broken down [27]. Then, their cognitive frailty may be improved through social participation [22].

Most studies have proven emotional support and companionship to impact older adults positively. Our study provides evidence consistent with Choi’s findings that a negative association exists between emotional support and cognitive frailty in older adults [16]. Living with a family member makes older adults feel richer inside, and older adults have more opportunities to talk to them in their daily life, and the person living with them listens patiently and gives them positive feedback. Older adults who live with their families receive more emotional support than those who live alone [45].Liu et al. also confirmed that when older adults met with their children or friends more often and received more frequent emotional support, their cognition and physical frailty would be improved [46, 47]. Maintaining emotional support can give older adults a sense of belonging and emotional satisfaction [46, 48], stimulate brain activity and release neurochemicals such as oxytocin and dopamine [49, 50], and further improve their cognitive frailty state [51]. Unlike previous studies [52], we found no significant correlation between emotional support and social participation in old adults. We explored the possibility that older adults may achieve the same effects as participating in social activities simply by receiving emotional support, thereby improving cognitive frailty. This finding further underscores the importance of emotional support for older adults.

According to Pecking order theory [38], older adults often prioritise informal care before seeking formal care. Therefore, receiving life care in informal care is the most prevalent phenomenon among the older adults. Our study provides evidence demonstrating a negative association between life care and cognitive frailty in the older adults. Positive life care can regulate the health behaviors of the older adults, clump of overall control smoking, diet regulation, weight control, exercise maintenance, controlling the risk factors of cognitive frailty in the older adults from the source [10], so as to reduce the prevalence of cognitive frailty in the older adults. The study of Wang also showed that receiving the life care of children can reduce the depression of the older adults and improve physical frailty [28] Meanwhile, in the mediation analysis, social participation mediated part of the association between the relationship of the life care and cognitive frailty, and positive life care adequately regulated the health behaviors of the older adults, make them have the objective conditions and endogenous motivation to participate in social activities. So as to impel the frequency of their social participation, through team cooperation and active communication in the process of social participation, the improvement of cognitive frailty of the older adults can be achieved from both physical and psychological perspectives [48]. Thus, older adults can also achieve improvements in cognitive frailty through social participation. Thus, older adults can also achieve improvements in cognitive frailty through social participation. For East Asia and other developing regions, informal care represents the most prevalent health-related determinant regularly encountered by older adults in their daily routines, with its widespread prevalence serving as a distinctive feature of developing countries. Consequently, these regions should recognize the critical significance of informal care mechanisms. This necessitates coordinated efforts to leverage the collective potential of many groups while implementing measures to enhance its utilization. Such comprehensive strategies aim to optimize the health outcomes of aging populations through targeted enhancement of informal care frameworks, thereby reducing the risk of adverse health outcomes in older adults.

Our study revealed the relationship between the three dimensions of informal care and cognitive frailty, with social participation playing a mediating role in the relationships between financial support and cognitive frailty, and life care and cognitive frailty. This has implications for improving cognitive frailty in China’s older population. Policy makers should continue to improve the informal care policy system and actively engage in multiple social activities. For those who are unable to participate in social activities, their life care should be strengthened in a timely manner, so as to realize the improvement of cognitive frailty.

There were several limitations in this study. First, it was a cross-sectional study, and causality could not be ascertained. Second, the measurement of emotional support was limited to contact frequency, which may fail to adequately capture its multidimensional characteristics—such as empathetic understanding, encouragement for feelings, and the expression of positive affect [50]. This narrow operationalization might also account for the non-significant mediating effects of social participation diversity and frequency in the association between emotional support and cognitive frailty. Third, there may be other mechanisms between informal care and cognitive frailty, such as psychological distress, sleep quality and so on, future research should explore more factors to explain other pathways between informal care and cognitive frailty. Fourth, there is no strict division between family and community care in the informal care of this study. Finally, while we did not impute missing values for our key variables, we acknowledge that this may limit generalizability, and future studies could explore the use of multiple imputation or full information maximum likelihood methods under MAR assumptions. Thus, it cannot fully account for the effects of family and community care on cognitive frailty.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that older adults who receive financial support, emotional support, and life care all have a significant improvement in their cognitive frailty state. Diversity as well as frequency of social participation mediates the relationship between informal care and cognitive frailty. The results provide empirical evidence to strengthen the protective effect of informal care on cognitive frailty among older adults. Not only does the diversity of social participation significantly affect cognitive frailty among older adults, but also the frequency of social participation has a positive impact on cognitive frailty among older adults. Accordingly, the government and relevant departments can formulate relevant measures to encourage the community to carry out diversified activities and adopt a variety of incentives to promote the social participation of older adults in order to adapt to the development trend of active aging.
