Free energy dissipation and a decomposition of general jump diffusions on ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} without detailed balance
Shuyuan Fan, Qi Zhang

Abstract
We analyze the thermodynamic structure of jump diffusions combining Brownian and Poisson noise, a class of stochastic dynamics relevant to nonequilibrium statistical physics. For such nonlocal dynamics, the free energy admits a full dissipation formula that decomposes into entropy production and housekeeping heat. A central result is a decomposition of the generator into symmetric and anti-symmetric parts with respect to the invariant measure ρ s ​ s \rho_{ss} . The symmetric sector corresponds to a reversible dynamics and yields a nonlocal Fisher information governing free-energy decay, whereas the anti-symmetric sector generates a canonical conservative flow that produces circulation but no dissipation. Several numerical examples demonstrate how this decomposition clarifies the structure of nonequilibrium stationary states in jump-driven systems.



Keywords:
Free energy dissipation; nonlocal equations; Lévy processes; Fisher information.

Introduction

The concept of free energy lies at the heart of statistical physics, serving as the fundamental bridge between microscopic stochastic fluctuations and macroscopic thermodynamic laws.
For a stochastic system coupled to a thermal environment,
the generalized free energy—mathematically formulated as the relative entropy with respect to a stationary measure—transcends its classical equilibrium definition to become a universal metric quantifying the ”distance” from the steady state.
Its temporal evolution encapsulates the essence of the Second Law:
the free energy dissipation reflects the monotonic loss of distinguishability as the system relaxes, acting as a robust Lyapunov functional that guarantees dynamical stability
[
27
,
9
,
20
]
.
Crucially, this dissipation is not merely a mathematical decay;
it physically encodes the time-irreversibility of the underlying trajectory and provides a rigorous quantification of the entropy production rate, thereby revealing the thermodynamic cost and the arrow of time embedded in the stochastic dynamics
[
33
,
35
,
15
,
30
,
40
,
12
]
.

Classically, for diffusion processes governed by the second-order Fokker–Planck equation,
the relationship between free energy and entropy is well-understood: in detailed-balanced systems, the free energy dissipation rate is exactly balanced by the total entropy production rate
[
30
,
40
,
11
]
.
However, for systems driven into a non-equilibrium steady state by non-conservative forces, this equality breaks down. The discrepancy led to the concept of “housekeeping heat”
Q_{hk}
, first introduced phenomenologically by Oono and Paniconi
[
28
]
to describe the heat dissipated solely to maintain the steady state.
This concept was later formalized by Hatano and Sasa
[
19
]
through a generalized fluctuation-dissipation relation.
More recently, a unified framework was established by Esposito and Van den Broeck
[
12
,
40
]
, who decomposed the total entropy production into an adiabatic part (associated with housekeeping heat) and a non-adiabatic part (associated with system relaxation).
In this framework, the free energy dissipation is linked strictly to the non-adiabatic entropy production,
while the housekeeping heat represents the energetic cost of breaking detailed balance.

Underpinning these thermodynamic phenomenologies is the geometric structure of the generator and associated Fokker-Planck operator.
For continuous diffusion processes, it is known that the drift vector field
b(x)
admits a Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition:
b(x)=-\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\phi(x)+v_{res}(x)
,
where
\phi
is a potential function linked to the stationary distribution and
v_{res}
is a divergence-free residual drift
[
2
,
31
,
3
,
11
]
.
This vector field decomposition corresponds to an operator-theoretic splitting of the generator
\mathcal{L}
into a symmetric part
\mathcal{L}_{s}
and an antisymmetric part
\mathcal{L}_{a}
in the weighted Hilbert space
L^{2}(\rho_{ss})

[
31
,
11
]
.
The symmetric part generates a reversible dynamics responsible for the relaxation towards equilibrium, while the antisymmetric part generates a measure-preserving conservative flow along the level sets of the stationary distribution.

While the aforementioned framework is well-established for continuous paths, nature is replete with phenomena exhibiting abrupt discontinuities.
In recent years, jump noise has been identified as a ubiquitous feature across diverse scales.
In biological systems, Lévy flights and jump-diffusions are employed to model the optimal foraging strategies of animals
[
39
,
4
]
, the active transport of organelles within viscoelastic cytoplasm
[
23
]
, and the heavy-tailed statistics of neuronal avalanches
[
5
]
.
In complex physical systems, jump components are essential for describing anomalous diffusion in turbulent flows
[
37
]
and tipping points in paleoclimate data driven by rapid environmental shifts
[
8
]
.
Furthermore, in quantitative finance, jump-diffusion models extend the classical Black-Scholes framework to capture the skewness and fat tails observed in asset return distributions
[
26
,
10
]
.
More recently, the time-reversal of jump diffusions has found cutting-edge applications in machine learning, particularly in score-based generative models where adding noise with jumps can improve sampling efficiency
[
42
,
14
]
.

Despite the broad phenomenological relevance of jump processes, the theoretical development of their non-equilibrium statistical mechanics lags behind that of their continuous counterparts.
Existing research has largely focused on two extremes: continuous Langevin dynamics and discrete-state Markov jump processes (Master equations)
[
36
,
25
]
.
For the intermediate regime of jump-diffusions—where the state space is continuous but trajectories are discontinuous—a unified thermodynamic framework is still under active development.
Recent works have begun to address the stochastic thermodynamics of Lévy flights and anomalous diffusion, investigating entropy production rates and fluctuation theorems in the presence of non-local operators
[
22
,
38
,
6
,
18
]
.
However, extending the geometric and operator-theoretic decompositions to this setting poses significant analytical challenges.
Unlike the local Laplacian operator in diffusions, the generator of a jump process is non-local, involving integro-differential operators.
Consequently, the standard definition of ”probability current” becomes non-local, making the characterization of the ”residual drift” and the associated conservative dynamics highly non-trivial.
Specifically, explicit formulations for the housekeeping heat and the spectral interpretation of free energy dissipation for general non-local jump diffusions remain less explored.

In this paper, we are interested in the jump diffusion
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
which is given by the following SDE on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

dX_{t}=b(X_{t})dt+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}a(X_{t^{-}})\circ dB_{t}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\sigma(X_{t^{-}},z)N(dt,dz),

(1.1)

where
B_{t}
is an
n

(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
equipped with a filtration
(\mathscr{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
\circ
denotes the Stratonovich integral, the constant
\beta:=\theta^{-1}
denote the inverse temperature,
b(\cdot)
is a drift from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
a(\cdot)=(a_{ij}(\cdot))_{n\times n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the diffusion coefficient,
N(dtdz)
be an independent
n

\lambda>0
and Lévy measure
\nu(dz)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, and the function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the coefficient of jump. We present some preliminary results for jump-diffusions
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and list some necessary assumptions for the SDE (

We assume that the process admits an invariant probability measure
\mu_{ss}(dx)=\rho_{ss}(x)\,dx
, and interpret
\rho_{ss}
as a steady state (either an equilibrium state or a nonequilibrium steady state) of the dynamics.
Following the standard viewpoint in stochastic thermodynamics, we define the generalized free energy of
X_{t}
relative to the stationary measure
\mu_{ss}
by the relative entropy

F(\rho(t))=\beta^{-1}\mathcal{H}(\mu_{t}\mid\mu_{ss})=\beta^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\rho(t,x)\,dx.

In Section
3
, combining this definition with the nonlocal Fokker–Planck equation, we derive an explicit formula for the free energy dissipation rate
dF/dt
in terms of the local and nonlocal probability currents
j^{\rm loc},j^{\rm nl}
.
More precisely, we obtain a decomposition of the form

\frac{dF(\rho(t))}{dt}=Q_{hk}(t)-\beta^{-1}e_{p}(t),

where
e_{p}(t)\geq 0
is the entropy production rate, and
Q_{hk}(t)\geq 0
is the housekeeping heat associated with the maintenance of a nonequilibrium steady state.
The entropy production rate
e_{p}(t)
contains both a local diffusive contribution and a nonlocal jump contribution, each expressed in a fluctuation–dissipation form involving suitable logarithmic ratios of forward and backward transition rates.
We also prove that
F(\rho(t))
is nonincreasing in time and establish a Clausius-type inequality

\theta\frac{dS(t)}{dt}-\frac{dQ(t)}{dt}=\theta e_{p}(t)\geq 0,

where
S(t)
is the Gibbs entropy and
Q(t)
is the heat flux exchanged with the environment.
These results extend to general jump diffusions the thermodynamic structure previously known for diffusions and Markov jump processes
[
12
,
40
,
24
,
25
,
43
]
.

A second main objective of this work is to clarify the structural origin of free energy dissipation in terms of the generator of the jump diffusion.
Motivated by the diffusion case
[
31
,
11
,
43
]
, we introduce the weighted Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\rho_{ss}(x)\,dx)
and study the adjoint structure of the generator
\mathcal{L}
with respect to the inner product

\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{ss}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)\psi(x)\rho_{ss}(x)\,dx.

We show in Section 4 that
\mathcal{L}
admits a canonical decomposition into a symmetric and an anti-symmetric part in this weighted
L^{2}
–space:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{s}+\mathcal{L}_{a},

where
\mathcal{L}_{s}
is self–adjoint with respect to
\mu_{ss}
, while
\mathcal{L}_{a}
is antisymmetric with respect to
\mu_{ss}
.

Here, the anti-symmetric operator
\mathcal{L}_{a}
encapsulates the ”rotational” component of the stochastic dynamics.
In the absence of detailed balance, the stationary condition
\mathcal{L}^{*}\rho_{ss}=0
is satisfied not locally by vanishing currents, but globally by the formation of closed current loops.
The operator
\mathcal{L}_{a}
generates this divergence-free (solenoidal) flow of probability, which we term the canonical conservative dynamics, so that the free energy dissipation due to
\mathcal{L}_{a}
vanishes identically:

F^{\prime}_{a}(\rho(t))=0.

In contrast, the symmetric part
\mathcal{L}_{s}
has a natural Dirichlet form associated with it and defines a nonlocal Fisher information functional
\mathcal{I}(\rho(t))
, so that the free energy dissipation due to
\mathcal{L}_{s}
is given by

\frac{dF(\rho(t))}{dt}=F^{\prime}_{s}(\rho(t))=\mathcal{I}(\rho(t)),

so that the housekeeping heat is exactly the gap between the total entropy production and the symmetric Fisher-information dissipation.
This provides a new interpretation of non-equilibrium housekeeping heat in terms of a difference between two equilibrium-like reversible dynamics, both formally governed by symmetric generators.

The relationship between entropy production, time–reversibility, and detailed balance for general jump diffusions has been recently clarified in
[
43
]
.
Under suitable assumptions, detailed balance, reversibility with respect to
\mu_{ss}
, a certain gradient structure of the drift and jump kernel, and the vanishing of the stationary entropy production rate are all equivalent (see Theorem
2.2
).
Our analysis refines this picture by identifying, in the absence of detailed balance, a canonical decomposition of the dynamics into a non-dissipative conservative part and a purely dissipative symmetric part, and by linking the free energy dissipation to a nonlocal Fisher information associated with the symmetric generator.

To illustrate the theory, we finally consider in Section 5 two numerical examples.
The first example is a jump–diffusion with a nonequilibrium steady state, where the entropy production rate and housekeeping heat have nontrivial steady values and the free energy decays to zero while the system remains out of equilibrium.
The second example is a jump diffusion with an equilibrium steady state (Gibbs measure), for which the housekeeping heat vanishes, and the free energy dissipation is entirely given by the entropy production rate.
These examples demonstrate how our decomposition clarifies the respective roles of symmetric and anti-symmetric dynamics in the thermodynamic behavior of jump diffusions.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we recall basic facts on jump diffusions and nonlocal Fokker–Planck equations, and we collect the assumptions ensuring existence of a unique strong solution, a smooth transition density, and an invariant measure.
In Section
3
, we introduce the generalized free energy, derive its dissipation formula, and obtain the decomposition into entropy production and housekeeping heat together with the Clausius inequality.
Section 4 is devoted to the
L^{2}(\rho_{ss})
–decomposition of the generator into symmetric and anti-symmetric parts, the notion of canonical conservative dynamics, and the characterization of free energy dissipation in terms of the symmetric Dirichlet form and Fisher information.
In Section 5, we present two explicit one-dimensional examples which illustrate the theoretical results.

with its invariant measure
\mu(dx)=\rho_{ss}(x)dx
is detailed balance.

3.

The drift
b(x)=-A(x)\nabla V(x)
, and the kernel have a discrete gradient structure

k(x,y)=e^{-\beta[V(y)-V(x)]/2}s(x,y),

where
s(x,y)=s(y,x)
is a nonnegative symmetric function from
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}
.

4.

The entropy production rate
e^{ss}_{p}=0
at the stationary measure
\mu(dx):=\rho_{ss}(x)dx
, where the entropy production rate is defined as (
3.5
).

3
Free energy dissipation

In this section, we study the thermodynamics for jump diffusions based on relative entropy.
Suppose that the stationary measure
\mu_{ss}
has density
\rho_{ss}(x)=Z^{-1}e^{-V(x)}
, where
V
is the quasi-potential.
We remark that under detailed balance condition, the drift
b(x)
and the jump kernel
k(x,y)
are determined by the potential
V(x)
,
and the stationary measure
\mu_{ss}
is given by the Gibbs measure:
\mu(dx)=Z^{-1}e^{-V(x)}dx
.
Thus the quasi-potential
V(x)
can be viewed as a natural extendion of the classical potential energy in thermodynamics.

Since the diffusion
X_{t}
is moving in the potential
V(x)
, the total internal energy of
X_{t}
is given by

U(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}V(x)\rho(t,x)dx=\mathbb{E}V(X_{t}).

Moreover, Gibbs entropy of the jump diffusion
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is

S(t)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(t,x)\log\rho(t,x)dx,

where
\rho(t,\cdot)
is the probability density of
X_{t}
.
Under the detail balance condition, thermodynamics theory yields that the total internal energy of the system changes due to exchange heat with the heat bath, and the free energy decreases until it reaches to its minimum at equilibrium state (see e.g.
[
43
]
for detials).
Thus the free energy of
X_{t}
is defined as

F(\rho(t)):=U(t)-\theta S(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}[V(x)-\theta\log\rho(t,x)]\rho(t,x)dx=\beta^{-1}\mathcal{H}(\mu_{t}|\mu_{ss}).

(3.1)

where
\theta=\beta^{-1}
is the temperature of the heat bath, and
\mathcal{H}(\mu_{t}|\mu_{ss})
is the relative entropy between
\mu_{t}
and the Gibbs measure
\mu_{ss}(dx)=Z^{-1}e^{-V(x)}dx
.

Without the detial balance condition, motivated by the detail balance case, one can define the generalized free energy of
X_{t}
by the relative entropy between
\mu_{t}
and
\mu_{ss}
:

F(\rho(t))=\beta^{-1}\mathcal{H}(\mu_{t}|\mu_{ss})=\beta^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\rho(t,x)dx.

Since
\log r\leq r-1
,
r\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

F(\rho(t))=-\beta^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\log\left(\frac{\rho_{ss}(x)}{\rho(t,x)}\right)\rho(t,x)dx\geq-\int[\rho_{ss}(x)-\rho(t,x)]dx=0.

The equality holds if and only if
\rho_{ss}(x)=\rho(t,x)
.

Combining with the nonlocal Fokker-Planck equation, the free energy dissipation is given by

\displaystyle\frac{dF(\rho(t))}{dt}=

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\rho(t,x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{t}\rho(t,x)(\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)+1)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\beta^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}[-b(x)\rho(t,x)+\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\rho(t,x)]^{T}\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)dx

\displaystyle-\beta^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}[\rho(t,x)k(x,y)-\rho(t,y)k(y,x)]\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}[b(x)\rho(t,x)-\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\rho(t,x)]^{T}(\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)-\nabla\log\rho(t,x))dx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}[\rho(t,x)k(x,y)-\rho(t,y)k(y,x)]\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)\rho_{ss}(y)}{\rho(t,y)\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(j^{loc}(t,x))^{T}(\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)-\nabla\log\rho(t,x))dx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}j^{nl}(t,x,y)\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)\rho_{ss}(y)}{\rho(t,y)\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)dydx.

(3.2)

We decompose

\frac{dF(\rho(t))}{dt}=Q_{hk}(t)-\beta^{-1}e_{p}(t),

where

\displaystyle Q_{hk}(t):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(b(x)\rho(t,x)-\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\rho(t,x))^{T}(\beta A^{-1}(x)b(x)-\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x))dx

(3.3)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}[\rho(t,x)k(x,y)-\rho(t,y)k(y,x)]\log\left(\frac{\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)}\right)dydx

(3.4)

is the housekeeping heat, and the entropy production rate is given by

\displaystyle e_{p}(t):=

\displaystyle\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(j^{loc}(t,x))^{T}A^{-1}(x)j^{loc}(t,x)}{\rho(t,x)}dx

(3.5)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}[\rho(t,x)k(x,y)-\rho(t,y)k(y,x)]\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)k(x,y)}{\rho(t,y)k(y,x)}\right)dydx.

(3.6)

We remark that the condition (
2.2
) is proposed to ensure that the entropy production rate is well-defined.

Note that under the detail balance condition, the dissipation rate of free energy (
3.1
) can be determined by the variation of Gibbs entropy and the change of internal energy.
By the variation of Gibbs entropy in the system is

\displaystyle\frac{dS(t)}{dt}=

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(t,x)\log\rho(t,x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}j^{loc}(t,x)\nabla\log\rho(t,x)dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}j^{nl}(t,x,y)\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho(t,y)}\right)dydx.

(3.7)

The change of internal energy is

\displaystyle\frac{dU(t)}{dt}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}V(x)\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\rho(t,x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla V(x)\cdot j^{loc}(t,x)dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}j^{nl}(t,x,y)[V(x)-V(y)]dydx.

(3.8)

Therefore, combining (
3
) and (
3
), the free energy dissipation under detailed balance condition is given by the entropy production rate alone:

\displaystyle\frac{dF(\rho(t))}{dt}=

\displaystyle\frac{dU(t)}{dt}-\theta\frac{dS(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(j^{loc}(t,x))^{T}A^{-1}(x)j^{loc}(t,x)}{\rho(t,x)}dx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}j^{nl}(t,x,y)\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)k(x,y)}{\rho(t,y)k(y,x)}\right)dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-e_{p}(t)\leq 0.

Next, we give the physical interpretation of the housekeeping heat
Q_{hk}(t)
.
Without the detial bablance condition, taking the quasi-potential
V(x)=-\beta^{-1}\log\rho_{ss}(x)
, the vector field can be decomposed as

A^{-1}(x)b(x)=-\nabla V(x)+A^{-1}(x)b(x)+\nabla V(x)=:-\nabla V(x)+f^{loc}(x)

where
-\nabla V
is the internal force from the potential
V
, and the divergence-free vector field
\beta f^{loc}=\beta A^{-1}(x)b(x)-\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)
is the external force from outside the system. This decomposition for diffusion processes was well studied by
[
2
,
31
]
.
Moreover, motivated by the case of the discrete jump processes in
[
25
]
, we also decompose the jump kernel as

\displaystyle\frac{k(x,y)}{k(y,x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)}\frac{\rho_{ss}(y)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\log\frac{\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)}+\log\frac{\rho_{ss}(y)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\beta\left(\beta^{-1}\log\frac{\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)}+V(x)-V(y)\right)

\displaystyle:=e^{\beta(f^{nl}(x,y)+V(x)-V(y))}

where
V(x)-V(y)
is the difference in potential between each
x
and
y
, and

f^{nl}(x,y)=-f^{nl}(y,x)=\beta^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)}\right)

(3.9)

is the external driving to the nonlocal transitions between each
x
and
y
.

Recall that the work
\delta W=\text{force}\times\delta x
, where
x
is the position,
\delta
is the variation or derivative in general senses. Then based on the probability currents
j^{loc}(t,x)
, and
j^{nl}(t,x,y)
, the change of total work
dW(t)/dt
done by the external force
f^{loc}(x)
and external driving
f^{\text{nl}}(x,y)
is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{dW(t)}{dt}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f^{loc}(x)\cdot j^{loc}(t,x)dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{x\}}j^{nl}(t,x,y)f^{nl}(x,y)dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\beta^{-1}(j^{loc}(t,x))^{T}(\beta A^{-1}(x)b(x)-\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x))dx

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{x\}}j^{nl}(t,x,y)\beta^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)}\right)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{hk}(t)

Remark 3.1
.

The identity
dW(t)/dt=Q_{hk}(t)
establishes that the housekeeping heat corresponds to the power input by the external forces in the general non-equilibrium setting. When external forces are absent
f^{loc}=0,f^{nl}=0
, the system satisfies detailed balance with respect to the Gibbs measure. In this case,
dW/dt=0
implies
Q_{hk}(t)=0
, and the free energy dissipation reduces to
dF/dt=-\beta^{-1}e_{p}(t)
, recovering the classical result for general Langevin dynamics
[
43
]
.

In the next theorem, we show that the housekeeping heat
Q_{hk}(t)
, entropy production rate
e_{p}(t)
are always nonnegative, and the free energy dissipation
dF(\rho(t))/dt
is always nonpositive.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every
t>0
, the housekeeping heat
Q_{hk}(t)
and entropy production rate
e_{p}(t)
satisfy

Q_{hk}(t)\geq 0,\quad e_{p}(t)\geq 0.

Moreover, the generalized free energy satisfies

\frac{dF(\rho(t))}{dt}\leq 0.

Proof:

For the housekeeping heat
Q_{hk}(t)
, since
\log r\leq r-1
, we have

\displaystyle Q_{hk}(t)\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\beta A^{-1}(x)b(x)-\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x))\beta^{-1}A(x)(\beta A^{-1}(x)b(x)-\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x))\rho(t,x)dx

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)-\nabla\log\rho(t,x))\beta^{-1}A(x)(\beta A^{-1}(x)b(x)-\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x))\rho(t,x)dx

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho(t,x)k(x,y)\left(\frac{\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)}-1\right)dydx

Using integration by parts, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)-\nabla\log\rho(t,x))\beta^{-1}A(x)(\beta A^{-1}(x)b(x)-\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x))\rho(t,x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)[b(x)\rho_{ss}(x)-\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\rho_{ss}(x)]\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla(b(x)\rho_{ss}(x)-\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\rho_{ss}(x))\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}dx,

For the nonlocal term, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho(t,x)k(x,y)\left(\frac{\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)}-1\right)dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)dydx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho(t,x)k(x,y)dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)-\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)dy\right)dx.

Recall that the stationary density satisfies the stationary Fokker-Planck equation, i.e.

\nabla(b(x)\rho_{ss}(x)-A(x)\nabla\rho_{ss}(x))+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)-\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)dy=0.

(3.10)

Combining above local and nonlocal terms, if follows that

\displaystyle Q_{hk}(t)\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(A^{-1}(x)b(x)-\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x))\beta^{-1}A(x)(\beta A^{-1}(x)b(x)-\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x))\rho(t,x)dx\geq 0.

For the entropy production rate
e_{p}(t)
, note that
j^{nl}(t,x,y)=\rho(t,x)k(x,y)-\rho(t,y)k(y,x)
and
\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)k(x,y)}{\rho(t,y)k(y,x)}\right)
are both positive or negative. Thus

e_{p}(t)=\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(j^{loc}(t,x))^{T}A^{-1}(x)j^{loc}(t,x)}{\rho(t,x)}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}j^{nl}(t,x,y)\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)k(x,y)}{\rho(t,y)k(y,x)}\right)dydx\geq 0.

Moreover, this equality holds if and only if
j^{nl}(t,x,y)=\rho(t,x)k(x,y)-\rho(t,y)k(y,x)=0
for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
t\geq 0
, and
j^{loc}(t,x)=0
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
t\geq 0
.

For the free energy dissipation, using the stationary Fokker-Planck equation (
3.10
) again, we have

\displaystyle\frac{dF(t)}{dt}

(3.11)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)-\nabla\log\rho(t,x))^{T}\beta^{-1}A(x)(\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)-\nabla\log\rho(t,x))\rho(t,x)dx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}[\rho(t,x)k(x,y)-\rho(t,y)k(y,x)]\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)k(x,y)}{\rho(t,y)k(y,x)}\right)dydx

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}j^{nl}(t,x,y)\log\left(\frac{k(x,y)\rho_{ss}(y)}{k(y,x)\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)dydx.

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\nabla\log\rho(t,x)-\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x))^{T}\beta A(x)(\nabla\log\rho(t,x)-\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x))\rho(t,x)dx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}j^{nl}(t,x,y)\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)k(x,y)}{\rho(t,y)k(y,x)}\right)dydx

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}j^{nl}(t,x,y)\left[\log\left(\frac{k(x,y)\rho_{ss}(y)}{k(y,x)\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)+\log\left(\frac{k(y,x)\rho_{ss}(x)}{k(x,y)\rho_{ss}(y)}\right)\right]dydx.

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0.

(3.12)

Here, we use the fact that

\log\left(\frac{k(x,y)\rho_{ss}(y)}{k(y,x)\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)+\log\left(\frac{k(y,x)\rho_{ss}(x)}{k(x,y)\rho_{ss}(y)}\right)=0.

This implies the dissipative property of the free energy.

\blacksquare

Our result reveals a fundamental thermodynamic structure underlying general jump-diffusion processes,
extending the classical understanding of free energy dissipation in Langevin systems to a broader class of stochastic dynamics with jumps.
In next remark, we discuss the connection between our decomposition of free energy dissipation and the adiabatic/non-adiabatic decomposition in stochastic thermodynamics.

Remark 3.2
.

The decomposition of the free energy dissipation rate

-\frac{dF(\rho(t))}{dt}=\beta^{-1}e_{p}(t)-Q_{hk}(t)

(3.13)

can be structurally aligned with the canonical decomposition of entropy production in stochastic thermodynamics, commonly referred to as the adiabatic and non-adiabatic split
[
12
,
40
]
.
Multiplying (
3.13
) by
\beta
, we obtain the Clausius-type inequality

e_{p}(t)=\beta Q_{hk}(t)+\left(-\beta\frac{dF(\rho(t))}{dt}\right)\geq 0.

(3.14)

Here, the total entropy production rate
e_{p}(t)
corresponds to the variation of the total entropy
\dot{S}_{tot}
. The term associated with the free energy decay,
-\beta\dot{F}(t)
, represents the
non-adiabatic entropy production rate
(often denoted as
\dot{S}_{na}
), which quantifies the dissipation due to the relaxation of the system towards its steady state
\rho_{ss}
.
Consequently, the term
\beta Q_{hk}(t)
corresponds to the
adiabatic entropy production rate
(denoted as
\dot{S}_{ad}
), which characterizes the dissipation required to maintain the non-equilibrium steady state against the non-conservative forces.
Specifically,
Q_{hk}(t)
provides the explicit expression for the housekeeping heat involved in the nonlocal transport of probability mass.

4
A decomposition of diffusion process

The analysis in the previous section has shown that, compared to a Langevin system, the free energy dissipation rate of system X under external forcing includes not only the entropy production rate but also an additional housekeeping heat term. This leads us to consider whether, within the overall dynamics of X, there exists a subsystem analogous to a Langevin system, whose free energy dissipation is entirely characterized by a certain form of entropy production, as in the Langevin case.

It is known that a Langevin system with a symmetric generator
\mathcal{G}
in
D(\mathcal{G})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mu)
, satisfies detailed balance and time-reversal symmetry at its stationary measure
\mu

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\mathcal{G}g(x)\mu(dx)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(x)\mathcal{G}f(x)\mu(dx).

Building on this structure, we further define the weighted Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\rho_{ss}(x)dx)
equipped with the inner product

\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)\psi(x)\rho_{ss}(x)dx,\quad\forall\varphi,\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

which is central to analyzing the generator’s adjoint structure under the stationary measure. This inner product induces the
\rho_{ss}
-weighted adjoint
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
of the generator
\mathcal{L}
, defined by the relation:

\langle\mathcal{L}\varphi,\psi\rangle=\langle\varphi,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\psi\rangle.

Therefore, our objective is to identify a component within
\mathcal{L}
that resembles a Langevin system—specifically, its symmetric part. This leads us to the following theorem:

Theorem 4.1
.

The generator of
X_{t}
can decompose as a symmetric operator and an anti-symmetric operator, that is,
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{s}+\mathcal{L}_{a}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{s}\varphi=

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)+\beta^{-1}\nabla\cdot(A(x)\nabla\varphi(x))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\Big(\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)\Big)\left(\frac{k(x,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}+\frac{k(y,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\rho_{ss}(y)dy,

(4.1)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}\varphi=

\displaystyle\Big[b(x)-\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)\Big]\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\Big(\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)\Big)\left(\frac{k(x,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}-\frac{k(y,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\rho_{ss}(y)dy.

(4.2)

Proof:

Recall that
\mathcal{L}\varphi(x)=\mathcal{L}_{loc}\varphi(x)+\mathcal{L}_{nl}\varphi(x)
and
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\varphi(x)=\mathcal{L}^{\ast}_{loc}\varphi(x)+\mathcal{L}^{\ast}_{nl}\varphi(x)=-j^{loc}(t,x)-\int j^{nl}(t,x,y)
, where
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}
is the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{L}
under usual inner product, now we compute the local part of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}_{loc}\varphi,\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big\{b(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)+\beta^{-1}\nabla\cdot(A(x)\nabla\varphi(x))\Big\}\rho_{ss}(x)\psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big\{\nabla\cdot\Big[-b(x)\rho_{ss}(x)\psi(x)+\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla(\rho_{ss}(x)\psi(x))\Big]\Big\}\varphi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi,\rho_{ss}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{*}(\rho_{ss}\psi)\rangle:=\langle\varphi,\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{\dagger}\psi\rangle.

And the non-local part

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}_{nl}\varphi,\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi(x)\rho_{ss}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}[\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)]k(x,y)dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\psi(x)\rho_{ss}(x)\varphi(y)k(x,y)dydx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\psi(x)\rho_{ss}(x)\varphi(x)k(x,y)dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(y)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{y\}}\psi(x)\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)dxdy-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)\rho_{ss}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\psi(x)k(x,y)dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)\rho_{ss}(x)\rho_{ss}^{-1}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\psi(y)\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)dydx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(x)\rho_{ss}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\psi(x)k(x,y)dydx

\displaystyle=\left\langle\varphi,\rho_{ss}^{-1}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\Big[\rho_{ss}(y)\psi(y)k(y,x)-\rho_{ss}(x)\psi(x)k(x,y)\Big]dy\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi,\rho_{ss}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{nl}^{*}(\rho_{ss}\psi)\rangle:=\langle\varphi,\mathcal{L}_{nl}^{\dagger}\psi\rangle

So we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\varphi(x)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{\dagger}\varphi(x)+\mathcal{L}_{nl}^{\dagger}\varphi(x)=\rho_{ss}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{loc}^{*}(\rho_{ss}\varphi)+\rho_{ss}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{nl}^{*}(\rho_{ss}\varphi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{ss}^{-1}(x)\nabla\cdot\Big[-b(x)\rho_{ss}(x)\varphi(x)+\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla(\rho_{ss}(x)\varphi(x))\Big]

\displaystyle+\rho_{ss}^{-1}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\Big[\rho_{ss}(y)\varphi(y)k(y,x)-\rho_{ss}(x)\varphi(x)k(x,y)\Big]dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{ss}^{-1}\mathcal{L}^{*}(\rho_{ss}\varphi).

The symmetric operator and the anti-symmetric operator are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{s}\varphi=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathcal{L}\varphi+\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\varphi\right],\quad\mathcal{L}_{a}\varphi=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathcal{L}\varphi-\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\varphi\right].

Then direct calculation shows that the symmetric operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
is given by (
4.1
), and the anti-symmetric operator
\mathcal{L}_{a}
is given by (
4.1
).

\blacksquare

Using the decomposition of the generator, we can express the free energy dissipation rate as

\displaystyle\frac{dF(\rho(t))}{dt}=

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}^{\ast}\rho(t,x)\left(\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)+1\right)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big[(\mathcal{L}_{a})^{*}\rho(t,x)+(\mathcal{L}_{s})^{*}\rho(t,x)\big]\left(\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)+1\right)dx

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle F^{\prime}_{a}(\rho(t))+F^{\prime}_{s}(\rho(t)).

In what follows, we will further compute the respective contributions of these two components to the overall dissipation and examine their relationship with both the entropy production rate and the housekeeping heat.

4.1
Canonical conservative dynamics

For the anti-symmetric operator
\mathcal{L}_{a}
, we introduce

v_{a}(x)=\Big[b(x)-\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)\Big],\quad k_{a}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{k(x,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}-\frac{k(y,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\rho_{ss}(y),\ \forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}\varphi:=

\displaystyle v_{a}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\Big(\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)\Big)k_{a}(x,y)dy,

and the Fokker-Planck (adjoint) operator associated with
\mathcal{L}_{a}
is

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{a})^{*}\varphi=

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(v_{a}(x)\varphi(x))+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\Big[\varphi(y)k_{a}(y,x)-\varphi(x)k_{a}(x,y)\Big]dy.

The dynamics generated by
\mathcal{L}_{a}
is a canonical conservative dynamics

\partial_{t}\rho_{a}(t,x)=(\mathcal{L}_{a})^{*}\rho_{a}(t,x).

The associated local and nonlocal probability current are given by

\displaystyle j^{loc}_{a}(t,x)=-v_{a}(x)\rho(t,x),

and

\displaystyle j^{nl}_{a}(t,x,y)=

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)k_{a}(x,y)-\rho(t,y)k_{a}(y,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{k(x,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}-\frac{k(y,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\Big(\rho_{ss}(y)\rho(t,x)+\rho_{ss}(x)\rho(t,y)\Big).

The canonical conservative dynamics (current) preserves the generalized free energy, we will show this property in the following lemma and proposition.

Lemma 4.1
.

The Fokker-Planck operator satisfies
(\mathcal{L}_{a})^{*}\rho_{ss}(x)=0
.

Moreover, the drift
v_{a}
satisfies

\nabla\cdot(v_{a}(x)\rho_{ss}(x))=(\nabla v_{a}(x)+v_{a}(x)\log\rho_{ss}(x))\rho_{ss}(x)=0

(4.3)

and
k_{a}(x,y)
vanishes upon integration over
y
i.e.

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}k_{a}(x,y)dy=0,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(4.4)

Proof:

Note that
\nabla j^{loc}_{ss}(x)=\nabla[(b(x)-\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x))\rho_{ss}(x)]=0
, and

\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)\cdot\rho_{ss}(x)-\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\rho_{ss}(x)=0.

It follows that

\nabla[v_{a}(x)\rho_{ss}(x)]=\nabla[b(x)\rho_{ss}(x)]-\beta^{-1}\nabla[A(x)\nabla\rho_{ss}(x)]=0.

For the nonlocal part, by the definition of
k_{a}(x,y)
, we have

\rho_{ss}(x)k_{a}(x,y)-\rho_{ss}(y)k_{a}(y,x)=\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)-\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x),\quad\forall x\neq y

Then we have
(\mathcal{L}_{a})^{*}\rho_{ss}(x)=0
.

Since
\rho_{ss}
satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}k(x,y)\rho_{ss}(x)-k(y,x)\rho_{ss}(y)dy=0,

combining with the definition of
k_{a}(x,y)
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho_{ss}(x)k_{a}(x,y)dy=0.

(4.5)

Then we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho_{ss}(y)k_{a}(y,x)dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho_{ss}(x)k_{a}(x,y)dy=\rho_{ss}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}k_{a}(x,y)dy=0

(4.6)

Since the density function is positive, we obtain (
4.4
).

\blacksquare

Proposition 4.2
.

The free energy dissipation associated with the anti-symmetric part is zero, i.e.

F^{\prime}_{a}(\rho(t))=\beta^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\ast}\rho(t,x)\left(\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)+1\right)dx=0.

(4.7)

Proof:

The free energy dissipation is

\displaystyle F^{\prime}_{a}(\rho(t,x))=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(t,x)v_{a}(x)\cdot(\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)-\nabla\log\rho(t,x))dx

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}(\rho(t,y)k_{a}(y,x)-\rho(t,x)k_{a}(x,y))\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}.

For
I_{1}
, by (
4.3
), we have

\displaystyle I_{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(t,x)v_{a}(x)\cdot(\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)-\nabla\log\rho(t,x))dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big(\nabla\cdot v_{a}(x)+v_{a}(x)\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)\Big)\rho(t,x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

For
I_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle I_{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho(t,x)k_{a}(x,y)\left[\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}\right)-\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\right]dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho_{ss}(x)k_{a}(x,y)\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\left[\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}\right)-\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\right]dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho_{ss}(x)k_{a}(x,y)\Bigg(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\left[\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}\right)-\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho(t,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}\left[\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)-\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}\right)\right]\Bigg)dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho_{ss}(x)k_{a}(x,y)\left[\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}+\frac{\rho(t,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}\right]\left[\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}\right)-\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\right]dydx.

By (
4.4
), we also have

\displaystyle I_{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho(t,y)k_{a}(y,x)\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho_{ss}(y)k_{a}(y,x)\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\left[\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)-\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}\right)\right]dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}\rho_{ss}(x)k_{a}(x,y)\left[\frac{\rho(t,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}+\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right]\left[\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)-\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}\right)\right]dydx.

(4.8)

Combining with above two equalities, we have
I_{2}=-I_{2}
, i.e.
I_{2}=0
. Thus we finally obtain (
4.7
).

\blacksquare

The vanishing of the free energy dissipation,
F^{\prime}_{a}(\rho)=0
, highlights the physical role of the anti-symmetric generator
\mathcal{L}_{a}
.
The operator
\mathcal{L}_{a}
drives the system in a ceaseless motion that preserves the stationary measure
\rho_{ss}
dynamically,
so that the free energy remains constant due to the associated canonical conservative flow.
This property analogous to the inertial motion in Hamiltonian mechanics, thereby creating circulation without dissipation.

Remark 4.1
.

With the nonequilibrium steady state
\rho_{ss}
, stationarity is maintained not by the detial balance, but by the condition that the net flux along any closed cycle sums to zero.
The vector field
v_{a}(x)
drives the probability mass transport along the level sets of the stationary density
\rho_{ss}
,
The jump kernel
k_{a}(x,y)
explicitly constructs these non-vanishing, divergence-free probability circulations (loops like
x\to y\to z\to x
).
Thus the canonical conservative flow creat a circulation flow that contributes to the breaking of detailed balance without altering the stationary denisty
\rho_{ss}
itself.

4.2
Symmetric reversible diffusion

The symmetric operator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
can be serveed as a infinitesimal generator for a jump-diffusion process
X^{sym}_{t}
, which is governed by the SDE

dX^{sym}_{t}=\Big[\beta^{-1}A(X^{sym}_{t})\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(X^{sym}_{t})+\beta^{-1}\nabla\cdot A(X^{sym}_{t})\Big]dt+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}a(X^{sym}_{t})dB_{t}+dJ_{t}.

(4.9)

where the jump part
J_{t}
has the jump kernel

k_{s}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{k(x,y)}{\rho_{ss}(y)}+\frac{k(y,x)}{\rho_{ss}(x)}\right)\rho_{ss}(y).

Since the generator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
is a self-adjoint operator with respect to the weight
L^{2}
sapce
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\rho_{ss}(x)dx)
,
by
[
43
, Theorem 5.1]
, the jump-diffusion process
X^{sym}_{t}
is time-reversible with the stationary density
\rho_{ss}
, and satisfies the detailed balance condition at the stationary denisty
\rho_{ss}(x)
, i.e.

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\log\rho_{ss}(x)\cdot\rho_{ss}(x)-\beta^{-1}A(x)\nabla\rho_{ss}(x)=0,

\displaystyle k_{s}(x,y)\rho_{ss}(x)-k_{s}(y,x)\rho_{ss}(y)=0.

Using the symmetric part
\mathcal{L}_{s}
, we can define a symmetric reversible jump diffusion by using the following Dirichlet form associated with
\mathcal{L}_{s}
.

Definition 4.1
.

We define the Dirichlet form of the symmetric generator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
as

\mathcal{E}(f,g):=-\langle f,\mathcal{L}_{s}g\rangle_{ss}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)(\mathcal{L}_{s}g)(x)\rho_{ss}(x)\,dx,\quad\forall f,g\in L^{2}(\rho_{ss}).

Equivalently,

\mathcal{E}(f,g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\nabla f(x))^{T}\nabla g(x)\rho_{ss}(x)dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y))K_{s}(x,y)dydx.

Now we define the Fisher information of the jump diffusions
X_{t}
via the Dirichlet form of the symmetric generator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
f(t):=d\mu(t)/d\mu_{ss}=\rho/\rho_{ss}
be the relative density. We define the Fisher information of the jump diffusions
X_{t}
as

\mathcal{I}[\rho(t)]:=\mathcal{E}(f(t),\log f(t))=-\langle f(t),\mathcal{L}_{s}\log f(t)\rangle_{ss}.

Note that the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}[\rho]
induced by
\mathcal{L}_{s}
admits a local–nonlocal decomposition

\mathcal{I}[\rho]=\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{loc}}[\rho]+\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{nl}}[\rho],

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is the diffusion part and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{nl}}
is a nonlocal carré du champ built from the symmetric jump kernel.

For the local part, since

\mathcal{L}^{loc}_{s}\log f=\rho_{ss}^{-1}\nabla\cdot(\rho_{ss}A\nabla\log f)
, after integration by parts, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rm loc}[\rho(t)]=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f\nabla\cdot(\rho_{ss}A\nabla\log f)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\nabla f)^{T}\rho_{ss}A\nabla\log f\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f\rho_{ss}(\nabla\log f)^{T}A\nabla\log fdx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}[(\nabla\log f(x))^{T}A(x)\,\nabla\log f(x)]\rho(x)dx.

For the nonlocal part,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\rm nl}[\rho(t)]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}K_{s}(x,y)\big(\log f(x)-\log f(y)\big)\big(f(x)-f(y)\big)dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{x\}}K_{s}(x,y)\,L\big(f(x),f(y)\big)\,\big(\log f(x)-\log f(y)\big)^{2}\,dydx.

where

K_{s}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\rho_{ss}(x)k(x,y)+\rho_{ss}(y)k(y,x)\Big),

and

L(u,v):=\frac{u-v}{\log u-\log v}

is the logarithmic mean.
This is the nonlocal analogue of a “Carré du champ” form:
it represents a nonlocal squared gradient of
\log f
weighted by the logarithmic mean.
The appearance of the logarithmic mean
L(u,v)
is fundamental here. It serves as the discrete/nonlocal analog of the density weight
\rho(x)
in the classical Fisher information. The identity
(u-v)(\log u-\log v)=L(u,v)(\log u-\log v)^{2}
allows us to interpret the entropy production as a squared nonlocal gradient norm weighted by
L(\rho(x),\rho(y))
, ensuring that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{nl}}
converges to the standard Fisher information in the diffusion limit.

By Proposition
4.2
, the free energy dissipation associated with
\mathcal{L}_{a}
is
0
, and all free energy dissipation of the original jump diffusion is carried by
\mathcal{L}_{s}
.
Using the definition of the Fisher information, we have the following result.

Proposition 4.3
.

The free energy dissipation of the jump-diffusion process
X_{t}
is determined solely by the symmetric part
\mathcal{L}_{s}
of the generator, i.e.

\frac{d}{dt}F[\rho(t)]=\frac{d}{dt}F_{s}[\rho(t)]=\langle f_{t},L_{s}\log f_{t}\rangle_{ss}=-\mathcal{I}[\rho(t)]\leq 0.

where
F_{s}[\rho(t)]
is the free energy functional associated with
\mathcal{L}_{s}
, and
\mathcal{I}
is the Fisher information of
X_{t}
.

The theoretical significance of the nonlocal Fisher information
\mathcal{I}[\rho]
lies in its connection to the exponential convergence of the system. specifically,
if the symmetric generator
\mathcal{L}_{s}
satisfies a Modified Log-Sobolev Inequality
[
41
,
7
]
:

F(\rho(t))=\mathcal{H}(\mu|\mu_{ss})\leq C_{I}\mathcal{I}(\rho(t)),

(4.10)

for some constant
C_{I}>0
,
then the free energy decays exponentially:

F(t)\leq F(0)e^{-\frac{t}{\beta C_{I}}}.

While establishing the precise conditions for (
4.10
) for general Lévy driven SDEs is a complex analytical problem depending on the tail behavior of the Lévy measure
[
16
]
,
the decomposition
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{s}+\mathcal{L}_{a}
clarifies that the relaxation rate is determined solely by the spectral properties of the symmetric part
\mathcal{L}_{s}
.

5.2
SDE with equilibrium steady state

Consider the following one-dimensional jump-diffusion process:

dY_{t}=-Y_{t}dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t}+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}(z-Y_{t})N(dt,dz),

(5.3)

where
B_{t}
is a one-dimensional standard Brownian motion and
N_{t}
is the Poisson random measure
dt\nu(dz)=\exp(-\frac{1}{2}z^{2})dzdt
.

The explicit expression for the density function of this process can be intuitively explained by its dynamics: at any time
t
, the distribution of the state
Y_{t}
is a mixture distribution. If no jump occurs in the interval
[0,t]
(with probability
e^{-\sqrt{2\pi}t}
), then
Y_{t}
follows the distribution of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process; if at least one jump occurs, then due to the reset property of the jumps, the distribution of
Y_{t}
is exactly the stationary distribution (the Gibbs measure). Therefore, let
Y_{0}=0
, we can use the combination of the jump part and the Gaussian part as its characteristic function, and directly obtain its density function using the inversion formula

\rho(t,y)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}cos(y\xi)\left[e^{-\sqrt{2\pi}t}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-2t})\xi^{2}\right)+(1-e^{-\sqrt{2\pi}t})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\xi^{2}\right)\right]d\xi.

And the invariant measure of the jump process
Y_{t}
is a Gibbs distribution
\mu(dy)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}y^{2})dy
.

The entropy production rate of system
Y_{t}
is given by

e_{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(t,y)\left(x+\frac{\rho^{\prime}(t,y)}{\rho(t,y)}\right)^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{y\}}\rho(t,y)e^{-\frac{1}{2}z^{2}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\rho(t,y)}{\rho(t,z)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}(y^{2}-z^{2})\right]dzdy.

Y_{t}
is an equilibrium state, therefore the free energy dissipation rate of
Y_{t}
is entirely given by its entropy production rate
