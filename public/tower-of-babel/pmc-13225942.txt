Clinical Outcomes and Use of Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator in Ischemic Heart Failure Patients with Reduced Ejection Fraction: A Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: Kyung Hoon Cho, Ki Hong Lee, Yong-Kyu Lee, Seok Oh, Yongwhan Lim, Joon Ho Ahn, Seung Hun Lee, Dae Young Hyun, Min Chul Kim, Doo Sun Sim, Young Joon Hong, Ju Han Kim, Youngkeun Ahn, Jang Hoon Lee, Joo-Yong Hahn, Yu-Ri Kim, Nam Sik Yoon, Hyung Wook Park, Weon Kim, Myung Ho Jeong, on behalf of the Korea Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry (KAMIR)-National Institutes of Health (NIH)-Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillators (ICD) Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLimited data exist regarding the real-world practices and clinical outcomes in patients with ischemic heart failure with reduced left ventricular ejection fractions (LVEFs). Using nationwide registry data from South Korea, we aimed to investigate long-term outcomes and clinical practices, especially implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICDs) implantation, in patients with reduced LVEFs at least 40 days after acute myocardial infarction (AMI). Of 13,056 patients with AMI between 2011 and 2015, we analyzed 350 (median age, 66 years [interquartile range, 56–75]) who had LVEFs <40% on follow-up transthoracic echocardiogram 40 days after the index event. The primary outcome was cardiac-cause mortality at 3 years. Secondary outcomes comprised major cardiovascular events as well as outcomes defined by the use of ICDs, cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillators (CRT-Ds), and electrophysiology studies. Among 350 patients, 39 (11.1%) died from cardiac causes during 3 years of follow-up. Eleven (3.1%) were hospitalized for ventricular tachycardia. The rate of ICD or CRT-D implantation up to 3 years was 5.7% (20/350). Cox time-to-event analysis revealed older age, LVEF <30%, diabetes mellitus, and previous MI or revascularization as positively associated with cardiac death, whereas the use of statins and body weight <67 kg were negatively associated. This nationwide Korean registry demonstrated that only 5.7% of patients who had reduced LVEFs after 40 days of AMI underwent ICD implantations over 3 years. Considering the high mortality, concerted efforts are needed to improve clinical outcomes for patients who may have been candidates for ICD implantation.Keywords: Defibrillators, Implantable; Myocardial Infarction; Percutaneous Coronary Intervention

INTRODUCTION

Patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) frequently present with acute decompensated heart failure or develop chronic heart failure after discharge.1,2,3,4,5 Even though the incidence of heart failure after AMI has declined, patients with heart failure continue to have considerably high mortality rates.6 Furthermore, heart failure is independently associated with poor clinical outcomes after AMI.7 Both patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fractions (LVEFs) and those with preserved LVEFs experience high mortality rates.8,9 It has been demonstrated that an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) reduces the risk of sudden cardiac death in selected patients with reduced LVEFs after MI.10,11 Recent guidelines recommend ICD implantation in patients with symptomatic heart failure of ischemic origin and a LVEF ≤35% despite ≥3 months of optimal medical therapy, as a class I recommendation.12,13

However, limited data exist regarding real-world clinical practice and long-term outcomes in patients with heart failure with reduced LVEFs <40% after AMI, especially in South Korea.14,15,16 Therefore, we aimed to investigate long-term outcomes and clinical practices, especially implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICDs) implantation, in Korean AMI patients with reduced LVEFs on at least 40 days follow-up transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE), drawing from the Korea Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry (KAMIR)–National Institutes of Health (NIH) database.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Patients and treatment

The KAMIR-NIH is a nationwide, prospective, multicenter registry that was designed to investigate real-world clinical practices and long-term outcomes in AMI patients treated from November 2011 through December 2015. The 20 tertiary university hospitals participating in the registry had onsite facilities for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and cardiac surgery, and they consecutively enrolled patients (Supplementary Methods). Data on baseline clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, and clinical outcomes were collected online by a clinical research coordinator. Among 13,056 patients diagnosed with AMI between 2011 and 2015, a subset of 350 patients (median age, 66 years [IQR, 56–75]) was identified for analysis. These patients exhibited LVEF <40% on follow-up TTEs performed 40 days after the index events, with exam days showing a median of 310 days (IQR, 212–367) and LVEF values a median of 33.0% (IQR, 28.0–37.0) (Fig. 1). Patients received loading doses of aspirin (300 mg) and a P2Y12 inhibitor (clopidogrel, 600 mg; ticagrelor, 180 mg; or prasugrel, 60 mg) before the PCI, followed by maintenance doses of these agents for at least 1 year. PCI was performed according to standard guidelines. The use of adjunctive drugs, thrombus aspiration, and intravascular imaging during PCI was left to the physician’s discretion. Medications, including renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system blockers, beta-blockers, and statins, were prescribed according to standard guidelines. The completeness of follow-up at 3 years (≥1,005 days) was 92.9% (325/350). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of each participating center (IRB No: CNUH-2023-020). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The anonymized data that support the results of this study can be made available upon reasonable request.

Study flowchart. The study sample was derived from the nationwide, prospective KAMIR-NIH (Korea Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry–National Institutes of Health). Out of 13,189 patients, patients with no follow-up TTE between 40 days and 1 year or with LVEF ≥40% on follow-up TTE were excluded. Finally, 350 patients who had LVEF <40% on follow-up TTE were analyzed. 2. Outcomes and definitions

The primary outcome measure was cardiac death at 3 years. The secondary outcome measures included sudden cardiac death, non-cardiac death, PCI, coronary artery bypass grafting, cerebrovascular accident, rehospitalization due to heart failure, rehospitalization due to ventricular tachycardia, ICD implantation, cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillator (CRT-D) implantation, and electrophysiologic study with radiofrequency ablation for ventricular arrhythmia at 3 years. Clinical events were recorded through chart review, interviews, or phone calls by trained research coordinators and confirmed by the principal investigator of each center. All data were collected in a web-based case report form. AMI diagnoses were adjudicated based on the universal definition of AMI. Death from a cardiac cause was defined as any death not definitively attributable to a non-cardiac cause. Successful PCI was defined as residual stenosis <30% with final TIMI (Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction) flow grade ≥2.

3. Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using R, version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous and categorical variables are expressed as mean±standard deviation or median (IQR) and the number of cases (percentage), respectively. Correlates of the primary endpoint were assessed using a Cox time-to-event multivariable regression analysis with backward elimination selection. All potential confounders were included as follows: age, sex, body weight <67 kg, symptom-to-door time >12 hours, LVEF <30%, Killip class at presentation>III, systolic blood pressure <100 mmHg, heart rate >100 beats/min, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, previous MI or revascularization, previous heart failure, previous cerebrovascular accident, family history of premature coronary artery disease, current smoking status, ST-elevation MI diagnosis, anemia, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio >3, creatinine clearance <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, peak troponin I >50 ng/mL, total cholesterol >240 mg/dL, successful PCI, multivessel disease, and use of beta-blockers, statins, or renin–angiotensin–aldosterone blockers. The following variables with missing values were included in the multivariable analysis: body weight (n=10), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (n=3), anemia (n=2), creatinine clearance (n=1), troponin I (n=36), total cholesterol (n=13), successful PCI (n=34), and multivessel disease (n=8). Missing values were managed using a multiple imputation method. The assumption of proportionality was assessed by log-minus-log plots. Collinearity diagnostics were performed using variance inflation factor and eigensystem analysis.

RESULTS

1. Baseline clinical and procedural characteristics

Of 13,189 patients, 3,919 (29.7%) underwent follow-up TTEs between 40 days and 1 year after the index hospitalization (Fig. 1). Among these, 350 had LVEFs <40% on follow-up TTEs, with a median follow-up LVEF of 33.0% (IQR, 28.0–37.0). Fig. 2 shows the distribution of patients stratified by follow-up LVEF. The number of patients stratified by LVEF on baseline echocardiogram are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. Of the 350 patients, 210 had follow-up LVEFs <35%. The median LVEF on baseline TTEs was 35.3% (IQR, 30.0–42.0) (Table 1). A history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, and cerebrovascular accidents was noted in 54.9%, 38.6%, 6.0%, and 8.0% of patients, respectively, and 17.7% of patients had a history of previous MI or myocardial revascularization. The median hemoglobin, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and total cholesterol values were 13.8 g/dL, 74.4 mL/min/1.73 m2, and 167 mg/dL, respectively (Table 2). Approximately two-thirds of patients were diagnosed with ST-elevation MI. More than half had a culprit lesion in the left anterior descending artery, and 61.7% had multivessel coronary disease. About 90% of patients underwent PCI, and of these, 97.5% were successfully revascularized.

2. Clinical outcomes

At 3 years post-AMI (median 1,095 days, IQR 1,031–1,095), 11.1% of patients (39/350) had died from cardiac causes (Table 3). Among these 39 patients, 6 (15%) experienced sudden cardiac death. Non-cardiac deaths occurred in 5.7% of patients (20/350). Overall, 3.1% of patients (11/350) were rehospitalized for ventricular tachycardia, 7.1% had recurrent MI, 14.8% underwent PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting, 2.9% experienced cerebrovascular accidents, and 24.9% were rehospitalized for heart failure. Supplementary Table 1 shows clinical outcomes at 3 years in patients with LVEF ≤35%. Out of 210 patients who had LVEFs <35%, 14.8% (31/210) had died from cardiac causes and 7.6% (16/210) had died from non-cardiac causes. 4.3% patients had rehospitalization due to ventricular tachycardia, 7.1% had recurrent MI, 16.2% underwent PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting, and 34.1% were hospitalized for heart failure. Clinical outcomes stratified by follow-up LVEF are shown in Supplementary Table 2. In the Cox time-to-event multivariable model, older age, LVEF on follow-up TTE <30%, diabetes mellitus, and previous MI or revascularization were positively associated with cardiac death at 3 years, whereas the use of statins and body weight <67 kg were negatively associated (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 3).

3. Devices and electrophysiology procedures

Despite the high mortality risk in this population, only 4.9% of patients (17/350) received an ICD within 3 years (Fig. 4). The rates of CRT-D implantation and electrophysiology study with radiofrequency ablation for ventricular arrhythmia were 0.9% (3/350) and 0.3% (1/350), respectively. 4. Comparison of clinical and procedural findings according to ICD implantation

When clinical and procedural findings were compared according to ICD implantation status, patients who received an ICD had significantly lower LVEF values on both baseline (26.1% [IQR, 20.8–30.8] vs. 36.0% [IQR, 30.0–43.0], p<0.001) and follow-up (27.5% [IQR, 21.5–29.0] vs. 33.9% [IQR, 28.0–37.0], p<0.001) TTE compared with those without ICD implantation (Table 4). Patients with ICDs were also more likely to receive beta-blockers at discharge (Table 5). No other statistically significant differences were noted.

DISCUSSION

Our study investigated long-term outcomes and real-world practice among AMI patients who had reduced LVEFs on follow-up TTEs at least 40 days after AMI events. Of 13,056 patients, 350 had LVEF <40% on follow-up TTEs. At 3 years, 11.1% died from cardiac causes, and 3.1% were rehospitalized due to ventricular tachycardia. The rates of ICD, CRT-D, and electrophysiology procedure usage over 3 years were only 4.9%, 0.9%, and 0.3%, respectively. For patients with LVEF <35%, the rates of ICD, CRT-D, and electrophysiology study usage over 3 years were only 7.6%, 1.0%, and 0.5%, respectively. In the Cox time-to-event multivariable model, older age, LVEF <30%, diabetes mellitus, and previous MI or revascularization were positively associated with cardiac death at 3 years, whereas statin use and body weight <67 kg were negatively associated. To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe real-world clinical practice for ICD implantation in ischemic heart failure patients with reduced LVEFs at least 40 days after AMI events using the nationwide registry.

Patients with heart failure after AMI experience considerably high mortality, and heart failure is one of the strongest predictors of death.17 Although there are conflicting results regarding clinical outcomes in patients with reduced vs. preserved LVEFs, both groups have high mortality rates.8,9 It has been demonstrated that an ICD reduces the risk of sudden cardiac death in selected patients with reduced LVEFs after MI.10,11 Recent guidelines recommend ICD implantation as a class I recommendation for patients with symptomatic heart failure of ischemic origin (unless they have experienced an MI within the preceding 40 days) and an LVEF ≤35% despite ≥3 months of optimal medical therapy.12,13

Limited data exist on real-world clinical practice and long-term outcomes in patients with heart failure with reduced LVEFs (<40%) following AMI events. Pokorney et al.14 analyzed 10,318 older AMI patients in the United Stages with LVEFs ≤35% during index hospitalizations between 2007 and 2010 and demonstrated a cumulative 1-year ICD implantation rate of 8.1%. Patients with ICD implantation were more likely to have had prior coronary artery bypass graft procedures, high peak troponin levels, in-hospital cardiogenic shock, and early cardiology follow-up. Judson et al.15 evaluated 12,893 patients in the United States with LVEF ≤35% at least 40 days post-MI between 2004 and 2017 and showed that 2,176 (16.9%) received ICDs. Younger age, fewer comorbidities, revascularization with PCI, and greater use of guideline-directed medical therapy were associated with increased odds of ICD implantation. Chia et al.16 evaluated 5,276 patients with symptomatic choronic heartfailure and reduced ejection fraction between 2012 and 2015 from the Asian Sudden Cardiac Death in Heart Failure (ASIAN-HF) registry involving 11 Asian regions. Among 3,240 ICD-eligible patients, 12% were ICD recipients. Utilization varied across Asia, from 1.5% in Indonesia to 52.5% in Japan, with a trend towards greater uptake in regions where ICD is government-reimbursed and out-of-pocket healthcare expenditure is lower. Compared with non-ICD recipients, ICD recipients were more likely to be older, have tertiary education and be residing in a high-income region. Cho et al.18 analyzed 485 patients between 2011 and 2014 from the Korea Acute Heart Failure Registry who had reassessed LVEF ≤35% at least 3 months after the index admission for acute heart failure syndrome. It was found that 20 out of 196 patients (10.2%) with ischemic heart disease underwent ICD implantation for up to 60 months.18 In the present study, among 350 patients with LVEF <40% on follow-up TTEs after AMI events, only 5.7% patients received ICDs or CRT-Ds within 3 years. The median follow-up LVEF was 33.0% (IQR, 28.0-37.0), and 210 of the 350 patients had LVEF <35%. Among 210 patients with LVEF <35% on follow-up TTEs, 8.6% patients received ICDs or CRT-Ds. Compared with patients without ICD implantation, those who received ICDs had lower LVEFs on baseline and follow-up TTEs and were more likely to be treated with beta-blockers at discharge.

At 3 years post-AMI, 11% patients had died from cardiac causes, and 3.1% had experienced rehospitalization due to ventricular tachycardia. Among the 39 cardiac deaths, 15% were sudden cardiac deaths. In assessing correlates of cardiac death at 3 years, older age, LVEF <30% on follow-up TTE, diabetes mellitus, and previous MI or revascularization were positively associated with cardiac death, indicating that greater attention should be paid to these high-risk factors. Meanwhile, the use of statins and body weight <67 kg were negatively associated with cardiac death. Although low body weight generally correlates with worse clinical outcomes after AMI,19,20 this paradoxical finding may be partially explained by the “survivor cohort effect,” wherein patients who underwent follow-up TTEs had already survived the early phase of AMI.

To improve the real-world use of ICDs among patients with LVEF ≤35% after AMI, the following strategies may be considered. First, the establishment of a real-time post-myocardial infarction registry would enable systematic identification and longitudinal tracking of eligible patients, ensuring timely reassessment of LVEF and documentation of guideline-directed medical therapy. This registry could be linked to a structured post-MI care pathway that standardizes follow-up imaging, medical optimization, and reassessment at guideline-recommended time points. Within the electronic health record, automated alerts triggered by LVEF ≤35% could prompt clinicians to initiate a predefined order set, including automatic referral for electrophysiology consultation, thereby reducing reliance on individual provider awareness. The integration of a dedicated heart failure or device coordinator nurse further supports this pathway by actively monitoring patient eligibility, coordinating follow-up visits, facilitating referrals, and addressing logistical barriers to ICD implantation. In parallel, embedded clinical decision support tools within the electronic health record can reinforce guideline adherence at the point of care. Finally, structured patient engagement and education initiatives—using standardized educational materials and shared decision-making frameworks—can address patient concerns, improve understanding of sudden cardiac death risk, and enhance acceptance of ICD therapy.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the KAMIR-NIH involves patients enrolled approximately ten years ago and only at teaching university hospitals in the Republic of Korea, which may limit the generalizability of the results. The study period (2011–2015) predates the widespread use of contemporary heart failure therapies such as angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors. Nonetheless, the KAMIR-NIH represents a nationwide, multicenter Korean registry reflecting high-quality standard clinical practice. Second, out of the 13,189 AMI patients in the KAMIR-NIH, 9,270 patients (70.3%) did not undergo follow-up TTE between 40 days and 1 year and were excluded from the study analysis. This indicates that the analyzed cohort represents a highly selected subgroup of post-MI patients, limiting generalizability. Recent guidelines recommend that an echocardiogram is advised 3–6 months after optimation of standard therapies for heart failure with reduced LVEFs to determine the need for the addition of newer pharmacological agents and implanted devices if there has not been a deterioration in clinical status.13 The very low rates of receiving follow-up TTE between 40 days and 1 year in the KAMIR-NIH highlight the need for greater attention in the real-world clinical practice for patients with reduced LVEFs after AMI. Third, the present study did not provide sufficient information on medication dosing, titration, or duration before ICD implantation. Fourth, the wide time range (40 days to 1 year) for follow-up echocardiography might affect the risk stratification. Fifth, of the 350 patients with LVEF <40% on follow-up TTEs, only 5.7% received ICDs or CRT-Ds within 3 years—too few to assess independent correlates of ICD implantation via multivariable analysis. Sixth, serial follow-up TTE results after initial follow-ups of up to 3 years were not included. Seventh, even when comparing the clinical and procedural findings according to the ICD implantation, we didn’t include the referral patterns or institutional practice differences, limiting the clinical applicability of the findings. Eighth, the present study did not include a multivariable analysis to compare between ICD and non-ICD groups due to the low number of ICD implantations. Finally, we could not determine whether ICD implantation improved clinical outcomes in this population. Among the 20 patients who received ICDs or CRT-Ds (median 368 days, IQR 222–551), only 4 died within 3 years. The all-cause mortality rates were 20% and 16.7% for those with and without ICDs or CRT-Ds, respectively (p=0.937). This result does not contradict the importance of primary prevention in patients with low LVEFs.

In conclusion, this real-world, nationwide Korean registry analysis revealed that only 5.7% of ischemic heart failure patients with reduced LVEFs (<40%) after 40 days of AMI events underwent ICD or CRT-D implantation over 3 years. For patients with LVEF <35%, the rate of ICD or CRT-D implantation over 3 years was only 8.6%. Furthermore, older age, LVEF <30% on follow-up TTE, diabetes mellitus, and previous MI or revascularization were independently associated with higher rates of cardiac death at 3 years. Concerted efforts are needed to improve clinical outcomes for patients with reduced LVEFs 40 days after AMI events.
