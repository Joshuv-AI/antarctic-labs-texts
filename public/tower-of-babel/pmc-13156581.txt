Unilateral agenesis of the right cerebellar hemisphere in a child: When one hemisphere is missing, the brain adapts
Authors: Hind Qajia, Saleck Choumad, Fadwa El Bakari, Yassine Eddahoumi, Nazik Allali, Latifa Chat, Siham El Haddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnilateral agenesis of the cerebellar hemisphere is a rare congenital anomaly, typically revealed by psychomotor delay or motor difficulties in children. We report the case of a 5-year-old boy who presented with psychomotor delay, in whom magnetic resonance imaging revealed agenesis of the right cerebellar hemisphere associated with vermian hypoplasia. This case illustrates the clinical variability of cerebellar malformations and underscores the importance of neuroimaging for accurate diagnosis, prognostic evaluation, and the planning of a multidisciplinary management approach.Keywords: Cerebellar agenesis, Vermian hypoplasia, Psychomotor delay, Pediatric neuroimaging, Cerebellar malformations

Introduction

The cerebellum is a critical structure for motor coordination, balance, and certain aspects of cognition, including language processing and executive functions. Congenital malformations of the cerebellum are uncommon, and unilateral agenesis of a cerebellar hemisphere is exceptionally rare. The incidence remains uncertain, as only small case series and isolated reports are described in the literature [1,2]. Magnetic resonance imaging is the gold standard for identifying cerebellar anomalies, evaluating the integrity of the vermis, and detecting any associated structural abnormalities, which is essential for clinical management and prognostic assessment.

Case report

A 5-year-old boy with no significant perinatal or family history was referred for evaluation due to psychomotor delay. Neurological examination revealed moderate hypotonia of the limbs, impaired balance, and difficulty with fine motor coordination. No sensory deficits or signs of intracranial pressure were observed. Brain MRI demonstrated complete agenesis of the right cerebellar hemisphere and hypoplasia of the vermis, while no other intracranial structural malformations were identified.

The brainstem was carefully evaluated, with particular attention to the morphology of the pons. Although slightly reduced in size, no clear structural abnormality or signal alteration was identified (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Unilateral cerebellar agenesis is extremely rare and may be congenital or result from prenatal acquired lesions, such as cerebellar infarction or hemorrhage [3,4]. Some cases are sporadic without an identifiable cause, while others are associated with genetic mutations affecting cerebellar development [5]. The mechanisms of cerebellar agenesis likely involve disruption of early neurogenesis or vascular insult during critical periods of cerebellar development, emphasizing the heterogeneity of its etiology.

Clinical manifestations are highly variable. Certain children remain asymptomatic, whereas others, like the patient described in this report, exhibit psychomotor delay, hypotonia, and balance difficulties. Cognitive and language impairments are also frequently observed and may be linked to the involvement of the vermis, which plays a role in higher cognitive processing [1,2,5]. Behavioral issues and seizures have also been reported in some cases [3], highlighting the broad spectrum of clinical presentations and the need for individualized assessment.

Magnetic resonance imaging is indispensable for confirming unilateral cerebellar agenesis and evaluating associated anomalies. Vermian hypoplasia is common and can exacerbate motor and cognitive deficits due to its central role in cerebellar-cerebral connectivity [6]. MRI is also essential for differentiating unilateral cerebellar agenesis from more complex posterior fossa malformations, such as Dandy-Walker complex or rhombencephalosynapsis, which carry distinct prognoses and management strategies [6].

Classification of posterior fossa malformations is essential for accurate diagnosis and communication. According to current concepts, cerebellar malformations can be categorized into hypoplasia, dysplasia, and agenesis, based on morphological and developmental criteria. Poretti and Boltshauser emphasized the importance of precise terminology in describing cerebellar anomalies [7], particularly distinguishing between hypoplasia and agenesis (Table 1).

Prognosis varies depending on the extent of cerebellar involvement, associated anomalies, and the compensatory capacity of the cerebral hemispheres. Some children develop significant motor deficits, while others demonstrate remarkable adaptation due to the plasticity of the developing brain [2,3]. Management should be multidisciplinary, including early psychomotor rehabilitation to enhance coordination and balance, speech therapy for language difficulties, regular neurological follow-up to monitor developmental progress, and genetic consultation when a hereditary cause is suspected. Early intervention can optimize functional outcomes and support cognitive and motor development.

Conclusion

Unilateral agenesis of the right cerebellar hemisphere with vermian hypoplasia is a rare but clinically important anomaly. Magnetic resonance imaging plays a pivotal role in diagnosis and in guiding a tailored management plan. The prognosis is highly variable and depends on lesion extent, associated anomalies, and the adaptive potential of the pediatric brain. This case highlights the remarkable capacity of the developing brain to compensate for major structural abnormalities and reinforces the importance of a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach to care.
