Biocrusts: Guardians of the Great Wall of China
Authors: Yulong Duan, Fasi Wu, Evangelos Petropoulos, Jie Lian, Chao Ma, Chunting Hou, Yuqiang Li, Wanfu Wang, Rongliang Jia
Published: 2026

Main text

China’s Great Wall, with its vast rammed-earth structures, faces severe weathering. Biocrusts—complex communities of microorganisms—could offer a low-cost, eco-friendly protective layer. Beyond reviewing existing knowledge, this article proposes a core hypothesis: biocrusts could offer a low-cost, eco-compatible protection paradigm, but their effectiveness mainly depends on a critical “beneficial/harmful” threshold set by climate, wall materials, and microbial succession stage. We detail how biocrusts stabilized the wall by reducing wind/water erosion, buffering temperature shifts, and reinforcing soil by emphasizing their dual protective/deteriorative nature and highlight the key knowledge gaps. To enable this paradigm shift, we propose research to clarify the thresholds and underlying mechanisms of biocrust functions across their successional stages and climatic gradients. Future multidisciplinary collaboration among microbiologists, materials scientists, and conservators through nationwide surveys, experiments, and monitoring should address a core question: how to cultivate beneficial biocrusts while minimizing risks? Such hypothesis-driven research could transform this “natural guardian” into a practical framework for sustainable protection for the Great Wall and other earthen heritage sites.

The Great Wall’s dilemma and biocrusts’ potential

The Great Wall of China, a United Nations Educational, Scientific, and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) World Heritage site, stands as a monumental testament to human ingenuity. Today, centuries of exposure have made weathering an existential threat. This vulnerability stems from the rammed-earth construction technique, which renders the structure highly susceptible to weathering. Common conservation methods—including physical reinforcement (e.g., earthen buttresses), chemical treatments (e.g., high-modulus potassium silicate-based agents), and protective shelters—are costly, labor intensive, and often ineffective at scale, leaving many sections unprotected. This has driven the search for sustainable, nature-based alternatives.

Interestingly, the wall itself may host a natural protective layer: biocrusts. These complex microbial communities—mainly consisting of cyanobacteria, lichens, and mosses—cover approximately 67% of the studied sections across a 600-km surveyed area.1,2 Rather than being detrimental, emerging research suggests these biocrusts act as a “living skin,” shielding the wall from degradation through centuries of ecological succession (Figure 1).

The dual role of biocrust on the Great Wall of China

Having withstood millennia of weathering, the surface of the Great Wall is colonized by biocrusts following a successional sequence from cyanobacteria crust to lichen crust and finally moss crust. While these biocrusts demonstrate a distinct protective function in conserving the structure, they would also carry multiple potential risks of deterioration. The overall effect on the Great Wall depends on the balance between these opposing influences. As a precious and fragile cultural heritage, the long-term preservation of the Great Wall requires future initiatives to focus on amplifying the protective functions of biocrusts while mitigating their damaging impacts.

Protective mechanisms and measurable effects

Unlike engineering-based approaches, biocrusts offer a distinct advantage in protecting the rammed-earth sections of the Great Wall as an active, nature-based strategy that holistically enhances resistance to multiple forms of erosion. Biocrusts protect the building structure through several interconnected mechanisms: (1) reducing near-surface wind speed to limit aeolian erosion, (2) buffering temperature fluctuations to lower thermal stress, (3) dissipating raindrop energy to prevent surface disruption, (4) slowing runoff to reduce water erosion, (5) limiting rainwater penetration into the wall, and (6) improving the mechanical stability and weathering resistance of the rammed earth (Figure 1).

Mechanical improvements are significant. Comparisons between biocrust-covered and bare sections showed notable gains in key stability indicators.1,2 These gains result from natural cementation: microbial secretions bind soil particles into a more cohesive and resilient surface matrix.

Protective efficacy varies with environmental conditions, biological community composition, and architectural micro-topography. In semi-humid regions, vascular plants complement biocrusts by stabilizing soil through roots and enhancing organic matter inputs. Synergies among biological organisms—promoted by carbon accumulation—create a stronger protective system than any component acting alone. Beyond their protective functions, biocrusts may contribute to the overall stability of structures and demonstrate how biocrusts transform not just the physical but also the chemical properties of the Great Wall’s surface.3

The dual nature of biocrusts and community synthesis challenges

Current evidence strongly supports the protective role of biocrusts on earthen structures such as the Great Wall. However, it is overly simplistic to view them as exclusively beneficial while ignoring potential disadvantages. The long-standing debate in conservation science revolves around whether biological growth on heritage structures causes biodeterioration or bioprotection. Studies on stone monuments indicate that the same biological communities can either protect or degrade cultural heritage, depending on environmental conditions, microbial composition, and material properties.4,5 This dual potential calls for a more nuanced understanding of biocrust dynamics under the specific conditions of the Great Wall.

On stone heritage, biofilms have demonstrated both detrimental and protective functions. Some microbial communities accelerate weathering through biochemical processes (e.g., acid secretion, chelation) and physical penetration through substrates.4,5 Alternatively, they offer protection by forming stable surface biofilm/coatings that reduce permeability, shield against radiation, and prevent mechanical abrasion. The balance between these opposing functions likely depends on specific environmental conditions and the composition of the microbial communities.

For the Great Wall, the prevailing scientific consensus leans toward net protective effects, especially for rammed-earth sections in arid and semi-arid regions. Yet this conclusion comes with important caveats. Firstly, most studies cover short- to medium-term time frames, whereas heritage conservation requires thinking in centuries, not decades. Secondly, climate change may alter the composition and behavior of biocrust communities, potentially shifting their net effects over time toward less predictable conditions. Thirdly, biocrust-induced moisture retention could exacerbate freeze-thaw cycles or salt migration, while the dehydration of biocrusts leads to mechanical folding that entrains soil particles and re-exposes the underlying soil—both of which require further study. Fourthly, biocrusts may attract small fauna such as ants and millipedes, thus introducing an additional source of mechanical disturbance to earthen sections (Figure 1).

Notably, biocrusts are not a universal solution due to inherent limitations that constrain large-scale application:

(1)High environmental specificity. Their protective role has been demonstrated primarily in arid and semi-arid climates. In semi-humid regions, excess moisture may promote water retention, salt accumulation, or vigorous plant growth, thus potentially damaging the structure.

The central challenge is to steer biocrust communities toward maximal benefit and minimal drawback. This hinges on two fundamental questions: (1) how can we synthesize and promote beneficial biocrust colonization on vulnerable sections of the Great Wall and beyond? (2) How can we maintain these communities in physiological states that optimize protective functions? Addressing these questions demands interdisciplinary research integrating microbiology, ecology, materials science, meteorology, and conservation practice.

Future perspectives

Despite promising evidence of biocrusts’ protective role, significant scientific gaps must be addressed before these natural communities can be effectively integrated into heritage conservation. Required research and actions can be summarized in three key points:

(1)Fundamental research on biocrusts along the Great Wall should be conducted across different climatic zones (arid, semi-arid, semi-humid, and humid) to identify dominant microbial taxa and quantitatively assess their ecological functions. Modern metagenomic approaches are recommended to pinpoint key functional species that enhance biocrust stability.

Fundamental research on biocrusts along the Great Wall should be conducted across different climatic zones (arid, semi-arid, semi-humid, and humid) to identify dominant microbial taxa and quantitatively assess their ecological functions. Modern metagenomic approaches are recommended to pinpoint key functional species that enhance biocrust stability.

(2)Practical methods must be developed to promote desirable biocrust colonization on vulnerable sections. This could include creating inoculation techniques using native microbial communities, optimizing growth conditions, and identifying wall sections that would benefit most via such interventions. Maintaining established communities over time remains a challenge. A proposed restoration framework—incorporating target species selection, vegetation construction, biocrust inoculation, and stability maintenance—offers a promising starting point.

In summary, biocrusts present a promising natural solution to protect the Great Wall by enhancing stability and reducing erosion. Realizing their potential requires understanding their dual nature and managing them carefully. Addressing these research priorities will develop effective strategies to safeguard these cultural treasures for future generations.
