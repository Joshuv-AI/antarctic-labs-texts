Evaluation of the knowledge of general dentists, pediatric dentists, and orthodontists in Turkey regarding the diagnosis and orthodontic management timing of traumatized teeth
Authors: İsmail Haktan Çelik, Hasan Camcı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the knowledge levels of general dentists, pediatric dentists, and orthodontists in Turkey regarding the diagnosis and the timing of orthodontic management of traumatized teeth, based on the 2020 guideline of the International Association of Dental Traumatology (IADT) and the orthodontic trauma guidelines published in 2008 and 2021.MethodsA digital survey consisting of 23 multiple-choice questions was created on the Google Forms platform, based on the dental trauma and orthodontic trauma guidelines. The survey was distributed electronically via WhatsApp or email to orthodontists (n = 56), pediatric dentists (n = 66), and general dentists (n = 134). The data obtained were analyzed comparatively using Chi-Square tests (P < 0.05).ResultsThe accuracy rate for the first six questions, focusing on the diagnosis of dental trauma, was high across all professional groups (ranging from 81.3% to 100%). However, the performance significantly decreased and varied in the subsequent questions (Q7–Q19), which assessed the timing of orthodontic force application. While no statistically significant difference was observed in overall performance among the groups, orthodontists showed significantly higher accuracy in questions related to orthodontic management timing (Q10- Regarding force timing after uncomplicated fractures, Q13- Regarding force timing after mild lateral luxation, Q17- Regarding force timing after root canal treatment, and Q19- Regarding force timing after autotransplantation reflecting their specialized training. Additionally, clinical experience was found to positively influence the rate of correct responses in some areas.ConclusionThe study found that Turkish orthodontists, pediatric dentists, and general dentists have limited knowledge of the orthodontic management of traumatized teeth. It emphasizes the need for additional training and a national guideline to improve clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07199-1.

Introduction

Maxillofacial trauma is a significant orodental and general health issue that can have medical, aesthetic, and psychological consequences for both children and their parents. Traumatic dental injuries are common in childhood, with the prevalence of injuries to permanent teeth ranging from 7.3% to 58.6% [1–4]. It has been reported that incisors are more commonly traumatized than other teeth [5]. The large differences in reported rates can be attributed to various factors, including the type of study, trauma classification, study population, geographic location, and cultural differences [6]. These injuries may not only require restorative care but also present orthodontic challenges that must be managed with precise clinical judgment.

The main factors that predispose individuals to traumatic dental injuries include severe overjet caused by maxillary incisor protrusion and inadequate lip support [7]. Andreasen et al. reported that the trauma rate in maxillary incisors was 45% in patients with an overjet greater than 9 mm, while this rate was halved in patients with an overjet of less than 9 mm [8]. This suggests that early orthodontic treatment is more effective in reducing the risk of trauma compared to treatment administered after puberty [9, 10].

It has been reported that more than 10% of individuals seeking orthodontic treatment have a history of traumatic dental injuries [11]. Traumatized teeth are considered more susceptible to complications when subjected to orthodontic movement. These complications include external root resorption, pulp necrosis, pulp canal obliteration, and an increased risk of ankylosis [12, 13]. Therefore, to achieve the best outcomes in the treatment of patients with a history of trauma, it is essential to conduct a thorough clinical and radiographic evaluation and carefully assess the trauma history [12].

Pediatric dentists frequently encounter dental trauma in children and are responsible for long-term follow-up and treatment planning. They are also involved in interceptive and preventive treatments during the mixed dentition period, which may include procedures involving tooth movement [14, 15]. In Turkey, general dentists are authorized to perform fixed orthodontic treatments following graduation, and studies have shown that a substantial proportion of general practitioners incorporate orthodontic procedures into their clinical practice [16]. Therefore, in addition to orthodontists, it is essential to evaluate the knowledge levels of pediatric and general dentists, given their potential involvement in the orthodontic management of traumatized teeth.

This study is based on three widely recognized clinical guidelines in the field: the International Association of Dental Traumatology (IADT) Dental Trauma Guideline, published in 2020 [17, 18] which covers the diagnosis and management of dental fractures, luxations, and avulsions; and the Orthodontic Management of Traumatized Teeth Guideline, published in 2008 and updated in 2021 [12, 13, 19], which provides detailed protocols regarding the timing of orthodontic treatment for traumatized teeth.

Awareness of the potential complications associated with orthodontic movement of previously traumatized teeth is essential for all dental professionals. This is not only a concern for orthodontists performing the treatment, but also for pediatric dentists and general dentistry who are involved in diagnosing trauma, initiating referrals, and providing follow-up care. However, despite the clinical relevance of this issue, there is limited information in the literature regarding whether dental professionals can accurately identify such cases and their knowledge of the appropriate timing for orthodontic intervention following dental trauma.

Clinical guidelines for the management of dental trauma are periodically updated, with evolving recommendations on both diagnosis and orthodontic management timing. However, it remains unclear to what extent dental professionals stay informed about these updates and incorporate them into clinical decision-making. For instance, a study by Sandler et al. in England reported that the majority of orthodontists had limited knowledge of trauma-related management principles [20]. A review of the existing literature indicates that no similar study has been conducted in Turkey to assess the diagnostic accuracy and knowledge of orthodontic management timing among dental professionals.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the knowledge levels and treatment approaches of general dentist, pediatric dentists and orthodontists in Turkey regarding the diagnosis of traumatized teeth and the appropriate timing for orthodontic force application, in accordance with current clinical guidelines (International Association of Dental Traumatology [IADT] Dental Trauma Guideline, 2020; Orthodontic Management of Traumatized Teeth Guideline, 2008 and 2021). The null hypothesis of the study was that there is no significant difference in knowledge levels among the different professional groups.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study utilized a 23-item multiple-choice questionnaire to assess the knowledge levels of orthodontists, pediatric dentists, and general dentists in Turkey regarding the diagnosis of dental trauma and the appropriate timing of orthodontic force application (Supplementary File 1). Ethical approval was obtained from the clinical research ethics committee of Afyonkarahisar Health Science University before the commencement of the study [ID: 2022/254].

The questions for the study were developed based on the general dental trauma guideline published in 2020 [17, 18], as well as the specific guidelines on the orthodontic management of traumatized teeth released in 2008 and updated in 2021 [12, 13, 19].The sample size calculation, conducted using G-Power software (version 3.0.10, Franz Faul, Christian-Albrechts-Universität, Kiel, Germany) (effect size = 0.3, α = 0.05, and 1 − β = 0.90), indicated that a minimum of 123 participants (n: 41 for each group) was required [20]. A total of 256 participants (170 female, 86 male) were included in the study. Every human participant provide their consent for filling the survey. These participants were categorized into three groups based on their professions: orthodontists, pediatric dentists, and general dentists. A pilot study was conducted with a group of orthodontists (N = 15), pediatric dentists (N = 15), and general dentists (N = 15) to test the clarity and understandability of the questions. Based on the data obtained from the pilot study, the internal consistency of the knowledge section of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, and a value of approximately 0.87 was found. This indicates that the questionnaire has a very good level of internal consistency. The participants in the pilot study were excluded from the main survey. The questions were prepared online using the Google Forms platform.

The questionnaire was divided into three sections: the first section collected demographic information, including age, gender, specialty, and years of professional experience; the second section consisted of six questions assessing knowledge of trauma definitions, derived from the general dental trauma guideline; and the third section included thirteen questions, based on the 2021 orthodontic trauma guideline, focusing on clinical diagnosis and the timing of orthodontic management of traumatized teeth.

The survey was sent to dentists who are members of the Turkish Orthodontic Association, the Turkish Pedodontic Association, and the Turkish Dental Association via email and WhatsApp. It was distributed to the participants through email or the WhatsApp messaging application (WhatsApp Inc., Menlo Park, California, USA) [21, 22].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS software (version 21.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the continuous variables. The Chi-square test was applied to analyze the obtained results. The Z-test was used for pairwise comparisons. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The answers to the questions we asked are provided in the attachment. (Supplementary File 2)

Demographic findings

Of the participants, 66.4% were female (mean age: 28.3 ± 4.3), and 33.6% were male (mean age: 29.8 ± 7.2). Among the dental professionals, 52.0% had 0–3 years of experience, 25% had 4–6 years of experience, 10.9% had 7–10 years of experience, and 12.1% had 10 years or more of experience.

Gender and accuracy rates

There was no significant difference in the accuracy rates between female and maleparticipants for all questions except for Q18 (Regarding force timing after healing of resorption). In Q18 (Regarding force timing after healing of resorption)., 71.4% of female physicians answered correctly, compared to 28.6% of male participants (p = 0.034).

Profession groups and accuracy rates

Participants answered Q1(Regarding defination of concussion injury) correctly at a rate of 81.3%,pediatric dentists, answered it at 90.9%, and orthodontists answered it at 80.4%. No significant difference was observed between the groups regarding specialization (p = 0.082). Similarly, Q2 (Recognition of subluxation injury)- Q6 (Regarding defination of avulsion injury)- which assessed definitions of dental trauma, were answered correctly at high rates (ranging from 82.1% to 100%) across all professional groups. The highest accuracy was observed for Q6 (Regarding defination of avulsion injury)(97.3%). No significant differences were detected among the groups in Q2 (Regarding defination of subluxation injury) to Q6 (Regarding defination of avulsion injury) (p = 0.248, 0.260, 0.518, 0.182, and 0.236, respectively). In Q2 (Regarding defination of subluxation injury), all three professional groups answered correctly at a high rate (82.1% to 90.9%) (p = 0.248, p = 0.260, p = 0.518, p = 0.182, p = 0.236, respectively).

In contrast, Q7 (Regarding force timing after uncomplicated fractures)- Q19 (Regarding force timing after autotransplantation), which focused on the clinical diagnosis and orthodontic management timing of traumatized teeth, showed lower and more variable accuracy rates (ranging from 5.5% to 65.2%). Among these 13 questions, statistically significant differences were found between the professional groups only for Q10 (Regarding force timing after uncomplicated fractures), Q13 (Regarding force timing after mild lateral luxation), Q16 (Regarding force timing after avulsion (non-ankylosed)), Q17 (Regarding force timing after root canal treatment), Q18 (Regarding force timing after healing of resorption), and Q19 (Regarding force timing after autotransplantation). These significant differences favored orthodontists in Q10 (Regarding force timing after uncomplicated fractures), Q13 (Regarding force timing after mild lateral luxation), Q17(Regarding force timing after root canal treatment), and Q19 (Regarding force timing after autotransplantation), while pediatric dentists had higher accuracy rates for Q16 (Regarding force timing after avulsion (non-ankylosed)) and Q18 (Regarding force timing after healing of resorption). Discussion

This study is the first to examine the approaches of orthodontists, pediatric dentists, and general dentists in Turkey regarding orthodontic tooth movements for traumatized teeth. Treatment protocols following traumatic dental injuries must be implemented thoroughly and accurately, as they significantly influence both short- and long-term prognoses. However, do dental professionals adhere to the current guidelines in the constantly evolving field of dental trauma? Do they apply the most up-to-date treatment protocols in their practice? There are limited studies in the literature addressing these questions. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the knowledge level of orthodontists, pediatric dentists, and general dentists in Turkey regarding traumatized teeth, and to assess whether their approaches to orthodontic tooth movements align with current guidelines.

Although online surveys typically yield lower response rates compared to paper surveys, they are often preferred due to their cost-effectiveness, time efficiency, and ability to reach a broad participant base [23]. This method, commonly used in similar studies, facilitates easy participation for respondents [21, 22].

This study involved participants from various regions of Turkey with differing levels of clinical experience. A total of 1,375 individuals were invited to participate in the survey, but only 256 participants responded (18% response rate). Although the response rate was relatively low, it remains comparable to similar survey studies published in the literature [20, 24] Despite the limited participation, the findings reveal inconsistencies in the treatment duration of such cases. Furthermore, the results underscore the substantial need for further education on the management of these cases.

Dental trauma is a significant clinical issue, particularly in children and adolescents, and can result in both aesthetic and functional impairments. Accurate diagnosis and timely management of such cases not only minimize the effects of trauma but also positively impact the long-term oral health and quality of life of patients. The management of dental trauma, especially in patients undergoing orthodontic treatment or prior to it, requires a multidisciplinary approach, supported by the available evidence.

A review of the literature reveals that in studies attempting to define the types of trauma associated with traumatized teeth, dental professionals generally lack competence in clinically identifying the different trauma types [24, 25]. However, in the current study, contrary to the findings of Andreasen et al. [8], it was observed that participants across all three professional groups provided a high percentage of correct answers regarding the definitions of dental trauma types. This outcome may be attributed to the fact that the definitions of dentoalveolar trauma are extensively covered in the undergraduate dentistry curriculum in Turkey, following a standardized approach.

Previous studies have shown that factors such as gender and professional experience did not significantly affect the rates of correct definitions and diagnoses [26, 27].In the current study, responses to the definitions were generally not influenced by variables such as gender and professional experience, which aligns with the existing literature. However, greater experience specifically in managing dental trauma was associated with more accurate responses. This trend has also been observed in other professional groups, such as emergency physicians (e.g., Wolfer et al., 2021) [28], whereas paramedics—who typically encounter dental trauma less frequently—did not demonstrate improved performance based on experience.

The guideline published by Kindelan et al. has made significant contributions to clinical practice by providing evidence-based recommendations for the treatment of orthodontic patients who have suffered dental trauma [12, 13]. In studies conducted in various countries based on this guideline, substantial discrepancies were observed between the recommended observation periods before orthodontic movement of traumatized teeth and the responses reported by participants [20, 29, 30]. In the present study, consistent with previous research, no significant deficiencies were found in the identification of trauma types; however, notable gaps and inconsistencies were observed in the knowledge and clinical decision-making of dental professionals regarding the appropriate timing of orthodontic intervention following dental trauma.

Orthodontists outperformed other professional groups on questions assessing the appropriate timing of orthodontic force application, specifically Q10 (Regarding force timing after uncomplicated fractures), Q13 (Regarding force timing after mild lateral luxation), Q17 (Regarding force timing after root canal treatment) and Q19 (Regarding force timing after autotransplantation). This finding may be attributed to orthodontists’ deeper knowledge of the periodontium and the biomechanics of tooth movements gained during their specialization training. However, despite the higher success rates among orthodontists compared topediatric dentists and general dentists, the percentages of correct answers were not very high. Interestingly, the information assessed in Q10 (Regarding force timing after uncomplicated fractures), Q13 (Regarding force timing after mild lateral luxation), and Q17 (Regarding force timing after root canal treatment) pertains to situations frequently encountered by pediatric dentists in clinical practice. However, pediatric dentists typically focus more on acute care after trauma, pulp vitality, tooth survival, and restorative treatment, as outlined in dental trauma guideline [17, 18]. This may contribute to their lack of sufficient knowledge regarding the clinical approaches to orthodontic movement of traumatized teeth. On the other hand, pediatric dentists performed better on Q16 (Regarding force timing after avulsion (non-ankylosed)) and Q18 (Regarding force timing after healing of resorption), possibly due to their more in-depth understanding of healing mechanisms and complication management such as inflammatory root resorption. Although this information is not commonly included in dental trauma guidelines, pediatric dentists answered these questions correctly more often [17, 18]. This may be due to their more extensive theoretical knowledge and clinical experience in the healing mechanisms of traumatized teeth and the management of complications such as inflammatory root resorption.

In the present study, unlike the first six questions that assessed the definitions related to dental trauma, the majority of general dentists answered ‘I don’t know’ in the 13 questions (Q7- Regarding force timing after uncomplicated fractures), Q19 (Regarding force timing after autotransplantation) concerning the clinical approach to teeth affected by dental trauma. This result may be attributed to the tendency of general dentists to respond with ‘I don’t know’ and refer patients when encountering situations that require specialized expertise, such as the management of dental trauma.

There is a range of opinions in the literature regarding the relationship between professional experience and the accuracy of responses. While some authors suggest that professional experience does not correlate with the accuracy of answers [26, 27], others argue that increasing professional experience leads to higher accuracy in responses [31–33]. In the current study, it was found that certain questions (Q2 (Regarding defination of subluxation injury), Q12 (Regarding force timing after extrusive luxation), Q15 (Regarding force timing after intrusion), Q16 (Regarding force timing after avulsion (non-ankylosed)) were not influenced by professional experience, whereas for other questions (Q14 (Regarding force timing after severe lateral luxation), Q17 (Regarding force timing after root canal treatment), and Q19 (Regarding force timing after autotransplantation)), the accuracy rate increased with experience, consistent with the existing literature. In line with these findings, Wolfer et al. [28] reported that emergency physicians with greater exposure to dental trauma cases demonstrated significantly better diagnostic accuracy and clinical decision-making. Conversely, paramedics, who rarely consider dental trauma in their routine practice, did not show a similar correlation between experience and knowledge. This highlights that experience alone is insufficient unless supported by exposure and education, underscoring the need for structured training in trauma management across all healthcare domains. Interestingly, dental professionals with less experience provided higher accuracy rates for three questions (Q1 (Regarding defination of concussion injury), Q13 (Regarding force timing after mild lateral luxation), and Q18 (Regarding force timing after healing of resorption)), which are thought to be influenced by clinical experience. This may be due to younger professionals being more familiar with current literature and guidelines, or relying more heavily on textbook-based knowledge, while their more experienced counterparts might use more interpretive and practical approaches.

Although the guidelines published by the International Dental Traumatology Association—frequently referenced by pediatric dentists—provide protocols for the clinical management of acute dental trauma, they lack specific recommendations regarding the timing of orthodontic intervention. The guidelines specifically detailing orthodontists’ approach to traumatized teeth are limited in number. It is evident that a unified guideline is needed in the literature that incorporates both orthodontists and pediatric dentists approaches to traumatized teeth. Collaboration between these two professional groups is essential to reach a consensus on standardized clinical protocols.

One of the limitations of the current study might be selection bias, as participation was voluntary, potentially leading to the overrepresentation or underrepresentation of certain groups. Although a comparable number of orthodontists, pediatric dentists, and general dentists were invited to participate in the study, the number of respondents varied across the groups. This limitation should be taken into account when interpreting the results of the current study.Additionally, the sample may be limited by specific age groups, socioeconomic backgrounds, or geographic regions, restricting the generalizability of the findings. Future research should aim to minimize these limitations by employing a more diverse and representative sample.

Conclusion

In clinical situations involving relatively complex treatment protocols, such as dental trauma, participants with specialist training demonstrated better knowledge than general dentists. However, the knowledge of participants across all occupational groups regarding the timing of orthodontic treatment for teeth that have sustained traumatic dental injuries was generally inadequate. Significant variations were observed in the percentage of correct answers among the dental professionals. Further studies are required to generalize these findings.
