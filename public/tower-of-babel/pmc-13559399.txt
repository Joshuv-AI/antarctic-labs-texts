Surgical stabilization of rib fractures in adults without flail chest: a scoping review of pain, quality of life, and clinical outcomes
Authors: Isabella Correia da Rocha, Ronald Luiz Gomes Flumignan, João Aléssio Juliano Perfeito, André Miotto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRib fractures are common after blunt thoracic trauma and cause severe pain, impaired ventilation, and pulmonary complications. Surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) is established for flail chest, but its role in non-flail patients remains uncertain, with heterogeneous patient selection, timing, and outcomes. This scoping review aimed to map the extent, nature, and gaps of the evidence on SSRF in adults with acute non-flail rib fractures, focusing on pain, quality of life, complications, and hospital resource use.MethodsFollowing Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR), we searched MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, LILACS, ClinicalTrials.gov, OSF Registries, and OpenGrey/Google Scholar from inception to 1 June 2026, without date or language restriction. Mixed studies were eligible only when non-flail data were separately extractable; others were treated as indirect evidence. Two reviewers independently screened, charted in duplicate, and appraised all studies.ResultsEleven studies were included: one randomized controlled trial (SOFRIB, n=236), one hybrid randomized/preference trial (NONFLAIL, n=110), eight cohort studies, and one single-arm series. NONFLAIL showed significantly lower pain scores with surgery at 2, 4, and 8 weeks; other studies showed a time-dependent pattern: worse pain immediately postoperatively but better pain at discharge or follow-up. Validated quality of life and pain-impact instruments were used in a minority of studies, with mixed results. Length of stay was generally longer with surgery. In SOFRIB, intensive-care-unit stay did not differ (P=0.17), whereas hospital stay was longer (+3.3 days) and pneumonia more frequent (11.4% vs. 3.3%) with surgery, alongside gains in general-health and pain-impact scores (all P<0.05).ConclusionsThe evidence is limited, heterogeneous, and dominated by observational designs, complemented by one controlled and one randomized trial. The most consistent signal concerns follow-up pain, whereas in-hospital resource use is unfavorable and quality of life mixed; these directions reflect consistency rather than established benefit. Adequately powered randomized trials using validated, time-resolved outcomes remain needed.Keywords: Rib fractures, surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF), pain management, quality of life, scoping review

Highlight box

Key findings

• Eleven studies (2016–2026: one randomized controlled trial, one hybrid controlled trial, eight cohorts, one single-arm series) address surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) in adults without clinical flail chest. The most consistent signal is lower pain at discharge and during follow-up after SSRF, despite higher pain in the first postoperative days; hospital stay is generally longer with surgery, no intensive care unit stay benefit was shown in the randomized trial, and quality of life results are divergent.

What is known and what is new?

• SSRF is established for flail chest, but its role in non-flail rib fractures remains uncertain, with heterogeneous indications, comparators, and outcome instruments.

• This review provides the first Joanna Briggs Institute-based evidence map dedicated to non-flail patients, separating direct from indirect (mixed-population) evidence, holding admission pain out as baseline, and charting comparator analgesic care and funding sources study by study.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The indication for SSRF in non-flail patients should remain individualized, with refractory or subacute pain as its best-supported rationale; an adequately powered randomized trial with time-resolved, validated pain and quality of life endpoints is the priority next step.

Introduction

Rib fractures are among the most frequent injuries after blunt thoracic trauma and carry substantial clinical consequences (1,2). They are commonly associated with severe pain, impaired ventilation, reduced chest-wall mobility, atelectasis, pneumonia, respiratory failure, prolonged hospitalization, and reduced quality of life after injury (1,3). The burden of these injuries is therefore not limited to the acute event but extends to functional recovery and the use of hospital resources.

Historically, rib fractures have been managed conservatively, with analgesia, respiratory physiotherapy, ventilatory support when required, and clinical monitoring. Over the past two decades, surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) has emerged as an important therapeutic option for selected patients (3,4). The strongest support for SSRF has historically been described in patients with flail chest; however, the evidence even in flail chest derives largely from small trials and observational series, and high-quality randomized comparisons of SSRF versus optimized non-operative care remain limited (2,4,5). SSRF has been associated with improved respiratory mechanics and reduced ventilator dependence in that setting (2,4), but these benefits should be interpreted with the corresponding uncertainty. The role of SSRF in adults with rib fractures without a flail-chest pattern remains considerably less well defined.

Because the distinction is central to this review, we define our terms explicitly. A “radiographic flail segment” denotes three or more contiguous ribs each fractured in two or more places on imaging, whereas “clinical flail chest” additionally requires paradoxical movement of that segment on clinical examination. These are not interchangeable: a patient may have a radiographic flail segment without clinically evident paradoxical motion. In this review, “non-flail” refers to patients without clinical flail chest (i.e., without paradoxical motion), and we record, study by study, how each source defined flail and non-flail injury and how patients with a radiographic flail segment but no paradoxical motion were classified (Table 1).

Recent studies and international position statements have begun to discuss SSRF in non-flail-chest patients, particularly in the presence of multiple displaced fractures, refractory pain despite optimized analgesia, impaired ventilation, or an elevated risk of pulmonary complications (1,4). A multicenter controlled clinical trial of SSRF in severe, non-flail fracture patterns (NONFLAIL) (8) and a subsequent multicenter randomized controlled trial (SOFRIB) (16) have further intensified this debate, yet the overall evidence base remains heterogeneous. Studies differ markedly in patient selection, surgical indication criteria, timing of surgery, comparator groups, outcome measures, follow-up duration, and the instruments used to assess pain and quality of life. Several studies also combine flail and non-flail populations, making it difficult to isolate the effect of surgery in the non-flail group specifically. As a result, clinicians lack a clear, consolidated picture of what is currently known, and what remains unknown, about SSRF in this population.

A scoping review is the appropriate methodology in this context. The objective is not to estimate a pooled effect size, which would be premature given the heterogeneity of designs and outcomes, but rather to systematically map the extent, nature, and distribution of the available evidence, and to identify gaps that should guide future research (17,18). This approach allows the inclusion of diverse study designs and outcome types under a single evidence map, clarifying where the literature converges, where it conflicts, and where it is silent.

Objectives

This scoping review aimed to map and synthesize the available evidence on SSRF in adults with acute rib fractures without a flail-chest pattern, with emphasis on pain, quality of life, clinical complications, and hospital resource utilization.

The review addresses the following question: what evidence is available on the use of SSRF in adult patients with acute rib fractures without a flail-chest pattern, particularly regarding pain, quality of life, clinical complications, and hospital resource utilization? The question was structured using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Population-Concept-Context framework:

❖ Population: adults aged 18 years or older with acute rib fractures without a flail-chest pattern (defined as the absence of clinically evident paradoxical chest-wall motion), admitted to hospital after blunt thoracic trauma. Although the protocol permitted penetrating mechanisms, all included studies enrolled blunt-trauma populations; no penetrating-trauma patients were identified in the eligible evidence, and the map should therefore be read as pertaining to blunt thoracic trauma.

❖ Concept: SSRF, regardless of implant type or surgical approach, including locked plates, intramedullary devices, moldable plates, or other rib osteosynthesis systems.

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews (17). We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1760/rc) (18).

Methods

Protocol and registration

A review protocol was prepared a priori and registered in the Open Science Framework (OSF) Registries (https://osf.io/htv3r/overview; protocol version 1.0). The protocol is publicly accessible through the OSF registry. No substantive deviations from the registered protocol were introduced during the conduct of the review.

Eligibility criteria

Sources of evidence were eligible if they enrolled adults aged 18 years or older with acute rib fractures without a flail-chest pattern (flail chest defined as three or more ribs each fractured in two or more contiguous places with clinically evident paradoxical motion; a radiographic flail segment without paradoxical motion was not, by itself, treated as flail chest) who underwent SSRF as the main intervention, regardless of implant type or surgical approach. Eligible outcomes were pain, quality of life, pulmonary complications, length of hospital stay, and mortality. To be eligible, a source had to report at least one of these prespecified outcomes; this outcome-based criterion is stated explicitly because a scoping review intended to identify gaps could otherwise be biased by selective outcome availability. Studies reporting other clinically relevant endpoints (pulmonary function, chronic chest-wall pain, non-union, implant removal, or return to activity) were retained and are noted where reported. All study designs were considered, including randomized controlled trials, prospective and retrospective cohorts, cross-sectional studies, and case series with at least 10 patients.

Studies of mixed (flail and non-flail) populations were eligible when data for patients without flail chest could be extracted separately. Mixed studies in which more than 50% of the sample was non-flail but non-flail outcomes were not separately extractable were not placed in the principal non-flail evidence map; instead, they were retained in a clearly labelled “indirect evidence” category, and their whole-cohort estimates are never displayed as non-flail-specific findings. A sensitivity synthesis restricted to the nine studies retained in the principal (direct) evidence map, i.e., excluding the two indirect-evidence studies, was performed to test the robustness of the map.

Studies were excluded if they enrolled children or adolescents (<18 years), addressed flail chest exclusively, evaluated procedures unrelated to SSRF (e.g., isolated pleurodesis or thoracotomy for hemothorax), or were single case reports, editorials, commentaries, letters without original data, review protocols without original data, or animal or in vitro studies.

No language restriction was applied: publications in any language were eligible, and non-English reports were assessed with the assistance of translation as required. No date restriction was applied, and the search covered each database from its inception to the most recent search date (1 June 2026).

Information sources

The search encompassed MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase (Elsevier), Cochrane CENTRAL (Cochrane Library), and LILACS (BVS./BIREME). Trial registries and grey literature were searched through ClinicalTrials.gov [National Institutes of Health (NIH)], OSF Registries, and OpenGrey/Google Scholar. Each database was searched from its inception, and the most recent search was executed on 1 June 2026. The protocol allowed for contacting study authors for missing or non-separable data (up to two attempts within a 14-day period); in practice, no authors were contacted, and gaps in the source reports were instead marked “not reported”.

Search

A pilot search strategy was developed for PubMed/MEDLINE combining controlled vocabulary (MeSH) and free-text terms for rib fractures and for surgical stabilization. The full PubMed strategy was as follows: (“rib fractures”[MeSH] OR “rib fracture*”[tiab] OR “chest wall fracture*”[tiab] OR “multiple rib fractures”[tiab] OR “displaced rib fractures”[tiab] OR “severe rib fractures”[tiab] OR “fractured rib*”[tiab]) AND (“fracture fixation, internal”[MeSH] OR “SSRF”[tiab] OR “surgical stabilization of rib fractures”[tiab] OR “surgical stabilization”[tiab] OR “rib plating”[tiab] OR “rib fixation”[tiab] OR “rib stabilization”[tiab] OR “chest wall stabilization”[tiab] OR “rib osteosynthesis”[tiab] OR “operative stabilization”[tiab] OR “surgical fixation”[tiab] OR “intramedullary fixation”[tiab] OR “locked plate”[tiab]).

The complete, line-by-line search strategy for every source (Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, LILACS, ClinicalTrials.gov, OSF Registries, and Google Scholar), each with its syntax, applied limits, date executed, and number of records retrieved, is provided in Appendix 1; only the MEDLINE/PubMed strategy is reproduced in the review report below. For Google Scholar, the exact query string, the date searched, the sorting/relevance setting, and the number of results screened are reported separately from other grey-literature sources; ClinicalTrials.gov, OSF Registries, and OpenGrey counts are likewise reported individually rather than combined (Appendix 1).

Selection of sources of evidence

All records were imported into and de-duplicated in Rayyan (Rayyan Systems Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA). Automatic de-duplication was applied first, followed by manual verification of near-duplicates. The machine-learning relevance-ranking function was not enabled; screening order followed simple chronological/alphabetical listing rather than predicted relevance. Manually identified duplicate records were merged and removed by consensus. Before formal screening, the two reviewers independently assessed a random sample of 20 records to calibrate the application of the eligibility criteria; disagreements during this pilot phase were discussed and the criteria refined by consensus before proceeding. This calibration step was not included in the final agreement calculation.

Screening proceeded in two phases. In Phase 1, the two reviewers (I.C.d.R. and R.L.G.F.) independently screened all titles and abstracts, resolving doubtful cases by inclusion. In Phase 2, the full texts of records retained in Phase 1 were independently assessed against the detailed eligibility criteria by both reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer/arbitrator (A.M.) consulted when consensus could not be reached. Inter-reviewer agreement (Cohen’s kappa) was substantial to almost perfect: κ=0.81 at the end of title/abstract screening and κ=0.89 at the end of full-text review. The flow of records through each stage was documented using a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.

Data charting process

Both reviewers independently charted all included studies in duplicate, using a standardized electronic form (REDCap) developed by the review team and piloted on five studies before formal use, in keeping with JBI guidance on calibrated charting forms. Discrepancies were reconciled by consensus, with a third reviewer (A.M.) consulted when agreement could not be reached. The protocol permitted contacting study authors for missing or ambiguous data (up to two attempts within a 14-day period); in the event, no authors were contacted, and unresolved data were recorded as “not reported”.

Critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence

Although critical appraisal is not mandatory in scoping reviews, an exploratory appraisal was conducted to contextualize the maturity and robustness of the available evidence, and not to determine inclusion. Each study was appraised with the tool matched to its specific design. Randomized comparisons were appraised with the Cochrane RoB 2 tool (19). Non-randomized intervention components, including the preference-allocated (self-selected) cohort embedded in NONFLAIL, were appraised with ROBINS-I (20) rather than RoB 2, because RoB 2 is valid only for randomized comparisons; consequently, NONFLAIL was appraised twice, applying RoB 2 to its 23 randomized participants and ROBINS-I to its 87 preference-allocated participants, and the two judgements are reported separately (Table 2). Observational cohorts were appraised with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), and the single-arm operative series [Peek 2022 (10)] with the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for case series (21), the tool prespecified in the protocol for single-arm series, instead of the NOS, which requires a comparison group. For the NOS, each study was scored across Selection (max 4 stars), Comparability (max 2 stars), and Outcome (max 3 stars), with bands of 7–9 (good), 4–6 (moderate), and 0–3 (low). For randomized comparisons, overall RoB 2 judgements are reported as one of the three mutually exclusive categories (“low risk”, “some concerns”, or “high risk”), never as more than one simultaneously; domain-level judgements, including a domain-specific assessment of whether the unavoidable lack of blinding for subjective pain and patient-reported outcomes could have influenced measurement, are presented in Figure S1. Each appraisal was reported individually and used solely to interpret the strength of the evidence in context; no study was excluded on the basis of its appraisal.

Synthesis of results

Given the descriptive aim of a scoping review, no meta-analysis was performed. Results were synthesized narratively and presented in descriptive tables. The synthesis was organized by type of outcome (pain, quality of life, complications, hospital stay), study design, timing of intervention (early ≤72 h vs. late >72 h), and indication criteria (e.g., three or more displaced fractures, refractory pain, respiratory compromise).

Pain was synthesized using pre-defined, clearly labelled time intervals: baseline/admission, postoperative days 1–7, discharge, 2–8 weeks, 3–6 months, and 12 months or more, and only within-study longitudinal comparisons were used to infer any trajectory. Values recorded at admission were classified as baseline and were not used to support an “early postoperative” worsening. This directly addresses the concern that a time-dependent pain pattern could otherwise be an artefact of comparing baseline severity with later post-treatment measurements.

The evidence map (Table 3) displays the reported direction of the between-group comparison for each outcome, but to avoid vote counting it is accompanied, wherever the primary reports allowed, by the effect estimate, its uncertainty interval, the denominator (number analysed), the time point, and whether the estimate pertains to the whole population or a separable non-flail subgroup (Table 3 and the expanded quantitative results in Table S3). Statistically non-significant trends are labelled “no significant difference” and are not assigned a favourable or unfavourable direction, so that a small underpowered study and a large trial do not carry equal visual weight.

Results

Selection of sources of evidence

The database and register searches identified 3,220 records [PubMed (n=920); Embase (n=1,863); Cochrane CENTRAL (n=53); LILACS (n=24); and reported separately, ClinicalTrials.gov (n=72), OSF Registries (n=54), OpenGrey (n=34), and Google Scholar (n=200); the combined register/grey-literature figure was 360; the exact per-source counts are given in Appendix 1]. After 2,040 duplicate records were removed, 1,180 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 1,063 were excluded. The full texts of 117 reports were sought and retrieved, and all 117 were assessed for eligibility. Following full-text assessment, 106 reports were excluded.

For the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram, each excluded full-text report was assigned a single, mutually exclusive primary reason for exclusion so that the reasons sum to 106: non-flail data not separately reported (n=48), wrong publication type (n=27), a majority of flail-chest patients (n=13), wrong comparator (n=9), wrong intervention (n=4), wrong outcome (n=3), and unclear population (n=2). Eleven studies met all eligibility criteria and were included. The selection process is summarized in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Figure 1).

PRISMA-ScR flow diagram of study identification and selection. OSF, Open Science Framework.

Investigator contact

No study authors were contacted for missing or non-separable data. This is acknowledged as a limitation: residual gaps [e.g., the unnamed quality of life instrument in Xu 2025 (13), or the non-separable non-flail outcomes in Peek 2022 (10) and Huelskamp 2026 (15)] reflect what was reported in the published sources rather than author-supplied clarification.

Characteristics of sources of evidence

The eleven included studies were published between 2016 and 2026 and comprised one randomized controlled trial [SOFRIB (16)], one multicenter controlled clinical trial with hybrid randomized/preference allocation [NONFLAIL (8)], eight cohort studies, and one single-arm operative series. Of the cohort studies, six were retrospective and two prospective (one multicenter); the single-arm series was retrospective. Five studies were conducted in China and three in the United States [NONFLAIL, SOFRIB, and Ahn 2025 (14)], with one each in Switzerland, the Netherlands/Switzerland, and Germany. Among studies with clearly delimited non-flail samples, analyzed sizes ranged from 78 to 452 participants. Two large studies with mixed flail/non-flail populations [Peek 2022 (10) and Huelskamp 2026 (15)] could not have their non-flail outcomes separated and are therefore reported as indirect evidence, not as part of the principal non-flail map. The two trials enrolled 110 (NONFLAIL) and 236 (SOFRIB) participants. Characteristics are presented in Table 1. Hoepelman 2023 (12) compared three centres that perform SSRF as standard care with three centres that treat such patients non-operatively, a design feature now stated explicitly in Table 1.

Critical appraisal within sources of evidence

All eleven studies were appraised with the tool matched to their design, and the results are summarized in Table 2. Among the observational cohorts scored with the NOS, six were rated good (7–9 stars) and two moderate (4-6); their principal strengths were propensity-score matching or multivariable adjustment [Ahn 2025 (14), Zhang 2023 (11), Hoepelman 2023 (12), Huelskamp 2026 (15)] and validated outcome instruments with adequate follow-up, while their principal weaknesses were the absence of formal matching in some studies, self-selection of the treatment arm [Li 2020 (9)], and historical controls introducing temporal confounding [Qiu 2016 (6), the highest risk of bias]. The single-arm Peek 2022 (10) series was appraised with the JBI case-series checklist rather than the NOS. NONFLAIL was appraised separately for its two components: its 23 randomized participants (RoB 2: some concerns) and its 87 preference-allocated participants (ROBINS-I: serious risk of bias, driven by confounding and selection because treatment was chosen by preference). SOFRIB, a parallel-group randomized trial analysed by intention to treat, received an overall RoB 2 judgement of “some concerns” for its primary outcome [intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay], driven by a baseline imbalance in flail-chest prevalence between arms and by post-randomization non-adherence in the SSRF group (13.2%); for the secondary pain and quality of life outcomes synthesized in this review, risk of bias was materially higher, reflecting additional concerns from asymmetric loss to follow-up (29–40%) and the unavoidable non-blinding of patient-reported measures in an open trial. Full domain-level judgements for both outcome families, and for NONFLAIL, are presented in Figure S1.

Results of individual sources of evidence

Pain

When pain was analysed within studies using the pre-defined intervals, and admission values were held out as baseline, a time-dependent pattern was apparent in several studies: pain measured in the first postoperative days (postoperative days 1–7) tended to be higher, with lower pain by discharge and at 2–8 weeks and 3–6 months [Zhang 2023 (11), Li 2020 (9), Zhang 2019 (7) to 6 months, Qiu 2016 (6) at 2 months]. Values previously labelled “early” in Zhang 2023 (11) and Li 2020 (9) were in fact recorded at admission and are now treated as baseline, not as an early postoperative outcome. This within-study pattern was corroborated at the trial level: in NONFLAIL (8), SSRF performed within 72 hours produced significantly lower numeric pain scores than medical management at 2, 4, and 8 weeks. In contrast, Ahn 2025 (14) reported worse pain and higher opioid requirements with surgery during the index admission, and Hoepelman 2023 (12) found no significant in-hospital difference and a non-significant trend toward worse pain at one year (labelled “no significant difference”, not assigned a direction). In SOFRIB (16), pain was a prespecified secondary outcome assessed with validated instruments (SF-36, PIQ-6). The direction of the SOFRIB secondary pain and quality of life outcomes is charted from the trial’s published structured abstract in Table 3. SSRF was associated with clinically meaningful improvement on SF-36 general health (+3.3; P=0.04), bodily pain (+4.0; P=0.02), and physical component summary (+3.3; P=0.04), each exceeding the minimal clinically important difference, and with a lower (better) PIQ-6 pain-impact score (−3.9; P=0.02). Return to work was more frequent with SSRF (62.5% vs. 38.7%) but did not reach significance (P=0.08). ICU length of stay, the primary outcome, did not differ significantly (mean difference 1.8 days, 95% confidence interval (CI): −0.8 to 4.4; P=0.17), while hospital length of stay was longer (+3.3 days; P=0.01) and pneumonia more frequent (11.4% vs. 3.3%; risk ratio 3.45; P=0.02) with SSRF. These figures have been cross-checked against the trial’s full-text accepted manuscript [Ang et al. (16)] and match exactly. Additional detail from the full text: PIQ-6 and SF-36 were assessed at a mean follow-up of 1.1–1.7 months (PIQ-6 n=65 non-operative/71 SSRF; SF-36 n=66/68), with 40.2% (non-operative) and 28.9% (SSRF) of randomized patients not contributing post-discharge surveys. Role-physical score also favored SSRF (37.9 vs. 34.3; P=0.05). In a timing-stratified sensitivity analysis, between-group PRO differences were concentrated at the ~1-week follow-up and were smaller and not statistically significant by 3 and 6 months.

Quality of life and functional outcome

Following the revised taxonomy, generic health-related quality of life, pain interference, physical function, and return to work are reported separately. Generic health-related quality of life (HRQoL): Zhang 2019 (7) reported higher SF-36 scores with surgery at 3 and 6 months; Hoepelman 2023 (12) found slightly lower EQ-5D-5L index values with surgery at one year (−0.1; P=0.04); SOFRIB measured SF-36 as a generic health-related quality of life secondary endpoint. Pain interference: SOFRIB additionally used PIQ-6, which captures the impact of pain rather than generic health status and is therefore reported under this separate heading. Physical function/recovery: Li 2020 (9) used recovery-time proxies. Participation/return to work: Qiu 2016 (6) used return to work. These are not treated as equivalent measures of quality of life. Peek 2022 (10), a single-arm study, compared postoperative EQ-5D values with a general reference population; because this provides no surgery-versus-non-operative contrast and is highly susceptible to differences in age, comorbidity, employment, and pre-injury health, it is used only to describe long-term outcomes after fixation and is NOT used as evidence for or against the comparative effectiveness of SSRF.

Complications

Pneumonia was lower with surgery in Xu 2025 (13) (9.8% vs. 21.6%; P=0.01) and Qiu 2016 (6) (4.6% vs. 17.0%; P=0.03), higher but non-significant with surgery in Hoepelman 2023 (12) (30% vs. 17%), and similar between groups in Ahn 2025 (14) and Huelskamp 2026 (15). In NONFLAIL, pleural-space complications during the index admission were significantly lower with surgery (0% vs. 10.2%; P=0.02). Several studies recorded pneumothorax and hemothorax as baseline admission findings rather than treatment outcomes. Regarding mortality, in-hospital mortality was 0% in both arms in most studies. Huelskamp 2026 (15) reported lower in-hospital mortality with surgery in the whole matched cohort (4.7% vs. 9.2%; P=0.005); however, this estimate pertains to a mixed flail/non-flail cohort, and in the non-flail subgroup the result was non-significant (hazard ratio 0.343; P=0.12). This mixed-cohort mortality estimate is therefore presented as indirect evidence only and is not displayed as a non-flail-specific survival benefit in the principal map (Table 3).

Hospital resource use

Length of stay was longer with surgery in most series [Xu 2025 (13), Ahn 2025 (14), Zhang 2023 (11), Hoepelman 2023 (12) (+4.9 days; P=0.02), Huelskamp 2026 (15) (+3 days; P<0.001)], with the exception of Qiu 2016 (6). In SOFRIB, the primary outcome of intensive-care-unit length of stay did not differ significantly (mean difference 1.8 days; 95% CI: −0.8 to 4.4; P=0.17). These length-of-stay findings require substantial caution and are reported descriptively rather than as a treatment effect. Patients selected for SSRF may have more displacement, greater pain, or associated thoracic injuries; surgery necessarily occurs after admission, so the admission-to-operation interval can itself lengthen total stay (time-related bias). None of the included studies aligned a common time-zero, treated surgery as a time-dependent exposure, or fully adjusted for injury severity and associated injuries in the length-of-stay comparison; longer stay should therefore not be read as caused by surgery.

Synthesis by surgical timing, indication, and comparator care

As prespecified in the “Methods”, the findings are organized by surgical timing and by the indication for fixation, and the non-operative comparator is characterized study by study (Table 4). Treatment effect in non-flail injury may depend more on indication and timing than on the binary presence or absence of surgery, so eligibility thresholds, displacement definitions, fracture number/location, operative timing, and the analgesic/respiratory-care regimen of the comparator arm are summarized together. Where a study did not report a given element, this is marked NR rather than inferred.

Sensitivity synthesis and expanded quantitative results

A sensitivity synthesis restricted to the nine studies retained in the principal (direct) evidence map [i.e., excluding the two indirect-evidence studies, Peek 2022 (10) and Huelskamp 2026 (15)] did not change the direction of the map: the time-dependent within-study pain pattern, the follow-up pain signal reinforced by NONFLAIL, the longer length of stay with surgery, and the absence of an ICU-stay benefit in SOFRIB all persisted, indicating that the overall picture is not driven by the mixed-population studies. A comprehensive quantitative results table, reporting, for each outcome and time point, the number analysed, the effect measure, its uncertainty interval, the adjustment method, and whether the estimate pertains to the whole population or a separable non-flail subgroup, is provided as Table S3, populated from the completed data extraction.

Synthesis of results

Across the eleven studies, the evidence on SSRF in non-flail chest was limited, heterogeneous, and still dominated by observational designs, although it now includes one randomized and one controlled trial. The synthesis revealed a recurring within-study time-dependent pain pattern and a descriptive association between surgery and longer hospital stay. Quality of life was assessed with a validated instrument in only four studies, with divergent directions of effect. A central tension is apparent: NONFLAIL and several smaller cohorts favour surgery for follow-up pain and pulmonary complications, whereas the largest and most rigorously matched studies [Ahn 2025 (14), Hoepelman 2023 (12)] and the SOFRIB primary outcome do not support an in-hospital or one-year benefit. These directions describe where the literature converges and conflicts; they are not a verdict on effectiveness.

Funding and competing interests of the included studies

In accordance with PRISMA-ScR item 22, the funding sources and industry/device-related competing interests of the included primary studies were charted and are summarized in Table S2. Of the eleven included studies, four reported no external funding and no competing interests [Xu 2025 (13), Zhang 2019 (7), Peek 2022 (10), and Huelskamp 2026 (15)], and one [Qiu 2016 (6)] did not state a funding source and declared no competing interests; two received institutional, non-industry support with no declared competing interests [Zhang 2023 (11), funded by an institutional new-investigator program, and Li 2020 (9), funded by the treating hospital]. One study [Ahn 2025, an SSRN preprint (14)] was funded by a hospital-network operator (HCA Healthcare), with no declared competing interests. The remaining three studies were funded by the same implant manufacturer (DePuy Synthes): Hoepelman 2023 (12), the largest non-flail cohort in this review, whose findings were directionally unfavourable to surgical fixation; SOFRIB [Ang 2026 (16)], the included randomized trial, whose findings favoured surgical fixation for several patient-reported pain and quality of life outcomes; and NONFLAIL [Pieracci 2020 (8)], the multicentre controlled trial, whose findings also favoured fixation for follow-up pain. The authors of Hoepelman 2023 (12) and SOFRIB declared no competing interests, whereas in NONFLAIL several authors declared industry relationships (including with DePuy Synthes, Acute Innovations, KLS Martin, Zimmer Biomet, and Johnson & Johnson); in each of these three trials, the sponsor is reported to have had no role in study design, data collection, analysis, or the decision to publish. That two of the three industry-funded trials reported findings favouring fixation while the third did not is discussed as a contextual factor for interpreting the evidence base, not as a basis for excluding or downweighting any study.

Discussion

Summary of evidence

This scoping review mapped the available evidence on SSRF in adults with acute rib fractures without a flail-chest pattern. Eleven studies published between 2016 and 2026 met the eligibility criteria: a randomized controlled trial [SOFRIB (16)], a multicenter controlled clinical trial [NONFLAIL (8)], eight cohort studies, and one single-arm operative series. The evidence base remains predominantly observational and geographically concentrated, with five of the eleven studies conducted in China. Despite growing clinical interest and international discussion of SSRF in non-flail patients (1,4), the underlying evidence remains immature.

These findings sit alongside prevailing international guidance. The World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) and Chest Wall Injury Society (CWIS) position paper (4) and the WSES-American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) thoracic-trauma guidelines (1) stop short of a strong recommendation for SSRF in non-flail patients, framing the indication as selective and individualized, a stance consistent with the present map, in which the literature points to benefit in some domains, neutrality in others, and persistent gaps.

Interpretation of pain

Calibrated to the certainty of the evidence. The domain in which the evidence is most internally consistent is pain: a within-study trajectory of higher pain in the first postoperative days giving way to lower pain by discharge and through follow-up, reinforced by the NONFLAIL trial’s lower numeric pain scores at 2, 4, and 8 weeks. We deliberately temper the strength of this conclusion. It rests on one hybrid controlled trial with only 23 randomized patients and on heterogeneous observational studies that differ in indication, pain scale, and follow-up, and in which formal minimal-clinically-important-difference (MCID) thresholds were rarely reported. The consistency across studies is best described as hypothesis-generating rather than as established treatment benefit, and it is not yet possible to state whether the observed pain differences exceed a clinically important threshold. Accordingly, pain reduction, particularly subacute and follow-up rather than immediate postoperative pain, is the most defensible candidate benefit of SSRF in carefully selected non-flail patients, but it requires confirmation in adequately powered randomized trials that pre-specify pain as a time-resolved (co-)primary endpoint and report against an MCID.

Integrating the appraisal into the synthesis

Aggregate NOS star totals convey limited information about the biases that actually threaten treatment comparisons, so we read the map through domain-level concerns: baseline severity imbalance and confounding by indication (most acute in the single-arm and historical-control designs and in the preference-allocated NONFLAIL subset), treatment selection, missing or non-separable patient-reported outcomes, loss to follow-up, outcome ascertainment, and adjustment for associated injuries. Viewed this way, the less favourable in-hospital results of the larger, better-matched studies [Ahn 2025 (14), Hoepelman 2023 (12), and the SOFRIB primary outcome] carry more weight for resource-use and in-hospital outcomes, whereas the favourable follow-up-pain signals from smaller studies are more vulnerable to residual confounding, with the important exception that the NONFLAIL randomized pain result and the coherent within-study pain trajectories are less easily explained by selection alone.

Length of stay

The consistent association between surgery and longer hospital stay should not be interpreted as a treatment effect. It is highly vulnerable to confounding by indication (sicker patients selected for surgery) and to time-related bias, because operation necessarily follows admission and the admission-to-surgery interval lengthens total stay. No included study aligned a common time-zero, adjusted fully for injury severity and associated injuries, or modelled surgery as a time-dependent exposure; longer stay is therefore reported descriptively only.

Analgesic strategies and the comparator

Pain and pulmonary outcomes depend heavily on the analgesic and respiratory-care regimen of the non-operative arm, which the included studies described inconsistently (Table 4). Epidural analgesia, paravertebral and erector-spinae plane blocks, systemic opioid protocols, pulmonary hygiene, non-invasive ventilation, and mobilization pathways each carry different failure risks and can materially shift the apparent benefit of surgery; heterogeneous or unreported comparator care is thus a major source of the divergent pain and pneumonia findings and a key reason the map cannot yet support a firm effectiveness claim.

Rib-fracture taxonomy, indication, and late consequences. Reported indications for SSRF (number, displacement, and location of fractures) plausibly influence both the decision to operate and the outcomes, so differences in fracture taxonomy across studies (Table 4) may partly explain the heterogeneity in effect direction. Equally important, and largely absent from the current evidence, are the late consequences of both pathways: chronic chest-wall pain, symptomatic non-union, chest-wall deformity or instability, and hardware-related complications or implant removal after SSRF, versus chronic pain and non-union after non-operative care. These longer-term outcomes are inconsistently captured and represent a priority for future study.

Implications and future research

For trauma and thoracic surgeons, the indication for SSRF in non-flail patients rests on a thin but improving base; pain, especially subacute pain, is its best-supported candidate rationale, outcome timing matters when counseling patients, and the single-arm and mixed-population data cannot establish comparative effectiveness. For guideline developers, quality of life, mechanical ventilation, tracheostomy, and surgical-site infection remain inconsistently reported across both arms. For researchers and funders, the central gap is an adequately powered, multicenter randomized trial that enrolls a clearly defined clinical-flail-free population; distinguishes radiographic flail segment from clinical flail chest; standardizes the non-operative analgesic/respiratory protocol; pre-specifies time-resolved pain (against an MCID) and generic and pain-specific quality of life as (co-)primary outcomes; and reports a consistent core outcome set including mechanical ventilation, tracheostomy, surgical-site infection, non-union, and implant removal. Feasibility is a real constraint: NONFLAIL randomized only 23 of 110 patients, and CWIS-NONFLAIL enrolled 110 patients across 12 level-1 trauma centres over roughly 2.5 years, so future trials will likely require large multinational networks, pragmatic or expertise-based/preference-tolerant designs, and possibly registry-embedded randomization to reach adequate power.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, consistent with scoping-review methodology, no formal risk-of-bias-driven exclusion or meta-analysis was performed; the appraisal was exploratory. Second, although eligibility was not restricted by language, non-English reports required translation, with a small residual risk of misclassification, relevant because much of the included literature originated in China. Third, two of the included studies were trials whose populations were not perfectly congruent with a strict non-flail definition: NONFLAIL allocated most patients by preference rather than randomization, and SOFRIB enrolled a broader population in which 14.8% (non-operative arm) to 29.0% (SSRF arm) had clinical flail chest, with outcomes reported for the whole cohort rather than stratified by flail status; SOFRIB’s contribution is therefore strong but not pristine, and its estimated ~78% non-flail composition (Table 1) should be borne in mind when interpreting its effect sizes. Fourth, two mixed flail/non-flail studies [Peek 2022 (10), Huelskamp 2026 (15)] could not have non-flail outcomes separated and are reported as indirect evidence only. Fifth, SOFRIB’s secondary pain and quality of life estimates, initially extracted from the trial’s published structured abstract, were subsequently cross-checked against the full-text accepted manuscript and confirmed to match exactly. Sixth, one included study [Ahn 2025 (14)] remained an unpublished preprint at the time of charting and is distinguished as such throughout. Finally, heterogeneity in pain scales, quality of life instruments, follow-up intervals, comparator regimens, and the recording of complications as baseline versus treatment outcomes constrained comparability. These limitations reflect the state of the underlying evidence as much as the review process itself.

Conclusions

The evidence on SSRF in adults with rib fractures without a flail-chest pattern is limited, heterogeneous, and dominated by observational designs, complemented by one controlled and one randomized trial. Across this evidence base, pain relief follows a within-study time-dependent course and is the outcome most consistently, though not yet conclusively, favouring SSRF, with numeric pain scores significantly lower after early fixation in NONFLAIL and coherent trajectories in several cohorts; whether these differences exceed a clinically important threshold is not established. Conversely, surgery is descriptively associated with a longer hospital stay, no significant ICU-stay benefit was demonstrated in SOFRIB, and quality of life remains poorly characterized with divergent results. These findings clarify what is known and what is missing rather than settling effectiveness. The principal implication for practice is that the indication for SSRF in non-flail patients should be individualized, with pain control, especially refractory or subacute pain, as its best-supported rationale, and with explicit acknowledgment of the remaining uncertainty for resource use and quality of life. The principal next step is an adequately powered randomized trial with the design features outlined above.
