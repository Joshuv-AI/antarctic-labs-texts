Resistance, Intentionality, and Endurance: Understanding Asylum-Seeking Mothers’ Agency During Resettlement in Montreal, Canada
Authors: Yufei (Mandy) Wu, Els Rommes, Rachel Kronick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsylum-seeking mothers are often cast as powerless and lacking agency as they resettle in a host country. With unprecedented numbers of families facing forced displacement, understanding womens' lived experiences of adaptation is critical to better serve their resettlement and health trajectories. This study aims to capture asylum-seeking mothers' sense of agency as they navigate resettlement in Montreal, Canada. We conducted 13 in-depth interviews asylum-seeking mothers and analyzed them using thematic analysis. We found that mothers experienced deprivation, discrimination, and precariousness in Canada that constrained their capabilities. Nonetheless, mothers continued to express agency in the form of i) resistance to the narrative of dependency ii) intentionality in their motherhood project, and iii) endurance through meaning-making. In highlighting asylum-seeking mothers' agency, this study may challenge the dominant discourse that characterizes these women as passive and helpless, instead supporting recognition of their capacities and drive for self-determination that can in turn guide research, policy and preventative and clinical interventions.Keywords: agency, resettlement, asylum seekers, mothers

According to UNHCR, an estimated 6.6 million people will be forcibly displaced and become asylum seekers in 2023 (UNHCR, 2023). In Canada, nearly 93,000 asylum claims were submitted in 2022, the highest number to date. More than 60% of these claims were filed in Quebec (Government of Canada, 2022). Asylum seekers are people who arrive in a host country seeking protection from harm or persecution in their country of origin. Their claims are always scrutinized under a process that determines if they meet the legal definition of a refugee or a person in need of protection. In Canada, the average wait time for the adjudication of a refugee claim is approximately two years, though in the case of an initial negative decision, this process may be prolonged considerably through a series of appeals.

The migration of asylum seekers is often characterized as one of involuntary displacement, suggesting powerlessness and lack of choice in their migration decision (Ghorashi, 2005; Oliver-Smith & Hansen, 2019). Migrant women—unlike men, who are cast as seeking economic mobility and opportunities—are especially at risk of being cast as victims who were forced as passive subjects to migrate. Mothers’ migration decision in particular is portrayed as a form of self-sacrifice to sustain their homes and fulfill their care responsibilities (Lutz, 2016; Tyldum, 2015). While those seeking refugee protection are indeed often forcibly displaced and face a limited range of options, the discourses emphasizing victimhood often fail to capture the resourcefulness and agency that seeking protection demands of refugeed people. For asylum-seeking mothers, asserting their ambitions beyond seeking protection, such as one of building a new life for their family and children, may be sidelined in the process of seeking refugee status. As Arthur Kleinman has pointed out: “trauma stories then become the currency, the symbolic capital with which they enter exchanges for physical resources and achieve the status of political refugee” (Kleinman et al., 1997, p. 10). Rather than positioning themselves as agents, refugee claimants are, by nature of the tribunal process, compelled to reduce their narrative to one solely based on trauma and violence and their identities to that of victims suffering from a pathology (Kirmayer, 2003). Conversely, migrant female domestic workers are at times characterized as “self-supporting agents” (Näre, 2014, p. 224) who are endowed with freedom and will that allow them to resist the system, despite the structural violence of those same systems (Benhabib et al., 1994; Briones, 2013). Bracke (2016) argued that this neoliberal conceptualization of resilience carries an ethical imperative that demands the good refugee woman to be constantly positioned in the process of overcoming, bouncing back from whatever life throws at her, even when that is complete displacement and losing almost everything.

Feminist scholars criticized this dichotomy of seeing women either as powerless victims of an oppressive system or as self-supporting agents that (should) actively resist the system, and advocated for a more nuanced analysis that explores how women can express agency by strategically negotiating their existence within structural constrains and vulnerabilities (Ayala & Murga, 2016; Kanal & Rottmann, 2021; Näre, 2014). Such analyses challenge this binary and allow “vulnerability [to] enter into agency” (Butler, 2016, p. 25), thereby restoring the voice and power of the ostensible victims and allowing complexities in their narratives. By recognizing the thoughtful and deliberate choices these mothers were able to make as they were forced to leave a place that had become dangerous, their displacement could be understood as a testament to their agency as “achievers” and “active subjects” (Ghorashi, 2005, p. 195) that, despite the risk, resisted the power that threatened them in their home countries (Essed, 2004; Yarar & Karakaşoğlu, 2022). As mothers enter a host society whose reception policies are often structured to constrain their agency, it is just as crucial for researchers to explore how asylum-seeking women express their agency in resettlement as it is to identify the ways they have been vulnerablized throughout migration and resettlement. Research that examines asylum-seeking mothers’ resettlement experiences through this more nuanced lens of agency has the potential to challenge the image of helplessness without overlooking the structural context.

Research Aims and Questions

This paper aims to understand asylum-seeking mothers’ expressions of agency during their resettlement experiences in Montreal. Specifically, this study asks: What are the structural constraints asylum-seeking mothers experience in resettlement, and how does their agency manifest within these constraints? Through grappling with mothers’ experiences, this research can challenge resettlement policies that thwart families’ self-determination and promote research and interventions that recognize and facilitate agency (Cleveland et al., 2018).

Theoretical Framework

One of the central issues in scholars’ conceptualizations of agency is the relationship between agency and power. While social systems are often thought to exert power over subordinated agents that exist within that system, this need not be a unidirectional relationship. Foucault proposed in his formulation of subjectification that agency of the subject is not what exists before subordination; rather, agency is the product of operations of power (Foucault, 1983). Giddens (1979) framed this idea in his structuration theory as a duality of structure where “structure is both the medium and outcome of the reproduction of practices” (p. 69). Hence, any social structure can be both constraining and enabling for the agent (Giddens, 1984). For this paper, we outline the construct of agency as understood by Ortner (2006) and Mahmood (2006). Their framework allowed us to interpret nuanced forms of agency that exist within and are enabled by the structure even when agents do not achieve emancipation through overtly and persistently challenging these constraints. Here, I start with the two forms of agency proposed by Ortner (2006), whose conceptualization is grounded in the structuration theory by Giddens (1979) and complemented by Sewell (1992), followed by an additional conceptualization proposed by Mahmood (2006).

Ortner (2006) argued that there are two ways to categorize agency: as a form of power, or as a form of intentionality. The forms these expressions of agency take are different. In a domination-resistance dialectic, agency is defined as a form of power, that is enacted through either dominance over others or resistance to subordination. This form of agency almost always involves actions contemplated or taken within systems of social relations (Giddens, 1979), usually determined or driven by resistance to the dominant structure.

When agency is framed as a kind of intentionality, it encapsulates one's capacity “for desiring, for forming intentions, and for acting creatively” (Sewell Jr, 1992, p. 20) in pursuing “a culturally constituted project” (Ortner, 2006, p. 145). These projects can be simple goals or full-blown “serious games […that] infuse life with meaning and purpose” that stem from cultural and individual values and ideals (Ortner, 2006, p. 145). Intentionality as an expression of agency contrasts with the domination/resistance model. In the domination/resistance model, an agent pursues a project that is defined “by the terms of the dominant party” (p. 145), which often involves “acting within relations of social inequality, asymmetry, and force” (p. 139). In the intentionality model, a project emerges from the agent's individual life, dreams, and beliefs and may thus exist “on the margins of power” (p. 147), that is, on the margins of the dynamics and structures of dominance and subordinance. Ortner (2006) also notes that intentionality-as-agency should be distinguished from routinized survival practices, as it is “the strong role of active (though not necessarily fully ‘conscious’ since a lot of times intentions are rationalized after the action) intentionality in agency that differentiates agency from routine practices” (p. 136).

Mahmood (2006) elaborated on Ortner's typology of agency, describing another category defined by endurance. Mahmood argues that while certain acts may appear, on the surface, to indicate that people are tolerating the “instruments of their own oppression” (p. 38), in fact they may be exhibiting “a capacity for action that […] specific relations of subordination enable and create” (p. 45). While this form of agency exists within power structures, its goal is not as straightforward as resistance, or confrontation with the dominant system and narratives. Nor is this agency similar to Ortner's intentionality, which manifests in actions that are in pursuit of the agent's own project. Mahmood's agency-as-endurance is illustrated in her ethnographic account of women's mobilization within mosques, termed the Mosque Movement, from 1995 to 1997 as part of the Islamic revival in Cairo, Egypt. Mahmood describes how Muslim women worked with, rather than resisted against, cultural and religious practices that may be seen as excluding and oppressing women by practicing endurance (sabr). In contrast to inaction or passivity, Mahmood (2006) proposes that these women “enabled their being, responsibility, and effectivity” by giving meaning and so choosing how they were to live within the structure of constraints (p. 42). Whether an act constitutes one of these kinds of agency or is a routinized practice depends on the meaning it is given in the particular context. Preparing a meal can be a neutral practice, or either one of the forms of agency. It can e.g., be an act of resistance if the person doing the cooking does so to oppose a structure in which they are not allowed to cook, it can be an act of intentionality if the person cooks the food of their home country to bolster their pride in their home culture, or they can prepare food as a form of endurance, when the person cooking experiences this as a demonstration or source of strength. In the end, it is whether a specific act in a particular context is experienced as empowering by the person doing it that decides whether it can be considered as a form of agency.

In summary, the theoretical framework used in this study combines the definitions proposed by Ortner (2006) and Mahmood (2006). As we have seen, Ortner (2006) theorized agency as i) a form of power that manifests in dominance and resistance to dominance and, alternatively, as ii) a form of intentionality in pursuit of a project independent of power relations. Mahmood (2006) proposed another form of agency as endurance marked by inaction and tolerance, generated through the struggle of people in subordinated positions. We thus situate our study and interpretations using these notions of resistance, intentionality, and endurance as manifestations of agency that are both limited and enabled by the structure in which asylum-seeking mothers resettle.

Methodology

In this study, we take a critical approach, meaning we aim to generate knowledge that is “firmly grounded within an understanding of social structures (social inequalities), power relationships (power inequalities), and the agency of human beings” (Bhavnani et al., 2014, p. 166). This stance lends itself to an analysis of social phenomena within a prevailing social structure, in our case forms of agency expressed in women's resettlement (Harvey, 1990). This methodology is particularly appropriate for our research as it will allow us to “shed light on oppressive dynamics and encourage emancipatory social change” (Levitt et al., 2021, p. 359), well-suited to research with marginalized communities (Paradis-Gagné & Pariseau-Legault, 2022). Practically, a critical approach urges researchers to engage in continuous reflexivity during data collection and interpretation, and values contextualized description of lived experiences (Bhavnani et al., 2014; Levitt et al., 2021).

Study Setting

This study was nested within a larger interdisciplinary project studying the implementation, adaptation, and consequences of a two-year community-based, multi-site, psychosocial program for asylum-seeking families in Montreal called “Welcome Haven.” Participants of the Welcome Haven program were recruited through local community organizations and the regional program for settlement and integration of asylum seekers. The core program consists of weekly in-person, three-hour drop-in sessions. The sessions were structured around an informational and recreational workshop to provide instrumental and emotional support for families, a shared communal meal to further foster community belonging, as well as arts-based interventions for children and youth. Following ethics approval from CIUSSS du Centre- Ouest-de-l’Île-de-Montréal (Project 2021-2461) in September 2021, the Welcome Haven program was implemented in October 2021.

Data Collection

Data was collected from March until July 2022 using semi-structured interviews (Spradley, 1979) with asylum-seeking mothers who had participated in at least two Welcome Haven workshops. As we aimed to include a diverse sample representative of participants that attended the Welcome Haven program, we relied on purposive sampling, specifically criterion and typical case sampling, as we selected the participants based on inclusion criteria deemed by our key informants from community organizations to be representative (Suri, 2011). The first author conducted all interviews with English-speaking participants and Spanish-speaking participants with the assistance of an interpreter, and two other research assistants conducted the interviews with French-speaking participants. All interviews lasted from one hour to two and a half hours.

All interviews were semi-structured starting with demographic questions followed by guiding, open-ended interview questions about resettlement experiences (e.g., what are some challenges that you have faced since your arrival?) and questions that elicited narratives of agency (e.g., as a mother, do you have different roles or power than you did before resettlement?). Most of the interviews were audio recorded following participant consent. In the case where a participant declined to be recorded, the first author endeavored to transcribe as much of the interview verbatim as possible during the interview.

Data Analysis

The transcripts were entered into a qualitative data management software (NVivo). Because the first author was English-speaking, all French interviews were initially translated with a reliable online software into English then reviewed and verified by the original interviewer to improve accuracy. For interviews conducted with interpreters (in Spanish), transcriptions were made of interpreters’ translations.

Coding was conducted both inductively and deductively following principles of thematic content analysis within a constructionist framework as described by Braun & Clarke (2006), as it “seeks to theorize the sociocultural contexts and structural conditions that enable the individual accounts that are provided” (p. 85). This approach requires thematic analysis at the latent level, which involves interpretative work that is not just descriptive but already theorized (Braun & Clarke, 2006). An initial coding map was developed with consultation of the second and third authors based on the common resettlement barriers and coping mechanisms from initial familiarization of the data combined with field experience and past literature. The first author coded all transcripts deductively following the coding map and inductively to generate other relevant codes. The codes were then iteratively reviewed and refined, and themes were generated and defined with interpretations that were consistent with our theoretical framework of agency in consultation with all authors.

Researcher Positionality

The researchers who conducted interviews identified as female and were present at most Welcome Haven workshops where they developed meaningful relationships with interviewees. Each researcher has a different ethnocultural background, immigration status, and position of relative sociodemographic privilege. The first author is an East Asian female in her early 20s with an immigrant background. Of the other two research assistants, one had lived experience of immigration and had grandparents who were refugeed, while the other was Québec-born with one foreign-born parent. Our migration experiences helped in relating to the adaptation challenges of the participants. Yet, we all had resident status in Canada as opposed to the interviewees, and were in positions of relative security, privilege, and economic stability. In addition, we did not share the same ethnocultural and racial backgrounds as the participants, though some of us have had experiences of racism and other forms of discrimination. The interviewers and participants had to navigate age-related dynamics (where the researchers were much younger than most women interviewed) as well as various power differences. In some instances, these negotiations led to a deepening of their bond, which enabled greater trust and openness. At other times we were prompted to reflect on the complexity of the researcher–participant dynamic (Deps et al., 2022).

Structural Constraints

Deprivation

Asylum-seeking mothers were regularly deprived of financial resources and access to services as a result of multiple structural constraints that were oftentimes interconnected to perpetuate further deprivation and exclusion. One of the structural constraints that contributed to their financial insecurity was the rejection of their professional credentials from home countries. Many of the mothers we interviewed were highly educated and held professional roles such as professors, lawyers, or medical doctors, which had not only ensured their economic independence previously but also provided them with a professional identity that was a source of pride. During resettlement, they discovered that their credentials were not accepted in Canada, and that their credentials were not readily converted nor could they enroll in further training as only primary and secondary education was funded for asylum seekers in Quebec (Cleveland et al., 2021). As Fatima pointed out: “[Before my migration] I was working in a lot of refugee camps [as a medical doctor] and now I am, myself, a refugee.” Instead of having a respected professional identity, as a refugee she now was someone, as she called it, “who has to start from zero.” Rosa, who had just finished a law degree before fleeing, explained that the devaluing of her degree directly affected her “morale and self-esteem.” Not being allowed to work at the level they were used to also meant most of them had to depend on welfare or take on low-wage jobs. During the interviews, they explained how their poverty limited their ability to make basic choices for themselves such as finding housing, or to fulfill their families’ basic needs including providing nutritious meals.

For Mary, the deprivation of access to subsidized daycare directly affected her ability to pay for legal fees to appeal a negative refugee decision:

If I can have money, I can take my son to daycare, I can find a better job, I [will] be able to pay my expenses like the rent, […] and the lawyers that I have to pay [for an appeal to the refugee decision] […]. But right now, I’m stuck in the middle.

Compared to pre-migration, when mothers often received childcare support from extended family and friends, this mother, along with a few others, felt trapped and “arrested” (Fatima) by poverty, a lack of access to daycare and a trusted network, which robbed them of their power to act by finding a job with better pay to improve the circumstances of their families. Mothers who did not work similarly expressed fear of their children not getting what they needed socially and developmentally because of the lack of daycare and education. Lack of childcare also compromised mothers’ ability to gain new skills such as attending French lessons, which delayed their integration.

Language barriers experienced by asylum-seeking mothers further deprived them of service access and consequently limited their parental role. Those who did not speak French or English reported limited ability to communicate with the teachers and administrative staff at their children's public schools. One Spanish-speaking mother reported that after consulting her son's French-speaking teacher on his academic progress, she still felt unsure on how to help her son with homework. Not being able to communicate with their children's school thwarted mothers’ ability to monitor their children's educational progress and guide them when necessary. Instead, their children's academic progress was completely dependent on the school. Beyond education, Fatima explained how the language barrier initiated a chain reaction that constrained her access to employment opportunities, which perpetuated her financial deprivation that eventually led to her “ability as a mother” being limited.

Discrimination

Most mothers experienced racism and discrimination during resettlement. In Quebec, a region in which speaking the French language is a sensitive and important issue (Banerjee, 2021), obstacles to accessing services sometimes occurred on the basis of not speaking French. For example, a Spanish-speaking mother from a South American country described being forced into mandatory quarantine despite having been fully vaccinated because her vaccination proof was in Spanish. Another Spanish-speaking mother reported being turned away from a healthcare clinic because a nurse insisted that the woman bring her own interpreter for the appointment, though ironically, upon returning, the mother learned the doctor could speak Spanish.

Next to language, several mothers gave instances of discrimination on the basis of being racialized, being an asylum seeker, or being seen as ethnically Other. As Fernanda said, “there was always an excuse of why they wouldn’t give us the apartment, it was like […] you have too many people, you are a refugee claimant, you are a Mexican … all these excuses.” Even though refugee claimants were supposed to be entitled to many of the same services as Canadian citizens (with important exceptions like access to subsidized daycare), many mothers had experienced status-based rejections from services and activities as simple as signing a petition, receiving a discount at a grocery store, getting a bank card, and occasionally in seeking healthcare attention, which Selma described as humiliating and “disheartening.” The effects of these instances of perceived discrimination were twofold: They limited women's ability to be integrated into the community and seek basic services such as housing and healthcare, and simultaneously these discriminatory acts conveyed messages of rejection essentially signaling to asylum-seeking mothers that they did not belong and were less worthy.

Precarity

Whereas deprivation and discrimination were experiences prevalent among newcomers, including immigrants and refugees, a significant constraint that was uniquely experienced by asylum seekers (and in some cases undocumented migrants) was the precariousness of their status and the uncertainty they felt because of the refugee claim process and eventual decision:

I’m trying to put things in order and trying to be that good person for me not to get any problem, but at the same time, I’m scared because like, I don’t have a guarantee here. At anytime, immigration will call [… and] knock at my door. (Tenneh)

Tenneh described a sense of fear and powerlessness as a result of her precarious status and the looming possibility of forced repatriation. Gloria also described her daily struggle with the idea of having to “start over,” asking questions like “who am I? where am I going?” These mothers articulated how their precarious status had disrupted their sense of continuity of life and forced them to reappraise (or think with fear about) their future.

Adriana shared her experience during a meeting with an immigration officer. Because of her anxiety elicited from the interrogative nature of the meeting and her lack of control over the decision, she found her ability to explain her reason to have chosen Canada compromised:

[The immigration officer asked] me: “Tell me in three sentences why you asked for refuge.” […] I didn’t even know what to say, because how do you sum up this? And she said: “Why didn’t you stay in the U.S.?” It was hard for me to explain that I didn’t want to stay in the U.S. because of the way they treat kids, because of racism. […] I heard of a mom and her kids being beaten up badly, just because [they were] Latino. So […] I didn’t want to stay there. But I didn’t tell her that. All these thoughts have crossed my mind, but I couldn’t get myself to tell her. [….] The words that came out of my mouth were: “Because I was afraid of bullying.” And she told me: “This is not a valid reason to not stay in the U.S.” [….] to this day, when I think about it, I get cold, I feel like crying. I know that we’re going to have a hearing, and […] I’m full of fear about […] the hearing. I live with that fear.

The uncertainty of her refugee status and the little power Adriana felt she had to express herself to ensure the outcome of the hearing left her feeling at the mercy of the immigration system, a system which she was not sure could hear her voice. Further thwarting asylum-seeking mothers’ agency was the time and financial cost that was often required to have legal counsel during the refugee claims process, especially when their initial claim was denied and they had to go through the arduous process of appeal. Although legal aid is theoretically free to asylum claimants in Quebec (Government of Canada, 2023), in practice there are few lawyers who accept legal aid mandate to represent claimants, resulting in most claimants having to pay out of their own pockets for legal counsel or to attend court without a lawyer. The anxiety and fear Adriana felt towards the hearing decision, combined with her limited financial resources and the power difference between immigration officials and claimants, resulted in her inability to speak up with control over her narrative.

Mothers’ Expressions of Agency

Resistance to the Narrative of Dependency

All the mothers expressed their eagerness to contribute to the community—whether it be the asylum-seeker community or Canadian society in general—and had taken both small and large initiatives to demonstrate their ability and willingness to help. Fatima stated: “I really want to be, very quickly, a positive part of the community. I do not want to be like someone who is always [helped by] the community and could not [give] back, that is not my nature.” Fatima elicited the common narrative imposed on asylum seekers as persons who are dependent and resisted that narrative by distinguishing herself as someone whose “nature” was to be “a positive part of the community.” The work many mothers dedicated themselves to was evidence of this effort. Three of the mothers we interviewed who had previously held high-status professional jobs worked as care attendants in residential long-term care homes for seniors during the pandemic, and expressed the joy they felt at being able to contribute, despite the risk to their own health during the height of the pandemic. Gloria described her internship at the seniors’ residence:

It was wonderful! It's good for me. [….] Some people have [dementia], some people are violent. There are some who have [lost] all their faculties. Helping those people […] That's what I want to do. [….] It was the mother role because they were babies. And you had to be their mom.

Gloria’s comparison of the seniors to “babies” shows how she viewed them as dependent on her maternal help. Contrasting to their common positionality as the helpless victim in the host society, these mothers were able to resist the dependent narrative in these caretaking facilities by proving their independence and capacity to help the needy. Even though the work was low wage and under-valued, the fact that a caretaking position—similar to “the mother role”—involved others who Gloria cast as even more dependent helped these mothers to temporarily free themselves from characterizations of dependency as refugee claimants. Mothers attributed their capacity for caretaking to their past experiences in taking care of their children (again a role reversal that cast them as independent) and their ability to empathize.

Gloria also expressed her desire to take initiative:

I started to think […] that [I] could have a business here, […] for me to stop feeling like I’m at the bottom of the ladder so I can […] get my confidence back… [So I …] keep [myself] busy [which] allows me to not see myself [as completely starting over] but […] as an entrepreneur.

Interviewer: So what would you have done as an entrepreneur?

Gloria: I’m thinking of a retirement home. [….] Everybody tells me [… the government] doesn’t let you do this, but because it's such a difficult challenge, I said I’m going. I’ve been through a lot of hard things. [….] Maybe [it will never] happen, but I’m breaking a path.

The connotation of “entrepreneur” implied someone who was resourceful, inventive, and ready to take on a difficult challenge. By understanding herself as an “entrepreneur,” Gloria resisted the position “at the bottom of the ladder,” envisioning instead that she might “move up.”

In addition to professional aspirations, most mothers shared examples of the ways they had already contributed to their community. One mother talked about volunteering with an association that helped asylum seekers. Another mother learned about the process of enrolling children in school during a Welcome Haven workshop and then helped her asylum-seeking friends enroll their children in school. Mary explained that she experienced being a participant in our research as an act of service and altruism: “I want you to know what is happening to me, [and] use that as a way of making some report or something that will be able to help other people.” Through the course of her interview, she enumerated the many ways she needed help, yet at the end she emphasized the contribution she still could make by sharing “what is happening to her,” resisting being depicted as someone who was only asking for help.

In addition to helping others, asylum-seeking mothers’ ability to take matters of their resettlement into their own hands also helped them to feel more empowered. Tenneh shared that she liked when the tasks of resettlement were not done for her:

Everything that you are doing, the social worker will not say: “I will call these people or text them for you.” You have to call them. So for me, it's a good way [for] me to be adapting [….] I never have the opportunity to [make] choice on my own. So I feel good, I feel proud.

Rosa shared her “feelings of powerlessness” when she was quarantining at a federal hotel, where she felt forced into a position of passivity as “everything was given,” referring to the basic needs (food, shelter) being provided to asylum seekers. Mothers’ struggle against having the tasks of daily life and resettlement done for them, and thus being put in a position of dependence, was a kind of resistance to being stripped of the many aspects of their self-determination.

Intentionality in the Motherhood Project

For most of these women, motherhood was the primary, most important project they actively pursued. Most mothers attributed their migration decision to protection of their children: “To see [my son] flourish, I can break any barrier. There are no barriers” (Cassandra). Another mother noted: “I’ve always wanted my kids to have that very good future […] I think that's what's been giving me strength to support them, it's a driving force for myself” (Daniela). These mothers made clear that their main goal and motivation in resettlement, if not in life, was to protect their children and offer them a better future. Two mothers in our sample sought asylum after fleeing gender-based violence. Aisha explained that choosing to leave an abusive relationship was part of her obligation as a mother to protect her children from the circumstances she deemed harmful and instead raise her children “the way [she] wanted to.” Despite the structural constraints she encountered, she was able to preserve an agentic position in her motherhood because of her ability to pursue goals that aligned with her values.

Part of these women's goal was to be a role model for their children. Rosa shared how her child viewed her:

My son said to me that anything my mom says she will do, she does it. So for me, coming here and having these moments that I have to push through has allowed me to be that example to them and leave them a sort of legacy that they will use as they navigate life in Canada.

Even though Rosa initially did not think to take pride in conquering mundane everyday challenges, because she confronted them with the intention of setting a commendable example for her children, they became expressions of agency as intentionality rather than mere routinized practices for survival. Similarly, most mothers chose to conceal their negative emotions from their children. Cassandra explained that “Sometimes I wait to take [my son] to daycare [before I] cry because I’m not going to cry in front of him. I have to be strong.” By suppressing negative emotions, these mothers’ intention was to protect their children from seeing their mothers’ weaknesses, which they perceived to conflict with the roles they aimed to convey as mothers. In contrast, Fernanda shared that she was able to open up more to her family in Canada by showing her “vulnerable side,” including crying. She felt she was able to give her children an “emotional education” that fostered a closer family relationship. In this case, her crying in front of her family was intentional and part of her efforts to teach her children.

Finally, mothers reported actions they took to ensure children's wellbeing during resettlement. Gloria shared her dream of a motherhood in which she enjoyed enough financial stability to take her children to diverse extracurricular activities. Instead, during resettlement, because of the social isolation and lack of service access, her life had been reduced to monotony. Nevertheless, she managed to create enriching moments with her children by turning off her phone to chat with them, watching movies, borrowing books from the community library, and listening to podcasts to learn new knowledge and skills. After receiving a donated piano, they took piano classes online and practiced together. Even without accessible extracurricular activities, Gloria was full of intentionality in her motherhood project, eager to create a happy childhood with different sources of knowledge and a close-knit family relationship for her children.

Endurance Through Meaning Making

In facing constraints outside of their control, one way that mothers endured was by giving meaning to these challenges. Aisha shared that when she was feeling down about her circumstances, she would remind herself that: “life is […] not meant to be smooth, it has to be some ups and downs.” A few other mothers shared their expectation of the resettlement process being difficult and emphasized that they sought to give it time. By normalizing and reframing their suffering, these mothers were able to make sense of the obstacles outside of their control that they nevertheless had to endure during resettlement.

Many mothers emphasized the importance of making the most of their present by refraining from looking back:

If I look back [at the past], I’m going to crash. […] I won’t be able to rest in this day and exist in this day. […] so for me it's about looking forward and being present and also surrounding myself with people that will help me and support me and make me feel welcomed. (Fernanda)

Fernanda was able to manage pre-migration trauma in resettlement by staying mindfully present. A few mothers mentioned during the interviews that their ability to focus on the present was facilitated by going to church and praying, as well as receiving community support. Similarly, Gloria shared how she focused on the present and practiced gratitude:

There was a moment when I said stop [thinking about the past]. Think of the wonders, think of all you have, think of the fact that you were able to get out of where you were. Stop thinking about the future, think about the present.

Gloria also mentioned that having a community social worker helped her stay mindful with the saying: “the storm is already here, now you have to learn to dance in the rain.” She noted in the interview: “it's true, I have to learn to dance in the rain. Today […] I can’t say completely that I dance in the rain, but at least I smile in the rain.” Gloria was able to endure “the rain”—the traumatizing past experiences and structural constraints she faced currently—by focusing on gratitude for the safety and possibilities offered by the present.

Mothers also gave meaning to their resettlement barriers by explaining how these challenges would serve their future. Gloria believed that the long wait time for her work permit was a God-sent opportunity for her to learn how to rest and recharge from her whole life of working. Fernanda reported she saw waiting as a chance to spend quality time with her children during a crucial developmental stage. Cassandra saw her transition from a relatively high socioeconomic status with maids at home to having to take care of own chores during resettlement as a process of becoming “human.” Aisha saw her struggle with the lack of childcare as a journey that was teaching her something and shaping her for something greater. Rosa explained how she gave meaning to the poverty she experienced during resettlement:

I [always] thought of myself as the least strong in my family but going through [resettlement] I realized that we all have that strength, we just have to find it. […] One example is getting welfare. Getting welfare is something I never would have done in [my country of origin]. Here I did it, and I had to overcome a certain pride and preoccupation with what others would have said.

Making meaning of their resettlement challenges allowed these mothers to endure these difficulties and narrativize their resettlement journey in an empowering way. Instead of lamenting the ways they had to passively accept structural constraints outside of their control, these mothers emphasized what the constraints enabled them to do, whether it be learning how to rest, or developing humility as a form of “strength.” Garnering control over the meaning of resettlement allowed these mothers to see themselves as agents who still had power as they endured the structural constraints that threatened to render them powerless.

Discussion

Through examining the accounts of asylum-seeking mothers, this study illustrates how different forms of structural power impose constraints on women and their families during resettlement. Yet, as much as the host society often cast mothers as destitute and powerless through deprivation, discrimination, and precariousness, the women in our study were not without agency. Rather, their agency was found in the ways they resisted the narrative of dependency; in their intentionality to fulfill their motherhood role; and in their endurance of suffering through giving meaning to their constraints.

Many of the structural constraints found in this study had been reported by past literature. A comprehensive overview of asylum seekers’ resettlement in Quebec conducted by Cleveland et al. (2021) highlighted the impact of financial deprivation and lack of subsidized daycare access for asylum-seeking mothers, especially single mothers: More than 60% single mothers experienced food insecurity compared to 34% in other groups, and the duration of their reliance on welfare was much longer as a result of their lack of access to subsidized daycare. Moreover, as identified in the current study, one of the main parenting concerns for non-francophone asylum-seeking mothers was their lack of ability to help with their children's homework (Cleveland et al., 2021). The current study provided further proof of these constraints and insight into the lived experiences of being subjected to these constraints.

First, we found asylum-seeking mothers expressed agency by performing professional or voluntary caretaking roles to help others, thereby resisting the dominant narratives which positioned them as powerless, passive, and in need of help. Similar forms of agency-as-resistance are documented in recent studies. Maculan (2022), for example, used ethnography to capture how asylum seekers in Italy resisted the unjust living policies in reception centers, such as the lack of personal space, by using clothes as curtains to create privacy in shared living spaces. Renkens et al. (2022) found that refugee parents resisted receiving healthcare (which pathologized children often without accounting for cultural difference) by refusing to seek out these services. Rommes and Chaer (forthcoming) showed that Syrian refugee parents defended their children against discrimination by emphasizing their identities as descendants of ancient civilization, thus resisting the host-society perception of refugees as weak and vulnerable. Our finding contributed to this growing body of literature that shed light on refugees’ and asylum seekers’ capacity for resistance against the dominant power, however invisible mothers’ power might be. Even without directly challenging the structural authority, their agency was evident.

Secondly, intentionality in the motherhood project was identified as another form of agency in our study. Although mothers in their acts of parenting were not directly challenging the way they were positioned, their intentionality lies in what Ortner (2006) termed “playing their own serious game” (p. 147) with its culturally significant goals. This resonates with the research of Ghorashi (2018). In her analysis of the agency of asylum seekers staying in a Dutch asylum-seeker center, she found that individual goals such as joining a soccer team or becoming a pilot were examples of the “serious games” asylum seekers play. This was described as agency in “a delayed form,” which was “inspired by dreams and desires without immediate actions” (p. 377). Both agency-as-intentionality (exemplified by motherhood goals and dreams in our study) and the “delayed form” of agency described by Ghorashi are used by those with limited power and resources who are nonetheless setting goals and pursuing their own projects.

It is important to clarify that the significance of the motherhood project may be best understood through an interdependent model of agency. In a Western paradigm that prioritizes individualistic goals, the emphasis on motherhood may risk reducing women's agency to being solely in the service of children and family, and thus reiterate pervasive stereotypes of women in the process. The interdependent model of agency, however, argues that “goals, desires, and needs of others in a relationship may be just as important as one's own” (Kitayama & Uchida, 2005, p. 139). In this model, one's pursuit of goals is inseparable from those of others, since the self is realized in relation to others. By understanding motherhood's goal as consistent with the interdependent model of agency, the mothers’ goal in prioritizing children's needs and wellbeing in this study is as significant as these mothers pursuing their own goals. Motherhood thus makes a significant culturally constituted project that is worth pursuing. This is not to say that the structural constraints were, in any way, supportive of the mothers. In their pursuit of motherhood projects, it was also clear that they would have rather wanted their children to have the resources their host country counterparts had, such as access to daycare, the language ability to help their children at school, or the financial resources to provide their children with an enriching environment. In this way, agency as intentionality is situated in between resisting power structures and not being able to change anything except the meaning they give to their constraints. Moreover, without the structural disadvantage these women faced, as women, migrants, and racialized Others, the projects and strategies they pursued may well have been different.

Finally, we found that when asylum-seeking mothers’ power was profoundly constrained, agency was expressed in the ways they endured resettlement challenges by making meaning, staying mindfully present, and practicing gratitude. Consistent with Mahmood’s (2011) agency-as-endurance, the mothers we interviewed were investing and struggling in the present liminal state in the hopes of achieving their dreams for the future. As found in our study, this type of agency is often supported through religious practices (Abi Zeid Daou et al., 2022; Renkens et al., 2022). Agency-as-endurance is a direct representation of Giddens’ (1984) “duality of structure,” where structure can be both constraining and enabling (p. 25), as the structural constraints these mothers must endure also enabled their meaning making. Ghorashi (2018) has observed similarly nuanced forms of agency in the Dutch asylum-seeking center. While she analyzed that the center constituted a “non-place” characterized by “inert emptiness” (p. 374), asylum seekers found ways to make this experience meaningful, as they stressed how “severe limitations in one's life do not have to limit one's inner world and power of imagination” (p. 384). In another study with Afghan refugees, endurance allowed the construction of ethical agency, which is the strength to make meaning of suffering and to accomplish the indispensable tasks of life. Thus, exerting ethical agency served to cultivate a respectable identity that was of cultural and collective worth (Hiitola et al., 2020). Similarly, the mothers in our study chose to regard the challenges they experienced during resettlement as opportunities for them to expand their skillsets and build stronger characters.

The current study is limited in several ways. Although we only interviewed 13 mothers, this sample provided a rich data set that allowed in-depth contextualized analysis. Nevertheless, the sample reflected those who sought asylum in Montreal but also attended our program, which may limit the representativeness of the sample. The majority of our sample were highly educated mothers who held professional degrees from their home countries, which might contribute to their strong reflexivity in articulating their capacity for resistance, intentionality, and endurance.

Conclusion and Implications for Research and Intervention

The findings of this study have the potential to shape how host societies represent refugee claimant mothers and also to help develop clinical and psychosocial interventions to better support their resettlement and wellbeing. Highlighting the role of agency in mothers’ fulfillment of their motherhood project during resettlement points to the importance of interventions that protect family functioning, cohesion, and the parental role. Considering how mothers had been deprived in their parental project (financially, informationally, and socially), psychosocial interventions that aim to restore social connections and facilitate access to health and social services (Koh et al., 2018; O'Shaughnessy et al., 2012) are likely to be especially supportive of these mothers’ agency.

Moreover, given asylum-seeking mothers often struggle to find spaces that recognize their capacities, providing opportunities for women to take an active role in their community may help them forge a pathway of integration into the host society (Ghorashi, 2005), and fortify their sense of agency (Ghorashi, 2021). This is critical, as agency itself has been found to contribute directly to wellbeing (Tip et al., 2020). Based on these findings, clinicians and program facilitators may also rethink the framing of interventions as unidirectionally offering help to asylum seekers, and rather invite asylum seekers to be leaders, co-designers, and facilitators in these programs. While the current study mainly found mothers to pursue projects and resist power in gender-normative ways such as through domestic and caretaking roles, future interventions and research should expand our current understanding by exploring how mother (and fathers) engage in meaningful cultural projects outside of their traditional gender roles and how structural barriers to fulfilling these projects can be addressed.

The policy implication fundamentally lies in recognizing asylum-seeking mothers’ desire and potential to take actions and contribute. Increasingly, policies in Quebec serve to disempower and limit asylum seekers (Cleveland et al., 2021). However, as this study has shown, asylum-seeking mothers are agentic actors who make the powerful decision to seek refuge and who have much potential to contribute to Québec and Canadian society. Policy and public discourse must shift from one that emphasizes the burden of refugees and asylum seekers (Nicolas, 2022) to one which, without dismissing the structural constraints they face (Embiricos, 2020), acknowledges their contribution.
