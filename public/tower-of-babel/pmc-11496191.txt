Intrathecal pain pump causing delayed acute flaccid paralysis: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Megan Finneran, Emilio Nardone
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key Clinical MessagePatients with intrathecal catheters for pain medication infusion should be aware of the possible complication of acute flaccid paralysis. While exceedingly rare, it should prompt immediate medical attention to rule out compressive pathology requiring intervention.Keywords: bupivacaine, case report, intrathecal, pain pump, paralysis

INTRODUCTION

Intrathecal infusion of pain medication to treat chronic medical problems has become relatively common. 1 A combination of opioids and anesthetics can be used to provide pain relief, decrease need for oral medications, and improve quality of life. 2 Bupivacaine, an anesthetic with an amide structure, is one such drug that functions by reversibly binding to the intracellular portion of voltage‐gated sodium channels, blocking sodium entrance into cells. 3 This blockage prevents depolarization, and in turn prevents the propagation of action potentials. 4 Transient neurological symptoms have been described with the use of local anesthetics administered in the intrathecal space, all such cases have been related to continuous infusion for spinal anesthesia. 5 , 6

Complications of intrathecal catheters have been reported from both programmable pain pumps and spinal cord stimulators. 1 , 7 , 8 , 9 Complications may arise from a compressive pathology related to the catheter, whether from a hematoma, abscess, or fibrosis accumulation. Other cases have described transverse myelitis after catheter insertion, with identifiable features on imaging. 10 However, this is the first case to describe acute paraplegia from a morphine and bupivacaine infusion that spontaneously improved within hours of onset.

CASE HISTORY

A 67‐year‐old female with past medical history of complete heart block with pacemaker device, chronic kidney disease stage IV, hypertension, and chronic pain with intrathecal pain pump presented to an outside emergency department (ED) via emergency medical services (EMS) with acute onset flaccid paralysis of the lower extremities and a complete sensory level below T4.

The SynchroMed II Medtronic pump was originally placed 8 years prior and had been replaced due to end‐of‐life status 6 years after implantation. At each appointment with her pain physician, 40 mL of medication was filled. The concentration of the pump included 20 mg/mL of morphine with a daily dose of 10 mg and 40 mg/mL of bupivacaine [1:2 ratio morphine: bupivacaine]. She had a personal therapy manager dose of 0.6 mg every 2 h with a maximum of eight doses per 24 hours. At her last pump refill 3 weeks prior to ED presentation, the infusion was increased from 7.2 mg to 10 mg per 24 h.

The patient reported that she self‐administered a bolus and within minutes experienced numbness in her left foot. Minutes after onset, she then felt numbness in the right foot that quickly developed to flaccid paralysis of both lower extremities that caused her to fall. She was unable to move off the floor and had to use a verbal command on her cell phone to call for help. She recalled numbness up to her chest and subjective weakness in the upper extremities at this time as well. Upon EMS arrival, she was hypotensive with systolic blood pressure between 60 and 70 mmHg.

On arrival in the ED, her blood pressure was 127/48, heart rate 94, respiration rate 20, temperature 97.7° Fahrenheit with 100% oxygen saturation on room air. She had flaccid paralysis of both lower extremities, absent reflexes to both lower extremities, and a sensory level up to the level of the nipple.

METHODS

Computed tomography (CT) thoracic spine in the ED showed continuity of the catheters and termination at the level of T4 (Figure 1). There was no identifiable compression.

Sagittal computed tomography (CT) thoracic spine without contrast (A) showed the tip of the catheter terminating at the level of T4, on the right side of the spinal cord. Axial CT (B) failed to demonstrate any compressive pathology.

Two hours after arrival to the ED, she began slowly regaining motor function and was able to wiggle the toes on her left foot. She was transferred to our facility for neurosurgical evaluation and to undergo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

RESULTS

Six hours later, she had regained almost complete strength in both lower extremities. She had persistent weakness in left hip flexion and knee extension (4/5), as well as persistent numbness below the level of T4.

MRI with and without contrast was negative for compressive pathology (Figure 2). No alterations were made to the patient's pain regimen. The numbness in the right leg improved over the following week; the left leg numbness continued. The patient was ultimately discharged to skilled nursing facility for continued therapy.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) thoracic spine without contrast (A) was negative for stenosis at the level of the catheter tip. MRI thoracic spine with contrast (B) was negative for enhancing fibrosis around the catheter tip or enhancement of the spinal cord to suggest myelitis.

DISCUSSION

The definitive cause of our patient's paraplegia remains unknown. Imaging showed continuity of the pump with no breakage of the catheter. No extravasation of fluid was seen along the track or at the tip of the catheter to suggest leakage. The pump was interrogated by a representative and was deemed to be performing normally. The spontaneous recovery of weakness supports a temporary anesthetic effect from bupivacaine. It is possible with the change in medication concentration to increase the patient's morphine, the increase of bupivacaine concentration caused an unintended consequence of paralysis. However, the occurrence of the paralysis 3 weeks after the change was made seems unusual. More likely, a positional change may have caused a temporary kink in the catheter, prohibiting one bolus from being administered. Then a subsequent bolus delivered after the catheter unkinked caused an overdose of the medication received at one time. Imaging did not reveal any fibrosis or other cause of compression around the catheters. There was no spinal cord pathology such as transverse myelitis observed.

There are no identified articles in the literature of a patient developing acute paralysis with spontaneous improvement in the setting of a programmable pain pump and associated intrathecal catheters. There are several articles of patients with intrathecal devices, including pain pump or spinal cord stimulator, that describe permanent leg weakness.

One report of 61 patients included eight with neurologic complications, all of which were traced to an outpatient pharmacy that inadvertently included methadone and methanol in the morphine administered via the pump. 1 Of these, three patients had permanent leg weakness due to abscess, despite laminectomy to evacuate the abscess. Myelitis was the cause of weakness in another case, in which a patient with chronic low back pain treated with a morphine pump developed acute paraplegia 16 months after implantation. 7 The patient was diagnosed with progressive necrotic myelopathy, a form of transverse myelitis. Three months after paralysis onset, the intrathecal morphine was discontinued, and the patient had significant worsening of his pain. His neurological status did not improve, and serial MRIs showed radiologic progression of spinal cord involvement. Another case report presented a patient who developed progressive paraplegia 10 years after spinal cord stimulator implantation. 9 CT myelogram showed fibrotic tissue around the catheter, causing thoracic stenosis at the level of the electrodes. Despite surgical removal, the patient's neurological status failed to improve.

Bupivacaine has become more frequent in use for intrathecal administration by programmable pump. A survey in 2000 reported that 68% of pain practitioners had trialed adding bupivacaine to morphine for intrathecal infusion, 12 and that percentage has undoubtedly risen. Neurotoxicity with bupivacaine has shown to vary among patients, with some experiencing weakness as low as 5 mg per day and others tolerating doses up to 15 mg per day. 13

With regards to the use of bupivacaine, one case of paraplegia was reported after an accidental continuous subdural infusion of levobupivacaine after a cesarean section. 11 Another case described acute‐onset quadriplegia and cardiac arrest after an interscalene nerve block of bupivacaine/ropivacaine. 13 The patient recovered completely within 30 hours. Another patient developed hypotension, lower extremity flaccid paralysis, obtundation, and status epilepticus immediately after a pump refill of morphine and bupivacaine. 14 The effects were believed to be due to a malfunction in which the medications were accidentally injected into the subcutaneous tissues around the pump and systemically absorbed, resulting in systemic absorption and neurotoxicity.

Transient neurological symptoms have frequently been reported related to anesthetics such as lidocaine or bupivacaine, but they have largely occurred after spinal anesthesia. One case report cited four patients who developed transient symptoms of motor weakness and paresthesias after bolus injection of 5% lidocaine into the subarachnoid space for surgery. 6 Permanent neurological deficits after spinal anesthesia are exceedingly rare, estimated to range from 0.01% to 0.7%. 5 Complications related to intrathecal administration of bupivacaine have been scarcely reported. One case was described of a patient who developed progressive lower extremity weakness and paresthesias 1 week after pump refill. 10 MRI demonstrated cauda equina enhancement, suggestive of arachnoiditis.

CONCLUSION

The paucity of reported cases related to acute paralysis in a patient with an intrathecal pain pump raises the importance of such literature. Indeed, our patient was understandably fearful when she developed symptoms of flaccid paralysis. Close observation without use of steroids permitted her spontaneous recovery. This case should encourage providers to alert patients to this possible side effect. Our patient's paralysis was precipitated minutes prior by paresthesias in the feet. Patients with intrathecal administration of anesthetics such as bupivacaine should be aware of the possibility of neurological complications and the necessity to seek medical attention.
