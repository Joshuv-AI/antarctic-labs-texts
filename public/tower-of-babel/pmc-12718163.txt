Comparative effects of sous vide cooking on physicochemical properties, volatile compound profiles, and metabolomic changes between rex and Hyla rabbits
Authors: Shuyun Liu, Hongjun Li, Bin Wen, Wenzhen Zhang, Zhifei He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the effects of sous vide cooking (SV) and breed differences on rabbit meat quality, this study employed gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) and widely targeted metabolomics to systematically compare the physicochemical properties, oxidative stability, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and metabolites changes of Chuanbai Rex rabbit (CR) and Hyla rabbit (HR) after SV cooking. Following SV cooking, CR exhibited significantly lower cooking loss and higher springiness and chewiness compared to HR (P < 0.05). VOCs analysis showed an increased proportion of aldehydes in SV-treated meat, with aldehydes and alcohols contributing most to the characteristic flavor. Metabolomics further revealed a greater upregulation of amino acids and its metabolites in CR. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these differential metabolites were mainly involved in ABC transporters, arginine biosynthesis, and glycerophospholipid metabolism pathways. This study provides valuable insights for optimizing rabbit meat processing and promoting the development of rabbit industry.Keywords: Oryctolagus cuniculus, Sous vide cooking, Texture, Flavor, Metabolomics

Introduction

With the growing awareness of health and nutrition among consumers, there has been a continuous increase in global demand for high-protein, low-fat meat products (Font-i-Furnols & Guerrero, 2025). This trend has driven the exploration and utilization of alternative high-quality protein sources beyond traditional meats. Globally, rabbit meat holds a niche but significant position, especially in countries like China, Italy, Spain, France and parts of Eastern Europe, where it has a long culinary tradition and is appreciated for its distinct taste and nutritional value (Kumar et al., 2025). As a non-conventional meat source, rabbit meat has garnered increasing attention from both consumers and researchers due to its high protein content, low fat levels, and fine muscle fiber structure. In addition to its notable nutritional profile, rabbit meat possesses several advantages, such as low connective tissue content, high digestibility, and ease of processing, making it a promising source of animal protein (Zamaratskaia et al., 2023). However, despite these advantages and its established presence in certain regions, its global consumption remains relatively low compared to major livestock species, and its market penetration is often limited by cultural preferences and processing infrastructure (Kumar et al., 2025; Rao et al., 2022). In particular, progress in the development and quality improvement of processed rabbit meat products has been insufficient. Currently, rabbit meat is predominantly available in the form of chilled, segmented cuts, with minimal variation in processing techniques and limited added value. Therefore, identifying and applying processing technologies that can significantly enhance the sensory qualities, flavor, and nutritional retention of rabbit meat is of great importance. Such advancements are essential not only for improving consumer acceptance but also for promoting the diversification and advancement of the rabbit meat industry, requiring a deeper understanding of underlying biochemical changes.

Sous vide (SV) cooking, an innovative and emerging precision thermal processing technique, has demonstrated considerable application potential in the meat processing industry in recent years (Latoch et al., 2023). This technology can significantly improve the textural and flavor properties of meat by heating vacuum-sealed ingredients at lower temperatures (typically 50–80 °C) for prolonged periods of time. Studies have shown that SV cooking is effective in decreasing cooking loss (Modzelewska-Kapituła et al., 2019) and enhancing water holding capacity while inhibiting lipid and protein oxidation compared to conventional high-temperature cooking methods (Yuan et al., 2022) and reducing nutrient loss (Onyeaka et al., 2022). Notably, SV technology exhibits distinct advantages in flavor control by preserving lipid-soluble flavor precursors, thereby contributing to the formation of a unique and well-balanced aroma profile. These attributes collectively influence the overall sensory quality of meat products (Yuan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023). However, research on the application of SV technology in rabbit meat processing remains limited, with most of the results focusing on traditional meat sources such as beef, pork and poultry (Chen et al., 2025; Sheng et al., 2024; Supaphon et al., 2021). Within the domain of rabbit meat, existing investigations are largely restricted to single breeds and primarily address basic physicochemical properties, fatty acid composition, and the changes of flavor and microorganisms during processing (Kačániová et al., 2024; Rao et al., 2022; Rasinska et al., 2019). Critically, a comprehensive investigation into the combined effects of SV processing and breed-specific responses on rabbit meat quality, particularly involving detailed volatile compound profiles, metabolomic alterations, and sensory evaluation, is currently lacking. Such exploration in crucial for leveraging SV technology to optimize rabbit meat products.

In fact, significant differences in meat quality and the adaptability to processing technologies may exist among different rabbit breeds due to variations in their genetic backgrounds, muscle fiber types, and fat deposition capabilities (Kuang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). For instance, the Hyla rabbit (HR) is a widely raised and typical meat breed, while the Rex rabbit, particularly the local breed — the Chuanbai Rex rabbit (CR) recently developed in China is renowned for its fur quality but has often been overlooked for its meat production potential (Liu et al., 2024). Notably, systematic research on the specific meat characteristics and processing adaptability of the CR, especially under modern cooking techniques like SV, remains critically limited, thereby significantly hindering its promotion and high-value development as a meat breed despite its untapped potential as a valuable new meat resource. Therefore, it is essential to employ scientific research methods to compare the quality differences of CR and conventional meat rabbit breeds under SV processing conditions and to explore the mechanisms underlying their different responses to heat treatment. This not only contributes to the discovery of the meat potential of Rex rabbits but also offers new insights for the expansion of the rabbit meat industry.

Recently, with the advancement of food flavor and metabolomics research technologies, emerging analytical techniques such as gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) and widely targeted metabolomics have been broadly applied in meat product research (Li et al., 2024; Weng et al., 2022). Based on the above background, the present study was conducted to systematically compare raw and cooked meat samples of HR and CR under uniform SV cooking conditions to assess the changes in physicochemical properties, oxidative stability, VOCs, sensory attributes and metabolite profiles. This integrated approach aims to comprehensively elucidate the breed-specific quality differences and underlying biochemical mechanisms occurring during SV processing. This study not only enriches the theoretical foundation of deep processing of rabbit meat and provides a reference for the optimization of SV technology in rabbit meat but also offers a practical pathway and scientific support for the development and industrialization of CR.

Materials and methods

All animal experimental protocols in this study were reviewed and approved by the Sichuan Academy of Grassland Science (Sichuan, China), in accordance with the principles of animal protection and welfare, as well as the relevant provisions of the animal welfare ethics regulations of the People's Republic of China. (Permit Number: 2024008).

Sample collection

A total of 30 male CR and 30 male HR, all in good health, with comparable body weights (2.0–2.5 kg) and an age of 75 days, were provided by the Sichuan Academy of Grassland Science (Sichuan, China). They were raised under standardized indoor conditions and had ad libitum access to fed and clean drinking water. All rabbits were slaughtered according to the method prescribed by Agricultural Industry Standard of the People's Republic of China (NY∕T 4272–2023). Specifically, at 75 days, the rabbit was hung upside down on the hooks and stunned with electric shock before bleeding from the neck. After exsanguinating (3 min), the skin, head, viscera and distal part of legs were removed. Subsequently, the carcasses were immediately transported to the laboratory under 2–6 °C conditions. After 24 h of postmortem aging, the muscle of M. longissimus thoracis et lumborum (LTL) was excised from each carcass and excess connective tissue and fat were removed. The LTL muscles from each breed were thoroughly mixed and stored at −20 °C for analysis. Only analytical or high-grade chemicals were used throughout the study.

Sample preparation

The LTL samples from both rabbit breeds were cut into meat cubes (approximately 2 cm × 2 cm × 2 cm). The samples were sealed in food-grade sous vide cooking bags, and the meat of CR and HR were labeled as CR-SVR and HR-SVR, respectively. Subsequently, following the method of Kačániová et al. (2024), the two varieties of rabbit meat samples were heated in a thermostat water bath at 65 °C for 60 min, immediately cooled in ice water for 20 min for analyzed. Untreated raw meat was used as control samples (CR-CK, HR-CK).

Sensory analysis

Sensory evaluation was carried out by 10 trained postgraduate students in food science (5 male and 5 females). During the evaluation process, panelists scored the samples according to established sensory evaluation criteria. The testing environment was quiet and odor-free. Panelists were required to rinse their mouths with purified water between different samples and were instructed to avoid communication with each other. These sensory evaluation criteria are detailed in Table S1.

Determination of color

For the color measurement of rabbit meat, a color photometer (CM-5, Konica Minolta, Japan) was calibrated before being used to evaluate the sample color. The parameters L*, a*and b* correspond to lightness, redness, and yellowness, respectively.

Determination of texture profile

With slight modification to the method of Sheng et al. (2024), the samples were cut into 1 cm cubes. Texture profile analysis was performed at 25 °C using a cylinder probe (P/ 36R) on a TA. XT Plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK). The probe was set to move downward at a speed of 2 mm/s, with a compression strain of 60 % and a trigger force of 5.0 g for all measurements.

TBARS

Lipid oxidation was assessed by measuring thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) according to the method described by Liu et al. (2024). Specifically, 5.0 g of the sample was homogenized with 20 mL of ultrapure water, followed by thorough mixing with 20 mL of 10 % (w/v) trichloroacetic acid. The mixture was filtered through double-layered filter paper, and 5 mL of the filtrate was then mixed with an equal volume of thiobarbituric acid (TBA) solution (0.02 mol/L). The reaction mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 40 min, cooled to room temperature, and the absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a spectrophotometer. A standard curve was generated using 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane, and the results were expressed as mg MDA/kg meat.

Total sulfhydryl contents

The total sulfhydryl content was quantified using a commercial assay kit (G0130F, Grace Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China), based on the 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) method. Results were expressed as μmol/g meat weight.

Analysis of VOCs

A 5.0 g portion of the homogenized sample was accurately weighed into a 20 mL headspace vial. Three replicates were prepared for each sample group. VOCs were analyzed using a Flavor Spec® GC–IMS system (G.A.S. Gesellschaft für analytische Sensorsysteme mbH, Dortmund, Germany) and the method used by Jin et al. (2023), with slight modification. Separation was achieved on an MXT-WAX capillary column (30 m × 0.53 mm × 1 μm). Samples were incubated at 80 °C for 20 min, and 500 μL of headspace gas was automatically injected at 85 °C using a heated syringe. Retention indices (RIs) of VOCs were calculated using a series of C4–C9 n-ketones including 2-butanone, 2-pentanone, 2-hexanone, 2-heptanone, 2-octanone, and 2-nonanone (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Beijing Co., Ltd., China) as external references. Identification of compounds was based on comparison of both RIs and drift times (DTs) with entries in the NIST library (version 2020) and the instrument's built-in IMS database.

Widely targeted metabolomics and data analysis

The analysis was conducted by MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Raw and processed rabbit meat samples were freeze-dried and ground (4 °C, 30 Hz, 20 s). A 20 mg aliquot of each powder was extracted with 400 μL methanol: water (7,3, v/v) containing internal standards. After vortexing (5 min) and ice incubation (15 min), samples were centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C). The supernatant (300 μL) was pre-cooled (−20 °C, 30 min), centrifuged again (3 min), and 200 μL was used for liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis.

Chromatographic separation was performed using an ExionLC™ AD system (SCIEX, USA) with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Milford, MA). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 % formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B), with a gradient from 95:5 to 10:90 (A: B) over 11 min. The column was maintained at 40 °C with 2 μL injection volume.

Detection was carried out on a QTRAP® 6500+ mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA) equipped with an ESI source operating in positive and negative ion modes. Ion source temperature was 500 °C, spray voltage was +5500 V/−4500 V. The gas settings were specified as 55 psi for ion source gas I, 60 psi for gas II, and 25 psi for curtain gas, and a high setting for collision gas. Data were acquired in MRM mode and processed with Analyst® software (version 1.6.3, SCIEX, USA). Metabolite identification and quantification were based on MetWare's self-constructed database (MWDB, Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) using retention time, ion transitions, and MS/MS spectra.

Data statistics and analysis

For the analysis of physicochemical properties and oxidation indices, an independent sample t-test was performed to compare the two rabbit breeds under the same processing conditions. The results are presented as means ± standard error (SE) and graphical were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.4 (GraphPad Software, USA). For the VOCs data, statistical analysis was conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS version 27.0.1 (IBM, Armonk, USA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test to determine significant differences. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Graphs were generated using Origin 2021 software (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were conducted using SIMCA-P14.1 software (Umetrics, Sweden). Additionally, VOCs heat map analysis and non-volatile metabolite data analysis were performed through the Metware cloud platform (https://cloud.metware.cn). Metabolite annotation and enrichment analysis were performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/).

Result and discussion

Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation of CR-SVR and HR-SVR samples following SV processing revealed nuanced differences in appearance, odor, taste, texture, and overall acceptability (Fig. 1). Both CR-SVR and HR-SVR produced acceptable sensory quality with moderate scores across all assessed attributes. However, distinct breed-specific sensory profiles emerged. As illustrated in Fig. 1, CR-SVR samples generally exhibited a more balanced and integrated sensory profile, characterized by superior muscle tissue morphology and elasticity. In contrast, HR-SVR samples showed a distinctly higher intensity in odor and taste (more pronounced meaty aroma attributes). Despite HR-SVR's advantage in perceived odor and taste intensity, the superior texture uniformity and visual appeal of CR-SVR led to a more favorable overall acceptability score.

Meat quality change

Cooking loss

Cooking loss can reflect the water retention capacity of meat. The cooking loss of HR-SVR (25 %) was significantly higher than that of CR-SVR (21 %) (P < 0.01), indicating breed-specific differences in water retention (Fig. 2A). CR exhibited superior water holding capacity compared to HR. Previous studies have demonstrated SV processing effectively reduces cooking loss and preserves juiciness in rabbit meat compared to traditional high-temperature methods (Rasinska et al., 2019), attributable to the combined effects of vacuum sealing preventing moisture evaporation and low-temperature cooking minimizing moisture expulsion. Cooking loss is intrinsically linked to meat juiciness. Breed differences in cooking loss have also been reported in other species. For instance, Suliman et al. (2021) documented significantly higher cooking loss in Harri (33.47 %) and Awassi (34.54 %) sheep compared to Najdi sheep. Such variations across breeds are potentially associated with differences in muscle fiber type, fiber diameter, and pH.

Effect of SV cooking on cooking loss (A) and color difference (B—D) of CR and HR. (B) L* value. (C) a* value. (D) b* value. Significant difference: * indicates p < 0.05; ** indicates p < 0.01; *** indicates p < 0.001; ns indicates no significant difference.

Color

Fig. 2B-D represent the color changes in CR and HR before and after SV cooking, respectively. The L* value of CR and HR increased after processing. This change is a common phenomenon for many processed meat products, and the loss of water during processing results in firmer meat that is able to reflect more light (Sujiwo et al., 2024). Regarding the a* value, CR-CK was significantly higher than HR-CK (P < 0.01), however, SV cooking had less effect on the a*value of rabbit meat. Typically, the -a* value is mainly related to the content and status of myoglobin in meat, and changes in this component are more limitedly affected during SV cooking (Sen et al., 2014). For the b* value, there were no significant differences between CR and HR, either before or after processing, which is in agreement with the findings of Stanisławczyk et al. (2024). The release of fatty acid oxidation products and the solubilization of meat fat during meat processing led to an increase on the b* value in meat.

Texture

Meat tenderness is considered the most important sensory attribute affecting consumer satisfaction. SV cooking exerted a measurable impact on the texture characteristics of both breeds, with breed-specific differences affecting the final eating quality (Fig. 3 A-D). SV cooking increased hardness in both groups, likely attributable to structural alterations in muscle fibers. Nevertheless, the relatively low temperature and prolonged heating duration inherent to SV cooking help preserve muscle moisture, leading to milder structural changes and thus a comparatively modest increase in hardness (Li et al., 2025) Post-processing springiness declined significantly in both breeds, with CR-SVR retaining higher elasticity than HR-SVR (P < 0.01). This parameter correlates strongly with myofibrillar protein solubility, water retention, and microstructural integrity. Yuan et al. (2025) reported that extended low-temperature cooking induces protein denaturation, consequently affecting meat elasticity. Similarly, chewiness decreased post-SV treatment, with CR-SVR values consistently exceeding those of HR-SVR (P < 0.05). This decline is mechanistically linked to collagen solubilization during SV cooking, which loosens meat structure and reduces mastication effort (Latoch et al., 2023). Cohesiveness signifies the meat's capacity to maintain its structure while chewing. There was no significant between CR and HR either before or after cooking, this finding is consistent with the typical effects of SV cooking on meat texture (Chen et al., 2025).

Lipid and protein oxidation

TBARS was used to assess the level of lipid oxidation in CR versus HR before and after SV cooking. As shown in Fig. 3E, the TBARS value of CR-CK was significantly lower than that of HR-CK, indicating a lower degree of lipid oxidation in CR meat, which is consistent with the findings of Liu et al. (2024), which may be due to the difference in fatty acid composition and content between CR and HR. TBARS values of CR-SVR and HR-SVR increased dramatically, indicating an enhancement in the accumulation of secondary oxidation byproducts in both breeds after processing, which consistent with the results from previous studies (Rasinska et al., 2019). While the vacuum environment of SV cooking limits oxygen exposure, the sustained thermal energy during the prolonged cooking period likely initiated or facilitated the breakdown of primary oxidation products into TBARS-reactive substances.

Total sulfhydryl content is an important indicator to assess protein oxidation and can reflect the degree of protein oxidation. Similar to the trend of lipid oxidation, the total sulfhydryl content of CR-CK was significantly lower than that of HR-CK, indicating a lower level of protein oxidation in CR (Fig. 3F). Protein oxidation is usually influenced by dietary fat content, antioxidant substances, and heat treatment conditions (Sheng et al., 2024). The total sulfhydryl levels decreased in both groups after SV cooking. Despite SV cooking inducing moderate protein oxidation, the degree of change was comparable between breeds. During the SV treatment, protein thermal denaturation and oxidation reactions occurred even at low temperatures, which led to a decrease in total sulfhydryl groups. Additionally, the reduction of oxygen exposure resulted in a slower rate of protein oxidation, causing a similar trend of decreased total sulfhydryl groups in both CR-SVR and HR-SVR. In conclusion, SV cooking significantly affected lipid and protein oxidation in different breeds of rabbit meat; however, there was no significant difference in the degree of oxidation between the two breeds after processing.

Analysis of VOCs in rabbit meat

Qualitative analysis of VOCs

Fig. S1 A-C show that rabbit meat before and after SV cooking had similar VOC species but with some differences in peak intensities. CR-SVR and HR-SVR had a more pronounced red color, representing an increase in the concentration of the associated VOCs compared to the CK groups, whereas a darker blue color implied a decrease in concentration. The results showed that the VOCs content of SVR groups were significantly higher than that of raw rabbit meat, which is consistent with the results of a previous study (Rasinska et al., 2019). A total of 45 distinct VOCs were detected, which included 19 aldehydes, 13 alcohols, 7 ketones, 2 furans, 2 esters, and 2 other substances (Table 1). Aldehydes were the highest among all VOCs, accounting for 42.22 %, which was closely related to their origin from the oxidative degradation of fatty acids (Zou et al., 2018), followed by alcohols and ketones, accounting for 28.89 % and 15.56 %, respectively (Fig. 4A). Aldehydes were the main flavor contributors in rabbit meat, and the trends of VOC changes of HR-SVR and CR-SVR were consistent compared to raw rabbit meat, with aldehydes proportions exceeded 70 %, while alcohols and ketones declined (Fig. 4B).

The fingerprint (Fig. 4C), combined with Table 1 data, revealed that SV cooking significantly increased aldehyde concentrations, particularly low-threshold compounds such as 1-hexanal and 1-nonanal in HR (P < 0.05). 1-Hexanal is the main product of linoleic acid oxidation, and as one of the most abundant aldehydes in all samples, its elevated level indicated that lipid oxidation of rabbit meat was enhanced during SV cooking. Increases in medium-chain aldehydes, such as heptanal and octanal, further emphasize that lipid oxidation still occurs under gentle heating conditions. Compared with traditional high-temperature cooking methods, SV performs better in preserving the flavor of fat and reducing the occurrence of browning (Baldwin, 2012). In addition, (Z)-6-nonenal, which is associated with the aroma of fresh cucumbers and green leaves, significantly increased after the SV treatment, implying the linolenic acid oxidation pathway was activated during the SV processing (Giua et al., 2013). (Z)-2-hexenal and (E)-2-pentenal are important VOCs in meat, characterized by an aroma commonly described as grassy and fishy. Although most aldehyde trends were similar across breeds, HR-SV exhibited significantly higher monomeric and dimeric levels of 1-nonanal, 1-octanal, and heptanal compared to CR-SVR (P < 0.05), indicating breed-specific oxidative stress responses, consistent with previous study (Liu et al., 2024). Alcohols, typically derived from aldehyde reduction or fatty acid degradation, have high odor thresholds and a moderate role in flavor modulation. In this study, 1-hexanol and 1-pentanol increased significantly after SV (P < 0.05), particularly in CR-SVR. 1-Hexanol imparts grassy notes and arises from lipid hydroperoxide degradation, and 1-pentanol derived from linoleic and arachidonic acid oxidation, contributes fruity aromas (Cheng et al., 2023). Ketones are derived from fatty acid and amino acid oxidation; most ketones have a fruity and creamy aroma. In the present study, the content of 3-hydroxy-2-butanone tended to decrease in both CR-SVR and HR-SVR, which is consistent with the findings of Cheng et al. (2023). 3-Hydroxy-2-butanone is produced from β-keto acids formed from triglycerides, which can impart a pleasant fatty flavor to meat products (Domínguez et al., 2014). The observed reduction in concentration is potentially attributable to its participation in the Maillard reaction. Conversely, the content of 2-butanone and 2-heptanone in rabbit meat was increased by SV cooking, with the latter playing a significant role in meat flavor development. Furan compounds are associated with a gravy-like aroma (Yang et al., 2025). Two furan compounds were detected in this study, and their concentrations increased after thermal processing. Among esters, ethyl heptanoate notably accumulated post-SV, likely via esterification of alcohols and short-chain fatty acids, contributing pleasant fruity notes despite their low sensory thresholds (Yeo et al., 2022). In conclusion, for raw rabbit meat, the contents of multiple VOCs were generally higher in HR than in CR, which might be related to the differences in fat content and flavor precursors between the two types of rabbit meat. As for the two groups of SV-treated samples, the overall trends of CR-SVR and HR-SVR were consistent, but the aldehyde and ketones contents of CR-SVR were still overall high, indicating that breed is an important factor affecting flavor formation after processing.

Multivariate statistical analysis of VOCs

The PCA plot (Fig. 5A) distinguished the four rabbit meat groups based on the first two principal components. A significant difference was observed between raw and processed rabbit meat. In addition, there was a clear separation between fresh CR and HR, highlighting the effects of breed differences and SV treatments on VOCs in rabbit meat. The OPLS-DA model used R2 and Q2 to differentiate between rabbit meat samples that showed significant variations. The four groups of samples were well clustered on the OPLS-DA score plots, with small within-group differences, while samples from different groups were completely separated. The parameters of the R2X, R2Y and Q2 models were 0.989, 0.972 and 0.952, respectively (Fig. 5B). After 200 permutation tests, the Q2 regression line intercept was determined to be negative, indicating a stable model with no overfitting (Fig. S1D). Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores was used to determine the key differences between CR and HR before and after processing in the OPLS-DA model. VOCs with VIP > 1 have a significant contribution (Cheng et al., 2023), as shown in Fig. 5 C-D; a total of 9 key VOCs (monomer and dimer) were identified: 1-hexanal-D; 1-hexanal-M; 2-butanone, 3-hydroxy-M; 1-hexanol-M; Ethyl heptanoate; n-pentanal-D; 1-pentanol-D; 1-nonanal-M; and 1-propanol-D. The results showed that aldehydes and alcohols contribute significantly to the flavor formation of rabbit meat after SV cooking.

Metabolomics analysis

Metabolite profiles of CR and HR meat

Meat contains a variety of flavor precursors, including amino acids, sugars, nucleotides, vitamins, and fatty acids, with flavor perception primarily derived from both volatile and non-volatile compounds. To comprehensively evaluate the effects of SV cooking on the metabolite profiles of rabbit meat, a widely targeted metabolomics analysis was conducted on four groups. In total, 1118 metabolites were identified in the present study (Table S2 and Fig. 6A), including 362 amino acids and its metabolites, 135 organic acids and its derivatives, 131 fatty acyls (FA), 100 glycerophospholipids (GP), 72 nucleotides and its metabolites, 70 benzene and substituted derivatives, 60 carbohydrates and its metabolites, 54 heterocyclic compounds, 35 alcohols and amines, 19 bile acids, 19 coenzymes and vitamins, 19 hormones and hormone related compounds, 18 aldehydes, ketones, and esters, 4 glycerides (GL), 4 sphingolipids (SP), 4 tryptamines, cholines, and pigments, and 12 other compounds. Fig. 6B displays the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 62.38 % of the total variance (PC1 = 36.05 %, PC2 = 26.33 %). Samples from each group clustered closely with their biological replicates, indicating high reproducibility. Clear separation was observed among the four experimental groups, particularly between HR-SVR and CR-SVR samples and their corresponding raw meat controls, suggesting that SV processing significantly alters the metabolite composition of rabbit meat. Compared to the 899 metabolites detected previously in raw Fujian yellow and New Zealand white rabbits (Zhang et al., 2025), this study found more extensive repertoire of metabolites associated with rabbit meat processing.

Screening of differential metabolites

The OPLS-DA model was used for analyzing the differences in metabolites among different rabbit breeds. According to Fig. 7A-C, CR-CK and CR-SVR, and HR-CK and HR-SVR were separated, indicating that significantly different metabolite profiles existed in rabbit meat before and after SV cooking. The model validation plots (Fig. S2 A-C) demonstrated Q2 values exceeding 0.9 for all groups, suggesting that the models were robust and reliable (Weng et al., 2022). Volcano plots illustrated the distribution of differential metabolites in each comparison group, where metabolites with VIP > 1 or P < 0.05 were considered significantly different (Fig. 7D-F; Table S3–S5). Interestingly, there were more upregulated metabolites than downregulated metabolites after SV cooking in both HR and CR. Specifically, 622 differential metabolites were detected between CR-CK and CR-SVR; among them, 385 metabolites were upregulated, with amino acids and its metabolites representing the largest upregulated class. The downregulated metabolites were primarily organic acids and its derivatives, as well as fatty acyls. For the HR-CK and HR-SVR, 577 differential metabolites were observed, including 313 upregulated metabolites, mainly 202 amino acids and its metabolites, while 264 metabolites were downregulated, which largely comprised organic acids and its derivatives, fatty acyls, and small peptides. These results imply that along with the SV cooking, there were more upregulated metabolites than downregulated metabolites in rabbit meat, they were mainly dominated by amino acids and its metabolites. Amino acid derivatives are essential flavor-presenting components that are released through protein hydrolysis during cooking to enhance meat flavor (Li & Liu, 2022). Therefore, SV processing significantly promotes protein hydrolysis in rabbit meat and leads to the release of amino acids and some small peptides, which are known flavor precursors that may contribute to the formation of rabbit meat flavor.

Moreover, differential analysis between CR-SVR and HR-SVR samples revealed 580 significantly altered metabolites, including 288 upregulated and 292 downregulated compounds (Table S5). The upregulated metabolites mainly included GPs, fatty acyls, amino acids and its derivatives, organic acids and derivatives, and nucleotides and related metabolites. Whereas the downregulated metabolites were primarily amino acids and its derivatives, along with organic acids and its derivatives, carbohydrates and its metabolites, benzene and substituted derivatives, and fatty acyls. GPs are the most common type of phospholipids, which can produce VOCs through non-enzymatic and enzymatic oxidation pathways, thus affecting the flavor of food and being an important flavor precursor substance in meat products (Weng et al., 2022). GPs is broken down into free fatty acids during cooking, and then fatty acids can be further oxidatively degraded into compounds such as aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols. Fatty acyls are likewise a key source of flavor and undergo lipid oxidation and Maillard reactions during heating, and differences in fatty acid composition in meat from different animal species can further affect the release and reactivity of fatty acyls during processing (Rasinska et al., 2018). Organic acids also play vital roles in modulating sensory attributes, while nucleotides are recognized as important taste-active compounds (Shi et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024). Previous studies have emphasized that breed and processing methods are critical determinants of rabbit meat quality (Rasinska et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2025). Consistent with these findings, our results demonstrate considerable breed-specific differences in metabolite profiles, which likely contribute to the distinctive flavor characteristics of rabbit meat products.

KEGG enrichment analysis of differential metabolites

To further elucidate the metabolic alterations in rabbit meat induced by SV cooking, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted for all samples. The top 20 significantly enriched pathways are shown in Fig. 8. The enrichment factor, defined as the ratio of the number of differential metabolites within a given pathway to the total number of annotated metabolites in that pathway, reflects the extent of pathway enrichment. In the CR group, SV cooking predominantly enriched pathways related to ABC transporters, vitamin B6 metabolism, biotin metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, and riboflavin metabolism. In contrast, the HR group exhibited significant enrichment in a broader range of pathways, including ABC transporters, vitamin B6 metabolism, thiamine metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, purine metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, and mineral absorption. Among these, the ABC transporter pathway, arginine biosynthesis, vitamin B6 metabolism, and riboflavin metabolism were significantly enriched in both rabbit breeds, suggesting they were the principal roles in mediating metabolic changes during SV cooking. ABC transporter proteins are widely found in all organisms and are one of the most widely identified protein families, and these transporters are essential in transporting various substances across membranes (Liu et al., 2013). As a critical flavor precursor, the altered metabolism of arginine can directly affect the organoleptic quality of meat (Dou et al., 2023). The enrichment of the metabolic pathway of vitamin B6 is likely associated with the degradation and transformation of this heat-sensitive vitamin and the accumulation of related enzymatic intermediates during heat treatment (Shaban et al., 2024). Furthermore, the enrichment of riboflavin metabolism points to alterations in energy metabolism and redox status in meat following SV cooking. Following the processing of the two rabbit breeds, CR and HR, under identical SV conditions, the differential metabolite enrichment analysis identified several metabolic pathways significantly associated with the variations in rabbit breeds and the quality of the processed meat (Fig. 8C). These pathways primarily encompassed glycerophospholipid metabolism, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, thermogenesis, pentose phosphate pathway, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, ferroptosis, and calcium signaling pathway. These metabolic differences not only reflect intrinsic breed-specific metabolic characteristics but also underscore the substantial impact of SV cooking on meat quality traits such as flavor, nutritional value, antioxidant capacity, and texture.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates distinct effects of SV cooking and rabbit breed on meat quality. Sensory evaluation indicated that CR meat scored higher than HR for muscle appearance, color, and overall acceptability, whereas HR received higher ratings for taste. After SV cooking, CR showed greater water-holding capacity, springiness, and chewiness. SV treatment increased the concentrations of major volatile compounds, particularly aldehydes and alcohols. Metabolomic analysis also showed higher levels of several amino acid–derived metabolites following SV cooking. Overall, this work offers guidance for optimizing SV protocols to improve selected meat-quality attributes and underscores the potential of local rabbit breeds for value-added processing.
