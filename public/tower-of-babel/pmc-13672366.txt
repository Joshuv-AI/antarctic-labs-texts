Reconstituting human and SV40 replisomes with Okazaki fragment maturation reveals synchronized leading- and lagging-strand synthesis
Authors: Amani Al-Amodi, Muhammad Tehseen, Vlad-Stefan Raducanu, Masateru Takahashi, Yujing Ouyang, Ammar U. Danazumi, Alfredo De Biasio, Samir M. Hamdan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eukaryotic lagging-strand synthesis requires iterative priming of Okazaki fragments (OFs) by Pol α, extension by Pol δ, and subsequent primer removal and ligation through Pol δ-mediated maturation of OFs (MOF). How the replisome coordinates these time-consuming lagging-strand processes while maintaining rapid and synchronized DNA synthesis remains unclear. Here, we reconstitute and compare the human and Simian Virus 40 (SV40) replisomes in the presence of MOF. In the human replisome, Pol α is recruited distributively to the CMG helicase for priming and transiently slows leading-strand synthesis by Pol ε, thereby directly coupling lagging-strand initiation to replisome progression. In contrast, Pol δ functions independently of CMG and remains highly distributive during both OF synthesis and MOF. Nevertheless, efficient recycling mechanisms of Pol δ maintain synchronization by prioritizing OF synthesis over MOF, resulting in prolonged accumulation of unligated OF intermediates without affecting leading-strand synthesis. In the SV40 replisome, where Pol δ directly interacts with the L-Tag helicase and synthesizes both DNA strands, leading-strand synthesis is similarly slowed by Pol α-dependent priming. However, MOF additionally slows leading-strand synthesis, revealing an additional layer of coordination required to maintain synchronization. These findings suggest a model in which the human replisome has evolved to simplify synchronization while maintaining replication speed and pave the way for a better understanding of its communication with other cellular processes.

Introduction

DNA replication is a fundamental yet inherently asymmetric process. The leading strand is synthesized continuously, while the lagging strand is replicated discontinuously as short Okazaki fragments (OFs) due to the strict 5′–3′ directionality of replicative DNA polymerases1,2 (Fig. 1A). This asymmetry demands precise coordination to ensure that leading-strand synthesis does not outpace lagging-strand synthesis, thereby preventing abnormal accumulation of single-stranded (ss) DNA. Although ssDNA is transiently stabilized by Replication Protein A (RPA), prolonged exposure increases the risk of replication stress, fork collapse, and genome instability3,4. Despite the successful reconstitution of replication in human, yeast, and Simian Virus 40 (SV40) systems5–10, the intrinsic mechanisms that synchronize leading- and lagging-strand synthesis remain unclear. At the eukaryotic replication fork, the Cdc45-MCM2-7-GINS helicase complex (CMG) unwinds parental dsDNA13,14, enabling three essential DNA polymerases to drive replication: Polymerase ε (Pol ε) continuously synthesizes the leading strand, while Polymerase α-primase (Pol α) generates RNA–DNA primers for the lagging strand, which Polymerase δ (Pol δ) then extends15,16 (Fig. 1A). Unlike Pol ε and Pol α, which are associated with CMG17–19, Pol δ lacks a direct interaction with the helicase20. OF synthesis begins with the recruitment of Pol α to the replisome, where it synthesizes an RNA–DNA hybrid primer (~ 20–30 nucleotides)21,22. Pol δ then extends the primer, requiring the processivity clamp Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen (PCNA), which is loaded by Replication Factor C (RFC)20. Pol ε also depends on PCNA for processivity but uniquely requires the RFC variant CTF18-RFC, ensuring its efficient loading at the replication fork23,24.

The discontinuous nature of lagging-strand synthesis imposes a major challenge to the replisome due to the need for frequent Pol α priming and the multi-step maturation of OFs (MOF), which requires gap filling, primer removal, and ligation25 (Fig. 1A). MOF is initiated when Pol δ displaces the RNA primer upon encountering the previous fragment, generating a 5′-flap structure that is cleaved by Flap Endonuclease 1 (FEN1) in a reaction termed nick translation (NT)25. PCNA then recruits DNA Ligase 1 (Lig1) to seal the nick, ensuring strand continuity25,26. Although MOF has been incorporated into a reconstituted yeast and human replisomes27,28, how Pol δ coordinates OF synthesis and MOF to ensure synchronization with leading-strand synthesis and the dynamics underlying this coordination remain poorly understood, especially given the distinct behaviors of human and yeast Pol δ25,29. Furthermore, primer synthesis is a key rate-limiting step in DNA replication across all domains of life, constrained by the kinetics of NTP binding and phosphodiester bond formation30. While helicase–primase interactions enhance this activity, their efficiency varies between systems19,31–34. Cryo-EM structures of the human and yeast CMG–Pol α complexes provide key insights into how these interactions facilitate primer synthesis19. However, it remains unclear whether this coupling influences leading-strand synthesis and its synchronization with Pol δ activities during OF synthesis and MOF.

In the human system, a particular challenge in lagging-strand synthesis arises from the limited processivity of the Pol δ–PCNA complex, which extends only 0.4 kilonucleotides (knt)35 compared to several knt in yeast36. This is attributed to fewer contacts between human Pol δ and PCNA, resulting in ~ 50-fold lower affinity of the human Pol δ–PCNA complex for DNA compared to yeast37–39. Furthermore, single-molecule experiments in a reconstituted yeast replisome established that although Pol δ can undergo concentration-dependent exchange, the exchange kinetics are relatively slow, with an average exchange rate of one Pol δ molecule every 16 min, allowing it to contribute to the synthesis of ~ 140 OFs40. In contrast, in humans, Pol δ–PCNA may dissociate before completing a single OF36, suggesting that multiple Pol δ molecules may contribute to the synthesis of each OF. Moreover, the reduced affinity of human Pol δ for PCNA compromises its ability to perform iterative cycles of NT with FEN1 during RNA primer removal25, whereas FEN1 enhances NT by Pol δ in yeast29. As a result, MOF in humans is ~ 55-fold slower than in yeast25,29. How the human replisome adapts to these constraints on Pol δ processivity to sustain continuous fork progression remains an open question. It is possible that protein–protein interactions within the human replisome stabilize Pol δ and promote its retention on DNA during OF synthesis and/or MOF. Supporting this idea, in the SV40 system, human Pol δ directly interacts with the viral large T-antigen (L-Tag) helicase, increasing its lifetime on DNA from ~2 sec to ~ 3 min35. In addition, the Pol32 subunit of yeast Pol δ (homologous to p66 in human) has been shown to stabilize its interaction within the replisome, likely through Pol α40,41.

To investigate how eukaryotic replisomes coordinate leading- and lagging-strand synthesis in the context of MOF, we reconstituted and compared two complementary systems: the human and SV40 DNA replication machineries supporting MOF. The human replisome captures the full complexity of eukaryotic replication, utilizing distinct polymerases for leading- (Pol ε) and lagging-strand (Pol δ) synthesis. In contrast, the SV40 system provides a simplified model in which a single polymerase, Pol δ, synthesizes both strands in conjunction with the viral large T-antigen (L-Tag) helicase42–44. Notably, SV40 more closely resembles prokaryotic replication, where the lagging-strand polymerase likely interacts directly with the helicase35. This fundamental difference between the two systems provides a unique opportunity to dissect the intrinsic mechanisms that ensure lagging-strand synthesis remains coordinated with the replisome in eukaryotes.

Results

Reconstitution of the human replisome in vitro

To reconstitute the human replisome from purified proteins (Fig. 1A), we assembled the core components, including the 11-subunit CMG helicase, the three DNA polymerases (Pol ε, Pol δ, and Pol α), the processivity clamp PCNA, its loaders (CTF18-RFC and RFC), RPA, and accessory proteins such as the Fork Protection Complex (FPC) and AND-1 (Supplementary Fig. 1), and followed similar reaction conditions and substrate design to those described previously (Fig. 1B)8. Briefly, CMG was first loaded onto an ~ 8.6 kb forked DNA template containing a primer for leading-strand replication. Pol ε was then assembled with the necessary components in the presence of only dATP and dCTP, allowing the replisome to be held at the 3′ end of the primer. Following a 5 min incubation, replication was initiated by adding RPA, ATP, rNTPs, dTTP, dGTP and [³²P]-dCTP. Analysis of the reaction products by alkaline gel electrophoresis revealed two distinct DNA species: long labeled fragments corresponding to leading-strand products and shorter fragments corresponding to OFs on the lagging strand (Fig. 1C).

We performed several control experiments to ensure compatibility between our reconstituted reactions and those described in the recently reconstituted human replisome8. Drop-out reactions confirmed activity of FPC and AND-1 components in leading-strand synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 2). Control reactions confirmed that CMG loading was essential for replication, as no synthesis was detected when CMG was omitted (Supplementary Fig. 3A, lane 20). In addition, we confirmed that in a minimal replisome lacking AND-1 and FPC, Pol α required direct interaction with CMG to initiate primer synthesis, as no priming activity was detected in the absence of CMG (Supplementary Fig. 4, lane 1), whereas priming and extension were observed when CMG was present (Supplementary Fig. 4, lanes 3 and 4). Finally, replacement of CMG with the strand-displacing ϕ29 DNA polymerase failed to support priming on the lagging strand (Supplementary Fig. 4, lane 5 and 6), consistent with prior observations in the yeast system10.

Previous experiments using both yeast and human reconstituted replisomes showed that increasing Pol α concentrations led to an increase in priming frequency and a decrease in OF size8,10, suggesting that Pol α priming is distributive, with more frequent priming events at higher enzyme concentrations. Consistent with these results, we report similar correlations among Pol α concentrations, priming frequency and OF size in our reconstituted human replisome (Supplementary Fig. 5). Collectively, these experiments support a unifying model in which Pol α is recruited to CMG through direct interactions, in agreement with the Cryo-EM structures of the human and yeast CMG–Pol α complexes19, and with biochemical evidence of CMG–Pol α co-migration in human8.

Pol α priming slows leading-strand synthesis in the human replisome

To examine coordination between leading- and lagging-strand syntheses, we systematically added lagging-strand components to a reconstituted leading-strand synthesis system. In the absence of Pol α and lagging-strand components, leading-strand synthesis proceeded at a median rate of ~ 1.8 kb min⁻¹ (Fig. 1D). This median rate reflects an average value for the entire population of replisome replication speeds in our reconstituted human system, whereas the ~ 4.4 kb min⁻¹ rate reported previously8 represents only the fastest-moving replisomes. Strikingly, the addition of Pol α reduced the leading-strand median synthesis rate to ~ 1.4 kb min⁻¹ (Fig. 1C, lanes 7–12; Fig. 1D), mirroring average replication fork velocities measured in human cells under physiological conditions45–49. This reduction suggests that Pol α priming slows leading-strand elongation. Control experiments confirmed that the slow down depended on primer synthesis, as omitting rNTPs (rC/G/U) abolished the effect in a statistically significant manner (Supplementary Fig. 3C), resulting in a leading-strand median synthesis rate of ~ 1.9 kb min⁻¹ (Supplementary Fig. 3B), similar to that observed in the absence of Pol α (Fig. 1D). Also, a side-by-side comparison of reactions containing Pol α without rNTPs to those lacking Pol α entirely showed no difference in the leading-strand synthesis profile, ruling out any inhibitory effect from Pol α alone (Supplementary Fig. 3A, compare lanes 4 and 21).

Next, we tested whether primer handoff from Pol α to RFC–PCNA influenced the replication dynamics. RFC-mediated loading of PCNA on the lagging strand did not affect the leading-strand synthesis profile, indicating that primer transfer does not contribute to fork-speed regulation (Supplementary Fig. 6). Similarly, the addition of Pol δ to initiate OF synthesis (Fig. 1C, lanes 13–18) resulted in robust lagging-strand synthesis without further slowing the leading strand (Fig. 1D).

To quantitatively assess the synchronization between leading- and lagging-strand synthesis, we measured the integrated intensity of synthesized DNA products over time (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 7). Both strands reached similar plateau levels, showing nearly identical nucleotide incorporation efficiency and only very limited strand displacement (SD) activity by Pol δ on the lagging strand. In the pre-plateau phase, lagging-strand synthesis followed closely behind leading-strand synthesis with a delay of less than 2 min at the synthesis midpoint (~ 50% total DNA synthesis), supporting the idea that replication fork progression is intrinsically synchronized. In addition, at their synthesis midpoints, the instantaneous synthesis rates of the two strands were found to be within ~ 1.2-fold of each other (Fig. 1E). This finding aligns with studies in yeast, where a minimal replisome (CMG, Pol ε, Pol δ, Pol α, RFC, PCNA, and RPA) maintained similar synthesis rates for both strands10. While our system includes the fully reconstituted human replisome, both studies consistently support the model of synchronized leading- and lagging-strand synthesis.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that Pol α’s interaction with CMG modulates leading-strand synthesis while all subsequent steps in OF synthesis, including primer handoff to PCNA and lagging-strand elongation by Pol δ, have no additional impact on leading-strand synthesis. Consequently, Pol δ must be able to rapidly extend the primer and recycle to compensate for the slow primer synthesis, keeping pace with leading-strand synthesis.

Pol δ is distributive and uncoupled during OF synthesis within the human replisome

Next, we investigated the processivity of Pol δ during lagging-strand synthesis within the human replisome. The distinct size distribution of leading- and lagging-strand products in our system provided a direct readout for Pol δ’s processivity. To assess polymerase retention on the DNA, we initiated replication for 1 min with pre-assembled wild-type Pol δ (Pol δWT) and subsequently introduced either a 1- or 2-fold excess of a polymerase-deficient mutant (Pol δᴰᵉᵃᵈ) to compete with dissociating Pol δWT (Fig. 2A). The leading-strand synthesis rate remained unaffected by Pol δᴰᵉᵃᵈ competition, further supporting the conclusion that primer handoff from Pol α to Pol δ and primer extension by Pol δ do not impact leading-strand progression (Fig. 2C). In other words, leading-strand synthesis is intrinsically uncoupled from Pol δ’s activities during OF synthesis within the replisome. Microscale thermophoresis (MST) binding assays further supported this interpretation, revealing that human Pol δ does not stably associate with CMG, whereas Pol ε binds CMG with a dissociation constant (Kᴅ) of 30.5 nM (Fig. 2D). Previous findings in yeast show that leading- and lagging-strand synthesis can be uncoupled under specific conditions, including lesion bypass, protein barriers, and nucleotide depletion50–52. Our results extend these findings by illustrating that OF synthesis by Pol δ is intrinsically uncoupled from leading-strand synthesis within the replisome under normal conditions.

The length and abundance of OFs progressively decreased with increasing Pol δᴰᵉᵃᵈ concentration, and this reduction was observed within 1.5 min of adding Pol δᴰᵉᵃᵈ (Fig. 2B, compare lanes 13–18 with 19–24). This reduction strongly suggests that Pol δWT frequently dissociates and reassociates from the primer-template strand during lagging-strand synthesis, consistent with its intrinsic distributive mode of action36,53. There are two possible mechanisms that could account for the observed inhibition by the catalytically inactive Pol δᴰᵉᵃᵈ: (1) complete dissociation of the Pol δWT from the primer-template junction followed by rebinding from solution, or (2) concentration-driven exchange in which the replisome-associated Pol δ is replaced by Pol δᴰᵉᵃᵈ. Our assay cannot formally distinguish between these two scenarios, as both would result in similar inhibition profiles upon addition of Pol δᴰᵉᵃᵈ. We therefore cannot exclude the possibility that a fraction of Pol δ remains bound to the replisome via CMG-independent protein contacts, while the exchange occurs at the level of primer-template engagement. However, if Pol δ were retained by the replisome upon dissociation from the primer-template, its high local concentration would be expected to outcompete Pol δᴰᵉᵃᵈ from solution for rebinding to the primer-template or the newly synthesized primer by Pol α. In contrast, single-molecule Pol δ exchange experiments in yeast show slow exchange kinetics within the replisome of 1 Pol δ per 16 min40, which indicates a more stable replisome-associated Pol δ. These findings align with previous findings that showed human Pol δ is inherently distributive compared to yeast Pol δ during both primer extension35,39,53,54 and MOF25,29, likely due to its lower affinity for PCNA37–39. Despite its distributive nature, human Pol δ was able to efficiently support robust OF synthesis and keep pace with leading-strand synthesis, demonstrating that Pol δ undergoes rapid and efficient recycling during OF synthesis.

MOF is slow and uncoupled from the human replisome

Previous results showed that the reconstituted human MOF reaction is slow25,55, raising the questions of whether Pol δ would acquire CMG-independent interaction within the replisome and if such interaction would enhance the rate of MOF and influence leading-strand synthesis. To address these questions, we systematically reconstituted the human replisome in the presence of MOF factors. The bimodal distribution of the lengths (longer leading-strand fragments and shorter OFs) of nascent strands observed in the previous experiments (Fig. 1C) reflects incomplete OF maturation due to the absence of processing factors. MOF involves RNA primer removal from the 5′ end of nascent OFs via NT by the Pol δ–PCNA–FEN1 complex to create ligatable DNA-DNA nicks, since human Lig1 cannot ligate RNA-DNA nicks25,26. A previous study identified primer removal via NT as the rate-limiting step in human MOF25. To test whether NT impacts leading-strand synthesis, we performed a time-course experiment in which FEN1 was included to support NT, but Lig1 was omitted to prevent nick sealing (Fig. 3A). Under these conditions, leading-strand synthesis reached its midpoint vicinity (~ 50% total DNA synthesis) after ~ 6 min (Fig. 3B) with a mean synthesis rate of ~ 1.3 kb min⁻¹ (Supplementary Fig. 8), similar to rates observed in the absence of NT (Fig. 1D). To reconstitute the maturation step, we added FEN1 and Lig1 to the replication reaction and monitored lagging-strand products over time (Supplementary Fig. 9B). This caused a progressive shift from short OFs to longer products and a steady increase in the full-length ~ 8.6 kb product, indicating ligation of OF intermediates into mature lagging strands. Control experiments showed that neither FEN1 nor Lig1 alone could support the formation of the full-length product, consistent with active MOF requiring both factors (Supplementary Fig. 10A–C). In the presence of MOF factors, accumulation of the full-length product visibly continued at much longer time points (Supplementary Fig. 9B, D), compared to NT alone (Fig. 3B).

To further validate that the additional full-length product reflects a completely ligated lagging strand, we employed a strand-specific nicking strategy that selectively cleaves the labeled leading strand at defined positions while leaving the labeled lagging strand intact (Fig. 3C). The nicking strategy resulted in complete conversion of the full-length leading-strand product into the expected fragments, confirming efficient and specific cleavage (Fig. 3C). In reactions containing lagging-strand synthesis components, OFs remained evident, whereas upon addition of MOF factors (FEN1 and Lig1), a full-length product persisted despite nicking, consistent with the formation of a continuous, ligated lagging strand (Fig. 3C). A time-course analysis of the nicked reactions in the presence of MOF (Fig. 3D) showed a progressive increase in the full-length ligated product, with a mean processing time (~50% full-length DNA synthesis) of ~ 14.5 min (Fig. 3E). Notably, the overall MOF efficiency reached ~ 60% (Fig. 3F), consistent with previous reconstitutions of isolated human and yeast MOF and NT25,29,55–57. Interestingly, the mean processing time (~ 50% full-length DNA synthesis) for leading-strand synthesis, determined from the nicking reaction, was ~ 6.5 min (Fig. 3E). This further supports our conclusion that MOF does not affect leading-strand synthesis and remains uncoupled from the replisome. This conclusion is further supported by the similarity between the limited yield and slow processing time observed in isolated human MOF reconstitutions (< 70% yield, ~ 3 min/OF25,55) and the values obtained here in the presence of the complete human replisome, suggesting that replisome interactions do not substantially enhance MOF activity.

In parallel experiments, we quantitatively compared NT-only (Fig. 3A) and MOF-containing replisomes (Supplementary Fig. 9B) by fitting the time-dependent accumulation of the full-length product (~8.6 kb) to an analytical kinetic model. NT data were well-fitted by a single incomplete gamma function (Fig. 3B), yielding a mean processing time (~ 50% full-length DNA synthesis) of ~ 6 min and a leading-strand median synthesis rate of ~ 1.4 kb min⁻¹, consistent with median product-length analysis (Supplementary Fig. 8). In contrast, MOF data required a bimodal fit with two incomplete gamma functions (Supplementary Fig. 9D), strongly supported by BIC analysis (> 10-unit decrease compared with the single-component model; Supplementary Fig. 9C, D). In the presence of MOF factors, a second, slower component emerged with a mean processing time of ~ 20 min. Since leading- and lagging-strand synthesis differ by < 2 min (Fig. 1E), this > 2-fold slower component likely reflects a slow MOF process. These analyses are in strong agreement with those obtained from the aforementioned nicking-reaction experiments.

Collectively, these observations indicate that MOF remains a slow process within the replisome, does not affect leading-strand synthesis, and that Pol δ is unprocessive and uncoupled during MOF. Nonetheless, Pol δ can rapidly synthesize OFs even in the presence of MOF, indicating its ability to recycle efficiently to prioritize OF synthesis over MOF, providing a mechanism that enables it to keep pace with leading-strand synthesis.

Pol α priming and MOF slow SV40 leading-strand synthesis

To determine whether leading-strand synthesis slowdown during primer synthesis, uncoupled lagging-strand synthesis, and slow MOF are conserved features of eukaryotic replication, we examined the fully reconstituted SV40 replisome (Supplementary Fig. 1). To track the replication dynamics, we used a 3.3 kb synthetic DNA template with a nucleotide-biased sequence10: one strand lacked dG, whereas the other lacked dC, allowing independent detection of leading-strand [³²P]-dCTP and lagging-strand [³²P]-dGTP synthesis (Fig. 4B). Attempts to generate a longer biased substrate for human replication were unsuccessful, but the use of separate polymerases in the human system still enabled direct synthesis coordination analysis across both systems. Replication was initiated by staging the reaction, first loading L-Tag, FPC, and Mcm10, followed by Pol δ, PCNA, RFC, RPA, Pol α, dNTPs, rNTPs, and ATP (Fig. 4B). FPC and Mcm10 were included based on recent single-molecule and bulk biochemical studies showing that these factors enhance fork progression in the SV40 system35. Time-course analysis confirmed the formation of both strand products (Supplementary Fig. 11A, B). Component drop-out experiments (Supplementary Fig. 11A) validated Pol δ’s strict dependence on ATP/rNTPs, L-Tag, PCNA, and RFC, consistent with its known PCNA requirement on primed ssDNA substrates59,60. Restriction enzyme digestion verified distributed replication across the template (Supplementary Fig. 12A), while primer synthesis by Pol α required L-Tag, as substitution with ϕ29 DNA Polymerase could not support Pol α priming (Supplementary Fig. 12B). This result mirrors our observations in the human system and is consistent with previous findings in yeast10.

As in the human replisome, Pol α slowed SV40 leading-strand synthesis (Fig. 4C), decreasing the median rate from ~ 1.0 kb min⁻¹ to ~ 0.7 kb min⁻¹ (Fig. 4D). The rate in the absence of Pol α matches previous single-molecule SV40 measurements35. To determine whether this slowdown was due to primer synthesis by Pol α or competition with Pol δ for the L-Tag, we performed reactions with Pol α in the presence or absence of rNTPs (rC/G/U) across different time points (Supplementary Fig. 13A). Blocking priming by omitting rNTPs restored leading-strand synthesis to the faster median rate of ~ 1.0 kb min⁻¹ (Supplementary Fig. 13B), indicating that primer synthesis causes the slowdown. Second, we compared reactions containing Pol α without rNTPs to those completely lacking Pol α (as in Fig. 4C) at the 3 min time point. The replication profiles were indistinguishable (Supplementary Fig. 13C), confirming that Pol α alone does not inhibit synthesis in the absence of priming. Together, these results rule out polymerase competition and support a model in which primer synthesis by Pol α slows leading-strand progression in the SV40 system.

To test whether leading- and lagging-strand synthesis remain synchronized, we quantified the integrated intensity of synthesized DNA products over time. Synthesis curves were correlated even more tightly than in the human system, with a midpoint difference (~ 50% total DNA synthesis) of only ~ 1 min, with similar midpoint rates and very similar upper plateau levels (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. 14), indicating synchronized replication synthesis in the presence of Pol α.

To examine the interplay between MOF and leading-strand synthesis, we assembled the SV40 replisome in the presence of FEN1 and Lig1 (Fig. 4C, lanes 15–21). Remarkably, MOF further slowed replication, reducing the leading-strand synthesis median rate from ~ 0.7 kb min⁻¹ to ~ 0.3 kb min⁻¹ (Fig. 4D). Control experiments confirmed that neither FEN1 nor Lig1 affected the leading-strand synthesis rate in the absence of Pol α at the concentrations used in our assays (15 nM FEN1 and 20 nM Lig1) (Supplementary Fig. 15A), ruling out competitive binding to PCNA as the cause of the slowdown. In addition, an M13-primer extension assay showed that FEN1 did not impair Pol δ’s primer extension activity, ruling out general inhibition through either catalytic interference or PCNA competition (Supplementary Fig. 16).

To investigate the cause of the leading-strand slowdown during MOF, we enabled NT but omitted Lig1. The results showed that NT alone reduced the leading-strand synthesis rate to the same extent as full MOF (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 15B). Next, we tracked lagging-strand synthesis over time using [³²P]-dGTP incorporation in the presence of MOF factors (Fig. 5B). The median DNA length of lagging-strand products progressively increased, reaching the synthesis midpoint (half-length DNA products) within ~ 13 min, whereas leading-strand synthesis reached the midpoint (half-length DNA products) within ~ 7 min (Fig. 5B–D). Notably, MOF in the SV40 system reached ~ 60% efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 15C), comparable to that observed in the human system despite the additional interactions between the lagging strand Pol δ and the L-Tag helicase. Discussion

Pol α priming couples leading- and lagging-strand synthesis in eukaryotic replication

By reconstituting the human and SV40 replication systems side by side, we were able to examine how Pol α priming influences fork progression across distinct replisomes. In both systems, engagement of Pol α is associated with a measurable reduction in leading-strand synthesis rate, revealing a conserved kinetic consequence of primer synthesis at the fork (Fig. 6A). This slowing might provide a previously unrecognized regulatory handle on Okazaki fragment length: by transiently reducing the rate of CMG–Pol ε elongation during priming, the replisome limits the amount of parental DNA exposed before each new primer is laid, thereby constraining the maximum size of the ensuing OF. Although priming events establish when lagging-strand synthesis can begin, our results indicate that overall fork coordination reflects the intrinsic kinetic architecture of the replisome, including the ability of Pol δ to efficiently complete each fragment. Recent structural analyses of the human replisome bound to Pol α19 provide insights into our biochemical observations. Pol α engages CMG at the leading edge, positioning Pol α’s catalytic PRIM1 domain near the lagging-strand exit channel of CMG (Fig. 6B). Tethering of Pol α to CMG is mediated by multiple interactions, primarily involving the N-terminal domain of PRIM2 of Pol α, and MCM3 and GINS of CMG (Fig. 6B). Given the slow RNA synthesis rate of Pol α30, a transient accumulation of ssDNA must occur between CMG and Pol α due to their opposing directional activities. Since the CMG–Pol ε structure remains unchanged upon Pol α binding19, we propose that fork slowing results from an allosteric effect on CMG translocation, potentially modulating the ATP-driven conformational cycling of the MCM C-tier motor domains. Alternatively, the entropic penalty associated with unwinding ssDNA under looped constraints may resist CMG translocation, providing a mechanistic basis for the regulation of leading-strand synthesis during replication.

The limited interaction surface between CMG and Pol α likely allows Pol α to transition between an autoinhibited apo state and a DNA-engaged priming state19. Our structural predictions further suggest that Pol α may retain CMG binding beyond primer synthesis and into the primer elongation phase19,61,62 (Fig. 6B). However, the extensive rearrangements required for transitioning from RNA priming to primer extension, particularly involving POLA1, POLA2, and the primer-template DNA (Fig. 6B), could transiently disrupt the interaction with the CMG, leading to Pol α dissociation. This dynamic behavior is consistent with the partially distributive nature of Pol α observed here, which was previously reported in human8 and yeast10, and is also supported by single-molecule studies of the yeast replisome40. Given that OF length is dictated by priming frequency, which in turn depends on Pol α levels, tight regulation of Pol α concentration is essential to ensure proper lagging-strand synthesis and its synchronization with leading-strand synthesis.

While the structural details of the SV40 L-Tag helicase bound to the full Pol α complex remain unknown, prior studies localize the interaction to the C-tier of L-Tag and the N-terminal domain of POLA263. This suggests that Pol α operates as an integral component of the SV40 replisome, likely enforcing a similar priming-dependent slowing mechanism as in the human system.

In bacteriophage T7, primer synthesis transiently pauses leading-strand synthesis, ensuring synchronization between the two strands64. An alternative model proposes that leading-strand synthesis proceeds uninterrupted at a rate determined by helicase unwinding, while lagging-strand primers are synthesized independently and extended at accelerated rates65,66. Our findings indicate that eukaryotic replisomes employ elements of both strategies: Pol α-mediated fork slowing ensures proper lagging-strand priming, while Pol δ synthesizes OFs in a fast yet distributive manner. Together, these mechanisms reduce excessive ssDNA exposure, enforce proper OF lengths, and support the inherently non-processive Pol δ–PCNA complex to remain synchronized with leading-strand synthesis.

Uncoupling OF synthesis and MOF governs human lagging-strand replication

Our findings indicate that human Pol δ does not stably interact with CMG under the conditions tested. Consistent with this, yeast Pol δ also does not bind CMG20, suggesting that a direct polymerase-helicase association is not a conserved feature of eukaryotic lagging-strand DNA synthesis. The uncoupling of Pol δ’s activities during OF synthesis and MOF in the human replisome is consistent with the observation that neither process affects the leading-strand synthesis rate (Fig. 6A). Consequently, MOF occurs spatially and temporally separated from the human replisome (Fig. 6C). This model aligns with in vivo studies showing that MOF can be delayed until bulk DNA replication is complete67.

Extending these observations to SV40, where Pol δ interacts with L-Tag, revealed an additional layer of coordination: MOF, particularly NT, significantly slowed leading-strand synthesis (Fig. 6A), suggesting that lagging-strand Pol δ is partially coupled to L-Tag during OF synthesis and FEN1-dependent NT (Fig. 6D). This slowing might provide a mechanism to reduce the accumulation of ssDNA and control the length of OFs until Pol δ dissociates from the nascent OF, thereby enabling L-Tag–Pol α to initiate primer synthesis. The slowing of leading-strand synthesis in SV40 likely results from an allosteric effect, where binding of the lagging-strand Pol δ, while performing the slow NT reaction, modulates L-Tag helicase activity (Fig. 6D). A recent study showed that the rate and processivity of SV40 leading-strand synthesis increased slightly upon the addition of catalytically inactive Pol δ, presumably through its direct interaction with L-Tag35, further supporting coordination between the helicase and bound DNA polymerases. Collectively, the comparison between human and SV40 replisomes in the absence and presence of MOF showed how uncoupling Pol δ activities during OF synthesis and MOF simplified the synchronization requirement in the human replisome to only accommodate the slow primer synthesis by Pol α (Fig. 6A). This simplicity ensures that leading-strand synthesis remains unimpeded while minimizing disruptions to lagging-strand synthesis, enhancing overall fork progression.

The intrinsic uncoupling of OF synthesis from the leading-strand synthesis under normal replication is consistent with previous observations under stress conditions. For example, a lagging-strand lesion can block Pol δ while leading-strand synthesis continues unaffected50, and the telomere-binding protein Rap1 can act as a protein roadblock, inducing lagging-strand gaps52. These studies indicate that leading-strand synthesis can proceed unimpeded even if there are gaps on the lagging strand. A more recent study showed that dNTP depletion stalls leading-strand synthesis, while CMG continues dsDNA unwinding, resulting in continued initiation of OF synthesis and accumulation of incomplete lagging-strand products. These excess OFs sequester and deplete RFC–PCNA, which directly impairs processive leading-strand synthesis during restart51, underscoring the importance of maintaining synchronization and limiting gap accumulation on the lagging strand.

Distributive and rapid recycling of Pol δ mediate synchronization in the human replisome

Our findings, together with previous studies25,35,36,53,68 indicate that human Pol δ is inherently distributive, frequently dissociating from the lagging-strand template before completing OF synthesis. This contrasts sharply with the yeast case, where Pol δ is highly processive within each OF39, and it is even retained within the replisome to synthesize ~140 OFs40. This retention within the replisome is thought to depend on the Pol32 subunit of Pol δ40, which mediates contacts with other replisome components such as the Pol1 subunit of Pol α-primase41,60. However, the human Pol δ may have a weaker interaction with Pol α due to its frequent dissociation from the DNA. We propose that this may explain why replicating human Pol δ in our experiment was effectively exchanged with Pol δ from solution, as opposed to the case in yeast, where it displays high processivity40.

Comparison of reconstituted MOF from human and yeast establishes the low processivity of human Pol δ during MOF25,29, likely due to weaker Pol δ–PCNA interactions37,38, which render NT by Pol δ and FEN1 more distributive25. This low processivity manifested itself in human MOF being ~ 55-fold slower than in yeast25. Here, we extend these findings to show that Pol δ remained unprocessive during MOF within the replisome, supported by the observations that the lagging-strand product lengths do not change in the presence of only the NT reaction and that MOF remained intrinsically a slow process, continuing well after leading- and lagging-strand synthesis have completed. However, it remains possible that additional regulatory pathways enhance maturation efficiency in vivo. For example, post-translational modifications of MOF factors may further regulate this process. Phosphorylation of human FEN1 has been reported to decrease its interaction with PCNA69, which may promote dissociation of FEN1 from the nick product and facilitate more efficient re-engagement of Pol δ. Finally, additional proteins may associate with the replisome and/or the MOF complex to enhance the rate and processivity of human MOF.

Importantly, the yield of MOF within the replisome remained comparable to that observed in previously reported isolated human MOF systems25,55. This observation suggests that replisome-associated interactions do not substantially enhance MOF efficiency. Instead, MOF appears to remain intrinsically distributive in the human system and to proceed with limited overall yield. Notably, inefficient intermediate utilization has also been observed in yeast, where primer utilization and productive clamp loading were identified as limiting factors70. Furthermore, even in the absence of the ligation step, both reconstituted human and yeast MOF systems exhibit limited yield during the NT sub-process, resulting in incomplete primer utilization25,29. It is therefore possible that the overall yield of MOF reconstitution is limited by earlier steps, such as PCNA loading70 or handover to Pol δ. Nevertheless, while previous studies generally examined a single MOF step and reported < 70% primer utilization in reconstituted NT and MOF reactions, our reconstitution requires ~ 5–12 OFs to undergo NT-mediated RNA primer removal followed by ligation to achieve complete lagging-strand maturation and generate the final full-length product, as estimated from the final product size relative to the average OF size (Supplementary Fig. 9A). Thus, the ~ 60% MOF yield observed here in the presence of the replisome is remarkable given the multiplicative effect of the limited efficiencies at each individual MOF step. Importantly, it has previously been shown that a considerable amount of Pol α-derived DNA is retained in the mature genome71, raising the possibility that the limited MOF yield observed here reflects a biologically relevant feature of human lagging-strand maturation rather than solely a reconstitution artifact.

The distributive nature of Pol δ during OF synthesis and MOF, combined with the slow MOF reaction, could challenge synchronization with leading-strand progression, even though Pol δ extends primers at a rapid rate of 240 nt s−1 29,35. The robust OF synthesis in the presence of MOF indicates that OF synthesis remained synchronized with leading-strand synthesis, supporting a model in which Pol δ–PCNA prioritizes the completion of OF synthesis over MOF, leaving OFs unligated for a prolonged time. This model aligns with in vivo findings showing that OF processing and ligation can occur post-replication, uncoupled from DNA synthesis67.

While the reduced processivity of human Pol δ during MOF could be perceived as a kinetic bottleneck, it may serve an essential role in polymerase recycling. Human cells contain an estimated ~ 1.96 × 10⁴ copies of Pol δ per cell72, requiring each polymerase molecule to be recycled thousands of times per S-phase (~ 1220–3060 times)72. The fully uncoupled and distributive nature of MOF may thus ensure the rapid turnover of Pol δ, supporting continuous lagging-strand synthesis without depleting the active polymerase pool36. In fact, human Pol δ exhibits the highest binding affinity for PCNA when PCNA is bound to a primer-template junction, rather than to nicked or flap DNA intermediates associated with MOF25. Furthermore, previous results show that human FEN1–PCNA outcompetes Pol δ–PCNA for the flap and nick substrates during NT25, which would act as a molecular brake for Pol δ’s engagement in MOF and enhance its recycling. These findings highlight the kinetic basis underlying Pol δ–PCNA association, which prioritizes OF synthesis over MOF and thus ensures synchronized strand synthesis. In contrast, yeast Pol δ is significantly more processive, remaining engaged longer during both OF synthesis40 and MOF29, reducing its dependence on frequent polymerase turnover39. However, since FEN1 stimulates NT in yeast29 it is possible that NT would enhance yeast Pol δ’s engagement in MOF, reducing its recycling kinetics upon colliding with the previous OF. On the other hand, the direct interaction between Pol δ and L-Tag35 in SV40 may facilitate polymerase redistribution between primer hand-off, OF synthesis, and MOF, ensuring that lagging-strand processing remains coupled to replisome progression, but at the cost of reducing the speed of the leading strand.

Biological implications of the distributive human Pol δ

The non-processive nature of Pol δ’s SD and NT in humans may offer a functional advantage by minimizing unnecessary re-replication of OFs and maintaining short flap lengths. In a processive mechanism, Pol δ could excessively extend beyond the RNA-DNA junction, generating longer flaps that necessitate additional resection before ligation. In contrast, a distributive approach ensures that Pol δ disengages once sufficient DNA synthesis has occurred, preventing excessive extension and maintaining efficient lagging-strand maturation. On the other hand, since Pol α lacks proofreading activity, errors introduced within Pol α-derived segments are only partially corrected during maturation. The NT machinery in yeast can processively replace the entire α-segment in the absence of Lig129, whereas in humans, only the first few nucleotides are removed25, leading to partial retention of Pol α-derived DNA in the mature genome71. This balance likely optimizes replication speed while preserving genome stability, reducing the risk of misincorporation or excessive re-synthesis at lagging-strand junctions.

This disparity in processivity may also reflect species-specific differences in OF length regulation. In yeast, nucleosome assembly constrains Pol δ’s SD activity, enforcing a uniform OF size that aligns with in vivo observations. However, on naked DNA templates, yeast Pol δ generates abnormally long and heterogeneous fragments12,73. In humans, where MOF is highly distributive, frequent Pol δ turnover and leading-strand regulation during primer synthesis might provide an alternative, nucleosome-independent mechanism for maintaining fragment size. Furthermore, the prolonged retention of nicks during MOF may serve as a regulatory checkpoint for chromatin assembly, ensuring that histone deposition and chromatin maturation occur efficiently before cell division74. This suggests that MOF is not merely a terminal processing step but an integrated mechanism linking replication to chromatin inheritance.

In summary, our study reveals intrinsic coordination mechanisms within the eukaryotic replisome, highlighting how polymerase dynamics and MOF are finely tuned to sustain genome integrity. The complete uncoupling of lagging-strand maturation in humans, unlike its partial integration in SV40, underscores distinct evolutionary strategies balancing replication efficiency with genome stability. Given the strong link between replication stress and cancer, future studies should investigate how these mechanisms adapt to oncogenic transformation, transcription-replication conflicts, and chromatin remodeling disorders. Defining how replication integrates with broader nuclear processes will be key to understanding genome maintenance and its implications for disease and therapy.

Quantification and statistical analysis

DNA synthesis rates were calculated following25,29,76. Briefly, gel images were analyzed in ImageJ for both the DNA ladder and experimental reaction lanes. The migration distance of each band of the DNA ladder was paired with its corresponding known size to generate a standard calibration curve. This calibration curve was then used for all experimental lanes to convert median or specific band migration distances of unknown products into DNA length. Background was accounted for by subtracting the scalar total integrated intensity of empty lanes (their average if multiple empty lanes were present and used). Each lane profile was corrected for multiple radiolabel incorporations by dividing each product row by its corresponding number of incorporated radiolabels as determined from the product sequences. Time points which show detector saturation were avoided during analysis. For reactions containing both leading- and lagging-strand products, the intensities of the leading- and lagging-strand products were quantified by integrating the signal above and below 3 kb, respectively. To quantify synthesis kinetics, median product lengths were determined for each time point and plotted against reaction time. This provides a clear mathematical advantage over using the mean product length, which would prematurely strongly curve and artificially depress the kinetic trajectory as soon as the fastest molecules reach the template boundary. As the reaction progresses and most molecules naturally converge to the maximum template length, the median correspondingly converges to the exact size of the full-length product with a natural width of the full-length product band. Excluding the top band would therefore artificially truncate the dataset and disregard completed DNA products. By using the median product length combined with sigmoidal fitting (described below), all synthesized DNA mass is incorporated into the analysis, thus maintaining the mathematical validity of the synthesis metrics as the reaction proceeds to completion.
