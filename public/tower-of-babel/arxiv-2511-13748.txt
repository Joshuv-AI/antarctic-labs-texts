Microscopic theory of quantum physics
Dennis M. Heim

Abstract
I present a microscopic framework in which quantum phenomena emerge from particle-particle interactions governed by Newton’s second law of motion. Within this approach, stationary states and quantized energy spectra arise naturally for the particle in a box. The same dynamics reproduces interference fringes in the double-slit experiment. Finally, I derive the Schrödinger equation from the underlying principles.

1
Introduction

A microscopic theory of quantum mechanics would be highly desirable.
Such a framework could reduce the number of independent axioms on
which the theory rests and, ultimately, provide new predictions open to experimental verification.

One of the earliest steps in this direction was taken by Madelung
[
2
]
,
who reformulated the Schrödinger equation in hydrodynamical terms.
However, his approach did not specify the underlying microscopic particle interactions
– a gap that the present work aims to address.

Another influential line of research originated with de Broglie and was later developed by Bohm
[
1
]
.
Their formulation introduces particles with deterministic dynamics but relies on
a “quantum potential” defined through solutions of the Schrödinger equation.
In contrast, the present approach derives the Schrödinger equation directly from Newton’s second law.

A related attempt was made by Nelson
[
3
]
,
who also derived the Schrödinger equation from Newtonian mechanics but with an additional stochastic Brownian component.
The framework developed here requires no stochastic terms.
Instead, it is based on a well-defined deterministic interaction force.

In summary, the novelty of this work lies in showing that quantum phenomena such as stationary states,
quantized energy levels, interference, and the Schrödinger equation itself
can be derived solely from deterministic particle-particle interactions without stochastic assumptions.

2
Particle dynamics

To describe the influence of quantum mechanics on an ordinary particle of mass
m
at position
x_{j}
,
I introduce an additional interaction force
F_{\mathrm{int},j}
in Newton’s second law

\displaystyle m\,\ddot{x}_{j}=F_{\mathrm{classical},j}+F_{\mathrm{int},j}.

(1)

Assuming that this interaction force is conservative, it can be expressed as

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{int},j}=-\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx_{j}}V_{\mathrm{int}}.

(2)

Here I define the repulsive interaction potential

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{int}}\equiv\frac{\hbar^{2}}{8m}\sum_{k}\left(\frac{1}{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}-\frac{1}{x_{k}-x_{k-1}}\right)^{2}

(3)

with next-neighbor ordering
x_{k-1}<x_{k}<x_{k+1}
.

This potential can be interpreted as arising from auxiliary, unobservable “dark” particles at positions
x_{k\neq j}
.
Each dark particle follows Newton’s second law in the same way as an ordinary particle,
but because they cannot be measured directly, their role is only indirect:
they influence the motion of ordinary particles through
V_{\mathrm{int}}
.
Remarkably, this postulate is sufficient to reproduce
quantized energy spectra, interference patterns,
and ultimately the Schrödinger equation.

3
Particle in a box

The dynamics described by equation (
1
) can be analyzed by solving it for an initial distribution of particles.
As a first application, I consider a particle confined in a one-dimensional infinite potential well of length
L=100\,\mathrm{nm}
.
Here I find that certain initial distributions reappear after a characteristic period duration
T_{n}
.
These distributions are stationary in the sense of standing waves.

4
Double slit

The double-slit experiment provides a fundamental test for any theory of quantum behavior.
Within the present framework, particles are simulated as they traverse
two slits while evolving under the interaction potential (
3
).

Figure
3
illustrates the outcome.
For comparison, the left panel shows the quantum mechanical probability density at
t=0\,\mathrm{ps}
,
while the right panel displays the corresponding distribution at
t=20\,\mathrm{ps}
.
In between, the central panel depicts deterministic trajectories evolving
under the microscopic interaction potential.
Although each particle follows a well-defined path, the ensemble reproduces the
characteristic interference pattern of quantum mechanics.
The distribution of particle impacts on the detection screen coincides with
the quantum mechanical probability density,
confirming that interference arises naturally from the microscopic dynamics.

This example demonstrates that interference, often regarded as a purely
probabilistic or wave-based phenomenon, can instead be explained as the emergent
result of deterministic particle-particle interactions.

how quantum-like interference emerges naturally from the microscopic dynamics.

5
Schrödinger equation

Having demonstrated that quantized energy spectra and interference patterns
emerge naturally from the microscopic dynamics, I now show how the Schrödinger
equation itself can be derived directly from the underlying equations of motion.

By inserting the interaction potential (
3
)
into equation (
2
), the force on particle
j
can be expressed as

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{int},j}=-\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx_{j}}\left[\frac{\hbar^{2}}{8m}\sum_{k}\left(\frac{1}{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}-\frac{1}{x_{k}-x_{k-1}}\right)^{2}\right].

Evaluating the derivative yields

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{int},j}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4m}\bigg\{\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{x_{j+2}-x_{j+1}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}\bigg)\frac{1}{(x_{j+1}-x_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle-\bigg(\frac{1}{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}\bigg)\frac{1}{(x_{j+1}-x_{j})^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle-\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}\bigg)\frac{1}{(x_{j}-x_{j-1})^{2}}

\displaystyle-\bigg(\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{x_{j-1}-x_{j-2}}\bigg)\frac{1}{(x_{j}-x_{j-1})^{2}}\bigg]\bigg\}.

Under the near-uniform spacing approximation,
x_{j+1}-x_{j}\approx x_{j}-x_{j-1}
,
this simplifies to

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{int},j}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\bigg\{\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{j+2}-x_{j+1}}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}}\bigg)\frac{1}{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}

\displaystyle-\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}}\bigg)\frac{1}{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}\bigg]\frac{\sqrt{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}}{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}

\displaystyle-\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}}\bigg)\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}

\displaystyle-\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{j-1}-x_{j-2}}}\bigg)\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}\bigg]\frac{\sqrt{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}}{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}\bigg\}\frac{1}{x_{j+1}-x_{j-1}}.

Introducing
R(x_{j})\equiv 1/\sqrt{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}
and taking the continuum limit

x_{j}-x_{j-i}\rightarrow 0
, the interaction force approaches

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{int},j}\rightarrow F_{\mathrm{int}}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\,\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{R}\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}R}{dx^{2}}\right]\equiv-\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\,V_{\mathrm{quant}}.

Here,
V_{\mathrm{quant}}
is the quantum potential, which was first identified by Bohm
[
1
]
.

As an alternative to the Newtonian formulation, the dynamics can be expressed
equivalently in terms of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2m}\left(\displaystyle\frac{dS}{dx}\right)^{2}+V_{\mathrm{class}}+V_{\mathrm{quant}}=0,

(5)

where
V_{\mathrm{class}}
denotes the classical potential and
S
the action.
In this formalism, the probability density
P\equiv R^{2}
obeys the continuity equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}+\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{P}{m}\displaystyle\frac{dS}{dx}\right)=0.

(6)

Multiplying (
6
) by the imaginary unit
\mathrm{i}
and
adding it to (
5
) yields the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\hbar\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\psi}{dx^{2}}+V\psi,

(7)

with the wave function defined by
\psi\equiv R\exp(\mathrm{i}S/\hbar)
.

Thus, rather than being postulated, the Schrödinger equation emerges naturally from the microscopic interaction framework.

6
Conclusion

In this work, I have proposed a microscopic theory in which quantum phenomena emerge
from deterministic particle-particle interactions governed by Newton’s law.
Stationary periodic dynamics naturally give rise to quantized energy spectra for
the particle in a box,
and the same framework reproduces interference patterns in the double-slit experiment.
Furthermore, the Schrödinger equation arises naturally from the collective equations
of motion, rather than being postulated.

Overall, these results suggest that quantum mechanics can be interpreted as an emergent description of underlying deterministic interactions. This perspective opens potential pathways for new predictions, extensions to relativistic systems, and applications in field-theoretical contexts.
