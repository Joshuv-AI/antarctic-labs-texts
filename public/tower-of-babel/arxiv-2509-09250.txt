Non-integrability of the Critical Systems for Optimal Sums of Eigenvalues of Sturm-Liouville Operators
Yuzhou Tian, Meirong Zhang

Abstract
The optimal lower or upper bounds for sums of the first m m eigenvalues of Sturm-Liouville operators can be obtained by solving the corresponding critical systems, which are Hamiltonian systems of m m degrees of freedom with m m parameters. With the help of the differential Galois theory, we prove that these critical systems are not meromorphic integrable except for two known completely integrable cases. The non-integrability of the critical systems reveal certain complexities for the original eigenvalues problems.

Introduction

We consider the following Dirichlet eigenvalue problem for the Sturm-Liouville operator

-y^{\prime\prime}+q\left(x\right)y=\lambda y,\qquad x\in\left[0,1\right],

(1.1)

subject to the Dirichlet boundary condition

\displaystyle y\left(0\right)=y\left(1\right)=0,

(1.2)

where
q\left(x\right)\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}:=L^{p}\left(\left[0,1\right],{\mathbb{R}}\right)
is
an integrable potential with the exponent
p\in\left(1,\infty\right)
. The real number
\lambda
is called an eigenvalue of system (
1.1
) if there is a nontrivial solution
y\left(x\right)
of system (
1.1
). It is
well-known that problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) exists
a sequence of eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}(q)<\lambda_{2}(q)<\cdots<\lambda_{m}(q)<\cdots,\qquad\lambda_{m}(q)\to+\infty\mbox{ as }m\to\infty.

In the spectral theory of Sturm-Liouville operator, the optimal estimates of eigenvalues have been the focus of investigation for mathematicians and physicists due to the that such eigenvalues problems are very useful for understanding the nonhomogeneous string equation and various dimensionally reduced wave equations, see
[
15
,
23
]
, etc. Since Krein’s seminal work in
[
23
]
, researchers have done substantive work and achieved enormous progress for the optimal estimates of eigenvalues. In the last few decades, the important developments of eigenvalues problems for Sturm-Liouville operator (
1.1

[

Besides the above eigenvalues problems, the estimates for sum of eigenvalues of ordinary differential operators are also very important in the spectral theory, see
[
12

17
,
18
,
24
,
25
]
and the references therein. For Sturm-Liouville operator (

), the sum of the first

eigenvalues can be described as the following optimization problems

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{m}^{-}:=\min\limits_{q\in B_{p,r}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\left(q\right)\;\text{and}\;\mathscr{E}_{m}^{+}:=\max\limits_{q\in B_{p,r}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\left(q\right),

(1.3)

where

With suitable selection of exponent

,

\displaystyle u^{\prime}_{i}=v_{i},\quad v^{\prime}_{i}=-\mu_{i}u_{i}+\varepsilon\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{2}\right)^{k-1}u_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,m,

(1.4)

with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(v_{i}^{2}+\mu_{i}u_{i}^{2}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}^{2}\right)^{k}

(1.5)

To the best of our knowledge, most studies have been dedicated to the integrability for Hamiltonian system of two degrees of freedom, see for instance

2
Non-integrability of system (
1.4
)

The main topics of this section will be investigated the non-integrability of Hamiltonian system (
1.4
) in sense of meromorphic.

Consider a complex symplectic manifold
M\subset\mathbb{C}^{2m}
of dimension
2m
with the standard symplectic form
\bm{\tilde{\omega}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}du_{j}\wedge dv_{j}
. Let
H:M\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic Hamiltonian. The Hamiltonian system with
m
degrees of freedom is given by

\displaystyle\dfrac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=X_{H}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\left(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial\mathbf{v}},-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{C},\quad\mathbf{x}=\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right)\in M,

(2.6)

where
\mathbf{u}=\left(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\right)
and
\mathbf{v}=\left(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\right)
are the canonical coordinates. We say that Hamiltonian system (
2.6
) is completely integrable or Liouville
integrable if there exists
m
non-constant functions
I_{1}\equiv H,I_{2},\ldots,I_{m}
satisfying the following conditions.

(i)

The functions
I_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
are functionally independent, that is, their gradients
\nabla I_{1},\ldots,\nabla I_{m}
are
linearly independent.

(ii)

The Poisson bracket of
I_{i}
and
I_{j}
is

\left\{I_{i},I_{j}\right\}=\sum_{l=1}^{m}\left(\frac{\partial I_{i}}{\partial v_{l}}\frac{\partial I_{j}}{\partial u_{l}}-\frac{\partial I_{i}}{\partial u_{l}}\frac{\partial I_{j}}{\partial v_{l}}\right)=0

for all
i,j=1,\dots,m
.

The above functions
I_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
are called first integrals of system (
2.6
). In addition, Hamiltonian system (
2.6
) is meromorphic completely integrable, or simply meromorphic integrable if its
m
functionally independent first integrals
I_{1}\equiv H,I_{2},\ldots,I_{m}
are meromorphic.

Let
\Gamma
be a non-equilibrium solution to system (
2.6
). Assume that
\Gamma
can be parameterized by time
t
, that is,

\displaystyle\bm{\varphi}:\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\rightarrow M\subset\mathbb{C}^{2m}

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\mathbf{u}\left(t\right),\mathbf{v}\left(t\right)\right).

Then the variational equation (VE, for short) along
\Gamma
is the linear differential system

\displaystyle\dfrac{d\mathbf{y}}{dt}=\dfrac{\partial X_{H}\left(\bm{\varphi}\left(t\right)\right)}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{y},\quad\mathbf{y}\in T_{\Gamma}M,

(2.7)

where
T_{\Gamma}M
is the tangent bundle
TM
restricted on
\Gamma
.

Let
N:=T_{\Gamma}M/T\Gamma
be the normal bundle
of
\Gamma

[
22
]
, and
\pi:T_{\Gamma}M\rightarrow N
be the nature projective homomorphism. The normal variational equation (NVE, for short) along
\Gamma
has the form

\displaystyle\dfrac{d\mathbf{z}}{dt}=\pi_{*}\left(T\left(\mathfrak{u}\right)\left(\pi^{-1}\mathbf{z}\right)\right),\quad\mathbf{z}\in N,

(2.8)

where
\mathfrak{u}=X_{H}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
with
\mathbf{x}\in M
, and
T\left(\mathfrak{u}\right)
is the tangential
variation of
\mathfrak{u}
along
\Gamma
, that is,
T\left(\mathfrak{u}\right)=\partial X_{H}/\partial\mathbf{x}
. Note that the above NVE is a
2\left(m-1\right)
-dimensional linear differential system. We can employ a generalization of D’Alambert’s method to get the NVE (
2.8
), see
[
29
]
.
Briefly speaking, we use the fact that
X_{H}\left(\bm{\varphi}\left(t\right)\right)
is a solution of the VE (
2.7
) to reduce its dimension by one. In effect, we typically restrict the equation (
2.6
) to the energy level
h=H\left(\bm{\varphi}\left(t\right)\right)
. Then the dimensionality of the corresponding VE (
2.7
) also can be reduced.

Morales and Ramis
[
29
]
proved the following classical theorem, which gives a necessary condition for the integrability of Hamiltonian system (
2.6
) in the Liouville sense.
For precise notions of differential Galois theory, see
[
33
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

(Morales-Ramis theorem, see
[
29
]
)
If Hamiltonian system (
2.6
) is meromorphically integrable in the Liouville
sense in a neighbourhood of a particular solution
\Gamma
, then the identity component of the Galois group of the NVE (
2.8
) is Abelian.

Theorem
2.1
establishes a relation between the meromorphic integrability and the differential Galois group of the NVE (
2.8
). However, it’s very difficult to decide the solvability of the differential Galois group of NVE (
2.8
) in applications.

The next theorem tells us that the identity component of the differential Galois group is invariant under the covering.

Theorem 2.2
.

(
[
29
]
)

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a connected Riemann surface and
\nabla
be a meromorphic connection over
\mathcal{M}
. Assume that
f:\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}
is a finite ramified covering of
\mathcal{M}
by a connected Riemann surface
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. Let
\nabla^{\prime}=f^{*}\nabla
be the pull back of
\nabla
by
f
. Then there exists a natural
injective homomorphism

\emph{Gal}\left(\nabla^{\prime}\right)\rightarrow\emph{Gal}\left(\nabla\right)

of differential Galois groups which induces an isomorphism between their Lie
algebras.

\displaystyle u^{\prime}_{1}=v_{1},\quad v^{\prime}_{1}=-\mu_{1}u_{1}+\varepsilon u_{1}^{2k-1},

Solving equation (

Integrating equation (

The VE (

Since

Inspired

\begin{split}&\overline{\Gamma}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}^{1},\\

\end{array}\right),

where

\mathbf{\tilde{\Lambda}}:=\text{diag}\;\Big(\varepsilon u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{1},\varepsilon\left(2k-1\right)u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{2},\varepsilon u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{3},\ldots,\varepsilon u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{m}\Big).

The VE (
2
) is also composed of
m
uncoupled Schrödinger equations

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}^{\prime\prime}=\mathbf{\tilde{\Lambda}}\bm{\eta},

that is,

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime\prime}_{1}=\left(\varepsilon u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{1}\right)\eta_{1},

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime\prime}_{2}=\left(\varepsilon\left(2k-1\right)u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{2}\right)\eta_{2},

(2.40)

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime\prime}_{j}=\left(\varepsilon u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{j}\right)\eta_{j},\quad j=3,\ldots,m.

Using Liouville’s formula
[
26
]
, the second equation of (
2.40
) is solvable due to the fact that it has a solution
\eta_{2}=u^{\prime}_{2}\left(t\right)
. Therefore, the corresponding normal variational equations (
\widetilde{\text{NVE}}
) along
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{0}
are given by

\begin{split}&\eta^{\prime\prime}_{1}=\left(\varepsilon u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{1}\right)\eta_{1},\\
&\eta^{\prime\prime}_{j}=\left(\varepsilon u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{j}\right)\eta_{j},\quad j=3,\ldots,m.\end{split}

(2.41)

Similarly, we can carry out the following Yoshida transformation

t\longmapsto z=\dfrac{\varepsilon}{k\mu_{2}}u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right),

and transform
\widetilde{\text{NVE}}
(
2.41
) into the algebraic normal variational equations (
\widetilde{\text{ANVE}}
):

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{2}\eta_{1}}{dz^{2}}+\left(\frac{1}{z}+\frac{1}{2(z-1)}\right)\dfrac{d\eta_{1}}{dz}-\left(\frac{\mu_{1}}{4\mu_{2}(k-1)^{2}z^{2}}+\frac{k\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}{4\mu_{2}(k-1)^{2}z(z-1)}\right)\eta_{1}=0,

(
\widetilde{\text{ANVE}}_{1}
)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{2}\eta_{j}}{dz^{2}}+\left(\frac{1}{z}+\frac{1}{2(z-1)}\right)\dfrac{d\eta_{j}}{dz}-\left(\frac{\mu_{j}}{4\mu_{2}(k-1)^{2}z^{2}}+\frac{k\mu_{2}-\mu_{j}}{4\mu_{2}(k-1)^{2}z(z-1)}\right)\eta_{j}=0,

(
\widetilde{\text{ANVE}}_{j}
)

\displaystyle j=3,\ldots,m.

The direct sum form of
\widetilde{\text{ANVE}}
is

\widetilde{\text{ANVE}}=\widetilde{\text{ANVE}}_{1}\oplus\widetilde{\text{ANVE}}_{3}\oplus\widetilde{\text{ANVE}}_{4}\oplus\cdots\oplus\widetilde{\text{ANVE}}_{m}
. For the
\widetilde{\text{ANVE}}_{1}
, all the possibilities of the differences of exponents are

\varrho=\pm\dfrac{1}{k-1}\sqrt{\dfrac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}}},\tau=\pm\dfrac{1}{2}\left(1+\dfrac{2}{k-1}\right)\;\text{and}\;\varsigma=\pm\dfrac{1}{2}.

By the same discussions as NVE (
2.22
), we obtain that the parameters
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
must satisfy

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}}\in\left\{\left(\left(k-1\right)\ell\pm 1\right)^{2}\big|\ell\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\bigcup\left\{\dfrac{\left(k-1\right)^{2}\left(2\ell+1\right)^{2}}{4}\Bigg|\ell\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}

(2.42)

if the identity components of the Galois groups of the
\widetilde{\text{NVE}}
(
2.41
) is Abelian.

The conditions (
2.29
) and (
2.42
) imply that

\dfrac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}\geq 1\;\text{and}\;\dfrac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}}\geq 1,

respectively. This
contradicts our assumption
\mu_{1}\neq\mu_{2}
. Consequently, either the identity components of the Galois groups of the NVE (
2.22
) or
\widetilde{\text{NVE}}
(
2.41
) is not Abelian.
By Theorem
2.1
, the lemma follows.

The proof is finished.

\Box

Lemma 2.4
.

If
k\geq 3
,
\mu_{1}\neq\mu_{2}
and
\mu_{1}\mu_{2}=0
, then Hamiltonian system (
1.4
) is not meromorphic integrable.

Proof
Our proof will be distinguished two cases: either
\mu_{1}=0,\mu_{2}\neq 0
, or
\mu_{1}\neq 0,\mu_{2}=0
.

Case 1:
\mu_{1}=0
and
\mu_{2}\neq 0
. For this case, we also restrict system (
1.4
) on the invariant manifold
\mathcal{N}_{1}
. Namely,

\displaystyle u^{\prime}_{1}=v_{1},\quad v^{\prime}_{1}=\varepsilon u_{1}^{2k-1}

(2.43)

with Hamiltonian

\displaystyle h=\dfrac{1}{2}v_{1}^{2}-\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2k}u_{1}^{2k}.

(2.44)

Analogously, we also consider the particular solution
\Gamma_{0}
in the proof of Lemma
2.3
, and compute the
\widehat{\text{NVE}}
along
\Gamma_{0}

\begin{split}&\xi^{\prime\prime}_{j}=\left(\varepsilon u_{1}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{j}\right)\xi_{j},\quad j=2,\ldots,m.\end{split}

(2.45)

Doing the change of variable

t\longmapsto z=\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2\mu_{2}}u_{1}^{2k-2}\left(t\right),

we attain the algebraic normal variational equations (
\widehat{\text{ANVE}}
):

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{2}\xi_{2}}{dz^{2}}+\dfrac{3}{2z}\dfrac{d\xi_{2}}{dz}-\frac{k\left(2z-1\right)}{8(k-1)^{2}z^{3}}\xi_{2}=0,

(
\widehat{\text{ANVE}}_{2}
)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{2}\xi_{j}}{dz^{2}}+\dfrac{3}{2z}\dfrac{d\xi_{j}}{dz}-\frac{k\left(2\mu_{2}z-\mu_{j}\right)}{8(k-1)^{2}\mu_{2}z^{3}}\xi_{j}=0,\quad j=3,\ldots,m,

(
\widehat{\text{ANVE}}_{j}
)

and denote by

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{ANVE}}=\widehat{\text{ANVE}}_{2}\oplus\widehat{\text{ANVE}}_{3}\oplus\cdots\oplus\widehat{\text{ANVE}}_{m}.

(2.46)

Performing the classical transformation (see (
B.3
))

\xi_{2}=\chi\exp\left(-\dfrac{3}{4}\int\dfrac{dz}{z}\right)=\chi z^{-3/4},

the
\widehat{\text{ANVE}}_{2}
becomes

\displaystyle\chi^{\prime\prime}=r\left(z\right)\chi,

(2.47)

where

\displaystyle r\left(z\right)=-\left(\dfrac{(k-3)(3k-1)}{16(k-1)^{2}z^{2}}+\dfrac{k}{8(k-1)^{2}z^{3}}\right).

(2.48)

Then, the poles of
r\left(z\right)
is
z=0
. The order of
z=0
and
z=\infty
is
o\left(0\right)=3
and
o\left(\infty\right)=2
, respectively. Using Proposition
B.2
to equation (
2.47
), only types (ii) or (iv) of Theorem
B.1
can appear. Working the second part of Kovacic’s algorithm (see Appendix
B
), we obtain that

\mathcal{E}_{\infty}=\left\{2+\ell\sqrt{1-\dfrac{(k-3)(3k-1)}{4(k-1)^{2}}}\;\Bigg|\;\ell=0,\pm 2\right\}\bigcap\mathbb{Z}=\begin{cases}\left\{0,2,4\right\},\;\text{if}\;k=3,\\
\left\{2\right\},\;\text{if}\;k\geq 4.\end{cases}\;\text{and}\;\mathcal{E}_{0}=\left\{3\right\}.

Straightforward computations show that the number
d=d\left(\bm{\varpi}\right)=\left(\varpi_{\infty}-\varpi_{0}\right)/2

is not an integer. Therefore, type (iv) of Theorem
B.1
holds. This means that the identity component of the
Galois group of the
\widehat{\text{ANVE}}
(
2.46
) is not Abelian. Thereby, the identity component of the
Galois group of the
\widehat{\text{NVE}}
(
2.45
) is also not Abelian. From Theorem
2.1
, it follows that the Hamiltonian system (
1.4
) for
k\geq 3
is meromorphic non-integrable with
\mu_{1}=0
and
\mu_{2}\neq 0
.

Case 2:
\mu_{1}\neq 0
and
\mu_{2}=0
. Substituting
\mu_{2}=0
into (
2.41
), we get the normal variational equations along
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{0}
:

\begin{split}&\eta^{\prime\prime}_{1}=\left(\varepsilon u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{1}\right)\eta_{1},\\
&\eta^{\prime\prime}_{j}=\left(\varepsilon u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right)-\mu_{j}\right)\eta_{j},\quad j=3,\ldots,m.\end{split}

(2.49)

After the change of variable

t\longmapsto z=\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2\mu_{1}}u_{2}^{2k-2}\left(t\right),

equations (
2.49
) become the algebraic normal variational equations

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{2}\eta_{1}}{dz^{2}}+\dfrac{3}{2z}\dfrac{d\eta_{1}}{dz}-\frac{k\left(2z-1\right)}{8(k-1)^{2}z^{3}}\eta_{1}=0,

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{2}\eta_{j}}{dz^{2}}+\dfrac{3}{2z}\dfrac{d\eta_{j}}{dz}-\frac{k\left(2\mu_{1}z-\mu_{j}\right)}{8(k-1)^{2}\mu_{1}z^{3}}\eta_{j}=0,\quad j=3,\ldots,m.

The analysis is exactly the same as Case 1. Thus, the Hamiltonian system (
1.4
) for

is meromorphic non-integrable with
\mu_{1}\neq 0
and
\mu_{2}=0
.

This lemma holds.

\Box

Theorem 2.5
.

The Hamiltonian system (

) is meromorphic completely integrable if and only if

and
\left(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

Appendix B
Kovacic’s algorithm

Let
\mathbb{C}\left(z\right)
be the field of rational functions in the variable
z
with complex coefficients. Consider the second order linear differential equation

\displaystyle\chi^{\prime\prime}=r\left(z\right)\chi,\quad r\left(z\right)\in\mathbb{C}\left(z\right).

(B.2)

It is well known that the differential Galois group
G
of equation (
B.2
) is an algebraic subgroup of
\text{SL}\left(2,\mathbb{C}\right)
. In 1986, Kovacic
[
21
]

characterized all possible types of
G
as follows.

Theorem B.1
.

(
[
21
]
)

The differential Galois group
G
of equation (
B.2
) is conjugated to one of the following four types:

(i)

G
is conjugated to a subgroup of a triangular group, and equation (
B.2
) admits a solution of the form
\chi=\exp\left(\int\omega\right)
with
\omega\in\mathbb{C}\left(z\right)
.

(ii)

G
is conjugate to a subgroup of

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathfrak{a}&0\\
0&\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\\
\end{array}\right)\Bigg|\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\}\right\}\bigcup\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\mathfrak{a}\\
\mathfrak{a}^{-1}&0\\
\end{array}\right)\Bigg|\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\}\right\},

and equation (
B.2
) admits a solution of the form
\chi=\exp\left(\int\omega\right)
, where
\omega
is algebraic of degree
2
over
\mathbb{C}\left(z\right)
.

(iii)

G
is finite and all solutions of equation (
B.2
) are algebraic over
\mathbb{C}\left(z\right)
.

(iv)

G=\emph{SL}\left(2,\mathbb{C}\right)
and equation (
B.2
) does not admit a Liouvillian solution.

Let
r\left(z\right)=\mathfrak{p}\left(z\right)/\mathfrak{q}\left(z\right)

with
\mathfrak{p}\left(z\right),\mathfrak{q}\left(z\right)\in\mathbb{C}\left[z\right]
relatively prime. The
pole
of
r\left(z\right)
is a zero of
\mathfrak{q}\left(z\right)
and
the order of the pole
is the multiplicity of the zero of
\mathfrak{q}\left(z\right)
.
The order of
r\left(z\right)
at
\infty
is defined by
\deg\mathfrak{q}-\deg\mathfrak{p}
.
Kovacic
[
21
]
also
provided the necessary conditions for types (i), (ii), or (iii) in Theorem
B.1
to occur.

Proposition B.2
.

(
[
21
]
)

For the first three types in Theorem
B.1
, the necessary conditions of occurrence are respectively as follows:

Type (i)

Each pole of
r\left(z\right)
must have even order or else have order
1
. The order of
r\left(z\right)
at
\infty
must be
even or else be greater than
2
.

Type (ii)

The rational function
r\left(z\right)
must have at least one pole that either has odd order greater than
2
or else has
order
2
.

Type (iii)

The order of a pole of
r\left(z\right)
cannot exceed
2
and the order of
r\left(z\right)
at
\infty
must be at
least
2
. If the partial fraction decomposition of
r\left(z\right)
is

r\left(z\right)=\sum_{i}\dfrac{\alpha_{i}}{\left(z-c_{i}\right)^{2}}+\sum_{j}\dfrac{\beta_{j}}{z-b_{j}},

then
\sqrt{1+4\alpha_{i}}\in\mathbb{Q}
for each
i
,
\sum_{j}\beta_{j}=0
, and if
\Delta=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j}\beta_{j}
, then
\sqrt{1+4\Delta}\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Remark B.3
.

The general second order linear differential equation

\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime}=a_{1}y^{\prime}+a_{2},\quad a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{C}\left(z\right),

can be transformed into the form (
B.2
) with

r\left(z\right)=\dfrac{a_{1}^{2}}{4}-\dfrac{a^{\prime}_{1}}{2}+a_{2}

via the change of the variable

\displaystyle y=\exp\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\int a_{1}dz\right)\chi.

(B.3)

Here, we recall the second part of Kovacic’s algorithm
[
21
]
. Let
r\left(z\right)\in C\left(z\right)
and
\Upsilon
be the set of poles of
r\left(z\right)
. Set
\chi^{\prime\prime}=r\chi
.

Step 1.

To each pole
c\in\Upsilon
, we calculate the set
\mathcal{E}_{c}
as follows.

(i)

If the pole
c
is of order
1
, then
\mathcal{E}_{c}=\left\{4\right\}
.

(ii)

If the pole
c
is of order
2
and
b
is the coefficient of
1/\left(z-c\right)^{2}
in the partial fraction decomposition of
r\left(z\right)
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{c}=\left\{2+\ell\sqrt{1+4b}\;\big|\;\ell=0,\pm 2\right\}\bigcap\mathbb{Z}.

(B.4)

(iii)

If the pole
c
is of order
o\left(c\right)>2
, then
\mathcal{E}_{c}=\left\{o\left(c\right)\right\}
.

(iv)

If the order of
r
at
\infty
is
o\left(\infty\right)>2
, then
\mathcal{E}_{c}=\left\{0,2,4\right\}
.

(v)

If the order of
r
at
\infty
is
2
and
b
is the coefficient of
1/z^{2}
in the Laurent expansion of
r\left(z\right)
at
\infty
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{c}=\left\{2+\ell\sqrt{1+4b}\;\big|\;\ell=0,\pm 2\right\}\bigcap\mathbb{Z}.

(B.5)

(vi)

If the order of
r
at
\infty
is
o\left(\infty\right)<2
, then
\mathcal{E}_{c}=\left\{o\left(\infty\right)\right\}
.

Step 2.

Let
\bm{\varpi}=\left(\varpi_{c}\right)_{c\in\Upsilon}
be a element in the Cartesian product
\prod_{c\in\Upsilon}\mathcal{E}_{c}
with
\varpi_{c}\in\mathcal{E}_{c}
. Define number

\displaystyle d:=d\left(\bm{\varpi}\right)=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\varpi_{\infty}-\sum_{c\in\Upsilon}\varpi_{c}\right).

(B.6)

We try to find all elements
\bm{\varpi}
such that
d
is a non-negative integer, and retain such elements to perform Step 3. If there is no such element
\bm{\varpi}
, then statement (ii) of Theorem
B.1
is impossible.

Step 3.

For each
\bm{\varpi}
retained from Step 2, we introduce the rational function

\theta=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{c\in\Upsilon}\dfrac{\varpi_{c}}{z-c}.

Then, we seek a monic polynomial
P
of degree
d
defined in (
B.6
) such that

\displaystyle P^{\prime\prime\prime}+3\theta P^{\prime\prime}+\left(3\theta^{2}+3\theta^{\prime}-4r\right)P^{\prime}+\left(\theta^{\prime\prime}+3\theta\theta^{\prime}+\theta^{3}-4r\theta-2r^{\prime}\right)P=0,

where monic polynomial
P
is a polynomial with the leading coefficent
1
.
If such polynomial
P
does not exist for all elements
\bm{\varpi}
retained from Step 2, then statement (ii) of Theorem
B.1
is untenable.

Assume that such a polynomial
P
exists. Let
\phi=\theta+P^{\prime}/P
and
\omega
be a root of

\omega^{2}-\phi\omega+\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\phi^{\prime}+\dfrac{1}{2}\phi^{2}-r\right)=0.

Then,
\chi=\exp\left(\int\omega\right)
is a solution of differential equation
\chi^{\prime\prime}=r\chi
.
