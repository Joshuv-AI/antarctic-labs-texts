Effect of Donor-recipient Size on Graft Growth in Pediatric Donor-to-pediatric Recipient Kidney Transplantation
Authors: Jing Wang, Chunyan Wang, Zhiqing Zhang, Meijie Hao, Yubo Sun, Rui Chen, Feng Liu, Yihui Zhai, Hong Xu, Qian Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background.Kidney transplantation is the optimal treatment for pediatric end-stage kidney disease, yet donor shortages have driven the use of pediatric donor kidneys through pediatric donor-to-pediatric recipient (PTP) kidney transplantation. This study aimed to investigate the effect of donor-recipient size on graft growth in PTP kidney transplantation.Methods.This retrospective cohort study included kidney transplant recipients who underwent regular follow-ups between January 2016 and December 2023 at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University. All donors were deceased and aged <18 y. Dynamic clinical data were obtained from medical records spanning the pretransplant to posttransplant period.Results.A total of 136 patients were enrolled, 93 of whom had an initial transplant kidney length. Cumulative average growth of kidney length was 5.5 mm at 1 mo, 9.9 mm at 3 mo, 15.3 mm at 6 mo, 22.3 mm at 1 y, 31.5 mm at 3 y, and 39.5 mm at 5 y posttransplant. The corresponding growth rates were 8.3%, 15.1%, 22.3%, 30.6%, 41.3%, and 53.4%. Kidney growth rates were negatively correlated with the donor-recipient body weight ratio at 3 mo, 6 mo, and 1 y posttransplant (P < 0.01). The mean estimated glomerular filtration rate at the last follow-up was 84.1 ± 31.8 mL/min/1.73 m2. Posttransplant estimated glomerular filtration rate showed a significant positive correlation with donor-recipient body weight ratio during the early posttransplant period (1, 3, and 6 mo; P < 0.01). Smaller grafts required approximately 6 mo to reach a normative size consistent with the recipients’ age.Conclusions.Grafts showed stable growth patterns and favorable short-term follow-up outcomes. Additionally, PTP kidney transplantation demonstrated both clinical value and practical utility.

INTRODUCTION

After decades of advances in transplant surgical techniques and immunosuppressive therapy, kidney transplantation has become the optimal treatment for patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). Compared with other renal replacement therapies, kidney transplantation can significantly improve the survival rate and life quality of patients with ESKD.1 However, the severe shortage of donor kidneys results in prolonged waiting time for many pediatric patients, exposing them to dialysis and ESKD complications. Therefore, expanding the donor pool in a responsible and ethical manner is a key strategy for addressing this challenge.

In recent years, the evolution of medical technology and economic development in China have led to substantial progress in pediatric kidney transplantation. Furthermore, the Ministry of Health and the Red Cross Society of China launched the Donation after circulatory Death program following the establishment of a national system for organ donation and transplantation in 2010,2 which greatly promoted the development of pediatric kidney transplantation. Given the persistent shortage of high-quality donor kidneys, the exploration of pediatric donor kidneys gradually commenced in 2012, leading to the development of pediatric donor-to-pediatric recipient (PTP) transplantation policy.

The PTP policy has been implemented in 3 large transplantation centers in China and has gained international peer recognition.3,4 Transplantation of kidneys from pediatric deceased donors weighing <15 kg into pediatric recipients have been shown to achieve satisfactory outcomes,5 increasing short-term survival rates and benefitting more pediatric patients with ESKD. Although recipients of small-donor kidneys often exhibit a lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in the early posttransplant period, long-term outcomes are favorable because of the progressive improvement in kidney function and capacity for adaptation.6

Currently, research on posttransplant outcomes in PTP kidney transplantation remains limited, with few studies specifically addressing graft growth patterns. This study was designed to explore the longitudinal growth of transplanted kidneys and identify factors influencing the recovery of kidney function following PTP transplantation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cohort

We retrospectively collected clinical data from pediatric patients who underwent kidney transplantation <18 y old and received regular follow-up at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University between January 2016 and December 2023. All donors were deceased pediatric patients aged <18 y. Data were obtained from the hospital’s medical system. This study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee and the Health Management Department (Approval No. 2024[322]). The requirement for written informed consent was waived in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical Data

Exclusion criteria were en bloc transplantation, living-related transplantation, early posttransplant graft dysfunction (<2 wk posttransplant), follow-up duration <6 mo, missing donor body weight data, and pretransplant kidney length data (Figure 1). Detailed clinical characteristics of recipients and donors were collected, including sex, age, cause of ESKD, donor and recipient body weight and height, dialysis duration and types before kidney transplantation, transplant kidney length under ultrasound, and serum creatinine at 1, 3, and 6 mo and then annually during follow-up.

Flowchart of the study.

Definitions

Cumulative kidney length growth was defined as the difference between kidney length at follow-up and at transplantation. Based on the concept of liver regeneration rate in liver transplantation,7 the growth rate of the transplanted kidney was defined as (the length of the kidney at the time of monitoring–the length at the time of transplantation)/the length at the time of transplantation. The revised Schwartz formula, 0.413× height (cm)/serum creatinine (mg/dL), was used to calculate the eGFR in children receiving kidney transplantation.8 The normative kidney length values were obtained from the largest available pediatric cohort study.9

The Protocol for Transplant Kidney Measurement

Kidney measurements were obtained under ultrasound guidance using a standardized protocol.10 Patients were positioned supine with their iliac fossae fully exposed before the examination. Quantitative image analysis was independently conducted by a senior ultrasound physician and a nephrologist with expertise in renal ultrasonography, and the mean values were used for statistical analysis. An ultrasound system (Siemens Acuson Sequoia 512) equipped with a 2–5 MHz probe was used to perform anteroposterior longitudinal and transverse scans to evaluate the kidney’s diameter, morphological features, and blood supply. Transplant kidney length, lateral diameter, and anteroposterior diameter were measured with an accuracy of 0.1 mm.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 24.0 and GraphPad Prism 8. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± SD, whereas nonnormally distributed variables were described as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (n [%]). Associations between variables were assessed using the Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ), followed by linear regression analysis. P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 136 pediatric patients with ESKD who received kidney transplantation were enrolled in this study (Table 1). The median follow-up duration was 21.7 mo (8.6–36.9 mo). Most patients received dialysis before kidney transplantation, with 8 (5.9%) on hemodialysis and 119 (87.5%) on peritoneal dialysis. The median age and weight of the donors were 2 y (0.8–3 y) and 12.4 kg (8.5–20 kg), respectively. The median length of donor kidney was 71.2 mm (60–85.8 mm). Recipient kidney function was summarized in Table S1 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A849), with the mean eGFR sustained at 84.1 ± 31.8 mL/min/1.73 m2 at the last follow-up. Two patients experienced graft failure during follow-up owing to thrombosis and BK nephropathy separately.

Longitudinal Growth of Transplant Kidney Under Ultrasound Monitoring

Among the participants, pretransplant donor kidney length was available for 93 donor kidneys. The mean increase in transplant kidney length was 5.5, 9.9, 15.3, 22.3, 31.5, and 39.5 mm at 1 mo, 3 mo, 6 mo, 1 y, 3 y, and 5 y posttransplant (Figure 2A). To dynamically describe the growth of the transplanted kidney, we introduced the concept of kidney length growth rate, which is calculated as the percentage increase relative to the pretransplant length. The growth rates at 1 mo, 3 mo, 6 mo, 1 y, 3 y, and 5 y were 8.3%, 15.1%, 22.3%, 30.6%, 41.3%, and 53.4%, respectively (Figure 2B).

The Relationship Between the Donor-recipient Body Weight Ratio and the Growth Rate of Transplant Kidney Length

In our study, the higher donor-recipient body weight ratio (DRBWR) indicated the recipient accepted larger donors, and the lower DRBWR indicated the recipient accepted smaller donors. The study demonstrated a negative correlation between DRBWR and the growth rate of transplant kidneys at 3 mo, 6 mo, and 1 y posttransplant, with Spearman coefficients of –0.51, –0.58, and –0.61, respectively (P < 0.01; Figure 3), indicating that the smaller donor kidneys had a faster growth rate during this period.

The Relationship Between DRBWR and the eGFR

A significant positive correlation was observed between DRBWR and eGFR at 1, 3, and 6 mo posttransplant, with Spearman’s correlations of 0.64, 0.43, and 0.43, respectively (P < 0.01; Figure 4). The results indicate that kidneys from larger donors had a higher eGFR in the early period, suggesting that a higher DRBWR was associated with better early kidney function. This correlation diminished over time and was not observed in subsequent follow-up evaluations. Additionally, we found different groups (DRBWR <0.5, 0.5–1, ≥1) did not show obvious significance in kidney function with mean eGFR at 79.3 ± 21.4, 87.1 ± 16.0, and 76.9 ± 21.6 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively, at last follow-up.

Kidney Allograft Length Versus Normative Length Related to Age for the Recipient Posttransplant

The ratio of donor kidney length to the recipients’ age-adjusted standard kidney length varied among participants. The median pretransplant ratio for the cohort was 0.82 (Figure 5A). Based on this initial ratio, recipients were categorized into 2 groups: those receiving smaller allografts (ratio <1) and those receiving larger allografts (ratio ≥1). In the smaller-allograft group, the median ratio increased from 0.79 pretransplant to 0.87 at 1 mo and 0.99 at 6 mo posttransplant (Figure 5B). Notably, 10.8% (10/93) of the donor grafts reached the full age-matched standard size as early as 1 mo posttransplant. During longitudinal follow-up, we observed that the transplanted kidneys consistently maintained age-matched standard sizes in pediatric recipients, demonstrating remarkable growth potential and adaptation to recipient development. In the larger-allograft group, the median initial ratio was 1.08, with only mild fluctuations observed during the first posttransplant year and no statistically significant changes (Figure 5C). Overall, smaller grafts required approximately 6 mo to reach the age-matched standard kidney size after transplantation.

DISCUSSION

This study revealed stable growth of allograft kidneys on serial ultrasonography and kidney function measurements during follow-up. The mean increase in allograft kidney length was 5.5 mm at 1 mo,15.3 mm at 6 mo, 22.3 mm at 1 y, and 31.5 mm at 3 y posttransplant, with corresponding mean eGFR values of 55.3 ± 23.8, 74.9 ± 20.0, 81.0 ± 25.6, and 86.6 ± 24.7 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Table S1, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A849). In addition, there was a negative correlation between the DRBWR and the growth rate of kidney length in the early posttransplant period, suggesting that smaller donor kidneys exhibited a more rapid growth rate. Conversely, DRBWR was positively correlated with eGFR, implying that larger donor kidneys were associated with a higher eGFR. However, this association was attenuated over time, and no significant difference in eGFR was observed at 6 mo posttransplant. Additionally, smaller allografts required approximately 6 mo to reach the age-appropriate standard kidney size. This longitudinal study provided a comprehensive characterization of allograft kidney growth dynamics in pediatric recipients under the PTP policy. By identifying the key determinants of graft growth and demonstrating their correlation with functional recovery, our findings help address a critical knowledge gap in posttransplant monitoring.

The PTP transplantation policy was introduced in 2012 and formally established in 2018 in China,4 which expanded the donor pool, reduced waiting time for patients with ESKD, and increased access to kidney transplantation. Pediatric donors were previously considered expanded-criteria donors and were underutilized in clinical practice because of the perceived risks of complications. Our center’s prior analysis of data from 3 leading pediatric transplant centers in China demonstrated favorable short- and medium-term outcomes with 1-y and 5-y graft survival rates of 97.6% and 95.5%, respectively.11 Moreover, transplantation from very small donors weighing <5 kg achieved promising short-term graft survival rates.4,5 These findings validate the clinical value and profound effect of using pediatric donors in kidney transplantation.

Concerns have been raised regarding whether kidneys from pediatric deceased donors contain an adequate number of glomeruli to sustain long-term function in recipients. Human nephrogenesis commences at the fifth week of gestation and ends at weeks 34–36.12 After this period, the number of glomeruli remains fixed; however, structural maturation continues, primarily through an increase in glomerular volume.13 Pathophysiological changes in the transplanted kidneys were complex and not fully elucidated. The solitary kidneys of living donors exhibit increased kidney plasma flow and eGFR, which leads to glomerular hypertrophy.14 Similarly, posttransplant graft length increase has been observed in adult recipients receiving kidneys from adult donors and considered a compensatory kidney hypertrophy. This increase in graft length is associated with an elevation in eGFR.15 Although the underlying mechanisms involved in the compensatory kidney enlargement remain unclear, proximal tubule hypertrophy has been identified as the major explanation in rat models with unilateral nephrectomy.16 Additionally, the release of various factors, such as platelet-derived growth factor and vascular endothelial growth factor, may contribute to this process. The renal sympathetic and renin-angiotensin systems have also been implicated in adaptive responses.17

Transplantation of smaller pediatric kidneys into larger recipients may lead to hyperfiltration injuries such as proteinuria, thrombosis, and glomerulosclerosis.18 However, as allograft kidneys develop, they exhibit a superior adaptive capacity that compensates for potential complications associated with size disparity.19-21 Conversely, transplantation of larger donor kidneys into smaller recipients have been associated with a higher incidence of acute tubular necrosis. Pediatric deceased donor kidneys achieve superior long-term eGFR compared with adult donor kidneys, with comparable graft survival rates.22 In our cohort, no difference in eGFR was observed at the last follow-up regardless of DRBWR(<0.5, 0.5–1, ≥1), suggesting that donor-recipient weight mismatch might not be a factor affecting short-term kidney function. These findings confirm that small kidneys, even deceased infant donor kidneys, also achieve satisfactory eGFR. Studies have revealed that very small grafts (from infant deceased donors) can grow rapidly, demonstrating remarkable recovery and growth capacity.23,24 Notably, by 1-y posttransplant follow-up, no significant difference in renal volume was observed between recipients of donors weighing below versus those weighing >10 kg.25 Nonetheless, long-term follow-up of these kidneys is important given the theoretical risks of hyperfiltration because of the proposed mechanism of glomerular hypertrophy.

This study confirms the clinical value of small-donor kidneys by demonstrating their consistent posttransplant growth patterns. The negative correlation between DRBWR and growth rates persisted up to 1-y posttransplant but subsequently disappeared. In addition, a positive correlation was observed between DRBWR and early posttransplant renal function within the first 6 mo, consistent with the findings reported by Park et al.26 Notably, allografts from small donors exhibited adaptive growth, reaching age-matched standard sizes and corresponding to somatic development—a pattern also supported by preclinical studies using pediatric-to-adult rat kidney transplantation models.27 During routine follow-up, clinicians must pay attention to the transplanted kidney length and compare it with the standard size corresponding to the recipients’ age. Significant deviations from the expected size at 6 mo warrant closer surveillance of graft growth and evaluation for potential complications.

Ultrasound serves as a noninvasive, accessible imaging in clinical practice, especially in pediatric patients. It is currently widely used for the measurement of transplant kidney morphology, such as kidney length and blood perfusion. Although computed tomography provides more reliable and objective for kidney volume measurement, its utility in serial dynamic monitoring was still limited because of the exposure of the radiation.28 Consequently, ultrasound has become the predominant imaging modality for routine measurement of transplant kidney length and for longitudinal size surveillance.10

This study has a few limitations. First, pretransplant kidney length was unavailable for approximately one-third of the grafts. Second, the median follow-up duration was 21.7 mo (8.6–36.9 mo), highlighting the need for long-term follow-up in future studies. Third, pretransplant ultrasonographic measurements were unavailable, therefore, we could not explore the transplant kidney volume change among these patients.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides a characterization of graft growth patterns in pediatric kidney transplantation using the PTP strategy. The findings highlight the clinical potential of using kidneys from pediatric deceased donors.
