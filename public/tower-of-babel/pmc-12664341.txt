Atypical Lemierre syndrome with thrombophlebitis of the external jugular vein: A case report
Authors: Yaimari José Treminio Juárez, Norving de la Cruz Vega Jarquin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lemierre syndrome is a rare and potentially fatal complication of acute oropharyngeal infection, typically causing thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein and sepsis. We present the case of a 65-year-old female patient with type 2 diabetes mellitus who presented with otalgia and left hemifacial pain for eight days. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the neck showed a collection in the left submandibular space. Venous Doppler revealed thrombosis in the left external jugular vein, representing an anatomical variant or an atypical presentation of the disease. In secretion culture, Klebsiella pneumoniae was isolated. The patient was treated with antibiotics and anticoagulants and showed clinical improvement, so she was discharged after fourteen days of hospitalization. Lemierre syndrome represents a diagnostic challenge due to its nonspecific clinical presentation however, thrombophlebitis of the head and neck veins is usually the key finding, even in atypical variants.

Introduction

Lemierre syndrome is an acute complication of a pharyngeal infection, secondary in most cases to Fusobacterium necrophorum [1]. Other etiological agents such as Streptococcus, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterococcus may also be involved [2]. Diagnosis remains a challenge in the face of other pathologies that may present with similar symptoms and signs. Approximately 3.6 million cases are reported worldwide each year, with a mortality rate of 5 %, mainly affecting young adults [3]. In this group, the presence of symptoms is often confused with more frequent benign pathologies, while in patients with chronic diseases they may be attributed to complications of their underlying condition. As this situation may delay the initiation of treatment, it is crucial to recognize the different forms of clinical presentation, both typical and atypical, as well as the diagnostic findings in imaging studies and cultures. These elements should be considered key to increasing clinical suspicion of Lemierre syndrome, thereby optimizing the time of identification and improving prognosis, especially in contexts where the suspicion of the disease is low.

Case presentation

We report the case a 65-year-old woman with diabetes and hypertension who presented at the emergency department with earache associated with yellow purulent otorrhea, inflammation, and pain in the left side of her face, accompanied by fever, halitosis, and odynophagia for eight days. The patient denied recent dental procedures or upper and lower respiratory tract infections. Upon admission, vital signs were within normal parameters, hyperglycemia was present, and a score of 1 was observed on the qSOFA scale. Physical examination revealed oral candidiasis, pharynx and tonsils with purulent exudate, as well as firm and painful swelling in the left cervical region. In addition, diffuse bilateral crackles were auscultated. The rest of the physical examination showed no relevant findings.

Discussion

Lemierre syndrome is an uncommon but potentially fatal complication of an anaerobic oropharyngeal infection that can evolve into septic thrombophlebitis of the ipsilateral internal jugular vein, followed by septicemia and metastatic septic emboli, most commonly in the lungs [4]. It is mainly caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum [5], though other pathogens such as Streptococcus pyogenes [6] have also been implicated. Recent evidence indicates that Streptococcus and Staphylococcus. are associated with a higher prevalence of internal jugular vein thrombosis (94 % of cases) compared with Fusobacterium. (63 %) This difference may be partly explained by a detection bias, since imaging is more frequently performed when Fusobacterium infection is suspected due to its strong association with septic emboli. [4]

Isolated cases caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae [7] have also been reported, such as the one described here. In our patient, isolation by blood culture was not achieved as blood cultures were negative and the pathogen was isolated from the culture of drained purulent material. Fusobacterium necrophorum has traditionally been considered essential for the diagnosis of Lemierre syndrome, as its isolation from blood or pus in patients with recent pharyngotonsillitis has been used as a diagnostic criterion. However, this assumption has been challenged because Fusobacterium necrophorum is difficult to grow, and multiple reports describe patients who, despite negative cultures or isolation of other bacteria, fulfill all other diagnostic criteria for Lemierre syndrome [8]. These findings support our diagnosis.

Valerio et al. [9] reported that atypical cases accounted for 53 % of all patients and frequently involved bacteria other than Fusobacterium necrophorum. In that study, external jugular vein thrombosis was observed in approximately 6 % of atypical cases. The present case, characterized by external jugular vein thrombosis and absence of Fusobacterium isolation, aligns with this atypical spectrum and reinforces this broader diagnostic concept. Moreover, the definition of Lemierre syndrome has evolved, as the diagnosis is no longer limited to thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein but may involve thrombophlebitis of any head or neck vein [8].

In a study conducted with 712 patients, venous thrombosis of the head or neck was detected in 84 % (n = 597) of the patients at the time of diagnosis, affecting the internal jugular veins in 74 % (n = 526) and cerebral veins in 20 % (n = 143). Internal or external jugular vein thrombosis was bilateral in 30 patients (4 %). A total of 583 (82 %) patients presented concomitant septic embolism, most frequently located in the lungs (71 %, n = 506) [9]. In our case, venous thrombosis was evidenced in the left external jugular vein, without involvement of the ipsilateral internal jugular vein, which corresponds to an uncommon presentation. A PubMed search yielded only 10 results in the last 10 years. One of them was the case described in 2022, where isolated thrombosis of the external jugular vein associated with Streptococcus intermedius was reported [10]. Another case was also documented with involvement of the external jugular vein, accompanied by cavitated pulmonary nodules [11].

Complications of the nervous system include infarcts, formation of brain abscesses, and involvement of the cavernous sinus and sigmoid sinus due to retrograde spread from internal jugular vein thrombosis [12]. A case of brain abscesses in watershed regions was reported in Spain [13]. As well as another case presenting with neuro-ophthalmologic complications [14]. Currently, surgical drainage, along with antibiotic therapy, is important in the management of treatment [15]. The antibiotic treatment of choice, despite the lack of randomized trials, must cover anaerobic germs, Fusobacterium necrophorum, Streptococci, and Staphylococci [16]. An antimicrobial treatment of three to six weeks is generally recommended [7]. In our patient, empiric antimicrobial therapy with imipenem–cilastatin and vancomycin was used, selected considering the presence of two active infectious foci and a high risk of infection by extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)–producing Gram-negative bacilli. After Klebsiella pneumoniae sensitive to ciprofloxacin was isolated from the abscess exudate culture, the carbapenem was replaced with ciprofloxacin according to the susceptibility profile. Vancomycin therapy was maintained, since the positive isolation came exclusively from the abscess sample, with no microbiological evidence from the pulmonary focus, and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) could not be excluded as a possible pathogen.

The use of anticoagulation in Lemierre syndrome has been the subject of considerable controversy. Anticoagulant therapy is considered potentially valuable for containing the initial septic thrombus and preventing its extension or recurrence of venous thromboembolism, especially intracranial [16]. Our patient, a 65-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity, and thrombophlebitis of the external jugular vein, was treated with anticoagulation as part of the management of Lemierre syndrome. This decision was based on the high likelihood of thromboembolic and septic complications. According to a recent meta-analysis [9] advanced age was independently associated with a higher probability of in-hospital thromboembolic or septic complications (OR 1.00; 95 % CI 0.99–1.02 per year increase). In the same study, major bleeding events occurred mainly in patients with thrombocytopenia, recent invasive procedures, or concomitant use of antiplatelet agents, none of which were present in our case. In this context, the combination of anticoagulation and targeted antimicrobial therapy was considered the most appropriate strategy to prevent new complications and optimize clinical outcomes, given the patient’s individual risk profile.

We acknowledge that abdominal and liver CT were not performed. In addition, the Klebsiella pneumoniae isolate was not tested for a hypervirulent or hypermucoviscous phenotype, as such assays are not routinely available in our setting. These aspects should be considered when interpreting the case.

Conclusion

Lemierre syndrome is an infrequent and potentially fatal disease, characterized by an oropharyngeal infection associated with septic thrombosis of the internal jugular vein and septic embolism, predominantly pulmonary. Its diagnosis requires high clinical suspicion, particularly in the presence of negative blood cultures. However, bacterial identification can be achieved from pus or exudate samples, and diagnosis should be supported by imaging studies such as contrast-enhanced cervicothoracic CT and Doppler ultrasound. In this case, thrombosis was evidenced in the external jugular vein, an uncommon location in the context of this syndrome. Early administration of antibiotics is associated with a significant reduction in mortality and systemic complications. The decision to initiate anticoagulation should be individualized according to the clinical characteristics of each patient.
