The global scope of heterogeneous vancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (hVISA): a meta-analysis of its clinical impact, molecular epidemiology, and diagnostic challenges across 15,165 isolates
Authors: Donghua Yang, Mengyun Gu, Xiao Tang, Liling He, Jun Zhang, Xingyang Wang, Yinan Hou, Yating Cui, li Chan, Jinhui Tian, Meiling Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo systematically evaluate the clinical impact of heterogeneous vancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (hVISA), characterize its global molecular epidemiology, and compare the accuracy of mainstream diagnostic screening methods.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, we systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science for relevant literature published up to February 28, 2025. We conducted meta-analyses to calculate pooled odds ratios (ORs) for clinical outcomes, pooled prevalence for staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec) types, and synthesized the diagnostic accuracy of phenotypic screening methods using a bivariate random-effects model.ResultsA total of 59 studies were included, with a pooled hVISA prevalence of 12.0%. In clinical outcome analyses, hVISA infection was associated with higher odds of vancomycin treatment failure (OR = 2.68, 95% CI: 1.74–4.14) and a modest increase in all-cause mortality (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.03–1.77). For persistent or recurrent bacteremia, the pooled estimate did not reach statistical significance (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 0.62–5.34), and substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies. Molecular epidemiology analysis showed that SCCmec types II (26%) and III (22%) were predominant globally, with marked geographic variation. In diagnostic performance analysis, agar screening methods demonstrated higher pooled sensitivity (0.81) compared with Etest-based methods (0.73), whereas specificity was slightly lower (0.93 vs. 0.96). Overall diagnostic accuracy was comparable between methods, with overlapping summary AUC values (~ 0.92).ConclusionhVISA represents a clinically relevant phenotype associated with an increased likelihood of vancomycin treatment failure and a modest elevation in mortality risk. The association with persistent bacteremia remains uncertain due to limited data and heterogeneity. Geographic variation in molecular distribution was observed. Although agar screening methods demonstrated higher sensitivity for detection, substantial between-study heterogeneity warrants cautious interpretation of pooled diagnostic estimates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13473-6.

Introduction

Vancomycin has long been recommended as a standard treatment for serious MRSA infections, particularly in settings where alternative agents are limited. Its mechanism of action involves inhibiting peptidoglycan precursors, thereby disrupting bacterial cell wall synthesis. However, under persistent antibiotic selective pressure, some vancomycin-susceptible S. aureus (VSSA) isolates have developed reduced susceptibility by thickening and altering their cell wall structure [1]. When the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of vancomycin for an isolate increases to 4–8 mg/L, it is classified as vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (VISA) [2, 3]. A growing body of evidence suggests that VISA isolates often evolve from a precursor phenotype known as heterogeneous vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (hVISA) [3]. Although a precise definition of hVISA remains a subject of debate, “heterogeneous resistance” generally refers to the presence of a subpopulation of cells capable of tolerating higher concentrations of vancomycin, hidden within a larger population that appears phenotypically susceptible (MIC ≤ 2 mg/L). This subpopulation typically occurs at a frequency of 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁴ colony-forming units [4].

This unique “cryptic” resistance phenotype poses several significant challenges to clinical diagnosis and treatment. First, from a clinical standpoint, hVISA is frequently missed by routine antimicrobial susceptibility testing due to its susceptible phenotype. This can lead to the unwitting continuation of vancomycin use, which may be suboptimal for these infections, potentially resulting in persistent infections, treatment failure, and poorer clinical outcomes [5–7]. Second, in laboratory diagnostics, the detection of hVISA is itself a contentious issue. The gold standard method, population analysis profile–area under the curve (PAP-AUC), is too complex and time-consuming for routine clinical use [8]. This has prompted the widespread adoption of various simplified screening methods, such as the Etest gradient method and agar screening [9–11]. However, the diagnostic accuracy of these methods varies considerably, and their comparative performance remains inconclusive, creating significant confusion for clinical microbiology laboratories in selecting an optimal detection strategy. Furthermore, at the molecular epidemiological level, critical questions remain. As a key subgroup of MRSA, does the composition of hVISA’s core resistance determinant, the staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec), follow geographical patterns similar to those of the broader MRSA population? Or does it reflect the global ascendancy of specific clonal lineages? Answering these questions is crucial for understanding the global transmission dynamics of hVISA, yet systematic evidence is currently lacking.

Given these clinical, diagnostic, and epidemiological uncertainties, previous literature has typically addressed only a single aspect of hVISA—often through narrative review without quantitative synthesis, or through meta-analyses limited to one dimension such as diagnostic performance [5, 8, 10, 11]. To address these important gaps, we conducted a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis to synthesize evidence across three key dimensions: (1) to evaluate the clinical impact of hVISA infection on clinical outcomes; (2) to characterize its global molecular epidemiological features; and (3) to quantitatively compare the diagnostic accuracy of mainstream phenotypic screening methods, thereby providing evidence-based guidance for clinical practice.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251140456). Clinical trial registration: Not applicable.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science for studies published up to February 28, 2025. The search strategy combined terms related to hVISA and Staphylococcus aureus using Boolean operators. Key search terms included (“heteroresistance” OR “hetero-resistance” OR “heterogeneous resistance” OR “hVISA”) AND (“Staphylococcus aureus” OR “MRSA” OR “methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus”). In PubMed and Embase, searches were restricted to the Title/Abstract fields and subject headings (e.g., MeSH, Emtree). In Web of Science, searches were conducted in the Topic field (TS), which encompasses titles, abstracts, and keywords. The detailed search strategies for each database are provided in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Material 1).

Study selection

Records identified from the searches were imported into Rayyan (Rayyan Systems Inc.) for deduplication and screening. Two reviewers (D.H.Y. and X.T.) independently screened the titles and abstracts to identify potentially relevant articles. Subsequently, the full texts of these articles were retrieved and assessed for eligibility by the same two reviewers. Any disagreements at either the title/abstract or full-text screening stage were resolved through discussion or, if consensus could not be reached, by a third reviewer (J.T.). A pilot calibration exercise was conducted on a small subset of records to ensure consistent application of the eligibility criteria before commencing the formal screening process.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction

Two reviewers (D.H.Y. and X.T.) independently extracted data from the included studies using a pre-designed, standardized data extraction form. Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus with a third reviewer (J.T.). The extracted information included: first author, year of publication, country/region, study design, sample size, patient characteristics, definition and detection methods for hVISA, and detailed data required for our three analyses (e.g., number of outcome events and total subjects in each group, number of isolates for each SCCmec type, and the TP, FP, FN, and TN values for diagnostic tests).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed by two independent reviewers using appropriate tools based on the study design. For observational studies evaluating clinical outcomes, the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist [12] for Cohort and Case-Control Studies was used. For diagnostic accuracy studies, the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool [13] was employed to evaluate the risk of bias and concerns regarding applicability. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Furthermore, the certainty of evidence for clinical outcomes was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.0) with the meta and mada packages.

Clinical outcomes and molecular epidemiology

For the prevalence and molecular epidemiology analyses, pooled prevalence estimates and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the metaprop () function.

For the clinical outcome analysis, pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated using the metabin () function. All syntheses were performed under a random-effects model, with the between-study variance (τ²) estimated using the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) method.

Statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test (with p < 0.10 indicating significant heterogeneity) and the I² statistic, where I² > 50% was considered to indicate substantial heterogeneity. When significant heterogeneity was present, subgroup analyses were conducted based on pre-specified variables (e.g., geographical region) to explore potential sources. for outcomes exhibiting substantial heterogeneity, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the stability of the pooled effect estimates and to assess whether any single study disproportionately influenced the results.

Diagnostic accuracy

Prior to synthesis, non-independent data from studies reporting multiple protocols within the same patient cohort were aggregated to ensure statistical independence. A bivariate random-effects model, implemented via the reitsma () function in the mada package, was then used to jointly synthesize sensitivity and specificity. Based on the model parameters, we calculated pooled positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR + and LR−) and diagnostic odds ratios (DOR).

Hierarchical Summary Receiver Operating Characteristic (HSROC) curves were constructed to visually represent and compare the overall diagnostic performance of different test families. The Area Under the Curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed using a Monte Carlo simulation method (parametric bootstrap with 1,000 iterations) based on the variance-covariance matrix of the model parameters, as the mada package does not provide analytical CIs by default.

Furthermore, coupled forest plots were generated using the meta package to display the pooled sensitivity and specificity for each subgroup in detail. The ‘Etest-based methods’ family included conventional Etest, Etest Gradient Diffusion (GRD), and Macromethod Etest (MET). The ‘Agar screening methods’ family included various brain-heart infusion (BHI) agar plates containing different concentrations of vancomycin, such as BHI with 4 mg/L vancomycin (BHIV4), BHI with 3 mg/L vancomycin (BHIV3), and other variants like the Growth Phase Method (GPM).

Assessment of publication bias

For clinical outcomes and molecular epidemiology, potential publication bias was evaluated for meta-analyses including at least 10 studies. Visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s linear regression test were employed to detect small-study effects. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant indicating the presence of publication bias.

For diagnostic accuracy studies, publication bias was evaluated using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test. To account for correlated data from studies reporting multiple test methods, cluster-robust standard errors (clustered by study ID) were applied. A p-value < 0.10 was considered indicative of potential publication bias.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Our initial search across PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science yielded a total of 1943 records. After removing 1002 duplicates, 941 records were screened based on their titles and abstracts. This led to the assessment of 110 full-text articles for eligibility. Finally, a total of 59 studies met the inclusion criteria. The complete study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1), and the full list of included studies is provided in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Material 2).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the JBI checklist and QUADAS-2 tool.

Observational Studies (JBI Checklist): Overall methodological quality was generally moderate to high. Most studies demonstrated a low risk of bias in defining inclusion criteria and describing subjects and settings (Items 1–2). However, the most significant methodological limitation identified was the insufficient identification and management of confounding factors (Items 5 and 6). Specifically, many studies failed to explicitly describe strategies used to control for potential confounders, which might influence the observed clinical impact of hVISA (Fig. 2a).

Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2 Tool): For diagnostic studies, concerns regarding applicability were low across all domains. In the risk of bias assessment, while patient selection and reference standards were generally well-conducted, two key areas showed limitations. First, the risk of bias for the “Index Test” was frequently rated as “Unclear”, as many studies did not report whether the test was interpreted without knowledge of the reference standard (blinding). Second, the “Flow and Timing” domain showed a high risk of bias in several studies, primarily due to inconsistencies in the interval between the index test and the reference standard (Fig. 2b).

Study characteristics

Among the 59 included studies, 16 studies provided data for the meta-analysis of clinical outcomes (treatment failure, mortality, or persistent bacteremia), 25 studies contributed to the molecular epidemiology (SCCmec typing), and 40 studies were available for evaluating diagnostic accuracy.

Geographically, the studies demonstrated a broad global distribution across five major regions. East Asia contributed the largest number of studies (n = 21, primarily from China and Korea), followed by the Americas (n = 16, predominantly from the USA and Brazil), South Asia (n = 10, mainly from India), Europe (n = 9, including Italy, Poland, and Turkey), and other regions including Oceania (n = 1) and Global multicenter studies (n = 2). The types of infections caused by hVISA were varied and included bloodstream infections, pneumonia, and osteomyelitis (Table 1). Across all included studies, the pooled prevalence of hVISA was 12.0% (95% CI: 9.1%–16.8%)(Supplementary Material 3).

Molecular epidemiology of SCCmec types in hVISA isolates

We conducted a meta-analysis to determine the pooled prevalence of the five major Staphylococcal Cassette Chromosome mec (SCCmec) types among hVISA isolates (Table 2).

The overall global distribution of SCCmec types in hVISA was diverse. The most common types were SCCmec type II, with a pooled prevalence of 26% (95% CI: 11%–45%), and SCCmec type III, with a pooled prevalence of 22% (95% CI: 8%–39%). Significant heterogeneity was observed across studies for all subtype analyses (I² > 77%, p < 0.0001).

To explore the source of this significant heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analyses based on geographical regions. Detailed results, including within-subgroup heterogeneity statistics, are presented in Table 2, and forest plots are provided in Supplementary Material 7–11.

The analysis revealed distinct regional patterns in SCCmec distribution. For SCCmec types I, II, IV, and V, prevalence varied significantly across geographical regions (p < 0.01 for all). This stratification also explained a substantial portion of the heterogeneity for types I, IV, and V, with within-subgroup I² values being markedly reduced in several regions (e.g., dropping to 0% in South Asia for type I).

Specifically, in East Asia, the predominant types were SCCmec III (41%) and SCCmec II (31%). In contrast, the molecular epidemiology in South Asia was characterized by a high prevalence of SCCmec type V (42%) and SCCmec type IV (20%). In the Americas, SCCmec type II was the dominant type (44%). whereas the proportion of SCCmec type III (12%) was considerably lower than that observed in East Asia (). Given the sufficient number of studies, publication bias was assessed for the representative SCCmec type III. Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 12) and Egger’s test (P = 0.58) indicated no significant publication bias.

Diagnostic accuracy of phenotypic methods for hVISA detection

As automated systems and standard broth microdilution methods were not specifically designed for hVISA screening and were insufficiently reported for pooled diagnostic comparison, the analysis was restricted to Agar Screen and Etest-based methods. The Agar Screen method demonstrated a pooled sensitivity of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.62–0.95), which was higher than that of the Etest Family (Sensitivity: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.53–0.90) (Fig. 4a). Conversely, the Etest Family exhibited superior specificity, with a pooled estimate of 0.96 (95% CI: 0.93–0.99), compared to 0.93 (95% CI: 0.85–0.98) for Agar Screen (Fig. 4b). Significant heterogeneity was observed in both sensitivity and specificity analyses for both methods (I2 > 90%, P < 0.001). Assessment of publication bias using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test indicated a potential bias (P = 0.08).

Discussion

Clinical implications of hVISA infections

The clinical significance of infections caused by heterogeneous vancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (hVISA) has long been a subject of debate. Our meta-analysis synthesized evidence from the existing literature regarding three key clinical outcomes: treatment failure, all-cause mortality, and persistent bacteremia.

Vancomycin Treatment Failure

Our analysis showed that patients with hVISA infections had higher odds of experiencing vancomycin treatment failure compared with those with VSSA infections. Treatment failure was defined as a composite endpoint encompassing adverse clinical responses, including persistent bacteremia, lack of clinical resolution, need for therapeutic modification, or death. According to the JBI appraisal, the overall methodological quality of the included studies was acceptable, although some cohorts reported incomplete follow-up or limited adjustment for potential confounders. Despite variability in patient populations and infection characteristics, the association between hVISA and treatment failure was directionally consistent across individual studies [14–22], and statistical heterogeneity was low.

Across the five contributing studies [14–18], glycopeptide therapy—primarily vancomycin—was consistently used as first-line treatment in accordance with prevailing MRSA management standards. Several cohorts explicitly defined appropriate empirical therapy as administration of at least one in vitro–active agent within 48 h, and rates of appropriate initial therapy were generally comparable between hVISA and VSSA groups [14, 15, 18]. However, treatment strategies were not entirely uniform. In cases of persistent bacteremia or inadequate clinical response, therapeutic modification was commonly implemented, including switching to alternative anti-MRSA agents such as daptomycin, linezolid, rifampicin, or combination regimens [14–17]. Therapeutic drug monitoring and vancomycin exposure parameters were inconsistently reported, and dosing strategies were not standardized. In addition, variation in infection type (e.g., bloodstream infection versus endocarditis), severity of illness, and source-control practices may have influenced outcomes [14–18].

Taken together, although vancomycin was broadly administered in a manner consistent with contemporary practice, heterogeneity in antimicrobial exposure and clinical management may contribute to residual confounding. The observed association between the hVISA phenotype and treatment failure should therefore be interpreted in the context of these clinical variations. This association may reflect reduced bactericidal activity of vancomycin against heteroresistant subpopulations, particularly in high-inoculum infections [20, 21, 23], or the impact of prior vancomycin exposure and selective pressure [18], rather than differences in initial antibiotic appropriateness alone.

All-Cause Mortality: Mortality and persistent bacteremia are typically regarded as critical indicators for evaluating therapeutic efficacy. Our pooled analysis of 16 studies demonstrated that the odds of all-cause mortality were 1.35 times higher in the hVISA group compared to the VSSA group. Although this difference was statistically significant, the effect size was relatively modest. Items related to outcome assessment and completeness of follow-up scored highly in the JBI appraisal, although residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. This suggests that mortality is a distal outcome influenced by a multitude of factors, including not only the resistance phenotype of the pathogen but also the patient’s underlying health status and the adequacy of treatment (e.g., therapeutic drug monitoring). Nevertheless, it is plausible that hVISA correlates with parameters known to impact mortality—such as high bacterial inoculum, persistent bacteremia, and elevated vancomycin MICs—thereby translating into a tangible clinical consequence: reduced survival [24, 25].

Persistent Bacteremia

In contrast, the analysis of persistent bacteremia yielded a higher, albeit non-significant, odds ratio for the hVISA group compared to the VSSA group. While the confidence interval crossed unity, the link is both biologically plausible and clinically significant. A hallmark of hVISA is the presence of a subpopulation of cells capable of surviving and slowly replicating at vancomycin concentrations that inhibit the major population [26, 27]. During therapy, vancomycin may effectively clear the majority of susceptible cells but fail to eradicate the resistant subpopulations, resulting in delayed bloodstream clearance and a prolonged infection course that increases the risk of treatment exhaustion and subsequent clinical failure [13, 20, 28]. This prolonged duration of bacteremia is not merely a laboratory finding; it is a potential contributor to the ‘war of attrition’ we described, increasing the risk of metastatic complications (e.g., endocarditis or osteomyelitis), prolonging hospital stays, ultimately contributing to clinically defined treatment failure [29–31]. Consequently, persistent bacteremia should be viewed as a critical clinical signal that may suggest an underlying hVISA phenotype, warranting consideration for therapeutic modification [32, 33]. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis by sequentially excluding individual studies to address the substantial heterogeneity (I²=80.3%) observed for this outcome. The conclusion of no significant association remained unchanged across all iterations. This lack of statistical significance may be attributed to sample size limitations and the complexity of infections included in the studies, factors which may have partially masked the independent impact of hVISA on this specific outcome. Additionally, factors such as indication bias and unmeasured clinical severity may also influence the observed associations in these observational studies.

Molecular epidemiology

A key aspect of understanding hVISA is tracing its origins within the broader MRSA population. While hVISA emergence is an adaptive response to vancomycin pressure, mediated by mutations in cell wall metabolism genes [34–38], the genetic background from which it arises is critical. We chose SCCmec typing as a molecular epidemiological tool for this purpose. Although the SCCmec cassette is not directly involved in the hVISA phenotype, its subtypes are classical markers that distinguish major MRSA evolutionary lineages, such as hospital-associated (HA-MRSA) and community-associated (CA-MRSA) strains [30, 32]. Analyzing the SCCmec distribution in hVISA isolates allows us to trace their clonal origins, infer evolutionary pathways, and assess public health risks.

Our global synthesis indicates that SCCmec types II and III are the dominant genotypes in hVISA isolates. These patterns are highly consistent with known clonal structures of HA-MRSA. SCCmec type II is strongly linked to the CC5 lineage, including the globally disseminated USA100 clone (ST5-SCCmec II) [39, 40], which has been a major driver of hospital-associated MRSA in North America and increasingly in Asia. SCCmec type III is characteristic of the pandemic ST239 lineage, a long-standing HA-MRSA clone prevalent in Asia, the Middle East, and South America. The predominance of types II and III in our dataset therefore reinforces the view that hVISA largely arises from established HA-MRSA lineages with long-term hospital adaptation [34, 36].

Subgroup analyses revealed pronounced geographical variation. In the Americas, SCCmec type II accounted for 44% of hVISA isolates, supporting the predominance of CC5-derived HA-MRSA lineages such as USA100 [26, 27]. In East Asia, both SCCmec II and III were common, consistent with the region’s historical dominance of CC5 and ST239 clones [34, 41]. This pattern suggests that hVISA in East Asia is primarily an “endemic” phenomenon arising from ongoing adaptive evolution within entrenched hospital MRSA populations.

In contrast, the SCCmec landscape in South Asia was distinct, with types IV and V dominating. These elements are frequently associated with CA-MRSA lineages, such as ST22 (EMRSA-15), ST8 (USA300), and notably ST772 (the Bengal Bay clone), a successful community-associated clone with increasing global spread [34, 41]. The presence of SCCmec IV/V among hVISA isolates raises the possibility that community-associated MRSA backgrounds may also serve as reservoirs for the emergence of the hVISA phenotype. This dual pattern—HA-MRSA consolidation in some regions and possible CA-MRSA involvement in others—highlights a more complex epidemiological landscape than previously appreciated. It underscores the need for expanded genomic surveillance to determine whether the hVISA phenotype is evolving independently within multiple clonal backgrounds or spreading through horizontal genetic acquisition across MRSA lineages.

Diagnostic accuracy and methodological optimization

The central challenge in hVISA detection stems from its intrinsic biological feature: the low-frequency presence of the resistant subpopulation [39]. As the gold standard PAP-AUC method is impractical for routine use [42], screening strategies are often plagued by issues of insufficient sensitivity, poor reproducibility, and lack of standardization. This methodological uncertainty is a significant contributor to the heterogeneity observed across studies [43, 44].

Our meta-analysis provides a rigorous quantitative evaluation, acknowledging that the results are characterized by substantial heterogeneity. While both agar screening and Etest methods possess high overall diagnostic accuracy (AUC > 0.9), the apparent superiority in sensitivity observed for Agar Screen (0.81 vs. 0.73) warrants cautious interpretation. This potential advantage in sensitivity is paramount for a screening tool. however, the lack of standardization across different laboratory protocols remains a major source of methodological uncertainty. Mechanistically, agar screening (e.g., using BHI agar with 3–4 mg/L vancomycin) provides a uniform sub-inhibitory environment that may more effectively enrich the low-proportion resistant subpopulation [45]. In comparison, the Etest method, despite its excellent specificity, showed lower sensitivity, potentially due to variability in inoculum density and the subjective interpretation of micro-colonies within the inhibition zone.

Furthermore, we conducted subgroup analyses within the two major methodological families—Agar Screen and Etest-based methods—to elucidate the impact of specific protocol variations.

Within the Agar Screen family (comprising BHIV3, BHIV4, BHIV6, and GPM), our results demonstrated that vancomycin concentration is a critical determinant of diagnostic performance. The BHIV3 method (3 mg/L) achieved perfect sensitivity (1.00 [95% CI: 1.00–1.00]), establishing it as a highly effective tool for ruling out hVISA, albeit with slightly reduced specificity (0.87). The widely adopted BHIV4 method (4 mg/L) provided the most favorable balance in our pooled analysis, yielding a sensitivity of 0.81 and specificity of 0.96. In stark contrast, BHIV6 (6 mg/L) exhibited unacceptably low sensitivity (0.22), indicating that higher vancomycin concentrations likely inhibit the growth of the resistant subpopulation, leading to false-negative results. Consequently, these findings suggest that vancomycin concentrations of 3–4 mg/L are optimal for screening purposes. Importantly, most diagnostic studies were judged as having low concerns regarding patient selection and reference standard applicability according to QUADAS-2, supporting the reliability of these pooled estimates; although in several studies blinding of index test interpretation was unclear, this risk of bias was unlikely to meaningfully affect the comparative performance of different protocols.

Within the Etest family (including Standard Etest, Etest GRD, and MET), the Standard Etest demonstrated relatively robust performance, with a pooled sensitivity of 0.84 [95% CI: 0.66–0.97] and a specificity of 0.97. Notably, it outperformed both the Etest GRD (Sensitivity: 0.72) and the MET method (Sensitivity: 0.48). This suggests that the Standard Etest remains a reliable alternative when agar-based screening is unavailable, whereas the GRD and MET methods should be interpreted with caution due to their higher risk of missed diagnoses.

These observations align with previous literature regarding the biological basis of hVISA detection. Khatib et al. previously noted that growth on agar plates supplemented with 4 mg/L and 3 mg/L vancomycin correlated well with VISA and hVISA phenotypes, respectively, mirroring the gold standard PAP-AUC results more accurately than Etest strategies [46, 47]. Similarly, studies by Riederer et al. and Agarwal et al. concluded that the BHIV4 method offered superior diagnostic precision compared to Etest-based screening approaches [29].

Conclusion

Heterogeneous vancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (hVISA) remains a clinically relevant phenotype, particularly given its association with an increased likelihood of vancomycin treatment failure. Its molecular epidemiology demonstrates substantial geographic variability, reflecting the evolving landscape of MRSA lineages.

Our findings provide a comprehensive synthesis of clinical, molecular, and diagnostic evidence. In the diagnostic domain, agar screening methods were associated with comparatively higher pooled sensitivity, whereas Etest-based approaches showed slightly higher specificity. However, given the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies, these results should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as comparative performance patterns rather than definitive practice recommendations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the causal relationship between hVISA and clinical outcomes requires confirmation through more rigorous study designs. Our meta-analysis, by its nature, synthesizes data from observational studies, which cannot fully eliminate confounding bias. Well-designed prospective cohort studies are needed to definitively establish this link. Second, significant heterogeneity was present in many of our analyses, and while subgroup analyses explored some sources, residual heterogeneity from methodological and clinical differences likely remains.
