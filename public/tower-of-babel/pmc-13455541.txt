Evaluation of The Use of Hydroxyurea in Treating Children With Sickle Cell Anemia in Central Africa's Rural Area
Authors: Gloire Mbayabo, Paul Lumbala Kabuyi, Mamy Ngole, Aimé Lumaka, Valerie Race, Gert Matthijs, Tite Mikobi Minga, Koenraad Devriendt, Chris Van Geet, Prosper Tshilobo Lukusa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSickle cell anemia (SCA) is highly prevalent in Central Africa. This disease causes severe manifestations in children, requiring treatment. Hydroxyurea (HU) is currently the most effective treatment for SCA. We evaluated the use of HU in children living in rural Central Africa.MethodsWe conducted a clinical trial using HU in the Kisantu Saint Luc Hospital (KSLH) in the DR Congo (DRC) from November 2017 to February 2020. SCA patients aged 6 months to 18 years, with a moderate to severe form of SCA (Adegoke score), were treated with an entry HU dose of 15 mg/kg per day, followed with gradual dose escalation of 5 mg/kg per day increments every 6 months, up to a maximally tolerated dose of 30 mg/Kg/day. To determine the clinical and biological response to the treatment (efficacy of HU), we compared the clinical and biological data collected during the first and second year of treatment to the baseline values.ResultsSixty‐nine patients (37 boys and 32 girls; sex‐ratio M/F 1.15) were eligible for the clinical trial using HU. Only 39 patients remained at the end of the clinical trial (56.5%). On average, the HbF increased to 3‐fold at 12 months and 3.3‐fold at 24 months, which was significantly different to the baseline. Thirty‐seven (80.4%) patients presented a good clinical response, whereas 9 (19.6%) did not.ConclusionThis clinical demonstrated the clinical and biological effectiveness of HU treatment in a cohort of young Congolese patients. In addition to tremendous logistical challenges, low adherence is the major threat to HU treatment in a rural area. There is a need to implement appropriate strategies to increase the adherence to the HU treatment in these rural settings. Introduction

Sickle cell anemia (SCA) is the most common monogenic disease worldwide. The World Health Organization considers this disease as the fourth public health priority, after AIDS, malaria, and tuberculosis [1, 2]. SCA constitutes a serious public health problem in Africa, especially in the DR Congo (DRC), where the incidence of homozygotes is around 2% [3]. This autosomal recessive condition leads to severe morbidity and early mortality, more pronounced in children below the age of 5 years. In contrast, heterozygote carriers are generally healthy [4].

Besides the SCA‐related problem, SCA patients in limited‐resource countries are also exposed to malnutrition, which is one of the major health problems in Africa [5]. Therefore, SCA patients face a vicious circle where malnutrition leads to an exacerbation of both acute and chronic complications of SCA, causing an increase in health expenses, resulting in the aggravation of poverty and further exacerbating malnutrition. There is a need to break this vicious circle by providing SCA patients with effective treatment such as hydroxyurea (HU). This drug increases the level of fetal hemoglobin (HbF) while decreasing the number of white blood cells (WBCs), platelets, and reticulocytes [6, 7, 8]. In addition, HU increases the deformability of red blood cells (RBCs) and reduces inflammation, oxidative stress, and the expression of adhesive molecules on erythrocytes’ membrane [9, 10]. This leads to a net decrease in the occurrence of vaso‐occlusive crises, ultimately improving the quality of life and enhancing survival of SCA patients. These benefits of HU treatment in children have been demonstrated by multiple studies worldwide. Hence, on December 21, 2017, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted regular approval for the use of HU (Siklos, Addmedica) in the reduction of the frequency of painful crises and the need for blood transfusions in pediatric patients presenting with recurrent moderate to severe painful crises [11].

In DRC, access to effective treatment for the management of SCA is currently a big challenge. Multiple known effective approaches, including bone marrow transplant, transfusion exchange and HU, remain inaccessible to Congolese patients. SCA children, especially in rural settings, typically receive only very basic treatment consisting of penicillin and folic acid.

We report the observations of a clinical trial evaluating HU treatment in children with SCA, followed in a rural hospital in the DRC.

Methods

Recruitment and Clinical Data Collection

This clinical trial using HU was conducted from November 2017 to February 2020 at Kisantu Saint Luc Hospital (KSLH). This institution is one of the major referral hospitals in the Kongo‐Central Province in the DRC, located 120 km away from the capital, Kinshasa. Patients were recruited from a cohort of 136 SCA children based on the following inclusion criteria:

‐ Be 6 months to 18‐year‐old

‐ Be 6 months to 18‐year‐old

‐ Attend SCA clinic in the Pediatric Department of KSLH on regular basis (considered as an indication of probable therapy compliance)

‐ Have confirmed SCA diagnosis on both capillary hemoglobin electrophoresis and molecular testing

‐ Present a moderate or severe form of SCA according to the Adegoke score [12]

‐ Show a low level of HbF (HbF < 15%)

‐ A written consent form is signed by parents/guardians after a consent process

The first year of the project consisted in an observation period where patients were evaluated monthly aiming to obtain baseline clinical land biological values. The following variables were recorded: age, gender, weight, height, nutritional state, and the occurrence and number of febrile episodes, hospitalizations, transfusions, acute chest syndrome, avascular bone necrosis, cerebral infarct, acute hepatic, or splenic sequestration, painful crises, and priapism. Blood samples were collected every 3 months for biological investigations.

The SCA scoring system proposed by Adegoke et al., for the classification of disease severity was scored for each patient using the baseline data. This score relies on six clinical parameters that are easy to assess (number of painful episodes, number of transfusions, number of hospitalizations, liver enlargement, splenic enlargement, and lifetime complications such as cerebrovascular disease, acute chest syndrome, avascular necrosis, etc.) and two standard biological parameters (packed cell volume and WBCs). This scoring system assesses patient's past condition in the previous 12 months and lifetime complications. A score between 0 and 5 was assigned to items (Table S1). The total score was equal to the sum of scores obtained for individual items and ranged from 0 to 34. This total score was used to distinguish three categories of disease severity: mild (total score < 8), moderate (8–17), and severe (> 17) (Table S3).

Biological and Molecular Data

Blood samples were collected in 4 mL EDTA‐coated tubes, stored in a cool box, and immediately transported. Capillary hemoglobin electrophoresis was done using the MINICAP Sebia Flex Percing phoresis Rel 8.6.3. DNA was extracted using the manual salting out method. Detection of the E6V mutation was performed using a PCR amplification of the exon 1 of the HBB gene and a subsequent restriction digestion with the DdeI enzyme, followed by agarose gel electrophoresis at alkaline pH [13]. The whole blood count was determined on automaton HUMACOUNT 60 TS. Bilirubin was determined using the Spectrophotometer HUMALYZER 2000 and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) using Cypres Kit for measuring LDH.

Clinical Trial Using HU

Patients were treated with an initial HU dose of 15 mg/kg per day, which was increased gradually in increments of 5 mg/kg per day every 6 months, to a maximally tolerated dose of 30 mg/Kg/day (Figure 1). Escalation was individualized according to clinical response and achievement 20% HbF threshold 14, 15, 16, 17]. Compliance with treatment was monitored by patient interviews and by counting the remaining tablets, along with objective laboratory studies (e.g., HbF, mean corpuscular volume [MCV]). We collected clinical data monthly and biological data every 3 months during the first year and semiannually over the second year of the clinical trial.

Response to HU Treatment

To evaluate the efficacy of HU treatment, we compared the clinical and biological data from the first and second year of treatment with the baseline data. This included acute manifestations (number of painful crises, transfusions, hospitalization) [18, 19, 20]; the increase in HbF levels, MCV and the decrease in the LDH level [6, 21]. Clinically, we consider as responders patients who reduced acute clinical manifestations by at least 50% compared to the baseline, using the Adegoke score [22]. Biologically, a responder was defined as reaching a HbF level of at least 20% or an increase in HbF levels of at least 3 times the baseline level [17, 20, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27].

Statistical Analysis

Data were collected using a survey form, then recorded onto an Excel file using Microsoft Excel 2013, and finally imported to SPSS software version 25.0 for statistical analysis. Qualitative data (sex, clinical, and biological response,) are presented as proportions whereas quantitative data (age, height, severity score, number of deaths, biological parameters) are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) when the distribution was normal and as median with range when the distribution was not normal. Columns and bars are used to provide clear data visualization. Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare means, and chi‐square and Fischer tests to compare proportions.

Results

Enrollment and Sociodemographic Characteristics of the Study Population

From the 136 SCA children followed at KSLH, 69 (37 boys and 32 girls; sex‐ratio M/F 1.15) met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the clinical trial using HU [28]. The median age was 9 years (interquartile range: 5.5–12.5 years). Among these 69 patients, 39 (56.5%) completed the clinical trial. Thirty patients (43.5%) discontinued the study: 7 died (10.1%), including 4 during the first year and 3 during the second year; 2 from Kisantu to distant villages, and 21 were lost to follow‐up without any apparent explanation (Figure 2).

Biological Response to HU Treatment

Regarding biological response, using the threshold of 20% HbF level, 2 (2.9%) patients achieved this level at the 3rd month of HU treatment, 21 (30.4%) at the 9th month, 15 (21.7%) at the 15th month, 3 (4.3%) at the 18th month, and 2 (2.9%) at the 24th month. When considering the increase of baseline HbF by a factor of 3, 1 (1.4%) patient achieved this at the 3rd month, 11 (15.9%) patients at the 9th month, 8 (11.6%) at the 12th month, 5 (7.2%) at the 18th month, and 2 (2.9%) at the 24th month (Figure 3). Over the 2‐year follow‐up, 49 of the 69 patients (81.7%) showed a good biological response (biological responders) and 11 (18.3%) did not (biological non‐responders). Forty‐three (71.6%) reached the threshold of 20% HbF level, with 27 (45%) of them achieving at least a threefold increase over baseline values. The mean dose required to reach an excellent biological response was 26.8 ± 3.4 mg/kg/day. As shown in Figure 3, the time needed to achieve this biological response varied from 3 to 24 months. Of those completing the trial, 7of 39 patients (18%) only reached this threshold after more than 1 year of treatment.

Eleven patients did not show biological response to treatment. Among them, three (27.3%) completed the trial and can thus be considered true non‐responders. The remaining non‐responders discontinued the study at 3 and 6 months (one patient at each time point) and at 9, 12, and 18 months (two patients at each time point). These may not have reached the dose needed to respond.

In 39 patients who completed the trial, we observed a mean increase in HbF of threefold at 12 months and 3.3‐fold at 24 months. The clinical response was assessed in 46 patients, of whom 37 (80.4%) showed a good clinical response (clinical responders) and 9 (19.6%) did not respond (clinical non‐responders). There was a significant reduction in hospitalizations days, from median of 4 (3–6; p25–p75) at baseline to a median of 0 (0–2; p25–p75) after 12 months (Table 2). This improvement was explained by a significant reduction in the number of painful crises and the need for transfusions (Table 2).

No adverse events were observed, either clinically or biologically (hematologic toxicity, liver, or renal failure). Therefore, adverse effects cannot explain the lack of response. From the group of 46 patients in whom the response was evaluated, 7 patients discontinued the study early. Of these, only one patient failed to respond both clinically and biologically; he left the study at 12 months. Of the remaining six patients, three responded both biologically and clinically, two only biologically, and two only clinically.

Discussion

We conducted a clinical trial to evaluate the potential of HU treatment to improve the health of children with SCA in a rural area of Central Africa. The most important finding was that HU treatment dramatically reduced the number of hospitalizations for transfusions or acute complications [29, 30, 31]. These benefits were already evident during the first year of HU treatment and persisted into the second year. In most patients, this was paralleled by the biological effects, including a decrease in the number of leucocytes, reticulocytes, platelets, and bilirubin, as well as an increase in MCV [8, 32]. In contrast, hemoglobin levels remained consistently low [33]. This is likely due to the limited‐resource setting in Central Africa, where malnutrition is a major health problem leading to iron deficiency and anemia, especially in rural areas. Moreover, infectious diseases such as malaria are widely observed in Central Africa [2, 34, 35]. Although iron studies were not evaluated, malaria was documented, and no statistical difference in hemoglobin levels was observed between patients with malaria and those without malaria infection.

These clinical and biological effects are mediated mostly by elevated HbF levels [31, 36]. However, some patients showed clinical improvement without a strong biological response. This confirms that HU, apart from increasing HbF levels, may improve the clinical state through other mechanisms, including the decrease in the number of WBC, platelets, and reticulocytes [8, 32, 37]. In addition, HU increases the deformability of RBC and reduces inflammation, oxidative stress, and the expression of adhesive molecules on the erythrocytes membrane [10, 32, 38, 39, 40]. Moreover, some patients show a good biological response without a good clinical response [25, 41, 42, 43]. In these patients, clinical improvement was minimal. The exact reasons for this finding remain unclear. However, we noticed that half of them tripled the initial HbF level but did not reach the 20% HbF threshold. In these patients and those without a good biological response despite achieving the MTD of HU, the observed changes were minimal and did not meet the criteria for a good clinical response: They continued to require transfusions and experienced VOCs, although less than before HU treatment.

Considering the biological response to HU, most patients (36/43, 83.7%) reached the threshold of 20% of HbF with the low dose of 20 mg/kg/day, while other patients required a high dose of HU [16]. This difference in response to HU may be related to genetic factors influencing individual pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics [44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. The genetic pathways regulating the HBF response to HU treatment are complex. Some studies have shown that the degree of HBF response to HU is influenced by specific genetic polymorphisms [41]. For instance, HbF response to HU treatment has been associated with SNPs within the 6q22.3–23.2 and 8q11–q12 regions, as well as in the ARG2, FLT1, HAO2, and NOS1 genes [42]. Other studies reported associations between HU response and polymorphisms in the guanosine triphosphate (GTP)‐binding protein gene [43]. In addition, HU effects are also mediated by pathways involving BCL11A [44].

We must point out that no apparent adverse effects were reported during the clinical trial. This assumes that the drug's tolerability is good in children, as reported in some African studies conducted in pediatric populations [16, 20, 49, 50]. The major limitation of this study is the high number of dropouts. Of the 69 patients included, only 39 completed the trial with a sex ratio of 1.29, despite the clear benefits of HU treatment and the fact that the treatment, medical visits, and additional examinations were entirely free of charge. Among the 30 patients who left the study, seven deaths were reported, with five attributed to severe anemia, one to respiratory distress, and another arrived at KSLH already dead. Two patients moved to another region, and the remaining 21 patients were lost to follow‐up. Dropouts patients were distributed almost evenly during the 2 years of the clinical trial. The reasons for this high proportion of dropouts are not clear [36]. One possible factor is that this study was conducted in a rural area where people depend on agriculture and farming for their living and the schedule of follow‐up consultations may not have fit well with their agricultural activities. Another explanation could be the low literacy of the population, which may have prevented parents from fully understanding the importance of regular follow‐up in managing the disease. In addition, mystical and religious beliefs may lead some parents to attribute the disease to supernatural causes and to doubt the benefits of HU treatment [51, 52]. To improve compliance of HU treatment in rural settings in DRC, several strategies can be considered: raising parents’ awareness of the benefits of HU and improving their literacy levels, combating the harmful mystical‐religious beliefs of the rural population concerning SCA, encouraging and supporting the existing organization of families of children with this disease, ensuring that HU is available to patients so that they can obtain it as soon as they need it.

Conclusion

This clinical trial demonstrated the effectiveness of HU treatment by achieving a good response clinically and/or biologically in most patients. This response appears early but requires continued treatment to maintain its effects. These results show that, despite significant logistical difficulties, HU treatment in a rural area in the DRC holds promise to improve the quality of life of children with SCA and their families.
