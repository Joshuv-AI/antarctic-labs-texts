Switching CDK4/6 inhibitors to overcome toxicity in HR-positive/HER2-negative advanced breast cancer
Authors: Chiara Benvenuti, Alessandro Viansone, Flavia Jacobs, Thomas Grinda, Jean Zeghondy, Mariangela Gaudio, Laura Clapis, Riccardo Gerosa, Jacopo Canzian, Caroline Poisson, Meriem Mokdad-Adi, Paola Tiberio, Giuseppe Saltalamacchia, Alberto Zambelli, Armando Santoro, Barbara Pistilli, Rita De Sanctis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimThere is limited data on the safety of switching between CDK4/6i to address toxicity-related permanent discontinuation in patients with HR+/HER2- advanced breast cancer (ABC).To overcome this issue, this international observational study included consecutive HR+/HER2- ABC patients treated with CDK4/6i at two cancer centers, who switched the CDK4/6i for toxicity. The study aimed to evaluate the safety, feasibility, and clinical impact of this approach.ResultsAmong 1413 patients, 67 (4.5%) switched CDK4/6i due to toxicity. The median time to switch was three months. Adverse events leading to switch were mainly hematologic and hepatic. Recurrence of the same toxicity occurred in 30% of patients, but with lower severity for neutropenia (p=0.047) and hepatotoxicity (p=0.005). Notably, among the 67 patients who switched CDK4/6i, only seven (10%) ultimately required permanent discontinuation of the second CDK4/6i for toxicity, primarily for abemaciclib-induced diarrhea, suggesting that switching may represent a promising strategy to avoid treatment interruption. Median progression-free survival from the start of the first CDK4/6i was 16.8 monthsConclusionIn conclusion, switching CDK4/6i appears to be a safe and pragmatic strategy to address toxicity-related permanent discontinuation of CDK4/6i.Keywords: breast cancer, toxicity, CDK4/6 inhibitors, treatment discontinuation, switch

Introduction

Cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 inhibitors (CDK4/6i) are a cornerstone in hormone receptor-positive (HR+)/Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2-negative (HER2-) breast cancer (BC) treatment. The safety profiles of abemaciclib, palbociclib and ribociclib are partially shared, but toxicities prevalence varies due to their distinct pharmacologic properties.1,2 Adverse events (AEs) are usually manageable through supportive measures, dose adjustments and interruptions.3,4 Pooled safety analyses showed that permanent discontinuation due to toxicity or poor tolerability varied by agent (8% for palbociclib and around 10-15% for ribociclib and abemaciclib) and was higher in adjuvant studies for both abemaciclib and ribociclib (18 – 20%).5,6

The optimal management of permanent CDK4/6i discontinuation due to toxicity remains incompletely defined. The undeniable superiority of the combination regimen over endocrine therapy (ET) alone supports switching to an alternative CDK4/6i to maximize effectiveness, but clashes with the risk of potential cross-toxicity due to partially overlapping safety profiles.

Evidence supporting the feasibility of CDK4/6i switching is limited to few case series.7-11 We conducted an international retrospective study to assess the safety, feasibility and clinical impact of switching CDK4/6i due to toxicity issues in patients with HR+/HER2- advanced breast cancer (ABC) treated in two cancer centers in Italy and France.

Materials and methods

This multicenter retrospective study included consecutive HR+/HER2- advanced breast cancer (ABC) patients who switched to an alternative CDK4/6i due to unacceptable toxicity, without disease progression, between 2019 and 2023 at Humanitas Research Hospital (Rozzano, Italy) and Gustave Roussy (Villejuif, France). Detailed patient, tumor, and treatment data were collected from electronic medical reports. The study was approved by the IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital Ethics Committee (Protocol identifying number 176/24). All patients signed the informed consent form in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

We conducted univariate analyses with Chi-Squared or Fisher’s exact test and Mann-Whitney tests. Time to CDK4/6i switch and treatment duration was described as median and range. Progression-free survival (PFS), calculated from the start of the first CDK4/6i treatment, was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method.

The primary objective of the study was to evaluate the safety and feasibility of the switching strategy. Secondary objectives included describing the types of CDK4/6i switches, comparing AEs of the initial and switched CDK4/6i, assessing the timing and reasons for switching and the PFS curves.

Results

Among 1413 consecutive patients diagnosed with HR+/HER2- ABC and treated with CDK4/6i and ET between 2019 and 2023, 67 (4.5%) underwent a switch of CDK4/6i for toxicity issues and were included in the present analysis. The median age was 58 years (range: 30-87). Most patients had visceral disease (64%) and received CDK4/6i as first-line therapy (82%).

Ribociclib was the most common prescribed first CDK4/6i (n=38, 57%), while palbociclib was the most frequently chosen CDK4/6i to switch to (n=31, 51%) (Figure 1). The median time to the switch was three months (range, 0-29 months), with no significant difference between the three compounds. In 64% of cases, the second CDK4/6i was initiated at full dose. The ET partner remained unchanged in all cases except for three patients who switched between different Aromatase inhibitors (AIs).

AEs leading to the CDK4/6i switch were mainly hematological and hepatic (Table 1). They were grade 2 in 18 patients (27%), grade 3 in 42 (63%) and grade 4 in seven (10%). Re-occurrence of the same toxicity leading to the first CDK4/6i discontinuation was observed in 20 patients (30%): grade 1 in six cases (9%), grade 2 in five (7%), grade 3 in eight (12%) and grade 4 in one (2%). Hematologic toxicities accounted for the majority (75%) and for all the grade 3-4 events, while the remaining AEs were transaminase increases, all graded 1-2. The severity of AEs was lower with the second CDK4/6i compared to upfront CDK4/6i for both neutropenia (p=0.047) and hepatotoxicity (p=0.005). Among the 67 patients who were potentially at risk of permanent CDK4/6i discontinuation due to toxicity, only seven (10%) ultimately discontinued the second CDK4/6i for this reason. In all but one case discontinuation of the second CDK4/6i for toxicity occurred with abemaciclib, with diarrhea being the main reason for treatment interruption.

The median PFS from first CDK4/6i initiation was 16.8 months (range, 3-54.5), with 30 patients still on treatment at the time of this analysis. Among the 18 patients who switched the CDK4/6i before radiological assessment, 16 achieved a radiologic or metabolic response with the second CDK4/6i (eight complete and eight partial responses).

Discussion

Our study showed that regardless of the type and severity of toxicities leading to the discontinuation of the initial CDK4/6i, the majority of patients benefited from switching without serious safety issues. The switch is a relatively early event occurring within the first months, even without all dose reduction options being exhausted, reflecting a tendency to prioritize early changes to enhance patient tolerability and adherence.

Although the same toxicity that led to switching recurred in 30% of cases, the discontinuation rate of the second CDK4/6i due to toxicity issues was markedly lower (10%). This low rate of discontinuation observed in our cohort strongly supports the clinical feasibility of this strategy, particularly when guided by a tailored approach. Further, three quarters of the cases of toxicity reoccurrence affected the hematologic area, a class-related and expected toxicity, which rarely translate into potential life-threatening conditions (i.e., febrile neutropenia). Recurrence of transaminases increase was grade 1-2 in all cases and did not lead to CDK4/6i discontinuation in any case, in agreement with literature data.12-15 Almost all patients who discontinued treatment due to liver toxicity were switched to palbociclib (14 out of 16), reflecting its lower risk for transaminases elevation.

The notable responses and survival outcomes with this switching strategy strengthen the need to maximize efforts to continue CDK4/6i treatment as long as there is clinical benefit. This strategy can be successfully implemented while ensuring patient safety.

Our results are in line with recent literature data7-11 and further support the feasibility of switching between CDK4/6i and the importance of carefully selecting the alternative drug on a case-by-case basis, tailoring the choice according to the specific toxicity, minimizing the risk of reoccurrence and improving tolerability. Overall, our findings emphasize that, in most cases, CDK4/6i treatment continuation is both feasible and well-manageable, without raising major safety concerns. A tailored approach, driven by the nature of the limiting toxicity and the strategic selection of the CDK4/6i with the lowest likelihood of eliciting a similar toxicity, may allow treatment continuation while maintaining an acceptable safety profile.

The study has remarkable strengths, including practical real-world data for allaying oncologists' concerns about cross-toxicity between CDK4/6i. However, despite the encouraging results, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces inherent biases, including potential selection bias and immortal time bias. In addition, the lack of data on patients who discontinued CDK4/6i due to toxicity without undergoing a switch but continuing ET alone, limits the definition of the overall population potentially eligible for this strategy and precludes a direct comparison with alternative management approaches. Nevertheless, in the context of the well-established benefit of CDK4/6i in combination with ET, strategies aimed at maintaining treatment exposure when feasible may be clinically relevant. Finally, a larger sample size is required to provide further validity to our results and establish this approach in daily clinics.
