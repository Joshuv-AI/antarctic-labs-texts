Concurrent Von Hippel-Lindau disease and enhanced S-cone syndrome leading to a distinct retinal phenotype
Authors: Maram AlEnazi, Abdulrahman H. Badawi, Wael A. Alsakran, Moustafa S. Magliyah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVon Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease and Enhanced S-cone syndrome (ESCS) are rare inherited disorders that independently predispose to vision-threatening retinal pathology through distinct mechanisms: VHL through hypoxia-induced activation of several angiogenic factors, which lead to the development of retinal capillary hemangioblastoma (RCH), and ESCS by disrupting photoreceptor development through NR2E3 variants. We describe the coexistence of these two conditions in a pediatric patient, confirmed by multimodal imaging, electroretinography, and genetic testing.MethodsA retrospective case report.ResultsA 13-year-old girl presented with poor vision in both eyes, mainly in dim light. Fundus examination revealed multiple small hypopigmented areas distributed nasally and temporally along the inferior arcades in both eyes, and multiple small RCHs distributed in all quadrants with superonasal exudations and macular choroidal neovascular membrane (CNVM) in the left eye. Full-field electroretinography (ff-ERG) showed nonrecordable rod responses, broadened rod–cone and cone responses, and a markedly reduced 30-Hz flicker amplitude. While the right eye was successfully managed conservatively, targeted focal laser and intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy led to regression of neovascular lesions and visual improvement in the left eye. Whole-exome sequencing identified a heterozygous VHL c.452T > C (p.Ile151Thr) variant and a homozygous NR2E3 c.932G > A (p.Arg311Gln) variant.ConclusionBoth VHL disease and ESCS manifested their characteristic retinal findings of RCH, along with the typical electrophysiological features and the CNVM associated with ESCS.

Introduction

Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease is a rare autosomal dominant cancer predisposition syndrome caused by pathogenic variants in the VHL gene. It is characterized by highly vascular tumors in multiple organs, including hemangioblastomas of the retina and central nervous system, renal cell carcinoma, pheochromocytoma, and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors.1 Retinal capillary hemangioblastomas (RCHs) are often the earliest manifestation and can lead to exudation, traction, and vision loss if untreated. At the molecular level, the VHL gene is a tumor suppressor gene that inhibits the accumulation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1a (HIF)1a) and suppresses RCH development. Pathogenic variants in the VHL gene lead to the accumulation of HIF-1a and activation of several angiogenic factors, including platelet-derived growth factor, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), erythropoietin, plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, and transforming growth factor a. These factors contribute to increased angiogenesis and RCH development.2

Enhanced S-cone syndrome (ESCS) is a distinct autosomal recessive retinal dystrophy caused by biallelic variants in NR2E3, a nuclear receptor gene critical for photoreceptor differentiation. In normal retinal development, NR2E3 suppresses excess S-cone production and promotes rod photoreceptor fate. Pathogenic variants disrupt this balance, leading to overrepresentation of S-cones, loss of rods, and structural instability of the retina.3 Clinically, ESCS presents with nyctalopia, high hyperopia, macular schisis, and characteristic electroretinographic findings, including absent rod responses, reduced cone responses, and short-wavelength hypersensitivity. Recent studies have also recognized retinochoroidal neovascularization, often manifesting as macular choroidal neovascular membranes (CNVM), as a vision-threatening complication of ESCS. The clinical spectrum of ESCS has expanded, revealing additional funduscopic features that aid in diagnosis, emphasizing the need for genetic screening of NR2E3 variants.4

Although both VHL and ESCS independently predispose to severe ocular pathology, the coexistence of these two rare genetic disorders has not previously been reported, as far as we are aware. The combination of angiogenic drive from VHL-associated vascular tumors and retinal instability from ESCS-related photoreceptor dysregulation creates a unique and challenging phenotype. Careful clinical evaluation, multimodal imaging, and genetic testing are therefore essential to establish the diagnosis and guide appropriate therapy in such complex cases.

We present the case of a 13-year-old girl with a heterozygous VHL variant and a homozygous NR2E3 variant who exhibited nyctalopia, hyperopia, macular schisis, RCH, and CNVM. This case highlights the importance of considering overlapping genetic etiologies in pediatric patients with atypical retinal presentations and illustrates the role of individualized multimodal therapy in preserving vision.

Case presentation

A 13-year-old girl presented to our tertiary ophthalmology center with complaints of poor vision in dim light and a gradually progressive visual decline since the age of six years. She was the child of consanguineous parents who were first cousins. Her family history was remarkable: her father and four paternal uncles, all of whom had died of systemic complications, were known to carry a diagnosis of VHL disease.

At presentation, her best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/30 in the right eye and 20/300 in the left eye. Cycloplegic refraction revealed high hyperopia of +7.50 diopters in the right eye and +8.25 diopters in the left. Anterior segment examination was unremarkable in both eyes.

Fundus examination revealed multiple small hypopigmented areas distributed nasally and temporally along the inferior arcades in both eyes. Additionally, multiple small RCHs were distributed in all quadrants with superonasal exudations and macular CNVM with few retinal hemorrhages in the left eye only (Fig. 1A and B). Fundus autofluorescence (FAF) of both eyes revealed multiple hyper-autofluorescent areas corresponding to hypopigmented lesions seen clinically, as well as a central macular hyper-autofluorescent area in the left eye. In addition, there were multiple hypo-autofluorescent areas at the sites of RCHs and the CNVM in the left eye (Fig. 1C and D). Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) showed no macular leakage in the right eye, while it showed fluorescein leakage at the site of CNVM with fluorescein blockage at the site of macular hemorrhages, as well as fluorescein leakage from the RCH lesions in the left eye (Fig. 1E and F). Spectral domain Optical Coherence Tomography (SD-OCT) showed macular schisis in the right eye and the CNVM with subretinal and intraretinal fluid in the left eye (Fig. 1G and H). Full-field electroretinography (ff-ERG) demonstrated globally reduced rod and cone responses in both eyes. The tracings showed nonrecordable rod responses, broadened rod–cone and cone responses, and a markedly reduced 30-Hz flicker amplitude, findings consistent with ESCS (Fig. 2). Whole-exome sequencing was performed at CENTOGENE lab in Germany, which is Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) certified, and identified a heterozygous VHL c.452T > C (p.Ile151Thr) variant and a homozygous NR2E3 c.932G > A (p.Arg311Gln) variant. The heterozygous c.452T > C (p.Ile151Thr) variant in VHL is classified as pathogenic for VHL disease based on the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria. The c.932G > A (p.Arg311Gln) variant in NR2E3 is classified as pathogenic in an autosomal recessive manner for ESCS based on the ACMG criteria. Discussion

This report describes a child with overlapping manifestations of VHL disease and ESCS, confirmed by genetic testing. As far as we are aware, the coexistence of these two rare disorders has not previously been documented, and the patient's presentation illustrates how distinct molecular pathways can converge to produce severe, vision-threatening retinal pathology at an unusually young age. A similar overlap of two unrelated hereditary disorders has been recently described, where retinitis pigmentosa coexisted with VHL disease, further illustrating the complexity of dual-gene presentations.5

RCH is the hallmark ocular manifestation of VHL, with prevalence estimates of up to 60-70% in affected patients. Histopathological studies have shown that the stromal cells within these tumors are the true neoplastic component; they harbor VHL inactivation and demonstrate upregulation of VEGF and other hypoxia-inducible genes, which drive the proliferation and leakage of surrounding vascular elements.6 This explains the exudation observed in our patient's left eye and provides a rationale for focal laser ablation, which eliminates the VEGF-producing cell population.6,7

In our case, adjunctive intravitreal bevacizumab further reduced exudation from the coexisting CNVM. For patients with persistent exudation and concomitant CNVM, intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy may be considered as an adjunct, though its effects are often transient and it does not address the underlying tumor.8 In ESCS, neovascularization is predominantly VEGF-mediated, supporting the use of anti-VEGF agents as a rational and effective strategy, whereas in VHL disease, HIF-driven dysregulation of multiple angiogenic pathways diminishes the relative efficacy of VEGF inhibition alone. Recent data further emphasize the complexity of VHL-related RCH and highlight the importance of individualized, multimodal treatment strategies to optimize both anatomical and visual outcomes.2

ESCS is caused by biallelic variants in NR2E3, a nuclear receptor regulating photoreceptor fate. NR2E3 normally suppresses S-cone lineage and promotes rod differentiation through interactions with neural retinal leucine zipper (NRL) and code-rod homeobox (CRX) transcription factors.9 The p. Arg311Gln variant disrupts these interactions, resulting in excess S-cones, rod paucity, and retinal instability.3 These mechanisms explain the patient's nyctalopia, high hyperopia, macular schisis, and the electroretinographic profile of absent rod responses with short-wavelength hypersensitivity.

The presence of homozygous p. Arg311Gln confers a severe ESCS phenotype, which is associated with early-onset retinal instability and a predisposition to secondary neovascular complications, such as CNVM and choroidal and subretinal fibrosis. The molecular defect in NR2E3 may further destabilize retinal architecture already compromised by VHL-related pathology, compounding the risk of vision-threatening complications at a young age.10

The p. Arg311Gln variant is classically associated with severe ESCS, characterized by early-onset progressive visual dysfunction, rapid disease progression, and poor rod function, particularly in pediatric patients.11,12 In contrast, our patient demonstrates a milder phenotype with preserved vision. One possible consideration is whether the coexistence of VHL disease may influence the retinal disease expression; however, current literature provides no evidence that systemic conditions such as VHL disease modify the retinal phenotype or natural history of ESCS. Identification of this variant through genetic testing remains clinically significant for confirming the diagnosis, providing prognostic information, and guiding management, including close monitoring for neovascular complications and timely use of anti-VEGF therapy when CNVM is present.12

The favorable response to bevacizumab in ESCS-associated CNVM is explained by the VEGF-driven pathogenesis of neovascularization in this context. Anti-VEGF agents such as bevacizumab effectively suppress neovascular leakage and exudation, leading to stabilization or improvement in retinal morphology and visual acuity. In ESCS, CNVMs often require less intensive anti-VEGF regimens than those in age-related macular degeneration, and visual prognosis is generally favorable if treated promptly.13 Thus, the molecular and structural consequences of the NR2E3 p. Arg311Gln variant create a permissive environment for VEGF-mediated neovascularization, and anti-VEGF therapy directly targets this mechanism, accounting for the observed clinical benefit.13,14

The coexistence of VHL disease and ESCS in this patient is noteworthy because both diseases independently promote angiogenesis through distinct mechanisms: HIF-1a accumulation, angiogenic factors, VEGF upregulation in VHL disease, and structural retinal instability with secondary neovascularization in ESCS.6,14 It is plausible that these processes acted synergistically, accelerating the onset of macular CNVM in this child at only 13 years of age. To date, ESCS has not been linked to systemic tumor-predisposition syndromes. Its allelic disorders, including Goldmann-Favré vitreoretinal dystrophy and clumped pigmentary retinal degeneration, remain confined to the eye.4,15,16 Thus, the simultaneous inheritance of VHL and NR2E3 variants in this case reflects independent genetic events—consanguinity facilitating homozygous NR2E3 disease,17 alongside paternal transmission of a heterozygous VHL variant—rather than a known syndromic association.

This case reinforces several clinical lessons. Multimodal imaging was essential to delineate the relative contributions of vascular tumors, neovascular membrane, and schisis. Genetic testing clarified the dual etiology, avoiding oversimplification under a single diagnosis. Management required an individualized approach: focal laser and anti-VEGF therapy in the eye with active RCHs and CNVM, and careful observation in the fellow eye with stable macular schisis. This combined strategy maintained and achieved functional and anatomical improvements in the affected eye and preserved vision in the fellow eye for one year of follow-ups.

Conclusion

This case expands the spectrum of ocular findings that may be encountered when distinct genetic disorders coexist. It highlights the importance of considering overlapping etiologies in pediatric patients with complex retinal phenotypes and illustrates how conventional therapies can be tailored successfully even in genetically heterogeneous diseases.
