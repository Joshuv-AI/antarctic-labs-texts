Pricing Combination Therapies: A Systematic Review of Value Attribution, Cost-Sharing Mechanisms and Policy Frameworks
Authors: Marta Trapero-Bertran, Carlos Martin-Saborido, Jorge Mestre-Ferrandiz, Nuria Amarilla, Lluís Segú
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCombination therapies are increasingly central to modern pharmacotherapy, particularly in oncology and other high-burden diseases. However, pharmaceutical pricing and reimbursement systems remain largely designed for single-product-single-indication interventions. When multiple patented medicines are used together, especially when owned by different manufacturers, conventional pricing frameworks may struggle to align prices with the value of the combination while preserving incentives for innovation and timely patient access.ObjectiveTo identify, describe, and critically assess the methods, models, and policy frameworks proposed in the literature to establish prices for combination therapies, with particular attention to value attribution mechanisms, cost-sharing arrangements between manufacturers, and budget impact considerations.MethodsA systematic literature review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and a pre-registered Open Science Framework protocol. Searches were performed in MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, EconLit, CRD databases, and grey literature sources for publications up to July 2025. Eligible studies analysed pricing approaches, economic models, reimbursement mechanisms, or policy frameworks relevant to combination therapies, including more recent multi-indication pricing literature. Given the heterogeneity of the literature, findings were synthesized using a structured narrative and thematic approach.ResultsSixty-nine studies met the inclusion criteria. The literature was dominated by conceptual and policy analyses, with relatively few empirical or implementation-oriented studies. Value attribution emerged as the central methodological challenge in pricing combination therapies. Several complementary approaches were proposed to operationalise value attribution, including adaptations of indication- or pathway-based pricing, manufacturer cost-sharing arrangements, managed entry agreements, and outcome-based reimbursement mechanisms. Empirical evidence suggests that health systems continue to rely primarily on pragmatic and often partial solutions rather than fully specified pricing frameworks. A complementary review of the multi-indication pricing literature indicates that, although the two fields address different pricing problems, they share important methodological and institutional lessons that can inform the development of pricing frameworks for combination therapies.ConclusionsThe literature provides a growing repertoire of conceptual approaches for pricing combination therapies but limited empirical evidence on implementation. Pricing frameworks should place value attribution at their core while combining complementary policy mechanisms adapted to national pricing and reimbursement systems. Lessons from multi-indication pricing provide a valuable foundation but require additional governance mechanisms to address value attribution, multi-manufacturer negotiation, and implementation challenges specific to combination therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40258-026-01065-z.

Key Points for Decisions Makers

This study provides one of the first comprehensive syntheses of pricing frameworks for combination therapies, linking them to the more developed literature on multi-indication pricing.It identifies a clear gap between sophisticated conceptual models and their limited real-world implementation, showing that current systems rely on fragmented and pragmatic solutions.The findings offer a structured policy roadmap and highlight the need for new institutional and negotiation mechanisms to align prices with value while ensuring sustainable access and innovation.

Introduction

Combination therapies are reshaping the landscape of pharmaceutical innovation and clinical practice. In several therapeutic areas, most notably oncology, infectious diseases, and immunology, treatment strategies increasingly rely on the concurrent use of multiple medicines targeting complementary biological pathways. By addressing disease mechanisms simultaneously, combination regimens often achieve better outcomes than monotherapies, including improved survival, delayed disease progression and reduced emergence of resistance. Landmark examples such as combination antiretroviral therapy for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and artemisinin-based combination therapies for malaria illustrate the transformative potential of multi-component treatment strategies, while also highlighting persistent challenges related to affordability and equitable access [1, 2].

Despite their clinical relevance, combination therapies pose fundamental challenges for pharmaceutical pricing and reimbursement systems, as well as for health technology assessment (HTA) frameworks. Existing pricing and HTA frameworks were developed primarily for single medicines assessed independently (and per indication). By contrast, combination therapies involve multiple products whose therapeutic values arise from their joint use, and sometimes, these products are used for multiple indications in isolation or with other medicines. Conventional pricing approaches may therefore struggle to ensure that prices reflect the value of the combination while preserving appropriate incentives for innovation and market entry [3, 4]. These challenges are particularly acute when the medicines included in a combination are developed by different manufacturers, raising complex questions regarding value attribution, revenue allocation, and negotiation structures.

Evidence from European policy discussions suggests that these challenges are already affecting patient access. Recent analyses indicate that access to on-patent oncology combinations may lag behind access to oncology medicines more broadly, partly because pricing and reimbursement systems are not well configured for multi-component regimens [5]. As the pipeline of combination therapies continues to expand, particularly in oncology and precision medicine, the need for workable pricing approaches is becoming increasingly urgent.

A central methodological challenge concerns the attribution of value across the individual components of a combination therapy. Because the clinical benefit is jointly produced, allocating incremental value (usually measured as ‘health outcomes’) to each component is inherently complex and sensitive to assumptions regarding therapeutic synergy, sequencing of treatment lines, and the availability of robust clinical evidence. Several conceptual frameworks have been proposed to address this issue, including symmetric attribution models, proportional allocation approaches, and outcome-based attribution mechanisms [3, 4, 6]. Moreover, countries use different types of evaluation models, although broadly speaking, models are either based on ‘efficiency’ (i.e., cost-effectiveness or cost-utility) or on added clinical value (and where the price depends partly on this added clinical value). This is important to consider as the proposed conceptual models for combination therapies can be country-dependent. Still, stakeholders increasingly emphasise that value attribution alone is unlikely to resolve access barriers unless accompanied by pricing and negotiation mechanisms capable of accommodating multiple manufacturers [5].

Uncertainty in the clinical evaluation of combination therapies further complicates value assessment. While the overall effectiveness of a combination regimen may be well established, the available evidence often does not permit a robust estimation of the marginal contribution of each individual component, which may depend substantially on the study design and the availability of evidence on individual treatment effects [7]. This creates uncertainty for value attribution and complicates pricing and reimbursement decisions, particularly when prices are expected to reflect the relative contribution of each medicine to the overall therapeutic benefit.

Affordability considerations add a further layer of complexity. Combination therapies can increase treatment costs substantially, particularly when several patented medicines are used concurrently. Budget impact analyses have long highlighted that the adoption of high-cost combination therapies must be considered within the constraints of fixed healthcare budgets and competing resource priorities [8]. More recent policy discussions confirm that affordability pressures remain a major barrier to timely patient access, especially for free-dose oncology combinations involving multiple on-patent agents [5, 9].

Importantly, the policy challenges associated with combination therapies resemble those previously documented for multi-indication medicines, in which a single product generates heterogeneous value across different clinical uses. Previous work, including the systematic review by Campillo et al. [10], showed how conventional single-price frameworks may struggle to accommodate such heterogeneity. Since then, the literature on multi-indication pricing has expanded considerably, including empirical analyses of cross-country variation in pricing and reimbursement practices [11]. However, the implications of these developments for the pricing of combination therapies remain insufficiently synthesised.

Combination therapies also introduce additional layers of complexity beyond those observed in multi-indication pricing, including the need for coordination between multiple manufacturers, potential competition law constraints, and the absence of established multi-party negotiation pathways. Without policy adaptation, these challenges may weaken incentives for investment in combination development and ultimately constrain future therapeutic innovation.

Against this background, this systematic review aims to identify, describe, and critically assess the methods, models, and policy frameworks proposed to establish prices for combination therapies, with particular attention to value attribution mechanisms, cost-sharing arrangements between manufacturers, and budget impact considerations. In doing so, the review seeks not only to synthesise a fragmented literature but also to clarify the extent to which existing pricing approaches can be adapted to combination therapies and where new policy solutions are required.

Methods

Study Design and Reporting Framework

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [12] guidelines and followed a pre-registered protocol in the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/8byr3). The review builds on prior work on pricing of multi-indication medicines and extends the analysis to the pricing of combination therapies.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies of any design were eligible if they proposed, described, analysed or evaluated pricing methods, economic frameworks, or policy approaches relevant to combination therapies.

Eligible sources included: peer-reviewed journal articles, health technology assessment (HTA) reports, policy papers, case studies, methodological contributions and, expert commentaries. Conference abstracts without an available full-text publication were excluded due to insufficient methodological detail.

Studies were required to address at least one of the following analytical domains: (a) value attribution across components of combination therapies; (b) pricing or reimbursement mechanisms for multi-component regimens; (c) cost-sharing arrangements between manufacturers; and (d) budget impact or affordability considerations specific to combination treatments.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple bibliographic databases and grey literature sources. The primary search was conducted in MEDLINE via PubMed, including Daily Update, Epub Ahead of Print, In-Process and non-indexed citations (coverage from 1946 to present).

The MEDLINE strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to combination therapies and economic evaluation, using the following core structure: (combination) AND (treatment* OR therap* OR regim* OR medicine*) AND (valu* OR cost* OR pay* OR pric* OR access OR eval* OR priorit* OR reimb* OR anal* OR commerc* OR economic*)

Noise terms unrelated to the pharmaceutical context were excluded. No study-type filters or language limits were applied, and results were limited to publications available up to July 2025. The search strategy was subsequently adapted to the syntax of additional databases, including: Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, EconLit, the Centre for Reviews and Dissemination (CRD) databases (Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects [DARE], HTA, and National Health Service Economic Evaluation Database [NHS EED]) and, the ASCO proceedings database. To capture relevant policy documents and non-indexed literature, grey literature searches were conducted using Google and Google Scholar, applying equivalent keywords and date limits. In addition, a small number of potentially relevant publications were identified through expert recommendation from researchers active in the field of pharmaceutical pricing and reimbursement and were assessed for eligibility using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as records retrieved through the database searches. Duplicate records across databases were removed prior to screening. Formal backward and forward citation tracking (snowballing) was not undertaken because the search strategy was designed to be highly comprehensive, combining multiple bibliographic databases with extensive grey literature searches to maximise coverage of the available evidence.

Because of the conceptual overlap between multi-indication pricing and combination therapy pricing, an additional targeted update of the literature on pricing of multi-indication medicines was conducted. This update was built on a previous systematic review on pricing of multi-indication drugs published by Campillo et al (2020) [10], in which several of the authors of the present study were involved. The update covered publications from September 2018 to July 2025 and was performed in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and EconLit using indication-specific terminology. The purpose of this supplementary search was two-fold: first, to update the evidence on pricing approaches for multi-indication medicines since the publication of the earlier review; and second, to examine whether the economic and policy challenges identified for these products—particularly the presence of heterogeneous therapeutic value across uses and the difficulty of aligning prices with use-specific benefits—also arise in the context of combination therapies. This approach allowed us to explore the extent to which the analytical and policy frameworks, developed for multi-indication medicines, may inform pricing strategies for combination treatments.

Study Selection

Study selection was conducted in two stages: title and abstract screening followed by full-text review. Screening was performed independently by two reviewers (JS and MTB; CMS and JMF) working in pairs. Disagreements regarding study eligibility were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (MTB or CMS).

The study selection process followed PRISMA reporting standards, and the final selection of studies was summarised in a PRISMA flow diagram.

Data Extraction

A standardised data extraction template was developed a priori and piloted on a subset of included studies. Extracted information covered nine analytical domains: (1) bibliographic metadata; (2) clinical characteristics of the combination regimen (including backbone and add-on components); (3) clinical outcomes and value metrics (including QALYs and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios); (4) pricing and cost structures; (5) value attribution methods; (6) governance and regulatory context; (7) equity and accountability considerations; (8) methodological quality indicators; and, (9) general analytical notes.

Key technical concepts—including backbone therapy, add-on components, single-pill combinations (SPC), separate-pill regimens (SAP), and “not cost-effective at zero price” (NCZP)—were harmonised using a predefined glossary to ensure consistency across studies.

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (JS and MTB; CMS and JMF), with cross-checking to ensure accuracy and completeness. For empirical case-study articles in which detailed regimen-level information was available, structured data were compiled into a dedicated analytical database. These data were analysed descriptively using Microsoft Excel and, where appropriate, Stata software, to explore patterns in value attribution, pricing structures, and cost-effectiveness outcomes across combination therapies.

Data Synthesis

Given the substantial heterogeneity of study designs and the predominantly conceptual nature of the literature, a meta-analysis was not considered appropriate. Instead, findings were synthesised using a structured narrative and thematic approach. Studies were grouped into major analytical categories reflecting the main pricing frameworks identified in the literature, including: (a) value attribution models; (b) adaptations of indication-based pricing; (c) cost-sharing and multi-manufacturer arrangements; (d) budget impact and financial control mechanisms; and (e) governance and negotiation frameworks.

Particular attention was given to the practical feasibility of proposed approaches, their data requirements, and their implications for HTA decision making, payer affordability, and incentives for pharmaceutical innovation.

Quality Appraisal

Given the diversity of study designs included in the review—ranging from non-empirical studies and policy analyses to empirical studies—no single formal risk-of-bias tool was considered applicable across all studies.

Instead, methodological quality was assessed descriptively using predefined indicators, including the adequacy of clinical endpoints, transparency of modelling assumptions, handling of uncertainty, robustness of extrapolation methods, and reporting of responder heterogeneity.

Quality considerations were incorporated into the narrative synthesis and were used to contextualise the strength and limitations of the available evidence.

After full-text review, 17 records were excluded, primarily because they did not provide substantive information on combination therapies or pricing mechanisms. A total of 62 studies were ultimately included in the qualitative synthesis (see S1 for the complete list of references).

Characteristics of the Included Studies

The included literature covers a wide range of analytical approaches, reflecting the evolving nature of the debate on the pricing of combination therapies.

Most studies were conceptual or policy-oriented, proposing frameworks or discussing institutional challenges associated with pricing medicines used in combination. A smaller number of studies provided economic modelling, examining strategic interactions between manufacturers or welfare implications of alternative pricing mechanisms. Only a limited subset of studies described empirical case studies or real-world examples of pricing arrangements for combination therapies.

Value Attribution: Conceptual Approaches and Implementation Mechanisms

The literature consistently identifies value attribution as the central methodological challenge in the pricing of combination therapies. The fundamental question is how the incremental therapeutic value generated by a combination should be attributed across its individual components so that prices appropriately reflect each medicine's contribution to the overall clinical benefit.

To operationalise this objective, the literature proposes several complementary approaches. Rather than representing mutually exclusive pricing frameworks, these approaches address different dimensions of the pricing problem and may be combined within the same pricing and reimbursement strategy. First, value attribution models provide the methodological basis for estimating and allocating the incremental therapeutic value generated by a combination therapy across its individual components. These models attempt to determine how much of the observed health benefit should be attributed to each medicine within the regimen, thereby allowing the price of each component to reflect its contribution to the overall clinical outcomes.

Second, the literature explores adaptations of indication- or pathway-based pricing. Although originally developed for multi-indication medicines, these approaches provide useful methodological lessons for combination therapies by illustrating how prices may be adjusted according to treatment context or therapeutic pathway when therapeutic value varies across clinical settings.

Third, several studies propose manufacturer cost-sharing agreements, particularly for combination therapies involving medicines developed by different companies. These arrangements seek to distribute revenues across manufacturers in a manner that is consistent with the value attributed to each component of the combination.

Fourth, managed entry agreements and budget control mechanisms are frequently discussed as complementary implementation tools. Financial caps, conditional reimbursement arrangements, and risk-sharing agreements can help manage uncertainty, affordability, and expenditure while facilitating access to high-cost combination therapies.

Finally, outcome-based reimbursement schemes have been proposed to align payment with observed patient outcomes in routine clinical practice. By linking reimbursement to real-world effectiveness, these approaches may help reduce uncertainty and support the practical implementation of value attribution principles.

Implementation Challenges

Although the methodological sophistication of proposed pricing frameworks has increased in recent years, their real-world implementation remains limited. The literature consistently highlights several structural barriers that complicate the operationalisation of pricing models for combination therapies.

A first major challenge concerns the availability of appropriate clinical evidence. Value attribution models and outcome-based payment schemes rely on detailed clinical and economic evidence to determine the contribution of each medicine within a combination regimen. However, many combination therapies have never been evaluated against their individual components in monotherapy, making it difficult to isolate the incremental contribution of each medicine. As a result, the evidence required to support value attribution is often unavailable or difficult to generate within conventional clinical trial designs.

Second, legal and governance constraints may limit the feasibility of certain pricing arrangements, particularly when they involve coordination between multiple manufacturers or require deviations from existing pharmaceutical pricing regulations.

Third, the literature emphasises coordination challenges between manufacturers, especially in situations where combination therapies involve products developed by different companies. Negotiating value attribution and revenue sharing in such cases can be complex and may raise competition antitrust concerns.

Finally, several studies highlight the difficulty of integrating new pricing models within existing HTA and reimbursement frameworks, which are typically designed for single-product (single-indication) evaluations rather than multi-component treatment regimens.

The updated literature confirms that multi-indication pricing and combination pricing share an important common challenge: aligning pharmaceutical prices with heterogeneous therapeutic value. However, the source of this heterogeneity differs between the two contexts. In multi-indication pricing, heterogeneity arises because the same medicine delivers different value across indications. In combination therapies, heterogeneity arises because the overall therapeutic benefit is jointly generated by multiple medicines, requiring that value be attributed across individual components before prices can be determined.

Despite these differences, several lessons from the multi-indication literature appear directly relevant to combination therapies. First, recent empirical evidence confirms that a single-price approach may generate inefficient launch sequencing, weak alignment between price and clinical benefit, and affordability pressures as indications expand. Second, the literature shows that health systems rarely implement pure indication-specific pricing. Instead, they rely on pragmatic mechanisms such as confidential discounts, managed entry agreements, weighted pricing approaches, and outcome-based reimbursement to better align prices with heterogeneous value while maintaining administrative feasibility. These experiences provide useful methodological and institutional lessons for combination pricing.

At the same time, the updated review also highlights the limits of transferring these approaches directly to combination therapies. Whereas multi-indication pricing primarily concerns allocating value across different uses of a single medicine, combination pricing introduces additional challenges that are largely absent from the multi-indication literature, including value attribution across multiple products, coordination between manufacturers, revenue-sharing arrangements, competition law considerations, and multi-party negotiation mechanisms. Consequently, the approaches developed for multi-indication medicines should be regarded as an important conceptual foundation rather than a complete solution for pricing combination therapies. Overall, the updated review supports the view that combination pricing should build on the methodological tools and institutional experience already developed for multi-indication medicines, while recognising that combination therapies require additional governance mechanisms to address their unique economic and institutional challenges.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesises the emerging literature on pricing frameworks for combination therapies and provides a structured overview of the conceptual models, policy proposals, and applied evidence currently available. To our knowledge, it is among the first systematic reviews to bring together the literature on combination therapy pricing while explicitly linking it to the more mature debate on multi-indication pricing. This linkage is particularly important because the multi-indication literature provides the strongest currently available evidence that a uniform price can generate inefficient launch patterns, weak alignment between price and clinical benefit, and increasing affordability pressures as indications expand. Combination therapies intensify each of these challenges while introducing additional issues related to bargaining power, complementarity, and value attribution across manufacturers. Our findings therefore suggest that combination-pricing policy should not be developed in isolation but should build on the methodological tools, data requirements, and institutional lessons already established in the multi-indication field, while recognising that combination therapies require additional governance mechanisms beyond those currently available for multi-indication pricing. Overall, the findings highlight both the increasing policy relevance of combination therapies and the substantial difficulties health systems face in aligning pharmaceutical prices with the therapeutic value generated by multi-component treatment regimens. A first key finding is the marked predominance of conceptual and methodological contributions relative to empirical evidence. Most studies proposed analytical frameworks or discussed policy approaches rather than documenting routine implementation. This suggests that the field remains at an intermediate stage of development: the problem is well recognised, several candidate solutions have been proposed, but few have been operationalised in a systematic and transparent manner. A central message of the review is therefore that no single access framework has yet emerged as a dominant or operationally validated solution for combination therapies. This likely reflects not only the early stage of development of the field, but also the context-specific nature of policy responses, as appropriate solutions may vary across countries depending on institutional, regulatory, and health system characteristics.

Second, the review underscores the central importance of value attribution. Because the therapeutic benefit of a combination arises from the interaction between multiple medicines, allocating incremental value across individual components represents a fundamental methodological challenge. The literature proposes a range of possible approaches, including proportional allocation of incremental value, symmetric attribution models, and outcome-based payment arrangements. However, even where these approaches appear conceptually sound, they typically require detailed clinical and economic evidence and may entail complex negotiations between manufacturers and payers.

Third, the findings reinforce the relevance of insights from the literature on multi-indication pricing. Both domains share a common underlying policy challenge: the misalignment between a uniform product price and heterogeneous therapeutic value across treatment contexts. In multi-indication pricing, heterogeneity arises because the same medicine delivers different value across indications. In combination pricing, heterogeneity arises because the value of one medicine depends on the presence of another (or others) and because the value of the overall regimen must be allocated across components. The multi-indication literature therefore provides an important conceptual and practical foundation, but it does not fully resolve the additional governance and bargaining problems introduced by multi-manufacturer combinations.

At the same time, combination therapies raise challenges that are not merely methodological but institutional. The need to allocate revenues across products developed by different manufacturers creates difficulties related to bargaining dynamics, competition law, confidentiality, and multi-party governance [13, 14]. In economic terms, this resembles a complementary-monopoly problem, in which decentralised pricing of jointly necessary inputs can lead to supra-optimal aggregate prices and underuse of socially valuable treatment combinations [13]. A game-theoretic perspective helps to clarify why these difficulties arise: when two or more firms supply complementary products that must be used together, each manufacturer may seek to maximise its own return without fully internalising the effect of its pricing decision on the affordability and uptake of the overall regimen [13]. As a result, even theoretically attractive frameworks may fail in practice unless supported by legal, contractual, and administrative mechanisms capable of enabling implementation [3, 4, 13, 14]. In this context, some health systems have begun to explore structured approaches to address these challenges; for example, the UK has proposed formal negotiation frameworks to facilitate agreements between manufacturers and payers while remaining compliant with competition law [15].

The review also highlights the growing importance of affordability and budget impact considerations. As the number of high-cost combination therapies increases, particularly in oncology, health systems are becoming increasingly concerned about the cumulative budgetary implications of multi-drug regimens. This has fuelled interest in managed entry agreements, financial caps, and outcome-based reimbursement models that can help manage uncertainty and expenditure while preserving patient access. A further challenge here will also be to devise a system to appropriately share the relevant arrangements across different products and companies.

Taken together, the findings suggest that sustainable pricing frameworks for combination therapies will require institutional innovation as well as methodological refinement. Health systems may need to combine value-based pricing principles with new contractual arrangements, data infrastructures capable of monitoring outcomes and utilisation, and governance mechanisms that support coordinated negotiation across manufacturers.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the literature on pricing frameworks for combination therapies remains relatively limited and heterogeneous, which constrains the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the comparative effectiveness of specific policy approaches. Second, many of the included studies were conceptual or methodological rather than empirical, reflecting the early stage of development of this field and limiting the availability of real-world implementation evidence. Third, although the search strategy was designed to be comprehensive and included multiple databases and grey literature sources, some relevant policy discussions, confidential pricing arrangements, or unpublished negotiation practices may not be captured in the publicly available literature. Finally, given the diversity of study designs and source types, the review relied on a structured narrative synthesis rather than formal quantitative pooling.

Despite these limitations, the review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence and identifies the principal methodological, institutional, and policy challenges that will need to be addressed as combination therapies become more prevalent.

Policy Implications for Pricing Combination Therapies

The findings of this review suggest several policy considerations for health systems seeking to address the pricing challenges associated with combination therapies. Although the literature remains dominated by conceptual contributions and empirical evidence is still limited, a number of practical lessons emerge regarding how pricing frameworks for combination therapies might be operationalised in real-world health systems.

First, health systems need to strengthen their ability to measure and monitor value at the level of specific treatment uses. Combination therapies often generate heterogeneous clinical value across patient populations and treatment contexts. Robust systems for tracking treatment use, clinical outcomes, and economic value are therefore essential to support more differentiated pricing approaches and to ensure that reimbursement decisions remain aligned with therapeutic benefit.

Second, policymakers may need to rely on hybrid or blended pricing mechanisms, rather than attempting to implement fully indication-specific or theoretically ideal pricing models. In practice, instruments such as confidential discounts, managed entry agreements, outcome-based reimbursement arrangements, and multi-year negotiation frameworks may provide more feasible pathways for aligning prices with value while maintaining administrative feasibility and negotiation flexibility.

Third, when combination therapies involve medicines developed by multiple manufacturers, pricing frameworks must incorporate mechanisms to allocate value and revenues across products. This may require new forms of negotiation structures, governance arrangements, and coordination mechanisms between manufacturers and payers. Without such institutional mechanisms, even theoretically sound pricing models may prove difficult to implement in practice.

Taken together, the synthesis of the reviewed literature suggests that developing sustainable pricing frameworks for combination therapies will require a combination of methodological innovation and institutional adaptation. Policy Implications for Pricing and Reimbursement Systems

The policy approaches identified in this review have important implications for national pricing and reimbursement (P&R) systems, particularly in jurisdictions where regulatory and institutional frameworks were originally designed for single-product interventions. Spain provides an illustrative case for examining how these approaches might be accommodated within an existing European regulatory and governance context.

In Spain, P&R decisions are shaped by the Spanish Agency of Medicines and Medical Devices (AEMPS) and the Directorate-General for the Common Services Portfolio of the National Health System and Pharmacy, within the legal framework established by national pharmaceutical legislation. In practice, pricing decisions are informed by clinical benefit, therapeutic alternatives, budget impact, and broader reimbursement considerations. While the current framework does not include explicit provisions tailored to combination therapies (nor to multi-indication medicines), several of the mechanisms identified in this review could, in principle, be adapted within the existing system.

In particular, managed entry agreements, outcome-based payment models, and negotiated price adjustments are already used for selected high-cost medicines and could potentially be extended to combination regimens. However, a key challenge arises when combinations involve medicines developed by different manufacturers. In such cases, attributing value across components and coordinating pricing negotiations require additional institutional arrangements and governance mechanisms.

This challenge is not purely technical but reflects deeper regulatory and market-structure tensions. As highlighted in the European legal framework, combination therapies operate at the intersection of pharmaceutical regulation, competition law, and intellectual property regimes. While EU legislation provides a harmonised pathway for the scientific evaluation and authorisation of medicinal products (including fixed-dose combinations), it does not directly address how value should be allocated across separately authorised products used in combination. As a result, economic and regulatory “grouping” may diverge, particularly in concomitant regimens, where multiple independently priced products jointly generate therapeutic value.

From a competition perspective, this creates potential risks related to coordination between manufacturers, exclusion of alternative regimens, or strategic use of regulatory and intellectual property instruments. These risks are especially salient in settings where one component of a combination may act as a clinical or commercial “gatekeeper”, influencing access to the full therapeutic regimen. Consequently, pricing frameworks for combination therapies must not only ensure efficiency and affordability but also remain compatible with competition law principles and avoid reinforcing anti-competitive market structures.

Experiences in other jurisdictions suggest that more explicit regulatory mechanisms may be needed to address these challenges. In Germany, for example, recent reforms introduced a statutory discount mechanism for certain combination therapies involving innovative medicines. Under §130e of the Social Code Book V (SGB V), a mandatory 20% rebate applies to medicinal products containing new active ingredients when used in designated combinations, unless a substantial additional benefit is demonstrated. This provision, introduced through the GKV Financial Stabilisation Act, reflects a policy shift whereby the burden of proof is placed on manufacturers to justify higher prices in the context of combination use. The consolidated text and an overview of its legislative process can be consulted on the official portal Gesetze-im-Internet.de (managed by the Federal Ministry of Justice) [15].

The implementation of the German statutory discount mechanism has required detailed administrative arrangements, including the definition of data transmission and billing procedures to identify eligible combinations within the statutory health insurance system. Despite initial legal challenges, German courts have generally upheld the framework, prioritising the financial sustainability of the healthcare system, provided that the measures remain proportionate. This example illustrates how national legislation can create specific rules to address the pricing challenges associated with combination regimens, particularly in multi-manufacturer contexts.

Other European countries have adopted different, although generally less formalised, approaches to addressing the challenges associated with combination therapies. In Sweden, reimbursement decisions have incorporated managed entry agreements and coverage with evidence development to facilitate access to high-cost combination regimens under conditions of uncertainty. In England, confidential commercial agreements and patient access schemes have been used to support reimbursement where conventional pricing arrangements proved difficult, while recent guidance has also explored structured negotiation mechanisms between manufacturers for combination therapies. At the European level, the European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries and Associations (EFPIA) and several policy reports have similarly highlighted the need for greater institutional flexibility and coordinated pricing arrangements to facilitate access to innovative combination therapies [5, 9, 14, 20].

Taken together, these international experiences suggest that the Spanish system could evolve through either regulatory adaptation or the development of dedicated negotiation pathways for combination therapies. In practice, this might involve combining value attribution methodologies with managed entry agreements or outcome-based reimbursement arrangements, allowing the total value of a combination therapy to be distributed across its components while maintaining affordability and transparency.

More broadly, the increasing prevalence of combination therapies indicates that P&R systems will need to evolve beyond frameworks designed for single-product interventions. Developing clear institutional pathways for the evaluation and pricing of combination therapies—while ensuring alignment with regulatory, competition, and intellectual property principles—could help ensure that innovative treatments reach patients in a timely and sustainable manner, while preserving appropriate incentives for pharmaceutical innovation.

Conclusions

Combination therapies are becoming an increasingly important component of modern pharmacotherapy, but they pose substantial challenges for pharmaceutical pricing and reimbursement systems that were largely designed for single-product interventions. This systematic review identifies a growing body of literature proposing methods and policy approaches to address these challenges, including value attribution models, indication- or pathway-based pricing adaptations, cost-sharing arrangements between manufacturers, and outcome-based reimbursement mechanisms.

At the same time, the review shows that empirical evidence on implementation remains limited and that major methodological and institutional challenges persist. Lessons from the expanding literature on multi-indication pricing are highly informative, but they cannot be transferred directly to combination therapies without additional mechanisms to address value attribution, multi-manufacturer negotiation, and governance constraints.

Combination therapies are likely to become an increasingly important test case for the next generation of pharmaceutical pricing policy. The challenge is no longer simply whether value differs across uses, but how health systems can translate that heterogeneity into workable, transparent, and sustainable pricing arrangements. Addressing this challenge will be essential if combination therapies are to achieve their clinical promise without undermining affordability or innovation incentives.
