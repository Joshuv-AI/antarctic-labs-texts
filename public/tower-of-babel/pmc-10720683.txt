To push or to pull? A clinical audit on the efficacy and safety of the pull and push percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy techniques in oncological patients
Authors: Piotr Kucha, Michal Zorniak, Mateusz Szmit, Rafal Lipczynski, Paulina Wieszczy‐Szczepanik, Aleksandra Kapala, Urszula Wojciechowska, Joanna Didkowska, Maciej Rupinski, Tomasz Olesinski, Tomasz Maj, Jaroslaw Regula, Michal F Kaminski, Wladyslaw Januszewicz
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe peroral “pull” technique and the direct “push” procedure are the two main methods for percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) placement. Although pull‐PEG is generally recommended as the first‐line modality, many oncological patients require a push‐PEG approach to prevent tumor seeding or overcome tumor‐related obstruction.ObjectiveWe aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of both PEG procedures in cancer patients.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed all consecutive PEG procedures within a tertiary oncological center. Patients were followed up with the hospital databases and National Cancer Registry to assess the technical success rate for PEG placement, the rate of minor and major adverse events (AEs), and 30‐day mortality rates. We compared those outcomes between the two PEG techniques. Finally, risk factors for PEG‐related adverse events were analyzed using a multivariable Cox proportional‐hazard regression model adjusted for patients' sex, age, performance status (ECOG), Body Mass Index (BMI), diabetes, chemoradiotherapy (CRT) status (pre‐/current‐/post‐treatment), and type of PEG.ResultsWe included 1055 PEG procedures (58.7% push‐PEG/41.4% pull‐PEG) performed in 994 patients between 2014 and 2021 (mean age 62.0 [±10.7] yrs.; 70.2% males; indication: head‐and‐neck cancer 75.9%/other cancer 24.1%). The overall technical success for PEG placement was 96.5%. Although the “push” technique had a higher rate of all AEs (21.4% vs. 7.1%, Hazard Ratio [HR] = 2.9; 95% CI = 1.9–4.3, p < 0.001), most of these constituted minor AEs (71.9%), such as tube dislodgement. The methods had no significant difference regarding major AEs and 30‐day mortality rates. Previous CRT was associated with an increased risk of major AEs (hazard ratio = 2.7, 95% CI = 1.0–7.2, p = 0.042).ConclusionThe risk of major AEs was comparable between the push‐ and pull‐PEG techniques in cancer patients. Due to frequent tube dislodgement in push‐PEG, the pull technique may be more suitable for long‐term feeding. Previous CRT increases the risk of major AEs, favoring early (“prophylactic”) PEG placement when such treatment is expected.Keywords: adverse effects, enteral nutrition, gastrostomy, percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy, pull PEG, push PEG

INTRODUCTION

Weight loss and malnutrition in oncological patients at any treatment stage remain clinical concerns. 1 Two general routes of delivering nutritional support include enteral nutrition (EN) and parenteral nutrition (PN). Enteral feeding has several benefits over PN, including fewer infections, reduced costs, better preservation of gut mucosa and immune function, and reduced hospital stay; therefore, EN is recommended over PN whenever possible. 2

Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) remains the first‐line modality for long‐term (>4 weeks) enteral feeding in patients who cannot meet their nutritional requirements due to diminished oral intake. 3 , 4 , 5 This particularly applies to oncological patients with obstructive tumors, such as head‐and‐neck cancers (HNC) and upper GI cancers. 3 PEG is generally preferred over surgical gastrostomy due to a lower rate of adverse events (AEs), more favorable cost‐effectiveness, and shorter procedural time. 3 , 6 The two primary methods of PEG placement include the peroral “pull” technique and the direct percutaneous “push” procedure. 7

The “pull” method was established in the 1980s by Gauderer and, for several years, was the most widely practiced method for PEG placement. 8 The “push” technique was later introduced to overcome the main limitation of the pull‐PEG, which requires unobstructed passage through the mouth to the stomach. 9 Since its initial implementation, the “push” method has undergone many modifications, including a double‐needle gastropexy device and a trocar with a peel‐away sheath enabling insertion of the PEG tube directly into the stomach through the abdominal wall. Both PEG procedures are minimally invasive, with peri‐procedural AE rates as low as 0.5%. 3 However, post‐procedural AEs are considerable, with a reported incidence rate of 4.8%–26.2%. 10

Although the “pull” technique is generally considered the first‐line modality, the recent European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) guidelines recommend push‐PEG access in patients with obstructive tumors to prevent tumor seeding or overcome tumor‐related obstruction. 3 , 11 Considering a few studies directly comparing both methods among cancer patients, we analyzed the feasibility and outcomes of both PEG techniques within a high‐volume tertiary oncology center.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and patient selection

In this retrospective clinical audit, we analyzed the hospital's electronic medical records to identify adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent PEG between January 2014 and December 2021 within the Department of Oncological Gastroenterology of the National Research Institute of Oncology in Warsaw. Moreover, all patients were linked with the National Cancer Registry using their Personal Identification Numbers (PESEL) to supplement any additional clinical data and evaluate the procedural outcomes, including post‐procedural mortality.

The collected data included patients' age, sex, BMI, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance score (0–4), tumor location, CRT status (pre‐treatment, active treatment, and post‐treatment), presence of diabetes, and dysphagia score (range: 0–4). In addition, the procedural data included PEG type, the diameter of the feeding tube, operator specialty (surgeon or gastroenterologist), endoscope model, and anesthesia type (local, sedation, or general).

Outcomes

The primary outcomes included the technical success of PEG placement and the AE rate for each PEG technique. The PEG placement was considered successful if performed within a single session and if feeding tube patency was proved during the first saline flush after the procedure (usually performed a few hours after procedure). The AE types were categorized as major or minor, and their severity has been classified according to the AGREE classification. 12 Major AE included bleeding (any symptomatic blood loss requiring endoscopic or surgical intervention and/or blood transfusion), infection (symptomatic infection at the PEG site requiring additional/prolonged antibiotic treatment and/or surgical management), perforation, peritonitis, buried bumper syndrome, and procedure‐related death (within 24‐h of the procedure). Minor AEs included peristomal leakage, tube dislodgement, and other AEs manageable within an outpatient setting (e.g., PEG‐tube repositioning).

Secondary outcomes included the 30‐day mortality rates and rate of AEs requiring surgical intervention. Mortality rates were obtained by analyzing the follow‐up data for any death record within 30 days after the procedure within the hospital records and the National Cancer Registry.

PEG procedures

All patients underwent the PEG procedure either with the “pull” or “push” techniques. They were performed under local anesthesia, intravenous sedation (Midazolam and Fentanyl), or general anesthesia (Propofol) with anaesthesiologic assistance. The sedation choice was made individually according to the patient's general condition and tolerance. The selection of the “push” or “pull” technique and the type of endoscope (ultra‐slim or standard) was based on the patient's anatomical conditions (e.g., degree of esophageal stenosis) and operator preference. Antibiotic prophylaxis was recommended before each procedure ‐ a single dose of 1.2 g amoxicillin and clavulanic acid i.v. or 1 g ceftriaxone i.v. PEG insertion was typically performed in a sterile setting with thorough skin disinfection and the use of sterile surgical drapes and gloves.

The “pull” technique was routinely performed with the Nutricia Flocare set (Nutricia Medical Devices, Hoofddorp, the Netherlands). The procedure was initiated by making a small incision with a scalpel at the endoscope‐generated trans‐illumination site on the abdominal wall. A puncture needle was subsequently introduced through the incision into the stomach under direct endoscopic guidance. Next, a stiff thread was passed through the needle, grasped with endoscopic biopsy forceps, and removed through the mouth. Subsequently, the string was fixed to the distal end of the feeding tube, which was pulled back through the esophagus, stomach, and abdominal wall. The choice of the PEG tube diameter (10Fr, 14Fr, or 18Fr) was made according to the degree of any esophageal stenosis and the intended purpose of the PEG (e.g., a larger diameter was chosen for decompressive PEGs).

The “push” technique was routinely performed using a double‐needle gastropexy device (Freka Pexact, Fresenius Kabi, Bad Homburg, Germany). Gastropexy consisted of two or three sutures placed approximately 2 cm from each other at the site of trans‐illumination. Afterward, a small incision between the sutures was made with a scalpel to insert a trocar with a peel‐away sheath. Next, a gastrostomy tube was inserted through the sheath into the stomach, and the PEG balloon was filled with saline for fixation (typically 5 mL). In our unit, we were not planning a routine push‐PEG replacement as long as it was functioning correctly.

Patients routinely remained in the hospital overnight after PEG placement for observation. On the day of the procedure, the feeding tube was flushed with saline solution and mobilized (180‐degree rotation and back). Regular feeding was initiated the next day.

Statistics

Quantitative variables were described as means, medians with standard deviation (SD), and interquartile ranges (IQRs), where appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages of the cohort. Patient characteristics were compared using Pearson's Chi‐squared test and Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, whereas the rate of AE was compared using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's Exact Test, where appropriate.

In the analysis of AE risk, the patients were followed‐up (FU) from the date of PEG placement until PEG removal (both planned and inadvertent), AE occurrence, death, or 31 December 2021, whichever occurred first. In the overall survival analysis, FU time ended at death or 31 December 2021, whichever occurred first. The survival function was estimated using the Kaplan‐Meier function, and the groups were compared using a log‐rank test. Risk differences were calculated as the difference in the proportional event rates during FU.

A multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression model was used to identify risk factors for PEG‐related major AEs and included several clinically relevant factors: patients' sex, age, performance (ECOG) status, BMI, diabetes, CRT status (pre‐treatment/active/post‐treatment), and type of PEG (push/pull). No variable selection algorithm was applied. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R Statistics version 3.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

The research proposal was reviewed and accepted by the Ethics Committee at the authors' institution (NIO Ethics Committee opinion no. 79/2022). Written informed consent was obtained from the patients before each PEG procedure. This report was written following the STROBE statement for cohort studies. 13

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics

We analyzed 1055 PEG procedures performed in 994 patients, of whom 698 were males (70.2%). The patients' mean age was 62.0 (±10.7) years. The spectrum of indications for PEG placement included: head and neck cancer (HNC); n = 754 (75.9%), esophageal/gastric cardia cancer; n = 158 (15.9%), brain tumor; n = 34 (3.4%), and other cancers; n = 48 (4.8%). More specifically, nearly half of the tumors were located at the oropharynx (n = 483; 48.6%), followed by esophageal (n = 147; 14.8%), and pharyngeal (n = 117; 11.8%) tumor locations. The mean patients' BMI score was 22.6 (±7.2) kg/cm2, and the median ECOG score was 1.0 (IQR 1.0–2.0), corresponding to restricted strenuous physical activity. The median dysphagia score was 2.0 (IQR 2.0–3.0), and 180 patients had tracheostomy at the time of the procedure (18.1%). PEG was performed before initiating the CRT treatment in 445 patients (44.8%). In 485 patients (48.8%) and 59 patients (5.9%), it was placed during or after oncological treatment, respectively. A scheduled PEG removal after finishing oncological therapy was documented in 107 patients (10.8%). A summary of the patients' characteristics is presented in Table 1.

Procedural characteristics

The yearly rate of PEG placements has been systematically increasing within our unit: from 58 procedures in 2014 to 210 PEGs in 2021 (Figure 1). The overall technical success for PEG placement was 96.5%. There were 37 failed procedures, with failure reasons as follows: esophageal stenosis impermeable using an endoscope (n = 13; 35.1%), no visible trans‐illumination (n = 10; 27.0%), poor tolerance of the procedure (n = 5; 13.5%), retention of residues in the stomach (n = 3, 8.1%), altered anatomy of the upper GI tract (n = 2; 5.4%), and dyspnea (n = 2, 5.4%). Of the completed procedures (n = 1018), 420 constituted pull‐PEGs (41.3%) and 598 push‐PEGs (58.7%). Sixty‐one patients required >1 PEG procedure (presented in Supplementary Table S5) Most PEG procedures were performed by gastroenterologists (n = 818; 77.5%), whereas 237 PEGs were performed by surgeons (n = 237; 22.5%). The ultra‐slim scope (GIF‐N180, GIF‐H190 N, Olympus Medical System, Tokyo, Japan) was used in 220 PEG procedures (20.9%). Four patients required esophageal dilatation prior to PEG placement due to a tight stenosis not passable with a slim scope. Most PEGs were performed under local anesthesia (64.6%). Comparison of the “push” and “pull” methods

The ratio of “pull” versus “push” procedures have changed significantly during the study in favor of the push‐PEG approach. Namely, the “push” technique constituted a minority of PEG placement techniques in 2014 (14.3%), whereas, in 2021, most of the PEG procedures were performed via the “push” technique (79.1%) (Figure 1).

The median post‐procedural FU time was 25.5 (IQR 6.2–52.1) months, and for push‐ and pull‐PEG specifically, it amounted to 13.5 (IQR 4.1–38.7) months, and 44 (12.1–70.6) months, respectively.

In general, the push‐PEG technique was associated with over a 3‐fold higher rate of AEs as compared to pull‐PEG (21.4% vs. 7.4%, Hazard Ratio [HR] = 3.2, 95% CI 2.1–4.8, p < 0.001), which was also demonstrated by a longer AE‐free survival rate, as compared to push‐PEG (Figure 2a). This difference, however, may be attributed to the frequency of minor AEs. The “push” technique had over a 6‐fold higher rate of minor AEs, of which most constituted tube dislodgement (65.6%) – a relatively rare event in pull‐PEGs (10.0%). The median time from push‐PEG tube placement to its dislodgement was 86 (IQR 35.3–179.0) days. On the other hand, although sporadic, the “buried bumper syndrome” only occurred in the pull‐PEG group (n = 3; 0.7%), and no such AE was noted after push‐PEGs.

With regard to major AEs, there was no significant difference between the “push” and “pull” techniques (6.0% vs. 3.8%, HR = 1.2, 95% CI = 0.7–2.3, p = 0.530). Also, the 30‐day mortality rates did not differ between the two methods (1.5% vs. 1.9%, p = 0.810), as illustrated in Figure 2b. The median time from PEG placement to any AE was 51.1 (IQR 11.0–160.6) days and 73.0 (IQR 40.2–175.2) days in push‐ and pull‐PEGs, respectively. Risk factors for PEG‐related adverse effects

Using a multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression model, we found that patients who had the PEG procedure performed after CRT had nearly a 3‐fold higher risk of major AEs than patients with PEG placed before the initiation of treatment (HR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.03–7.20, p = 0.042). Interestingly, none of the other clinical criteria included in the model has been shown to affect the rate of major AEs. As expected, the male sex and higher ECOG class were associated with increased overall post‐procedural mortality. The model results are presented in Suppl. Tables S1–S4.

DISCUSSION

Our retrospective clinical audit within a high‐volume oncology center shows that both push‐ and pull‐PEG techniques are feasible in cancer patients, with an overall technical success rate of 96.5%. In addition, we compared the safety profile of the two methods and found that they showed no significant difference in the major AE rate, AE rate requiring surgical intervention, and 30‐day mortality.

Notwithstanding, due to frequent tube dislodgement in push‐PEG (and a generally higher rate of minor Aes), the “pull” technique seems a more feasible option for long‐term feeding. However, due to the potential risk of tumor seeding, the ESGE guidelines recommend push‐PEG access in patients with HNC and esophageal cancer. 3 , 11 As reported by Ellrichmann M. and colleagues, nearly a quarter of patients with oropharyngeal/esophageal cancers had malignant cells in brush cytology samples taken from the PEG tubing and the transcutaneous incision site. 14 The risk factors for tumor seeding included age, tumor type (squamous cell carcinoma), and initial tumor stage. 14 However, the significance of the positive cytology at the gastro‐cutaneous tract remains unknown, as none of the patients had macroscopically visible tumor masses at follow‐up. 14 In our follow‐up analysis, we did not report a single case of tumor seeding which would manifest as macroscopically visible tumor at the PEG site during follow‐up. Altogether, our data suggest that the “pull” technique should not be outright rejected in patients with HNC/esophageal cancer. This particularly applies to individuals with early disease, with a retained passage from the mouth to the stomach.

From a broader perspective, there is a growing utility of the PEG procedures. A recent nationwide registry‐based study showed that, on average,>8000 PEGs are performed annually in Poland, and a >2.5‐fold increase in these procedures was observed from 2010 to 2020. 14 Our findings mirror this general trend: we have witnessed a nearly 4‐fold increase in PEGs performed in our unit within 7 years (2014–2021). Moreover, we highlighted the changing trends in PEG placement techniques, showing the growing utility of the push‐PEGs, which constituted nearly 80% of PEGs in the last year of our study period (2021). Most likely, the “push” technique became an available option for many oncological patients in our institution who previously would have been only eligible for PEG placement via surgical route.

Only a few direct comparative studies between “push” and “pull” methods of PEG placement have been carried out in cancer patients. Most reports support the recommendation of the “pull” method due to higher rates of overall Aes, tube dislocations, and occlusions in the push‐PEG technique. 15 For example, a recent study from Brazil on HNC patients has shown that the overall, major, minor, immediate, and late complications, as well as 30‐day mortality rates, were similar between the methods. However, the push‐PEG group presented more often with minor bleeding and feeding tube dysfunctions. 16 In addition, a recent large retrospective analysis of >800 patients has shown that push‐PEG was more often associated with periprocedural bleeding (p = 0.002) and tube dislodgements (p < 0.001). On the other hand, the pull‐PEG was more commonly associated with buried bumpers (p < 0.001), infected placement sites (p = 0.019), and granulation tissue formation (p = 0.044). 17 Notably, in our cohort, the buried bumper syndrome was a sporadic event (3/420; 0.7%).

Our report supports pull‐PEG as a more durable option for long‐term feeding as we observed frequent tube dislodgement in the push‐PEG, occurring in 14% of the patients. This value is similar to previous reports, with rates of dislodgement reported up to 13%. 18 The median time from tube insertion to dislodgement was 86 days, which corresponds to a late dislodgement according to ESGE guidelines (>4 weeks). 3 , 11 A bedside balloon‐type replacement tube can be placed through the established tract in such cases. With this in mind, it is of the utmost importance that these patients remain under the supervision of the Nutrition Clinic for regular monitoring of the PEG tube position within the first months after its placement. A recent study from our unit has shown that an organized care program provided by the specialized nutrition team results in reduced rates of Aes, prevents down‐dosing of oncological treatment and reduces weight loss in patients with HNC. 19

Another key finding in our study was that PEGs performed in patients following oncological treatment (CRT) had an almost 3‐fold higher risk of major Aes than patients before such treatment (HR = 2.7, 95% CI = 1.0–7.2, p = 0.042). This finding supports the beneficial role of early (“prophylactic”) PEG rather than “reactive” PEG placement. A previous meta‐analysis has shown that prophylactic PEG placement in HNC patients resulted in less weight loss and higher quality of life at 6 months. 20 However, a more significant long‐term PEG dependence was also reported. 20 , 21

Additionally, a multivariable regression analysis revealed that higher ECOG class (ECOG 3–4 vs. 0–1 HR 3.26, 95% CI 2.54–4.19, p < 0.001) and male sex (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.03–1.46, p = 0.025), were associated with increased risk of post‐procedural death (Supplementary Table S3). We believe that the increased post‐procedural mortality is a reflection of the general survival disadvantage in males, and this has been documented for several malignancies, including head‐and‐neck cancer and esophageal cancer. 22 The possible explanations include a more advanced stage at diagnosis in males, higher co‐morbidity prevalence, and poorer health‐related lifestyle behaviors. 22

To date, this is the most extensive report on the safety and feasibility of both PEG procedures in oncological patients. We based our findings on robust clinical data, including follow‐up with national databases. As our unit is the reference center for cancer patients, we had access to each patient's complete oncological treatment reports. This enabled a thorough analysis of procedural outcomes at different stages of oncological treatment. Although the retrospective nature of the study may be seen as a limitation, in fact, it represents real‐life clinical practice. A full spectrum of operators performed the procedures with varying specialties (gastroenterologists or surgeons) and diverse experience in endoscopy.

However, several limitations of our study must be acknowledged. First, despite the large capacity of our cohort, our report remains a single‐center study with all the inherent biases regarding the standardization of procedures and patterns of decision‐making. Second, regarding clinical follow‐up, we grossly relied upon medical records within our unit. Therefore, some of the long‐term FU data may be missing. For example, the low rate of documented planned PEG removals (∼10%) is most likely related to missing FU data rather than the actual rate of removed PEGs. Third, regarding purely procedural‐related limitations, we did not routinely perform tube replacements in push‐PEGs. Of note, the ESGE guidelines recommend replacement of balloon‐type PEG tubes at 3–6 month intervals to prevent balloon failure. Lastly, we did not have data on the type and amount of feeding formulas given to patients and we did not monitor the changes in the nutritional status (including BMI) before and after PEG feeding tube insertion.

In conclusion, this retrospective clinical audit within a high‐volume oncology center showed that both push‐ and pull‐PEG methods are equally effective and safe in cancer patients. Because of its higher durability, we believe that the “pull” technique should not be top‐down rejected in patients with esophageal and HNC cancer and should still be considered as the first‐line option if the passage from the mouth to the stomach is retained. Lastly, patients undergoing PEG after CRT have an almost 3‐fold increased risk of developing major AEs. This finding favors the “prophylactic” PEG placement rather than the “reactive” placement in patients who will inevitably receive oncological therapy.
