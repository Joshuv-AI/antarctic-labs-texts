Ingested twin ball magnets in a Nepalese boy: swallowed but not forgotten!
Authors: Ashish Lal Shrestha, Saukat Ali, Satyam Rimal Upadhyay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Swallowed foreign body in children is a common and frequent emergency, with most of them passing through the gut without complications. Ingesting multiple magnets or a magnet along with another metallic object, however, poses a significant risk to the bowel due to potential complications such as obstruction, pressure necrosis, perforation, and fistulation.Case presentation:We report a 5-year-old boy presenting with an alleged history of swallowing twin ball magnets a month prior to presentation, remaining largely asymptomatic until imaging revealed their presence. Acutely, an invasive procedure was avoided by a successful endoscopic retrieval, while close, protracted follow-up showing no complications further reduced the morbidity.Clinical discussion:Swallowed twin magnets may go unnoticed in children, presenting later with varied complications. A high index of suspicion is imperative for diagnosis, while knowledge of the hazards associated with delay can guide the modality of management.Conclusion:Swallowed twin magnets, whenever accessible, can be successfully managed by endoscopic retrieval; however, the possibilities of perforation, bleeding, and bowel fistulation require vigilant observation.

Introduction

Children are naturally predisposed to oral exploration of their environment, resulting in the ingestion of objects that are frequently inedible. The usual age for such a presentation is thought to be between 6 months and 3 years, while older children are also not exempt from this naturally instinctive curiosity[1]. While different types of ingested foreign bodies pose varied risks to the intestine, magnets in particular are a growing concern due to their widespread availability as small, high-powered toys and household items. Owing to the strong attractive forces between them and their tendency to trap the bowel wall in between – causing pressure necrosis, perforation, and fistula formation – magnets stand out as a special threat to the children’s playroom[2–4]. To avoid these complications, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends prompt endoscopic or surgical removal of ingested magnets[5]. Case presentation

A 5-year-old boy was brought to the Pediatric Surgery Outpatients with an alleged history of accidental ingestion of twin ball magnets a month prior. Notably, throughout this period, he had remained entirely asymptomatic. Clinical assessment revealed normal vital signs and stable hemodynamic status, while abdominal examination was unremarkable. The plain abdominal radiograph showed the attached radio-opaque twin ball magnets in the upper abdomen, and a subsequent contrast-enhanced computed tomography scan of the abdomen identified the high-density foreign bodies in the upper abdomen without evidence of intestinal perforation or obstruction. The location of the objects was predicted to be in the pylorus or the duodenal bulb, as shown in Fig. 1. Considering the proximal location of the magnets and the likelihood of retrieval via endoscopic access, parents were counseled accordingly. At the same time, a backup plan for laparoscopic or open abdominal exploration was also explained in the event of its failure. During the gastroduodenoscopy, the proximal magnet was successfully retrieved from the fourth part of the duodenum using Roth’s net apparatus, as shown in Fig. 2; however, the distal magnet could not be seen. It was suspected to be located in the proximal jejunum and was expected to pass spontaneously in the stools. No obvious bleeding or fistulous opening was encountered perioperatively. Postoperatively, he was observed for hemodynamic monitoring with serial abdominal examinations for early detection of entero-enteric fistula or perforation, owing to the fact that the magnets had been neglected and retained for a prolonged period. Fasting with naso-gastric decompression was initiated, along with parenteral alimentation and administration of intra venous broad-spectrum antibiotics. After 48 hours, the Ryle’s tube was removed, and he was allowed oral fluids, which were gradually upgraded to a liquid diet and later to a normal diet. He continued to progress well in the postoperative period, which was largely uneventful.

A course of oral laxatives was prescribed to facilitate the expulsion of the distal magnet. Post-procedural abdominal radiograph showed gradual migration of the distal magnet, as shown in Fig. 3, with subsequent identification in stools on the 6th day after endoscopic removal. Discussion

Ingested foreign bodies in children are a common reason for presentation to the emergency department. While 80% of these cases are managed expectantly, awaiting spontaneous expulsion in stools, a small minority may develop complications and require operative intervention.

Of these, accidentally swallowed magnet has been noted to be a growing concern in the past few decades. With their increasing use, easy availability, and much stronger attractive power, neodymium magnets that are frequently used in toys seem to pose a specific and potential health hazard in children. Although not considered risky if swallowed alone, multiple magnets, when ingested, particularly at varied intervals, are associated with perforation, obstruction, volvulus, fistulation, and hemorrhage of the intervening bowel wall.

Therefore, not just the number but also the temporal profile of swallowing is crucial in planning an intervention. The European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy has categorized swallowed multiple magnets as “urgent,” recommending their removal within 24 hours in order to avoid complications that tend to increase in severity with time[6]. Similarly, NASPGHAN (North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition) consensus guidelines consider this condition as “High Risk” and recommend retrieval at the earliest[7].

When the location is endoscopically accessible, esophagogastroduodenoscopy, balloon enteroscopy, or even colonoscopy may be attempted, utilizing the advantages of minimal access and patient safety. The success of this approach nears 90% based on available expertise, the location of magnets, and the time lapse since ingestion.

Surgical removal, however, may be necessary in symptomatic children when the location is inaccessible for endoscopic retrieval or for magnets that are non-progressive on serial radiography. Failure of endoscopy may be another indication for operation in up to 50% of cases.

The general lack of awareness of the complications of swallowed asymptomatic magnets, coupled with an unreliable history, can delay presentation, putting these children at an increased risk of developing severe subsequent gastrointestinal events. The delayed presentation observed in the index case also underscores a critical challenge in pediatric emergency medicine. This issue can be further compounded by diagnostic inaccuracies in imaging, such as bowel perforation being accurately localized in ultrasound in only up to 71% of cases or even as low as 46% in erect abdominal radiographs.

Similarly, entero-enteric fistula, often without peritoneal free air or leakage, may go completely unnoticed until features of blind loop syndrome manifest much later. Herein, the small- to medium-sized duodenojejunal fistulas in a closed and tight state can possibly be managed conservatively. Until larger studies are available addressing the long-term outcomes and complications associated with magnet-related fistulas, the optimum approach to them remains controversial.

The possibility of a fistula in the index case was considered in view of a long-standing history. Consequently, close follow-up was maintained even after an uneventful postoperative recovery for 2 years. Failure to demonstrate any symptoms during this period led to the eventual dismissal of further follow-ups or investigations.

Conclusion

This report emphasizes the need for increased awareness among parents and healthcare providers about the dangers of magnet ingestion, even in asymptomatic children. Early diagnosis and prompt intervention are crucial to prevent severe complications. Successful endoscopic retrieval can avoid major surgical undertakings; however, the possibilities of perforation, bleeding, and bowel fistulation require vigilant observation.
