Introduction

Developmental motor competence is fundamental for children's participation in activities of daily living, academic performance, recreational activities, and organized sports. Developmental Coordination Disorder (DCD) is one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorders, affecting approximately 5–6% of school-aged children worldwide. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), DCD is diagnosed when (A) the acquisition and execution of coordinated motor skills are substantially below that expected for chronological age, (B) motor deficits significantly interfere with activities of daily living, academic achievement, vocational activities, leisure, or play, (C) symptom onset occurs during the early developmental period, and (D) the motor difficulties are not better explained by intellectual disability, visual impairment, or another neurological condition affecting movement (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 2013; Wilson et al., 2012).

Children with DCD frequently demonstrate impaired balance, poor coordination, delayed motor skill acquisition, reduced muscular fitness, and limitations in functional mobility. These impairments often result in decreased participation in physical activities and sports, reduced self-esteem, social isolation, anxiety, and an increased risk of sedentary behavior and secondary health complications throughout adolescence and adulthood (Smits-Engelsman et al., 2013). Early identification and evidence-based intervention are therefore essential to improve motor competence, participation, and long-term health outcomes.

Conventional rehabilitation for children with DCD is predominantly therapist-led and clinic-based. Although these approaches have demonstrated effectiveness, they often require prolonged therapist supervision, repeated hospital visits, and considerable financial and logistical resources, limiting their accessibility and long-term sustainability, particularly in rural and resource-constrained settings (Zwicker et al., 2013; Blank et al., 2019). In addition, therapist shortages, travel requirements, scheduling difficulties, and reduced adherence to long-term rehabilitation programmes may further compromise treatment effectiveness. Family-centered rehabilitation has therefore emerged as an alternative model that actively involves parents in intervention delivery. Parent-led home-based programmes can increase therapy intensity, promote practice within natural environments, enhance skill generalization, improve adherence, and reduce healthcare costs while empowering families to participate in their child's rehabilitation (Melnyk et al., 2004).

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have further expanded opportunities for objective movement assessment during rehabilitation. Markerless motion capture systems based on computer vision, such as Google MediaPipe Pose, enable automatic estimation of body landmarks from conventional smartphone videos without requiring wearable sensors or specialized laboratory equipment. These systems can quantify clinically relevant movement parameters including joint kinematics, movement symmetry, coordination, movement smoothness, movement speed, movement variability, movement accuracy, and task completion time, providing objective feedback to therapists and families while supporting remote monitoring of rehabilitation progress (Polatajko and Mandich, 2004; Prinsen et al., 2018). Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility and validity of AI-based pose estimation for quantitative movement analysis in rehabilitation settings, although evidence in pediatric neurorehabilitation remains relatively limited.

Current evidence suggests that parent-led rehabilitation programmes can improve motor performance, participation, and adherence, while AI-based movement analysis offers objective and scalable assessment of motor function. However, these approaches have largely been investigated independently. Few studies have integrated structured parent-delivered neuromotor training with AI-assisted movement analysis into a comprehensive rehabilitation programme for children with DCD. Furthermore, existing studies have often been limited by small sample sizes, lack of standardized parent training, absence of objective biomechanical outcome measures, limited long-term follow-up, or non-randomized study designs. Consequently, there remains insufficient high-quality evidence regarding the effectiveness of combining family-centered rehabilitation with AI-supported movement analysis to improve motor proficiency and sports participation in children with DCD.

To address these gaps, the present study proposes a structured Parent-Led Neuromotor Training Programme integrated with AI-based movement analysis using Google MediaPipe Pose within an assessor-blinded randomized controlled trial. This integrated approach aims to provide an accessible, family-centered, and technology-assisted rehabilitation model capable of enhancing motor proficiency, objectively monitoring movement quality, and promoting participation in sports and physical activities among children with DCD.

Trial design

This study will be conducted as an assessor-blinded, two-arm, parallel-group randomized superiority controlled trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The superiority design was selected because the intervention combines structured parent-led neuromotor rehabilitation with AI-based movement analysis, both of which are expected to provide additional clinical benefits beyond conventional physiotherapy. The study will be carried out in the Department of Pediatric Physiotherapy at Datta Meghe Institute of Higher Education & Research, Acharya Vinoba Bhave Rural Hospital. The target population will include children aged 7–14 years diagnosed with Developmental Coordination Disorder. Participants will be recruited using simple random sampling, and allocation will be performed through computer-generated random numbers with concealed allocation. The study will follow assessor blinding to minimize assessment bias. Children fulfilling the inclusion criteria, including diagnosis based on DSM-5 criteria and below age-expected motor performance on standardized assessments such as the Movement Assessment Battery for Children-2 (MABC-2), will be included. Children with neurological disorders, severe intellectual disability, major sensory impairments, or behavioral conditions affecting participation will be excluded. Primary outcome measures will include motor proficiency assessed using standardized tools such as the Bruininks-Oseretsky Test of Motor Proficiency (BOT-2) or MABC-2, along with sports participation measures evaluating the frequency and duration of engagement in physical activities. Secondary outcomes will include AI-based movement metrics such as symmetry index, movement variability, and coordination score, as well as parental adherence, engagement, satisfaction, balance, and agility outcomes. Following ethical approval and trial registration, eligible participants will undergo baseline assessment, after which they will be randomly allocated into intervention and control groups. The intervention group parents will receive structured neuromotor training delivered by physiotherapists focusing on motor skills and sports-related activities. The study module will be implemented in sequential stages including development of the module and validation using the Delphi method prior to the main intervention phase. Parents will be instructed to conduct the training program at home for 30–45 min per session, 5 days per week, for a duration of 12 weeks. Parents in the intervention group will also record videos of their children performing specific motor tasks at regular intervals. These videos will be analyzed using an AI-based movement analysis system employing pose estimation techniques to generate quantitative metrics related to movement quality, coordination, and symmetry. Based on these outputs, individualized feedback will be provided to parents to modify and optimize the training program. The control group will receive standard care, including routine advice regarding physical activity without structured intervention or AI-based monitoring. Outcome assessments will be conducted at baseline, mid-intervention (6 weeks), post-intervention (12 weeks), and follow-up (24 weeks) by blinded assessors. Data collected will include motor proficiency scores, sports participation levels, AI-derived movement metrics, and parental adherence. All data will be systematically recorded, verified, and analyzed using appropriate statistical methods. The effectiveness of the intervention will be determined by comparing changes in outcome.

AI-based motion capture acquisition protocol

The AI-assisted movement analysis will be performed using Google MediaPipe Pose, an open-source, markerless human pose estimation framework capable of estimating 33 anatomical landmarks from RGB video images. To ensure reproducibility and consistency across participants, all video recordings will follow a standardized acquisition protocol.

Parents will record videos using a smartphone (Android or iOS) equipped with a minimum 1080p (1,920 × 1,080 pixels) camera resolution at 30 frames per second (fps). Recordings will be performed using the rear camera whenever possible to maximize image quality. The camera will be positioned on a stable tripod approximately 2.5–3.0 m from the participant, with the optical axis perpendicular to the frontal plane of the child during frontal tasks and perpendicular to the sagittal plane during side-view assessments. The camera height will be adjusted to approximately the level of the child's pelvis to ensure full-body visualization throughout movement execution.

Signal processing and AI data processing

Raw landmark coordinates obtained from MediaPipe Pose will be exported into Python for further analysis. To reduce measurement noise, all coordinate trajectories will be processed using a fourth-order zero-lag Butterworth low-pass filter with a cut-off frequency of 6 Hz.

Short periods of missing landmark detection (< 5 consecutive frames) will be reconstructed using cubic spline interpolation. Recordings with missing data exceeding 5 consecutive frames or more than 5% of the total recording duration will be excluded from analysis and repeated.

Following preprocessing, two-dimensional joint kinematics and movement descriptors will be automatically calculated using customized algorithms developed in Python.

Operational framework of the intervention

The intervention will be delivered over 12 weeks, 5 days per week, for ~30–45 min per session. It will follow a standardized phased framework consisting of parent preparation, foundational neuromotor training, progressive task-oriented training, sport-specific skill development and consolidation. All intervention families will receive the same core programme. Individualization will be restricted to predefined progression or regression criteria based on the child's performance, safety, adherence and AI-derived movement feedback.

Parent competency assessment and certification

Before initiating the home-based intervention, each participating parent or caregiver allocated to the intervention group will complete a structured training programme delivered by a pediatric physiotherapist. Training will include explanation, therapist demonstration, guided practice, return demonstration, video-recording procedures, exercise progression, safety monitoring, documentation, and data-privacy requirements.

Parental competency will be evaluated using a 12-item Parent Competency Assessment Checklist developed specifically for the intervention. The checklist evaluates the parent's ability to prepare a safe environment, position the child, demonstrate the prescribed balance, coordination, task-oriented, and sports-related activities, provide appropriate instructions and feedback, follow exercise dosage and progression criteria, identify adverse responses, record standardized videos, document adherence, and comply with safety and privacy procedures.

Each item will be scored as 0, 1, or 2, where 0 indicates inability or incorrect performance, 1 indicates partial performance or performance requiring prompting, and 2 indicates correct and independent performance. The maximum score will be 24. Parents will be required to obtain a minimum score of 22 out of 24 and achieve the maximum score on all safety-critical items before being certified to conduct the intervention independently. Parents who do not achieve the threshold will receive additional training followed by reassessment. Home-based training will commence only after the competency criterion has been fulfilled.

Parent training and intervention fidelity

Prior to the initiation of the intervention, all parents allocated to the intervention group will participate in a standardized competency-based training programme conducted by experienced pediatric physiotherapists. The training programme is designed to ensure that every parent acquires the minimum level of knowledge and practical competency required to safely and consistently administer the home-based neuromotor training programme. The training will include education regarding Developmental Coordination Disorder (DCD), its clinical characteristics, functional implications, and the rationale for the intervention. Parents will receive detailed demonstrations of each intervention activity, followed by supervised hands-on practice sessions to ensure correct execution of the exercises. Additional training components will include instruction on safe handling techniques, appropriate exercise progression, standardized AI-based movement recording procedures, home safety precautions, and proper documentation of daily home practice.

Upon successful completion of the training programme, each parent will receive a standardized intervention package comprising a printed parent manual, an illustrated exercise guide, QR code-linked instructional videos demonstrating each exercise, an AI recording guide outlining camera positioning and recording procedures, and a structured logbook for documenting daily intervention activities. The competency of each parent will be verified before independent implementation of the intervention to ensure that all participants possess the required skills to deliver the programme safely and effectively.

To minimize variability and maintain consistency throughout the trial, all parents will receive identical intervention materials and standardized instructions. Every participant will be provided with the same parent manual, identical exercise dosage and frequency, standardized instructional videos, uniform progression criteria, and the same equipment list. Parents will be instructed not to modify, replace, or progress any exercise independently without prior approval from the supervising pediatric physiotherapist. Any modification to the intervention protocol will only be implemented following clinical review to ensure uniformity across all participants.

Intervention fidelity will be monitored throughout the study using multiple complementary strategies. Weekly teleconsultation sessions will be conducted to reinforce correct exercise techniques, address parental concerns, and encourage adherence to the intervention protocol. AI-generated movement recordings submitted by parents will be reviewed weekly to verify correct exercise performance and recording quality. Parents will also maintain weekly exercise logbooks documenting the completion, duration, and any challenges encountered during home practice. Standardized video recordings of intervention sessions will be submitted every week for therapist evaluation, while random therapist observations will be conducted periodically to verify protocol adherence. In addition, monthly fidelity audits will be performed using a predefined fidelity checklist to identify protocol deviations and provide corrective feedback whenever necessary.

A standardized fidelity checklist will be completed weekly by the supervising pediatric physiotherapist for every participant. The checklist will evaluate whether the exercises were performed correctly, the prescribed dosage was maintained, safety precautions were followed, AI-recorded videos met the required quality standards, parents adhered to the intervention protocol, children actively participated in the sessions, and whether any protocol deviations occurred. Stage II: validation of the module

The developed intervention module will undergo content validation using the Delphi method. A panel of experts including pediatric physiotherapists, neurodevelopmental specialists, sports rehabilitation experts, and AI/biomechanics professionals will review the module for relevance, clarity, feasibility, clinical applicability, and comprehensiveness. Suggestions and modifications provided by experts will be systematically incorporated to improve the intervention content and implementation process.

The content of the module will be developed based on evidence-based physiotherapy protocols, pediatric motor learning principles, task-oriented training approaches, activity-based rehabilitation models, and sports skill acquisition frameworks. Exercises will be selected and graded across domains including balance, postural control, coordination, agility, fine motor skills, dexterity, hand–eye coordination, and sports-related activities. Each intervention activity will include standardized instructions, dosage parameters, progression criteria, safety precautions, and therapist guidance to ensure consistency across participants.

Sensitisation sessions will also be conducted for parents and therapists to ensure uniform understanding and delivery of the intervention procedures. Parents will be trained regarding exercise execution, monitoring strategies, video recording procedures, and safety precautions for home implementation.

Stage III: implementation to the main study (pilot RCT)

Following validation and standardization, the finalized intervention module will be implemented in the main randomized controlled trial. Participants allocated to Group A will undergo the 12-week parent-led neuromotor training programme under physiotherapist supervision. The intervention will combine conventional physiotherapy management with structured home-based neuromotor and sports training supported by AI-based movement monitoring.

The finalized module will include structured session formats, defined progression levels, integrated AI-feedback systems, monitoring protocols, and adherence strategies to ensure reproducibility and scalability. Outcome measures including motor proficiency, sports participation, AI-derived movement metrics, balance, agility, and parental adherence will be assessed at baseline, mid-intervention, post-intervention, and follow-up stages by blinded assessors. The programme is expected to provide an accessible, family-centered, and technology-supported rehabilitation approach capable of improving functional performance and sports participation in children with developmental coordination disorder within home and community settings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31.0. The distribution of continuous variables will be examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test, histograms, and Q–Q plots. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics will be summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables will be reported as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, depending on their distribution, whereas categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and percentages.

The primary analysis will follow the intention-to-treat principle, whereby all randomized participants will be analyzed in the groups to which they were originally allocated. A supplementary per-protocol analysis will be conducted among participants who complete the intervention and meet the predefined adherence criteria.

The primary effectiveness analysis will examine the group × time interaction for the BOT-2 total motor composite score, with particular emphasis on the adjusted between-group difference in change from baseline to 12 weeks. Analyses of the MABC-2 and all remaining outcomes will be considered secondary.

Changes in outcomes measured at baseline, 6 weeks, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks will be analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. The models will include fixed effects for treatment group, time, and the group × time interaction, with a participant-specific random intercept to account for within-participant correlations arising from repeated measurements. Baseline outcome values and relevant prespecified covariates will be included where appropriate. The principal treatment effect will be determined from the group × time interaction, particularly the adjusted between-group difference at 12 weeks and 24 weeks.

Model assumptions, including normality and homoscedasticity of residuals, will be assessed. Appropriate transformations, robust estimation procedures, or generalized mixed-effects models will be used where assumptions are not satisfied. Adjusted mean differences will be reported with 95% confidence intervals, corresponding p-values, and standardized effect sizes, including Cohen's d, where appropriate.

Linear mixed-effects modeling will permit the inclusion of participants with incomplete repeated measurements under the missing-at-random assumption. The amount, pattern, and reasons for missing data will be reported. Where the proportion of missing data is substantial, multiple imputation will be performed as a sensitivity analysis. Additional sensitivity analyses will compare intention-to-treat, per-protocol, and complete-case findings.

The BOT-2 total motor composite score will be treated as the primary outcome. Secondary outcomes will include MABC-2, CAPE, balance, agility, parental outcomes, and AI-derived movement parameters. To reduce the risk of type I error arising from multiple secondary comparisons, p-values will be adjusted using an appropriate procedure, such as the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method. All statistical tests will be two-sided, and statistical significance for the primary outcome will be set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Developmental Coordination Disorder (DCD) is one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorders, affecting approximately 5–6% of school-aged children and substantially influencing motor coordination, balance, functional mobility, physical activity participation, and quality of life. Children with DCD frequently experience persistent motor impairments that limit participation in sports, recreational activities, and everyday tasks, often leading to reduced self-confidence, social isolation, and long-term sedentary behavior. Previous studies have demonstrated that task-oriented interventions, motor learning approaches, and parent-supported rehabilitation can improve motor performance and participation; however, access to prolonged therapist-supervised rehabilitation remains challenging because of workforce shortages, travel requirements, financial costs, and limited availability of specialized pediatric rehabilitation services, particularly in low-resource and rural settings (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 2013; Wilson et al., 2012; Smits-Engelsman et al., 2013; Zwicker et al., 2013; Blank et al., 2019). These limitations highlight the need for accessible, family-centered, and technology-assisted rehabilitation models that extend therapy into the child's natural environment.

The present protocol proposes an assessor-blinded randomized superiority controlled trial evaluating a Parent-Led Neuromotor Training Programme integrated with AI-based movement analysis. A superiority design was selected because the intervention incorporates additional therapeutic components beyond conventional physiotherapy, including structured parent-mediated rehabilitation, action observation, motor imagery, AI-assisted movement monitoring, and individualized feedback, all of which are hypothesized hypothesized to produce superior improvements in motor proficiency and sports participation compared with conventional physiotherapy alone.

Previous investigations have shown that parent-mediated and home-based rehabilitation programmes can improve therapy adherence, increase practice intensity, enhance family engagement, and facilitate motor-skill generalization in children with neurodevelopmental disorders. Likewise, recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of markerless motion-capture technologies and AI-based pose-estimation systems for objectively quantifying movement characteristics during rehabilitation. Nevertheless, these approaches have largely been investigated independently, and there remains limited evidence integrating structured parent-delivered neuromotor training with AI-supported movement analysis in children with DCD. Furthermore, previous studies have frequently been limited by relatively small sample sizes, absence of standardized parent training, limited objective biomechanical outcome measures, or lack of randomized controlled designs. Consequently, this study seeks to address an important gap by combining family-centered rehabilitation with objective AI-assisted movement analysis within a rigorously designed randomized controlled trial (Melnyk et al., 2004; Polatajko and Mandich, 2004; Prinsen et al., 2018; Hullumani and Qureshi, 2026; Qureshi et al., 2024).

One of the major strengths of the proposed intervention is the integration of Google MediaPipe Pose, a markerless motion-analysis framework capable of estimating body landmarks from conventional smartphone videos. This technology enables objective quantification of clinically relevant movement characteristics, including joint kinematics, movement symmetry, coordination, movement smoothness, movement accuracy, movement variability, movement speed, and task-completion time. These objective metrics may complement conventional clinical outcome measures by supporting individualized progression, remote monitoring, and data-driven clinical decision-making while reducing dependence on frequent hospital visits. Previous rehabilitation studies have demonstrated encouraging validity and reliability of markerless motion-analysis systems, although evidence specifically involving children with DCD remains limited. Accordingly, AI-derived metrics in the present study will supplement, rather than replace, standardized clinical assessment (Wagh et al., 2025; Latreche et al., 2023; Lam et al., 2023).

Although Google MediaPipe Pose offers a relatively inexpensive and scalable approach for markerless movement analysis, several technical limitations should be acknowledged. Accuracy may be influenced by lighting conditions, camera position, clothing, partial body occlusion, rapid multi-planar movements, background complexity, and smartphone camera quality. To minimize these challenges, the present protocol incorporates standardized video-acquisition procedures, predefined quality-control criteria, automated preprocessing, repeat-recording protocols, and periodic technical monitoring. These measures are expected to improve the reliability and reproducibility of AI-derived movement metrics while maintaining the feasibility of home-based rehabilitation.

Successful implementation of the intervention also depends on active parental participation. Completing approximately 60 home-based sessions of 30–45 min over 12 weeks may impose a considerable time commitment for families, particularly those balancing work, school, and caregiving responsibilities. Furthermore, effective participation requires access to a compatible smartphone, adequate internet connectivity for video transfer or tele-supervision, and sufficient digital literacy to record and upload videos correctly. To address these challenges, parents will receive standardized competency-based training, detailed intervention manuals, demonstration videos, tele-supervision, weekly video review, adherence monitoring, and ongoing technical support. Where internet connectivity is limited, offline recording with subsequent uploading during hospital visits or when connectivity becomes available may reduce implementation barriers. Although the AI platform itself is freely available, wider implementation will require consideration of clinician training, secure data storage, technical support, and overall cost-effectiveness before large-scale adoption.

Privacy and security of participant video recordings also represent important ethical considerations. All videos will be anonymized using coded study identifiers, securely stored on password-protected institutional servers with restricted access, and managed in accordance with institutional ethics and data-protection policies. Personally identifiable information will not be included in video file names, and recordings will not be shared outside the approved research team without appropriate consent.

Despite the strengths of the proposed protocol, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study will be conducted at a single tertiary-care teaching hospital in India, which may limit the generalisability of findings to other healthcare systems, socioeconomic settings, or cultural contexts. Differences in parental involvement, rehabilitation infrastructure, technology availability, and digital literacy may influence implementation in other regions. In addition, because parents and treating therapists cannot be blinded to group allocation, some degree of performance bias may be unavoidable despite assessor blinding and standardized intervention protocols. Variability in parental adherence, home environments, participant motivation, and technological factors may also influence intervention outcomes. Nevertheless, these challenges reflect real-world implementation conditions and therefore enhance the ecological validity of the study.

If demonstrated to be effective, this intervention has the potential to provide an accessible, scalable, and family-centered rehabilitation model that combines conventional physiotherapy with objective AI-assisted movement monitoring. Such an approach may improve motor proficiency, movement quality, and participation in sports while reducing barriers associated with frequent hospital-based rehabilitation. Future multicentre randomized trials should evaluate long-term effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, implementation fidelity, digital accessibility, and cross-cultural applicability before widespread integration into pediatric rehabilitation practice, school-based physical activity programmes, and community health services.
