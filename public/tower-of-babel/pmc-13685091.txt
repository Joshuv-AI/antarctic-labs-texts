Correlation of whole-body fat content and its distribution with lumbar fat content under different age, gender and BMI conditions: quantitative analysis of MRI based on deep learning
Authors: Junjie Fang, Jiazhen Zhu, Aie Liu, Chuanli Cheng, Chao Zou, Aizhu Sheng, Fang Gan, Bin Chen, Guangyu Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe correlation between abnormal distribution of systemic fat deposition and fat content in the vertebral body remains unclear, and AI segmentation offers novel approach for large-scale quantitative analysis.ObjectiveTo explore the effects of gender, age, and body mass index on the correlation between whole-body fat content/distribution and lumbar spine fat content using deep learning based MRI quantification technology.MethodsOne hundred thirteen volunteers (63 males/50 females; 47.7 ± 13.3 years) who underwent coronal whole-body and lumbar sagittal MRI examination were enrolled. Through automatic segmentation and quantification, total adipose tissue (TAT), body-fat percentage (BF%), liver fat fraction (LFF), subcutaneous fat fraction (SAT/TAT), internal fat fraction (IAT/TAT), abdominal subcutaneous/visceral fat ratio (ASVFR), erector spinae–multifidus fat fraction (ESFF), psoas major fat fraction (PMFF) and lumbar vertebral fat fraction (VFF) were obtained. Pearson correlation was then used for stratified analysis based on sex, age (≤ 45, 46–59, ≥ 60 years) and BMI (underweight/normal/over-weight/obese). All preset variables were entered into a Lasso model (10-fold cross-validation) to identify independent VFF determinants and to calculate R² and the root-mean-square error (RMSE).ResultsVFF correlated moderately with ESFF and PMFF (r_ESFF=0.53, r_PMFF=0.39), weakly with TAT and BF% (r_TAT=0.23, r_BF%=0.26), and rarely with LFF (r = 0.08). Fat distribution exhibits “site-specificity”, with a relatively significant correlation between ASVFR and VFF (r=-0.40), a weak positive correlation between IAT/TAT and VFF (r = 0.29), and a slightly negative association between SAT/TAT and VFF (r=-0.29). Correlations between VFF and relevant indicators exhibited stratified differences by gender, age, and BMI, with significant gender-specific variations, insignificant age-specific variations, and ESFF being the sole indicator prominently and positively correlated with VFF across all BMI strata. The multivariate model (R² = 0.63) identified ESFF, PMFF, and ASVFR as the core drivers of VFF, rather than whole-body fat or liver fat.ConclusionParavertebral muscle fat infiltration and visceral-fat predominance are associated with higher lumbar marrow fat accumulation, which tends to be more effective in the female, elderly and overweight groups. The constructed multi-variable model may provide a quantitative imaging framework for future studies investigating vertebral marrow fat and bone health.Keywords: Deep learning, MRI, Whole-body fat distribution, Lumbar vertebral fat fraction, Paravertebral muscle fat infiltration

Background

Fat, muscle, and bone are indispensable functional units of human body, playing a structural role in maintaining physical health. In recent years, the incidence of obesity and related metabolic diseases has continued to rise, and the comorbidity of obesity with sarcopenia and osteoporosis (OP) poses an increasingly significant public health concern [1, 2]. Studies have shown that abnormal fat deposition in paraspinal muscles and bone marrow may be a key pathological link in these diseases, but the correlation between the distribution and accumulation of systemic fat and paraspinal constitution components has not been fully elucidated [3]. Whether there are differences in these changes among age, gender, and different body mass index (BMI) populations is not well reported and deserves further exploration.

The commonly used BMI and waist to hip ratio (WHR) in clinical practice can only reflect the overall level of obesity, but cannot distinguish the distribution characteristics of fat accumulation (such as subcutaneous fat and visceral fat) or local fat content. Traditional imaging methods such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), although capable of assessing whole-body fat content, have limitations in accurately distinguishing fat distribution and evaluating muscle quality [4]. Comparably, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has high soft tissue resolution and does not involve radiation, making it an ideal tool for quantifying local fat infiltration. It provides a new way to explore the relationship between fat distribution and bone health [5, 6]. However, conventional manual segmentation method for MR images is time-consuming and highly dependent on the operator’s experience, which limits its clinical application in large-scale population studies.

Over recent years, the development of deep learning technology has brought a revolutionary breakthrough to medical image analysis. Convolutional neural networks have demonstrated excellent performance in the automatic segmentation of muscle and fat tissues, with a segmentation accuracy of over 90% compared to manual annotation, and can significantly improve the efficiency of data analysis [7, 8]. The 3D-Unet network model has achieved high consistency with manual annotation by radiologists in the automatic segmentation and quantitative measurement of body fat tissue [9]. Fully automated deep learning pipelines such as FatSegNet have also realized rapid and accurate segmentation of adipose tissue in abdominal Dixon MRI [10]. These studies have not only verified the feasibility of deep learning in MRI fat analysis but also provided new directions for its clinical application.

However, previous studies have mostly focused on the relationship between local adipose tissue infiltration or abdominal cross-sectional adipose tissue distribution and vertebral bone density [5, 10, 11], lacking a comprehensive analysis of the correlation between whole-body adipose tissue distribution and local fat accumulation, including subcutaneous fat, visceral fat, muscle fat, and bone marrow fat, and their correlation with vertebral bone mass. In addition, the differences in the impact of fat distribution patterns on lumbar vertebral fat deposition among populations with different genders, ages, and BMI have not been clarified [12, 13].

Therefore, this study aims to utilize deep learning-based MRI whole-body adipose tissue automatic segmentation and extraction technology to construct a multidimensional correlation model among whole-body adipose tissue distribution, paraspinal muscle fat content and lumbar spine fat content, as well as to investigate the effects of different gender, age, and BMI on the above correlations.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

A total of 132 volunteers who came to Ningbo NO.2 Hospital for physical examination were prospectively recruited and underwent coronal whole-body and sagittal lumbar MR examinations. The inclusion criteria were: (1) Adults over 18 years old, regardless of gender; (2) no active infection, malignant tumor, or acute disease; (3) no use of drugs affecting fat metabolism in the past 3 months. The exclusion criteria were: (1) severe lumbar fracture or tumor; (2) systemic diseases (such as hyperthyroidism and Cushing’s syndrome); (3) history of treatment with hormones or osteoporosis-related drugs; (4) poor-quality MRI images. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Hospital of Ningbo (Approval No.: YJ-NBEY-KY-2021-128-01), and all subjects signed the informed consent form.

Data acquisition

Whole-body MR examination

All MR examinations of the volunteers were acquired using a 1.5T magnetic resonance scanner (uMR586, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China). According to the volunteers’ height, 7–9 overlapping axial image sequences were collected to fully cover the area from neck to knees. A slab-based 3D radiofrequency-spoiled gradient-echo sequence with six echoes was adopted to collect MR data and obtain proton density fat fraction (PDFF) images. The k-space trajectory was Cartesian. Imaging parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) = 10.5 ms; echo times (TE) = 1.67 ms, 3.15 ms, 4.63 ms, 6.11 ms, 7.59 ms, 9.07 ms (6 echoes in total); flip angle = 3°; field of view (FOV) = 300 mm×400 mm; matrix = 126 × 224; slice thickness = 6 mm; and no gap. To mitigate T1-bias, a relatively thick slab was used, consisting of 20 partitions (slices) per bed position. During chest and upper abdominal imaging, breath‑holding was performed under verbal instruction without navigator gating [14]. Subjects received standardized breathing training before scanning. For 9 volunteers with poor breath-holding cooperation, the in-plane imaging matrix was reduced from 126 × 224 to 99 × 176 to minimize the breath-hold acquisition time. Other key imaging parameters were kept unchanged. All images were immediately checked for motion artifacts, with compromised series being repeated promptly. Only artifact‑free images were used for analysis. The total time for the whole-body scanning was less than 10 min, and each subject generated approximately 160 images.

Lumbar MR examination

The sagittal scanning range covered lumbar spine and the surrounding muscle tissue. The imaging parameters were: TR = 12.0 ms; TE = 2.1 ms, 3.1 ms, 4.1 ms, 5.1 ms, 6.1 ms, 7.0 ms; flip angle = 3°; FOV = 300 mm×400 mm; matrix = 112 × 182; slice thickness = 4 mm; and no gap. The scanning time was less than 5 min, and each subject generated approximately 24 images.

Fat-water separation algorithm

The six-echo gradient-echo images were processed using MATLAB software with a hierarchical iterative linear-fitting algorithm (HILA) developed by our group [15]. A 6-peak fat spectrum model was used, with iterative correction for T2* decay and eddy current phase errors. Fat-water separation was performed voxel‑wise without internal or external reference phantoms. A small flip angle (3°) was used to reduce T1 bias. Partial volume effects were accommodated by simultaneous estimation of water and fat fractions without binary thresholding. Automatic fat segmentation and extraction

Two independent deep learning segmentation models were employed in this study. First, a customised lumbar vertebra and paravertebral muscle segmentation model was developed by our group using an nnU-Net architecture (Fig. 1). This model automatically segmented the L1–L5 vertebral bodies, psoas major muscle (L1-L5 level), and erector spinae–multifidus muscle group (L1-L5 level). Manual segmentation for model training and validation was performed using ITK‑SNAP software on MRI PDFF maps. Segmentation was performed according to previously published [16–24], including the psoas major muscle and the posterior paraspinal muscle compartment consisting of the multifidus and erector spinae muscles. The combined evaluation of the multifidus and erector spinae was adopted based on their shared biomechanical function in spinal stabilization, anatomical continuity, and evidence demonstrating similar patterns of fatty degeneration associated with aging, lumbar degeneration, and chronic low back pain [11, 12, 25–30].

Segmentation was performed for the psoas major muscle and the combined erector spinae–multifidus muscle compartment [16–24]

A total of 107 consecutive cases (from 132 volunteers) were randomly divided into a training set (n = 51) and an independent test set (n = 56) by simple randomization. Baseline characteristics including age, sex, and BMI were well balanced between groups (all P > 0.05). Manual annotations were independently performed by two attending radiologists with 6 and 10 years of musculoskeletal MRI experience, respectively. All manual segmentations were fully blinded to automated segmentation results. Any discrepancies were resolved by a chief radiologist with 15 years of experience. The model development set (n = 51) was used for 5‑fold cross‑validation for network training and optimization. The independent test set (n = 56) was used to evaluate final automated segmentation performance, with results averaged from two blind manual segmentations to serve as a reliable reference standard. Second, a whole-body (from neck to knees) fat automatic segmentation model provided by our collaborative team was used (Fig. 2), which has been previously validated and published [14]. This model segmented subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), internal adipose tissue (IAT), abdominal fat, and liver tissue. Both models output volumetric data and fat fractions for subsequent quantitative analysis.

Fat quantification indicators

Based on the results of the automatic segmentation mentioned above, the quantitative indicators of fat in volunteers were calculated, including Total Adipose Tissue (TAT), Body Fat Percentage (BF%), Liver Fat Fraction (LFF), Subcutaneous Fat Ratio (Subcutaneous Adipose Tissue/Total Adipose Tissue, SAT/TAT), Internal Fat Ratio (Internal Adipose Tissue/Total Adipose Tissue, IAT/TAT), Abdominal Subcutaneous/Visceral Fat Ratio (ASVFR), Erector Spinae–Multifidus Fat Fraction (ESFF), Psoas Major Fat Fraction (PMFF), and Vertebral Fat Fraction (VFF). Statistical methods

Sample size was predefined by statistical software (PASS 2021, v21.0.5, NCSS Company, USA) based on the primary endpoint VFF content. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or as median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normal data. Categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages). Normality was verified via Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed by Levene’s test. Between-group comparisons of continuous baseline indicators (age, BMI) were conducted using independent two-sample t-tests (normal distribution with equal variance) or Mann–Whitney U tests (non-normal distribution/heterogeneous variance). Inter-group differences in the categorical variable (gender) were examined by Fisher’s exact test. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to explore the univariable associations between imaging-derived fat indicators and VFF. Correlation coefficients were reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. To account for multiple testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied within the family of correlation analyses. All candidate variables were standardized using Z-score normalization prior to model development. To identify potential predictors of VFF while accounting for multicollinearity, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model with 10-fold cross-validation was applied to determine the optimal regularization parameter (α). Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²) and root mean square error (RMSE). The predictive performance of the multivariable LASSO model was compared with that of univariable models. All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.8 (scikit-learn library), and figures were generated using Matplotlib 3.5.

Evaluation of segmentation performance

Inter-observer reliability of manual segmentation was assessed using Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Automated segmentation accuracy was evaluated using DSC, Intersection over Union (IoU), precision, and recall for vertebral bodies (L1–L5), psoas major, and erector spinae muscles. Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median as appropriate.

Inter‑observer reliability of manual segmentation for the lumbar structures was excellent (Table 2). In the 5‑fold cross‑validation training set, the segmentation model achieved high performance for vertebral bodies, psoas major, and erector spinae muscles (Table 3). In the independent test set (n = 56), DSC, IoU, precision, and recall were all consistently high across L1–L5, psoas major, and erector spinae structures (Table 4). These results confirmed the excellent accuracy, robustness, and anatomical consistency of the automated segmentation pipeline for subsequent fat and muscle quantification.

General clinical characteristics of the study subjects

Demographic features of subjects are presented in Table 5. According to the exclusion and inclusion criteria, a total of 113 adult subjects aged 18–75 years (47.7 ± 13.3) were ultimately included in this study, Among them, 63 were males (55.8%) and 50 were females (44.2%). According to age stratification, there were 46 subjects in the young group (≤ 45 years), 40 subjects in the middle-aged group (46–59 years), and 27 subjects in the elderly group (≥ 60 years). According to BMI stratification, the underweight group (< 18.5 kg/m²) had 7 cases (6.2%), the normal BMI group (18.5–23.9 kg/m²) had 34 cases (30.1%), the overweight group (24–27.9 kg/m²) had 49 cases (43.3%), and the obese group (≥ 28 kg/m²) had 23 cases (20.3%). The SAT/TAT ratio, ASVFR, and ESFF of females were significantly higher than those of males (P < 0.01), while the IAT/TAT ratio was significantly lower than that of males. TAT presented an approximately linear doubling trend, with values of 11.9 L, 19.8 L, 25.4 L, and 35.9 L in the underweight, normal BMI, overweight, and obese groups, respectively (P < 0.05). However, BF% tended to stabilize in the overweight group, indicating that the growth of BF% slowed down after BMI > 24, with absolute volume expansion becoming dominant. Additionally, aging was associated with “fat redistribution”, manifested as decreased TAT yet increased visceral/bone marrow/muscle fat infiltration, with VFF increasing by 15%, ESFF increasing by 71%, and PMFF increasing by 26%, while TAT decreasing by 22%.

Correlation between VFF and whole-body fat indicators

The correlation between VFF and whole-body composition parameters is detailed in Fig. 3. It has been revealed that VFF correlated with most adipose tissue indicators through Pearson correlation analysis (Table 6). Specifically, VFF showed moderate positive correlations with both ESFF and PMFF (r_ESFF = 0.53, r_PMFF = 0.39), suggesting that the overall fat level of paraspinal muscles was closely related to VFF (Fig. 4). A moderate negative correlation existed between VFF and ASVFR (r = -0.40), indicating that higher ratios of abdominal subcutaneous fat to visceral fat allude to lower VFF values. VFF showed positive correlation (r = 0.29) and negative correlation (r=-0.29) with IAT/TAT and SAT/TAT, respectively, indicating a complementary relationship between these two parameters. Meanwhile, VFF also showed positive correlations with both BF% and TAT (r_BF% = 0.26, r_TAT = 0.23), suggesting that BF% and TAT account for only a limited proportion of VFF variability. As for LFF, the Pearson correlation coefficient r was 0.08, meaning LFF was virtually uncorrelated with VFF.

The correlation between body composition parameters and VFF among different age groups

Pearson correlation coefficient showed that the correlation between paraspinal muscle fat fraction (ESFF and PMFF) and VFF tended to increase with age. The r_ESFF of the young, middle-aged, and elderly groups were 0.43, 0.63, and 0.67, respectively, while the r_PMFF of the young, middle-aged, and elderly groups were 0.32, 0.48, and 0.49, respectively. The two indicators reflecting SAT/TAT and ASVFR, are negatively correlated with VFF, however, the negative correlation of the latter is more pronounced, especially in the elderly group (r=-0.51) (Table 8). However, there was no significant difference in the correlation coefficients of various indicators among the three age groups (P > 0.05).

The correlation between body composition parameters and VFF among different BMI groups

Through Pearson correlation analysis, it was shown that ESFF is the only indicator significantly positively correlated with VFF among different BMI groups (r = 0.42–0.51, P < 0.05), suggesting that a consistent and significant relationship between paravertebral muscle fat and vertebral fat fraction. spans the entire weight spectrum. In normal weight and overweight groups, LFF, ESFF, and PMFF are significantly positively correlated with VFF, while in the obese group, only ESFF maintains a significant positive correlation with VFF. This indicates that in high BMI populations, fat infiltration in the erector spinae muscle can better reflect increased bone marrow fat content than visceral fat content (Table 9).

Lasso regression model for screening independent variables related to whole-body fat content that affect VFF

The optimal regularization parameter (α = 0.0567) was determined through 10-fold cross-validation. Using Lasso regression, six independent variables affecting VFF were selected from eight indicators related to whole-body fat content (TAT, SAT/TAT, IAT/TAT, BF%, LFF, ASVFR, ESFF, and PMFF) and four conventional indicators (age, gender, height, weight) (Table 10) (Fig. 5). Among them, ESFF had the largest standardized coefficient (0.424, P < 0.001), followed by Age (0.267, P < 0.001), PMFF(0.183, P < 0.001), TAT(0.151, P < 0.001), BF%(0.082, P < 0.01) and ASVFR(-0.076, P < 0.01). The coefficient of determination (R²) of the model was 0.63, and the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) was 4.02%, which significantly outperformed the single-variable model. The scatter plot of predicted values and actual values showed that most samples were concentrated in the vicinity of the diagonal (Fig. 6), indicating a moderate-to-good fitting effect of the model.

Discussion

This study adopted deep learning-driven MRI automatic segmentation technology to achieve accurate segmentation and quantitative analysis of adipose tissue in multiple parts of the whole body, including subcutaneous, intra-abdominal, liver, lumbar vertebral and paravertebral muscle adipose tissue. It systematically explored their correlation with VFF from three dimensions: whole-body fat content, distribution pattern, and local organ fat deposition. Unlike traditional local cross-sectional studies that only focus on the distribution of fat in a single region [10, 11, 31], this study comprehensively revealed the factors related to the distribution and content of whole-body fat influencing lumbar vertebral fat accumulation through multi-parameter integrated analysis. Previous studies have confirmed that the accuracy of deep learning in medical image segmentation can reach more than 90% of manual annotation, and it can substantially enhance the feasibility of large-scale population studies [32, 33]. In this study, we employed two validated deep learning segmentation models: a previously developed whole-body adipose tissue segmentation model and a newly developed lumbar vertebra and paravertebral muscle segmentation model. Comprehensive validation was performed for the newly developed lumbar spine model. Independent testing showed excellent segmentation accuracy for L1–L5 vertebral bodies, psoas major, and erector spinae–multifidus muscles, with DSC, IoU, precision, and recall all above 0.93 in the independent test set. These results confirm the high accuracy, robustness, and anatomical consistency of our deep learning pipeline for quantitative muscle, fat, and vertebral body analysis.

The study found weak positive correlations between TAT, BF%, and VFF (r_TAT = 0.23 and r_BF% = 0.26). These findings suggest that overall adiposity may have a limited association with vertebral marrow fat content, although the relatively small effect sizes indicate limited explanatory power. One possible explanation is that obesity-related metabolic and inflammatory alterations may influence the vertebral marrow microenvironment, as suggested in previous studies [34, 35]. However, inflammatory biomarkers were not assessed in the present study. Compared with the increase in TAT, the impact of local paraspinal muscle fat infiltration on VFF was more significant. ESFF and PMFF had the strongest correlation with VFF (r_ESFF = 0.53 and r_PMFF = 0.39, respectively), and their standardized coefficients in the Lasso regression model were also relatively high (0.424 for ESFF, 0.183 for PMFF, respectively), suggesting a close association between paravertebral muscle fat infiltration and lumbar vertebral fat accumulation. This result is consistent with the “muscle-bone fat linkage effect” found in previous studies—paravertebral muscles are key structures for maintaining lumbar stability, and the decrease in muscle strength caused by fat replacement can change the biomechanical environment of the spine, inducing compensatory fat deposition in the vertebral body [36]. These findings suggest that ESFF and PMFF may have potential value as imaging indicators associated with vertebral marrow fat content. On the other hand, the decrease in ESFF or PMFF might represent a potential indicator for assessing osteoporosis treatment efficacy. The correlation coefficient between LFF and VFF was merely 0.08, which is much lower than the correlation between paraspinal muscles fat deposition and VFF, indicating that the metabolic pathways of bone marrow fat and liver fat are relatively independent. This implies that liver fat content alone cannot be used as a substitute indicator for VFF, further bolstering the notion that ‘localised ectopic fat’ may be more closely associated with VFF than systemic lipid overflow [37]. It also alludes that relying solely on monitoring LFF to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of osteoporosis is of limited value.

The impact of fat deposition distribution pattern on VFF showed distinct “site specificity”. ASVFR showed a moderate negative correlation with VFF (r = -0.40), while IAT/TAT exhibited a moderate positive correlation with VFF (r = 0.29). These findings are consistent with the “visceral fat metabolic toxicity” theory—visceral fat has high metabolic activity, and the free fatty acids and adiponectin secreted can aggravate insulin resistance through the portal vein system, activate fat synthesis-related pathways (such as PPAR-γ), and promote the accumulation of lumbar bone marrow fat [38]. However, these molecular pathways were not directly evaluated in this study. The negative correlation of SAT/TAT (r = -0.29) suggests that subcutaneous fat may play a protective role by storing excess energy and buffering metabolic stress [39]. In clinical settings, ASVFR may serve as a sensitive indicator of ‘fat redistribution’. Its increase indicates a decrease in VFF and an improvement in bone formation, given the well-known inverse relationship between VFF and vertebral bone mass [40].Stratified analysis revealed population heterogeneity in lumbar vertebral fat deposition. In terms of gender differences, the correlations of ESFF and PMFF with VFF in females (r_ESFF = 0.69, r_PMFF = 0.62, respectively) were significantly stronger than those in males (r_ESFF = 0.34, r_PMFF = 0.20, respectively), which may be related to the accelerated fat infiltration of muscles caused by the decrease in estrogen levels in females after menopause [38]. The average age of females in this study was 47.1 ± 12.6 years, and 48% of them were in perimenopause or postmenopause. In terms of age, the correlations of ESFF and PMFF with VFF tended to increase with age, which is consistent with an age-dependent coupling between paraspinal muscle fat infiltration and vertebral bone marrow fat [41]. The BMI stratification results are relatively complex. In the overweight and obese population groups, the correlations between VFF and SAT/TAT, as well as between VFF and ASVFR, showed a weak negative trend (r_SAT/TAT = -0.15 and r_ASVFR =-0.13 for the overweight group; r_SAT/TAT = -0.12 and r_ASVFR = -0.10 for the obese group, respectively). These negative correlations are weaker than those in the underweight and normal-weight groups, suggesting that the protective effect of subcutaneous fat is diminished under obese conditions. This phenomenon may be related to the disruption of compensatory bone protection mechanisms caused by excessive fat deposition [42]. This study integrated six core variables by constructing a Lasso multi-variable model (R²=0.63): muscle fat infiltration (ESFF, PMFF), whole-body fat (BF%, TAT), age, and fat distribution (ASVFR). Its prediction efficiency was superior to the single-indicator model, preliminarily forming a multivariable analytical model for exploring factors associated with vertebral marrow fat content. The relatively high standardized coefficient (0.424) between ESFF and VFF in the model highlights the importance of improving muscle quality in lumbar health management, while the negative standardized coefficient (-0.076) between ASVFR and VFF suggests that reducing visceral fat accumulation may become a potential strategy in preventing vertebral bone marrow fat deposition.

However, this study has certain limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small. Under the conditions of α = 0.05 and effect size f²=0.15, the test power (1-β) is 0.82, which can meet the needs of main effect analysis, yet may limit the analysis power of subgroups with fewer cases. Secondly, in the gender stratification analysis, the specific impact of postmenopausal estrogen reduction on VFF was not further analyzed. Third, muscle fat infiltration was only quantified in the lumbar paravertebral muscles (erector spinae and psoas major), and peripheral skeletal muscles were not assessed. Thus, we cannot confirm whether the association between muscle fat and vertebral fat fraction reflects a local spinal biomechanical effect or systemic myosteatosis. Fourth, because of the cross-sectional design, causal relationships between body fat distribution and vertebral fat fraction cannot be established. Future studies can adopt a multi-center large-sample cohort design, combined with metabolomics technology to explore the possible molecular mechanisms of the influence of whole-body fat distribution and content on bone marrow fat, and carry out clinical trials such as exercise intervention and drug therapy to verify the clinical application value of the model as an early evaluation biomarker for clinical OP treatment efficacy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, deep learning-based automatic segmentation and fat extraction enable reliable quantification of the distribution and content of whole-body fat. This study reveals that vertebral fat deposition has a relatively weak correlation with whole-body fat accumulation and no overall significant correlation with hepatic fat, yet is strongly associated with paraspinal muscle fat infiltration and visceral fat predominance. The correlations between body fat distribution, content, and VFF exhibit stratified differences among different gender, age and BMI populations. The constructed multi-variable model may provide a quantitative imaging framework for future studies investigating vertebral marrow fat and bone health.
