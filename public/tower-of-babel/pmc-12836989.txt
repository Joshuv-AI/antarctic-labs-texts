Background

According to Global Cancer Statistics, there were 10.3 million cancer-related deaths and 19.3 million new cases of cancer globally in 2022. By 2050, the number of cancer cases is predicted to reach 35 million (2024) [1]. In 2022, roughly 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million cancer-related deaths were reported from the general population, often associated with comorbidities [2]. According to the WHO report 2025, an estimated 400,000 children and adolescents of 0–19 years old develop cancer every year. The most common types of childhood cancer include leukemia, brain tumors, lymphomas, and solid tumors such as neuroblastoma and Wilms tumor [3]. In high-income countries, where comprehensive services are generally accessible, more than 80% of children with cancer are cured. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), less than 30% are cured [4].

The goal of cancer therapy is to either completely eradicate the illness or significantly extend life while maintaining a high standard of living [5]. Nowadays, a sizable number of anti-cancer medications are authorized to treat cancer [6], and Chemotherapy is the main therapeutic option for managing cancer and was selected by patients [7, 8]. However, chemotherapeutic agents cause a significant amount of adverse drug reactions to patients [9].

Adverse drug reaction (ADR) is the harmful, unpleasant or unintended reactions to medicines that occur at doses normally used for treatment (WHO definition of ADR) [10, 11]. ADRs are common problems with chemotherapeutic agents among pediatric cancer patients [12] and are common during childhood neoplastic involving different organs [13–15]. The most common chemotherapy-related ADRs observed among pediatric cancer patients were vomiting, anemia, neuropathy, neutropenia, febrile neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and mucositis, which involve organ systems including the blood and gastrointestinal [16, 17].

Children who are exposed to chemotherapy may experience symptoms that significantly impair their quality of life [12, 18]. Additionally, patients, payers, caregivers, and society as a whole bear a heavy financial cost as a result of chemotherapy-related adverse drug reactions [19], and even require hospital admission [20]. Global data are available, but evidence specific to Africa is limited. Several systematic reviews have looked at chemotherapy-related toxicities in children with cancer around the world, but most of this information comes from high-income countries where supportive care is common [21]. Recent studies have suggested several ways to reduce chemotherapy-induced adverse drug reactions (ADRs) [22]. Supportive care measures backed by evidence, such as optimized anti-nausea regimens that include olanzapine for severe nausea and vomiting, along with the preventive use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factors, have shown notable decreases in treatment-related toxicities [23].

In African settings, where resources for support are scarce, the rates or patterns of ADRs may differ or be higher [24]. There is no robust, quantitative estimate of the overall prevalence of chemotherapy-related ADRs among African pediatric oncology patients. Hence, this study aimed to assess the pooled prevalence of chemotherapy-related adverse drug events among children with cancer in Africa.

Methods and materials

Africa is one of the largest continent with different nations in the world. It has an area of 30.37 million sq. km, and it has more than 1,503,977,695 populations in, January 2025. This systematic review and meta-analysis was based on the recommended methodology and adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-Analysis for Protocols (PRISMA 2020 [25] and the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guideline [26]. The study was registered at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251003306. The results were reported on the basis of the PRISMA statement, and the article screening and selection process was demonstrated through a PRISMA flow diagram.

Searching strategy

A comprehensive search of various articles using common databases, including Google Scholar, Cochrane Library, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, Scopus, EMBASE, and PubMed, was conducted to identify relevant studies. Only articles published until November 16, 2025, were considered in this review. The search terms were “pediatrics”, “prevalence”, “adverse drug reaction’’, “chemotherapy, and “Africa’’. The search strategy was based on keywords using “Medical Subjects Headings (MeSH)” and “All fields” by linking “AND ‘and “OR’. Results were limited to studies conducted in humans and available in the English language only. The search activity was done by BBS, TNL, GGA, AAG and LUS.

Data extraction and quality assessment

All articles retrieved from different databases were exported to the EndNote citation manager for Windows Version X9 (Thomson Reuters, Philadelphia, PA, USA), where duplicates were identified and removed. Three independent reviewers (BSS, TNL and GGA) screened all articles for eligibility criteria, and they began by screening the abstract and title, followed by full-text screening. The quality of the articles was assessed using the Newcastle– Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (adapted for cross-sectional studies) [27]. Disagreements were resolved by inviting a fourth and fifth investigators (LUS and AAG) to participate. The articles were critically appraised by the following criteria from the tool: representativeness of the sample (1 score maximum), sample size (1 score maximum), non-respondent (1 score maximum), ascertainment of exposure (2 score maximum), comparability of outcome based on study design (2 score maximum), outcome assessment (2 score maximum), and statistical analysis (1 score maximum). All the included studies assessed through the tool with a score of ≥ 5 was included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. After the quality rating, no study was dismissed. Microsoft Excel with a standardized extraction format was used by three investigators for data extraction. The excel spread sheet includes the first author’s name, sample size, publication year, country, study design, prevalence of adverse drug reaction. The PECO statement states: Pediatric patients admitted to hospitals in African countries are the population; Hospitalization or treatment in oncology wards are the exposure; Healthcare settings across African hospitals are the context; and incidence adverse drug events are the result.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Observational studies, studies published until February 10, 2025, studies conducted in Africa, pediatrics patients taking chemotherapy and aged < 18 years; and articles published in English were included in this study.

Exclusion criteria

Expert opinions, case reports, case series, review articles, and letters to the editor were excluded from the study.

Statistical analysis

STATA version 17 statistical software was used for the analysis. Pooled prevalence was reported as percentages with a 95% CI. Forest plot and I2 statistics heterogeneity test were calculated to examine the existence of heterogeneity. We assumed no, low, medium, and high heterogeneity across studies if the I2 values were 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75%, respectively. The existence of heterogeneity is declared if the I-squared (I2) result is ≥ 50% and the p-value is less than 0.05. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were calculated to explore the source of heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated for the magnitude of chemotherapy-induced adverse drug events among children with cancer by using funnel plots and Egger’s test.

Outcome measurement

Researchers gathered and analyzed data from various studies conducted in Africa to determine the pooled prevalence of chemotherapy-induced adverse drug events among children with cancer in Africa. The researchers used a systematic approach to identify relevant studies and extract data from them.

Results

Search results

Initially, 472 articles were identified through searches of different databases. Of the identified studies, 363 were removed because of duplication. 90 articles were dropped due to their titles and abstracts, and the remaining 19 articles were critically appraised based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, 5 full-text articles were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Magnitude of chemotherapy-induced ADR among pediatric cancer patients

The results of 9 studies revealed that the pooled magnitude of chemotherapy-induced ADRs among pediatric cancer patients in Africa was 62.03% (95% CI: 36.93–87.13). This meta-analysis identified considerable heterogeneity across the studies (I2 = 99.4%, P-value = 0.000). Therefore, this result needs subgroup analysis (Fig. 2).

The pooled prevalence of chemotherapy induced ADRs among pediatric cancer patients in Africa, 2025

Subgroup analysis

We conducted a subgroup analysis of the magnitude of chemotherapy-induced ADRs among pediatric cancer patients based on different variables (i.e. sample size, country, and year of publication). Sub-group analysis based on sample size revealed that the highest pooled prevalence of chemotherapy-induced ADRs was among less than 100 sample size, 66.78% [95% CI: (40.63–92.92)] (Fig. 3). The other subgroup analysis was performed based on the year of publication, where the pooled prevalence of chemotherapy-induced ADRs among pediatric cancer patients was higher, 69.83% [95% CI: (53.46–86.20)] among articles published after 2020 G.C. (Fig. 4). Finally, the pooled prevalence of chemotherapy-induced ADRs among pediatric cancer patients was highest, 81.48% [95% CI: (72.72–90.24)] in African countries grouped as Others (Fig. 5).

Publication bias

Funnel plots (visual inspection) and Egger statistical tests at the 5% significance level were used to assess the presence of publication bias. The result of funnel plot showed symmetry (Fig. 6) for pooled estimates of chemotherapy induced ADRs among pediatric cancer patients, and the Egger test did not show evidence of statistically significant publication bias (p = 0.159).

Funnel plot of the pooled prevalence of chemotherapy-induced ADRs among pediatric cancer patients in Africa, 2025

Leave one out analysis

By excluding each study individually, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was used to determine the effect of a single study on the pooled prevalence of chemotherapy induced ADRs among pediatric cancer patients in Africa. According to our findings, no single study had a significant impact on the pooled prevalence of chemotherapy induced ADRs among pediatric cancer patients in Africa. Thus, omitting one study does not significantly influence the overall effect size estimate (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Chemotherapy plays an important role in treating cancer in children. Chemotherapy drugs available for pediatric cancer has grown. However, patients experience many undesirable events from chemotherapy [36–38]. Additionally, there are also shortcomings in how current chemotherapy treats children with cancers [36]. Unlike adults, pediatric patients are prone to more ADEs due to their larger body surface area, which makes pediatric patients particularly vulnerable to experiencing a range of ADEs [15].

The overall rate of chemotherapy-induced ADEs among pediatric cancer patients in Africa was 62.03% (95% CI: 36.93–87.13). This rate is much higher than the figures reported outside Africa. Systematic reviews and multicenter studies done in Europe and Asia have reported comparable rates of chemotherapy-related ADEs among pediatric oncology patients, ranging from 14% to 67% [39, 40]. However, a study conducted among pediatric oncology patients reported an adverse drug event prevalence of 46% [41], and in studies conducted in Saudi Arabia and the United Kingdom, the rate of ADEs ranged from 33% to 58% [42, 43]. These might be due to better monitoring systems, improved supportive care, access to newer and safer chemotherapy drugs, and strict adherence of treatment protocols outside Africa. On the other hand, a study done in China was (72.25%) [44]. However, lower than other studies conducted in India (89.07%) [45]. This may result from limited access to supportive therapies like growth factors and antiemetics, late diagnosis of cancer, nutritional deficiencies, and the inclusion of mild or moderate side effects in the definition of ADEs.

The subgroup analysis revealed notable differences in the pooled prevalence of chemotherapy-induced ADEs among pediatric cancer patients across sample size, publication year, and study region. Studies with smaller sample sizes (< 100 participants) showed the highest pooled prevalence of ADEs; 66.78% (95% CI: 40.63–92.92). This might be because of more intensive patient monitoring and reporting, which often capture even mild reactions. Similarly, studies published after 2020 showed a higher pooled prevalence of 69.83% (95% CI: 53.46–86.20) compared to earlier publications. This trend reflects better pharmacovigilance practices [46]. The higher prevalence in studies published after 2020 may also signal growing awareness and improved ADR detection; however, it underscores the urgent need for strengthened pharmacovigilance systems, capacity building among healthcare providers, and evidence-based optimization of chemotherapy protocols to minimize preventable treatment-related harm in resource-limited settings. Regionally, the pooled prevalence was 36.08% [95% CI: -0.23-72.40] in Ethiopia and 81.48% [95% CI: 72.72–90.24] in other African countries, suggesting considerable heterogeneity likely related to differences in chemotherapy protocols, supportive care capacity, and reporting systems.

Implications of the study

For clinical practice

Healthcare workers should implement enhanced pharmacovigilance systems to detect and manage ADRs promptly. Nutritional and psychological support programs should be integrated into pediatric oncology care to enhance patient resilience against treatment-related toxicities.

For policy

Strengthening supply chains for essential medicines, including antidotes and supportive therapies, will help reduce ADE-related mortality and morbidity.

Global relevance

The findings offer strategies to improve or encouraging a multidisciplinary approach, involving oncologists, pharmacists, nurses, and nutritionists, that can improve patient outcomes in Ethiopia and similar low-resource settings.

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review and meta-analysis offer valuable insights into the prevalence of Chemotherapy-induced ADRs among pediatric cancer patients in Africa, providing reliable data to guide healthcare interventions. However, it has several limitations, including heterogeneity observed (I² = 97.5%), potential publication bias, the small number of included studies (n = 5), which limits statistical power and generalizability, and varying study quality. Moreover, we limited the search to articles that were written in the English language. Due to these relevant articles that were published in a non-English language were not included. Furthermore, most of the primary studies did not provide detail information about ADR assessment method, classified chemotherapy regimen, follow up and mean age of participants thoroughly, which restricted our ability to add them to the summary table.

Conclusion

The burden of Chemotherapy-induced ADRs is found to be high. While these medications are crucial for treating childhood cancers, it’s critical to recognize, manage, and mitigate the adverse effects to improve treatment outcomes and optimize the quality of life for these vulnerable patients. As a result, health care professionals involved in cancer treatment should be prepared to deal with chemotherapy-related adverse drug reactions. Routine ADR surveillance should be integrated into pediatric oncology wards in Africa, and enhanced pharmacy monitoring may help mitigate ADRs in chemotherapy wards.
