Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) stands as one of the most pressing global issues, acknowledged by the World Health Organization (WHO) as one of the top ten threats to public health [1]. In Canada, over 12,900 deaths in 2019 were linked to resistant infections, with 2,900 directly attributed to AMR [2]. Additionally, the increasing rate at which existing antibiotics are losing their effectiveness exceeds the pace of innovation in developing new drugs or alternative treatments, contributing to escalating levels of resistance [3].

While AMR is a naturally occurring phenomenon, the excessive use and misuse of antibiotics are pivotal factors accelerating this resistance. In Alberta, Canada, physicians constitute the largest group of antibiotic prescribers, accounting for 80% of all oral antibiotic prescriptions [4]. Alarmingly, almost 40% of these prescriptions are deemed inappropriate (i.e., never appropriate medication given the diagnosis codes, or no diagnosis codes associated with dispensation) [5]. The College of Physicians & Surgeons of Alberta (CPSA), as a medical regulator, plays a crucial role in advocating for responsible antibiotic prescribing. CPSA supports antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) initiatives and develops resources to educate physicians on prudent antibiotic usage.

Leveraging Alberta’s Tracked Prescription Program data [6], CPSA introduced the MD Snapshot-Prescribing report (Snapshot) in 2016 [7]. This interactive and personalized tool presents physicians with information on their prescribing practices. In the fourth quarter of 2021, an antibiotic reporting section was integrated into the Snapshot, and 11,324 Alberta physicians who prescribed at least one antibiotic, opioid, benzodiazepine, or Z-drug received an individualized Snapshot. To the best of our knowledge, the Snapshot is a distinctive interactive educational resource reporting physicians’ prescribing behaviours in Alberta, Canada.

Extensive research, both in Canada and internationally, has been dedicated to understanding physicians’ antibiotic prescribing behaviour, responsible medication management (stewardship), and the multifaceted factors that influence antibiotic prescriptions [5, 8, 9]. Findings suggest a modest decrease in antibiotic use is attributed to various factors related to physicians (e.g., age, educational background, specialty, and years of experience), patients (e.g., diagnostic uncertainty and perceived patient expectations), and healthcare systems (e.g., time pressure, workload, financial considerations, guidelines/policies) [10–12]. Strategies such as educating physicians on guidelines, regular reminders, and public/patient awareness campaigns were recommended to enhance antibiotic stewardship and promote appropriate use [12, 13].

There remains a gap in research pertaining to the knowledge and perspectives of Canadian physicians concerning antibiotic prescribing practices. Therefore, the aim of our study was to bridge this gap by exploring Alberta physicians’ perspectives related to AMR, antibiotic stewardship, and antibiotic prescribing. Additionally, we aimed to investigate the impact of the Snapshot on physicians’ prescribing behaviour, self-reflection regarding antibiotic prescriptions, and their strategies regarding AMR.

Methods

Study design, settings, and participant recruitment

We utilized descriptive qualitative inquiry to explore how Alberta physicians consider AMR and how they incorporate the Snapshot reports into their antibiotic prescribing practices. Our study adhered to quality markers in qualitative research and followed the reporting guidelines outlined in the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research [14].

Physicians registered in Alberta, who had prescribed at least one antibiotic to a patient in the prior quarter and received at least one Snapshot report were eligible for participation. We purposefully recruited participants by promoting the study in the CPSA’s monthly electronic newsletter. Furthermore, we utilized CPSA’s social media platforms such as Twitter, Facebook, and LinkedIn to extend our outreach. Prospective participants were encouraged to express their interest by contacting the study team. Prior to interview, the participants who were interested in participating in the study and contacted the CPSA, were approached by the researcher responsible for participants’ recruitment (HHM).

Data generation and analysis

We organized virtual focus group interviews through the ZOOM video conferencing platform and followed a semi-structured interview guide (Supplementary Material 1 - Interview guide). A team of researchers experienced in qualitative and quantitative research (NK, FG, and IH) developed the interview guide. The questions were open-ended, allowing participants to share their perspectives and experiences in depth. Participants received a study information letter detailing the study’s purpose, procedures, privacy, confidentiality, and an informed consent form. They were informed about their rights to refuse participation or withdraw from the study at any point, up to one month after participating, without needing to provide a reason, and that doing so would not affect their relationship with the CPSA or the University of Alberta.

All participants provided written informed consent for their participation, the recording of the focus group interview, and the anonymous use of quotes from the interviews. Furthermore, all information and consent discussions were conducted prior to the focus group interview sessions, ensuring that participants had the opportunity to ask questions and confirm their understanding of the study.

Seven focus group interviews took place between May and June 2023. Each session lasted approximately 90 min and was facilitated by the Principal Investigator (NK). All interviews were audio recorded and subsequently transcribed for analysis. NVIVO version 12 software was utilized to code and manage focus group interview data.

The transcripts of focus group interviews underwent thematic content analysis, a widely employed analytic technique in descriptive qualitative inquiry. This process involved the researcher examining participants’ intentions by identifying, coding, and categorizing the primary patterns observed in the data [15]. One member of the research team (IH) coded and analyzed all the transcripts of focus group interviews. Coding trees were developed, and analytical themes formulated, which were then reviewed and discussed with the Principal Investigator (NK). The final major themes and sub-themes emerged after a comprehensive discussion among the research team, based on patterns evident across the participants’ responses. Additionally, to ensure internal validity, another member of the research team (HHM) reviewed portions of the transcripts to validate the accuracy of data representation and the identified themes.

All research team members contributed to reviewing the findings to create a more comprehensive, robust, and credible interpretation of the participants’ data. This collaborative approach helped to minimize individual biases and enhance the rigor and trustworthiness of the study.

Results

Participant characteristics

The study comprised seven focus groups involving 21 Alberta physicians. On average, participant physicians were 42.2 years old, with ages ranging from 29 to 66. The majority (71%) of participants fell within the age range of 30 to 45 years. Thirteen (57.1%) of the 21 physicians were female. Participants’ years of practice varied from 1 to 34 years, with half (52%) of participants having practiced between 10 and 25 years. Notably, 90% of the participants were family doctors (Table 1). While detailed information on practice setting (e.g., walk-in clinics versus longitudinal family practice) was not systematically collected, participants described a range of practice during focus group discussions.

Theme 1. Physicians’ knowledge about antimicrobial resistance and stewardship

Physicians’ knowledge about antimicrobial resistance

Many participants acknowledged a growing awareness of AMR and highlighted a shift towards more cautious use of antibiotics.

“I think compared to even when I first started going through training…there is an increasing awareness of more judicious antibiotics. I do see practice changes happening in my practice and other peoples’ practices around me.” (Participant, Focus Group 1 [FG1]).

Concurrently, physicians shared diverse views on the severity of AMR. While some noted minimal resistance in their practices, others emphasized a noticeable increase, especially in cases of recurring infections. Moreover, several participants highlighted challenges in urgent care settings, particularly in managing multi-drug-resistant pathogens that cause urinary tract infections (UTIs), and in caring for patients from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, including individuals experiencing homelessness and refugees. These patients may face barriers to education and access to medications, which can sometimes necessitate prescribing antibiotics, despite careful efforts to minimize their use.

Physicians’ approaches to better practices in antimicrobial stewardship

Overall, participants exhibited diverse attitudes and actions towards antimicrobial stewardship, showcasing a spectrum of responses. While some participants indicated limited engagement or awareness in specific stewardship activities, they mentioned their commitment to responsible antibiotic use through cautiously prescribing them. Conversely, others highlighted active engagement in initiatives aimed to reduce antibiotic prescriptions and advocated for guidelines such as Choosing Wisely Recommendations [16] to promote conscientious practices. Additionally, some physicians participated in committees or networks dedicated to implementing guidelines promoting prudent antibiotic use.

“I sit on a committee with our primary care network, called Green for Health. One of the things we’ve been trying to do is provide some tools that have an Alberta focus on how family physicians can ‘green’ their practices. And so, one of the things that we’re planning for next year… is looking at reducing antibiotic prescription use and how that can impact the environment. So, we’re trying to promote the Alberta Choosing Wisely Recommendations on the primary care side for antibiotics.” (Participant FG3).

Interdisciplinary collaboration emerged as a key theme, with physicians emphasizing the importance of sharing knowledge across healthcare disciplines. They described instances where they had colleagues well-versed in antimicrobial stewardship, or received updated guidelines themselves, indicating a continuous learning process that contributes to better practices in antimicrobial stewardship.

Some participants noted that patients were influenced by campaigns like Choosing Wisely. This influence led patients to question antibiotic prescriptions and gain a better understanding of their appropriate usage. From physicians’ perspective, this increased patient involvement fostered a collaborative approach between healthcare providers and patients. Both sides were more aligned regarding the appropriate use of antibiotics.

Furthermore, most focus group physicians stressed the significance of personal dedication to best practices in antibiotic prescription. They prioritize judicious antibiotic use by strictly adhering to guidelines, opting for narrower spectrum options, and minimizing specific types like fluoroquinolones whenever possible. Continual self-improvement remains a focal point in their commitment to antimicrobial stewardship.

“I just try to be a good steward every day by following Choosing Wisely guidelines, Bugs & Drugs, making sure I’m not over prescribing on a duration, even for an actual infection.” (Participant FG6).

Theme 2. Antibiotic prescribing practices

The frequency and reasoning behind antibiotic prescribing practices

The frequency of antibiotic prescribing varied widely among participants, influenced by patient demographics, practice setting, diagnostic approach, specific health issues encountered, and follow-up required. Physicians highlighted that patient populations have an effect in antibiotic prescribing frequency. Those who see a higher proportion of younger patients mentioned common reasons for prescribing antibiotics such as strep throat, otitis media, and UTIs. Additionally, practitioners working with patients experiencing homelessness noted frequent skin infections and dental issues that often require antibiotic treatment.

“I think it’s more than one or two times a day. I see a lot of…homeless people, here. Skin infection every day. Dental infection, typically, every day. So, one or two, that would be the minimum.” (Participant FG4).

According to physicians’ responses, antibiotic prescription practices differed among settings like urgent care, family practice, walk-in clinics, and specialty centers (e.g., sexual health centers or clinics for new immigrants). Common reasons for prescribing antibiotics included UTIs, sinusitis, strep throat, otitis media, skin/soft tissue infections, and occasionally pneumonia.

“I think frequency [of antibiotic prescribing] is very dependent on where you’re working, what kind of work you’re seeing. So, at the urgent care, I can’t really think of a shift that I haven’t prescribed at least one kind of antibiotic for something. Family practice, oftentimes you’ll go through a full day and not, or even like a week sometimes and not prescribing antibiotics.” (Participant FG2).

Barriers influencing appropriate antibiotic prescribing practices

Many physicians found it challenging to navigate conversations where patients insist on antibiotics despite professional advice against their necessity. Some patients become upset or even confrontational when denied antibiotics, which can be emotionally difficult for physicians. Several participants recognized the external pressures faced by physicians, particularly in walk-in clinics or urgent care settings, characterized by high patient turnover. Balancing patient expectations alongside clinical judgment becomes notably more difficult in these fast-paced environments.

“The main reason for the visit is that ‘I need an antibiotic. I need an antibiotic.’ I have told them they don’t need antibiotics. They get mad. ‘I came for nothing. You wasted my time.’ And that can ruin your day. Even though you’re doing the right thing, it still affects you personally. That’s a challenge too, right, with tough patients.” (Participant FG4).

Participants also pointed out several factors influencing physicians’ approaches to antibiotic prescriptions, such as the business aspect, economic incentives, various practice models, and payment structures. For instance, in some clinic setups with high patient volumes, there’s a priority placed on rapid prescriptions rather than comprehensive explanations, which differs from a more meticulous and time-consuming approach.

“And I think that is the challenge because in a walk-in clinic, which is often owned by physicians, this is their business, right. You have a waiting room full of people, they’re trying to get through all those people simply because of the volume, but also perhaps for business reasons. It’s a lot easier to just give that prescription. It’s over in two minutes. The patient is happy, the doctors made their visit, rather than sit down and spend time and potentially have an unpleasant encounter.” (Participant FG7).

The need for effective communication, patient education, and navigating these challenges ethically and professionally was a recurring theme in the discussions among physicians. Furthermore, most participants stressed that building trust and rapport with patients is vital. When patients lack an established relationship with a physician, they might be less inclined to follow advice, leading to increased challenges in managing expectations.

Resources utilized for antibiotic prescribing practices

The responses from participants regarding their resources for supporting antibiotic prescribing varied but included several commonly used tools and references.

The primary resource mentioned was Bugs & Drugs [17], recognized as a valuable tool due to its regular updates and regional specificity in selecting appropriate antibiotics based on local resistance patterns. Participants also highlighted the utilization of other tools like Spectrum [18] or its updated version, Firstline [19], which is widely known and used in Alberta. These resources were praised for ensuring uniformity in prescribing medications for prevalent conditions.

“So, what I appreciate about being in Alberta is, I think the uptake of both Bugs & Drugs and Spectrum, which I think is now changed to Firstline as an app, everybody that I work with knows about it, everybody goes to it, everybody uses it. There is very consistent prescribing for very common conditions, right.” (Participant FG2).

Some participants mentioned using various digital resources alongside Bugs & Drugs [17] for antibiotic prescribing, such as UpToDate [20], RxFiles [21], and Lexicomp [22]. Opinions on UpToDate [20] varied: some found it overwhelming due to its extensive information, while others valued its integration into their Electronic Medical Records (EMR). The resource was noted for providing concise dosage information, similar to Bugs & Drugs [17]. DynaMed [23], a Canadian evidence-based resource similar to UpToDate [20], was highlighted for its graded evidence and specific antibiotic recommendations.

A few participants specified seeking guidance from infectious disease specialists in instances involving complex medical cases or scenarios requiring expertise beyond standard references. Furthermore, many participants recognized the significant value of consulting pharmacists, especially in clinics where clinical pharmacists are accessible.

“Yes, my main clinic is fortunate to have a clinical pharmacist most days of the week there. For more complicated things, I will consult, especially if I’m worried about QT prolongation, or what’s a third line option for this relatively uncommon thing. We’re pretty fortunate to have that. But it’s a big clinic and most clinics probably don’t have that resource readily available.” (Participant FG7).

However, there were concerns among some participants about staying updated with regional resistance patterns, suggesting the need for better resources or tools to access local resistance data for more appropriate antibiotic treatments.

Theme 3. Insights from the MD Snapshot-prescribing report

Education and self-reflection tool

The Snapshot report was generally regarded as a valuable educational and self-reflective tool by most focus group participants. It helped doctors reduce unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions by providing personalized profiles that allowed them to review patient histories and identify situations where antibiotics might not have been needed. Physicians caring for patients who have chronic pain found it useful for tracking opioid and benzodiazepine prescriptions, prompting reflection on individual cases and exploring alternative treatments tailored to each patient.

“For me, any data about my behaviour or the way I prescribe, it’s good. It’s always given me a second thought on why I’m doing this. Am I doing it the right way? Of course, it’s not going to stop me prescribing antibiotics if needed. But it changed the way I prescribe. I pay more attention and I give more time. Honestly, it helped me with the opioid and benzo before, and now with the antibiotics.” (Participant FG4).

Furthermore, many participants saw the Snapshot as an educational resource promoting evidence-based prescribing and aiding patient education on antibiotic use. Participants used it to confirm their practice patterns, compare their standing among peers, track trends over time, and improve their practice through self-reflection.

“Yes, it made me want to prescribe less antibiotics, that’s for sure.” (Participant FG1).

However, some participants doubted the practicality of the metric identifying patients with multiple prescribers in the MD Snapshot report. They paid attention to challenges in managing multiple prescribers, particularly in episodic care settings, and suggested checking EMR before prescribing. While they valued trend monitoring, they emphasized the need for data that better suited their specific roles and responsibilities such as Pharmaceutical Information Network.

Punitive tool

A third of participants expressed initial discomfort and emotional involvement upon the release of the Snapshot report. Many felt a strong sense of being scrutinized by a physician regulator, despite the CPSA’s intention for the tool to serve as an educational resource rather than a punitive measure. This discomfort led to concerns that their legitimate prescription practices – especially for patients with serious conditions like cancer or in palliative care- might be reflected poorly in the data. Additionally, some participants worried that having a large patient panel or working more hours could unfairly slope their prescribing percentages compared to their peers. These concerns were compounded by apprehensions about potential punitive consequences, such as the risk of having their license revoked, even though the regulator did not intend to enforce punitive measures.

“The first one that came out. I was working at the walk-in at that time. And although a lot of those opioids I hadn’t initiated, I was continuing them for patients who were dependent on them. That one, when I read it, felt very punitive to me, but I think a lot has changed since then.” (Participant FG7).

Yet, most participants observed a significant change in perception over time - shifting from a punitive standpoint to viewing it as an instrument for improvement. They emphasized its potential as both an educational resource and a means to increase awareness regarding prescribing patterns.

Snapshot interpretation challenges and future iteration recommendations

Participants in the focus groups talked about challenges and the complexity of the report that they encountered while interpreting the Snapshot and proposed recommendations for future iterations. One notable issue was the difficulty some physicians faced in grasping the concept of antibiotic Defined Daily Doses (DDDs) [24] and understanding how these values were calculated and applied in their prescribing practices. They found it challenging to correlate the prescribed doses with the DDD metric and expressed a need for clearer explanations or illustrative examples that could clarify the calculation method and its relevance to their clinical practice.

“I actually don’t really fully get the Antibiotic DDDs. I know that there’s an explanation down at the bottom, and I’m not sure I understand how it’s calculated.” (Participant FG1).

Another challenge highlighted by certain participants was the complexity of interpreting the report, especially when dealing with numerous brief patient encounters. Physicians specifically struggled to make sense of data related to patients with whom they had brief interactions, impacting their ability to derive meaningful insights from the information provided.

“So, the vast majority of patients…many of the patients on there are patients that I see very transiently. And so sometimes it makes it difficult to make meaning of the report.” (Participant FG2).

Some participants expressed concerns about being labeled as high prescribers due to uncertainty about percentile rankings. They felt that their prescribing practices were justified given the patient load and the prevalence of common diseases requiring prescriptions. Additionally, certain participants faced difficulties with the report’s interface, especially in finding specific breakdowns of antibiotic data. They found certain aspects challenging to comprehend and emphasized the need for a more user-friendly and easily understandable presentation of information within the report.

Participants thought it was essential to contextualize prescriber type (e.g., emergency room, urgent care, family practice) for specific antibiotics to enhance medical history consolidation.

“If you can take this feedback to the [CPSA], to create a context, maybe even a further analysis of the MD snapshots, in terms of kind of practice or location, that will give more context. Because if you want to compare someone that is working in emergency room, where they have to deal with patients coming in with infection and things that require them to prescribe antibiotics and compare with somebody that is just doing a normal practice, it’s not going to be the same. And then at the end of the day, they say, compared to your peers. This is not your peers, because everyone is working in different locations.” (Participant FG7).

Many physicians recommended presenting data to show antibiotics prescribed by diagnoses to aid in distinguishing appropriate from inappropriate antibiotic use.

“I’d like to see the data broken down based on diagnosis. So, as opposed to just saying, okay, you prescribed this many antibiotics, but then you don’t really know, how many of those were because somebody had chlamydia and how many of those were some sort of upper respiratory symptoms. And so, having it broken down based on diagnosis might be helpful to say, okay, were all those reasons that I prescribed it correct indications, or maybe not?” (Participant FG3).

Discussion

This qualitative study delved into the perceptions and experiences of Alberta physicians concerning antibiotic prescribing, AMR, and antibiotic stewardship. Our aim was to understand how the interactive Snapshot report influenced physicians’ practices. Among the significant findings was the growing awareness among physicians regarding AMR and the excessive use of antimicrobials in Canada, including Alberta. This finding aligns with earlier studies that highlighted the recognition of the emergence of AMR within healthcare systems [5, 9–11, 25].

The responses underscored the pressing need for sustained efforts to decrease the risk of AMR through keen awareness, education initiatives, and judicious prescribing practices, all of which should be reinforced and guided by evidence-based prescribing guidelines and system-wide support. Subsequently, continuing professional education, the implementation of comprehensive policies and guidelines, along with regular reporting of a provider’s prescription profile have the potential to enhance appropriate prescribing practices. A concept previously investigated and documented in the existing literature [5, 12, 13].

Different levels of awareness and attitudes regarding antimicrobial stewardship were observed among physicians, leading to a wide range of responses. Some physicians showed limited knowledge and involvement, while others actively participated in stewardship initiatives. These findings align with a study conducted in Indonesia, which identified varying levels of knowledge and beliefs (a composite score which included perceived threat score, perceived self-efficacy score, perceived benefit score, and perceived barrier score) of physicians regarding antibiotic stewardship to be able design training to meet their needs. Although 86% of physicians had moderate scores knowledge about antimicrobial stewardship, only 17% of physicians scored high on beliefs [26]. Likewise, physicians in our study emphasized the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration and knowledge-sharing across healthcare, increasing patient awareness of AMR, and appropriate antimicrobial use as essential components of effective antimicrobial stewardship [27, 28].

The study revealed a significant variability in antibiotic prescribing practices among Alberta physicians, influenced by various factors including patient demographics, practice settings, and the health issues encountered. Younger patient populations often led to common antibiotic prescriptions for conditions like strep throat, otitis media, and UTIs, while practitioners serving homeless populations noted frequent skin infections and dental issues requiring antibiotics. Practice settings like urgent care, family practice, and specialty centers exhibited differing prescription trends for UTIs, sinusitis, strep throat, otitis media, skin/soft tissue infections, and occasionally pneumonia. These findings are consistent with many studies that investigated factors related to patient demographics [10, 29], practice settings and patient volume [11] and the clinical condition of patients at the time of prescription [30, 31].

We identified various obstacles affecting the appropriate prescription of antibiotics, with a particular emphasis on the difficulties faced during patient interactions where there was a clash between patients insisting on antibiotics and professional advice. This challenge was notably pronounced for physicians, especially in fast-paced settings like walk-in clinics with high patient turnover. Our findings are like other research indicating that patients often expect immediate results and a tangible return on their investment of time and resources, contributing to the inappropriate prescribing of antibiotics [32, 33]. Additionally, external pressures such as business considerations, economic incentives, and practice models were observed to influence participants’ prescribing behaviors.

Physicians proposed crucial strategies, including patient education, and trust-building, particularly in unfamiliar patient-physician relationships. These suggestions are comparable with previous studies emphasizing the importance of consistent and ongoing patient-specific and public education on appropriate antibiotic usage [34, 35]. Moreover, cultivating a robust patient-physician rapport is recognized as a crucial factor in mitigating inappropriate antibiotic use and tackling AMR [36–38].

Aligning with previous research [25], our study found that physicians draw on a range of resources to support antimicrobial prescribing and enhance their knowledge. In our study, several tools, including “Bugs & Drugs [17]”, “Spectrum”/“Firstline” [18, 19], “UpToDate” [20], “RxFiles” [21] and “Lexicomp” [22] were frequently employed, contributing to effective and evidence-informed prescribing practices. Seeking advice from infectious disease specialists and consulting readily available clinical pharmacists were also identified as valuable channels. Nonetheless, concerns were expressed about the necessity for improved resources to access regional resistance data, facilitating more precise antibiotic prescribing.

The CPSA’s Snapshot report was considered a valuable educational and self-reflective tool. It helped reduce unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions by providing personalized profiles that encouraged healthcare providers to review patient histories. Participants found it beneficial for promoting evidence-based prescribing and patient education on antibiotic use. It also enabled trend monitoring and self-reflection to improve practice and decrease inappropriate use of antibiotics. Despite initial discomfort and concerns about being scrutinized by the regulator, participants’ perceptions shifted over time. They began to view the Snapshot as an instrument for improvement and increased awareness of prescribing patterns, rather than solely as a punitive tool.

Our findings are in line with results from studies in Ontario, Canada [9] and the United Kingdom [39], as they target AMS that include comparative data. For instance, the UK study reported an approximate 5% reduction in antimicrobial prescribing when letters from England’s Chief Medical Officer, providing feedback on prescribing practices relative to the norm, were sent to primary care physicians in the top 20% of antimicrobial prescribers [39].

Physicians in our study also provided valuable suggestions on how the CPSA, as a medical regulator, can influence antibiotic prescribing practices. Participants emphasized the importance of providing physicians with information suggesting updates on the CPSA website with current evidence, including resistance rates and changes in treatment guidelines. Another suggestion involved the CPSA participating in an initiative to integrate Antimicrobial Stewardship into Health Information Systems, incorporating guidelines and alerts into EMRs for real-time prompts during care. Recommendations were also made for the CPSA to facilitate communication between healthcare professionals and patients, providing tools to handle unnecessary antibiotic requests by patients effectively.

Lastly, many highlighted the CPSA’s potential to recognize and incentivize physicians’ reflective practices through Continuing Medical Education credits or a tracking system rewarding time spent on antibiotic stewardship-related quality improvement work.

Limitations

This study presents several limitations that warrant consideration. Primarily, the participant pool predominantly comprised Alberta family doctors, potentially constraining the applicability of our findings to other medical specialties, diverse regions, or healthcare systems characterized by distinct demographics and structures. However, our findings indicated that there were no significant differences between the perspectives of family physicians and those of the single pediatrician and critical care physician on issues related to AMR, antibiotic prescribing practices, and the MD Snapshot. Given this lack of variation in viewpoints, we did not identify or highlight differences in our manuscript.

Additionally, the study’s reliance on a relatively small sample of Alberta physicians may further limit the generalizability of our results. We did not systematically collect detailed data on practice setting (e.g., walk-in versus longitudinal family practice), which may limit interpretation of how prescribing practices vary across clinical contexts. Another limitation is the potential selection bias, as participants self-selected by responding to study recruitment material, which may have attracted physicians with a higher level of engagement and/or interest in antimicrobial stewardship. Furthermore, the study is also dependent on self-reported perceptions and experiences of physicians, introducing the possibility of bias and subjective interpretations. However, we implemented a comprehensive methodology and thorough exploration of various themes to mitigate potential bias, enhancing the robustness of our findings. Despite these limitations, our study contributes valuable insights into understanding physicians’ perspectives and experiences related to AMR, antimicrobial stewardship, and antibiotic prescribing practices.

Conclusions

Qualitative inquiry has become a standard practice in medical research for delving into intricate subjects and applying innovative approaches within the targeted field. In the context of antibiotic prescribing and AMR, this method has proven beneficial in gaining insights into healthcare providers’ perspectives on their antibiotic prescribing practices and patterns.

This study sheds light on the complex landscape of antibiotic prescribing practices among Alberta physicians. The findings underscore the need for continuous efforts to address antimicrobial resistance through heightened awareness, education initiatives, and evidence-based prescribing guidelines. The diverse range of factors influencing prescribing practices, including patient demographics, practice settings, and external pressures, emphasizes the importance of tailored interventions.

CPSA’s MD Snapshot-Prescribing report emerged as a valuable tool in this context, facilitating self-reflection and personalized feedback for physicians. Despite initial concerns, it appears instrumental in reducing unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions and promoting evidence-based practices. Our research supports the effectiveness of targeted antimicrobial stewardship interventions, as seen in other regions, and provides practical suggestions for the medical regulators to influence prescribing practices positively. Recommendations for the CPSA, including updates on antibiotic resistance information, integration of stewardship into health information systems, and incentives for reflective practices, present actionable steps to enhance antibiotic stewardship.

This work contributes valuable insights into antibiotic prescribing complexities, offering a foundation for future research and interventions to optimize antibiotic use and decrease AMR risk.
