The impact of creatine levels on musculoskeletal health in the elderly: a mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Minping Hong, Junyan Wang, Liqin Jin, Keng Ling
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMusculoskeletal health among the elderly emerges as a pivotal public health concern against the backdrop of a globally aging population. Creatine, popular within fitness circles, has emerged as a subject of scientific inquiry for its potential benefits on elderly’ musculoskeletal well-being. Despite extensive documentation of its effects on athletic performance, investigations into creatine’s long-term contributions to the musculoskeletal health of the elderly are comparatively limited. Utilizing publicly accessible genetic datasets, this study aimed to explore the potential causal link between creatine levels and a variety of musculoskeletal health markers in the elderly population, such as osteoarthritis (OA), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), osteoporosis (OP), bone-specific alkaline phosphatase, bone mineral density(BMD), serum calcium levels, and reduced hand grip strength in individuals aged 60 and above.MethodsOur methodological approach was grounded in Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis, with a primary emphasis on the Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW) technique, to explore potential causal links under specific assumptions and limitations inherent to MR analysis.ResultsA significant inverse relationship was observed between creatine levels and reduced hand grip strength in individuals aged 60 and above (OR = 0.64, b=-0.44, p = 0.01), suggesting higher creatine levels might be beneficial in maintaining hand grip strength. Conversely, analysis of other bone health parameters, including bone-specific alkaline phosphatase, bone mineral density, serum calcium levels, and conditions such as osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and osteoporosis, yielded p-values exceeding 0.05, denoting no statistically significant associations.ConclusionsThis study provides preliminary evidence indicating a link between creatine levels and decreased hand grip strength among the elderly, notably in individuals aged 60 and older. This finding is significant for understanding the potential impact of creatine supplementation on elderly Musculoskeletal health and underscores the need for evidence-based decision-making in nutritional supplementation. To robustly validate these observations, it is essential to conduct future randomized controlled trials with large sample sizes.Keywords: Creatine levels, Elderly musculoskeletal health, Hand grip strength, Mendelian randomization, Nutritional supplements

Introduction

Creatine, a naturally occurring compound synthesized in the human body, has garnered significant attention in the realm of sports nutrition and exercise physiology [1]. As a dietary supplement, creatine has been lauded for its potential to enhance athletic performance, particularly in high-intensity, short-duration activities such as soccer and sprinting [2–4]. Its efficacy in augmenting phosphocreatine stores in muscles, thereby facilitating rapid adenosine triphosphate (ATP) regeneration during physical exertion, has been well-documented in numerous studies [5,6]. As a result, creatine supplementation has been integrated into the routine of numerous athletes and individuals engaged in physical training, establishing it as one of the predominant ergogenic aids utilized globally.

Conversely, musculoskeletal health, encompassing conditions like osteoarthritis (OA), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), osteoporosis (OP), remains a critical concern in the realm of orthopedics and geriatrics. OA, characterized by the degeneration of joint cartilage, leads to pain and limited mobility [7]. RA, an autoimmune disorder, results in joint inflammation and deformity [8]. OP, on the other hand, is marked by reduced bone density, rendering bones fragile and prone to fractures [9]. These conditions not only impair physical function but also significantly diminish the quality of life, making bone health a paramount area of medical research.

The intertwining of the domains of creatine metabolism and musculoskeletal health is illuminated by emerging evidence and studies suggesting a potential beneficial link between creatine supplementation and bone health [10–12]. Notably, some research indicates that creatine might play a role in promoting bone formation and reducing bone resorption [13]. The underlying mechanism might be tied to creatine’s ability to enhance cellular energy availability, which could be crucial for osteoblasts, the bone-forming cells [14]. Additionally, creatine might exert its effects through the stimulation of satellite cells, which can support muscle growth and, indirectly, bone strength due to the muscle-bone interaction [15].

The foundational hypothesis is that creatine supplementation could act as an adjunct to traditional treatments for bone-related disorders, potentially aiding in the prevention of conditions like osteoporosis or accelerating recovery from fractures. Such insights have sparked optimism among health professionals and individuals alike, especially those at an increased risk for bone ailments. Given the profound implications of these findings, there is a growing emphasis on further rigorous scientific investigations to validate and understand the depth of the relationship between creatine and bone health.

Mendelian Randomization (MR) offers a unique advantage in this context. By utilizing genetic variants as instrumental variables, MR can provide insights into the causal relationships between exposures and outcomes, circumventing many of the confounding factors and biases inherent in observational studies. In this study, we utilize the MR approach to explore the potential causal relationship between creatine levels and key indicators of musculoskeletal health in elderly. This endeavor aims to offer clearer insights into a topic of significant concern for public health, particularly given the increasing prevalence of musculoskeletal -related issues in aging populations.

Materials and methods

Research approach

Utilizing a two-sample MR technique, we investigated a potential causal relationship between Creatine levels and OA, RA, OP, alkaline phosphatase (bone), bone mineral density(BMD), calcium levels, and low hand grip strength (60 years and older). In MR, genetic variations act as surrogates for risk determinants. To ensure a valid causal interpretation through instrumental variables (IVs), three essential criteria are required:

A direct relationship between the genetic variation and the exposure.

Absence of a link between the genetic variation and any confounders of the exposure-outcome connection.

The genetic variation affects the outcome exclusively through the exposure.

All incorporated studies received the necessary ethical approvals, and participants provided informed consent.

Data Sources for Creatine levels through Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS).

Summary statistics pertinent to Creatine levels were sourced from the GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The study performed a GWAS on 7,822 Sample size, with 2,545,661 Number of SNPs. The study’s population primarily consisted of individuals of European descent.

Musculoskeletal health GWAS data sources

The GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) provided summary statistics for a range of conditions and factors related to musculoskeletal health, including OA, RA, OP, alkaline phosphatase (bone), BMD, calcium levels, and low hand grip strength (60 years and older). For OA, the study involved 484,598 participants, with 39,515 cases and 445,083 controls, examining approximately 9,587,836 SNPs. In the context of RA, the sample consisted of 484,598 individuals, with 5,427 cases and 479,171 controls, evaluating around 9,587,836 SNPs. For OP, the study included 484,598 participants, comprising 7,751 cases and 476,847 controls, and analyzed approximately 9,587,836 SNPs. Regarding alkaline phosphatase (bone), the dataset encompassed assessing 501,428 SNPs. In the study of BMD, there were 365,403 individuals, examining around 10,783,906 SNPs. For the analysis of calcium levels, the sample size was 400,792 participants, evaluating approximately 4,218,949 SNPs. Lastly, for low hand grip strength in individuals aged 60 and older, the study covered 135,468 subjects, with 13,601cases and 121,867 controls, analyzing around 9,324,464 SNPs. The study’s population primarily consisted of individuals of European descent. The study included both male and female participants.

Instrumental variables (IVs) selection

Guided by recent scientific findings, we set a significance level for IVs associated with each characteristic at 1 × 10[− 5 [16,17]]. Utilizing the TwoSampleMR package in R ( [18], we optimized the selection of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). The clumping parameter was activated, with a secondary significance level set at 1 × 10− 5. We delineated a criterion for linkage disequilibrium (LD) r^2 at 0.001 and demarcated a clumping proximity of 10,000 kb.

Statistical approach

Evaluations were performed utilizing R programming environment (version 4.3.1). The causal link between Creatine levels and Musculoskeletal Health was assessed via methods like inverse variance weighting (IVW) ( [19], weighted median ( [20], and mode-based techniques ( [21], primarily through the TwoSampleMR package. Heterogeneity among IVs was tested using Cochran’s Q statistic. In cases of significant heterogeneity, a random-effects IVW replaced the fixed-effects approach. The MR-Egger method was employed to address potential horizontal pleiotropy, with significance in its intercept indicating its presence ( [22]. The MR-PRESSO technique further refined the analysis by identifying and excluding potential pleiotropic outliers ( [23]. Funnel plots were incorporated to validate the results’ consistency and robustness, respectively.

Results

Probing the impact of creatine levels on musculoskeletal health

Our in-depth analysis revealed a statistically significant negative correlation between creatine levels and the risk of low hand grip strength in individuals aged 60 and over, using the IVW method (OR = 0.64, b=-0.44, p = 0.011). This finding suggests that higher creatine levels may help reduce the risk of low hand grip strength in the elderly, highlighting the potential therapeutic benefits of creatine supplementation (Fig. 1). A similar protective effect was confirmed through the Weighted median method, which also showed a decrease in risk (OR = 0.56, b=-0.58, p = 0.01).

For other musculoskeletal health markers such as OA (IVW: OR = 1.00, b = 0.01, p = 0.82), RA(IVW: OR = 0.99, b=-0.01, p = 0.87), OP(IVW: OR = 1.01, b = 0.01, p = 0.78), alkaline phosphatase (bone) (IVW: OR = 2.56, b = 0.94, p = 0.16), BMD(IVW: OR = 0.98, b=-0.02, p = 0.53), calcium levels(IVW: OR = 0.95, b=-0.06, p = 0.43), our findings did not demonstrate statistically significant associations (p > 0.05). These results suggest that the effects of creatine may be specific to muscle strength rather than directly influencing bone health, a mechanism that warrants further investigation. (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In the present study, we employed MR methodologies to investigate the potential causal relationship between creatine levels and musculoskeletal health among the elderly population. Notably, we identified a significant inverse correlation between elevated creatine levels and reduced grip strength, providing empirical support for the hypothesis that higher creatine levels may be associated with a decrease in grip strength among the elderly. However, no significant impact of creatine levels was observed on other indicators of skeletal health, suggesting a potential specificity of creatine’s role in the musculoskeletal health of the elderly.

While our application of MR offers a powerful approach to infer potential causal relationships, the inherent assumptions of MR analysis necessitate a cautious interpretation of these findings. Future experimental studies are essential to validate and elucidate the precise biological mechanisms underlying the observed association between creatine levels and musculoskeletal health in the elderly.

Our findings suggest that higher creatine levels in the elderly may be associated with a reduced risk of declining hand grip strength. This may reflect the role of creatine in musculoskeletal health, especially in supporting bone and muscle function in the elderly. Furthermore, low hand grip strength in individuals aged 60 and above is considered a key indicator of elderly bone health, closely related to overall skeletal health and function ( [24,25]. Studies have shown that low hand grip strength is significantly associated with decreased bone density, osteoporosis, and an increased risk of fractures ( [26,27].

The discovery of creatine’s impact on hand grip strength in our study aligns with previous research on the effects of creatine on athletic performance and muscle health. For example, a comprehensive review highlighted creatine’s role in improving sport and exercise performance, augmenting exercise training adaptations, and mitigating recovery time in athletes and recreational weightlifters ( [3]. Additionally, creatine supplementation has been shown to enhance muscular strength, power, and endurance, and increase lean body mass in the elderly, as well as improve functional capacity ( [28,29]. Our study adds to this body of evidence by providing new insights into bone health in the elderly. In this respect, recent findings suggest that creatine supplementation, especially when combined with resistance training, can lead to greater adaptations in skeletal muscle than training alone, which is particularly important for elderly ( [30]. These findings are significant in understanding the context of creatine supplement use in the elderly, especially considering the close relationship between bone health and overall health status. However, it is important to consider that hand grip strength can also be influenced by a variety of factors, including but not limited to lifestyle and genetic predispositions, suggesting a multifactorial aspect to this relationship.

This research provides critical insights into musculoskeletal health and the application of creatine supplements among the elderly. However, our study faces several limitations that suggest caution in the generalization of the findings. The predominance of participants of European ancestry may limit the applicability of our results to the global elderly population. Moreover, while the use of GWAS data offers a broad genetic overview, it may not fully capture all interactions affecting creatine levels and musculoskeletal health outcomes. The methodologies used to measure both creatine exposure and health outcomes also introduce potential variability and error.

Given these limitations, although MR suggests some evidence of causality, well-designed randomized controlled trials with large samples are essential to confirm the impact of creatine on grip strength and broader musculoskeletal health. Future studies should not only aim to validate these preliminary findings through rigorous experimental designs but also explore the specific mechanisms by which creatine influences elderly bone health. These investigations should consider how these findings can be applied in clinical practice to enhance bone health and overall health conditions in the elderly.

Conclusions

Utilizing Mendelian Randomization, our study elucidates a notable negative association between creatine levels and low hand grip strength (60 years and older), while associations with other bone health parameters remain statistically non-significant. These findings emphasize the importance of a disorder-specific approach in understanding the implications of creatine supplementation on bone disorders. Further research is necessitated to validate these findings and explore underlying mechanisms.
