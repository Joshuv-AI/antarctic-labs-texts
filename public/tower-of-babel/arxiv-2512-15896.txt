Energy-Energy Correlators in e + ​ e − e^{+}e^{-} and Deep Inelastic Scattering
Yuxun Guo, Werner Vogelsang, Feng Yuan, Wenbin Zhao

Abstract
We study energy-energy correlators (EECs) in e + ​ e − e^{+}e^{-} annihilation and deep inelastic lepton-hadron scattering (DIS), focusing on aspects of nonperturbative physics in these observables. We introduce the EEC jet functions and investigate the infrared (IR) behavior of both small-angle EECs and angle-integrated EECs by performing explicit one-loop calculations. The factorization and universality of the EECs in these processes are demonstrated. A matching scheme is proposed to smoothly connect kinematic regions where different scaling behaviors with jet energy are observed. In combination with the next-to-leading order correction, this matching provides a good description of the EEC data and PYTHIA simulations in high-energy e + ​ e − e^{+}e^{-} annihilation. Predictions for DIS processes for future electron-ion collider kinematics are also presented.

To describe the collinear behavior for the EEC observables, we introduce EEC jet functions, which can be constructed from di-hadron fragmentation functions, summing over all hadrons weighted with their energy fractions. II
EEC Jet Function

For any generic process that produces two “observed” hadrons in the final state, we have the following two types of contributions
57
:

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}\sigma^{(h_{1}h_{2})}}{\text{d}z_{1}\text{d}z_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\int\frac{\text{d}x_{1}}{x_{1}}\frac{\text{d}x_{2}}{x_{2}}\frac{\text{d}\hat{\sigma}_{ij}}{\text{d}x_{1}\text{d}x_{2}}D_{i}^{h_{1}}\left(\frac{z_{1}}{x_{1}}\right)D_{j}^{h_{2}}\left(\frac{z_{2}}{x_{2}}\right)

(2)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}\int\frac{\text{d}x}{x^{2}}\frac{\text{d}\hat{\sigma}_{i}}{\text{d}x}I\!\!D_{i}^{h_{1}h_{2}}\left(\frac{z_{1}}{x},\frac{z_{2}}{x}\right)\ ,

where the first term represents two individual fragmentation processes, for which two partons
i
and
j
, produced via a hard-scattering
cross section
\hat{\sigma}_{ij}
, give rise to the two hadrons through standard single-hadron fragmentation functions
D_{i}^{h}
. For the second term in (
2
)
the pair of hadrons comes from a single parton
i
that is produced with partonic cross section
\hat{\sigma}_{i}
and fragments
according to a di-hadron fragmentation function
I\!\!D_{i}^{h_{1}h_{2}}
. In the above equation,
x_{1,2}
and
x
represent the energy (momentum) fractions
associated with the fragmenting partons. For simplicity, we omit all other kinematic variables and the scale dependence in the fragmentation functions.

If we sum over all hadrons in the final state, we can immediately derive sum rules associated with
momentum conservation in the fragmentation process
83
;
57
,

\displaystyle\int\text{d}zz\sum_{h}D_{i}^{h}(z,\mu)=1\,,

(3)

\displaystyle\int\text{d}z_{1}z_{1}\sum_{h_{1}}I\!\!D_{i}^{h_{1}h_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2};\mu)=(1-z_{2})D_{i}^{h_{2}}(z_{2},\mu)\,.\;\;\;\;

(4)

In particular, the second equation builds a connection between the di-hadron fragmentation functions and the single-hadron ones. The above equations become an important tool when we study the EEC observables.

II.1
Integrated EEC Jet Function

Applying the above idea to the EEC observables, we should also have two contributions. Therefore, a generic EEC observable can be computed from the following di-hadron differential cross section,

\displaystyle\int\text{d}z_{1}\text{d}z_{2}z_{1}z_{2}\frac{\text{d}\sigma(h_{1}h_{2})}{\text{d}z_{1}\text{d}z_{2}}

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ =\int\text{d}z_{1}\text{d}z_{2}z_{1}z_{2}\sum_{ij,h_{1}h_{2}}\hat{\sigma}_{ij}D_{i}^{h_{1}}(z_{1})D_{j}^{h_{2}}(z_{2})

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ +\int\text{d}z_{1}\text{d}z_{2}z_{1}z_{2}\sum_{i,h_{1}h_{2}}\hat{\sigma}_{i}I\!\!D_{i}^{h_{1}h_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2})\ .

(5)

For the EEC observables, we sum over all final-state hadrons. Clearly, the first term can be simplified with just partonic calculations because of the momentum conservation
relation (
3
), and it can be computed in perturbative QCD. However, the second term will inevitably involve nonperturbative physics because of
the residual fragmentation function. To describe this physics, we introduce an integrated EEC jet function:

\Gamma_{i}(\mu)\equiv\sum_{h_{1}h_{2}}\int\text{d}z_{1}\text{d}z_{2}z_{1}z_{2}I\!\!D_{i}^{h_{1}h_{2}}(z_{1},z_{2},\mu)\ .

(6)

According to the momentum sum rule (
4
), the above
\Gamma_{i}(\mu)
can also be simplified as

\Gamma_{i}(\mu)=1-\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}(\mu)\ ,

(7)

where
\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}(\mu)
is the third moment of the single-hadron fragmentation function,

\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}(\mu)=\sum_{h}\int\text{d}zz^{2}D_{i}^{h}(z,\mu)\ .

(8)

We are now ready to derive the evolution equations for the above nonperturbative quantities. These are necessarily related to the (time-like) DGLAP evolution of the fragmentation functions, which are well known to take the following form:

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\log\mu^{2}}\bm{D}^{H}(z,\mu)=\left(\bm{D}^{H}\otimes\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{T}\right)(z,\mu)\ .

(9)

Here we have written the fragmentation functions as flavor-space vectors:
\bm{D}^{H}(z,\mu^{2})\equiv(D_{q}^{H},D_{g}^{H})
, where the singlet quark combination
is defined as
q=\sum_{f}(q_{f}+\bar{q}_{f})
. The time-like DGLAP splitting kernel
\hat{{\bm{P}}}_{T}
is then a matrix in flavor space:

\hat{{\bm{P}}}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{qq}&P_{qg}\\
P_{gq}&P_{gg}\end{pmatrix}\ ,

(10)

where we dropped the
T
subscript for simplicity and implicitly assume a time-like DGLAP kernel hereafter. In Mellin moment space, the splitting kernel
\hat{{\bm{P}}}
is also known as the anomalous dimension matrix,

\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{(N)}\equiv-\int\text{d}x\ x^{N-1}\hat{\bm{P}}(x)\ .

(11)

We define the Mellin moments of the fragmentation functions accordingly as:

\overline{\bm{D}}^{H}(N;\mu)\equiv\int\text{d}z\ z^{N-1}\bm{D}^{H}(z;\mu)\ ,

(12)

whose evolution equations read:

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\log\mu^{2}}\overline{\bm{D}}^{H}(N;\mu)=-\overline{\bm{D}}^{H}(N;\mu)\cdot\hat{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(N)}\ ,

(13)

according to the Mellin convolution theorem. From the relation
\Gamma^{\prime}(\mu)=\overline{\bm{D}}^{H}(N=3;\mu)
it is now obvious that

\begin{split}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\log{\mu^{2}}}\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}(\mu)=-\bm{\Gamma}^{\prime}(\mu)\cdot\hat{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(N=3)}\ .\end{split}

(14)

Combining the above with Eq. (
7
), we find the evolution of
\Gamma_{i}(\mu)
, which contains an inhomogeneous term:

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\log{\mu^{2}}}\bm{\Gamma}(\mu)=-\bm{\Gamma}(\mu)\cdot\hat{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(3)}+\bm{1}\cdot\hat{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{(3)}\ ,

(15)

where the second term is the inhomogeneous contribution. The above evolution can be verified by applying the scale evolution for di-hadron fragmentation functions
57
in Eq. (
6
). In practice, we can solve the evolution equation for
\Gamma^{\prime}
and make predictions for the integrated EEC.

In what follows, we perturbatively expand the anomalous dimensions and splitting kernels as

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\left(\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\right)^{\,n+1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{(N)}_{n},

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{P}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\left(\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\right)^{\,n+1}\hat{\bm{P}}_{n}(x),

(16)

where
\alpha_{s}
is the strong coupling. For simplicity of notation, we refer to the leading-order terms

\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{(N)}_{0}
and
\hat{\bm{P}}_{0}(x)
simply again as

\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{(N)}
and
\hat{\bm{P}}(x)
throughout this paper.

The leading-order anomalous dimensions then read:

\displaystyle\gamma_{qq}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{C_{F}}{2}\left[3+\frac{2}{N(N+1)}-4S_{1}(N)\right]\,,

(17)

\displaystyle\gamma_{gq}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=-C_{F}\frac{N(N+1)+2}{(N-1)N(N+1)}\,,

(18)

\displaystyle\gamma_{qg}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=-2T_{F}n_{f}\frac{N(N+1)+2}{N(N+1)(N+2)}\,,

(19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\gamma_{gg}^{(N)}&=\frac{C_{A}}{2}\Bigg[\frac{4}{N(N+1)}-\frac{12}{(N-1)(N+2)}\\
&\qquad\qquad+4S_{1}(N)\Bigg]-(11C_{A}-4T_{F}n_{f})/6\,,\end{split}

(20)

where
C_{F}=4/3
,
C_{A}=3
,
T_{F}=1/2
and
n_{f}
is the number of active flavors. The harmonic sum
S_{1}(N)\equiv\psi(N+1)+\gamma_{E}
can be expressed in terms of the digamma function
\psi
and the Euler constant
\gamma_{E}
. Specifically for
N=3
, one has

\hat{\bm{\gamma}}^{(3)}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{25}{12}C_{F}&-\frac{7n_{f}}{30}\\[2.84526pt]
-\frac{7}{12}C_{F}&\frac{7}{5}C_{A}+\frac{1}{3}n_{f}\end{pmatrix}\ .

(21)

This matrix has two positive eigenvalues, approximately 6.14 and 2.51 for
N_{c}=3
and
n_{f}=5
, indicating that asymptotically
\Gamma_{q,g}^{\prime}(\mu)\to 0
and therefore
\Gamma_{q,g}(\mu)=1-\Gamma_{q,g}^{\prime}(\mu)\to 1
in the
\mu\to\infty
limit.

On the other hand, in the nonperturbative region, i.e.,
b_{T}\gg 1/\Lambda_{\rm QCD}
, it has been realized that the scaling of the EEC has a different behavior
41
. We will show in the following that this observation is consistent with the above one-loop result and the evolution equation. In this regime, to illustrate the scaling behavior in the infrared region, we can Fourier transform the evolution equation back to momentum space,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\Gamma}_{q}(\mu,q_{T})}{\partial\ln\mu^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\int{dx}x^{4}\left\{\hat{P}_{qq}(x){\Gamma}_{q}(\mu,xq_{T})\right.

(35)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \left.+\hat{P}_{gq}(x){\Gamma}_{g}(\mu,x{q_{T}})\right\}\ .

Taking the small-
q_{T}
limit, we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\Gamma}_{q}(\mu,0)}{\partial\ln\mu^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\int{dx}x^{4}\left\{\hat{P}_{qq}(x){\Gamma}_{q}(\mu,0)\right.

(36)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \left.+\hat{P}_{gq}(x){\Gamma}_{g}(\mu,0)\right\}\ .

This leads to the renormalization group equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\Gamma}_{q}(\mu,0)}{\partial\ln\mu^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\left({\Gamma}_{q}(\mu,0)\gamma_{qq}^{(5)}+{\Gamma}_{g}(\mu,0)\gamma_{gq}^{(5)}\right)\ ,\;\;\;

(37)

which is consistent with the result in Ref.
41
. Furthermore, we can also expand Eq. (
35
) at small
q_{T}
in terms of powers
(q_{T}^{2})^{n}
. We note that in the Fourier inverse of the evolution equation (
32
) from
b_{T}
- to
q_{T}
-space odd powers of
q_{T}
vanish.
A scaling behavior follows for each contribution with
(q_{T}^{2})^{n}
to the expansion of
\Gamma_{q}(\mu,q_{T})
, with an associated
anomalous dimension
\gamma_{ij}^{(5+2n)}
. This is again consistent with Ref.
41
.

It is important to note that in the perturbative region, i.e.,
q_{T}\gg\Lambda_{\rm QCD}
, the unintegrated EEC jet function has a power behavior
\Gamma_{q}(\mu,q_{T})\sim 1/q_{T}^{2}
. Substituting this into Eq. (
35
), we arrive at the same conclusion as that in Eq. (
34
), i.e., the anomalous dimension is
\gamma_{q}^{(3)}
for large
q_{T}
, instead of the
\gamma^{(5)}
for
q_{T}\to 0
.

In principle, one should be able to solve the above evolution equation with a certain initial condition, e.g.,
\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}(\mu=\mu_{b},b_{T})\approx\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}(\mu_{b})\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{\rm NP}(b_{T})
, where
\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}(\mu_{b})
is the integrated EEC jet function at scale
\mu=\mu_{b}
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{\rm NP}(b_{T})
a nonperturbative model in
b_{T}
-space. This strategy follows the common procedure in TMD phenomenology
39
. We expect that one can explore a number of methods to solve the evolution equation, especially an iterative method as used in Ref.
71
. The solution has to respect both constraints discussed above. We will come back to this in a future publication. In the following, we will make use of the above analytic insights to model the EEC jet functions with all-order resummation included.

II.3
Resummation and Matching Between Different Scaling Regions

Clearly, the EEC jet functions have different scaling behaviors in the perturbative and nonperturbative regions. This indicates it may not be possible to obtain a universal solution for their evolution equations, and a matching between the perturbative and nonperturbative regions will be necessary. A numerical solution is still possible for the evolution equations. In the following, we will discuss how to improve the LLA solution with additional constraints from the scaling in the nonperturbative region by performing such a matching.

The LLA solution for the evolution takes the following form:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{\rm LLA}(\mu,b_{T})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{j}(\mu_{b})\text{P}{\exp\left[-\int_{\mu_{b}^{2}}^{\mu^{2}}\frac{d\mu^{\prime 2}}{\mu^{\prime 2}}\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime})}{2\pi}\gamma^{(3)}(\mu^{\prime})\right]_{ji}}\ ,

(38)

where
P
denotes path ordering, and we parameterize the EEC jet function at the lower scale
\mu_{b}

by the integrated EEC jet function
\Gamma_{j}
of Sec.
II.1
, also at scale
\mu_{b}
. When Fourier transformed back to
q_{T}
-space, the above resummation formula will encounter the nonperturbative region. We follow the so-called
b_{*}
prescription
49
,
b_{T}\to b_{*}=b_{T}/\sqrt{1+b_{T}^{2}/b_{\textrm{max}}^{2}}
, choosing
b_{\textrm{max}}=1.5\ \rm GeV^{-1}
. With that, we also include a nonperturbative contribution. Our final resummation result for
\widetilde{\Gamma}
reads

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i,\textrm{res}}^{\rm LLA}(\mu,b_{T})=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{\rm LLA}(\mu,b_{*})e^{-\Lambda_{i}b_{T}}\ ,

(39)

where the
\Lambda_{i}
(
i=q,g
) are free parameters that are fitted to experimental data. Following Refs.
24
;
71
, we have adopted a simple exponential form for the nonperturbative part of the EEC jet functions. With the above result, we obtain the LLA unintegrated EEC jet function in transverse-momentum space,

\Gamma_{i}^{\rm LLA}(\mu,q_{T})=\int\frac{d^{2}b_{T}}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{iq_{T}\cdot b_{T}}\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i,\textrm{res}}^{\rm LLA}(\mu,b_{T})\ .

(40)

However, as mentioned above, at low transverse momentum the scaling behavior changes.
In order to improve the prediction, we introduce a modified hard factor, defined by

\begin{split}{\cal C}_{ji}(\mu)=\sum_{k}&\,\text{P}\exp\left[\int_{\mu_{0}^{2}}^{\mu^{2}}\frac{d\mu^{\prime 2}}{\mu^{\prime 2}}\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime})}{2\pi}\gamma^{(3)}(\mu^{\prime})\right]_{jk}\\
&\times\text{P}\exp\left[-\int_{\mu_{0}^{2}}^{\mu^{2}}\frac{d\mu^{\prime 2}}{\mu^{\prime 2}}\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu^{\prime})}{2\pi}\gamma^{(5)}(\mu^{\prime})\right]_{ki}\ ,\end{split}

(41)

where
\mu_{0}
is a parameter that represents the scale where the behavior for
q_{T}\to 0

41
takes over.
In the range
50
GeV to
1
TeV we observe a decrease by about
20\%
for
{\cal C}_{qq}(\mu)
from this formula. This effect needs to be taken into account for performing precision calculations.

In addition, we also need to adopt a matching scheme, linking the low-

(

(

\Lambda
is the parameter defining the nonperturbative part of the EEC jet function introduced above. A simple matching can be achieved as follows,

\Gamma_{i}^{\textrm{match}}(\mu,q_{T})=\Gamma_{j}^{\rm LLA}(\mu,q_{T})\left(1+\left({\cal C}_{ji}(\mu)-1\right)e^{-{q_{T}^{2}\over\Lambda_{i}^{2}}}\right)\ ,

(42)

with the same parameter
\Lambda_{i}
as used in the nonperturbative part in Eq. (

). In Fig.

as functions of

.

\Gamma^{\prime}(\mu_{\rm init})

\mu_{\rm{init}}=2

\Gamma^{\prime}_{q}(\mu_{\rm init})=0.246
and

\Gamma_{i}(\mu_{\rm init})=1-\Gamma^{\prime}_{i}(\mu_{\rm init})=(0.754,0.824)
. We have also chosen
\Lambda_{g}\approx\Lambda_{q}=4.37
GeV, which gives a reasonable description of the near-side EECs in

\gamma^{(3)}

III
EECs in
e^{+}e^{-}
Annihilation

EECs in
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation were first studied in the classic papers by Basham
et al.

30
;
31
;
29
. In order to study the angular integrated EECs and small-angle EECs, we follow the formalism developed for hadron production in
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation, for which factorization in terms of fragmentation functions has been shown
13
. Our goal is to keep the IR behavior for these observables and demonstrate factorization in terms of the EEC jet functions introduced in the previous section.

III.1
Integrated EECs

Let us start with the differential cross sections for inclusive-hadron and hadron pair productions in
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation, which have been computed in perturbation theory
previously, see, e.g., Refs.
13
;
57
. For single-hadron production, we have

\frac{d\sigma(e^{+}e^{-}\to h+X)}{dz}=\sigma_{0}\int_{z}\frac{dx}{x}D_{i}^{h}(z/x,\mu)H_{i}(x,\mu)\ ,

(43)

where
z
is the fraction of the virtual photon’s momentum
q
carried by the hadron and as before
D_{i}^{h}(z,\mu)
represents the fragmentation function for parton
i
to hadron
h
. In the above equation,
\sigma_{0}
is the leading-order cross section and
H_{i}
is the partonic one. Up to one-loop order, we write

\displaystyle H_{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(1-x)+\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{q}(x)\ ,

(44)

\displaystyle H_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{g}(x)\ ,

(45)

where
C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{q}
and
C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{g}
are listed in the Appendix for convenience.

From the above, we can derive the one-point energy correlator,

\Sigma_{1}=\sum_{i}\frac{E_{i}}{Q}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}}\int dz\frac{1}{2}z\sum_{h}\frac{d\sigma^{h}}{dz}\ ,

(46)

where
d\sigma^{h}
is a short-hand notation for
d\sigma(e^{+}e^{-}\to h+X)
.
By applying energy conservation of the fragmentation functions,
\int dz\sum_{h}D_{i}^{h}(z,\mu)\equiv 1
, it has long been realized that the above
\Sigma_{1}\equiv 1
as well. This can be easily checked with the following results,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}=\sigma_{0}\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{s}}{\pi}\right)\ ,

(47)

\displaystyle\int dxC^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{q}(x)=2\ ,

(48)

\displaystyle\int dxx\left(C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{q}(x)+C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{g}(x)\right)=2\ .

(49)

We now turn to two-particle productions, where the differential cross section contains two components,

\displaystyle\frac{d\sigma^{h_{1}h_{2}}}{dz_{1}dz_{2}}=\left.\frac{d\sigma^{h_{1}h_{2}}}{dz_{1}dz_{2}}\right|_{\mathrm{hom.}}+\left.\frac{d\sigma^{h_{1}h_{2}}}{dz_{1}dz_{2}}\right|_{\mathrm{inhom.}}\ ,

(50)

associated with homogeneous di-hadron fragmentation and two separate individual fragmentations, respectively.
The homogeneous term takes the same form as above for single-particle production,

\left.\frac{d\sigma^{h_{1}h_{2}}}{dz_{1}dz_{2}}\right|_{\mathrm{hom.}}=\sigma_{0}\int_{z_{1}+z_{2}}\frac{dx}{x^{2}}I\!\!D_{i}^{h_{1}h_{2}}\left(\frac{z_{1}}{x},\frac{z_{2}}{x},\mu\right)H_{i}(x,\mu)\ ,

(51)

where
I\!\!D_{i}^{h_{1}h_{2}}(\frac{z_{1}}{x},\frac{z_{2}}{x},\mu)
is again the di-hadron fragmentation function, and
H_{i}
has been defined above.

The integrated two-point EEC is calculated from the above differential cross section as

\Sigma_{2}\equiv\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{E_{i}E_{j}}{Q^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}}\int dz_{1}dz_{2}\frac{1}{2}z_{1}z_{2}\sum_{h_{1},h_{2}}\frac{d\sigma^{h_{1}h_{2}}}{dz_{1}dz_{2}}\ .

(52)

At the leading order, we have

\Sigma_{2}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\Gamma_{q}(Q)\right)\ ,

(53)

where the first term comes from the inhomogeneous contribution and the second from the homogeneous one.

From the coefficients listed in the Appendix, we can also compute the one-loop corrections. First, the homogeneous term is easy to evaluate, we have

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}^{\textrm{hom.}(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{0}}{2\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\left[-\left(-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}+\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\gamma_{qq}^{(3)}\Gamma_{q}(\mu)\right.

(54)

\displaystyle-\left(-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}+\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\gamma_{gq}^{(3)}\Gamma_{g}(\mu)

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{131}{12}\Gamma_{q}(\mu)-\frac{71}{36}\Gamma_{g}(\mu)\right]\ ,

where
1/\hat{\epsilon}\equiv 1/\epsilon+\log(4\pi)-\gamma_{E}
. The first two terms contain collinear divergences that are related to the renormalization of
\Gamma_{q}(\mu)
at one-loop order. The last line represents the NLO corrections, and the coefficients are obtained from
\int dxx^{2}C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{q,g}(x)
, respectively.

Similarly, we also compute the inhomogeneous term, which has a collinear divergence as well:

\Sigma_{2}^{\textrm{inhom.}(1)}=\frac{\sigma_{0}}{2\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}C_{F}\left[-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}\frac{3}{2}+\frac{3}{2}\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}-\frac{89}{24}\right]\ .

(55)

The above is derived from the two-parton differential cross sections; for details, see the Appendix. In particular, in dimensional regularization, the collinear divergence comes from the following angular distribution at small angle:

\left.\frac{d\Sigma_{2}}{d\zeta}\right|_{\zeta\to 0}=\frac{\sigma_{0}}{2\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}C_{F}N_{\epsilon}\left[\frac{3}{2}\frac{1}{\zeta^{1+\epsilon}}+{\rm finite\penalty\ term}\right]\ ,

(56)

where
\zeta=(1-\cos\theta)/2
with
\theta
the angle between the two hadrons. Furthermore,
N_{\epsilon}=\frac{\Gamma(1+\epsilon)\Gamma^{2}(1-\epsilon)}{\Gamma(1-2\epsilon)}(\frac{Q^{2}}{4\pi\mu^{2}})^{-\epsilon}
. The “finite term” part contains the regular contribution when
\zeta\to 0
, including both real and virtual pieces. Clearly, the
1/\zeta
behavior corresponds to a collinear divergence that needs to be taken care of by proper renormalization of the inhomogeneous part of
\Gamma_{q}(Q)
at this order. The final result for
\Sigma_{2}
can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}}{\sigma_{0}}\Sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\left(-\frac{89}{24}\right)C_{F}\right)

(57)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\Gamma_{q}(Q)+\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\left[\frac{131}{12}\Gamma_{q}(Q)-\frac{71}{36}\Gamma_{g}(Q)\right]\right)\ .

At one-loop order,
\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}=\sigma_{0}(1+\alpha_{s}/\pi)
(we note that
\sigma_{0}/\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}
has been calculated perturbatively up to NNLO
80
). It is interesting to note that asymptotically,
Q^{2}\to\infty
, we have
\Gamma_{q}(Q)\sim\Gamma_{g}(Q)\to 1
and
\Sigma_{2}\to 1
. This provides an important cross check of our above results, in particular, for the inhomogeneous contributions.

, we show the comparison between the above LO and NLO results to PYTHIA8 simulations for the integrated EEC in

We observe striking overall agreement between NLO theory and PYTHIA8, which becomes excellent as soon as

GeV.

III.2
Near-Side EEC

Focusing on the near-side EEC, we have, at the leading order,

\left.2\frac{d\Sigma_{2}^{(0)}}{\pi Q^{2}d\zeta}\right|_{\zeta\to 0}=\Gamma_{q}^{(0)}(\mu,q_{T})\ ,

(58)

where
q_{T}^{2}=\zeta Q^{2}
, again with
\zeta=(1-\cos(\theta))/2\approx\theta^{2}/4
at small angle. The quark carries energy
E_{q}=\overline{Q}=Q/2
, so that
q_{T}=2E_{q}\sqrt{\zeta}
. Equation (
58
) defines the unintegrated EEC jet function
\Gamma_{q}^{(0)}(\mu,q_{T})
, describing the distribution in opening angle
\theta\propto q_{T}
for di-hadron fragmentation. At small angle the unintegrated EEC jet function is nonperturbative. Its normalization is given by

\Gamma_{q}^{(0)}(\mu)=\int d^{2}q_{T}\Gamma_{q}^{(0)}(\mu,q_{T})\ ,

(59)

where the left side is the integrated EEC jet function. Following the same strategy as for single-inclusive hadron production in
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation
13
, one can compute one-loop corrections for the near-side EEC from the EEC jet function. A similar factorization like Eq. (
43
) will follow,

\left.2\frac{d\Sigma_{2}^{e^{+}e^{-}}}{\pi Q^{2}d\zeta}\right|_{\zeta\to 0}={\sigma_{0}\over\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}}\int{dx}{x^{4}}H_{i}\left(x,{Q\over\mu}\right)\Gamma_{i}\left(\mu,q_{T}\right)\ ,

(60)

where
E_{i}=x\overline{Q}
and
q_{T}^{2}=\zeta x^{2}Q^{2}
. Again, we make sure the EEC is properly normalized. In terms of the Fourier transform to
b_{T}
space we have

\displaystyle\left.2\frac{d\Sigma_{2}^{e^{+}e^{-}}}{\pi Q^{2}d\zeta}\right|_{\zeta\to 0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sigma_{0}\over\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}}\int{dx}{x^{2}}H_{i}\left(x,{Q\over\mu}\right)

(61)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int\frac{b_{T}d\bar{b}_{T}}{(2\pi)}J_{0}({\sqrt{\zeta}Q\bar{b}_{T}})\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}\left(\mu,{\bar{b}_{T}\over x}\right)\ ,\;\;\;\;\;\;

where the EEC jet function in
b_{T}
space is defined as

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}(\mu,b_{T})=\pi\int dq_{T}^{2}J_{0}(q_{T}b_{T})\Gamma_{i}(\mu,q_{T})\ .

(62)

Using this definition and rearranging Eq. (
60
), we obtain the
b_{T}
-space factorization formula

\left.2\frac{d\Sigma_{2}^{e^{+}e^{-}}}{\pi Q^{2}d\zeta}\right|_{\zeta\to 0}={\sigma_{0}\over\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}}\int\frac{b_{T}db_{T}}{(2\pi)}J_{0}({\sqrt{\zeta}Qb_{T}})\widetilde{\Sigma}_{2}(Q,b_{T})\ ,

(63)

where

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{2}(Q,b_{T})\equiv\int{dx}{x^{2}}H_{i}\left(x,{Q\over\mu}\right)\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}\left(\mu,{b_{T}\over x}\right)\ .

(64)

The leading order is simple since
H_{q}^{(0)}(x)=\delta(1-x)
, and we have

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{2}^{(0)}(Q,b_{T})=\widetilde{\Gamma}^{(0)}_{q}(Q,b_{T})\ .

(65)

At one-loop order, we need to take into account real and virtual contributions. As before, we can again classify these contributions into homogeneous and inhomogeneous ones. For the homogeneous contribution, we can make use of the known results for single-parton differential cross sections. For example, for the quark EEC contribution we integrate out the phase space of the radiated gluon and antiquark. The differential cross section for the quark and gluon distributions in
e^{+}e^{-}\to q\bar{q}g
can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d\sigma^{q}}{dx}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\left[\left(-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}+\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\hat{P}_{qq}(x)+C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{q}(x)\right],\;\;\;\;\;\;

(66)

\displaystyle\frac{d\sigma^{g}}{dx}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\left[\left(-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}+\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\hat{P}_{gq}(x)+C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{g}(x)\right].\;\;\;\;\;\;

(67)

From this, we find for the EEC at small angle,

\displaystyle\left.2\frac{d\Sigma_{2}^{(1)}}{\pi Q^{2}d\zeta}\right|_{\zeta\to 0}={\sigma_{0}\over\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\int dxx^{4}

(68)

\displaystyle\times\left\{\Gamma_{q}^{(0)}(\mu,q_{T})\left[\left(-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}+\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\hat{P}_{qq}(x)+C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{q}(x)\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\Gamma_{g}^{(0)}(\mu,q_{T})\left[\left(-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}+\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\hat{P}_{gq}(x)+C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{g}(x)\right]\right\}\ .

Fourier transforming the above to
b_{T}
-space, we obtain the homogeneous contribution to
\widetilde{\Sigma}_{2}
at one-loop order,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Sigma}_{2}^{\textrm{hom.}(1)}=\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\int dxx^{2}

(69)

\displaystyle\times\left\{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{q}^{(0)}(\mu,{b_{T}\over x})\left[\left(-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}+\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\hat{P}_{qq}(x)+C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{q}(x)\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}^{(0)}(\mu,{b_{T}\over x})\left[\left(-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}+\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\hat{P}_{gq}(x)+C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{g}(x)\right]\right\}\ .

For the inhomogeneous contribution two partons are close to each other and each of them separately fragments into a hadron; see previous subsection.
We have

\left.2\frac{d\Sigma_{2}^{(1)}}{\pi Q^{2}d\zeta}\right|_{\zeta\to 0}={\sigma_{0}\over\sigma_{\textrm{tot}}}\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\left(\gamma_{qq}^{(3)}+\gamma_{gq}^{(3)}\right)\frac{1}{\zeta^{1+\epsilon}}\ .

(70)

It is again convenient to express the final result in Fourier transform
b_{T}
-space,

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{2}^{\textrm{inhom.}(1)}=\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\left(\gamma_{qq}^{(3)}+\gamma_{gq}^{(3)}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}+\ln\frac{\mu_{b}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\ .

(71)

Adding the homogeneous and inhomogeneous contributions, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Sigma}_{2,\textrm{tot}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\left\{\int dxx^{2}\left(-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}+\ln\frac{Q^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\left[\widetilde{\Gamma}_{q}^{(0)}\left(\mu,{b_{T}\over x}\right)\right.\right.

(72)

\displaystyle\times\hat{P}_{qq}(x)\left.+\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}^{(0)}\left(\mu,{b_{T}\over x}\right)\hat{P}_{gq}(x)\right]

\displaystyle+\left.\left(\gamma_{qq}^{(3)}+\gamma_{gq}^{(3)}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{\hat{\epsilon}}+\ln\frac{\mu_{b}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\int dxx^{2}\left[\widetilde{\Gamma}_{q}^{(0)}\left(\mu,{b_{T}\over x}\right)C_{q}^{e^{+}e^{-}}(x)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\widetilde{\Gamma}_{g}^{(0)}\left(\mu,{b_{T}\over x}\right)C_{g}^{e^{+}e^{-}}(x)\right]\ ,

where the first term is just part of the one-loop result for
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{q}^{(1)}(Q,b_{T})
in Eq. (
30
) of Sec.
II.2
.
The above result demonstrates the following factorization formula at one-loop order:

\widetilde{\Sigma}_{2}^{(1)}(Q,b_{T})=\int dxx^{2}\left[H_{q}^{(0)}\widetilde{\Gamma}_{q}^{(1)}+H_{i}^{(1)}(x)\widetilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{(0)}\right]\ ,

(73)

with the hard coefficients

\displaystyle H_{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(1-x)+\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{q}(x)\ ,

(74)

\displaystyle H_{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}C^{e^{+}e^{-}}_{g}(x)\ .

(75)

To simplify the above expressions, we have set the factorization scale to
\mu=Q
. The complete scale dependence can be restored from the above one-loop results (
72
) for the factorization formula (
64
). It is important to note that the scale dependence is the same as that for single particle production in
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation.

To carry out resummation of logarithms in
b_{T}
, we can apply the resummed
EEC jet functions
\Gamma_{i}
derived in Sec.
II.3
, for instance within LLA in Eq. (
39
):

\widetilde{\Sigma}^{\textrm{res,LLA}}_{2}(Q,b_{T})=\int dxx^{2}{\bf H}\left(x,{Q\over\mu}\right)\cdot{\bf\Gamma}_{\textrm{res}}^{\textrm{LLA}}\left(\mu,{b_{T}\over x}\right)\ .

(76)

As shown in Ref.
68
, in LLA, the above result will lead to the same asymptotic expansion as given in Ref.
62
. With hard factors computed at one-loop order, we should be able to expand the resummation result beyond the LLA, where additional contributions have to be taken into account including full evolutions at NNLL, following the
lines in Ref.
62
. We will come back to this in a future publication.

III.3
Comparison to Experimental Data

Applying the factorization result derived above with the improved EEC jet function from the previous section, we obtain the final result for the EEC at small angle in
e^{+}e^{-}

EECs in Deep Inelastic Scattering

Lepton-nucleon scattering offers further exciting opportunities for studying EECs. We devote this section to EEC observables in DIS processes. Because EECs are constructed from hadrons in the final state, we can base our analysis on the well established semi-inclusive hadron production in DIS (SIDIS),

V
Conclusion

We have investigated energy–energy correlators (EECs) in electron–positron annihilation and deep-inelastic scattering. Focusing on perturbative corrections to the correlators, we have derived NLO expressions for them. Although the EECs are infrared safe at fixed angles, integrating over the angle introduces infrared sensitivity related to di-hadron fragmentation functions. To handle the collinear limit, we have introduced EEC jet functions constructed from these fragmentation functions, which turn out to be universal across the two different processes we have considered. The scale dependence of the (integrated) jet functions is surprisingly mild, which is primarily due to momentum-conservation properties of the underlying fragmentation functions.

Our analysis includes explicit one-loop calculations and the derivation of evolution equations in transverse-coordinate
b_{T}
space, enabling resummation of logarithms in
b_{T}
for the unintegrated EEC jet functions. The resummation for the unintegrated EEC jet functions can be derived analytically for two distinct kinematic regions. In the perturbative regime (
1/b_{T}\sim q_{T}\gg\Lambda_{\rm QCD}
), we can apply the leading logarithmic approximation, and the scaling behavior is controlled by the relevant anomalous dimension
\gamma_{ij}^{(3)}
. However, in the non-perturbative post-confinement region (
q_{T}\sim 0
), the scaling behavior is controlled by a different anomalous dimension,
\gamma_{ij}^{(5)}
. Observing this difference, we have proposed a matching between the two regions, which smoothly connects the perturbative and nonperturbative regimes.

With only one parameter determining the matching point, our proposed scheme provides a very good description of PYTHIA simulations for the EECs in
e^{+}e^{-}
annihilation over a wide range of energies. Sizable NLO corrections are also found in these comparisons, calling for future computations of even higher-order corrections. Meanwhile, it is also important to solve the full evolution equation. Further, more detailed phenomenological
studies will be needed in order to assess the opportunities and potential that the future EIC presents for the study of EECs.
