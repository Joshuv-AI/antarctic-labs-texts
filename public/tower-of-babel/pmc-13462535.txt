Inferring genetic values and variances from pre-processed phenotypes
Authors: Daniel Gianola, Olga Ravagnolo, Chris-Carolin Schoen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It is common in quantitative genetics to submit phenotypes to pre-processing prior to data analysis. The objective is to make computations simpler. In areas where field data is observational, e.g., studies of complex traits, records are often pre-adjusted for nuisance factors such as location, demographical structure and macroenvironments, that may mask expression of genetic values. The nuisance effects may be estimated by ordinary or generalized least-squares. We studied consequences of ignoring pre-correction of nuisance (fixed) effects using a mixed linear model framework supplemented by analyses of real and synthetic plant and animal breeding data sets. Pre-correction induces changes in model rank and sometimes distorts inferences. We also examined prediction of random effects with pre-corrected data in situations with dense or sparse information on the levels of fixed effects. Variance component analyses with ANOVA, minimum norm quadratic unbiased estimation), maximum likelihood and Bayesian methods were also dealt with. A Braford cattle data set with birth weight of calves as response variable illustrated effects on prediction of phenotypes. Our main conclusions are: 1) the impact of ignoring pre-correction depends on model complexity relative to sample size. 2) Pre-processing must be used with caution in inference as it may destroy data structure of the data and obscure the understanding of a problem under investigation.

Introduction

In quantitative genetics phenotypes are often submitted to some pre-processing prior to data analysis. An example is that of “pre-correcting” body weights of calves by the age of their mother (e.g.,1) prior to estimation of heritability or genetic correlations. Likewise, gene expression and high-throughput RNA measurements are typically “normalized” (e.g.,2), i.e., centering or re-scaling aimed at removing extraneous variability such as batch effects, previous to serious analysis. Typically, the objective is to make post-processing computations simpler. In areas where field data is observational, e.g., as in study of complex traits, records are often pre-adjusted for nuisance factors, e.g., location, demographic structure and macro-environmental effects, that mask expression of genetic values. The nuisance effects may be estimated by ordinary (OLS) or generalized (GLS) least-squares. Subsequently, estimation of variance components and breeding values proceeds using the adjusted records as pseudo-phenotypes. Examples are traits such as “residual feed intake” in cattle. Here, feed consumption records are adjusted by metabolic body weight, number of days in lactation, body weight change during lactation, number of days, etc. First, a linear regression of feed intake on these variables is fitted. Second, regression residuals become pseudo-phenotypes and are analyzed with a mixed linear model as if the distributions of random variables were unaffected by pre-correction (e.g.,3).

Another example is when a mixed effects linear model with similarity among individuals defined by a relationship matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\mathbf{A})$$\end{document} produces pedigree-based best linear unbiased predictions (ABLUP) of breeding values. These ABLUPs are used as pseudo-phenotypes in a subsequent analysis where a genomic relationship matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{G}$$\end{document}4 is employed to obtain genomic breeding value predictions (GBLUP). The pseudo-phenotypes are assumed free of nuisance effects, presumably eliminated in the pedigree-based analysis. However, their distribution depends on the values of genetic and environmental (co) variance components used for computing ABLUP. To mitigate such dependence, a complicated procedure called “de-regression”5 is carried out prior to genome-enabled prediction

Duenk and Bijma6 studied how pre-correction affected the quality of variance component estimates. Using simulation and simple models, they detected a bias dependent on the scenario considered. Simulations studies consider narrow circumstances that are seldom representative of the unbalanced data structures of animal breeding or of incomplete designs in plant breeding. In theory, the distributions of random variables in a linear model differ between pre-corrected and raw data7,8. As statistical specifications and data structure become increasingly intricate, the more difficult the evaluation of impacts of pre-correction on inference is.

We examined consequences of ignoring pre-correction of nuisance (fixed) effects using a mixed linear model framework supplemented by analyses of real or synthetic plant and animal breeding data sets. Section “Models with and without pre-correction” discusses how pre-correction induces changes in model rank. Section “Pre-correction and BLUP” addresses BLUP with pre-corrected data and presents examples of situations with dense or sparse information on the levels of fixed effects. This section also studies a setting with a non-random association between breeding values and the levels of a nuisance factor. Section “Pre-correction and variance components” takes on variance component estimation. After a toy start with ANOVA, MINQUE (minimum norm quadratic unbiased estimation), maximum likelihood and Bayesian methods are discussed. A synthetic maize example illustrates REML (“residual” maximum likelihood) with univariate and bivariate models fitted to raw or pre-corrected data. A Braford cattle data set with birth weight of calves as response variable illustrates a Bayesian treatment. The impact of ignoring pre-correction on inferences of variance components and heritability and on predictive ability of models that are mis-specified for an important random factor are discussed. The paper ends with a brief Conclusions section. Some technical details are in Appendices 1–3.

Example 2: sparse information on fixed effects

A model with 100 fixed effects, 100 contemporary groups and 595 individual genomic breeding values was simulated. Each subject had one record of performance so N=595. True values of fixed effects were \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta }^{\prime }=\left( 0.1,0.2,...,9.9,10.0\right)$$\end{document} and contemporary group and residual effects were drawn at random from N(0, 4) and N(0, 5), respectively. The problem will be examined later on in the paper.

Pre-correction and variance components

Likelihood-based estimates of dispersion parameters in models with two or more random effects are asymptotically unbiased, but possess finite sample bias. Pre-correction accentuate the bias unless it is accounted for. To study bias stemming from pre-correction, estimators must have closed forms, which are not available when likelihood or Bayesian inference is adopted.

Bias can be evaluated for methods based on quadratic forms, however. To be useful in quantitative genetic, methods must accommodate unbalanced data, mixed models and settings where the number of levels of random effects exceeds sample size, e.g., when genomic markers are model inputs. Such requirement precludes Henderson’s18 methods 1 (ANOVA) for random models and method 3 for mixed models.

Rao8,19 proposed variance component estimation by minimizing weighted distances between quadratic forms on phenotypes and quadratic form on random effects (e.g., contemporary or genetic values). MINQUE (minimum norm quadratic unbiased estimation) is unbiased by construction: quadratics are equated to their expectations and the estimating equations are linear in the variances. There is no unique MINQUE because calculation of estimates requires choosing some positive arbitrary weights, and all unbiased for any choice of positive weights. It does not require normality.

We take on ANOVA with a simple balanced model as a canonical example, followed by consideration of MINQUE.

Example 4: REML with simulated maize data

The R “sommer” package21,22 has a simulated data set from crosses between 20 maize inbred lines in each of two heterotic groups. Example 5: Bayesian and predictive analysis of birth weight in Braford cattle

Data. The data was provided to INIA by “Sociedad de Criadores de Braford y Cebú”, Uruguay. Birth weight of calves was the target trait. The original data file had 5086 animals. Records were kept if: (1) “commercial” animals); (2) calvings from 1997-2022, August-December (spring calving), and mothers born August-December; (3) dams between 24 and 120 months of age at calving; (4) calves with sire, dam and sex recorded, and (5) birth weights 20-50 kg. After edits, N=3853 birth weights of calves from 68 sires and 1505 dams with records remained. Progeny group sizes were 1-86 calves per bull (median=56) and 1-9 calves per cow (median=2). Management groups were formed from an informal clustering of herds; groups were combined into a single factor with 15 levels. The number of calves per management level was 13-2550; the level with the most calves represented grazing in pasture, natural or with some improvement.

Accommodating such effects requires estimating two genetic variances and their genetic covariance, plus additional refinements (e.g.,23,24). Our data does not allow precise estimation of the needed parameters so a simpler model was adapted, sufficient for illustrating impact of pre-correction.

In “Random layout”, the two partitions are representative of the entire data set and predictions generalize to the conceptual population. This setting, however, is not suited to plant and animal breeding because populations undergo genetic change due to selection. i.e., there is genetic trend. Past information is used to predict performance in a future population that may differ genetically and also possibly subject to changing environmental circumstances. To mimic the situation in animal breeding, in the “Year of birth layout”, the N=3853 records divided into arbitrarily chosen disjoint sets: animals born on or before 2014, and those born in 2015-2022. The first set was used for training \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( N_{train}=2922\right)$$\end{document} and the second set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( N_{test}=931\right)$$\end{document} for model testing. Computations as in the “Random layout”, with 20000 bootstrap samples of prediction-predictand test set pairs. Prediction was worse than in the “Random layout”, with lower correlations and larger prediction errors. Mean absolute prediction errors in the “Year of birth layout” ranged from 4.0 to 4.6 kg (Fig. 10), versus 3.5–3.6 kg in the “Random layout” (Fig. 9), highlighting the dependence of prediction ability on testing design. When using the benchmark model, the predictive correlation (see green vertical bar in Fig. 10) was -0.02 and mean absolute prediction error was 4.3 kg, i.e., no marked difference with any of the three mis-specified models.

In a nutshell, differences in inference between the three mis-specified models and the benchmark model (i.e.,with a permanent environmental effect of a dam) did not translate into detectable differences in predictive performance. These results are consistent with expectations: the rank of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{X}$$\end{document} was much smaller than N, so the alteration in distributions produced by pre-processing was mild. The relatively better performance of the random effects model corroborates a common fact in machine learning (e.g.,29): simple models often outperform more complex specifications. The latter consume more information from the data at hand and may be less robust against violation of assumptions or sample idiosyncrasies.

Conclusion

This paper studied impacts on inference of variance components, estimation of breeding values and predictive ability from using a type of pre-processing of phenotypes often applied in animal breeding prior to genetic analysis. For simplicity, phenotypes are often pre-adjusted using estimates of fixed effects of nuisance factors, with the ultimate objective being estimation of genetic parameters, prediction of breeding values or out-of-sample prediction. An ideal situation is when all pertinent effects are accounted for in a single-step model. In spite of significant advances in methods for single-step analysis (e.g.,30,31) the type of pre-correction mentioned above is not uncommon (e.g.,6).

We studied pre-correction with OLS because the algebra is simpler than with GLS. In BLUP, accounting for the OLS-based correction properly produces estimates of of random effects that are equal to those resulting from a mixed model (including the nuisances) fitted to the raw data. We used analysis as much as possible. First, variance component estimation was examined. The canonical one-way balanced layout (between and within-family components), showed that the covariance structure is modified via an introduction of correlations that do not exist in the raw data. Variance estimates from using pre-corrected data naïvely have an intrinsic bias with respect to the target estimands. The bias dissipates as the number of levels of random effects and the number of observations per level increases. Such “baby” model, however, does not guide as to what happens in a mixed model with unbalanced data and in situations where the number of levels of some random effects exceeds sample size. To that effect, we examined MINQUE, a procedure based on quadratic forms that does not require distributional assumptions. A naïve analysis yielded incorrect inferences on the meaning, amount and partition of the total variance, and of derived parameters such as heritability.

Subsequently, we argued using heuristic reasoning that maximum likelihood and Bayesian estimates from analyses ignoring pre-correction typically understate point estimates and overstate precision relative to a correct analysis, thus giving incorrect inference.

Since it is difficult arriving at a general picture that addresses all scenarios and mixed model specifications, case studies were employed to obtain insights. Using “pseudo-simulation”, three settings were examined. We corroborated what was expected from theory: using the adjusted data naively will have mild effects when p/N is small. Analyses with MINQUE revealed that the sampling distributions of estimates of variance components from naive and “proper” analyses can be drastically different. A maize data set was used to study how pre-correction affected univariate and bivariate REML estimates of variances for grain yield and plant height. The analysis gave results that were consistent with the heuristics-based expectation indicated above.

Inference and prediction are different “ball games”. The state of nature is unknown in inference and the quality of estimates is gauged relative to model assumptions. Lacking a mechanistic theory for complex traits, our linear models provide a local approximation. If assumptions are incorrect (e.g., ignoring pre-correction) the quality of the approximation is degraded. For instance, when studying pre-correction and BLUP, the näive analysis grossly overstated model-derived precision of estimates of breeding values. On the other hand, in out-of-sample prediction the parameters are instruments for constructing a predictive distribution with yet-to be-observed quantities viewed as missing data. The quality of predictions can be measured once “missing” variables, such as future phenotypes, are observed. We examined the extent to which pre-correction affected estimates of variance parameters and prediction quality for birth weight using two validation scenarios and a Braford cattle data set. Variance component inference depended on whether or not pre-correction was accounted for properly. None of the validation scenarios captured meaningful differences in predictive ability between analyses accounting for or ignoring pre-correction for nuisances. A model taken as benchmark did not improve predictions over “smaller” models with or without correction. This finding corroborates that the predictive ability of a simpler model may not be worse than that delivered by a more complex specification even when the latter fits to the training data set better (e.g.,29). Finding clear differences between prediction machines, irrespective of their degree of sophistication, is not easy in applied quantitative genetics32,33

Main conclusions are: (1) impacts of ignoring pre-correction with observational data are innocuous when p/N is smaller than 0.05 but attention may be warranted if it exceeds 0.10. (2) While ignoring pre-correction affects the distribution of estimates of breeding values markedly, out-of-sample prediction of future performance may be affected less adversely by a naïve analysis. (3) Pre-processing must be used with caution in inference because it may obscure the understanding of a problem under investigation.
