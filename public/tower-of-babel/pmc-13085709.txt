Early blood DNA methylation patterns associated with glycemic progression in a prospective Indian cohort
Authors: Gopika Satheesh, Aneesh K. Asokan, Gadadharan Vijayakumar, Arumugam Rajavelu, Sudha Narayana Rao, Krishnankutty Chandrika Sivakumar, Abdul Jaleel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is rapidly increasing in India, yet molecular markers that reflect early disease susceptibility remain limited. Epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation may reflect early metabolic vulnerability preceding overt dysglycemia. In this study, we examined genome-wide DNA methylation patterns in a pilot subset nested within a prospective Indian cohort using Nanopore sequencing and assessed their associations with previously identified metabolite predictors from the same cohort.ResultsGenome-wide DNA methylation profiling was performed on buffy-coat DNA from 12 participants who were normoglycemic at baseline and later classified into normoglycemia, prediabetes, or T2DM based on their glycemic status at 6-year follow-up. At baseline, gene-level aggregation of CpG methylation revealed directionally consistent hypermethylation of seven genes (ABCG1, ADARB2, BCL2, DLC1, EGFLAM, SYK, ZNF516) in individuals who later developed T2DM, while those progressing to prediabetes exhibited six hypermethylated (ABCG1, FLT3, LCP1, MBP, NCOA2, TCF7L2) and five hypomethylated genes (ZFHX3, PAX6, PTPRN2, ERC1, HIPK1). ABCG1 showed consistent hypermethylation across both groups. Longitudinal within-individual comparisons identified additional gene-associated methylation changes, including ANK1, IQSEC1, and RUNX1, and shared alterations in CACNA1C, KANSL1, PTPRN2, and TTC34, while six genes showed stage-dependent directional shifts in methylation (ASB3, EFR3A, PCSK5, KLHL14, PDE4C, UNC5C). Correlation analyses at baseline suggested associations between ABCG1 and EGFLAM methylation, fasting glucose, phosphatidylethanolamine [PE (20:3_18:0)] and insulin sensitivity indices.ConclusionThis pilot longitudinal study suggests that gene-associated DNA methylation changes in blood may be detectable prior to the onset of dysglycemia. These findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, highlighting candidate genes and epigenetic–metabolic associations for targeted validation in larger, independent cohorts using alternative analytical approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02064-6.

Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by progressive insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction, with hyperglycemia often manifesting only in later stages [1]. By the time of clinical diagnosis, many individuals have already developed microvascular and macrovascular complications, which substantially contribute to the morbidity and mortality associated with T2DM.

India carries one of the largest global burdens of diabetes, with an estimated adult prevalence of 11–12% and an earlier age of onset compared to Western populations [2]. This earlier and more aggressive disease course is not fully explained by lifestyle or genetic predisposition, suggesting a role of epigenetic mechanisms that mediate interactions between environment and gene expression. Nutritional and metabolic factors are known to modulate epigenetic signalling within host cells, influencing gene regulation and metabolic programming. Disturbances in nutritional balance have been associated with long-term alterations in DNA methylation and chromatin structure, predisposing individuals to obesity, insulin resistance and T2DM [3–6].

Among epigenetic modifications, DNA methylation has emerged as a promising biomarker for T2DM risk. Prospective epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS) in European and South Asian cohorts, including the London Life Sciences Prospective Population (LOLIPOP) and EPIC-Norfolk, have identified CpG sites in ABCG1, PHOSPHO1, SOCS3, SREBF1, TXNIP, TCF7L2, and FTO that are associated with incident T2DM [7, 8]. A follow-up study further demonstrated that methylation of ABCG1 and PHOSPHO1 at baseline was associated with future T2DM risk, reinforcing the role of DNA methylation in reflecting early metabolic vulnerability [9]. Notably, methylation at these loci also correlates with established risk factors such as body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, fasting insulin, triglycerides, and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), suggesting that DNA methylation signatures integrate both genetic susceptibility and environmental influences on diabetes risk [9].

The existing evidence is cross-sectional and derived from European populations or South Asians living abroad. Within India, research remains limited. A recent systematic review emphasized the potential of DNA methylation biomarkers but highlighted the absence of large, prospective studies in Indian populations [10]. The Indian Diabetes and Metabolic Health (InDiMeT) study described methylation and lipidomic dysregulation in adipose tissue and peripheral blood mononuclear cells, but its case–control design precludes evaluation of methylation changes preceding disease onset [11]. To date, no longitudinal cohort studies in India have profiled DNA methylation prior to the development of T2DM.

Since preventive interventions are most effective before overt hyperglycemia develops, identifying early DNA methylation patterns associated with progression from normoglycemia to T2DM is of critical importance. To address this gap, we conducted an exploratory DNA methylation analysis within a previously established prospective cohort of normoglycemic adults. In our recent work, we demonstrated that specific metabolic alterations within this cohort predicted future T2DM risk [12]. Building on these findings, we examined whether DNA methylation patterns at baseline show directional differences associated with subsequent glycemic progression over a 6-year follow-up period in this young Indian cohort. We further assessed longitudinal and between-group methylation patterns and explored baseline methylation–metabolite relationships using previously identified metabolite predictors. The analyses presented here are exploratory and intended to prioritize candidate genes for targeted validation in larger, independent cohorts.

Methods

Experimental design

A cohort of 110 normoglycemic men and women aged 18–40 years from our baseline study [13] were enrolled. Individuals with known illnesses, diabetes, nutritional disorders, allergies, regular medication use, or active attempts at weight reduction were excluded. At 6-year follow-up, 94 participants (aged 24–46 years) were reassessed, while 16 were lost due to migration or withdrawal of consent.

According to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria [14], participants were classified at follow-up as normoglycemic (Fasting Blood Sugar (FBS) < 100 mg/dL, HbA1c: < 5.6%), prediabetes (FBS: 100–125 mg/dL, HbA1c: 5.7% − 6.4%) or T2DM (FBS > 126 mg/dL, HbA1c: > 6.5%). At follow-up, 34 participants remained normoglycemic, 51 developed prediabetes, and 9 were diagnosed with T2DM. The recruitment, follow-up, and classification of participants, and sample section for genome-wide methylation analysis, are summarized in Fig. 1.

Flowchart illustrating participant recruitment, follow-up classification based on glycemic status, and selection of samples for genome-wide methylation analysis

Participants enrolled at baseline were re-contacted, and those who agreed to participate provided written informed consent. Prior to testing, they were advised to maintain their usual diet, avoid unusual physical activity, and refrain from intentional exercise for three days. On the study day, fasting venous blood samples were collected, followed by administration of a standardized mixed meal previously described in detail [13]. Postprandial blood samples were collected at 30 min intervals for 120 min, plasma was separated, and aliquots were stored at − 80 °C for biochemical analyses. Buffy coat was simultaneously isolated from blood samples and stored at − 80 °C for DNA extraction.

For the pilot DNA methylation analysis, buffy-coat samples from two males and two females were selected from each outcome group (normoglycemia, prediabetes, T2DM) at both baseline and follow-up, balanced for age and sex across groups. Within this selected subset, baseline body mass index (BMI) and insulin sensitivity indices (OGIS), were comparable across groups (Table 1). Genome-wide DNA methylation profiling was performed using Oxford Nanopore Technology (Genotypic Technology Private Limited, Bangalore, India).

Clinical and biochemical parameters

Anthropometric measurements included weight, height, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, hip circumference, and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR). Biochemical parameters, including glucose, insulin, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), lipid profile, and C-reactive protein (CRP), were measured using standardized laboratory protocols. To assess insulin sensitivity and β-cell function, we employed HOMA-IR (Homeostasis Model of Insulin Resistance), HOMA-β (Homeostasis Model Assessment of β-cell function) [15], along with the Oral Glucose Insulin Sensitivity Index (OGIS) [16].

DNA extraction and quality control

Genomic DNA was isolated from buffy coat samples stored at − 80 °C using the Blood and Cell Culture DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat. no. 13323). Purity and concentration of genomic DNA was assessed using Nanodrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Scientific; 2000). Integrity of DNA was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis and concentrations were quantified by Qubit dsDNA HS Assay kit (Invitrogen, Cat. no. Q32854). All samples yielded high molecular weight DNA suitable for Nanopore sequencing.

Library preparation and nanopore sequencing

High molecular weight DNA was fragmented using Covaris g-TUBE to generate double-stranded DNA fragments of appropriate size, followed by end-repair and A-tailing (NEBNext Ultra II End Repair Kit, New England Biolabs, MA, USA). Sequencing adapters (Oxford Nanopore SQK-NBD114.96) were ligated with NEB Quick T4 DNA Ligase, and barcoding was performed with native barcoding kits (NB01–NB24). Libraries were purified with AMPure XP beads, quantified by Qubit, and pooled. Sequencing was performed on a Nanopore PromethION P24 platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK) using FLO-PRO114M flow cells. Across 24 human samples, a total yield of ~ 30–45 GB of long-read data per sample was obtained, with mean read quality scores of 14.4–16.2 and average coverage of 10 -15X.

Base calling and read alignment

Base calling was conducted using Guppy v6.4.6 + ae70e8f [17], to generate high-accuracy FASTQ reads. Modified base (5mC) detection was performed concurrently using a reference genome (GRCh38-p15) with Minimap2 v2.24-r1122 [18], producing BAM files.

Methylation detection and annotation

Modified base (5mC) calls were extracted from BAM alignments using modbam2bed v0.5.2, retaining CpG methylation events with a minimum read depth ≥ 5. The resulting bedMethyl files were annotated against the reference genome using BEDTools v2.31.0 [19], and chromosomal CpG distributions were obtained. For each sample, 2.0–5.4 million CpG methylation sites were identified, of which ~ 49–52% mapped to annotated gene regions. Annotations were performed using HOMER (Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment), classifying methylation events within promoter regions, transcription start site (TSS), gene bodies (exons/introns), CpG islands, and intergenic regions. Downstream analyses focused on gene-level and CpG-island–level methylation patterns rather than individual CpG-level inference.

Quality control considerations

Quality control (QC) was assessed at multiple stages of the workflow. Pre-sequencing QC included evaluation of DNA purity, integrity and concentration. At the sequencing level, read yield, mean read quality scores and genome-wide coverage were monitored. For methylation analysis, CpG sites were retained if supported by a minimum read depth of ≥ 5. Global 5-methylcytosine (5mC) distributions were examined to assess overall consistency across samples.

Nanopore-derived methylation profiles were compared with publicly available bisulfite sequencing–based PBMC methylomes from healthy adults to assess concordance at gene and CpG-island levels. Substantial overlap was observed at these levels, indicating technical concordance of gene-associated methylation summaries derived from nanopore sequencing (Supplementary methods). More extensive CpG-level QC metrics, orthogonal validation, and leukocyte cell-type deconvolution were not included in the present analysis.

Differential methylation analysis

Differential methylation analysis was performed using MethylKit v1.26.0 [20]. Three exploratory comparisons were performed:

Hypermethylated and hypomethylated CpG sites were identified using logistic regression models with multiple testing correction. No CpG sites reached genome-wide significance after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Accordingly, downstream analyses focused on effect size and directional consistency rather than strict statistical thresholds.

A methylation difference threshold of ≥ 25% between groups was applied and CpG sites were aggregated at the gene level. Genes were considered consistent if ≥ 75% of individuals within a group (≥ 3 of 4) demonstrated concordant directionality (hyper or hypo methylation). Redundant CpG entries were removed based on chromosome–position–gene mapping. Directional shift analysis further identified genes exhibiting changes in predominant methylation direction across glycemic progression (e.g., genes hypermethylated in normoglycemia but predominantly hypomethylated in prediabetes or T2DM, and vice versa).

Metabolomics data source and integration

Plasma metabolomics data used was derived from our previously published prospective analysis conducted in the same cohort [12]. In that study, mass spectrometry-based untargeted metabolomics identified metabolites including phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) (20:3_18:0), 3β,7α-dihydroxy-5-cholestenoate, and tridecanoic acid as predictors of future T2DM risk. In the present study, metabolomics data were not regenerated or reprocessed. Previous measured metabolite abundance at baseline were integrated with DNA methylation data for exploratory correlation analysis. Detailed metabolomics methodology has been described in the original publication [12].

Pathway and correlation analyses

Functional relevance of genes showing gene-associated DNA methylation patterns was assessed using Enrichr (Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes; KEGG 2021 Human) [21]. Uniquely hypermethylated and hypomethylated genes within each glycemic group were analyzed separately. Enrichment significance was evaluated using Fisher’s exact test, and pathway relevance was ranked by the combined score, which integrates the p value and z-score. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and pathways having adjusted p < 0.25 considered significant [22].

To explore multi-omics associations, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between baseline gene-associated DNA methylation patterns, previously identified metabolites, and clinical variables. Correlation matrices were generated using the Corrplot package in R (4.3.3). p values were corrected using the Bonferroni method, with p < 0.05 considered significant. These analyses were used to identify concordant patterns across molecular and clinical measures.

Statistical analysis and data visualization

Clinical and biochemical variables were compared among groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing in IBM SPSS Statistics 24.0. Heatmaps were generated using the ggplot2 package in R (version 4.3.3), representing median methylation values per gene to depict group-level differences. A Sankey plot was created with the networkD3 package in R to visualize directional methylation shifts, and Venn diagrams were prepared in Microsoft PowerPoint to illustrate overlap of genes showing gene-associated DNA methylation patterns across comparisons.

Genome-wide 5mC methylation patterns across baseline and follow-up samples

Genome-wide average 5-methylcytosine (5mC) levels across normoglycemia, prediabetes, and T2DM are shown in Fig. S1. In the normoglycemia group, 5mC levels remained relatively stable from baseline to follow-up, with only minor inter-individual variations. By contrast, the prediabetes group showed a reduction in global 5mC levels at follow-up. Participants who developed T2DM exhibited greater inter-individual variability, with several showing reduced 5mC levels at follow-up.

To investigate whether early DNA methylation differences were present before overt glycemic impairment, we next focused on baseline methylation profiles of individuals who subsequently progressed to prediabetes or T2DM.

Gene-associated DNA methylation differences at baseline

Even at baseline, when all participants were clinically normoglycemic, individuals who later developed prediabetes or T2DM showed directionally different gene-associated methylation patterns relative to those who remained normoglycemic (Fig. 2). At the level of individual sample-to-sample comparisons, a large number of genes containing one or more CpG sites showing methylation differences were observed. Participants who progressed to T2DM showed a greater extent of gene-associated methylation changes than those who developed prediabetes, although substantial inter-individual variability was evident across pairwise comparisons.

At baseline, 26 genes showed directionally consistent DNA methylation differences in participants who later developed T2DM (Fig. S2a), of which 88% exhibited hypermethylation pattern (Fig. S2b). These genes were identified based on gene-level aggregation of CpG sites showing methylation differences rather than significant EWAS hits. Table S2 lists these genes with their chromosomal positions, functions, and methylation status.

Among these, seven (including ABCG1, ADARB2, BCL2, DLC1, EGFLAM, SYK, and ZNF516) maintained the same directional classification at follow-up. Literature-based evidence supporting the relevance of these genes to T2DM, along with the observed direction of DNA methylation changes in this cohort, is summarized in Table 2. CpG genomic regions were assigned based on standard genomic annotation of detected CpG sites, as described in the Methods. These genes span diverse biological functions, including lipid transport, apoptosis regulation, and membrane trafficking.

Aberrant baseline DNA methylation associated with future prediabetes

In individuals who later progressed to prediabetes, 29 genes showed consistent methylation direction at baseline, of which 55% were hypermethylated and 45% were hypomethylated (Fig. S3a–b). Both shared and unique gene-level alterations were observed relative to the normoglycemic group. A detailed list of genes showing concordant directional CpG methylation changes at baseline in individuals who progressed to prediabetes is provided in Table S3.

Table 3 highlights selected genes with prior evidence linking them to T2DM pathogenesis. For example, ABCG1 and TCF7L2, exhibited hypermethylation at baseline in our cohort, while PTPRN2, showed hypomethylation, consistent with prior findings. Other genes such as PAX6, MBP, and NCOA2 previously associated with T2DM through functional or expression studies, also showed methylation differences in our cohort.

Genomic localization of genes showing methylation differences at baseline

Annotation of CpG sites at baseline revealed that, in the future T2DM group, most CpG sites were located within intronic regions (73%), followed by promoter–TSS regions (27%). Promoter-associated methylation changes were consistently observed in CELF5, PAX5, PRICKLE1, LRRC4C, and PRDM1, whereas ABCG1, ADARB2, BCL2, and DLC1 predominantly showed intronic methylation (Table S2).

Similarly, among those who later developed prediabetes, 79% of CpG sites were intronic and 21% were promoter-localized, commonly affecting STK3, ANKFY1, HIPK1, PAX6, CACNA1A, and EXOC2 (Table S3).

Longitudinal gene-associated methylation changes

Longitudinal within-individual comparisons of DNA methylation profiles between baseline and follow-up samples revealed distinct patterns across glycemic groups (Fig. 3). While normoglycemic individuals exhibited a relatively balanced distribution of hyper- and hypomethylated gene-associated methylation changes, the prediabetic and T2DM groups demonstrated greater inter-individual variability and a higher number of genes containing CpG sites with methylation differences across time (Fig. 3). These longitudinal patterns reflect gene-level summaries of CpG methylation changes.

Gene-level methylation patterns across glycemic groups

Gene-level variations across glycemic groups were assessed by comparative analysis of gene-associated CpG methylation directionality. Venn diagram analysis revealed a predominance of group-specific gene-level alterations, with the T2DM group showing the highest number of exclusive candidates (Fig. 4a).

In the normoglycemia versus prediabetes comparison, Dystonin (DST) showed a predominant hypomethylation direction in prediabetes, while FOXP1 and PTCH1 remained largely stable (Fig. 4b). Consistent with these directional summaries, gene-wise median percentage methylation values demonstrated modest directional shifts for DST, while FOXP1 and PTCH1 showed only subtle differences with substantial inter-individual overlap (Fig. S4a).

In the normoglycemia versus T2DM comparison, ANK1, IQSEC1, and ZFHX3 showed hypermethylation in T2DM, whereas CELF2, RERE, and SLC35F1 displayed hypomethylation. PAX5 showed no substantial directional change (Fig. 4c). Gene-level percentage methylation plots further illustrate these patterns at the individual-sample level, highlighting greater inter-individual variability in the T2DM group (Fig. S4b).

In the prediabetes versus T2DM comparison, RUNX1 and protocadherin genes (PCDHGA1–3, PCDHGB1) showed hypermethylation direction, while RBFOX1 shifted direction from hypomethylation in prediabetes to hypermethylation in T2DM. CAMTA1 remained consistently hypomethylated. NFIA, HIVEP3, ZIC4, and PTPRT demonstrated stable methylation direction profiles (Fig. 4d). Corresponding percentage methylation distributions are shown in Fig. S4c.

Among genes shared across all three glycemic groups, CACNA1C exhibited increased hypomethylation in T2DM. KANSL1 and PTPRN2 showed differing methylation directions across comparisons, with reduced hypermethylation from normoglycemia to prediabetes and a shift toward hypermethylation in T2DM, whereas TTC34 remained unchanged (Fig. 4e). These trends are further visualized using gene-wise methylation percentages across all three glycemic states (Fig. S4d).

Pathway enrichment of genes with altered methylation directions

Distinct pathway-level alterations (adjusted p < 0.25) were observed across glycemic categories (Table S4). In normoglycemia, 20 uniquely hypermethylated and 11 hypomethylated genes were detected. Hypermethylated genes were enriched in Rap1, Ras, and PI3K–Akt signalling, while hypomethylated genes mapped to sulfur metabolism, selenocompound metabolism, insulin secretion, and mTOR/Wnt signalling.

In prediabetes, 11 hypermethylated and 17 hypomethylated genes were identified. Hypermethylated genes were associated with lipolysis in adipocytes and Notch signalling, and hypomethylated genes with glycosphingolipid biosynthesis. In T2DM, 24 hypermethylated and 14 hypomethylated genes were detected. Hypermethylated genes predominantly participated in fatty-acid elongation, butanoate, and tryptophan metabolism, while hypomethylated genes were enriched in glyoxylate, propanoate, and branched-chain amino-acid degradation pathways.

Directional shifts in DNA methylation patterns

We further investigated genes that showed a change in their predominant methylation direction as glycemic status worsened (Fig. 5a–g). Several genes that were predominantly hypermethylated in the normoglycemic state became hypomethylated in prediabetes (ASB3) or T2DM (EFR3A, PCSK5). Conversely, KLHL14, PDE4C, and UNC5C transitioned from hypomethylation to hypermethylation with glycemic progression. The Sankey plot summarizes changes in predominant gene-level methylation direction across pairwise baseline glycemic group comparisons, reflecting between group patterns rather than CpG-level switching or within-individual reversals.

Gene–metabolite–clinical correlation network at baseline

At baseline, exploratory correlation analyses identified associations among selected gene-level methylation measures, metabolites, and clinical indices (Fig. 6). ABCG1 methylation correlated positively with fasting blood sugar (FBS), 3β,7α-dihydroxy-5-cholestenoate, and glycemic status, and inversely with PE (20:3_18:0) and OGIS, suggesting an association between ABCG1 hypermethylation, impaired insulin sensitivity and altered lipid metabolism. EGFLAM showed a similar pattern, displaying positive correlations with FBS and glycemic status, and negative associations with PE (20:3_18:0) and OGIS.

Among metabolites, PE (20:3_18:0) correlated negatively with HbA1c, age, FBS, and glycemic status, further linking phosphatidylethanolamine metabolism to deteriorating glycemic control. Collectively, these correlations highlight coordinated associations between selected methylation markers, lipid-related metabolites, and clinical measures at baseline. These findings highlight candidate epigenetic–metabolic relationships for follow-up in larger studies.

Summary of gene-level methylation findings across analyses

At baseline, seven genes (ABCG1, ADARB2, BCL2, DLC1, EGFLAM, SYK, and ZNF516) showed predominant hypermethylation patterns in individuals who later developed T2DM, while those progressing to prediabetes showed six genes with hypermethylation (ABCG1, FLT3, LCP1, MBP, NCOA2, and TCF7L2) and five genes with hypomethylation (ZFHX3, PAX6, PTPRN2, ERC1, and HIPK1). Genes observed across all three glycemic categories included CACNA1C, KANSL1, PTPRN2, and TTC34. Directional analysis revealed dynamic remodeling of methylation directions, with ASB3, EFR3A, and PCSK5 shifting from hyper- to hypomethylation, while KLHL14, PDE4C, and UNC5C moved from hypo- to hypermethylation during progression. Discussion

In this pilot analysis nested within a longitudinal cohort of adults who were normoglycemic at recruitment, we identified gene-associated DNA methylation patterns at baseline that were associated with glycemic status at follow-up. Additionally, we observed gene-associated methylation changes over a 6-year follow-up period. Collectively, these findings suggest that blood-based methylation differences may be detectable before the onset of dysglycemia and may reflect early molecular alterations associated with progression toward T2DM.

Importantly, although several of the genes identified in this study have been previously reported in epigenome-wide association studies conducted largely in European or migrant South Asian populations, their longitudinal blood-based methylation patterns have been minimally explored in young Indian adults residing in India. Prospective epigenetic datasets from Indian cohorts that capture gene-associated DNA methylation differences preceding glycemic progression remain limited. Thus, the present pilot study contributes longitudinal evidence from this underrepresented population. Accordingly, these findings extend existing EWAS literature by placing known T2DM-associated genes within a population-specific and longitudinal framework rather than proposing novel disease genes.

At baseline, several genes showed differential DNA methylation among participants who later developed prediabetes or T2DM, highlighting candidates for further investigation. Hypermethylation of ABCG1 was among the most consistent alterations. ABCG1 encodes a cholesterol and phospholipid transporter, and its hypermethylation has been repeatedly associated with incident T2DM and insulin resistance [7, 9, 23, 37]. In our study, this association was observed at the level of gene-aggregated methylation directionality rather than significant CpG-level effects. Functionally, impaired ABCG1 activity disrupts lipid efflux, β-cell secretory granule homeostasis, and insulin secretion [38]. In our cohort, hypermethylation of ABCG1 correlated with reduced plasma levels of phosphatidylethanolamine [PE (20:3_18:0)], a metabolite previously identified as a predictor of T2DM in this cohort [12], suggesting a possible relationship between lipid metabolism and methylation status that warrants validation in larger studies.

Other baseline findings also align with prior evidence. Hypomethylation of PTPRN2, observed in participants progressing to prediabetes, is consistent with its role in vesicle trafficking and impaired insulin secretion [36, 39]. Hypermethylation of BCL2, a key apoptotic gene, may contribute to β-cell loss, although direct functional inference cannot be drawn from methylation data alone [26, 28]. TCF7L2, a well-established genetic locus for T2DM, showed hypermethylation at baseline in those who progressed to prediabetes, consistent with reports linking its methylation to insulin secretion [33, 34, 40]. PAX6, which is critical for islet development and insulin biosynthesis, was hypomethylated in the prediabetes group [35], which may reflect dysregulated response rather than a defined compensatory mechanism [41, 42].

Several additional baseline genes exhibited methylation differences that expand the pool of potential candidates. Hypermethylation of ADARB2 contrasts with its reported hypomethylation in overweight individuals with elevated leptin [24] suggesting tissue- or disease-specific regulation in T2DM. DLC1 known to harbour risk SNPs for T2DM, showed methylation changes in our pilot dataset; indicating possible epigenetic involvement that requires confirmation. Interestingly, while previous studies reported SYK hypomethylation in diabetic kidney disease [28] and upregulation in diabetic peripheral neuropathy [43], our observation of SYK hypermethylation may reflect context-dependent regulation rather than a consistent disease mechanism. Hypermethylation of FLT3 in prediabetes, is consistent with reduced expression in db/db mice [29] and may counteract elevated circulating FLT3 levels associated with increased T2DM risk [30]. Similarly, MBP and NCOA2, both reported as downregulated in T2DM, were hypermethylated in our study, directionally consistent with reduced expression but this does not establish causality [31, 32]. EGFLAM, ZNF516, LCP1, ZFHX3, ERC1, and HIPK1 have not been previously implicated in T2DM and therefore represent novel, exploratory candidates identified in this pilot analysis.

Both prediabetes and T2DM groups exhibited a predominance of intronic methylation, suggesting that gene-body methylation changes may occur early in dysglycemia [44]. However, the relatively higher frequency of promoter–TSS methylation in T2DM (e.g., PAX5, PRDM1, LRRC4C) compared to prediabetes (STK3, PAX6, HIPK1) may reflect evolving regulatory alterations with disease progression, but this observation requires validation in larger cohorts.

Longitudinal within-individual comparisons revealed additional genes showing methylation changes over time. Hypomethylation of DST in prediabetes may relate to cytoskeletal remodeling linked to leukocyte dysfunction and endothelial impairment in metabolic disease [45]. Hypermethylation of ANK1 in T2DM is consistent with prior epigenetic and genetic associations [46], while hypermethylation of IQSEC1 and RUNX1 has been reported in other metabolic or exercise-related contexts [47, 48]. Hypomethylation of CACNA1C and CELF2 may reflect altered immune or inflammatory states [49–51], although these interpretations remain speculative in the absence of functional data. Other genes (ZFHX3, RERE, SLC35F1, PCDHGA1–3, PCDHGB1, RBFOX1, KANSL1) have not been directly implicated in T2DM, and may represent novel candidates requiring further investigation.

Directional analysis highlighted glycemic stage-specific methylation changes across glycemic categories (Fig. 5). ASB3, EFR3A, and PCSK5 shifted from hypermethylated in normoglycemia to hypomethylated in dysglycemia, while KLHL14, PDE4C, and UNC5C shifted from hypomethylation to hypermethylation. Hypomethylation of ASB3 is noteworthy since ASB3 has been linked to cardiovascular and renal disease [52], however these directional changes summarize group-level trends across glycemic stages and provide a framework for prioritizing genes for longitudinal validation. Pathway enrichment analyses suggested a gradual shift in methylation-associated pathways from signalling-related processes in normoglycemia toward metabolic pathways in overt T2DM. Early hypermethylation of ADAMTS3, EFNA3, and TIAM1 is consistent with altered regulation of Rap1/Ras/PI3K–Akt signalling, a central regulator of insulin signalling and cellular stress responses [53–56], while hypomethylation of CHRM3 may enhance insulin secretion [57]. In contrast, hypermethylation of HADHA [58] and altered PDE4C methylation [59] highlight impaired fatty-acid oxidation and dysregulated lipid signalling. In the absence of genome-wide significant CpG sites, pathway-level patterns were examined to provide a descriptive overview of pathways represented by the shortlisted genes, rather than evidence of pathway-level dysregulation.

The metabolomics analysis previously conducted in this cohort identified phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) (20:3_18:0), 3β,7α-dihydroxy-5-cholestenoate, and tridecanoic acid as predictors of T2DM [12]. In the present study, integration of methylation data with these metabolite measures was performed in an exploratory manner to assess potential co-variation between epigenetic and metabolic features at baseline.

At the gene level, ABCG1 hypermethylation was associated with lower circulating PE (20:3_18:0) levels. While ABCG1 has been implicated in phospholipid transport [60–62], the observed association highlights a potential link between lipid metabolism and methylation status that warrants further investigation. Similarly, associations involving BCL2, SYK and NCOA2 may reflect broader metabolic or inflammatory states rather than direct regulatory relationships.

The correlation network analysis at baseline revealed associations, among glycemic indices, lipid traits, and key genes (ABCG1 and EGFLAM), suggesting early metabolic derangement in T2DM. The attenuation of these correlations at follow-up may reflect therapeutic or adaptive responses.

Beyond disease-specific mechanisms, the observed methylation differences may also overlap with age-related epigenetic remodeling. Accelerated epigenetic aging has been associated with glucose intolerance and insulin resistance [63–65], and it is plausible that some methylation changes observed in our cohort may not only reflect glycemic state but also represent aging-related epigenetic remodeling.

This study has several notable strengths, including its prospective design, 6-year follow-up, and the use of Nanopore methylome sequencing for direct methylation detection. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The number of methylation samples was relatively small (n = 12 individuals, 24 paired samples), which constrained statistical power, limited genome-wide significance testing and precluded robust predictive modelling such as ROC analysis. Methylation differences were therefore summarized primarily at the gene level using directional trends rather than CpG-level false discovery rate–significant associations. As a pilot analysis, the study focused on identifying reproducible longitudinal methylation patterns, an approach commonly used to generate hypotheses for subsequent validation in larger cohorts [66–69].

Second, whole-blood methylation profiles were analyzed without adjustment for leukocyte cell-type composition and Nanopore-based methylation calling at modest coverage may have higher error rates compared with array-based or bisulfite sequencing platforms. Nevertheless, benchmarking against published PBMC methylomes demonstrated substantial concordance at the gene and CpG-island levels, supporting the technical reliability of the nanopore-derived methylation patterns reported here.

Although the current findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, the value of this study lies in its prospective design and its focus on an underrepresented Indian population, providing population-specific longitudinal epigenetic data that remain scarce in the T2DM literature. Notably, the predominance of intronic and gene-body CpG associations suggests possible involvement of regulatory mechanisms beyond promoter-centric control, highlighting the potential for locus-specific epigenetic modulation during glycemic progression. Replication in larger and independent cohorts, incorporating cell-type–resolved analyses and orthogonal methylation assays, will be required to validate the candidate genes identified, and to clarify their functional relevance in glycemic progression and T2DM risk.

Conclusion

This exploratory study suggests that gene-associated DNA methylation changes in blood may be detectable years before the onset of dysglycemia in a prospective Indian cohort. Several genes previously implicated in T2DM, including ABCG1, PTPRN2, BCL2, TCF7L2, and PAX6, displayed consistent methylation trends, providing internal concordance with prior literature. In addition, a set of less well-characterized genes emerged as candidates associated with glycemic progression, expanding the pool of targets for future investigation. Correlation with previously identified metabolite predictors revealed patterns of co-variation between selected methylation features, lipid-related metabolites, and clinical measures at baseline. Together, these observations suggest that integrated epigenetic and metabolic profiling provide useful insight into early molecular alterations associated with glycemic progression.

Given the pilot nature of this study, the small sample size, and the dependence on gene-level directional methylation trends, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Future studies should prioritize targeted validation in larger Indian and South Asian cohorts, ideally using orthogonal approaches such as bisulfite sequencing of sentinel CpGs (e.g., in ABCG1, PTPRN2, RBFOX1, RUNX1, CACNA1C), cell type-resolved analyses, and expression studies combined with metabolomics. These approaches will be essential to assess reproducibility, biological relevance, and the potential translational utility of blood-based DNA methylation markers in the early T2DM development.
