Gambling in Online Gaming among Youth in Hong Kong: Does Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) and Online Gambling Tendency Play a Role?
Authors: Weixi Kang, Raymond Chi-Fai Chui, Nicolson Yat-Fan Siu, Joyce Yuen-Sum Lee, Wing-Yan Lau, Chi-Keung Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the growing concerns of gambling in online gaming among youth in Hong Kong, understanding contributing factors of potential for gambling in online gaming becomes increasingly important. This study aims to test the associations between internet gaming disorder (IGD), online gambling tendency, and the potential for gambling in online gaming among Hong Kong youth. Data from a sample of 1,661 youth from July 2020 to August 2020, and were analyzed using multiple regressions and mediation models. The results showed that both IGD (b = 0.04, p < 0.001, 95% C.I. [0.03, 0.04]) and online gambling symptoms (b = 0.04, p < 0.001, 95% C.I. [0.03, 0.04]) were positively associated with the potential for gambling in online gaming (standardized factor score). Mediation analyses with 10,000 bootstrap samples indicated significant indirect effects of IGD on gambling-in-gaming via online gambling symptoms (b = 0.01, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.00, 0.01]) and of online gambling symptoms on gambling-in-gaming via IGD (b = 0.01, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.01, 0.01]), controlling for demographics. These findings suggest that when online games and online gambling share similar reward and monetization features (e.g., rapid feedback, intermittent reinforcement, and chance-based purchases), adolescents may be more likely to develop problems in both domains. Developmentally appropriate intervention and prevention are needed to reduce the potential for gambling in online gaming among Hong Kong youth.

Introduction

Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD)

In 2013, the American Psychiatric Association recognized Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) as a condition requiring further study and included it in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5; American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Subsequently, the World Health Organization included Gaming Disorder (including online gaming) under “Disorders Due to Addictive Behaviours” in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11; World Health Organization, 2018). In ICD-11, gaming disorder is characterized by persistent gaming behavior (typically evident for at least 12 months) involving impaired control over gaming, increasing priority given to gaming over other activities, and continuation or escalation despite negative consequences, resulting in significant functional impairment.

A recent meta-analysis reported a pooled prevalence of Internet Gaming Disorder among adolescents of 7.1% (95% CI [5.6%, 8.5%]), while also highlighting substantial heterogeneity across diagnostic criteria, assessment scales, and study contexts (Zhou et al., 2024). Beyond prevalence, IGD has been linked to a range of adverse psychosocial outcomes, including depressive symptoms, social anxiety, and suicidality (Bielefeld et al., 2017; Carli et al., 2012; Colder Carras et al., 2020; Dieris-Hirche et al., 2017; González-Bueso et al., 2018; Ko et al., 2012; Lachmann et al., 2017). IGD is particularly relevant in adolescence because gaming is highly normative in youth digital life, while self-regulatory capacities (e.g., impulse control and future-oriented decision-making) are still developing (Ghali et al., 2023; Kang et al., 2022). This developmental context is important for interpreting gaming-related harm: the same reward features that make games engaging (e.g., rapid feedback, intermittent reinforcement, social rewards) may also amplify risk for escalated use among a subset of adolescents, especially when gaming becomes a primary coping strategy for stress or negative affect (Milani et al., 2018). In addition, the contemporary gaming ecosystem increasingly includes monetization and chance-based mechanics (e.g., microtransactions, randomized rewards), which may strengthen theoretical and practical links between problematic gaming and gambling-related behaviors (King, 2018; Zendle & Cairns, 2019).

In Hong Kong, Wang et al. (2014) found that 94% of a sample of 503 high school students had played video or internet games, with 15.6% showing tendencies toward gaming addiction. Zhu et al. (2021) reported that 83.0% of children and adolescents in primary and secondary schools engaged in video gaming during the COVID-19 pandemic; among these participants, 20.9% exhibited excessive gaming behaviors and 5.3% showed signs of pathological gaming addiction. Taken together, Hong Kong data suggest that gaming is highly prevalent among youth, and a meaningful minority may experience problematic patterns, underscoring the importance of examining IGD alongside other risk behaviors within digital environments.

Online Gambling

Online gambling refers to placing monetary stakes on uncertain outcomes via internet-enabled platforms, including online casinos, poker, sports betting, and other wager-based products (Hing et al., 2022). Compared with land-based gambling, online gambling may be characterized by greater accessibility (anytime/anywhere), perceived anonymity, rapid event cycles, and high-intensity product design (e.g., continuous betting opportunities), which can increase the likelihood of persistent engagement among vulnerable individuals (Hing et al., 2022). A recent systematic review focusing on adolescents reported that approximately 5–15% of adolescents engage in online gambling, although estimates vary substantially across countries and measurement approaches (Montiel et al., 2021). For Hong Kong, the University of Hong Kong study (2022) found that 0.9% of participants engaged in online gambling, with some spending over $1000 per month and even borrowing money for it; the study also noted that the sample size for online gambling was insufficient to draw reliable conclusions. Although prevalence estimates vary, the potential harms associated with online gambling can be substantial, including financial strain, relationship disruption, and elevated risks of depression, anxiety, substance use problems, and suicidality (Hing et al., 2022; Potenza et al., 2019).

Conceptually, it is important to distinguish online gambling participation (any engagement) from problematic online gambling (loss of control and impairment), as adolescents may encounter online gambling directly or indirectly through gambling-adjacent content and communities even before meeting clinical thresholds.

Gambling in Online Gaming and eSports

Beyond traditional online gambling, a growing body of work highlights “gambling-like” or gambling-adjacent activities embedded in gaming and eSports ecosystems (DeCamp, 2021; Greer et al., 2019; King, 2018; Schneider, 2015; Zendle & Cairns, 2019). This literature indicates that the boundary between gaming and gambling is increasingly blurred; however, much of the evidence is correlational and therefore cannot establish causal pathways (e.g., from loot box spending to later gambling disorder). A key question is whether these gaming-related exposures (e.g., chance-based purchases and wagering communities) operate as a “gateway,” reflect shared vulnerabilities (e.g., impulsivity and reward sensitivity), or both.

Loot boxes represent monetized, chance-based in-game purchase mechanics (microtransactions) with uncertain rewards, and a substantial proportion of young people report spending on them (Gambling Commission, 2019; Parent Zone, 2018). Across studies, loot box spending has been associated with gambling-related cognitions and problematic/disordered gambling (Brooks & Clark, 2019; Drummond et al., 2020; King & Delfabbro, 2020; Li et al., 2019; Macey & Hamari, 2019; Zendle, 2020; Zendle & Cairns, 2019), although these associations may be confounded by shared risk factors (e.g., impulsivity and sensation-seeking) in cross-sectional designs. Consistent with these concerns, several jurisdictions have introduced or proposed regulatory responses (e.g., probability disclosures, restrictions, or calls for bans), reflecting ongoing debate about whether and when loot boxes should be treated as gambling products, particularly for minors (Hern, 2018).

Other gambling-adjacent behaviors further contribute to gaming–gambling convergence, including skin betting (wagering using in-game cosmetic items as virtual value, often via third-party sites) (Gambling Commission, 2019; Macey & Hamari, 2019; Parent Zone, 2018), social simulated gambling games accessible via video games and smartphone apps (King, 2018; Zendle, 2020), and eSports betting (wagering on multiplayer videogame competitions) (Greer et al., 2019; Schneider, 2015; Zendle, 2020). Adolescent engagement in simulated gambling products has been documented across contexts (Elton-Marshall et al., 2016; Veselka et al., 2018), and gambling sponsorship/advertising in eSports may increase exposure to gambling cues (Greer et al., 2019). Prior studies suggest that eSports betting may be strongly associated with problematic gambling, sometimes more so than traditional sports betting (Macey & Hamari, 2019). Overall, the literature suggests that gaming–gambling convergence is multifaceted and is associated with gambling-related harms (Hing et al., 2022; Shaffer et al., 2020), potentially through repeated exposure to chance-based reward structures, cognitive distortions (e.g., illusion of control), reinforcement learning processes, and normalization through peer and online community influences. This provides a rationale to examine how IGD and online gambling tendencies jointly relate to adolescents’ gambling engagement within gaming environments, particularly in settings where youth gaming is widespread.

Gambling Research and Services in Hong Kong

Much research has been conducted in Hong Kong related to gambling (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, 2017; The University of Hong Kong, 2017, 2022), but very few primarily focused on gambling in online gaming and eSports environments among youth. The more recent research on potential gambling in online gaming was conducted by Junior Chamber International North District, Sunshine Lutheran Centre, and Caritas Institute of Higher Education (2020). Using about 400 clinical subjects, this study suggested that in-game purchases can be considered as a type of potential gambling with a few extremely high spending cases. Nevertheless, the above study was limited by its selective and restrictive sampling of clinical subjects.

In Hong Kong, free counselling and treatment services for at-risk and addicted gamblers are provided by Tung Wah Group of Hospitals Even Centre, Caritas Addicted Gamblers Counseling Center, Methodist Centre, Zion Social Service - Yuk Lai Hin Gambling Counseling Centre, and Sunshine Lutheran Centre (Centre for Health Protection, 2020). Moreover, the Ping Wo Fund, established by the government in 2003, sponsors NGOs and schools to organize anti-gambling events and promote anti-gambling messages among adolescent and youth communities (The Government of HKSAR, 2018), and funds counselling and treatment centers and research related to gambling disorder (The Government of HKSAR, 2018). Although existing local gambling counselling and treatment services have started paying more attention to the convergence between gaming and gambling, there remains a lack of empirical evidence linking IGD, online gambling symptoms, and adolescents’ gambling engagement within gaming contexts in Hong Kong.

Objectives & Hypotheses

Although prior studies have examined aspects of gaming–gambling convergence (e.g., loot boxes, simulated gambling, and eSports betting), fewer studies have tested IGD, online gambling symptoms, and adolescents’ gambling engagement within gaming contexts within a single analytic framework—particularly in Hong Kong youth. The present study therefore examines whether IGD and online gambling tendency are independently associated with the potential for gambling in online gaming, and whether these links operate through bidirectional indirect pathways. Conceptually, gaming and gambling share overlapping reinforcement structures and reward sensitivities, and exposure to gambling-like mechanics within games may increase opportunities for gambling-related engagement; however, existing evidence is largely correlational and may reflect both exposure effects and shared vulnerabilities (Bandura, 1986; Blaszczynski & Nower, 2002; Brand et al., 2019; Caplan, 2010; King & Delfabbro, 2019; Macey & Hamari, 2019; Strack & Deutsch, 2004; Zendle et al., 2020). Accordingly, we test the following hypotheses:

H1: IGD is positively related to the potential for gambling in online gaming.

H1: IGD is positively related to the potential for gambling in online gaming.

H2: Online gambling tendency is positively related to the potential for gambling in online gaming.

H2: Online gambling tendency is positively related to the potential for gambling in online gaming.

H3: Online gambling tendency mediates the positive relationship between IGD and the potential for gambling in online gaming.

H3: Online gambling tendency mediates the positive relationship between IGD and the potential for gambling in online gaming.

H4: IGD mediates the positive association between online gambling tendency and the potential for gambling in online gaming.

H4: IGD mediates the positive association between online gambling tendency and the potential for gambling in online gaming.

Methods

Research Design

The study used a cross-sectional online survey. This project was approved by the Research Office and the Human Research Ethics Committee (Reference No: CP/RC/2020/0010) at the Hong Kong Shue Yan University. Data were collected using an online survey administered between July and September 2020 in two batches. Participants were recruited via secondary schools, postsecondary institutions, and youth social service agencies, with recruitment intended to cover different geographic areas in Hong Kong. A total number of 1,661 respondents with valid responses were included according to the following inclusion and exclusion criteria in the final data analysis.

Inclusion criteria

Participants were included if they (i) completed the online survey during the study period, (ii) provided informed consent (and parental consent where applicable), and (iii) provided valid responses on the primary variables (IGD, online gambling symptoms, and the potential for gambling in online gaming) and covariates.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded responses that indicated insufficient engagement or invalid data, including (i) survey completion time < 300 s, and (ii) invalid/missing values coded as “99” on key variables used to compute the gambling-in-gaming factor score and covariates.

Measures

Demographic Information

Demographic information, including sex, age, highest educational qualification, current occupation, average monthly income, and region of residence were added as co-variates in the analyses.

Internet Gaming Disorder Scale – Short Form (IGDS – SF9)

IGDS-SF9 was developed by Pontes and Griffins (2015) and it is a widely used measure for Internet gaming disorder. Several cross-cultural psychometric studies were implemented to validate the test in over 15 languages across the world. This measure was the first standardized psychometric tool to assess disordered Internet gaming in accordance with the nine Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) criteria described in the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) published by the American Psychiatric Association (2013). This 9-item measure utilizes a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). The total scores can range from 9 to 45, with higher scores being indicative of higher degrees (risks) of gaming disorder. The Chinese version of IGDS-SF9 was validated in Hong Kong and Taiwan with high reliabilities (α = 0.93 for Hong Kong participants and α = 0.94 for Taiwanese participants) (Leung et al., 2020). The internal constituency of this scale in our sample confirmed high reliability (α = 0.89).

Online Gambling Symptom Assessment Scale (OGSAS)

OGSAS was developed by modifying the widely used and validated scale to assess the severity of online gambling symptoms (GSAS, Kim et al., 2009). The OGSAS includes 12 items to assess average online gambling use (items 1–4), average online gambling frequency (items 5–7), excitement caused by online gambling (item 9), excitement or pleasure associated with winning online (item 10), emotional distress caused by online gambling (item 11), and personal difficulties from online gambling (item 12). Each item scores from 0 to 4 and the total score ranges from 0 to 48. Based on the total score, individuals can be classified with mild (8–20), moderate (21–30), severe (31–40), and extreme (41–48) online gambling symptoms. Those who are identified with moderate, severe, and extreme symptoms would tend to be addicted to online gambling (addictive tendency in online gambling). The results of the validation study confirmed that the modified OGSAS has high reliability (α = 0.83) in terms of assessing the online gambling symptoms (Kalkan & Griffins, 2021). The internal constituency of this scale showed modest internal consistency in this non-clinical sample (α = 0.64).

Potential for Gambling in Online Gaming

The potential for gambling in online gaming was measured by four questions including “Q19 During last month, how often did you engage in the below activities (e.g. made bets on win or lose in online games, engage in online games and make money, purchased/traded in-game gashapon, treasure, weapons, characters, clothes or life of characters)?”, “Q20) During last month, how many hours did you spend to engage in the below activities (e.g. made bets on win or lose in online games, engage in online games and make money, purchased/traded in-game gashapon, treasure, weapons, characters, clothes or life of characters)?”, “Q22) During last month, how often did you engage in/watch eSports and gamble (e.g. made bets on eSports matches)?”, “Q23) During last month, how many hours did you spend on engaging in/watching eSports and gambling (e.g. made bets on eSports matches)?”. We utilized a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (never) to 5 (very often). The internal constituency of responses to these questions in our sample confirmed high reliability (α = 0.73).

Analysis

All analyses were conducted in MATLAB (R2021a). Before hypothesis testing, we screened the data for invalid responses according to the predefined exclusion criteria and computed scale scores for IGD (IGDS-SF9 total) and online gambling symptoms (OGSAS total). The outcome variable (potential for gambling in online gaming) was operationalized using a one-factor solution based on exploratory factor analysis of the four indicators, and the resulting factor score was standardized for subsequent analyses. We then computed descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations among the primary study variables.

To test H1–H2, we conducted two multiple linear regression models with the standardized factor score as the dependent variable: (i) IGD as the focal predictor and (ii) OGSAS as the focal predictor. In both models, sex, age, highest educational qualification, occupation, monthly income, and region of residence were included as covariates. Model results are reported as unstandardized coefficients (b) with 95% confidence intervals and two-tailed p-values.

To test H3–H4, we performed mediation analyses using the MATLAB Mediation Toolbox with 10,000 bootstrap samples to estimate indirect effects and their 95% confidence intervals while adjusting for the same covariates. For distributional assumptions, we examined the distributions of the primary variables (descriptive indices and skewness/kurtosis) and relied on (i) the large sample size and (ii) bootstrap inference for indirect effects to reduce sensitivity to non-normality. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p < 0.05.

Multiple Regression

The first multiple regression model with the IGDS-SF9 score and demographics as predictors to predict the potential for gambling in online gaming indicated that sex, occupation, and place of residence were not significant predictors (p > 0.05). Age was marginally significant, b = 0.01, p = 0.05, 95% C.I. [0.00, 0.02]. The highest educational qualification was a significant negative predictor, b = − 0.05, p < 0.001, 95% C.I. [-0.08, -0.02]. Income was a significant positive predictor, b = 0.04, p < 0.01, 95% C.I. [0.01, 0.06]. Finally, the IGDS-SF9 score was a strong positive predictor, b = 0.04, p < 0.001, 95% C.I. [0.03, 0.04]. The model explained 11% of the variance in the potential for gambling in online gaming (R² = 0.11; Table 4).

Discussion

The aim of the study was to understand the interplays between Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD), online gambling symptoms, and the potential for gambling in online gaming. We collected and analyzed data from Hong Kong youth and found that both IGD and online gambling symptoms were positive predictors of gambling potential in online gaming. Mediation models further suggested that online gambling symptoms partially mediated the association between IGD and gambling potential in online gaming, and that IGD also partially mediated the association between online gambling symptoms and gambling potential in online gaming.

Consistent with H1, we found that IGD was positively related to the potential to gamble in online gaming. This association is consistent with the broader literature indicating that adolescents with problematic gaming often show impaired control, heightened reward sensitivity, and reinforcement-driven engagement patterns that may co-occur with other risky online behaviors (King & Delfabbro, 2016; Petry & O’Brien, 2013; Kuss et al., 2017). Recent Hong Kong evidence similarly suggests that IGD clusters with other risky online behaviors and mental-health correlates in adolescents, supporting the view that gaming-related vulnerabilities may generalize across digital risk contexts (Tsui & Cheng, 2021). Moreover, contemporary convergence research highlights that modern gaming environments increasingly contain gambling-like affordances (e.g., monetized chance-based features) that may facilitate cross-domain movement from gaming-related engagement to gambling-related engagement (González-Cabrera et al., 2023; King & Delfabbro, 2016). Taken together, these findings suggest that IGD-related vulnerabilities and the gaming ecosystem may jointly increase adolescents’ likelihood of engaging in gambling-related behaviors within gaming contexts.

Consistent with H2, online gambling symptoms were positively associated with the potential for gambling in online gaming. This pattern aligns with the idea that both gambling and gaming can be driven by shared psychological processes, including reward seeking and reinforcement learning, and can occur on overlapping digital platforms where transitions between activities are relatively frictionless (Gainsbury et al., 2014; King & Delfabbro, 2016; Pontes & Griffiths, 2014). Importantly, recent reviews of esports-related betting and gambling indicate that esports gamblers tend to be younger and show elevated gambling-risk profiles, suggesting that gaming-adjacent gambling contexts may represent a distinct, higher-risk segment of gambling involvement (Mangat et al., 2024). Relatedly, recent evidence suggests that problematic engagement with gambling-like features (e.g., loot boxes) may operate as a “hinge” between gaming- and gambling-related problems, highlighting how digital monetization structures may contribute to gaming–gambling convergence (González-Cabrera et al., 2024).

Consistent with H3, online gambling symptoms partially mediated the positive association between IGD and gambling potential in online gaming. This finding is consistent with the possibility that adolescents who experience problematic gaming may be more likely to develop gambling-oriented motivations or behaviors when gambling opportunities are embedded in or adjacent to gaming environments, particularly given familiarity with digital payment systems and reinforcement-driven engagement patterns (Gainsbury et al., 2014; King & Delfabbro, 2016; Petry & O’Brien, 2013). Recent longitudinal evidence among adolescents further suggests that engagement with gambling-like monetization (loot box purchasing) can predict later online gambling participation over a short follow-up window, supporting the plausibility of a sequential pathway from gaming-adjacent gambling exposure to gambling involvement (González-Cabrera et al., 2023). At the same time, the broader literature continues to caution that shared vulnerabilities (e.g., impulsivity and sensation-seeking) may contribute to both gaming and gambling problems; therefore, the mediation results should be interpreted as compatible with multiple underlying causal structures rather than definitive temporal mechanisms.

Conversely (H4), IGD partially mediated the association between online gambling symptoms and gambling potential in online gaming. This suggests that in adolescents with gambling symptoms, problematic gaming may function as a behavioral conduit that increases exposure to gambling-like opportunities within games and reinforces persistent engagement patterns, thereby amplifying gambling-related involvement within gaming contexts (Gainsbury et al., 2014; King & Delfabbro, 2016). This bidirectional mediation pattern is also compatible with the view that gaming–gambling convergence may involve mutually reinforcing processes rather than a single “gateway” trajectory, particularly in contemporary digital environments where gaming, monetization, and gambling-adjacent content are increasingly integrated (González-Cabrera et al., 2024; Mangat et al., 2024).

Despite the strengths of the current research, there are some limitations. First, this study is cross-sectional, which cannot establish a causal effect. Second, we did not control for other confounding factors including family and psychosocial factors, which are known risks for gambling. Third, we focused on youth from Hong Kong, which may make it hard to generalize our findings to contexts outside of Hong Kong. Finally, the internal consistency of the online gambling tendency measure was modest in the present study. This may partly reflect the use of a non-clinical adolescent community sample, in which gambling-related symptoms are likely to be less prevalent, more variable in presentation, and more weakly intercorrelated than in clinical or treatment-seeking samples. Nevertheless, the modest reliability may have introduced measurement error and attenuated observed associations; therefore, findings related to online gambling tendency should be interpreted cautiously and replicated in future studies using psychometrically stronger measures and/or higher-risk samples.

There are several theoretical and practical implications of the present findings. Theoretically, our results support a convergence–reinforcement account of gaming–gambling co-risk in adolescence. Rather than viewing gaming and gambling as separate behaviors that merely co-occur, the observed associations and partial mediations suggest cross-domain escalation, whereby vulnerability in one domain may increase susceptibility in the other within a shared digital reward ecology (e.g., rapid feedback, intermittent reinforcement, and monetized chance-based mechanics). Specifically, the partial mediation by IGD in the association between online gambling tendency and gambling engagement in gaming contexts is consistent with the possibility that adolescents with gambling risk may be drawn toward intensive gaming patterns that further increase exposure to gambling-like features. Conversely, the mediation by online gambling tendency in the association between IGD and gambling engagement in gaming contexts suggests that adolescents with problematic gaming may be more likely to develop or express gambling-oriented motivations and behaviors when gambling-like opportunities are embedded in or adjacent to gaming. Taken together, these bidirectional partial pathways align with a mutually reinforcing risk architecture, which is broader than a simple one-way “gateway” narrative; however, causal inference cannot be established given the cross-sectional design.

Practically, these findings indicate that prevention and early intervention should address gaming and gambling risk jointly, particularly for adolescents. At the education and prevention level, school- and community-based programmes should move beyond generic anti-gambling messaging and explicitly target gambling-like mechanics in games (e.g., randomized rewards, in-game spending prompts, and eSports betting exposure), including training on common cognitive distortions (e.g., illusion of control, near-miss effects) and self-regulation strategies. At the platform and consumer-protection level, policymakers and industry stakeholders could consider safeguards that reduce youth exposure and impulsive spending, such as clearer probability disclosures for randomized rewards, age-appropriate spending limits or purchase “friction” (e.g., cooling-off periods, confirmation prompts), and salient warnings after repeated purchases or prolonged play. In Hong Kong, these measures could complement existing youth gambling prevention initiatives by incorporating gaming-specific risk pathways, and service providers may consider brief dual screening for IGD and online gambling symptoms to identify adolescents who may benefit from early support.
