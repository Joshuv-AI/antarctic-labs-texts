A tripartite RpoS-CRP-cAMP-Cra circuit orchestrates metabolic reprogramming during stress adaptation
Authors: Wenbo Chen, Yong Feng, Lin Liu, Rao Ma, Antoine Danchin, Conghui You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe alternative sigma factor RpoS (σS) governs stress adaptation in many bacterial clades, yet how it coordinates stress responses with metabolic demands remains poorly understood. Thus, by integrating RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, and physiological and molecular assays, we redefined the RpoS regulon in Escherichia coli, expanding it to 667 genes and demonstrating that metabolic genes unexpectedly predominate over stress-response targets. RpoS directly drives a massive metabolic reprogramming event: it activates glycolytic and sugar transport genes while simultaneously repressing the trichloroacetic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation. This metabolic pivot is finely tuned by a novel RpoS-CRP-cAMP-Cra tripartite circuit to balance energy efficiency with survival during stationary phase. Furthermore, we provide a mechanistic framework for RpoS-mediated bidirectional regulation rooted in σS-factor competition, where transcriptional efficiency in the absence of RpoS dictates the regulatory mode: highly efficient promoters are repressed by RpoS displacement, whereas weak promoters are activated. This study advances our understanding of transcriptional precision during bacterial adaptation and offers an architectural blueprint for synthetic metabolic engineering.

Introduction

Sigma factors bind to the core RNA polymerase to form holoenzymes (Eσ) [1, 2] and initiate gene transcription. In Escherichia coli, several sigma factors are identified. They include the housekeeping sigma factor σ70 (σD), encoded by rpoD, and six alternative sigma factors: σ24 (σE, rpoE), σ28 (σF, fliA), σ32 (σH, rpoH), σ19 (σI, fecI), σ54 (σN, rpoN), and σ38 (σS, rpoS). As the housekeeping sigma factor, σD regulates genes involved in fundamental cellular processes including basal metabolism, growth, and division. Alternative sigma factors, in contrast, induce specific genes in response to particular stresses—e.g. σN is activated under nitrogen starvation [3], while σH responds to heat shock [4]. Some also control structural genes; σF, for instance, regulates flagellar biosynthesis [5].

Among these, σS (RpoS) is the most critical alternative sigma factor [6]. It plays a central regulatory role during stationary phase, enabling cells to cope with multiple stresses including nutrient limitation, oxidative stress, osmotic pressure, and acid stress, earning it the designation of “general stress response sigma factor.”

RpoS levels are tightly controlled at transcriptional, translational, and post-translational levels. Transcriptional regulation of rpoS mainly involves CRP-cAMP (CRP is also referred to as “catabolite activator protein,” CAP) [7], ArcA [8], Fur [9], and ppGpp [10, 11]. Translationally, RpoS is primarily regulated by small RNAs such as DsrA [12, 13], while post-translational regulation involves proteolytic degradation by the ClpXP protease [14, 15]. Under these combined regulations, RpoS accumulates predominantly during stationary phase [14]. Notably, our previous work showed the RpoS levels increase as growth rate (GR) slows during exponential phase [16], indicating a significant regulatory role of RpoS in slowly growing cells.

Current knowledge suggests RpoS directly regulates ∼300 promoter-controlled genes [17]. Beyond stress resistance, RpoS also regulates several genes involved in central carbon metabolism—the core of cellular metabolic activity, encompassing glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle, etc. Key glycolytic genes (fbaB, pfkB, pykF, gapA, gpmM, pfkA, pgi, and gpmA) are directly activated by RpoS [18–20], while two TCA cycle genes (sucAB) are directly repressed [21, 22]. One PTS component gene, crr, involved in sugar transport, was also found to be controlled by RpoS directly in an in vitro SELEX screening assay [22]. This raises the question: does RpoS play a broader role in carbon/energy metabolism than previously reported? Furthermore, CRP-cAMP and Cra [16, 23–25] are well-established key regulators of central metabolism—how does RpoS-mediated regulation compare to theirs? These questions require a comprehensive investigation.

Most genes in the catalogued RpoS regulon are activated by RpoS, with activation attributed to the competition of sigma factor at the promoter DNA [17]. However, studies on RpoS-mediated repression are limited, leaving the mechanisms still unclear. Early studies often lacked direct evidence of RpoS binding to its repressed promoters, leading to the hypothesis of indirect repression [26]. However, two subsequent studies challenged this hypothesis. In 2014, a genome-wide ChIP-chip/microarray study provided evidence that RpoS directly represses >100 genes [27]. A 2015 study on Salmonella demonstrated that RpoS-mediated repression of succinate dehydrogenase genes requires a specific RpoS DNA-binding site [28], which supports the model of sigma factor competition at the promoter DNA level. Yet, more direct evidence is needed, and a key unanswered question remains: why does sigma factor competition lead to activation in some cases but to repression in others?

The present study employed integrated RNA-seq and ChIP-seq techniques under high-RpoS conditions to redefine the RpoS regulon, expanding it to at least 1.5× the number of genes identified in previous estimates, with genes involved in metabolism now predominating over those related to stress response. We demonstrated the systematic involvement of the regulation of central carbon metabolism by RpoS and identified a regulatory circuit composed of RpoS, CRP-cAMP, and Cra that jointly controls this metabolism. Finally, we studied the molecular basis of the bidirectional regulation controlled by RpoS, proposing that the regulation results from competition between sigma factors and that the transcriptional efficiency in the absence of RpoS binding determines the mode of regulation—high efficiency leads to repression, while low efficiency leads to activation.

Materials and methods

Construction of bacterial strains

All strains in this study were derived from E. coli K12 wild-type strain NCM3722 and are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Four types of derivatives were individually constructed using the λ-Red recombination method [29] as reported [30]: gene knockout (KO) mutants, promoter replacement mutants, strains carrying C-terminal 6× His-tagged proteins, and strains with promoter-lacZ fusion.

To construct gene KO strains, including CY1302 (△rpoS::kan), CY1317 (△rpoS::kan, △clpX::cm), CY1319 (△clpX::cm), CY1325 (△clpX::kan), and CY1565 (△cra::kan, △clpX::cm), the kanamycin or chloramphenicol resistance cassettes were amplified from plasmids pKD4 or pKD3, respectively. Each resistance cassette was flanked by 50-bp sequences homologous to the regions upstream and downstream of the target gene. These fragments were introduced into cells carrying the helper plasmid pKD46 via electroporation. Putative KO strains were selected on antibiotic plates and confirmed by DNA sequencing. The kanamycin resistance gene in CY1317 was further flipped out using plasmid of pCP20 [29] to generate strain CY1539.

To construct the promoter replacement strain CY1551 (Pcra::kan-Plpp), a Cra overexpression (OE) strain, we first amplified two DNA fragments. The first fragment contained a kanamycin resistance gene (kan) flanked by 50 bp of sequence upstream of the cra promoter, amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using a kanamycin-resistant plasmid as the template. The second fragment contained the lpp promoter (spanning positions −1 to −476 bp relative to the lpp start codon ATG) fused to 50 bp of sequence downstream of the cra start codon and was amplified using wild-type E. coli genomic DNA as the template. These fragments were fused via splicing overlap extension (SOE) PCR to generate a chimeric fragment flanked by homologous sequences surrounding the cra start codon and the upstream cra promoter region. This chimeric fragment was electroporated into wild-type E. coli carrying the pKD46 plasmid. Transformants were selected on antibiotic plates and verified by DNA sequencing. Subsequently, the rpoS KO strain CY1572 (Pcra::kan-Plpp, ΔrpoS::cm) was constructed from strain CY1551 using the standard gene KO protocol described above.

Strains carrying C-terminal 6× His-tagged proteins—including CY1335 (rpoS-6× His, derived from CY1325), CY1444 (cra-6× His, derived from NCM3722), and CY1445 (cra-6× His, derived from CY1302)—were constructed as follows. A kanamycin resistance fragment, flanked by 50-bp homologous sequences targeting the insertion site and containing the 6× His coding sequence (CATCACCATCACCATCAC) followed by a stop codon at its 5′ end, was amplified. This fragment was electroporated into recipient cells harboring pKD46. Transformants were screened on antibiotic plates and confirmed by sequencing.

Strains carrying promoter–lacZ fusions—including CY1561 and CY1562 (Picd-lacZ in backgrounds CY1319 and CY1539, respectively) and CWB32 and CWB33 (PmodA-lacZ in backgrounds CY1319 and CY1539, respectively)—were constructed as follows. Two fragments were amplified: (i) a kanamycin resistance gene with 50 bp of sequence upstream of the lacZ promoter at its 5′ end, using a kanamycin-resistant plasmid as the template; and (ii) the target promoter region (icd: −1 to −582 bp; modA: −1 to −401 bp, relative to their respective start codons) fused to 50 bp of sequence downstream of the lacZ start codon, using wild-type E. coli genomic DNA as the template. These fragments were fused via SOE-PCR to create chimeric constructs flanked by homologous sequences surrounding the lacZ start codon and the upstream promoter region. Each chimeric fragment was electroporated into the respective recipient strain carrying pKD46. Transformants were selected on antibiotic plates and verified by sequencing.

To construct the RpoD OE plasmid, a fragment containing the rpoD coding sequence and its 500-bp upstream promoter region was amplified from NCM3722 genomic DNA. The restriction sites EcoRI and HindIII were introduced at both ends of the fragment, which was then cloned into the pBR322 vector. The resulting construct, verified by DNA sequencing, was designated pCWB15. Plasmid pCWB15 was transformed into strains CY1561 and CWB32 to generate strains CWB16 and CWB34, respectively. As controls, the empty pBR322 vector was transformed into CY1561 and CY1562 to generate strains CWB20 and CWB21. Similarly, to construct the fliA OE plasmid, a fragment containing the fliA coding sequence and its 500-bp upstream promoter region was amplified from the genomic DNA of NCM3722. The sites EcoRI and HindIII were added to both ends, and the fragment was cloned into pBR322. The verified construct was designated pCWB38. Plasmid pCWB38 was transformed into strain CWB32 to generate strain CWB41. Concurrently, the empty pBR322 vector was transformed into strains CWB32 and CWB33 to generate strains CWB36 and CWB37, respectively. Details of these plasmids are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Cell growth in water bath shaker

Batch cultures were grown in N−C− minimal medium [K2SO4 (5.74 mM), K2HPO4 (78.02 mM), KH2PO4 (34.54 mM), MgSO4 (0.37 mM), and NaCl (42.78 mM)] supplemented with one of the four carbon substrates [22 mM glucose (glc), 20 mM succinate (suc), 20 mM sorbitol (sor), and 60 mM acetate (ace)] as a sole carbon source. Alternatively, cultures were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth. All growths were carried out in three steps in a 37°C water bath shaker shaking at 250 rpm as previously described [30]. The three steps included: (i) a seed culture in LB broth; (ii) a preculture; and (iii) the experimental culture, both in N−C− minimal medium or LB-rich medium. Specifically, a single colony of E.coli from fresh LB agar plate was inoculated into liquid LB and grown at 37°C with shaking (seed culture). Cells were then diluted 10²–10⁴-fold into N−C− minimal medium or LB-rich medium and grown overnight at 37°C with shaking (preculture). Finally, cells from the overnight preculture were diluted 10²–10⁴-fold into pre-warmed N−C− minimal medium or LB-rich medium and incubated at 37°C with shaking for the experimental culture.

Cell growth in microplates

To investigate RpoS-mediated regulation of the central carbon metabolism, the wild-type strain NCM3722, the RpoS− strain CY1302, the RpoS KO strain CY1317, and the RpoS OE strain CY1319 were individually grown in LB-rich medium to stationary phase. Each culture was then diluted from 104- to 105-fold to obtain the same value of starting OD600 and was inoculated into N−C⁻ minimal medium supplemented with a single carbon source (4.4 mM glc, 4 mM suc, 4 mM sor, or 12 mM ace for NCM3722 and CY1302; 22 mM glc, 20 mM suc, 20 mM sor, or 60 mM ace for CY1317 and CY1319). Growth was monitored in a 24-well plate (LABSELECT, Beijing, China) using a microplate reader (KLAB Technology, Shanghai, China) at 37°C with shaking at 483 rpm. OD600 was recorded every 20 min. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and growth curves were plotted as mean OD600 ± SD over time.

To verify that RpoS and Cra coregulate central carbon metabolism genes in an antagonistic manner, the “superglycolysis” strain CY1565, the “super-TCA” strains CY1572, CY1317, CY1319, CY1302, and NCM3722 were individually grown in LB-rich medium to stationary phase, diluted 104- to 105-fold to obtain the same value of starting OD600, and inoculated into N−C⁻ minimal medium supplemented with 4.4 mM glc, 4 mM suc, 4 mM sor, or 12 mM ace as the sole carbon source. Growth of each culture was then monitored in the microplate reader as described above.

RNA-seq assay and analysis of transcriptome data

To investigate putative RpoS-responsive genes by RNA-seq, we compared specific pairs of strains: the RpoS− strain CY1302 versus the wild-type strain NCM3722, and the RpoS KO strain CY1317 versus the RpoS OE strain CY1319. First, each of the four strains was grown in N−C− minimal medium with 20 mM sor as the carbon source. All cultures were incubated in a 37°C shaking water bath following the three-step protocol as previously described. Bacterial cells for total RNA extraction were harvested during the mid-exponential phase at an OD600 of ∼0.4. Total RNA extraction and RNA-seq library preparation were performed according to established methods [31]. Raw reads obtained from strand-specific RNA sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (PE125) were filtered, and adapters were trimmed. The resulting clean reads were mapped to the E. coli NCM3722 genome (chromosome accession no. NZ_CP011495.1) using Bowtie2. Gene expression levels were quantified using the RPKM (reads per kilobase per million mapped reads) method. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each condition. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the compared conditions were identified using DESeq2, with a threshold of adjusted q-value <0.01 and |log₂ fold change (FC)| > 0.58 (corresponding to FC > 1.5 or FC < 0.67).

ChIP-seq assay and analysis of ChIP-seq data

The ChIP-seq assay was performed according to established procedures [32]. In this study, three independent biological replicates were conducted for strain CY1335 (carrying a C-terminal RpoS-6× His fusion in the CY1325 background), and two independent replicates each were performed for strains CY1444 (carrying a C-terminal Cra-6× His fusion in the NCM3722 background) and CY1445 (carrying a C-terminal Cra-6× His fusion in the CY1302 background).

Briefly, strains CY1335, CY1444, and CY1445 were individually grown in N−C− minimal medium supplemented with 20 mM sor as the sole carbon source. At the mid-exponential growth phase, 40 ml of culture was cross-linked with 1.1 ml of 37% formaldehyde. Following cross-linking, chromatin was sonicated to generate soluble sheared fragments with an average DNA length of 200–500 bp. A 20 μl aliquot of the sheared chromatin was stored at −20°C to serve as the input DNA control. Subsequently, 100 μl of chromatin was immunoprecipitated using an anti-6× His tag antibody (Abcam, ab9108) following standard ChIP protocols.

Sequencing libraries were constructed using the INEXTFLEX® ChIP-Seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina® Sequencing (NOVA-514120, Bioo Scientific) and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform with 150 bp paired-end reads.

For data analysis, low-quality reads were filtered out using Trimmomatic (version 0.38). The remaining clean reads were mapped to the E. coli NCM3722 genome (chromosome accession no. NZ_CP011495.1) using BWA (version 0.7.15). Potential PCR duplicates were removed using SAMtools (version 1.3.1). Peak calling was performed with MACS2 (version 2.1.1.20160309) using default parameters and a q-value cutoff of <0.01, with input DNA serving as the background control. The fold enrichment of each peak, representing the signal strength, was calculated and averaged across replicates.

To analyze RpoS-binding motifs, promoter regions (−50 to −1 bp relative to the transcription start site) of the identified peaks were examined using MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme). The motif logo with the lowest E-value was reported.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Batch cultures of the strains to be tested were grown in N−C− minimal medium supplemented with 20 mM sor as the carbon source until the mid-exponential phase. Total RNA was then extracted as described previously [31]. The cDNA templates were synthesized following the manufacturer’s instructions for the EasyScript® All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (One-Step gDNA Removal) (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Real-time quantitative PCR assays were performed using a qTOWER real-time PCR thermocycler (Analytik Jena). The expression level of each gene was normalized to that of lon, which served as the reference gene. All reactions were run in triplicate. Relative expression was analyzed using the ΔΔCt quantification method of qPCRsoft v3.2, where Ct refers to the cycle threshold. Two to four independent experiments were conducted under each condition. The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Western blot assay

The wild-type strain NCM3722 was grown in N−C− minimal medium supplemented with either 22 mM glc or 20 mM sor as the carbon source until it reached the log phase (OD600 ≈ 0.4). The RpoS KO strain CY1317 and the RpoS OE strain CY1319 were grown in N−C− minimal medium supplemented with 20 mM sor as the carbon source until they reached the log phase (OD600 ≈ 0.4). The cells were centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 3 min and resuspended in 1× phosphate-buffered saline. An aliquot equivalent to 0.1 OD600 of cells from each sample was boiled in SDS–PAGE sample buffer (GenStar, Beijing, China) for 10 min. The prepared samples were subjected to SDS–PAGE. For immunodetection, membranes were incubated with appropriate primary and secondary antibodies, followed by treatment with SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Images were captured using a Tanon-5200 imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). The primary antibody used was anti-RpoS (CUSABIO, Wuhan, China), and the secondary antibody was HRP-conjugated goat antirabbit IgG (Abmart, Shanghai, China).

β-Galactosidase activity assay

The strains to be tested were grown in N−C− minimal medium supplemented with 20 mM sor as the carbon source. At various OD600 (∼0.2 to ∼0.4) following the growth, three to five cell samples (1 ml) were quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Then, β-galactosidase activity was assayed as reported in [30].

Analysis of amino acids

Amino acid analysis was performed using a Thermo Trace 1310 gas chromatograph coupled with a TSQ 9000 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (GC-MS/MS), equipped with a DB-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent J&W Scientific, CA, USA). HPLC-grade reagents, amino acid standards, methoxyamine hydrochloride, and BSTFA containing 1% Trimethylchlorosilane (TMCS) were used for sample preparation. Samples were extracted with precooled methanol/water (3:1, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid and succinic-2,2,3,3-d4 as an internal standard. The extracts were ultrasonicated, incubated at −20°C, and centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatants were dried and subjected to derivatization via oximation (at 37°C for 90 min) followed by silylation (at 70°C for 60 min). Chromatographic separation was achieved using helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.2 ml/min. A 1 μl sample was injected in splitless mode with an injector temperature of 260°C, and separation was performed using a gradient oven program. Mass spectrometric detection was carried out in electron ionization mode with an ion source temperature of 300°C and a transfer line temperature of 280°C. Data were acquired in selected reaction monitoring mode over a mass range of m/z 40–600. Amino acids were identified by comparison with authentic standards and quantified using external calibration curves, with manual correction of peak integration where necessary.

Results

Deciphering the RpoS regulon

Comparing transcriptomes under conditions of high versus low levels of transcription or sigma factor can reveal a more comprehensive set of its targets. To fully characterize the genes controlled by RpoS, we investigated two scenarios featured by high levels of RpoS.

First, our previous transcriptomic data showed that during the log phase of bacterial growth, the level of RpoS expression increases as the GR decreases [16] (Fig. 1A); specifically, the RpoS expression was found to be three times higher when sor—a carbon source supporting slow growth—was used as the sole carbon source when compared to that of glucose. Therefore, comparing the transcriptomes of the RpoS wild-type strain (RpoS wt) NCM3722 and the RpoS KO strain (RpoS−) CY1302 grown in sor could uncover more RpoS-regulated genes (Condition 1). Second, RpoS is subject to degradation by the ClpXP protease [14, 15] (Fig. 1B), leading to RpoS significant accumulation in a clpX KO strain. Therefore, comparing transcriptomic changes between the clpX single KO (resulting in RpoS OE; referred to here as the RpoS OE strain, CY1319) and the clpX rpoS double KO (RpoS KO strain, CY1317) is expected to identify a substantial number of RpoS-regulated genes (Condition 2). The western blot analysis confirmed that the RpoS levels increased markedly under both conditions, with greater accumulation observed in the RpoS OE strain (CY1319) (Fig. 1C and D).

We analyzed the transcriptomic changes of strains under these two conditions, with three biological replicates for each. Using thresholds of q < 0.01 and |log₂ FC| > 0.58 (corresponding to FC > 1.5 or FC < 0.67), we identified 601 DEGs in the first condition (Fig. 1E) and 689 DEGs in the second (Fig. 1F) (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5).

To determine which genes are directly regulated by RpoS, we performed a ChIP-seq assay to map the in vivo DNA binding sites of RpoS under conditions of significant RpoS accumulation (i.e. in the clpX KO background). The ChIP-seq experiments were performed with three biological replicates. In total, 1114 binding sites located upstream of the start codon of genes were identified (Fig. 1G) (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7), covering the promoter regions of over 1300 genes, since some binding sites were located in overlapping regions of divergent promoters.

By integrating together the RNA-seq and ChIP-seq results, we identified a total of 667 genes whose expression is directly regulated by RpoS (Supplementary Table S8). To our knowledge, our study reports the largest number of genes directly regulated by RpoS to date (Fig. 1H), significantly expanding the reported RpoS regulon [27, 33]. Among the 667 genes, 380 genes are reported for the first time as being directly controlled by RpoS (Fig. 1I). RpoS specifically activates the expression of 478 genes (driven by 370 promoters) and represses that of 189 genes (controlled by 137 promoters), highlighting its dual role in transcriptional regulation (Fig. 1J). We also observed that the average FC of genes activated by RpoS significantly exceeds that of genes repressed by RpoS (Fig. 1K). Using the MEME software, a consensus binding motif of RpoS located in the −10 region of these promoters was identified (Fig. 1L), which is consistent with previously reported motifs [20, 34].

RpoS regulates core carbon metabolism by activating glycolysis and inhibiting the TCA cycle

We performed a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the biological functions of genes directly regulated by RpoS. The results revealed that among the top 20 enriched pathways, 15 were pathways involved in metabolism, indicating that the majority of genes within these top 20 pathways are associated with metabolism (Supplementary Fig. S1). Furthermore, most of these metabolism-related genes were linked to carbon source metabolism and energy metabolism (Fig. 2A). This implies that a primary function of RpoS is to control carbon source and energy metabolism. Notably, these top 20 pathways encompass nearly 200 genes directly controlled by RpoS, a number substantially larger than that of RpoS-controlled genes related to the stress response (Supplementary Fig. S2).

In the regulation of carbon and energy metabolism, the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway and the TCA cycle play pivotal roles. We found that RpoS systematically regulates the expression of genes functioning in both pathways. Figure 2B displays the FCs of DEGs belonging to these pathways, while Fig. 2C illustrates their specific positions within the metabolic networks.

Consistent with previous reports [18–20], genes in the glycolysis pathway are generally activated by RpoS, and our work provides the first systemic and direct evidence of this regulatory feature in vivo. Additionally, although several glycolytic genes were excluded from the genes we retained for the RpoS regulon due to modest expression changes (|log₂ FC| < 0.58, Supplementary Fig. S3), our ChIP-seq analysis revealed RpoS-binding peaks at their promoters. This suggests that these genes are also directly activated by RpoS, albeit to a lesser extent. The expression of several genes involved in carbon source transport is also positively regulated by RpoS, including ptsG and crr (functioning in glc transport) and manXYZ (functioning in mannose transport). Both glc and mannose require the glycolysis pathway for catabolism.

In contrast, the expression of TCA cycle genes is predominantly directly repressed by RpoS, with the exception of acnA, an isozyme of acnB. Previous reports indicate that the expression of acnA is activated by RpoS [35], which aligns with our data. We observed that the levels of acnB expression are substantially higher than those of acnA (Supplementary Fig. S4A and S4B). As previously reported, this suggests that AcnB serves as the primary catabolic enzyme, whereas AcnA functions mainly as a maintenance or survival enzyme during nutrient limitation or oxidative stress [35]. Notably, the combined expression of these two genes is higher in the presence of RpoS than in its absence (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Therefore, we conclude that the net regulatory effect of RpoS on the aconitate hydratases encoded by acnB and acnA in the TCA cycle is repressive.

Similarly, maeB and pck, located at the entry point of the gluconeogenesis pathway, are repressed by RpoS. Interestingly, we discovered that maeA, an isozyme of maeB, is directly activated by RpoS, despite showing a modest transcriptional change (|log₂FC| < 0.58, Supplementary Fig. S3). While the expression levels of maeB and maeA do not differ significantly (Supplementary Fig. S4D and S4E), the total expression of these two genes is higher in the RpoS-deficient background than that in the wild type (Supplementary Fig. S4F). Thus, we propose that the net regulatory mode of RpoS on the malate dehydrogenases encoded by maeB and maeA in the gluconeogenesis pathway is also repressive.

Given the tight coupling between the TCA cycle and ATP production via oxidative phosphorylation, we next examined the response to RpoS of the genes involved in this process. We found that RpoS directly represses the expression of genes encoding NADH dehydrogenase I and ATP synthase within the oxidative phosphorylation [36] system (Fig. 2D). Key proteins involved in oxidative phosphorylation are depicted in Fig. 2E.

Figure 2F presents the first report of RpoS-binding peaks at the promoters of these core carbon and energy metabolism genes. In total, 14 novel RpoS-binding peaks were identified.

Since amino acid synthesis is closely linked to core carbon metabolism [30, 37], we measured the intracellular levels of 20 free L-amino acids in strains CY1317 (RpoS KO) and CY1319 (RpoS OE) when they were grown to the log phase. Among all detected amino acids showing significant changes (Fig. 3A), the levels of gly, ser, and cys—whose carbon skeletons are derived from the glycolysis pathway [38]—were significantly higher in the RpoS OE strain than in the RpoS KO strain (Fig. 3B). Conversely, the levels of Asp and Asn, whose carbon skeletons are derived from the TCA cycle [39], were significantly higher in the RpoS KO strain than in the RpoS OE strain (Fig. 3B). These findings further corroborate the activating role of RpoS in the glycolysis pathway and its repressive role in the TCA pathway.

Subsequently, we investigated the growth of RpoS wild-type and OE strains, along with their corresponding RpoS KO counterparts, on glycolytic or gluconeogenic carbon sources (Fig. 3C and D). Glycolytic carbon sources, such as glc and sor [16], require the glycolysis pathway to be metabolized. Gluconeogenic carbon sources [16], such as ace and suc, rely on the TCA pathway. We found that the wild-type strain NCM3722 exhibited a shorter lag phase than the RpoS-deficient strain CY1302 when grown in a medium with glc or sor as the sole carbon source, indicating more rapid utilization of these substrates by the wild-type. Conversely, when grown on suc or ace, the lag phase of strain CY1302 was shorter than that of NCM3722, suggesting that the RpoS-deficient strain utilizes these carbon sources more rapidly (Fig. 3C). Similar growth patterns were observed for strains CY1317 and CY1319 under these conditions (Fig. 3D). These results provide further evidence supporting the repressive regulation of TCA cycle-related genes and the activating regulation of glycolysis-related genes by RpoS.

Notably, the regulatory effect of CRP-cAMP on genes involved in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and the TCA cycle contrasts with that of RpoS. Specifically, two glycolytic genes are activated by RpoS but repressed by CRP-cAMP, classifying them into the “Crp_R RpoS_A” group. Conversely, genes encoding TCA cycle and gluconeogenic enzymes are repressed by RpoS but activated by CRP-cAMP, placing them in the “Crp_A RpoS_R” group. Interestingly, both CRP-cAMP and RpoS coactivate four genes related to sugar transport, which are categorized as the “Crp_A RpoS_A” group. Figure 4B summarizes the number of genes in each category. Overall, CRP-cAMP and RpoS exhibit antagonistic regulation over the majority of genes related to central carbon metabolism. We next quantitatively compared the regulatory effects of CRP-cAMP and RpoS on this set of central carbon metabolism-associated genes (Fig. 4C). Our results reveal that CRP-cAMP exerts a significantly stronger regulatory influence on these targets than RpoS. Albeit with distinct regulatory influences, CRP-cAMP and RpoS coordinately modulate the central carbon metabolism pathway. While they coactivate the expression of genes involved in carbon source transport, they exert antagonistic effects on the regulation of glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and the TCA cycle (Fig. 4D).

RpoS and Cra coregulate central carbon metabolism antagonistically

Cra is yet another key transcription factor regulating central carbon metabolism [16, 25, 42]. Its activity is inhibited by fructose-1-phosphate (F1P), a metabolite of the glycolytic pathway [43, 44]. When bacteria are grown on gluconeogenic carbon sources, F1P levels are lower than those observed during growth on glycolytic carbon sources [45], resulting in higher Cra activity. Consequently, genes activated (or repressed) by Cra directly or indirectly exhibit higher (or lower) expression levels under gluconeogenic conditions compared to glycolytic conditions [16].

We integrated our earlier findings with the responses of central carbon metabolism-related genes to gluconeogenic and glycolytic carbon sources with their responses to RpoS (Fig. 5A). The data reveal that RpoS and Cra exert antagonistic regulatory effects on the expression of these genes. Specifically, RpoS activates glycolysis-related genes, whereas Cra represses them. Conversely, RpoS represses TCA cycle-related genes, while Cra activates them. Furthermore, our previous work showed that while CRP-cAMP and RpoS coactivate genes involved in carbon source transport, Cra represses these same genes, indicating that RpoS and Cra also exert antagonistic regulation on carbon transport genes. Supplementary Fig. S5A summarizes the number of genes coregulated by RpoS and Cra, highlighting the pervasive antagonistic relationship between these two regulators in controlling central carbon metabolism.

Given this widespread interaction, we hypothesized a broader connection between Cra and RpoS. Using ChIP-seq to map in vivo DNA binding of Cra, we identified Cra binding peaks in the promoter regions of over 200 genes (Supplementary Table S9). This ChIP-seq analysis was performed in two strains: one carrying wild-type RpoS and the other lacking RpoS. Notably, ∼75% of these promoters also harbor RpoS-binding peaks (Supplementary Fig. S5B), suggesting a tight interplay between Cra and RpoS in gene regulation. Supplementary Fig. S5C illustrates representative ChIP-seq binding peaks of both Cra and RpoS at the promoters of several genes, including central carbon metabolism markers (acnB, icd, and pykF) and an amino acid transporter gene (proV).

In summary, RpoS and Cra coregulate the central carbon metabolism genes in an antagonistic manner (Supplementary Fig. S5D). RpoS activates glycolysis and carbon source uptake genes while repressing the TCA cycle. In contrast, Cra represses glycolysis and carbon source uptake genes while activating the TCA cycle. Nevertheless, it is reasonable to infer that RpoS and Cra execute distinct, stage-specific regulatory functions in E. coli central carbon metabolism at their respective activity maxima: RpoS dominates the stationary phase, whereas Cra is the primary regulator during exponential growth especially when bacteria are grown in glycolytic carbon sources.

To further substantiate these antagonistic regulations, we engineered strains designed to favor either glycolytic or TCA metabolic fluxes. As shown in Fig. 5B, in an RpoS OE background, further deletion of cra (Cra KO) eliminates Cra-mediated repression of glycolysis and activation of the TCA cycle. This amplifies the RpoS-driven activation of glycolysis and repression of the TCA cycle, creating a “superglycolysis” system expected to utilize glycolytic carbon sources more efficiently. Conversely, in a Cra OE background, deletion of rpoS removes RpoS-mediated repression of the TCA cycle and activation of glycolysis, resulting in a “super-TCA” system optimized for gluconeogenic carbon source utilization (Fig. 5C). We constructed strains harboring each of these two systems. Notably, in the Cra OE background, Cra was overexpressed ∼20-fold compared to the wild-type strain (Supplementary Fig. S6). The distinct growth phenotypes of the two systems on glycolytic versus gluconeogenic carbon sources validated our hypothesis: the strain harboring the “superglycolysis” system exhibited optimal growth on glycolytic carbon sources, whereas the strain carrying the “super-TCA” system performed better on gluconeogenic carbon sources (Fig. 5D and E).

Molecular mechanism of the RpoS-mediated dual regulation of gene expression

It is widely believed that RpoS directly activates gene expression by competing with other sigma factors, particularly RpoD. We investigated whether sigma factor competition also governs RpoS-mediated direct repression.

If the sigma factor competition hypothesis applies to RpoS-mediated direct repression, promoters with high RpoS-binding occupancy will tend to be the most down-regulated upon RpoS OE. We conducted a Pearson correlation analysis integrating ChIP-seq and RNA-seq datasets of 189 RpoS-repressed genes regulated by 137 promoters (Supplementary Tables S6–S8) and found that RpoS chromosomal DNA binding signals have a statistically significant positive correlation with the FCs in RpoS-mediated target gene repression (Fig. 6A), which supports the competition hypothesis.

The competition mechanism underlying RpoS-mediated repression was further verified at the individual gene level. We used icd as an example given the widespread repressive effect of RpoS on genes in the TCA cycle (Fig. 2). Based on the conserved RpoS-binding motif, an RpoS-binding site overlapping with the −10 region of the known RpoD binding site in the icd promoter was identified (Fig. 6B). This indicates that RpoS may repress icd expression by competing with RpoD. Next, we constructed icd promoter-lacZ fusions in both of the RpoS KO and RpoS OE strains, generating strains CY1561 and CY1562, respectively. The Picd-lacZ activity in the RpoS KO strain CY1561 was 1.5 times that in the RpoS OE strain CY1562 (Supplementary Fig. S7A), consistent with our previous transcriptomic data and confirming that the expression of icd is repressed by RpoS. We then introduced an empty pBR322 vector into these strains, respectively, generating strains CWB20 and CWB21. The expression pattern of Picd-lacZ remained unchanged in these two strains indicating that the empty vector did not affect gene expression (Fig. 6C).

We further transformed the RpoS OE strain (CY1562) with a pBR322 plasmid carrying overexpressed RpoD (Supplementary Fig. S8A), generating strain CWB16. In the CWB16 stain, the Picd-lacZ expression increased significantly to a level comparable to that in the RpoS KO strain (CWB21) (Fig. 6C), suggesting that RpoS-mediated repression is abolished when RpoD is overexpressed. This supports the idea that RpoS and RpoD compete for binding to the core enzyme in the regulation of the icd expression.

We also investigated another gene, modA, which has been reported to contain binding sites for both RpoD [46] and RpoF [47]. Consistent with previous studies, our earlier work confirmed that the modA promoter is strongly regulated by RpoF [48]. An RpoS-binding peak was also detected in the modA promoter region (Fig. 6D). The predicted RpoS-binding site overlapped with the −10 region of the RpoF binding site (Fig. 6E), suggesting that RpoS could regulate modA by competing with RpoF in binding to the core enzyme.

We constructed a modA promoter with lacZ fusion and confirmed that the modA expression was indeed repressed by RpoS (Supplementary Fig. S7B). When RpoF was overexpressed in the RpoS OE strain (Supplementary Fig. S8B), the modA expression increased (Fig. 6F), indicating that high levels of RpoF counteracted RpoS-mediated repression. This suggests that the modA promoter has higher transcription efficiency when bound by RpoF than by RpoS. In contrast, OE of RpoD in the RpoS OE strain (Supplementary Fig. S8C) did not alter the PmodA-lacZ expression (Fig. 6F), indicating that competition between RpoS and RpoD is not significant for modA regulation. Together, these results support the conclusion that RpoS represses modA expression by competing with RpoF.

In summary, these results support that RpoS-mediated direct repression stems from sigma factor competition at relevant promoter regions. This prompted us to explore the mechanisms that govern RpoS-exerted regulatory mode, whether positive or negative.

We first compared the RpoS-binding affinity to these groups of promoters obtained from ChIP-seq experiments. It appeared that RpoS showed no significant binding difference to either one of these two groups as judged from the strength of the RpoS-binding signals (Supplementary Fig. S9A). This suggests that the binding affinity is not the decisive factor in whether RpoS activates or represses gene expression.

Next, we analyzed the binding of previously reported sigma factors to the promoters of genes identified in this study as either activated or repressed by RpoS. Based on the information collected in the EcoCyc [49] and RegulonDB [33] databases, we observed that the majority of promoters—whether activated or repressed by RpoS—are recognized by sigma 70 (RpoD) (Supplementary Fig. S9B), with the remaining recognized by others.

Using previously obtained transcriptomic data, we found that genes activated by RpoS exhibited significantly lower expression levels than those repressed by RpoS, particularly under high RpoS-expressed conditions (Fig. 6G). These transcriptomic data were derived from wild-type strains where all sigma factors are present. Although the presence of RpoS may influence the interpretation of the response of non-RpoS sigma factors (σD, F…: e.g. RpoD), the consistently lower expression of RpoS-activated promoters and higher expression of RpoS-repressed promoters suggest that, in the absence of RpoS, the difference in σD, F… response between the two groups would be even more pronounced. We further compared the expression of these genes in the two RpoS null backgrounds (strains CY1317 and CY1302). In both cases, the genes with RpoS-activated promoters showed significantly lower expression than those with RpoS-repressed promoters (Fig. 6H). This evidence suggested that the RpoS-activated promoters have a weaker intrinsic response to σD, F…. When bound by the holoenzyme occupied by σD, F… (EσD, F…), these promoters led to relatively weak transcription, but transcribed more efficiently when binding EσS. In contrast, RpoS-repressed promoters exhibited a strong σD, F… response, transcribing more efficiently when binding EσD, F…, and less efficiently when binding EσS.

In summary, we propose that the dual regulatory role of RpoS—encompassing both activation and repression—originates from competitive interactions among sigma factors (Fig. 7). For RpoS-repressed promoters, prior binding by non-RpoS sigma factors (e.g. RpoD) sustains high transcriptional efficiency. When RpoS outcompetes these factors for promoter occupancy, transcriptional efficiency declines, leading to the repression of gene expression. Conversely, for RpoS-activated promoters, non-RpoS sigma factor binding results in low transcriptional efficiency, whereas RpoS binding enhances transcription, driving gene activation. Thus, the transcriptional efficiency under non-RpoS sigma factor binding conditions serves as the key determinant of the RpoS-mediated regulatory mode.

Model for the bidirectional regulation of gene expression by RpoS via sigma factor competition in E. coli. At low cellular levels of RpoS, one of the non-RpoS sigma factors (σD,F…) dominates core RNAP binding, forming RNAP holoenzyme of EσD,F…. For RpoS-repressed promoters, EσD,F… drives strong transcription (-), whereas for RpoS-activated promoters, it mediates only weak transcription (+). At high RpoS levels, σS outcompetes other sigma factors to form the EσS holoenzyme. For promoters repressed by EσS, it supports only weak transcription (-), leading to RpoS-mediated repression. Conversely, for promoters activated by EσS, it drives strong transcription (+), resulting in RpoS-mediated activation. This model proposes that the regulatory outcome of RpoS is determined by the inherent transcriptional activity of the promoter when occupied by non-RpoS sigma factors.

Discussion

With this study, our understanding of RpoS-mediated regulation has reached an unprecedented level of sophistication. This framework allows us to deepen our understanding of transcriptional precision in bacterial adaptation, particularly as bacteria enter the stationary phase.

First, this work significantly expands the RpoS regulon (Fig. 1), thereby providing a comprehensive understanding of its regulatory roles through the functional characterization of a larger number of genes directly controlled by RpoS. Our data reveal that the number of metabolic genes regulated by RpoS far exceeds that of genes related to environmental stress (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. S2), indicating that RpoS plays a crucial role in metabolic regulation.

Among the various metabolic pathways, central carbon metabolism plays a central role. We have discovered that RpoS exerts direct and systematic regulation on this metabolism: it activates the expression of genes related to glycolysis and genes encoding transporters for glycolytic carbon sources, while simultaneously repressing the expression of genes involved in gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 2B–E). Thus, when cells enter the stationary phase, the accumulation of RpoS reprograms the central metabolic system. What advantages does this reprogramming offer bacteria?

During the stationary phase, bacteria face multiple environmental stresses, and accumulated RpoS plays a central role in this adaptation by orchestrating stress resistance through the induction of stress-responsive genes [6, 17, 50] (Supplementary Fig. S2). However, in the presence of molecular oxygen—a growth condition explored in the present study, combating oxidative stress is a critical survival strategy in this phase, as responses to stresses like nutrient limitation [19, 31], osmotic pressure [51, 52], and acid stress [53, 54] are consistently associated with the induction of oxidative stress response proteins—key markers of oxidative stress. A complementary strategy involves reducing intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. The coupling of the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation is a major endogenous source of ROS, as these pathways inherently produce ROS during energy metabolism [55]. RpoS mitigates oxidative damage by suppressing the expression of TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation-related genes, thereby lowering ROS levels. Concurrently, to maintain basic energy requirements, RpoS activates the glycolytic pathway to sustain ATP production. Alternatively, this metabolic reprogramming could redirect cellular metabolic resources toward the biosynthesis of storage compounds and secondary metabolites, a physiological outcome that likely functions to deter competing microbial species in the niche [56, 57]. Thus, through remodeling central carbon metabolism—suppressing energy-intensive pathways while activating glycolysis—RpoS enables bacterial metabolism to balance survival and energy efficiency, adapting precisely to the growth constraints of the stationary phase.

Moreover, this study pioneers the discovery that RpoS acts as a key, direct regulator of central carbon metabolism—a metabolic network previously thought to be only dominated by the transcriptional control of CRP-cAMP and Cra [25]. By bringing this new regulatory layer to the fore, this work reshapes our understanding of how bacteria coordinate growth-supporting pathways under stress. To contextualize these findings, it is critical to compare the regulatory contributions of RpoS to those of CRP-cAMP and Cra in the central carbon metabolism. By referring to our prior research [16], we observed that RpoS exhibits antagonistic effects on glycolysis and TCA cycle-related genes relative to CRP-cAMP and Cra (Fig. 8). In contrast, RpoS acts synergistically with CRP-cAMP to positively regulate carbon source transporter genes. Consistent with our findings, a recent study reported that RpoS and CRP-cAMP coregulate the response of E. coli to acid stress [58]. However, given the distinct regulatory magnitudes of CRP-cAMP and RpoS on the regulation of central carbon metabolism (Fig. 4A), we propose that CRP-cAMP remains the predominant regulator of the central carbon metabolism, especially in the log phase of fast-growing bacteria, with RpoS functioning as an auxiliary modulator whose supportive regulatory activity is much enhanced during stress adaptation. This auxiliary role of RpoS could also be extended to its functional interplay with Cra during their joint control of central carbon metabolism (Fig.5A).

Arrows represent activating effects and bars represent repressive effects. P1–P3, C1–C3, and R1–R3 individually indicate regulations mediated by CRP-cAMP; Cra and RpoS. Thicker lines correspond to stronger regulatory strengths mediated by CRP-cAMP.

Ultimately, these various regulatory configurations allow for the finely tuning of the expression of central carbon metabolism genes, thereby precisely matching the energy and metabolic intermediate requirements of cells in the stationary phase. The exquisite control exerted by the RpoS-CRP-cAMP-Cra circuit over the central carbon metabolism not only underscores the physiological importance of this pathway but also highlights its role as a master coordinator of bacterial adaptation to stress, ensuring survival through metabolic flexibility and stability.

Finally, current understanding of how RpoS represses gene expression remains limited; our research has addressed this gap by exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying RpoS-mediated bidirectional regulation of gene expression (Fig. 6). Consistent with its activation mechanism, our analysis indicates that the repression mechanism similarly arises from competition among sigma factors (Fig. 7). Moreover, we propose that the key determinant of the regulatory mode RpoS exhibits upon successful competition and binding to its target promoter is the transcriptional efficiency under prior binding by other sigma factors (e.g. RpoD). For promoters with high transcriptional efficiency when bound by RpoD, RpoS binding results in transcriptional repression. Conversely, for promoters with low efficiency, RpoS binding significantly enhances transcription, leading to activation. In support of our model, a report demonstrated that RNA polymerase bound by σD exhibits stronger binding to RpoS-repressed promoters compared to RpoS-activated promoters [27]. This finding suggests that the repressed promoters may have higher transcriptional efficiency than the activated ones, aligning with our hypothesis that prior transcriptional efficiency dictates the regulatory outcome of RpoS. This integrated model not only clarifies the molecular basis of RpoS-mediated bidirectional regulation but also provides a mechanistic framework for predicting how RpoS modulates gene expression across different promoter contexts, advancing our understanding of bacterial stress adaptation at the transcriptional level.

In conclusion, this study systematically unravels the RpoS regulon and elucidates the molecular mechanisms underlying RpoS-mediated bidirectional gene regulation. Through integrated physiological and molecular analyses, it demonstrates how bacteria dynamically balance stress response pathways with central carbon metabolism under adverse environmental conditions. This homeostatic control is orchestrated by a sophisticated regulatory circuit comprising RpoS, CRP-cAMP, and Cra, which seamlessly integrates stress adaptation signals with metabolic flux control. Consequently, this regulatory axis emerges as a promising target for the precision modulation of central carbon metabolism in synthetic biology and metabolic engineering applications, providing a molecular blueprint to optimize microbial resilience and metabolic efficiency in both industrial and environmental contexts.
