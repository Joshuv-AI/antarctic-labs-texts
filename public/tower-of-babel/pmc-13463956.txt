Knowledge, attitude, and practice toward post-stroke depression among stroke caregivers: a cross-sectional study and path analysis
Authors: Wei Zhang, Haoxiang Zhang, Yong Xu, Haixia Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-stroke depression (PSD) significantly affects stroke recovery, and caregivers play a crucial role in managing this condition. However, little is known about caregivers’ knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) regarding PSD in China. This study aims to evaluate the KAP of caregivers of stroke patients concerning PSD and identify factors influencing these dimensions. A cross-sectional study was conducted at the China Rehabilitation Research Center, Beijing. A total of 497 valid questionnaires, covering demographic information, knowledge, attitude, and practice related to PSD, were collected from caregivers of stroke patients. Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics, logistic regression, and structural equation modeling (SEM). The mean age of caregivers was 42.65 ± 13.97 years, with the majority being women (62.98%) and married (81.49%). The average knowledge score was 18.37 ± 9.77 (57.4% of the maximum score), indicating insufficient knowledge. The scores of attitude and practice were 33.17 ± 3.89 (82.92%) and 22.51 ± 3.13 (90.04%), respectively, indicating a positive attitude and favorable practice. Significant differences were found in knowledge based on gender, education, income, stroke duration, and whether patients had undergone depression screening. Knowledge was positively associated with attitude (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.08–1.15, p < 0.001) and practice (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02–1.12, p = 0.006). SEM analysis revealed positive associations between knowledge, attitude, and practice (all p < 0.001), with knowledge and attitude influencing practice. Caregivers exhibited insufficient knowledge about PSD, particularly regarding the recognition of PSD symptoms, its potential severity (e.g., association with self-harm or suicidal tendencies), and appropriate management strategies, but displayed positive attitudes and practices. Factors like stroke duration, depression screening, and caregiver education significantly influenced these dimensions. These knowledge gaps may limit caregivers’ ability to identify and respond appropriately to PSD, despite their generally positive attitudes and practices. The findings suggest the need for targeted and structured educational interventions to improve caregivers’ ability to recognize and manage PSD, which may ultimately enhance patient outcomes and caregiving quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05020-2.Keywords: Post-stroke depression, Caregivers, Knowledge, Attitude, Practice, Structural equation modeling, Stroke

Introduction

Stroke is a globally prevalent and severe neurological disorder with high incidence and mortality, ranking third among the primary causes of death and fourth among causes of disability-adjusted life years [1]. The consequences of a stroke are wide-ranging and can be influenced by the type of stroke, the specific brain region affected, the patient’s age, any coexisting health conditions, and the timeliness of the treatment [2]. While acute therapy has improved outcomes, enabling better survival and functional recovery, the effects of stroke extend beyond physical health, often leading to mental health challenges such as post-stroke depression (PSD) [3]. PSD is characterized by emotional symptoms, including low mood, loss of interest, self-blame, and despair, which can severely hinder recovery, impair rehabilitation outcomes, and prolong recuperation periods [4, 5].

In the recovery process, stroke patients heavily rely on the support of their family members or relatives [6, 7]. In China, due to cultural norms and limited primary healthcare resources, family members are the primary caregivers for post-stroke patients [8]. However, inadequate knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) among caregivers may hinder their ability to provide optimal care, leading to unfavorable patient outcomes [9–11]. Caregivers’ awareness and attitudes toward PSD significantly influence their approach to managing stroke-related complications, which can notably impact rehabilitation effectiveness and reduce the occurrence of long-term effects [12, 13].

The structured approach known as the KAP survey methodology provides a systematic way to assess caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors [14, 15]. Several studies have explored knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding depression among different populations in China, including university students, adolescents, and community residents, consistently showing insufficient knowledge, persistent stigma, and gaps in symptom recognition and help-seeking behavior [16, 17]. For example, studies among university students and adolescents reported inadequate knowledge despite relatively positive attitudes, while stigma and limited recognition of depressive symptoms remained common barriers to appropriate mental health practices [16]. These findings suggest that public understanding of depression in China remains suboptimal and may influence caregiving behaviors and health-seeking practices. Therefore, understanding caregivers’ KAP of PSD would be necessary to reveal deficiencies in awareness and guide future care strategies. Despite the importance of this issue, although several studies have investigated KAP regarding PSD in China, including among patients and their family members, research specifically focusing on caregivers of stroke patients remains limited, particularly in the context of PSD [18, 19]. Therefore, this study aims to explore the KAP of caregivers regarding PSD, with the goal of identifying key areas for improvement. We hypothesized that caregivers of stroke patients may demonstrate relatively more positive attitudes and practices due to caregiving experience, but still have insufficient knowledge regarding PSD compared with expected optimal levels. Our findings will contribute to enhancing support and guidance measures during rehabilitation, ultimately improving recovery outcomes and quality of life for stroke patients.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the China Rehabilitation Research Center, Beijing, between June 10th, 2023, and August 2nd, 2023. The study enrolled caregivers of stroke patients, including both family caregivers (e.g., parents, children, spouses, and other relatives) and paid caregivers. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of China Rehabilitation Research Center (CRRC-IEC-RF-SC-005-01), and all participants provided informed consent.

Inclusion criteria were the following: (1) caregivers linked to patients diagnosed with “stroke” under the “Chinese Diagnostic Criteria for Major Cerebrovascular Diseases 2019”; (2) patients either underwent inpatient rehabilitation at our hospital post-2020 or were current inpatient/outpatient stroke patients based on the same diagnosis criteria, provided that their caregivers met the minimum caregiving duration requirement; (3) caregivers who have provided care for a minimum of three months (self-reported) and could complete the survey questionnaire; caregivers with less than three months of caregiving experience were excluded to ensure sufficient exposure to the caregiving process. Caregivers with a history of mental illness and those displaying reluctance to participate in the survey were excluded. Non-caregiving individuals (i.e., those not directly involved in the daily care of stroke patients) were also excluded to ensure the relevance of responses. Both family and paid caregivers were included to comprehensively capture real-world caregiving patterns in stroke care settings.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was designed by the authors based on previously published relevant literature [10, 15] and the “Chinese Expert Consensus on Clinical Practice of Post-Stroke Depression”, which provides standardized diagnostic criteria and clinical assessment recommendations for PSD. A preliminary pilot survey involving a small sample of 44 respondents was carried out to assess reliability. The resulting Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.867 indicated an acceptable level of internal consistency.

The final questionnaire included four sections: demographic information from both caregiver and patient, the dimension of knowledge, the dimension of attitude, and the dimension of practice. In this cross-sectional observational study, data regarding patients’ post-stroke depression status were collected via caregiver self-report. Given the non-interventional design, the study relied entirely on these self-reported data without independent clinical diagnostic confirmation.

In the demographic section, the variable “relationship with the stroke patient” included categories such as parents, children, spouses, other relatives, and paid caregivers, to distinguish between family and non-family caregiving roles. The knowledge dimension comprised 16 questions. For questions 1–15, scoring was based on a 0, 1, 2 points system, where 2 points were awarded for complete understanding, 1 for partial understanding, and 0 for no understanding. Additionally, for question 16, 2 points were given for correct answers and 0 for incorrect/unclear answers. Thus, the knowledge score ranged from 0 to 32. The attitude dimension consisted of 8 questions, evaluated on a five-point Likert scale where (5) indicated extremely positive and (1) extremely negative, resulting in a score range of 8 to 40. The practice dimension encompassed 5 questions, evaluated on a five-point Likert scale where (5) indicated extremely compliant and (1) extremely non-compliant, yielding a total practical dimension score of 5 to 25.

Questionnaire distribution and data quality control

Between June 10 and August 2, 2023, data were collected using an online questionnaire. Caregivers of stroke patients completed the questionnaire on the Sojump platform (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd; https://www.wjx.cn/app/survey.aspx) by scanning a QR code distributed by the investigators via WeChat. The investigator team consisted of ten nurses, twenty rehabilitation therapists (including ten physiotherapists and ten occupational therapists), and three doctors. They were responsible for participant recruitment, questionnaire distribution, explanation of questionnaire content, questionnaire collection, preliminary screening, and data extraction. Using a convenience sampling approach, investigators distributed the questionnaire to eligible caregivers in a one-on-one manner, mainly targeting caregivers of patients receiving rehabilitation services at our hospital. All investigators received standardized training before data collection. During the survey, investigators provided clarification when participants had questions. Participants were encouraged to complete the questionnaire independently, and investigators only provided neutral assistance when clarification was requested to minimize potential bias. Any issues encountered during data collection were reported to the study initiators and discussed to ensure consistent implementation. All investigators had at least a bachelor’s degree, and several held a master’s degree. Most had previous research experience and were familiar with questionnaire-based survey procedures.

To guarantee the quality and completeness of the questionnaire survey, each IP address could only submit the questionnaire once, and all questions in the questionnaire were mandatory. The completeness, internal coherence, and reasonableness of all questionnaires were checked by the investigators. To ensure quality control further, during the data cleaning, questionnaires completed in less than 90 s (high probability of answering at a venture as the answer time is too short), the option does not follow normal logic, any part of KAP questionnaire all selects the same option were considered invalid questionnaire.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using the formula for cross-sectional studies: α = 0.05, where =1.96 when α = 0.05, the assumed degree of variability of p=0.5 maximizes the required sample size, and δ is admissible error (which was 5% here). The theoretical sample size was 480 which includes an extra 20% to allow for subjects lost during the study.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were presented as means and standard deviations, whereas categorical data were represented as n (%). Before analysis, a normality test was conducted for continuous variables. If the data had a normal distribution, a t-test was applied for group comparisons. In cases where normal distribution was not met, the Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney test was employed. ANOVA was employed for comparisons of three or more groups of continuous variables with a normal distribution and uniform variance. When the assumption of normal distribution was not met, the Kruskal-Wallis analysis of variance was used. The above-mentioned analyses were run using the software SPSS 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to explore factors of KAP. The following scoring criteria were used to categorize KAP levels. Participants scored at ≥75% of the total possible score range in knowledge, attitude and practice dimension were considered as sufficient knowledge, positive attitude, and appropriate practice respectively. Scored at 60% to 80% of the total the total possible score range were categorized as moderate knowledge, attitude and practice, while scored less than 60% of the total possible score range were deemed as insufficient knowledge, negative attitude, or inappropriate practice. Variables exhibiting a significance level of P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were subsequently included in the multivariate analysis. This process yielded odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs). Structural equation modeling (SEM) was also used to explore possible path associations among the three KAP dimensions and between each dimension and their questions. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 represented statistical significance.

Results

The final count of valid questionnaires totaled 497. Among the 700 collected questionnaires, 203 were excluded, including 163 due to logical inconsistencies identified through pre-set consistency checks, 32 due to exceptionally short response times (< 100 s), and 5 due to respondents being younger than 18 years. The demographic characteristics and KAP scores of the caregivers are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the participants was 42.65 ± 13.97, and the majority were women (62.98%), married (81.49%), residing in urban areas (58.95%), with Bachelor’s Degree or Above (56.74%), employed (either full or part-time; 55.73%), and had a household monthly per capita income in the previous year (in CNY) of < 5000 (45.07%). Among stroke patients, the average age was 64.31 ± 32.00; most were men (60.36%), had not undergone depression screening (57.95%), and had been diagnosed with hypertension (71.43%) as a comorbid condition. The knowledge, attitude, and practice mean scores were 18.37 ± 9.77 (on a maximum of 32: 57.40%), 33.17 ± 3.89 (on a maximum of 40: 82.92%), and 22.51 ± 3.13 (on a maximum of 25: 90.04%), respectively, suggesting that the caregivers had insufficient knowledge, positive attitudes, and favorable practice toward PSD among patients. Based on the predefined thresholds, 156 (31.39%) caregivers were classified as having sufficient knowledge, while 341 (68.61%) had insufficient knowledge. For attitude, 398 (80.08%) caregivers were categorized as having a positive attitude and 99 (19.92%) as having a negative attitude. For practice, 456 (91.75%) caregivers demonstrated appropriate practice, whereas 41 (8.25%) had inappropriate practice (Table S1).

Knowledge

Differences in caregivers’ knowledge scores were observed based on gender (women displayed higher knowledge levels), education, current employment status, and monthly per capita income. Additionally, variations were noted depending on the time since the first occurrence of stroke in the patient, the patient’s ability to independently perform activities of daily living (ADLs), and whether the patient had undergone depression screening (all p < 0.001). The gender of the stroke patient (p = 0.099) nearly reached significance as an influencing factor. Notably, except for Question 8, most respondents had only partial understanding of items in the knowledge dimension, with percentages ranging from 30.78% to 43.66%. However, Question 8, which referred to stroke as a cerebral infarction excluding cerebral hemorrhage, had a relatively high accuracy rate of 66.60%. The response “Unfamiliar” was chosen by 16.10% to 34.61% of participants, with the highest unfamiliarity observed regarding the association between severe PSD and self-harm or suicidal tendencies (Table S2).

Attitude

In the attitude dimension, differences were found among caregivers based on their education (p = 0.031) and the time since the first occurrence of stroke in the patient (p = 0.050), while the gender of the patient came close to reaching significance (p = 0.086). In the corresponding questionnaire, most caregivers (62.58% to 77.67%) strongly agreed with all six items related to positive recommendations (questions 1–2, 4–6, 8). For most respondents (approximately 90%), they strongly agreed or agreed with these recommendations. However, Question 3 exhibited a scattered pattern of responses, particularly regarding whether a patient’s worse quality of life and negative emotions required the doctor’s attention, with “strongly disagree” being the most common response (31.99%). The majority of caregiver experienced sadness or anxiety during the caregiving period, with 35.41% strongly agreeing and 32.39% agreeing (Table S3).

Practice

Regarding practice, caregivers’ scores differed based on their monthly per capita income (p = 0.011), the time since the first stroke occurrence (p = 0.002), and whether the patient had undergone depression screening (p < 0.001). The majority of respondent (approximately 90%) provided positive responses (strongly agreeing and agreeing together). Specifically, 56.14% to 62.78% of participants chose “strongly agree,” while 27.77% to 36.02% selected “agree” (Table S4).

Multivariate analysis

The multivariate analysis results showed that age (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95 − 0.10, p = 0.018), stroke course (2–3 years) (OR = 4.29, 95% CI: 1.99–9.25, p < 0.001), stroke course (> 3 years) (OR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.22–4.26, p = 0.010) were associated with good knowledge. Those caregivers whose patients had not undergone depression screening (OR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.14–0.37, p < 0.001) or were unsure (OR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.06–0.29, p < 0.001) had lower odds of knowledge about PSD versus caregivers of patients who underwent screening. When the patient had post-stroke sequelae, the caregiver had lower odds of knowledge (OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.24–0.99, p = 0.046) compared to no sequelae (Table 2). In addition, knowledge was significantly positively associated with a positive attitude (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.08–1.15, p < 0.001). Caregivers whose patients had duration since the first occurrence of stroke > 3 years ago had higher odds of attitude versus a period of < 6 months (OR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.21–4.82, p = 0.013) (Table 3). Knowledge (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02–1.12, p = 0.006), attitude score (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.16–1.39, p < 0.001) and self-employed and freelancer caregivers (OR = 6.12, 95% CI: 1.30–28.90, p = 0.022; n = 87) were associated with good practice (Table 4).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the few studies that specifically analyzed the KAP toward PSD among caregivers of stroke patients in China, particularly using a path analysis approach. The caregivers demonstrated positive attitudes and favorable practices in dealing with PSD but had insufficient knowledge levels. Furthermore, there were significant correlations between KAP scores and various demographic factors. Similar to previous studies conducted among stroke patients and their family members in China, our findings indicate insufficient knowledge but relatively positive attitudes and practices, and confirm that knowledge is positively associated with both attitude and practice. Compared with previous studies that primarily focused on patients or combined patients and family members, the present study specifically targeted caregivers and further explored the interrelationships among knowledge, attitude, and practice using a structural model. This approach provides a more focused understanding of caregiving-related factors and highlights the unique role of caregivers in PSD management. Multivariate analyses revealed significant relationships between KAP dimensions, as knowledge positively influenced both attitudes and practices. Finally, SEM path analyses confirmed associations between knowledge and attitude, attitude and practice, and knowledge and practice. The model fit indices indicated an acceptable fit according to commonly used thresholds (e.g., RMSEA < 0.08), although some indices (IFI, TLI, and CFI) were slightly below the more stringent criterion of 0.90, suggesting that the model could be further improved. These findings are also consistent with previous studies conducted among family members of stroke patients in China, which reported insufficient knowledge despite relatively positive attitudes and practices toward stroke-related care [20]. Similar patterns have also been observed in studies focusing on PSD patients and their family members, where knowledge deficits coexist with more favorable attitudes and practices [19]. Furthermore, evidence from broader caregiver research indicates that caregivers of stroke patients often experience greater psychological burden and depressive symptoms compared with the general population, which may further influence their health-related behaviors and caregiving practices [21]. These comparisons suggest that while caregiving experience may promote more proactive attitudes and practices, disease-specific knowledge, particularly regarding PSD, remains insufficient and requires targeted improvement. Compared with the general population, where knowledge and attitudes toward depression are often limited and influenced by stigma and poor symptom recognition, caregivers in this study demonstrated more proactive attitudes and practices, likely due to their direct caregiving experience, although important disease-specific knowledge gaps remained.

Medical staff within clinical settings worldwide frequently shows insufficient knowledge regarding the symptoms and therapeutic approaches for PSD. This deficiency often leads to misdiagnosis, treatment delays, and even the absence of appropriate interventions for PSD [22]. In turn, studies conducted across the globe, including France [23], North America [24], Malawi [25], and China [26] have shown that family caregivers generally have limited knowledge regarding patient care, rehabilitation, and the overall complications of stroke, including PSD. Similarly, the caregivers’ responses in our study revealed a relatively low level of understanding regarding PSD. Specifically, caregivers showed limited understanding in recognizing PSD symptoms, awareness of its potential severity (such as the risk of self-harm or suicidal ideation), and appropriate management strategies. These deficiencies may delay early identification and intervention. In previous research, caregivers have claimed that a lack of education on stroke complications and lack of support from the health care system and the government has resulted in an urgent need for basic nursing skills and social support [18], a situation that is also observed in China [26–28].

KAP studies on post-stroke caregivers have been conducted in Türkiye [11], Bangladesh [9], Iran [10], and China [19]. Our results differed from those assessments, in which caregivers evidenced relatively high knowledge levels related to risk factors, symptoms, and good action about general stroke complications. Although none of those publications mainly focused on PSD, two analyzed whether the caregivers knew that depression was a post-stroke complication, with 84.8% [10] and 6.9% [11] of the participants being aware of the fact, versus 30.78% in our study. These inconsistencies could be related to local particularities, which demand education policies according to each social situation. Despite the mentioned differences, a common finding emerged in our correlation between higher knowledge levels and caregivers possessing advanced educational qualifications [11] and being employed [10]. Moreover, consistent with our results, a significant connection was found between patients’ knowledge and practice concerning PSD. In their study, Farpour et al. (2023) also noted a notable correlation between caregivers’ knowledge and practice concerning potential stroke complications.

In the attitude dimension, nearly 70% of our respondents claimed to have frequently experienced sadness or anxiety. These data are consistent with numerous other reports from China [8, 27] and overseas [24, 29], with caregivers experiencing psychological overwhelm. Immediately post-stroke, caregiver burden is linked to weakened coping abilities and subsequent anxiety, while over the long term, these effects persist [30]. The caregivers’ compromised health is directly correlated with stroke survivors’ physical, cognitive, and psychological well-being and the extent of time devoted to hands-on care [30].

Regarding practice, certain outcomes from our study diverged from previous reports. E.g., higher practice scores were detected in the urban population versus rural regions of Iran [10], while no significant difference was detected in our population sample. On the other hand, when adherence to evidence-based practice [31] for screening, prevention, and management of patients with PSD was analyzed in another Chinese hospital, low compliance was observed, with percentages of adherence ranging from 0% to 49.4%, depending on different audits. Conversely, caregivers from the present study exhibited favorable levels of practice.

The present study has several limitations. First, selection bias may exist due to the relatively high exclusion rate of questionnaires, primarily resulting from logical inconsistencies and short response times under strict data quality control criteria. In addition, caregivers with less than three months of caregiving experience were excluded, and caregiving duration was self-reported, which may further limit the representativeness of caregivers, particularly those in the early stage of stroke care. Second, potential information and misclassification biases must be acknowledged. As detailed in the methodology, post-stroke depression status was determined without objective medical record validation. The lack of independent verification for formal physician diagnoses or actual antidepressant usage implies that patients’ true clinical status might be misclassified by caregivers. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data inherent to KAP surveys may introduce social desirability bias, warranting careful consideration when interpreting the findings. Third, some statistical limitations should be noted. The relatively small proportion of participants classified as having sufficient knowledge may affect the stability and statistical power of the logistic regression models. Additionally, the wide confidence interval observed for self-employed/freelancer caregivers suggests potential instability of the estimate, likely due to the limited sample size within this subgroup. Finally, the single-centered design may limit the generalizability of the findings, and although the overall sample size was substantial, subgroup-specific nuances may not have been fully captured. Furthermore, the study did not incorporate standardized psychological assessment tools, which may have limited the depth of mental health evaluation. Future research should involve multi-center studies with larger and more diverse populations, incorporate standardized diagnostic and psychological assessment tools, and further explore long-term caregiving outcomes. In addition, structured educational interventions—such as hospital-based training, follow-up programs, and digital health tools (e.g., mobile applications or WeChat-based platforms)—may help improve caregivers’ recognition and management of PSD and should be evaluated in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights a critical gap between knowledge and behavior among caregivers of stroke patients regarding PSD. While caregivers demonstrated generally positive attitudes and practices, insufficient knowledge—particularly in recognizing symptoms, understanding severity, and implementing appropriate management—may hinder early identification and effective intervention. These findings underscore the importance of integrating structured caregiver education into routine stroke care, including targeted training during hospitalization and continued support after discharge. From a policy perspective, incorporating caregiver-focused mental health education into rehabilitation programs and leveraging digital health platforms may enhance the scalability and accessibility of such interventions. Strengthening caregiver capacity in PSD management has the potential to improve both patient outcomes and caregiver well-being, ultimately contributing to more comprehensive stroke rehabilitation care.
