Impact of Tracheostomy on Dysphagia in Children
Authors: Antara Gupta, Seckin O Ulualp, Ashley B Brown, Felicity Lenes‐Voit, Cynthia Wang, Ron B Mitchell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo evaluate the characteristics of dysphagia in children with tracheostomy and determine the effect of tracheostomy tubes on swallowing function.MethodsA retrospective chart review on demographics, past medical history, and swallowing function of children who underwent tracheostomy. Video fluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) and flexible endoscopic evaluation swallowing reports were utilized to define swallowing abnormalities. Prevalence of swallowing abnormalities, penetration aspiration scale, and functional oral intake scores were compared using Wilcoxon signed rank test, chi‐square, and McNemar's tests. A p < 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsA total of 194 (37%) of the 525 children had symptom‐based dysphagia before and 282 (54%) had symptom‐based dysphagia after tracheostomy placement (p < 0.001). VFSS was performed in 65 children before tracheostomy and 225 children after tracheostomy. The rate of oral phase impairment (88%) was higher than swallow triggering (53%), pharyngeal phase (67%), and esophageal phase (2%) impairment after tracheostomy (p ≤ 0.001). Rates of penetration (28% vs. 16%) and aspiration (28% vs. 17%) were higher with thin than thick liquids after surgery (p = 0.002). In 25 children who had VFSS before and after tracheostomy, rates of abnormal swallow triggering (40% to 80%, p = 0.004) and thick liquid penetration (12% to 40%, p = 0.008) increased significantly after tracheostomy. Penetration aspiration scale score decreased in 14 children. FOIS score increased in 21.ConclusionsDysphagia is a common symptom in children requiring tracheostomy. Oral intake and penetration‐aspiration severity improve in most children after tracheostomy. Clinicians should be cognizant of persistent swallowing dysfunction after tracheostomy to prevent the respiratory and nutritional consequences of dysphagia.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: children, dysphagia, swallowing dysfunction, tracheostomy

Introduction

Tracheostomy is critical in the care and survival of children with respiratory compromise, or upper airway obstruction. While tracheostomy alleviates respiratory compromise and provides access for airway clearance, tracheostomy tubes can impair swallowing function. Since the association between tracheostomy and dysphagia was reported in 1966, the effect of tracheostomy tubes on swallowing biomechanics in adults has been widely studied [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Tracheostomy tubes contribute to swallowing impairment by altering laryngeal elevation, swallow triggering, glottic closure, subglottic air pressure, and esophageal compression in adults [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Compared to children without tracheostomy, altered laryngeal elevation and delayed swallow triggering were documented in a case series of four toddlers with tracheostomy and no dysphagia [7]. The presence of a tracheostomy tube may not account for the sole mechanism of dysphagia in all adults and children with tracheostomy, as dysphagia may also be secondary to the underlying medical condition [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

A wide variety of conditions, including aerodigestive tract abnormalities, neurologic disorders, cardiopulmonary disease, genetic disorders, and incoordination of sucking, swallowing, and breathing, may cause dysphagia in children [12]. Children requiring tracheostomy are medically complex with a high risk for dysphagia due to multiple comorbidities [13, 14]. Dysphagia has previously been reported as occurring in 70% to 83% of children with tracheostomy [10, 11, 14]. Multiple phases of swallowing were impaired in children with tracheostomy [10, 11]. Assessment of dysphagia before and after tracheostomy potentially delineates the impact of tracheostomy on swallowing function in children with tracheostomy. We hypothesized that tracheostomy would increase the prevalence of dysphagia in children. The objectives of this study were to evaluate the characteristics of dysphagia in children with tracheostomy and determine the effect of tracheostomy tubes on swallowing function.

Material and Methods

The study was approved by the local institutional human research review board with a waiver of informed consent for the retrospective review of deidentified data. Children who had undergone tracheostomy from January 2014 to December 2025, at a tertiary care children's hospital, were identified retrospectively through the electronic medical records search. Inclusion criteria were age less than 18 years and completion of swallowing function assessment with video fluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS). Exclusion criterion was a lack of swallowing assessment before tracheostomy and age greater than 18 years.

Data pertaining to age, gender, ethnicity, race, past medical history, dysphagia diagnosis, indication for tracheostomy, and results of pre‐and post‐tracheostomy swallowing assessments were obtained. As in prior studies, subjective diagnosis of dysphagia was based on the presence of clinical symptoms [10, 11]. Subjective assessment of dysphagia was used to determine the prevalence of dysphagic symptoms. Dysphagia was objectively assessed with the use of VFSS. VFSS reports were reviewed to extract information on oral phase impairment, swallow triggering impairment (delayed or absent swallow initiation), pharyngeal phase impairment, esophageal phase impairment, penetration aspiration scale (PAS), and functional oral intake scale (FOIS) before and after tracheostomy [15, 16]. The PAS scale is an 8‐point ordinal scale: 1 = Material does not enter the airway; 2 = Enters the airway but remains above the vocal folds; ejected; 3 = Enters the airway, remains above the vocal folds; not ejected; 4 = Contacts the vocal folds; ejected; 5 = Contacts the vocal folds; not ejected; 6 = Passes below the vocal folds; ejected; 7 = Passes below the vocal folds; not ejected despite effort; 8 = Passes below the vocal folds; no attempt to eject (silent aspiration). The FOIS scale is a 7‐point ordinal scale: 1 = Tube‐dependent with no oral intake; 2 = Tube‐dependent with minimal/inconsistent oral intake; 3 = Tube‐dependent with consistent oral intake of liquids or foods; 4 = Total oral intake of a single consistency; 5 = Total oral intake of multiple consistencies requiring special preparation; 6 = Total oral intake with no special preparation, but with specific food limitations; 7 = Total oral intake with no restrictions. VFSS and FEES reports were reviewed to obtain further information on pharyngeal phase impairment including aspiration and penetration. Aspiration was defined as the passing of the bolus below the true vocal folds, and penetration was when the bolus entered the airway but not below the true vocal folds. Presence of aspiration and penetration was documented for thin and thick liquids. Thin liquids were Level 0 and thick liquids were Level 1, Level 2, Level 3, and Level 4 based on International Dysphagia Diet Standardization Initiative levels [17].

The prevalences of dysphagic symptoms, impaired swallowing phases, penetration, and aspiration were determined in the entire group and cohort groups defined by age, gender, ethnicity (Hispanic and Non‐Hispanic), race (Black/African American, Asian, White and Other/Unknown), gestational age designation, and comorbidity before and after tracheostomy. Age subgroups included the following: infant (age < 1 year), toddler (1 ≤ age < 3 years), preschooler (3 ≤ age < 6 years), gradeschooler (6 ≤ age < 12 years), and teenager (12 ≤ age < 18 years).

Statistical comparisons of the prevalence of dysphagia between groups were performed using χ 2 and McNemar's tests. Categorical data were presented as counts with percentages. Comparisons of PAS and FOIS scores before and after tracheostomy were performed using the Wilcoxon signed rank test as appropriate. All analyses were performed with R Studio Statistical Software (Version 2024.12.1 + 563). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. This study adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for observational study.

Results

About 525 children (281 male, 244 female, age range: 2 days to 17 years) underwent tracheostomy. The group was 54% male, 62% infants, 65% non‐Hispanic, 54% White/Caucasian, 52% term, and 97% patients had comorbid conditions (Figure 1). Comorbid conditions were congenital heart disease in 191 children, congenital syndrome in 349, craniofacial abnormality in 118, gastrointestinal abnormality in 297, neurological disorder in 336, airway abnormality in 315, and failure to thrive in 48. The indication for tracheostomy was airway abnormality in 102 (19%), neurologic disease in 13 (2%), chronic respiratory failure/pulmonary disease in 281 (54%), a combination of airway and neurological abnormalities in 5 (1%), neurological and pulmonary abnormalities in 25 (5%), airway and pulmonary abnormalities in 95 (18%), and combination of neurological, airway, and pulmonary abnormalities in 4 (1%). 95% (498/525) needed mechanical ventilation, 63% (331/525) used a Passy Muir valve, and 95% (497/525) had an inflatable trach cuff.

Pre‐tracheostomy and post‐tracheostomy prevalence of dysphagia. The prevalence of dysphagia post‐tracheostomy increased in the entire group and cohorts of gender, age, race, ethnicity, gestational age designation, and comorbidity (*p < 0.001).

The prevalence of dysphagia complaint was 37% in (194/525) children before tracheostomy. Of the 331 without dysphagia complaint before tracheostomy, 282 (85%) had dysphagia complaint after tracheostomy. The prevalence of dysphagia complaint was similar among subgroups of gender, ethnicity, race, gestational age, and comorbid conditions before tracheostomy. Toddlers had a higher rate of dysphagia complaint before tracheostomy than infants (p ≤ 0.001), preschoolers (p ≤ 0.001), gradeschoolers (p ≤ 0.001), and teenagers (p = 0.04). The prevalence of dysphagia complaint after tracheostomy increased from 37% to 91% (p < 0.001) in the entire group and cohort groups defined by age, gender, ethnicity, race, gestational age designation, and comorbidity (p < 0.001) (Figure 1). All children with dysphagia complaint before tracheostomy (100%) continued to have dysphagia after tracheostomy.

VFSS was performed in 65 children before tracheostomy and 225 children after tracheostomy. The majority of children had oral phase (69%) and pharyngeal phase impairment (70%) before tracheostomy (Table 1). Oral phase impairment (88%) occurred more frequently than swallow triggering (53%), pharyngeal phase (67%), and esophageal phase (2%) impairment after tracheostomy (p ≤ 0.001) (Table 1). The rate of oral phase impairment after tracheostomy (88%) was greater than before tracheostomy (69%) (p < 0.001). The rates of penetration and aspiration with thin and thickened liquids were similar before tracheostomy (Table 2). Rates of penetration (28%) and aspiration (28%) with thin liquids after tracheostomy were higher than penetration (16%) and aspiration (17%) with thickened liquids (p = 0.002) (Table 2). The rate of aspiration with thick liquids after tracheostomy (17%) was less than before tracheostomy (29%) (p < 0.001).

Of the 20 children who tolerated Passy Muir Speaking Valve (PMV), 19 had dysphagia before and after tracheostomy. Of the 5 children who could not tolerate PMV, four had dysphagia before and after tracheostomy and one did not have dysphagia before and after tracheostomy.

In 25 children who had VFSS before and after tracheostomy, the rates of abnormal swallow triggering (40% to 80%, p = 0.004) and thick liquid penetration (12% to 40%, p = 0.008) increased after tracheostomy (Figure 2). Males, infants, non‐Hispanics, and children with comorbid conditions had newly developed penetration, aspiration, and abnormalities of the oral phase, pharyngeal phase, and swallow triggering (Figure 2). Abnormalities of swallow triggering after tracheostomy significantly increased in infants (p = 0.02), non‐Hispanic children (p = 0.03), and children with comorbid conditions (p = 0.004) (Figure 2). Thick liquid penetration after tracheostomy occurred in more males (p = 0.03) and children with comorbid conditions (p = 0.03). PAS score decreased in 14 children and increased in 4 children (Figure 3). In between‐group comparison, PAS score after tracheostomy (median = 2, IQR = 1–4) was not different than PAS score before tracheostomy (median = 2, IQR = 1–4, p = 1.0) (Figure 3). FOIS score increased in 21 of the 25 children after tracheostomy (Figure 4). FOIS score after tracheostomy (median = 3, IQR = 3–5) was greater than FOIS score before tracheostomy (median = 2, IQR = 1–3) (p ≤ 0.001) (Figure 4). Of the eight children who had a FOIS score of 1 (tube‐dependent with no oral intake) before tracheostomy, five progressed to a FOIS score of 2 (tube‐dependent with minimal or inconsistent oral intake), one to a FOIS score of 3 (tube‐dependent with consistent oral intake of liquids or foods), one to a FOIS score of 5 (total oral intake of multiple consistencies requiring special preparation), and one to a FOIS score of 6 (total oral intake with no special preparation but with specific food limitations) after tracheostomy.

Discussion

The impact of tracheostomy on swallowing function in children remains unclear due to the lack of studies evaluating swallowing function before and after tracheostomy. In a previous study, the prevalence of dysphagia was 80% (64/80) in infants and toddlers with tracheostomy for airway obstruction or long‐term ventilation requirements [11]. In a cohort of 44 children aged 3 months to 17 years, who underwent tracheostomy for airway obstruction and neurological disorders, 70% had dysphagia [10]. Presence of dysphagia symptoms before tracheostomy has not been examined in previous studies. In the present study, dysphagia complaint was present in 37% of the children before tracheostomy and dysphagia persisted in all children after tracheostomy, with additional children also being diagnosed with dysphagia after tracheostomy. It is important to note that most children in our cohort had comorbid conditions highly associated with dysphagia. Prevalence of dysphagia in comorbid conditions including neurological disorders, congenital heart disease, gastrointestinal disorders, and chromosomal abnormalities, varies depending on the severity of the comorbidity [18]. Our finding of preexisting dysphagic symptoms in children with tracheostomy supports the critical role of dysphagia evaluation before and after tracheostomy when delineating the actual impact of tracheostomy on swallowing function in children. In addition, a clear understanding of dysphagia prevalence supports caregivers, clinicians, and researchers in recognizing the need for early swallowing assessment in children undergoing tracheostomy.

Based on a symptom‐based evaluation of dysphagia, the majority of children who did not have dysphagia prior to tracheostomy developed it after undergoing the procedure in the present study. The prevalence of newly diagnosed dysphagia in children with tracheostomy has not been reported. In previous studies, dysphagia prevalence ranged from 70% to 80% in tracheostomized children [10, 11]. In the present study, the prevalence of dysphagic symptoms was 91% after tracheostomy. The higher rate of dysphagia in our cohort is potentially due to differences in demographics, comorbid conditions, and study settings. Our cohort included children with a wide age range and various comorbidities at a tertiary care children's hospital. Previous studies reported a high dysphagia prevalence in infants and toddlers [10, 11]. The preponderance of infants and toddlers in our cohort possibly contributed to the increased rate of dysphagia. Separation from caregivers, prolonged hospital stays, underlying medical conditions, and long‐term parenteral nutrition have been postulated as causes of increased rates of dysphagia in younger children [11]. The roles of neural and craniofacial development, as well as the interaction between them, should be considered as potential contributors to the increased rate of dysphagia in infants and toddlers [19]. Worsening dysphagia has been documented in children with congenital disease, neurological conditions, respiratory abnormalities, craniofacial abnormalities, Down syndrome, and prematurity [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. The increased prevalence of dysphagia seen across multiple subgroups emphasizes that, especially in medically complex populations, tracheostomy continues to be a substantial risk factor for worsening swallowing function and this effect may be particularly pronounced in children already vulnerable to dysphagia by virtue of their underlying conditions.

In the present study, instrumental assessment of dysphagia in children with tracheostomy was performed in 225 children. Instrumental evaluation of swallowing phases revealed impairment of the swallow triggering, oral, and pharyngeal phases in most tracheotomized children. The rate of delayed swallow triggering (53%) in our cohort is similar to a previous report of 45% of toddlers having delayed swallow triggering [7]. Delayed swallow triggering also occurred in adults with tracheotomies [4]. Delayed initiation of swallowing may increase the risk of bolus spillage entering the airway [7]. In a South African cohort of infants and toddlers, the oral phase and pharyngeal phase abnormalities occurred in 90% and 61% of children, respectively [11]. In the Netherlands, oral phase and pharyngeal phase abnormalities occurred in 70% of children aged from 3 months to 17 years [10]. Findings of the present study and earlier studies support that oral and pharyngeal phase abnormalities are common in tracheotomized children [10, 11]. Oral phase abnormalities have been attributed to a potential effect of the tracheostomy tube on tongue position and movement and the underlying comorbid conditions influencing the masticatory muscles and movements of the cheek and lips [10, 11, 26]. Pharyngeal phase abnormalities in adults have been attributed to the tracheostomy tube causing a loss of subglottic air pressure and reducing the duration of glottic closure, laryngeal elevation, and anterior rotation [3, 6, 27]. In children, tracheostomy tube presence has been associated with reduced superior excursion of the arytenoid and epiglottis, slower laryngeal movement, and delayed vestibule closure [7]. Delayed vestibule closure resulted in laryngeal penetration in children with tracheostomy [7]. In our cohort, the prevalence of laryngeal penetration was 28% with thin liquids and 16% with thickened fluids, similar to a prior study reporting laryngeal penetration in 34% of children [11]. The rate of aspiration in our cohort (28%) was similar to the previously reported aspiration rate (31%) in infants and toddlers [11]. Our findings of increased rates of penetration and aspiration with thin liquids than with thickened liquids were expected as thickened liquids are easier to swallow [28].

In 25 children who had VFSS before and after tracheostomy, more children developed delayed swallow triggering and penetration with thickened liquid. The mechanisms leading to delayed swallow triggering and thickened liquid penetration were not studied in the present study; however, less powerful swallow, tracheostomy tube tethering the larynx, and slow vestibule closure have been implicated as causes of impaired airway protection in children with tracheostomy [7, 10]. Furthermore, our findings identified that males, infants, non‐Hispanics, and children with comorbid conditions are at risk for penetration and aspiration, as well as impaired oral phase, pharyngeal phase, and swallow triggering. PAS and FOIS scores improved in the majority of children. Improvement of FOIS and PAS scores supports the beneficial effect of tracheostomy on swallowing function in this group of children. Our finding of most children with PMV having dysphagia before and after tracheostomy is similar to a previous study documenting that the presence of PMV did not decrease laryngeal aspiration or penetration in children with tracheostomy [29]. In the present study, the comparison of swallowing function before and after tracheostomy provided evidence that tracheostomy tubes impacted swallowing function in this group of children. We acknowledge that the possible progression of comorbidities during the interval between before and after tracheostomy may have contributed to the increased dysphagia prevalence and cannot be excluded. Even so, most children showed improved oral intake post‐tracheostomy. Because of the timing of the post‐tracheostomy evaluations, it was not possible in this study to assess changes in swallowing function over time or the potential impact of feeding therapy on improvement in oral intake.

The present study is the first to report formal dysphagia assessment before and after tracheostomy to isolate the tracheostomy tubes' effect on swallowing function in children. Clinical applications of our findings include counseling caregivers, decision‐making, and treatment planning. However, there were limitations to the study inherent to the retrospective study design. Since the study setting is a single, urban, tertiary children's hospital, our findings may not be applicable in other settings. Furthermore, the present study is limited by the number of patients, which influenced the analysis of subgroups and variables. The heterogeneity of the cohort makes the results largely generalizable to children undergoing tracheostomy at a tertiary care children's hospital. However, the lack of equal proportions of children in age, ethnicity, and race categories can also be considered a confounding variable when interpreting the results. Additionally, an overwhelming majority of children had comorbid conditions. Therefore, our findings cannot be extrapolated to the effect of tracheostomy tubes in children without comorbidities. The small number of patients who had both pre‐ and post‐tracheostomy swallowing evaluation with VFSS limits the generalizability of how a tracheostomy tube specifically impacts swallowing function. Quantitative VFSS analysis was not performed in the present study; however, objective assessment of structural movements including, but not limited to, the extent and timing of hyoid bone displacement, pharyngeal constriction, and pharyngeal clearing ratio, can provide means to quantify physiological impairment rather than relying solely on subjective descriptors [30, 31]. The role of bedside dysphagia evaluation methods, including cervical auscultation of the larynx, the Modified Evans Blue Dye test, or the use of questionnaires and observational scales was not evaluated in the present study. Prospective studies comparing swallowing function before and after tracheostomy are needed to assess the effect of tracheostomy tubes on swallowing function in children with comorbidities and healthy children in settings with different levels of patient care. It is hoped that these findings will be used to generate hypotheses for future studies.

Conclusions

Dysphagia is a common symptom in children requiring tracheostomy. Oral intake and penetration‐aspiration severity improve in most children after tracheostomy. Clinicians should be cognizant of the increased risk of delayed swallow triggering and penetration of thick liquids, as well as the persistence of other swallowing abnormalities following tracheostomy to prevent respiratory and nutritional consequences of dysphagia. The long‐term sequelae of dysphagia in children with tracheostomy merit further investigation.
