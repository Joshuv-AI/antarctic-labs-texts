Patient-Reported Outcomes Following Two-Tined Three-Site Conventional Radiofrequency Ablation of Genicular Nerves for Chronic Knee Pain
Authors: Mingda Chen, Sercan Tosun, Nicolas R Thompson, Kush Goyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Genicular nerve radiofrequency ablation (GNRFA) has emerged as an effective therapy for chronic knee pain. Although some studies have evaluated its effectiveness, there is limited real-world data on the long-term patient-reported outcome (PRO) data for GNRFAs.Objective: This study aimed to evaluate PROs following conventional GNRFAs performed using two-tined electrodes and the classical three-site technique targeting the superior medial, superior lateral, and inferior medial genicular nerves.Methods: We performed a retrospective chart review to gather data from consecutive GNRFAs at an academic medical center from 2016 to 2023. Inclusion criteria were all adult patients who received a conventional GNRFA performed using the classical three-site technique using two-tined electrodes. All included subjects were confirmed to have a positive diagnostic response (≥50% Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) reduction) to a previous genicular nerve block. Primary outcomes were NRS pain scores, Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Global Health, and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9).Results: A total of 31 patients underwent 38 GNRFAs. Relative to the pre-procedure baseline, the average NRS reduction post-procedurally was -3.17 during 0-3 months (p<0.0001), -1.31 during 3-6 months (p<0.05), and -1.92 during 6-12 months (p<0.05). Half of all patients maintained pain relief (NRS reduction greater than or equal to a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of -2.0) for 11.17 months (95% CI: 7.29, 13.40). Although there was a positive trend for improvement in the quality-of-life measures, no statistically significant post-procedure improvement or worsening was observed during all time periods for PROMIS Physical Health, PROMIS Mental Health, and PHQ-9. Lastly, a history of depression was associated with treatment failure.Conclusion: In this real-world cohort, conventional GNRFA using the classical three-site technique demonstrated robust short-term pain reduction, and at least half of the cohort maintained clinically meaningful pain improvements for 11 months. For chronic knee pain, GNRFA is a reasonable therapeutic option in appropriately selected patients.

Introduction

Genicular nerve interventions such as genicular nerve block (GNB) and genicular nerve radiofrequency ablation (GNRFA) have emerged as meaningful treatment options for painful knee osteoarthritis (OA) [1]. Patients who receive a genicular nerve intervention usually present with persistent knee pain despite conservative management but have not yet received a total knee arthroplasty (TKA) or experienced post-TKA knee pain that is refractory to conservative management options. Both GNB and GNRFA are minimally invasive interventions that target genicular nerves to achieve sensory denervation of the knee joint capsule [2-5]. A GNB uses a local anesthetic with or without corticosteroid to numb the genicular nerves [6], while a GNRFA uses traditional or cooled radiofrequency ablation to create localized lesions to inactivate the genicular nerves [1].

Classically, GNRFA procedures are performed using a three-site technique to target the superior medial genicular nerve (SMGN), superior lateral genicular nerve (SLGM), and inferior medial genicular nerve (IMGN) [6,7]. In recent years, innovative approaches have targeted additional sensory nerves such as the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve, the recurrent fibular nerve, and the nerves to the vastus medialis, vastus intermedius, and vastus lateralis muscles [1,8-13]. Currently, there is ongoing debate over the ideal selection of genicular nerve targets as well as patient selection criteria for these procedures.

Over the past decade, increasing evidence has supported the efficacy and effectiveness of GNRFA. Although multiple GNRFA trials have been performed in controlled clinical settings, there have been a limited number of retrospective studies that examined the long-term patient-reported outcome (PRO) data (e.g., 8-12+ months post-procedure) for GNRFAs in real-world clinical practice. Additionally, there remains significant variation in insurance coverage for GNRFA, where many insurance companies still do not cover GNRFA despite published evidence supporting its effectiveness at relieving chronic knee pain. Despite recent studies that highlighted the effectiveness of GNRFAs [14-17], there is still a need for data on the long-term therapeutic effect (i.e., 6 months to >1 year) of GNRFAs.

Across a study period of eight years, the aim of this study was to gather a real-world cohort of chronic knee patients treated by conventional GNRFA with long-term follow-up. We focused on procedures performed using the classical three-site radiofrequency ablation approach, the predominant technique at our institution. The primary outcomes were patient-reported pain scores and quality-of-life questionnaires collected at office visits. The three main PROs of interest were the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) for knee pain levels, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) for depression severity rating, and the Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Global Health questionnaire.

Materials and methods

Overview and study design

We performed a retrospective review of the electronic medical records to identify consecutive GNRFA procedures performed from January 2016 to December 2023 at Cleveland Clinic, a large academic tertiary care center in Cleveland, Ohio, by a single fellowship-trained physician. All procedures were performed with a two-tined electrode system (single burn at each of the three sites). Ethical approval was obtained from the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board (approval number: 23-734) prior to study initiation.

Procedure technique

The patient's knee was flexed with a bolster support to optimize the view of the knee joint space, and then the knee was prepped/draped in a sterile fashion. Three skin wheals were raised using 1% preservative-free lidocaine (0.5 mL per site) near the SMGN, SLGN, and IMGN based on bony landmarks. In the anteroposterior (AP) plane with the patella centered, a skin wheal was placed for local anesthesia. Next, 20-gauge, 100-mm two-tined radiofrequency rhizotomy needles were introduced and advanced under direct fluoroscopic visualization in the AP and lateral planes. The distal tip of the needle was advanced across ~60-90% of the femoral or tibial shaft on lateral view, staying 1-2 mm superficial to the periosteum in the AP view (Figure 1). The SMGN target was the confluence of the medial femoral shaft and the medial epicondyle. The SLGN target was the confluence of the lateral femoral shaft and lateral femoral condyle. The IMGN target was the confluence of the medial tibial shaft and tibial flare. After proper needle placement was confirmed, sensory and motor stimulation was performed prior to lesioning. Using the Stryker MultiGen radiofrequency generator (Kalamazoo, Michigan, United States), single lesioning was performed at 80°C for 90 seconds at each of the three sites simultaneously. After lesioning, we administered 0.75% preservative-free bupivacaine at each site (0.5 mL at each site).

Study population

We gathered consecutive patients (aged 18 years or older) who underwent a GNRFA for painful knee OA during the study period. Study inclusion criteria required a positive diagnostic response (≥50% NRS reduction) to a diagnostic GNB prior to their GNRFA. Patients with a Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grade less than 2 were excluded because they did not have definitive knee OA [18,19]. We also excluded those who did not follow up post-procedurally. KL grading was performed on weight-bearing knee radiographs by two reviewers (blinded to outcomes data). We also excluded GNRFAs performed using modified approaches (i.e., expanded protocols, cooled/bipolar radiofrequency ablation) to avoid bias from procedure/technique heterogeneity and due to the inadequate sample size. We manually reviewed patient charts, radiology reports, prior medical imaging, and intra-procedural fluoroscopy images to ensure adherence to the eligibility criteria.

Data collection

We performed a manual chart review to the collected data on the following pre-procedural variables: basic demographic information, relevant comorbidities including depression, anxiety disorder, fibromyalgia, chronic pain syndrome, meniscal tear (confirmed on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)), diabetes, alcohol abuse, and gout, smoking status, chronic corticosteroid use, and prescription opioid usage (quantified by morphine milligram equivalents (MME)). We also gathered knee OA characteristics (duration of knee pain prior to procedure and symptom laterality) and a history of knee OA treatments (intra-articular steroid injections, visco-supplementation, GNBs, lower extremity surgery, and physical therapy). All variables were manually collected and verified based on the information from clinical assessment notes, procedural notes, radiology reports, medication dispense reports, and problem lists.

Data were collected on the following periprocedural variables: American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, sedative use, procedure laterality, KL grade at procedure, and pre-procedural NRS scores. At post-procedure follow-up visits, we collected data on PRO measures, utilization of adjuvant treatments (number of knee OA physical therapy sessions), complications, and interval knee surgery. We used existing validated and standardized questionnaires for collecting all PROs, including the NRS pain scores, PROMIS Global Health 10-item questionnaire (includes Physical Health (PH) and Mental Health (MH) components) [20], and PHQ-9 [21]. We only used pre-procedure PRO scores within three months of the procedure date because utilization of older scores may not represent an accurate baseline for comparison.

Statistical analysis

For GNRFA outcomes, all PRO scores were grouped into the following post-procedure follow-up periods: 1-90 days (0-3 months), 91-182 days (3-6 months), 183-365 days (6-12 months), and 366+ days. We used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired comparisons between pre-procedure and post-procedure PRO scores. We selected the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of -2.0 for NRS rating of knee pain as previously reported in the literature [22-24]. We defined a non-response subgroup and a corresponding positive response subgroup to compare potential factors associated with poor treatment response. Non-response (not clinically significant) was defined as <50% NRS score reduction [24] or <75% patient-reported pain relief within three months post-procedurally. We used Welch's t-test (assuming unequal variance) to evaluate potential factors suspected to be associated with non-response to treatment. Analyses were performed at the procedure level. Multiple comparisons were corrected by the Bonferroni approach when applicable. All tests were two-sided. We used available-case analysis to handle all missing data. All data analyses were performed in R Version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Demographic and procedure characteristics

A total of 38 GNRFAs (45 knees) from 31 patients were eligible for analysis during the study period. The average age in this study was 66.1±12.8 years, with a majority of female patients (61.3%) (Table 1). Most patients were obese (22/31, 71% BMI ≥30) with an average BMI of 35.60 (SD 9.71). The patients were from diverse backgrounds (51.6% White, 32.3% Black, 12.9% multi-racial or other, and 3.2% Asian), and 16.1% Hispanic. The majority of patients were referred by orthopedic surgery/sports medicine (35.48%, 11/31), spine medicine/physical medicine and rehabilitation (19.35%, 6/31), or neurology/neurosurgery (16.13%, 5/31).

Of the GNRFAs performed, 81.6% (31/38) were first-time procedures, and 18.4% (7/31) were repeat procedures. A small proportion of patients (5/31, 16.1%) reported active prescription opioid use at the time of their procedure, with an average daily opioid use of 24.50±5.12 MME/day. Before their first GNRFA, the majority of patients had an intra-articular corticosteroid knee injection (23/31, 74.2%), and some also had visco-supplementation (12/31, 38.7%).

Pre-procedurally, the average duration of knee pain was 5.64 (SD 4.16) years. The majority of patients (63%) had severe knee OA (KL grade 4) at the time of procedure. There were five patients (16.1%) with a history of TKA prior to their first GNRFA, and the subgroup comparison did not show statistically significant outcome differences comparing the post-TKA versus native knees (p>0.05). Post-procedurally, only 21% of patients went to their prescribed post-intervention physical therapy (Table 2). Overall, the average duration of follow-up in this sample was 11.39 months (SD 9.84). We did not observe any adverse events or complications related to the GNRFA procedure in this cohort.

GNRFA outcomes

In paired comparisons to the pre-procedure baseline, the mean NRS score reduction post-procedurally was -3.17 during 0-3 months (p<0.0001), -1.31 during 3-6 months (p<0.05), and -1.92 during 6-12 months (p<0.05) (Table 3; Figure 2). There was no significant difference from baseline after one year post-procedurally. Subjectively, the mean patient-reported percent relief following their GNRFA was 81.6%. Although there was a positive trend for improvement in the quality-of-life measures, no statistically significant post-procedure improvement or worsening was observed during all time periods for PROMIS-PH, PROMIS-MH, and PHQ-9 (p>0.05; Table 3).

After their GNRFA procedure, 50% of patients maintained a NRS reduction greater than or equal to the MCID of -2.0 for at least 11.17 months (95% CI: 7.29, 13.40) (Figure 3). A history of depression was associated with non-response (n=12 GNRFAs; p=0.01944). Of note, the following factors were not significantly associated with treatment failure (p>0.05): BMI, KL grade, physical therapy adherence, fibromyalgia, chronic pain syndrome, pre-procedural opioid usage, and prior knee surgery. Lastly, nearly a quarter of all patients (22.6%, 7/31) underwent a TKA after their GNRFA, with an average time to TKA of 25.2 months.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully gathered long-term follow-up data on a real-world cohort of chronic knee patients treated by conventional GNRFA performed using the classical three-site approach. In the context of existing literature, our findings are consistent with existing evidence on short-term and long-term GNRFA outcomes. Overall, we found both statistically and clinically significant reductions in NRS pain scores during the 0-3-month follow-up period with some enduring pain relief that lasted 3-12 months post-procedurally.

The magnitude of NRS reduction in this study is similar to multiple GNRFA studies during the 1-3-month follow-up period and diminished after three months post-procedure and beyond [2,25-27]. The findings of this GNRFA study are consistent with existing literature [28], although we only observed minor improvements (not statistically significant) in PROMIS Global Health scores post-procedurally [17]. In terms of the magnitude of NRS reduction, our results showed slightly greater pain reduction than some studies [5,29-32] and slightly lower than others [25-27,33-35]. The slight variations in the magnitude of the NRS changes can be attributed to procedure techniques and case-mix complexity. Compared to our study, the NRS pain reductions reported by cooled GNRFA studies and expanded protocols were slightly higher and more enduring [9,13,36-41] although not indicative of superiority. At our practice, expanded protocol GNRFAs were occasionally offered (when appropriate) to patients who achieved some but <6 months of substantial relief from their classical three-site GNRFA.

Compared to existing GNRFA studies, a similar proportion of our patients underwent TKA post-procedurally [39], with a mean time to TKA of >2 years after their initial procedure. Based on the results of the post-TKA knee subgroup analysis, we found no significant outcome differences between the native knee subgroup and post-TKA knee subgroup, supporting existing evidence that GNRFA can be effective for post-TKA knee pain [16,42].

A history of depression was associated with non-response to GNRFA. Depression can influence knee pain [43] and is a predictor of persistent pain after TKA [44]. Patients with a history of depression responded worse to GNRFA than those without depression, which is consistent with other reports in the literature [24,45,46]. Taken together, GNRFA patients with pre-existing depression may benefit from pre-/post-procedural mental health treatment and participation in a multidisciplinary pain program or functional restoration program [47,48].

Lastly, our patient cohort is a complex population with a high burden of comorbidities because many were previously treated and referred by orthopedic or sports medicine practitioners. An interventional spine practice often manages knee OA in the context of additional comorbidities such as spine pathology, chronic pain syndrome, fibromyalgia, anxiety, and depression. In contrast, a predominantly orthopedic or sports medicine practice more often encounters patients with isolated knee pathology and fewer overlapping chronic pain or psychosocial conditions. This distinction highlights the greater clinical complexity and multifactorial background of our patient population.

Strengths and limitations

This study's main strength is its long study period (eight years) and the use of multiple different PRO measures (NRS, PROMIS-PH, PROMIS-MH, PHQ-9) to examine GNRFA outcomes. This real-world cohort consisted of many patients who often presented to our institution with late-stage knee OA (based on KL grade), who were previously treated by other orthopedic, sports medicine, and physical medicine and rehabilitation providers. One limitation is our sample size of GNRFAs, which is limited to a single provider's clinical practice. Although we performed linear mixed model analyses in an attempt to account for repeated measures and potential confounding (such as age, sex, BMI, and opioid use), the mixed model results (not shown due to being less appropriate given the limited sample size) were not vastly different than results from the unadjusted models. A limitation of our institution's electronic records is the lack of other knee outcome measures, such as the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) and Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC). Despite gathering long-term follow-up information for many patients, we observed that a sizable portion of patients were lost to follow-up after six months post-procedure or followed up at another institution. Because data collection was limited to our own institution's health records, we may have missed patient history and follow-up information from other health systems. Given the limitations intrinsic to all retrospective studies, the findings of the risk factor analysis should be interpreted within the limitations of its study design, such as non-standardized follow-up time points, incomplete data/attrition, and variation in follow-up documentation. Because we did not include any comparison/control groups, we can only report our observations and refrain from making definitive claims about treatment or technique superiority/inferiority. Moreover, the PRO changes may be confounded by other aspects of patients' medical management, including psychological support, home exercises, and over-the-counter oral analgesics. Lastly, the findings from this cohort at a large academic medical center are likely more generalizable to certain metropolitan populations but less applicable to rural populations.

Conclusions

GNRFA has been demonstrated as an effective option in the treatment course for chronic knee pain and symptomatic knee OA. In this real-world cohort, conventional GNRFA using the classical three-site technique demonstrated robust short-term pain reduction, and at least half of the cohort maintained clinically meaningful pain improvements for 11 months. For chronic knee pain, GNRFA is a reasonable therapeutic option in appropriately selected patients.
