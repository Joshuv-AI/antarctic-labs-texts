Complexity of exact sampling of the first passage of a stable subordinator Zhiyi Chi 1 1 1 Department of Statistics, University of Connecticut, U.S.A. Email: zhiyi.chi@uconn.edu


Abstract
We consider the exact sampling of the first passage of a stable subordinator across a non-increasing regular barrier. First, the sampling is reduced to one from a bivariate distribution parameterized by the index α \alpha of the subordinator and a scalar z z independent of the barrier. Then three algorithms are devised for different regions of ( α , z ) (\alpha,z) , using the acceptance-rejection method without numerical inversion or integration. When combined, the algorithms allow the exact sampling of the first passage to be done with complexity O ⁡ ( 1 + | ln ⁡ ( 1 − α ) | ) O(1+|\ln(1-\alpha)|) .

Introduction

The first passage event of a stochastic process is an important topic
in probability (cf.
[
1
,
10
]
and the numerous references therein). Due to
potential applications, the exact sampling of the first passage of a
stable or tempered stable subordinator was investigate in depth
recently
[
3
,
5
,
8
]
. In

[
8
]
, a fast algorithm was developed to sample
the first passage of a stable
subordinator of index
\alpha\in(0,1)
. When the barrier is
non-increasing and regular enough, e.g. differentiable, the
complexity of the algorithm is
O(\delta^{-3}+|\ln\alpha|+\ln N)
, where
\delta
denotes
1-\alpha
from now on and
N
is the
required number of precision bits of the output. Although
N
is
exogenous to the first passage, it is an integral part of the
bound as the algorithm requires numerical inversions and
integrations. This paper shows that exact sampling of the first
passage can be done at complexity
O(1+|\ln\delta|)
without
numerical inversions or integrations.

In exact sampling, it is generally accepted that numerical inversion or
integration cannot be done exactly in a finite amount of time, while
closed form functions are idealized to allow exact evaluation in a
fixed amount of time. By convention, power function, exponential
function, logarithm, and trigonometric functions are closed form
functions. In this paper, the Gamma function
\Gamma(x)
is also
regarded as closed form. However, in reality, no real-valued
functions can be evaluated exactly by a digital computer, and
non-exact operations such as numerical inversion or integration can be
highly precise and fast, while exact methods such as the series method

[
6
]
can have large numerical errors (
[
9
]
, p. 11). Thus, the notion of exact sampling
is not entirely aligned with numerical precision. In view of
this, to make sense of the exact sampling that we will search for,
we conceptualize it as encoding a distribution in terms of a
relatively small (random) number of i.i.d.
\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)
variables and
calls to evaluate closed form functions, where
\operatorname{Unif}(a,b)
denotes
the uniform distribution on
(a,b)
. This interpretation turns the
issue of exact sampling into one of representation, hence bypassing
the practical issue of numerical precision. It underlies almost every
acceptance-rejection (AR) algorithm in practice.

Let
S=(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a stable subordinator with
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(e^{-rS_{t}})=\exp(-tr^{\alpha})
,
r\geq 0
. The first passage of
S
across a
barrier is characterized by the time
\tau
when it occurs as well as
the pre-passage “undershoot”
S_{\tau-}
and the jump
\Delta S_{\tau}=S_{\tau}-S_{\tau-}
at the moment of passage. By definition, the
barrier is a function
b(t)
and
\tau=\inf\{t>0:S_{t}>b(t)\}
.
For a non-increasing regular barrier,
\tau
is easy to sample

[
3
]
, so it is enough to consider how to sample

(S_{\tau-},\Delta S_{\tau})
conditioning on
\tau
. The first
passage occurs either by creeping, i.e., continuously moving across
the barrier, or by jumping across the barrier. These two cases are
equivalent to
\Delta S_{\tau}=0
and
\Delta S_{\tau}>0
, respectively.
The conditional probability of creeping given
\tau
is found in

[
8
]
, so the problem is reduced to the sampling
of
(S_{\tau-},\Delta S_{\tau})
in the event of passage by jumping,
which can be further reduced to that of
S_{\tau-}
alone; see
Algorithm SFP-Alg in
[
8
]
. Thus, the
complexity of the sampling of the first passage is essentially
determined by how to sample
S_{\tau-}
conditioning on
\tau
and

\Delta S_{\tau}
being positive.

As will be seen shortly, the distribution of
S_{\tau-}
conditioning
on
\tau=t
and
\Delta S_{\tau}
being positive can be reduced to one
parameterized by
\alpha
and

\displaystyle z=\delta[\alpha/b(t)]^{\alpha/\delta}t^{1/\delta}.

(1.1)

Furthermore,
z
has a representation independent of the barrier. The
parameters play an important role in the paper. In
section

2
,
the conditional sampling of
S_{\tau-}
is formulated as the sampling
from a bivariate probability density function (p.d.f.) which is
parameterized by
\alpha
and
z
. The p.d.f. is the normalized
version of a certain function
\chi_{\alpha,z}
. In
section

3
, two
simple algorithms are given, one having complexity
O(1+z^{-1}(1+\delta/z)^{2})
and the other
O(\delta^{-2}/C_{z})
, where
C_{z}>0
is a
decreasing function of
z
. Given any
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
and

z_{0}>0
, a combination of the algorithms can sample from the
normalized
\chi_{\alpha,z}
with complexity uniformly bounded for all

(\alpha,z)
with
\alpha\leq\alpha_{0}
or
z\geq z_{0}
. However, for

(\alpha,z)
close to
(1,0)
, the combination has complexity

O(\delta^{-2})
. To reduce the complexity, most of the paper is
dedicated to the sampling for such
(\alpha,z)
. In
section

4
, for
several elementary distributions, sampling routines are obtained with
complexity uniformly bounded for their parameters. Using these
routines as building blocks, in
sections

5
and
6
, an algorithm is devised with
complexity
O(|\ln\delta|)
for all
(\alpha,z)
close to
(1,0)
. By
combining the algorithm with those in
section

3
, the normalized

\chi_{\alpha,z}
can be sampled with complexity
O(1+|\ln\delta|)
for all

z
. In
section

7
, experiments that compared the
algorithms and incorporated them to sample the first passage
efficiently are reported. All the proofs are collected in

appendix

A
.

5
Main routine of the third algorithm

This section supplies the main routine of the third algorithm to
sample from the normalized
\chi_{\alpha,z}(y,\theta)
. It actually works
for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
z>0
. The next section develops the

O(|\ln\delta|)

overhead as well as
O(|\ln\delta|)
number of operations within each

Henceforth, denote

\displaystyle\ell(y)=\ln(1+y).

(5.1)

Since

,
under the mapping

,

\ell\#f(v)=f(e^{v}-1)e^{v}=f(y)(y+1)\quad\text{with~}y=e^{v}-1.

Proposition 5.1
.

Put

\displaystyle m_{t}(y)=\frac{\mathbf{1}\!\left\{0<y<1/t\right\}}{[\ell(y)]^{\alpha}},\quad r_{t,1}(y)=\frac{\mathbf{1}\!\left\{y\geq 1/t\right\}e^{-ty}}{[\ell(1/t)]^{\alpha}},\quad r_{t,2}(y)=\frac{\mathbf{1}\!\left\{y>0\right\}e^{-ty}}{c_{\alpha}}.

(5.2)

Define

\displaystyle F_{\alpha}(y,t)=m_{t}(y)+r_{t,1}(y)+r_{t,2}(y),\quad G_{\alpha,z}(y,\theta)=c_{\alpha}e^{\alpha}H_{\alpha}({\theta})e^{-zH_{\alpha}({\theta})}F_{\alpha}(y,zH_{\alpha}({\theta})).

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha,z}(y,\theta)\leq G_{\alpha,z}(y,\theta),\quad\inf_{z>0}\frac{\int\chi_{\alpha,z}}{\int G_{\alpha,z}}\geq\frac{1}{2e^{\alpha+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(y+1)^{-\alpha}e^{-y}\,\mathrm{d}y.

is an envelope of
\chi_{\alpha,z}
. However,

. Denote by

the mapping

), let

. Then from (

(\ell\otimes\operatorname{Id})\#G_{\alpha,z}(v,\theta)=\frac{c_{\alpha}e^{\alpha}}{z}\tau e^{-\tau}[\varphi_{0,\ell(1/\tau),\delta}(v)+\ell\#r_{\tau,1}(v)+\ell\#r_{\tau,2}(v)].

\varphi^{*}_{0,\ell(1/\tau),\delta}
defined in
(

\displaystyle P_{\alpha,z}(v,\theta)=\frac{c_{\alpha}e^{\alpha}}{z}\tau e^{-\tau}[\varphi^{*}_{0,\ell(1/\tau),\delta}(v)+\ell\#r_{\tau,1}(v)+\ell\#r_{\tau,2}(v)].

7.1
Numerical issues

To implement
Algorithms

3.1
,
3.2
and

, several numerical issues
need to be addressed in particular for

close to 1. In

]

Now turn to
