Inhibition of bruton’s tyrosine kinase directly alleviates pathogenic CD4+ T cell response via PPAR-γ signaling in autoimmune uveitis
Authors: Zhiqiang Xiao, Zhuang Li, Wenjie Zhu, Guanyu Chen, Xiaoshuang Luo, Zhiqing Mai, Minzhen Wang, Haixiang Huang, Zuoyi Li, Yuxun Shi, Yanyan Xie, Hao Wu, Xiaoqing Chen, Dan Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK), a key regulator of immune responses, has been extensively studied in B cells and innate immunocytes. Small-molecule BTK inhibitors (BTKi) have shown remarkable efficacy in multiple preclinical and clinical studies for autoimmune diseases via mediating the activation of B cells and innate immunocytes. Recent studies have demonstrated functional BTK expression in T cells, contributing to T cell activation and the pathogenesis of aplastic anemia. Whether BTK is involved in uveitis, the CD4+ T cell-mediated autoimmune disease, and whether it could be a therapeutic target remain unclear.MethodsWe assessed BTK expression and phosphorylation levels in CD4+ T cells from Autoimmune uveitis (AU) patients and experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) mice. By administering BTKi to EAU mice, we evaluated its effects on ocular and systemic inflammation levels through fundus photography, histopathology, qPCR, and flow cytometry. In vitro, we assessed its impact on the Th1/Th17/Treg differentiation, and the pathogenicity of CD4+ T cells. Transcriptomic sequencing and flow cytometry were employed to explore the potential mechanism by which BTK regulates CD4+ T cells.ResultsBTK expression and activation were upregulated in CD4+ T cells from AU patients and EAU mice. BTK inhibition suppressed the polarization of Th17 and Th1 cells, but not Treg differentiation. BTK inhibition upregulated the PPAR-γ signaling, alleviating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in CD4+ T cells, thereby reducing ocular and systemic inflammation in EAU mice. Additionally, BTKi exhibited significant anti-inflammatory effects in CD4+ T cells from AU patients.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the critical role of BTK in CD4+ T cell-driven AU. BTK inhibition ameliorates oxidative stress and inflammatory responses via PPAR-γ involved signaling in CD4+ T cells and suppresses their differentiation to Th1/Th17 to restore immune homeostasis, thereby mitigating AU. These findings identify BTK as a potential therapeutic target and provide a theoretical foundation for the clinical application of BTKi in AU.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03622-0.

Background

Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK), a non-receptor tyrosine kinase of the Tec family, was traditionally thought to be expressed predominantly in B cells, where it plays a critical role in B cell receptor (BCR)-mediated proliferation, maturation and activation [1]. However, emerging evidence has revealed that BTK is also expressed in a variety of immune cells, including macrophages [2], neutrophils [3], and microglia [4], where it regulates their activation and phenotypic plasticity, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases such as multiple sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and systemic lupus erythematosus. Notably, a recent study from Xu and Cao’s team demonstrated that BTK is also expressed in T lymphocytes and plays a role in the pathogenesis of autoimmune-mediated aplastic anemia [5]. This finding addressed the long-standing neglect regarding BTK expression and functions in T cells and provided a theoretical foundation for targeting BTK in T cell-mediated autoimmune diseases.

Autoimmune uveitis (AU) is an inflammatory disease primarily mediated by CD4+ T cells. This irreversible ocular disorder severely impairs vision and causes blindness, with a blindness rate reaching 25–45% [6]. Globally, AU contributes to approximately 10% of all blindness cases, and this proportion increases to one-quarter of legal blindness cases in developing countries [7]. Experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU), a well-established animal model for AU [8], has been used in almost all related pharmacological evaluations and mechanistic investigations. Previous studies from our group and others have demonstrated that pro-inflammatory CD4+ T cell subsets, including Th1/Th17, are the primary pathogenic drivers of AU, and suppressing the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into Th1/Th17 subsets, inhibiting their pathogenicity are critical therapeutic strategies [9–11]. However, the regulatory mechanisms governing the differentiation of these pathogenic cells in AU and whether BTK plays a role in this process warrants further exploration.

Small-molecule BTK inhibitors (BTKi) represent the primary therapeutic strategy for targeted BTK modulation, owing to their distinct advantages such as high selectivity, superior tissue penetrability, favorable safety profiles and oral bioavailability [12–14]. Researchers have actively pursued the development of novel small-molecule BTK inhibitors, expanding their applications from initial use in B-cell malignancies to autoimmune diseases. Preclinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of the BTKis including evobrutinib, tolebrutinib etc., in mouse model of autoimmune encephalomyelitis [4, 15–17], rheumatoid arthritis [18], systemic lupus erythematosus [19], and alcohol-associated liver disease [3]. Excitingly, recent Phase 3 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have reported significant success of small-molecule BTK inhibitors in treating autoimmune conditions, including non-relapsing secondary progressive multiple sclerosis [20], immune thrombocytopenia [21], and chronic spontaneous urticaria [22]. Nevertheless, whether small-molecule BTKi can ameliorate AU remains unclear.

In this study, we revealed the up-regulated expression and a high activation of BTK in CD4+ T cells from AU, and BTKi significantly alleviated EAU. Mechanistically, we illustrated that the BTK inhibition effectively suppressed CD4+ T cells’ differentiation towards Th1/Th17 and their pathogenicity, and restored immune homeostasis in AU. Our study revealed the role of BTK in pathogenic CD4+ T cells, and provided a theoretical foundation for the clinical application of BTKi in AU.

Methods

Mice

In this study, female C57BL/6J mice aged 6 to 8 weeks were used. All mice were kept in a specific pathogen-free facility with cycles of 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness alternate and fed standard laboratory chow ad libitum. All mice were maintained at the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-sen University.

Subjects

This study enrolled 15 patients with active Behçet’s disease uveitis (BDU) and 21 healthy subjects, all recruited from the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center of Sun Yat-sen University. Participants were required to be aged between 18 and 60 years, with age and gender matched between two groups. The diagnosis of active BDU was based on the latest clinical criteria, including clinical manifestations, slit-lamp microscopy, and fluorescein angiography findings. Active BDU was defined as the presence of anterior chamber or vitreous cells in at least one eye, or retinal vascular leakage observed on fluorescein angiography. Healthy controls were required to have no history of autoimmune or inflammatory diseases. Participant information is provided in the Table.S1.

Induction of EAU

For active immunization-induced EAU [23], C57BL/6J mice were subcutaneously injected with 200 µg of hIRBP1 − 20 peptide (GL Biotech, Shanghai, China) emulsified in an equal volume of Complete Freund’s Adjuvant (CAT#F5506, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) containing 2.5 mg/ml Mycobacterium tuberculosis (CAT#231141, BD, USA) on day 0. Additionally, 200 ng of Pertussis toxin (CAT#180, List Biological Laboratories, USA) was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) on days 0 and 2 post-immunization.

Scoring of EAU

Clinical scoring

On day 14 after EAU inducing, the fundus images of mice by Micron IV Retina Imaging Microscope (PHONIX) were used for clinical scoring. The scores evaluation was on a scale of 0–4 based on the severity of inflammation [23]. In details, no lesion (score 0); few lesions or vasculitis (score 0.5); <5 lesions or mild vasculitis (score 1); >5 lesions or large vasculitis (score 2); large confluent lesions (score 3); retinal detachment (score 4).

In addition, retinal angiography was supplied. Mice were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 100ul 1% sodium fluorescein solution (CAT#46960-25G-F, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and monitored for retinal vessels.

Pathological scoring

Mice were sacrificed on the day 14 after EAU inducing, and eyes were isolated and fixed with eyeball fixation solution overnight, and were then dehydrated, embedded and sectioned into 10 μm for H&E staining. The pathological scores were measured and evaluated under microscopy [23]. In details, no lesion (score 0); few inflammatory infiltrations (score 0.5); mild retinal folds (score 1); moderate retinal folds or retinal detachment (score 2); extensive retinal folds or large retinal detachment (score 3); diffuse retinal detachment (score 4).

Drug Administration

Starting from day 3 or day 8 post-EAU induction, mice in the BTKi-treated group received oral gavage of Evobrutinib (CAT#S8777, Selleck, USA), a highly selective BTKi, at a dose of 3 mg/kg, prepared in a vehicle solution containing 2% DMSO, 5% Tween-80, 20% PEG, and 73% water. The dosage of these solvent components is maintained within the mouse-tolerable range [24–26]. Control mice received an equivalent volume of the vehicle. Administration was performed once daily until day 13.

For GW9662 (PPAR-γ antagonist) administration, GW9662 (1 mg/kg) or vehicle (1% DMSO + 2% Tween-80 + 10% PEG + 87% water) was administered intraperitoneally daily from day 3 to day 13 post-EAU induction.

B Cells Depletion

For depletion of B cells in mice, 250 µg of purified anti-mouse CD20 antibody (Cat#152115, BioLegend, USA) or isotype IgG (Cat#400671, BioLegend, USA) was administered via tail vein 3 days before EAU inducing. The efficiency of B cell depletion was subsequently assessed by flow cytometric analysis of peripheral blood collected from the tail vein two days post-antibody administration, as well as of lymph nodes harvested on the day of tissue collection.

T Cell Apoptosis Assay

CD4+ T cells isolated were seeded at 2 × 10^5 cells/well in 96-well plates and stimulated with anti-mouse CD3/CD28 microbeads (1 bead per 8 cells) in the presence of either the vehicle or various concentrations of BTKi. After 72 h, cells were collected and stained using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (CAT#BMS500FI/100, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometry was performed to analyze Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) staining.

T Cell Differentiation

Naïve CD4 +T cells (CD44 − CD62L+) were isolated from naïve mice using a naïve CD4 +T cell isolation kit (CAT#19765A, StemCell Technologies, CA), with purity confirmed by flow cytometry (> 95%). Cells were seeded at 2 × 10^5 cells/well in anti-CD3 (CAT#100239, BioLegend, USA) pre-coated 96-well plates and cultured under Th17, Th1, or Treg polarizing conditions in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% FBS and either the vehicle or varying concentrations of BTKi.

Th17 polarization

Cells were stimulated with 5 µg/mL anti-CD28 (CAT#102115, BioLegend, USA), 50 ng/mL IL-6 (CAT#406-ML-005/CF, R&D Systems, USA), 5ng/mL IL-23(CAT#589002, BioLegend, USA), 10 µg/mL anti-IFN-γ (CAT#505833, BioLegend, USA), 10 µg/mL anti-IL-4 (CAT#504121, BioLegend, USA), and 1 ng/mL TGF-β (CAT#BE0057, Bio X Cell, USA) for 5 days, with fresh medium replaced on day 3.

Th1 polarization

Cells were stimulated with 2 µg/mL anti-CD28 (CAT#102115, BioLegend, USA) and a Th1 differentiation kit (CAT#10953, StemCell Technologies, CA), for 5 days, with fresh medium replaced on day 3.

Treg polarization

Cells were stimulated with 2 µg/mL anti-CD28 (CAT#102115, BioLegend, USA), 10 ng/mL TGF-β (CAT#BE0057, Bio X Cell, USA), and 10 ng/mL IL-2 (CAT#575402, BioLegend, USA) for 4 days.

After differentiation, cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the proportions of Th17 (CD4+ IL-17A+), Th1 (CD4+ IFN-γ+), and Treg (CD4+ CD25+ FOXP3+) cells.

T Cell IRBP response Assay

On day 14 post-immunization, lymph node cells were isolated from actively immunized EAU mice and seeded at 2 × 10^5 cells/well in 96-well plates. Cells were stimulated with hIRBP1 − 20 (10 µg/mL) and cultured in the presence of either the vehicle (0.05% DMSO) or varying concentrations of BTKi for 72 h. Then, cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the proportions of IL-17A+, IFN-γ+, and GMCSF+ CD4+ T cells respectively.

For the BTKi-GW9662 co-treatment assay, the BTKi group received 1 µM Evobrutinib (0.05% DMSO), while the BTKi + GW9662 group received both 1 µM Evobrutinib and 1 µM GW9662 (final 0.05% DMSO). Both non-IRBP and vehicle control groups were administered an equivalent concentration of DMSO.

Generation of MHC Class II (I-Ab) IRBP4 − 15 Tetramers (Tet)

MHC class II (I-Ab) tetramers presenting the immunodominant peptide IRBP4 − 15 (HLFQPSLVLDMA) were engineered to detect TCRα/β specific for the IRBP1 − 20/MHC-II complex. The extracellular domains of I-Ab α- and β-chains were individually cloned into a pCDH-MSCV-MCS-EF1a-GFP-PURO lentiviral backbone. The IRBP4 − 15 peptide was fused to the β-chain N-terminus via a 13-amino acid flexible linker [27], while complementary leucine zipper motifs with a 6×His tag (β-chain) and AviTag (α-chain) were incorporated for heterodimer formation and purification [28]. After co-expression in murine JAWSII dendritic cells, heterodimers were isolated from lysates using anti-His magnetic beads (Beyotime), biotinylated with BirA enzyme (Beyotime) via the AviTag, eluted with 6×His peptide, and assembled into tetramers using PE-Cy7-conjugated streptavidin (eBioscience). These tetramers were subsequently used in flow cytometric assays to identify IRBP-specific (Tet+) CD4+ T cells.

Adoptive transfer experiment

IRBP-specific CD4+ T cells were transferred into naïve recipient mice to directly induce uveitis [23]. Briefly, lymph node cells were isolated from actively immunized EAU donor mice and stimulated with hIRBP1 − 20 (10 µg/ml) for 72 h. Subsequently, IRBP-specific CD4+ T cells were sorted by flow cytometry and intravenously injected into naïve recipient mice at a dose of 2 × 10^6 cells per mouse.

Flow Cytometry

Tissues were homogenized into single-cell suspensions in RPMI medium supplemented with 2% FBS and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. Cells were washed with PBS and stained using the Zombie NIR™ Fixable Viability Kit (CAT#423106, BioLegend, USA) for 10 min. After washing, surface staining was performed by incubating cells with surface antibodies at room temperature for 15 min.

For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were stimulated with Cell Stimulation Cocktail (Invitrogen, USA) in 10% FBS-supplemented medium for 4–6 h. Cells were then collected, stained for viability and surface markers as described above, and fixed/permeabilized using the FOXP3 Fix/Prem Buffer (CAT#421401, Biolegend, USA) and Permeabilization Buffer (CAT#00–8333-56, Invitrogen, USA). Intracellular antibodies were added, and cells were stained overnight at 4 °C.

For phosphoprotein staining, cells were fixed with BD Phosflow FixBuffer I (CAT#557870, BD Bioscience, USA) at 37 °C for 10–15 min, followed by extensive PBS washing. Membrane permeabilization was then performed using Phosflow PermBuffer II (CAT#558050, BD Bioscience, USA) on ice for 30 min. After washing with PBS, antibodies were applied for overnight staining at 4 °C.

The detail of the antibody used in this study was shown in Table.S2. Samples were analyzed on a Cytek flow cytometer (Cytek, USA), and data were processed using FlowJo software (v10.4).

PBMC Stimulation Assay

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from participant blood samples using Ficoll (CAT#17144003, Cytiva, Sweeden) density gradient centrifugation. PBMCs were seeded at 2 × 10^5 cells/well in 96-well plates and stimulated with anti-human CD3/CD28 beads (CAT#11131D, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) (1 bead per 4 cells) in the presence of either vehicle or varying concentrations of BTKi. After 72 h of culture, cells were harvested for flow cytometric analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Retinal tissue were dissected from each group and homogenized for RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated with the Tissue RNA Purification Kit Plus (CAT#RN002plus, ESScience, China), and its concentration/purity was determined by NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was performed with PrimeScript RT Master Mix (CAT#RR360A, TaKaRa Bio Inc., Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. qPCR assays were conducted using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (CAT#RR820A, TaKaRa Bio Inc., Japan). mRNA levels of Ifng, Il17a, Il6, and Tnf (primer sequences synthesized by Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) were normalized to the endogenous control gapdh and calculated via the 2–ΔΔCt method.

Bulk RNA Sequencing (Bulk RNA-seq)

On day 14 post-immunization, lymph node cells were isolated from four EAU mice and stimulated with hIRBP1− 20. Cells were cultured in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% FBS and either the vehicle (0.1% DMSO) or 1µM BTKi for 72 h. Then cells were stained using the Zombie NIR™ Fixable Viability Kit and anti-CD4 antibody. Viable CD4+ T cells (zombie− CD4+) were sorted using a flow cytometer (BD, USA), with a purity exceeding 99%.

Total RNA was extracted from CD4+ T cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. RNA purity and concentration were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), while RNA integrity was verified with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

The libraries were sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw fastq files were preprocessed with fastp [29] to filter out low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 [30]. Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM [31], and read counts per gene were obtained using HTSeq-count [32]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted in R (v 3.2.0) to assess sample reproducibility.

Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 [33], with significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those with a Q value < 0.05 and a fold change >2 or < 0.5. Hierarchical clustering of DEGs was carried out in R (v 3.2.0) to visualize gene expression patterns across groups and samples.

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted based on the hypergeometric distribution, covering Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG pathways, and WikiPathways. Significant terms were identified using R (v 3.2.0), and results were visualized as column diagrams and bubble diagrams.

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s unpaired two-tailed t-test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons with correction for multiple testing was applied. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.005, ****p < 0.001, and nsp refers to no significancy. For animal experiment data with substantial intra-group standard deviation, we have reported 95% confidence intervals for all comparisons (Table. S3). All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.

To clarity the in vivo relevance, we further analyzed BTK expression and activation in CD4+ T cells of EAU mice and blank control mice. Our results revealed that both BTK expression and its phosphorylation levels were significantly elevated in CD4+ T cells from EAU mice compared to blank controls (Fig. 1C, D). In addition, they were much higher in Th17 cells from EAU compared with those from blank control mice. However, in Treg cells, BTK phosphorylation showed no significant differences between EAU mice and blank control, although a modest upward trend was observed in the EAU group. (Fig. 1E, F). We further evaluated the preference of BTK expression and activation between Th17 and Treg in vivo. In accordance with results in vitro, in EAU mice, a significantly higher level of BTK expression and phosphorylation were observed in Th17 cells compared with Treg cells (Fig. 1E). These findings uncovered heterogeneous BTK expression and activation states across CD4+ T cell subsets, implicating a potential role of BTK in driving EAU via pathogenic CD4+ T cell lineages.

Vehicle-treated EAU mice exhibited significant retinal edema, vascular dilation, tortuosity, leakage, and perivascular sheathing, whereas BTKi-treated EAU mice showed no apparent lesions, with a significant reduction in the clinical scores (Fig. 2B, C). The fluorescein angiography demonstrated substantial mitigation of retinal vascular lesions by BTKi (Fig. 2B). In addition, pathology showed that vehicle-treated EAU mice had pronounced retinal folds, structural disorganization, and inflammatory cell infiltration with significantly higher pathological scores than BTKi-treated mice (Fig. 2B, D). We further collected retinal tissue for qPCR analysis, and the results demonstrated significantly reduced mRNA levels of inflammatory cytokines such as Il17a, Ifng, Tnf, and Il6 in the BTKi group (Fig. 2E). We also collected retinal tissue and intraocular fluids for flow cytometry, and found that BTKi treatment markedly reduced the infiltration of CD45 + immune cells, particularly CD4+ T cells, including IL-17 A+, IFN-γ+, TNF-α+, and GMCSF + inflammatory subsets (Fig. 2F). First of all, we confirmed BTKi inhibited the BTK expression and phosphorylation in CD4+ T cells of EAU mice (Fig. 3A, B). Following, we examined the proportions of Th17, Th1, and GMCSF+ CD4+ T cells, which are the pathogenic subsets driving inflammation in EAU. As anticipated, BTK inhibition significantly reduced the proportions of these T cell subsets both in the lymph nodes and the spleen (Fig. 3C, E). To exclude the involvement of B cells, we administered a purified anti-mouse CD20 antibody to deplete B cells prior to EAU induction (Fig.S3A). Successful B cell depletion was confirmed by flow cytometry analysis (Fig.S3B, C). We observed that EAU could be successfully induced in B cell-depleted mice (Fig.S3D). Consistently, the BTKi-treated mice exhibited a lower clinical and pathological score compared with the vehicle group (Fig.S3D, E). Moreover, flow cytometric analysis also demonstrated that BTK inhibition reduced the proportions of Th1, Th17, and GMCSF+ CD4+ T cells in lymph nodes and spleen of B cell-depleted EAU mice (Fig.S3F-G). These indicate that BTK inhibition ameliorates the CD4 + T cell-mediated immune inflammation, which occurs independently of B cells.

To further assess the activation of CD4+ T cells, we evaluated the expression of CD69, an activation marker of CD4+ T cells, and the results showed that the proportion of CD69+ CD4+ T cells was markedly reduced in BTKi-treated EAU mice (Fig. 3G, H), indicating suppressed T cell activation. Considering that in EAU mice, naïve CD4+ T cells (CD44- CD62L+) undergo activation and turned into effector populations (CD44+ CD62L-) upon IRBP stimulation, we further quantified the proportions of these two subsets. The results showed that BTKi significantly decreased the proportions of effector CD4+ T cells while increasing the proportion of naïve CD4 + T cells in the draining lymph nodes (Fig. 3I-K), and similar results were observed in the spleen (Fig. 3L-N).

Our findings indicate that BTK inhibition alleviates systemic immune inflammation in EAU mice by modulating the activation and composition of pathogenic CD4+ T cell subsets.

BTK Inhibition impairs Th17/Th1 polarization in vitro

To further investigate how BTK inhibition modulates CD4+ T cell to suppress immune inflammation, we evaluated its effects on CD4 + T cell polarization. First, we treated CD4+ T cell with three concentrations (100 nM, 1 µM, and 10 µM) of BTKi, and found that BTKi concentrations did not significantly affect lymphocytes viability (Fig. S4A, B) and apoptosis (Fig. S4C-E) in vitro. As known, NF-κB is a critical transcription factor that promotes the transcription of various inflammatory genes in immune cells. Among its subunits, p65 plays a pivotal role in regulating NF-κB transcriptional activity through phosphorylation, thereby triggering immune inflammation [35]. In addition, excessive oxidative stress also reflects cellular inflammatory phenotypes. Our previous work showed that reactive oxygen species (ROS) drove CD4+ T cell inflammation and exacerbated AU [11]. Here, our data showed that BTK inhibition significantly downregulated ROS and p-p65 levels, as well as TNF-α (the NF-κB downstream inflammatory cytokine) in CD4 + T cells under IRBP stimulation in vitro (Fig. 6E, G, Fig.S6B). Similarly, in vivo experiments demonstrated that BTKi decreased ROS levels of ocular tissue and CD4 + T cells (Fig.S6A, Fig. 6F), and decreased p-p65 levels of CD4 + T cells (Fig. 6H) were also observed in EAU mice. These results indicated that BTK inhibition alleviates oxidative stress and inflammation by reducing ROS levels and NF-κB activation in CD4 + T cells.

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPAR-γ), a member of PPARs family, is widely expressed in various immune cells and plays a crucial role in modulating oxidant stress [36] and inflammation [37, 38]. And in our sequencing data, the significant upregulation of the PPAR signaling was observed (Fig. 6B, D). Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis also revealed significant interactions among the PPARs signaling pathway, oxidative stress and damage pathways, and the NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 6I). Further, flow cytometry demonstrated that BTKi significantly upregulated PPAR-γ expression in CD4 + T cells of EAU mice (Fig. 6J) or under IRBP stimulation (Fig. 6K). We next used the PPAR-γ specific antagonist 2-Chloro-5-nitro-N-phenylbenzamide (GW9662), which could covalently bind to the PPAR-γ and block the PPAR-γ signaling [39, 40]. We found that GW9662 prevented the immunomodulatory effects on CD4 + T cells of BTKi in vitro (Fig. 6L). In vivo experiments also indicated that co-administration of GW9662 with BTKi reduced the therapeutic effects of BTK inhibition in EAU (Fig. 6M).

Collectively, BTK inhibition ameliorates oxidative stress and immune inflammation in CD4+ T cells of EAU, and the PPAR-γ signaling is involved in this procedure.

Discussion

Growing evidence suggests that BTK plays a critical role in autoimmune diseases, and researchers are continuously developing novel small-molecule BTK inhibitors for their treatment. In this study, we elucidated the role of BTK in CD4+ T cell-mediated AU and demonstrated that inhibiting BTK effectively alleviates inflammation in both the mouse model and CD4+ T cells from AU patients. Mechanistically, BTK inhibition suppresses the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into pathogenic Th17/Th1 subsets and ameliorates oxidative stress and inflammation in CD4 + T cells with PPAR-γ signaling involvement. This study highlights the pivotal role of BTK in autoreactive CD4+ T cells and provides a theoretical foundation for the therapeutic application of the small-molecule BTK inhibitors in AU.

BTK was initially discovered for its role in X-linked agammaglobulinemia [41]. Patients with BTK gene mutations fail to produce antibodies due to defective B cell development, leading to severe humoral immunodeficiency. Subsequent studies revealed the importance of BTK in myeloid cells, including monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, and neuroimmune cells like microglia [2–4]. For a long time, BTK was thought to be absent in T cells due to its low transcriptional levels [42]. However, during the treatment of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) patients with BTK inhibitors, researchers observed a reduction in total T cell numbers, their activation/exhaustion markers and Th17 subset frequency [43, 44]. Additionally, combining BTK inhibitors with CAR-T cell therapy reduced the risk of cytokine release syndrome [45]. These findings suggested a potential role of BTK in T cells. Similarly, studies on autoimmune encephalomyelitis showed that BTK inhibitors effectively reduced effector T cell proportions and cytokine secretion, although these therapeutic effects have been conventionally interpreted as secondary to B cells or myeloid cells modulation rather than direct T cell-intrinsic regulation [16, 46]. It was not until 2020 that Xu et al. demonstrated that BTK, despite low mRNA levels, is expressed at protein levels comparable to those in B cells and mediates T cell activation through the TCR/PLC-γ pathway, promoting autoimmune aplastic anemia [5]. Nevertheless, the understanding of BTK in T cells remains limited, necessitating further investigation into its role in autoreactive T cells.

AU is a primarily CD4+ T cell-mediated inflammatory disease [9], of which pathogenesis is not fully understood. In this study, we observed significant upregulation of BTK expression and activation in CD4+ T cells from patients with AU and EAU mice, suggesting that BTK may critically regulate CD4+ T cell responses in AU pathogenesis. Prior studies have demonstrated elevated BTK expression and phosphorylation in effector/memory T cells, with BTK mediating CD4+ T cell activation in aplastic anemia [5]. Consistent with these findings, our results also established that BTK signaling is essential for CD4+ T cell activation and effector differentiation in EAU mice. Notably, we extended these observations by systematically comparing BTK activity across functionally distinct CD4+ T cell subsets including Th17 and Treg. Our data revealed a striking preferential enhancement of BTK expression and activation in pro-inflammatory Th17 cells compared to Treg cells. These results implicated BTK as a pivotal regulator of AU pathogenesis, particularly within pro-inflammatory CD4+ T cell populations, and provided a mechanistic rationale for targeting BTK in therapeutic interventions in our following experiments.

BTKi were initially developed for B cell-related lymphomas. The success of ibrutinib and acalabrutinib revolutionized the treatment of CLL [47]. Subsequently, as understanding of BTK biology advanced, the potential of BTKi in autoimmune diseases began to emerge [4, 5, 15, 16, 20–22]. Our study demonstrated that BTKi alleviated ocular and systemic inflammation in EAU mice without affecting body weight or liver/kidney pathology (Fig.S1). Interestingly, its immunoregulatory effects rely on the suppression of the polarization of pathological CD4 + T cell subsets and inflammatory cytokine secretion in IRBP-specific CD4 + T cells, while having no significant impact on Treg. This selectivity likely reflects differential dependence on BTK signaling during subset polarization. Although we observed elevated BTK phosphorylation in Treg-polarizing CD4 + T cells compared to undifferentiated Th0 cells, Treg differentiation itself was found to be BTK-independent. In contrast, Th17 cells, which exhibited much higher BTK expression and activation than Tregs, demonstrated marked reliance on BTK signaling. BTK inhibition significantly impaired Th17 polarization and abolished the pathogenic capacity of IRBP-specific CD4+ T cells, as shown by reduced the secretion of Th17-related cytokine like IL-17A/GMCSF and attenuated disease induction in vivo. These findings collectively establish BTK as a non-redundant regulator of Th17 differentiation and pathogenicity. Moreover, its immunoregulatory effects were also validated in CD4+ T cells from AU patients, suggesting that BTK inhibitors hold promise as a novel therapeutic agent for AU.

PPAR-γ, a member of PPARs family, is widely expressed in immune cells and involved in immune tolerance [38]. Downregulated PPAR-γ was observed in many autoimmune diseases including psoriasis [37] and multiple sclerosis [38]. Studies also have shown that PPAR-γ inhibits the expression of inflammatory cytokines [38, 48] and differentiation towards Th1 and Th17 in CD4+ T cells [37, 49, 50]. Additionally, PPAR-γ’s effects on ROS reduction and oxidative stress mitigation also contribute to its anti-inflammatory role. Numerous studies have linked oxidative stress to the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases, including AU [51–56], where it forms a vicious cycle with immune inflammation. ROS strongly activates inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and STAT, promoting the transcription of cytokines and driving T cell polarization toward Th17 [55, 57], while subsequent immune activation further exacerbates oxidative stress. PPAR-γ mitigates oxidative stress by inhibiting NADPH oxidase to reduce ROS generation [58] and upregulating antioxidant proteins [59], avoiding inflammation caused by redox imbalance. Our study found that BTK inhibition reduced ROS levels and NF-κB activation, while upregulated the PPAR-γ in CD4+ T cells. And the PPAR-γ inhibition prevented BTKi’s immunoregulatory effects. These indicated that PPAR-γ is involved in the immunomodulatory effect of BTK inhibition, although the direct relationship between BTK and PPAR-γ has not been revealed currently. According to the previous studies, extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2), an important molecule downstream of BTK signaling [60–63], can induce the phosphorylation of PPAR-γ directly, leading to its functional downregulation and degradation [64–66]. Thus, the downregulation of ERK1/2 signaling induced by BTK inhibition could provide a plausible explanation for the PPAR-γ upregulation observed after BTK inhibition in our study.

However, there are also several limitations in our study. In addition to CD4+ T cells and B cells, other immune cells including neutrophils and microglia also contribute to autoimmune uveitis directly or indirectly [67–70]. Although we have demonstrated that the immunoregulatory effect of BTK inhibition on CD4+ T cells occurs independently of B cells, existing literature indicates that BTK also contributes to autoimmune pathogenesis by modulating macrophages and neutrophils [4, 5, 15, 16]. We therefore hypothesize that BTK inhibition may alleviate uveitis via these additional cellular pathways, a possibility that warrants systematic future validation. Furthermore, while our study implicates the involvement of PPAR-γ in the immunoregulatory effects of BTK inhibition, the precise underlying mechanisms require further elucidation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study innovatively demonstrates the critical role of BTK in the pathogenic CD4+ T cells from AU, and establishes that BTK inhibition alleviates AU by ameliorating oxidative stress and immune inflammation in CD4+ T cells with PPAR-γ signaling involvement, and suppressing Th1 and Th17 polarization. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the immune-inflammatory mechanisms of CD4+ T cells in AU, but also provide a novel therapeutic target for AU treatment. Furthermore, this study lays a solid theoretical foundation for the clinical application of small-molecule BTKi in AU, paving the way for the development of more precise immunomodulatory therapies.
