Potential roles of pseudobulbs in mediating signal responses and hormone translocation during floral bud dormancy release in Pleione bulbocodioides
Authors: Xiu-Mei Dong, Shi-Bao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pleione bulbocodioides, a world-renowned ornamental orchid, exhibits a pronounced winter dormancy in its floral buds, thereby presenting a spring flowering characteristic. Currently, the mechanisms underlying floral bud dormancy and its release in this orchid remain unclear. In this study, we investigated the regulatory mechanism of floral bud dormancy release in P. bulbocodioides using integrated physiological and transcriptome analysis. We observed that floral bud dormancy in P. bulbocodioides was significantly released within a one-week period, which was accompanied by a sharp decrease in abscisic acid (ABA) content and a simultaneous increase in gibberellin (GA) content and the GA/ABA ratio in both floral buds and pseudobulbs. Importantly, the levels of these hormones are significantly higher in the pseudobulbs than in the corresponding buds. Transcriptomic analysis identified ABA2-2 (transcript_30713) and GA20ox (transcript_29841) as key genes involved in ABA and GA biosynthesis, respectively. Furthermore, we speculate that pseudobulbs may serve as critical hubs for signal perception and hormone redistribution, supplying buds with phytohormones via transporters such as ABCGs and NPFs. This transport process is potentially regulated by day-length signaling, mediated by CONSTANS (CO) and phytochrome A (PHYA). Our results provide valuable insights into the key internal factors involved in floral bud dormancy release in Pleione species, and highlight the potential role of pseudobulbs in this process, thereby providing a theoretical basis for manipulating flowering time in this genus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09200-7.

Introduction

Pleione (Orchidaceae), a genus of perennial herbaceous plants comprising about 30 species, is renowned for its ornamental value. Its members are primarily distributed in mountainous regions from the Himalayas to the Indo-China Peninsula [1]. Pleione species are widely appreciated for their large, colorful flowers, and their leafless flowering period. While most species within this genus bloom in spring, four are known to flower in autumn. Our previous study demonstrated that both spring-flowering and autumn-flowering species complete floral bud differentiation during the summer. However, autumn-flowering species immediately bloom after completion of floral bud differentiation, while spring-flowering species enter a dormancy phase until March or April of the following year. The dormancy of floral buds is the main reason for the differentiation in flowering time among different Pleione species [2]. To date, the mechanism underlying floral bud dormancy and bud break in spring-flowering Pleione species remains unclear.

Pleione bulbocodioides is a typical spring-flowering taxon whose floral buds exhibit a pronounced winter-dormancy characteristics. In autumn, after the leaves and roots have completely senesced, the newly formed pseudobulbs enter winter dormancy, while the already-differentiated lateral floral buds enter dormancy. When spring arrives, new roots emerge from the base of dormant floral buds, dormancy is gradually released, and the buds elongate to bloom [3]. This natural phenology confines its flowering period to March-April, severely limiting off-season production and commercialization. Therefore, elucidating the factors influencing floral bud dormancy release in P. bulbocodioides is crucial for achieving precise regulation of flowering-time and for providing theoretical support for its industrial cultivation.

Dormancy refers to a temporary suspension of visible growth in meristematic tissues, such as seeds, shoot apices, and buds. It serves as a survival strategy that enable biennial or perennial plants to survive under unfavorable environmental conditions. Growth resumes only when favorable conditions return and dormancy is broken [4–6]. Bud dormancy, a typical form of plant dormancy, is categorized into three stages: para-dormancy, endo-dormancy and eco-dormancy [7]. Among these, the release of endo-dormancy is a critical step for ensuring flowering in ornamental plants. This process is known to be regulated by multiple internal and external factors, including plant hormones, nutrients, light, and temperature [5, 6, 8–10]. However, our previous observations indicated that, even under identical conditions, the flower buds of autumn-flowering Pleione species proceed directly to flowering after completing floral bud differentiation, whereas those of spring-flowering species enter dormancy. Moreover, even when temperature and light conditions are held constant, spring-flowering Pleione species still initiate dormancy after a specific growth period. This suggests that environmental factors such as temperature and light may not be the primary triggers for dormancy induction in spring-flowering Pleione species. Instead, the process appears to be governed predominantly by endogenous physiological mechanisms.

Plant hormones are crucial internal factors that regulate bud dormancy. Among them, abscisic acid (ABA) promotes the establishment and maintenance of bud dormancy, whereas gibberellic acid (GA) plays a key role in dormancy release. Importantly, ABA and GA do not act independently but function antagonistically yet coordinately to regulate both dormancy induction and release [11–13]. Additionally, auxins, cytokinins, ethylene, brassinosteroid and jasmonic acid also participate in modulating dormancy induction, maintenance, and release [10, 14–16]. This regulation occurs at multiple levels, encompassing hormone biosynthesis, catabolism, perception, and transport. The best-characterized ABA transporters are ATP-binding cassette G-family (ABCG) proteins and NITRATE TRANSPORTER 1/PEPTIDE TRANSPORTER (NPF) family members, with NPF also serving as GA transporters [17]. In addition, sugars will eventually be exported transporter (SWEET) family proteins function as both a GA transporter and sugar transporter to regulate seed germination in rice [17, 18]. Functional studies underscore the importance of this hormonal balance. For instance, silencing of PsNCED3 promoted bud break in tree peony [19]. In potato (Solanum tuberosum), overexpressing of GA2ox, and GA20ox, both led to early sprouting, confirming that endogenous GAs terminate dormancy and promote bud break [20]. Furthermore, PavGA20ox-2 may also influence sweet cherry (Prunus avium) dormancy release by interacting with GA receptors (GID1s) [21].

Day length influences plant dormancy primarily by modulating hormone synthesis and signaling pathways [22, 23]. Short days (SD) induce the expression of genes involved in ABA biosynthesis while suppressing GA synthesis. In contrast, long days enhance GA biosynthesis, leading to an altered GA/ABA ratio that promotes dormancy release [23, 24]. In winter-dormant species, long days promote growth, whereas short days inhibit bud elongation and induce bud dormancy. CONSTANS (CO) and FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) are recognized as key regulators of SD signaling [25]. Among light-sensing pathways, phytochrome-mediated pathway plays a major role. Five phytochrome genes (PHYA-PHYE) have been identified, all contributing to bud dormancy regulation [9]. PHYA overexpression can inhibit bud growth and accelerate leaf abscission, whereas its suppression promotes bud formation and development [26]. Besides photoperiod, prolonged chilling also induces GA biosynthesis and inhibits ABA biosynthesis to promote bud dormancy release [19, 27]. While, the roles of GA and ABA involved in the release of dormancy is not only dependent on chilling. In tree peony (Paeonia suffruticosa), the process of bud dormancy release is hormone-mediate, but temperature-independent [28].

In P. bulbocodioides, the dormancy of its already-differentiated floral buds was broken earlier under normal condition (20 ℃ /15 ℃) than under lower temperatures (15 ℃ /10 ℃ and 10 ℃ /5 ℃). In addition, the levels of starch and soluble sugar in pseudobulbs increased during dormancy release under these three temperature treatments [29]. The rise in total soluble sugars accompanies the shift from the dormant to the non-dormant state and is crucial for floral bud dormancy release in Rhododendron delavayi Franch [30]. As dormancy is released, soluble sugars not only supply energy but also act as signaling molecules [30–32]. The high concentrations of sucrose and glucose will reduce the sensitivity of plant cells to GA and increase the sensitivity of plant cells to ABA, thereby promoting dormancy induction and maintenance [33]. To date, the functions and regulatory networks of many key genes and metabolites related to bud dormancy in dicotyledonous plants have been largely elucidated [19, 27, 34–39]. However, key genes involved in bud dormancy and bud break in Orchidaceae, the largest monocot family, are still poorly understood. In addition, carbohydrate metabolism and plant hormone signal transduction were activated to break dormancy in Lilium pumilum [40]. The roles of hormones and soluble sugars in Pleione bud dormancy release are still unclear.

In this study, we performed an integrated analysis of hormone/sugar-targeted metabolomics and transcriptomics in P. bulbocodioides to identify key metabolites and genes underlying floral bud dormancy release in spring-flowering Pleione. Although hormones and soluble sugars are known to be implicated in dormancy induction and release, it remains unclear whether the relevant signals or metabolites are transported from pseudobulbs to the buds during dormancy release in P. bulbocodioides. Therefore, we further examined the dynamic changes in ABA, GA and soluble sugars, as well as the expression patterns of genes involved in their biosynthesis and transport in pseudobulbs across the dormancy-release period.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Pseudobulbs of Pleione bulbocodioides were grown and identified by Wei Zhang, and voucher specimens are deposited in Herbarium of Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Kunming, China), The bar code number is 1,498,261 [41]. In the winter of 2021, three-year-old dormant pseudobulbs of P. bulbocodioides (without leaves and roots) were cultivated in a greenhouse at the Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The pseudobulbs were maintained under 35%-40% full sunlight, and at temperatures ranging from 18 °C to 28 °C until 12 January 2022 (20220112), after which weekly watering was initiated. The cultivation substrate was composed 70% pine bark (1 cm × 1 cm), 20% moss, and 10% humus.

Buds and pseudobulbs samples were collected respectively at four distinct stages during the release of bud dormancy, as determined by the length of newly emerged roots of floral buds. The first sampling took place on 26 January 2022 (20220126), when no new roots were present. Subsequent samples were collected on 14 February, 25 February, and 2 March 2022, when the new roots had reached approximate lengths of 0.1 cm, 0.5 cm, and 1.0 cm, respectively. Each biological replicate consisted of 15 buds or pseudobulbs. Three biological replicates were collected at 10:00 AM on each sampling date. The collected tissues were immediately shredded, frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at -80 °C for subsequently analysis.

RNA preparation, PacBio Iso-Seq library preparation, sequencing and data analysis

RNA extraction was performed using the same method as described in our previous study [42]. Total RNA from buds at four stages was fully mixed in equal quantities to construct PacBio Iso-Seq library. PacBio Iso-Seq library preparation, sequencing and subsequent data analysis were conducted by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd (www.metware.cn) following established protocols [43].

Illumina transcriptome (RNA-Seq), transcriptome analysis, and gene expression validation

RNA preparation, RNA sequencing, transcriptome data analysis, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‐PCR) were conducted using the same method as described in our previous study [42]. Primers for qRT‐PCR are listed in Supporting Information: Table S4.

Hormone-targeted metabolome analysis

Buds from three biological replicates, each consisting of fifteen plants, were used for endogenous phytohormone quantification. The extraction, detection, and analysis of total endogenous phytohormones were performed by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd (www.metware.cn) using an AB Sciex QTRAP6500 LC-MS/MS platform. Approximately 50 mg of bud sample grounded in liquid nitrogen, was added 1 mL methanol/water/formic acid (15:4:1, V/V/V), with 10 µL internal standard mixed solution (100 ng/mL) and vortexed immediately for 10 min. Then, the mixture was centrifugated at 12,000 g for 5 min under 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to clean plastic microtubes, followed by evaporation to dryness and dissolved in 100 µL 80%methanol (V/V), and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter for further LC-MS/MS analysis. Phytohormone contents were quantified by comparing the peak areas of each endogenous compound with those of their corresponding internal standard.

Pseudobulbs ABA and GA content detection

Pseudobulbs from three biological replicates, each consisting of fifteen plants, were used to quantify endogenous abscisic acid (ABA), and gibberellic acid (GA). ABA content was determined following the procedure described in our previous study [42]. GA content was measured using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (SBJ-P183, Nanjing SenBeiJia Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Sugar-targeted metabolome analysis

Buds from three biological replicates, each biological replicate consisting of fifteen plants, were used to quantify soluble sugars. Sugar contents were detected by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd (www.metware.cn) using an Agilent 8890-5977B platform. Approximately 20 mg of bud sample grounded in liquid nitrogen, was added 500 µL methanol: isopropanol: water (3:3:2 V/V/V), vortexed immediately for 3 min and then sonicated for 30 min. After extraction, the sample was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 3 min under 4 °C. Subsequently, 12.5 µL of the supernatant was mixed with 20 µL internal standard (250 µg/mL) and evaporated under a stream of nitrogen gas. The sample transferred to a lyophilizer for freeze-drying. The dried sample was added 100µL solution of methoxyamine hydrochloride in pyridine (15 mg/mL), and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Then, 100 µL of BSTFA was added into the mixture, which was vortex-mixed and then maintained at 37 °C for 30 min. Finaly, the derivatized sample was diluted to an appropriate concentration and analyzed by GC-MS. Sugar contents were quantified by comparing the peak areas of the endogenous sugars with those of their corresponding internal standards.

Pseudobulbs fructose and xylose content detection

Pseudobulbs from three biological replicates, each consisting of fifteen plants, were used to quantify sugar contents (fructose and D-xylose) using commercial assay kits (BC4395 and BC2455, Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Means and standard deviation (SD) for all data were calculated using Microsoft Excel 2003 (Microsoft, Redmond, WD, USA). Significant differences among groups were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Tukey’s post hoc test was performed for multiple comparisons, with the significance level set at p < 0.05. Different letters above the bars indicate statistically significant differences.

Results

Identification of bud dormancy-release stages in P. bulbocodioides

In this study, we observed that in Pleione bulbocodioides, no visible roots were present on the floral buds from October 2021 to late 31 January 2022, indicating that the dormancy period lasted approximately from October to the following February. A transition phase occurred in February, marked by the emergence of new roots from the base of floral buds. Dormancy release was observed in March, when the new roots grown to approximately 1.0 cm in length. Concurrently, the floral buds elongated from about 1.5 cm to 3.0 cm in length. Based on these morphological changes in the roots associated with the floral buds, we defined four distinct stages of floral bud dormancy and bud break (S1-S4) in P. bulbocodioides (Fig. 1).

Transcriptome analysis of bud dormancy release-related genes and KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs

To identify genes differentially expressed during floral bud dormancy-release period, we conducted transcriptome analysis of floral buds collected across four distinct dormancy-release stages. Due to the lack of a reference genome for Pleione species, PacBio Iso-Seq sequencing was employed to obtain full-length transcripts (isoforms) from P. bulbocodioides floral buds. Using a combination of PacBio Iso-Seq and RNA-Sequencing, a total of 25,769 unique full-length transcripts were assembled through pipeline processing. Among these, 23,496 transcripts were considered expressed, with FPKM (fragments per kilobase per million) values greater than 1 in at least one sample (Table S1). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the first principal component (PC1) accounted for 55.4% of the total variance and clearly separated stages S1–S3 from stage S4 (Fig. 2A). Global gene expression patterns varied distinctly across the four stages (Fig. 2B). We identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between each pair of developmental stages (Fig. 2C) and assigned these DEGs to KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathways. The 20 most significantly enriched pathways are presented in Fig. 2D. The results highlighted that the most dynamically regulated pathways during floral bud dormancy release were “plant hormone signal transduction”, “glycolysis/gluconeogenesis”, “carbon metabolism”, and “carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms”.

Changes of bud phytohormones across bud dormancy-release stages

To identify key phytohormones involved in the floral bud dormancy-release of P. bulbocodioides, we performed hormone-targeted metabolomics profiling. PCA was used to evaluate hormonal differences across the four distinct dormancy release stages. The first principal component (PC1) explained 35.8% of the total variance, clearly separated stages S1–S3 from stage S4. The second principal component (PC2) explained 23.84% of the total variance and distinguished stage S1 from stages S2–S3. Notably, the greatest metabolic divergence was observed between stage S4 and stages S1–S3 (Fig. 3A). A total of 69 annotated metabolites were detected in at least one sample, among which 42 exhibited differential accumulation (Fig. 3B-C, Table S2). The accumulation dynamics of five major hormonal compounds, including abscisic acid (ABA), cytokinins (isopentenyl adenine, iP), gibberellin (GA20), jasmonic acid (JA, JA-ILE), and salicylic acid (SA), were shown in a bar chart (Fig. 3C). The results revealed that ABA and iP levels remained elevated from stages S1 to S3, but decreased significantly at S4. In contrast, the contents of GA20, JA, and JA-ILE were low at stages S1-S3, and increased significantly at S4. The GA20/ABA ratio exhibited a similar increasing trend during dormancy release. Conversely, SA content did not differ significantly across the dormancy-release stages (Fig. 3D).

Identification of ABA and GA biosynthesis hub genes across bud dormancy-release stages

To investigate the expression patterns of hub structural genes involved in ABA and GA biosynthesis, we identified homologous sequences in P. bulbocodioides using BLASTP and Pfam searches with known protein queries. Among the genes identified in the transcriptome dataset, the ABA biosynthesis pathway included 1 PSY, 1 PDS, 1 ZDS, 1 Z-ISO, 3 CRITSO, 1 LCYB, 1 BCH, 4 ZEP, 2 NCED, 2 ABA2, and 1 AAO3, while the GA biosynthesis pathway contained 1 CPS/KS, 1 KO, 1 KAO, 2 GA13ox, 4 GA20ox, 1 GA3ox, and 2 GA2ox (Table S1). Heatmaps based on FPKM values illustrated the expression profiles of these biosynthesis genes. ABA biosynthesis hub genes, including PSY (transcript_16280), ZEP (transcript_22794), NCED (transcript_12833), ABA2 (transcript_30713), and AAO3 (transcript_376), showed lower expression levels in floral buds at stage S4. In contrast, GA biosynthesis genes GA20ox (transcript_27746, and transcript_29841) were highly expressed at stage S4 (Fig. 4A-B). The expression patterns of selected hub genes were further validated using qRT-PCR. The results confirmed that among ABA biosynthesis genes, only ZEP (transcript_22794) and ABA2-2 (transcript_30713) were downregulated from stage S1 to S4, whereas GA biosynthesis gene GA20ox (transcript_29841) was upregulated during the same period (Fig. 4C-D).

Changes of the accumulation, biosynthesis and transportation of ABA and GA in pseudobulbs across bud dormancy-release stages

To investigate the potential role of pseudobulbs in floral bud dormancy release, we measured the contents of ABA and GA in pseudobulbs at four dormancy-release stages. The results showed that ABA content did not differ significantly between S1 and S2 but decreased markedly at S3 and S4. In contrast, both GA content and GA/ABA ratio increased significantly only at the S4 stage. Notably, when comparing across tissues, the concentrations of ABA and GA in pseudobulbs (Fig. 5A-C) were substantially higher than those in the corresponding floral buds (Fig. 3D), suggesting that pseudobulbs may serve as a reservoir for these hormones. We further examined the expression patterns of differentially expressed ABA and GA biosynthesis genes using qRT-PCR. The expression of ZEP (transcript_22794), and ABA2-2 (transcript_30713) was upregulated from S1 to S3 but downregulated from S3 to S4. Meanwhile, the expression of GA20ox (transcript_29841) peaked at S2 and subsequently declined from S2 to S4 (Fig. 5D–E).

To explore whether ABA and GA are transported from pseudobulbs to floral buds to regulate dormancy-release process, we analyzed the expression profiles of putative genes encoding hormone transporters in the transcriptome data. These included genes encoding ABCG, NPF, and SWEET family members in floral buds (Fig. 6A–C). We further validated the expression patterns of key transporter genes in both pseudobulbs and buds via qRT-PCR. In floral buds, the expression of several ABCGs (transcript_5984, transcript_11787, transcript_10592, transcript_2356, and transcript_307) and one NPF (transcript_15973) was downregulated at S4, while SWEET genes showed no significant changes across stages (Fig. 6D). In pseudobulbs, the expression patterns of transporter genes were more variable, ABCG3-3 (transcript_5984), and ABCG11-11 (transcript_10592) were upregulated at S2 and S3 but downregulated at S4; ABCG28-2 (transcript_2356) was downregulated from S1 to S4; ABCG42-4 (transcript_307) was highly expressed at S2; NPF3.1-1 (transcript_12358) was upregulated at S3 and S4, whereas NPF3.1-5 (transcript_15973) was downregulated at S2 to S4. SWEET genes did not exhibit consistent expression changes in pseudobulbs (Fig. 6E).

Changes of the accumulation and transportation of sugars across bud dormancy-release stages

To identify key sugars involved in the dormancy release of P. bulbocodioides floral buds, we performed sugar-targeted metabolomics analysis. PCA was used to evaluate sugar profile differences across dormancy release stages. PC1 explained 45.14% of the total variance, clearly separating stages S1-S3 from stage S4. PC2 explained 19.41% of the total variance and distinguished stage S1 from stages S2-S3. Notably, the greatest differences in sugar composition were observed between stage S4 and stages S1-S3 (Fig. 7A). A total of 25 annotated sugars and their catabolites were detected, among which only six metabolites showed differential accumulation across stages (Fig. 7B-C, Table S3). The accumulation patterns of two differentially accumulated sugars, fructose and xylose, were presented in bar charts. Xylose content increased significantly at S4, while fructose exhibited no consistent accumulation pattern during bud dormancy release (Fig. 7D). However, neither fructose nor xylose showed significant changes in pseudobulbs across the four stages (Fig. 7E).

To explore the molecular basis of sugar accumulation during dormancy release, we also examined the expression patterns of starch and sucrose metabolism and sugar transport-related genes in our transcriptome data. A total of 7 SWEET genes were identified (Fig. 6C), but none showed significant expression changes in floral buds during dormancy release (Fig. 6D). Similarly, the expression of metabolism genes and other sugar transporter family genes SUT (sucrose transporter) and MST (monosaccharide transporter) did not correlate with the accumulation of xylose at S4 (Figure S1). This lack of correlation suggests that the increase in xylose content may result from post-transcriptional regulation rather than from transcriptional activation of metabolic or sugar transport genes.

Identification of key regulatory pathways and genes involved in bud dormancy release

To identify potential key light- and temperature-signaling genes involved in dormancy release in P. bulbocodioides floral buds, we performed homology searches using BLASTP and Pfam with known CO, FT, PHY(A-E), and DAM (1–6) protein sequences as queries. A total of 8 CO, 5 PHYA, 2 PHYB, 3 PHYC, and 13 DAM-like MADS-box genes (sharing 28% to 49% sequence identity with DAMs) were identified in the transcriptome data (Table S1). Expression patterns of these regulatory genes, based on FPKM values, were visualized in heatmaps (Fig. 8A, Figure S2). Light-signaling genes such as CO (transcript_13624) and PHYA (transcript_2084 and transcript_4309) showed low expression levels in floral buds at stage S4. The expression profiles of these genes were further validated using qRT-PCR. In floral buds, the qRT‐PCR results were consistent with the transcriptome data. In pseudobulbs, CO (transcript_13624) expression decreased from stage S1 to S4, while PHYA (transcript_2084 and transcript_4309) expression was higher at stages S2 and S3 (Fig. 8B-C). Since temperature-signaling genes did not show significant expression changes in the transcriptome data during dormancy release stages, no further qRT‐PCR validation was performed.

Discussion

ABA and GA play crucial roles in the bud dormancy and bud break of P. bulbocodioides

Bud dormancy in plants is a complex physiological process regulated by a combination of internal signals and external environmental factors [6, 10]. Through quantitative analysis of endogenous hormones during the floral bud dormancy release in Pleione bulbocodioides, this study revealed that abscisic acid (ABA) and gibberellins (GA) play key antagonistic roles in this process. Our results indicated that ABA content in floral buds remained at a relatively high level during both the dormancy and the transition from dormancy to release (S1-S3). It is well established that ABA acts as a critical signaling molecule in stress responses, and effectively suppresses cell division and elongation. Furthermore, ABA content varies during the establishment, maintenance and release of bud dormancy. For example, ABA levels increased at the initial stage of bud dormancy and then gradually decreased during dormancy break in grape (Vitis vinifera) [44]. In tree peony (Paeonia suffruticosa), the ABA accumulation reduced during the bud endo-dormancy release [19]. In this study, when dormancy was released and new roots began to elongate (from 0.5 cm to 1 cm), a significant decreasing trend in ABA content was observed in P. bulbocodioides (Figs. 1 and 3). This change pattern strongly suggested that low ABA content was a necessary prerequisite for the floral bud dormancy release of P. bulbocodioides. In contrast, the content of GA20 showed an increasing trend during the floral bud dormancy release (Fig. 3D). GAs is widely recognized as growth-promoting hormones that stimulate floral development and dormancy break [45]. Our data indicated that the rise in GA20 level coincided with root elongation, implying that GA20 may directly activate meristematic tissues and promote cell proliferation in P. bulbocodioides.

The dynamic balance between these two hormones constitutes a central regulatory mechanism of dormancy: high levels of ABA effectively maintain dormancy and the plant’s ability to withstand unfavorable conditions; as the favorable season approaches, internal signaling cues promote GA biosynthesis and ABA catabolism. The increase in GA levels ultimately drives bud burst and growth. Importantly, the change in GA/ABA ratio may play a more pivotal role in regulating bud dormancy-release than changes in either hormone alone. An increase in GA20/ABA likely act as an internal switch triggering P. bulbocodioides floral bud dormancy release, which is consistent with those in tea plant (Camellia sinensis) [38], sweet cherry [46], and tree peony [19]. Our findings support the classical model that bud dormancy is co-regulated by the antagonistic interaction between ABA and GA. In addition, previous study has shown that IAA level and auxin-signaling pathways positively involved in dormancy transition. For instance, IAA concentrations in tea remain low throughout the dormancy period and peaked as dormancy release [47]. Our result showed an increase in IAA content from stage S3 to S4, indicating that IAA may also facilitate floral bud dormancy release and subsequent bud elongation in P. bulbocodioides. In contrast, the CK content decreased steadily from S1 to S4, implying a potential negative role of CK in regulating floral bud dormancy release in this species.

We prioritized hub genes involved in the biosynthesis and signaling pathways of these hormones to elucidate the molecular mechanisms governing the floral bud dormancy release in P. bulbocodioides. Based on their distinct expression patterns, ABA2-2 (transcript_30713) and GA20ox (transcript_29841) emerged as candidate hub genes for ABA and GA biosynthesis, respectively (Fig. 4). This suggests that the molecular mechanisms driving dormancy release vary among different species [11–13, 27, 38, 43, 48, 49]. However, P. bulbocodioides lacks established transgenic systems, we are unable to provide direct evidence for this hypothesis. Interestingly, the expression of most biosynthesis-related genes remained stable in floral buds during dormancy release but changed significantly in pseudobulbs (Figs. 4 and 5). According to gene expression patterns, we speculate that ABA and GAs are largely synthesized and stored in pseudobulbs prior to the onset of unfavorable environmental conditions. Upon the return of favorable growth conditions, these hormones and their signals may be transported from the pseudobulbs to the floral bud to regulate bud dormancy transition.

Pseudobulbs potentially serve as a reservoir to supply phytohormones to buds across bud dormancy-release stages

Pseudobulbs, as key storage organs for water, nutrients, and metabolites, play an important role in the survival and stress tolerance of orchids [50–52]. Our results demonstrated that pseudobulbs accumulate high levels of ABA during the dormancy maintenance phase. Under unfavorable conditions, it stores and sustains elevated ABA concentrations, thereby suppressing bud burst and promoting dormancy. Meanwhile, GA is also stored in the pseudobulbs, though its bioactivity or transport to bud may be suppressed (Figs. 3 and 5). These results suggested that pseudobulbs are not only serve as water [50, 53, 54] or nutrient storage [52, 55], but also serve as an important hormone (ABA, GA) storage center during bud dormancy regulation in P. bulbocodioides.

As environmental conditions turn favorable (e.g., long days length, rising temperatures), genes associated with hormone metabolism and transport are activated in the pseudobulb, thereby triggering a reconfiguration of hormonal dynamics. The specific expression of several transporter genes, including members of the ABCG and NPF families, were observed in the pseudobulbs. These genes showed significant changes during the release of floral bud dormancy in P. bulbocodioides, indicating that they may play distinct roles in hormone transport. For instance, ABCG28-2 (transcript_2356) and NPF3.1-5 (transcript_15973) at stage S1 may initiate the transport of ABA from pseudobulbs to floral buds. In the later stages, altered expression of genes, including ABCG3-3 (transcript_5984), ABCG11-1 (transcript_10592), and ABCG42-4 (transcript_307) contributes to the reduced ABA export. Meanwhile, NPF3.1-1 (transcript_12358) may be involved in transporting GA from pseudobulbs to floral bud at the S3 and S4 stages, resulting a rapid increase of GA20 content in floral bud at the S4 stage (Fig. 6). According to these results, we speculate that pseudobulb functions not merely as a passive storage site, but more potentially as an active regulatory hub for hormone redistribution and signal transduction. The strategy of utilizing pseudobulbs as hormone storage organs likely confers substantial ecological adaptive value: it enables the plant to accumulate and sequester inhibitory hormones under adverse conditions, respond rapidly to environmental fluctuations, and initiate bud burst through targeted hormone allocation when conditions turn favorable, ultimately enhancing survival and reproductive fitness.

In addition to hormones, soluble sugars also play important roles in bud dormancy regulation. Our sugar-targeted metabolomics revealed that xylose content in floral buds increased significantly at S4 (Fig. 7D). Xylose is a major component of hemicellulose, and its accumulation may reflect active cell wall remodeling during bud elongation [30]. In contrast, fructose showed no consistent accumulation pattern during dormancy release, suggesting that it may not serve as a primary regulatory sugar in this process. Interestingly, neither xylose nor fructose changed significantly in pseudobulbs across the four stages (Fig. 7E), implying that sugar metabolism may be more locally regulated within the buds rather than mobilized from storage organs. However, further investigation is needed to clarify the functional roles of specific sugars in bud dormancy release in P. bulbocodioides.

Light-regulated pathway might be a key regulatory pathway involved in P. bulbocodioides floral bud dormancy release

From an ecological adaptation perspective, light and temperature are important environmental signals for bud dormancy and bud break in perennials [36, 56]. However, in this study, the expression of all identified DAM-like MADS-box genes showed no significant differences among four bud dormancy-release stages (Figure S2). Whereas, we observed that the expression of CO (transcript_13624), a key regulator of short days signaling, steadily decreased during the dormancy release process in both buds and pseudobulbs, indicating that day length may play important roles in floral bud dormancy release of P. bulbocodioides (Fig. 8). Among the light-response pathways, the phytochrome-regulated pathway is the most prominent. We observed that the expression of PHYA decreased during the process of bud dormancy release, which is consistent with the observations in Populus [26]. Moreover, it has been reported that multilayered interactions exist between light signaling and hormonal regulatory networks [23, 31, 57]. Based on the observed patterns of gene expression and metabolite accumulation, we further speculate that light signals, mediated by CO and PHYA, may regulate the spatial distribution of ABA and GA between pseudobulbs and buds, thereby promoting floral bud dormancy release in P. bulbocodioides (Fig. 9). The pseudobulb may not only serve in hormone storage, but also directly perceive environmental signals and initiate downstream responses.

Conclusions

In this study, we observed a substantial release of bud dormancy within just one week. Concurrently with dormancy release, ABA content decreased markedly, while both GA content and the GA/ABA ratio increased significantly in both floral buds and pseudobulbs. More importantly, the contents of ABA and GA in the pseudobulbs are significantly higher than that in the floral buds, and the changes are also more pronounced in pseudobulb. Based on the observed patterns of gene expression and metabolite accumulation, we propose that pseudobulbs may act as critical organs for signal perception and hormone supplying across P. bulbocodioides bud dormancy-release stages. These findings offer a theoretical foundation for understanding the floral bud dormancy-release transition in Pleione species, thereby providing a way (exogenous hormone treatment) for manipulating flowering time in its industrial cultivation.
