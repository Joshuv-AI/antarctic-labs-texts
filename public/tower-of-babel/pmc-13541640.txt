Hantavirus outbreak on a cruise ship: Public health challenges
Authors: Bin Liu, Chengfang Yang, Jingyu Zhu, Xiaowei Guo, Jie Luan, Jianbo Ba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A rare hantavirus outbreak on MV Hondius in May 2026 caused 8 cases and 3 deaths.•Cruise ship confined spaces and HVAC systems facilitated aerosol transmission.•Jurisdictional disputes delayed critical medical evacuations.•Lack of isolation and diagnostics exposed cruise ship medical vulnerabilities.•Pathogen research and robust maritime emergency protocols are needed.

Introduction

In early May 2026, a confirmed hantavirus outbreak aboard a cruise ship triggered heightened global public health scrutiny. A severe cluster of respiratory illnesses, attributed to hantavirus, erupted on the Motor Vessel (MV) Hondius, a Dutch polar expedition cruise ship navigating the Atlantic [1]. This rare cross-species transmission event serves as a stark warning that the intersection of the booming eco-tourism sector and modern maritime travel presents unprecedented challenges to the global public health system. This paper provides an in-depth analysis of hantavirus characteristics and transmission dynamics, the inherent infectious disease risks within the cruise ship environment, the associated public health challenges, and recommendations for future improvements. The aim is to offer scientific insights to enhance global cruise public health management.

Outbreak

The MV Hondius departed from Ushuaia, Argentina, on April 1, 2026, navigating a South Atlantic itinerary with scheduled stops in Antarctica, South Georgia, Nightingale Island, Tristan da Cunha, St. Helena, and Ascension Island before its final arrival in Cabo Verde. The ship carried 57 crew members, 13 guides, and a single physician, with onboard medical facilities limited to providing only basic care [2].

The outbreak was retrospectively traced to April 6, when a 70-year-old Dutch male passenger presented with fever, headache, and mild diarrhea. His condition deteriorated rapidly into severe respiratory distress five days later, resulting in his death onboard on April 11. Subsequently, his wife disembarked at St. Helena and flew to Johannesburg, South Africa. She deteriorated enroute and succumbed to the illness shortly after arrival. On April 27, a British passenger developed acute dyspnea and was medically evacuated to Johannesburg for emergency treatment. On May 2, a German passenger also died onboard. As of May 27, a total of 13 epidemiologically linked cases have been identified (11 confirmed and 2 probable cases), resulting in 3 fatalities. One patient remains under intensive care in South Africa, while the remaining cases present with relatively mild symptoms (Fig. 1) [3]. This outbreak poses profound challenges to maritime public health risk management—specifically, the effective prevention and containment of rare pathogens within the confined, high-density, and transnational environment of a cruise ship. Addressing this requires not only technical interventions, such as ventilation system design, isolation conditions, and testing capabilities, but also navigating complex legal jurisdictions by delineating Flag State versus Port State responsibilities, and enhancing international coordination for medical evacuations.

Similar to the previously reported outbreak on the Diamond Princess cruise ship, this outbreak occurred in a confined environment with high population density, recirculating central air conditioning, constrained medical resources, and complex logistical challenges in coordinating international evacuations. However, the transmission biology of the pathogen differs substantially. Unlike previous outbreaks caused by common pathogens such as influenza or norovirus, hantavirus represents a rare and highly lethal threat. Its initial emergence in a cruise setting imposes significantly more stringent demands on public health emergency response protocols.

Characteristics and transmission of hantavirus

Hantavirus is an enveloped, single-stranded negative-sense RNA virus classified within the genus Orthohantavirus, family Hantaviridae. These zoonotic pathogens are primarily maintained in rodent reservoirs. Hantaviruses are categorized into two distinct groups based on their pathogenicity. The first encompasses viruses such as the Hantaan virus, which are prevalent in Eurasia and cause Hemorrhagic Fever with Renal Syndrome. The second is prevalent in America and includes ANDV, which triggers Hantavirus Pulmonary Syndrome (HPS). HPS is a severe clinical manifestation marked by acute respiratory failure and significant pulmonary involvement, carrying a notably high mortality rate [5].

The natural reservoirs for hantavirus are predominantly rodents, including multiple species of rattus, mus, and microtus. The primary route of human infection is the inhalation of aerosols contaminated with rodent excreta (urine, feces, or saliva). Other transmission routes include the ingestion of contaminated food or water, direct contact with infected animals. While human-to-human transmission is generally not associated with hantavirus infections, the Andes virus is a notable exception with documented cases of person-to-person transmission [6], [7]. The localized and confined environment, combined with prolonged cohabitation, facilitated limited ANDV transmission through close contact or the inhalation of aerosols generated from infected individuals’ excretions.

Epidemiological investigations indicated that the index case (the Dutch male) and his wife had engaged in trekking in the Patagonia region prior to embarkation. They stayed in wooden cabins located in habitats potentially of the long-tailed pygmy rice rat (oligoryzomys longicaudatus), the natural reservoir of the ANDV endemic to southern South America (along the Argentina-Chile border) [3]. This is currently one of the best-substantiated hypotheses that the patients were exposed to virus-laden aerosols in a terrestrial environment, boarded the vessel during the incubation period, and subsequently developed symptoms within the confined cruise setting. Current evidence suggests subsequent human-to-human transmission onboard the ship. The analysis of the sequences showed no more than one single nucleotide polymorphisms difference per individual case, strongly suggesting that the outbreak likely arose from a single zoonotic spillover event, or from a very small number of closely related spillover events [8]. However, the hypothesis that infected wild rodents infiltrated the ship during port resupply, with their excreta producing infectious aerosols, cannot be ruled out due to the difficulty of detecting rodent traces on the vessel. Nevertheless, conclusively determining the source of this outbreak requires cruise fumigation results, robust pathogenic and epidemiological evidence.

Epidemic risks associated with the cruise ship environment

Enclosed spaces and aerosol-mediated transmission

The MV Hondius shares structural characteristics with conventional cruise ships, notably its high density of windowless, airtight cabins reliant on central heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems. Hantavirus virions, with a diameter of approximately 80 to 120 nm, fall within the respirable aerosol range and can remain viable for nearly 2 weeks at room temperature [9], [10]. The microclimate of cruise cabins and the HVAC system facilitate the persistence and rapid dissemination of viral aerosols throughout the vessel [11].

Rodent infestation and environmental persistence

As mobile maritime communities, cruise ships possess structural compartments, such as warehouses, engine rooms, and cargo holds, which serve as potential reservoirs for rodents. Consequently, rodent activity during voyages poses a significant risk, as aerosols generated from dried excreta can be distributed ship-wide via the ventilation system. Furthermore, although hantavirus is susceptible to ultraviolet (UV) radiation [12], the windowless architecture and enclosed nature of cabins severely limit natural light exposure. This lack of ultraviolet irradiation impedes viral inactivation, thereby extending the environmental persistence of the virus.

Challenges in global public health response

Legal jurisdiction and allocation of responsibilities

Pursuant to the International Health Regulations (IHR 2005) [13] and the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) [14], legal jurisdiction during public health emergencies involves complex, overlapping roles. While Article 24 of the IHR requires flag states to ensure vessel sanitary standards, they often lack extraterritorial authority to enforce mandatory directives during outbreaks. Conversely, Article 27 grants port states the right to deny entry if they lack adequate health capacities, such as Cabo Verde’s refusal to dock the MV Hondius due to insufficient isolation and testing facilities. However, refusing port states remain obligated to authorize passage to the nearest suitable alternative port. This establishes a liability grey area where flag states must typically fund medical repatriations but cannot force port entry, and port states can legally deny docking yet must still arrange alternative accommodations.

Onboard public health resources and emergency response capabilities

The MV Hondius suffered from severe deficiencies in medical resource allocation and emergency outbreak management. Although staffed with one physician and 57 crew members in compliance with the Cruise Lines International Association (CLIA) and flag state regulations, this minimal medical team was completely overwhelmed by three fatalities and multiple critically ill patients, rendering essential epidemiological investigations impossible. The ship's diagnostic limitations, specifically the lack of rapid hantavirus testing and environmental screening, led to delayed isolation as early symptoms mimicked common respiratory illnesses. Furthermore, while the vessel had adequate facilities for enteric diseases, it lacked respiratory isolation infrastructure due to central air conditioning systems and non-airtight cabins that precluded effective containment of respiratory infections. Compounding these issues, onboard facilities could not support advanced critical care for conditions like acute respiratory failure, ultimately necessitating emergency medical evacuations.

Health surveillance and prevention of importation risks

Operating as a mobile maritime community, the cruise ship exhibited profound vulnerabilities in its health surveillance and risk mitigation frameworks. Internal monitoring was compromised by the operator's failure to conduct port-specific epidemiological assessments. Routine screening could not distinguish early hantavirus symptoms from influenza, highlighting the need for targeted travel-history questionnaires. Additionally, inadequate vector control during multi-port calls created potential ingress pathways for rodent reservoirs via provisions or luggage. These systemic deficits were compounded by a critical delay in emergency escalation. Despite the first fatality on April 11, formal notification to the World Health Organization (WHO) was deferred until May 2 [3]. This temporal lag inadvertently facilitated sustained viral transmission within the vessel's confined environment.

Scientific implications and recommendations

Critical research gaps

The MV Hondius outbreak exposes critical gaps in maritime public health preparedness, underscoring three urgent research priorities. First, quantitative models are needed to characterize pathogen transmission dynamics in confined shipboard ecosystems, particularly aerosol dispersion via central HVAC systems. Second, although ANDV human-to-human transmission is documented [6], its efficiency in maritime versus terrestrial settings remains unclear, necessitating rigorous elucidation of key epidemiological parameters such as transmission thresholds and critical contact patterns. Finally, while hantavirus vaccines exist, ANDV-specific development remains nascent, and tailored deployment strategies for mass-gathering environments like cruise ships are largely absent.

Strengthening cruise ship epidemic response capabilities

A comprehensive maritime health surveillance system requires an end-to-end framework integrating source control, process management, and emergency response. Source control must be fortified through mandatory pre-boarding travel declarations, strict symptom screening supported by dynamic early-warning mechanisms for endemic regions, rigorous vector eradication to eliminate reservoir habitats, and upgraded ventilation systems to maximize air exchange in communal areas. Concurrently, enhancing operational readiness demands prioritized professional training and logistical support for crew and medical staff, reinforced by regular drills on personal protective equipment and environmental decontamination protocols. Furthermore, seamless coordination with onshore facilities at ports of call is essential to establish efficient medical evacuation pathways, while multi-scenario simulation exercises remain critical to validating early detection, warning, and rapid containment capabilities.

Public education and risk mitigation

To mitigate risks from rare pathogens like hantavirus, targeted health advisories for polar and South American itineraries must explicitly inform passengers to heighten risk perception. This should be reinforced through mandatory pre-boarding briefings that educate travelers on prodromal symptoms and reporting protocols, fostering prompt symptom disclosure. Additionally, personal protection guidelines should recommend N95 respirators when entering enclosed, potentially rodent-infested environments. In such areas, suspicious surfaces should be sanitized with a 1:10 household bleach dilution, while dry sweeping or vacuuming must be strictly prohibited to prevent the aerosolization of infectious particles.

Conclusions

The outbreak aboard the MV Hondius stands as a rare and highly lethal public health emergency, serving as a stark wake-up call for global public health management. It underscores the urgent imperative to bolster fundamental scientific research and fortify the emergency response capabilities within the maritime sector. Future scientific inquiry must prioritize novel and rare pathogens, with a specific focus on elucidating the evolutionary dynamics, viral origins, and transmission mechanisms of infectious diseases (Fig. 2). Concurrently, it is essential to strengthen transnational public health coordination, upgrade onboard medical infrastructure, and institutionalize routine emergency drills to comprehensively elevate operational readiness for cruise ship outbreaks.
