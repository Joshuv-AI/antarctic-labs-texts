Thai Transplant Care Practice Guideline on Immunosuppressive Therapy for Adult Kidney Transplantation
Authors: Suwasin Udomkarnjananun, Napun Sutharattanapong, Nuttasith Larpparisuth, Korntip Phonphok, Tanun Ngamvichchukorn, Peenida Skulratanasak, Pitchamon Inkong, Suwikran Wongpraphairot, Opas Traitanon, Piyavadee Homkrailas, Kajohnsak Noppakun, Natavudh Townamchai, Thai Transplantation Society
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Thai Transplantation Society developed these clinical practice guidelines to establish a comprehensive standard for immunosuppressive therapy in adult kidney transplantation. While rooted in evidence‐based medicine, the objective of this document extends beyond local application, serving as a practical model for low‐to‐middle‐income countries, particularly within the Asia‐Pacific and Southeast Asian regions, where specific genetic profiles and resource limitations require tailored management strategies. This publication addresses a critical need in the field, as there has been no updated comprehensive international consensus on immunosuppression since the KDIGO Clinical Practice Guideline in 2009, over 17 years ago. These guidelines bridge this gap by synthesizing modern clinical data with practical applicability. The scope of the guidelines is extensive, covering all aspects of immunosuppression usage including induction, maintenance, therapeutic drug monitoring, and the treatment of rejection. Uniquely, this document addresses specific challenges relevant to resource‐limited settings, offering protocols for cost reduction through the use of generic drugs and the co‐administration of cytochrome P450 3A4 and 3A5 inhibitors to minimize dosage requirements. Furthermore, it provides distinct guidance for immunosuppression in special clinical settings, including management during periods of fasting, pregnancy, and malignancy. This guideline ultimately benefits clinicians by offering a pragmatic, updated reference that balances therapeutic efficacy with economic sustainability.

Chapter 1: Introduction to Immunosuppressive Drugs

Solid organ transplantation offers improved survival and quality of life for patients with irreversible end‐stage organ failure. Despite these benefits, long‐term graft survival necessitates lifelong immunosuppression. Although modern immunosuppressive regimens have improved graft outcomes, they carry the risk of toxicity, specifically infection and malignancy, stemming from over‐immunosuppression. Therefore, these clinical practice guidelines were developed to address management strategies specific to the Thai population. Clinical decision‐making must be individualized, accounting for patient comorbidities and specific reimbursement coverage.

Immunosuppressive therapy is categorized into two phases: induction and maintenance. Induction therapy involves the administration of high‐intensity immunosuppression immediately post‐transplant to prevent acute rejection. Conversely, maintenance therapy employs reduced dosing to prevent long‐term allograft rejection while minimizing toxicity. The majority of these agents exert their effects through lymphocyte inhibition. Side Effects of Immunosuppressants

The adverse event profiles of immunosuppressants vary by drug class and individual agent. Prompt recognition and management of these toxicities are essential for ensuring therapeutic success and preserving patient quality of life. Modifications to the regimen, such as dose reduction or agent substitution, must always be weighed against the risk of compromising immunosuppressive efficacy. Drug Interactions Affecting Immunosuppressant Levels

Maintenance of therapeutic immunosuppressant concentrations is critical for graft survival. Subtherapeutic exposure increases the risk of allograft rejection, while supratherapeutic levels may lead to complications such as nephrotoxicity or opportunistic infections. Since most immunosuppressants undergo hepatic metabolism, they are susceptible to interactions with concomitantly administered drugs. In some clinical scenarios, these interactions are leveraged therapeutically; for instance, azole antifungals, diltiazem, or verapamil may be coadministered with calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) or mTOR inhibitors to ‘boost’ levels, thereby allowing for dose reduction and cost containment. However, the alteration or discontinuation of these interacting agents poses a significant risk of subtherapeutic exposure and subsequent rejection. Thus, a comprehensive understanding of these pharmacologic interactions is essential for the management of transplant recipients.

Both CNIs and mTOR inhibitors share a primary metabolic pathway via the hepatic cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A) isoenzyme system. Consequently, co‐administration of these classes may result in competitive metabolic inhibition and elevated serum concentrations of both agents. Furthermore, any external agent that modulates CYP3A activity is likely to impact the pharmacokinetics of both CNIs and mTOR inhibitors similarly. Although utilized less frequently in modern regimens, azathioprine carries a critical interaction with allopurinol (a xanthine oxidase inhibitor). Co‐administration blocks azathioprine metabolism, precipitating severe myelotoxicity, including profound leukopenia and thrombocytopenia. Consequently, this combination should be avoided. If co‐administration is clinically mandatory, the azathioprine dosage must be reduced to 25%–30% of the standard dose to mitigate toxicity.

Administration Protocols

Methylprednisolone is another immunosuppressant used in induction therapy. It is administered intravenously at a dose of 250 to 1000 mg/day for 3 to 5 days, starting on the day of kidney transplantation surgery. Subsequently, the dosage is tapered to oral prednisolone.

Basiliximab is administered intravenously at a dose of 20 mg for two doses. The first dose is given prior to the kidney transplantation surgery, and the second dose is given on Day 4 post‐transplantation.

Rabbit anti‐thymocyte immunoglobulin (r‐ATG) is administered via a large vein or central venous line at a dose of 1 to 1.5 mg/kg/day for 3 to 9 days post‐transplantation (the recommended total cumulative dose is 3 to 13.5 mg/kg). Patients should receive premedication with corticosteroids, paracetamol, and antihistamines approximately 30 min to 1 h prior to r‐ATG administration to reduce infusion‐related reactions. Patients should receive CMV prophylaxis after receiving r‐ATG. However, retrospective studies indicate that using low‐dose r‐ATG (total dose of 2 mg/kg) is effective in preventing organ rejection without differing significantly from basiliximab, and does not increase the risk of overall infections, including CMV infection [10].

Comparative studies between basiliximab and r‐ATG found that r‐ATG is associated with a lower incidence of graft rejection and may improve graft survival rates, particularly in recipients of deceased donor kidneys [11]. However, among lymphocyte‐depleting agents, there is an increased risk of CMV and BK virus infections, as well as leukopenia and thrombocytopenia [7, 12].

In cases where the patient is at very high risk for organ rejection, desensitization should be performed prior to kidney transplantation. Pre‐transplant desensitization processes may include various methods such as plasmapheresis, double filtration plasmapheresis, immunoadsorption, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) administration, and rituximab administration. The number of plasmapheresis sessions depends on the quantity of alloantibody or ABO antibody requiring removal. Protocols for IVIG and rituximab administration may vary by institution [13, 14, 15]. Patients should receive premedication with corticosteroids, paracetamol, and antihistamines prior to high‐dose IVIG and prior to rituximab administration.

Rationale and Evidence

The current standard regimen consists of a CNI, an antimetabolite, and corticosteroids, which have distinct mechanisms of action for preventing organ rejection. Tacrolimus is the primary CNI, and MPA is the primary antimetabolite currently used. The use of this standard regimen is based on a large randomized controlled trial, the ELITE‐Symphony study [16], which included 1645 transplant recipients from 15 countries. Patients were randomized to receive one of four regimens: standard‐dose cyclosporine (target trough level [C0] 100–200 ng/mL), low‐dose cyclosporine (C0 50–100 ng/mL), low‐dose tacrolimus (C0 3–7 ng/mL), and low‐dose sirolimus (C0 4–8 ng/mL). The definition of “low‐dose” in this study referred to doses lower than the standard doses commonly used at that time; for instance, the tacrolimus C0 target of 3–7 ng/mL was considered low compared to the standard era target of 10–15 ng/mL. All patient groups in this study received MMF 2 g/day combined with prednisolone and daclizumab as induction therapy, except for the high‐dose cyclosporine group which did not receive any induction therapy. The study results showed that the group receiving tacrolimus combined with MMF and corticosteroids had better kidney allograft function, a lower incidence of acute rejection, higher graft survival rates, and lower treatment failure rates compared to groups receiving other immunosuppressive regimens at 1 to 3 years of follow‐up [16, 17].

An alternative standard regimen is the use of an mTOR inhibitor (everolimus or sirolimus) combined with tacrolimus and corticosteroids, based on the TRANSFORM study (Transplant Efficacy and Safety Outcomes with an Everolimus‐based regimen). This was the largest RCT in kidney transplant recipients to date, including 2226 patients from 186 hospitals [18]. Patients were randomized to receive either a standard‐dose CNI combined with MPA (tacrolimus level C0 5–8 ng/mL or cyclosporine level C0 200–300 ng/mL) or a reduced‐dose CNI regimen (de novo CNI minimization) combined with everolimus (tacrolimus level C0 2–4 ng/mL or cyclosporine level C0 25–50 ng/mL combined with everolimus level C0 3–8 ng/mL). Both groups received basiliximab induction therapy. The results showed that the group using everolimus combined with reduced‐dose CNI had an incidence of biopsy‐proven acute rejection and proportion of patients with eGFR < 50 mL/min/1.73 m2 at 12 months post‐transplant that was non‐inferior to the group receiving standard‐dose CNI. Simultaneously, the benefit of using an mTOR inhibitor with reduced CNI was a lower incidence of CMV and BK virus infections. However, the group using mTOR inhibitor combined with reduced CNI had a higher rate of drug discontinuation due to side effects. These results were consistent at both 12‐ and 24‐months post‐transplant [18, 19]. Nevertheless, long‐term results still require further study, particularly regarding DSA monitoring (which was not performed in all patients in this study). Furthermore, patients classified as high risk for rejection (according to the definitions of participating centers, such as having PRA higher than the specified threshold) were not included in this study; therefore, the use of mTOR inhibitors with reduced‐dose CNI in patients at high risk for rejection still requires further study.

Systematic review and meta‐analysis data found that tacrolimus reduced the rate of graft loss between 6 months and 3 years post‐transplant by 44% compared to cyclosporine. Additionally, tacrolimus reduced the rate of acute rejection at 1 year post‐transplant by 51% compared to cyclosporine [20]. However, the use of tacrolimus increased the incidence of post‐transplant diabetes mellitus (PTDM), hair loss, tremors, and gastrointestinal side effects more than cyclosporine [20, 21]. Data from the Thai Transplant Registry confirmed that tacrolimus use is associated with reduced graft loss and patient mortality when compared with cyclosporine [22].

Consideration for switching the CNI drug from tacrolimus to another agent should have clear indications, such as cases where the patient experiences severe side effects from tacrolimus even if drug levels are within the standard range, such as thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES), which are potentially life‐threatening conditions if not resolved [23, 24]. In such cases, it is recommended to switch tacrolimus to cyclosporine or a drug in the mTOR inhibitor group. However, there is insufficient evidence to recommend whether cyclosporine or an mTOR inhibitor should be the first choice in this situation. The decision should consider the risk of rejection and current renal function. It should be noted that cyclosporine and mTOR inhibitors can also cause TMA and PRES [24, 25]. In patients who have received cyclosporine for a long time and are stable, with normal renal function and no proteinuria, it is not necessary to switch from cyclosporine to tacrolimus. Surveillance allograft biopsy and/or DSA monitoring may be useful in helping decide to adjust the immunosuppressive regimen in this group of patients.

For some patients who cannot tolerate the side effects of tacrolimus, or are anticipated to be intolerant (e.g., hair loss or uncontrolled blood sugar), consideration may be given to switching from tacrolimus to cyclosporine. Patients should be informed about the lower efficacy of cyclosporine in preventing allograft rejection. Therefore, patients selecting cyclosporine combined with MPA and corticosteroids should be those at low or moderate risk of rejection. Additionally, the use of cyclosporine may increase the risk of CNI nephrotoxicity more than tacrolimus [26, 27, 28, 29, 30] and may result in poorer long‐term renal function [31]. One consideration when using cyclosporine with MPA is drug–drug interaction; cyclosporine can inhibit the enterohepatic recirculation of MPA, resulting in lower blood MPA levels in patients receiving cyclosporine compared to those receiving tacrolimus, which may affect efficacy in preventing acute rejection or may necessitate an increased MPA dose to maintain sufficient immunosuppression [32, 33].

Rationale and Evidence

Calcineurin Inhibitors (CNIs) are the main immunosuppressive medications for kidney transplant recipients. Because of their narrow therapeutic index properties, they require therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) [40, 41]. Especially during the immediate postoperative period, TDM should be performed every 2–3 days [34]. Whole blood samples should be used for measuring CNI levels because CNI is primarily distributed in red blood cells, and Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) is the preferred anticoagulant. The area under the concentration‐time curve is the most precise TDM. However, it requires multiple blood samples at different time intervals, which is not practical. The trough (C0) concentration of tacrolimus (TAC), measured as a single time point, is more practical and exhibits a good correlation with AUC(0–12), providing a coefficient of determination (r 2) of 0.82–0.94 [35, 42, 43]. Furthermore, most clinical studies use the C0 level as a predictor of transplant outcomes, both efficacy and adverse events. Thus, the C0 level is recommended to monitor TAC exposure [40, 41]. Nevertheless, if the C0 is suspicious not a good representative of the drug exposure (e.g., allograft rejection or CNI toxicity happened while C0 level is within the target range), AUC(0–12) measurement may be considered, with a target of 100–150 ng·hr/mL [41, 42]. AUC can be measured by using the trapezoidal rule or limited sampling strategies (LSS), as shown in Table 6.

Rationale and Evidence

Due to its complicated pharmacokinetics, the manufacturer and general practice recommend fixed‐dosing administration of mycophenolic acid (MPA). Non‐linear absorption kinetics, enterohepatic circulation, and inter‐ and intrapatient variability affect MPA exposure. Combination with tacrolimus and cyclosporine yields different results; specifically, tacrolimus increases the AUC of MPA, while cyclosporine decreases it. There is insufficient evidence to support the notion that MPA monitoring improves transplant outcomes [50, 51]. Furthermore, the trough (C0) concentration of MPA provides a poor to fair correlation with AUC (r 2 = 0.16–0.63). Thus, it is not recommended to use C0 for monitoring [52]. Monitoring of MPA may be considered in patients with a high immunologic risk, whenever certain conditions or co‐administered drugs that may alter drug concentration are present, or whenever CNI is withdrawn [51, 53]. If considered, the full dose‐interval AUC(0–12) should be used, estimated by limited sampling strategies, together with maximum a posteriori Bayesian estimation (MAP‐BE) or multiple linear regression. At least 2 time point measures for mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), and at least 3–4 time point measures for enteric‐coated mycophenolate sodium (EC‐MPS) are required. The target AUC(0–12) of 30–60 mg·hr/L is recommended [52]. Regression equations estimating AUC for MPA are shown in Table 8. Rationale and Evidence

Because mTOR inhibitor has a narrow therapeutic index, TDM is required. Like CNI, it is mainly distributed in the red blood cells; EDTA blood should be used for drug concentration measurement. Rationale and Evidence

Currently, there are no antiviral drugs with proven efficacy to inhibit BK virus replication. Treatment for BK virus infection in transplant recipients therefore focuses on the optimization of immunosuppression, with the objective of allowing T‐lymphocytes specific to the BK virus to recover and control the infection. The primary strategy involves a stepwise reduction of maintenance immunosuppression, starting with halving the mycophenolic acid dosage, lowering CNI trough levels, and minimizing prednisolone. If the viral load remains persistently high despite these initial reductions, switching from mycophenolic acid to an mTOR inhibitor can be considered while continuing to reduce calcineurin inhibitor trough levels. For CMV infection treatment, the strategy consists of reducing immunosuppressants to an appropriate level to allow the immune system specific to the infection to recover, in conjunction with antiviral administration. Further details regarding immunosuppressant adjustment and antiviral administration can be studied in the guidelines for transplant recipients with infections.

However, in cases where patients develop cancer after kidney transplantation, they should receive standard cancer treatment combined with consideration of immunosuppressant reduction, particularly in viral‐associated cancers like PTLD and Kaposi's sarcoma, and surveillance for increased risk of organ rejection.

It has been found that mTOR inhibitors can inhibit the growth of T‐cells and B‐cells via the mTOR pathway, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and angiogenesis, which may result in the cure of Kaposi's sarcoma and reduce the chance of recurrence. However, side effects that may arise from this drug class should be monitored. Neither prednisolone nor MPA has been found to increase the risk of developing cancer post‐transplantation.

Patients with the following history: long‐term dialysis, family history of kidney cancer, acquired cystic kidney disease, or analgesic nephropathy should undergo screening for renal cell carcinoma by ultrasonography due to a high risk of developing cancer.

Rationale and Evidence

Generally, as patients age (from 65 years and above), immune system function tends to decline, resulting in a reduced risk of organ rejection. Conversely, the risk of infection and cancer increases, including the chance of adverse drug reactions. Therefore, consideration of reducing the potency of immunosuppressants in this patient group may be an appropriate option [64, 65] to help reduce side effects.

Rationale and Evidence

Corticosteroids act by inhibiting nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB), which is a key factor in the function of cytokines and T‐cells; thus, corticosteroids can help prevent organ rejection post‐transplant. However, the use of this drug class has several side effects, such as weight gain, osteoporosis, cataracts, growth retardation in children, hypertension, hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and increased risk of infection.

In practice, corticosteroid administration begins immediately after kidney transplantation, followed by gradual dose tapering or consideration of withdrawal [66, 67], depending on immunological risk and risk of side effects. Stopping corticosteroids after more than 1 week post‐transplant may increase the risk of future rejection. Therefore, if a patient has received corticosteroids for longer than 1 week post‐surgery, it is recommended to continue corticosteroids at a low dose (not exceeding 5 mg/day). In cases where the cause of kidney failure is glomerulonephritis, particularly IgA nephropathy, consideration should be given to continuous corticosteroid administration to prevent recurrent glomerular disease [68].

Rationale and Evidence

Transplant recipients can become pregnant when their kidney function is within normal criteria. Additionally, prior to pregnancy, there should be no significant proteinuria or organ rejection. MPA and mTOR inhibitors may cause fetal abnormalities during organogenesis. Therefore, stopping drugs that may affect the fetus should be considered prior to pregnancy. For drugs in the mTOR inhibitor group, they reduce the chance of having children in male patients because they result in reduced sperm quantity (oligospermia) and may also cause reduced testosterone levels. Rationale and Evidence

Chronic allograft dysfunction or chronic allograft injury, formerly called chronic allograft nephropathy, is one of the major causes of graft loss. Patients will experience a gradual decline in kidney function. Diagnosis requires a kidney biopsy, which often reveals pathology as IFTA. The majority of causes come from chronic antibody‐mediated rejection or CNI toxicity.

In cases where chronic allograft dysfunction is found to be caused by CNI toxicity, consideration may be given to using immunosuppressants in the mTOR inhibitor group, which act by inhibiting the growth of T‐cells and B‐cells. Side effects of this group include slow wound healing, lymphocele, oral ulcers, hyperlipidemia, interstitial pneumonitis, and proteinuria. The mechanism of proteinuria from mTOR inhibitors involves the reduction of protein reabsorption at the tubules and injury to podocyte cells. Therefore, use of this group should be avoided in patients with pre‐existing proteinuria. Results from the CONVERT study found that switching from CNI to sirolimus helped improve kidney function, specifically in patients with eGFR greater than 40 mL/min and a urine protein‐to‐creatinine ratio not exceeding 0.11 g/g (Table 11).

Rationale and Evidence

In cases where the patient must be NPO, CNI immunosuppressants require adjustment of form and dosage to maintain immune system control efficacy. It is recommended to switch from oral form to intravenous injection because oral drugs may have inconsistent absorption during NPO. The starting intravenous dose of tacrolimus is 1 in 4 of the oral doses, and cyclosporine is 1 in 3 of the oral doses. Monitoring blood drug concentration until steady level (Csteady) is important. Generally, this level is reached after 3 to 5 times the drug's half‐life, or approximately 48 h. However, blood can be drawn to check drug levels before this time to assess trends.

Setting target levels for Csteady can be calculated from target C0 levels or target AUC(0–12) using the formulas provided [74]. This is because the graph of drug level versus time will have a rectangular shape when administered via continuous infusion.

Tacrolimus intravenous injection form can be mixed with 5% dextrose in water (D5W) or normal saline solution (NSS) to a concentration of 0.004 to 0.020 mg/mL (equivalent to 4 to 20 mg in 1000 mL solution). The mixed solution is stable for 24 h. Administration via continuous infusion. Use of tubing made from polyvinylchloride should be avoided because polyvinylchloride can adsorb tacrolimus. Additionally, the injection contains castor oil, which may dissolve phthalates from polyvinylchloride and may cause anaphylaxis. Therefore, the patient should be monitored closely, especially during the first 30 min of administration.

Cyclosporine intravenous injection form can be mixed with D5W or NSS to a concentration of 0.5 to 2.5 mg/mL. The mixed solution is stable for 24 h. Administered via continuous infusion or, in some institutions, may be mixed and given every 12 h. Use of tubing made from polyvinylchloride should be avoided because it can adsorb cyclosporine. The injection also contains castor oil, carrying similar risks of phthalate dissolution and anaphylaxis, so close monitoring is required, especially in the first 30 min.

Corticosteroids should be used in intravenous form, with dosage adjusted appropriately to the patient's condition, such as stress dose. Following these guidelines will help the patient receive immunosuppression appropriately and safely during NPO. When the patient can eat and drink, they can return to the original form of immunosuppressants.

Rationale and Evidence

Allograft rejection, the major complication and cause of allograft dysfunction, is classified into two histopathological groups: T‐cell mediated rejection (TCMR) and antibody‐mediated rejection (ABMR). Most allograft rejection is treated by intensification of immunosuppression. However, allograft dysfunction can be caused by other causes. Infection is one of those that requires a reduction in immunosuppression. Thus, the kidney transplant recipients with allograft dysfunction should be evaluated for causes and may require a kidney allograft biopsy. A biopsy performed due to allograft dysfunction is called an indicated biopsy, whereas some centers perform an allograft biopsy as scheduled, referred to as a surveillance biopsy. In cases where a histological finding reveals allograft rejection without an increase in serum creatinine or proteinuria, it is referred to as subclinical rejection.

Diagnosis and classification of allograft rejection are based on the Banff classification, which was first published in 1991 and periodically updated [75, 76]. The diagnosis of ABMR and TCMR can be established simultaneously.

Rationale and Evidence

All healthcare benefit schemes in Thailand, Civil Servant Medical Benefit Scheme (CSMBS), Social Security Scheme (SSS), Universal Coverage Scheme (UCS), and others, cover kidney transplantation. The reimbursement is divided into two parts. First is for the kidney transplant operation, depending on immunologic risk, the use of induction therapy, and postoperative complications. Second is for the maintenance of immunosuppressive medication, which is a monthly flat rate and decreases over time.

The cost of immunosuppressive medication may exceed coverage, potentially hindering the kidney transplant program. Thus, reducing the cost of immunosuppressive drugs, weighing patient safety, and transplant outcome may be considered to maintain the growth of the kidney transplant program. The application of other drugs that interact with immunosuppressive drugs to increase their concentration is a commonly used option. Close monitoring and patient advice are essential.

CNI is mainly metabolized in the liver by CYP3A4 and CYP3A5. Drugs inhibiting CYP3A4 decrease CNI metabolism, resulting in an increase in CNI levels. Thus, co‐administration requires a lower dosage of CNI. Non‐dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (diltiazem and verapamil) and azoles (fluconazole and ketoconazole) are commonly used for this purpose.

The transplant recipients who are CYP3A5 expressers have a higher rate of CNI metabolism and require a higher dose of CNI to achieve the target. The prevalence of CYP3A5 expressers is 50%–60% of the Thai population [88, 89]. Co‐administration of CYP3A4 inhibitors is pronounced benefit in this group. The CYP3A5 expressers can be identified through a pharmacogenetic test for CYP3A5 polymorphism or by screening for a trough concentration per daily dose (C0/D) ratio [90]. The C0/D ratio of tacrolimus < 1.05 ng/mL/mg suggests a fast or extensive metabolizer. This ratio should be calculated only when the drug reaches the steady state, which requires at least 7 days after transplantation. High‐dose corticosteroids may also affect CYP3A4 and require a higher dosage of tacrolimus [91].

Co‐administration of diltiazem or verapamil allows for a reduction in CNI dosage of approximately 20%–50%. A clinical study using diltiazem 30 mg every 12 h since the day of transplantation in patients who are CYP3A5 expressers or have C0/D ratio < 1.05 ng/mL/mg, and blood pressure is not lower than 110/70 mmHg, increases tacrolimus concentration [92]. Co‐administration of azoles allows for a reduction in CNI dosage of approximately 50%.

However, strict caution must be exercised regarding the clinical risks associated with withdrawing these interacting drugs, as the timing of CNI dose reversal depends on the pharmacokinetic washout of the specific inhibitor. When non‐dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (e.g., diltiazem) are discontinued, the CYP3A4/5 inhibitory effect dissipates rapidly; therefore, the CNI dose should generally be increased concurrently to prevent rapid subtherapeutic levels. Conversely, azole antifungals (e.g., fluconazole) have a prolonged half‐life of roughly 30 h, meaning CYP3A4/5 inhibition persists for 5 to 7 days after the last dose. Increasing the CNI dose on the exact day an azole is stopped can precipitate acute CNI toxicity. Instead, the CNI dose should be adjusted gradually over the subsequent days. In all cases, rigorous patient education and prompt therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM)—ideally performed within 3 to 5 days of discontinuation—are recommended during the inhibitor's washout period to safely restore pre‐inhibitor baseline CNI requirements.

Clinical studies demonstrating the efficacy and safety of immunosuppressive drugs in kidney transplantation primarily use the original drugs. Once the drug patent expires, generic drugs can be manufactured at a lower price. It is ideally bioequivalent to the original ones, but the evidence is limited [93]. Most immunosuppressive drugs in kidney transplantation have a narrow therapeutic index. The tapering of immunosuppressants can begin with either the anti‐metabolite group or the CNI group first, depending on the consideration of appropriateness for each individual patient. Discontinuation of prednisolone may be considered as the final step. As immunosuppression is withdrawn, patients must be closely monitored for graft intolerance syndrome (GIS), a state of chronic, immunologically driven inflammation directed at the failed organ. Clinically, GIS presents with localized symptoms such as graft tenderness, graft enlargement, and gross hematuria, alongside systemic inflammatory signs including persistent fever, malaise, weight loss, and elevated inflammatory markers (e.g., C‐reactive protein) that frequently exacerbate ESA‐resistant anaemia. If these symptoms of GIS develop during the tapering phase, they can often be managed initially by maintaining or temporarily increasing low‐dose corticosteroids. However, if medical management fails or the patient develops severe, refractory symptoms (such as persistent fever, intractable graft pain, or recurrent gross hematuria), a graft nephrectomy is indicated [96, 97].

Rationale and Evidence

Simultaneous liver and kidney transplantation has advantages in immunosuppressant management. Receiving organs from the same donor makes the immunological assessment less complex than receiving organs from different donors. Specifically, when one organ is the liver, it possesses mechanisms that help reduce the risk of organ rejection in several ways, such as having a large volume of blood supply and the ability of the liver to regenerate, which helps reduce the amount of alloantibody. Additionally, it is an organ with a reticuloendothelial system consisting of Kupffer cells, myeloid cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, which play important roles in helping to eliminate various foreign antigens [103].

Simultaneous liver and kidney transplantation can prevent or reduce the risk of kidney allograft rejection [105, 106]. There are not yet studies with sufficient methodology to identify clear guidelines for induction therapy usage. However, it has been found that not using induction therapy, using IL‐2RA, and using ATG result in no difference in allograft survival [102]. Some studies show that patients receiving ATG may have higher mortality rates than patients receiving IL‐2RA or no induction therapy [107]. There are not yet studies with sufficient evidence in cases where patients have pre‐transplant DSA to conclude the role of desensitization or ATG use in induction on patient and graft survival. It has been found that there is no significant difference in kidney allograft survival between patients with pre‐transplant DSA and patients without pre‐transplant DSA in the group undergoing simultaneous liver and kidney transplantation [103].
