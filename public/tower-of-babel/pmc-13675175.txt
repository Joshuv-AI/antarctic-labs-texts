A Phase 1/2 study of ontorpacept (TTI-621) in combination with doxorubicin in patients with unresectable or metastatic high-grade leiomyosarcoma
Authors: Sujana Movva, Victoria Allgood, Rashmi Chugh, Lara E Davis, Howard H Bailey, Mohammed Milhem, Varun Monga, Steven Attia, Ankit Mangla, Ingmar Bruns, Luke Kuttschreuter, Dalila Bouyoucef-Cherchalli, Caimiao Wei, Gloria H Y Lin, Hong Zhang, Ilan Pomerance, Mihaela Druta, Sant Chawla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOntorpacept, a recombinant signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα)–blocking fusion protein, has antitumour activity by disrupting CD47–SIRPα signalling. This Phase 1/2 study examined ontorpacept with doxorubicin in patients with leiomyosarcoma.MethodsEligible patients were ≥18 years with metastatic or locally advanced high-grade soft-tissue sarcomas (high-grade leiomyosarcoma in Phase 2). In Phase 1, patients received escalating doses of ontorpacept plus doxorubicin. Phase 2 dose expansion evaluated ontorpacept doses 0.2, 1.0, and 2.0 mg/kg plus doxorubicin. The primary endpoints were safety (Phase 1); and objective response rate (ORR) (Phase 1/2).ResultsSeventy-six patients were enrolled (Phase 1, n = 9; Phase 2, n = 67). No dose-limiting toxicities occurred in Phase 1. The most common treatment-related adverse events were neutrophil count decreased/neutropenia (grade ≥3 in 66% of patients). In Phase 1, one patient receiving ontorpacept 2.0 mg/kg had a confirmed partial response. In Phase 2, six patients receiving ontorpacept 0.2 mg/kg (ORR, 18.8%; 95% CI, 7.2–36.4) and one patient receiving 2.0 mg/kg (ORR, 4.5%; 95% CI, 0.1–22.8) had confirmed partial responses.ConclusionsThis first study of CD47 inhibition with chemotherapy in leiomyosarcomas shows manageable safety, supporting further investigation of ontorpacept dosing and schedule, in combination with doxorubicin and as monotherapy.Subject terms: Molecular medicine, Oncology

INTRODUCTION

Leiomyosarcoma, a type of soft-tissue sarcoma, is an aggressive malignant mesenchymal tumour [1, 2]. Anthracycline-based therapy with doxorubicin or doxorubicin in combination with trabectedin/dacarbazine is considered standard therapy for first-line treatment of patients with metastatic or advanced soft-tissue sarcomas [3–7]. However, response to doxorubicin monotherapy can vary, with response rates of approximately 13% to 23% and median progression-free survival (PFS) of 6 to 7 months [4, 8, 9]. In addition, cumulative cardiac toxicity may limit doxorubicin use [10]. Consequently, a more effective treatment option is needed for patients with advanced leiomyosarcoma.

CD47 is a transmembrane glycoprotein expressed on all hematopoietic cells and on epithelial and mesenchymal cells [11, 12]. Soft-tissue sarcomas, including leiomyosarcoma, express high levels of CD47, which is associated with poor prognosis [13]. CD47 binds to signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) on the surface of macrophages to suppress their phagocytic activity, thus enabling tumour cells to evade immune-mediated clearance [14].

Blocking CD47 has therefore emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy [15]. CD47 blockade increased phagocytosis of human leiomyosarcoma cells in vitro and reduced tumour size in leiomyosarcoma-bearing mice [16]. Doxorubicin has been shown to induce tumour cell death through translocation of calreticulin to the cell surface of macrophages, promoting tumour cell phagocytosis [17]. Combining doxorubicin with CD47-targeted antibodies resulted in enhanced antitumour activity and increased macrophage-mediated killing in preclinical studies [18, 19].

Ontorpacept (TTI-621) is a recombinant soluble fusion protein that combines the Fc region of human immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) with the CD47-binding domain of human SIRPα. Ontorpacept binds to CD47 on the surface of tumour cells to override CD47-mediated inhibition of phagocytosis. The IgG1 Fc region of ontorpacept assists in macrophage activation through engagement of Fcγ receptors. This dual mechanism distinguishes ontorpacept from other antibody-based therapies targeting CD47–SIRPa, which are largely IgG4-based and typically require co-administration of a complementary agent to deliver a robust prophagocytic signal and achieve full activity [20–23].

Combining ontorpacept with doxorubicin may enhance the clinical benefit of doxorubicin monotherapy in tumours that express CD47 and have high numbers of macrophages, such as leiomyosarcoma. Therefore, we conducted a Phase 1/2 study to determine the maximum tolerated dose of ontorpacept in combination with the standard dose of doxorubicin in patients with soft-tissue sarcomas (Phase 1), followed by an expansion cohort (Phase 2) to evaluate safety and antitumor activity of the combination in patients with unresectable or metastatic high-grade leiomyosarcoma.

METHODS

Patients

This was a multicentre, open-label study. Phase 1 enrolled patients with locally advanced or metastatic high-grade leiomyosarcoma, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, myxofibrosarcoma, dedifferentiated liposarcoma, angiosarcoma, or epithelioid sarcoma. In Phase 2, enrolment was restricted to high-grade leiomyosarcoma (pathology confirmed at central laboratory). Other key eligibility criteria included age (≥18 years), European Cooperative Oncology Group performance status 0 or 1, and up to one prior treatment (gemcitabine±docetaxel). Prior radiotherapy was allowed. Patients were excluded if they had inadequate cardiovascular function, uncontrolled infectious disease, or central nervous system involvement (except previously treated stable brain metastases) or were receiving systemic steroid therapy within 7 days before study treatment.

Study Design and Treatment

In the dose-escalation Phase (Phase 1), following a standard 3 + 3 design, patients received escalating doses of ontorpacept (0.2, 0.7, and 2.0 mg/kg) in combination with standard-dose doxorubicin (75 mg/m2). Based on the known safety profile and observed efficacy of ontorpacept monotherapy, the dose of 0.2 mg/kg was selected as a starting dose in Phase 1 (Fig. 1) [24]. Prior evaluation of TTI-621 [25] demonstrated that dose levels of ontorpacept up to 2.0 mg/kg were tolerable, informing the selection of the two additional doses of 0.7 mg/kg and 2.0 mg/kg for Phase 1. Ontorpacept was administered on days 1 and 8 of each 21-day cycle from Cycles 1 to 6. Doxorubicin was co-administered on day 1 of each cycle and limited to a maximum of six cycles. After a maximum of six cycles of the combination, patients who achieved stable disease (SD) or better were allowed to continue receiving ontorpacept as monotherapy on Days 1 and 15 of 28-day cycles until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. Patients who completed at least four cycles of the combination with SD or better, but experienced prohibitive toxicity assessed by the treating physician as related to doxorubicin, were allowed to continue to ontorpacept monotherapy; however, if ontorpacept was discontinued due to toxicity during the co-administration treatment period, doxorubicin was also discontinued. In Phase 1, the cohorts were enroled consecutively, and patients were allocated to the currently open cohort. New cohorts were not opened until the last patient in the current cohort completed at least one 21-day cycle (the DLT observation period).

In the dose-expansion Phase (Phase 2), two cohorts—low dose (0.2 mg/kg) and high dose (2.0 mg/kg) ontorpacept—were initially planned based on safety data from Phase 1 to further evaluate the safety, efficacy, and pharmacodynamics of the combination. Treatment followed the same Phase 1 schedule. After the final Phase 1 cohort was enroled, the Phase 2 low dose 0.2 mg/kg cohort was opened for recruitment, concurrently with the DLT evaluation of patients receiving ontorpacept high dose 2.0 mg/kg in Phase 1. Once the DLT evaluation period was completed and the 2.0 mg/kg dose was established to have a manageable safety profile, recruitment into the Phase 2 high dose 2.0 mg/kg cohort was initiated; patients were enrolled in the low and high doses cohorts on an alternating basis.

Thrombocytopenia is an overlapping toxicity of both drugs [24, 26]. In a prior study of TTI-621 evaluating doses from 0.05 mg/kg to 2.0 mg/kg [25], the primary grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse event [TRAE] was acute but reversible thrombocytopenia in 24% of patients [25]. Since the thrombocytopenia was reversible and not associated with bleeding, it was considered non-dose-limiting, and dose levels of ontorpacept up to 2.0 mg/kg were considered tolerable. In the current Phase 2, the incidence of grade 3/4 thrombocytopenia was higher than anticipated in the 2.0-mg/kg cohort. A decision was made to terminate the 2.0-mg/kg cohort and open a new cohort to evaluate an intermediate dose of ontorpacept (1.0 mg/kg) in combination with doxorubicin.

Radiologic imaging was performed every 6 weeks for the first 18 weeks (while patients were receiving the combination), every 8 weeks during the monotherapy portion (until Week 50, or until the patient had been enrolled for 1 year), then every 12 weeks thereafter. Treatment continued until disease progression, development of unacceptable toxicities, or any adverse event (AE) that resulted in two or more dose reductions or dose interruption for >42 days of ontorpacept or doxorubicin. Patients deriving meaningful clinical benefit were permitted to continue treatment in the study at the treating investigator’s discretion beyond progression after discussion with the sponsor.

Prophylaxis with dexrazoxane for cardiotoxicity began by initiation of Cycle 5 when cumulative doxorubicin exposure reached 300 mg/m2. Beginning with Cycle 2, prophylactic use of granulocyte colony–stimulating factor to prevent febrile neutropenia was allowed based on American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines at the investigator’s discretion.

Outcome measures

The primary objective of Phase 1 was to assess the safety of escalating doses of ontorpacept in combination with doxorubicin.

The primary endpoint of Phase 2 was assessment of safety and the objective response rate (ORR). ORR was defined as complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) as assessed by the investigators per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours version 1.1 and confirmed ≥4 weeks after initial observation.

Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), PFS, disease control rate (DCR; CR + PR + SD), duration of response, duration of disease control, time to progression, time to new metastases as assessed by the investigators, and safety. PFS was defined as the time from the first ontorpacept dose to progressive disease (PD) or death by any cause, whichever was first. Patients without PD or death or with either PD or death after two or more missing/inadequate disease assessments or with either PD or death after the start date of alternate anticancer therapy were censored at their last evaluable assessment before the missed assessments or new anticancer therapy. OS was defined as the time from the first ontorpacept dose to death by any cause. Patients without a death event were censored at the last date they were known to be alive.

AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) version 26.1. AE information was collected from the date each patient provided informed consent to a minimum of 30 days after the last administration of study treatment. Safety endpoints were summarised based on the on-treatment period, defined from the time from the first dose of study treatment through either 30 days after the last dose of study treatment or the start of a new anticancer therapy, whichever occurred first. Severity of AEs and laboratory abnormalities were graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5. AEs of special interest were coded using standardised MedDRA queries (SMQs) (Supplementary Table S2). Infusion-related reactions (IRRs) were coded using a single SMQ term. DLTs were defined as specific AEs occurring during cycle 1 related to ontorpacept or the combination of ontorpacept and doxorubicin (Supplementary Methods).

Biomarker analysis

Serum cytokine samples were collected at Cycles 1 and 3 Day 1 (pre-dose, end of infusion, 60 minutes after end of infusion) and Cycles 1 and 3 Day 8 (pre-dose) throughout Phases 1 and 2. Samples were analysed using the Luminex MAP Microsphere Immunoassay, details in Supplementary Methods.

Multiomyx multiplexed immunofluorescence analysis on baseline tumour tissue biopsy was performed by NeoGenomics (see Supplementary Methods). A panel of 13 protein markers was evaluated— CD47, CD11c, CD163, CD3, CD4, CD68, CD8, CD83, FOXP3, HLA-DR, NKp46 (CD335), programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), and a tumour cell marker desmin.

Deep-resolution human T-cell receptor (TCR) beta sequencing was conducted on each of the blood-derived samples by Adaptive Biotechnologies (see Supplementary Methods).

Whole-exome sequencing of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples collected during screening was conducted by Personalis, Inc. (see Supplementary Methods). Detailed mutation types and impact factors were read out by the SnpEff tool (https://pcingola.github.io/SnpEff/) [27]. Oncoprints were created by filtering genes of high and moderate impact as well as through the GuardantOMNI panel and sarcoma-relevant genes.

Statistical analysis

In Phase 1, three dose levels of ontorpacept in combination with the standard dose (75 mg/m2) of doxorubicin were anticipated, with at least nine and up to approximately 18 participants enrolled to evaluate dose levels—following a standard 3 + 3 dose-escalation design. For Phase 2, a Simon’s two-stage design was used for the 0.2 and 2.0 mg/kg initial dose cohorts, assuming a baseline ORR of 10% with doxorubicin monotherapy and an alternative ORR of 25% with the combination therapy, with 90% power and a one-sided type 1 error of 0.20. With these assumptions, each dose-level cohort in the Phase 2 study required 18 participants evaluable for response in stage 1; with at least two confirmed responses, an additional 13 participants were to be enrolled in stage 2, for a total of 31 participants per dose-level cohort. The combinations were considered worthy of further study if at least five confirmed responses were observed in either 31-participant expansion cohort. Safety and antitumour activity were assessed in all patients who received at least one dose of study treatment. For reporting AEs, data were combined across dose levels. TRAEs were attributable to ontorpacept, doxorubicin, or the combination. DLTs were evaluated in patients in Phase 1 who received the full dose of ontorpacept and doxorubicin in Cycle 1 and were followed for the entire 21-day cycle.

Continuous and categorical endpoints were summarised descriptively. The two-sided 95% CIs for ORR and DCR were calculated using the Wilson score method. PFS, OS, and duration of disease control were summarised using the Kaplan-Meier method.

For longitudinal cytokine data, only patients who were enrolled in the 0.2- and 1.0-mg/kg ontorpacept doses were included due to prioritisation of cohorts for sample analysis. A mixed-effect model of repeated measures with unstructured covariance was fit to obtain point estimates. The two-sided 95% CIs were calculated using the Kenward-Roger method at each time point. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to the unadjusted P values of the contrast between 60 minutes after infusion and pre-infusion to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at 5%. An additional requirement of a fold-change of ≥4 for the contrast was also applied to declare a cytokine “signal.” Among the cytokine signals, the association between the increase (peak) of cytokines and the decrease (nadir) in platelets and lymphocytes in the first week post treatment was examined by Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to adjust for multiple cytokine signals to ensure an FDR of 5%.

For TCR sequencing data, only those patients enrolled to receive ontorpacept 0.2 mg/kg were included in the analysis due to small sample sizes of other dose levels. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied for pairwise comparison between time points. For TCR parameters that had more than two time points, the Jonckheere-Terpstra trend test was also conducted. The P values were adjusted by the Benjamini-Hochberg method at 5% FDR. For immunohistochemistry data, the Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test was used to assess the association between best overall response (BOR) and CD47 expression levels. P values for pairwise comparisons between PD, SD, and PR were calculated.

RESULTS

Patients and treatment

A total of 90 patients were screened; 76 were enroled and treated in the study (n = 9 in Phase 1, n = 67 in Phase 2). The majority of patients were female (66.7% in Phase 1, 73.1% in Phase 2), and the overall study population was aged 25 to 77 years (Table 1). Most patients were White (84.2%), and all patients had unresectable and/or metastatic disease (86.8% with metastatic disease). In Phase 1, 4/9 patients (44.4%) had no prior treatment regimen, and 5/9 patients (55.6%) had one prior treatment regimen. In Phase 2, 45/67 patients (67.2%) had no prior treatment regimen, and 22/67 patients (32.8%) had one prior treatment regimen (limited to gemcitabine ± docetaxel; 4 patients also received radiation therapy). No patient had prior doxorubicin.

In Phase 1, patients received 0.2, 0.7, or 2.0 mg/kg ontorpacept (n = 3 each). The median (range) number of ontorpacept doses was 20.0 (4.0–39.0): 20 [4–38] in the 0.2-mg/kg cohort, 16.0 (4.0–23.0) in the 0.7-mg/kg cohort, and 21.0 (4.0–29.0) in the 2.0-mg/kg cohort. The median (range) ontorpacept treatment duration was 34.1 (6.0–74.1) weeks: 34.1 (6.0–74.1) weeks in the 0.2-mg/kg cohort, 28.0 (6.0–45.1) weeks in the 0.7-mg/kg cohort, and 39.0 (6.0–60.0) weeks in the 2.0-mg/kg cohort.

In Phase 2, patients received ontorpacept 0.2 mg/kg (n = 32, exceeding the planned sample size for this dose level by one patient, due to two screened patients who simultaneously met the eligibility criteria), 1.0 mg/kg (n = 13), or 2.0 mg/kg (n = 22). Enrolment in the high-dose cohort was terminated at 22 patients, following the observation of a higher-than-expected rate of grade 3/4 thrombocytopenia. An intermediate dose–level cohort was subsequently opened to evaluate ontorpacept 1.0 mg/kg, in combination with doxorubicin. No formal sample size calculation was performed for this new cohort. Overall, the median (range) number of ontorpacept doses was 12 [1–41]: 16 [3–41] in the 0.2-mg/kg cohort, 9 [4–17] in the 1.0-mg/kg cohort, and 8.5 [1–28] in the 2.0-mg/kg cohort. The median (range) ontorpacept treatment duration was 24.1 (1.0–88.0) weeks: 26.5 (5.9–88.0) weeks in the 0.2-mg/kg cohort, 18.0 (6.0–33.0) weeks in the 1.0-mg/kg cohort, and 17.4 (1.0–57.7) weeks in the 2.0-mg/kg cohort.

A total of 40/76 patients (52.6%) received six cycles of doxorubicin treatment. For all patients treated (Phases 1 and 2), the median (range) number of doxorubicin doses received was 6.0 (1.0–6.0), with a median (range) treatment duration of 18.0 (3.0–25.0) weeks.

Safety

No DLTs were observed in Phase 1, and the maximum tolerated dose was not established. All 76 patients had at least one treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE), and 75 (98.7%) had at least one TRAE. The most common (>50% of patients) all-causality, any-grade TEAEs were neutrophil count decreased/neutropenia grouped terms] (69.7%), fatigue (68.4%), platelet count decreased/thrombocytopenia [grouped terms] (67.1%), nausea (64.5%), and anaemia (56.6%) (Supplementary Table S1).

The most common (>50% of patients) TRAEs of any grade were neutrophil count decreased/neutropenia [grouped terms] (69.7%), platelet count decreased/thrombocytopenia [grouped terms] (67.1%), fatigue (63.2%), nausea (61.8%), and anaemia (55.3%) (Table 2). Overall, 35/76 patients (46.1%) discontinued doxorubicin for any reason (including PD), with 12/76 (15.8%) discontinuing due to TRAEs. The most common TRAEs (>2% of patients) resulting in discontinuation of doxorubicin (alone or doxorubicin and ontorpacept) were thrombocytopenia (4/76 [5.3%]) and decreased ejection fraction (2/76 [2.6%]). TRAEs that resulted in discontinuation specifically of the doxorubicin-ontorpacept combination in 4/76 (5.3%) patients were cardiac disorders (cardiac hypertrophy, cardiotoxicity, and myocardial fibrosis in the same patient), enterocolitis, endocarditis, and decreased ejection fraction—in one patient (1.3%) each. Additionally, one patient discontinued ontorpacept single agent during the maintenance Phase due to a TRAE (platelet count decreased), bringing the number of patients who discontinued ontorpacept due to TRAEs to 5/76 (6.6%).

In total, 48/76 patients (63.2%) had TEAEs leading to ontorpacept dose interruption, and 10/76 (13.2%) had TEAEs leading to ontorpacept dose reduction. In total, 36/76 patients (47.3%) had grade ≥3 platelet count decreased/thrombocytopenia: three patients (33.3%) in Phase 1) and 33 (49.3%) in Phase 2. One patient in the 2.0 mg/kg Phase 1 group had grade 4 treatment-related thrombocytopenia (did not meet DLT criteria), while in the 2.0 mg/kg Phase 2 group, 1 patient had grade 3 and 1 patient had grade 4 treatment-related thrombocytopenia. There were 3/76 patients (3.9%) who had grade 3 haemorrhage events, all of which occurred in Phase 2. These 3 patients had grade 3 or 4 thrombocytopenia just before, or on the date of onset of haemorrhage, however, the events were assessed as unrelated to study drugs by the investigator. There were no grade 4/5 haemorrhage events. Grade ≥3 anaemia was reported in 21/76 patients (27.6%): four (44.4%) in Phase 1 and 17 (25.4%) in Phase 2, all of which were considered treatment-related. IRRs were observed in 36/76 patients (47.4%): two (22.2%) in Phase 1 and 34 (50.7%) in Phase 2. One IRR in Phase 1 was grade ≥3, and 4 IRRs in Phase 2 were grade ≥3. Seven (9.2%) patients had an abnormal left ventricular ejection fraction event measured by echocardiogram, two (2.6%) of which were assessed as clinically significant and related to doxorubicin in the Phase 2 0.2-mg/kg cohort; both occurred during the on-treatment period.

A total of 27 deaths (35.5%) occurred: four (44.4%) in Phase 1 and 23 (34.3%) in Phase 2. Death related to worsening condition under study was reported in 18 patients (23.7%). The reason for death was unknown in six patients (7.9%), as these deaths occurred after the AE information collection period. Deaths related to AEs (pulmonary embolism), post-surgery complications, and respiratory failure were reported in one patient (1.3%) each. No TRAEs led to death in the safety analysis set.

Biomarker analysis

Following study drug infusion, there was an increase in proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines associated with innate and macrophage activation, including macrophage inflammatory protein (MIP) 1α, MIP-1β, monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1, MCP-2, MIP-3A, tumour necrosis factor α (TNFα), chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 10 (CXCL-10, IP-10), interleukin (IL)-8, IL-6, and IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA) (Fig. 3a). These increases were transient, typically peaking at the end of infusion or 1 hour post infusion and returning to baseline levels 1 week after last infusion. Generally, the cytokine induction was slightly greater at a dose of 1.0 mg/kg compared with 0.2 mg/kg. Cytokine and chemokine induction was observed in all patients and did not correlate with clinical response. Additionally, the level of cytokine/chemokine increase was generally lower at Cycle 3 than at Cycle 1. Assessment of the relationship between cytokines/chemokines and common haematologic AEs showed a statistically significant correlation (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05, Spearman correlation ≤−0.35) between changes in IL-1RA, IP-10, MCP-2, MCP-1, MIP-3A, and IL-6 and reductions in platelet counts, with greater induction of cytokine/chemokine observed in patients who experienced higher decreases in platelet counts (Supplementary Fig. S1). However, no statistically significant relationship was identified for anaemia and neutrophil counts.

Forty-six baseline biopsy specimens collected from Phase 1 and Phase 2 were analysed for cell surface CD47, PD-L1 expression, and immune markers. No clear association was observed between CD47 expression in baseline tumour biopsies and BORs (Fig. 3b). Similarly, no clear association was found between immune cell profile, including macrophages, CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells, CD4-regulated T cells, natural killer cells, CD11c+ myeloid cells, PD-L1 expression, and clinical responses (Supplementary Fig. S2). On-treatment biopsy specimens were excluded from this analysis due to the limited sample size.

Blood samples collected before and after treatment were analysed by TCR sequencing to assess the clonality, richness, and pharmacodynamics of the TCR repertoire. There was a trend toward increasing Simpson’s clonality and decreasing repertoire richness across visits, with fewer downsampled rearrangements at each visit compared with the preceding visits. Additionally, there was a trend toward decreasing downsampled singletons breadth at both Cycle 3 Day 1 and Cycle 5 Day 1, indicating a lower relative abundance of rare clones in the blood at later visits compared with Cycle 1 Day 1 (Supplementary Fig. S3). Compared with blood samples from Cycle 1 Day 1, Cycle 5 Day 1 samples exhibited lower Morisita indices and a trend toward more expanded clones than Cycle 3 Day 1 samples, but this was not observed with the number of contracted clones. These observations suggest that peripheral repertoires continue to diverge from Cycle 1 Day 1 compositions through Cycle 5 Day 1. However, no clear correlation was observed between changes in peripheral T-cell repertoire metrics and clinical responses due to the small number of samples from PR and PD patients (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Whole-exome sequencing was performed on 45 baseline tumour biopsy specimens from both Phases 1 and 2. An oncoprint was generated by filtering for tumour genes of interest and the impact of the mutations (Fig. 3c). Consistent with existing literature [28, 29], the most frequent alterations were observed in TP53 (40%), ATRX (29%), and RB1 (11%). Additionally, NOTCH2, FAT1, MED12, PDGFRB, NOTCH3, and SMARCA4 emerged as the top mutated genes, each with a prevalence of ≥7% (Fig. 3c). An additional oncoprint without pre-filtering for tumour genes of interest is shown in Supplementary Fig. S4. Due to the limited sample size, no clear association was found between common oncogenic or likely oncogenic genomic alterations in leiomyosarcoma genes and prognosis within the study cohort.

DISCUSSION

This Phase 1/2 study evaluated the safety of ontorpacept in combination with doxorubicin in patients with unresectable or metastatic high-grade soft-tissue sarcomas and antitumour activity in leiomyosarcoma. Doxorubicin-based therapy is considered standard therapy for first-line treatment of these patients; however, the response to single-agent doxorubicin can be variable [8, 30–34].

Recent studies have explored the use of doxorubicin in combination with cytotoxic and targeted agents as a strategy to improve patient outcomes. A study of doxorubicin plus trabectedin demonstrated significantly improved PFS and OS in patients with leiomyosarcoma; however, toxicity was high [3, 4]. Adding the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitor nivolumab to a chemotherapy backbone of doxorubicin and dacarbazine in leiomyosarcoma showed encouraging clinical activity [35]. However, in separate studies, adding doxorubicin to PD-1 inhibitors did not notably improve outcomes in leiomyosarcoma and other soft-tissue sarcomas [36–38], highlighting the need for novel approaches.

Leiomyosarcomas express high levels of CD47, associated with poor prognosis, [13]—likely resulting from suppression of macrophage phagocytic activity through binding of SIRPα [14]. Blocking CD47 has therefore emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy. Several approaches targeting the CD47–SIRPα interaction, in combination with antibody and/or cytotoxic chemotherapy regimens, have been investigated. Responses in early trials have been variable depending on tumour type—including ORRs of up to 5% in non–small cell lung cancer, 39-67% in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and 73% in gastric cancer [39–43]—and the combination of doxorubicin with CD47-targeted antibodies has shown encouraging antitumour activity in preclinical studies [18, 19]. In haematologic malignancies such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and T-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, responses ranging from 25% to 29% have been noted with monotherapy—even at 0.2 mg/kg [24]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to have examined CD47 inhibition in combination with a chemotherapy backbone for the treatment of soft-tissue sarcomas.

Although the 0.2-mg/kg expansion cohort did meet the primary endpoint, the ORR of 18.8% and PFS of 6 months suggest that ontorpacept did not meaningfully improve the response to doxorubicin [4, 6]. Overall, four patients achieved PR during the single-agent ontorpacept treatment Phase. Additionally, of all the patients who achieved a PR, six had ongoing responses for at least 1 year. The highest confirmed ORR was observed with ontorpacept 0.2 mg/kg, however, this study was not designed to compare activity across dose levels. The lower activity at 2.0 mg/kg may reflect a more narrow therapeutic window in the setting of overlapping haematologic toxicity (notably thrombocytopenia), leading to dose interruptions/reductions and reduced effective exposure. Preclinical data also suggest that chemotherapy given after anti-CD47 therapy can impair development of antitumour immune memory, whereas chemotherapy given first may preserve synergy [44]. Thus, the day 1 co-administration of doxorubicin with ontorpacept in this study could have limited immune-mediated benefit. Future studies should optimise CD47-directed combinations by explicitly evaluating dose density and sequencing (e.g., chemotherapy priming or alternative schedules) to maximise efficacy while maintaining tolerability.

Ontorpacept binds minimally to human erythrocytes, a property that has been attributed to the affinity for CD47 and the unique structure of CD47 in erythrocyte membranes [21]. This characteristic is expected to reduce the risk of anaemia and avoid the large CD47 antigen sink on erythrocytes that could inhibit exposure to ontorpacept. Thrombocytopenia is a known toxicity of ontorpacept, believed to result from macrophage-mediated platelet clearance. Although no DLTs were observed during cycle 1 of the 2.0-mg/kg dose-escalation Phase, evidence of thrombocytopenia emerged during Phase 2, leading to dose modifications and treatment delays, and the introduction of an intermediate dose 1.0 mg/kg. For patient safety, the 2.0-mg/kg dose was ultimately discontinued during the study. A prior report has indicated that onartacept-associated thrombocytopenia is reversible and typically resolves within one week, possibly due to compensatory release of splenic sequestration or bone marrow production [24]. Notably, haematological toxicity and cardiomyopathy were the main reasons for discontinuation of doxorubicin alone, aligned with its known adverse event profile [10, 26]. Given the absence of treatment-related haemorrhagic events and previous observations of the transient nature of the thrombocytopenia, reconsideration of the Day 1 and Day 8 dosing schedules used in this study may be warranted.

The increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines following ontorpacept infusion indicates robust innate immune activation, consistent with its mechanism of action. A slightly greater cytokine induction at higher doses of ontorpacept was noted, but correlation with clinical activity was not observed. Strikingly, the inverse correlation between cytokine/chemokine induction and platelet counts, as well as the return of levels to baseline 1 week later, suggests that Day 1 and Day 8 dosing of ontorpacept may be justified; however, it would require protocol tolerance for the expected thrombocytopenia. Biomarker analyses did not identify a correlation between baseline CD47 expression, immune infiltrate profiles or peripheral T-cell repertoire metrics and clinical outcomes, which underscores the biological complexity of leiomyosarcoma and highlights the challenges of biomarker development in this disease.

In conclusion, the combination of ontorpacept and doxorubicin had manageable safety in patients with advanced leiomyosarcoma. As this study was limited by its non-randomised design, it was not possible to determine the specific contribution of ontorpacept over doxorubicin or indolent disease biology during maintenance. Additional studies are needed to assess efficacy in other soft-tissue sarcomas, where ontorpacept benefit is expected, and to optimise dosing.
