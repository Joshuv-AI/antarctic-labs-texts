Abstract
. Each of Ramanujan’s series for 1 π \frac{1}{\pi} is of the form ∑ n = 0 ∞ z n ​ ( a 1 ) n ​ ( a 2 ) n ​ ( a 3 ) n ( b 1 ) n ​ ( b 2 ) n ​ ( b 3 ) n ​ ( c 1 ​ n + c 2 ) \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}\frac{(a_{1})_{n}(a_{2})_{n}(a_{3})_{n}}{(b_{1})_{n}(b_{2})_{n}(b_{3})_{n}}(c_{1}n+c_{2}) for rational parameters such that the difference between the arguments of any lower and upper Pochhammer symbols is not an integer. In accordance with the work of Chu, if an infinite sum of this form admits a closed form, then this provides a formula of Ramanujan type . Chu has introduced remarkable results on formulas of Ramanujan type, through the use of accelerations based on Ω \Omega -sums related to classical hypergeometric identities. Building on our past work on an acceleration method due to Wilf relying on inhomogeneous difference equations derived from Zeilberger’s algorithm, we extend this method through what we refer to as an iterative approach that is inspired by Chu’s accelerations derived using iteration patterns for well-poised Ω \Omega -sums and that we apply to introduce and prove many accelerated formulas of Ramanujan type for universal constants, along with many further accelerations related to the discoveries of Ramanujan, Guillera, and Chu.

2.
Formulas of Ramanujan type

The formulas

(3)

\frac{3\pi^{2}}{4}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2}{27}\right)^{n}\left[\begin{matrix}1,1,1\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\frac{4}{3},\frac{5}{3},\frac{3}{2}\end{matrix}\right]_{n}(10n+7)

and

(4)

\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{4}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2}{27}\right)^{n}\left[\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\frac{5}{6},1,\frac{7}{6}\end{matrix}\right]_{n}(5n+1)

are described as being of
Ramanujan type
by Chu and are highlighted as main results in the work of Chu
[
21
]
and
have inspired our new results highlighted in Section
2.1
below. The Chu formulas in (
3
)
and (
4
) recall Ramanujan’s formula
[
8
, pp. 352–354]

[
39
]

(5)

\frac{27}{4\pi}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2}{27}\right)^{n}\left[\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{2},\frac{2}{3}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
1,1,1\end{matrix}\right]_{n}(15n+2)

involving the same convergence rate as in the Chu series in (
3
)–(
4
). Observe that the Ramanujan
series among (
2
) and (
5
) and the series in the Chu formulas in
(
3
)–(
4
) are all of the form

(6)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}\frac{(a_{1})_{n}(a_{2})_{n}(a_{3})_{n}}{(b_{1})_{n}(b_{2})_{n}(b_{3})_{n}}(c_{1}n+c_{2})

for rational values
a_{i}
,
b_{i}
,
c_{i}
, and

such that the difference between the arguments of any lower and upper
Pochhammer symbols is never an integer, i.e., so that any given combinations of rising factorials are irreducible, in terms of not being
reducible to rational functions. A series of this form that admits a closed-form evaluation is said to be of
Ramanujan type
,
with reference to works by Chu
[
20
,
21
]
and by Chu and Zhang
[
25
]
.

According to Chu and Zhang
[
25
]
, the first known formula of Ramanujan type is such that

(7)

\frac{2}{\pi}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\left[\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
1,1,1\end{matrix}\right]_{n}(4n+1),

noting the absolute convergence rate of
1
for the series displayed in (
7
), in contrast to the very fast
converging series among (
2
)–(
5
). The rational expansion in
(
7
) was first discovered in 1859 by Bauer
[
5
]
via a Fourier–Legendre expansion. The same
formula in (
7
) was rediscovered by Ramanujan and was, famously, included in Ramanujan’s first letter to
Hardy, and hence the expression
Bauer–Ramanujan formula
in reference to the series expansion in
(
7
)
[
14
]
. There is a rich history surrounding the Bauer–Ramanujan formula
and its many different proofs

[
14
]
, motivating, from interdisciplinary perspectives and in view of the many different
areas in mathematics associated with the known proofs of (
7
),
the investigation as to how
new techniques can be applied to generate formulas of Ramanujan type.

As suggested by the equivalence of (
1
) and (
2
), the combination of rising factorials
involved within the summand in (
2
) may be rewritten with binomial coefficients or factorials with integer-valued
arguments (and without any remaining Pochhammer symbols with non-integer arguments), according to the
Gauss
multiplication formula

\Gamma(nx)=\left(2\pi\right)^{\frac{1-n}{2}}n^{nx-\frac{1}{2}}\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\Gamma\left(x+\frac{k}{n}\right)

for the
\Gamma
-function such that
\Gamma(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{x-1}e^{-t}\,dt
for
\Re(x)>0
,
referring to Rainville’s classic text
[
38
]
for related background material. Each of Ramanujan’s series for
\frac{1}{\pi}
can
be similarly rewritten with binomial coefficients, and in such a way so that the theory of elliptic functions can be used to prove and
generalize these formulas for
\frac{1}{\pi}
, by expressing the generating functions for the associated combinations of binomial
coefficients using the special functions known as the
complete elliptic integrals
. This elliptic functions-based approach was
pioneered by the Borwein brothers
[
10
]
, who, famously, first proved all of Ramanujan’s formulas
for
\frac{1}{\pi}
. In closely related ways, the theories of elliptic functions to alternative bases associated with Ramanujan’s series
were developed by Berndt, Bhargava, and Garvan
[
9
]
and heavily rely on modular relations and theta
functions. In contrast, if a series of the form shown in (
6
) is such that its Pochhammer symbols cannot be combined to
form integer-valued factorials (without any rising factorials with non-integer arguments), then, typically, such series cannot be
expressed, at least in any direct or meaningful way, using classical properties of special functions such as elliptic or theta functions. In
this case, we say that the combination of Pochhammer symbols involved is
irregular
.

Chu has introduced formulas of Ramanujan type involving irregular combinations of Pochhammer symbols and involving a variety of
different convergence rates, and the remarkable nature of such results is given by how the generating functions-based approach
suggested above cannot be applied. For example, through a recursive argument related to the
\Omega
-sums that are due to Chu
and Zhang
[
25
]
, Chu
[
21
]
proved that

(8)

21\pi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{27}{512}\right)^{n}\left[\begin{matrix}1,\frac{2}{3},\frac{4}{3}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\frac{5}{4},\frac{11}{8},\frac{15}{8}\end{matrix}\right]_{n}(77n+68).

Again with the use of recursions for
\Omega
-sums, Chu
[
20
]
has proved formulas of Ramanujan type as in

(9)

\frac{21\sqrt{2}}{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{27}\right)^{n}\left[\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{4},\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
1,\frac{11}{12},\frac{19}{12}\end{matrix}\right]_{n}(28n+15)

and

(10)

\frac{1036\sqrt[3]{4}\pi}{999\sqrt{3}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{27}\right)^{n}\left[\begin{matrix}2,\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{6}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\frac{10}{9},\frac{13}{9},\frac{16}{9}\end{matrix}\right]_{n}(7n+3),

noting the highly irregular combinations of Pochhammer symbols involved in
(
9

3.
An iterative approach

In our previous work on two-term hypergeometric recurrences
[
15
]
,
this concerned building upon an acceleration method due to Wilf
[
42
]

by applying Zeilberger’s algorithm
to bivariate hypergeometric functions involving multiple free parameters, such as

(60)

F(n,k):=\left[\begin{matrix}a,b\vskip 2.84526pt\\
n,n\end{matrix}\right]_{k},

in the hope that the inputting
F
-function would yield, according to Zeilberger’s algorithm,
a two-term, inhomogeneous recurrence satisfying certain additional conditions. For the sake of brevity,
we omit a full discussion on the technical details and background material
concerning Wilf’s method
[
42
]
, inviting the interested reader
to review our past works on this method
[
12
,
15
]
.
As a follow-up to our applications of Wilf’s method using input functions as in (
60
),
we then considered how this method could be further extended with the use of

of Pochhammer symbols
[
12
]
,
so as to be applicable to input functions such as

(61)

F(n,k):=\frac{(a)_{k+f}(b)_{k+e}}{(n)_{k+d}(n)_{k+c}}.

This method of shifted indices has been shown to be powerful, and has been applied

[

to produce formulas of Ramanujan type of a similar nature
relative to the new results highlighted in Section

.
This leads us to take Wilf’s method even further, by extending the method of shifted indices
using an

approach described below. Our past results concerning Wilf’s method are special
cases of our new and iterative approach described below.

3.1.
The iteration pattern (1, 1)

The iterative approach described as follows is inspired by
what Chu et al. have described as an
iteration pattern

given by expressing

\Omega(a;b,c,d,e)
in terms of

\Omega(a+\rho_{a};b+\rho_{b},c+\rho_{c},d+\rho+d,e+\rho_{e})
, where, for

\Re(1+2a-b-c-d-e)>0
, we write

\Omega(a;b,c,d,e):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(a+2k)\left[\begin{matrix}b,c,d,e\vskip 2.84526pt\\
1+a-b,1+a-c,1+a-d,1+a-e\end{matrix}\right]_{k},

with this acceleration method having been pioneered
in the above referenced Chu–Zhang
contribution Chu and Zhang
[
25
]

on the acceleration of Dougall’s
{}_{5}F_{4}
-summation.
As expressed in our previous work
[
12
]

our acceleration techniques relying on Zeilberger’s algorithm may be seen as more versatile,
in the sense that it does not rely on summands for
\Omega
-sums of the above form.

Let
F(n,k,a)

be hypergeometric when interpreted as function of
n
and
k
, for a fixed parameter
a
.
Suppose that Zeilberger’s algorithm applied to
F(n,k,a)

produces a two-term recurrence. For the sake of clarity, we let this recurrence be a first-order recurrence,
but our iterative method may be applied more broadly, writing

p_{1}(n,a)F(n+1,k,a)+p_{2}(n,a)F(n,k,a)=G(n,k+1,a)-G(n,k,a)

for polynomials
p_{1}(n,a)
and
p_{2}(n,a)
in
n
(again for a parameter
a
)
and for a function
G(n,k,a)
that is hypergeometric with respect to
n
and
k
,
writing
G(n,k,a)=R(n,k,a)F(n,k,a)
for a rational function
R(n,k,a)
in
n
and
k
.
We also assume that the input function
F(n,k,a)
is such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}G(n,k+1,a)
vanishes (given specified bounds for
n
and
a
). With this assumption, by writing

(62)

\mathfrak{f}(n,a):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}F(n,k,a),

we find that a telescoping phenomenon
obtained from the above difference equation gives us that

(63)

p_{1}(n,a)\,\mathfrak{f}(n+1,a)+p_{2}(n,a)\,\mathfrak{f}(n,a)=-G(n,0,a).

By setting
g_{1}(n,a):=-\frac{G(n,0,a)}{p_{2}(n,a)}

and
g_{2}(n,a):=-\frac{p_{1}(n,a)}{p_{2}(n,a)}
, we proceed to rewrite the difference equation in
(
63
) so that

(64)

\mathfrak{f}(n,a)=g_{1}(n,a)+g_{2}(n,a)\,\mathfrak{f}(n+1,a).

We also suppose that
F(n,k,a)
, when viewed as bivariate function of
a
and
k
,
is hypergeometric, letting
n
be seen as a fixed parameter. We write

(65)

\mathcal{F}(n,k):=F(m,k,n)

for a parameter
m
. For the sake of clarity, we suppose that the application of
Zeilberger’s algorithm to

\mathcal{F}(n,k)
produces a first-order recurrence, but, again,
our iterative method may be applied more broadly.

Let
\mathcal{F}(n,k)
satisfy

(66)

q_{3}(n,m)\,\mathcal{F}(n+1,k)+q_{4}(n,m)\,\mathcal{F}(n,k)=\mathcal{H}(n,k+1)-\mathcal{H}(n,k)

according to Zeilberger’s algorithm, for polynomials
q_{3}(n,m)
and
q_{4}(n,m)

in
n
and for a hypergeometric function
\mathcal{H}(n,k)

satisfying
\mathcal{H}(n

k)

=

\mathcal{S}(n

k)

\mathcal{F}(n

k)

for a rational function
\mathcal{S}(n,k)
. We then rewrite (
66
) so that

q_{3}(n,m)\,F(m,k,n+1)+q_{4}(n,m)\,F(m,k,n)=\mathcal{H}(n,k+1)-\mathcal{H}(n,k),

and we write
\mathcal{H}(n,k)=\mathcal{H}(n,k,m)

and
\mathcal{S}(n,k)=\mathcal{S}(n,k,m)
.
We then write

p_{3}(n,a)=q_{3}(a,n)
and

p_{4}(n,a)=q_{4}(a,n)
and

\mathcal{G}(n,k,a)=\mathcal{H}(a,k,n)
, yielding

(67)

p_{3}(n,a)F(n,k,a+1)+p_{4}(n,a)F(n,k,a)=\mathcal{G}(n,k+1,a)-\mathcal{G}(n,k,a),

F(n,k,a)=\mathcal{R}(n,k,a)\mathcal{G}(n,k,a).

By applying the summation operator
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\cdot
to both sides,
we assume that the input function
F(n,k,a)
is such that a telescoping phenomenon

(68)

p_{3}(n,a)\,\mathfrak{f}(n,a+1)+p_{4}(n,a)\,\mathfrak{f}(n,a)=-\mathcal{G}(n,0,a),

\mathfrak{f}

62

g_{3}(n,a)=-\frac{\mathcal{G}(n,0,a)}{p_{4}(n,a)}

and
g_{4}(n,a)=-\frac{p_{3}(n,a)}{p_{4}(n,a)}

(69)

\mathfrak{f}(n,a)=g_{3}(n,a)+g_{4}(n,a)\,\mathfrak{f}(n,a+1)

Explicitly, by setting

3.3.
Organization

Formulas of Ramanujan type may be regarded as ideal in terms of the (non-constant) polynomial factor
being minimal (i.e., linear). It is only in highly exceptional cases that
series satisfying the conditions associated with (

admit closed forms, especially for irregular combinations of Pochhammer symbols,
and hence our emphasis on our new formulas of Ramanujan type.
This leads us to put a premium
on closed forms for series with polynomial factors that may be regarded as minimal,
and, hence, we restrict our attention to polynomial factors of degree not exceeding

\pi

4.2.
Unary iterations

The repeated application of the recursion in (
64
)
provides a “unary” version of our extended and iterative method.
According to the notation in (
64
)
for the bivariate function
\mathfrak{f}(n,a)
, the recurrence in
(
64
) is of iteration pattern
(1,0)
.
By writing
\mathfrak{f}(n,a)=\mathfrak{f}(n)
in this case,
we obtain a recurrence of iteration pattern
(1)
.
For a bivariate hypergeometric function
F(n,k)
that satisfies the desired conditions
from our past work concerning Wilf’s method

[
12
,
15
]

algorithm and that does not involve a parameter
\alpha
, by writing
F(n,k,\alpha):=F(n,k)

for a free parameter
\alpha
, the degenerate recursion
F(n,k,\alpha)=F(n,k,\alpha+1)

is such that a modified version of the iterative
approach in Section
3
reduces to the unary case, and hence how our iterative method extends
the past research works on the unary case
[
12
,
15
,
42
]
.
So, it would be consistent, in regard to the application of our iterative method, to further consider the unary case.
Moreover, since formulas of Ramanujan type, as defined in
Section
2
, are a main object of study in this paper,
this motivates our derivation of new formulas of this type
through the unary case.

By inputting

(96)

F(n,k):=\frac{(n-a)_{k+f}(b)_{k+e}}{(n+a)_{k+d}(2n)_{k+c}}
