From silenced to connected: measuring language-based identity expansion through digital media
Authors: Naif Ergün, Ziyattin Yıldırımçakar, Mehmet Şirin Filiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial media have become a central arena for identity construction, particularly for minority communities whose languages and cultures have been historically marginalized. Drawing on self-expansion theory, this study examines how social media facilitate language-based identity expansion within the Kurdish community in Türkiye. Despite long-standing suppression of the Kurdish language and culture, digital platforms provide new spaces for linguistic reconnection, cultural expression, and the reinforcement of collective identity. While self-expansion theory has traditionally focused on interpersonal contexts, little empirical research has examined identity expansion as a collective, language-centered process in digital environments.MethodsAn exploratory sequential mixed-methods design was employed. In the qualitative phase, semi-structured interviews with Kurdish participants informed the development of the Scale of Language-Based Identity Expansion through Digital Media (SLIEDM). In the quantitative phase, the scale was evaluated through exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). In Study 2, a cross-sectional survey tested associations between language-based identity expansion and key social-psychological variables, including group identification, life satisfaction, age, and patterns of language use on digital media.ResultsFactor analyses supported a unidimensional structure of the SLIEDM with strong psychometric properties. Quantitative findings demonstrated that engagement in one’s mother tongue on digital media was associated with language-based identity expansion and collective identification processes. Language-based identity expansion was positively associated with the use of Kurdish on digital media, in-group identification, and life satisfaction, whereas age was negatively associated with identity expansion. Group identification and native language use on social media emerged as significant predictors of identity expansion.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that social media function as a critical context for language-based identity expansion among marginalized groups. Digital engagement in one’s mother tongue supports language-based identity expansion by strengthening group identification and enhancing psychological well-being. These results extend self-expansion theory beyond interpersonal domains to collective, language-centered identity processes in digital environments. The study highlights the importance of language use in online spaces as a psychological and cultural resource for minority communities and provides a validated instrument for future research on identity expansion across diverse sociopolitical contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04669-1.

Introduction

According to We Are Social [1], 5.64 billion people globally (68.7%) use the internet, and 5.31 billion (64.7%) are active on social media platforms, figures that continue to rise annually. As societies become more embedded in digital environments, these platforms have introduced both new opportunities and challenges. Although social media was initially designed for constructive purposes, it has increasingly become a platform for fostering personal growth, accelerating information exchange, and facilitating both global and local communication.

While social media is often associated with globalization, which threatens ethnic minorities by amplifying hate speech and inciting real-world violence, as demonstrated by Facebook’s determining role in the Rohingya genocide in Myanmar [2] and its facilitation of ethnic conflict in Ethiopia through rapid, widespread hate mobilization [3], it also contributes to the rise of digital nationalism and the strengthening of in-group bonds [4]. Yet, the academic focus has largely remained on the problems digital media generate [5], while their positive contributions; such as enhancing communication, supporting language learning, and promoting cultural exchange, remain comparatively underexplored. One such underexamined benefit is the role of social media platforms in facilitating self-expansion and identity expansion, especially among disadvantaged or minority groups. This study explores how individuals express and reconstruct their self-perception and identity in digital environments and examines the constructive impacts of this engagement in everyday life. While identity formation in digital spaces has been studied in general terms, far less attention has been given to identity expansion, particularly how online expression relates to group identity and belonging.

Language-based identity expansion, as conceptualized in this study, differs from general social media use and broader notions of identity formation in digital environments. While prior research has examined how individuals present themselves or engage with identity-related content online [6–8], these approaches typically focus on expression, representation, or identity construction in a general sense. In contrast, the present study focuses specifically on the process of expansion, namely, how engagement with one’s mother tongue in digital environments contributes to the development, enrichment, and strengthening of collective identity. More specifically, the SLIEDM assesses individual experiences of identity enrichment that emerge through participation in collective ethnolinguistic digital spaces.

This distinction is important because language, particularly in minority contexts, functions not only as a medium of communication but also as a symbolic and political resource [9–11]. Accordingly, language-based identity expansion captures a more specific process than general digital participation: it reflects how individuals actively expand their sense of self through linguistic practices that are embedded in group belonging and cultural continuity. For this reason, a dedicated scale is required to measure this construct, as existing instruments do not capture the intersection of language use, digital engagement, and collective identity expansion [12, 13]. This study addresses that gap by examining the Kurdish community in Türkiye.

It was once assumed that globalization, accelerated by digital media, would lead to the erosion of ethnic, religious, and cultural identities. However, as Smith [14] argued, globalization has not diminished nationalism or group identification; rather, it has intensified their relevance. Today, we observe growing efforts to revive endangered languages and cultures [15], as well as increased in-group identification among diasporic or displaced communities via social media. Digital media platform–driven globalization, therefore, not only connects individuals across regions but also supports local language revitalization [16] and reinforces national or ethnic identity. This perspective is especially pertinent for minority groups, who often face systemic exclusion and cultural suppression. For such communities, digital media platforms provide crucial spaces to reconnect with cultural heritage, assert linguistic identity, and engage with wider networks – ultimately fostering processes of self-expansion and identity expansion.

From a socio-psychological perspective, the self and identity are understood as cognitive constructs shaped by both internal processes and external social contexts [17]. Self-expansion theory [18, 19] posits that individuals seek to broaden their horizons by acquiring new skills, perspectives, and roles. Originally developed in the context of close interpersonal relationships, recent research has extended the theory to domains such as the workplace [20], media engagement [21], branding [22], and digital interaction [12]. Initially applied to close relationships, self-expansion theory has since been extended to group contexts, demonstrating that individuals grow through group affiliation and shared experiences [23]. Group membership often requires prioritizing collective interests, leading to a blurring of individual and group boundaries and fostering a shared identity [19]. Identity, or belonging to a social group, also improves well-being [24]. If the self can expand by integrating new knowledge and experiences, then identity can similarly grow through exposure to new cultural, social, and digital stimuli. This process is especially critical for disadvantaged communities [15, 25].

Existing self-expansion models have largely emphasized individual-level growth and agency, framing expansion as the integration of external resources to enhance personal goals [20, 26]. This perspective, however, overlooks how marginalized and minority groups often construct their self-concept through collective identification, particularly in response to exclusion and discrimination [27]. In such contexts, social media emerges as a crucial arena for identity expansion, enabling individuals not only to preserve cultural heritage but also to diversify and perform it through shared narratives, linguistic practices, and symbolic representations [6, 11]. Yet, no existing measure appears to capture language-based collective identity expansion through digital media. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a novel instrument to address this gap.

Social media platforms further allow communities to bypass restrictive state structures, sustain transnational connections, and publicly articulate nationalist sentiments, thereby reinforcing in-group cohesion and visibility [28, 29]. While often associated with global homogenization, social media in practice facilitates a form of collective self-expansion in which digital globalization intensifies rather than erodes ethnic belonging and cultural visibility [7, 30]. For this reason, examining identity expansion through social media is necessary: it shifts the lens from purely individual growth to collective processes, highlighting how digital spaces function as vital sites for negotiating belonging, strengthening solidarity, and expanding minority identities in the contemporary life.

Existing research highlights that individuals derive personal self-esteem from identification with social groups, a phenomenon well captured by Jetten et al.’s [31] identity resource model, which demonstrates that membership in multiple significant groups enhances personal self-esteem, with collective self-esteem serving as the mediating mechanism. However, the psychological benefits of group membership may vary depending on whether identity processes operate primarily at personal or collective levels. Moreover, enhanced social identification has been linked to reductions in anxiety and depression symptoms, underscoring the mental health benefits of strong group belonging [32]. In digital contexts, research indicates that authentic self-expression on social media, integral to identity expansion, yields positive psychological outcomes; individuals who present themselves genuinely report significantly higher levels of life satisfaction, regardless of personality traits [8]. Similarly, a two-wave panel study found that self-expansion experiences within virtual environments predict increases in both self-esteem and life satisfaction over time [33]. Collectively, these findings suggest that language-based identity expansion through digital media, when it strengthens group identification and enables authentic self-expression, is instrumental in reinforcing self-esteem, enhancing psychological well-being, and improving perceived quality of life.

Identity expansion through digital media

The internet serves as a medium for self-expansion, through which individuals engage in online activities that contribute to personal growth, a process referred to as online self-expansion [12]. Digital media enable users to explore new identities, acquire knowledge, connect with diverse communities, and enhance their self-concept. Mobile technologies also play a role; for instance, smartphones support self-expansion through access to resources, group participation, and social engagement [13].

According to Aron and colleagues [18, 19], self-expansion occurs through two mechanisms: First, a motivational drive to broaden the self by gaining new perspectives, skills, and resources; and second, the pursuit of novel, challenging experiences that promote personal development [34]. Although digital environments facilitate both mechanisms, little empirical research has directly examined identity expansion via digital media.

Identity expansion among disadvantaged groups through digital media

Understanding the self and identity is essential for analyzing the dynamics of social inequality. Callero [35] argues that examining how disadvantaged groups construct their identities reveals mechanisms of exclusion and marginalization. Members of these groups often internalize discrimination, leading to feelings of devaluation and psychological distress, whereas, by contrast, privileged individuals tend to view discrimination as situational [36].

Social Identity Theory provides a valuable framework for understanding how identity is constructed among minority and marginalized groups. Tajfel [37] emphasized that individuals from minority backgrounds often experience tension between personal and social identities due to their marginalized status. Similarly, Christensen and Kerper [38] demonstrated that perceptions of group membership influence both in-group and out-group attitudes. Supporting identity development among disadvantaged individuals is therefore essential for addressing inequality and promoting inclusion. As Smith and Henry [39] note, the psychological self extends beyond the individual to encompass social groups, making group membership a central component of self-concept. This underscores the social environment’s role in shaping identity, particularly for disadvantaged populations.

While identity formation has traditionally been shaped by direct social interaction, digital media now provide new pathways for self-expansion. Social media content related to users’ cultural, historical, and linguistic backgrounds enables a deeper sense of identity [40]. By engaging with such content and participating online discussions, individuals can reconnect with their heritage [6]. In doing so, social media support both personal self-expansion and collective identity development, helping individuals integrate new cultural, social, and political dimensions into the self. For many marginalized communities, this digital engagement is deeply intertwined with language, which serves as a primary vehicle for expressing, preserving, and expanding cultural identity [41].

The present study

This study investigates how social media use contributes to identity expansion among disadvantaged groups, focusing on the Kurdish ethnic minority in Türkiye. Previous research on self-expansion has primarily focused on interpersonal relationships [18, 19] or impersonal contexts, including intergroup and media-based interactions [12, 13, 20–22, 26]. However, identity expansion among minority communities has received limited empirical attention. Addressing this gap, the present study explores how disadvantaged groups expand their sense of self and collective identity through digital media. While Kurdish populations are distributed across Iran, Iraq, Syria, and Türkiye, this study focuses specifically on Kurds in Türkiye, who have long faced linguistic and cultural marginalization.

Language holds vital significance for ethnic communities, so much so that the language’s name is often used to refer to the ethnic group itself [42], reflecting the deep connection between language and ethnic identity [43]. Given its role in preserving a shared identity, language maintenance and revitalization are considered central to nationalist discourse [44]. Language also serves as a key psychological bond among group members, symbolizing ethnic belonging and facilitating cultural transmission [45]. As Kymlicka and Patten [9] argue, language is not merely a means of communication but a fundamental carrier of cultural values and a primary tool for constructing identity. In the case of the Kurdish community in Türkiye, the historical suppression of Kurdish has made the use of the language an especially significant act of identity affirmation, and social media now provide an unprecedented space for revitalizing and expanding this linguistic heritage.

Kurds, like many minority groups, have historically faced linguistic repression [10, 46]. State-imposed language restrictions have contributed to what Hassanpour et al. [47] term "linguicide". The concept of silencing, as used in this study, refers not only to formal restrictions on language use through legal or institutional mechanisms. It also encompasses more subtle processes of marginalization, including the exclusion of a language from education, public discourse, and media representation. In the Kurdish context in Türkiye, such silencing has historically operated through both explicit prohibitions and implicit sociopolitical pressures that discourage the public use of Kurdish, thereby limiting its visibility and legitimacy [10, 11, 47].

In response to these constraints, digital media have increasingly functioned as counter-public spaces [48, 49], where marginalized groups can articulate identities, circulate alternative narratives, and challenge dominant linguistic hierarchies. For Kurdish users, this includes practices such as sharing content in Kurdish (despite limited standardization), producing and disseminating Kurdish-language videos, engaging in political and cultural discussions, and publicly displaying linguistic identity through hashtags, comments, and audiovisual content. These practices not only increase the visibility of Kurdish in the digital sphere but also enable users to reclaim linguistic agency and collectively resist processes of silencing. These dynamics were also reflected in our qualitative interviews, where participants described social media as one of the few spaces in which they could freely use Kurdish, express cultural belonging, and connect with co-ethnic audiences without institutional constraints.

Building on this, digital media extend these counter-public dynamics by enabling sustained linguistic revitalization and identity formation. As Hassanpour [10] argues, linguistic identity remains a core aspect of Kurdish nationalism, and digital media now play an increasingly vital role in sustaining it.

Historically, media has played a pivotal role in identity construction. Anderson [25] highlights the role of print media in promoting nationalist ideologies. Building on this, Hassanpour’s Nationalism and Language in Kurdistan (1918–1985) emphasizes how traditional media contributed to Kurdish identity construction. In recent years, visual and auditory technologies and social media have become central to this process. Sheyholislami [11] examined Kurdish-language television, while Mahmod [50] explored the role of social media in shaping diaspora identity and activism. Unlike previous studies, which focused primarily on the Kurdish diaspora, this research investigates how language preservation and identity expansion occur through digital media platforms within the national context. Although grounded in the Kurdish case, the study addresses a broader phenomenon: intragroup identity expansion, the process by which individuals deepen their ethnic, cultural, linguistic, and national identities through interaction with members of their own community in digital media platforms.

This study employed an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design. In Study 1, qualitative semi-structured interviews were first conducted with Kurdish participants in Türkiye to generate items for the Scale of Language-Based Identity Expansion through Digital Media (SLIEDM). These items were then validated through quantitative analyses, including Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). In Study 2, a cross-sectional survey was conducted to examine associations between SLIEDM scores and relevant psychological and behavioral variables, including group identification, self-esteem, life satisfaction, age, and language use patterns.

Study 1: scale development and validation

Method

Process and research design of the study

Prior to data collection, the research was carefully designed and submitted for ethical review. Approval was granted by the Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of [University Name] (No: 125338, 26/12/2023). The study consisted of two overarching studies. Study 1 involved scale development and validation and consisted of three sequential phases: a qualitative phase for item generation, followed by Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). Study 2 consisted of a subsequent cross-sectional survey examining associations between SLIEDM scores and related psychological and behavioral variables [51]. In this phase, the surveys were designed to measure language-based identity expansion through digital media, assess participants’ language use practices in both daily life and online contexts, collect demographic information, and evaluate the psychometric properties of the SLIEDM, including its factor structure, reliability, and validity. Overview of the research process and study design

The research process proceeded through the following stages reflected in Fig. 1: meeting for the research, searching the literature, preparing the research design, obtaining ethical approval, conducting qualitative interviews to create the item pool, having the item pool assessed by experts, conducting and analyzing Study 1 (EFA and CFA), conducting and analyzing Study 2, and finally reporting the study results.

1a. Qualitative phase (Interview descriptions)

In the initial phase of the study, the Scale of Language-Based Identity Expansion through Digital Media (SLIEDM) was generated through qualitative interviews with Kurdish participants in Türkiye. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 12 individuals selected through purposive and maximum variation sampling in order to ensure diversity in age, gender, educational background, and patterns of digital media use. All participants were Kurdish, resided in Türkiye, and were at least 18 years old. Participants ranged in age from 23 to 33 years (M = 26.25). Seven participants were male and five were female. Regarding educational background, four were currently pursuing or had completed undergraduate studies, seven were pursuing or had completed graduate studies, and one held a postgraduate degree. Occupationally, the sample included four students, one teacher, and seven self-employed participants. All respondents reported using at least one digital media platform, with Instagram and X being the most frequently mentioned.

The main data collection instrument in this qualitative stage was a semi-structured interview form consisting of 10 open-ended questions. The guide was developed collaboratively by the study authors, two academics specializing in Kurdish language, and one psychology academic. Questions focused on participants’ experiences of using Kurdish on digital media, as well as the emotional, cultural, and political meanings attached to such use. After an initial draft and pilot implementation, the interview guide was revised in line with feedback. Interviews were conducted in Kurdish, the preferred language of both participants and researchers, by the second and third authors between January and March 2024. Each session lasted approximately 30–40 min and took place face-to-face. With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and later transcribed in Kurdish. Participants were recruited through personal networks and digital platforms. Informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. The procedure followed an organized sequence of recruitment, consent, interview administration, audio recording, and transcription. Initial thematic analysis was carried out using the Kurdish transcripts.

The analytical process began with transcription of the recordings, followed by thematic analysis [52]. Manual coding was initially favored because the interviews were conducted in Kurdish and many software programs have limited capacity for accurately processing Kurdish-language data. Each of the three researchers independently coded the transcripts and then compared their interpretations. Through this collaborative process, descriptive codes were refined, overlapping codes were merged, and related codes were organized into broader subthemes and themes. Following the initial manual coding stage, the dataset was translated into English and imported into MAXQDA to assist in reviewing and refining the analysis. At this stage, redundant codes were removed, inconsistencies were resolved, and the hierarchical structure among themes, subthemes, and codes was reorganized. This process strengthened the credibility, dependability, and transparency of the findings. Trustworthiness was established according to Lincoln and Guba’s criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [53].

Findings from this qualitative phase were subsequently used to construct the item pool for the quantitative phase of the study.

1b. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) descriptions

Sample for Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

A total of 406 individuals initially participated in this study. According to the inclusion criteria, participants were required to be of Kurdish ethnicity, reside in Türkiye, and be at least 18 years old. One participant reported being 16 years old, two identified as Turkish, one as Arab, one as Assyrian, and one stated that they did not know their ethnicity. These six responses were therefore excluded from the analysis, and the dataset was revised accordingly. Following data cleaning, the final sample consisted of 400 participants (101 females, 296 males, 3 unknown) aged between 19 and 75 years (M = 36.88; SD = 8.91). Regarding education, 220 participants (55.0%) held a bachelor’s degree, 121 (30.3%) had postgraduate qualifications, 34 (8.5%) had a high school education or less, and 25 (6.2%) had an associate degree.

In daily life, 200 participants (50.0%) reported frequently using their mother tongue, 135 (33.8%) always, 43 (10.8%) occasionally, 19 (4.8%) rarely, and 3 (0.8%) never. Regarding use on digital platforms, 148 participants (37.0%) reported frequent use, 97 (24.3%) always, 86 (21.5%) sometimes, 62 (15.5%) rarely, and 7 (1.8%) never.

Materials

Scale of language-based identity expansion through digital media (SLIEDM)

A pool of 36 items was generated from participant interviews and evaluated by six academics: two social psychologists, one psychological counsellor, two digital behaviour specialists, and one sociologist. Three experts reviewed the Turkish version and three reviewed the Kurdish version. The evaluation occurred in two stages. First, each item was rated as suitable, unsuitable, or in need of revision, with optional comments. Second, items were assessed on four criteria: (1) language-based identity, (2) digital behaviour, (3) item clarity, and (4) identity expansion through digital media, using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never agree, 5 = Totally agree). Experts generally endorsed the items, recommending minor revisions. Mean ratings across criteria ranged from 4.17 to 4.83, indicating strong content validity.

Process and data analysis

In this phase, the preliminary item pool of the Scale of Language-Based Identity Expansion through Digital Media (SLIEDM), developed from qualitative interviews, was quantitatively evaluated. The item pool was prepared bilingually, with each Kurdish item followed by its Turkish translation to accommodate participants with limited Kurdish literacy. After expert review and revisions, quantitative data were collected online via Google Forms, including a consent form and other measures. Data were collected between May 2024 and July 2024, and the survey link was distributed via social media platforms (e.g., Instagram, X, and WhatsApp groups). The survey included the 36-item SLIEDM, demographic questions (age, gender, education), language use variables (frequency of Kurdish use in daily life and on digital media), and a control question to ensure data quality. The variables included in the surveys comprised language-based identity expansion (measured using the SLIEDM), demographic characteristics (age, gender, education), language use behaviors (frequency of Kurdish use in daily life and on digital media), and an attention check (control question) to ensure data quality. Participation was voluntary. Data quality was ensured by screening responses for eligibility criteria, completeness, and attention check performance. Participants who did not meet the inclusion criteria or failed the control question were excluded from the analysis. No substantial missing data or response patterns indicating careless responding were observed.

During the Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), all items except the first (Skewness = 1.986; Kurtosis = 2.826) showed skewness and kurtosis within ± 2; 20 items had skewness and 30 had kurtosis within ± 1. As values between –3 and + 3 are acceptable for normality [54, 55], the data were considered normally distributed. Bivariate correlations in the EFA ranged from 0.56 to 0.90.

Results

The result of exploratory factor analysis

An Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) using the maximum likelihood method initially indicated a three-factor structure across the 36 items, explaining 75.06% of the total variance. In the first round of analysis, all items had communalities above 0.40. When all 36 items were included, 35 loaded onto the first factor, one loaded only on the second factor, and six showed cross-loadings on both the first and second factors. No items loaded on the third factor because a threshold of |.30| was set as the minimum for factor loading. Although some items appeared in two factors, the remaining factors each contained more than two items. Items were distributed across factors based on two criteria [56]: (1) a factor loading of at least 0.50 to be considered strong and (2) exclusion of any item loading above 0.32 on multiple factors.

If an item’s loading exceeded 0.32 on more than one factor, it was removed, and the EFA was re-run with the remaining items. This process was repeated until the factor structure stabilized. Most removed items were those focused on individual gain. Theoretically, this finding aligns with the conceptual framework, as these excluded items emphasized self-interest and individual identity, whereas the retained items reflected social dimensions and better aligned with the construct of language-based identity expansion through digital media.

The final EFA revealed a single-factor structure consisting of 22 items. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant, χ2(231) = 11,648.893, p < 0.001, and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.975, indicating excellent sample suitability [56]. This single factor had an eigenvalue greater than 1.00 and explained 74.91% of the total variance. Factor loadings for the 22 items are provided in the supplementary materials. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.984, indicating excellent internal consistency (see the items in Additional File 1).

1c. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) descriptions

Sample for confirmatory factor analysis

A total of 371 individuals initially participated in this study. According to the inclusion criteria, participants were required to be of Kurdish ethnicity, reside in Türkiye, be at least 18 years old, and correctly answer a control question. One participant identified as Arab, one as Turkish, and 52 participants failed the control question. These participants were excluded from the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). After data cleaning, the final sample consisted of 317 participants (88 females, 228 males, 1 unknown) aged between 18 and 70 years (M = 35.26; SD = 9.75). Regarding education, most participants (168; 53.0%) were studying for or had completed a bachelor’s degree.

In daily life, 172 participants (54.2%) reported frequently using their mother tongue, 86 (27.1%) always, 39 (12.3%) occasionally, 19 (6.0%) rarely, and 1 (0.3%) never. Regarding use on digital platforms, 121 participants (38.2%) reported frequent use, 68 (21.5%) always, 67 (21.1%) sometimes, 56 (17.7%) rarely, and 5 (1.6%) never. Most participants (51.4%) reported spending 1–3 h per day on digital platforms.

Materials

Scale of language-based identity expansion through digital media (SLIEDM)

The 22-item version identified in the EFA was subjected to Confirmatory Factor Analysis in an independent sample to test the factor structure and psychometric properties of the scale.

Self-expansion questionnaire

The original 14-item scale by Lewandowski and Aron [57] was adapted to eight items rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Never, 5 = Very Much). Two bilingual academics, native in Kurdish and Turkish, translated the items, and three additional academics evaluated translation quality on a three-point scale (1 = Not Good, 2 = Should Be Changed, 3 = Good). All reviewers rated both versions as “Good.” The Self-Expansion Questionnaire was included in this phase to assess criterion validity of the newly developed scale.

Process and data analysis

Data for the CFA phase were collected between August 2024 and October 2024 using the same online data collection process as in the EFA phase. Quantitative data were collected online via Google Forms, including a consent form and study measures, and participation was voluntary. To ensure data quality, responses were screened for eligibility criteria, completeness, and attention check performance. Participants who did not meet the inclusion criteria or failed the control question were excluded from the analysis. No substantial missing data or clearly invalid response patterns were observed.

In the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), 22 items were analyzed, and only the first item (Skewness = –2.43; Kurtosis = 5.66) exceeded the ± 2 threshold. As values between –3 and + 3 are acceptable for normality [54, 55], the data were considered normally distributed. Bivariate correlations in the CFA ranged from 0.49 to 0.80, except for EILDPS18, which showed no significant correlation with other items.

Results

The results of the confirmatory factor analysis

The Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) initially showed poor model fit, with RMSEA = 0.106 and CFI = 0.90, indicating that the model was not acceptable without modification. Based on modification indices, a limited number of correlated error terms were specified (Items 2–3, 3–4, 3–6, 5–21, 15–19, and 20–21; see Fig. 2). The model demonstrated acceptable fit based on CMIN/df, CFI, TLI, and SRMR values. However, the RMSEA value remained slightly above the recommended threshold of 0.08 (RMSEA = 0.082), suggesting that the model still required some improvement. Additionally, one item (EILDPS18) had a very low regression weight (0.02) and was therefore removed from the model. After this item was excluded and further adjustments were made, the overall model fit remained acceptable χ2 (183) = 665.355, CMIN/df = 3.64, CFI = 0.940, TLI = 0.940, SRMR = 0.0288, RMSEA = 0.082. Given the gender imbalance in the sample, measurement invariance across gender was further examined using multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (MG-CFA). The results supported configural invariance (CFI = 0.940, RMSEA = 0.063) and metric invariance (CFI = 0.941, RMSEA = 0.060), with negligible changes in model fit (ΔCFI = 0.001). Additionally, constraining item intercepts did not substantially reduce model fit (CFI = 0.943, RMSEA = 0.058), providing further support for measurement invariance across gender.Fig. 2CFA path diagram of the SLIEDM

CFA path diagram of the SLIEDM

The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) and Composite Reliability (CR) were calculated to assess the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the scale construct. According to Fornell and Larcker [58], a CR value above 0.70 indicates sufficient composite (construct) reliability. Similarly, an AVE value greater than 0.50 demonstrates convergent validity [59]. In this study, the CR was 0.98 and the AVE was 0.696, both exceeding the recommended thresholds. Additionally, the CR values for all factors were higher than their corresponding AVE values, supporting convergent validity [60]. The internal reliability of the final 21 items, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.976, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Following the validation and reliability analyses of the novel scale (SLIEDM), we also assessed criterion validity by testing the correlation between the SLIEDM and the Self-Expansion Questionnaire, which was adapted for the Kurdish population in Türkiye for this research. As expected, the two scales were positively and moderately correlated (r = 0.36, p < 0.001).

Study 2: associations between the SLIEDM, satisfaction with life, self-esteem and in-group identification

Method

Sample

The study included 765 participants. Eligibility criteria required being Kurdish, residing in Türkiye, aged 18 or older, and correctly answering a control question. Four Arabs, four Turks, one Armenian, one Syriac, four minors, and 105 who failed the control question were excluded. The final sample comprised 646 participants (183 females, 460 males, 3 unspecified; M = 35.84, SD = 9.47; range = 18–71). Over half (53.7%, n = 347) held or were pursuing a bachelor’s degree. Regarding language use, 52% (n = 336) frequently spoke their mother tongue in daily life. On digital media, 37.8% reported frequent use, 22.1% always, 21.7% sometimes, 16.7% rarely, and 1.7% never.

Materials

All materials were translated into Kurdish and Turkish by two bilingual academics with English literature backgrounds. Three additional academics evaluated the translations for meaning and quality on a three-point scale (1 = Not good, 2 = Should be changed, 3 = Good). All reviewers rated the items as “3” in both languages, confirming the accuracy of the translations. The study was then conducted, and the scales’ validity and reliability were statistically verified. Results are presented below.

Rosenberg self-esteem scale (RSES)

The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale [61], adapted to Turkish by Çuhadaroğlu [62], includes 10 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (Very True–Very False), with five reverse-coded items. Higher scores indicate greater self-esteem. The Turkish version reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.77 and test–retest reliability of 0.75. In this study, CFA showed suboptimal fit even after five modifications (χ2(29) = 164.109, p < 0.001; CMIN/df = 5.66; CFI = 0.96; TLI = 0.94; SRMR = 0.059; RMSEA = 0.085). Composite Reliability (CR) and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) were used to assess construct validity [58, 59]. CR was 0.881 and AVE 0.437, indicating adequate reliability but insufficient convergent validity. However, since CR exceeded AVE, this was deemed acceptable per Yaşlıoğlu [60]. The internal reliability in this study was 0.88, indicating strong consistency.

Satisfaction with life scale (SLS)

The Satisfaction with Life Scale [63], adapted into Turkish by Durak et al. [64], includes five items rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree, 7 = Strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction. The original scale’s reliability was 0.81. In this study, CFA with one modification showed excellent fit (χ2(4) = 3.086, p > 0.05; CFI = 1.00; TLI = 1.00; SRMR = 0.007; RMSEA = 0.00). Construct reliability and validity were examined via Composite Reliability (CR) and Average Variance Extracted (AVE). CR (0.878) and AVE (0.592) exceeded recommended thresholds of 0.70 and 0.50, respectively [54, 55], and CR was higher than AVE, confirming convergent validity [60]. The internal consistency coefficient was 0.88.

In-group identification measure (GIM)

The Social Identification Scale [65] measures individuals’ identification with their social group through 14 items across five subscales on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree, 7 = Strongly agree). Subscale reliability coefficients ranged from 0.80 to 0.91. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) showed a good model fit without modification (χ2(67) = 310.403, p < 0.001; CFI = 0.979; TLI = 0.971; SRMR = 0.027; RMSEA = 0.075). Construct reliability and validity were assessed using Composite Reliability (CR) and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) [58, 59]. CR values (0.85–0.98) and AVE values (0.70–0.94) met recommended thresholds, and all CR values exceeded AVE values, supporting convergent validity [60]. The total scale’s reliability was 0.96, with subscale alphas between 0.83 and 0.98.

Process and data analysis

Data were collected using Google Forms, which was selected as a practical and accessible platform compatible with both mobile and desktop devices. It also facilitated participation among geographically dispersed respondents, supported bilingual survey presentation, and enabled the secure export of structured response data for statistical analysis. The platform also allowed the use of required-response settings and attention-check items to support data quality. The survey link was distributed on social media, and participation was voluntary. Correlation and multiple linear regression analyses were performed. The data showed normal distribution, with kurtosis and skewness values ranging between − 0.891 and 2.55, all within the acceptable range of − 3 to + 3 [54, 55]. Regression assumptions regarding sample size, outliers, normality, linearity, homoscedasticity, multicollinearity, and independence of errors were checked [66]. No outliers were found, and the sample size of 646 satisfied the criterion n = 50 + 8m [67]. Residual plots confirmed normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity. Multicollinearity was not an issue, as correlations were below 0.80, VIF values ranged from 1.01 to 1.48, and TOL values from 0.68 to 0.99 [68]. The Durbin–Watson statistic of 1.99 indicated independence of errors [68].

Discussion

This study aimed to (1) develop a novel scale for identity expansion through social media based on interview data, and (2) to examine relationships between this new scale and group identification, self-esteem and life satisfaction using an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design. The findings of Study 1 demonstrated that a valid and reliable measure, the Scale of Language-Based Identity Expansion through Digital Media (SLIEDM), was successfully developed based on interviews with participants who used Kurdish on social media. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) further examined the factor structure, model fit, and psychometric properties of the SLIEDM. The results indicated that the revised model achieved acceptable fit standards [69, 70], confirming the scale’s validity and reliability.

While social media is often associated with global connectivity, minority groups use these platforms to strengthen transnational ties with dispersed co-ethnic communities [28]. Moreover, social media enables users to circumvent state-imposed restrictions, promoting ethnic identification and public expression of identity and nationalist sentiment [29]. From this perspective, digital globalization may not erode but rather intensify ethnic identity as individuals reaffirm their national or cultural heritage [30]. Interviews with Kurdish participants supported this view, showing that Kurdish participants actively use social media to increase the visibility of the Kurdish language, a language still subject to legal and political constraints in Türkiye, thus transforming digital spaces into platforms for in-group identification and the expansion of linguistic and cultural presence.

Existing self-expansion scales primarily focus on individual-level goals and personal growth by incorporating external resources. These models conceptualize self-expansion as a process of enhancing individual agency. However, members of minority groups often define their self-concept through collective identification, particularly in response to exclusion and discrimination [27]. In such contexts, social media serves as a medium to reinforce group identity [7]. In line with this, our study with Kurdish participants in Türkiye, a marginalized ethnic group, found that social media is primarily used to express Kurdish identity and foster connections with co-ethnics. Kurdish users employ digital media not only to preserve their language but also to diversify it. This aligns with Sheyholislami's [11] findings, where satellite television bridged dialectal differences, and the Internet was used to express regional and orthographic variations.

Kurdish identity is predominantly articulated through language, with identity constructed on an ethnolinguistic foundation [11]. This connection heightens sensitivity to the Kurdish language, often turning linguistic expression into an act of cultural and political affirmation. As a result, Kurdish becomes central to digital expression, where users articulate political views, religious beliefs, and everyday practices, reinforcing group identification [11]. Our findings indicate that participants strongly associate nationalism with language, prompting the development of the SLIEDM scale. Many Kurds in Türkiye lack formal education in Kurdish, leading to varying literacy levels [71]. Kurdish-literate users engage with social media through written Kurdish, while others rely on audio-visual content. To ensure accessibility, we presented the scale items in both Kurdish and Turkish.

Previous studies have explored the relationship between social media, technology, and self-expansion, developing scales to measure this construct [13, 72–74]. These scales primarily focus on digital engagement’s impact on individual self-expansion. In contrast, our study conceptualizes self-expansion through social integration, specifically identity expansion rooted in group belonging. We examined language-based identity expansion and found a significant relationship between group identification and this form of self-expansion. Our results indicate that group identification significantly predicts language-based identity expansion. These findings align with existing literature, such as Brünker et al. [6], who showed that users construct and perform collective identity on social media through shared narratives, hashtags, and other symbolic practices that enhance in-group cohesion and visibility. Similarly, Velasquez and Montgomery [7] demonstrated that strong group identification among Latino users in the U.S. positively influenced political expression on social media, highlighting the interplay between identity and digital self-representation.

In Study 2, our goal was not to generate entirely new findings, but rather to examine the relationships between the newly developed scale and established psychological constructs. In doing so, it also tested our central claim by demonstrating whether language-based identity expansion is indeed associated with group identification. The findings indicated that the SLIEDM was significantly positively correlated with group identification, life satisfaction, and the use of native language on digital media and in social life, and significantly negatively correlated with age.

One of the most notable findings of this study is the absence of a significant relationship between language-based identity expansion (SLIEDM) and self-esteem. At first glance, this result may appear to diverge from theoretical expectations, as both Self-Expansion Theory [18, 19] and the Identity Resource Model [31] suggest that meaningful social ties and identity-based affiliations may enhance the self. However, this finding may be better understood through the distinction between personal and collective levels of identity. Brewer and Gardner [75] proposed that the self can be organized at personal, relational, and collective levels, with different psychological processes operating across each domain. Similarly, research on collective self-esteem has shown that evaluations of one’s social group are conceptually related to, yet distinct from, global personal self-esteem [76–78]. While self-esteem measures such as Rosenberg’s scale primarily assess personal self-evaluation, the SLIEDM scale was developed to capture identity expansion through ethnic-linguistic belonging and participation in digital spaces. Accordingly, it may reflect collective identity enrichment, group-based affirmation, or a stronger sense of shared belonging rather than global personal self-worth.

This interpretation is supported by the positive association observed between SLIEDM and ingroup identification, indicating that stronger attachment to one’s ethnic group corresponds with greater language-based identity expansion. This is consistent with Social Identity Theory, which posits that part of the self-concept derives from meaningful group memberships [79].

At the same time, the lack of association between self-esteem and ingroup identification is not inconsistent with prior research. The self-esteem hypothesis within social identity research has received mixed support, with stronger group identification not always corresponding to higher personal self-esteem [80, 81]. Luhtanen and Crocker [76] likewise distinguished personal self-esteem from collective self-esteem, suggesting that positive evaluations of one’s group may operate separately from global self-regard.

This pattern may be especially relevant in marginalized or minority group contexts. For members of socially devalued groups, ingroup identification may function more as a source of solidarity, resilience, and cultural continuity than as a direct determinant of personal self-worth [82, 83]. Thus, language-based identity expansion may strengthen collective belonging without necessarily increasing individual self-esteem.

Overall, these findings suggest that language-based identity expansion may operate more strongly through collective identity processes than through personal self-worth. The SLIEDM was also associated with life satisfaction, age, and native language use. Notably, the link between native language use and identity expansion appears to be a novel finding. Future research should examine whether language use should be considered a key variable in identity expansion research, particularly in cultural or minority group contexts, and whether SLIEDM is more closely associated with collective self-esteem, group-based well-being, communal empowerment, or other multidimensional indicators of psychological adjustment than with global self-esteem measures alone.

Limitations and future implications

This study offers significant theoretical and methodological contributions but also highlights areas for further development. Although it investigates identity expansion through language within the Kurdish community, none of the scale items explicitly mention Kurdish. Instead, the Scale of Language-Based Identity Expansion through Digital Media (SLIEDM) centers on the use of one’s mother tongue, making it adaptable to other minority or linguistic groups, with Kurds serving as the initial case. The scale’s broader application was limited by linguistic and geographic diversity among Kurds [84]. Variations in dialects, alphabets, and national contexts [84] made it difficult to include all Kurdish subgroups. Consequently, this research focused on Kurds in Türkiye, while future studies could extend the investigation to Kurdish populations in other countries, adapting the scale as necessary.

The sample is notably imbalanced in terms of gender, with a substantially higher proportion of male participants. This imbalance may reflect broader gendered patterns in digital access and participation, particularly in contexts where sociocultural norms and structural inequalities shape online engagement. As a result, the findings may disproportionately reflect the experiences of male users, which limits generalizability across genders. However, multi-group confirmatory factor analysis indicated that the measurement model was invariant across gender at the configural and metric levels, suggesting that the scale operates similarly across male and female participants despite the imbalance in sample composition.

The study also relies on participants who are active in digital environments, which introduces a potential digital divide bias. Individuals with greater access to technology, higher digital literacy, and more frequent social media use are more likely to be represented in the sample. This may underrepresent groups such as older individuals or those with limited access to digital resources.

Although the scale is framed in terms of “mother tongue” use, the present validation is specific to the Kurdish–Turkish context. Linguistic, sociopolitical, and cultural dynamics unique to Kurds in Türkiye may shape both digital practices and identity processes in ways that are not directly transferable to other minority groups. Therefore, caution is needed when generalizing these findings beyond this context.

Measurement limitations should also be acknowledged. Although the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale demonstrated acceptable reliability, its convergent validity fell below recommended thresholds (AVE = 0.437), which may have weakened the observed relationship.

The mixed-method design employed here strengthens the study by offering a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon. However, future research could explore long-term or causal effects through longitudinal or experimental designs. The results also indicate that language-based identity expansion may intersect with other symbolic elements, such as regional dress or national symbols. Therefore, if a new scale on this topic is to be developed, it should consider ethnic or group symbols beyond language.

Although this study developed a scale and examined its associations with selected psychological variables, the phenomenon of identity expansion through digital media warrants further investigation with a broader range of constructs. Future research should specifically explore how digital identity expansion relates to levels of depression, anxiety, and stress, as well as to individuals’ sense of belonging and their adaptation to the societies in which they live. In particular, examining the impact of digital identity expansion on the Kurdish population, both within Türkiye and across other areas such as Iraq, Iran, Syria, and European countries, would provide valuable insights into how minority groups negotiate identity, well-being, and social integration in diverse sociopolitical environments.
