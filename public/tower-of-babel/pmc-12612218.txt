Clinical and genetic predictors of persistent chemotherapy-induced alopecia despite scalp cooling in breast cancer
Authors: Haseen Lee, Nayeon Kim, Sooyeon Kim, Ji-Yeon Kim, Yeon Hee Park, Hee-Kyung Ahn, Juhee Cho, Danbee Kang, Jin Seok Ahn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractScalp cooling is commonly used to reduce chemotherapy-induced alopecia (CIA) and has been suggested to aid hair regrowth after treatment. However, a subset of patients still experience persistent CIA (PCIA) despite undergoing scalp cooling. In this post hoc analysis of two prospective trials, we investigated clinical and genetic factors associated with PCIA among 123 women with stage I–III breast cancer treated with anthracycline- and/or taxane-based chemotherapy and scalp cooling. PCIA, defined as incomplete hair regrowth at 6 months post-chemotherapy, was observed in 12% of patients. Tamoxifen monotherapy was identified as a strong independent risk factor (adjusted odds ratio, 11.66; 95% CI, 1.87–120.20), while variants in chr20p11 and HLA-DQB1 showed non-significant trends. Hair thickness remained significantly lower in the tamoxifen monotherapy. These findings suggest that tamoxifen may impair follicular recovery after chemotherapy, highlighting the importance of individualized counseling and close dermatologic follow-up in patients undergoing scalp cooling.Subject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced alopecia (CIA) is one of the well-documented and distressing side effects of cancer treatment1,2. Although CIA is often regarded as a temporary condition, up to 30–40% of patients with breast cancer experience persistent chemotherapy-induced alopecia (PCIA), in which hair regrowth remains incomplete or absent even 6 months after the end of chemotherapy3. PCIA is associated with significant and long-lasting psychosocial distress, often greater than that caused by temporary hair loss during treatment4.

Scalp cooling has emerged as an effective intervention for reducing CIA5. Recent evidence suggests that it plays a protective role by facilitating hair regrowth after treatment6–8. A randomized controlled trial9 showed that scalp cooling reduced the incidence of PCIA from 69% to 11%, indicating its possible role in preserving follicular integrity during chemotherapy and supporting the transition back to the hair growth phase. Consequently, clinical expectations have advanced from simply minimizing hair loss to promoting hair regrowth after chemotherapy6–9.

Despite its growing use and high expectations, a substantial proportion of patients still experience PCIA, even after receiving scalp cooling9,10. For these individuals, disappointment can be profound, especially if it is assumed that scalp cooling guarantees full recovery. Importantly, the mechanisms underlying regrowth failure in this group may differ from those in patients who did not receive scalp cooling. However, these mechanisms and the factors associated with PCIA in scalp-cooled patients have not been systematically studied and remain poorly understood. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a pooled analysis of patients with breast cancer who received scalp cooling during chemotherapy in two clinical trials. Our objective was to identify the clinical, treatment-related, and genetic factors associated with PCIA despite scalp cooling.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

A total of 123 patients who received scalp cooling during chemotherapy were included in the analysis. The mean age was 45.6 years. The most common chemotherapy regimen was TAC (n = 40), followed by TCHP (n = 34). During chemotherapy, 58.5% of patients overall maintained ≥75% of baseline hair thickness (No CIA). The incidence of CIA varied by regimen, with anthracycline-based regimens (e.g., AC, TAC, AC → T) showing higher CIA rates than non-anthracycline regimens (e.g., TC, TCHP) (Supplementary Table 1). Six months post-chemotherapy, 15 patients (12%) met the criteria for PCIA.

Factors associated with PCIA after scalp cooling

The univariable odds ratio (OR) for tamoxifen monotherapy group was 8.08 (95% CI: 1.69–59.41), compared to patients who did not receive endocrine therapy. Aromatase inhibitors monotherapy (OR 2.05; 95% CI: 0.31–16.54) and tamoxifen with LHRH agonists (OR 1.25; 95% CI: 0.14–11.05) also showed a trend toward increased risk of PCIA, but these associations were not significant. Other factors such as older age, receipt of AC, TAC or AC → T which is doxorubicin-containing chemotherapy (OR 1.55; 95% CI: 0.49–5.91), and the presence of the chr20p11 variant (OR 1.95; 95% CI: 0.49–6.48) or HLA-DQB1 variant (OR 1.45; 95% CI: 0.49–4.57) similarly showed numerically increased odds of PCIA without reaching statistical significance (Table 2). Maintenance of <75% hair thickness during chemotherapy was also not significantly associated with the subsequent development of PCIA. In multivariable logistic regression, adjuvant tamoxifen monotherapy remained independently associated with a significantly increased risk of PCIA, with an adjusted OR of 11.66 (95% CI: 1.87–120.20) (Table 2).

Hair thickness and density trajectories by type of endocrine therapy

Six months after chemotherapy, there were no significant differences in hair thickness among the endocrine therapy groups except for the tamoxifen monotherapy group (Fig. 1). In this group, hair thickness remained reduced from baseline, with an estimated mean change of −6.2 µm (95% CI: −15.1–2.7), whereas patients who did not receive endocrine therapy showed partial recovery (+5.5 µm; 95% CI: −0.5–11.5). Compared with the no endocrine therapy group, the tamoxifen monotherapy group had a significantly lower hair thickness at 6 months, with an adjusted between-group difference of −11.7 µm (95% CI: −20.7 to −2.8). Patients receiving tamoxifen with LHRH agonists (−3.5 µm; 95% CI −12.2–5.3), aromatase inhibitor monotherapy (−0.9 µm; 95% CI −9.7–8.0), and aromatase inhibitors with LHRH agonists (−3.4 µm; 95% CI −13.3–7.1) also showed lower recovery of hair thickness compared with those receiving no endocrine therapy, although these differences were not statistically significant. In terms of hair density, no significant differences were observed between baseline and 6 months post-chemotherapy across endocrine therapy groups, including tamoxifen monotherapy (0.3 hairs/cm2; 95% CI −15.5–6.0), tamoxifen with LHRH agonists (2.3 hairs/cm2; 95% CI −13.0–17.6), aromatase inhibitor monotherapy (−3.1 hairs/cm2; 95% CI −18.7–12.4), and aromatase inhibitors with LHRH agonists (−1.4 hairs/cm2; 95% CI −19.1–16.4; Table 3).

Discussion

In our post hoc analysis, 12% of the patients developed PCIA 6 months after receiving chemotherapy despite the use of scalp cooling during treatment9,10. Notably, the degree of hair retention during chemotherapy is not predictive of PCIA9, suggesting that long-term follicular recovery is governed by distinct biological factors. Among these, genetic variants, such as chr20p11 and HLA-DQB1, were only modestly associated with PCIA. In contrast, tamoxifen therapy was the most prominent risk factor that was independently associated with a significantly increased risk of PCIA and a sustained reduction in hair thickness.

Although the incidence of PCIA was lower in our scalp cooling cohort than in the non-cooling historical dataset, the finding that 12% of patients experienced persistent hair loss is still clinically significant9,10. To prevent disappointment and encourage proactive coping strategies, especially among high-risk individuals, it is crucial to educate patients about this risk. Importantly, the lack of an association between hair retention during chemotherapy and the subsequent development of PCIA suggests that the efficacy of scalp cooling in preventing acute CIA does not necessarily translate into successful long-term regrowth. This distinction reinforces that PCIA, despite scalp cooling reflects additional biological processes that affect follicular recovery11. Identifying predictors of PCIA despite scalp cooling would allow for more personalized treatment counseling, refine patient selection, and guide complementary strategies.

Our analysis suggests that an underlying genetic predisposition may influence the risk of PCIA, even in the presence of scalp cooling. Although variants in chr20p11 and HLA-DQB1 had modest effect sizes, consistent with prior research linking these loci to androgenic alopecia and alopecia areata, respectively12, they were not significant predictors. These variants may contribute to altered follicular cycling, miniaturization, or immune-mediated disruption of follicular recovery13. However, their effects in our study were small and not statistically significant. Further studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are needed to clarify whether genetic predisposition meaningfully influences long-term follicular recovery. Certain predispositions may eventually help counsel patients about residual risk, even with scalp cooling if validated in future studies.

In our study, tamoxifen therapy was the most significant independent risk factor for PCIA. This association is supported by the pharmacological action of tamoxifen as a selective estrogen receptor modulator2,14. Scalp hair follicles express both estrogen receptor alpha (ER-α) and beta (ER-β), and estrogen signaling is essential for maintaining the anagen (growth) phase of the hair cycle2,15. Tamoxifen directly antagonizes these receptors, potentially disrupting the transition back to the anagen phase following chemotherapy and contributing to sustained hair thinning2,16,17. In contrast, aromatase inhibitors reduce systemic estrogen levels but do not directly interfere with receptor signaling at the follicular level2,18,19. Similarly, although LHRH agonists induce deeper systemic estrogen suppression, they act centrally by downregulating the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis and appear to exert minimal direct effects on hair follicles20,21. Moreover, central suppression by LHRH agonists may induce compensatory feedback mechanisms that mitigate follicular stress or modulate the local effects of tamoxifen22. These pharmacodynamic differences may explain why tamoxifen monotherapy was more strongly associated with PCIA than its combination with LHRH agonists. The specificity of tamoxifen action on hair follicles underscores the need for additional mechanistic research. Future studies comparing predictors of PCIA in cooled versus non-cooled patients may further clarify whether scalp cooling modifies the influence of endocrine therapy. In clinical practice, patients initiating tamoxifen treatment, particularly those concerned about post-treatment alopecia, may benefit from dermatological monitoring, anticipatory counseling, and early intervention to promote hair regrowth and reduce long-term psychosocial distress2,23.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively modest, particularly for subgroup analyses stratified by endocrine therapy type or genetic profile, which may have limited the statistical power to detect smaller associations. Second, although prescribed endocrine therapy was maintained without switching during the 6-month observation period, actual medication adherence outside of outpatient visits could not be fully verified. This potential variability in adherence may have influenced follicular recovery but was not captured in our dataset. In fact, adherence rates during the first year often exceed 80%, with significantly higher discontinuation observed only after several years of therapy24. Third, our study was conducted at a single center with a homogeneous population. Given known differences in hair structure and density across ethnicities, findings may not generalize to other populations25,26. Thus, the generalizability of our findings to more diverse racial and ethnic groups require further validation.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that PCIA occurred in a notable proportion of patients despite scalp cooling, with 12% affected 6 months post-chemotherapy. Genetic variants such as chr20p11 and HLA-DQB1 were not statistically significant predictors of PCIA, although they may warrant further study in larger and more diverse cohorts. The most prominent and clinically actionable factor was tamoxifen monotherapy, which was independently associated with persistent alopecia and sustained hair thinning. These findings highlight the importance of counseling patients about residual risk, even with scalp cooling, and suggest future opportunities to refine supportive strategies based on individual risk profiles.

Methods

Study design and population

We performed a post hoc analysis of participants enrolled in two clinical trials evaluating the effectiveness of scalp cooling among women with breast cancer undergoing chemotherapy at Samsung Medical Center, Seoul, Korea9,10. The present analysis focused exclusively on patients who received scalp cooling to identify factors associated with PCIA despite intervention.

Eligible patients were women aged 20–69 years with newly diagnosed stage I–III breast cancer receiving neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy that included anthracycline-containing regimens (e.g., doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide [AC], docetaxel/doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide [TAC], or AC followed by docetaxel [AC → T]). Patients were excluded if they had other active malignancies, preexisting alopecia or scalp disorders, prior chemotherapy exposure, or comorbid conditions that could affect hair growth. All the participants underwent scalp cooling using the Paxman Scalp Cooling System 2 (Paxman Coolers, Huddersfield, UK)9,10.

This study followed the STROBE guideline (Supplementary Table 2). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center (IRB No. 2019-12-148). All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Scalp cooling procedure

Scalp cooling was performed 30 min before and continued for 90 min after each chemotherapy session. The cooling cap circulated the liquid coolant at a constant temperature. The patients were allowed short breaks to enhance their comfort. Analgesics were administered to treat cold-related discomfort. Hair care kits were used to standardize home management during the study period.

Outcome definitions

The primary outcome was PCIA, which was defined as absent or incomplete hair regrowth 6 months after chemotherapy3,9,10. PCIA was diagnosed when either hair density or hair thickness at 6 months fell more than two standard deviations below the individual’s baseline measurement using Folliscope 5.0 (Lead M, Seoul, Korea). Hair thickness was measured using a 60× lens, and hair density was measured using a 15× lens at the midline of the scalp vertex9,10. Participants were instructed not to shave their heads during follow-up.

Risk factor definitions

Information on patient demographics (age, marital status, education level, and employment status), clinical characteristics (menopausal status, type of surgery, chemotherapy regimen, sequence of chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, radiation therapy, and comorbidities), and behavioral factors (smoking and alcohol consumption) were collected via structured questionnaires and medical records. Information on alcohol consumption and family history of alopecia was obtained using a standard questionnaire.

Alopecia-related genetic variants and hair structure genes were identified for the risk factor analysis. Genotyping was performed to assess the presence of specific variants associated with alopecia and hair regrowth. Genetic markers tested included chr20p11 (androgenic alopecia-associated), IL2RA, HLA-DQB1 (alopecia areata-associated), and EDAR (a gene associated with hair thickness). DNA was extracted using a buccal swab collection kit. Patients were instructed to rub the inside of their cheek approximately seven times. Genetic testing was performed once during one of the scheduled visits (visit 1 to visit 6 or visit 8). The samples were processed by the Theragen Etex Bio Institute, where DNA extraction was followed by quantification of DNA concentration and purity. Genotyping was performed using qPCR- or microarray-based platforms. Based on the genotyping results, polygenic risk scores were calculated using genome-wide association study-derived effect sizes to estimate genetic predisposition for hair loss. All genetic data were anonymized, and individual identifiers were removed to ensure confidentiality in accordance with the institutional and certification standards for direct-to-consumer genetic testing.

Statistical analysis

We first compared the baseline characteristics between patients with and without PCIA using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables, as appropriate.

To identify the independent predictors of PCIA, we performed multivariate logistic regression analysis adjusted for age, chemotherapy regimen (AC, TAC or AC → T vs. TC or TCH), endocrine therapy type (none, tamoxifen monotherapy, tamoxifen + LHRH agonist, aromatase inhibitor monotherapy, or aromatase inhibitor + LHRH agonist), hair changes during chemotherapy, and relevant genetic markers (including chr20p11 and HLA-DQB1). Endocrine therapy exposure was carefully reviewed, and no regimen switching occurred during the 6-month observation period. All the variables were analyzed as complete cases, and no imputation was performed for missing covariates.

To assess longitudinal changes in hair thickness and density, we used mixed-effects linear models with repeated measures across four time points: baseline (pre-chemotherapy), during chemotherapy, 1 month post-chemotherapy, and 6 months post-chemotherapy. Outcome variables included continuous measures of hair caliber (µm) and hair density (hairs/cm2). Fixed effects included visit time, potential risk factors (e.g., endocrine therapy type, age, chemotherapy type, and genetic factors), while random intercepts at the patient level (enrollment number) accounted for intraindividual correlation. Analyses were conducted using complete-case data; missing values in hair change metrics were handled through filtering or imputation when required for trajectory modeling. Interaction terms between visit time and each potential risk factor were specified to estimate trajectory differences across subgroups. Time-point-specific contrasts and difference-in-differences were tested, and estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals using Wald-based methods.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.
