An integrated hybrid HAZOP and fuzzy analytic hierarchy process framework for enhanced safety risk prioritization in process industries
Authors: Ahmad Soltanzadeh, Mohsen Mahdinia, Mohsen Sadeghi-Yarandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Safety risk assessment is crucial for managing hazards in chemical process industries. This study aimed to develop and validate a semi-quantitative risk assessment method, termed HAZOP+, tailored for these industries. The research was conducted in 2025 using a multi-phase approach. A two-stage Delphi survey involving 41 domain experts was carried out to identify and validate relevant risk factors. Subsequently, the Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP) was employed to establish hierarchical relationships among the factors and assign relative weights. The proposed HAZOP+ model was validated through a reality check, a field study in a large-scale chemical process industry, and an independent peer review. Severity factors (weight 0.602) influenced overall risk more than probability factors (0.337). Key severity sub-factors were human injury (0.199) and operational interruption (0.191); key probability sub-factors were reliability (0.190) and redundancy (0.179). The model showed strong content validity (CVR = 0.805, CVI = 0.927) and reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88). In the case study, HAZOP+ provided more precise risk estimations than traditional HAZOP, especially for high-risk scenarios. The HAZOP+ method effectively enhances traditional HAZOP from a qualitative risk identification tool into a semi-quantitative risk assessment and ranking framework. By integrating expert consensus through the Delphi method, hierarchical weighting using fuzzy AHP, and practical validation, it addresses the subjectivity and qualitative limitations of conventional approaches. This technique enables safety professionals to more effectively identify, evaluate, and prioritize risks, ultimately enhancing operational safety and optimizing resource allocation.

Introduction

Process industries are vital sectors that transform raw materials into finished products through a series of chemical, physical, or biological processes. These industries encompass oil and gas, petrochemical, pharmaceutical, chemical, and food processing plants, among others. The operation of process industries often involves the handling of hazardous chemicals under extreme conditions, such as high pressure and elevated temperatures, which significantly heighten the inherent risks. The continuous or batch nature of these processes necessitates precise control and monitoring to ensure safe operations1–5.

Process industries are increasingly adopting large, complex, and automated systems to meet the growing demands for product quality and production efficiency. However, uncontrolled consequences such as production losses, equipment damage, human injuries, and environmental issues can arise when faults occur in process plants. Consequently, the reliability and safety of process systems in these industries have garnered significant attention4,6–8.

Chemical disasters have left devastating marks throughout history, resulting in immense human suffering and environmental damage. Incidents such as the Bhopal gas tragedy (1984), the Chernobyl nuclear disaster (1986), and the Toulouse chemical factory explosion (2001) serve as grim reminders of the catastrophic consequences of industrial accidents. These events led to thousands of deaths, long-term health complications, and significant ecological harm. Studying these disasters is essential for enhancing safety protocols and preventing future tragedies in the chemical and nuclear industries4,9.

Due to the complexity and scale of operations, process industries are highly susceptible to various hazards, including fires, explosions, toxic releases, and environmental contamination. Equipment failures, human errors, and deviations from standard operating procedures can exacerbate these risks and potentially lead to severe accidents with devastating consequences for human life, the environment, and the economy. Therefore, understanding the hazardous nature of process industries and implementing robust risk management strategies are essential for safeguarding personnel, assets, and the environment7,10.

Risk assessment is a fundamental component of occupational health and safety management, aimed at identifying, analyzing, and evaluating potential hazards that may cause harm in the workplace (ISO 31000, 2018). The primary objective is to reduce the likelihood and severity of accidents by implementing effective control measures. The risk management process typically involves systematic steps, including hazard identification (HAZID), risk analysis, risk evaluation, and risk control11–13.

A recent study revealed that four of the top 20 most catastrophic incidents in history occurred within the past two years, two of which took place in the United States. Refineries accounted for 39% of the 100 largest financial losses, followed by petrochemical plants at 26% and upstream operations at 24%. The minimum financial threshold for inclusion in the report was set at $175 million per incident. Notably, the report emphasizes a declining risk tolerance at refineries, particularly those over 30 years old, which face significantly higher probabilities of loss. This underscores the necessity of employing dynamic and accurate risk assessment tools tailored to the unique characteristics of process industries to enhance safety in these accident-prone sectors through proactive planning measures14.

Effective risk assessment is crucial in process industries because of the significant potential for hazardous events that can jeopardize human safety, environmental integrity, and economic assets3,7. Various process hazard analysis (PHA) methodologies have been developed, including HAZOP, what-if analysis (What-If Checklist), failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA), fault tree analysis (FTA), event tree analysis (ETA), consequence modeling analysis, and bow-tie analysis15.

Among the various risk assessment techniques, the Hazard and Operability Study (HAZOP) is recognized as one of the most systematic and widely utilized methods for identifying potential deviations and hazards in complex industrial processes3,16–18. HAZOP is a structured, team-based qualitative technique that examines process parameters such as flow, pressure, temperature, and composition to identify potential deviations from the design intent that could result in unsafe conditions. By employing guide words and prompts, HAZOP enables a thorough evaluation of process design and operation, revealing hidden risks that may not be apparent through conventional analysis15–17,19,20.

HAZOP employs a structured, team-based approach that divides the process into discrete nodes and utilizes predefined guide words to systematically explore potential deviations from the design intent, such as variations in flow, pressure, temperature, and composition. This method facilitates the identification of hidden risks and operability issues that may not be evident through conventional engineering analyses. The strength of HAZOP lies in its multidisciplinary teamwork, which harnesses diverse expertise to uncover complex hazard scenarios16–18,20–22.

The significance of HAZOP in process industries is highlighted by its demonstrated effectiveness in preventing incidents and enhancing operational safety. HAZOP facilitates the early detection of potential failure modes and operational weaknesses, enabling the timely implementation of corrective actions before accidents occur16,21,22. However, despite its advantages, traditional HAZOP has limitations in quantitatively prioritizing risks. This shortcoming has prompted the integration of multi-criteria decision-making techniques, such as Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP), to improve its decision-support capabilities.

Research gap

While the traditional HAZOP method is widely recognized for its systematic approach to hazard identification and operability analysis in process industries17, it has several limitations. Primarily, HAZOP is predominantly qualitative and relies heavily on expert judgment, which can introduce subjectivity and inconsistency in risk evaluation. Furthermore, traditional HAZOP lacks a robust quantitative framework to prioritize identified risks based on multiple, often conflicting criteria such as likelihood, severity, and detectability. This shortcoming can hinder effective decision-making for risk mitigation, particularly in complex industrial environments where numerous factors influence risk levels.

Traditional HAZOP (per IEC 61882) focuses on qualitative deviation analysis for hazard identification. We have clarified that HAZOP+ builds on this foundation by transforming it into a semi-quantitative tool without replacing its identification role. To highlight its added value, we now explicitly compare HAZOP+ with HAZOP+LOPA. While LOPA is effective for quantifying safeguards in high-risk scenarios23, HAZOP+ emphasizes multi-criteria weighting of risk factors (e.g., severity and probability sub-factors) using FAHP, which better manages uncertainty in expert-driven environments. Unlike sequential applications, HAZOP+ integrates the Delphi method to achieve consensus on risk factors during HAZOP node analysis, followed by FAHP weighting applied selectively to prioritized deviations (not every deviation, to maintain efficiency).

To address these challenges, the present study proposes a semi-quantitative extension of HAZOP method called HAZOP+, which integrates the strengths of traditional HAZOP with a multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) method—specifically, FAHP. This combined method enhances the risk assessment process by providing a structured, semi-quantitative framework that enables more accurate and objective prioritization of risks.

Main objectives of the study

The primary aim of this study is to develop an enhanced risk assessment methodology referred to as HAZOP+ that integrates the traditional HAZOP technique with FAHP. This integration seeks to address the limitations of existing approaches in process industries. The specific objectives include:

Comprehensive Review: Conduct an extensive literature review of current risk assessment methods and the conventional HAZOP technique, identifying gaps and areas for improvement.

Method Development: The objective is to design an innovative HAZOP+ approach that integrates both quantitative and semi-quantitative elements, thereby improving risk prioritization and the accuracy of decision-making.

Expert Validation: The objective is to develop and validate the HAZOP+ methodology by incorporating expert feedback through the Delphi technique, thereby ensuring its reliability and applicability in real-world industrial contexts.

Multi-Criteria Integration: This approach employs FAHP to weight and prioritize risk factors by considering various criteria, including probability, severity, human factors, and technical aspects.

Practical Application: This study aims to demonstrate the effectiveness and applicability of the HAZOP+ method in enhancing safety risk management within process industries, ultimately contributing to safer and more resilient industrial operations.

Literature review

Despite HAZOP widespread acceptance and proven effectiveness, traditional HAZOP exhibits several limitations. Primarily, it relies heavily on qualitative expert judgment and consensus, which can introduce subjectivity and variability in hazard identification and risk ranking24. Furthermore, HAZOP lacks a quantitative or semi-quantitative framework for risk prioritization, making it challenging to effectively allocate resources and prioritize mitigation actions in complex industrial environments. The absence of explicit weighting for multiple risk criteria diminishes the transparency and reproducibility of decision-making processes. unlike fuzzy-TOPSIS25 which ranks hazards post-HAZOP, HAZOP+ integrates weighting during analysis for real-time prioritization.

To address these limitations, researchers have proposed integrating HAZOP with MCDM techniques that offer structured, quantitative support for risk evaluation. The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), developed by Saaty (1980), is a widely utilized MCDM method that decomposes complex decisions into hierarchical criteria, facilitating pairwise comparisons and the calculation of weights to prioritize alternatives26. However, classical AHP assumes precise inputs, which may not adequately capture the inherent uncertainty and vagueness in expert judgments concerning risk factors.

FAHP extends AHP by integrating fuzzy logic theory, enabling decision-makers to articulate preferences using linguistic terms represented by fuzzy numbers. This methodology enhances the robustness of the weighting process in the face of uncertainty and ambiguity, which is especially pertinent for safety risk assessments where data may be incomplete or imprecise27–29. FAHP has proven effective in a variety of applications, including supplier selection, energy management, and, notably, industrial safety risk evaluation29,30.

Furthermore, existing approaches often overlook the dynamic and interdependent nature of risk factors in complex process systems. There is a pressing need for integrated models that consider multiple dimensions of risk—such as probability, severity, detectability, and human factors—in a structured, transparent, and adaptable manner. Combining HAZOP with FAHP and expert validation through the Delphi method presents a promising solution to these challenges. This approach leverages qualitative insights alongside quantitative rigor to enhance safety risk management. Several studies have successfully implemented hybrid HAZOP-MCDM frameworks to enhance risk prioritization in process industries. A study conducted by Nguyen et al. (2022) developed an integrated fuzzy-HAZOP-best-worst approach for adaptive safety-risk mitigation in chemical plants. The key findings demonstrated that this framework effectively prioritizes critical hazards by evaluating operational, environmental, economic, and occupational risks using fuzzy-BWANP. A study by Almousa et al. (2025) proposed the integration of resilience engineering into traditional HAZOP studies to enhance process safety in industrial facilities. The key findings indicated that while HAZOP identifies vulnerabilities, the incorporation of resilience metrics significantly improves systems’ ability to withstand, adapt to, and recover from disruptions. The authors developed a modified HAZOP framework that embeds resilience principles throughout both the design and operational phases16.

A study conducted by Jamshidi Solukloei et al. (2022) developed a hybrid fuzzy-HAZOP/Ant Colony System (ACS) method to assess the combined risks of fire, explosion, and toxic release in process industries. The key findings demonstrated that integrating fuzzy set theory with ACS algorithms enhanced the accuracy of risk evaluations by effectively addressing uncertainties in expert judgments and the complexities of parameter relationships31.

A study conducted by Marhavilas et al. (2023) introduced a novel hybrid methodology combining AHP/MCDM with HAZOP, and Decision-Making Risk Assessment (DMRA) through Safety Level Colored Maps (SLCM) for petrochemical risk assessment. The key findings indicated that this integrated approach effectively identified high-risk nodes, ranked hazards, and visualized safety status using color-coded mapping32.

A study conducted by Severi et al. (2022) developed a FTOPSIS-HAZOP approach to identify critical hazards in biogas upgrading plants. This method combines fuzzy logic, AHP, and TOPSIS to enhance traditional HAZOP analyses. The key findings indicated that the biotrickling filter contributed 33.3% to the overall risk, while the PHA production tank accounted for 17.8%. Additionally, biogas and recycling streams were found to represent 83.4% of the operational risks25.

A critical review of recent hybrid HAZOP-MCDM approaches (Table 1) reveals a growing trend toward integrating fuzzy logic and multi-criteria decision-making to address subjectivity and uncertainty in risk assessment. While existing methods offer valuable advancements—such as resilience metrics, optimization algorithms, and visual risk mapping—they often exhibit limitations in terms of computational complexity, static analysis, or dependence on extensive expert input. Unlike sequential integrations, HAZOP+ introduces a structured consensus-driven approach. It employs the Delphi method to achieve expert agreement on risk factors during HAZOP deviation analysis, followed by selective FAHP weighting applied only to high-priority deviations. This integrated yet efficient process reduces subjectivity while maintaining practicality. Furthermore, HAZOP+ explicitly focuses on capturing and propagating epistemic uncertainties through fuzzy sets, enhancing robustness in weight assignment, extensive coverage of components and subcomponents —a gap noted in several prior studies.

Therefore, according to studies conducted in this field, the present study proposes the HAZOP+ methodology. By decomposing risk into hierarchical criteria and sub-criteria that encompass technical, human, operational, and environmental aspects, HAZOP+ aims to provide a comprehensive, user-friendly, and balanced risk assessment framework tailored to the complexities of modern process industries.

Method

Study design and case study context

This mixed method study was designed and conducted in 2025 with the primary objective of developing HAZOP+, a semi-quantitative extension of HAZOP for assessing safety risks in chemical process industries. This innovative approach is based on the Hazard and Operability Study (HAZOP) methodology, which provides a structured framework for identifying process deviations, and integrates complementary tools such as the Delphi method for achieving expert consensus, the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) for multi-criteria decision-making, and fuzzy logic for managing inherent uncertainties in risk evaluations.

The research was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical guidelines and regulations established by the Ethics Committee of Qom University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.MUQ.REC.1404.111). All participants—including process engineers, safety experts, and operational personnel—provided written informed consent prior to their involvement. The comprehensive study protocol, which encompassed methodological procedures, data collection instruments, participant interactions, and risk assessment frameworks, underwent a thorough ethical review and received formal approval from the committee. This ensured full compliance with international standards for ethical research involving human subjects, such as those outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

The empirical component of the study was conducted at a major petrochemical complex in southern Iran, chosen for its representativeness of large-scale operations in the region. This facility comprises integrated process units spanning the entire value chain—from feedstock reception and preprocessing, through chemical reactions and separation stages, to final product purification and storage. The site handles hazardous substances, including volatile organic compounds (VOCs), flammable solvents, and reactive intermediates under high-pressure and high-temperature conditions, exemplifying the typical risk profiles encountered in petrochemical industries. This setting enabled real-world validation of HAZOP+, facilitating the identification of critical deviations and the evaluation of the methodology’s effectiveness in enhancing process safety management (PSM) practices, in alignment with standards such as ISO 31,000 for risk management and IEC 61,511 for functional safety in process industries.

HAZOP+ framework development

Hazard and operability study (HAZOP)

Hazard and Operability Study (HAZOP) is a systematic brainstorming process designed to assess the presence of hazards in equipment and the degree of vulnerability associated with their operation. This technique is one of the key risk assessment tools that provides managers with critical information, enabling them to make informed decisions to enhance safety and conduct operations safely. The HAZOP method emphasizes the identification of hazards and operational issues in equipment through a structured approach, focusing on identifying hazards and performance. HAZOP is utilized during the design phases of new processes or projects, for significant process modifications, and for periodic reviews of hazards in upcoming operations. The U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), along with other standard-setting organizations worldwide, recognizes the HAZOP method as an acceptable approach for risk assessment15,33. Originally designed as a risk assessment tool, HAZOP is now acknowledged as an industry standard. This structured methodology enables the analysis of potential deviations, allowing for the proactive identification of hazards and the implementation of measures to mitigate risks early in the design process. It also facilitates an understanding of hazards during process modifications and examines how deviations from the intended design may occur, as well as how systems may diverge from performance objectives that pose risks to personnel and operations34. HAZOP is a systematic technique used for hazard identification and risk assessment in process industries. The methodology is divided into six steps: (1) identify hazards by asking what could potentially cause a hazardous event; (2) describe the consequences and assign a severity level to each; (3) estimate the probability of the initiating event; (4) assess the risk based on severity and probability without considering any layers of protection; (5) reassess the risk by taking into account the layers of protection; and (6) decide whether to accept the risk or implement corrective actions16,19,21,31.

Integration mechanism of HAZOP, Delphi, and FAHP methods

HAZOP identifies deviations in process nodes, such as no flow or high pressure. The Delphi method achieves consensus on risk factors—specifically, probability and severity sub-factors—through two rounds, requiring a 75% agreement threshold. FAHP is selectively applied to high-priority deviations (not all, to maintain efficiency) to weight factors using triangular fuzzy numbers, resulting in semi-quantitative scores. This approach is integrated rather than sequential: Delphi informs HAZOP outputs, which then feed into FAHP. Compared to HAZOP combined with LOPA (which quantifies safeguards), HAZOP+ emphasizes uncertainty handling in multi-criteria weighting.

Delphi method for expert consensus on risk factors

One of the most renowned group decision-making techniques is the Brainstorming method, alongside the Delphi Method. The Delphi Method is a systematic approach to gathering information, characterized by its predictive nature. In other words, it is employed in research contexts where precise data and specific statistics regarding the problem at hand are lacking. Consequently, this method involves consulting a panel of experts in the relevant field to achieve a consensus and arrive at a final conclusion. This method comprises several stages, and the accuracy of the final information derived from it depends entirely on the level of expertise of the specialists regarding the problem and research at hand. Today, the Delphi Method is recognized as a valuable approach for problem-solving and information gathering in the fields of management and economics, particularly in business management. This method was initially employed by the U.S. military research organization known as RAND (Research and Development), with its origins tracing back to Olaf Helmer and Norman Dalkey in 1950. After approximately ten years, the Delphi technique was introduced and applied across various disciplines. This structured process is based on the premise that the opinions of experts in each scientific field are the most reliable for predicting future outcomes. Consequently, unlike traditional survey research methods, the validity of the Delphi method does not depend on the number of participants but rather on the scientific credibility of the experts involved. Typically, a Delphi study includes between 5 and 20 participants. Furthermore, there is no consensus regarding the optimal number of rounds for the Delphi technique; however, most studies recommend conducting 2 to 3 rounds29,35,36.

A panel of 45 experts from safety, process engineering, chemical engineering, and industrial management was invited via purposive sampling, with 41 completing all rounds (response rate: 91%). Inclusion criteria were: minimum 10 years of experience in process safety, direct involvement in HAZOP studies, and familiarity with risk assessment methodologies. The sample size was determined based on common practices in Delphi studies for risk assessment, where 15–50 experts are considered sufficient for achieving reliable consensus. Attrition was minimal (n = 4), primarily due to scheduling conflicts.”

Consensus threshold was 80% in Round 2. Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) was employed to measure agreement among experts’ rankings of risk criteria (e.g., severity sub-factors like human impact and environmental damage), with W values exceeding 0.7 in the final round indicating strong consensus.

Analysis of validation and reliability assessment methods

This study employed a mixed-methods approach to evaluate the validity and reliability of the developed model. The validation process consisted of two main components: independent peer review by experts and field testing under real-world conditions. The process was finalized through a specialized expert panel, where research findings were analyzed in conjunction with field data and the knowledge of subject matter experts (SMEs).

For the quantitative assessment, the reliability of the research instrument was evaluated using three key metrics:

Content Validity Ratio (CVR): Assesses the importance of model components.

Content Validity Index (CVI): Evaluates the overall conceptual appropriateness.

Cronbach’s Alpha Coefficient: Measures the internal consistency of measurement scales.

This triangulated validation strategy ensures both construct validity, through expert verification, and statistical reliability, via psychometric testing, thereby aligning with best practices in methodological research.

Fuzzy analytic hierarchy process (FAHP) for factor weighting

The Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) is one of the most renowned multi-criteria decision-making methods, developed by Thomas L. Saaty in the 1970s. This method is employed when multiple options and various quantitative or qualitative criteria are available for decision-making. By establishing a specific goal and utilizing different criteria or metrics—each assigned a weight—this approach enables the selection of the most favorable option for a given objective, while also ranking the other alternatives based on their overall weighted scores37.

Fuzzy logic is a form of multi-valued logic in which the truth values can take any real number between zero and one, including the endpoints themselves. This theory is employed to implement the concept of partial truth, allowing values to exist on a continuum between completely true and completely false. For instance, if black is represented by the number zero and white by the number one, then gray would correspond to a value closer to zero. In essence, fuzzy logic acknowledges the significance of the continuum between these two extremes38,39.

Various studies have integrated the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) with fuzzy logic to rank and weight criteria and sub-criteria, resulting in the development of various Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP) approaches. This study adopted Chang’s FAHP method due to its simplicity and accuracy in producing results. Fuzzy numbers exist in different forms, with triangular fuzzy numbers (TFNs) being the most practical. A TFN is represented as A = (l, m, u), where: u (upper bound) denotes the maximum possible value of the fuzzy number. The lower bound (l) represents the minimum possible value, while the most probable value (m) indicates the peak value of the fuzzy set.

Any inconsistencies in pairwise comparisons can distort the final results; therefore, expert judgments must be validated using a consistency ratio (CR). A CR of ≤ 0.1 indicates acceptable consistency, while inconsistent responses are discarded. After validating the expert inputs, aggregated pairwise comparison matrices were created using the geometric mean to derive the weights of criteria and sub-criteria. The hierarchical composition principle was applied to compute the final weights by multiplying the importance of the criteria by the option weights, resulting in a priority vector27,40–42.

Model validation

The validation of the HAZOP+ model followed a rigorous two-tier process to ensure both its theoretical robustness and practical applicability. This process combined independent peer-review benchmarking by 15 subject matter experts (SMEs) with empirical field verification through case studies and assessments based on real-world operational experience.

Peer-review benchmarking by SMEs

In the first tier, the HAZOP+ framework underwent independent peer-review benchmarking. A group of 15 Subject Matter Experts (SMEs), each specializing in safety engineering, process engineering, chemical engineering, and industrial management, reviewed the model. These experts evaluated the framework’s structure, methodology, and theoretical foundations, providing critical feedback on its conceptual accuracy, comprehensiveness, and alignment with industry best practices. This benchmarking process ensured that the HAZOP+ model adhered to established standards while incorporating innovative elements that enhanced its effectiveness in real-world applications.

Empirical field verification

The second tier of validation involved empirical field verification of the model through case studies and assessments of operational experience within the selected petrochemical facility. In this study, process deviations were identified and weighted. For example, “No Flow” deviation in reactor feed line: Causes (pump failure, valve closure); Consequences (pressure buildup, potential explosion). The HAZOP+ model was applied to real-world scenarios, enabling the research team to evaluate its practical effectiveness in identifying and assessing safety risks within the operational environment. Field data—including observations of process operations, safety incident records, and expert feedback from plant personnel—were systematically integrated into the validation process.

A mapping logic was applied to align the semi-quantitative risk categories with recorded accident outcomes: Level 1 (Acceptable) was compared to incidents with “No Damage,” Level 2 (Tolerable) to those with “Minor Damage,” and Level 3 (Unacceptable) to accidents causing “Severe Damage.” This allowed for a direct comparison of predictive accuracy between HAZOP + and traditional HAZOP.

Final validation phase: comparative analysis and cross-verification

In the final validation phase, the HAZOP+ framework underwent a comparative analysis against benchmarks established by the SMEs. This analysis ensured that the model’s predictions and recommendations aligned with expert assessments and industry standards. To further validate the model, the research team conducted extensive expert panel discussions to cross-verify the findings, integrating both theoretical insights from the SMEs and practical data collected from the field. These discussions provided a deeper understanding of any discrepancies or challenges encountered during field implementation and facilitated refinement of the model.

The systematic integration of field data with specialized SME knowledge during this final phase ensured that the HAZOP+ model was not only theoretically sound but also capable of delivering reliable, actionable results in complex industrial settings. This comprehensive validation approach ultimately confirmed both the theoretical and practical applicability of the developed framework, establishing its value as a dependable tool for safety risk evaluation in the chemical process industries.

Results

This study was conducted with the active participation and collaboration of 41 experts (response rate: 91%) in the fields of safety (n = 12), process engineering (n = 11), chemical engineering (n = 13), and industrial management (n = 5). The mean age of the expert team was 43.11 ± 6.24 years, and their mean work experience was 15.62 ± 6.09 years. Among the participants, 29.0% held a PhD (n = 12), 49.0% possessed a Master’s degree (n = 20), and 22.0% had a Bachelor’s degree (n = 9).

Findings from the Delphi study

This Delphi study was conducted over three rounds.

First round of Delphi method

In this round, various studies on safety risk assessment in chemical process industries utilizing the HAZOP methodology were first examined and evaluated. Based on these studies, and considering the significance of various parameters in the two main factors of this technique—namely, the probability of occurrence and severity—a new algorithm for safety risk assessment, titled HAZOP+, was designed and developed. This algorithm incorporates two main factors and twelve parameters.

The probability of occurrence factor is influenced by six parameters: reliability, system redundancy, Incident Learning, human reliability, technical inspections, and maintenance data.

The severity factor encompasses six parameters: human injury, financial loss, operational disruption, strategic impact, environmental consequences, and effects on organizational reputation.

In this round, 41 experts from the panel were asked to provide their opinions on the significance and desirability of various factors and parameters in the development of the HAZOP+ risk assessment technique. Additionally, they were invited to suggest any other factors or parameters that could be included. Subsequently, the results of the first round of the Delphi study were analyzed. The findings indicated that all 41 members of the expert panel responded regarding the importance and desirability of the factors and parameters that influence the estimation of the risk number in the HAZOP+ method. The analysis revealed that no new items were proposed by the experts for this algorithm during the first round.

Second Delphi round

After collecting expert opinions and analyzing the results of the first Delphi round, modifications were made to the proposed method during the second round based on the experts’ feedback. The revised method was then sent to the expert panel for further comments. In this round, the participating experts were also asked to reassess the importance and desirability of the presented items. Additionally, they were invited to suggest any factors or parameters that could enhance the HAZOP+ risk assessment technique. Subsequently, the results of the second round of the Delphi study were analyzed. The analysis revealed that no new items were proposed by the experts for this algorithm during the second round. Furthermore, the coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated to be 0.04, significantly lower than the threshold value established for this study (> 20%)43. Consequently, based on this coefficient of variation, the Delphi study was concluded at this stage. It is noteworthy that the level of consensus among the respondents was determined to be 80%. This Delphi study was completed after two rounds, and based on its findings, the HAZOP+ risk assessment technique was developed.

Assessment of validity and reliability

The assessment of the validity and reliability of the developed HAZOP+ risk assessment technique revealed a content validity ratio (CVR) of 0.805, which exceeds the minimum acceptable CVR of 0.31. Additionally, the content validity index (CVI) was found to be 0.927, surpassing the minimum acceptable CVI of 0.79. Furthermore, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated to be 0.88, indicating good internal reliability, as a value between 0.8 and 0.9 is considered acceptable.

For expert validation, the results were initially shared with a panel of 15 independent SMEs who assessed their alignment with professional experience and domain knowledge. Discussion

Risk assessment is a fundamental practice within process industries, essential for identifying, evaluating, and mitigating potential hazards to ensure operational safety and sustainability. Due to the inherently complex nature of these industries, which are characterized by high-risk processes involving hazardous materials, precise and reliable risk assessment methods are critically important. Among the various risk assessment methodologies, Hazard and Operability (HAZOP) studies have long been regarded as one of the most structured and comprehensive approaches for identifying potential hazards and operational deviations16,23,44. However, traditional HAZOP methods have several limitations, including subjective judgments by practitioners, qualitative assessment outputs, and a lack of systematic weighting for various risk factors. These shortcomings can result in inconsistent risk evaluations and ineffective prioritization of safety measures. Therefore, there is a pressing need to improve traditional HAZOP methodologies to achieve a more robust, objective, and quantifiable evaluation of risks in process industries.

To address these limitations, this study introduces HAZOP+, a semi-quantitative extension of traditional HAZOP methodology with FAHP. The incorporation of FAHP into HAZOP adds a structured quantitative dimension, effectively mitigating the subjectivity inherent in qualitative assessments and enabling a more accurate representation of expert judgments. The resulting HAZOP+ methodology not only enhances clarity and consistency in risk evaluations but also facilitates better-informed decision-making processes, thereby improving overall safety management in process industries.

The developed HAZOP+ method integrates two primary dimensions of risk: Probability and Severity. Each dimension consists of distinct sub-factors, with corresponding weights determined using the FAHP methodology.

The results indicate that severity carries the highest weight (0.602), underscoring the critical importance of consequences in process industries risk assessment. This finding closely aligns with contemporary literature, which emphasizes the significance of severity in overall risk evaluation within high-risk industries45. Under the Severity dimension, sub-factors such as Human Injury (0.199), Operational Interruption (0.191), and Financial Loss (0.168) were identified as highly significant. This aligns with recent studies that emphasize the impact of human injury and operational disruptions in safety risk assessments46,47. In the context of probability, sub-factors such as Reliability (0.190) and Redundancy (0.179) emerged as primary indicators, contributing to an aggregate weight of 0.398. Recent research confirms that improving both equipment and human reliability significantly reduces the likelihood of operational incidents, thereby reinforcing the importance of these sub-factors48,49. Moreover, the integration of FAHP has been shown to effectively reduce subjectivity, in line with other hybrid risk-assessment models discussed in recent studies27,28,30.

As previously mentioned, the developed HAZOP+ model assigns the highest weight to the Severity dimension (0.602), which significantly exceeds the weight of the Probability dimension (0.398). This prioritization underscores the critical importance of consequence-based factors in risk management within process industries. These industries are characterized by operations involving hazardous materials and complex environments, where severe consequences—especially those affecting human safety, operational continuity, and financial stability—demand greater emphasis in risk assessments.

Among the severity sub-factors, human injury (0.199) emerged as the most critical. This finding is intuitively justifiable, as human safety and the prevention of fatalities and serious injuries form the cornerstone of any effective safety management system. Previous studies strongly support this conclusion. The study conducted by Mahdinia et al. revealed that human injuries are the most significant subfactor associated with the severity component in the risk assessment of the construction industry30. Similarly, Ayyildiz and Gumus underscored the severe human consequences as paramount in risk assessments for hazardous material transportation, further reinforcing the emphasis on minimizing human injury risks50.

The second highly weighted severity sub-factor, Operational Interruption (0.191), underscores the industry’s acknowledgment of the significant impact that operational disruptions have on organizational efficiency and economic performance. Today, industries function within tightly integrated supply chains and production schedules, where even minor interruptions can lead to cascading financial repercussions and reduced market competitiveness. This finding aligns with the work of Mahdinia et al. The results of the study by Mahdinia et al. also indicated that operational interruption is one of the most significant sub factors related to the severity component in risk assessment within the construction industry30.

Within the Probability dimension, Reliability (0.190) was identified as the most significant sub-factor. Reliability in the context of process industries refers to the consistent and dependable performance of equipment, instrumentation, and operational procedures under predetermined conditions. High reliability is paramount because equipment failures or procedural lapses can lead to severe accidents or disruptions. For instance, in refineries, chemical plants, and pharmaceutical facilities, the reliability of critical safety systems, pumps, valves, and control instrumentation directly influences operational stability and hazard prevention. Recent literature emphasizes that reliability is essential for accident prevention, asserting that enhancing equipment reliability significantly reduces unexpected downtimes and potential safety incidents. Recent studies support the critical importance of equipment and process reliability. Dang et al. (2025) found that reliability is pivotal in the risk assessment of advanced technological systems, such as hydrogen-powered aircraft, emphasizing its role in preventing systemic failures and ensuring operational safety51. The study conducted by Soltanzadeh et al. revealed that reliability is a critical sub-factor associated with the probability component when evaluating the safety and security risks of chemicals in the chemical and process industries29.

Redundancy (0.179), which closely follows reliability, underscores the importance of backup systems and duplication in critical operational areas. The emphasis on redundancy and its high weighting reflect an industry trend aimed at mitigating single-point failures. This aligns closely with findings from Yazdi (2017), which indicate that incorporating redundancy significantly reduces system vulnerabilities in chemical processing facilities52. Redundancy involves the integration of backup systems or multiple independent pathways to ensure continued functionality when primary systems fail. In process industries, redundancy is essential because single-point failures in critical equipment, such as emergency shutdown systems (ESDs), pressure relief valves (PRVs), or cooling systems, can quickly escalate into catastrophic events. For instance, redundant control loops, secondary pumps, and backup power supplies are standard safety measures designed to maintain process continuity and safety during unexpected failures. Research consistently demonstrates that incorporating redundancy significantly enhances resilience against process disturbances and markedly reduces overall risk probability53,54.

To ensure the robustness and credibility of the developed HAZOP+ method as a comprehensive risk assessment and ranking tool, validity and reliability tests were conducted. The Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was determined to be 0.805, significantly exceeding the minimum acceptable value of 0.31 for a panel of 35 experts, which indicates strong content validity. Similarly, the Content Validity Index (CVI) achieved a score of 0.927, well above the acceptable threshold of 0.79, thereby confirming the relevance and clarity of the assessment criteria. Furthermore, the internal consistency of the instrument was verified using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.88, demonstrating good reliability (0.8 < α ≤ 0.9). Collectively, these results validate the methodological rigor and suitability of HAZOP + for quantitative risk evaluation in process industries.

The significant dominance of the severity dimension (weight = 0.602) over probability (0.337) in the HAZOP+ model is not merely an outcome of expert preference but reflects a fundamental risk philosophy embedded in high-hazard process industries. This weighting aligns with a consequence-centric or precautionary risk management paradigm, where the potential magnitude of an adverse outcome is prioritized over its likelihood when the consequences are catastrophic or irreversible. This approach is rooted in several interrelated factors:

First, from a safety ethics and social responsibility perspective, the primary imperative in industries handling hazardous materials is to prevent loss of life, serious injury, and major environmental damage. Low-probability, high-severity events—such as a toxic release or a major explosion—can have devastating and lasting impacts on communities and ecosystems, as tragically illustrated by historical accidents like Bhopal. Therefore, risk assessment frameworks must be inherently biased towards identifying and mitigating severe consequences, even if their estimated frequency is low. Second, economic and operational resilience considerations drive this emphasis. The financial impact of a major accident—including direct asset loss, business interruption, regulatory fines, litigation, and reputational harm—can be existential for an organization. A single severe event can outweigh the cumulative cost of numerous minor incidents. Furthermore, modern process plants are often part of complex, interdependent supply chains. A severe operational interruption in one unit can cascade, causing widespread production losses that far exceed the local damage. Third, this weighting mirrors the implicit logic of many international safety standards and regulations, which mandate rigorous analysis and control measures specifically for scenarios with the gravest potential consequences. The framework thus formally incorporates a regulatory and normative expectation to ‘focus on the worst-case’.

Finally, from a systems engineering viewpoint, process plants are characterized by tight coupling and interactive complexity. In such systems, the relationship between a deviation and its ultimate consequence is often non-linear and can escalate rapidly due to unforeseen interactions. Prioritizing severity accounts for this inherent uncertainty in consequence pathways, ensuring that analysis captures the potential for disproportionate outcomes.

Therefore, the higher weighting of severity in HAZOP + is a deliberate methodological feature that translates core safety principles—precaution, social responsibility, economic sustainability, and regulatory compliance—into a quantifiable risk evaluation logic. It ensures that the risk ranking process systematically elevates scenarios with catastrophic potential, guiding resource allocation towards safeguards that prevent the most unacceptable outcomes, regardless of their calculated probability.

To address uncertainties inherent in expert judgments during risk assessments, the HAZOP+ methodology integrates fuzzy logic within the FAHP framework, enabling the representation of vague or imprecise inputs through triangular fuzzy numbers. This approach effectively captures and propagates uncertainty from pairwise comparisons to final risk weightings, reducing subjectivity while maintaining flexibility in complex process environments. For example, by modeling linguistic variables as fuzzy sets, HAZOP+ mitigates the propagation of epistemic uncertainties, ensuring more robust prioritization of deviations. This approach aligns with recent advances in uncertainty handling, as discussed in studies on expert judgment in sociotechnical systems55 and uncertainty modeling in digitalized process risk assessments56, thereby enhancing the method’s credibility and applicability for both academic research and industrial practice.

While the traditional HAZOP methodology primarily serves as a risk identification tool, systematically examining process deviations and potential hazards, it inherently lacks the capacity for risk evaluation and prioritization. In developing HAZOP+, we have specifically enhanced and expanded this conventional approach into a comprehensive risk assessment and ranking methodology. By incorporating FAHP, HAZOP+ aligns closely with the intrinsic nature of hazards within process industries, enabling the quantification and structured prioritization of identified risks. This advancement addresses the critical need within these industries for methods capable of not only identifying risks but also effectively evaluating and ranking them based on their probability and severity. Consequently, this significantly enhances clarity and precision in decision-making related to safety management.

For validation purposes, the HAZOP+ method was implemented alongside the traditional HAZOP approach in a large-scale process industry setting. A comparative analysis of the results demonstrated that HAZOP+ offers a more accurate and nuanced estimation of high-level risks. Specifically, while traditional HAZOP effectively identifies potential hazards, it lacks the ability to quantitatively differentiate among risk levels. In contrast, HAZOP+ enhances risk ranking capabilities, enabling better prioritization of critical risks based on integrated probability and severity measures. This improvement underscores the added value of HAZOP + for more precise risk assessment and decision-making in complex industrial environments.

Suggestions for future research

Future studies could focus on expanding the HAZOP+ methodology by incorporating real-time data streams from industrial IoT sensors and control systems. This integration would facilitate dynamic and continuous risk assessment, allowing organizations to respond proactively to emerging hazards. Furthermore, the application of machine learning algorithms and advanced mathematical techniques, such as data mining, can significantly enhance the predictive accuracy of risk evaluations, minimize errors, and enable comprehensive sensitivity analyses to identify the most influential risk factors.

Another promising approach is to expand the model’s scope by incorporating organizational and human factors, such as safety culture, employee behavior, and management practices. These elements significantly influence risk profiles but are often overlooked in quantitative assessments. By including these factors, we can achieve a more comprehensive understanding of safety risks and enhance the effectiveness of interventions.

Finally, validating and customizing the HAZOP+ framework across various process industries—including pharmaceuticals, petrochemicals, and food processing—as well as in diverse geographic and regulatory contexts, will enhance its adaptability and generalizability. Implementing the method across a broader range of industries will strengthen its robustness and encourage wider acceptance.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated a semi-quantitative extension of risk assessment methodology, HAZOP+, by integrating traditional HAZOP with FAHP tailored for the process industries. This hybrid approach effectively addresses the limitations of qualitative HAZOP by introducing a quantitative and systematic method for weighting and prioritizing risk factors.

The validation results confirmed the robustness and reliability of the developed method. Furthermore, the method demonstrated strong internal consistency.

The analysis of risk dimensions revealed that the Severity factor holds the greatest significance (weight = 0.602), underscoring the critical impact of consequences in risk management within the process industry. Within this dimension, Human Injury (0.199) and Operational Interruption (0.191) were identified as the most significant sub-factors, emphasizing the paramount need to ensure personnel safety and maintain operational continuity. The Probability dimension (weight = 0.398) also plays a vital role, with Reliability (0.190) and Redundancy (0.179) emerging as key determinants in mitigating the likelihood of hazardous events. A comparison of HAZOP+ results with traditional HAZOP in large-scale process industries demonstrated the enhanced accuracy and practical utility of the proposed method in estimating and ranking high-level risks. This improvement facilitates better decision-making and resource allocation for safety management.
