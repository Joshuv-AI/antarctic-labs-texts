Reconciling Hessian-Informed Acceleration and Scalar-Only Communication for Efficient Federated Zeroth-Order Fine-Tuning
Zhe Li, Bicheng Ying, Zidong Liu, Chaosheng Dong, Haibo Yang

Abstract
Recent dimension-free communication frameworks in Federated Learning (FL), such as DeComFL, significantly reduce per-round communication by transmitting only scalars via zeroth-order stochastic gradient descent (ZO-SGD). This method is particularly advantageous for federated fine-tuning of Large Language Models (LLMs). Yet, the high variance in ZO gradient estimation typically leads to slow convergence. Although leveraging Hessian information is known to enhance optimization speed, integrating this into FL presents significant challenges. These include clients’ restrictions on local data and the critical need to maintain the dimension-free communication property. To overcome this limitation, we first introduce a generalized scalar-only communication FL framework that decouples dimension-free communication from standard ZO-SGD, enabling the integration of more advanced optimization strategies. Building on this framework, we propose HiSo, a fast federated fine-tuning method via H essian- i nformed zeroth-order optimization and S calar- o nly communication. Specifically, it leverages global curvature information to accelerate convergence while preserving the same minimal communication cost per round. Theoretically, we establish convergence guarantees that are independent of the global Lipschitz constant, and further show that HiSo achieves faster rates when the global Hessian exhibits a low effective rank – a common phenomenon in LLMs. Extensive experiments on benchmark datasets and LLM fine-tuning tasks confirm that HiSo significantly outperforms existing ZO-based FL methods in both convergence speed and communication efficiency.

Introduction

The recent success of large language models (LLMs) has spurred growing interest in deploying and fine-tuning these models across diverse devices and personalized settings
(
Naveed et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2023
)
.
Federated fine-tuning has emerged as a promising paradigm in this context, enabling on-device model personalization without centralizing private user data
(
Kairouz et al. 2021
;
Cho et al. 2024
)
.
Yet, the massive parameter size of LLMs presents fundamental scalability challenges for federated fine-tuning, primarily due to the prohibitive communication cost of transmitting high-dimensional model updates
(
Wu et al. 2025
;
Jia et al. 2025
)
.
To mitigate this issue, recent work has proposed using zeroth-order optimization (ZOO) to enable dimension-free communication in federated learning (FL)
(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
;
Li et al. 2024b
)
.
In particular, DeComFL
(
Li et al. 2024b
)
encodes both uplink and downlink communication using shared random seeds and scalar-only updates, achieving communication cost independent of model dimension.
This framework is especially attractive for federated LLM fine-tuning, where communication is a dominant bottleneck.

However, the practical effectiveness of ZOO-based FL remains limited due to its seriously slow convergence.
A key factor is that LLMs often exhibit heterogeneous and anisotropic curvature across their parameter space
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
;
Yao et al. 2021
;
Benzing 2022
)
, making it difficult for vanilla ZO-SGD to adaptively scale updates.
While prior work has shown that second-order information—such as Hessians or their diagonal approximations—can significantly accelerate convergence
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
;
Ye et al. 2018
;
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2024
)
,
estimating Hessian approximation and applying such curvature-aware techniques in FL are already non-trivial.
The challenge becomes even more pronounced in dimension-free communication frameworks, where transmitting any Hessian-related information reintroduces costs that scale with model size—directly contradicting the goal of scalar-only communication.
This tension leads to our research question:

Can we accelerate federated ZO fine-tuning while preserving dimension-free communication?

To answer this question, we propose a new FL framework that decouples scalar-only communication from vanilla ZO-SGD, enabling the integration of Hessian-informed optimization.
Within this framework, we introduce HiSo, an efficient FL algorithm via
H
essian-
i
nformed zeroth-order optimization and
S
calar-
o
nly communication.
Specifically, it captures second-order information through diagonal Hessian approximation without increasing communication cost.
HiSo maintains the scalar-only communication while significantly improving convergence via Hessian-informed preconditioning.

Our theoretical and empirical results and contributions can primarily be summarized as follows:

•

We propose a flexible FL framework with scalar-only communication in both uplink and downlink, which supports a broader class of optimization algorithms beyond vanilla ZO-SGD.

•

Under this framework, we develop HiSo, a fast federated fine-tuning method via
H
essian-
i
nformed zeroth-order optimization and
S
calar-
o
nly communication.
It utilizes global Hessian information to speed up convergence while preserving dimension-free communication.

•

Theoretically, we propose a novel condition to get a tight estimation of the variance of Hessian-informed ZO gradient under the low-effective rank assumption. With this treatment, we prove that HiSo can achieve a convergence rate independent of model dimension and function smoothness in non-convex settings—marking the first such result for ZO methods in FL. In addition, our analysis generalizes the state-of-the-art DeComFL framework and, importantly, extends the theoretical guarantees to multiple local updates—a key component of practical federated learning that DeComFL does not support in its convergence analysis.

•

Empirically, HiSo achieves up to 5
\times
faster convergence than DeComFL, while delivering higher test accuracy than all ZO baselines across all tasks.
Compared to first-order baselines, up to 90 million times communication savings can be gained.

Related Work

Adaptive Gradient Methods & Hessian-Informed Zeroth-Order Optimization.

To accelerate first-order FL, adaptive FL algorithms (e.g., FedAdam, FedYogi, FedAdagrad

By adaptively adjusting learning rates or applying momentum techniques, these methods significantly outperform vanilla FedAvg in terms of convergence speed and final accuracy.
Parallel to this line, recent advances in ZOO have shown its effectiveness in gradient-free learning, especially when gradients are unavailable or expensive to compute.
To further enhance convergence speed and stability, several studies

proposed Hessian-informed ZOO methods that incorporate second-order information, such as diagonal Hessian approximations, as preconditioning to improve the quality of gradient estimation and reduce variance, which shows the acceleration in centralized settings.

Communication-Efficient Federated Learning & Scalar-Only Communication.

Communication efficiency is a critical challenge in FL primarily due to the frequent transmission of high-dimensional model updates between clients and the server
(
Kairouz et al. 2021
;
Jia et al. 2025
)
.
Numerous methods have been proposed to reduce communication overhead in FL, including compression techniques used to reduce the size of transmitted data
(

;

;
Hönig et al. 2022
;
Su et al. 2024

,
parameter-efficient methods, such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
(

;
Guo et al. 2024
)
to transmit only a low-rank trainable matrix representing model updates.
Moreover, ZOO has also been introduced to the FL context.
FedZO
(
Fang et al. 2022
)
integrates ZO-SGD into FL, but its communication heavily relies on the model dimension.
DeComFL
(
Li et al. 2024b
)
pioneeringly exploited the intrinsic properties of ZO gradients—specifically, their decomposition into gradient scalars and perturbation vectors determined by random seeds—to achieve dimension-free communication overhead in LLM fine-tuning.
Yet, it suffers from slower convergence due to the nature of ZO-SGD.

3.1
Zeroth-Order SGD and Scalar Representations

In this paper, we focus on the randomized gradient estimator (RGE) for performing ZO gradient estimation.
It is also commonly referred to as Simultaneous Perturbation Stochastic Approximation (SPSA)
(
Spall 1992
;
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
)
.
Given a scalar-valued loss function
f(x)
where
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the forward-style RGE is

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}f(x)\!=\!\frac{1}{\mu}\big(f(x+\mu u)\!-\!f(x)\big)u,\;u\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{d}),

(1)

where
u
represents a random direction vector sampled from a standard Gaussian distribution and
\mu
is a small constant, commonly termed the smoothing parameter, controlling the perturbation step size.

An intriguing attribute of RGE is its efficient representation using only two scalars.
First, we introduce a gradient scalar
g:=\frac{1}{\mu}(f(x+\mu u)-f(x))\in{\mathbb{R}}
, which serves as a scaling constant capturing the directional derivative.

g
can also be explained as an approximate value for the directional gradient.
Second, due to the deterministic nature of pseudo-random number generators, the random direction vector
u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
can be uniquely determined by a random seed
s
.
Hence, the estimated gradient
\hat{\nabla}f(x)
can be efficiently expressed by two scalars.
Crucially, this compact representation significantly enhances the efficiency of model updates in zeroth-order optimization (ZOO) frameworks.
To illustrate, consider ZO-SGD update rule shown in Fig.
1
:

\displaystyle x_{R+1}=x_{R}-\frac{\eta}{\mu}\big(f(x_{R}+\mu u_{R})-f(x_{R})\big)u_{R}=x_{R}-\eta g_{R}u_{R}=\cdots=x_{0}-\eta\sum_{r=0}^{R}g_{r}u_{r}

(2)

This implies that, given the initial point
x_{0}
, a few number of gradient scalars
\{g_{r}\}
and random seeds
\{s_{r}\}
are sufficient to reconstruct
x_{R}
, irrespective of the dimensionality
d
of
x
.
This representation will play a crucial role in the dimension-free communication FL algorithm that follows.

3.2
Federated Learning with Dimension-Free Communication

We consider a FL scenario with
M
clients, each owning a local loss function
f_{i}
.
The goal is to collaboratively minimize the global loss function across all clients without sharing their private data:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f({\bm{x}})=\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}f_{i}({\bm{x}}),\;\;\;\;\mbox{where $f_{i}({\bm{x}}):=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt[F_{i}({\bm{x}};\xi_{i})]$.}\vskip-2.84526pt

(3)

A typical FL round consists of two communications:
1)
Downlink Communication
: The server broadcasts the current aggregated global model to a subset of clients;
2)
Uplink Communication
:
The selected clients return their locally updated model to the server. Both can be an expansive communication operation when the number of parameters
d
is large.

The core idea of dimension-free communication in FL
(
Li et al. 2024b
)
is leveraging the scalar representation of ZO-SGD to avoid transmitting the full models. To illustrate that, consider the following global model update rule with the notation that
x_{r,\tau}^{(i)}
denotes client
i
’s model at the
r
-th round and
\tau
-th local update step and
x_{r}
denotes the
r
-th global model:

\displaystyle x_{r+1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|C_{r}|}\sum_{i\in C_{r}}x_{r,\tau}^{(i)}=x_{r}+\frac{1}{|C_{r}|}\sum_{i\in C_{r}}(x_{r,\tau}^{(i)}-x_{r})=x_{r}-\eta\frac{1}{|C_{r}|}\sum_{i\in C_{r}}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}g_{r,k}^{(i)}u_{r,k},

(4)

where
C_{r}
is the set of sampled clients in the
r
-th round,

u_{r,k}
are generated by shared random seeds across all clients, ensuring that all clients move along consistent directions.
It enables that the global aggregation step in the server is simply computing an average of the gradient scalars:
g_{r,k}=\frac{1}{|C_{r}|}\sum_{i\in C_{r}}g_{r,k}^{(i)}
from the local gradient scalar
g_{r,k}^{(i)}=\big(f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)}+\mu u_{r,k})-f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)})\big)/\mu
.

Figure 2:
An illustration of one-round update with 2 sampled clients and 3 local updates.
They share the same direction for each local update with different lengths.
To arrive
x_{r+1}
for both clients, it requires
7 steps
– 3 local updates, reset and 3 updates with global values.

Uplink Communication.

From Eq. (
4
), it is straightforward to see that sampled clients only need to transmit local gradient scalars
g^{(i)}_{r,k}
to the server for global aggregation.

Downlink Communication.

ZO scalar representation only captures relative updates; hence, it is crucial to ensure that the server and all clients start from the same starting point.
To achieve this, a model-reset mechanism is introduced: after completing their local updates in each round, every participating client resets its local model to the initial model, which is the global server model by induction.
With this reset mechanism, the downlink communication can be conceptualized similarly to Eq. (
4
), with the distinction that clients may miss participation in multiple rounds.

Unlike the standard FL algorithm, reconstructing instead of pulling model is used for catching the current server model through global gradient scalars and random seeds from preceding missed rounds.
Hence, the server necessitates recording the client’s last participation round, historical random seeds, and the global gradient scalars.

4.1
Find a Better Ascent
\Delta x^{(i)}_{r,k}
Direction

In this section, we utilize the proposed generalized framework to design a novel algorithm superior to ZO-SGD based FL but crucially still maintaining the dimension-free communication characteristic.
The core challenge in the preceding framework is identifying an effective ascent direction,
\Delta x^{(i)}_{r,k}
, that is constructible solely from scalar values and current state information. While Zeroth-Order Stochastic Gradient Descent (ZO-SGD) meets these requirements, a superior alternative can be found.

Recall that the ZO methods’ slow convergence is due to its dependency on random search directions
(
Ma and Huang 2025
)
.
More specifically, recall the Eq. (
1
) with
u\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I)
, which uniformly searches all directions in the
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
space, is the update direction regardless of the scalar
g
.
A natural extension is that we can guide the search direction with an invertible matrix
H_{r}
.
Suppose
H_{r}
is given, the Line 11 in Algorithm
1
can be formulated as the following sub-optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{g\in{\mathbb{R}}}\;\;

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)})-\Delta x_{r,k}^{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}

(Ascent Direction)

(5)

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}\;\;

\displaystyle\Delta x_{r}^{(i)}=g\cdot H^{-1/2}_{r}u_{r,k},\;\;u_{r,k}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times 1}

(Scalars Representation)

(6)

It will be clear later why we use this strange
H^{-1/2}_{r}
notation instead of
H_{r}
directly. Solving the above least-squares problem, we have

\displaystyle g^{o}=(u^{\mathsf{T}}_{r,k}H^{-1}_{r}u_{r,k})^{-1}u_{r,k}^{\mathsf{T}}H^{-1/2}_{r}\nabla f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)})

(7)

Note
(u^{\mathsf{T}}H^{-1}u)^{-1}
is a scalar that is independent of iterates
{\bm{x}}_{r,k}^{(i)}
. Hence, we can absorb it into the learning rate. Next, note that

u^{\mathsf{T}}_{r,k}H^{-1/2}_{r}\nabla f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)})=\frac{1}{\mu}\big(f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)}+\mu H^{-1/2}_{r}u_{r,k})-f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)})\big)+{\mathcal{O}}(\mu)
.
Hence, we obtain the following update rule

\displaystyle\boxed{\Delta x_{r,k}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{\mu}\big(f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)}+\mu H^{-1/2}_{r}u_{r,k})-f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)})\big)H^{-1/2}_{r}u_{r,k}}

(8)

Now it should be clear why we use the notation
H_{r}^{-1/2}
after we take the expectation of
\Delta x_{r,k}^{(i)}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\Delta x_{r,k}^{(i)}\approx\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptH^{-1/2}_{r}u_{r,k}u_{r,k}^{\mathsf{T}}H^{-1/2}_{r}\nabla f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)})=H^{-1}_{r}\nabla f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)})

(9)

When
H_{r}
is well-approximated Hessian matrix, the expectation of gradient descent follows the Newton-style gradient descent
(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
.
The first-order counterpart of
\Delta x_{r,k}^{(i)}
is called natural gradient since it can be viewed as a pre-conditioned gradient
(
Amari 1998
)
.
Recalling the linear transformation property of Gaussian Distribution, the update equation
8
can be more concisely written as the following form

\displaystyle\Delta x_{r,k}^{(i)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}[f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)}+\mu z_{r,k})-f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)})]z_{r,k},\;\;z_{r,k}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,H^{-1}_{r})

(10)

This formulation also aligns with recent work by
Ye et al. 2025
and
Zhao et al. 2024

4.2
Learning Global Curvature without Extra Communication Cost

A follow-up question for the above formulation is how to find this
H_{r}
matrix.
One plausible approach is, again, utilizing the zeroth-order gradient estimators to approximate directional second derivatives

\displaystyle u^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla^{2}F(x)u\approx\frac{F(x+\mu u)+F(x-\mu u)-2F(x)}{2\mu^{2}},\;\;u\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{d})

(11)

However, this approach has two limitations:
1) this requires an additional function evaluation per direction and extra communications; 2) forming the full
d\times d
Hessian is both costly and unnecessary. Instead, we only seek a diagonal preconditioner, akin to Adam’s per-coordinate scaling
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
)
1
1

1

More accurately, our method resembles RMSProp as it currently is without a momentum term. Momentum could be incorporated without additional communication costs using the same technique presented in this section. Given the existing length of this paper, we will not elaborate on this momentum extension here.
. Recall the global update term
\Delta x_{r,k}
approximates the value of the gradient and it can be constructed by scalars only as discussed before. Further, notice this value is needed for the reconstruction step. Hence, we have a free variable to approximate the diagonal Hessian through the following proposed rule. We only update the Hessian at the beginning of one communication round with
\tau
-local update steps followed by the exponential moving averaging (EMA).

\displaystyle H_{r+1}=H_{r,\tau}=

\displaystyle(1-\nu)H_{r,\tau-1}+\nu\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in S_{r}}{\rm Diag}([\Delta x_{r,\tau}]^{2}+\epsilon I)

\displaystyle H_{r,1}=

\displaystyle(1-\nu)H_{r}+\nu\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in S_{r}}{\rm Diag}([\Delta x_{r,0}]^{2}+\epsilon I),

(12)

where
\epsilon
is a small number to make sure that
H_{r+1}
is strictly positive definite.

This Adam-style approach, similar to its first-order counterparts
(
Reddi et al. 2020
)
, has two advantages.
First, the diagonal matrix approximation avoids the
d^{2}
storage requirement for the Hessian matrix, which makes the proposed method scalable with the large-scale model.
Second, the vector
\Delta x_{r,k}
can be represented by the scalars, so the server and clients can reconstruct this global Hessian without any extra communication cost.

5.1
Hessian, Variance of ZO Gradient, and Low Effective Rank Assumption

To lay the foundation for analyzing HiSo, we first examine a basic component of ZO: the estimation of the variance term.
It provides essential insights into Hessian-informed ZO methods.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|u\|_{\Sigma}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptu^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma u,

(13)

\displaystyle u\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times 1},

where
\Sigma
is some semi-positive Hessian matrices
2
2

2

For a non-convex function, Hessian may contain some negative eigenvalues.
One possible choice of
\Sigma
can be the absolute eigenvalues of the Hessian.
.
The standard
L
-smoothness assumption implies that
\|\Sigma\|\leq L
. Consequently, the preceding quantity can be upper-bounded as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|u\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\leq\|\Sigma\|\cdot\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|u\|^{2}\leq Ld,

(14)

Note that the upper bound derived above can be quite large if the dimension
d
is large.
This dependence on dimensionality is a well-known factor leading to a typically slow convergence rate of ZO methods
(
Nesterov 2013
)
.
Fortunately, this bound only represents a worst-case scenario.
Motivated by empirical observations that the Hessian of trained large language models (LLMs) possesses relatively few eigenvalues significantly far from zero
(
Papyan 2020
;
Yao et al. 2020
;
Wu et al. 2020
)
,
Malladi et al. 2023
proposed a low-effective rank assumption. This spectral property, where most eigenvalues are concentrated near zero, is illustrated in Fig.
4
(left).
To utilize this assumption, we need to treat the variance more carefully:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|u\|_{\Sigma}^{2}=\Tr(\Sigma\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptuu^{\mathsf{T}})=L\Tr(\Sigma/L):=L\kappa,

(15)

where

Convergence Results

We first present some standard assumptions that will be used to establish the convergence results.

Assumption 0

(
L

.

Suppose the global loss function

L

\|\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|

Assumption 0

(Unbiased Stochastic Gradients with Bounded Variance)
.

The stochastic gradient computed by clients is unbiased with bounded variance:

\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt[\nabla f_{i}(x;\xi)]=\nabla f_{i}(x)

\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\left\|\nabla f_{i}(x;\xi)-\nabla f_{i}(x)\right\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}_{s}

\forall x
, where
\xi
represents a data sample.

Assumption 0

(Bounded Heterogeneity)
.

The cost function satisfies
\|\nabla f_{i}(x)-\nabla F(x)\|\leq\sigma_{G},\forall x
.

Assumption 0

(Bounded Learned Hessian)
.

The learned Hessian has
0<\beta_{\ell}\leq\|H_{r}\|\leq\beta_{u},\forall r
.

The last assumption is common in Hessian-informed algorithms
(
Maritan et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
)
or Adam-style algorithms
(

Reddi et al. 2020

This assumption also implies

Appendix

Appendix A
Conclusion

In this paper, we first present a new federated learning framework that supports scalar-only communication in both uplink and downlink, enabling the integration of a broader class of optimization algorithms beyond vanilla zeroth-order SGD.
Building on this foundation, we propose HiSo, a Hessian-informed federated fine-tuning algorithm that leverages diagonal Hessian approximations to accelerate convergence while preserving scalar-only communication efficiency.
From a theoretical perspective, we introduce a novel variance characterization for Hessian-informed zeroth-order gradients under a low-effective-rank assumption.
This allows us to establish a convergence rate that is independent of both model dimensionality and function smoothness in non-convex settings—a result not previously achieved by any zeroth-order method in federated learning.
Our analysis further generalizes the DeComFL framework and extends its theoretical guarantees to support multiple local updates, a critical component in practical FL deployments.
Empirically, HiSo consistently outperforms existing baselines, delivering higher test accuracy, up to about 5
\times
faster convergence, and substantially lower communication overhead.
These results demonstrate the practical viability and theoretical soundness of unifying curvature-informed optimization with scalar-only communication in federated fine-tuning.

Appendix B
Limitations

The proposed method is currently limited by its treatment of the loss function
f_{i}
as a generic one, without considering model-specific module structures. This is in contrast to modern parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods that often exploit properties like low-rank decomposition (e.g.,
W=AB^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k_{1}\times r}
and
B\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k_{2}\times r}
and
r\ll k_{1},k_{2}
).
It is important to note that this explicit low-rank decomposition is distinct from the ‘low effective rank’ of the Hessian discussed in this paper.
Consequently, there is potential to further refine our approach by designing Hessian information specifically tailored for PEFT methods such as LoRA or GaLore.

), the following proof is for the general update rule of
H_{k}
. Hence, we just need to focus on the property of
H_{k}
instead of combining the update rule and revisit it later. We further denote
z_{k}=H_{k}^{-1/2}u_{k}
,
z_{k}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,H_{k}^{-1})
to simplify the update rule:

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k}-\frac{\eta}{\mu}z_{k}\Big({\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k}+\mu z_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}})-{\bm{f}}(x_{k})\Big),

(26)

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{k+1}W_{k}

(27)

Because of the shared seeds and Hessians,
{\bm{z}}_{k}
is a variable that has no client index subscripts. Using directional gradient approximation

\displaystyle f(x+\mu z)=f(x)+\mu z^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla f(x)+\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}z^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}f(x+tz)dt\right)z,

(28)

the update rule can be concisely written as

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k}-\eta z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k})+O(\mu\eta),

(29)

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{k+1}W_{k},

(30)

To manage notational complexity and the handling of intricate coefficients, we adopt the
O(\mu\eta)
notation.

Since this paper concentrates on addressing client sampling and local updates in federated learning, the analysis of the zeroth-order approximation error is intentionally simplified.
This approach facilitates a clearer understanding of the distinct error sources in the federated setting, without sacrificing proof rigor.

We define the (virtual) centralized iterates
{\bar{x}}_{k}:={\bm{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}_{u}
and
{\bar{y}}_{k}:={\bm{y}}_{k}\mathds{1}_{u}
.
The recursion of centralized iterates
{\bar{x}}_{k}:={\bm{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}_{u}
is

\displaystyle{\bar{x}}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{k+1}W_{k}\mathds{1}_{u}

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big({\bm{x}}_{k}-\eta z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\Big)w_{k}+O(\mu\eta)

(32)

where we define
w_{k}:=W_{k}\mathds{1}_{u}
. It is straightforward to see that if
k\neq r\tau
,
w_{k}=\mathds{1}_{u}
; if
k=r\tau
,
w_{k}
is the random selection vector with each entry having
m/M
probability to be
1/m
and 0 otherwise. Hence, we have the following two cases to handle with

\displaystyle\boxed{{\bar{x}}_{k+1}=\begin{cases}{\bar{x}}_{k}-\eta z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})+O(\mu\eta)&k\neq r\tau,\\
\hat{x}_{k}-\eta z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})+O(\mu\eta)&k=r\tau.\end{cases}}

(33)

where we denote

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k}=

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k}w_{k},

(34)

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})=

\displaystyle\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\mathds{1}_{u}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\nabla f_{i}(x_{k}^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times 1},

(35)

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})=

\displaystyle\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}_{k})w_{k}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in C_{r}}\nabla f_{i}(x_{k}^{(i)})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times 1}.

(36)

Above two centralized recursions will be the main reference the following proof.

D.3
Key Lemmas

D.3.1
Lemmas about Gaussian Variables

The rest proof is built on top of the following two fundamental lemmas about the Gaussian distribution.

Lemma 0

(Fourth-Order Moment of Gaussian Vector)
.

Suppose that the random vector
z\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\Lambda)
where
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix.
For any symmetric matrix
W
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptzz^{\mathsf{T}}Wzz^{\mathsf{T}}=\Tr(W\Lambda)\cdot\Lambda+2\Lambda W\Lambda.

(37)

If
u\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I)
, i.e., drawing from a standard Gaussian distribution, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptuu^{\mathsf{T}}Wuu^{\mathsf{T}}=\Tr(W)\cdot I+2W.

(38)

Proof.

Let the matrix
\Psi=zz^{\mathsf{T}}Wzz^{\mathsf{T}}
.
For each element
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\Psi[i,j]=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptz_{i}z_{j}(\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}z_{i^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}}W[i^{\prime},j^{\prime}])=2\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptz_{i}^{2}z_{j}^{2}W[i,j]=2\Lambda_{i}\Lambda_{j}W[i,j],

(39)

where the second equality holds because the zero-mean property of
z
and
z_{i}
is independent of each other.
For the diagonal elements,

\displaystyle\Psi[i,i]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptz_{i}^{2}(\sum_{i^{\prime},j;}z_{i^{\prime}}z_{j^{\prime}}W[i^{\prime},j^{\prime}])=\sum_{i^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptz_{i}^{2}z_{i^{\prime}}^{2}W[i^{\prime},i^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptz_{i}^{2}\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptz_{i^{\prime}}^{2}W[i^{\prime},i^{\prime}]+\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptz_{i}^{4}W[i,i]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda_{i}\sum_{i^{\prime}}\Lambda_{i^{\prime}}W[i^{\prime},i^{\prime}]+2W[i,i]\Lambda_{i}^{2},

(40)

where we utilize the fact that
\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptz_{i}^{4}=3\Lambda_{i}^{2}
.
Lastly, combining the above two results into a concise matrix notation, we establish

\displaystyle\Psi=\Tr(W\Lambda)\cdot\Lambda+2\Lambda W\Lambda

(41)

For the standard Gaussian distribution case, we just need to substitute
\Lambda=I
into equation
37
.
∎

Lemma 0

(Gaussian Smoothed Function)
.

We define a smooth approximation of objective function
f
as
f^{\mu}(\cdot)
that can be formulated as

\displaystyle f^{\mu}(x):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int f(x+\mu u)e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}}d{\bm{z}}=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt[f(x+\mu)],

(42)

where
\mu>0
is the smoothing parameter, and
{\bm{z}}
is one n-dimensional standard Gaussian random vector.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\frac{f(x+\mu u)-f(x)}{\mu}u=\nabla f^{\mu}(x),\;\;{\rm where\ }u\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I)

(43)

Above equality implies the ZO gradient is an unbiased estimate of the gradient of the smoothed function
f^{\mu}
.

Proof.

See the proof in
(
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
;
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
)
.
∎

D.3.2
Variance Lemma for Sampling Noise

Before we present the main proof, we first bound the variance of
\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})
.

Lemma 0
.

Suppose
f_{i}
is
L
-smooth and the local cost functions satisfy the data heterogeneity assumption
\sigma_{G}^{2}
.
For any semi-positive definite matrix
\Sigma
, the variance of the sampled gradient
\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})
satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle 2\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{\Sigma}+\frac{2}{m}\|\Sigma\|(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})+\frac{2L^{2}}{M}\|\Sigma\|\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F},

(44)

where
m
is the number of sampled clients per round and
M
is the total number of clients.

Proof.

For any semi-positive matrix
\Sigma
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}})\|_{\Sigma}^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})-\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}})\|_{\Sigma}^{2}

(45)

where the inequality utilizes Jensen’s inequality.

Next, noticing that the variance identity for any weighted distance
\|\cdot\|_{\Sigma}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|{\bar{x}}_{k}-\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt{\bar{x}}_{k}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|{\bar{x}}_{k}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}-\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt({\bar{x}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt{\bar{x}}_{k})-\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt(\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt{\bar{x}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}})\Sigma{\bar{x}}_{k}+\|\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt{\bar{x}}_{k}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|{\bar{x}}_{k}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}-\|\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt{\bar{x}}_{k}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}

(46)

Combining with the fact that
\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt_{w_{k}}\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}})=\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})
, we establish

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}})\|_{\Sigma}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}(\bar{x}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}})-\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|_{\Sigma}^{2}+\|{\nabla F}({\bar{x}}_{k})\|_{\Sigma}^{2}

(47)

The first term in the above equality can be further bounded through the data heterogeneity assumption that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}(\bar{x}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}})-\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|_{\Sigma}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\big\|\sum_{i\in C_{r}}\Big(\nabla f_{i}(\bar{x}_{k};\xi_{k})-\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\Big)\big\|_{\Sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{mM}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\nabla f_{i}(\bar{x}_{k};\xi_{k})-\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\big\|_{\Sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\|\Sigma\|(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})

(48)

where the second equality holds since the zero-mean property. Substituting the above results back to equation
45
, we arrive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle 2\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{\Sigma}+\frac{2}{m}\|\Sigma\|(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})+2\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})-\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}})\|_{\Sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{\Sigma}+\frac{2}{m}\|\Sigma\|(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})+2L^{2}\|\Sigma\|\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}/M

(49)

where we applied the
L-
Lipschitz condition and Jensen’s inequality in the last step.
∎

D.4
Descent Lemma

Lemma 0
.

When
\eta\leq\left\{\frac{\beta_{\ell}}{mL},\frac{1}{8\rho_{k}}\right\}
, the virtual centralized iterates
\bar{x}_{k}
of one round satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF(\bar{x}_{(r+1)\tau+1})\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF(\bar{x}_{r\tau+1})-\frac{\eta}{4}\sum_{j=r\tau+1}^{(r+1)\tau}\|\nabla F(\bar{x}_{j})\|^{2}_{H_{r}^{-1}}+O(\eta^{2}\mu)

\displaystyle{}+\frac{4\tau\eta^{2}}{\beta_{\ell}m}\sum_{j=r\tau+1}^{(r+1)\tau}\rho_{k}(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})+\frac{2L}{mM}\sum_{j=r\tau+1}^{(r+1)\tau}\|{\bm{x}}_{j}-{\bar{x}}_{j}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}

(50)

where
\rho_{k}=\Tr(H^{-1/2}_{k}\Sigma_{k}H^{-1/2}_{k})+2\|H^{-1/2}_{k}\Sigma_{k}H^{-1/2}_{k}\|
.
\hfill\qed

Proof.

Recall there are two random variables in the main recursion Eq. (
33
), one is the ZO random direction
z_{k}
and the other is the client sampling vector
w_{k}
.
First, taking the conditional expectation over
w_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt_{w_{k}}{\bar{x}}_{k+1}={\bar{x}}_{k}-\eta z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})+{\mathcal{O}}(\eta\mu)

(51)

for any iteration
k
.
Then, taking conditional expectation over
z_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt{\bar{x}}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle{\bar{x}}_{k}-\eta H_{k}^{-1}\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})+{\mathcal{O}}(\eta\mu)

(52)

As a result of Assumption
1
, there is a semi-positive definite matrix
\Sigma_{y}\preceq L\cdot I_{d}
such that the global loss function satisfies

\displaystyle F(x)\leq F(y)+\langle\nabla F(y),x-y\rangle+\frac{1}{2}(x-y)^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{y}(x-y).

(53)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle F({\bar{x}}_{k+1})\leq F({\bar{x}}_{k})+\langle\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k}),{\bar{x}}_{k+1}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}({\bar{x}}_{k+1}-{\bar{x}}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{k}({\bar{x}}_{k+1}-{\bar{x}}_{k})

(54)

Now, substituting Eq. (
33
) into the above expansion and taking the conditional expectation, we will establish the following two cases.

Local Update Iteration:

When the iteration
k
is not the communication iteration, i.e.
k\neq r\tau
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF({\bar{x}}_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\eta\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}H_{k}^{-1}\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})+O(\eta^{2}\mu)

\displaystyle{}+\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt[\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{k}z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})]

(55)

First, we focus on the cross term

\displaystyle-\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}H_{k}^{-1}\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})=

\displaystyle-\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}H_{k}^{-1}\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})+(\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k}))^{\mathsf{T}}H_{k}^{-1}\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H^{-1}_{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}

(56)

Because of Assumption
4
, we have
\beta_{u}^{-1}\leq\|H_{k}^{-1}\|\leq\beta_{\ell}^{-1}
, which implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta_{\ell}}\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta_{\ell}N}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\nabla f_{i}({\bar{x}}_{k})-\nabla f_{i}(x_{k}^{(i)})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L^{2}}{2\beta_{\ell}N}\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}

(57)

Substituting back, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF({\bar{x}}_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\frac{\eta}{2}\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}+\frac{\eta L^{2}}{2\beta_{\ell}N}\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle{}+\eta^{2}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt[\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{k}z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})]}_{:=Q}

(58)

Next, the key is this quadratic term. Leveraging Lemma
1
, we establish

\displaystyle Q=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt_{w_{k}}\left(\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}\Big(\Tr(\Sigma_{k}H^{-1}_{k})H^{-1}_{k}+2H^{-1}_{k}\Sigma_{k}H^{-1}_{k}\Big)\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\Tr(\Sigma_{k}H_{k}^{-1})+2\|H^{-1/2}\Sigma_{k}H^{-1/2}\|)\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt_{w_{k}}\|\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\|_{H_{k}^{-1}}^{2}

(59)

where we utilize the following inequality in the last step

\|x\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}\Sigma_{k}H_{k}^{-1}}=\Tr(H_{k}^{-1/2}xx^{\mathsf{T}}H_{k}^{-1/2}H_{k}^{-1/2}\Sigma_{k}H_{k}^{-1/2})\leq\|H_{k}^{-1/2}\Sigma_{k}H_{k}^{-1/2}\|\|x\|_{H^{-1}_{k}}^{2}.

For simplicity, we introduce the matrix
\Xi_{k}=H^{-1/2}_{k}\Sigma_{k}H^{-1/2}_{k}
. Plugging the previous sampling noise variance result (
49
), we establish

\displaystyle Q\leq(\Tr(\Xi_{k})+2\|\Xi_{k}\|)\Big(2\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}+\frac{2}{\beta_{\ell}m}(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})+\frac{2L^{2}}{\beta_{\ell}M}\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}/M\Big)

(60)

This
\Tr(\Xi_{k})+2\|\Xi_{k}\|
is the key quantity that we will encounter repeatedly. To further reduce the notation, we denote
\rho_{k}=\Tr(\Xi_{k})+2\|\Xi_{k}\|

Combining all the above results, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF({\bar{x}}_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\Big(\frac{\eta}{2}-2\eta^{2}\rho_{k}\Big)\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}+O(\eta^{2}\mu)

\displaystyle{}+\Big(\frac{\eta L^{2}}{2\beta_{\ell}M}+\frac{2\eta^{2}L^{2}\rho_{k}}{\beta_{\ell}M}\Big)\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}+\frac{2\eta^{2}\rho_{k}}{\beta_{\ell}m}(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})

(61)

When
\eta\leq\frac{1}{4\rho_{k}}
, the coefficients can be simplified into

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF({\bar{x}}_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\frac{\eta}{4}\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}+O(\eta^{2}\mu)

\displaystyle{}+\frac{\eta L^{2}}{\beta_{\ell}M}\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}+\frac{2\eta^{2}\rho_{k}}{\beta_{\ell}m}(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})

(62)

Communication Iteration:

When the iteration
k
is the communication iteration, i.e.
k\neq r\tau
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF({\bar{x}}_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\eta\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}H_{k}^{-1}\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})+O(\eta^{2}\mu)

\displaystyle{}+\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\left(\hat{x}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}-\eta\eta z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{k}\left(\hat{x}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}-\eta\eta z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\eta\overline{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}H_{k}^{-1}\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})+O(\eta^{2}\mu)

\displaystyle{}+2\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\left(\hat{x}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{k}\left(\hat{x}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\right)+2\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt[\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{k}z_{k}z_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{\nabla{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}}_{k})]

(63)

Next, we notice that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\left(\hat{x}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{k}\left(\hat{x}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\right)\leq

\displaystyle L\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|\hat{x}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\|^{2}=\frac{L}{mM}\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}

(64)

Utilizing previously established result Eq. (
61
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF({\bar{x}}_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\Big(\frac{\eta}{2}-4\eta^{2}\rho_{k}\Big)\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}+O(\eta^{2}\mu)

\displaystyle{}+\Big(\frac{L}{m}+\frac{\eta L^{2}}{2\beta_{\ell}}+\frac{4\eta^{2}L^{2}}{\beta_{\ell}M}\rho_{k}\Big)\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}+\frac{4\eta^{2}\rho_{k}}{\beta_{\ell}m}(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})

(65)

When
\eta\leq\frac{1}{8\rho_{k}}
, the coefficients can be simplified into

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF({\bar{x}}_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\frac{\eta}{4}\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}+O(\eta^{2}\mu)

\displaystyle{}+\Big(\frac{L}{mM}+\frac{\eta L^{2}}{\beta_{\ell}M}\Big)\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}+\frac{4\eta^{2}\rho_{k}}{\beta_{u}m}(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})

(66)

We further require the learning rate
\eta\leq\frac{\beta_{\ell}}{mL}
to establish

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF({\bar{x}}_{k+1})\leq

\displaystyle F({\bar{x}}_{k})-\frac{\eta}{4}\|\nabla F({\bar{x}}_{k})\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-1}}+O(\eta^{2}\mu)

\displaystyle{}+\frac{2L}{mM}\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}+\frac{4\eta^{2}\rho_{k}}{\beta_{\ell}m}(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})

(67)

Combining Two into One Round:

Combining the above two results and iterating from
k=r\tau+1
to
k=(r+1)\tau
, we establish

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF(\bar{x}_{(r+1)\tau+1})\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271ptF(\bar{x}_{r\tau+1})-\frac{\eta}{4}\sum_{j=r\tau+1}^{(r+1)\tau}\|\nabla F(\bar{x}_{j})\|^{2}_{H_{r}^{-1}}+O(\eta^{2}\mu)

\displaystyle{}+\frac{4\tau\eta^{2}\rho_{k}}{\beta_{\ell}m}(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})+\frac{2L}{mM}\sum_{j=r\tau+1}^{(r+1)\tau}\|{\bm{x}}_{j}-{\bar{x}}_{j}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}

(68)

where we can absorb the coefficients on the consensus term
\|{\bm{x}}_{j}-{\bar{x}}_{j}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}
into
2L/mM
since above we already require the learning rate
\eta\leq\frac{\beta_{\ell}}{mL}
. Also, we replace
H_{k}
by
H_{r}
since it is not updated within one communication round.

\hfill\qed

D.5
Consensus Lemma

Lemma 0
.

When
\eta\leq\frac{\beta_{\ell}}{4(\tau-1)}\sqrt{\frac{1}{L(d+2)}}
, the sum of the consensus error of one round is bounded by the following term

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=r\tau+1}^{(r+1)\tau}\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.42271pt\|{\bm{x}}_{k}-{\bar{x}}_{k}\mathds{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\|^{2}_{F}\leq 4\eta^{2}(\tau-1)^{2}M\beta_{\ell}^{-1}\|\Phi_{r}\|(\sigma_{G}^{2}+\sigma_{s}^{2})+O(\eta^{2}\mu^{2})

(69)

where
\Phi_{r}:=\Tr(H_{r}^{-1})+2H_{r}^{-1}
.
\hfill\qed

Proof

Appendix E
Multi-Perturbation Version

Following our detailed examination of ZO-gradient variance, it is evident that reducing this variance is crucial for enhancing the performance of ZO-based methods. In this context,
multi-perturbation sampling in ZO-SGD can be viewed as analogous to mini-batching in standard SGD
, where multiple samples are used to improve the quality of the gradient estimate.

In terms of HiSo, the multi-perturbation version is simply replacing the finding
\Delta x_{r,k}^{(i)}
step by the following:

\displaystyle\boxed{\begin{aligned} \mbox{for\ }p&\;=0,1\cdots,P-1:\\
&u_{r,k,p}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,I)\\
&g_{r,k,p}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{\mu}[f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)}+\mu H^{-1/2}_{r}u_{r,k,p})-f_{i}(x_{r,k}^{(i)})]\\
\Delta x_{r,k}^{(i)}&=H_{r}^{-1/2}\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=0}^{P-1}g_{r,k,p}^{(i)}u_{r,k,p}\end{aligned}}

(87)

Notice for the multi-perturbation version, we need to transmit
P
random seeds to generate
p
random vector
u_{r,k,p}
. Moreover,
P
local gradient scalars
g_{r,k,p}^{(i)}
are required to be communicated as well.

At the server side, the aggregation step now is required to average
P
values separately:

\displaystyle\boxed{\Delta x_{r,k}=\frac{1}{\tau|C_{r}|}\sum_{i\in C_{r}}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\Delta x_{r,k}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau-1}\Bigg[\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=0}^{P-1}\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{|C_{r}|}\sum_{i\in C_{r}}g_{r,k,p}^{(i)}\right)}_{:=g_{r,k,p}}H^{-1/2}_{r}u_{r,k,p}\Bigg]}

(88)

Notice we can switch the order of summation in above equations because
u_{r,k,p}
is common among all clients. This aggregated gradient scalar
g_{r,k,p}
stands for the
r
-th round,
k
-th local update, and
p
-th perturbation.
P
gradient scalars together with
P
random seeds are sufficient to reconstruct the global
\Delta x_{r,k}
. For the reconstruction step, everything is the same.

F.1
Baseline Selection

We select a broad range of classic baselines to cover both first-order and zeroth-order optimization methods commonly used in FL.

First-order methods:

FedAvg is the most classic first-order FL algorithm.
FedAdam, FedYogi and FedAdagrad are representatives of adaptive gradient-based methods designed to accelerate convergence.

Zeroth-order methods:

FedZO is the first FL method to incorporate ZO-SGD into client local updates.

First-order methods (e.g., FedAvg, FedAdam, FedYogi and FedAdagrad) consistently achieve high test accuracy, but at the cost of extremely large communication volumes, often exceeding hundreds of gigabytes to several terabytes per client.
This level of communication overhead is quite challenging and even impractical for real-world federated fine-tuning, especially on edge devices or mobile platforms.
For ZO baselines, FedZO’s communication cost is still quite high since it is required to transmit
d
-dimensional update.
DeComFL addresses this high communication cost by enabling the scalar-only communication pattern, achieving several orders of magnitude lower communication cost.
However, these ZO approaches suffer from limited optimization efficiency and often underperform in accuracy compared with first-order baselines, particularly on large-scale models and complex tasks.

Our proposed method, HiSo, is the first to break this trade-off.
It maintains the scalar-only or dimension-free communication paradigm, yet consistently outperforms ZO baselines in test accuracy.
For example, on SST-2 with the OPT-1.3B model, HiSo achieves 90.34% test accuracy—slightly lower than FedAdam (92.86%) but with a
10^{4}\times
reduction in communication (7.81 KB vs. 0.79 TB).
On QQP, HiSo also outperforms all ZO methods across all model sizes, achieving both higher accuracy and dramatically lower bandwidth usage.
A similar trend holds on the SQuAD dataset, where HiSo consistently surpasses ZO baselines in F1 score while maintaining kilobyte-level communication.
Notably, on OPT-350M and OPT-1.3B, HiSo not only outperforms ZO baselines in test accuracy but also achieves over 100
\times
less communication cost compared to first-order baselines.
Moreover, compared with the most related baseline - DeComFL, HiSo achieves higher test accuracy, faster convergence speed, and less communication overhead.

The key to this performance lies in HiSo’s Hessian-informed preconditioning and the use of multiple perturbations per round, which together yield more accurate ZO gradient estimates under low-rank curvature.
These results demonstrate that, contrary to conventional expectations, it is possible to achieve second-order convergence behavior with near-zero communication overhead—a major step toward practical and scalable federated fine-tuning of LLMs.
