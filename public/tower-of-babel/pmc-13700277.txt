Background

Relapsing polychondritis (RP) is a rare autoimmune disease characterized by recurrent inflammation and progressive destruction of cartilaginous structures throughout the body [1]. Airway involvement occurs in a substantial proportion of patients and may lead to laryngotracheal stenosis and tracheobronchomalacia, both of which are associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Management of airway involvement includes systemic corticosteroids, immunosuppressive therapy, and interventional procedures such as tracheostomy or airway stenting in severe cases [2]. Although airway stenting can improve airway patency, it is associated with complications including migration, obstruction, and granulation tissue formation.

Airway management in patients with tracheal stents presents unique challenges [3–9]. Stent migration, in particular, can significantly distort airway anatomy, making endotracheal intubation difficult and increasing the risk of ineffective ventilation. These challenges are further amplified in emergency settings.

We report a case of emergency airway management in a patient with RP complicated by tracheal stent migration, highlighting key technical challenges and clinical considerations.

Case presentation

Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient’s family.

A 71-year-old man (height: 167 cm; weight: 67 kg) with a known history of RP presented to the emergency department with respiratory distress and abdominal pain following a fall from approximately 7 m. He had undergone placement of a tracheobronchial stent at another institution one year earlier for airway collapse; details regarding the stent type and prior medical therapy were limited.

On presentation, the patient exhibited signs of respiratory failure, including tachypnea and hypoxemia (SpO₂: 90% on supplemental oxygen). His blood pressure was 96/59 mmHg, and his heart rate was 102 beats per minute. Arterial blood gas analysis was not obtained. Imaging findings were suggestive of intra-abdominal injury with suspected bleeding. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a 9.4-cm L-shaped metallic tracheobronchial stent located approximately 3 cm below the vocal cords, with distal extension into the left main bronchus (Fig. 1A-D, Supplemental Digital Content video 1). The stent had migrated cephalad. Notably, the lateral opening window-designed to align with the carina to allow ventilation of the right lung had shifted proximally, resulting in impaired right lung ventilation. Auscultation revealed markedly diminished breath sounds over the right lung field, consistent with functional obstruction. Due to progressive respiratory failure, emergency tracheal intubation was indicated and performed by an experienced anesthesiologist. Rapid sequence induction with cricoid pressure was undertaken due to the high risk of aspiration. Following induction, the patient was positioned supine. During video laryngoscopy, a large volume of gastric contents was observed regurgitating into the airway. A 7.0-mm endotracheal tube was rapidly inserted; however, resistance was encountered during advancement. The cuff was therefore inflated at the level of the vocal cords to limit aspiration (Fig. 1E). Suction-assisted laryngoscopy and airway decontamination (SALAD) were performed immediately [10, 11]. After complete clearance of gastric contents, the cuff was deflated. A flexible bronchoscope (outer diameter: 4 mm; reusable) was introduced to guide further advancement of the endotracheal tube. Bronchoscopy demonstrated misalignment between the tracheal lumen and the migrated stent. Under direct visualization, the endotracheal tube was redirected and successfully advanced into the stent lumen. Further bronchoscopic evaluation revealed significant airway collapse in the distal left bronchus, consistent with tracheobronchomalacia (Fig. 1F, Supplemental Digital Content video 2). The right bronchial tree could not be adequately visualized due to stent malposition.

Mechanical ventilation was initiated with a positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 8 cm H₂O to mitigate airway collapse. Despite these measures, ventilation remained suboptimal, with persistently reduced breath sounds over the right lung. An attempt to reposition the stent was performed by a specialist (a pulmonologist), but was unsuccessful. Rigid bronchoscopy was not performed due to the lack of equipment in the intensive care unit (ICU). The patient was subsequently managed in the ICU with ventilatory support and anti-inflammatory therapy. Despite these interventions, his condition deteriorated, and he developed severe pulmonary infection and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). He died two weeks after intubation.

Discussion

Airway involvement in RP is a major determinant of prognosis and may result in life-threatening complications due to progressive airway collapse [1–2]. Tracheobronchomalacia, caused by destruction of cartilaginous support, leads to dynamic airway narrowing and impaired ventilation. Airway stenting is an established therapeutic option for severe airway compromise; however, it is associated with complications, particularly stent migration. Migration can significantly alter airway anatomy and create substantial challenges for airway management.

In this case, a L-shaped membrane-covered metallic airway stent was placed to prevent airway collapse due to tracheobronchomalacia, the migration of the stent was confirmed by the opening window which allows air to continue flowing into right bronchus was moved away from its intended landmark (the carina). The membrane prevents the stent from “embedding” into the airway wall, there is a higher risk of the stent shifting especially under a deceleration injury, which block ventilation of the right lung in the present case.

Further, cephalad migration of the stent resulted in misalignment between the stent lumen and the native airway, making advancement of the endotracheal tube technically difficult. In addition, displacement of the stent’s lateral opening window impaired ventilation of the right lung. Underlying tracheobronchomalacia further exacerbated airway instability.

A key technical concern in such cases is inadvertent placement of the endotracheal tube between the stent and the airway wall rather than within the stent lumen. This may lead to ineffective ventilation and potential airway injury. Flexible bronchoscopy is therefore essential, as it allows real-time visualization and precise guidance of tube placement [5, 8, 9].

Only a few reports have described the anesthetic management of patients with RP. The risk justifies the use of anesthetic techniques that do not require tracheal intubation, therefore regional anesthesia is preferred for RP patients [4]. If general anesthesia is indispensable, a clear understanding of the degree of airway involvement is essential to proper intraoperative management. Given reported difficulties in airway management including: could not establish effective mask ventilation or laryngeal mask ventilation [5], laryngotracheal stricture [6], intubation caused tracheal tears [7], and subglottal swelling may lead to life-threatening postanesthesia deterioration of the respiratory system [8].

The occurrence of significant gastroesophageal regurgitation further complicated airway management. Immediate cuff inflation at the level of the vocal cords, followed by prompt suctioning, was critical in reducing the risk of aspiration. The SALAD technique proved valuable in this context [10].

Despite successful bronchoscopic intubation, ventilation remained compromised due to the combined effects of stent malposition and airway collapse. Attempts at stent repositioning were unsuccessful, underscoring the importance of early consideration of advanced airway interventions, such as rigid bronchoscopy, in similar scenarios. Previous reports have highlighted the complexity of airway management and the need for repeated airway interventions in patients with RP [9]. These findings emphasize the importance of multidisciplinary planning and preparedness for difficult airway management.

Conclusion

This case highlights the challenges of emergency airway management in patients with RP and tracheal stents, particularly when stent migration alters airway anatomy. Bronchoscopy-guided intubation is essential to ensure correct tube placement. Early recognition of stent-related complications and preparedness for advanced airway interventions are critical to optimizing patient outcomes.
