Developing a prediction model to predict the incidence of metastasis in lung cancer patients using artificial intelligence techniques
Authors: Mehrnaz Asadi Gharabaghi, Marsa Gholamzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIf metastasis can be predicted, appropriate management of lung cancer will be possible to improve prognosis. Next, Data normalization and feature engineering were applied. Imbalanced data was addressed using Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE). Introduction

According to a report by GLOBOCAN, lung cancer was the most commonly diagnosed cancer in 2022, with an increasing rate of incidence in recent years [1, 2]. Lung cancer accounted for approximately 2.5 million new cases of any cancer, or one in eight cancers worldwide (12.4% of all cancers globally) [3, 4]. Metastatic progression of lung cancer is a critical challenge in medicine [5]. One of the reasons for the high mortality rate in lung cancer is mainly due to the occurrence of metastasis before clinical symptoms appear and the late diagnosis [2, 6]. Usually, 40 to 50% of lung cancer patients die due to metastatic spread of the cancer, to the point where the survival rate does not exceed five years [5, 7]. These complications highlight the critical importance of early detection of metastasis in lung cancer [8]. If early detection of metastasis is possible, targeted interventions will be possible and cancer progression will be limited; in addition, the survival rate of patients will be significantly increased and treatment outcomes will be improved [9]. If metastasis could be predicted, comprehensive diagnostic approaches and personalized medicine strategies will be possible in the management of this disease and improve prognosis [10, 11].

The utilization of machine learning (ML) techniques in various fields of medicine has revolutionized the field of health sciences [12–15]. Recent advances in ML have revolutionized the study of metastasis by enabling complex analysis of multi-modal data, including genomic, imaging, and clinical datasets [16, 17]. Supervised learning models, such as random forests or decision tree algorithms, have been employed to predict metastatic risk using laboratory data, gene expression profiles, or radiomic features extracted from CT scans [18]. Unsupervised techniques, including clustering algorithms applied to single-cell RNA sequencing data, have identified metastatic subpopulations and novel biomarkers [19]. Such approaches enhance diagnostic accuracy and enable the early prediction of metastasis, thereby employing such innovative approaches can assist physicians in designing more personalized and effective treatments. These technologies are particularly valuable in aggressive cancers that metastasize rapidly, such as lung, breast, and colorectal cancers [10, 20]. Despite these advancements, challenges persist in model interpretability, dataset heterogeneity, and clinical translation.

Therefore, the use of machine learning methods in predicting lung cancer metastasis can also significantly improve the accuracy and speed of diagnosis. By analyzing a huge amount of clinical, laboratory, and disease history data, machine learning algorithms are able to identify complex patterns and risk factors associated with metastasis that may be hidden from traditional methods [21]. This approach not only aid clinicians to make personalized treatment decisions, but also allows for earlier diagnosis and more accurate prediction of disease progression [22]. As a result, applying these technologies and the creation of a predictive model can lead to improved treatment outcomes, reduced care costs, and increased life expectancy for patients who suffer from lung cancer. Therefore, the main objective of this study is to develop a prediction model with appropriate performance for predicting lung cancer metastasis to other organs based on patient data.

Literature review

Machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) methods have been proved significant potential in predicting and characterizing lung cancer metastasis [19, 23–25]. These approaches enable the development of clinical tools to identify high-risk patients and optimize personalized treatment strategies [26]. Random Forest (RF) models are a type of ensemble learning algorithm that combines multiple decision trees for prediction [27]. The constructed “forest” is actually a group of “decision trees” [28]. According to a study by Munai et al. [23], the RF model has shown strong performance in predicting brain metastases in extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC), and achieved an AUROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve) of 0.896 using clinical features such as bone metastasis, extended liver metastasis, and treatment history. In other studies, ML has emerged as a powerful tool for predicting metastatic risk and outcomes, with extreme gradient boosting (XGB) models achieving AUC values of up to 0.96 in predicting the risk of metastasis in patients with rectal cancer [29], and showing strong performance (AUC 0.786) in predicting 1-year survival of NSCLC patients with bone metastasis in some studies [30].

ML models employ feature selection techniques like LASSO regression and permutation significance analysis to identify key predictive variables, including tumor stage, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels, and histological subtypes. These variables collectively explain 78–92% of the predictive power in metastasis risk assessment [29, 31]. Deep learning approaches, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have been applied to predict malignancy and metastasis in pulmonary nodules on CT imaging data, with models with AUROCs of 80.37% for malignancy and 86.44% for metastasis outperforming junior physicians and matching senior radiologists in diagnostic accuracy [25]. These ML-based insights into metastatic mechanisms pave the way for personalized treatment strategies and early intervention protocols, although further validation is needed to ensure clinical applicability.

Methodology

Initially, the patient’s records data were retrieved from the open-source Kaggle database. Because the data was taken from an open-source platform, it does not contain identifiable information. Therefore, specific details about the study setting (e.g., primary or secondary care) were not available. However, the dataset included a wide range of clinical and demographic variables from a large cohort of lung cancer patients.

In initial phase of analysis, to understand data elements and have deep knowledge of the dataset, exploratory data analysis (EDA) and descriptive analysis were performed. Then, data preprocessing as the fundamental step for developing machine learning models was performed and unnecessary features were removed. The data preprocessing process involves data cleaning, data integration, data transformation, data reduction or dimensionality reduction. As it is shown in Fig. 1, steps such as classification model training, model evaluation, algorithm tuning, comparing model performance were done one after the other to achieve the best model. The experiments were conducted using Python 3.8] with the related machine learning libraries. All code was executed on a Google Collab with 16 GB RAM, Intel i7 processor.

Preprocessing process

First, the features investigated by pulmonologists and unnecessary features such as ID column was deleted. To handle missing values, the dataset was examined for the presence of missing values in each column using imputation methods. Then all nominal categorical variables were encoded to convert them to numerical values. Outliers and duplicated values were omitted to handle outliers. Correlation analysis was completed to investigate the correlation between potential features and target value. The primary outcome of interest was the incidence of metastasis, defined as the presence of metastases to other organs (yes/no) based on clinical assessments in patients’ medical records.

Due to the presence of different measuring units, the data normalization method was performed using the StandardScaler function, which standardizes variables by scaling to unit variance. Due to the high dimension of a dataset, feature engineering and feature selection was performed to reduce the number of features.

Feature engineering

Since features have a significant role in model accuracy, feature engineering has become indispensable in machine learning projects. In this phase, new feature is created based on two other features which have the same meanings. Next, feature importance technique was employed to rank and select most important features. One effective method for selecting the most important and relevant features is to use a random forest classifier model, which provides insights into the importance of the features assigning ranks to each feature.

Managing imbalanced data

The employed dataset was not balanced, which means that most of the records belonged to one category. Classification of imbalanced data is biased towards the majority categories. The symmetric minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) was applied to the whole dataset to facilitate the performance of various classifiers.

Model development and tuning

Since the main objective of our study is to predict metastasis or in other words the occurrence of metastasis and the absence of metastasis, supervised classification models were used in this survey. After splitting data, 80% of the data was used for training the model and 20% of the data was used for testing. A diverse set of machine learning algorithms to develop classification task were selected. Based on multiple methodological approaches, eight prominent machine learning algorithms were selected to predict target value, including several prevalent classification models such as Decision Tree classifiers, Random Forest, and Boosting algorithms. Evaluation metrics and explainability analysis

To identify the optimal predictive model, we comprehensively evaluated all developed machine learning models using multiple performance metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, Confusion Matrix, and ROC-AUC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve). Model performance assessment necessitates a multifaceted evaluation approach, as no single metric can comprehensively capture predictive efficacy. While accuracy quantifies the proportion of correctly classified instances, ROC-AUC provides a more nuanced assessment of the model’s discriminative capabilities across different class boundaries. By systematically employing diverse evaluation metrics derived from the Confusion Matrix—specifically precision, recall, and F1-Score—we critically examined the trade-offs between false positive and false negative predictions. To mitigate potential overfitting and ensure robust model validation, we implemented stratified k-fold cross-validation using 5 and 10 folds, generating a more reliable and statistically sound performance estimation.

Traditional machine learning models often lack interpretability, which has led to the development of explainable AI techniques such as SHAP (Shapley Incremental Description). A leading framework for model independence, SHAP interprets machine learning outputs by quantifying the contribution of individual features to predictions, based on collaborative game theory and Shapley values [32]. Unlike alternative approaches, SHAP provides accurate and consistent explanations by fairly distributing the predictive influence across features, ensuring mathematical clarity and precision [33]. This approach is particularly valuable for interpreting complex models such as XGBoost, as it reveals how each feature—both positive and negative—shapes individual predictions, bridging the gap between performance and interpretability.

The overall rate of metastasis in our dataset was 25.11% (i.e., 75,330 out of 300,000 patients had metastasis), which was the primary endpoint of this study.

Data preprocessing

After consulting domain experts, irrelevant features were systematically removed from the entire dataset. Missing data were imputed using median imputation to maintain dataset integrity. Categorical variables were encoded to numeric categories using label encoding. We engineered a novel smoking intensity level metric by combining relevant features from the lung cancer dataset to potentially enhance model performance.

Investigate the correlation between predictive variables and target value using heatmap analysis, followed by feature selection with Random Forest’s feature importance ranking technique which objectively evaluated each variable’s predictive power. After careful analysis, 15 most discriminative features were identified and visualized in Fig. 3. Then, for feature scaling, we applied StandardScaler with zero mean and unit variance normalization to standardize variables and mitigate potential bias.

The dataset was stratified into training and testing subsets using an 80:20 split to ensure representative sampling. Initial analysis revealed an imbalanced distribution of the target variable (metastasis incidence). To address this problem, Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was implemented exclusively on the training dataset, which generated synthetic samples to balance class representation without introducing bias to the test set. This preprocessing approach was specifically designed to prevent overfitting and maintain the statistical integrity of our subsequent machine learning model.

These models were trained using Python to develop a robust classification approach. A confusion matrix was generated for each model, and standard evaluation metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score) were calculated. Initial performance metrics were assessed using the imbalanced dataset. Subsequently, the SMOTE algorithm was applied to address class imbalance, recalculating model performance after oversampling. The evaluation results demonstrated significant improvements across all metrics after implementing SMOTE in terms of accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Discussion

Developing a robust prediction model for lung cancer metastasis to other organs based on patient data is a critical step towards improving early diagnosis and treatment outcomes. In this study, we implemented and evaluated several machine learning models to predict the most effective approach and compared their performance with various metrics. The evaluation results highlight the importance of addressing and considering the solution to the class imbalance problem and demonstrate the superior performance of the XGBoost model over other models, which consistently outperformed other classifiers in the evaluation metrics.

A major challenge in developing predictive models for medical datasets, such as those involving metastases, is the inherent class imbalance of the target variable, where the population of the minority class (e.g., patients with metastases) is often smaller than that of others with metastasis. This imbalance can lead to biased models that favor the majority class, resulting in poor prediction performance for the minority class. In this study, initial evaluations using the imbalanced dataset showed suboptimal performance on the original data, with AUC values averaging around 0.56 and moderate F1 scores. These findings are consistent with previous research that emphasized the detrimental effects of class imbalance on model performance [34]. To overcome this issue, we applied the artificial minority oversampling technique (SMOTE), which artificially balances the dataset by generating artificial samples from the minority class [35]. These results highlight the effectiveness of SMOTE in enhancing model performance and its critical role in developing reliable predictive models for imbalanced medical datasets [36].

This superior performance can be attributed to XGBoost’s ability to handle complex and nonlinear relationships in the data through an ensemble approach, which combines multiple weak learners to create a robust predictive model [37]. XGBoost’s internal regularization and gradient-based optimization techniques help prevent overfitting, a common challenge in machine learning. ROC curve analysis confirmed XGBoost’s high discriminatory power, evidenced by the highest true positive rate (TPR) and the lowest false positive rate (FPR) at optimal cutoff points, crucial for accurate lung cancer metastasis to other organs prediction. The high F1 score achieved by XGBoost indicates a balance between precision and recall, enhancing its reliability for clinical decision support. While XGBoost outperformed other models, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting also showed commendable performance, although slightly lower. This aligns with previous studies highlighting the advantages of ensemble methods [38]. Basic models like logistic regression and decision trees demonstrated relatively lower performance in AUC and F1 scores.

The results of this study align with previous findings on the development of machine learning models to predict metastasis. The performance of XGBoost in our study is particularly noteworthy when compared to similar studies in this context. For example, Zhang et al. (2020) [39] developed a prediction model for lung cancer metastasis to other organs using a combination of clinical and imaging data, achieving an AUC of 78% with a Random Forest classifier. Although their results are acceptable, our study demonstrates that XGBoost can achieve even higher performance (AUC = 82%) when combined with Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), highlighting the potential of advanced ensemble methods in classification tasks.

However, it is important to note that the performance of machine learning models can vary depending on the dataset and underlying subjects. For instance, Yuan et al. (2019) [40] developed a Gradient Boosting model with an AUC of 0.843 for predicting metastasis in patients with ovarian cancer achieved by XGBoost in our study. These comparisons underscore the effectiveness of our approach and the potential of XGBoost as a robust tool for predicting lung cancer metastasis to other organs. In another study, Qiu et al. [29] revealed that XGB model achieved a best performance among other ML models for predicting the risk of metastasis in patients with rectal cancer. Additionally, the development of a high-performing prediction model for metastasis has significant implications for clinical practice. Early and accurate prediction of metastasis can enable timely interventions, potentially improve patient outcomes and reduce healthcare costs.

Since the XGBoost model showed great potential in predicting lung cancer metastasis to other organs due to its ability to handle complex, high-dimensional datasets while minimizing overfitting, SHAP was used to interpret this model. Using SHAP (Shapley Augmented Description) values, we can interpret the model’s decisions and identify key predictive features, such as tomo size or clinical markers, that contribute most to metastatic risk. This transparency increases clinical utility and enables clinicians to prioritize high-risk patients for targeted interventions. Furthermore, XGBoost’s robustness to imbalanced data—common in medical datasets—makes it particularly well-suited for metastasis prediction, where early detection is crucial but positive cases may be rare. According to the findings of this study, overall, XGBoost, combined with SHAP analysis, offers a powerful tool for advancing personalized oncology and improving metastatic risk classification.

This aligns with prior studies highlighting XGBoost’s efficacy in handling heterogeneous medical data [33, 41, 42], though our work extends these findings by explicitly quantifying feature importance for metastatic risk via SHAP, a methodological advance over prior black-box applications. Notably, our model identified tumor size as the strongest predictor, corroborating recent evidence of its prognostic role [43, 44] while also revealing novel associations, such as BMI, which has not been previously linked to metastasis in lung cancer. Although this finding may initially seem counterintuitive, it is consistent with recent findings from studies that suggest that metabolic factors, including body composition and visceral fat, can significantly influence cancer progression through biological mechanisms [45, 46].

The key novelty of our approach lies in the integration of explainable AI (XAI) with high-performance ensemble learning, bridging the gap between predictive accuracy and clinical interpretability—a critical limitation in earlier machine learning-based metastasis models [47]. However, external validation in multi-institutional datasets is needed. Second, while SHAP clarifies feature contributions, biological causality remains speculative without experimental validation.

Limitation

While the results are promising, this study also has some limitations. First, the study was conducted on a single-center dataset, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, our dataset did not include detailed information on the specific sites of metastasis (e.g., brain, bone, liver), which restricted our ability to evaluate the model’s performance in predicting organ-specific metastatic patterns—a clinically relevant outcome. Future studies incorporating site-specific metastasis data could further refine the utility of such predictive tools. Third, although the role of higher cancer stage as a predictor of poorer survival has been noted in several previous studies, our analysis did not reveal a significant association between cancer stage and survival outcomes in our dataset. This unexpected finding may be related to several factors, including an unbalanced distribution of patients across different stage subgroups or the presence of unmeasured confounding variables that were not captured in our dataset. We acknowledge this as a limitation of our study and recommend that future research investigate this relationship with larger, more evenly distributed cohorts. Fourth, while we focused on TNM staging as the primary prognostic factor (given its established role in treatment decisions), the inclusion of diverse non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) histologic subtypes in a unified cohort may overlook subtle biologic differences. However, evidence suggests that histologic subtyping has limited prognostic impact within the same TNM stage, supporting our approach. Future research should be conducted on larger and more diverse datasets to ensure robustness. Lastly, the computational cost of training XGBoost and other boosting algorithms can be prohibitive, especially when modeling on very large datasets. Finally, future studies could include integrating additional techniques, such as feature selection or hybrid sampling methods, to enhance the performance of the model.

Further suggestion

We recommend that our predictive model for lung cancer metastasis be used at multiple key points in clinical practice: initially at diagnosis to guide staging and treatment planning, after definitive therapy to inform surveillance and follow-up strategies, and, if feasible, at various time points during ongoing follow-up as new clinical data become available. This approach aligns with current evidence and maximizes the model’s clinical relevance by enabling dynamic, personalized risk assessment and timely intervention throughout the patient’s care course. Such efforts could translate our tool into clinical decision-support systems, ultimately personalizing surveillance strategies for high-risk patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results are consistent with and build upon prior research in the field of lung metastasis prediction. The superior performance of XGBoost, combined with the effectiveness of SMOTE in addressing class imbalance, provides a robust framework for developing predictive models in similar medical contexts. However, further research is needed to explore hybrid approaches, improve model interpretability, and validate the proposed approach on larger, more diverse datasets.
