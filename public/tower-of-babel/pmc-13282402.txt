A self healable dielectric elastomer artificial muscle
Authors: Jie Mao, Jiahao Li, Xiaorong Dou, Huiyao Zhao, Peiyao Zhong, Li-Juan Yin, Yingwu Luo, Qibing Pei, Zhi-Min Dang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDielectric elastomers are soft electroactive polymers capable of large-strain actuation with the moniker artificial muscle. However, current dielectric elastomers exhibit limited operational stability when operated at strain and energy density in the neighborhood of natural muscles, due to mechanical fatigue or electrical breakdown. In this work, we design a dielectric elastomer comprising a bimodal network structure and zwitterionic side groups to overcome electro-mechanical instability and enable self-healing. The resulting material demonstrates a stably operable strain of 125% at a field of 25 MV m−1 over 150,000 cycles and can heal from mechanical or electrical damage for longer lifespan. It allows the demonstration of bionic arms and grippers outperforming human arms and hands and capable of recovery from mechanical damage and electrical breakdown.Subject terms: Polymers, Actuators

Introduction

Natural muscles exhibit several key characteristics, including self-adjustable stiffness, high stress output (~0.35 MPa), rapid response speed, and self-healing capabilities1–4. These critical properties are missing in synthetic systems such as prosthetic limbs, exoskeletons, and soft robots5,6. As functional analogs to natural muscles7, artificial muscles (AMs) represent a class of flexible intelligent materials or actuation devices capable of biomimetic movements7,8, such as extension, bending, and torsion under external stimuli9,10 (Fig. 1a). Various smart materials and actuators have been developed for artificial muscles11. Among these, dielectric elastomers (DEs) have attracted attention thanks to their versatile control with electric field and high electro-mechanical energy conversion efficiency12,13. A basic dielectric elastomer actuator (DEA), composed of a dielectric elastomer film and two flexible electrodes, operates on a principle similar to that of a parallel-plate capacitor. When an external electric field stimulates the upper and lower flexible electrodes of the DEA, positive and negative charges accumulate on the upper and lower surfaces of the dielectric material, respectively. These charges attract each other, generating Maxwell stress within the material (Supplementary Fig. 1b). DEA has shown promise in various applications with laboratory success14–18. However, commercialization and practical use of DEAs remain elusive due to a lack of stable operability and durability at a performance level comparable to natural muscles19.

A critical challenge for DEAs is their vulnerability to electromechanical degradation during sustained operation. In practical applications, dielectric elastomers are often required to maintain large deformations (>100% areal strain) over extended periods, which can lead to electromechanical fatigue, dielectric breakdown, and irreversible material damage. These failure modes stem from two primary factors, the first of which is electromechanical instability induced by coupled electromechanical effects or competition between electric field forces and elastic restoring forces. When electric field forces exceed the elastic restoring forces, the film collapses in the thickness direction20–24, causing instantaneous dielectric breakdown in the dielectric elastomer and ultimately limiting the actuation performance and operational stability. The second factor is the molecular-chain scission, creep, and electrode delamination induced by cyclic loading and Joule heating25. The combined action of cyclic high voltage and mechanical stretching induces molecular chain scission, while mechanical cycling-induced creep and stress relaxation further significantly diminish fatigue life. When the cross-linked and entangled network is stretched, localized stress first develops at its cross-linking points. Once the yield strength is exceeded, a stress-concentration effect arises and induces main-chain scission26–28. Concurrently, under high electric fields, leakage current induces Joule heating accumulation, accelerating molecular chain scission and electrode-dielectric interface degradation15,27. Conventional strategies have exposed inherent limitations in simultaneously achieving stability and high performance for long-term dielectric elastomer service. This compels a paradigm shift from passively delaying damage to actively repairing damage, where integrating self-healing functionality with superior electromechanical properties emerges for developing durable, high-performance artificial muscles.

Hitherto, current strategies for enhancing dielectric elastomers' stability and durability have mainly pursued two approaches, increasing dielectric constant to lower driving fields and reducing Joule heating, thus preventing thermal breakdown or improving electrode-elastomer interfaces through compliant electrode development to extend lifetime29. Introducing self-healing functionality into dielectric actuators is highly challenging, as the key lies in the difficulty of balancing self-healing with dielectric properties. Although previous studies have focused on developing self-healing dielectric actuators 29–31, such as Hunt et al., who employed a two-phase dielectric structure by constructing a fluid-sponge composite as the dielectric layer to achieve self-healing through the dynamic fluidity of the material, existing systems often suffer from issues including limited actuation strain and insufficient healing efficiency under high strain, despite achieving self-healing capability. Although self-healing polymers/elastomers based on mechanisms such as hydrogen bonds32, π–π stacking33, and supramolecular assembly34 have also made progress, none of these have focused on optimizing dielectric properties. For example, researchers such as Li35 demonstrated that incorporating dynamic disulfide bonds into poly(urethane-urea) (PUU)-based dielectric elastomers imparts self-healing properties to the material. However, this significantly reduces the dielectric breakdown strength of the elastomer, resulting in relatively poor electromechanical performance. Consequently, how to endow dielectric elastomers with self-healing properties to extend their service life while maintaining high actuation performance is a critical challenge for advancing artificial muscle technologies.

Herein, we report a strategy for simultaneously improving the dielectric breakdown strength and actuation strain of dielectric elastomers with self-healing capability via introducing the zwitterion (sulfobetaine methacrylate, SB) into the bimodal network of dielectric elastomers. Zwitterions contain both cationic and anionic groups within a single molecule, exhibiting the strong electrostatic interactions generated by the oppositely charged ions of zwitterionic groups between adjacent polymer chains, which confer the material with self-healing capabilities after mechanical and electrical damage (Fig. 1b, c). Moreover, the zwitterion groups introduce deep traps inside the material to capture free charge carriers, thus reducing leakage current and enhancing the breakdown strength of dielectric elastomer to about 95 MV m−1, which is twice that of conventional acrylic elastomers (Fig. 1d, e). As a result, the dielectric elastomer with zwitterionic side groups exhibits an ultrahigh area strain of up to 272%, high specific power of 1520 W kg−1 and the specific energy of 152 J kg−1, surpassing most of the previously reported dielectric elastomers (Fig. 1f). Furthermore, we demonstrate a dielectric elastomer artificial muscle with intrinsic self-healing capability and a soft gripper maintaining object manipulation capability after healing from cactus spine penetration.

Results

Design of bimodal-zwitterionic network dielectric elastomers

To optimize the mechanical properties of dielectric elastomers, we fabricated a series of dielectric elastomer films featuring a bimodal network structure and zwitterionic side groups via photoinitiated free-radical polymerization (Supplementary Fig. 1). The samples are designated as P(BA-xSB), where x represents the weight fraction (wt%) of SB in the dielectric elastomer (Supplementary Table 1 for formulations) and the FTIR spectra and gel fraction of these samples are provided in Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3. The incorporation of varying zwitterionic content and bimodal network structure significantly altered the stress-strain behavior of dielectric elastomers (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). The bimodal network design, achieved using cross-linkers of disparate chain lengths, leverages long-chain segments for large deformations, while short-chain segments enhance modulus at moderate strains15. This unique polymer network structure topology imparts a strain-hardening effect that counteracts rising Maxwell stress and overcomes electromechanical instability, enabling large and stable electro-actuation deformations (Supplementary Figs. 4c and 5). Furthermore, the electrostatic interactions of zwitterionic groups could establish physical cross-links between the adjacent polymer chains, resulting in exceptionally low mechanical loss (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e). The molecular dynamics simulations (detailed in Supplementary Information, Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7) validated the experimental results. The simulation results revealed the changes in sulfobetaine (SB) incorporation on total energy, kinetic energy, and potential energy. Critically, the non-bond energy of the dielectric elastomer with zwitterionic side groups was more than double that of one without. This stronger negative value indicates enhanced non-bond interactions driven by electrostatic forces between charged functional groups.

Mechanism of Zwitterion boosts dielectric breakdown strength

The dielectric constant (εr) and breakdown strength (Eb) are key parameters governing the specific energy density of actuation. The dielectric constant increases steadily with higher zwitterionic (SB) content (Supplementary Fig. 8), primarily attributed to enhanced polarization from dipoles of zwitterion charged groups. Weibull analysis of electrical breakdown data confirmed high reliability, with all materials exhibiting shape parameters (γ) > 10 (Fig. 2a). All zwitterion-modified P(BA-SB) copolymers exhibit significantly higher Eb than PBA (45 MV m−1). Specifically, Eb increased with SB content up to 1 wt%, yielding a maximum of about 95 MV m−1 for P(BA-1SB), before decreasing at higher SB concentrations. This non-monotonic behavior demonstrates the strong yet composition-dependent influence of copolymerized zwitterion on Eb. Comparative testing confirmed networks with various zwitterionic side groups consistently exhibited higher breakdown strength than those without (Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating the effectiveness and versatility of enhancing dielectric breakdown strength via zwitterion modified strategy.

The leakage current analysis (Fig. 2b) revealed that P(BA-SB) maintains significantly lower current density than PBA at high fields, consistent with its superior Eb. The charge transport in DE follows a hopping conduction model where trap characteristics dictate hopping distance36. Zwitterion-free PBA showed a hopping distance of 4.9 nm, while P(BA-1SB) networks minimized this distance to 1.4 nm (~65% reduction) (Supplementary Fig. 10), confirming increased trap density and deeper traps that substantially improve the breakdown strength (Eb). To further validate the trapping mechanism, the thermally stimulated depolarization current (TSDC) measurements (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 11) demonstrate that SB units form efficient charge trapping centers within the polymer matrix. These traps immobilize free charges/holes and dissipate carrier energy through collision, scattering, and trapping. This suppresses secondary ionization, avalanche breakdown, and electrical treeing, and ultimately enhances the dielectric breakdown strength (Eb). However, excessive SB content forms electron transfer channels (Supplementary Fig. 12), causing direct charge transit and degraded dielectric breakdown strength. The electronic structure calculations (including electrostatic potentials, Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital, LUMO/Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital, HOMO, Density of States) also provide trapping mechanistic insights. The zwitterionic groups in P(BA-SB) exhibit intensified electrostatic potential gradients (Supplementary Fig. 13), enhancing charge trapping and impeding charge migration under the electric field. Lower LUMO (–0.64 eV vs. –0.53 eV) and higher HOMO (–5.69 eV vs. –5.75 eV) levels compared to PBA (Supplementary Fig. 13b and c) indicate enhanced electron and hole trapping capability of P(BA-SB), respectively. Simulation results demonstrate that zwitterionic side groups (SB) introduce charge traps into the dielectric elastomer network. UV–Vis spectroscopy confirms a reduction in optical bandgap (5.43 eV→5.37 eV, Supplementary Fig. 13d, e), consistent with computational predictions.

Electrical actuation properties

Electro-actuation performance is a critical metric for assessing dielectric elastomers as artificial muscles. We use the home-made dome shape device to test the electro-actuated area strain without pre-stretch (Supplementary Fig. 14). Owing to the enhanced dielectric breakdown strength, P(BA-1SB) achieves 272% electroactive area strain at nominal electric field of 32.5 MV m−1(equivalent to a real electric field strength of 96.8 MV m−1) (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 15a, b and Supplementary Movie 1), which is 5 times greater than that of PBA. The electromechanical sensitivity (βes), which critically indicates electro-actuation performance of dielectric elastomers, of P(BA-1SB) is 65, while the value for PBA is only 10 (Fig. 2e). The electroactive area strain (discrete points) is plotted alongside the analytical predictions derived from the electromechanical-coupling theory developed by Suo et al.3 (solid curves; Supplementary Fig. 15d), which shows high electromechanical sensitivity but suppressed instability of P(BA-1SB). The electro-actuation performance of our material at lower electric fields surpasses that of the most previously reported dielectric elastomers (Supplementary Fig. 15c).

The static blocked output force was measured using a high-precision force sensor fixed at a constant height above the actuator during deformation under an applied electric field (Supplementary Fig. 15e, f). The actuator generated sufficient forces to lift weights 320 times its own mass (Fig. 2f, for detailed experimental procedures, please refer to the Supplementary Information). Integrating the force-displacement curve and normalizing by actuator mass yielded an experimental energy density of 23.5 J kg−1, exceeding that of typical biological muscles (8 J kg−1). The specific energy and power of P (BA-1SB) at dynamic cyclic response state were evaluated using a pure shear type DEA (Supplementary Fig. 16a). In Fig. 2g, h, we investigated the specific energy and power under varying frequencies and loads. The actuator attained maximum specific energy of 152 J kg−1 and specific power of 1520 W kg−1 at 5 Hz with a 115 g load, which is nearly four times higher than that of natural muscle (Supplementary Fig. 16c–e and Supplementary Movie 2). Implementing multilayer designs of planar DEAs (Supplementary Fig. 17) can boost the actuator output, enabling 1.3 kg payload lifting (Supplementary Fig. 16b and Supplementary Movie 2). Overall, P(BA-1SB) combines large area strain, high specific energy, and high specific power, outperforming natural muscle and most dielectric elastomers (Fig. 2i).

Self-healing properties and high-performance long cyclic lifespan

We selected the P(BA-1SB) material with high electroactivity to evaluate the self-healing properties of dielectric elastomers incorporating zwitterionic side groups. The electrostatic interactions between the positively and negatively charged groups in zwitterions provide the intrinsic driving force for self-healing. In addition, the physical cross-linking formed by electrostatic interactions between zwitterions enables higher healing efficiency at 60 °C (Zwitterionic groups serve as strong physical cross-linking points within the polymer network. These cross-links are formed through ionic interactions, which stabilize the network structure at low temperatures. When the temperature rises to 60 °C, the enhanced mobility of polymer chain segments enables the reformation of broken ionic bonds). Figure 3a shows the healing of a crack (about 80 μm) observed under a microscope. The healing is completed in ~60 h. The fabrication process of the film crack and a discussion of its geometric properties are provided in Supplementary Fig. 18. Figure 3b, c show the mechanical properties of P(BA-1SB) elastomer films in their initial state and after different healing times. As healing time increases, the mechanical properties of healed damaged film closely approximate those of an undamaged film, achieving a recovery efficiency of 90% in 60 h. Macroscale recovery is demonstrated by healed dumbbell-shaped films regaining weightlifting, twisting, and stretching capabilities (Supplementary Fig. 18a).

To understand the driving force for the self-healing, we conducted temperature-dependent FTIR spectroscopy (40–160 °C, with 20 °C increments) of the P(BA-1SB) film. As temperature rises, the vibrational frequency of the SO3− group shifts to higher wavenumbers, while the C−N+ bond shifts to lower wavenumbers (Fig. 3d, e and Supplementary Fig. 19). This suggests a shift toward ion-pair formation of the SO3− and [N(CH₂)₂(CH₃)₃]⁺ ions at higher temperature37. Due to the exceptionally strong hydrophilicity of zwitterions, even when incorporated into polymer networks, a small fraction of zwitterionic moieties can absorb ambient moisture. At ambient temperature, both the anionic and cationic groups within zwitterions may form hydration complexes with water molecules. Upon heating, the reduction of water molecules disrupts these hydration shells, leading to a dramatic enhancement of electrostatic interactions between the oppositely charged groups. The intensified electrostatic interactions between opposite ions promote physical crosslinking and facilitate the realization of self-healing behavior. 2D correlation spectroscopy (2DCOS) from temperature-variable FTIR was used to clarify the thermal response sequence of different groups (Fig. 3f, g). According to Noda’s criteria and the signs in the synchronous and asynchronous spectra38, the order of response to increasing temperature for different groups is:1460 → 1072 → 1456 → 1128 cm⁻¹ (→ means prior to; see Table S4 for details). This sequence (sulfobetaine-sulfobetaine ion pairs > unpaired sulfobetaine) indicates the presence of abundant electrostatic interactions within the polymer network. Systematic assessments across varied environments quantified how conditions impact repair. We measured mechanical property recovery, documented the healing process (Supplementary Figs. 20–22), and evaluated restored electromechanical function. Experiments confirmed that, compared to other repair conditions, elevated temperatures were confirmed to accelerate healing kinetics and optimize the recovery of performance (Supplementary Fig. 23). To study the healing of P(BA-SB) from electrical damage, we conducted the electrical treeing experiment as shown in Supplementary Fig. 24. The material was subjected to electrical trees through needle-tip discharge, causing dendritic void-like defects. After 30 h of self-healing at 60 °C, the main branch dimensions shrank and the small electrical trees completely healed (Fig. 3h, i). Additionally, we supplemented the study with microscopic images of electrical tree healing at 40 °C and 100 °C to examine the influence of temperature on the self-repair process (Supplementary Fig. 24b and Supplementary Fig. 24c). The results demonstrate that the self-healing process is facilitated by enhanced polymer chain mobility at higher temperature, with electrostatic interactions between zwitterionic moieties providing the intrinsic driving force for microdefect repair.

Since dielectric breakdown induces progressive carbonization during electrical cycling, it drives cumulative damage that reduces the dielectric properties versus pristine states (Supplementary Fig. 25a). While the P(BA-1SB) retained 75% of its original breakdown strength even after two cycles of electrical breakdown healing (Supplementary Fig. 25b). It is noteworthy that the lifespan of the dielectric elastomer film directly determines durability of the actuator and its overall cost. Under an electric field of 25 MV m−1, the cyclic lifespan of PBA can reach 30,000 cycles when dielectric breakdown may take place. Unlike conventional dielectric elastomers, where such an event leads to terminal failure, P(BA-SB) self-heals via electrostatic interactions between the positively and negatively charged ionic groups in zwitterions, which extend the lifespan to over 180,000-cycles in total (Fig. 3j). This is a dielectric elastomer offering greater than 100% actuation strain with a long operational lifetime (Fig. 3k).

Demonstration and application of bioinspired muscles and soft grippers

A DEA arm was fabricated to demonstrate the electromechanical performance of P(BA-1SB) as an artificial muscle. The dielectric elastomer was positioned between the upper and lower arms of a connected artificial limb, as depicted in Fig. 4a. Under applied electric fields, the dielectric elastomer (DE) undergoes area expansion, thereby driving controlled arm articulation. To begin with, we tested the swing amplitude of artificial arms with different masses under an electric field (Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27) and selected the arm with a mass of 50.5 g for the following experiments. We demonstrate the integrated functionality of this actuator by coupling its electromechanical response with intrinsic self-healing properties of the material. As shown in Fig. 4c, the artificial muscle-driven arm achieved a maximum swing angle of 21° at an initial frequency of 2.5 Hz and a field of 25 kV m−1. Subsequently, as shown in Fig. 4b, we simulated mechanical damage to the film in the active area by puncturing it with a fine needle. Due to the destruction of the work area, the film cannot undergo electroactive deformation, and the arm cannot swing. After a period of self-healing (about 40 h, 60 °C), these minor damages were repaired, and the arm could still raise up, with a reduced swing angle of approximately 13° (Supplementary Movie 3). During long term operation of the artificial muscle, the electrical breakdown of the elastomer film is inevitable (the occurrence of such electrical damage is entirely random in timing), as shown in Fig. 4b. After the breakdown and subsequent repair over a certain period (about 40 h, 60 °C), the film was reassembled into the artificial arm, which could still raise up (Fig. 4b), however, the puncture destroyed some of the chemical cross-linking points. The subsequent repair relied on weaker electrostatic interactions between zwitterions. The swing amplitude was reduced. As shown in Fig. 4d, regardless of the type of damage, the swing angle of the arm under the same electric field first increases and then decreases with increasing frequency, reaching a maximum at 2.5 Hz. Overall, Supplementary Fig. 28 shows that the performance after mechanical damage repair is superior to that after electrical damage repair. This is because electrical damage can lead to carbonization of some chain structures, making the repair of electrical damage more challenging.

Discussion

In summary, a molecular design strategy utilizing zwitterion-functionalization and bimodal networks significantly improved the actuation performance of dielectric elastomers. This approach concurrently enhances breakdown strength, suppresses electromechanical instability, and enables intrinsic self-healing capabilities. The resulting material exhibits recovery from both mechanical and electrical damage. Through intrinsic self-healing mechanisms, it addresses factors that limit dielectric elastomer lifespan, including dielectric breakdown and chain scission induced by cyclic high voltage, mechanical stretching, and Joule heating from leakage currents, thereby extending the operational lifetime under large deformations (>100%). There remains room for optimization in reducing the healing time. We attribute the currently prolonged healing duration primarily to restricted chain segment mobility: the undamaged fraction of zwitterionic groups within the material remains capable of forming electrostatic interactions (i.e., physical crosslinking) at 60 °C, which hinders the rate of molecular chain rearrangement and diffusion. Polymer chains require sufficient time to overcome the energy barrier to achieve effective chain entanglement and interfacial healing. Therefore, future efforts will focus on enhancing chain segment mobility and lowering the energy barrier for physical cross‑link reorganization, aiming to reduce the healing time to a more practically relevant range. Simultaneously, evaluating the healing kinetics for more probable micro-scale damages will enable a more accurate assessment of their applicability in DEA. While a more moderate healing temperature and faster healing are desired, P(BA-SB) preliminarily demonstrates the feasibility of achieving an artificial muscle capable of large deformation, high energy density, and high specific power, providing essential material support for advanced soft actuators and robots in increasingly complex and extreme operational scenarios.

Methods

Materials

Urethane diacrylate (catalog name: CN9021 NS) was purchased from Sartomer. Neopentyl glycol propoxylate diacrylate (PNPDA, 98%) was obtained from Macklin. Butyl acrylate (BA, 99%), 2-Hydroxy-2-Methyl-1-phenyl-1-Porpanone (HMPP, 99%), and [2-(Methacryloyloxy)ethyl]dimethyl-(3-sulfopropyl)ammonium hydroxide (zwitterionic sulfobetaine methacrylate 98%) were sourced from Shanghai Aladdin. Tetrahydrofuran (THF) and methanol were purchased from Sinopharm. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Fabrication of dielectric elastomers

BA served as the monomer, CN9021 NS and PNPDA as crosslinkers, SB as the functional monomer, and HMPP as the photo-initiator. Detailed recipes are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The preparation procedure is outlined in Supplementary Fig. 1: first, SB was dissolved in methanol, and the other components were added sequentially. The mixture was stirred magnetically for 1 h. After degassing the well-mixed precursor under vacuum for 15 min, it was injected into a mold consisting of two glass plates separated by a 300 µm‑thick silicone rubber spacer. Curing was carried out by exposing the mold to UV light (254 nm, 6 × 15 W, 1 cm distance) for 30 min. After curing, the film was placed in a 60 °C vacuum oven to remove methanol, yielding a film approximately 250 µm thick.

Fabrication of multilayer P(BA-SB) actuators

The multilayer actuator, comprising three individual units, was fabricated to achieve a high output force under AC. The process involved: Step (1) Mixing the precursor solution (~15 min) and casting it in a mold with 300 μm spacers between glass plates, followed by UV curing (254 nm, 90 W) and vacuum drying at 60 °C to remove methanol, yielding a ~200 μm dielectric elastomer film. Step (2) Laser-cutting the film into 7 × 5 cm sheets, coating each with 5 × 3 cm carbon grease electrodes, and assembling the three layers into the final actuator for testing.

Characterizations

The chemical composition of the dielectric elastomer films was characterized by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy using a PerkinElmer Spectrum II spectrometer (USA) in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode with a diamond crystal as the reflection element. Uniaxial tensile testing was performed on a universal testing machine (model: UTM4203, manufacturer: SANS) at a constant extension rate of 20 mm/min until rupture to obtain stress-strain data. The dielectric properties of the material in the frequency range of 102 to 106 Hz were measured with a dielectric spectrometer (model: Keysight 4980L, manufacturer: Polyk). The electrical breakdown strength of the elastomer was characterized using a high-voltage tester (model: BDJC-50 kV, manufacturer: Beijing Beiguang Jingyi Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd). The electro-actuation performance was evaluated by coating both sides of the dielectric elastomer film with carbon grease electrodes, connecting them to an external high-voltage power supply via copper wires, and then measuring the actuation response. The leakage current density of the elastomer film was measured with a PolyK leakage-current test system. The electric field was increased in steps of 1 MV m-1, and leakage-current data were recorded for 10 min using a Keithley 6514 electrometer. Thermally stimulated depolarization current (TSDC) was measured with a Keithley 6514 electrometer following the procedure: the sample was first polarized at 80 °C under an applied voltage for 30 min with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1, then, while maintaining the electric field, it was cooled to −90 °C at a rate of −20 °C min−1, after holding at −90 °C for 5 min, the field was removed, and the sample was heated to 150 °C at 3 °C/min, while the resulting current was measured. First-principles calculations were performed using Materials Studio software. Long-chain polymer models of PBA and P(BA-SB) were constructed separately in the software. After geometry optimization to obtain the minimum-energy configuration, electrostatic potential calculations were carried out with the DMol3 module to analyze electron density and electrostatic distribution. Subsequently, the electronic density of states and band gap were calculated. An amorphous cell was built based on the optimized model, and after cell optimization, the density functional theory (DFT) data of the system were obtained through DMol3 calculations, which were then correlated with experimental results for theoretical validation. Variable-temperature FT-IR spectroscopy was conducted in transmission mode on an FT-IR spectrometer, heating a sealed sample from 40 to 160 °C in 20 °C intervals. Two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy analysis of the temperature-dependent FT-IR spectra was performed using 2D Correlation Spectroscopy Analysis v1.22, and the 2D maps were plotted with the Origin program.
