Is Red Cell Distribution Width the Universal Biomarker of Disease Severity and Outcome?
Authors: Amarja Ashok Havaldar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
How to cite this article: Havaldar AA. Is Red Cell Distribution Width the Universal Biomarker of Disease Severity and Outcome? Indian J Crit Care Med 2024;28(12):1087–1088.

Red blood cells (RBCs) are the highest number of cells in the body. Hemoglobin, present in the RBCs, binds to oxygen and helps in oxygen transport. The key drivers of oxygen delivery are hemoglobin, cardiac output, and saturation.

(DO2, Delivered oxygen; CO, Cardiac output; Hb, Hemoglobin; SaO2, Arterial oxygen saturation; PaO2, Partial pressure of oxygen)

Red blood cells are discoid or biconcave in shape and don't have a nucleus. The morphology of RBC helps to pass through the blood vessels easily. Various conditions can cause alteration in the size and shape of the RBC called anisopoikilocytosis, and lead to increased red cell distribution width (RDW).

Red cell distribution width is one of the erythrocyte indices calculated from the standard deviation (SD) of mean corpuscular volume (MCV) divided by MCV multiplied by 100.

The average value of RDW is 11–15%.1 This parameter is available as a part of a complete hemogram.

Factors that can Affect RDW

Anemia and iron stores: Conditions like nutritional deficiency, reduced production, increased hemolysis, and the presence of hemoglobinopathies like sickle cell disease are associated with the increased RDW.1,2

Evidence

In recent years, RDW has been used to predict short-term or long-term outcomes in different conditions with inflammatory or infective etiology.

Critically ill: A study by Purtle et al. showed RDW >15.8% was associated with a higher odds (odds ratio OR) 8.8 (95% confidence interval [CI] 7.15–10.83) of mortality.7 Similarly, post-emergency surgery, RDW >15.8% at the time of discharge had OR 2.12 (95% CI. 1.1–3.83) and was associated with poor outcomes.8 A good correlation was seen between RDW and severity assessment scores like simplified acute physiology score (SAPS).9

Sepsis-Wu et al. showed higher RDW >16% was associated with higher 30-day mortality in septic patients. Higher RDW was associated with severe illnesses like requirement of mechanical ventilation, shock, bacteremia, and requirement of renal replacement therapy.10

Acute myocardial infarction: The study published in the current issue of the IJCCM included 50 patients with ST elevation myocardial infarction.11 It was a case-control study. The authors compared RDW in patients admitted with acute STEMI with controls. This study compared RDW with the Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) score and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Higher RDW had a positive correlation with GRACE score and a negative correlation with LVEF. Among 50 patients, 4 patients died, and RDW was significantly higher in non-survivors (56.6.0 ± 8 fL, p = 0.002). Also, higher RDW was seen with higher severity of disease. A progressively significant increase in RDW was observed from single-vessel to triple-vessel disease patients (p = 0.013).

The important observation was that patients were not anemic and still had higher RDW. Probably, low cardiac output resulted in impaired oxygen delivery, and tissue hypoxia affected RDW. There are various prediction scores available in MI. Red cell distribution width makes it a low-cost biomarker for risk prediction.

Chronic heart failure: Felker et al. described that increased RDW as a prognostic marker with hazard ratio 1.17 (CI: 1.10–1.25, p < 0.0001) associated with higher morbidity and mortality.12 Negative correlation was seen with hemoglobin and RDW. However, it was not statistically significant.12

Stroke: Ratio of Hb/RDW was a better predictor of mortality in stroke patients compared to RDW.13

Using RDW as a trend rather than a single value: Zhang et al. showed that repeated measurements of RDW did not improve the prediction of hospital mortality. Normal life span of RBC's can decrease to 30 days in critical illness. Hence, a single measurement at the time of admission can help in predicting outcome as suggested by this study.14 However, Kim et al. showed a change in RDW from the baseline and at 72 hours was associated with the worst outcome in patients of sepsis.15

The published studies have shown RDW as a promising tool for outcome prediction in critically ill patients. Comparing RDW with other disease-specific scores, RDW remained a robust parameter for outcome prediction in different age-groups and in various disease conditions.16 Disease-specific scores like GRACE or severity of illness score like acute physiology and chronic health evaluation (APACHE II) require multiple parameters, and calculations can have errors, whereas RDW is a single low-cost, easily available, and objective parameter.

Although RDW is a good prediction tool, no interventions are currently proposed to modify RDW. Knowing whether alteration in RDW has any mortality benefit will be interesting.
