Tumor cell-intrinsic PD-1 regulates chemotherapy resistance in colorectal cancer cells by activating downstream MAPK signaling
Authors: Ho Kit Mok, Sophie Sneddon, Jianhua Ren, Donald T. T. Yapp, Isabella T. Tai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) remains a significant clinical challenge. Immunotherapy against programmed cell death 1 protein (PD-1) in CRC has limited success. Intriguingly, CRC cells express PD-1 (ciPD-1) intrinsically, and we report here its function with respect to chemotherapy.MethodsWe evaluated the associations between ciPD-1 expression and disease progression, overall survival, and upregulation of survival pathways in human CRC tumors. Expression levels of ciPD-1 in CRC cells were modulated to evaluate its biological role in vitro.ResultsHigh expression levels of PD-1 in CRC tumors are associated with inferior outcomes; these tumors are also more aggressive and drug-resistant. Expression levels of ciPD-1 in CRC cells increase during 5-FU or CPT-11 treatment and are accompanied by upregulation of cell survival pathways. When ciPD-1 is inhibited, the cell-killing effects of 5-FU or CPT-11 were significantly increased. Our data show that ciPD-1 signaling occurs via MAPK signaling in CRC cells to support survival under stress conditions.ConclusionsCRC tumors with high levels of ciPD-1 are more aggressive and associated with inferior outcomes. Reducing ciPD-1 levels in CRC cells make them more sensitive to chemotherapy. The aggregate results suggest that using a PD-1 inhibitor with first-line treatments in CRC could improve therapeutic efficacy. Proposed biological role of ciPD-1 in CRC cells. After exposure to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or nutrient deprivation, ciPD-1 expression is elevated in CRC cells. Subsequently, ciPD-1 activates MAPK and AKT signaling pathways to increase proliferation and differentiation, resulting in drug resistance and tumor growth in CRC cells. The administration of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy with chemotherapy in CRC cells could abrogate ciPD-1 activity and enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy to overcome resistance.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) affects a significant number of individuals worldwide; globally CRC is the third most common cancer and the second most frequent cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. Early detection and removal of early-stage tumors followed by adjuvant chemotherapy based on 5-fluorouacil (5-FU) is the best curative option, but many tumors develop resistance, and 17-21% of patients recur after removal of the primary tumor [2, 3]. Late-stage CRC tumors are treated with 5-FU-based chemotherapy [4] in combination with irinotecan (FOLFIRI) or oxaliplatin (FOLFOX). Cetuximab or panitumumab, antibody therapies targeting epidermal growth factor receptor, are available for patients with wild-type KRAS tumors [5]. Bevacizumab is used with chemotherapy to target vascular endothelial growth factor, but only with moderate success [5]. Despite the development of new treatments for CRC in the past two decades, advanced or recurrent CRC remains a formidable challenge for public health bodies [6] because these tumors are more aggressive, refractory to therapy and often metastatic [1, 7–10]. Immunotherapy offers more options to CRC patients, particularly with respect to modulation of anti-tumor T-cell activity, but only after first and second-line treatment options are exhausted. Unfortunately, not many CRC patients respond for reasons that are unclear, but possibly related to low levels of T cell infiltration into the tumor and a low mutation burden leading to lower tumoral antigen presentation to activate immune responses [11, 12]. The best-known example of immunotherapy in CRC is perhaps targeting the PD1/PD-L1 axis in CRC, but only a small subset of CRC patients (3% to 15%) who have high levels of microsatellite instability or who are mismatch repair (MMR)-deficient respond [13, 14].

Recently, the presence of cell-intrinsic PD-1 (ciPD-1) in various tumor types, including melanoma, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), non-small cell lung cancer cell (NSCLC), thyroid cancer cells, esophageal adenocarcinoma, and Merkel cell carcinoma has been reported [15–24]. In melanoma, HCC, and thyroid cancer cells ciPD-1 was shown to promote tumor growth by activating the mTOR/S6/eIF4E or SHP2/RAS/MAPK pathways [15, 17, 21]. The ciPD-1+ tumor populations were also reported to behave as tumor-initiating cells or cancer stem cells [15, 20, 23]. In contrast, expression of ciPD-1 in NSCLC suppressed tumor growth by inhibiting AKT and ERK1/2 pathways, albeit this was based on a single case report [18]. In keeping with this, PD-1 blockade of chemoresistant NSCLC cells potentiated chemotherapy effects and delayed the onset of chemoresistance [20]. These findings highlight the possibility that ciPD-1 exerts context-specific regulatory functions within the tumor, beyond its well-known role in immune response suppression.

We therefore examined the relationship between ciPD-1 and survival on outcomes in CRC patients, and subsequently examined its role in CRC cell response to 5-FU and CPT-11 in vitro. Our results indicate that CRC patients with high expression levels of ciPD-1 had significantly inferior survival outcomes. Stress conditions, including exposure to chemotherapy, increased basal levels of ciPD-1 in CRC cell lines. Drug-resistant CRC cell lines had higher levels of ciPD-1 compared to their drug-sensitive counterparts but inhibition of ciPD-1 sensitized cells to drug treatment. The in vitro data also show ciPD-1 activates KRAS downstream of MAPK and AKT signaling suggesting that ciPD-1 may enhance cell survival under stress. In aggregate, our patient and cell-line data strongly suggest that the ciPD-1 signaling cascade in CRC has a pro-tumorigenic relationship. The data further indicate that suppressing ciPD-1 and its downstream effectors in combination with first or second-line chemotherapy is a novel therapeutic strategy worth considering in the clinical management of CRC.

Materials and Methods

Patient cohorts and bioinformatic data analysis

Publicly available gene expression data linked to clinical information were retrieved from three sources: (1) TCGA via the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) application and cBioPortal [25, 26]; (2) The personalized OncoGenomics (POG) program at BC Cancer; and (3) the GEO database. TCGA dataset contained 594 CRC samples, of which 341 were colon adenocarcinomas (COAD), 118 were rectal adenocarcinomas (READ), and 135 were uncategorized. Additional transcriptome-sequencing-based gene expression data (RNA-sequencing data of metastatic CRC tumors) of 73 CRC samples from the POG program at BC Cancer were also analyzed. The GEO database (GSE50760) comprising 18 paired normal, CRC primary and liver metastatic tumors was also included in this study [27]. The total number of patients with CRC analyzed in this study was 685. Data on expression levels of MAPK family genes in CRC were obtained from the GEPIA2 (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis) database (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/) [28]. It is important to note that TCGA datasets consist of tumor samples with at least 80% tumor tissue and less than 20% necrotic material [29]. Samples in the POG dataset were reviewed by pathologists and scored for tumor content. Only samples with tumor tissue of 40% or more were analyzed further[30]. The sample inclusion criteria for GEO datasets are determined by the authors. The contributing authors for GSE 50760 have not reported their inclusion criteria [27].

Gene set enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway analysis for gene expression data

Gene expression levels of the top and bottom expressing PD-1 patient profiles from TCGA, GSE50760, and POG, were compared and the genes with significant differential expression were analyzed for gene set enrichment (GSEA) using the Molecular Signatures Database (V2024.1.Hs) [31]. GSEA was carried out by computing overlaps with hallmark gene sets (H), canonical pathways (CP) and KEGG legacy gene sets (C2), obtained from the Broad Institute. Genes in Gene Set (K), Genes in Overlap (k), k/K and p value were used to rank the pathways enriched in each phenotype.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human colorectal cell lines RKO, MIP-101, SW480, HT-29, DLD-1, CCL-227, HCT-116 and CCD841-CoN were used in this study. Cell lines were purchased from the ATCC with the exception of MIP-101, which is a legacy cell line [32] that was gifted to Dr. Tai. All the cell lines used in this study were grown from frozen stock that had been previously been tested for mycoplasma and authenticated by Applied Biologicals Materials Inc., Canada. Cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with high glucose and L-glutamine (Gibco, Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% newborn calf serum (NCS) or fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Invitrogen), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Invitrogen) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The cell lines RKO [33], MIP-101 [34], DLD-1 [33], HCT-116 [33], and CCL227 (ATTC product sheet) are reported to be microsatellite unstable, while the cell lines SW480 [33] and HT-29 [35] are microsatellite stable.

RNA interference

siRNA interference was carried out as previously described [36]. Briefly, RKO and MIP-101 cells were plated in six-well plates at 80% confluency. After 24 h, cells were transfected with PD1 siRNA or the scramble control siRNA (ABM) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Cells were selected with puromycin (1 μg/mL).

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Invitrogen). First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The expression of PDCD1 (PD-1 mRNA) was determined by qRT-PCR using EvaGreen qPCR Mastermix-ROX (ABM) in a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with primers specific to the sequences of genes of interest (PD-1-F’: 5’-GCCACCATTGTCTTTCCTAG-3’; PD-1-R’: 5’-AAGAGCAGTGTCCATCCTCAG-3’). Relative expression differences were calculated using 2−ΔΔCTmethod with reference to β-Actin.

Cell viability and proliferation

RKO, MIP-101, and the corresponding 5-FU, and CPT-11 resistant cell lines were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1500–3000 cells per well for 24 h followed by treatment with the PD-1 inhibitor Nivolumab (Nivo) with and without 5-FU or CPT-11 for 48 h. Cell viability was determined by the MTS (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2 H-tetrazolium, inner salt) viability assay (Promega) using a VersaMaxmicroplate reader (version 4.8, Molecular Devices). The assay was carried out according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The overall cell viability of treated cells was calculated relative to the non-treated control. Experiments were done in triplicate at a minimum.

Colony formation assay

CRC cells were seeded (500–1000 cells/well; 6-well plates) and incubated with 5-FU or CPT-11 together with Nivo for 7–10 days. The media was changed every two days. At the experimental endpoint, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with methanol for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were stained with 0.25% crystal violet. The number of colonies formed in the treated group was subsequently calculated as a fold change of colonies formed in the control untreated wells. Experiments were done in triplicate.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

CRC cells were seeded on glass coverslips and fixed (4% PFA in PBS, 15 min, RT). Cells were permeabilized (0.15% Triton-X100, 15 min, RT); after washing with PBS, the cells were blocked (1% BSA in TBST, 1 hr). PD-1 antibody (1:20) (#86163S, CST) was added to the cells and left at 4 °C overnight. Secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa FluorTM 488 together with DAPI for nuclear staining was added (1 hr, RT). Slides were mounted and visualized with a ZEISS Apotome 3 microscope. Images were acquired from at least two independent samples.

Western blot analysis

Whole cell lysates were extracted using either NETN buffer (5 M NaCl, 0.5 M EDTA, 1 M Tris-HCl, NP-40) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitor or direct lysis. Protein lysates were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore) for western blot analyses. Primary antibodies against PD-1 (1:1000, #86163S, CST), p42/44 MAPK (1:1000, #4696, CST), p-p42/44 MAPK (1:1000, #4370, CST), MEK (1:1000, #8727, CST), p-MEK (1:1000, #9154, CST), AKT (1:1000, #2920, CST), p-AKT (1:1000, #4060, CST), mTOR (1:1000, #2972, CST), p-mTOR (1:1000, #2974, CST), and β-ACTIN (1:5000, #A5316, Sigma Aldrich) were incubated at 4 °C overnight. After washing, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit antibody (CST). The signals were visualized using the enhanced chemiluminescence method. Blot images were quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software. Experiments were done in duplicate at a minimum, and representative images are shown in the figures.

In vitro maintenance of drug-resistant CRC cells

Drug-resistant clones of RKO and MIP-101 cells were previously established in the Tai lab by subjecting CRC cells to continuous exposure of 5-FU or CPT-11 concentrations [36]. In brief, RKO and MIPI-101 cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of 5-FU until they remained consistently viable at 100 or 200 µM, respectively. Similarly, CPT-11-resistant clones of RKO and MIP-101 cells were established by continuous exposure of the cells to CPT-11 concentrations until they were consistently viable at 70 or 100 µM, respectively. The resistant clones are indicated by the drug name in subscript, e.g., RKOCPT-11 indicates that these RKO cells are resistant to CPT-11 in relation to the parental RKO cells.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical significance of proliferation assay and cell death analysis results was determined by Student’s t test using GraphPad Prism. The displayed results show the means and the standard deviations, and those with p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001). All tests are two-sided. Data points were excluded if they deviated from the mean by more than 3 standard deviations. The Chi-square test was employed to examine the correlation of protein expression in CRC samples. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to analyze overall survival and disease-free survival, and a log-rank test was used to determine the statistical significance.

Results

ciPD-1 expression increases with tumor stage and is significantly associated with inferior outcomes

Stomach cancer has the highest levels of ciPD-1 (7.04 ± 1.52), while kidney chromophobe cancer has the lowest (3.63 ± 1.80) within the TCGA cancer panel (Fig. 1a). Moderate levels of expression are seen in adenocarcinomas of the colon (COAD) and rectum (READ). Transcriptome sequencing data retrieved from TCGA and GTEx showed upregulation of PDCD1 mRNA expression in human CRC tumor samples (5.65 ± 1.47) compared with normal tissues (4.20 ± 1.55) (p-value < 0.0001****; Fig. 1a). We analyzed the GSE50760 cohort containing matched sets of normal, primary, and metastatic CRC tumors from 18 patients and found that PDCD1 mRNA levels increased within the same patient from matched normal, primary CRC to metastatic tumors in the liver. Over 60% of CRC tumors from the GSE50760 cohort had higher expression levels of PDCD1 compared to their matched normal tissues (Fig. 1b). Survival data from TCGA dataset further indicated that high mRNA expression of PDCD1 was significantly associated with poor disease-specific survival (p = 0.0202*) and overall survival (p = 0.0094**; Fig. 1d, e). The probability of disease-specific survival for patients with low PD-1 was 80% at 5 years vs. 65% for patients with high PD-1 (Fig. 1d; p < 0.0202*). ciPD-1 expression is strongly associated with the activation of the KRAS/MAPK pathway in patient tumors

The TCGA and GSE50760 clinical cohorts were analyzed to investigate associations between hallmarks of cancer, survival pathways and ciPD-1 expression levels using GSEA, Hallmarks and KEGG Pathways. In TCGA, the top and bottom 10% of ciPD-1-expressing patient profiles were extracted and compared. (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In the GSE50760 cohort, the comparison was made between metastatic and primary tumor samples, or metastatic and normal samples. The results indicate that several key hallmarks of tumorigenic survival were highly upregulated in the high ciPD-1 expressing tumors from TCGA database and in paired metastasized vs. primary samples in the GSE50760 cohort (Fig. 2a, c). Examples include the KRAS and hypoxia signaling networks, and the P53 and ROS pathways. TCGA data show that patients with high PD-1 levels had upregulated KRAS activity (Fig. 2b; normalized enrichment score (NES) = 2.17; NOM p-value = 0.002, FDR q-value = 0.001). In the GSE50760 cohort, the NES for upregulated KRAS activity was 1.44 (NOM p-value = 0.000, FDR q-value = 0.017) in metastatic tumor samples. Similar findings were noted in stage IV metastatic CRC tumors (POG dataset). Further analysis of TCGA dataset, using the GEPIA2 analyses, showed that PDCD1 expression was positively correlated with levels of MAPK1 (R = 0.38), MAPK2 (R = 0.34), MAP2K1 (R = 0.38), AKT1 (R = 0.39), AKT2 (R = 0.35) (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). MAP2K1 protein (0.824 ± 0.668 vs 0.284 ± 0.316, p-value < 0.0001) and mRNA expression (10.28 ± 0.543 vs 10.13 ± 0.463, p-value = 0.0161) levels were also significantly higher in the PD-1-High expressing patient group, further corroborating our idea that ciPD-1 is linked to KRAS downstream of MAPK and AKT signaling in CRC cells (Supplementary Fig. 2e–h). This was further supported by the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 2e, f). In TCGA dataset, the MAPK signaling pathway was found to be upregulated in PD-1-High patients (NES = 1.71, NOM p-value = 0.016, FDR q-value = 0.097) (Fig. 2e). Similarly, in the GSE50760 dataset, the MAPK signaling pathway in metastatic tumors was upregulated with respect to primary tumors (NES = −1.18 when normal vs metastatic tumors, NOM p-value = 0.233, FDR q-value = 0.734) (Fig. 2f).

CRC cell lines upregulate ciPD-1 expression levels under stress conditions

We subsequently used immortalized CRC cell lines to examine the effects of ciPD-1 in vitro decoupled from the immune system. We first examined PD-1 expression levels in a panel of cell lines using the normal human colon epithelial cell line, CCD841-CoN, as a baseline control. The PDCD1 mRNA levels, quantified by RT-qPCR, showed that RKO was the highest expressing cell line (6.65 ± 0.633) while DLD-1 (1.79 ± 0.299) was the lowest expressing cell line (Fig. 3a). Corroborating the mRNA levels, the CRC cancer cell lines showed varying expression of ciPD-1 with RKO and SW480 cells having high ciPD-1 expression (168.03-fold change and 59.43-fold change respectively) while HT-29 and DLD-1 cells had low ciPD-1 protein expression (2.07-fold change and 1.34-fold change respectively) (Fig. 3b). We note that mRNA expression levels can be a good indicator of protein levels, but the two readouts are not necessarily synonymous. There are multiple post-transcriptional or post-translational mechanisms that can affect the production of the final protein. In addition, the half-lives of mRNA expression and protein expression may be different. We subsequently examined the relationship between ciPD-1 and treatment resistance. We used 5-FU- and CPT-11-resistant RKO and MIP-101 cell lines previously established in our lab [36]. Intriguingly, the expression levels of ciPD-1 were higher in 5-FU- and CPT-11-resistant CRC cells derived from RKO and MIP-101 cells compared to the parental cells (Fig. 3c, d). In 5-FU- and CPT-11-resistant RKO cells, ciPD-1 levels were 5.48-fold and 3.48-fold higher, respectively, than the parental cells. Similarly, with 5-FU- and CPT-11-resistant MIP-101 cells fold-changes of 1.25 and 5.87, respectively, in ciPD-1 expression were seen.

We then challenged chemo-naive RKO and MIP-101 cells with different stress conditions and found that ciPD-1 levels increased in response to the stress. Upon treatment with radiation (2 to 4 Gy), 5-FU (15 to 30 μM) and CPT-11 (15 to 30 μM), ciPD-1 expression levels were enhanced by 2.11- to 2.46-fold, 2.10- to 2.74-fold, and 1.29- to 3.63-fold, respectively, in RKO cells (Fig. 3e). In MIP-101 cells, ciPD-1 expression levels were increased by 1.47- to 1.91-fold increase in the radiation challenge, 1.10- to 1.26-fold increase in 5-FU treatment, and 1.18- to 1.63-fold increase in CPT-11 treatment (Fig. 3e). The observed increase in ciPD-1 was further confirmed by IF analyses (Fig. 3f). Our data also showed that culturing RKO and MIP-101 cells in serum-free media, to mimic nutrient deprivation, increased ciPD-1 expression levels by 7.30-fold and 1.84-fold, respectively (Fig. 3e).

Modulating ciPD-1 expression by siRNA or Nivolumab sensitizes CRC cells to 5-FU and CPT-11, resulting in synergistic cell death

Expression levels or the activity of ciPD-1 in RKO and MIP-101 cells were subsequently modulated by inhibition with siRNA interference or Nivolumab (Nivo), respectively, to assess the effect of ciPD-1 on cell viability and treatment efficacy of 5-FU and CPT-11. Nivo alone had no significant effects on the cells’ viability (MTS) and colony forming ability, but combining Nivo with 5-FU and CPT-11 resulted in significantly reduced colony formation and proliferation, particularly the combination of Nivo and CPT-11 (Fig. 4a). In RKO cells, the combination treatment of Nivo and 5-FU resulted in more than 50% cell death compared to no drug control (CTL), whereas more than 90% cell death was observed in the Nivo and CPT-11 combined treatment (Fig. 4a, upper panel). The combination treatment of Nivo and 5-FU induced 70% of cell death, while Nivo and CPT-11 induced 80% of cell death in MIP-101 cells compared to CTL (Fig. 4a, bottom panel). The observed effects of the combination treatments were found to be synergistic as well. In Nivo and 5-FU combo treatment, the observed cell death was 1.35-fold and 1.15-fold higher than the Bliss index in RKO and in MIP-101 cells, respectively (Fig. 4b, upper panel; see also Supplementary Material). Discussion

The advent of immunotherapy has brought unparalleled benefits and success to the treatment of certain cancers, such as melanoma, breast, bladder, cervical, head and neck, Hodgkins lymphoma, liver, and lung cancers [37, 38]. In particular, the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint axis has been studied extensively in T cells and other immune cells as a therapeutic target. In the tumor microenvironment (TME), PD-1 is mainly expressed on immune cells, and is regarded as an immune exhaustion marker for deactivated T cells, B cells, and monocytes. When PD-1+ T cells bind to PD-L1 in the TME, signaling cascades that reduce PD-1+ T cell proliferation or induce PD-1+ T cells apoptosis are activated. The net result is decreased production of anti-tumor cytokines that allows for continued tumor growth [39]. However, blocking PD-1/PD-L1 binding with inhibitors of PD-1, such as nivolumab or pembrolizumab restores T cell activity against tumor cells [40]. This strategy has been highly successful in treating advanced cases of melanoma, NSCLC, advanced kidney, and liver cancers [41–45]. Unfortunately, in CRC, the effectiveness of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade treatment is limited to a small subset of CRC patients who are MSI-H/dMMR. The recent discovery that tumor cells can also produce PD-1 intrinsically (ciPD-1) has generated much interest in its role outside the immune system. The role(s) of ciPD-1 in the cancer cell and its effects on cancer cell biology appear to be context-specific and cancer-type dependent. For example, the expression of ciPD-1 in melanoma, HCC, and thyroid cancer cells was reported to suppress cell growth [15, 16, 21]. Similarly, in other studies where nivolumab was used to inhibit ciPD-1 expression in lung cancer and CRC, the authors reported that lowering ciPD-1 levels enhanced tumor growth [18, 19, 46]. These studies imply that ciPD-1 has tumor suppressive effects; however, in non-small cell lung cancer, the expression of ciPD-1 was associated with chemoresistance [20], indicating it can also exert pro-tumor effects. In CRC, reports show that ciPD-1 can act as a tumor suppressor in CRC [46]; however, other groups have shown that anti-PD-1 regimens decreased tumor volume and increased Granzyme B infiltration levels while decreasing the number of immunosuppressive FoxP3+ cells [47]. Our own results argue for a pro-tumor effect in CRC within the context of chemotherapy, but direct comparisons with other studies are difficult to make because the cell lines used have different genetic backgrounds. This apparent difference further highlights that the role of ciPD-1 in cancer remains poorly defined, and more definitive studies are required to fully understand its biological effects in cancer cells, including CRC.

The initial impetus for our study was to examine how expression levels of PD-1 affect patient outcome and tumor phenotypes in CRC. Using data from TCGA (Fig. 1), we observed that PD-1 expression levels were strongly associated with inferior outcome (overall survival of 83.24 months vs. 43.20 months for low and high PD-1, respectively; p = 0.0094**). This information points towards the importance of PD-1 as a potential prognostic marker in CRC for survival, but it is also important to understand the factors associated with high expression of PD-1 in CRC tumors. The GSE50760 data, which collates genomic information from normal tissue, the primary and metastatic tumor from the same patient, show that PD-1 levels increase with disease progression, with 56% of metastatic tumors demonstrating higher levels of overall PD-1 than the primary tumors. This observation raises the question of whether it is intrinsic genetics or external factors that cause this increase, and what the implications are for treating the tumor. GSEA data further imply that expression of PD-1 is associated with survival pathways, including the MAPK signaling cascade; this was further corroborated by publications indicating that ciPD-1 expression activated the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway in thyroid and breast cancers [21, 48]. Our analysis of the data from TCGA, GEO, and POG has one caveat. The samples from each dataset have different inclusion criteria with respect to tumor content. TCGA tumors had at least 80% tumor material (TCGA website), POG tumors must have at least 40% tumor content [30] whereas the inclusion criteria for the GEO dataset were not reported [27]. In view of this, we believe that the clinical outcomes associated with ciPD-1 in the TCGA dataset are likely still valid, but the conclusions drawn from the POG and GEO datasets need to be confirmed in larger patient cohorts where the tumor content of the analyzed samples is known more definitively.

These observations led us to examine what functional roles ciPD-1 may have in CRC with respect to treatment and cell-survival. We first, therefore, examined whether ciPD-1 was involved in a stress response when CRC cells were placed under environmental stress. The results indicate that when RKO and MIP-101 cells were exposed to radiation, 5-FU, CPT-11, or deprived of nutrients, ciPD-1 levels increased (Fig. 3e). The results imply that upregulation of ciPD-1 is one response under these stress conditions and led us to further examine how ciPD-1 affects cellular response to chemo-stress with respect to 5-FU and CPT-11, two drugs that form the backbone of chemotherapy in advanced CRC [49]. In keeping with the initial stress experiments, RKO and MIP-101 cells were first exposed to Nivo (15–30 μM in 48 h) to determine whether inhibiting ciPD-1 influenced the cells’ viability. The results showed that Nivo reduced the levels of ciPD-1 (Fig. 5b) but had no effect on cell viability (48 h; Supplementary Fig. 3) or colony formation (Fig. 4a). We then compared the effects of 5-FU and CPT-11 as single agents and in combination with Nivo. As expected, both 5-FU and CPT-11 reduced cell viability (10–20 μM) and colony formation (1-2 μM) by approximately 30% in RKO and MIP-101 cells. Interestingly, this was accompanied by an increase in expression levels of ciPD-1. More importantly, the combined effects of Nivo and 5-FU or CPT-11 reduced cell viability and colony formation significantly compared to single-agent treatment. In fact, the treatment effects of 5-FU and CPT-11 combined with Nivo were found to be synergistic (Fig. 4b; see also Supplementary Material), and reduced cell viability by an additional 5-22%.

Similar results were obtained with siRNA knockdown of ciPD-1; siPD-1-B and -C successfully suppressed expression of ciPD-1 compared to the non-targeting controls. When treated with 5-FU or CPT-11, cell viability was significantly reduced in cells containing siPD-1-B and -C (Fig. 4d). The results show that reducing expression levels of ciPD-1 by Nivo or siRNA interference has no effect on cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 3), but clearly increases cell kill by 5-FU and CPT-11. This dataset implies that ciPD-1 plays a specific role in protecting CRC cells from chemotherapy but is not required for cell proliferation. We therefore examined the state of ciPD-1 in variants of RKO (RKO5-FU, RKOCPT-11) and MIP-101 (MIP-1015-FU, MIP-101CPT-11) in which our group had previously induced resistance to 5-FU and CPT-11 [36]. The basal levels of ciPD-1 in the resistant variants of RKO and MIP-101 were found to be higher than their parental counterparts. Since the resistant strains are maintained in the presence of 5-FU and CPT-11, we cannot conclusively say that ciPD-1 levels are constitutively activated in the absence of drug. However, it is worth noting that these cell lines (unlike their parental counterparts) continue to proliferate under these conditions (100-200 μM of 5-FU or 30-75 μM of CPT-11). Inhibition of ciPD-1 with Nivo in RKO5-FU and RKOCPT-11 resulted in cells that reverted to a chemo-sensitive phenotype, by again responding to 5-FU and CPT-11 (Fig. 4e, f). The IC50s of the cell lines are tabulated in the Supplementary Material. (5-FUIC50 = 327.5 ± 23.15 (with Nivo) vs. 5-FUIC50 = 431.2 ± 6.09 (refractory); CPT-11IC50 = 302.6 ± 7.659 (with Nivo) vs. CPT-11IC50 = 373.8 ± 15.03 (refractory) respectively.

The results obtained in these experiments indicate that when ciPD-1 levels/activities are reduced by inhibition (Nivo) or siRNA interference, the parental and resistant RKO and MIP-101 cells become more sensitive to drug treatment. This fact would argue that expression of ciPD-1 has a protective effect on cells exposed to 5-FU and CPT-11 and may be a factor associated with inherent resistance. The higher levels of ciPD-1 found in the resistant cells intriguingly also suggest that the observed increase in ciPD-1 under chemostress may be one step towards the development of drug resistance in CRC cells. An additional point of note is that the combination of Nivo with 5-FU or CPT-11 treatment shows synergistic effects on cell viability, although Nivo as a single agent has no cytotoxic effects. This further corroborates our contention that ciPD-1 is cytoprotective in CRC cells against conventional drugs, such as 5-FU and CPT-11.

The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway conveys signals originating at the cell membrane to internal targets and has a crucial role in numerous biological processes [50]. This pathway is often disrupted in cancers associated with RAS mutations that cause continuous activation of the MAPK pathway, resulting in unchecked cell growth and resistance to drugs that induce apoptosis [51, 52]. Our findings showed that MAPK and AKT activity levels follow ciPD-1 levels, which would lend credence to the idea that they are players in mitigating chemo-stress in CRC, which are supported by other similar findings in thyroid and triple negative breast cancers as well [21, 48].

The aggregate in vitro data we present here show a strong correlation between ciPD-1 and the cells’ ability to withstand cytotoxic stress. Our data also indicate the observed effect is mediated through the MAPK signaling pathways. Our working model for the role of ciPD-1 in CRC therefore suggests that RKO and MIP-101 cells respond to chemotherapy by upregulating ciPD-1 to activate survival pathways such as MAPK. The interaction between PD-1 and its binding ligand, PD-L1, to regulate T-cell activity is well studied as an immunotherapy target [53], although in CRC only a small proportion of patients respond to anti-PD-1 therapies [13, 14]. In our studies reported here, the work was done with CRC cell lines in the absence of immune cells and exogenous factors such as PD-L1 in an attempt to focus on ciPD-1’s stand-alone role in CRC cells. We recognize that in the tumor microenvironment there will very likely be interactions between ciPD-1 and PD-L1. The studies reported here support a preliminary framework we are constructing to understand the effects of ciPD-1 on CRC cells under chemotherapeutic stress, and we will build on these data in future studies to include the effect of PD-L1 on ciPD-1.

The studies we report here raise several interesting possibilities regarding the expression of cellular PD-1. The association between expression of ciPD-1 and patient outcomes indicates that the protein may find a role as a prognostic marker for survival outcomes, or potentially latent chemoresistance. More intriguing are results indicating that ciPD-1 mediates cell survival during exposure to chemotherapy. At present, Nivo is used as third-line immunotherapy in chemo-refractory CRC; however, our results suggest that targeting ciPD-1 during initial chemotherapy may have additional, if not synergistic, benefit. If the expression of ciPD-1 and its downstream MAPK activity is reduced during early treatment with 5-FU or CPT-11, this could enhance drug efficacy and potentially delay or prevent the rise of chemoresistance by preventing clonal selection of high-expressing ciPD-1 cells in the tumor population. In fact, there are several clinical trials examining the use of Nivo during first and second-line chemotherapy that are on-going, indicating this is a feasible therapeutic approach.

For example, in a 3-year follow-up of the phase III CheckMate 649 Trial, the authors examined the first-line Nivo plus chemotherapy treatment for advanced gastric, gastroesophageal junction, and esophageal adenocarcinoma. Nivo plus chemotherapy improved overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and the objective response rate (ORR) [54]. In previously untreated advanced urothelial carcinoma, combination therapy of Nivo plus gemcitabine-cisplatin resulted in significantly better outcomes (OS, PFS, ORR) than chemotherapy alone [55]. In resectable NSCLC, the use of combination treatment of Nivo and chemotherapy extended the median event-free survival from 20.8 months to 30.2 months, with no significant increase of adverse events [56]. Meanwhile, in advanced CRC with RAS/BRAF mutated patients, a phase II study of Nivo in combination with FOLFOXIRI/bevacizumab as first-line treatment met its primary ORR endpoint and supported a phase III randomized-controlled study [57]. In another phase II study of Nivo plus FOLFOX6 and bevacizumab in advanced CRC, the primary endpoint of PFS was not met, but the combination treatment provided better PFS rates and a higher response rate compared to the group without Nivo treatment [58]. These ongoing clinical trials corroborate our hypothesis that using Nivo in combination with chemotherapy in first-line treatments will improve overall response rates in cancer patients

In summary, the Patient data presented here indicate that ciPD-1 is strongly associated with advanced CRC and poor outcomes. Our in vitro data further show that in the absence of an immune response, ciPD-1 mediates cell survival through the MAPK cascade in CRC cells exposed to chemotherapy (Graphical Abstract). Our studies further describe one biological role of ciPD-1 in CRC. However, the definitive biological role of ciPD-1 is still open to conjecture, and further studies will likely uncover other cellular behaviors that is mediated by this protein.
