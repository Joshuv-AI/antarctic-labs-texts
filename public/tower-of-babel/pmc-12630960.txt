Serum heavy metal levels from tobacco smoke and Buerger’s disease
Authors: Ali Asghar Asadollahi Shahir, Amir Hossein Khoshakhlagh, Fateme Mehrzad, Amir Ghaderi, Agnieszka Gruszecka-Kosowska, Somayeh Ghorbani, Freshteh Haerifar, Shervin Sadat Hashemian Esfahani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTobacco smoke is toxic, genotoxic, mutagenic, and carcinogenic, and tobacco use is the most significant risk factor for the development of Buerger’s disease. Thromboangiitis obliterans (TAO), also known as Buerger’s disease, is a rare, inflammatory, occlusive peripheral vascular condition of unknown etiology that typically affects small- and medium-sized arteries and veins. The aim of this study was to determine the concentrations of selected heavy metals, namely As, Cd, Pb, and Tl, in individuals diagnosed with Buerger’s disease and to compare them with levels found in healthy individuals, both nicotine-dependent and non-dependent. The study was conducted on 30 volunteers divided into three groups: 10 patients diagnosed with Buerger’s disease based on the Shionoya criteria (9 males and 1 female), 10 healthy smokers (9 males and 1 female), and 10 healthy non-smokers (8 males and 2 females). Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference in the mean concentrations of Pb and Cd across the groups. Multiple comparison tests indicated that patients with Buerger’s disease had significant higher mean levels of Pb (24.73 µg/dL) and Cd (5.88 µg/dL) compared to both control groups. A significant difference was also observed in As levels, which were notably elevated in the group of patients with Buerger’s disease. These findings suggest a potential association between heavy metal exposure and the pathogenesis of Buerger’s disease, warranting further investigation.Keywords: Tobacco smoke, Buerger’s disease, Arsenic, Cadmium, Lead, ThalliumSubject terms: Biochemistry, Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Tobacco is the second most commonly used psychoactive substance worldwide, with more than one billion smokers worldwide1. Although the prevalence of tobacco use has declined in many high-income countries, it remains prevalent in low- and middle-income countries2.

Tobacco-related diseases result from the biological consequences of repeated inhalation exposure to cigarette smoke and its toxic compounds, which are released during combustion. Tobacco smoke is toxic, genotoxic, mutagenic, and carcinogenic, and has been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes3. Tobacco use is the most significant risk factor for patients with Buerger’s disease, although its underlying mechanism remains unclear4.

Thromboangiitis obliterans (TAO), also known as Buerger’s disease, is an inflammatory, occlusive peripheral vascular disease condition of unknown etiology that primarily affects small- and medium-sized vessels5. Buerger’s disease predominantly occurs in young to middle-aged men who smoke, and most researchers agree that exposure to tobacco, whether first-hand and second-hand, is a prerequisite for its onset and progression6. The global prevalence and incidence of Buerger’s disease remains unclear, largely due to the scarcity of population-based studies and potential diagnostic biases. Buerger’s disease is believed to have a global distribution, although precise data are lacking. While finding vary, the disease appears to be more prevalent in regions such as the Mediterranean and Asia compared to Western Europe and North America4. It is also more commonly observed among individuals of lower socioeconomic status7. While the exact etiology and pathophysiology of Buerger’s disease remain unknown, several contributing factors have been implicated, including tobacco exposure, genetic predisposition, immune responses, and coagulation abnormalities4. There is a strong association between tobacco use and both the onset and recurrence of the disease, making tobacco exposure a major risk factor. Additional risk factors include gender, race, and age—typically between 20 and 45 years7. Smoking cessation remains the cornerstone of treatment, as patients who continue to use tobacco face a higher chronic disease progression and complications following surgical interventions8.

Tobacco plants absorb toxic metals from the soil, leading to relatively high concentrations of these heavy metals in their leaves. Despite this, there are no regulations to prevent or control heavy metal contamination in tobacco products and cigarettes. As a result, data on heavy metal concentrations in cigarettes and other tobacco products worldwide remain scarce9. Toxic heavy metals are among the oldest known poisons. Although metals naturally occur in the environment through geological and biological processes9, their widespread distribution, ability to accumulate in human tissues, and potential to cause toxicity even at low levels make them particularly concerning. Some metals, such as copper (Cu) and iron (Fe), are essential for life3. However, other metals, including cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), arsenic (As), chromium (Cr), nickel (Ni), thallium (Tl), and mercury (Hg), are toxic even at low levels. Even essential metals can become harmful when present in excessively high concentrations10. Tobacco contains approximately 4700 compounds, many of which are toxic, including aromatic hydrocarbons, aldehydes, ketones, and heavy metals11. Chronic exposure to heavy metals has been linked to an increased risk of cancer in susceptible individuals3. Additionally, As, found in tobacco, water supplies, and some narcotics such as opium and cocaine, may increase the risk of developing Buerger’s disease12. A review article (12) stated that Pb is one of the most toxic metals, causing damage to the liver, kidneys, and nervous system, as well as weakening of bones and affecting white and red blood cells. It is also linked to diabetes and cardiovascular diseases. This study also suggested that Pb and Cd levels could potentially serve as biomarkers for Buerger’s disease13.

However, research on the utility of these heavy metals for early diagnosis or risk assessment remains limited. Pb and Cd, along with other toxic metals, are implicated in vascular pathology through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation. In a study by14, patients with Buerger’s disease exhibited significantly higher blood levels of Pb and Cd compared with both healthy controls and individuals with atherosclerotic peripheral arterial occlusive disease (PAOD). Although preliminary evidence points to As, Cd, Pb, and Tl as potential biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognosis of Buerger’s disease, the findings are still inconclusive and require further validation. The present study sought to advance understanding of heavy metal involvement in the disease’s pathogenesis.

The above context highlights smoking as a primary cause of Buerger’s disease, with the heavy metal content in cigarette smoke contributing to smoke-related diseases. Therefore, we hypothesize that the levels of these metals may also influence the development of Buerger’s disease. Since no previous studies have assessed heavy metal concentrations in patients with Buerger’s disease, our study is the first to compare the blood levels of As, Cd, Pb, and Tl in patients with Buerger’s disease and a control group comprising healthy smokers and non-smokers.

Materials and methods

Research subjects

Participants for the three study groups were selected based on the predefined inclusion criteria. Patients with Buerger’s disease were recruited from Panjm Azar and Sayyad Shirazi hospitals in Gorgan as well as Ayatollah Rouhani Hospital in Babol. The control groups consisted of healthy individuals, with and without nicotine dependence, selected from among the companions of patients at the same hospitals. Regarding the selection of patients with Buerger’s disease, we used patients who had a definitive diagnosis of the disease and met the definitive criteria for Buerger’s disease on angiography. All of these patients had radiological records and were definitively diagnosed with Buerger’s disease by the vascular surgeon. On the other hand, all of the Buerger’s patients had a long history of diagnosis and all of them had undergone repeated amputations of the affected limbs. One of the reasons why the average age of the Buerger’s group was higher than the other two groups was that the Buerger’s patients were definitively diagnosed cases with a long history of Buerger’s disease.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: a confirmed diagnosis of Burger’s disease based on the Shionoya criteria18 for the patient group; verified nicotine dependence based on the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence and the Nicotine Dependence Syndrome Scale (NDSS); confirmed absence of nicotine dependence in healthy controls (based on self-reports and status as patient companions); and provision of written informed consent by all participants. Exclusion criteria included: lack of participant cooperation; presence of severe mental illness (only psychotic conditions); diagnosis of degenerative brain diseases such as dementia or Alzheimer’s disease; current or past substance use disorders (excluding nicotine); self-reported history of heavy metal poisoning; and occupational exposure to heavy metals (e.g., battery manufacturing, soldering, painting, crystal production, cement factories, iron smelting, and work in power plants).

For all patients, the MMSE test was used to quickly assess mental status in order to exclude those who were currently receiving psychiatric treatment. In the present study, none of the participants scored below the standard limit and none were excluded. Also, the patient history and medication records did not report alcohol or drug use; use of antioxidant, vasodilator, or trace element medications; use of e-cigarettes or combustible tobacco; and kidney or liver disease or genetic history.

Blood samples collection

Although Tl is typically excreted in higher concentrations in urine, blood sample was used for the analysis of all investigated metals. Due to the severe course of Buerger’s disease, including limited mobility, ischemic pain, and frequent hospitalizations or amputations, urine sample collection from patients is significantly hindered. Therefore, in the present study, blood sample was used to analyze investigated heavy metal concentrations.

In this study, anti-contamination protocols were carefully followed and a skilled person was used for blood collection. Plastic tubes containing K2EDTA (F.L. Medical s.r.l. Unipersonale, Torreglia—Italy) was used as blood collection vessels. Sterile syringe and heavy metals-free polyethylene containers were used to minimize the risk of contamination. To measure heavy metals, 5 cc of whole blood was poured into a vial of CBC containing anticoagulant K2EDTA19. Labels were applied to the specimens and kept at − 20 °C until transfer to a laboratory for assay. All laboratory equipment and containers were thoroughly cleaned prior to use by rinsing with dilute nitric acid (HNO₃, Merck) followed by ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm) to avoid contamination. Following sample analysis, the results were compared across the three study groups.

Heavy metal concentrations

An atomic absorption spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer, Model 3030 with HGA 400 Programmer) equipped with Deuterium Back ground Corrector and graphite furnace was used to determine the precise concentrations of As, Cd, Pb, and Tl in the collected blood samples. An appropriate hollow cathode lamp for each metal was installed in the GFAAS and the optimal slit and wavelength settings were applied. Blood sample pre-treatment consisted of a five-fold dilution with a dilute surfactant. Simultaneous background correction was used to eliminate small and non-specific absorption signals from the blood matrix. The heavy metals levels were accurately measured in blood samples20. All heavy metals analyses were performed by a qualified expert. Study results were shared with participants upon completion of the research.

Nicotine dependence symptom scale (NDSS) questionnaire

The Nicotine Dependence Symptom Scale is a multifactorial instrument developed by Schiffman et al.21 to assess nicotine dependence based on the DSM-IV-TR criteria. This scale includes 19 items grouped into 5 factors: drive (arousal for smoking), priority (preference), tolerance, continuity (adherence), and stereotypy (stereotyped behavior). Schiffman et al.21 reported a reliability coefficient of 0.84 for the total score, and subscale reliabilities 0.76 (drive), 0.69 (priority), 0.55 (tolerance), 0.63 (continuity), and 0.70 (stereotypy).

In Iran, the content validity of this questionnaire was examined in the study by Zahrani et al.22, and deemed satisfactory by clinical experts. Inter-rater agreement was measured using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W = 0.50) and Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r = 0.61), both statistically significant at P < 0.05. Reliability was further confirmed using Cronbach’s alpha, with an overall internal consistency of 0.80. Subscale alphas were also acceptable: drive (0.81), priority (0.71), tolerance (0.75), continuity (0.72), and stereotypy (0.68)21.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were described using mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, and median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. The assumption of normality was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk test, along with visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Variables such as Pb and Cd followed a normal distribution and were compared among the three groups using one-way ANOVA. As the assumption of homogeneity of variances was violated, Dunnett’s T3 post hoc test was applied for pairwise comparisons. Other variables did not meet the assumption of normality and were analyzed using the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test. Since post hoc comparisons are not directly provided in SPSS following a significant Kruskal–Wallis result, Mann–Whitney U tests were conducted to compare heavy metal levels between the Buerger’s disease group and each of the two control groups. To control for the confounding effect of age and duration of smoking, a multiple linear regression model was used. Since the variables of age and duration of smoking had a high linear correlation of 0.914, only one of them was entered into the model to prevent collinearity. To control for the effect of age on heavy metal levels in all three groups, the variables of age, group, and the interaction effect of group and age were included in the model. Since the interaction effect was not significant, it was removed from the model. Since the healthy control group without nicotine did not smoke, the duration of smoking was meaningless. Therefore, this variable was not collected for them. Thus, to control for the confounding effect of duration of smoking on heavy metal levels in the two healthy smokers and patients with Buerger’s disease, the variables of group and duration of smoking were included in the model, and since the interaction effect of these two variables was not significant, it was removed from the model. Because the number of patients with Buerger’s disease was limited, in this study, we considered two control groups to increase the power of the study. On the other hand, to estimate the parameters more accurately, a bootstrap based on 1000 bootstrap samples was used to estimate the standard deviation and confidence interval. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 23, with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

No published clinical studies have directly investigated the specific impact of As, Cd, Pb, or Tl from tobacco smoke on the development or progression of Buerger’s disease in humans. Recent clinical studies and meta-analyses reveals a notable gap in direct, high-quality evidence comparing heavy metal levels in Buerger’s disease patients versus healthy controls. The current literature consistently identifies tobacco smoking as a central risk factor for Buerger’s disease, but the role of individual heavy metals from tobacco smoke has not been systematically examined in Buerger’s disease cohorts23.

Existing literature on Buerger’s disease has historically focused on smoking and immune-mediated vascular injury as primary etiological factors, with only occasional case reports or small studies suggesting possible roles for environmental toxins, including heavy metals. However, these are not robust or systematic findings and are not represented in the recent clinical literature.

Elevated blood Pb and Cd levels have been associated with increased risk of cardiovascular diseases. For instance, a study by Lin et al.24 found a strong association between blood Cd levels and increased risk of peripheral artery disease, a condition with clinical overlap with Buerger’s disease. The study by Prozialeck et al.25 revealed that Cd levels were significantly higher in smokers compared to non-smokers, showing cigarette smoking introducing Cd might lead to its bioaccumulation and increased pathological risks over time. Similarly, a study by Tellez-Plaza et al.26 reported that elevated Pb levels were linked to increased risk of cardiovascular diseases. These studies suggest that Pb and Cd exposure may contribute to the pathogenesis of vascular diseases, including Buerger’s disease. Chronic As exposure has been linked to various cardiovascular diseases. A study by Navas-Acien et al.27 found that As exposure was associated with increased risk of cardiovascular diseases. These findings support the notion that As exposure may play a role in the development of vascular diseases, including Buerger’s disease.

Although direct causation between elevated blood levels of Pb, Cd, and As and Buerger’s disease was not definitively established yet, several mechanisms suggest how these heavy metals could contribute to the pathogenesis of this vascular disorder. Elevated levels of Pb and Cd have been shown to induce chronic systemic inflammation, as evidenced by higher inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP)28. Exposure to these metals induces oxidative stress and persistent inflammation, both of which contribute to vascular damage and dysfunction29. Oxidative stress can damage vascular endothelium, promote thrombosis, and exacerbate vascular inflammation—all central features of Buerger’s disease29. Heavy metals like Pb, Cd, and As can directly injure endothelial cells, leading to dysfunction, increased vascular permeability, and a pro-thrombotic state30. Endothelial injury is a critical initiating event in Buerger’s disease31,32. The pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative effects of these metals can activate coagulation pathways, increasing the risk of thrombus formation in small and medium vessels, which is a hallmark of Buerger’s disease31,32. Elevated serum levels of Pb, Cd, and As have been associated with increased levels of cardiovascular stress biomarkers such as growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF-15) and soluble suppression of tumorigenicity 2 (sST2), indicating ongoing vascular stress and injury13. Pb, Cd, and As are associated with increased blood pressure and abnormal lipid profiles, further heightening cardiovascular risk33. They have been also linked to the development of subclinical atherosclerosis (early artery hardening), arterial stenosis (narrowing), and calcification, all of which can lead to reduced blood flow and tissue damage29,34,35 as well as they are associated with a higher risk of peripheral artery disease, which is closely related to Buerger’s disease and other disorders involving compromised blood flow to the limbs29,36. Epidemiological studies consistently show a linear dose–response relationship between exposure to these metals and increased risk of cardiovascular diseases, including coronary heart disease and peripheral vascular disorders33,36. The mechanisms—oxidative stress, inflammation, and endothelial injury—are all central to the pathogenesis of vascular diseases like Buerger’s29,35.

Tl is not considered a risk factor for Buerger’s disease. The established and most significant risk factor for Buerger’s disease is tobacco use, particularly cigarette smoking. Tl is a chemical element sometimes used in medical imaging (such as thallium-201 scintigraphy) to assess blood flow or tissue viability, but it is not implicated as a causative or risk factor for Buerger’s disease37,38. Elevated blood levels of Pb, Cd, and As can contribute to the pathogenesis of Buerger’s disease by promoting chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and thrombosis. These mechanisms create a vascular environment conducive to the development and progression of the disease, although further research is needed to establish a direct causal relationship. In summary, lead, cadmium, and arsenic are recognized as significant environmental risk factors for vascular diseases because they trigger biological processes that damage blood vessels, promote atherosclerosis, and impair circulation29,34,36.

While only few studies have directly assessed the presence of heavy metals in patients with Buerger’s disease, our findings suggest that elevated levels of Pb, Cd, and As may play a role in the disease’s onset or progression. These metals, commonly found in tobacco products and the environment, are known to accumulate in vascular tissues and contribute to vascular inflammation and occlusion, which are hallmark features of Buerger’s disease.

Limitations of the study

The present investigation lacked adjustment for various environmental confounders that may independently modulate circulating concentrations of As, Cd, Pb, and Tl, independent of tobacco exposure or the presence of Buerger’s disease. Occupational exposure represents a significant contributor to heavy metal burden, particularly in industries such as metallurgy, battery manufacturing, welding, mining, and agricultural activities involving As-based pesticides39. Additionally, residential proximity to industrial emission sources, including smelting plants, high-traffic roadways, and waste incinerators, may elevate heavy metal exposure through inhalation of contaminated airborne particulates40. Other relevant exposure pathways of these metals include ingestion of contaminated drinking water41, consumption of foods grown in polluted soils42 and dietary intake of seafood and rice with high heavy metal content43. Indoor environments may also serve as sources of exposure via biomass fuel combustion, inhalation of lead-containing dust from deteriorating paint, or passive tobacco smoke. Specifically, As exposure primarily arises from contaminated groundwater, seafood (notably shellfish), rice, and As-based pesticides; Cd exposure is linked to industrial emissions, phosphate fertilizers, contaminated vegetables, and shellfish; Pb exposure is associated with legacy lead-based paints, contaminated water systems, industrial pollution, and urban dust; while Tl exposure, though less extensively characterized, is mainly related to coal combustion, cement production, smelting, and consumption of crops grown in contaminated substrates. The absence of systematic evaluation or control for these environmental sources constitutes a potential confounding factor, thereby limiting the capacity to ascribe elevated heavy metal concentrations solely to tobacco use or the pathogenesis of Buerger’s disease. Also, alcohol consumption can modify the toxicokinetics of heavy metals. Human epidemiological data indicate that alcohol synergistically amplifies the adverse health effects of elevated As and Pb, including increased risk of bone loss, via multiplicative interaction effects44. Co-exposure studies further demonstrate that ethanol enhances arsenic absorption and may potentiate its vascular toxicity45,46. While direct studies on alcohol—Tl interactions are lacking, Tl is known to exert toxicity via oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and prolonged tissue retention, mechanisms plausibly exacerbated by chronic ethanol-induced metabolic stress45,47. These pathways suggest that alcohol may act as a co-factor, amplifying heavy metal–induced vascular injury and potentially accelerating the inflammatory and occlusive processes characteristic of Buerger’s disease. Furthermore, in this study, urine samples were not used to measure Tl levels. Therefore, Tl results should be interpreted with caution and future studies should use urine samples for Tl levels. Moreover, we did not assess the nutritional intake and dietary variability of participants (e.g., diet rich in seafood), which may have influenced the outcomes. Also, all patients with Buerger’s disease have had repeated amputations and are advanced in the course of the disease. Therefore, comparing heavy metal levels with the severity of Buerger’s disease may not be possible in this study. In addition, because of limited funding, we did not examine the C-reactive protein (CRP) and gene expression involved in Buerger’s disease and it is suggested for a future study. Moreover, there is a possibility of recall bias (such as smoking history and dietary habits) in Buerger’s disease. Therefore, the results should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, the levels of heavy metals in different samples such as blood; urine; tissue; hair and nails certainly differ, so it is suggested that these metals be measured in other samples in future studies. Ultimately, the limited sample size constrains the generalizability of the results, thereby diminishing the statistical power to identify minor impacts across diverse health outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the present research provides valuable insights that contribute to addressing critical gaps in understanding the role of heavy metal exposure in the pathogenesis of Buerger’s disease.

Conclusions

In this study, a statistically significant difference was observed in the mean blood levels of Pb, Cd, and As between the study groups. The levels of Pb and Cd were significantly higher in patients with Buerger’s disease (24.73 µg/dL and 5.88 µg/dL, respectively) compared to both healthy smokers and non-smokers. Similarly, As levels were significantly elevated in the Buerger’s disease group relative to the control groups. Our findings indicate that patients with Buerger’s disease have significantly higher blood concentrations of Pb, Cd, and As compared to healthy controls. Although causality cannot be established in a cross-sectional design, the results support the hypothesis that chronic exposure to toxic heavy metals may contribute to the etiology or progression of Buerger’s disease. Future studies with larger sample sizes and mechanistic analyses are warranted to confirm these findings and elucidate the underlying biological pathways.
