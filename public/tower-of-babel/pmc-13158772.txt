Nine-grid segmentation strategy for photoacoustic imaging of microcirculation oxygenation dynamics in the thenar muscle
Authors: Zheng Qu, Cong Mai, Bin Ouyang, Lidai Wang, Xin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrocirculatory dysfunction is central to the pathophysiology of diseases such as sepsis, diabetes, and cardiovascular disorders. While clinical tools like near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) and transcutaneous oxygen monitoring (TcPO₂) offer global oxygenation insights, they lack the spatial resolution for localized microvascular assessment. Photoacoustic computed tomography provides sub-millimeter oxygenation imaging, yet resolving ultra-fine structures for morphology-based functional analysis remains challenging. To address this, we developed the Nine-Grid Segmentation Strategy (NGSS), a significance-analysis framework integrated with a non-invasive vascular occlusion test (VOT). Using the microcirculation-rich thenar muscle as the imaging site, NGSS characterizes each pixel across two physiological dimensions: occlusion response and reperfusion efficiency. The NGSS framework categorizes pixels based on their oxygenation trajectories during occlusion and reperfusion, assigning each to one of three functional states: significant increase, non-significant change, or significant decrease. This two-dimensional classification generates a nine-type map capturing the spatial heterogeneity of tissue microregions. Our results demonstrate that even without discerning specific microvascular morphologies, NGSS evaluates tissue oxygenation on a hundred-micron scale. The analysis reveals distinct oxygenation patterns and perfusion efficiencies across phenotypes, providing quantitative volumetric distributions. NGSS offers a novel, high-resolution, non-invasive approach for microcirculatory assessment, showing significant promise for both clinical monitoring and fundamental vascular research.Key word: Photoacoustic Computed Tomography (PACT), Microcirculation, Vascular Occlusion Test (VOT), Oxygenation dynamics, Segmentation analysis

Introduction

Microcirculation is a highly intricate and dynamic network comprising arterioles, capillaries, venules, and arteriovenous shunts [1], [2]. These small vessels primary interface for oxygen exchange, nutrient delivery, and metabolic waste clearance and play a pivotal role in maintaining tissue viability [3], [4]. Unlike macrocirculation, which governs the bulk blood transport through large arteries and veins, microcirculation operates at the tissue level, dynamically regulating perfusion in response to metabolic demand. Its functional integrity is critical for homeostasis, and its disruption can precipitate tissue hypoxia, inflammation, and organ failure [5], [6].

Microcirculatory dysfunction is recognized as a central pathophysiological mechanism in a wide range of diseases, including sepsis [7], [8], diabetes mellitus [9], [10], trauma [11], [12], peripheral vascular disease [13], and various forms of shock [14], [15]. In many of these conditions, microvascular alterations often occur earlier than measurable changes in systemic hemodynamic parameters, making microcirculation a valuable target for early diagnosis and treatment monitoring. However, assessing microvascular function in vivo remains a considerable challenge, primarily due to the lack of imaging tools that can resolve its fine spatial and functional heterogeneity.

Laser Doppler flowmetry [16] and Doppler ultrasound (US) [17] are widely used to monitor blood circulation, providing readouts of velocity and perfusion. However, in the microcirculation, where perfusion volumes are small and flow speeds are low, their sensitivity diminishes and subtle changes can be difficult to detect. Contrast-enhanced US [18] can boost detectability by using microbubble agents and perfusion-kinetic analyses, yet the resulting metrics primarily reflect perfusion rather than the oxygenation state of the microvasculature. Consequently, even with contrast enhancement, these modalities cannot quantify local hemoglobin oxygenation or hypoxic burden, underscoring the need for complementary oxygenation-sensitive imaging.

Several clinical techniques have been employed to evaluate tissue oxygenation as a surrogate for microcirculatory function, with near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) and transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO₂) among the most widely used. NIRS offers a noninvasive and continuous means of monitoring hemoglobin oxygenation (sO₂) based on differential light absorption [19], [20], [21]. However, its signals are averaged over a relatively large and poorly defined tissue volume that includes arteries, capillaries, and veins. As a result, it lacks the spatial resolution necessary to distinguish between vessel types or capture regional heterogeneity [22]. TcPO₂, on the other hand, measures the diffusion of oxygen through the skin by heating a small surface area and assessing the partial pressure of oxygen transcutaneously [23], [24]. While it provides valuable information about superficial tissue oxygenation, the measurement is susceptible to local skin perfusion, temperature, and sensor positioning [25]. Moreover, like NIRS, TcPO₂ lacks the ability to resolve the microvascular architecture or differentiate between vessel compartments spatially.

Photoacoustic (PA) imaging combines the rich optical contrast of hemoglobin absorption with the high spatial resolution of US detection, enabling label-free mapping of blood oxygenation in vivo [26], [27]. Optical-resolution photoacoustic microscopy has demonstrated excellent capability for resolving capillary-level oxygenation in superficial tissues, but its application is restricted to depths within 1 mm due to light scattering and attenuation [28], [29]. In contrast, photoacoustic computed tomography (PACT) allows for deeper imaging to depths of several centimeters, while maintaining hundreds of microns resolution for vascular mapping [30], [31], [32]. However, PACT cannot distinguish the fine structure of microvessel subtypes, limiting its functional interpretability.

In this study, we leveraged time-resolved PACT data acquired during the vascular occlusion test (VOT) to reveal the spatial heterogeneity of oxygenation dynamics at a hundred-micron scale. To systematically characterize this heterogeneity, we developed a significance-analysis-based Nine-Grid Segmentation Strategy (NGSS), which classifies microregions into nine distinct non-arterial categories based on their oxygenation response during occlusion and reperfusion efficiency following release. Unlike conventional VOT assessments (e.g., NIRS or tcPO2) that generally report bulk desaturation or reoxygenation slopes or minima, NGSS significantly improves biomarker quantification by shifting from global metrics to spatially resolved phenotyping. This framework resolves the differentiated microregion-level performance under VOT challenges and reveals distinct oxygenation trajectories: during occlusion, in addition to the expected regions of significant decrease, there exist microregions with minimal fluctuation (non-significant change) and even those exhibiting a paradoxical significant rise. Upon reperfusion, while most regions display hyperemic responses, three characteristic recovery trends emerge across different phenotypes, reflecting the relative reperfusion efficiency of specific locations. Furthermore, NGSS estimates the volumetric proportions of these microregion classes, providing distributions that can serve as potential references for medical diagnosis. By offering sub-millimeter spatial resolution and significance-level phenotypic readouts, NGSS complements existing monitoring modalities and provides a scalable framework for functional vascular imaging.

Methods

Dual-modal US-PA system setup

To measure oxygen saturation in tissues, we employed a dual-wavelength laser system for PA imaging (Fig. S1(a)). The system utilized a 700 nm laser with OPO output (Amplitude, SL I 20) and a 1064 nm Nd:YAG pulsed laser (Spectral Physics). These specific wavelengths were selected due to the significant differences in the absorption spectra of hemoglobin at these points. The intensity variations between the two wavelengths enable rapid distinction of blood oxygenation levels. Both lasers functioned at a pulse repetition frequency (PRF) of 20 Hz. The chosen wavelengths work at energy fluence levels of 5.4 mJ/cm² (@700 nm) and 15 mJ/cm² (@1064 nm), respectively, compliant with ANSI safety standards [33]. Laser light was delivered via a customized split-pattern fiber bundle that generated 4 cm2 rectangular beams positioned on either side of a 128-element linear US probe (Verasonics, L11–4V). This probe was integrated with two fiber bundles into a single detection unit, operating at a center frequency of 6.25 MHz with a 96% bandwidth. The reconstructed PA point-spread function yielded a full-width at half-maximum of 377.3 µm (lateral), 292.9 µm (axial) and 760 µm (elevational), derived from Gaussian fits to the corresponding line-spread profiles (see Supplementary Fig. S1). Effective acoustic coupling between the probe and the skin was achieved using US gel.

System control, including laser excitation, US signal acquisition, and PA signal detection, was managed by a research platform (Verasonics, Vantage 256). To synchronize PA and US imaging (Fig. S1(b)), an US sequence transmitted five plane waves at angles of –18°, –9°, 0°, 9°, and 18°, with a 100 µs interval between pulses. Once the US data acquisition was completed, the system triggered the laser for PA excitation. PA signals were recorded immediately after laser firing, and the entire imaging sequence was completed within 50 ms, enabling a frame rate of 20 Hz. 3D datasets were acquired using a continuous linear stage (Zaber X-MCB2) translating at 5 mm/s. Dual wavelengths were fired at the same spatial location with an inter-pulse delay of 5 ms, corresponding to 25 µm motion, far below the system’s spatial resolution along the scan direction. Thus, the paired measurements can be treated as spatiotemporally co-registered for sO₂ estimation. For image reconstruction, a weight-based Delay-and-Sum (DAS) beamforming algorithm was applied to both US and PA data.

During the measurement process, slight hand movements are unavoidable. If uncorrected, these shifts can lead to inaccurate comparisons of blood oxygen levels at the same anatomical location over time, potentially causing misinterpretation of physiological changes. Nine-grid segmentation strategy

To systematically characterize the spatial heterogeneity of microvascular oxygenation dynamics, we developed a significance-analysis-based NGSS. This analytical framework evaluates the temporal variations of oxygen saturation (∆sO₂) at the microregion level during the VOT to classify pixels into distinct physiological phenotypes. Prior to classification, arterial pixels identified by consistently high oxygen saturation (sO₂ > 90%) and non-significant fluctuations during the VOT were masked out. For the remaining non-arterial microregions, the NGSS evaluates their responses during the late occlusion phase (7–8 min) and the stable reperfusion phase (9–10 min). Based on a statistical thresholding criterion (using a one-sided 95% significance criterion), these regions are categorized into three states during occlusion (significant downward, non-significantly changed, and significant upward) and three corresponding states during reperfusion. Cross-combining the responses from these two phases yields nine distinct microvascular categories. Because the design of specific evaluation windows (e.g., 7–8 min for occlusion) and the selection of statistical threshold values are inherently data-driven, the detailed mathematical derivation and step-by-step establishment of the NGSS criteria are presented alongside the corresponding findings in the Results section.

Near-infrared spectroscopy

NIRS is a non-invasive technique used to measure blood oxygen saturation levels. It operates on the principle that different substances absorb near-infrared light at varying wavelengths. Specifically, NIRS measures differential absorption of oxy- and deoxy-hemoglobin. [22] This study analyzed the NIRS data collected using the Enginmed system (Enginmed, N17) and compared it with the PA results.

Transcutaneous oxygen pressure

The TcPO2 was measured using a Clark-type electrode, a non-invasive method for assessing the partial pressure of oxygen (pO₂) at the skin surface and underlying tissues [38], [39]. The probe consisted of a small chamber containing silver and platinum electrodes enclosed within an oxygen-permeable membrane, preheated to 42–45°C to induce arterialized cutaneous blood flow [40]. This localized heating promoted vasodilation and increased oxygen diffusion through the skin. The measured values were automatically adjusted to a standard temperature of 37°C. [25]

Before measurement, subjects were required to rest in a quiet room maintained at 25°C and to abstain from tobacco and alcohol for at least 12 h. TcPO2 was subsequently measured in designated angiosome regions. In this study, all measurements were used the same device (Radiometer, TCM4), and the results were compared with the PA results.

Experiments in vascular occlusion test

The thenar eminence was strategically selected as the region of interest (ROI) for PACT monitoring due to its unique anatomical and optical properties. Specifically, the palmar epidermis typically contains much lower melanin content than other skin regions, serving as a highly transmissive 'photoacoustic window' that minimizes epidermal signal interference and maximizes light penetration. Furthermore, this region possesses a dense superficial microvascular plexus immediately beneath the melanin-deficient epidermis. This specific configuration ensures a high contrast-to-noise ratio and consistent signal acquisition, allowing for reliable sO2 quantification regardless of individual skin pigmentation levels.

The following protocols are used for the VOTs in this paper. A blood pressure (BP) cuff was placed on the upper arm of the volunteers. The experiment commenced with the collection of a 4-minute baseline, followed by the inflation of a blood pressure cuff to a range of 160–170 mmHg to ensure complete occlusion of superficial arteriovenous blood flow for a duration of 4 min [41]. The final 4 min involved releasing the cuff pressure, allowing the natural recovery of blood flow [42]. PA and US imaging data were acquired at a frame rate of 20 Hz throughout the experiment. Each measurement cycle spanned 2 s, capturing 40 averaged frames representing the temporal result at each time point. Due to memory constraints, dual-modal data were manually stored every 30 s. However, real-time data storage is achievable with current technology, and 30-second interval did not impact the study's conclusions.

Results

VOT characterization using PACT

Dynamic PA measurements were obtained during a 12-minute VOT performed in nine healthy volunteers (aged 23–38 years). The protocol included a 4-minute baseline phase (0–4 min), a 4-minute occlusion phase via BP cuff (4–8 min), and a 4-minute reperfusion phase (8–12 min). Fig. 1(a) illustrates representative PA results from a 23-year-old male volunteer. sO₂ gradually declined during occlusion, followed by rapid recovery after cuff release. US-guided segmentation [43] (detail in Supplementary Note S2) was employed to analyze distinct anatomical regions: a peripheral region (2 mm × 20 mm, spO₂) and a muscle region (2 mm × 20 mm, stO₂), as shown at the top left of Fig. 1(a). The peripheral region received its strongest PA signal contribution primarily from the cutaneous microvascular plexus, followed by subcutaneous arterioles and venules embedded within the superficial fat layer. The latter region mainly encompasses the thenar muscles and was used to assess intramuscular perfusion. Across all regions, mean sO₂ levels decreased by 7.1% during occlusion and rebounded by 9.2% post-release (Fig. 1(b)). Although spO₂ and stO₂ values at the 8-minute time point were significantly different (Fig. 1(c), P = 0.017), both followed similar temporal trends, and the maximum absolute change from baseline for either measure was less than 11.6%. The shaded area around the lines represents the standard error (SE). This indicates that average oxygenation changes across large tissue regions followed only a subtle trend.

Refining the ROI to a smaller area (1.5 mm × 5 mm) revealed amplified physiological responses (Fig. 1(d)). PA signals at 700 and 1064 nm showed apparent opposite changes during occlusion, correlating with hemoglobin deoxygenation. The mean sO₂ level change in this region exceeded 36.0%. Post-release, sO₂ levels rebounded by 16.3%, underscoring the sensitivity of PA measurements in localized microvascular changes.

We compared results against two established non-invasive methods, NIRS and TcPO₂, to validate the PACT-derived blood oxygen measurements. Practical illustrations of the three measurement methods are provided in Supplementary Fig. S4. NIRS data (n = 4, Fig. 1(e)), sampled every 2 s during VOT, revealed a marked decline in blood oxygen levels, with a maximum reduction of 32.5% during the occlusion phase. TcPO₂ measurements (Fig. 1(f)) demonstrated a pronounced physiological response, showing a 49.8 mmHg decrease in oxygen partial pressure (pO₂) and a 9.2 mmHg rise in carbon dioxide partial pressure (pCO₂) under occlusion. These findings highlight impaired oxygen delivery and metabolic byproduct accumulation (e.g., CO₂) during vascular obstruction, consistent with ischemic tissue responses [44].

A direct comparison between PACT and NIRS was performed by analyzing absolute differences in three parameters: stO₂, spO₂, and overall sO₂. As shown in Fig. 1(g), PACT and NIRS measurements aligned closely during baseline and recovery phases (average difference <5%), confirming methodological consistency under stable physiological conditions. However, significant discrepancies emerged during occlusion across all metrics (stO₂, spO₂, and sO₂).

Spatially averaged PACT data from larger regions showed only mild changes in oxygenation. In contrast, smaller regions (e.g., 1.5 mm × 5 mm) exhibited significantly sharper drops of up to 36.0% during vascular occlusion. These pronounced declines were further supported by consistent trends observed in NIRS and TcPO₂ measurements. These results reveal pronounced spatial heterogeneity in the PA measurements, reflecting local differences in microvascular density, vascular distribution.

To further investigate spatial variations in oxygenation, we performed 3D imaging using PACT. The scanning area is indicated by the yellow box in Fig. 2(a). A motorized step-scanning approach was used to acquire a volumetric dataset covering approximately 1.7 cm × 2.5 cm × 0.3 cm of the thenar muscle. The starting depth was set at 1 mm below the skin surface, utilizing the US-guided segmentation described in Supplementary Note S2 for precise anatomical localization. Along the scanning direction, we acquired 68 frames and then interpolated them to 170 frames, yielding a reconstructed volume with an isotropic voxel size of approximately 100 µm. Each 3D volume is completed within 3.4 s, VOT oxygenation dynamics evolve on the minute scale, rendering both the dual-wavelength delay and the intra-volume timing spread negligible relative to the physiological time constants. The resulting 3D PA images (Fig. 2(d)) were processed using US-derived skin surface data to remove superficial signals, thereby enhancing the visualization of underlying vascular structures. Using the same vascular occlusion protocol, we generated 3D sO₂ maps. Maximum amplitude projection (MAP) images on the x-z plane (Fig. 2(e)) revealed detailed vascular networks and enabled a comprehensive spatial assessment of oxygenation changes during occlusion and reperfusion.

The 3D projections provided a clearer view of regional trends in blood oxygen changes. Excluding arteries, most vessels showed a decrease in sO₂ during occlusion, consistent with an ischemic response, followed by recovery in sO₂ after release. However, some vascular structures exhibited slight reductions or even unexpected increases in sO₂ during occlusion, suggesting the presence of local compensatory mechanisms [45], [46] such as arteriovenous shunting.

Three representative vessels (dashed boxes in Fig. 2(e)) were selected for further analysis. Rectangular boxes in Fig. 2(e) serve only as search windows; quantitative sO₂ time courses were derived from manually delineated vessel-level ROIs as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S5.Vessel 1 was located at the center of the area, where the most pronounced oxygen decrease was observed, with a 14.5% drop, possibly reflecting capillary oxygen extraction (Fig. 2(f)) [47]. Vessel 2, a vein, showed a 3.7% decrease (Fig. 2(g)), while vessel 3 exhibited an unexpected 9.3% increase during occlusion (Fig. 2(h)), likely due to compensatory shunting from nearby arteries [48], [49].

To further characterize oxygenation responses, we performed a microregion-level significance-response segmentation based on the observed sO₂ dynamics. Guided by the NIRS readouts and the overall PA time courses, we identified 7–8 min (late occlusion) as the most prominent yet relatively stable intervals for evaluation. Accordingly, we used the 1-min window mean (three samples) as the test statistic. This window length effectively suppresses high-frequency disturbances and improves robustness, while avoiding over-smoothing and potential bias associated with excessively long averaging.

Next, because baseline sO₂ fluctuations were small and approximately symmetric [50], [51], we modeled the baseline variation as Gaussian (Supplementary Fig. S6) and constructed decision thresholds using the baseline standard deviation (SD, σ). Since the test statistic is the 3-point window mean, its SD decreases to σˉ(σˉ=σ/3) under an independent and identically distributed assumption. With this normalization, a ±σ criterion corresponds to an exceedance probability of ∼8.3% (two-sided) or ∼4.2% (one-sided) (derivation in Supplementary Note S4). We therefore identified microregions exhibiting significant physiological changes within a hypothesis-testing framework, using a one-sided criterion of P < 0.05.

We further quantified the discrepancy between the Gaussian approximation and the empirical distribution. The threshold derived from the Gaussian model achieved a mean accuracy of 92.9% across subjects (Supplementary Note S5), enabling substantial computational simplification without compromising precision. Importantly, the proposed strategy estimates a subject-specific baseline threshold, allowing the decision boundary to adapt to measurement stability, improving cross-subject comparability, and reducing false detections relative to a fixed global threshold.

Because arteries have almost no sO₂ change during VOT [52], we removed pixels with sO₂ consistently above 90% [53] and ∆sO₂ changes remaining within σ throughout the VOT to exclude arterial region (ArtR). Based on the average ∆sO₂ during the final minute of occlusion, each pixel was classified into one of three categories:

For the O-SDR categories (G1–3), baseline sO₂ levels range from 79.9% to 92.1%. During the occlusion phase, the mean sO₂ within these microregions decreases by more than 23.6%. The sO₂ decrease in these regions is primarily driven by capillaries within the microcirculation. The significant discrepancy between the sO₂ changes measured in Fig. 1(b) and Fig. 1(e) arises because PA imaging exhibits higher sensitivity to larger vessels, whereas NIRS is more sensitive to microvasculature. Notably, during vascular occlusion, the sO₂ dynamic trends within the O-SDR microregions identified by NGSS align more closely with the results obtained from NIRS, as shown in Supplementary Note. S6. This alignment strongly supports the practicality of the NGSS-based microregion segmentation. Following the release of the occlusion, G1 pixels (with a baseline sO₂ of 79.9%) experience a rapid post-occlusion rebound of 17.5%. In contrast, G2 pixels (baseline sO₂ of 91.7%) recover to levels slightly above the baseline, while G3 pixels (baseline sO₂ of 92.1%) exhibit a gradual upward trend without a sharp rebound.

In the O-NCR group (G4–6), baseline sO₂ values range from 75.2% to 86.9%. These pixels show minimal change during occlusion, behaving more like functional veins. After the blockage, G4 pixels briefly surged to 93.1% due to efficient perfusion, G5 pixels remained stable, and G6 pixels declined, possibly because they received deoxygenated blood from neighboring capillaries.

For the O-SUR categories (G7–9), baseline sO₂ levels are relatively low, ranging from 65.2% to 74.7%. During the occlusion phase, all O-SUR types exhibit an oxygenation increase exceeding 16.8%, which may be attributed to the arteriovenous shunting phenomenon within the tissue. Upon release of the occlusion, these groups initially show a decline, reflecting the closure of the shunt channels. Specifically, G7 pixels recover to high blood oxygen levels due to efficient perfusion; G8 pixels display a less pronounced decline owing to partial perfusion; whereas G9 pixels continue to decline as they receive deoxygenated blood from the capillaries, only returning to baseline around the 10–11 min mark when oxygen-rich blood finally arrives.

With US-guided structural segmentation, we further analyzed the dynamic sO₂ changes across the peripheral and muscle region. Fig. S2 presents the dynamic sO₂ changes in nine types of non-arterial microregions within these two tissues. Compared to the clear tissue separation trend observed in Fig. 1(c), the sO₂ changes in the peripheral and muscle regions in Supplementary Fig. S7 are highly similar across microregions subtypes. This suggests that when sO₂ is assessed at the microregion subtype level, the tissue background has a minimal effect on the oxygenation behavior of the same type of vessel. The physiological properties of the vessels, such as their functional status and blood flow patterns, likely play the dominant role.

This finding also indicates that the differences observed between tissues in Fig. 1(c) are not due to variations in individual microregion responses but are more likely attributed to the differing distributions of the nine microregions types between the two tissues. This structural distribution difference aligns with tissue-specific microcirculatory configurations.

To further investigate the spatial organization of the identified hemodynamic patterns, we mapped one distribution (Corresponding results in Fig. 1(a)) of the nine NGSS-classified bins back onto the grayscale PACT images. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S8, each color-coded sub-image (G1–G9) reveals the distinct spatial localization of pixels belonging to its respective category.

Microregion volume ratio determined by NGSS

After applying the NGSS classification, the volumetric ratio for each microregions type was calculated by dividing the number of pixels in each grid by the total number of pixels. Fig. 5 shows our analysis of volume ratios across nine microregions types (G1–9). In Fig. 5(a), the horizontal axis represents the volume ratio of peripheral microregions, while the vertical axis represents that of tissue microregions. Each black dot corresponds to an independent measurement (n = 9), and the red asterisk indicates the mean value of nine measurements. The marginal density curves provide smooth estimates of the data distribution using kernel density estimation.

Volumetric ratios analysis of vascular types. (a) Scatter plots of volumetric ratios of NGSS-defined vascular types (G1-9) in the peripheral (x-axis) and muscle (y-axis) region. The blue line represents equal value. (b) Average volumetric ratios of O-SDR, O-NCR, and O-SUR, and ArtR microregions (± SE, n = 9). (c) Volumetric ratios of different perfusion efficiency (R-SUR, R-NCR, and R-SDR); (d-e) Radar chart of vascular type proportions in peripheral (d) and deep tissue (e), respectively.

The scatter plot clearly demonstrates distinct variations in volume proportions and perfusion efficiency across different microregion types. Fig. 5(b) summarizes the overall composition of these microregions: ArtR accounts for 10.5%, O-SDR for 40.0%, O-NCR for 29.8%, and O-SUR for 19.7%. These results can be explained by established physiological principles. Specifically, the arterial blood volume within skeletal muscle is relatively limited [58], [59]; in contrast, the oxygenation responses in O-NCR and O-SUR are primarily contributed by veins, which serve as the predominant blood volume reservoirs [56], [57]. The oxygenation dynamics of O-SDR are mainly driven by capillaries, whose blood volume typically equals or exceeds that of arteries. This proportion may be particularly pronounced in small muscles with dense capillary networks, such as the thenar muscles [60].

We also analyzed the relationship between the proportion of significant-change microregions and the baseline thresholds across nine volunteers. The results showed no correlation between the significant proportion and the threshold, demonstrating the feasibility of the current thresholding approach. Further details can be found in Note S7 of the Supplementary Information.

Additionally, in high-perfusion microregions of O-NCR and O-SUR, and normal-perfusion O-SDR types, the mean values deviate significantly from the proportionality line. These microregions types constitute the main components with larger volume proportions, suggesting that the observed inter-tissue differences in Fig. 5(c) arise from differing microregions type distributions in the peripheral and muscle region, rather than significant variations in the same microregions types performance across tissues.

Finally, the radar charts in Fig. 5(d-e) further illustrate the distribution of microregions volume ratios. Most values lie above the central dashed line, while low-perfusion microregions are predominantly located in the lower segment. Although this pattern is typical of healthy volunteers, patients with underlying vascular pathology may exhibit higher volume ratios in the lower segment. These findings suggest that radar chart-based visualization may serve as a valuable tool for the screening or classification of vascular abnormalities.

Discussion

The microcirculation, as the terminal site for oxygen exchange and cellular metabolism, plays a central role in maintaining organ function under both physiological and pathological conditions. However, microvascular dysfunction exhibits substantial heterogeneity across individuals, anatomical locations, and disease stages. Current monitoring techniques, such as NIRS and TcPO₂, provide only regional or superficial assessments and lack the spatial resolution to capture microvascular variability. Thus, a significant unmet need in critical care is the ability to monitor microcirculatory function dynamically and quantitatively, with both spatial and temporal precision.

To address this challenge, we developed the NGSS, an analytical framework combining PACT with VOT. While PACT has demonstrated significant clinical potential in monitoring oxygenation within well-defined macro-vessels, the NGSS framework further empowers PACT to classify complex micro-regions. NGSS enables microregions classification evaluating oxygenation dynamics and recovery efficiency, offering an integrated approach to assess tissue oxygen consumption and volumetric composition. For instance, our findings showed that O-SUR microregions are more prevalent in muscle (comprising 22% of the total volume) than in the peripheral region (17%), suggesting the presence of anatomical pathways that facilitate oxygen bypass[61], [62]. Similarly, O-NCR microregions were more abundant in the muscle region (35%) than in the peripheral region (30%), reflecting enhanced venous return capacity in metabolically active tissues[62], [63]. These observations highlight the oxygenation heterogeneity across tissue types, features that are difficult to detect using conventional tools.

Supported by the presented data, prospective clinical use of the NGSS includes: (i) identifying microcirculatory heterogeneity and shunting in sepsis; (ii) functional screening and longitudinal follow-up in diabetic microangiopathy; and (iii) assessing vascular reactivity in older patients. From physiological principles[64], we derive and discuss the following possible scenarios. In sepsis, physiologic reasoning suggests perfusion-oxygenation uncoupling with venous ‘arterialization’ and adjacent low-sO₂ patches. NGSS is expected to show a smaller and slower O-SDR sO₂ decrease during occlusion and generally limited recovery during reperfusion, with many regions exhibiting absent or only mild responses, thereby shifting the final microregion-type proportions. In diabetes, capillary rarefaction and low baseline venous sO₂ lead to heterogeneous occlusion responses dominant by small declines with occasional focal strong drops; reperfusion is slow and limited, which also modifies the final NGSS proportion readouts. In aging, reduced vascular compliance and impaired endothelium-dependent dilation manifest as baseline vascular proportion changes and greater heterogeneity. Occlusion-phase decreases can resemble those of healthy subjects, whereas reperfusion rises are slower, again altering NGSS-derived proportions.

Additionally, understanding the target regions and limitations of each detection method is crucial for accurately interpreting results and making informed clinical decisions. PACT, NIRS, and TcPO₂ are based on distinct physical principles; therefore, discrepancies in their readings under identical conditions are expected rather than contradictory. PACT measures the optical absorption of hemoglobin with centimeter-scale penetration depth and high spatial resolution, generating sO₂ maps that reveal tissue heterogeneity and arteriovenous contrast; however, its sensitivity to extremely small vessels remain limited. NIRS integrates multiple scattered light within a relatively large sampling volume and reports volume-averaged stO₂ (a mixture of arterial and venous blood), but it possesses limited spatial resolution and is insufficiently sensitive to larger vessels. TcPO₂ utilizes heated electrodes to record skin tissue pO₂ (mmHg), reflecting the superficial microcirculation, though its dynamic changes are slower and smoother. Clinically, these differences suggest that no single metric should be interpreted in isolation. For instance, PACT exhibits higher sensitivity to larger vessels compared to NIRS but may underestimate the sO₂ of the smallest capillaries due to current sensitivity constraints. This inherent hardware limitation creates a discrepancy between PACT and NIRS signals, as the former is more susceptible to "contamination" from larger vessels with weaker responses. In light of this distinction, we proposed the NGSS framework, which segment tissue into microregions based on their specific dynamic responses. By decoupling high-response signals (e.g., O-SDR) from large-vessel signals, NGSS prevents unique phenotypes from being masked by spatial averaging.

Although NGSS provides a new path for microcirculatory assessment, several limitations remain to be addressed. First, the spatial resolution and system sensitivity of PACT still constrain measurement, high-frequency and high-quality ultrasound probes can further refine the results of microregion segmentation. Second, future systems with fast, programmable wavelength switching could improve the accuracy of sO₂ estimation. However, the OPO used in this study tunes relatively slowly (∼15 nm/s) and requires manual adjustment, which limits the feasibility of multi-wavelength acquisition for minute-scale VOT dynamics. In addition, a more stable high-speed motorized stage together with a higher-repetition-rate laser would further enhance 3D scanning capability, enabling larger-area volumetric assessment. Regarding the algorithm, we utilize each subject’s baseline standard deviation based on statistical significance, which can be flexibly adjusted according to the requirements of specific statistical analyses. A current limitation of this thresholding approach is the lack of a strict, one-to-one correspondence between classification results and physiological categories. Future studies could focus on correlated physiological experiments to establish thresholding strategies that align more rigorously with anatomical structures. Finally, this study is limited by a relatively small sample size and a lack of multicenter validation, making large-scale clinical studies essential for further clinical translation.
