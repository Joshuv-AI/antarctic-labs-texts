Prevalence and determinants of healthy and balanced diet among office workers in a sedentary working environment: evidence from Southern Sri Lanka
Authors: Janaka Godevithana, Champa Jayalakshmie Wijesinghe, Millawage Supun Dilara Wijesinghe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAn unhealthy diet is a key risk factor for non-communicable diseases (NCD), which account for a significant number of premature deaths and disability-adjusted life years worldwide. Office workers are reported to have unhealthy and unbalanced diets, while being sedentary due to the nature of their work, placing them at a greater risk of NCD. This study aimed to determine dietary intake and associated factors among sedentary office workers in Southern Sri Lanka.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 518 sedentary workers in 20 offices in the Galle district. Socio-demographic, health and work-related factors and dietary practices were assessed using a self-administered questionnaire. Twenty-four-hour dietary recall was used to assess dietary intake, which was converted into the number of servings from each food group. Healthy dietary intake was defined as ‘adherence to the numbers of servings recommended in Food Based Dietary Guidelines for Sri Lankans for more than three food groups including cereal and cereal-based foods, fruits, and vegetables, with the consumption of one or no unhealthy food per day’.ResultsOnly 4.6% (n = 24) of the participants consumed a healthy diet, while a considerable proportion had the recommended intake of cereal-based foods, vegetables and fish, meat and pulses (65.3%, 65.8% and 50.8%, respectively). Intake of fruits, dairy products, nuts and seeds was low among the participants. Meal skipping and group eating were significantly associated with an overall unhealthy diet. Stratified analysis showed sex as an effect modifier for the association between group eating and unhealthy diet. In multivariate analysis, none of the factors showed a significant association with healthy dietary intake.ConclusionsThe dietary intake of sedentary office workers was not up to the recommendations. Meal skipping and group eating were associated with unhealthy dietary intake. This study recommends introducing interventions to improve the intake of fruits, dairy products, nuts, and seeds for sedentary office workers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20935-6.

Background

A healthy diet is broadly defined as “a pattern of food intake that has beneficial effects on health or at least no harmful effects” [1]. Although a universal definition of a poor quality or unhealthy diet has yet to be formed, a consensus has been reached on some of its features such as a high intake of processed food, trans- and saturated fat, added salt and sugar, sugar-sweetened beverages and a low intake of fresh fruits, vegetables, nuts, and whole grain products [2]. Food-Based Dietary Guidelines (FBDG) generally recommend having a varied and balanced diet while increasing fruit and vegetable intake and reducing fat, sugar, and salt intake [1]. According to the United Nations Food and Agricultural Organization (UNFAO), dietary guidelines published by more than 100 countries provide similar recommendations [3]. Correspondingly, the FBDG for Sri Lankans (FBDG-SL) also recommend having a variety of foods in adequate amounts [2].

The Global Burden of Diseases (GBD) study noted that unhealthy or suboptimal dietary practices accounted for 11 million deaths and 255 million disability-adjusted life years (DALY) in 2017 [3]. Several studies have identified the causal and protective effects of diet on cardio-metabolic disorders [4].

As highlighted by both systematic reviews and observational studies, dietary risk factors are found to be more common among office workers, and their dietary intake does not meet global or local recommendations [5]. Excess salt intake was higher in India (47.2%) and low fruit and vegetable intake was also higher among office workers in India [5]. However, a comprehensive study of the overall dietary intake of office workers has not been conducted globally or locally. The published studies have focused mainly on the intake of selected food groups, such as fruits, vegetables and salt, rather than assessing the overall intake of a healthy and balanced diet [5]. Only two previous studies have been conducted among Sri Lankan office workers in 2015 and 2017, which reported an increased intake of carbohydrates and added sugar and a decreased intake of fruits and vegetables [6, 7]. However, research findings focusing on the overall healthiness of the diet of office workers are still lacking not only locally, but also in the global literature.

The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics of America has proposed a socio-ecological model for the factors affecting diet and dietary behaviour [8]. It describes these factors under broad categories, such as individual factors, environmental settings, sectors of influence, and social and cultural norms and values. Individual factors such as age, sex, and ethnicity were identified as determinants of dietary intake and dietary behaviour [8]. Understandably, nutritional knowledge was found to be associated with better dietary behaviours in several studies in both high- and low-income populations [9, 10] and work-related factors such as busy schedules and time constraints were associated with unhealthy dietary practices among the working population in USA [11, 12]. However, there is a notable lack of studies assessing the factors associated with dietary intake in developing countries, particularly among high-risk populations such as office workers.

In Sri Lanka, 52.3% of the total population participates in the workforce, 57.6% of which consists of professionals, technical and associate workers, and clerical categories who perform office-based duties except in the Departments of Health and Education [13]. Thus, a significant proportion of workers are sedentary due to their nature of work, and their diet can play a major role in the risk of developing non-communicable diseases (NCD) in the workforce.

While accounting for 17.4% of the total years of life lost (YLL) and 12.7% of the total DALY globally [14], NCD has been the leading cause of death in Sri Lanka in the recent years, which contributed to more than three-fourths of deaths [15] and most of the DALYs in the country in 2019 [16]. World Health Organization’s (WHO) STEPwise approach to NCD risk factor surveillance (STEPS) conducted in Sri Lanka in 2015 revealed a high prevalence of unhealthy diet and physical inactivity among both males and females in general [17], however, the office workers being a more educated and an economically privileged group, it would be worthwhile to investigate whether the dietary intake of this specific population differs from the reported dietary intake of the general population.

As an unhealthy and unbalanced diet is a well-established risk factor for developing future NCDs and the office workers’ risk for NCD is high due to the sedentary nature of their work, it is important to study the prevalence and determinants of unhealthy and unbalanced diets among this population. Further, an in-depth understanding of unhealthy and unbalanced dietary intake among office workers is essential in planning and implementing interventions to improve diet quality, thereby preventing the future risks of NCD. On the other hand, socio-economic and cultural factors associated with unhealthy and unbalanced diets in developing countries are yet to be explored, particularly among the young, apparently healthy and working populations. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the dietary intake and related practices, and the factors associated with unhealthy and unbalanced diets among office workers in a sedentary working environment in Southern Sri Lanka.

This study will enhance the understanding of the extent and correlates of unhealthy and unbalanced dietary intake among office workers locally, while enriching the global knowledge base with evidence from the socio-economic and cultural context of a developing country in Southeast Asia. Moreover, this study defines a healthy and balanced diet based on FBDG Sri Lanka, therefore, it will contribute to the global evidence on adherence to FBDG in different populations. Lastly, the findings of this study will provide directions on specific groups and factors to be targeted in changing dietary behaviours among office workers, which will enrich and shape future interventions.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was a cross-sectional survey conducted in twenty government offices representing 19 Divisional Secretariat areas (a middle-level administrative division) in Galle District, Southern Sri Lanka. The selected offices included Divisional Secretariat offices and local authority offices that carry out administrative functions as per the directions given by central and local governments. The main office functions were clerical tasks and internal management work, performed by development officers, management assistants, and administrative and management officers.

Study participants

Development officers, management assistants, and administrative and management officers in the selected offices were recruited for the study. Workers who engaged in physical exertion during their duty and on special dietary plans were excluded from the sample.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using the formula suggested by Lwanga and Lameshow [18] to estimate a population proportion. The prevalence of healthy dietary intake was assumed to be 0.3, as reported by the 2016 STEPS Survey for the general Sri Lankan population as corresponding data for office workers were not available [17]. An alpha error of 5% and a precision of 5% were allowed in the sample size calculation. As cluster sampling was performed, a design effect of 1.58 was also incorporated in calculating the sample size.

A multistage, mixed sampling method was used to achieve the desired sample size. Twenty offices were selected purposively at the first stage to include offices with 30–40 clerical-type workers in each office. From each office, a cluster of 30 workers were selected randomly from the employees’ name list using a random number generator and were invited to participate in the study.

Study instruments and data collection

Data on socio-demographic characteristics, health and work-related factors and dietary practices were collected using a pretested, self-administered (survey) questionnaire. Participants were informed in advance to produce their clinical records (investigation reports, clinic records etc.) at the time of data collection to verify health-related data. The weight and height of the participants were measured by trained data collectors according to standard procedures described in the European Health Examination Survey (EHES) [19], and body mass index (BMI) was calculated.

The data on dietary intake of a typical office day were obtained by a trained medical officer using 24-hour dietary recall, which was supplemented by a picture guide to determine portion sizes. A computer software was used to calculate the number of servings described in the FBDG for Sri Lankans 2011.

Healthy dietary intake was defined as ‘adherence to the number of servings recommended in FBDG for Sri Lankans for more than three food groups including cereal and cereal-based foods, fruits and vegetables, with one or no unhealthy food per day’.

Statistical analysis

Sample characteristics were described under socio-demographic, health, and work-related factors. Dietary practices and intake were also described using descriptive statistics, and generalization of measures to the population was done with measures of dispersion [95% confidence interval (CI), standard deviation (SD), and inter-quartile range (IQR)]. Independent variables were categorized as shown in Table 4. Body mass index, which was considered as an associated factor of dietary intake, was categorized using the cutoff values recommended for the Asian population by WHO [20] and a regional consensus [21]. Dietary intake (dependent variable) was categorized as overall healthy or unhealthy intake based on the FBDG for Sri Lankans as defined above.

Chi-square test was used to assess the statistical significance of the observed associations between variables at a probability level of 0.05. Multivariate analysis was conducted using multiple logistic regression to detect the determinants of overall healthy dietary intake. The forward selection method was used to select variables for the model, and p values for entry and exit were kept at 0.05 and 0.1, respectively.

Ethical and administrative clearance

The study was conducted adhering to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki on ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee, Faculty of Medicine, University of Ruhuna (Registration No. 2020/P/105). Administrative approval was obtained from all relevant authorities (District Secretary Galle District, Provincial Minister of Local Government, and Regional Director of Health Services Galle District). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Workers identified with health issues were referred to the nearest healthcare facility with their consent.

Results

Five hundred and eighteen office workers from 20 offices participated in the study (response rate 84.9%). The median age of the participants was 39.5 (IQR = 8) years, and the ages ranged from 23 to 59 years. The majority were females (81.9%), and approximately 95% were educated up to the General Certificate of Education (GCE) Advanced Level or above. Health status and dietary practices of the office workers

The mean BMI of the sample was 24.2 (SD = 3.9) kgm−2, and 56.9% of the participants had a normal BMI according to the cut-off values recommended for Asian populations. However, more than half of the participants were overweight or obese (38.2% and 24.7%, respectively). Only 11.5% of the participants had a personal history of NCD (diabetes 3.7% and hypertension 2.9%), while 64.8% of participants had a family history of NCD in a first-degree relative.

Snacking was common among office workers, and almost all participants had snacks as a part of their routine office day diet. Approximately 60% reported having more than one snack per day. Group eating and meal skipping were also prevalent among the participants (65.3% and 32.3%, respectively). Dietary intake of the office workers

When considering the intake of different food groups, the mean and median number of servings consumed were within the recommended range for cereal-based foods and vegetables (Table 3), and nearly two-thirds of the participants had recommended numbers of servings of these two food groups (65.3% and 65.8%, respectively). However, only half of the participants (50.8%) reached the recommended number of servings of fish, meat, and pulses, although the mean and median intakes were higher than the recommended range. The consumption of fruits, dairy products, nuts and seeds was low, with the mean and median number of servings consumed falling far below the recommended number of servings. The consumption of unhealthy foods was greater among participants with a mean/median of two, and only 36.7% consumed one or no unhealthy foods. Stratified analysis was conducted to assess the association of unhealthy dietary intake with meal skipping and having meals in groups within different categories of sex, education level, BMI and history of NCD. A significant difference between the odds ratios of group eating and unhealthy dietary intake was noted when stratified as males and females (p value for test of homogeneity of odds ratio = 0.036). However, stratified analysis did not reveal any other confounders or effect modifiers.

Discussion

This study focused on healthy dietary intake, related dietary practices and associated factors among office workers in Sri Lanka. The findings revealed that a majority of the office workers in in this study do not consume an overall healthy and balanced diet and the reported intake of fruits, dairy products and nuts and seeds was low compared to national dietary guidelines. Group eating and meal skipping were common dietary practices observed among the office workers. None of the demographic and health related characteristics or dietary practices of the office workers emerged as significant predictors of their unhealthy dietary intake. This discussion will mainly focus on the significance of above findings in light of the existing evidence, taking into account the representativeness of the study sample of the office worker population in the local context. Further, the implications of the findings for practice will be discussed with directions for future research.

Most of the participants (81.9%) in the present study were females. Although most of the workers in Sri Lanka are males, composition or sex distribution may vary in different sectors [13]. Most workers (94.8%) were educated up to the GCE Advanced Level or had completed tertiary education. Posts of development officers and management assistants require a degree and GCE Advanced Level educational qualifications, respectively. The findings of the current study concerning sex distribution and education level are consistent with those of a similar study conducted in the Colombo district [6]. Similarly, the percentages of participants using public transport and walking to reach the workplace are consistent with the data from the census of public sector workers in 2016, where 46.7% used public transport, and 5.7% walked to reach the office [13]. This indicates the representativeness of our study sample of the office workers in a sedentary working environment in the local context.

The reported mean BMI was 24.2 kgm−2, and 32.2% of the participants had a BMI in the normal range, while 38.2% and 24.7% were overweight and obese, respectively (Table 2). A similar study conducted among office workers in the Colombo district reported a mean BMI of 24.11 kgm−2 [6]. A comparable mean BMI (23.8 ± 3.6 kgm−2) was reported in a study among office workers in South India, which could be due to similarities in their socio-cultural environment, dietary patterns and economic statuses with those of Sri Lanka [22]. In contrast, a study conducted among Nepali industrial workers had reported a lower percentage with a normal BMI, overweight and obesity (46.8%, 35.8% and 11.1%, respectively) [23] However, a study of university workers in Saudi Arabia reported a higher mean BMI of 28.3 kgm−2, with 35.9% of the participants having a normal BMI and 31.0% being overweight and 30.7% being obese, reflecting the potential influence of the differences in socio-economic backgrounds [24]. Many studies on office workers conducted in the Southeast Asian countries have failed to report BMI values. Considering that BMI is a well-known factor influencing the dietary intake and eating behaviours [25, 26], the comparison of present study findings with global research data should be attempted with caution as mean BMI values tend to change with the social, cultural and economic context.

The prevalence of NCD remained lower at 11.5% in the current study compared to Sri Lankan labour force data (17%) [27]. This can be explained by the relatively younger population in the present study, with ages ranging from 23 to 59 years (median = 39.5 years and IQR = 8), whereas the labour force data included people over 60 years as well. Moreover, the reported prevalence of diabetes and hypertension were lower in this study than in the study conducted in 2017 by Swarnamali et al., involving government office workers in the Colombo district of Sri Lanka. In contrast, several studies conducted in India, Nepal and Thailand have reported a higher prevalence (35.1%, 84.5% and 27.8% respectively) of chronic NCD among working populations [22, 23, 28], while a lower prevalence of diabetes (9.4%) and hypertension (5.6%) has been reported in a study conducted among state workers in Ethiopia [29]. The current study considered only the participants with a confirmed diagnosis of chronic NCDs, possibly overlooking the undetected cases. This might have contributed to the low prevalence of chronic NCDs among the study sample.

Dietary intake among office workers

Consistent with Swarnamali et al. (2017), the current study reports an almost similar mean number of servings consumed from various food groups, except cereal-based and unhealthy foods among Sri Lankan office workers. In contrast to the higher consumption of cereal-based or carbohydrate-containing food in the former study (15.4 servings), the current study reports only a marginally high level of consumption of cereal-based foods. However, the method used to calculate servings was not specified in the former study, making comparisons difficult. Another large-scale Sri Lankan study reported a marginally high consumption of carbohydrates among public-sector workers [7].

Of all food groups, cereal-based food (the main contributor to dietary carbohydrates) was the most consumed. Consumption of fish, meat, pulses, and vegetables was within the range recommended in FBDG-SL. In contrast, consumption of fruits, dairy products, nuts, and seeds was far below the recommended level. Comparable studies assessing dietary intake among office workers at the regional level are lacking in the published literature. However, in a study conducted in Vietnam, a marginally low carbohydrate intake among young female workers was highlighted [30]. The few existing studies support the low fruit and vegetable intake among office workers observed in this study. A study among Saudi university employees also highlighted a lower consumption of vegetables and fruits, with only 14% of the participants consuming five or more servings of fruits and vegetables [24]. Similarly, studies from both developing and developed countries report of a lower proportion of workers meeting the recommended fruit and vegetable consumption requirements [31]. The findings of the present study add to the existing knowledge base by a comprehensive assessment of the consumption pattern of all food groups among office workers. Further, it highlights the low consumption of not only fruits, but also other food such as dairy products or nuts and seeds contributing to an overall unhealthy and unbalanced dietary intake. Thus, the study establishes the need for interventions to increase consumption of fruits, dairy products, nuts and seeds.

Factors associated with dietary intake of the office workers

A higher education level has been identified as an important factor associated with healthy dietary intake in several studies [8, 9, 12, 32, 33]. However, a secondary analysis of a large cohort of French women reported an inverse association between a healthy diet and university education compared to an education below a diploma [34]. The current study failed to show any association between education level and a healthy dietary intake. Similarly, socio-economic status was not associated with healthy dietary intake, as identified in many previous studies [32, 34]. We believe that the relative homogeneity of the sample concerning the education level and socio-economic status may have contributed to the observed lack of association between these variables and a healthy dietary intake. The existing evidence on the association of age with healthy dietary intake has been inconclusive. Some studies have shown that older age is associated with a healthy dietary intake [34, 35], whereas others have reported the opposite [32, 33]. The current study also failed to establish a statistically significant association between age and healthy dietary intake.

The current study fails to establish an association between healthy dietary intake and sex of the participants. However, sex has been identified as a factor associated with food choices and healthy dietary intake. A review of nutritional surveys and studies in several Western countries confirmed the link between female sex and healthy dietary intake [36]. On the other hand, the same review pointed out few studies that showed no significant association between sex and dietary intake. Another study among Mexican Americans showed that young males are more prone to unhealthy eating [37]. However, the existing studies on the association between healthy dietary intake and sex are mainly conducted in the context of developed countries and evidence is lacking in the local or regional context, where the social norms and cultural influences may differentially affect the dietary intake in males and females [38, 39]. Pointing towards this complex interrelationship, the sex of the office worker was found to act as an effect modifier in the association between group eating and unhealthy dietary intake observed in the present study.

Anthropometric measurements such as weight, BMI, waist circumference and body composition are proven to be associated with dietary intake, often as an outcome. A whole grain-based diet with vegetables, fruits, seafood and dairy products was associated with better anthropometric measurements [40]. Several studies have highlighted the association of high BMI, overweight and obesity with diets high in carbohydrates, fat and sugar and low in vegetables and fruits [41–43]. However, the current study did not show an association between dietary intake and BMI. While a high BMI is often a result of an unhealthy dietary intake, it is also possible that the high BMI status may in turn affect the dietary intake of an individual, promoting the adoption of healthy eating behaviours. For this reason, BMI was included merely as a factor associated with dietary intake, but not as an independent or dependent variable. Findings like the present study have been reported in studies of office workers in Malaysia [44] and among Mexican Americans [37]. The former study claims that the homogeneity of the study sample and the study’s cross-sectional nature could have led to these findings [44], which could also explain the current study’s findings.

Although the association between unhealthy dietary intake and NCD has been well documented in recent years [3, 45], the current study failed to show such an association. The reported prevalence of NCDs was lower in the study sample than in the labour force data [27]. Therefore, we assume that NCD status and undetected cases in the sample might have been underreported, affecting the detection of any association with unhealthy dietary intake.

Previous studies have highlighted that eating together can increase the amount of food consumed and total energy intake [46, 47]. Furthermore, meal skipping and a busy work schedule were identified as factors associated with unhealthy dietary intake and poor diet quality through studies conducted in other settings [11, 12, 48, 49]. The current study revealed an association of unhealthy diet with group eating and meal skipping during the univariate analysis, however, these variables did not show an association with dietary intake in the multivariate analysis.

Group eating and unhealthy dietary intake were associated differently among males and females. This indicates that sex can act as an effect modifier in the association between group eating and unhealthy dietary intake. Although no previous research evidence points to sex as an effect modifier for the association between group eating and unhealthy dietary intake, several studies highlighted the effect of sex on different associations. A study conducted in Brazil showed an association between high salt intake and hypertension where the association was greater in women [50]. The association between the healthy eating index and hypertension in the United States was shown to be inversely linear in females while in males, it was U-shaped [51]. A significant interaction was noted between diet quality and sex resulting in changes in BMI among Australian adults [52]. Therefore, sex is considered as an effect modifier for different associations with dietary intake. Further, in a network analysis of factors associated with nutrition, sex was identified to have a central role in interaction [53]. In the current study, the interaction term was not significant in the multivariate analysis and the interpretation must be done cautiously as the male representation in the study was low.

When considering the strengths, the study was conducted to cover an entire administrative district. As sample characteristics are comparable with existing research evidence, the sample can be considered representative of Sri Lankan government office workers in a sedentary work environment. Thus, the study findings can be generalized to all workers in a similar work environment. The 24-hour dietary recall was used for dietary assessment, and it was supplemented with a picture guide and computer software was used to calculate serving sizes, minimizing the errors in calculation. Therefore, the dietary assessment was valid and reliable. However, the relative homogeneity of the sample hinders the generalization of results to a broader population. On the other hand, we have excluded private sector offices because of three reasons. First, the sedentariness of the office worker could not be established in private sector offices as workers are involved in different types of work compared to government workers. Second, private offices were clustered in urban areas failing to achieve a representative sample of the entire district. Third, the number of clerical workers in private sector offices was less than in government sector offices. Therefore, the generalization of the research findings, especially for workers in sectors other than the public sector, would be limited. Though the sample consisted of a higher percentage of females and better-educated participants we believe that it will not affect the generalizability because it is the usual composition in office workers in a sedentary working environment. Further, it should be noted that the establishment of the diagnosis of NCD among participants was done by referring to existing clinical records. While this could be viewed as an advantage on one hand in terms of the accuracy of the reporting, it might also have contributed to an under-reporting of NCD which are yet undetected, with the resulting failure to show the expected association between unhealthy diet and NCD. In addition, cross-sectional design also fails to establish the temporality of associations especially of BMI and NCD with the dietary intake.

As the study indicates that office workers failed to meet recommendations in FBDG-SL, the health system and the policymakers must take the necessary initiatives to make the dietary intake of office workers healthy. As it was noted in the study low consumption of certain food groups affected the overall healthiness of the diet. Therefore, policy level and health interventions should focus on increasing availability, accessibility and awareness on these low-consumed food groups. On the other hand, studies that include wider groups of workers including private and informal sector workers and long-term follow-up to identify effects of unhealthy eating will add more insights to the findings of the current study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, dietary intake was not up to the recommendations of FBDG-SL among office workers in a sedentary working environment. Though cereal-based food and vegetable intake met the recommended number of servings, the consumption of fruits, and dairy products was low. Dietary practices such as group eating and meal skipping were associated with unhealthy dietary intake in univariate analysis but were not confirmed by the multivariate analysis.

The current study recommends interventions to increase consumption of fruits, dairy products, nuts and seeds among office workers in a sedentary work environment. We recommend further studies to assess the effect of practices such as group eating and meal skipping on healthy dietary intake in this population.
