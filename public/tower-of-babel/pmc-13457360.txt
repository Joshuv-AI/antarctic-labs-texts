Single-cell immunoprofiling reveals a dysfunctional-like immune microenvironment and malignant phenotype in aging penile squamous cell carcinoma

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPenile squamous cell carcinoma (PSCC) can affect men of any age, but we know little about the differences between non-old and old patients with PSCC, which severely limits the precise diagnosis and treatment of PSCC.MethodsWe collected 2 non-old and 3 old PSCC samples and performed single-cell RNA sequencing and single-cell TCR-Seq analysis. Background

Penile cancer accounts for approximately 0.5% of all malignant tumors in men in developed countries such as the United States, whereas in certain African, Asian and South American regions, it can reach up to 10%. Over 95% of penile cancers are penile squamous cell carcinomas (PSCC) (Clark et al. 2013). Furthermore, studies have shown that PSCC is significantly more prevalent in developing countries worldwide (Montes Cardona and García-Perdomo 2017). Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the known etiologic agent for roughly 30%−50% of PSCC cases worldwide (Alemany et al. 2016; Guimarães 2020; Feber et al. 2015). The standard treatment regimen for penile cancer is limited to surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy (Clark et al. 2013; Agarwal et al. 2014; Pagliaro et al. 2010), with a poor prognosis and a 5-year survival rate of approximately 50%. Advanced PSCC is treated primarily with cisplatin-based chemotherapy, with a median overall survival (OS) of between 5 and 12 months (Pagliaro et al. 2010). Unfortunately, most patients with progression or recurrence respond poorly to salvage therapy and the overall survival time is less than 6 months (Wang et al. 2015). Therefore, it is urgent to investigate the molecular mechanisms that are involved in PSCC progression in order to improve our understanding of tumor development, and thus search for and develop novel therapeutic approaches.

Aging is the most important risk factor for various tumors, and the incidence of tumors increases with age, peaking at 85 years old (White et al. 2014). Studies have indicated that the accumulation of DNA damage in cells during aging may lead to tumorigenesis. This is because DNA damage can induce gene mutations in cells and then result in tumor formation (Vaddavalli and Schumacher 2022). In addition, the function of the immune system declines during aging, which may promote the development of tumors (Lian et al. 2020). Meanwhile, aging was proven to enhance melanoma proliferation and resistance to treatment through the sustained upregulation of the fatty acid transporter protein FATP2 in aged fibroblasts and increased uptake of fatty acids in the tumor and its surrounding microenvironment (Fane et al. 2020). There is also a correlation between aging and PSCC, and the incidence of PSCC rises with age, peaking around the age of 60, although it can occur in younger men as well (Hakenberg et al. 2015). However, the role of aging in PSCC development remains unclear so far.

In recent years, the rapid development of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technique has enabled researchers to use next-generation sequencing to perform transcriptome analysis of complex samples at the single-cell level (Papalexi and Satija 2018), which provides a powerful tool for in-depth exploration of the complex mechanisms of aging and aging-associated tumors. In this study, we performed scRNA-seq on 5 PSCC tissue samples. By comparing 2 cases of non-old PSCC tissues with 3 cases of old PSCC tissues, we conducted an analysis to confirm the variances in cellular profiles between the non-old and older groups within PSCC and found a more distinct aging-associated secretory phenotype of tumor cells in older PSCC patients. And we also found similarities between old HNSC samples and old PSCC samples. We identified novel subclusters of aging-associated tumor cells and T cells, which provide fresh perspectives for the precision treatment of PSCC and the prognosis of patients.

Methods

Tissue samples collection

The 5 PSCC tissue samples (2 non-old: age ≤ 50; 3 old: age ≥ 65, the complete clinical information for the five patients shown in Supplementary Table 1) were collected from the Department of Urology, Hunan Cancer Hospital, and all patients signed an informed consent form, and the project was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Cancer Hospital (KY2024504).

Preparation of single-cell suspensions

The primary tumor tissue is dissociated into a single-cell suspension by a combination of mechanical and enzymatic dissociation. The tissue block was cut into fine pieces, and an appropriate amount of preheated enzymatic solution was added. Subsequently, incubate and digest for a few minutes at a 37 °C thermostat with gentle shaking. The digestion was then terminated by adding complete medium at a ratio of 1:5. Filtration of digested samples through a 70-μm cell sieve was followed by centrifugation at 500 × g for 8 min at room temperature, and the cells were resuspended with the addition of 1 mL of PBS after complete removal of the supernatant. Finally, the separation of single-cell suspensions, the percentage of erythrocytes, and the number and viability of cells were all evaluated under a microscope.

Single cell capture and library sequencing

We utilized the SeekOne® DD Single Cell 5' Transcriptome Platform for single cell capture and library construction. Firstly, we combined gel beads containing Barcoded Beads information with a mixture of cells and enzymes to achieve single cell isolation capture by water-in-oil structure. Then the Barcoded Beads were crushed and broke up oil droplets for PCR amplification using cDNA as a template. A standard high-throughput single-cell sequencing library was finally constructed. The cDNA was enzymatically cleaved into fragments of about 200 ~ 300 bp, added with the sequencing connector P5 and sequencing primers, and PCR amplification was conducted to obtain the DNA library. Finally, the constructed library was sequenced using the double-end sequencing mode of Illumina sequencing platform.

Sequencing data quality control and gene expression quantification

We used SeekSoul Tools, the official SeekOne analysis software, to perform data quality statistics on the raw data, compare the reference genome, gene expression quantification and effective cell filtering (Griffiths et al. 2018).

Cell clustering analysis

Subsequently, the R package Seurat (Butler et al. 2018) was used for further quality control, analysis and data exploration of single-cell transcriptome data. We filtered out cells with nFeature_RNA greater than 5000, less than 400 or percent mitochondrial genes more than 10% to retain high quality cells. Doublet detection was performed by using DoubletFinder package. After that, we further homogenized the expression then used Harmony (Korsunsky et al. 2019) for data merging as well as batch effect correction. The nonlinear dimensionality reduction method, U-MAP was used to map the high-dimensional cellular data into a two-dimensional space, clustering the cells with similar expression patterns together and separating cells with different expression patterns farther away, to visualize the intercellular variability more intuitively. Eventually, we manually perform cellular annotation by the marker genes of known cells.

Analysis of subclusters of marker genes

In order to resolve the transcriptional regulation pattern of each cell subcluster and to further screen for specifically expressed gene markers from each subcluster, we used the rank-sum test of Seurat to analyze differentially expressed genes in different cell subclusters (Camp et al. 2017). The screening conditions were (Clark et al. 2013) gene expression fold logFC.threshold ≥ 0.5, p ≤ 0.01, and the gene was expressed in more than 25% of cells in the target subcluster.

Cell cycle analysis

We used the R package Seurat to calculate a cell cycle score for each cell based on the expression of marker genes (Macosko et al. 2015) in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle.

Enrichment analysis

Differential gene enrichment analysis was performed via the Metascape online tool (http://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1) (Zhou et al. 2019). Gene set enrichment scores were calculated using the GSVA package based on the expression of the corresponding gene set in each cell for each gene set (Liberzon et al. 2015).

Analysis of cell–cell communication

We use the CellChart package in R to analyze intercellular communication between two groups. The parameters of the CellChart package are as follows: min.cells = 5, probability threshold < 0.01.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned and sequentially baked, dewaxed and hydrated. Then antigen retrieval was performed with citrate retrieval solution, followed by blocking of endogenous peroxidase with 3% H2O2 and then blocked with blocking solution for 1 h. Subsequently, the corresponding primary antibody (DCN: Cat# P00319, ProMab Biotechnologies, Inc.; KITLG, Cat# 32239, ProMab Biotechnologies, Inc.; GPX4: Cat# A1933, ABclonal, Inc.; EPCAM: Cat# 32011P, ProMab Biotechnologies, Inc.) was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Next, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was added dropwise and incubated for 1 h at room temperature before staining with diaminobenzidine (DAB) color development kit (CWBIO, CW2069S). After that, each section of tissue was incubated with a drop of hematoxylin for a few minutes and then rinsed with running water for 1–2 min. Finally, pictures were taken with a light microscope after dehydration and mounting.

Online database analysis

Immunohistochemical data of DCN, MS4A6A, TIMP1, KITLG, EPCAM, NECAB3, and MIF in non-old HNSC and old HNSC samples were downloaded from The Human Protein Atlas database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). Correlation analysis of DCN, IL1B, KITLG, MMP13, TIMP1, EPCAM, NECAB3, and MIF with the prognosis of HNSC patients was performed using the BEST online tool (https://rookieutopia.com/app_direct/BEST/) based on the TCGA HNSC cohort data.

Immunofluorescence

The baking, dewaxing, hydration, antigen retrieval and antibody (CD24: Cat# 10600–1-AP, Proteintech Group, Inc.; MIF: Cat# 20415–1-AP, Proteintech Group, Inc.) incubation of the tissue sections were consistent with those of immunohistochemistry. After the secondary antibody incubation was completed, DAPI staining was added dropwise to each piece of tissue, which was then observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope.

β-galactosidase staining

OCT-embedded fresh tissue samples were sectioned and washed three times with PBS. Then add appropriate amount of β-galactosidase staining fixative and fix for 15 min at room temperature. After washing three times with PBS, moderate amount of staining working solution was added and incubated at 37 °C overnight. On the next day, the slices were blocked with mounting medium and then observed and photographed under a light microscope.

Macrophage generation and treatment

Normal human peripheral blood 10 mL was collected in the amount of 10 mL, and PBMCs were isolated in vitro. The serum-free RPMI1640 medium was cultured in vitro for 2 h to obtain adherent monocytes and replaced with complete medium of RPMI1640 (M-CSF, 20 ng/mL) for in vitro induction to M0 macrophages (6 days). On the seventh day it was replaced with new RPMI1640 medium and MIF (Cat# 300–69-5, PeproTech)/Vehicle was stimulated in vitro for 48 h. After 48 h, macrophages of each group were collected.

Flow cytometry

Cell surface molecule staining was analyzed as previously reported using the flow cytometer (Cytec, USA) (Hu et al. 2022). APC-anti-human CD68 (Cat# 333810), and FITC- anti-human HLA-DR (Cat# 307604) were purchased from Biolegend (USA). Data analyses were conducted with FlowJo 10.0 software.

Masson staining

The paraffin sections were dewaxed and stained with Weigert’s hematoxylin stain kit for 5–10 min. Then it was differentiated with an acidic ethanol differentiation solution for 5–15 s, returned to blue with Masson’s bluing buffer for 3–5 min, and then washed with distilled water for 1 min. Ponceau-acid fuchsin solution was used for 5–10 min, followed by the weak acid working solution for 1 min. Next, it was dehydrated with 95% ethanol and anhydrous ethanol. Finally, the sections were dried with xylene three times and mounted with neutral balsam.

Results

Single-cell transcriptome landscape of old PSCC

We collected 2 non-old and 3 old PSCC tissue samples, which were digested into single-cell suspensions. The scRNA-seq was used to construct single-cell profiles of PSCC for visualization and analysis, and the results were finally validated experimentally by immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence (Fig. 1A). After obtaining the scRNA-seq data of these samples, we first performed dimensionality reduction analysis, and removed the batch effects using the Harmony package. The data indicated that the five different samples could be well integrated together (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, we divided the five samples into two groups of non-old and old for uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) presentation, and it was revealed that each cell cluster was present in both groups (Fig. 1C). After further clustering and classifying these cells, we identified 28 high-confidence cell clusters (Fig. 1D). Based on the expression of typical gene markers of cell clusters (Fig. 1E), we conducted cell type annotations and confirmed 8 major cell types (Fig. 1F): B cell, endothelial cell (EC), fibroblast, mast cell, myeloid cell, myoid cell, T cell, and tumor cell. The expression of marker genes for 8 annotated cell types in 28 identified clusters can be seen in Supplementary Fig. 1A-H. Relative to the non-old group, the proportion of T cells decreased, and the proportion of fibroblasts and myoid cells increased in the old group (Fig. 1G). Immunohistochemical staining of DCN, the marker gene for fibroblast cells, further verified that the proportions of these two cell types were significantly altered in the old group (Fig. 1H). Masson staining also indicated that fibroblasts were clearly elevated in the older group (Fig. 1I). These results suggested that there was an obvious difference in the ratio of fibroblasts between the non-old and old groups in PSCC. In addition, fibroblast markers were also highly expressed in the old group of head and neck squamous carcinoma (HNSC) and suggested a poor prognosis (Supplementary Fig. 2A-C).

SASP of tumor cell are notable

By GSVA enrichment analysis, we found that senescence was more significantly upregulated in tumor cells (Fig. 2A). The results in G2/M phase cell cycle scoring reflected that senescent tumor cells had evidently lower G2M phase scores (Fig. 2B). We also compared SASP marker expression levels on tumor cells individually, which showed that the marker expression of SASP was indeed generally higher than the non-old group (Fig. 2C). Next, we performed β-galactosidase staining for the two groups separately, and positive signals could be seen in more cells in the old group with darker colors, which implicated that the cells in the old group were in a more senescent status (Fig. 2D). Moreover, we stained IL1B and KITLG expressed by tumor cells in both groups, and the results were consistent with the data from scRNA-seq, and their positivity rates were both higher in the old group (Fig. 2E).

The SASP markers KITLG, MMP13, and MMP14 were also highly expressed in the old group of HNSC tumor cells from GSE103322 (Supplementary Fig. 3A), while KITLG and TIMP1 also had stronger positive signals in the old HNSC tissue samples (Supplementary Fig. 3B-C). Finally, by analyzing the publicly released gene microarray data for PSCC, we confirmed that these two genes have a favorable marker value for predicting the prognosis of patients (Fig. 2F). Additionally, analysis of the HNSC data using the BEST website also showed that high expression of IL1B and KITLG was associated with poor prognosis (Fig. 2G), as well as upregulated MMP13 and TIMP1 (Supplementary Fig. 3D).

Senescence-associated tumor cell subcluster exhibit enhanced malignant phenotypes and antioxidant capacity

The results mentioned previously suggested that the SASP of tumor cells was more evident in old group, so we assumed the existence of a class of senescence-related cell subclusters in tumor cells. Thus, we extracted the tumor cells and performed further dimensionality reduction clustering and classification on them. Among the four subclusters of 0–3 that were separated out, the proportion of the C3 subcluster was notably reduced in the old group, whereas the C2 subcluster was almost absent in the non-old group and only appeared in the old group, which indicated that the C2 subcluster might be a subcluster of senescence-associated tumor cells (Fig. 3A-B). For these two specific subclusters, we used enrichment analysis to explore their potential functions, and the results demonstrated that the C2 subcluster was mainly involved in oxidative stress response, cytoskeleton assembly, cell proliferation, cell migration and adhesion, and wound healing, while the C3 subcluster was associated with immune functions such as immune regulation, immune response, leukocyte activation, and cytotoxicity of natural killer cells, which suggests that it may have stronger immunogenicity to induce anti-tumor immune responses of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 3C-D). The C2 subcluster scored higher in tumor phenotypes such as tumor migration, invasion, and proliferation after we analyzed the four tumor cell subclusters for tumor-associated malignant phenotypes, representing that C2 is most probably a cell subcluster that facilitates tumor progression (Fig. 3E). Since the C3 subcluster was enriched in a variety of immune-related signals, we analyzed the expression of HLA in the 4 subclusters and found that the C3 subcluster had a significantly higher level of HLA expression, which also indicated that the C3 subcluster may be more immunogenic and was able to activate anti-tumor immune responses (Fig. 3F). In addition, we also conducted a pseudotime analysis of these tumor cells, which showed that with senescence, the tumor cell subclusters were mainly transformed from other subclusters to the senescence-associated C2 subcluster of the pro-cancer type (Fig. 3G). Finally, we also verified the expression of the antioxidant-associated gene GPX4 and the gene EPCAM, which negatively regulates cell adhesion, in both groups by immunohistochemistry, and both GPX4 and EPCAM were more highly expressed in the old group compared with the non-old group (Fig. 3H); co-immunofluorescence staining for p21 and GPX4 (a gene highly expressed in the C2 subcluster) demonstrated the correlation between senescence signals and the abundance of the C2 subcluster (Fig. 3I).

We also performed a dimensionality reduction analysis of HNSC tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. 4A), demonstrating the distribution of tumor cells in the non-old and old groups, with a notable increase in the proportions of the C2 subclusters and a decrease in the C3 subcluster in old group (Supplementary Fig. 4B). We found that the expression levels of cell adhesion markers GPC3 and NECAB3, and antioxidant marker SLC39A14 were significantly elevated in the C2 subcluster (Supplementary Fig. 4C), whereas antigen presenting-related genes HLA-B, HLA-C, HLA-F, and HLA-DPA1 were obviously downregulated in the C2 subcluster, suggesting that there may be immune escape in this subcluster (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Pseudotime analysis revealed that tumor cells were mainly shifted from other subclusters to C2 subcluster (Supplementary Fig. 4E). These results suggest that the C2 subcluster in HNSC is similar to the C2 subcluster in PSCC, and they may be senescence-associated tumor cell subclusters. Moreover, immunohistochemistry displayed a significant enhancement in the positive signals of cell adhesion markers EPCAM and NECAB3 in the old group of HNSC (Supplementary Fig. 4F), with their heightened expression also correlating with a poorer tumor prognosis (Supplementary Fig. 4G).

CD24+ T cells were enriched in the old PSCC

T cells are the most abundant immune cells in PSCC and the most vital cell type that plays a tumor immune-related role. Therefore, we reclustered the T cells into a total of 10 subclusters (Fig. 4A). NKG7, CD8A, CD4, and MKI67 are common markers for T-cell clustering, and it was shown in a UMAP plot that 2 cell clusters (cluster 8 and cluster 9) barely expressed these markers, which displayed that these 2 clusters might be novel T cell subclusters (Fig. 4B). Analysis of the marker genes of these 2 clusters revealed that CD24 was highly expressed in cluster 9 and TNF was highly expressed in cluster 8. We further compared the expressions of these two markers in the 10 subclusters and investigated the distribution of different types of T cells in the two groups, and we found that CD24 + T cells were almost exclusively distributed in the old group, but TNF + T cells were apparently reduced in the old group (Fig. 4C-D). In view of this alteration, we implemented a pseudotime analysis, which suggested that CD24 + T cells were exactly produced as patients aged (Fig. 4E). GZMB, GZMH, and GZMK are well-known factors for T cells to exert anti-tumor effects, and we found that CD24 + T cells hardly expressed these genes by comparing their expressions in different T cell subclusters, which suggests that the anti-tumor effect of CD24 + T cells is low (Fig. 4F). By analyzing the expression of immune checkpoint genes, we found that the expression of exhaustion-related genes (PDCD1, LAG3, HAVCR2, TOX) in T cells from the two groups. Notably, HAVCR2 and TOX were significantly differentially expressed between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 5A-B), these genes were also lowly expressed in CD24 + T cells (Supplementary Fig. 6A-D). Furthermore, we found that the main functions of these CD24 + T cells were enriched in signaling pathways related to cell senescence, apoptosis, and fibrosis (Fig. 4G). Based on the above results, we believe that the cell subcluster is a dysfunctional-like T cell. We also detected the expression of CD24 by fluorescence assay, and the CD24 had more positive signals in the old group (Fig. 4H-I). In addition, we also performed the single-cell T-cell receptor library sequencing (scTCR-seq) for the two groups of samples, but there was no significant difference in Unique Clones, Clonal Group, Clonal Indices, and Amino Acid Residues (Fig. 4K-L). Immunohistochemical staining for HAVCR2 on samples from both groups and found that HAVCR2, a marker of dysfunctional-like T cells, was relatively higher in the Old group (Fig. 4J), implying that the T cell differences between the old group and non-old group were still mainly in the T cell subclusters rather than cell surface receptors.

Pro-carcinogenic role of tumor-associated macrophages in senescent PSCC

Myeloid cells in the immune microenvironment have a critical function in tumor development, so we also performed a clustering analysis and identified three important types of myeloid cells by cellular annotation: dendritic cells (DCs), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). The percentages of these three types of cells in the two groups of samples were not significantly different (Fig. 5A-B). Studies have shown that macrophages typically have two main activation types: M1 and M2 (Shapouri-Moghaddam et al. 2018; Locati et al. 2020). We utilized CD86 and CD80 to label M1 macrophages and CD163 and MRC1 to label M2 macrophages, but the expression of these markers did not specifically divide into two groups as expected (Fig. 5C). We analyzed the expression of these polarization markers (CXCL9/10, IL1B, FCGR3A, APOE, SPP1) in macrophages. However, we observed that these genes were also expressed in the subset of macrophages located in the upper-left region of the UMAP plot, and they still did not allow discrimination of macrophage subtypes (see Supplementary Fig. 7A-F), suggesting that TAM in PSCC could not be typed according to the conventional M1, M2. We determined the differences in TAM between the two groups as a whole and found that the old group had relatively higher scores in angiogenesis (Fig. 5D). For the aspect that macrophages can secrete matrix metalloproteinases (MMP), we focused on the four proteases: MMP2, MMP9, MMP14, and MMP13. Compliant with the above findings, they were upregulated obviously in the old group, and these data showed that macrophages in senescent PSCC samples may have a stronger pro-carcinogenic effect (Fig. 5E). Finally, we performed differential and enrichment analyses of DC and pDC in the two groups, and there were fewer differential genes in DC and pDC and fewer differential signaling pathways enriched between the two groups (Fig. 5F-I).

Disturbed cellular communication in senescent PSCC samples

Abnormal cell communication is one of the common characteristics of cellular senescence. We compared the communication strength of the non-old and old groups and discovered that the old group had stronger cell communications (Fig. 6A). Then we analyzed the differences in the input and output signals of different cell types in the two groups and it was shown that the output signals of T cells were reduced in the senescent group, while the input signals of tumor cells were significantly enhanced. Meanwhile, we noticed that the input and output signal strengths of fibroblasts in the old group were both apparently elevated (Fig. 6B). Next, we investigated the specific signal flow changes of samples in both groups and observed that TNF, IFN, PTN signal flow was stronger in the non-old group, while MIF and FN1 were stronger in the old group (Fig. 6C). After further analyzing these input–output signals between cell types, we found that the MIF signal of fibroblasts was substantially increased in the old group, which was the same trend in HNSC and suggested a poor prognosis in HNSC (Supplementary Fig. 8A-B). Therefore, we mapped the interaction network for this signal, which was exhibited that fibroblasts sent significantly enhanced MIF signals to T cells, myeloid cells, tumor cells, and B cells (Fig. 6D-E). Immunofluorescence also confirmed a higher positive rate of MIF signaling in the old group (Fig. 6F). We detected the expression of macrophage antigen presentation related gene HLA-DR after MIF treatment by flow cytometry and found that its expression was significantly increased. (Fig. 6G). This suggests that the continuous activation of MIF signaling may lead to an increase in antigen presentation and ultimately induce T cell exhaustion.

Discussion

Aging refers to the gradual decline in physiological functions, leading to an increasing risk of disease. Multiple studies have shown that there are large differences in tumors developed in younger and older people (Shah et al. 2021; Bleyer et al. 2008; Siegel et al. 2021, 2020). Abundant senescence-associated pathways in young breast cancer patients, which involve cytokines, growth factors, and inflammation, ultimately cause tumor progression, malignant transformation, and proliferation (Krtolica et al. 2001; Gosselin et al. 2009; Di et al. 2014; Mavrogonatou et al. 2020). It is unclear whether tumors in the younger and the older have distinct biological characteristics in PSCC. Our study identified features such as reduced T cells and enhanced myeloid cells in the tumor tissues of older PSCC. It implies that non-old and older PSCC patients may require different treatment regimens, and their responses to immunotherapy may also vary.

The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) is one of the most prominent features of aging. The important pathophysiological processes, such as cancer, were closely related to cellular senescence, which was initially considered as inhibiting cancer development by limiting the proliferative capacity of damaged cells (Collado and Serrano 2010). Subsequent research has revealed that the accumulation of senescent cells in the tumor microenvironment can drive tumorigenesis in a paracrine manner via SASP (Faget et al. 2019; Alessio et al. 2019; Carpenter et al. 2021). Additionally, SASP can induce systematic pro-inflammatory changes and mediate inflammatory processes in the microenvironment (Cuollo et al. 2020). According to our findings, SASP was most pronounced in tumor cells in PSCC. In prostate cancer, fibroblasts were senescent in old patients, which were metabolically active, releasing high levels of epithelial growth factor and matrix metalloproteinases to alter the surrounding microenvironment and promote growth and tumor progression in human prostate epithelial cells (Coppé et al. 2006; Chang et al. 2000; Currid et al. 2006; Khwaja et al. 2006). It has been shown that macrophages and endothelial cells were the primary senescent cell types in KRAS-driven lung tumors in mice. These macrophages display cellular senescence and SASP features in normal senescent lungs, and the removal of senescent cells or macrophages significantly reduces the tumor burden and improves survival (Haston et al. 2023). In glioblastoma patients, senescent cell types are malignant (GFP +) tumor cells, neuroglial cells (GFAP +, OLIG2 +) or microglia/macrophages (IBA1 +), which can alter the tumor microenvironment and lower the survival rate (Salam et al. 2023). In contrast, our study found that senescent tumor cells in PSCC may play a pro-carcinogenic role and their expression of SASP-associated factors has significant prognostic marker value. Moreover, we identified a subcluster of senescence-associated PSCC tumor cells. This subcluster has enhanced invasive and migratory potential and exhibits stronger antioxidant activity. This suggests that treatment targeting this subcluster of tumor cells may be a potential therapeutic modality for senescent PSCC patients.

Aging affects the tumor immune microenvironment through multiple mechanisms (Chen et al. 2023; Prasanna et al. 2021; Chibaya et al. 2022). It has been reported that senescence can reprogram the immune cell composition of HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinoma, thereby impacting its prognosis (Yu et al. 2022). We not only found alterations in immune cell abundance in senescent PSCC patients, but also alterations in T cell subclusters were a major feature of senescent PSCC patients, and the CD24 + T cell subcluster was a senescence-associated T cell subcluster. CD24 + T cells are a specific type of T cells that play a vital role in regulating immune responses, which can modulate other immune cell activities and contribute to maintaining the balance and stability of the immune system (Zhang et al. 2023; Li et al. 2006). Furthermore, CD24 + T cells are regarded as functioning necessarily in autoimmune diseases and tumor immunosurveillance (Carl et al. 2008). CD24 expressed in tumor cells can interact with Siglec-10, a molecule on macrophages, to transmit inhibitory signals, suppress destructive inflammatory responses, and reduce phagocytosis of tumor cells by macrophages, thereby promoting tumor survival (Barkal et al. 2019). Meanwhile, it was demonstrated that CD24 + T cells were significantly enriched in cellular senescence and apoptosis-related signals, implying that in addition to its function of inhibiting macrophages, CD24 may also be related to the functional status of T cells.

Altered cellular communication is another essential feature of cellular senescence (López-Otín et al. 2023; Fafián-Labora and O'Loghlen 2020). Altered intercellular communication includes enhanced inflammatory responses, decreased immune surveillance, and altered human genome and flora communication, ultimately resulting in ecological dysregulation (López-Otín et al. 2023). We observed that the intensity of cellular communication was significantly enhanced in the tissues of senescent PSCC patients, most notably by increased MIF signaling from fibroblasts to other cells, a cytokine that plays a key role in promoting tumor growth, modulating immune and inflammatory responses, and supporting tumor-associated angiogenesis (Sumaiya et al. 2022; Yasasever et al. 2009). Studies have shown that MIF can regulate the polarity of tumor-associated macrophages (TAM), which usually differentiate to form tumor-promoting M2-type TAM in tumors, accelerating tumor cell proliferation, metastasis, and suppressing anti-tumor immune responses (Figueiredo et al. 2018). Besides, MIF is an immunomodulator in the tumor microenvironment, inducing the formation of a tumor-suppressive immune microenvironment. In advanced melanoma, MIF promotes tumor progression and metastasis by inducing the differentiation of suppressive immune cells (Yaddanapudi et al. 2016). Drews-Elger et al. (2014) found that tumor cells secreted MIF factors into the microenvironment, which in turn recruited a large number of S100A8-positive myeloid cells to form a suppressive immune microenvironment that facilitates the immune escape of tumor cells. This evidence indicates that MIF promotes tumor development by affecting the function of immune cells and reducing the body’s immune defense. Given the critical promotional role of MIF in tumorigenesis and progression, targeting MIF may be another therapeutic strategy for senescence-associated PSCC.

Remarkably, we found that senescent samples in HNSC also exhibited some similar features to senescent PSCC samples, which generally showed increased MIF signaling and greater fibroblast enrichment. Furthermore, senescence-related secretory phenotype was more prominent in tumor cells of senescent HNSC samples, and a higher expression of senescence-associated secretory phenotypic genes tends to predict a poorer prognosis for HNSC patients. This evidence imply that senescent PSCC may share similar mechanisms with senescent HNSC, and drugs targeting HNSC may also be potential therapeutic agents for PSCC patients.

Certainly, our study also has some limitations. First, since PSCC is a relatively rare tumor, the number of samples we can obtain is small, and this limits our validation of the prognostic marker value of IL1B in an independent PSCC cohort and the clinical application value of guiding clinical classification management of patients. And this also limits the statistical power and generalizability of our conclusions regarding aging‑related biology. Second, although the two patient groups were comparable in HPV status and treatment history, we cannot rule out the possibility that unmeasured factors such as differences in immune baseline or comorbidities, independent of age, may have influenced immune infiltration and the observed phenotypes. Third, while we validated the MIF‑macrophage axis using exogenous MIF stimulation, we did not perform blocking experiments (e.g., anti‑MIF or CXCR4/CD74 blockade), and therefore causal evidence remains incomplete. These limitations are carefully considered in the interpretation of our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study constructed a single-cell immune profile of senescent PSCC for the first time and systematically addressed the effects of aging on PSCC and identified IL1B as a candidate marker suggested by our PSCC scRNA-seq and supported by HNSCC datasets, providing the theoretical basis for the classification management and treatment of PSCC patients.
