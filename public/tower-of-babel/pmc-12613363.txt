Measuring depression in Wolof-speaking communities using the CESD-10 in rural Senegal
Authors: Yatma Diop, Lori E. Skibbe, Anne Fernald, Amy K. Nuttall, Ann M. Weber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression is a global issue affecting millions of people worldwide, yet access to validated measures of depressive Symptoms varies greatly, particularly in Sub-Saharan African countries. The study took place between 2013 and 2015 and assesses the psychometric properties of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale, 10-item version (CESD-10 scale) in Wolof, the most widely spoken language in Senegal.MethodsParticipants were 514 Wolof-speaking caregivers in rural Senegal. A professional bilingual translator translated the CESD-10 from French to Wolof; the scale was then back translated by a research assistant and examined by a research team of native Wolof speakers to ensure that the concepts in the scale were linguistically and culturally relevant. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Wolof CESD-10 and responses were given on a 4-point Likert-style.ResultsAn initial confirmatory factor analysis of the scale using half of the sample indicated that factor loadings for the two positively worded items, “enjoy things” and “happy,” were low and did not fit the model well. After dropping the two positively worded items, a second CFA was conducted using the other half of the sample. The model fit was acceptable and indicated that items were associated with one factor: χ2 (18) = 29.38, p < .05, CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.05, and SRMR = 0.03. Item loadings ranged from 0.37 to 0.72 and Cronbach’s alpha was reasonable (α = 0.78). Caregivers with more depressive symptoms resided in households with less wealth and had children with lower weight and height, providing evidence of validity for the translated measure.ConclusionThe Wolof CESD-10 is valid for the rural Senegalese population of caregivers, many of whom do not have access to adequate mental health care. Access to the Wolof CESD-10 could help physicians and other care providers in Senegal to assess depressive symptoms more effectively, allowing for better identification and treatment for those experiencing high levels of depression.

Introduction

Depression is a major mental health problem that affects over 280 million people worldwide [1]. Although the severity of depressive symptoms varies, people with depression experience feelings that can interfere with daily activities and interrupt sleep [2]. As one population particularly affected by depression, it is estimated that approximately 20% of mothers in developing countries experience depressive symptoms after childbirth, although limited access to mental health services in some low-income countries may lead to underestimates in this area [3, 4]. Furthermore, maternal depression is associated with more child health problems, including a higher risk of hospitalization and mortality for children during their first year of life [5, 6]. To fully understand and treat depression in African countries, professionals require access to validated measures of depression, which are often not available. Indeed, the lack of appropriate measures for depression in Sub-Saharan Africa makes it challenging to monitor and address the problem through policy and program planning, which is a core component of the WHO Comprehensive Mental Health Action plan [7].

Mental health constructs, such as depression, need to be measured in ways that represent people’s language and culture to ensure that content remains meaningful for the communities where these measures are used [8]. Words like “depression” and “anxiety” do not have direct equivalents in many African contexts [9, 10]. As a result, Western measures of depression cannot simply be translated directly into the local languages most commonly used in Africa - they must be translated with care towards how depression is conceptualized within those contexts. Considerations for developing or adapting a mental health instrument in Africa should include working closely with local people to achieve linguistic and conceptual equivalences of the tool [11]. Linguistic equivalence is important because, in addition to the nonexistence of some mental health terms in some African languages [12], participants may not understand questions if they do not follow the conventions of the local languages used [13]. For example, when validating a depression screening tool in rural Mali, Lasater and colleagues [14] commented that some questions did not make sense locally.

As one of the most well studied self-report measures of depressive symptoms in Africa, the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CESD) Scale has long and short versions that assess Symptoms, such as feeling alone or experiencing restless sleep. The longer version is a 20-item measure designed to assess the epidemiology of depressive symptomology in the general population [15]. The longer scale was reduced to a ten-item instrument, called the CESD-10, which has similar predictive accuracy to the long-form scale, so has become more popular [16, 17]. The CESD-10 has been validated in indigenous languages spoken in some African countries, including South Africa (i.e., Zulu, Xhosa, and Afrikaans; [18]), Rwanda (i.e., Kinyarwanda [19]),, Kenya, Malawi, Tanzania, Zambia, and Zimbabwe [20]. Nevertheless, the continent is diverse, with over 2000 languages represented [21]. Thus, many people currently living in Africa do not have access to a measure of depression in their native language.

Validation work with the CESD-10 has sometimes indicated that the measure is best represented by one-factor, including a study conducted in an Afrikaans-speaking community in South Africa [18]. Other studies have uncovered a two-factor solution representing depressed affect and positive affect [22, 23]. In the two-factor cases, eight items represent the depressed affect factor, including reports of restless sleep and loneliness. The positive affect factor includes only two items - ‘feeling happy’ and ‘feeling hopeful’. This two-factor structure has been found in some African settings, including Zimbabwe [20] and South Africa [18]. Notably, in instances of the CESD-10 scale yielding more than one-factor, the factors still reflect a higher-order construct of depression [24].

To our knowledge, there is no validated measure of depression available in Senegal, where Wolof is the most widely spoken language [25]. The Wolof language has a general concept for mental health, “Feebaru Xel” or “Illness of the spirit,” but it does not have a specific name for depression [26]. Thus, extra attention is needed to ensure that the concept of depression is accurately portrayed in this language. Prior work in the area of studying depression in Senegal is limited to persons with chronic health conditions and has relied on Western measures such as the Beck Depression Inventory [27] and the DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria [28] without information about their validity for the Senegalese context. Given that these measures were used with specialized populations without considering their psychometric properties, they do not provide accurate information about the mental health status of the general population in Senegal.

Although depression is relevant for the Senegalese population at large, there are a number of contextual and cultural factors that place mothers and other caregivers at particular risk for having depression. Most likely due to gendered social norms, many women in Senegal feel that they cannot make their own healthcare decisions. An analysis of a subsample of over 10,000 female participants from the Senegal 2023 Demographic and Health Survey reported that only 10.73% felt they had autonomy over their healthcare decisions [29]. More importantly, women who reported more health autonomy were less likely to feel depressed. Relatedly, the societal expectation of married women in the household leads them to assume primary caregiving responsibilities, in addition to other expectations that in-laws place on them. This creates family dynamics that put an emotional burden on women, leading to marital dissatisfaction and conflict with in-laws, which both have been found to be risk factors associated with postpartum depression among women in Senegal [30]. In addition to these sociocultural factors, women in Senegal also face economic challenges, including a low labor force, which may influence their mental health [31].

Like most Sub-Saharan African countries, mental health services in Senegal mainly consist of “mental hospitals,” which are often located in urban areas [32]. Although staff in these hospitals include psychiatrists, few people have access to mental health care with licensed professionals; in Senegal, the average ratio is 0.3 psychiatrists per 100,000 inhabitants, a number well below recommendations from the World Health Organization [33]. People living in rural areas predominantly rely on services provided within a community-based health care setting, where staff are rarely, if ever, trained to provide mental health care services. Approximately 40% of people living in Sub-Saharan African countries, including Senegal, access mental health care in this way [34]. The lack of valid screening tools in local languages hinder health care workers’ ability to understand who might benefit from mental health care services in Senegal. There is consensus among global experts that developing and validating brief mental health screening tools is a priority research area [35].

There are considerable challenges in obtaining good estimates of the prevalence of depression in the general population or among caregivers in Sub-Saharan Africa due to a lack of standardized global measures and routine testing in the majority of the world. However, there have been efforts in Senegal to understand the prevalence of postpartum depression (PPD) among mothers, statistics that we can tentatively compare to the prevalence in other Sub-Saharan Africa regions and globally. More specifically, a study conducted with mothers during postnatal care in Dakar, the capital city of Senegal, showed a PPD prevalence of 25.41% [30], although others have reported a prevalence rate as high as 53% [36]. While these discrepancies in prevalence may be due to methodical aspects, including non-representative sampling and differences in scales used, the findings indicate that depression is prevalent among caregivers of young children in Senegal. In addition, a global meta-analysis of postpartum depression, including 565 studies in 80 countries, reported a PPD depression prevalence of approximately 18% across Sub-Saharan Africa, with substantial variation by region: 40% in Southern Africa, 20.2% in West Africa and 13.6% in Eastern Africa [37]. The same study found depression in 17.22% of the world’s population, which is slightly lower than the prevalence found in Sub-Saharan Africa. These estimates reflect the burden of depressive symptoms among mothers globally, and specifically in the Sub-Saharan African regions, therefore pointing to the importance of increasing screening in countries like Senegal.

The present study sought to validate a Wolof translation and adaptation of the CESD-10 for use among caregivers of young children living in rural Senegalese communities. One of the most vulnerable populations globally are mothers of young children who, at an aggregated level, consistently show higher levels of depression relative to the general population [38]. Based on research conducted in Western societies, it is estimated that one in ten mothers experience depression during their children’s first year of life [39]. Not only is maternal depression important at the individual level, but it has lasting implications for the ways in which children develop. Mothers who reported many depressive Symptoms when their children were between 15 and 24 months of age had children with lower executive function at 24 months of age [40]. Higher levels of maternal depressive symptoms have been also linked to smaller academic gains [41], lower social skills [42] and lower language and cognitive skills for children [43].

Using data from a study of parenting practices in rural Senegal, we examined the validity of the Wolof version of the CESD-10 by testing the factor structure of the scale and assessed its internal reliability. We also examined the scale’s divergent validity with respect to household wealth and child nutritional status: factors shown to be associated with maternal depressive symptoms in low-income settings [44–46].

Method

Participants

Wolof-speaking caregivers of young children aged between 4 and 31 months (M = 18.05 months, SD = 7.42, n = 514) were recruited from 24 communities in Senegal. Caregivers ranged in age from 12 to 64 years (M = 27.30, SD = 8.08); most caregivers (93.9%) were the children’s biological mothers. The remaining 6.1% were either grandmothers, other female family members, or neighbors. Most caregivers (78.3%) had education levels that did not go beyond elementary school; only 11.5% had attended school at the secondary level. Many of the caregivers (41.3%) could not read or write in any language. All caregivers were enrolled in Tostan’s parenting program, which aimed to encourage positive parent-infant interactions [47].

The recruitment of participants started from December 27, 2012, to January 14th, 2013. The study was concluded in 2015. Caregivers were asked to consent to participate in the research and to provide consent for their children’s participation in the study. Research assistants read the consent form to caregivers because they were mostly illiterate. We carefully explained the nature of the research and received oral consent from caregivers for participating in the study and their children’s participation. We told them that participating in the research was voluntary, that they had the choice to skip questions that made them uncomfortable, and that they could withdraw from the study at any time.

Measure

The depressive symptoms of mothers in rural Senegal were measured using a translated version of the short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), which is composed of 10 items. Eight of the items were designed to measure negative affect and two were created to assess positive affect. Caregivers were asked to rate the frequency of each symptom or mood during the past seven days (e.g., everything an effort, restless sleep). Responses were given on a 4-point Likert-style ranging from never/rarely (scored as 0) to most of the time (scored as 3). Positive affect items were reverse coded to calculate a total summed score of all items (range from 0 to 40).

Translating the CESD-10 into Wolof

Following procedures used in other translations of the CESD-10 and other health-related scales [48, 49], a professional bilingual translator translated the scale from French to Wolof, the main language of Senegal and of the participants. To ensure conceptual equivalence, a research assistant who had not seen the initial French version back-translated the Wolof version into French to verify the accuracy of the translation. Any discrepancies between the original and the back-translated versions were discussed by a research team comprising eight native Wolof speakers with experience in survey research and interviews, as well as a strong understanding of the sociocultural context of Wolof-speaking communities in rural Senegal. The team spent hours working together, paying particular attention to the culturally appropriate wording of the items to ensure the comprehensibility of each item to Wolof-speaking participants in the communities where the study was conducted.

The cultural contextualization of the scale’s items was a critical part of the translation process. Challenges faced during this process included finding a culturally and linguistically equivalent term for “depressed” as understood in the Western psychiatric sense. As a group composed of translators and native Wolof research assistants, we agreed to use a locally understood phrasing, “nit ku am lu ko soof,” that, in some sense, conveys a blend of disgust, sadness, and emotional exhaustion. We should also note that the symptom “could not get going” was initially understood as “mënatoo woon jëm kanam” in Wolof, which translates to the sense of stagnation or lack of progress related to personal growth and life goals. Therefore, since the item “could not get going” instead refers to a lack of energy to start doing things or a lack of motivation, we proposed “Sawaroo woon,” which refers to an overall sense of low energy to do things.

The translation phase was followed by pilot testing the Wolof version of the scale to give members of the field team practice in administering the CESD-10 scale, assess its reliability, and solicit feedback from participants. Eighteen Wolof-speaking caregivers living in three rural communities were consented into the pilot study and read a short introductory paragraph stating that taking care of children is a demanding task, and many caregivers are sometimes swamped or tired. Participants were administered 14 questions from the longer CESD depression scale, which included the CESD-10. Participants were then interviewed about their depressive Symptoms and their understanding of the questions. The internal reliability for all 14 questions was very good (α = 0.88); reliability remained high (α = 0.86) for the subset of ten questions used for the current study. Information from the piloting phase resulted in a final refined CESD-10 scale that we used in this validation study (See Table 1).

Analytic strategy

Confirmatory factor analysis for the Wolof version of the CESD-10 scale

Analyses were conducted using Mplus version 8.6 [50]. There were four participants who did not complete the CESD-10; therefore, our confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) were conducted on 510 participants. There was minimal missingness at the item level (see Table 2), and we did not find any demographic variables that significantly predicted missingness; full information likelihood estimation was used to account for missing data. First, the sample was split into two random subsamples. The first sample (n = 249) was used for an initial CFA with an exploratory component. In this analysis, we evaluated model fit in conjunction with modification indices and standardized residuals to evaluate model misspecification following recommendations by Brown [51]. We tested the fit of the final model selected in this analysis with another CFA conducted on the second sample (n = 261). In all CFA models, the variance of the latent depression factor was fixed to 1 for model identification so that all item loadings could be freely estimated. Model fit was assessed using multiple fit indices: χ2 [47]; CFI [52] and TLI [53], for which values above 0.90 indicate acceptable fit; and SRMR and RMSEA [54], for which values below 0.08 suggest acceptable fit.

Reliability and validity

In addition to testing the factor structure of the scale, we examined internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha, item-test correlation (ITC), which measures the association between each item on the scale and the total score, and item-rest correlation (IRC), a measure of the relations between each item on the scale and the total score when an item in question is excluded. We interpreted ITC and IRC using the Guidelines that a coefficient above 0.50 indicates a moderate association, and above 0.80 indicates a strong association [55, 56]. Cronbach’s alpha was interpreted based on values of ≥ 0.90 indicating excellent internal consistency, ≥ 0.80 as good, ≥ 0.70 as acceptable, ≥ 0.60 as questionable, and < 0.50 as poor [57]. In the final step, we assessed divergent validity by testing the association between the CESD-10 total scores and household wealth index derived from multiple wealth-related variables (i.e., ownership of livestock assets and durable goods, access to running water and electricity, and materials used for floor, walls, and roof), and children’s height and weight z-scores compared to the median height and weight of children of the same age and sex [58].

Initial confirmatory factor analysis

Item factor loadings using a one-factor model from the first half of the data and first CFA are shown in Table 3. Model fit indices suggested poor model fit: χ2(35) = 117.30, p <.001, CFI = 0.86, TLI = 0.82., RMSEA = 0.10, and SRMR = 0.07. The standardized factor loadings for two positively worded items (item 3 “enjoyed things” and item 10 “felt happy”) were low and not statistically significant for item 3. Standardized factor loadings for all other items ranged from 0.41 to 0.82 and significantly loaded onto the depressive symptoms factor. Modification indices suggested large residual covariances between “Not being loved” (item 4) and “No sympathy” (item 2; modification index = 15.06) and between items “Everything an effort” (item 7) and “Restless sleep” (item 9; modification index = 28.74).

Reliability and validity

Results of the item-test and item-rest correlations, including average inter-item covariance and Cronbach’s alpha, are presented in Table 4. When we included all ten items of the scale, the overall Cronbach’s alpha was reasonable (α = 0.75). The two positively worded items had lower correlations with the full test (item-test), and lower correlations with the test minus the item (item-rest), particularly for the item “enjoy”. The overall inter-item correlations and test alpha were higher when one or both of the positively worded items were removed. After taking out the two positively worded items of the analysis, similar to what we did in the CFA, Cronbach’s alpha increased to 0.78, signaling the 8-item scales without the two positively worded items has an acceptable internal consistency.

Item-test and Item-rest correlation

In addition, we assessed the validity of the scale by calculating the correlation between total depression scores (all items) and caregivers’ household wealth index. Caregivers who reported more depressive symptoms were more likely to live in less wealthy households (r = −.15, p =.001). Further, caregivers with more depressive symptoms had children with lower weight (r = −.11, p <.05) and height (r = −.12, p <.05) z-scores adjusted for children’s age and sex.

Final confirmatory factor analysis

In the CFA of the final model using the second half of the data, we dropped the two positively worded items and added two parameters to account for residual covariances between two pairs of items (see Fig. 1). Low factor loadings of the positively worded items and their poor item-rest correlation provided combined evidence of a method effect rather than evidence for a two-factor model for depressive symptoms. Figure 1 presents standardized parameter estimates from the second CFA, all of which were significant at the p <.001 threshold. The model fit was acceptable: χ2(18) = 29.38, p <.05, CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.05, and SRMR = 0.03. The loadings ranged from 0.37 to 0.72.

Discussion

Depression is a widespread concern throughout Africa, but clinicians are limited in the tools available to screen for depression, as the measures used to assess depressive symptoms are only available and validated in some languages. The present study worked to increase the availability of depression measures within Senegal by evaluating the reliability and validity of the Wolof version of the CESD-10 among caregivers of young children in rural villages. Results from our CFA analyses indicated that this modified scale (without positively worded items) has strong psychometric properties and represents a one-factor structure. This finding aligns with work conducted using measures of depression in other African languages [18]. A one-factor solution composed entirely of negatively worded items was also found among other non-western samples who used the CESD-10, including for people living in China and Taiwan [59, 60].

The items that best fit a one-factor structure for depression were those that depicted negative experiences of Senegalese caregivers. Although scholars have noted that there is fear of social stigma related to African women’s experiences and health in general [61–63], Senegalese women were willing to report depressive symptoms that could interfere with their daily lives. One strength of our scale is that the items were checked with community members to ensure that they were culturally and linguistically appropriate. Although care should still be taken to ensure client confidentiality as well as client’s rights to refuse to answer questions that make them uncomfortable, results from the present work indicate that assessing depressive feelings is appropriate among Wolof-speaking caregivers in Senegal.

In contrast, the two positively worded items (i.e., happy and ‘enjoy things’) fit the model poorly, with low factor loadings and item-rest correlations. Thus, as noted in previous studies, the two items may represent a separate ‘positive affect’ factor that is closely related to, but distinct from, a measure of depression [24, 64], and they may have been interpreted by respondents as conceptually different [65]. Alternatively, other studies have suggested that including two positively worded items within a measure of depression can confuse respondents, who might expect the question format to be consistent with the negatively worded items [23]. In the context of the current study, the words ‘mbégte’ (happiness) and ‘tawféex, which translates to a general state of being in peace and ‘enjoying things’, are closely related concepts in Wolof and probably not interpreted as the opposite of feeling depressed. Method effects due to including negatively and positively worded items in the same scale are well-established to result in problematic factor structures [66]. Based on prior work in this area, we thus attributed the poor fit of these two items to a method effect and dropped them. The decision to drop the positively worded items is consistent with other factor analytic work on the CES-D, including in low- and middle-income countries [67].

After dropping the positively worded items from the scale, the eight remaining items yielded an overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.78, indicating satisfactory internal consistency. The satisfactory internal consistency, coupled with the CFA results, represents an important finding as it emphasizes the suitability of this Wolof scale for use by mental health clinicians and primary care physicians throughout Senegal, therefore increasing the availability of mental health screening tools in Senegal. The brief CES-D scale is transparent and easily used by Senegalese healthcare workers, many of whom do not have formal training in mental health [68]. The scale can also be used by health professionals, including psychiatrists in Senegal, who, to the best of our knowledge, do not have a depression screening tool in Wolof, the most commonly spoken language in the country [25]. However, since mental health services and professionals are extremely limited in numbers in Senegal [69, 70], the use of the short version of the CES-D by primary care physicians, as supported in previous research [71, 72] will not only lessen of the burden of the few mental health professionals, but also contribute to the early detection of depressive symptoms for better treatment.

Findings related to the validity of the CES-D 10 align with previous research showing that this survey measures an important construct for persons living in Senegal. Our findings demonstrated a negative correlation between maternal depressive symptoms and indicators of families’ economic status and children’s nutritional status [73–76]. The correlations revealed that caregivers who reported higher depressive symptoms came from less wealthy households and had children of lower weight and height for age and sex, providing evidence that the Wolof depression scale has acceptable discriminant validity. The scale can thus be used to assess the effectiveness of interventions aimed at improving maternal mental health, therefore helping the most vulnerable caregivers and their children in Senegal.

In sum, the current study provides compelling evidence regarding the strong psychometric properties of the abbreviated Wolof CES-D 10 scale. However, it is important to interpret the findings in light of the fact that our sample represented caregivers who lived in rural areas of Senegal. There is a substantial variability of depressive symptoms across urban and rural settings [77, 78], so the variability of scores may be even greater if used across multiple settings. Also, the literacy rate is higher in urban Senegal than in the rural parts of the country [79]. Therefore, caregivers in urban Senegal might respond differently to the scale’s items due to their knowledge about depression and understanding of the scale due to disparities in health literacy [80].

Limitations of the study

The cross-sectional nature of the study limits our ability to assess the stability of the Wolof CESD-10 scale over time, which hinders our ability to evaluate the causal relationships between depression, household wealth, and child health characteristics. In addition, although self-report scales are standard in studies like ours, they can lead to social desirability bias or underreporting, mainly in countries like Senegal, where depression is stigmatized [81]. The scale can benefit from future efforts to study depressive symptoms in a more diverse population in Senegal over time. Future research should also explore the factors associated with depression among caregivers in Senegal. Lastly, we acknowledge the complexity of the Wolof language and the challenges of finding culturally and linguistically equivalent psychological terms that are used in Western languages. Therefore, future research in Senegal should further explore locally grounded expressions of psychological distress. In this sense, we also encourage future research to employ qualitative methods for a deeper understanding of how mental health symptoms are expressed and understood within local contexts.

Conclusion

The availability of the abbreviated Wolof CESD-10 responds to the call of global mental experts to make the development and validation of brief mental health screening tools a priority research area, given the scarcity of such tools in low-income countries [35]. The Wolof version of the scale is locally, linguistically, and culturally relevant for the rural Senegalese population of caregivers, many of whom do not have access to adequate mental health care. As the first study to investigate the psychometric properties of the CESD-10 scale in Wolof, this research is a crucial first step in developing mental health screening tools in the country. Wolof-speaking populations represent more than 90% of the Senegalese population. Thus, this brief scale allows for a more standardized assessment of depressive symptoms in Senegal, which can inform targeted interventions and facilitate early detection and appropriate treatment, especially for vulnerable groups. Notably, the scale has important clinical implications. Given the limited number of mental health professionals in the country, the brevity of the scale makes it easy and quick for clinicians to use, therefore allowing them to save time. It can also be used by primary care physicians or community health workers during routine maternal health visits for early detection of individuals who may need a referral to mental health services.
