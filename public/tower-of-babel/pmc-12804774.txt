Baicalin protects against myocardial fibrosis through inhibition of DOT1L/COL-1 pathway during diabetic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Qiuling Xu, Zhuoxi Liu, Liyuan Wang, Guoyang Liu, Long Zhao, Hui Yue, Yong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) requires a systematic approach for effective management. Focusing on key signaling pathways in myocardial fibrosis could offer novel therapeutic approaches for DCM. The objective of this research is to investigate new biomarker and the underlying mechanisms by which baicalin intervenes in the progression of DCM. Swiss Target Prediction databases and weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) were employed to collect and analyze targets of baicalin and DCM. Intersection targets were obtained through venn online website, network visualization was conducted through STRING database and Cytoscape 3.7.2 software. Molecular docking validation was completed via AutoDock Vina software. Western blotting, RT-qPCR and histology were carried out to validate the direct targets of baicalin and explore the molecular mechanism during DCM progression. In mice induced to DCM, we observed that disruptor of telomeric silencing 1-like (DOT1L) was upregulated. Baicalin (BC) reduced myocardial fibrosis in mice as indicated by the reduced expression levels of fibrosis markers COL-1 and TGF-β. The inhibitory effect of BC was reversed by overexpressed DOT1L and further enhanced by silenced DOT1L. Our results indicate that cardiomyocyte DOT1L exerts a pathogenic role in DCM and BC specifically targets DOT1L/COL-1 signaling to reduce myocardial fibrosis in DCM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31765-w.Keywords: Baicalin, DOT1L, Cardiomyocytes, Diabetic cardiomyopathySubject terms: Cardiology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases

Introduction

Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) represents a prevalent and serious complication among individuals with diabetes1. DCM is characterized by changes in myocardial structure and function, with myocardial fibrosis being one of its important pathological manifestations2. In the process of DCM, myocardial fibrosis may result in heightened myocardial stiffness and reduced compliance, and this, in turn can cause dysfunction in cardiac relaxation and contraction, ultimately leading to heart failure3. Clinical studies have shown that approximately 50% of DCM patients will eventually die from heart failure, with a much higher mortality rate than the general population4. Not only that, myocardial fibrosis also increases the risk of arrhythmia, further endangering the patient’s life and health5.

At present, the treatment of DCM in clinical practice mainly focuses on managing risk factors like hyperglycemia, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, as well as symptomatic treatment of complications such as heart failure and arrhythmia6. However, these conventional treatment methods often fail to effectively reverse the progression of DCM and cannot fundamentally improve patient prognosis. Therefore, exploring new drugs and targets that can effectively prevent and treat DCM has become a research hotspot and key issue that urgently needs to be addressed in the current cardiovascular field.

Baicalin is a major bioactive derivative found from dried roots of Radix Scutellariae7. Previous studies have demonstrated that baicalin exhibits diverse biological functions in various disease models8. Regarding cardiac effects, baicalin was proven to have potential application value in cardiovascular disorders9. Baicalin has been reported to prevent DCM progression by its antioxidative and lipid-lowering effects10. Moreover, baicalin may mitigate cardiomyocyte death and modulates autophagy via the AMPK/mTOR pathway11. Nevertheless, novel direct protein targets through which baicalin exerts its effects remain largely unknown and the therapeutic potential of baicalin has yet to be well explored in DCM.​

Materials and methods

Swiss target prediction

Swiss Target Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) was used to predict targets of baicalin. The compound’s SMILES string, validated via ChemDraw Ultra 14.0, was uploaded to the server and filtered by species (Homo sapiens).

Data acquisition

The GSE26887 dataset was obtained from the Gene Expression Ensemble Database (GEO) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and normalized as previous described12,13.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

To conduct WGCNA, we applied the WGCNA R package in this study. RNA-seq data from diabetic patients (n = 7) and control samples (n = 5) in GSE26887 dataset were filtered14. Using a soft-power threshold, a scale-free network was built. Modules were detected and merged based on eigengene similarity. Module eigengenes were correlated with clinical traits. Associate modules with phenotype data to identify modules significantly correlated with phenotype.

Construction of protein-protein interaction (PPI) network

The baicalin-diabetic cardiomyopathy intersection targets were input into the STRING database, the species were selected as “Homo sapiens”, and a minimum required interaction score of 0.4 was established. Cytoscape 3.7.2 software was carried out in this process.

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyze

GO enrichment analyze was executed via the clusterProfiler R package. Enrichment was calculated via hypergeometric test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction using enrichGO and AnnotationDbi. Significantly enriched terms were clustered by semantic similarity using simplifyEnrichment to reduce redundancy, identifying biological themes and pathways associated with the gene set.

Molecular docking

Download the three-dimensional structure of the ligand from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank and optimize it using SYBYL-X 2.0 software. The structure of the receptor was gotten from the RCSB database, water molecules and metal ions were removed using mgltools_win32_1.5.6 software. The structure of the target protein was gained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. The binding affinity between the compound and target protein was evaluated using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 software, and the docking results were visualized via PyMol.

Drugs

Baicalin was bought from Macklin (Shanghai, China). RPMI 1640 medium (#L210KJ) and penicillin-streptomycin solution (#S110JV) were obtained from Shanghai Yuanpei Biotechnology; Fetal bovine serum (#11011 − 8611) was gained from Zhejiang Tianhang Biotechnology; PBS buffer (# P1010-2 L) was purchased from Wuhan Servicebio Technology; Collagen I Antibody (#AF7001), disruptor of telomeric silencing 1-like (DOT1L) Antibody (#DF2490) and GAPDH Antibody (#AF7021) were obtained from Affinity (Suzhou, China). HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (#SA00001-2) were obtained from Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology.

Cell cultures

The cardiac myocytes HL1 were sourced from Biochemistry and Cell Biology (Shanghai, China), and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) medium (Gibco, Eggenstein, Germany) supplemented with 1000 mg/L of D-glucose, as well as fetal bovine serum (10%) added with penicillin and streptomycin(100 U/mL). Media containing high glucose (HG) consisted of 33 mmol/L glucose. Primary cardiac myocyte cultures were established from the ventricles of neonatal C57BL6 mice as described previously15. Myocardial fibroblasts from mice were obtained from Zhongqiaoxinzhou company (Shanghai, China). For Baicalin (BC) treatment, Cells were pretreated with 20 or 40µmol/L BC for 1 h and then it was exposured to 33 × 10− 3 m glucose for 8 h.

Experimental animal study

All animal care and experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Mudanjiang Medical University (Approved number: IACUC-20240928-319). This study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were acquired from Vital River (Beijing, China). Mice were fed a control diet (10% kcal% fat, Huafukang, Beijing, China) or an HFD (60% kcal% fat, Huafukang, Beijing, China) during the total 20 weeks to induce DCM according to previous reports16. DCM mice were randomized into DCM mice (DCM, n = 6), DCM mice treated with 50 mg/kg baicalin (DCM + baicalin-L, n = 6) and DCM mice treated with 100 mg/kg baicalin (DCM + baicalin-H, n = 6). In baicalin treatment groups, baicalin (at doses of 50 or 100 mg/kg) was delivered via oral gavage once every two days over an 8-week period. The diabetic group and control group were given DMSO solution following the same regimen17. Blood glucose concentrations and body weights were monitored at regular intervals. At the end of study, mice were injected pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg body weight) into the abdominal cavity for anesthesia before executing them using cervical dislocation method, followed by toe pinching to confirm death.

Metabolic measurements

During glucose tolerance test (GTT), blood samples from mice were collected at 0, 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 90 min, and 120 min following i.p. injection of dextrose (2 g/kg). Insulin tolerance tests (ITTs) were conducted via intraperitoneal injection of regular insulin (1 U/kg), with blood samples subsequently gathered at 0, 30, 45, 60, 75, and 90 min. Blood glucose levels were measured by Accu-Chek Performa glucometer (Roche, Basel, Switzerland)18.

Histology

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded heart tissue sections were utilized for histological examinations. Sirius red staining was applied to the sections to evaluate fibrosis, while H&E staining was also performed for routine histological analyze. These sections were examined using a bright-field microscope (DP71, OLYMPUS).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). cDNA was prepared using a Prime Script RT Master Mix Kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). RT-qPCR assays were performed using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ Kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). Sequence of primers were showed in Supplementary Table 1. Data analyze was conducted in a 2−ΔΔCt method.

Vector transfection and silencing RNA (siRNA) interference

Overexpression control (OE-ctrl), DOT1L overexpressing (OE-DOT1L) plasmids, DOT1L siRNA and its control siRNA were synthesized by VectorBuilder (Guangzhou, China). 50µM si-DOT1L(or its control siRNA) was transfected to HL1 cells and primary cardiomyocytes. Vector transfection was done using Lipofectamine 2000 (Aladdin, Shanghai, China). Sequence of siRNAs were showed in Supplementary Table 2.

Western blot

Protein from cells and tissues were collected using A protease inhibitor cocktail (Biotool, Houston, USA) and RIPA lysis buffer (Biotool, Houston, USA). Equal sample amounts (30 µg) of proteins (blended with 5X SDS loading buffer and ddH₂O) were heated at 99 °C for 5 min, samples were electrophoresed to SDS-PAGE gel and separated at 115 V for 80 min. Then proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane at 200 mA over 1 h. Membranes were then blocked with 5% milk and subjected to primary antibodies Collagen 1 (COL-1, #AF7001, Affinity, liyang, China), DOT1L (#DF2490, Affinity, liyang, China), and GAPDH (#AF7021, Affinity, liyang, China) in 4 °C for 12 h19. Membranes are rinsed in TBST and then subjected to secondary antibodies. Following spotted with the ECL chemiluminescent reaction(Lablead, Beijing, China), signals were captured by Chemiluminescence imaging system (Servicebio, Wuhan, China).

Statistics

All results were exhibited as mean ± SD. All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software. Three independent experiments were conducted to identify significant differences between means. Differences between two groups were compared by a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, while multiple comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. P < 0.05 represents statistically significant.

Results

Candidate target of Baicalin for the treatment of DCM patients

To investigate the target of baicalin in the treatment of DCM patients, we first used SwissTarget Prediction to predict the target of baicalin (Supplementary Table 3). Secondly, the core genes in the myocardial tissue of DCM patients were analyzed by WGCNA. We used the GSE26887 dataset to conduct transcriptome analyze on myocardial tissues from diabetic failure patients (n = 7) and control samples (n = 5). A scale-free topological network (β = 10) was accomplished in Fig. 1A and B. Using the hierarchical clustering method, the co-expressing genes were classified into five modules and color-coded, where the blue module was most significantly associated with DCM patients (Fig. 1C-D and Supplementary Table 4). The genes in the blue module obtained by WGCNA analyze were intersected with baicalin target gene to obtain 36 candidate genes (Fig. 1E). To further analyze the molecular mechanisms by which candidate target genes regulate DCM progression, we performed GO functional enrichment analyze (Supplementary Table 5). The first 10 pathways are shown in Fig. 1F, and these pathways are mainly concentrated in metabolic process, catalytic activity and response to chemical, etc.

(D) Module-trait correlation heatmap of DCM patients and non DCM patients, blue represents positive correlation. (E) Venn diagram of the intersection DEGs and hub genes. (F) GO enrichment analyze of the candidate genes obtained in (E) (colors represent different P-values, Sizes of the circles represent the count).

Molecular docking of baicalin with hub targets

To further explore the hub targets of baicalin in improving DCM, we performed PPI network analyze on 36 candidate targets. DOT1L, CARM1, PRMT1, DNMT1 and SUV39H1 were selected as potential drug action targets (Fig. 2A). We used baicalin as ligand and five potential drug targets as receptors for molecular docking analyze using the AutoDock Vina software. The molecular docking results indicated that baicalin could interact with five potential drug targets, and the binding energies with DOT1L, CARM1, PRMT1, DNMT1 and SUV39H1 were − 9.6, -9.4, -7.7, -7.5 and − 6.5 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. 2B). The binding energy of baicalin with DOT1L is the lowest, indicating that baicalin has the strongest binding affinity with DOT1L. According to the results of molecular docking, we found that DOT1L is an important target of baicalin in DCM patients, which is used for subsequent experimental verification.

Molecular docking of baicalin with hub targets. (A) Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analyze on 36 candidate targets. (B) Molecular docking diagram of baicalin with hub targets.

Baicalin prevents high glucose-induced myocardial fibrosis in vitro​

To investigate whether baicalin has the effect of preventing myocardial fibrosis, we used Western blot and RT-qPCR techniques to assess fibrosis markers in HL1 cells and primary mouse cardiomyocytes. Western blot results showed that baicalin reduced levels of fibrosis markers COL-1 and TGF-β in both HL1 cells and primary mouse cardiomyocytes. Comparing with the HG group, baicalin at 20 µmol/L and 40 µmol/L both decreased COL-1 and TGF-β protein levels in HL1 cells (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). Similarly, in primary cardiomyocytes, COL-1 and TGF-β expressions were also significantly suppressed by baicalin (Fig. 3B). RT-qPCR results further confirmed that baicalin downregulated the mRNA levels of Col1a1 and Tgfb1 in a dose-dependent manner in HL1 cells (Fig. 3C-D). The relative expression of Col1a1 and Tgfb1 in baicalin-treated primary mouse cardiomyocytes were also decreased (P < 0.01), at the highest concentration tested (Fig. 3E-F). These findings indicate that baicalin effectively suppresses myocardial fibrosis at both the protein and gene levels in cardiomyocytes.

Baicalin inhibits the progression of DCM in mice​

Given the potential of baicalin to inhibit myocardial fibrosis in vitro, we further established a mouse model of dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) to evaluate its effect on the progression of DCM in vivo. DCM model mice treated with baicalin (100 mg/kg/day via oral gavage ) exhibited significant improvements in body weight gain compared to the untreated DCM group (Fig. 4A). ITT and GTT tests showed that high dose of baicalin treatment alleviated glucose intolerance in DCM mice (Fig. 4B) and increased insulin sensitivity in mice (Fig. 4C). Histological analyze revealed that baicalin (100 mg/kg/day) markedly reduced myocardial fibrosis (Fig. 4D). Sirius red staining indicated that the fibrotic area in the baicalin-treated group accounted for 1.3% ± 0.2% of the total tissue area, significantly lower than 4.6% ± 2.1% in the DCM control group (P < 0.05; Fig. 4D).

Baicalin suppresses DOT1L expression in cardiomyocytes​

Our network pharmacology analyze showed that baicalin may directly regulate the DOT1L signaling during DCM. To reveal the potential molecular mechanism by which baicalin exerts its antifibrotic effect, we focused on the histone methyltransferase DOT1L and detected its expression changes after baicalin treatment. Western blot analyze of mouse heart tissues and cultured cardiomyocytes showed that baicalin treatment (40µmol/L) significantly decreased DOT1L protein expression. In HL1 cells and primary cardiomyocytes, baicalin reduced DOT1L expression, respectively (Fig. 5A-B). Similarly, In DCM mice, DOT1L levels in the baicalin-treated group were sharply decreased than those in the DCM control group (P < 0.01; Fig. 5C). These results suggest that baicalin may exert its antifibrotic effects by suppressing DOT1L.​

Baicalin inhibits myocardial fibrosis via the DOT1L/COL-1 pathway​

As we found the correlation between baicalin and DOT1L expression in the above results, we conducted gene overexpression and silencing experiments to further investigate the role of the DOT1L/COL-1 pathway in baicalin-mediated inhibition of myocardial fibrosis. First, we overexpressed the DOT1L gene in HL1 cells and primary cardiomyocytes to observe its effect on the antifibrotic action of baicalin. Overexpression of DOT1L in HL1 cells and primary cardiomyocytes abolished the inhibitory effects of baicalin on COL-1 expression. In both DOT1L-overexpressing HL1 cells and primary cardiomyocytes, baicalin failed to reduce COL-1 protein levels (Fig. 6A and B). RT-qPCR analyze showed that baicalin-induced suppression of Col1a1 and Tgfb1 mRNA expression was also reversed by DOT1L overexpression (Fig. 6C-D).

Effects of overexpressed DOT1L on the expression of COL-1 in HL1 cells and primary cardiomyocytes. HL1 cells and primary cardiomyocytes were transfected with either a plasmid overexpressing DOT1L (OE-DOT1L) or a control plasmid (OE-Ctrl). (A) Western blot analyze of DOT1L and COL-1 in HL1 cells under HG. Right panel showing quantification of relative DOT1L and COL-1 by Image J. (B) Western blot analyze and quantification of DOT1L and COL-1 in primary cardiomyocytes under HG. (C,D) RT-qPCR analyze of cardiac fibrosis gene expression in HL1 cells (C) and primary cardiomyocytes (D). Data in all panels are shown as mean ± SD (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01).

Next, we silenced the DOT1L gene (Fig. 7A-B) and explored its impact on the antifibrotic effect of baicalin. Conversely, silencing DOT1L expression using siRNA potentiated baicalin’s antifibrotic effects. In DOT1L-silenced HL1 cells, baicalin further decreased COL-1 protein levels in both HL1 cells and primary cardiomyocytes compared to baicalin treatment alone, respectively (Fig. 7C and E). Similarly, the RT-qPCR analyze revealed that the combination of DOT1L silencing and baicalin treatment led to reduction in Col1a1 and Tgfb1 mRNA levels, significantly greater than baicalin treatment alone (Fig. 7D and F). These findings strongly support that baicalin inhibits myocardial fibrosis through the DOT1L/COL-1 pathway.

Discussion

Diabetes represents a pressing worldwide public health concern, characterized by escalating prevalence rates and disease-associated mortality. DCM represents a major complication of diabetes, occurring independently of hypertension, coronary disorders, valvular heart conditions, or other heart-related diseases20. Emerging evidence highlights DCM as a significant contributor to mortality among all diabetes-related complications, thus developing more effective therapeutic drugs for DCM is currently urgent issues to be addressed21. In this study, biomarkers were identified in DCM patients. During WGCNA analyze, it was found that DOT1L, CARM1, PRMT1, DNMT1 and SUV39H1 were candidate biomarkers in DCM patients. After GEO datasets analyze and molecular docking, we found DOT1L to be a novel potential target of BC in DCM. Further, we validated the potential mechanisms of BC in the treatment of DCM.

Baicalin is mainly obtained and separated from the dried roots of Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi22. Studies have shown that baicalin reduces the levels of TNF-α and IL-6 in model of acute myocardial infarction23. In cardiovascular disease, baicalin has anti atherosclerosis effect by suppressing NF-κB signaling pathway and diminishing the release of inflammatory cytokines and decrease damage of inflammatory reaction to vascular endothelial cells24. In addition, SOD and GSH activity can be enhanced by baicalin and damage to vascular walls caused by oxidative stress was rescued by baicalin25. Myocardial fibrosis is indispensable in the development of cardiovascular pathologies associated with diabetes. Baicalin can alleviate the degree of myocardial fibrosis by suppressing fibrosis related factors and signaling pathways. Research has found that baicalin can reduce transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), type I and type III collagen while inhibiting the proliferation and activation of cardiac fibroblasts through TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway26. Consistent with previous research, this study found baicalin reduced expression of fibrosis markers (COL-1, TGF-β1) both in DCM mice and cardiomyocytes. Further, we analyzed mRNA levels of Col1a1 and Tgfb in myocardial fibroblasts and found that collagen production were upregulated by baicalin in myocardial fibroblasts (supplementary Fig. 1).

As a methyltransferase, DOT1L is associated with embryonic development and normal growth, maintaining a stable population of tissue stem/progenitor cells27. It catalyzes the methylation of histone H3 lysine 79 to modulate transcriptional activation28. Tang et al. also reported that DOT1L affects cell cycle as well as and DNA repair29. DOT1L is also associated with the progression of several solid malignancies including colon cancer, ovarian cancer and breast cancer30. Recently, DOT1L was shown to be critical in the progression of diabetes and its complications31. In another study, DOT1L prevents podocyte injury through modulating PLCL1-mediated fatty acid metabolism during diabetic kidney disease32. It was also reported that DOT1L is a modifier of kidney fibrosis at both genetic and epigenetic levels via DOT1L and HDAC2-mediated regulation of Edn1 transcription33. Deletion of DOT1L in thermogenic adipocytes potently by modulating H3K79 methylation in mice undergoing diet-induced diabetes34. In this study, DOT1L is also found to promote collagen production in myocardial fibroblasts (supplementary Fig. 1). Through molecular docking analyze in this study, it was found that baicalin has good binding ability with DOT1L, and further experiments confirmed that DOT1L is a key regulatory factor of baicalin in inhibiting myocardial fibrosis.

Conclusions

This study revealed the molecular mechanism of baicalin in DCM by inhibiting DOT1L regulation of COL-1 and TGF-β1, providing new experimental and theoretical evidence supporting the potential use of baicalin in DCM. Although myocardial fibrosis is a part of the complex pathological mechanism of DCM, future research still needs to explore whether baicalin protects heart through other mechanisms. In addition, besides DOT1L, baicalin may also exert its effects by regulating other targets, and these potential mechanisms deserve further investigation.
