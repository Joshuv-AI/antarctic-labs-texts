Incidence rates of diagnosed anxiety and depressive disorders in Québec, Canada: changes in trends after the COVID-19 pandemic using interrupted time series methods
Authors: Emmanuelle Arpin, Victoria Massamba, Louis Rochette, Isaora Dialahy, Amelie Quesnel-Vallée, José Ignacio Nazif-Muñoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThe COVID-19 pandemic had profound effects on mental health worldwide. Within Canada, the province of Québec experienced high increases in mental health distress and implemented particularly strict public health measures. These unique characteristics motivate the examination of the local experience of the COVID-19 pandemic. This study aimed to examine changes in incidence rates of diagnosed anxiety and depressive disorders in Québec, Canada before and after the COVID-19 pandemic.DesignAnalyses drew on administrative health records from the Québec Integrated Chronic Disease Surveillance System from January 2010 to March 2022 (147 months).SettingThe setting for analyses was the province of Québec, Canada.ParticipantsThe analytical sample covered January 2010 to March 2022 (147 months) for all individuals aged 15 years and older (men and women) eligible for the Québec public health insurance plan.Primary and secondary outcome measuresInterrupted time series methods were used to estimate counterfactual trends of monthly incidence rates of diagnosed anxiety and depressive disorders under conditions with and without the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. Analyses adjusted for local public health policies, including the enactment of the Cannabis Act (2018) and major provincial health system reforms (2015). As we drew on count data, we used generalised linear modelling techniques with Poisson distribution to estimate predicted and counterfactual incidence rates of diagnosed anxiety and depressive disorders over time. We report regression results as the incidence rate ratio (IRR) with 95% CIs.ResultsFollowing the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, monthly incidence rates of diagnosed anxiety disorders were 4.9% higher (IRR: 1.049, 95% CI 0.955 to 1.153), but there was insufficient evidence of a statistically significant change; there was a statistically significant increase in the slope by 1% (IRR: 1.010, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.018). For diagnosed depressive disorders, monthly incidence rates were rather 3.6% lower (IRR: 0.965; 95% CI 0.904 to 1.030), although not statistically significant; there was a statistically significant increase in the slope by 1.4% (IRR: 1.014; 95% CI 1.008 to 1.020). Results from the counterfactual trends suggest that incidence rates of diagnosed anxiety disorders would have been lower (observed rates are higher), while incidence rates of depressive disorders would have been higher (observed rates are lower) had the COVID-19 pandemic not occurred.ConclusionsFindings encourage further consideration of the indirect consequences of public health crises and related responses on population mental health. They also highlight the need to examine dynamics in local jurisdictions and support reflection on policy investments in mental health protection and promotion.Keywords: COVID-19, Anxiety disorders, Depression & mood disorders, STATISTICS & RESEARCH METHODS, Cannabis, Health policy

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic had profound effects on population mental health worldwide, with early studies suggesting substantial increases in the prevalence and burden of major depressive and anxiety disorders.1–3 The unprecedented nature of the crisis, characterised by the rapid spread of SARS-CoV-2, strict lockdown measures and significant disruptions to daily life, was initially associated with a sharp rise in mental health symptoms. For instance, early global estimates indicated a 27.6% increase in cases of major depressive disorders and a 25.6% increase in cases of anxiety disorders during 2020.3

Theories from the social sciences and psychology, emphasising the psychological consequences of large-scale disasters, can shed some insight into these trends. Certain theories emphasise how increased stress, social isolation and economic uncertainty during large-scale crises contribute to heightened mental health risks.4 First, the stress-vulnerability model helps explain how such widespread crises can exacerbate existing vulnerabilities, particularly in individuals who are already at risk for mental health disorders due to genetic, environmental or socio-economic factors.5 The pandemic created an environment ripe for stress, with financial instability and heightened anxiety about the virus compounding pre-existing conditions, making individuals more susceptible to developing or worsening mental health issues. second, cognitive-behavioural theory (CBT) underscores the role of individual perception in shaping emotional responses, suggesting that people with negative cognitive biases, such as catastrophising or rumination, may have been particularly vulnerable to the mental health impacts of the pandemic. The rapid spread of misinformation, along with the uncertainty surrounding public health measures, likely exacerbated feelings of anxiety and depression in those prone to negative thinking patterns. Lastly, the theory of social support6 posits that the lack of in-person interaction and social connections, due to lockdowns and social distancing, may have undermined essential emotional and instrumental support systems, intensifying a population-level mental health crisis.

However, another arm of research challenges the assumption that the pandemic led to a sustained mental health crisis across all populations. Longitudinal studies suggest that while an initial spike in symptoms was observed at the onset of the pandemic, mental health outcomes varied over time, with many individuals demonstrating resilience or recovery as the pandemic progressed.7 These findings resonate with different theories. First, psychological resilience theory, which posits that individuals can develop the capacity to adapt positively to adversity, particularly when they have access to adaptive coping mechanisms, a sense of agency and strong social support networks.8 Even in the face of significant stressors, some individuals demonstrate remarkable resilience, using the crisis as an opportunity for personal growth or positive transformation. second, post-traumatic growth theory9 further expands on this idea, suggesting that individuals may experience increased personal strength, deeper relationships or a re-evaluation of life priorities following a traumatic event, such as a global pandemic. For example, some individuals may have found new meaning in life by reassessing their values or focusing on what truly matters in the wake of the pandemic’s challenges. Lastly, the social ecological model highlights how external factors, such as regional responses to the pandemic, community resilience and healthcare access, also shape individual outcomes.10 People in regions with robust healthcare systems, effective public health measures and strong community networks may have experienced better recovery rates due to the availability of resources and support, underscoring the importance of contextual factors in the mental health trajectory. Ultimately this body of research theoretically challenges the notion of a universal mental health crisis, suggesting that while the pandemic undoubtedly caused significant distress for many, it also triggered processes of recovery, adaptation and growth for others, pointing to the diverse ways in which populations responded to the same crisis.

Depression and anxiety may have followed distinct trajectories during the pandemic, shaped by different socio-economic, jurisdictional and individual psychological factors. While uncertainty about the course of the pandemic may have contributed to elevated anxiety levels, delays in access to psychiatric care may have exacerbated depressive symptoms. It is also important to acknowledge that anxiety and depression exist on a continuum, with untreated anxiety often progressing toward depression.11–13 Therefore, it is crucial to recognise that these two conditions may have experienced divergent symptom trajectories over the course of the pandemic.

Québec, Canada provides a unique context for examining the mental health outcomes of depression and anxiety during the pandemic. Although the province, like many others, experienced an initial rise in both conditions,14 increases in self-reported poor mental health and diagnosed conditions were relatively lower in Québec compared with other provinces.15 These findings are particularly striking given that Québec implemented some of the strictest public health measures in Canada during the pandemic.16 For example, a night curfew was enforced for 6 months starting in January 2021, with substantial fines for non-compliance, alongside restrictions targeting unvaccinated individuals. Moreover, as elsewhere, Québec’s entire healthcare system was negatively affected by the pandemic, with significant delays in surgeries and routine visits to healthcare providers.

Local policies and programmes likely played a role in how anxiety and depressive disorders may have unfolded in the province. For example, Québec’s healthcare system underwent two major reforms in 2015. The first was the introduction of the National Mental Health Action Plan 2015–20.17 In response to challenges in access to mental health services, the plan aimed to ensure care continuity for mental health conditions—diagnoses, treatment plans—by integrating specialised psychiatric care in primary care. And second, to improve efficiency and access to care across the network, Québec underwent a major administrative overhaul of its healthcare system with the implementation of Bill 10.18 In addition, the Cannabis Act, introduced in October 2018, is a federal law that ensured the development of a legal and controlled market to develop, purchase and consume cannabis products across Canada.19 Although federally enacted, each province retained the prerogative to develop its own regulation. Québec imposed some of the strictest regulations, being the only province to prohibit the possession and use until the age of 21 and product regulation to deter youth consumption (eg, no sale of edibles that were deemed appealing to youth, namely sweets, confectionaries, desserts and cannabis-infused chocolates).20 These local factors may have influenced the evolution of mental health conditions, in tandem with the pandemic.

By examining population-level trends in diagnosed depression and anxiety in Québec, we aim to understand how these outcomes evolved before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. While our empirical focus is on Québec, we situate our findings within the broader international literature to consider whether observed patterns align with or diverge from those reported elsewhere. Our analyses are guided by theoretical frameworks such as the stress-vulnerability model, CBT and social support theory, which provide lenses for interpreting population-level patterns in mental health outcomes. Using administrative health records, we focus on the influence of local policies, health system changes and population-level factors on the incidence of these disorders. This case study underscores the importance of region-specific analyses for capturing diverse mental health trajectories during the pandemic and the contextual factors shaping them.

Methodological approach

Data

Analyses drew on linked, retrospective population-based administrative health records from the Québec Integrated Chronic Disease Surveillance System or the QICDSS (Système intégré de surveillance des maladies chroniques du Québec, SISMACQ), curated by the Institut national de santé publique du Québec (INSPQ).21 The QICDSS includes data on publicly insured health services for the entire population of Québec. Specifically, the QICDSS includes linked information from five administrative health databases starting in January 1996. The analyses for this study specifically drew on hospitalisation and physician claims to track information on diagnoses. Further information on the QICDSS is included in online supplemental appendix 1.

We obtained aggregated, monthly incidence rates per 100 000 population for diagnosed anxiety and depressive disorders. The analytical sample covered January 2010 to March 2022 (147 months) for all individuals aged 15 years and older (men and women) eligible for the Québec public health insurance plan. This period provided sufficient pre- and post-intervention observations around the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic (provincial government state of emergency declaration on 13 March 2020), allowing us to apply an interrupted time series (ITS) approach.22 23 This design accounts for underlying temporal trends and helps differentiate the impact of the pandemic from pre-existing health and social policy changes, while acknowledging the possibility of other unmeasured events that could have influenced observed trends.

Variables

The primary outcomes were monthly counts of incident diagnoses of anxiety and depressive disorders among individuals aged 15 years and older. Age- and sex-standardised incidence rates per 100 000 population were computed for descriptive purposes only. These were derived using direct standardisation with 5-year age groups (15–19, 20–24, …, 85+) and the 2011 Québec census population as the reference standard. A fixed 2011 standard population was used to ensure comparability of age-standardised rates over time and to avoid artificial variation introduced by changes in the population age structure across study years. Age-specific rates were calculated first and then weighted using the standard population distribution. Diagnoses were based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10). Online supplemental appendix 2 lists the ICD codes included in the measures of diagnosed anxiety and depressive disorders. Individuals were classified as having a diagnosed anxiety or depressive disorder if they had at least one relevant ICD-9 or ICD-10 code recorded during a physician visit or hospitalisation. For hospitalisation records, diagnoses could be listed in either primary or secondary position.

We included a number of independent variables in our models. First, we included time in months since the start of the study period (January 2010) to represent the pre-pandemic period. Next, we included dummy variables to mark interruptions in the time series. We included a year-month indicator to mark the COVID-19 pandemic, specifically March 2020 (2020-03), where ‘1’ indicates time after March 2020, or as of April 2020, and ‘0’ indicates time before April 2020. March 2020 is coded as ‘0’. To better adjust changes over time, we included similar dummies to account for other interruptions caused by the aforementioned local policies (eg, ‘1’ since October 2018 to represent the introduction of the Cannabis Act and ‘0’ before its introduction, as well as ‘1’ since April 2015 to represent the two major health system reforms in Québec and ‘0’ before these reforms). The dummy variables described above signal any immediate changes in the outcome variable or a change in level in a statistical sense, following these events. We also introduced interaction terms between time and each dummy variable described above. These interaction terms represent a change in slope (ie, rate of change) caused by the interruptions in the series. In addition, we included a year-month indicator of the modernisation of the physician payment system in Québec to control for any mechanical breaks (ie, 2016-04). Finally, we included controls for the proportion of diagnoses for anxiety disorders completed by family doctors.

Statistical approach

We drew on ITS modelling techniques to estimate predicted and counterfactual trends in monthly incidence rates for diagnoses of anxiety and depressive disorders.24 Our primary goal was to assess the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly the trends in mental health disorders, had the pandemic not occurred. It is challenging to isolate the effect of a large-scale policy change or public health crisis. ITS modelling techniques represent a useful tool, as they enable the examination of the impact of multiple policies over time by creating counterfactual trends. Practically, this implies estimating trends had a policy not occurred, or said otherwise, had the pre-event trends continued.

We followed the approach outlined by Bernal and colleagues, adapted for public health interventions.25 As we drew on count data, we used generalised linear models (GLM), with a Poisson distribution and log link to model monthly counts of incident diagnoses, incorporating the log of the population as an offset. From the estimates produced with the counts of monthly cases, we transformed our estimates back to incidence rate ratios (IRR).

When using time series data, it is important to ensure that trends follow a stationary process. In a statistical sense, this implies that the trends have a time-invariant mean and variance. Estimates produced with non-stationary data can lead to spurious results. We ran a series of tests to assess the stationarity of our trends (ie, to confirm the absence of a unit root). As Dickey-Fuller tests confirm the stationarity of monthly incidence rates over time, no differencing of the trends was necessary. Results are available in online supplemental appendix 3.

Additionally, diagnostic tests confirmed the presence of overdispersion. As such, we adjusted our models to provide robust standard errors. Furthermore, as overdispersion was present in our models, we ran a series of negative binomial specifications. Results are robust and available in online supplemental appendix 4.

We pre-specified a full ITS model for each outcome, including baseline level, pre-intervention trend, immediate level change following policy changes and change in post-intervention slope. Given the relatively small number of parameters in each model, we did not conduct automated stepwise selection procedures or data-driven elimination of covariates. In addition to this a priori specification, we evaluated a limited set of alternative ITS formulations to assess model robustness. These alternatives included models with and without seasonal adjustment terms and alternative functional specifications of the time trend where appropriate. All candidate models were estimated on identical outcome series.

Model selection was based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). These criteria were used descriptively to compare competing pre-specified models rather than to eliminate individual coefficients. When AIC and BIC identified different preferred models, BIC was given greater weight due to its stronger penalty for model complexity and its greater emphasis on parsimony in relatively short time series. Importantly, the final selected specification was consistent with the a priori ITS framework, and all main findings were robust across alternative model specifications. AIC and BIC values for all candidate models are found in the online supplemental appendix 4.

We report regression results as the IRR with 95% CIs. The IRR can be interpreted as a relative increase (value>1) or decrease (value<1) of the monthly incidence rate, following interruptions in the series (ie, public health crisis or policy change). A value of 1 represents no change imposed from the interruptions in the series. We applied robust standard errors. Time series plots comparing actual, predicted and counterfactual values were also produced to graphically represent results. All the analyses were conducted using Stata 18.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to examine trajectories of diagnosed anxiety and depressive disorders in Québec, Canada, in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic. While global evidence has documented rapid and significant increases in mental health problems,1–3 it is essential to assess whether local trends followed or not similar patterns. Using ITS modelling techniques to estimate counterfactual trends, we find that monthly incidence rates of diagnosed anxiety and depressive disorders would have diverged in the absence of the pandemic. Specifically, our results show that the pandemic was associated with 896 additional diagnosed cases of anxiety disorders compared with what would have been expected without COVID-19, whereas there were 423 fewer diagnosed cases of depressive disorders than the counterfactual projection. This contrasting pattern underscores how the pandemic influenced mental health outcomes differently across mental health disorder types, highlighting the importance of region-specific analyses to understand the heterogeneous trajectories of anxiety and depression.

For diagnosed anxiety disorders, our findings indicate that rates would have been lower had the COVID-19 pandemic not occurred. This result is consistent with national trends in Canada14 26 and reinforces the idea that the context of the pandemic—marked by uncertainty, social isolation and continuous exposure to health-related threats—heightened levels of anxiety in the population. These patterns align with social science theories emphasising the adverse psychological impacts of large-scale disasters.4 In particular, the stress-vulnerability model suggests that crises of this magnitude can amplify pre-existing susceptibilities, especially among individuals already vulnerable to mental disorders due to genetic, environmental or socioeconomic factors.5 This framework helps explain why, in a context of pervasive stressors and reduced coping resources, anxiety-related diagnoses rose more sharply than what counterfactual trends would have predicted.5

In addition, our results highlight the importance of considering local, contextual factors. Specifically, we observe how the increased rate of diagnosed anxiety disorders following the legalisation of cannabis in 2018 was accelerated after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. This increase, observed in our study and by others,27 28 suggests that contextual factors in tandem, such as the public debate on legalisation of cannabis and public health measures during the pandemic, may have influenced the evolution of these diagnoses.

For diagnosed depressive disorders, our counterfactual estimates suggest that rates would have been higher in the absence of the COVID-19 pandemic. This is exemplified by the significant change in level following the pandemic. However, after a period of time, there would be an expected catch-up in the incidence rate with an increase over time, thus suggesting a curvilinear effect. This finding diverges not only from the observed trends for diagnosed anxiety disorders but also from global evidence showing significant increases in depressive disorders during the pandemic.3 Three complementary explanations may account for this pattern. First, limited access to general practitioners and psychiatric specialists at the onset of the pandemic likely restricted opportunities for consultation, treatment and diagnosis of mental health disorders.29 Second, a clinical perspective highlights the temporal sequencing of mental health conditions: anxiety disorders frequently precede depressive disorders along the continuum of mental illness.11–13 Finally, it is also possible that depression rates were lower than expected at the time because healthcare providers may have attributed depressive symptoms during the pandemic as ‘normal’ given the challenging social and sanitary circumstances, rather than evidence of psychopathology.30 Taken together, these factors suggest that the lower-than-expected rate of diagnosed depressive disorders early in the pandemic may have reflected both a reduced access to care and a delayed manifestation of depressive symptoms.

This study includes a number of strengths. First, the examination of diagnosed health conditions from the high-quality administrative data produced by the QICDSS ensures a reduction of measurement error. Next, although not mentioned above, we leveraged QICDSS’s regional coverage to explore variation across Québec’s 18 health regions. This served as a relevant sensitivity check, given documented differences in COVID-19 spread and public health measures over time.31 32 Our findings showed no significant regional differences. However, the absence of more granular data may mask subtler within-region heterogeneity that cannot be detected with aggregated comparisons.

Our findings should be interpreted in light of the following limitations. First, reliance solely on administrative health records, rather than self-reported symptoms, constrains our ability to capture the full spectrum of mental health disorders.33 34 While administrative data offer advantages in diagnostic accuracy and longitudinal consistency, they inevitably omit individuals with undiagnosed conditions or subclinical symptoms. Complementary surveys with detailed questionnaires or qualitative studies would therefore be valuable to better understand societal attitudes and experiences related to public health measures. Furthermore, the reliance on administrative data implies that our data may be affected by synchronicity in trends, meaning that possible divergence between anxiety and depression trends may reflect changes in diagnostic practices or coding practices rather than distinct epidemiological trends. Second, we did not have information on additional control variables, such as gender distribution, comorbidities and socioeconomic status, all of which would have enriched the analysis by accounting for unobserved heterogeneity. Disaggregated data by sex would have been particularly pertinent, as the empirical literature finds significant sex differences in the distribution of anxiety and depression.35

Finally, disentangling which components of the pandemic most directly influenced mental health conditions remains challenging (eg, infection from the virus itself or the strict public health measures imposed or both in combination). The use of ITS modelling techniques allowed us to overcome the challenge of empirically isolating the effect of a policy of interest through the creation of counterfactual trends. Poisson models were contrasted with negative binomial specifications, and results were robust across model specifications, supporting the stability of the findings. However, by design, ITS capture the overall effect of the pandemic onset as an intervention period, making it difficult to isolate the contribution of distinct measures such as confinement policies, remote work arrangements or restrictions on social gatherings. A direct comparison with another province with less stringent public health measures would indeed be a useful approach to better isolate the potential effect of policy differences. However, this was not feasible due to the lack of comparable, harmonised incident-level data across provinces and such comparative analyses were beyond the scope of the present study, which focuses on within-province temporal trends using a consistent data source. We note that previous Canadian evidence suggests regional variation in changes in depressive symptoms during the COVID-19 pandemic, with some Atlantic provinces reporting smaller increases compared with other regions.36 However, causal attribution to differences in policy stringency remains uncertain and warrants further investigation.

We also note a visually apparent shift in the depression series around 2016–2017, with a smaller but similar pattern observed for anxiety disorders. While our interrupted time-series specification included pre-defined structural breaks for major policy and system changes (including the 2015 health system reforms and the 2016 physician payment modernisation), these adjustments may partially account for this mid-period variation. However, this pattern may also reflect other unmeasured system-level or behavioural changes during that period. Although our modelling strategy was based on a priori policy cut-points rather than data-driven break detection, future work could further evaluate this feature using complementary approaches such as joinpoint or fully data-driven segmented regression analyses.

Conclusion

In the aftermath of the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, significant changes in population mental health were observed on a global scale. Locally, trends may have differed in their trajectories, in part due to local dynamics. This study finds that in Québec, Canada, there were differences in trends in incidence rates of diagnosed anxiety and depression following the pandemic. Theories from the social sciences and local policy dynamics help to explain these diverging trends. Results encourage future pandemic-related research on population mental health to examine dynamics in local jurisdictions, as well as deeper reflection on policy investments in mental health protection and promotion.
