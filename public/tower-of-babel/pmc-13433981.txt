Interpreting the clinical utility and generalizability of a multitask perioperative prediction model
Authors: Xiao-Min Wo, Guo-Ming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis Matters Arising comments on Yoon et al.’s multitask gradient boosting model for predicting acute kidney injury, postoperative respiratory failure and in-hospital mortality after non-cardiac surgery. We ask for clarification on the clinical decision context and threshold ranges in the decision curve analyses, the absolute impact of the model for rare outcomes, and calibration and potential recalibration in external cohorts to support implementation.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cardiology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

We read with interest the article by Yoon et al., who developed and externally validated a tree-based multitask gradient boosting machine (MT-GBM) to predict acute kidney injury (AKI), postoperative respiratory failure (PRF) and in-hospital mortality after non-cardiac surgery using 16 routinely available preoperative variables1. The authors should be congratulated for addressing an important perioperative problem with a scalable and interpretable model evaluated across three centres. We have several questions regarding the decision curve analyses, the interpretation of performance for rare outcomes, and calibration in external cohorts that may further aid implementation of this work.

First, the clinical decision problems underlying the decision curve analyses are not completely clear. Yoon et al. reported that the MT-GBM model provides a net benefit comparable to that of single-task models and is modestly superior to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, particularly for AKI at lower threshold probabilities1. However, the downstream decisions to which these curves apply are not specified, for example, whether the curves are intended to guide direct ICU admission versus ward care, escalation of kidney-protective strategies or enhanced postoperative monitoring. Interpretation of the net benefit in decision curve analysis depends critically on defining the decision, the relative weight assigned to false-positive and false-negative classifications, and a range of threshold probabilities that clinicians would plausibly consider for each outcome2,3. Clarifying which specific perioperative decisions the decision curves are intended to inform for AKI, PRF and mortality, and how the plotted threshold ranges were chosen to reflect realistic risk tolerances in those settings, would substantially help readers.

Second, PRF and in-hospital mortality were relatively rare in all cohorts (approximately 0.9–1.8% and 0.6–2.9%, respectively)1, yet several AUROCs exceeded 0.90. Yoon et al. appropriately reported the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) and F1 scores, acknowledging that precision–recall metrics are more informative in imbalanced data1,4. Nonetheless, the practical meaning of these gains may remain difficult for clinicians to gauge. For readers to better appreciate the magnitude of clinical impact, it would be helpful if the authors could present, for one or two clinically plausible thresholds— for example, thresholds close to current practice for ICU admission or enhanced monitoring—the corresponding positive and negative predictive values and the associated net benefit, translated into the number of additional true events detected and additional false positives generated per 100 or 1000 patients compared with the ASA classification alone. Such absolute numbers would complement the AUROC and AUPRC values and help clinicians judge how often the MT-GBM model would change decisions in day-to-day practice for these rare outcomes.

Third, the multicentre design and use of distinct validation cohorts with differing ASA distributions and emergency surgery rates are major strengths1. From the perspective of deployment, however, the details of calibration in each external cohort are important. The authors state that calibration curves and metrics are provided in the Supplementary material and highlight the need for further validation in other jurisdictions1. In line with current guidance for prediction model reporting and implementation5, it would be informative to know whether any systematic miscalibration was observed in the external cohorts—for example, consistent overprediction in higher-risk strata or underprediction in lower-risk strata—and whether simple recalibration strategies, such as intercept and slope adjustment, would be required before local implementation in hospitals with different baseline risks or surgical case mixes. Explicit reporting and brief discussion of calibration slopes, intercepts and Brier scores for each external cohort, together with practical advice on whether ‘local recalibration’ is advisable, would further assist institutions in considering adoption of the MT-GBM model.

In summary, we regard the study by Yoon et al. as an important contribution toward scalable, multitask prediction of postoperative complications via the use of routinely collected perioperative data. Clarifying the clinical decision context and threshold ranges of decision curve analyses, quantifying absolute gains for rare outcomes, and elaborating on calibration and potential recalibration in external settings could further strengthen the interpretability and real-world applicability of this model in perioperative care.
