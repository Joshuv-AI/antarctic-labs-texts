t-Product and t-STP of Cubic Matrices with an Application to Hyper-Networked Systems
Daizhan Cheng, Zhengping Ji

Abstract
Control systems with tensor-valued state transitions require a product that specifies both how coefficients act on each frontal slice and how different slices interact. This paper develops a t-semi-tensor product (t-STP) on cubic matrices that retains the circular coupling of the t-product while allowing rectangular coefficient slices to act on a fixed state space. The construction combines the dimension-keeping semi-tensor product (DK-STP) bridge with circular convolution, overcoming the absence of cross-slice coupling in a slice-wise DK-STP. It provides a compact coefficient description of a structured class of dynamical operators, with fewer stored entries when the coefficient slices have fewer columns than rows. For a fixed number of frontal slices, we establish associative algebra and module structures and describe the associated Lie algebra and Lie groups. These structures make coefficient composition and exponential evolution consistent, while equivalent classical matrix realizations connect the tensor formulation to control analysis of cubic matrix-based dynamics. A specified supply-network game illustrates how the construction organizes interacting chain flows, reproduces the classical trajectories, and supports a globally convergent payoff-gradient adjustment law with explicit damping. The example quantifies coefficient storage while clarifying that the state dimension is unchanged and that the same economy is available to a classical implementation retaining the factorization.

Introduction

With recent advances in data science and computational methods, the analysis of higher-order data structures, particularly multiway arrays or hypermatrices, has attracted considerable attention because of their applications in signal processing
[
27
]
, computer vision
[
33
]
, and neuroscience
[
2
]
. Once a basis is fixed, a tensor can be represented by its components in a hypermatrix
[
4
]
. A real hypermatrix of order
d
is an array
A=(a_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}})
, where
i_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,n_{k}\}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. Equivalently, it is a map from the product of these index sets to
\mathbb{R}
. We denote the space of such arrays by
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
and refer to
[
29
]
for a general introduction.

Among higher-order arrays, those of order three provide a direct extension of matrices and occur frequently in data analysis
[
23
]
. In this paper, we call them
cubic matrices
; their three dimensions need not be equal. We consider arrays

A=(a_{i,j,k}),\qquad i\in\{1,\ldots,m\},\quad j\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\quad k\in\{1,\ldots,s\},

(1)

where
m,n,s
are positive integers. The space
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
is the main setting of our study.

Several product operators can be defined on cubic matrices, and by iteratively applying the product, one can derive dynamics on cubic matrix sets. The choice of product determines which interactions the resulting algebra and dynamical model can represent. For control applications when the state transition law is stored in data of multiway arrays, it is useful to have a product that admits a clear matrix representation, supports repeated multiplication, and accommodates the dimensions of the coefficient and state arrays. These considerations set the basis for comparing the products and their associated dynamical systems below.

The t-product developed by Kilmer and collaborators
[
23
,
24
]
provides an effective framework for operations on cubic matrices. It represents tensor multiplication through a block-circulant matrix and permits structured computations while retaining the organization of the data. Related t-product methods have been used in imaging and tensor approximation
[
1
,
24
]
, and tensor functions have also been developed within this framework
[
30
]
. Kernfeld et al.
[
21
]
generalize tensor products by replacing the Fourier transform with an arbitrary invertible linear transform. Within such tensor-product algebras, truncated tensor singular value decompositions also provide optimal approximation results and economical representations of multiway data
[
25
]
. These developments show why the choice of multiplication matters for both analysis and representation.

In particular, t-product-based dynamical and control systems have been studied as structured models with tensor-valued states
[
17
,
31
]
. Their matrix representations connect tensor operations with familiar tools from linear systems theory. For order-three tensors, a dynamical system over cubic matrices collects several sets of state variables in a single array. Each frontal rectangular slice may represent a subsystem, while multiplication describes interactions among these subsystems without discarding their organization in the state representation. For example, different slices may collect real-valued variables associated with different markets or interacting parts of a network. The product used in the model then specifies how the slices influence one another.

Tensor methods and hypermatrix constructions have been applied to biological systems
[
34
]
, image processing
[
3
]
, and control
[
6
,
31
]
. These applications involve different tensor products and modeling assumptions; for example, the multilinear systems in
[
6
]
use the Einstein product. A broader introduction to tensor-based dynamical systems and the products used in their construction can be found in
[
7
]
.

Despite these advantages, the t-product requires matching inner dimensions and equal numbers of frontal slices
[
24
]
. In particular, a left-acting autonomous t-product system uses square frontal slices in its coefficient tensor, although its state slices may be rectangular. The question addressed here is how to retain this slice coupling while describing the state operator by rectangular coefficient slices. In this paper, we propose a construction that separates two modeling choices: a dimension-dependent bridge handles the inner dimensions, and circular convolution specifies the coupling between slices. Whereas the transform-based generalization in
[
21
]
changes the transform used to define multiplication, our construction retains circular coupling and modifies the multiplication between coefficient and state slices. We study the resulting coefficient factorization and the algebraic conditions that make its use consistent in products, trajectories, and compositions. Comparing the constructions also clarifies which interaction patterns each can represent.

To generalize the t-product and relax the inner-dimension requirement, we first extend the dimension-keeping semi-tensor product (DK-STP) of matrices to cubic matrices and examine the resulting control systems. The DK-STP is a variation of the classical semi-tensor product (STP) of matrices. The STP generalizes conventional matrix multiplication to factors with arbitrary inner dimensions
[
12
]
, with engineering applications surveyed in
[
28
]
, and has been used to analyze Boolean and finite-valued networks
[
11
,
19
]
, finite games
[
13
,
9
,
20
]
, and dimension-varying systems
[
10
]
. When dimensions do not match, however, the classical STP can enlarge the resulting arrays. The DK-STP was introduced to retain the outer dimensions of the product
[
8
]
. For matrices
A
and
B
, it has the form
A\Psi B
, where the bridge matrix
\Psi
is determined by their inner dimensions. This provides a prescribed way to combine factors whose dimensions are incompatible under ordinary multiplication. Bridge matrices also arise in observer-based realizations of control systems
[
15
]
; here their role is to prescribe a coefficient action while keeping the chosen tensor state space fixed.

One motivation for this DK-STP-based construction is to reduce the number of coefficients used to describe a structured dynamical operator. For a state
X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times q\times s}
, a t-product coefficient tensor has
m^{2}s
entries, whereas a rectangular coefficient tensor
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times s}
has
mps
entries. When
p<m
, the latter description is smaller. This saving does not reduce the
mqs
state variables or permit every linear operator on that state space to be represented with fewer independent parameters. Rather, it results from imposing a bridge factorization and a specified coupling structure. The construction is therefore relevant when these restrictions express an acceptable interaction model; in that case, the smaller coefficient array defines the corresponding operator exactly. This differs from choosing Tucker factors
[
26
]
or a truncated tensor singular value decomposition
[
25
]
to approximate a given data tensor: the bridge fixes a coordinate-aggregation rule before coefficient fitting. We make this distinction explicit and compare equally structured tensor and classical representations in the numerical example.

The slice-wise DK-STP construction, however, has a limitation for coupled models: each frontal-slice subsystem evolves independently of the other state slices for prescribed inputs. To combine rectangular coefficient slices with interactions between state slices, we introduce the t-semi-tensor product (t-STP), which combines the DK-STP bridge with the circular coupling of the t-product. For a fixed number of frontal slices, this product is associative and reduces to the t-product when the inner dimensions match. It therefore provides an algebraic framework for a class of coupled linear and nonlinear control systems over cubic matrices. At the level of the linear state action, the t-STP is a rectangular coefficient factorization of a structured t-product operator; its purpose is to make this factorization part of a consistent product and dynamical formulation.

Our proposed product also induces algebraic structures on cubic matrices. We study the associated algebra and module structures, the Lie algebra defined by the product commutator, and the Lie group obtained by adjoining an identity and considering invertible elements with scalar coordinate one. Their matrix representations clarify how the tensor constructions relate to conventional algebra and which properties can be transferred between the two settings. The link is the standard passage from associative multiplication to commutators and groups of units
[
16
]
. Its role here is to make explicit how rectangular tensor coefficients compose, how an identity is supplied when no numerical two-sided identity exists, and how coefficient exponentials act on the state. These results connect the product construction to the evolution operators used in the control systems.

From an application perspective, hypermatrices provide a natural way to organize the multiway interactions of a hypergraph
[
32
]
. Tensor-based hypergraph controllability has also been studied through polynomial dynamics on vertex states
[
5
]
. Here the state variables are instead assigned to supply chains, so the hypergraph organizes the decisions while the chosen product specifies their interaction
[
14
]
. A supply network involving manufacturers, wholesalers, and markets offers a simple example, with one scalar state associated with each supply chain. We use this setting to construct a synthetic game in which the 24 chains are the players and their flows evolve by payoff-gradient adjustment with explicit damping. This example shows how the proposed coefficient description can be used in a concrete coupled model: we specify the payoffs, compare tensor and classical trajectories and coefficient storage, and establish convergence to the unique Nash equilibrium.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

(i)

We define a t-STP for cubic matrices with a fixed number of frontal slices, combining a bridge between incompatible inner dimensions with circular coupling between slices. This gives rectangular coefficient arrays a consistent action on a fixed tensor state space, while retaining the cross-slice interactions absent from the slice-wise DK-STP. The construction brings these two features into one associative product and identifies the restricted class of t-product operators that it represents.

(ii)

We establish the corresponding algebra and module structures and describe the associated Lie algebra and Lie group, with explicit attention to the adjoined identity and a faithful matrix representation. These results explain how rectangular coefficients can be composed, exponentiated, and related to invertible evolution operators despite the possible absence of a numerical identity. Their significance is to supply a rigorous link between the proposed product and its dynamical use, through explicit realizations of standard algebraic constructions.

(iii)

We formulate the resulting control systems, derive their equivalent classical realizations, and give a numerical supply-network game illustrating the coefficient-storage advantage and the restrictions imposed by the representation. The realizations make standard matrix analysis available and distinguish a smaller coefficient description from a reduction in state dimension. Explicit coefficients, payoffs, and convergent trajectories demonstrate the construction in a reproducible model; comparison with an equally factorized classical implementation clarifies the scope of the storage benefit.

2
Preliminaries

Two ingredients will be used in our construction of the product of cubic matrices: the t-product describes circular interactions between frontal slices, while the matrix DK-STP handles incompatible inner dimensions. We first recall the t-product and its matrix representation, then introduce the bridge and formal identity needed for rectangular coefficients. This order separates the coupling rule from the dimension rule before combining them in Section
5
.

2.1
t-Product of Cubic Matrices

For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
, the
k
-th frontal slice is the matrix
A^{(k)}=(a_{i,j,k})_{i,j}\in\mathcal{M}_{m\times n}
. Fixing the first or second index similarly gives a horizontal or lateral slice. We work with frontal slices in a fixed order and define

A_{u}=\unfold(A)=\begin{bmatrix}A^{(1)}\\
A^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
A^{(s)}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{M}_{sm\times n}.

(2)

The inverse map is denoted by
\fold
; its target dimensions are determined by context. Unfolding is a linear bijection and changes only the arrangement of entries. It is distinct from
\Gamma(A)
, which has size
sm\times sn
and repeats the slices as blocks. A general higher-order array can also be matricized by partitioning its ordered indices into row and column indices and using lexicographic order within each group, but no such additional grouping is needed here.

Definition 1

(t-product,
[
24
]
)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p\times s}
. Define

\Gamma(A)=\bcirc(A)=\begin{bmatrix}A^{(1)}&A^{(s)}&\cdots&A^{(2)}\\
A^{(2)}&A^{(1)}&\cdots&A^{(3)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A^{(s)}&A^{(s-1)}&\cdots&A^{(1)}\end{bmatrix},

(3)

and

A*B=\fold\bigl(\Gamma(A)\unfold(B)\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times s}.

(4)

For
s=1
,
\Gamma(A)=A^{(1)}
. When
m=n=1
,
\Gamma(A)
is the usual circulant matrix of the vector of entries.

Definition 2

(Slice-wise transpose)
.

The slice-wise transpose
A^{T_{f}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times s}
is defined by
(A^{T_{f}})^{(k)}=(A^{(k)})^{\mathsf{T}}
. More generally, permuting tensor modes permutes their index ranges along with their positions. We distinguish
T_{f}
from the usual t-product transpose, which also reverses the order of slices
2,\ldots,s

[
24
]
; no transpose convention is needed for the algebraic results below.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times s}
have first slice
I_{n}
and all other slices zero. A tensor
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times s}
is invertible under the t-product if there is
B
in the same space such that
A*B=B*A=\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}
.

Directly from the definition,
\mathcal{I}_{m}^{s}*A=A=A*\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}
for
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
. The equivalence between tensor and matrix invertibility is proved in Proposition
4
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
. Its Frobenius norm is

\|A\|_{F}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{s}a_{i,j,k}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(5)

2.2
Properties of the t-Product

We recall the t-product properties needed later and include proofs to specify the conventions. Associativity makes repeated products unambiguous, the module action connects coefficients to states, and the map
\Gamma
transfers these operations to ordinary matrices. These are the properties used in the dynamical constructions below. Addition and scalar multiplication are componentwise, so
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
is a real vector space of dimension
mns
, and its additive group is Abelian.

For an integer
r
, let
\langle r\rangle_{s}
be the unique integer congruent to
r
modulo
s
in
\{0,\ldots,s-1\}
. In convolution proofs only, write
\widehat{A}_{r}=A^{(r+1)}
for
r=0,\ldots,s-1
. Definition
1
gives

\widehat{(A*B)}_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\widehat{A}_{\langle k-j\rangle_{s}}\widehat{B}_{j}.

(6)

This convention uses zero-based indices only to simplify modular sums; the original slice notation remains one-based.

Proposition 1
.

The t-product is bilinear and associative whenever the inner matrix dimensions match and all factors have the same number of frontal slices.

Proof.

Bilinearity follows from (
6
). For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p\times s}
, and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q\times s}
, the
k
-th zero-based slice of
(A*B)*C
is

\sum_{\ell=0}^{s-1}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\widehat{A}_{\langle k-\ell-j\rangle_{s}}\widehat{B}_{j}\widehat{C}_{\ell}.

Expanding
A*(B*C)
and substituting
j=\langle r-\ell\rangle_{s}
gives the same sum. Ordinary matrix associativity completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2
.

The space
V=\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times s}
is a unital associative real algebra under the t-product, with identity
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}
. Its units form a group. The space
\mathbb{R}^{n\times q\times s}
is a unital left
V
-module under t-product multiplication.

Proof.

Proposition
1
proves associativity and distributivity, and Definition
3
provides the identity. Products of units are units because
(A*B)^{-1}=B^{-1}*A^{-1}
; inverses and the identity belong to the same space. For the module, bilinearity gives the two distributive laws, associativity gives
A*(B*X)=(A*B)*X
, and
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}*X=X
supplies the unit-action law.
∎

Proposition 3
.

The map
\Gamma
is linear and injective. For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p\times s}
,

\Gamma(A*B)=\Gamma(A)\Gamma(B).

(7)

Proof.

Linearity follows blockwise, and the first block column recovers
\unfold(A)
, proving injectivity. With zero-based block indices, the
(i,j)
block of the product on the right is

\sum_{r=0}^{s-1}\widehat{A}_{\langle i-r\rangle_{s}}\widehat{B}_{\langle r-j\rangle_{s}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{s-1}\widehat{A}_{\langle i-j-\ell\rangle_{s}}\widehat{B}_{\ell}=\widehat{(A*B)}_{\langle i-j\rangle_{s}}.

These are exactly the blocks on the left.
∎

Proposition 4
.

On
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times s}
,
\Gamma
is a unital algebra homomorphism into
\mathcal{M}_{ns\times ns}
and an isomorphism onto its block-circulant image. A tensor
A
is a unit if and only if
\Gamma(A)
is invertible, in which case

\Gamma(A^{-1})=\Gamma(A)^{-1}.

(8)

Consequently, its restriction to the group of units is a group homomorphism.

Proof.

Proposition
3
proves linearity, injectivity, and multiplicativity, and
\Gamma(\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s})=I_{ns}
. If
A*B=B*A=\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}
, their images are inverse matrices. Conversely, the inverse of an invertible matrix is a polynomial in that matrix by the classical Cayley–Hamilton theorem. Hence
\Gamma(A)^{-1}
belongs to the unital algebra
\Gamma(V)
; write it as
\Gamma(B)
. Multiplicativity and injectivity give
A*B=B*A=\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}
.
∎

Remark 5
.

Thus
\Gamma
is a faithful representation, not a map onto the entire matrix algebra. We use its inverse only on its image. This precise statement supplies the algebra, ring, monoid, and group correspondences without requiring a separate universal property.

2.3
The DK-STP

The t-product above couples slices but still requires compatible inner matrix dimensions. This subsection reviews the matrix DK-STP
[
8
]
, which supplies the bridge used to relax that requirement. Bridge matrices also arise in observer-based realizations
[
15
]
; here they are used to prescribe a tensor coefficient action on a fixed state space.

Definition 4
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{m\times n}
,
B\in\mathcal{M}_{p\times q}
, and
\ell=\lcm(n,p)
. Their DK-STP is

A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B=(A\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\ell/n}^{\mathsf{T}})(B\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\ell/p})\in\mathcal{M}_{m\times q}.

(9)

Proposition 6
.

The bridge representation is

A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B=A\Psi_{n\times p}B,\qquad\Psi_{n\times p}=(I_{n}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\ell/n}^{\mathsf{T}})(I_{p}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\ell/p}).

(10)

The product is bilinear and associative for arbitrary matrix sizes, and agrees with ordinary multiplication when
n=p
.

Proof.

Factor the first replicated matrix as
A(I_{n}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\ell/n}^{\mathsf{T}})
and the second as
(I_{p}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\ell/p})B
. This gives (
10
). Bilinearity follows. If
C\in\mathcal{M}_{u\times v}
, both associations of the triple product equal
A\Psi_{n\times p}B\Psi_{q\times u}C
. Finally,
\Psi_{n\times n}=I_{n}
.
∎

Remark 7
.

The bridge is fixed by the two inner dimensions, not fitted separately to the factors. Its unnormalized all-ones convention is used throughout this paper. It specifies how coordinates are aggregated and replicated; choosing this product is therefore a modeling assumption. For example,

\Psi_{2\times 3}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&0\\
0&1&2\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

The bridge permits multiplication of rectangular coefficients of a fixed shape, but such an algebra need not contain an identity. An identity will be needed for the constant term of the exponential and for invertible compositions. We therefore state explicitly how to adjoin one.

Proposition 8
.

The fixed-shape space
V=\mathcal{M}_{m\times n}
, with ordinary addition and scalar multiplication and product
A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B=A\Psi_{n\times m}B
, is a finite-dimensional associative real algebra. If
m\neq n
, it has no numerical two-sided identity. For any associative real algebra
V
, the vector space
\widetilde{V}=\mathbb{R}\oplus V
becomes a unital associative algebra under

(\alpha,A)(\beta,B)=(\alpha\beta,\alpha B+\beta A+AB),\qquad\mathsf{e}=(1,0),

(12)

where
AB
denotes the product of
V
in this formula.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from Proposition
6
. A numerical identity
E
would satisfy
E\Psi_{n\times m}=I_{m}
and
\Psi_{n\times m}E=I_{n}
, since the corresponding multiplication must fix every matrix of
V
. Rank inequalities imply both
m\leq n
and
n\leq m
. For the unitization, distributivity is immediate. The
V
component of either association of
(\alpha,A)(\beta,B)(\gamma,C)
is

\alpha\beta C+\alpha\gamma B+\beta\gamma A+\alpha BC+\beta AC+\gamma AB+(AB)C,

where
(AB)C=A(BC)
. The scalar component is
\alpha\beta\gamma
, and
(1,0)
is a two-sided identity.
∎

We identify
A\in V
with
(0,A)
and write
\alpha\mathsf{e}+A
for
(\alpha,A)
. The dimension of
\widetilde{V}
is
1+\dim V
, even when
V
already has a native identity. In that case the formal and native identities remain distinct unless an explicit quotient or identification is stated. Invertibility in
\widetilde{V}
means existence of a two-sided inverse in
\widetilde{V}
. Section
6
constructs the associated group and Lie algebra, avoiding any use of an undefined rectangular numerical identity.

3
Dynamical Systems over Cubic Matrices

We first use the t-product-based dynamical system as a reference for the two constructions that follow in later sections. An action of a monoid
G
on a set
V
is a map
\pi:G\times V\to V
satisfying
\pi(ab,x)=\pi(a,\pi(b,x))
and
\pi(e,x)=x
. Such an action is also called an
S
-system. When
V
is a topological space and each map
x\mapsto\pi(a,x)
is continuous, it is an action by continuous maps. A continuous-time autonomous system additionally specifies a time-indexed semigroup of such maps. Here the exponential map supplies this semigroup, and inputs give a controlled evolution. This viewpoint connects the algebra of Section
2
to trajectories, as powers of the coefficient define its exponential, whose action propagates the initial state.

3.1
Analytic Functions of Cubic Matrices

The main function needed for linear dynamics is the exponential. We introduce polynomial and analytic functions through
\Gamma
, following the t-function framework of
[
30
]
; the accompanying polynomial and spectral identities show precisely which ordinary matrix operations are being transferred.

For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times s}
, define

A_{*}^{[0]}=\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s},\qquad A_{*}^{[k]}=\underbrace{A*\cdots*A}_{k\text{ factors}},\quad k\geq 1.

(13)

For a scalar polynomial
p(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}z^{k}
, put
p_{*}(A)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}A_{*}^{[k]}
.

Lemma 9
.

For every such polynomial,
\Gamma(p_{*}(A))=p(\Gamma(A))
. In particular,
p(\Gamma(A))=0
implies
p_{*}(A)=0
.

Proof.

Apply Proposition
4
to each power, including the constant term. The implication follows from injectivity.
∎

Theorem 10

(Cayley–Hamilton identity)
.

If
p
is the characteristic polynomial of
\Gamma(A)
, then
p_{*}(A)=0
.

Proof.

The matrix Cayley–Hamilton theorem gives
p(\Gamma(A))=0
; apply Lemma
9
.
∎

Definition 5
.

A scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is a scalar t-eigenvalue of
A
if there is a nonzero
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1\times s}
such that
A*x=\lambda x
. The product and unfolding are extended complex-linearly here.

Corollary 11
.

The scalar t-eigenvalues of
A
are exactly the eigenvalues of
\Gamma(A)
, with eigenvectors related by
x\mapsto\unfold(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{ns}
.

Proof.

Unfolding the defining equation gives
\Gamma(A)\unfold(x)=\lambda\unfold(x)
. Unfolding is bijective, so the implication also holds in reverse.
∎

This statement concerns the scalar spectral notion just defined; it is not an identification of all tensor eigenvalue conventions.

Definition 6
.

Let
f
have a scalar Taylor series at zero with radius of convergence
r>0
:

f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}z^{k},\qquad|z|<r.

(14)

For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times s}
with
\|\Gamma(A)\|_{2}<r
, define

f_{*}(A)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}A_{*}^{[k]}.

(15)

Real Taylor coefficients give a real tensor; otherwise the target is complexified.

The matrix series converges absolutely in spectral norm. Its partial sums lie in the finite-dimensional, hence closed, image of
\Gamma
. Therefore (
15

\Gamma(f_{*}(A))=f(\Gamma(A)).

(16)

The sufficient condition
\sqrt{s}\|A\|_{F}<r
can also be used, since
\|\Gamma(A)\|_{2}\leq\|\Gamma(A)\|_{F}=\sqrt{s}\|A\|_{F}
. The condition
\|A\|_{F}<r
alone is not sufficient: the two-slice scalar tensor
(0.6,0.6)
has Frobenius norm less than one, while its circulant matrix has eigenvalue
1.2
.

Example 12
.

The functions

\exp_{*}(A)=\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{*}^{[k]}}{k!},\qquad\sin_{*}(A)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}A_{*}^{[2k+1]}}{(2k+1)!}

.

3.2
t-Product-Based Control Systems

With the exponential defined, the transition from tensor multiplication to a state equation is direct. The following formulation recalls t-product systems of the type studied in
[
17
,
31
]
and makes their classical realization explicit. It provides the baseline for identifying what changes when rectangular coefficient slices are introduced.

Consider the continuous-time system

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t)=A*x(t)+B*u(t),\\
y(t)=C*x(t),\end{cases}

(17)

where
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q\times s}
,
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q\times s}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times s}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p\times s}
, and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n\times s}
. Then
y(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times q\times s}
. Assume the input is locally integrable. The system has a unique absolutely continuous state trajectory on every finite time interval.

Proposition 13
.

The solution with initial state
x(0)
is

x(t)=\exp_{*}(tA)*x(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\exp_{*}((t-\tau)A)*B*u(\tau)\,d\tau.

(18)

With
X=\unfold(x)
,
U=\unfold(u)
, and
Y=\unfold(y)
, the equivalent matrix system is

\dot{X}=\Gamma(A)X+\Gamma(B)U,\qquad Y=\Gamma(C)X.

(19)

Proof.

Definition
1
gives (
19
). The ordinary variation-of-constants formula and (
16
) give (
18
) after folding. Unfolding is a linear bijection, proving existence, uniqueness, and equivalence. The integral is taken entrywise.
∎

Remark 14
.

The state slices in (
17
) may be rectangular. It is the coefficient of its autonomous left action that has square slices. The unfolded state has
nsq
scalar entries; a complete vectorization yields the state matrix
I_{q}\otimes\Gamma(A)
.

One may also apply
\Gamma
to the state, obtaining a larger block-circulant matrix-valued realization. That realization is equivalent only when its initial state and inputs are restricted to the corresponding images of
\Gamma
. The unfolding in (
19
) avoids this redundant enlargement. Classical results transfer through this bijection when their admissible input and output structures are preserved. Computational savings require an implementation that exploits the structure, rather than following from a change of notation alone.

4
Dynamical Systems over Cubic Matrices via the DK-STP

We now relax the inner-dimension restriction while keeping the slices separate. Applying the matrix DK-STP to corresponding slices gives the simplest tensor extension and shows what the bridge accomplishes on its own. Its lack of cross-slice state coupling will then motivate the t-STP in Section
5
.

4.1
Kronecker Product and DK-STP of Cubic Matrices

The Kronecker convention below fixes the ordering of slices, including the explicit repetition lifts discussed later. The slice-wise DK-STP itself is defined first for a common depth.

Definition 7
.

For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q\times t}
, the tensor Kronecker product
A\otimes B\in\mathbb{R}^{mp\times nq\times st}
is specified by

(A\otimes B)_{(i-1)p+i^{\prime},\,(j-1)q+j^{\prime},\,(k-1)t+k^{\prime}}=a_{i,j,k}b_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}.

In particular, its frontal slice numbered
(k-1)t+k^{\prime}
is
A^{(k)}\otimes B^{(k^{\prime})}
.

Definition 8
.

For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q\times s}
, the slice-wise DK-STP is

(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B)^{(k)}=A^{(k)}\Psi_{n\times p}B^{(k)},\quad k=1,\ldots,s.

(20)

It belongs to
\mathbb{R}^{m\times q\times s}
.

Example 15
.

Let

\displaystyle A^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle A^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle B^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle B^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&2\\
1&-2\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}.

Using (

(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B)^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&4\\

4&0\end{bmatrix}.

(21)

Thus a

coefficient slice can act on a

Proposition 16
.

The slice-wise DK-STP is bilinear and associative for any three factors with common depth
s
. For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q\times t}
,

, and

(A\otimes B)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,(C\otimes D)=(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,C)\otimes(B\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,D).

(22)

Proof.

Bilinearity and associativity hold in every slice by Proposition

. In (

), the matrix dimensions match in each relevant slice, so the

slice on the left is

(A^{(i)}\otimes B^{(j)})(C^{(i)}\otimes D^{(j)})=(A^{(i)}C^{(i)})\otimes(B^{(j)}D^{(j)}).

This is the corresponding slice on the right.
∎

Remark 17

(Explicit lifts and their scope)
.

For a multiple
\theta
of
s
, define the consecutive repetition lift

by

(\mathcal{R}_{s}^{\theta}A)^{(k)}=A^{(1+\lfloor(k-1)/(\theta/s)\rfloor)},\quad k=1,\ldots,\theta.

Thus a depth-three tensor lifts to depth six as
(A^{(1)},A^{(1)},A^{(2)},A^{(2)},A^{(3)},A^{(3)})
. Under Definition

, this is

, with the all-ones factor on the right. One can define a binary slice-wise product of unequal-depth arrays by first lifting both to their least common multiple. The systems and algebraic results below use a fixed depth; in particular, no lifting rule is implicit in a power or differential equation.

Example 18
.

For the slice-wise lifted product, let

have three slices

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-1&2\\

\displaystyle C^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}5&2\\

\displaystyle C^{(4)}

\displaystyle C^{(5)}=C^{(6)}

For fixed

, the space

is an associative real algebra under the slice-wise DK-STP. Because multiplication is independent in each slice, invertibility can likewise be considered slice by slice. To describe the corresponding shifted units, let
U=\mathcal{M}_{m\times n}
with matrix DK-STP and form the product algebra
(\widetilde{U})^{s}
. Write

for its identity.

Definition 9
.

An element
J+A_{0}
,
A_{0}\in V_{D}
, is invertible if there exists
B_{0}\in V_{D}
such that, in
(\widetilde{U})^{s}
,

(J+A_{0})(J+B_{0})=(J+B_{0})(J+A_{0})=J.

(23)

Here

is a tuple of formal units, not an ordinary tensor of rectangular identity matrices. Invertibility is asserted for
J+A_{0}
, not for
A_{0}
alone.

Proposition 19
.

The invertible elements of the form
J+A_{0}
form a group, the direct product of
s
copies of the group of units of the form
\mathsf{e}_{U}+A^{(k)}
in
\widetilde{U}
.

Proof.

Multiplication in
(\widetilde{U})^{s}
is componentwise. Products retain scalar coordinate one in each component. The scalar coordinate of an inverse must also be one, so inverses have the stated form. Associativity and the identity come from the product algebra. These observations prove both the group property and the direct-product description.
∎

4.2
DK-STP-Based Control Systems

The coefficient and state tensors can have different column dimensions. The module statement below ensures that a coefficient still acts on the chosen state space and that successive coefficient actions compose consistently. We then write the slice equations to make the resulting coupling pattern visible.

Proposition 20
.

Let
R_{D}=(\mathbb{R}^{n\times p\times s},+,\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,)
and
V=\mathbb{R}^{n\times q\times s}
. The action
(A,X)\mapsto A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,X
makes
V
a left module over the algebra
R_{D}
.

Proof.

The action is bilinear. For each slice, both
A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,(B\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,X)
and
(A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,B)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,X
equal
A^{(i)}\Psi_{p\times n}B^{(i)}\Psi_{p\times n}X^{(i)}
. Thus the associative action law holds. The action extends to a unital action of the formal unitization by
(\alpha\mathsf{e}+A)\cdot X=\alpha X+A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,X
.
∎

Consider

\dot{x}=A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,x+\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}B_{k}u_{k}(t),\qquad y=C\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,x,

(24)

where
x,B_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q\times s}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p\times s}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n\times s}
, and
u_{k}
are scalar, locally integrable inputs. For each slice
i=1,\ldots,s
, set
H_{i}=A^{(i)}\Psi_{p\times n}
. The component form and its solution are

\dot{x}^{(i)}=H_{i}x^{(i)}+\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}B_{k}^{(i)}u_{k}(t),\qquad y^{(i)}=C^{(i)}x^{(i)},

(25)

x^{(i)}(t)=e^{tH_{i}}x^{(i)}(0)+\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-\tau)H_{i}}B_{k}^{(i)}u_{k}(\tau)\,d\tau.

(26)

The ordinary matrix variation-of-constants formula proves this expression; in particular, the first term has the required initial value.

Nonlinear systems can also be built on the fixed state space
V
:

\dot{x}=F_{D}(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}G_{D,k}(x)u_{k}(t),\qquad y=h_{D}(x).

(27)

For example, use ordinary constant tensors in
V
and positive-degree DK-STP polynomials in
x
for
F_{D},G_{D,k}
. Convergent power series are allowed on domains where the series and their derivatives converge. These maps take values in
V
, whereas a nonzero scalar constant times a formal unit generally does not. The output map
h_{D}
is defined separately with its stated target space. We assume smooth maps and, for local trajectory statements, piecewise continuous inputs.

For this slice-wise polynomial construction, slice
i
of each vector field depends only on
x^{(i)}
. Thus prescribed inputs give no cross-slice state coupling. An arbitrary smooth map on
V
, or feedback that depends on several slices, need not have this property; the decoupling statement concerns this specific construction. It is appropriate for independent subsystems. We next combine the bridge with circular convolution to introduce cross-slice interactions directly in the product.

5
The t-STP and t-STP-Based Systems

The preceding two types of systems provide complementary ingredients: the t-product couples frontal slices, whereas the slice-wise DK-STP permits rectangular coefficient slices. We combine them by inserting the same matrix bridge into every term of the t-product convolution. This gives a common product for the coupled state equation and for repeated coefficient multiplication.

5.1
The t-STP

In the unfolding,
I_{s}\otimes\Psi_{n\times p}
first maps each slice of the right factor to compatible row coordinates, and
\Gamma(A)
then mixes the slices by circular convolution. Folding restores the tensor shape without changing its entries.

Definition 10
.

For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q\times s}
, define

A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}B=\fold\!\left(\Gamma(A)(I_{s}\otimes\Psi_{n\times p})\unfold(B)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times q\times s}.

(28)

Equivalently, in the zero-based notation of (
6
),

\widehat{(A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}B)}_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\widehat{A}_{\langle k-j\rangle_{s}}\Psi_{n\times p}\widehat{B}_{j}.

(29)

Since
\Psi_{sn\times sp}=I_{s}\otimes\Psi_{n\times p}
, (
28
) can also be written as
\fold(\Gamma(A)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,\unfold(B))
. The equality of bridges follows by substituting
\lcm(sn,sp)=s\lcm(n,p)
in (
10
). If
n=p
, this is the t-product. The depth
s
is fixed throughout the following algebraic constructions.

Example 21
.

For the tensors of Example
15
, the t-STP has slices

\displaystyle(A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}B)^{(1)}

\displaystyle=A^{(1)}\Psi B^{(1)}+A^{(2)}\Psi B^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\
6&0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle(A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}B)^{(2)}

\displaystyle=A^{(2)}\Psi B^{(1)}+A^{(1)}\Psi B^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\
-2&8\end{bmatrix},

where
\Psi=\Psi_{2\times 3}
. The bridge handles the inner-dimension mismatch, and the off-diagonal terms introduce the interaction between slices.

To use this binary construction in a dynamical system, repeated products must have a well-defined meaning. The next result shows that the bridges remain compatible under reassociation when the depth is fixed. It is the common basis for coefficient powers, module actions, and the Lie commutator studied later.

Proposition 22

(Fixed-depth associativity)
.

For each fixed
s
, the set
\bigcup_{m,n\geq 1}\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
is a semigroup under the t-STP. The product is bilinear on any two fixed-shape factors.

Proof.

Closure follows from Definition
10
. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q\times s}
, and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{u\times v\times s}
. Expanding
((A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}B)\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}C)^{(k+1)}
gives

\sum_{\ell=0}^{s-1}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\widehat{A}_{\langle k-\ell-j\rangle_{s}}\Psi_{n\times p}\widehat{B}_{j}\Psi_{q\times u}\widehat{C}_{\ell}.

Expanding
A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}(B\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}C)
and reindexing as in Proposition
1
gives the same expression. Matrix associativity proves equality. Bilinearity follows from the same finite-sum formula.
∎

Remark 23

(Why depth is fixed)
.

An unequal-depth binary extension can be defined by lifting the factors with
\mathcal{R}_{s}^{\theta}
and then applying (
28
). It does not give an associative product on all depths. Indeed, for scalar-slice tensors
a=(1)
,
b=(1)
,
c=(1,1)
, this extension, denoted here by
\diamond
, gives

(a\diamond b)\diamond c=(2,2),\qquad a\diamond(b\diamond c)=(4,4).

Both statements follow from unnormalized circular convolution at depth two. Therefore Proposition
22
does not assert a semigroup over arbitrary third-mode sizes. A different lifting rule would require a separate compatibility and associativity proof.

Corollary 24
.

For fixed
n,p,s
,
R_{*}=\mathbb{R}^{n\times p\times s}
is an associative real algebra under the t-STP. For each
q
,
V=\mathbb{R}^{n\times q\times s}
is a left
R_{*}
-module with action
\pi:R_{*}\times V\to V
,
\pi(A,X)=A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}X
.

Proof.

The product preserves the shape of
R_{*}
and the action preserves the shape of
V
. Bilinearity supplies the additive laws; Proposition
22
supplies both algebra associativity and the associative action law. As for any such module, the unitized action is
(\alpha\mathsf{e}+A)\cdot X=\alpha X+A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}X
.
∎

5.2
t-STP-Based Control Systems

We now keep the dimensions and scalar input channels of (
24
) and replace only the slice-wise product by the t-STP. This isolates the effect of introducing circular state coupling. The matrix
H_{A}
below then makes both the resulting trajectories and the coefficient restrictions accessible to classical linear-systems analysis:

\dot{x}=A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}x+\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}B_{k}u_{k}(t),\qquad y=C\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}x.

(30)

Thus
x,B_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q\times s}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p\times s}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n\times s}
, and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times q\times s}
. Let

H_{A}=\Gamma(A)(I_{s}\otimes\Psi_{p\times n})\in\mathcal{M}_{ns\times ns}.

(31)

Proposition 25
.

For locally integrable inputs, the unique trajectory of (
30

,

, and

\dot{X}=H_{A}X+\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}B_{k,u}u_{k}(t),\qquad Y=\Gamma(C)X,

(32)

and

X(t)=e^{tH_{A}}X(0)+\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-\tau)H_{A}}B_{k,u}u_{k}(\tau)\,d\tau.

(33)

Proof.

Unfold (
30

\Psi_{n\times n}=I_{n}
, giving

. The standard matrix solution gives (

∎

The matrix solution already determines the trajectory. To express the same propagation intrinsically through the coefficient algebra, form the unitization
\widetilde{R}_{*}
. Define
A_{\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}}^{[0]}=\mathsf{e}
and
A_{\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}}^{[k]}
by repeated t-STP for

. Choose a submultiplicative norm on this finite-dimensional unital algebra, for example the operator norm of its faithful left regular representation. If

has scalar Taylor radius

f_{\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}}(A)=f(0)\mathsf{e}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}A_{\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}}^{[k]}

converges whenever

and all finite times. The module action gives

\begin{split}x(t)={}&\exp_{\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}}(tA)\cdot x(0)\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}\int_{0}^{t}\exp_{\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}}((t-\tau)A)\cdot B_{k}\,u_{k}(\tau)\,d\tau.\end{split}

(35)

Indeed, unfolding the action of

. Thus (

Remark 26

(Advantage and restriction of the factorization)
.

Set
K^{(i)}=A^{(i)}\Psi_{p\times n}\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}
. Then
H_{A}=\bcirc(K)
and
A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}x=K*x
. The t-STP therefore gives a rectangular coefficient description of a structured t-product operator. If

,
A
has

entries instead of the

entries of an unrestricted

, but each block

has rank at most

and its rows belong to the fixed row space of

. Not every square-slice t-product coefficient has this factorization.

The restriction also affects stability:
\rank H_{A}\leq sp<sn
when

, so the autonomous system
\dot{X}=H_{A}X
cannot have an asymptotically stable origin on the full state space. Damping or additional feedback can remove this obstruction; it must be specified separately if it lies outside the bridge-factorized family. Furthermore,
\vct(X)
evolves under

, so the autonomous left action does not mix different columns of

.

Remark 27
.

When
p=n
, the state operator in (
30
) reduces to the t-product state operator. Its bank of scalar input channels need not coincide with the tensor-input parameterization of (
17
); both describe linear input maps once their channels are specified. Replacing the t-STP by the slice-wise DK-STP instead gives (

), with a different coupling pattern.

The same product also supplies nonlinear interaction terms: positive-degree powers stay in the state space and mix slices through convolution. For nonlinear systems let
V=\mathbb{R}^{n\times q\times s}
, let
U\subseteq V
be open, and choose smooth maps

and

. Then

\dot{x}=F(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{\mu}G_{k}(x)u_{k}(t),\qquad y=h(x)

(36)

is a well-defined control-affine system, with local solutions for piecewise continuous inputs. A particular analytic class is obtained from ordinary constant tensors in

and positive-degree t-STP polynomials in

, or convergent series on an appropriate open domain. For example,
F(x)=D+ax+b(x\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}x)
, with

and

, takes values in

and introduces cross-slice nonlinear terms. General analytic vector fields need not be scalar-coefficient functions of one algebra element. In particular, the formal constant

in (

) cannot be inserted into (

) as an ordinary rectangular state tensor. The output

is a map, not necessarily a vector field.

6
Lie Groups and Lie Algebras via t-STP

The previous section used products and exponentials to describe state evolution. We now examine the algebra of their composition: the commutator records the dependence on the order of coefficient actions, and the group of units contains their invertible exponentials. Fix
m,n,s
and put
V=\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
with the t-STP. The following constructions use the finite-dimensional associative algebra from Proposition
22
. They extend the rectangular matrix setting of
[
8
]
using the standard relation between associative algebras and matrix Lie structures
[
16
]
. They concern the product commutator, which should not be identified without further argument with the Lie bracket of nonlinear vector fields in (
36
).

Lemma 28
.

For
A,B\in V
, the block-circulant map satisfies

\Gamma(A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}B)=\Gamma(A)(I_{s}\otimes\Psi_{n\times m})\Gamma(B)=\Gamma(A)\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,\Gamma(B).

(37)

Thus it is an injective algebra homomorphism into the rectangular matrix algebra
\mathcal{M}_{sm\times sn}
equipped with its DK-STP, not in general into a square matrix algebra with ordinary multiplication.

Proof.

The
(i,j)

block on the left by (

. Injectivity and linearity hold as in Proposition

∎

For the linear coefficient actions

on
\mathbb{R}^{m\times q\times s}
, associativity gives
T_{A}T_{B}-T_{B}T_{A}=T_{A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}B-B\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}A}
. Thus the following commutator measures the effect of exchanging two such actions and remains representable by a tensor of the same shape.

Proposition 29
.

The bracket

[A,B]_{\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}}=A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}B-B\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}A

(38)

makes
V
a real Lie algebra, denoted by
\mathfrak{gl}_{*}(m\times n\times s,\mathbb{R})
. For
m=n
it is the commutator Lie algebra of the t-product algebra.

Proof.

Bilinearity and skew-symmetry follow from the product. By associativity, suppressing the product sign in this calculation only, the three terms of the Jacobi identity are

\displaystyle{}[[A,B],C]

\displaystyle=ABC-BAC-CAB+CBA,

\displaystyle[[B,C],A]

\displaystyle=BCA-CBA-ABC+ACB,

\displaystyle[[C,A],B]

\displaystyle=CAB-ACB-BCA+BAC.

Their sum is zero. When
m=n
, the bridge is

and the t-STP equals the t-product.
∎

To pass from infinitesimal coefficients to invertible compositions, the zero coefficient must correspond to an identity action. Adjoin one formal identity by
\widetilde{V}=\mathbb{R}\oplus V
with multiplication (
12

(\alpha\mathsf{e}+A)(\beta\mathsf{e}+B)=\alpha\beta\mathsf{e}+\alpha B+\beta A+A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}B.

(39)

This unitization of the convolution algebra differs from the product of slice unitizations used for

in Definition

. Both identities are formal, but they belong to different algebras.

Definition 11
.

An element
z\in\widetilde{V}
is a unit if there exists
w\in\widetilde{V}
with
zw=wz=\mathsf{e}
. Define

\mathrm{GL}_{*}(m\times n\times s,\mathbb{R})=\{\mathsf{e}+A:A\in V,\ \mathsf{e}+A\text{ is a unit in }\widetilde{V}\}.

(40)

To study invertibility and smoothness with ordinary matrix tools, we use the left regular representation of the unitized algebra. The additional coordinate records the formal identity, and the first column retains the coefficient itself even when distinct coefficients have the same left action on the original algebra. Let
D=mns
, fix the coordinate map
a(A)=\vct(\unfold(A))\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and let
L_{A}\in\mathcal{M}_{D\times D}
satisfy

\rho(A)=\begin{bmatrix}0&0_{1\times D}\\
a(A)&L_{A}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\widetilde{\rho}(\alpha\mathsf{e}+A)=\alpha I_{D+1}+\rho(A).

(41)

Proposition 30
.

The map
\widetilde{\rho}
is a faithful unital algebra representation on
\mathbb{R}^{D+1}
. An element
z\in\widetilde{V}
is invertible if and only if
\widetilde{\rho}(z)
is invertible as an ordinary matrix. For a specified pair
z,w
, the identity
w=z^{-1}
is equivalent to
\widetilde{\rho}(w)=\widetilde{\rho}(z)^{-1}
. The restriction
\rho
is an injective Lie algebra homomorphism into
\mathfrak{gl}(D+1,\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Associativity gives
L_{A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}B}=L_{A}L_{B}
. Multiplying the two block matrices yields

\rho(A)\rho(B)=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
L_{A}a(B)&L_{A}L_{B}\end{bmatrix}=\rho(A\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}B).

The first column recovers
a(A)
, so
\rho
is injective; the upper-left entry of
\widetilde{\rho}(\alpha\mathsf{e}+A)
recovers
\alpha
. Formula (
39
) now proves unital multiplicativity. In fact
\widetilde{\rho}(z)
is the matrix of left multiplication by
z
on
\widetilde{V}
in the basis beginning with
\mathsf{e}
. If this matrix is invertible, there is a unique
w
with
zw=\mathsf{e}
. Then
z(wz-\mathsf{e})=0
, and injectivity of left multiplication by
z
implies
wz=\mathsf{e}
. The reverse implication follows by representing an inverse. The claim about a specified pair follows by faithfulness. Finally, multiplicativity gives
\rho([A,B]_{\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}})=[\rho(A),\rho(B)]
.
∎

Theorem 31
.

The set (
40
) is a Lie group of dimension
D=mns
. Its Lie algebra is
\mathfrak{gl}_{*}(m\times n\times s,\mathbb{R})
. Moreover,

\mathsf{e}+A\longmapsto I_{D+1}+\rho(A)

(42)

is a faithful smooth group representation and an isomorphism onto an embedded Lie subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(D+1,\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

The product of two elements of (
40
) has scalar coordinate one by (
39
). The scalar-coordinate map
\alpha\mathsf{e}+A\mapsto\alpha
is an algebra homomorphism, so the inverse of an element with scalar coordinate one also has scalar coordinate one. The identity is
\mathsf{e}
, and associativity is inherited from
\widetilde{V}
. This proves the group axioms.

By Proposition
30
, the group is the open subset

\{\mathsf{e}+A:\det(I_{D+1}+\rho(A))\neq 0\}

of the affine space
\mathsf{e}+V
. Multiplication is polynomial. Matrix inversion is smooth on the invertible matrices, and the lower part of the first column of the inverse recovers the coordinate vector of its
V
component; inversion in this group is therefore smooth. Its tangent space at
\mathsf{e}
identifies with
V
. The differential of (
42
) is
\rho
, so the induced Lie bracket is the commutator (
38
), by Proposition
30
.

The representation is injective and has a smooth inverse on its image, obtained from the same first column. Its image is
(I_{D+1}+\rho(V))\cap\mathrm{GL}(D+1,\mathbb{R})
, an open subset of an affine subspace with the inherited embedded manifold structure. Thus it is an embedded Lie subgroup and the asserted isomorphism follows.
∎

This group contains the evolution operators already used in Section

: for
A\in V
, the element

has scalar coordinate one and inverse
\exp_{\mathbin{\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,_{\!*}}}(-tA)
. Its image under

is
\exp(t\rho(A))
, and its module action gives the homogeneous solution in (

). Thus the group and the dynamical formulation describe the same coefficient exponential in two representations.

Remark 32
.

The representation (
41
) proves existence of a faithful classical realization of the algebraic structures. Its size
D+1
is not a computational compression claim. The smaller state-action representation

from (

8
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we propose a framework based on DK-STP to generalize the t-product of cubic matrices and the corresponding dynamical systems.
To relax the inner-dimension compatibility requirement, we have extended the DK-STP to cubic matrices and described the resulting slice-wise control systems. To allow interactions between frontal-slice subsystems, we have introduced the t-STP, combining the circular coupling of the t-product with the bridge construction of the DK-STP. For a fixed number of frontal slices, we have established its algebraic properties and described the associated Lie algebra and Lie group. The t-STP contains the t-product as the case of matching inner dimensions and permits rectangular coefficient slices. A supply-network game illustrates the construction, its equivalent classical realization, and a globally convergent payoff-gradient adjustment law. The principal representational advantage is a smaller coefficient description for the specified operator class; the state dimension is unchanged. The construction therefore extends the coefficient description of t-product systems
[
17
,
31
]
while retaining their circular coupling, and complements transform-based tensor products
[
21
]
, which vary the transform used to define the multiplication.

The resulting framework connects control systems over cubic matrices with structured classical systems. Its usefulness depends on whether the bridge factorization and circular slice coupling are appropriate for the application. Relative to the Einstein-product systems in
[
6
]
, the present formulation fixes a particular circular and bridge-factorized operator structure. The Lie structures express the standard commutator and unit-group constructions
[
16
]
for this algebra, with the coefficient exponential linking them to the trajectories. Several questions remain for further investigation.

(i)

Linear control problems.
The classical realization provides a route to studying controllability, observability, stability, and control design. A useful next step is to determine which results and design procedures preserve the prescribed tensor structure and admissible input channels, and when that structure can be exploited computationally. When
s=1
, the state space reduces to a matrix space, so the fixed-depth construction also applies to matrix-valued systems. Existing tensor-based system analyses
[
17
,
6
,
31
]
provide reference formulations; the additional question here is whether a chosen design stays within the bridge-factorized coefficient family.

(ii)

Nonlinear control systems.
The space
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times s}
is linearly isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{mns}
. Accordingly, geometric methods for nonlinear control systems
[
18
]
can be applied when the maps defining the dynamics are well-defined smooth vector fields on this space. The product-generated vector fields considered here have additional algebraic structure. Understanding how this structure affects accessibility, feedback design, and computation is an open direction.

(iii)

Higher-order hypermatrices.
An array
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times\cdots\times m_{d}}
, with
d>3
, can be reshaped into a third-order array after grouping its indices. This suggests a way to extend the present constructions. However, the induced product and coupling depend on the chosen grouping, and their relation to the original multiway interactions requires further study. General tensor decompositions
[
26
]
and products defined through other invertible transforms
[
21
]
offer possible comparison frameworks, but compatibility with the chosen grouping would have to be established.

(iv)

Coefficient storage and computational performance.
For a state
X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times q\times s}
, the rectangular coefficient tensor
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p\times s}
stores
mps
entries. Its classical realization has additional block-circulant, Kronecker, and bridge structure, which should be retained in a fair computational comparison. Future work should quantify when this representation improves storage or runtime relative to equally structured alternatives, and assess the approximation error when the model is fitted to data. Extensions involving different numbers of frontal slices require a compatible lifting rule and a separate associativity analysis. Low-rank tensor numerical methods
[
22
]
illustrate another route to economical representations. Comparing or combining them with the fixed bridge requires accounting for both approximation error and implementation cost.
