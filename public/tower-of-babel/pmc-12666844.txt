Genetic signatures of responsiveness in idiopathic normal pressure Hydrocephalus: Insights from whole-exome and LASSO-based analysis
Authors: Tomáš Moravec, Zdeněk Musil, Marek Brabec, Martin Majovský, Ondra Petr, Petr Zach, Vladimír Musil, David Netuka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH) is a potentially reversible neurological disorder characterised by the triad of gait disturbance, cognitive decline, and urinary incontinence, together with ventriculomegaly despite normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure (1–3). Although traditionally considered non-genetic and multifactorial, emerging data suggest that rare pathogenic variants and cilia-related mechanisms may contribute to its pathogenesis and modulate responsiveness to CSF diversion (8–12).MethodsWe performed whole-exome sequencing (WES) in 33 consecutive patients with clinically and radiologically confirmed iNPH who underwent a standardised 120-h lumbar drainage (LD) protocol. Responders were defined as those showing ≥20 % improvement in gait speed or ≥3-point increase in Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score compared with baseline. Variants were filtered according to ACMG/AMP guidelines (19) and population allele frequency (minor allele frequency <1 %). Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression (20) with ten-fold cross-validation was applied to identify gene-level predictors of LD responsiveness.ResultsAfter filtering, 110 genes with at least one pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant were retained for modelling. LASSO regression at the λ.1se threshold identified a five-gene panel—FANCD2, ATR, ORAI1, MUC1, and RP1L1—involved in DNA damage response, calcium signalling, epithelial barrier integrity, and ciliary architecture (21–28). The internally derived model achieved an accuracy of 81.8 %, sensitivity 68.8 %, specificity 94.1 %, and positive predictive value 91.7 % for prediction of LD response.ConclusionsRare pathogenic variants in genes linked to genome stability, immune and calcium signalling, and ciliary structure may influence LD responsiveness in iNPH. The five-gene model represents a biologically plausible, hypothesis-generating tool for preoperative risk stratification. Validation in larger, multicentre cohorts, integration with shunt outcomes, and functional studies are required before any clinical implementation.Keywords: Idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus, Lumbar drainage, Whole-exome sequencing, Genetic biomarkers, DNA damage response, Cilia

Introduction

Idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH) is a late-onset, potentially reversible neurological syndrome characterised by gait disturbance, cognitive impairment, and urinary incontinence in the presence of ventriculomegaly and normal CSF opening pressure (Adams et al., 1965; Bradley, 2001; Williams and Malm, 2016; Mori et al., 2012; Graff-Radford, 2007). Classically considered idiopathic and multifactorial, iNPH was long viewed as a non-genetic entity. However, accumulating evidence suggests that subtle structural, vascular, inflammatory, and genetic factors may interact to determine susceptibility and clinical course (Williams and Malm, 2016; Keong et al., 2016; Oliveira et al., 2019; Hale et al., 2024; Munch et al., 2023; Piccinin et al., 2025).

The pathophysiology of iNPH remains incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms include impaired CSF absorption, altered intracranial compliance, venous outflow disturbance, and dysfunction of ependymal and choroid plexus epithelium (Bradley, 2001; Graff-Radford, 2007; Keong et al., 2016; Czosnyka and Pickard, 2004). Increasing attention has focused on the role of motile and primary cilia in CSF dynamics and ventricular homeostasis. Disruption of ciliary structure or function has been causally linked to congenital and paediatric hydrocephalus through mutations in ciliary and planar cell polarity genes (Furey et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2020). Recent population-based and exome sequencing studies suggest that variants in cilia-related and junctional genes may also contribute to adult-onset hydrocephalus, including cases clinically diagnosed as iNPH (Hale et al., 2024; Munch et al., 2023; Piccinin et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2022).

Extended lumbar drainage (LD) over several days is widely used as a dynamic test to predict shunt responsiveness in iNPH and generally shows greater sensitivity than a single CSF tap test (Walchenbach et al., 2002a; Kazui et al., 2015; Tarnaris et al., 2009; Ishikawa et al., 2012). However, LD is invasive, resource-intensive and not perfectly accurate; false negatives and false positives occur (Walchenbach et al., 2002a, 2002b; Kazui et al., 2015; Tarnaris et al., 2009). Molecular or genetic markers that could complement LD and imaging-based assessments would be of substantial clinical value.

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) enables systematic detection of rare, potentially pathogenic variants. Studies in congenital hydrocephalus have highlighted the involvement of genes related to ciliary assembly, cell polarity, and DNA damage response (DDR) (Furey et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2020). Evidence in iNPH remains limited, and no previous study has systematically combined WES data with predictive modelling of treatment responsiveness.

In this exploratory pilot study, we performed WES in a well-defined cohort of iNPH patients undergoing a standardised 120-h LD protocol. We used penalised regression (LASSO) to identify gene-level predictors of LD responsiveness. We hypothesised that rare pathogenic variants in genes involved in DDR, calcium signalling, epithelial barrier function, and ciliogenesis would be associated with variability in clinical improvement following CSF diversion.

Materials and methods

Study population

Consecutive adult patients with suspected iNPH were prospectively evaluated at the Department of Neurosurgery, Military University Hospital Prague, between June 1, 2022 and January 1, 2023.

Inclusion criteria were

(i)presence of at least two components of the classic triad (gait disturbance, cognitive decline, urinary incontinence);

presence of at least two components of the classic triad (gait disturbance, cognitive decline, urinary incontinence);

(ii)ventriculomegaly on MRI or CT with Evans' index >0.30;

ventriculomegaly on MRI or CT with Evans' index >0.30;

(iii)normal lumbar CSF opening pressure;

normal lumbar CSF opening pressure;

(iv)no alternative diagnosis sufficient to explain the symptoms, in accordance with international guidelines (Williams and Malm, 2016; Mori et al., 2012; Ishikawa et al., 2012).

no alternative diagnosis sufficient to explain the symptoms, in accordance with international guidelines (Williams and Malm, 2016; Mori et al., 2012; Ishikawa et al., 2012).

Exclusion criteria included: prior central nervous system infection, subarachnoid or intracerebral haemorrhage, intracranial tumour or major space-occupying lesion, severe traumatic brain injury, or other structural or neurodegenerative conditions (e.g. advanced Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease) considered the primary cause of symptoms. Patients with secondary NPH or incomplete clinical or genetic data were excluded.

Demographic and baseline clinical characteristics were collected, including age, sex, comorbidities, gait performance, cognitive function, and urinary continence.

Lumbar drainage protocol

All enrolled patients underwent a standardised extended LD protocol. A lumbar drainage catheter was inserted at the L3–L4 or L4–L5 level under sterile conditions. Continuous CSF drainage was maintained for 120 h using a closed system, with a target drainage rate of approximately 5–10 mL/h, adjusted according to clinical status to avoid overdrainage. Daily CSF volumes and any procedure-related complications were recorded.

Clinical assessments were performed at baseline (pre-LD) and after completion of the 120-h LD. Gait was evaluated using a standardised 10-m walk test, and gait speed (m/s) was calculated. Cognitive function was assessed with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), using validated language versions with standard administration procedures.

Primary outcome

The primary endpoint was binary LD responsiveness. Patients were classified as responders if they showed.

•≥20 % improvement in gait speed on the 10-m walk test, and/or

•≥3-point increase in MoCA score

Secondary outcomes were analysed descriptively and used to confirm the robustness of the primary classification but were not incorporated into the genetic prediction model.

Whole-exome sequencing

Peripheral blood samples were collected from all participants. Genomic DNA was extracted using standard protocols and quality-controlled. WES was performed using a clinical exome capture kit (Prep with Exome 2.5 Enrichment kit) and paired-end sequencing on an Illumina platform.

Mean on-target coverage exceeded 100 × , with ≥95 % of targeted bases covered at ≥20 × in all samples. Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome using BWA-MEM.

Variant calling was performed with GATK HaplotypeCaller following best practice guidelines. Joint genotyping and quality filtering were applied to generate a high-confidence variant set.

Variant annotation, filtering, and classification

Variants were annotated using ANNOVAR and Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP). Variants were retained if they met all of the following criteria.

•minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1 % in population databases (gnomAD, 1000 Genomes);

minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1 % in population databases (gnomAD, 1000 Genomes);

•predicted to be deleterious or damaging by at least two independent in silico tools (e.g. SIFT, PolyPhen-2, CADD);

predicted to be deleterious or damaging by at least two independent in silico tools (e.g. SIFT, PolyPhen-2, CADD);

•located in exonic or canonical splice-site regions.

located in exonic or canonical splice-site regions.

Variant classification followed ACMG/AMP guidelines (Richards et al., 2015). Only variants classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic were included in the genetic modelling. For each gene, a binary indicator variable was generated to denote the presence of ≥1 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant in that patient. This resulted in 110 genes with at least one qualifying variant in the cohort.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons between responders and non-responders were performed using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Exploratory univariate logistic regression analyses were first conducted to screen individual gene-level predictors for association with LD response. Given the small sample size and high dimensionality, these analyses were interpreted descriptively without formal correction for multiple testing.

To develop a parsimonious multivariable model and reduce overfitting, we applied LASSO logistic regression using the glmnet package in R (Friedman et al., 2010). All 110 gene-level binary predictors were considered. Ten-fold cross-validation within the dataset was used to determine the regularisation parameter λ. The final model was selected using the one-standard-error rule (λ.1se) to favour robustness and simplicity.

Model performance was assessed by calculating accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and by inspecting the confusion matrix. All reported metrics are internally derived, as no independent external validation cohort was available. This limitation is explicitly addressed in the Discussion.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Military University Hospital Prague (108/17–62/2022) All participants provided written informed consent for participation and genetic analysis, in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Thirty-three patients with clinically and radiologically confirmed iNPH fulfilled inclusion criteria and completed the LD protocol and WES. Sixteen patients (48.5 %) met the definition of LD responders, while 17 (51.5 %) were classified as non-responders.

The mean age of the cohort was 72.4 ± 5.3 years; 16 patients (48.5 %) were male. There were no statistically significant differences between responders and non-responders in age, sex distribution, baseline gait speed, baseline MoCA scores, or baseline ICIQ-SF scores (Table 1). Conventional demographic and clinical parameters thus did not adequately distinguish responders from non-responders.

Whole-exome sequencing output

WES met all predefined quality criteria. After application of allele frequency, functional prediction, and ACMG/AMP classification filters, 110 genes with at least one pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in at least one individual were retained as candidate predictors for LD responsiveness.

Univariate logistic regression

Exploratory univariate logistic regression identified several genes with nominal association signals. FANCD2 showed the strongest trend, with variants more frequent among non-responders and a p-value approaching significance (p = 0.056), suggesting a potential link between DDR-related defects and diminished clinical improvement. Given the small sample size and multiple testing burden, these findings were considered hypothesis-generating only.

Stepwise logistic regression

A stepwise logistic regression approach using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) identified a multigene model including FANCD2, ATR, ORAI1, MUC1, and RP1L1. This model yielded an apparent accuracy of 75.8 %, sensitivity 62.5 %, specificity 88.2 %, and PPV 83.3 %. Due to the known limitations and overfitting risk of stepwise selection in small cohorts, we prioritised penalised regression results for inference.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary analyses confirmed greater mean improvements in gait speed and MoCA scores among LD responders compared with non-responders, consistent with the primary endpoint definition. Urinary continence assessed by ICIQ-SF also demonstrated a trend towards improvement in responders. These results support the clinical validity of the responder classification. Inclusion of continuous secondary outcome measures in exploratory models did not materially change the selected gene panel.

Functional overview of selected genes

The five genes identified by LASSO are biologically plausible in the context of iNPH.

•FANCD2 and ATR are central components of the Fanconi anaemia and DDR pathways, preserving replication fork stability and genome integrity (Kee and D'Andrea, 2010; Kottemann and Smogorzewska, 2013; O'Driscoll et al., 2003).

•ORAI1 encodes a key pore-forming subunit of store-operated calcium channels that regulate immune cell and glial activation (Zhang et al., 2005; Zhou et al., 2022).

ORAI1 encodes a key pore-forming subunit of store-operated calcium channels that regulate immune cell and glial activation (Zhang et al., 2005; Zhou et al., 2022).

•MUC1 is a transmembrane mucin involved in epithelial barrier integrity and immune modulation, with established roles in chronic inflammation and CNS tumour biology (Kufe, 2009; Chen et al., 2021).

MUC1 is a transmembrane mucin involved in epithelial barrier integrity and immune modulation, with established roles in chronic inflammation and CNS tumour biology (Kufe, 2009; Chen et al., 2021).

These converging pathways suggest that impaired genome maintenance, altered calcium-dependent signalling, dysregulated epithelial/immune interfaces, and ciliary abnormalities may collectively influence the capacity of the CNS to respond to changes in CSF dynamics.

Discussion

This exploratory pilot study indicates that rare pathogenic variants in selected genes may modulate responsiveness to CSF diversion in iNPH. By integrating WES with penalised regression, we identified a five-gene panel—FANCD2, ATR, ORAI1, MUC1, and RP1L1—associated with LD responsiveness. These genes map to mechanistic domains already implicated in hydrocephalus and related CNS disorders: DNA damage response, calcium signalling, epithelial barrier function, and ciliary architecture (Furey et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2020; Hale et al., 2024; Munch et al., 2023; Piccinin et al., 2025; Kee and D'Andrea, 2010; Kottemann and Smogorzewska, 2013; O'Driscoll et al., 2003; Zhang et al., 2005; Zhou et al., 2022; Kufe, 2009; Chen et al., 2021; Katoh and Nakayama, 2013; Narita et al., 2010; Pala et al., 2018; Banizs et al., 2005; Czosnyka et al., 2004).

FANCD2 and ATR are crucial guardians of genomic stability. Dysregulation of DDR pathways can impair the survival and function of neural progenitors and ependymal cells, potentially predisposing to subtle structural vulnerabilities and reduced plasticity (Kee and D'Andrea, 2010; Kottemann and Smogorzewska, 2013; O'Driscoll et al., 2003). ORAI1-mediated store-operated calcium entry is central to microglial and astroglial activation; its dysfunction may blunt adaptive immune and repair responses following CSF diversion (Zhang et al., 2005; Zhou et al., 2022). MUC1 contributes to epithelial and ependymal integrity and shapes inflammatory signalling; aberrant MUC1 activity is associated with immune evasion and chronic inflammation (Kufe, 2009; Chen et al., 2021). RP1L1, though canonically linked to retinal ciliopathies, shares domains with motile ciliary proteins and, by extension, may reflect broader ciliary dysfunction relevant to CSF flow regulation (Katoh and Nakayama, 2013; Narita et al., 2010; Pala et al., 2018; Banizs et al., 2005; Czosnyka et al., 2004).

The functional diversity of these genes raises the question whether the model captures coherent biology or overfitted noise. However, emerging work suggests cross-talk between DDR, ciliary assembly, and inflammatory signalling pathways (Furey et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2020; Ji et al., 2022; Kee and D'Andrea, 2010; Kottemann and Smogorzewska, 2013; O'Driscoll et al., 2003; Narita et al., 2010; Pala et al., 2018; Banizs et al., 2005). We therefore propose that this gene set may represent converging vulnerabilities affecting the ependymal–CSF interface and its ability to adapt to altered hydrodynamics, rather than unrelated random signals. Nonetheless, this interpretation remains hypothesis-generating and requires rigorous validation.

From a clinical standpoint, the five-gene model achieved high specificity and PPV in this cohort, suggesting possible application in identifying patients likely to benefit from CSF diversion. In practice, a genetic panel could one day complement LD and imaging to refine surgical decision-making, particularly in borderline candidates. At present, however, the model should not guide care: all performance estimates are internally derived in a small sample and must be treated with caution.

Our findings are consistent with recent literature implicating ciliary and junctional genes in hydrocephalus (Furey et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2020; Hale et al., 2024; Munch et al., 2023; Piccinin et al., 2025), and extend these insights by linking specific gene-level patterns with treatment responsiveness rather than diagnosis alone. They also support the view that iNPH encompasses biologically distinct subgroups: some may have predominantly mechanical CSF flow impairment, while others may harbour underlying cellular, ciliary, or genomic vulnerabilities that influence reversibility (Czosnyka and Pickard, 2004; Goh et al., 2022).

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged.

First, the sample size (n = 33) is modest, inherently limiting statistical power and increasing the risk of model overfitting. Although LASSO regression with cross-validation reduces this risk, the reported performance metrics (accuracy, specificity, PPV) are based solely on internal validation and are likely optimistic. An independent validation cohort was not available and is essential before clinical application.

Second, variant classification relied on ACMG/AMP criteria and multiple in silico tools (Richards et al., 2015), without functional assays or segregation analysis. Some variants classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic may be of uncertain clinical significance in the context of iNPH.

Third, the primary endpoint was LD responsiveness rather than definitive postoperative shunt outcome. While LD is widely used to predict shunt benefit (Walchenbach et al., 2002a; Kazui et al., 2015; Tarnaris et al., 2009; Ishikawa et al., 2012), it is an imperfect surrogate. Long-term follow-up data on shunt outcomes were incomplete at the time of analysis and will need to be incorporated in future studies.

Fourth, the analysis used gene-level presence/absence coding and did not model cumulative burden, polygenic risk, or gene–gene and gene–environment interactions. The current model may therefore capture only a subset of the underlying genetic architecture.

Finally, this is a single-centre study and may not reflect broader population heterogeneity.

Future directions

Future research should focus on multicentre validation of the five-gene panel in larger, ethnically diverse iNPH cohorts with systematic long-term follow-up after shunt surgery. Such studies should evaluate whether the genetic signature predicts not only LD response but also sustained postoperative improvement and complication risk.

Integrating genomic data with quantitative imaging (e.g. CSF flow MRI, diffusion tensor imaging), CSF and plasma biomarkers, and detailed clinical phenotyping may enable more accurate, multimodal prediction models. Pathway enrichment and network analyses should be employed to determine whether the implicated genes cluster within specific molecular circuits beyond chance.

Functional studies in neural progenitor and ependymal cell models, as well as in vivo models of hydrocephalus, will be essential to dissect how perturbations in DDR, calcium signalling, mucin biology, and ciliary architecture jointly influence CSF dynamics and brain tissue resilience. In the long term, such work may support the development of targeted therapies or personalised neurosurgical strategies guided by an individual's genetic and molecular profile.

Conclusion

Whole-exome sequencing combined with penalised regression in a carefully characterised iNPH cohort identified rare pathogenic variants in FANCD2, ATR, ORAI1, MUC1, and RP1L1 as potential predictors of responsiveness to lumbar drainage. These findings support a multifactorial biological model in which DNA damage response, calcium-dependent signalling, epithelial barrier function, and ciliary architecture collectively influence disease expression and reversibility.

The five-gene model demonstrated high specificity and positive predictive value in this pilot dataset, suggesting promise for future risk stratification tools. However, given the small sample size, internal-only validation, and surrogate outcome, these results must be interpreted as exploratory. Rigorous external validation, integration with shunt outcomes, and functional mechanistic studies are mandatory next steps before clinical translation.
