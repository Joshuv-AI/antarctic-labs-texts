Solving Inverse Problems with Deep Linear Neural Networks: Global Convergence Guarantees for Gradient Descent with Weight Decay
Hannah Laus, Suzanna Parkinson, Vasileios Charisopoulos, Felix Krahmer, Rebecca Willett

Abstract
Machine learning methods are commonly used to solve inverse problems, wherein an unknown signal must be estimated from few indirect measurements generated via a known acquisition procedure. In particular, neural networks perform well empirically but have limited theoretical guarantees. In this work, we study an underdetermined linear inverse problem that admits several possible solution operators that map measurements to estimates of the target signal. A standard remedy (e.g., in compressed sensing) for establishing the uniqueness of the solution mapping is to assume the existence of a latent low-dimensional structure in the source signal. We ask the following question: do deep linear neural networks adapt to unknown low-dimensional structure when trained by gradient descent with weight decay regularization? We prove that mildly overparameterized deep linear networks trained in this manner converge to an approximate solution mapping that accurately solves the inverse problem while implicitly encoding latent subspace structure. We show rigorously that deep linear networks trained with weight decay automatically adapt to latent subspace structure in the data under practical stepsize and weight initialization schemes. Our work highlights that regularization and overparameterization improve generalization, while overparameterization also accelerates convergence during training.

Introduction

Machine learning approaches, especially those based on deep neural networks, have risen to prominence for solving a broad class of inverse problems. In particular, deep learning approaches constitute the state of the art for various inverse problems arising in medical imaging (e.g., MRI or CT)

On the other hand, there is a strong need for theory: understanding the behavior of deep neural networks is crucial when they are deployed in critical applications such as medical imaging.

When training a neural network to solve an underdetermined inverse problem on finitely many training samples, there are many possible solution mappings the neural network could learn. We seek to understand the role of weight decay regularization and latent low-dimensional structure in the training data in determining which solution is selected when training via gradient descent. In general, we expect that a solution mapping that leverages the latent low-dimensional structure is less likely to overfit to the training data and hence be less sensitive to small perturbations in test samples. Convergence of gradient descent for deep linear networks

Several works study the dynamics of gradient descent for training deep linear neural networks in general regression tasks under different assumptions.
For example, Du and Hu
[
35
]
show that gradient descent starting from a random Gaussian initialization will converge at a linear rate to a global minimizer of the

loss (
\lambda=0
) as long as the hidden layer width
scales linearly in the input dimension and depth; a closely related work by Hu et al.
[

demonstrates that the hidden layer width no longer needs to scale

Similarly, Arora et al.
[
37

study convergence of gradient descent when (i) weight matrices
at initialization are approximately balanced and (ii) the problem instance satisfies
a “deficiency margin” property ruling out certain rank-deficient solutions – a condition later removed
by the analysis of Nguegnang et al.
[
38
]
.
On the other hand, Xu et al.
[
39
]
show that gradient descent converges to a global
minimum for linear neural networks with two layers and mild overparameterization
without

any assumptions on the initialization; however, their proof does not readily extend to
neural networks of arbitrary depth
L
.
Shamir
[
40
]
studies gradient descent on deep linear networks when the dimension and hidden width are both equal to one.
Other results include Kawaguchi et al.
[
41
]
and Laurent and von Brecht
[
42
]
, who
show that under certain assumptions, all local minima are global.
Finally, a number of works focus on gradient flow for deep linear neural networks
[
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
]
, the continuous-time analog of gradient descent.

All the works mentioned so far study gradient descent or gradient flow
without any explicit regularization.
In contrast, Arora et al.
[
49
]
study the
\ell_{2}
-regularized objective for deep linear networks but do not focus on the effects of the regularization in the analysis. Instead, they show that depth has a preconditioning effect that accelerates convergence. However, their analysis for the discrete-time setting relies on
near-zero initialization and small stepsizes. Lewkowycz and Gur-Ari
[
50
]
study the regularization effect of

infinitely wide
neural networks with positively homogeneous activations, finding that model performance peaks at approximately

[

]
, inspired

, show that gradient descent updates of deep linear networks traverse a “small” subspace when

Finally, Wang and Jacot
[

study the implicit bias of (stochastic) gradient

Main result

In this section, we present our main result. Recall that we are interested in solving (
2
),
for the special case where
f_{\{W_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}}
is a deep linear network, using gradient

W_{\ell}(t+1):=W_{\ell}(t)-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\{W_{\ell}(t)\}_{\ell=1}^{L};(X,Y)),\quad\text{for $t=0,1,\dots,T-1$.}

(

Concretely, we want to minimize the following loss function:

\mathcal{L}(W_{L:1};(X,Y)):=\frac{1}{2}\lVert W_{L}\cdots W_{1}Y-X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lVert W_{\ell}\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}^{2},

(3)

where
W_{j:i}:=\prod_{\ell=j}^{i}W_{\ell}

We consider weight matrices of the following sizes:

W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}\times m},\;\;W_{2},\dots,W_{L-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{w}\times d_{w}},\;\;\text{and}\;\;W_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{w}}.

(4)

Having fixed the architecture, we introduce two mild assumptions under
which our results hold.

Assumption 2.1

(Restricted Isometry Property)
.

The measurement matrix
A
from (
1

satisfies the following:
there exists
\delta>0
such that,
for all vectors

(1-\delta)\lVert x\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert Ax\rVert^{2}\leq(1+\delta)\lVert x\rVert^{2}.

(5)

In particular, we assume
\delta=\frac{1}{10}
for ease of presentation in establishing our bounds;
one could choose any other small value.

Assumption
2.1
is standard
in the compressed sensing literature

Note that for Assumption

Assumption 2.2

The weight matrices

d_{w},&\ell=2,\dots,L.\end{cases}

(6)

To motivate our main theorem, we now describe the reference estimator against which we compare the trained neural network.
Consider an “oracle” operator defined as the minimum-Frobenius-norm linear operator that interpolates the training data:

W_{\mathrm{oracle}}=\arg\min_{W}\|W\|_{F}^{2}\text{ subject to }WY=X.

(7)

The unique solution to the minimization problem in (
7
) is given by
W_{\mathrm{oracle}}=XY^{\dagger}=R(AR)^{\dagger}
(see

C.4
), and one can show that
W_{\mathrm{oracle}}
is robust to Gaussian noise in the measurements at test time, see
Lemma

C.5
for the exact formulation.
Note that in our setting, since
X
is rank-
s
, the oracle operator
W_{\mathrm{oracle}}
is also rank-
s
. This suggests that an inverse mapping that takes advantage of the low-dimensional structure in the data will be more performant than one that does not.

Although knowledge of the structure in our data would suggest the use of the oracle estimator
W_{\mathrm{oracle}}
, practitioners routinely train neural networks to solve inverse problems without

knowledge of what structure their data might exhibit.
This motivates us to consider whether training a neural network via gradient descent will result in a learned mapping that is close to an oracle solution.
Because we can precisely describe the oracle operator
W_{\mathrm{oracle}}
in our setting, we can analyze the distance between the oracle and the learned neural network mapping. Indeed, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Given a linear operator
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
, the operator norm distance between
W
and the oracle mapping
W_{\mathrm{oracle}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
defined in (
7

\lVert W-W_{\mathrm{oracle}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\lesssim\frac{\lVert WY-X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}}{\sigma_{\min}(X)}+\lVert WP_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}

(8)

where
P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the range of

Proof.

Using the characterization of

as
W_{\mathrm{oracle}}=XY^{\dagger}
proven in

\displaystyle\lVert W-XY^{{\dagger}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle=\lVert WYY^{{\dagger}}-XY^{{\dagger}}+W(I-YY^{{\dagger}})\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert WY-X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\lVert Y^{{\dagger}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}+\lVert WP_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\lVert WY-X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}}{\sigma_{\min}(X)}+\lVert WP_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}

where the final inequality comes from the fact that Assumption

implies that

\lVert Y^{{\dagger}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sigma_{\min}(Y)}}\lesssim\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sigma_{\min}(X)}}

\square

Theorem 2.4
.

Let Assumptions

(9)

where

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{t\in\mathbb{N}\mid\lVert W_{L:1}(t)Y-X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\frac{C_{1}\gamma\lVert X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}}{L}\right\},

(10a)

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\frac{2L\kappa^{2}(X)\log(d_{w})}{\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{d}{m}}\right\rceil

Then, as long as the hidden layer width satisfies

(11)

gradient descent (

)
produces iterates that satisfy

\displaystyle\lVert W_{L:1}(t+1)Y-X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\left(1-\frac{1}{32\kappa^{2}(X)}\right)\lVert W_{L:1}(t)Y-X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}},&t<\tau;\\

(12)

\displaystyle\lVert W_{L:1}(T)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{d_{w}}\right)^{C_{3}},

(13)

with probability at least

3
Robustness to noisy test data

Training a network on the regularized objective with gradient descent leads to
a learned mapping that closely approximates the oracle mapping
W_{\mathrm{oracle}}
.
As a result, training with weight decay parameter
\lambda>0
guarantees robustness to noise within a bounded number of iterations, while training with
\lambda=0
allows the training error to be driven arbitrarily close to zero while the error on a noisy test sample remains bounded away from zero for any number of iterations.
The following corollary formalizes this by considering a test instance with noisy measurements.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
(W_{1}(T),\dots,W_{L}(T))
be the weight matrices of a deep linear
network trained for
T
iterations in the setting of
Theorem

2.4
(i.e.,
\lambda>0
,
T
as in (
10b
), and noise-free training data as in (
1
)).
Consider a test data point
(x,y)
satisfying
y=Ax+\epsilon
, where
x\in\mathrm{range}(R)
and

\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{m})
.
Then, the output of the network
W_{L:1}(T)y
satisfies

\lVert W_{L:1}(T)y-x\rVert\lesssim\left(\gamma\kappa(X)\sqrt{\sr(X)}+\frac{1}{d_{w}^{C_{3}}}\right)\lVert y\rVert+\sigma\sqrt{s}.

(20)

with probability
of at least
1-\exp\left(-\Omega(d)\right)-\exp\left(-\Omega(s^{2})\right)
with respect to both the weight initialization and the noise.

Conversely, let
(W^{\lambda=0}_{1}(t),...,W^{\lambda=0}_{L}(t))

be the weight matrices of a deep linear network trained in the setting of
Theorem

2.4
except that
\lambda=0
.
Then, for any
\beta>0
, there exists an iteration
T^{\prime}
such that, for all
t>T^{\prime}
,
the training reconstruction error satisfies
\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)Y-X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\beta\lVert X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}

while for all
\alpha>0
, the test error satisfies

\displaystyle\lVert W^{\lambda=0}_{L:1}(t)y-x\rVert

\displaystyle\gtrsim\sigma\left(\sqrt{\frac{d(m-s)}{m}}-(1+\sqrt{\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}))\sqrt{\frac{d\kappa^{4}(X)\sr(X)}{m}}-\sqrt{s}\right)

(21)

\displaystyle\quad-\beta\kappa(X)\sqrt{\sr(X)}\lVert y\rVert.

with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-\Omega(d)\right)-\exp\left(-\Omega(s^{2})\right)-\exp\left(-\Omega\left((m-s)^{2}\right)\right)-\alpha
.

The benefit of weight decay can be deduced from the qualitative behavior of the two bounds: on one hand,
the error in (
20
) can be driven
arbitrarily close to
\sigma\sqrt{s}
– which is unimprovable in general – by choosing
\gamma
sufficiently small and
d_{w}
sufficiently large.
On the other hand, when
m\gtrsim\kappa^{4}(X)s
(which is order optimal for bounded
\kappa(X)
),
training without weight decay incurs a
test error of at least
\sigma\sqrt{d}-\beta\kappa(X)\sqrt{\sr(X)}\lVert y\rVert
with high probability. In high dimensions,
this lower bound can be significantly larger than (
20
) unless
\beta
is large, which means that

W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)
is a poor fit
to the training data.

Remark 3.2
.

Theorem

2.4
reveals that when the weight decay parameter
\lambda
is very small, gradient descent requires many iterations to drive the off-subspace error close to zero. For that reason, it is advantageous to choose
\gamma
(and hence
\lambda
) as large as possible without compromising robustness to noise at test time.
From the above corollary, a reasonable choice of
\lambda
to balance the bias-variance tradeoff and maximize test-time robustness can be determined.
Choosing
\gamma
so that
\gamma\kappa(X)\sqrt{\sr(X)}\propto\nicefrac{{1}}{{d_{w}^{C_{3}}}}
(and therefore
\lambda\propto\frac{\sigma_{\min}^{2}(X)\sqrt{m}}{d_{w}^{C_{3}}\kappa(X)\sqrt{d\sr(X)}}
) balances the reconstruction error with the off-subspace error in the test-time performance bound.

Proof of
Corollary

3.1
.

At a high level, we will compare the errors of mappings learned with or without weight decay to the error of the oracle mapping,
\lVert W_{\mathrm{oracle}}y-x\rVert
.
Because the oracle mapping is robust to noise, it remains to bound the difference between the predictions made by the learned and oracle mappings.
For the upper bound in (
20
), we control this difference by leveraging
Lemmas

2.3
and
2.4
.
For the lower bound in (
21
), we argue that when training without weight decay, driving the training error to zero results in a learned mapping that approximates the oracle on the subspace spanned by the training measurements, but the off-subspace component of the learned mapping is unable to move away from its poorly behaved initialization, resulting in a lack of robustness at test time. We now give the detailed proof.

Upper bound with weight decay

We first prove (
20
). We have

\lVert W_{L:1}(T)y-x\rVert\leq\lVert W_{L:1}(T)-W_{\mathrm{oracle}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\lVert y\rVert+\lVert W_{\mathrm{oracle}}y-x\rVert.

(22)

In
Lemma

C.5
,
we show that
\lVert W_{\mathrm{oracle}}y-x\rVert\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{s}

with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-\Omega(s^{2})\right)
over the randomness in the noise.
By plugging the bounds on the reconstruction and off-subspace errors in
Equations

12
and
13
from
Theorem

2.4
into the bound on the distance to the oracle mapping in
Lemma

2.3
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert W_{L:1}(T)-W_{\mathrm{oracle}}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\lVert W_{L:1}(T)Y-X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}}{\sigma_{\min}(X)}+\lVert W_{L:1}(T)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\gamma\lVert X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}}{\sigma_{\min}(X)}+d_{w}^{-C_{3}}

\displaystyle=\gamma\kappa(X)\sqrt{\sr(X)}+d_{w}^{-C_{3}},

with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-\Omega(d)\right)
over the initialization.
Returning to (
22
), we conclude that

\lVert W_{L:1}(T)y-x\rVert\lesssim\left(\gamma\kappa(X)\sqrt{\sr(X)}+d_{w}^{-C_{3}}\right)\lVert y\rVert+\sigma\sqrt{s},

with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-\Omega(d)\right)-\exp\left(-\Omega(s^{2})\right)
.
This yields the upper bound (
20
).

Lower bound without weight decay

We turn to the lower bound in
Equation

21
.
By the reverse triangle inequality, we obtain

\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)y-x\rVert\geq\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}y\rVert-\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}-W_{\mathrm{oracle}}\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\lVert y\rVert-\lVert W_{\mathrm{oracle}}y-x\rVert.

(23)

Once again, we know from
Lemma

C.5
that
\lVert W_{\mathrm{oracle}}y-x\rVert\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{s}

with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-\Omega(s^{2})\right)
.

Learned mapping approximates oracle on
\mathrm{range}(Y)

Given
\lambda=0
, our convergence analysis
in
Proposition

4.9
suggests that the reconstruction error decreases monotonically at a
rate of
1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{32\kappa^{2}(X)}}
with probability
1-\exp\left(-\Omega(d)\right)
over the initialization (see Remark
4.10
). Consequently, for any

\beta>0
there exists a
T^{\prime}
such that
\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)Y-X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\leq\beta\lVert X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}
for
t\geq T^{\prime}
.
Applying
P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}=YY^{\dagger}
and the characterization
W_{\mathrm{oracle}}=XY^{\dagger}
in
Lemma

C.4
, we see that

\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}-W_{\mathrm{oracle}}\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}=\lVert(W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)Y-X)Y^{\dagger}\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\lesssim\frac{\beta\lVert X\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}}{\sigma_{\min}(X)}=\beta\kappa(X)\sqrt{\sr(X)}

(24)

where
the inequality uses the facts that
W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)
attains small reconstruction error and Assumption
2.1
implies that
\sigma_{\min}(Y)\gtrsim\sigma_{\min}(X)
(see
Lemma

C.3
).

Initialization behaves poorly on
\mathrm{range}(Y)^{\perp}

We now consider the term
\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}y\rVert
. Because
Ax\in\mathrm{range}(Y)
, we see that

\displaystyle\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}y\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\epsilon\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(0)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\epsilon\rVert-\lVert(W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(0)-W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t))P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\epsilon\rVert.

We show in
Lemma

B.1
that

\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(0)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\epsilon\rVert\gtrsim\sigma\sqrt{\frac{d(m-s)}{m}}

(25)

with probability
1-\exp{(-\Omega((m-s)^{2}))}-\exp{(-\Omega(d))}
. (Notice that
W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(0)=W_{L:1}(0)
, because the initalization is independent of
\lambda
.)

Learned mapping stays near initialization

To complete the proof, we leverage bounds derived in the proof of our main result (
Proposition

4.9
). Specifically,
our analysis shows that when training without weight decay, the learned mapping
W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}
cannot move very far from its initialization; we have the bound

\lVert W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(0)-W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t)\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d\kappa^{4}(X)\sr(X)}{m}}

(26)

with probability
1-\exp(-\Omega(d))
over the initialization. (We note that for
\lambda=0
, (
26
) can be obtained by simplifying the bound on
\lVert F(t)-C_{\mathsf{prod}}^{t}F(0)\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}
in
Equation

48
from
Proposition

4.9
, with
F
and
C_{\mathsf{prod}}
defined in
Equation

35
and
Equation

37
).
In
Lemma

B.2
, we use standard concentration arguments to show that, given a matrix
M
,
\lVert M\epsilon\rVert\leq\sigma(1+\sqrt{\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}})})\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}
with probability at least
1-\alpha
with respect to the randomness in the noise
\epsilon
.
Combined with
Equation

26
, this implies that

\lVert(W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(0)-W_{L:1}^{\lambda=0}(t))P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\epsilon\rVert\lesssim\sigma(1+\sqrt{\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}})})\sqrt{\frac{d\kappa^{4}(X)\sr(X)}{m}}

(27)

with probability
1-\alpha-\exp(-\Omega(d))
.
Finally, we obtain the lower bound in (
21
) by combining the bounds on each term in
Equation

23
from
Equations

25
,
27
, and
24
.

\square

4.1

We may assume that we have exactly

for some

.

4.2
Preliminary derivations

It is convenient to be explicit in our notation about the normalization factors at initialization.
For that reason, throughout the remainder of the proof, we will consider the equivalent loss function

\mathcal{L}(\{W_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1,\dots,L};(X,Y)):=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert{d_{w}^{-\frac{L-1}{2}}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}W_{L:1}Y-X}\right\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{\lVert W_{\ell}\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}}{d_{\ell}}

(34)

under the assumption that
(W_{\ell}(0))_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

for all
\ell=1,\ldots,L
.
We use the shorthand

F

for the learned mapping from measurements to signals:

F:=d_{w}^{-\frac{L-1}{2}}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}W_{L:1}.

(35)

We use

U

to refer to the network predictions on the training samples
X
, and

\Phi

to denote the training residuals;
we will further break
U
an
\Phi
into components corresponding to the
well-conditioned and “small” parts of
X=X_{\mathsf{r}}+X_{\mathsf{small}}
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} U&:=FY,\\
\Phi&:=U-X,\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} U_{\mathsf{r}}&:=FAX_{\mathsf{r}},\\
\Phi_{\mathsf{r}}&:=U_{\mathsf{r}}-X_{\mathsf{r}},\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} U_{\mathsf{small}}&:=FAX_{\mathsf{small}},\\
\Phi_{\mathsf{small}}&:=U_{\mathsf{small}}-X_{\mathsf{small}}.\end{aligned}

(36)

We shall also write
C_{\mathsf{prod}}^{(\setminus{i})}

and
C_{\mathsf{prod}}
for the following products appearing in our proofs:

C_{\mathsf{prod}}^{(\setminus{i})}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{L}\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda}{d_{j}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad C_{\mathsf{prod}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{L}\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda}{d_{i}}\right).

(37)

Given the gradient with respect to
W_{i}
of the loss in
Equation

34
, the gradient descent updates are

W_{i}(t+1)=\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda}{d_{i}}\right)W_{i}(t)-\eta d_{w}^{-\frac{L-1}{2}}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}W_{L:i+1}(t)^{\mathsf{T}}\Phi(t)Y^{\mathsf{T}}W_{i-1:1}(t)^{\mathsf{T}}\quad\text{for $1\leq i\leq L$.}

(38)

The above leads to the following decomposition of the product weight matrix at step
t
:

\displaystyle W_{L:1}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &C_{\mathsf{prod}}W_{L:1}(t)+E_{0}(t)\\
&-\eta d_{w}^{-\frac{L-1}{2}}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}C_{\mathsf{prod}}^{(\setminus{i})}W_{L:i+1}(t)W_{L:i+1}^{\mathsf{T}}(t)\Phi(t)Y^{\mathsf{T}}W_{i-1:1}^{\mathsf{T}}(t)W_{i-1:1}(t),\end{aligned}

(39)

with

containing all
O(\eta^{2})
terms. This is essentially the decomposition in
[
35
, Section 5]
, modified because of the effect of weight decay in
Equation

38
. For the sake of brevity, we do not repeat the argument here.
Multiplying both sides of (
39
) from the right by
d_{w}^{-\frac{L-1}{2}}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}AX_{\mathsf{r}}
, subtracting
X_{\mathsf{r}}
, taking norms, and applying the triangle inequality and the bound
|1-C_{\mathsf{prod}}|\leq\nicefrac{{2\eta\lambda}}{{m}}
from
Lemma

C.7
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\Phi_{\mathsf{r}}(t+1)\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}

Lemma 4.14

4.6
Step 3: Convergence off the subspace

In this section, we show that the off-subspace error depends on the hidden width.
To determine the behavior off the subspace, we must consider the projection onto
{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}
, which we denote
P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}.

Note that
P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}=P_{\ker(Y^{\mathsf{T}})}
and so

\displaystyle W_{1}(t+1)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}

\displaystyle=W_{1}(t)\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda}{m}\right)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}-\eta\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{w}^{L-1}m}}W_{L:2}^{\mathsf{T}}\Phi(t)Y^{\mathsf{T}}P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda}{m}\right)W_{1}(t)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda}{m}\right)^{t+1}W_{1}(0)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}.

By event
\mathcal{B}(t)
from
Equation

63

\displaystyle\lVert W_{L:1}(t)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\leq\lVert W_{L:2}(t)\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\lVert W_{1}(t)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\lesssim d_{w}^{\frac{L-1}{2}}\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda}{m}\right)^{t}\lVert W_{1}{(0)}P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}.

Normalizing both sides, we obtain that the learned mapping

satisfies

\displaystyle\lVert F(t)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda}{m}\right)^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\lVert W_{1}(0)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}.

Using the bound

\lVert F(T)P_{\mathrm{range}(Y)}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\lesssim\left(1-\frac{\eta\lambda}{m}\right)^{T}\sqrt{\frac{d_{w}}{m}}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{T\eta\lambda}{m}+\frac{1}{2}\log(d_{w})\right)=d_{w}^{-\frac{3}{2}},

5
Conclusions and future directions

Adaptivity of nonlinear networks to nonlinear structure

This work proves that neural networks can automatically adapt to subspace structure in training data when trained with weight decay using standard initialization and hyperparameter schemes; future work includes extending this framework beyond the simplified setting we consider here to explore nonlinear networks and more complex models of training data structure.
For example, a natural next step would be to study what kind of low-dimensional structure neural networks with one ReLU layer can automatically adapt to when trained with
\ell_{2}
-regularized gradient descent.
As shown in
Figure

1(b)
, preliminary experiments suggest that this procedure can lead to robustness in the setting of data from a union of subspaces model, which is known to closely approximate patches from real-world images
[
61
]
.

Benefit of depth

Previous work has suggested a significant advantage of deeper networks over shallower ones in adapting to low-rank structure
[
34
,
30
,
31
]
.
Consistent with these findings, preliminary experiments in
Section

D.1.1
suggest that depth is beneficial for both the regression and the “off-subspace” errors: in
Figure

4
we see that larger depth, at least up to a certain point, leads to faster convergence. This phenomenon is not covered by our main theoretical result, but constitutes an interesting direction for future work.

Lemma A.1

by
f(x)=\lVert Mx\rVert
.
For any

,

\left|\lVert Mx\rVert-\lVert My\rVert\right|\leq\lVert M(x-y)\rVert\leq\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\lVert x-y\rVert.

Therefore
f
is
L
-Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
L=\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
.
From Theorem 5.2.2 in
[
62
]
it follows that if

is
L
-Lipschitz and
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
then for all
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{{|f(X)-\mathbb{E}f(X)|\geq t}\right\}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{c^{2}L^{2}}\right)

(105)

for some constant
c>0
.
Applying this with
f(x)=\lVert Mx\rVert
,
L=\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}
,
t=c\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\sqrt{\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}})}
, we get that

\lVert MX\rVert\lesssim\mathbb{E}[\lVert MX\rVert]+\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{op}}\sqrt{\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}})}\leq\mathbb{E}[\lVert MX\rVert]+\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\sqrt{\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}})}

with probability at least
1-\alpha
.
Using Jensen’s inequality and the expected value of a quadratic form, we see that

\mathbb{E}[\lVert MX\rVert]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\lVert MX\rVert^{2}]=\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

Therefore,

\lVert M\varepsilon\rVert=\sigma\lVert MX\rVert\lesssim\sigma(1+\sqrt{\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}})})\lVert M\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}

with probability

\square

Lemma C.2
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{q}
have i.i.d. Gaussian elements with
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times n_{i-1}}
,
n_{0}=n
,
and
n_{i}\gtrsim q
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{\big\lvert\lVert A_{q}\cdots A_{1}y\rVert^{2}-\lVert y\rVert^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{q}n_{i}\big\rvert\geq 0.1\lVert y\rVert^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{q}n_{i}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{q}n_{i}^{-1}}\right)\right),

(106)

where
y
is any fixed vector.

Proof.

Note that for any
A_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert A_{i}y\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\langle(A_{i})_{j,:},y\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(d)}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\lVert y\rVert^{2}g_{i}^{2}\qquad(g_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1))

\displaystyle\overset{(d)}{=}\lVert y\rVert^{2}Z_{i},

where
Z_{i}\sim\chi^{2}_{n_{i}}
, a
\chi^{2}
-random variable with
n_{i}
degrees of freedom. As a result,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\lVert A_{i}y\rVert^{2}}\right]=\lVert y\rVert^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{i}}\right]=\lVert y\rVert^{2}\cdot n_{i}.

Moreover, since
A_{1},\dots,A_{q}
are independent, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\lVert A_{q}\dots A_{1}y\rVert^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{E}\left[{\lVert A_{q}(A_{q-1}\dots A_{1}y)\rVert^{2}\mid A_{1},\dots,A_{q-1}}\right]}\right]

\displaystyle=n_{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\lVert A_{q-1}\dots A_{1}y\rVert^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=n_{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{E}\left[{\lVert A_{q-1}\dots A_{1}y\rVert^{2}\mid A_{1},\dots,A_{q-2}}\right]}\right]

\displaystyle=n_{q}\cdot n_{q-1}\mathbb{E}\left[{\lVert A_{q-2}\dots A_{1}y\rVert^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\dots

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{q}n_{i}\cdot\lVert y\rVert^{2},

by iterating the above construction.

We now prove
Equation

106
. Let
\lVert y\rVert=1
for simplicity;
then
\lVert A_{q}\dots A_{1}y\rVert^{2}\sim Z_{q}Z_{q-1}\dots Z_{1}
, where
Z_{i}\sim\chi^{2}_{n_{i}}
.
The moments of a random variable
X\sim\chi^{2}_{k}
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{\lambda}]

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\lambda}\Gamma(\frac{k}{2}+\lambda)}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})}=\frac{2^{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{k+2\lambda}}\left(\frac{k+2\lambda}{2e}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}+\lambda}}{\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{k}}\left(\frac{k}{2e}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}}\left(1+O(1/k)\right),

for all
\lambda>-k/2
, with the second equality furnished by a Stirling approximation.
Following Eq. (20) in
[
35
]
, we obtain the following upper bound:

\mathbb{E}[X^{\lambda}]\leq\exp\left(\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{k}-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{2\lambda}{k}\right)+\lambda\log k\right)\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\right),\quad\forall\lambda\geq-\frac{k}{4}.

(107)

To bound the upper tail in
Equation

106
, we argue that for any
\lambda>0
and any small constant
c>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{Z_{q}\dots Z_{1}\geq\exp(c)\prod_{i=1}^{q}n_{i}}\right\}\leq\exp\left(-\lambda c+2\lambda^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{n_{i}}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{q}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n_{i}}\right)\right),

(108)

similar to Appendix C in
[
35
]
but replacing
m^{q}
by
\prod_{i=1}^{q}n_{i}
and adjusting the probability bound accordingly.
Under our assumption that

, we see that

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{q}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{n_{i}}\right)\right)\lesssim\left(1+\frac{O(1)}{q}\right)^{q}\leq O(1)

(109)

using the definition of the exponential.
Optimizing the term

over

yields

\lambda_{\star}=\frac{c}{4\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{n_{i}}}.

Plugging in value of
\lambda_{\star}

leads to

\displaystyle-\lambda_{\star}c+2\lambda_{\star}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{n_{i}}=-\frac{c^{2}}{4}\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{q}n_{i}^{-1}}+\frac{c^{2}}{8}\cdot\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{n_{i}}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}}=-\frac{c^{2}}{8}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{q}n_{i}^{-1}}.

Choosing

completes the proof.

We now derive the lower bound in

106

Given

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{Z_{q}\dots Z_{1}\leq\exp(-c)\prod_{i=1}^{q}n_{i}}\right\}\leq\exp\left(\lambda c+2\lambda^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{n_{i}}-2\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{1}{n_{i}}\right)

Lemma C.3
.

Given a matrix

whose columns lie in
\mathrm{range}(R)

By the min-max principle, the

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}(AM)=\max_{S:\dim(S)=j}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in S\\
\lVert v\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\lVert AMv\rVert.

\mathrm{range}(R)

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}(AM)\leq\sqrt{1+\delta}\max_{S:\dim(S)=j}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in S\\

Lemma C.4
.

The unique solution of the minimization problem

W_{\mathrm{oracle}}=\arg\min_{W}\|W\|_{F}^{2}\text{ subject to }WY=X

(112)

is given by
W_{\mathrm{oracle}}=XY^{\dagger}=R(AR)^{\dagger}
.

Proof.

All solutions of the linear equation

are of the form

W=XY^{\dagger}+M(I-YY^{\dagger})

(113)

with
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
an arbitrary matrix.
Given the above and noticing that the two summands are a mapping onto the row space of
Y
and a mapping onto the orthogonal complement of that row space. Therefore the two mappings are orthogonal two each other and we can rewrite the minimization problem as

\arg\min_{W}\lVert W\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}=\arg\min_{M};\lVert XY^{\dagger}+M(I-YY^{\dagger})\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}=\lVert XY^{\dagger}\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\arg\min_{M}\lVert M(I-YY^{\dagger}\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}

(114)

Minimizing over
M
we obtain

and therefore the minimizer is
XY^{\dagger}
as claimed. Plugging in the definitions of
Y
and
X
we obtain

XY^{\dagger}=RZ(ARZ)^{\dagger}=RZZ^{\dagger}(AR)^{\dagger}=R(AR)^{\dagger}

since
Z
has full row rank.

\square

Theorem C.6

(Weierstrass)
.

The following inequality holds:

1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(1-x_{i}\right)^{w_{i}},\quad\text{for all $x\in[0,1]$ and $w_{i}\geq 1$}.

(116)

Proof.

We prove the inequality by induction on the number of
terms. For the base case
n=1
, consider the function

h(w)=(1-x_{1})^{w}-(1-wx_{1}),\quad\text{with}\;\;h^{\prime}(w)=(1-x_{1})^{w}\log(1-x_{1})+x_{1}

Clearly
h(1)=0
, so it suffices to show
h
is increasing on
[1,\infty)
. Starting from the inequality

\log(1-x_{1})\geq\frac{x_{1}}{x_{1}-1},

we have

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(w)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{x_{1}(1-x_{1})^{w}}{x_{1}-1}+x_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{1}(1-x_{1})^{w}+x_{1}(x_{1}-1)}{x_{1}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{1}\left[(1-x_{1})-(1-x_{1})^{w}\right]}{1-x_{1}}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad\text{for all $w\geq 1$,}

since
(1-x_{1})\in(0,1)
. This proves the claim
for
n=1
.

Now suppose the claim holds up to some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We have

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{n+1}(1-x_{j})^{w_{j}}

\displaystyle=(1-x_{n+1})^{w_{n+1}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-x_{j})^{w_{j}}

\displaystyle\geq(1-w_{n+1}x_{n+1})\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-x_{j})^{w_{j}}

\displaystyle\geq(1-w_{n+1}x_{n+1})\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}x_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}w_{j}x_{j}+w_{n+1}x_{n+1}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}x_{j}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}w_{j}x_{j},

where the first inequality follows from the base
case, the second inequality follows by the inductive
hypothesis and the last inequality follows from
nonnegativity of
\{w_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
and
\{x_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
. This completes the proof.

\square

Lemma C.9

D.3
Union of Subspaces Experiment

In this subsection, we describe the union-of-subspaces (UoS) model used to generate the
synthetic training data in
Figure

1(b)
.
The UoS model stipulates that each vector in the input
data belongs to one of
k
subspaces. Formally, there exists a
collection
\mathcal{R}:=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{k}\}
, where
R_{i}\in O(d,s)
,
such that
x^{i}\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{range}(R_{j})
for all
i
.
In our experiments, we generate training samples from the union-of-subspaces model as follows:

•

Sample
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
according to
the procedure described in
Section

D.2
.

•

For each
j=1,\dots,k
:

1.

Sample a
d\times s
random Gaussian matrix
R_{j}^{\mathrm{full}}
.

2.

Set
R_{j}
equal to the
Q
factor from the reduced QR factorization of

R_{j}^{\mathrm{full}}
.

•

For each
i=1,\dots,n
:

1.

Sample
R\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{R})
.

2.

Set
X_{:,i}=RZ_{:,i}
.

D.3.1
Neural network architecture for the union-of-subspaces model

The inverse mapping for linear inverse problems with data from a union-of-subspaces model is in general nonlinear for
k>1
— as
a result, deep linear networks are not a suitable choice for learning
the inverse mapping.
Nevertheless, it is known that the inverse mapping is approximated to arbitrary accuracy
by a piecewise-linear mapping (see
[
63
]
), which can be realized as a multi-index model of the form
g(V^{\mathsf{T}}x)
for
suitable
V
and vector-valued mapping
g
. Guided by this, we use a
neural network architecture defined as follows:

f_{\{W_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}}(x)=W_{L}\left(W_{L-1}W_{L-2}\cdots W_{1}x\right)_{+},

(122)

where
W_{1},\dots,W_{L}
are learnable weight matrices and

[\cdot]_{+}
denotes the (elementwise) positive part, equivalent to using a ReLU activation at the
(L-1)^{\text{th}}
hidden layer.
Indeed, recent results
[
30
]
suggest that neural
networks of the form (
122
) are biased
towards multi-index models such as the one sought to approximate the
inverse mapping. Finally, all the networks from
Figure

1
were trained for
100,000
iterations with learning rate
\eta=\nicefrac{{m}}{{2L\sigma_{\max}^{2}(X)}}
.
