Social Determinants of Preoperative Polysomnography and Surgical Outcomes in Pediatric Tonsillectomy
Authors: Austin Bridges, Varun Pasapula, Zoe Zhang, Ron B Mitchell, Seckin Ulualp, Stephen R Chorney, Felicity Lenes‐Voit, Cynthia S Wang, Yann‐Fuu Kou, Kenneth Lee, Christopher Liu, Romaine F Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo evaluate how sociodemographic and neighborhood factors are associated with use of preoperative polysomnography (PSG) in children undergoing tonsillectomy, and to assess whether these factors are associated with return for postoperative PSG, surgical success, or postoperative complications.MethodsWe analyzed 940 children undergoing tonsillectomy at a tertiary children's hospital (2016–2024). Multivariable logistic regression estimated associations between sociodemographic factors (race, ethnicity, insurance, language), neighborhood opportunity (Child Opportunity Index [COI]), and preoperative PSG. Sequential models examined pathways of association. Introduction

Adenotonsillectomy is the first‐line surgical treatment for pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), with hundreds of thousands performed annually [1]. Guidelines recommend polysomnography (PSG) when diagnostic uncertainty exists or comorbidities such as Down syndrome, neuromuscular disorders, craniofacial anomalies, or obesity are present [1, 2, 3]. Despite these recommendations, PSG use varies widely; fewer than 10% of children receive preoperative PSG [4, 5].

Prior research has shown that sociodemographic and neighborhood factors (race, ethnicity, language, insurance, and neighborhood opportunity) are associated with access to diagnostic testing and specialty care [6, 7, 8, 9]. Social determinants of health (SDOH) frequently are associated with entry points into surgical care, including referrals and initial evaluations, even when downstream hospital‐based care is standardized [10]. Most pediatric tonsillectomy research has examined either clinical predictors of PSG use or postoperative outcomes in isolation, without linking social factors to the full care pathway [11, 12, 13, 14]. This leaves unresolved whether differential testing rates are consistent with appropriate risk stratification or structural inequity. Prior work documents that Black and publicly insured children face barriers to surgical care for sleep‐disordered breathing [6, 7], Yet whether the same groups are correspondingly less likely to receive preoperative testing, or whether diagnostic and surgical access diverge, remains unexamined. No prior study has linked the sociodemographic patterning of preoperative PSG to downstream surgical success within a single cohort.

The aim of this study was to evaluate how race, ethnicity, primary language, insurance status, and neighborhood opportunity are associated with preoperative PSG use among children undergoing tonsillectomy at a tertiary care pediatric center. Secondary objectives assessed whether these factors are associated with use of postoperative PSG, surgical success, or complications. We hypothesized that SDOH would be associated with preoperative PSG use but that downstream surgical outcomes would be associated primarily with preoperative OSA severity and medical complexity rather than sociodemographic characteristics [10, 15, 16]. By linking diagnostic pathways to surgical outcomes, this study sought to distinguish potential inequities in access from patterns consistent with risk‐informed evaluation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

A retrospective cohort study was conducted at Children's Health Dallas, a tertiary pediatric referral center affiliated with UT Southwestern Medical Center. The UT Southwestern Institutional Review Board approved the study (STU‐2023‐0260) with a waiver of informed consent.

Participants

This was a secondary analysis of a cohort assembled for an institutional study of pediatric tonsillectomy practice patterns over time. Tonsillectomies (with or without adenoidectomy) performed between January 2016 and December 2024 were identified from an institutional operating‐room reporting dashboard maintained in Epic (approximately 2000 procedures annually). Children were excluded if they were younger than 2 years (precluding CDC BMI‐percentile calculation), had a tracheostomy at the time of surgery, or underwent revision tonsillectomy. The study period was divided into 2‐year blocks; within each block, every eligible case was assigned a uniform random number (Stata runiform(), seed 12,345), and cases with the lowest values were retained (a period‐stratified random sample with equal selection probability within each interval), yielding 940 children. The parent study's sample size was determined by its primary aim: detecting change over time in the prevalence of obesity and of severe OSA (AHI > 10 events/h), each from 10% to 30%, which required approximately 250–350 children at 80% power (α = 0.05); the achieved sample of 940 exceeded this. No a priori power analysis was performed for the sociodemographic associations examined here; accordingly, null secondary findings are interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no association.

Exposures and Covariates

Race and ethnicity were recorded by caregiver report. Race was categorized as White, Black, Asian, American Indian/Alaska Native/Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander (AIAN/NHPI), or Other/Unknown. Ethnicity was categorized as Hispanic or non‐Hispanic. Primary household language was dichotomized as English or non‐English to approximate limited English proficiency. Insurance was grouped as commercial, Medicaid, or other.

Neighborhood context was measured with the Child Opportunity Index (COI) version 3.0, a nationally normed, census‐tract–level composite of children's neighborhood resources developed by the diversitydatakids.org project at Boston University [17]. The COI 3.0 aggregates 44 indicators across three domains (education, health and environment, and social and economic) into a single opportunity score for each U.S. census tract, with higher scores indicating greater neighborhood opportunity. Each child's residential census tract was linked to the COI and analyzed as a standardized z score and by national quintiles. In sensitivity analyses, a zip code–level social capital index replaced COI.

Obesity was defined as body mass index at or above the 95th percentile for age and sex per Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth charts. Medical complexity was defined a priori as the presence of Down syndrome, a craniofacial anomaly, or congenital cardiac disease. When preoperative PSG was available, OSA severity was characterized using the apnea‐hypopnea index (AHI); severe OSA was defined as AHI greater than 10 events per hour.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was preoperative PSG receipt (yes/no). Secondary outcomes were: (1) any postoperative complication, defined as a composite of post‐tonsillectomy hemorrhage, unplanned readmission within 30 days, unplanned intensive care unit (ICU) admission, or severe respiratory event; (2) return for postoperative PSG among children with preoperative PSG; and (3) surgical success among children with both pre‐ and postoperative PSG, defined as postoperative AHI less than 5 events per hour.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages; continuous variables as medians and interquartile ranges. Group differences were assessed using χ 2 tests and Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests.

Multivariable logistic regression estimated aORs and 95% CIs for the association between sociodemographic characteristics and preoperative PSG receipt, adjusting for age, sex, obesity, and medical complexity. Sequential models progressively added insurance, neighborhood measures, and language to examine attenuation of race and ethnicity estimates.

Missing COI values were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations under a missing‐at‐random assumption (20 imputed datasets; estimates combined using Rubin's rules). To assess the missing‐at‐random assumption, we compared sociodemographic characteristics (race, ethnicity, primary household language, and insurance) between children with and without available COI data. Sensitivity analyses included complete‐case models and substitution of the social capital index for COI.

Among children with preoperative PSG, logistic regression modeled return for postoperative PSG. Stabilized inverse probability weights (IPWs) for follow‐up, truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles, were applied to logistic regression models of surgical success to account for differential follow‐up. For the PSG–complication association, propensity scores were estimated from a logistic regression model, and doubly robust models combined inverse‐probability‐of‐treatment weighting with covariate adjustment. All tests were 2‐sided (α = 0.05). Analyses were conducted using Stata 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas).

Preoperative Polysomnography

Preoperative PSG was performed in 389 of 940 children (41.4%; 384/925 [41.5%] in the analytic cohort). In unadjusted analyses, PSG use was higher among Black than White children, Hispanic than non‐Hispanic children, Medicaid‐insured than commercially insured children, and children from non‐English‐speaking households. PSG use declined stepwise across increasing COI quintiles, from approximately half of children in the lowest‐opportunity quintile to one quarter in the highest (Table 1; Figure 1).

In multivariable models, Black race, non‐English language, Medicaid insurance, and lower COI each remained associated with higher odds of preoperative PSG (Table 2). Black children had higher odds than White children (aOR, 1.79; 95% CI, 1.18–2.72). Children from non‐English‐speaking households had more than twice the odds of those from English‐speaking households (aOR, 2.26; 95% CI, 1.45–3.52). Medicaid insurance (aOR, 1.52; 95% CI, 1.06–2.17) and lower neighborhood opportunity (aOR per 1‐SD increase in COI, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.67–0.99) were also independently associated with PSG receipt. Obesity (aOR, 2.49; 95% CI, 1.77–3.51) had the second‐highest association with preoperative PSG after medical complexity (aOR, 4.05; 95% CI, 2.17–7.59). Hispanic ethnicity was not independently associated with PSG after full adjustment (aOR, 1.16; 95% CI, 0.79–1.72; Figure 2).

Follow‐Up Polysomnography and Surgical Success

Among 384 children with preoperative PSG, 170 (44.3%) had postoperative PSG. Higher preoperative AHI (aOR per unit, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.04–1.07) and medical complexity (aOR, 2.19; 95% CI, 1.08–4.45) were associated with follow‐up; sociodemographic and neighborhood factors were not (Table 3).

Among 200 children with both pre‐ and postoperative PSG, 143 (71.5%) achieved surgical success. After IPW adjustment for differential follow‐up, higher preoperative AHI remained associated with lower odds of surgical success (aOR per unit, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.94–0.98). Race, ethnicity, insurance, language, and neighborhood opportunity were not associated with surgical success (Table 3). Several point estimates fell below 1.0 with wide confidence intervals: Black race, aOR 0.45 (95% CI, 0.11–1.88); non‐English language, aOR 0.55 (95% CI, 0.18–1.64); and medical complexity, aOR 0.47 (95% CI, 0.17–1.34).

Postoperative Complications

Overall, 83 of 937 children (8.9%) experienced at least one postoperative complication: post‐tonsillectomy hemorrhage in 57 (6.1%), readmission within 30 days in 55 (5.9%), ICU admission in 11 (1.2%), and severe respiratory events in 8 (0.9%). Complications were more common among children with preoperative PSG than those without (11.3% vs. 7.1%). In doubly robust models, preoperative PSG remained associated with increased complication odds (aOR, 2.07; 95% CI, 1.09–3.93; Table 4).

Discussion

Preoperative PSG use was associated with both clinical and social factors, with higher use among Black children, children from non‐English‐speaking households, Medicaid‐insured children, and those from lower‐opportunity neighborhoods, associations that persisted after adjusting for obesity and medical complexity. Obesity was the second‐strongest factor associated with preoperative PSG after medical complexity, consistent with current practice guidelines that identify obesity as an indication for preoperative sleep testing in selected children with suspected OSA. In contrast, sociodemographic factors were not associated with surgical success, which was associated with OSA severity. Postoperative complications were more frequent among children who had a PSG, but this finding likely reflects confounding by indication rather than any effect of PSG itself.

The sequential regression models characterize the social patterning of PSG use. The crude association between Hispanic ethnicity and PSG attenuated by approximately 80% once insurance, neighborhood opportunity, and language were added. This pattern suggests that the observed association with Hispanic ethnicity may be explained, in part, by structural factors including Medicaid insurance, lower‐opportunity neighborhoods, and non‐English language. The association for Black race, by contrast, showed only 22% attenuation despite progressive adjustment, suggesting that additional unmeasured factors may be relevant. Primary household language remained a stable, independent correlate across all models. These patterns point to a clinical decision environment in which social and clinical signals are intertwined.

Clinical practice guidelines establish indications for PSG in children with suspected OSA [3]. In our cohort, sociodemographic factors remained associated with PSG use even after adjustment for guideline‐indicated clinical risk. The data cannot determine the mechanisms underlying this differential testing. Several explanations are plausible. First, clinician decision‐making may vary by patient race. Visits with Black families during pediatric surgical consultations show greater clinician verbal dominance and less elicitation of family concern [18], which could lower the threshold for objective testing. Implicit racial and ethnic bias among healthcare professionals has been associated with treatment decisions and patient‐clinician communication [19, 20]. Second, insurance‐based protocols may be associated with differential PSG use independent of clinical assessment; Medicaid prior‐authorization, in particular, can mandate objective testing before surgical authorization [21]. Third, language discordance may constrain history‐taking. Clinicians may substitute objective testing for aspects of the clinical assessment that are more difficult to complete, paralleling the broader association between limited English proficiency and altered perioperative care [22]. More generally, when the clinical history is harder to obtain, whether because of language discordance, lower health literacy, or less documented prior care, clinicians may substitute objective testing for elements of the assessment they cannot readily complete; this diagnostic‐substitution pathway need not involve bias and may be the most parsimonious explanation for several of the observed associations. Finally, family preferences may also be relevant. Families with prior negative healthcare experiences may seek objective diagnostic confirmation before consenting to surgery, but in most contexts mistrust is associated with reduced rather than increased engagement with invasive testing [23, 24], making a family‐driven explanation for higher PSG use less intuitively aligned with these data. Surveys of families and clinicians, audits of institutional ordering protocols, and direct observation of consultations are needed to clarify their relative contributions.

The disconnect between social patterning of diagnostic testing and subsequent surgical outcomes is a central finding. Race, ethnicity, language, insurance, and neighborhood opportunity were not associated with surgical success once children received care; success was associated with OSA severity and medical complexity, consistent with established literature identifying baseline disease severity as the primary correlate of persistent OSA [25, 26, 27]. Technical surgical outcomes appear equitable within this tertiary system, though power was limited (n = 200; 143 successes) and several sociodemographic point estimates fell below 1.0 with wide confidence intervals. Our cohort reflects upstream referral patterns; however, the broader equity question may lie earlier in the care pathway, in who reaches tertiary surgical evaluation at all.

Children who underwent preoperative PSG had higher postoperative complication rates (11.3% vs. 7.1%), a difference that persisted in doubly robust models (aOR, 2.07; 95% CI, 1.09–3.93). This most likely reflects confounding by indication: the clinical and social factors associated with obtaining PSG are also associated with higher surgical risk [11]. PSG therefore likely functions as a marker of higher‐risk care, and residual confounding by severity is expected even after extensive adjustment [10].

Our results extend the pediatric disparities literature. While Sutton et al. [6] and Boss et al. [7] document that Black and publicly insured children face barriers to receiving tonsillectomy, our data show a different pattern at the diagnostic phase: these same groups were more likely to undergo preoperative PSG. We add to prior work in three ways. By integrating the neighborhood‐level COI, we found that neighborhood factors were independently associated with PSG after adjustment for medical complexity [11, 28]. Pathway modeling suggested that insurance was associated with much of the observed Hispanic ethnicity‐PSG association, whereas language and Black race remained distinct, independent correlates. And by linking diagnostic use to downstream outcomes, we found similar surgical success across sociodemographic groups among children who received care.

This study is strengthened by a large, randomly sampled cohort spanning 9 years, detailed PSG and perioperative data, neighborhood‐level measures, and electronic medical record data. Methodological strengths include multiple imputation, inverse‐probability weighting for differential follow‐up, and doubly robust estimation. As a single‐center study, these findings may not generalize to regions with different referral patterns, payer mixes, or PSG availability. Residual confounding by unmeasured severity (tonsillar hypertrophy grade, craniofacial morphology, oxygen reserve) likely biases the PSG‐complication association away from the null; the underlying association is probably smaller than observed. We also lacked individual‐level measures of parental educational attainment and health literacy; because area‐level opportunity imperfectly proxies these family‐level factors, residual confounding by household socioeconomic position and literacy cannot be excluded, and some association attributed to neighborhood or language may operate through these unmeasured pathways. Area‐level SDOH measures cannot capture family‐level socioeconomic status; within‐neighborhood heterogeneity would attenuate SDOH associations toward the null, suggesting our estimates may understate the magnitude of association. Postoperative PSG was obtained in fewer than half of children with preoperative PSG; if higher‐risk children were less likely to return, surgical success rates may be overestimated, though weighting methods mitigated this bias. The 41% preoperative PSG rate exceeds the < 10% rate reported nationally, reflecting upstream referral selection. SDOH associations are likely attenuated relative to a community‐based sample, in which children most exposed to upstream barriers may never reach tertiary surgical evaluation. Tonsillectomy technique (intracapsular vs. extracapsular) was not extracted from the medical record. If technique varied by sociodemographic group, it could confound both bleeding risk and persistent apnea. The parent project was powered for obesity prevalence, not for the secondary outcomes examined here, so several null findings for sociodemographic predictors of surgical success and follow‐up may reflect insufficient power rather than true absence of association. Finally, we lacked data on insurance benefit design. Because many commercial plans carry high deductibles and copayments, out‐of‐pocket costs for polysomnography may deter privately insured families from testing while largely sparing Medicaid‐insured families; this unmeasured cost pathway could contribute to the insurance differences in PSG use that we observed. Taken together, the principal biases operate in opposing directions and thus bracket rather than uniformly distort the findings: unmeasured disease severity biases the PSG–complication estimate away from the null, so its true magnitude is likely smaller than observed, whereas area‐level measurement and tertiary referral selection bias the sociodemographic associations toward the null, so their true magnitude is likely larger than observed.

Conclusion

Social and neighborhood factors were associated with preoperative PSG use. Sociodemographic factors were not associated with surgical success, though power was limited in that subset and clinically meaningful disparities cannot be excluded. Differential PSG use may reflect a mix of clinician decision‐making, system‐level requirements such as insurance protocols, and family preferences that this study cannot disentangle. Quality improvement efforts should monitor PSG utilization by sociodemographic characteristics to assess whether observed patterns are more consistent with clinical need or with structural and decisional factors, including institutional protocols, communication patterns, and implicit bias. Observational, qualitative, and audit studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms. The more pressing equity question lies upstream: ensuring that vulnerable children are not under‐referred or under‐evaluated before reaching tertiary care.
