A hybrid cluster-then-predict machine learning radiotherapy knowledge-based planning framework for similarity matching using holistic target-OAR constellation geometry
Authors: Trent Benedick, J Daniel Chu, Stephanie Zhou, Raphiporn Duangrat, John Asbach, Ryan H B Smith, Anh H Le, Brent Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRadiotherapy treatment planning is currently premised on individual clinical experience and use of many dose based optimization and knowledge-based planning (KBP) models. This study introduces and validates a novel KBP algorithm that matches cases based on a holistic model of the target to Organ at Risk (OAR) constellation geometry to assist during treatment plan creation by identifying retrospective cases with similar target-OAR constellation geometry for use as reference cases to serve as planning templates.MethodsWe developed a multi step algorithm to calculate geometric similarity between radiotherapy cases. The method extracts quantitative geometric features (Overlap Volume Histograms and Spatial Target Signatures) to characterize the spatial relationships between all target volumes and OARs. Component, partial, dissimilarities between corresponding features are calculated with an Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD). A final, clinically relevant similarity score is computed using a hybrid cluster-then-predict machine learning approach (a hard-gated Mixture-of-Experts architecture), where cases are first partitioned by spectral clustering, then separate supervised models are trained within each cluster to predict case similarity.ResultsThe algorithm was validated with a knowledge base of 192 retrospective Head and Neck Cancer cases. A leave-one-out cross-validation demonstrated a strong correlation (r = 0.8009) between our KBP model’s geometry based similarity score and actual dose similarity. Additionally, a clinical evaluation with an ROC analysis yielded a high area under the curve (AUC) of 0.8797, confirming the model’s ability to identify cases the clinicians determined were clinically relevant as reference cases to serve as planning templates.ConclusionsThis study validates our novel KBP algorithm that successfully identifies clinically relevant retrospective cases using a comprehensive geometric model of target-OAR constellations. By providing personalized patient specific, knowledge base best practice reference case data before beam configuration and treatment plan creation, this method provides a useful new tool to increase standardization, improve plan quality, and assist clinicians during initial plan creation.Keywords: Radiation therapy, Treatment planning support, Decision support, Knowledge-based treatment planning, Machine learning

Introduction

Safely delivering curative doses of radiation to tumor target volumes requires minimizing exposure to surrounding healthy tissues and Organs at Risk (OARs) to limit normal tissue toxicity. Highly conformal treatment plans that account for the complex spatial relationships between the target volumes and adjacent anatomy can increase the therapeutic index of radiotherapy. Modern planning techniques, such as Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (VMAT), allow for rapid spatial fall-off in deposited dose, making the precise geometric arrangement of the target-OAR constellation a critical determinant of plan quality.

In External Beam Radiotherapy (EBRT), the beam field arrangement and configuration are fundamentally dictated by the patient-specific anatomy. Because the spatial distribution of OARs relative to the target limits the achievable dose fall-off, these geometric relationships drive both treatment plan quality and complexity [1–3]. It has been shown that cases with similar shape and size and location tend to be treated with similar dose distributions [4, 5]. In this paper, we present an algorithmic method to match radiotherapy cases based on target-OAR constellation geometry, a holistic measure of the tumor position and shape relative to surrounding anatomy. This approach identifies similar retrospective cases to serve as reference cases for benchmarking and planning support.

Previous work in planning and structural comparisons

Individualized treatment planning for radiotherapy is a comprehensive process which considers many patient- and tumor-specific factors. The initial treatment planning concerns are tumor type, staging, and size which dictate the prescribed dose required for locoregional control as well as physician-delineated Clinical Target Volumes (CTVs) for each level of dose prescription. The tumor location, shape, and position relative to OARs and the surface influence the treatment configuration. The position of surrounding OARs dictates the beam selection and modulation required to spare healthy tissues, meaning the achievable dose distribution is inherently constrained by the spatial orientation and angular proximity of healthy tissues relative to the target volumes throughout the treatment arc. Consequently, the fundamental goal of Knowledge-Based Planning (KBP) is to identify these achievable dose distributions to drive the optimization process [1, 6–8].

Treatment planning support attempts to assist in the identification of an optimal treatment plan reflecting these concerns. Most current work in algorithmic radiotherapy treatment planning support focuses on direct methods to synthesize and recommend optimized dose distributions or Dose-Volume Histogram (DVH) objectives for a specific patient. Many of these methods rely on KBP approaches where insights are derived from retrospective case data and fitted to the current planning cases through algorithmic methods based on the relation of the current planning case to the retrospective case or different aspects of the current case’s identity. The most prevalent approaches focus on comparisons of DVHs from current plan cases to either retrospective cases or derived models to help predict optimal DVHs for current planning cases [7–11]. Algorithmic approaches in use range from direct registration Dice comparisons to multi-step computational models. Some recent multi-step and multi-factor computation methods have started to integrate aspects of the radiotherapy cases other than their dose distributions to identify an optimal treatment plan. In particular, several methods integrate components of partial geometry-based matching as a part of an overall dose matching method to identify optimal DVHs from a knowledge base of retrospective cases [12–14]. Previous work has established that radiotherapy cases with similar target positioning relative to surrounding anatomy tend to receive similar dose treatments, which supports direct consideration of geometry as a potentially more informative anatomic proxy for achievable dose distributions [4, 5].

Sporadic efforts have incorporated partial aspects of the spatial geometric relationship of the target volumes to surrounding anatomy as part of overall dose matching and optimization algorithms. This previous geometry-based work primarily falls into two categories. One of the approaches relies on direct machine learning methods of direct or modified registration of tumors, target volumes, or OARs between cases followed by evaluation of meshed object similarity. This work has been limited to specific pathologies, particularly cervical and pancreatic cancer, and stages where the target location, shape, size, and relation to the surrounding OARs is highly consistent between patients [7, 8, 12–14]. The other approach uses the creation of quantitative metrics to define spatial relationships of anatomical objects and target volumes. However, as the purpose of these quantitative metrics have been to assist in optimal DVH prediction for particular OARs, they have not been used on multiple OARs simultaneously or with the intention of creating a comprehensive overall model of the target-OAR constellation geometry [4, 5, 15–17]. Our work advances these trends by using pairwise quantitative features between all target volumes and all OARs to construct a holistic model of target-OAR constellation geometry.

Target-OAR constellation similarity matching

We present a method to create a holistic and comprehensive model of target-OAR constellation geometry and a method to match cases based on constellation geometry similarity. Most other KBP methods are used for plan optimization after initial plan configurations with dose data as the core inputs. In contrast, this approach enables, during initial plan configuration of a current case, the identification and use of treatment plans from anatomically similar retrospective cases as references and templates prior to the creation of dose data [18–21]. This method builds upon previous work and infrastructure for modeling target-OAR constellations. However, the linear models employed in the prior work applied uniform weighting to each target-OAR relationship, which proved insufficient for accurately matching cases based on their complex, non-linear target-OAR constellation [22]. Therefore, we expand on this work and infrastructure with a novel cluster-then-predict hard-gated mixture of experts (MoE) machine learning architecture that reflects the non-linear anatomical complexity of spatial geometric relationships. Relying on an evenly weighted measure of each target-OAR relationship is unlikely to be optimal as clinicians prioritize certain OARs and anatomical relationships during planning.

Our comprehensive target-OAR constellation modeling combines multiple quantitative features each characterizing different aspects of the constellation to give quantitative representation to the anatomical structure of the case and move towards more personalized medicine. This method uses two quantitative features, the Overlap Volume Histogram (OVH) and the Spatial Target Signature (STS), to quantify the pairwise distance and spatial distributional relationship between target volumes (the Gross Target Volumes [GTVs] and CTVs) and OARs. For inter-case comparisons corresponding quantitative features are matched together to enable simultaneous use of all the features composing our comprehensive target-OAR constellation geometry during similarity matching. The comprehensive similarity matching model is powered by machine learning methods trained from dose similarity between cases to give a more accurate representation of the relation between anatomical similarity and desired dose distribution. This method, through comparison of multiple GTVs and CTVs, incorporates the prescribed dose levels and volumes implicitly reflecting and including the staging and desired dose distribution.

Methods

Dataset and materials

This study utilizes a knowledge base of 192 head and neck VMAT cases from Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center SUNY Buffalo, which were used for model evaluation. All cases were treated with a simultaneous integrated boost (SIB) technique delivered in 35 fractions. Treatment plans were generated using the Varian Eclipse treatment planning system (TPS) for delivery via coplanar VMAT on a Varian 23EX Clinac. Prescriptions consisted of 70 Gy to the high-dose therapeutic volumes (2.0 Gy/fraction) and 56 Gy to the elective prophylactic nodal volumes (1.6 Gy/fraction).

The OVHs and STSs are calculated between each OAR in our OAR set to each target volume in our set of target volumes. The OAR set includes the left parotid, right parotid, right cochlea, left cochlea, right submandibular, left submandibular, and thyroid. These structures were selected based on the consistency of contouring across the retrospective cohort; OARs that were frequently omitted or incompletely contoured were excluded from the feature set to ensure model stability. The target volume set includes the 70 GTV - tumour, 70 GTV - nodal, 70 CTV, and 56 CTV. The 192 cases were selected for inclusion based on contour completeness; specifically, each was required to contain at least four of the seven specified organs-at-risk (OARs) and three of the four specified target volumes to ensure a robust feature set for modeling. While 155 of the 192 cases had all four target volumes, 37 cases (19%) did not have 70 GTV - nodals. These OAR and target volume sets result in 28 unique target-OAR combinations for analysis.

Algorithm framework

Our stepwise similarity matching method to calculate the target-OAR constellation geometry similarity between cases (Fig. 1) is based on data created in the course of standard clinical practice as extracted from DICOM RT files. We first characterize the structural geometric identity of each case with extracted quantitative features (Fig. 1a). We calculate an OVH and an STS from each GTV and CTV to each OAR for each case and merge the results into a database indexed by case, target, and OAR to permit simultaneous comparison of equivalent features. The similarity between the matched quantitative feature (OVH and STS) pairs are then calculated with an Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD) (Fig. 1b). Lastly, the OVHs, STSs, and individual EMD values calculated between two cases are used as inputs for a hybrid cluster-then-predict machine learning method trained using dose similarity as the ground truth target variable (Fig. 1c). All of these components will be described in full detail in the following sections.

Feature extraction for geometric modeling

To model the geometric relationship of tumor to healthy tissues an OVH and STS is calculated between each target volume and each OAR. GTVs and CTVs are used instead of Planning Target Volumes (PTVs) so as to reflect anatomic volumes within the target distributions. This method could be applied to PTVs; however, PTVs are geometric expansions that reflect radiation-center specific setup uncertainty and consequently do not carry additional geometric information.

Overlap volume histogram (OVH)

The first quantitative feature we use to characterize the target-OAR constellation geometry is the OVH. The OVH has already been validated in helping identify radiotherapy cases with similar dose distributions and serves as a baseline for additional quantitative feature development [5, 17]. It effectively considers the volumetric distribution of an OAR relative to the nearest surface of the GTV or CTV in a manner that is reflective of the real world conditions that influence clinicians when creating a high quality treatment plan. The OVH robustly represents the proximity of an OAR to the target volume because proximity is highly associated with dose exposure to the OAR. The OVH has been proven to help retrieve cases with similar DVHs for the OARs from which the OVH was calculated. This method builds on previous work considering only one OVH for one OAR by calculating and considering OVHs for multiple target volumes and all OAR during retrieval of similar cases.

Spatial target signature (STS)

The second quantitative feature we use to quantitatively characterize the target-OAR constellation geometry is the STS which builds on the OVH through consideration of direction and target volume. The OVH measures the scalar distributional distance of each OAR to the nearest surface of the target volume but lacks directional information. The STS is the distribution of all vectors pointing from the OAR centroid to each voxel in the target volume (Fig. 3) [5]. This directly takes into account the relative position, shape and orientation of the target volume to an OAR addressing this key weakness of the OVH. The other important difference between the STS and OVH is that the STS is based on the voxels in the target volume whereas the OVH is based on the voxels in the OAR. This means the STS can directly account for the size of the target volume which is important for inter-case comparisons. While this approach better captures target volume heterogeneity, using the OAR centroid may lose some precision for long curving or irregularly shaped OAR such as the jaw. We consequently include both the OVH and STS in our model as they are complementary and cover each other’s weaknesses.

Similarity calculations

The similarity calculation process is a multi-step algorithm designed to compare the target-OAR constellation geometry of two cases. First, the individual geometric feature distributions (OVH and STS) are determined for each case. These distributions are compared using statistical distance metrics to quantify feature-level disparity. Finally, these individual metrics are aggregated into a global similarity score using a composite distance measure and hybrid machine learning model. The specific mathematical definitions of these metrics are detailed in the subsections below.

Earth mover’s distance

An EMD is used as the first step in determining overall similarity between two radiotherapy cases because it is able to represent the difference between corresponding quantitative features. In this EMD method corresponding quantitative features from two cases are paired (i.e. STSs or OVHs calculated between the same OAR type and same target volume type). An EMD is calculated between these paired features to quantify their geometric disparity. Intuitively, EMD solves the optimal transport problem: it calculates the minimum work required to transform one histogram into the other, defined as the amount of ‘earth’ (probability mass) multiplied by the distance it must be moved [23, 24]. The total number of EMD calculated for a pair of cases therefore depends on the number of OARs, CTVs and GTVs that the two cases have in common. If two cases contain different sets of OARs or target volumes, the missing EMDs are stored as null values. This feature pairing approach combines the many individual metrics, quantitative features, without biasing the later models with heterogeneous missing feature categories. For STS EMD computation, each bin is treated as a weighted point in spherical coordinate space, and optimal transport is computed directly in this 3D space without flattening. Calculating the EMD for each corresponding feature pair before overall aggregation, ensures an equivalent comparison of all the different aspects of a case’s constellation geometry [23, 24]. This use of EMDs facilitates a similarity matching method that simultaneously and proportionally accounts for the spatial relationships between multiple OARs, GTVs, and CTVs.

Gower’s distance

To provide the machine learning model with a high level description of geometric similarity, we use a modified Gower’s Distance to create a single composite similarity score between two cases based on the individual EMDs between all equivalent features [25]. The overall distance is computed by evenly weighting the EMDs to produce a baseline score where 1 represents the greatest similarity. Hybrid cluster-then-predict machine learning framework

The final overall similarity value is calculated by a hybrid cluster-then-predict machine learning method trained to weigh the importance of each target-OAR relationship based on clinical relevance. Our machine learning method uses data driven approaches to discover how geometric features and relationships both influence clinical decision making and predict dose similarity to incorporate these insights into the final similarity score.

We implement a hybrid cluster-then-predict approach using a hard-gated MoE architecture. This multi-stage framework was designed to discover which geometric features are most predictive of clinically meaningful similarity. A proxy ground truth for similarity was constructed by calculating the dose similarity between all pairs of retrospective cases in our knowledge base. Dose similarity between case pairs was constructed by comparing the DVHs of corresponding ROIs (both OARs and target volumes) with EMDs and aggregating them with a modified Gower’s distance. Relying on DVH based dose similarity serves as a useful, albeit partial, proxy for true clinical relevance. By condensing 3D spatial dose distributions into 1D histograms, this metric inherently results in the loss of spatial information regarding the specific location of dose fall-off, potentially masking clinically meaningful differences in tumor location and local anatomy that would influence expert judgment. Despite these limitations, DVH based overall similarity provides the necessary quantifiable, objective, and computationally efficient ground truth required for training supervised machine learning models, which can then be validated against the more nuanced but subjective clinical classifications.

Stage I: unsupervised clustering

In the first stage, unsupervised spectral clustering partitions cases into groups based on their aggregate dose similarity profile. Each case is characterized by its mean dose similarity to all other cases in the knowledge base, effectively quantifying each case’s position on the spectrum from highly typical (similar to many other cases) to atypical (an outlier with unique geometry-dose relationships). Since dose similarity correlates with anatomical similarity, this stratification reflects underlying geometric patterns while enabling the subsequent supervised models to learn cluster-specific feature-to-dose mappings [4, 5].

Stage II: meta-classification

In the next stage, new cases (for which dose has not been calculated) are assigned to their appropriate cluster based on geometric features with meta-classification based on distance to centroid. Light gradient-boosting machine (LightGBM) meta-classification uses the top 100 most predictive case-level geometric features (identified via importance analysis from the initial pool of aggregate features, binary indicators, and individual STS and OVH histogram bins) to learn which cluster to assign cases to based on their anatomical features and distance to cluster centroid.

Stage III: supervised similarity regression

In the last stage, supervised learning, multiple machine learning models are trained separately within each cluster. Considered models include linear and tree-based ensembles; Random Forest, Linear Regression, Gradient Boosting Regression, Support Vector Regression, K-Nearest Neighbors, Elastic Net, Extra Trees, Hist Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost. Using dose similarity as the target variable, these models learn to predict dose similarity from geometric features. For each case pair, the input features include the OVHs, STSs and the EMDs (calculated between corresponding OVHs and STSs for the case pair), aggregated scores (Gower’s distance of STS and OVH EMDs), and binary ROI presence indicators for node dose levels.

This results in a total of 186 feature categories for model training, including 6 aggregate metrics, 12 binary indicators, 56 EMDs, 56 OVHs, and 56 STSs. To capture high-resolution spatial data while maintaining model parsimony, OVHs were binned at a 10-bin resolution and STSs were computed at a 1,000-bin resolution before being reduced to 100 principal components (explaining 75% of the variance) via Principal Component Analysis (PCA). When expanded across the possible ROI pairings, this results in a final input dimensionality of 6,234 expanded features for the pairwise regression models. Each cluster specific model learns to assign greater weight to those geometric relationships that are most influential in determining dose similarity for cases within that anatomical subgroup, effectively capturing the clinical priorities that guide treatment planning.

In order for case pairs to be suitable for inclusion in clustering and modeling they must have a minimum of 16 target-OAR relationships in common; a least 16 OVH EMDs and 16 STS EMDs calculated between them. As paired clinical cases seldom contain identical sets of OARs, CTVs, and GTVs, the cluster-then-predict pipeline was explicitly designed to handle missing data. Depending on the specific algorithm, missing feature values are either handled natively (e.g., LightGBM, CatBoost) or managed via median imputation, ensuring that absent structures do not bias the similarity prediction.

This hard-gate MoE architecture assigns each new case to one cluster based on its geometric features, then applies that one cluster’s trained model to generate the refined similarity score. Our hard-gating design improves interpretability and computational efficiency while maintaining strong predictive performance.

Once trained, the cluster-then-predict method generates refined similarity scores for any new current planning case. This allows the similarity matching process to move beyond simple geometric aggregations and instead leverage data-driven and clinical insights to produce scores that better reflect the aspects of anatomy most relevant to clinicians. This method constitutes a novel framework for radiotherapy KBP that integrates holistic spatial anatomy with learned importance weightings. By capturing the clinical priorities inherent in the treatment planning process, this framework reflects the complex spatial relationships and planning priorities used by experts to identify optimal reference cases.

Leave-one-out cross-validation dose prediction evaluation

The dose similarity between a pair of cases is calculated as the evenly weighted modified Gower’s distance of the EMDs calculated between the DVH curves of corresponding ROIs.

Cases were parsed into our system, a containerized environment, whereupon the STSs and OVHs were calculated between the set of OARs and target volumes for each case. Then EMDs were calculated between every case pair for corresponding STSs, OVHs, and DVHs. The Gower’s distance of the DVH EMDs was then calculated to represent the dose similarity between cases and serve as the target variable. A Gower’s distance was also calculated based on the STS and OVH EMDs between each case pair to serve as an overall descriptive input feature for model training. All case pairs were then clustered based on each case’s OVHs, STSs, and the EMDs and Gower’s distance calculated between them. A model was then trained for each cluster where the input features were the same features used for clustering and the target feature was the overall dose similarity.

For the cluster-then-predict models, we perform a leave-one-out cross validation where each case was excluded, all other cases were clustered by spectral clustering on their geometric features, and separate machine learning models were trained for each cluster. The excluded case was then matched to its nearest cluster and its similarity calculated using the trained models for that cluster. This process was repeated for all cases to prevent data leakage and simulates the intended use of the model, matching new cases to retrospective cases. The calculated similarity was then benchmarked against the dose similarity to evaluate the accuracy of the models we constructed.

Clinical receiver operating characteristic evaluation

To evaluate whether the algorithm’s similarity assessments align with expert clinical judgment, we use a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis with direct clinical evaluations. Five evaluation cases and a cohort of 53 candidate cases were randomly selected from the 192 case knowledge base. Clinical experts classify each evaluation case to each candidate case (“clinically relevant” or “not relevant”) based on their clinical similarity and utility as references for each other. A majority vote established the gold standard binary classification for each case pair. To prevent data leakage, the 5 evaluation cases were strictly excluded from the model training set. The 53 candidate cases were included in the training pool to simulate a clinical retrieval scenario where the system identifies matches from a pre-indexed database of known cases. The evaluation used our hybrid learning cluster-then-predict method with 6 spectral clusters where the validation set consisted of the 5 evaluation cases and the training set consisted of the remaining 187 cases. Although the clinical evaluation utilized five evaluation cases, the pairwise comparison methodology resulted in a total sample size of N = 265 decision points (50 positive). A post-hoc power analysis indicated that this sample size provides > 99% statistical power to detect an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.85 compared to a null hypothesis of 0.50 at ɑ=0.05 [26].

Computational implementation

All calculations were performed in a containerized environment, deployable on any standard clinical workstation or server. For these calculations 10 cores and 10 GB of memory were allocated to the container. The parsing, feature extraction, EMD calculation, and Gower’s calculator for the 192 cases took 48 h with 90% of the time taken up by the EMD STS calculations. For each type of clustering for each k, number of clusters, clustering and modeling took 25 min. The proposed use case of the system, uploading a single new case, calculating its quantitative features, EMDs, Gower’s distance, assigning it to a cluster, and predicting its similarity with the model for that cluster takes 5 min.

Results

Leave-one-out cross-validation dose prediction results

Since identifying clinically relevant cases is the primary goal of our algorithm, the underlying assumption is that anatomically similar cases will have similar dose distributions. Therefore, we conducted an evaluation to directly compare the algorithm’s anatomical similarity score against the dose similarity between cases. We validated the target-OAR constellation matching model using the dataset of 192 retrospective radiotherapy cases from our knowledge base, resulting in 17,780 case pairs with geometric features and dose similarities that met the minimum inclusion criterion of 16 common target-OAR relationships.

The hybrid learning cluster-then-predict approach using spectral clustering indicated substantial improvements in similarity matching and dose similarity prediction in all configurations (Table 1). Agglomerative, spectral, K-means, DBscan and GMM clustering were each tested from 2 to 15 clusters with optimal performance achieved with 6 spectral clusters with clusters and performance shown in Figs. 4 and 5. The best performing models, Extra Trees and CatBoost, reached particularly high correlations of r = 0.7965 and r = 0.8009, indicating approximately two thirds of dose similarity variance is explainable by geometric features alone.

Tree-based ensemble models demonstrated robust performance. Random Forest (r = 0.7933), Hist Gradient Boosting (r = 0.7897), and LightGBM (r = 0.7846) all maintained correlations near 0.8 while even the lowest performing tree ensemble, XGBoost, achieved a robust correlation of r = 0.7693. Linear models performed poorly beyond 5 clusters, confirming the non-linear relationships between geometric features and dose similarity. To compare against previous approaches, leave-one-out cross validation was also used for predecessor retrieval methods: OVH-only, STS-only, Left Parotid only, and only Gower’s distance of the OVH and STS EMDs. Compared to previous approaches which just used one STS or OVH between the Left Parotid and the target volume (correlations of 0.3028 and − 0.1322) or aggregations of all STS EMDs or OVH EMDs (correlations of 0.5413 and − 0.2400), our methods suggest a marked improvement. The weak negative OVH correlations (r ≈ -0.1 to -0.2) provide negligible predictive value, reflecting OVH’s limitation as a 1D descriptor that captures only distance-from-PTV, losing the directional information critical to dose distribution. A survey of DVH-based KBP retrieval techniques when used on their intended datasets found an average correlation between 0.7 and 0.9 [7–9, 27–29]. Our approach indicates an improvement upon previous geometry-based retrieval methods and supports their parallel implementation prior to treatment planning: our results show recapitulation of DVH-based KBP techniques using non-dose based geometric features available prior to treatment planning.

This dose-based validation suggests that our geometric features capture the anatomical relationships that drive dosimetric outcomes, providing the foundation for clinical utility. This indicates that by learning from dose data, our method successfully identifies cases with similar dose distributions based on pre-treatment anatomical structure and relative target volume positional distribution, which is the primary goal for the clinical utility of this KBP tool.

Clinical receiver operating characteristic results

The ROC evaluation classification was performed by three expert clinicians with the same 192 retrospective cases and 6 spectral clusters with the 5 evaluation cases left out as the validation set (Fig. 6). Evaluation of inter-rater agreement yielded a Fleiss’ kappa of 0.7528 and an 88.3% unanimous agreement rate, confirming high reproducibility across the clinical experts. The results indicate robust performance, with several machine learning models achieving AUCs above 0.8 (Table 2). Random Forest achieved the highest performance with an AUC of 0.8797, followed by Hist Gradient Boosting (0.8522), LightGBM (0.8443), Extra Trees (0.8183), and CatBoost (0.8169). These results indicate an improvement over the standard geometric baselines and closely approach the performance of the ground truth dose similarity, suggesting that our geometric features and cluster-then-predict architecture successfully capture the majority of the clinical relevance signal reflected in the dose distributions.

The dose similarity score achieved an AUC of 0.8843, suggesting dose similarity is a strong proxy for clinical relevance and validating our training methodology. This not only indicates the validity of the clinician’s classifications of the case pairs as being reflective of delivered dose, but it also suggests the validity of the premise of the dose similarity evaluation. It proves dose similarity is a good proxy for the ground truth, the clinical classification of similarity, and is a valid target variable for model training.

Comparing dose similarity and clinical evaluation performance, dose matching showed more uniform performance across models with most correlations falling between 0.75 and 0.80. Clinical evaluation showed greater differentiation, with advanced methods substantially outperforming simpler approaches. Linear Regression performed poorly in clinical evaluation (AUC = 0.5350) and only discovered negligible correlations in the dose matching evaluation, while tree-based models excelled in both contexts but achieved even higher clinical relevancy scores.

This performance differential between the models suggests that clinical experts consider anatomical factors beyond dose similarity and achievability when determining case relevance. Factors such as treatment complexity, beam angle configurations, and planning confidence may influence clinical similarity judgments in ways that our comprehensive geometric features successfully capture but that are not directly reflected in final dose distribution similarity. To benchmark against previous approaches, the ROC analysis was also performed on some predecessor retrieval methods: OVH-only, STS-only, Left Parotid only, and only Gower’s distance of the OVH and STS EMDs. In contrast to the old methods relying on only one STS or OVH between the Left Parotid and the target volume (AUCs of 0.6709 and 0.5535) or aggregations of all STS EMDs or OVH EMDs (AUCs of 0.0714 and 0.2869), our cluster-then-predict method indicates substantial improvement.

The exceptional clinical performance (AUCs > 0.85) for multiple models provides evidence indicating our method’s clinical utility. Specifically, an AUC of 0.8797 indicates that there is an 88% probability that a randomly selected clinically similar case pair will be assigned a higher similarity score by the algorithm than a randomly selected dissimilar pair, representing near expert level performance in similarity assessment. The high average AUC suggests that the similarity metric is not only computationally robust, but also clinically meaningful. It indicates that the algorithm successfully identifies cases that clinicians would agree are relevant references for treatment planning.

Conclusion

We have developed and validated a novel method to holistically model and match radiotherapy cases based on their target-OAR constellation geometry similarity. By calculating comprehensive quantitative features (OVH and STS) from all relevant OARs to all CTVs and GTVs our system creates a comprehensive representation of each case’s unique anatomical structure. By aggregating these individual similarity measures via our cluster-then-predict model, the algorithm captures the holistic target-OAR constellation geometry. This effectively weights each geometric relationship according to the same clinical priorities used by clinicians when constructing treatment plans.

Because tissue density (OAR type) and tumor depth are the primary determinants of the delivered dose for radiotherapy cases, our algorithm which accurately matches cases based on the similarity of tumor position relative to surrounding OAR is able to identify clinically relevant cases. Furthermore, our machine learning model of anatomy based matching causes our identification of similar cases to reflect and weight the anatomical positional relationships most important to clinicians during development of treatment plans and their dose distribution.

The evaluations of our model affirm its clinical utility. The clinical evaluations suggest its practical value. The ROC analysis resulted in an average AUC of 0.8797 for the top performing model (Random Forest), indicating a robust ability to distinguish cases that clinicians consider relevant for comparison. Furthermore, the dose matching evaluation provides additional reinforcement for our similarity matching process’s utility. At its optimal configuration of 6 clusters, the leave-one-out evaluation of our top model (CatBoost) showed a correlation of r = 0.8009. This suggests that our algorithm is successful in its primary goal: identifying cases whose treatments would be relevant based on the similarity of their target-OAR constellation geometry.

Compared to previous work that often relies on single features or single OAR PTV relationships, our multi-feature, multi-OAR, multi-target volume approach provides a more robust and comprehensive holistic model of anatomical similarity. The results indicate that this holistic approach successfully captures the geometric complexities that influence treatment planning and more accurately identifies cases by target-OAR constellation similarity.

While our results demonstrate the effectiveness of this framework for a specific H&N cohort, there are limitations regarding generalizability, sensitivity, and stability. The generalizability of the specific 6-cluster model is constrained to H&N cases within the anatomical distribution of the current study. However, the clustering framework itself is site-agnostic; it is designed to algorithmically identify the optimal cluster count for any new dataset, anatomical site, or treatment modality, facilitating automated adaptation to new clinical contexts without manual reconfiguration. The sensitivity of the method is intrinsically linked to the characteristics of the training dataset. Because the framework is designed to automatically retrain as the knowledge base expands, its performance is inherently sensitive to the anatomical range of the knowledge base. This sensitivity is a functional requirement of the matching paradigm; the primary value of the method lies in identifying retrospective cases with high anatomical similarity to the query case, rather than attempting to extrapolate outside the distribution of the known clinical data. The stability and reproducibility of cluster assignments are maintained through spectral clustering on pairwise similarity graphs. Although cluster definitions will evolve as the knowledge base expands to incorporate additional datasets with different anatomical sites and modalities and is retrained, the framework ensures that query cases are consistently mapped to the most relevant anatomical subgroup.

Future work will focus on refining the feature set, expanding the dataset, and improving machine learning modeling and algorithms to improve the dose similarity predictive ability of the target-OAR constellation models. Integrating additional data, including different cancer types and treatment modalities, will further enhance the knowledge base and the generalizability of the system to other cancer modalities and treatment types. Specifically we seek through multi-institutional collaboration to include datasets of non-coplanar VMAT for peripheral brain cancer and non-coplanar SBRT where the beneficence of target-OAR constellation geometry matching model would be greatest. In those use cases initial beam number selection and configuration is the most complicated thereby making reference to a similar previous case during plan conception more useful. Given the varying performance of the machine learning models tested, acquiring larger datasets will facilitate the training of advanced neural networks. This will allow for more accurate and comprehensive models of target-OAR constellation similarity. Additionally, we will seek to acquire and integrate radiotherapy outcomes data into our knowledge base. This will enable the research and discovery of relationships between target-OAR constellation geometry, treatments received and outcomes which will be available for best practice discovery, benchmarking, and quality assurance. Ultimately, this target-OAR constellation matching method provides a powerful, new tool that can be used early in the treatment planning process to standardize care, improve plan quality, and increase clinician confidence by providing evidence-based, patient-specific reference cases.
