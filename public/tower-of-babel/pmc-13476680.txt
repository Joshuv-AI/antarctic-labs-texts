Self-Management Ability and Its Associations with Pruritus Severity, Self-Perceived Burden, and Psychological Resilience in Adults with Atopic Dermatitis: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Qin Zou, Jihui Chen, Weihao Zhang, Hui Gou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate self-management ability among adults with atopic dermatitis (AD) and examine its associations with pruritus severity, self-perceived burden, and psychological resilience.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 585 adults with AD. Self-management, pruritus, self-perceived burden, and psychological resilience were assessed using the Chronic Disease Self-Management Study Measures (CDSMS), pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), Self-Perceived Burden Scale (SPBS), and Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), respectively. The CDSMS total score was the primary outcome; its two subscale scores were secondary outcomes, and the three behavioral domain scores were exploratory outcomes. Univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses were used to estimate associations.ResultsThe mean CDSMS total score was 62.53 ± 9.55. In the primary multivariable model, older age (β=−0.06, 95% CI −0.11 to −0.02; P=0.003) and greater self-perceived burden (β=−0.23, 95% CI −0.35 to −0.11; P<0.001) were associated with lower CDSMS scores, whereas greater psychological resilience was associated with higher scores (β=0.30, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.39; P<0.001). Pruritus showed a borderline inverse association (β=−0.44, 95% CI −0.87 to 0.00; P=0.050). Alcohol use, pruritus, and physical and emotional burden were associated with lower self-efficacy, whereas optimism and tenacity were associated with higher self-efficacy. Older age and being single were associated with poorer self-management behaviors; estimates for divorced and widowed participants were based on very small subgroups and were considered exploratory. Associations varied across the cognitive symptom management, physician communication, and exercise domains.ConclusionAmong adults with AD, poorer overall self-management was associated with older age and greater self-perceived burden, whereas greater psychological resilience was associated with better self-management. Associations differed across self-efficacy, overall self-management behaviors, cognitive symptom management, communication with physicians, and exercise. Assessment of pruritus, perceived burden, coping resources, communication difficulties, and age-related barriers may help tailor self-management support; longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether such support improves outcomes.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a common chronic inflammatory skin disease characterized by recurrent eczematous lesions, xerosis, and persistent pruritus.1 Its prevalence varies across countries, age groups, and case definitions, and systematic evidence indicates that AD remains common in both children and adults.2 Adult AD may persist from childhood or begin during adulthood and is associated with substantial physical, psychological, and socioeconomic burden, including impaired sleep, emotional distress, reduced daily functioning, social limitations, and diminished quality of life.3,4 Pruritus is among the most prominent and distressing symptoms and may interfere with sleep, mood, work productivity, and adherence to treatment.3,5

Because AD is chronic, recurrent, and strongly influenced by daily care behaviors, effective disease control depends not only on pharmacological treatment but also on patients’ self-management ability. Self-management in AD includes regular skin care, avoidance of triggers, appropriate medication use, symptom monitoring, communication with healthcare professionals, and maintenance of healthy behaviors.6,7 Previous studies have suggested that self-management support and patient education are important components of eczema care;8 however, the optimal strategies for improving self-management in patients with eczema or AD remain insufficiently established.9 Therefore, identifying factors associated with self-management ability may help clinicians develop more targeted and patient-centered interventions for adults with AD.

Self-management ability may be influenced by both symptom-related and psychosocial factors. Severe pruritus may reduce patients’ confidence and motivation to perform daily disease-management behaviors. Self-perceived burden, which refers to patients’ feelings that their illness imposes physical, emotional, or economic pressure on others, may also negatively affect disease adjustment and self-care behaviors.10,11 In contrast, psychological resilience, defined as the capacity to adapt to adversity and recover from stress, may serve as a protective psychological resource in chronic disease management.12 Patients with higher resilience may be more likely to maintain positive coping, treatment adherence, and confidence in managing long-term symptoms. Self-efficacy, as an important component of self-management, may also be closely related to patients’ perceived ability to control symptoms, communicate with physicians, and maintain health-related behaviors.10

However, evidence regarding self-management ability and its associated factors in adults with AD remains limited.10 Few studies have simultaneously examined the relationships of self-management with pruritus, self-perceived burden, and psychological resilience. Self-management is multidimensional and includes self-efficacy, overall self-management behaviors, cognitive symptom management, communication with physicians, and exercise. Accordingly, this study aimed to assess self-management ability among adults with AD and examine its associations with pruritus severity, self-perceived burden, and psychological resilience. We hypothesized that greater pruritus severity and self-perceived burden would be associated with poorer self-management, whereas greater psychological resilience would be associated with better self-management.

Methods

Study Design

This cross-sectional survey was conducted from June 2025 to May 2026 to describe self-management ability and determine its determinants among adults with atopic dermatitis at West China Hospital, Sichuan University.

This study received approval from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 2024.1420). All methodologies were implemented in compliance with pertinent guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the survey. Clinical trial registration number: not applicable.

Participants

The study cohort comprised adults aged 18 years or older with AD who attended the dermatology outpatient clinic or were admitted to the dermatology department of a tertiary Grade A hospital in Southwest China. AD was diagnosed by dermatologists in accordance with the Chinese guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of AD.13 The Chinese guideline emphasizes chronic or recurrent pruritic eczema, characteristic age-related morphology and distribution, and personal or family atopic history, which broadly overlap with the Hanifin–Rajka and UK Working Party frameworks. It also recognizes simplified adult or adolescent criteria developed for Chinese patients to improve sensitivity to local clinical phenotypes.14 Convenience sampling was used. Eligible patients were recruited irrespective of AD severity; no severity-based selection or stratification was applied.

The inclusion criteria for the study were defined as follows: 1) participants aged 18 years or older; 2) a clinically confirmed diagnosis of AD; 3) maintained clear consciousness with the ability to read independently and communicate effectively; and 4) expressed willingness to participate in the study after being fully informed of its objectives and procedures.

The exclusion criteria were specified as follows: 1) presence of comorbid severe dermatological or systemic conditions, including severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal insufficiency, or malignant tumors; 2) other known independent conditions that could significantly impair sleep, such as severe sleep apnea syndrome; 3) experience of major stressful life events within the preceding month, such as bereavement; 4) pregnancy or lactation; and 5) cognitive impairment or psychiatric disorders that would impede the ability to complete questionnaires cooperatively.

Sample Size

This cross‑sectional study calculated its sample size using the standard formula n=Z2α/2×P(1−P)/d2. Since no large-scale national data were available regarding the prevalence of adequate self-management ability among Chinese patients with AD, we conservatively set the expected proportion P at 50%, as suggested in a previous study.10 With a 95% confidence interval (Zα/2=1.96) and a margin of error d=0.05, the estimated minimum sample size was 385. Considering a 30% anticipated rate of invalid electronic questionnaires, the final target sample size was set at 501 participants. Ultimately, 585 patients were enrolled in this study, meeting the required sample size.

Measurement Instruments

Chronic Disease Self-Management Study Measures (CDSMS)

The CDSMS were developed by Lorig et al and comprise two components: the 15-item Self-Management Behaviors Scale and the 6-item Self-Efficacy for Managing Chronic Disease Scale.15 The behavior scale covers exercise, cognitive symptom management, and communication with physicians and has a total score of 0–75. The self-efficacy scale has a total score of 0–60. The combined CDSMS total score ranges from 0 to 135, with higher scores indicating better self-management. The original measures demonstrated satisfactory reliability, with Cronbach’s α coefficients ranging from 0.72 to 0.91.15,16

Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (NRS)

Pruritus intensity was assessed on an 11-point scale ranging from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe pruritus. A score of 0 indicates no pruritus, scores of 1–3 indicate mild pruritus, scores of 4–6 indicate moderate pruritus, scores of 7–8 indicate severe pruritus, and scores of 9–10 indicate extreme pruritus.17,18 The NRS was treated as a symptom-specific measure and not as a surrogate for overall AD severity.

Self-Perceived Burden Scale (SPBS)

The SPBS developed by Cousineau et al, consists of 10 items assessing patients’ perceived burden across three dimensions: physical burden, financial burden, and emotional burden. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”), yielding a total score of 10–50. Higher scores indicate a greater level of self-perceived burden. The 10-item abbreviated version of the scale has demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.85.19

Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC)

The CD-RISC developed by Connor and Davidson, consists of 25 items covering three dimensions: strength, optimism, and tenacity.20 Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not true at all”) to 4 (“true nearly all of the time”), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate greater psychological resilience. The CD-RISC has been widely used in clinical settings to assess psychological resilience among patients.21,22

Data Collection and Quality Control

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire. Sociodemographic information included sex, age, place of residence, educational level, occupation, income, marital status, and disease duration. Lifestyle-related characteristics, including smoking and alcohol consumption, were also recorded.

Before data collection, all investigators received standardized training to ensure consistency in questionnaire administration and familiarity with the questionnaire content, survey procedures, and relevant precautions. During the survey, investigators used standardized instructions to explain the study purpose, questionnaire completion procedures, and relevant requirements to the participants. For items that participants found difficult to understand, investigators provided neutral and non-directive explanations without influencing their responses.

Questionnaires were administered only after written informed consent had been obtained from all participants. Both electronic and paper-based questionnaires were used, with identical content and item order. Paper questionnaires were mainly provided to older participants or those who did not have access to or were unable to use a smartphone, whereas electronic questionnaires were provided to participants who were able to complete the survey using a mobile device. All questionnaires were distributed, completed, and collected on site.

After completion, all questionnaires were reviewed by designated research personnel for completeness, logical consistency, and response quality. Questionnaires were excluded if they contained incomplete responses, unresolved logical inconsistencies, clearly random or invalid response patterns, or an electronic completion time of ≤1 minute. These procedures were implemented to ensure the completeness, accuracy, and reliability of the collected data.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and MSTATA software. Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation and median (Q1, Q3), and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. The Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for unadjusted group comparisons, as appropriate. The CDSMS total score was designated as the primary outcome. Self-efficacy and self-management behaviors were secondary outcomes, and cognitive symptom management, communication with physicians, and exercise were exploratory outcomes. All outcomes were modeled as continuous variables. Pruritus NRS was entered as a continuous predictor in regression; its severity categories were used only for descriptive interpretation. SPBS and CD-RISC total scores were continuous predictors in the primary model, whereas their domain scores were examined in secondary or exploratory models. Age and BMI were continuous in regression. Categorical predictors were dummy coded using the reference groups shown in the tables. Candidate predictors were defined according to the study objective, a conceptual framework covering demographic, symptom-related, perceived-burden, and resilience factors, and prior literature. In the primary model, age and sex were retained as demographic adjustment variables because of their clinical relevance, while NRS, SPBS total score, and CD-RISC total score were the principal exposures. For secondary and exploratory models, univariable P<0.10 was used only as a supplementary parsimony screen; clinically or conceptually relevant variables could be retained irrespective of univariable significance, and no automated stepwise procedure was used. Linearity and homoscedasticity were evaluated using scatterplots and residual-versus-fitted plots, residual normality using Q–Q plots, and influential observations using standardized residuals and Cook’s distance. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance, with VIF <5 and tolerance >0.20 considered acceptable. Because several correlated secondary and exploratory outcomes were analyzed, no formal multiplicity adjustment was applied. These analyses were considered hypothesis-generating; effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals were emphasized, and nominal P values were interpreted cautiously because of the increased risk of type I error. All tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant for the primary analysis.

Exploratory Analyses of Self-Management Behavior Domains

Cognitive symptom management, communication with physicians, and exercise were examined as separate continuous outcomes. Because these related domains were derived from the same instrument, analyses that included one CDSMS domain as a correlate of another were exploratory and intended to describe conditional co-occurrence rather than causal or directional relationships.

Cognitive Symptom Management

Older age (β=−0.03, 95% CI −0.05 to −0.02; P<0.001) and smoking (β=−1.40, 95% CI −1.97 to −0.83; P<0.001) were associated with lower cognitive symptom-management scores. Higher BMI (β=0.09, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.14; P<0.001), physician communication (β=0.43, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.53; P<0.001), exercise (β=0.14, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.21; P<0.001), physical burden (β=0.16, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.31; P=0.033), and emotional burden (β=0.14, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.21; P<0.001) were associated with higher scores. Tenacity was inversely associated (β=−0.08, 95% CI −0.13 to −0.02; P=0.004). NRS, economic burden, and strength were not statistically significant (Figure 1). Discussion

This study identified different correlates of overall self-management, self-efficacy, self-management behaviors, and three behavioral domains among 585 adults with AD. The clearest overall pattern was poorer CDSMS performance with older age and greater perceived burden and better performance with greater resilience. Pruritus was more clearly associated with self-efficacy than with reported behaviors. Several domain-level associations were less intuitive and should be interpreted as exploratory because the outcomes were correlated, multiple tests were conducted, and the design was cross-sectional.

At the CDSMS total-score level, older age, greater self-perceived burden, and lower resilience were associated with poorer overall self-management. Older adults may face practical barriers involving treatment routines, physical capacity, access to digital information, or health literacy; however, these mechanisms were not measured directly.23 Greater perceived burden may reduce the psychological and practical resources available for daily management, while resilience may help patients maintain management activities during symptom fluctuations. Reviews of eczema self-management similarly emphasize that clinical, psychological, and practical demands interact and may require structured, individualized support.4,24 The borderline association with pruritus should not be overinterpreted, particularly because NRS reflects one symptom rather than overall AD severity.

Self-efficacy was lower among participants with alcohol use, more intense pruritus, and greater physical and emotional burden, and higher among those with greater optimism and tenacity. Persistent itch and disease-related disruption may be particularly relevant to patients’ confidence in managing AD, while resilience-related resources may support coping.25 Nevertheless, temporal direction cannot be established: successful management may also reinforce optimism and persistence. Alcohol use was recorded only as a binary variable, so the observed association may reflect unmeasured lifestyle or social factors rather than a direct relationship. Psychological interventions have improved patient-reported outcomes in adults with AD, supporting further investigation of modifiable coping resources.26

The self-management behaviors scale showed a different pattern. Older age and being single were associated with lower behavioral scores, whereas physical burden was positively associated. The divorced estimate was large but based on only three participants and should not be used for clinical inference. Marital status may reflect practical or interpersonal support, but social support was not measured. Greater physical burden may prompt more frequent self-care or treatment-seeking rather than improve behavior; reverse direction and residual confounding are equally plausible.27,28 The absence of an adjusted association between self-efficacy and behavior suggests that confidence and reported activity may not correspond directly, although construct overlap and model specification may also contribute.

The exploratory CDSMS-domain models showed close associations among cognitive symptom management, physician communication, and exercise. These findings may reflect clustering of active self-management practices, but the domains came from the same instrument. Their coefficients may therefore be influenced by construct overlap, shared-method variance, mathematical interdependence, multicollinearity, or suppression. The inverse coefficient for tenacity and the positive coefficients for physical or emotional burden in cognitive management should not be interpreted as evidence that tenacity is harmful or burden beneficial. Structured education and itch-coping interventions remain clinically relevant, but the present data cannot demonstrate intervention effects t.29,30

Communication with physicians was poorer among single participants and better among those with higher education, while cognitive symptom management was positively associated with communication. Education may facilitate understanding, question formulation, and participation in consultations, although health literacy was not measured. Shared decision-making research in eczema underscores the importance of education, clinicians initiating treatment discussions, and recognition of patient preferences.31,32 Differences between patient and clinician treatment priorities in the Asia–Pacific region further support two-way, patient-centered communication.33 The widowed association was based on 10 participants and remains highly uncertain.

Exercise was inversely associated with male sex, BMI, and emotional burden, and positively associated with several behavioral or burden measures. Emotional difficulties may present a barrier to activity,34 while the positive associations with physical and economic burden may reflect compensatory behavior, reverse direction, or residual confounding. Previous studies have reported lower physical activity among people with AD and associations between greater objective severity and reduced activity.35,36 The positive coefficient for alcohol use must not be interpreted as beneficial, and the widowed estimate is too sparse for clinical inference.

Clinically, the findings support a multidimensional assessment rather than reliance on a single self-management score. Patients may benefit from evaluation of pruritus, perceived physical and emotional burden, resilience, communication difficulties, and age-related practical barriers. Support could include clear written or digital instructions, individualized itch-management education, shared decision-making, and referral for psychological support when needed. These suggestions are consistent with reviews of education and self-management support in eczema,37 but intervention studies are required before effectiveness can be inferred from the present associations.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about temporal sequence or causality. Second, the principal clinical, behavioral, and psychological variables were self-reported and may be affected by recall and social-desirability bias. Third, no objective measures of AD severity or extent—including EASI, SCORAD, body-surface-area involvement, investigator global assessment, or treatment intensity—were collected. Pruritus NRS is symptom-specific and cannot substitute for overall severity; residual confounding by severity and treatment therefore remains likely. Fourth, alcohol and smoking were measured without quantity, frequency, or duration, and other lifestyle and social-support factors were not assessed. Fifth, the divorced (n=3) and widowed (n=10) subgroup estimates are unstable and imprecise. Sixth, no formal multiplicity adjustment was applied across the correlated secondary and exploratory analyses, increasing the risk of type I error. Finally, associations among components of the same CDSMS instrument may reflect construct overlap, shared-method variance, mathematical interdependence, or suppression. Domain-level findings should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating and independently replicated.

Conclusion

Among adults with AD, older age and greater self-perceived burden were associated with poorer overall self-management, whereas greater psychological resilience was associated with better self-management. Associations differed across self-efficacy, self-management behaviors, cognitive symptom management, communication with physicians, and exercise. Clinicians may consider assessing pruritus, perceived burden, coping resources, communication difficulties, and age-related practical barriers when planning self-management support. Future longitudinal studies should incorporate objective AD severity and treatment intensity, and intervention studies should test whether support tailored to these factors improves self-management and patient outcomes.
