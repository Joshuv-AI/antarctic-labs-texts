Magnetoconductivity due to electron-electron interaction in a non-Galilean–invariant Fermi liquid
Tatia Kiliptari, Dmitrii L. Maslov

Abstract
The T 2 T^{2} -scaling of resistivity with temperature is often viewed as a classic hallmark of a Fermi-liquid (FL) behavior in metals. However, if umklapp scattering is suppressed, this scaling is not universally guaranteed to occur. In this case, the resistivity behavior is influenced by several factors, such as dimensionality (two vs. three), topology (simply- vs. multiply-connected Fermi surfaces), and (in two dimensions) the shape (convex vs. concave) of the Fermi surface (FS). Specifically for an isotropic spectrum, as well as for a two-dimensional (2D) convex FS, the T 2 T^{2} term is absent, and the first non-zero contribution scales as T 4 ​ ln ⁡ T T^{4}\ln T in 2D and as T 4 T^{4} in 3D. In this paper, we study the T T -dependence of the resistivity, arising from electron-electron interactions, in the presence of a weak magnetic field. We show that, for an isotropic FS in any dimensions and for a convex 2D FS, the T 2 T^{2} term is also absent in both Hall and diagonal components of the magnetoconductivity, which instead scale as B ​ T 4 ​ ln ⁡ T BT^{4}\ln T and B 2 ​ T 4 ​ ln ⁡ T B^{2}T^{4}\ln T , respectively, in 2D and as B ​ T 4 BT^{4} and B 2 ​ T 4 B^{2}T^{4} in 3D. The FL-like scaling, i.e., B ​ T 2 BT^{2} and B 2 ​ T 2 B^{2}T^{2} of the Hall and diagonal conductivities is recovered for a concave FS in 2D. Furthermore, we show that, for an isotropic spectrum, magnetoresistance is absent even in the presence of electron-electron interactions. Additionally, we examine the high-temperature limit, when electron-electron scattering prevails over electron-impurity one, and show that all the components of the conductivity tensor saturate in this limit at values that are determined by impurity scattering but, in general, differ from the corresponding values at T = 0 T=0 .

I
Introduction

It is well established that electron-electron (
ee
) interactions can influence the resistivity of a non-Galilean invariant Fermi-liquid (FL) metal.
The
T^{2}
scaling of resistivity, which is regarded as a characteristic feature of
FL behavior in metals, can be understood through the Pauli exclusion principle. This principle indicates that at low temperatures, only quasiparticles near the Fermi energy, within a width on the order of
T
, participate in binary collisions. It is important to note, however, that this argument pertains to the inverse quasiparticle relaxation time
1/\tau_{\mathrm{ee}}
rather than the resistivity itself. For example, a Galilean-invariant FL has zero resistivity but finite thermal conductivity and viscosity, whose temperature dependences follow from the
T^{2}
scaling of
1/\tau_{\mathrm{ee}}
.
This is because in a Galilean-invariant system velocities are proportional to momenta, and conservation of momentum automatically implies conservation of the electric current. Hence, a momentum relaxation mechanism is needed in order to achieve a steady-state current.

In metals, the presence of a lattice breaks Galilean invariance, allowing for current relaxation through umklapp scattering
[
1
]
which conserves quasi-momentum up to a reciprocal lattice vector. For umklapp scattering to be permitted, the momentum transfer must be on the order of a reciprocal lattice vector. This requires two conditions to be met: (i) the Fermi surface (FS) must be sufficiently large
[
2
]
, and (ii) the interactions must be of sufficiently short range
[
3
]
. In conventional metals, these conditions are typically satisfied due to the high density of charge carriers and effective screening of Coulomb interactions. As a result, umklapp collisions occur at rates comparable to
1/\tau_{\mathrm{ee}}
and
\rho\propto T^{2}
.

Nevertheless, there are cases where these conditions fail to hold. For example, the first condition
is breached in systems with low carrier densities, such as degenerate semiconductors, semimetals, and the surface states of three-dimensional topological insulators. The second condition can also be compromised when a metal is tuned to the vicinity of a Pomeranchuk-type quantum phase
transition
[
3
]
. The interest in conduction mechanisms due to electron-electron (
ee
) interaction but without umklapps has been rekindled by recent experiments, in which a pronounced
T^{2}
- behavior of the resistivity was observed in low-density electron systems, when umklapps can be safely ruled out
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

If umklapp processes are suppressed and the temperature is too low for electron-phonon interaction to be effective, the primary mechanism for current relaxation becomes electron-impurity
ei
collisions. However, normal
ee
collisions can also influence resistivity under certain conditions. This occurrence depends on the following properties of the Fermi surface (FS): (i) dimensionality (two vs. three), (ii) topology (simply vs. multiply connected FS), and (iii)
shape (convex vs. concave)
[
10
,
11
,
3
,
12
,
13
]
. For example, the
T^{2}
term is absent
not only for a Galilean-invariant FL but also for any isotropic spectrum both in two and three dimensions.
In two dimensions (2D), the conditions are more restrictive. Namely, the
T^{2}
term is absent for a simply connected and convex, yet otherwise arbitrarily anisotropic FS. This occurs because the
T^{2}
term originates from collisions between electrons restricted to move along the FS contour. As a result, momentum and energy conservation severely reduce the number of possible scattering channels, such that a convex FS contour behaves similarly to the integrable one-dimensional case, where no relaxation is possible
[
12
]
.

Whether the
T^{2}
is present is determined entirely by a change of the total (group) velocity of two electrons due to a collision (proportional to a change in the electric current):

\Delta\bm{\varv}=\bm{\varv}_{\mathbf{k}}+\bm{\varv}_{\mathbf{p}}-\bm{\varv}_{\mathbf{k-q}}-\bm{\varv}_{\mathbf{p+q}},

(1)

where
{\bf k}
and
{\bf p}
are the initial momenta of electrons and
{\bf q}
is the momentum transfer.
For example, in a Galilean-invariant system, i.e, a system with parabolic spectrum
\varepsilon_{\bf k}=k^{2}/2m
, one has
\bm{\varv}_{\bf k}={\bf k}/m
, and same for other velocities, such that
\Delta\bm{\varv}
vanishes identically due to momentum conservation (in this case, not only the
T^{2}
term but all higher-order terms are absent). For a system with isotropic but non-parabolic spectrum,
\Delta\bm{\varv}
vanishes if all momenta are taken to lie on the FS. Expanding near the FS, one obtains
T^{4}\ln T
and
T^{4}
for the leading non-zero term in 2D and 3D, respectively. Likewise, a 2D convex FS allows only for two scattering channels–the Cooper channel, in which
{\bf k}=-{\bf p}
, and the swap channel, in which
{\bf p}={\bf k}-{\bf q}
, such that
\Delta\bm{\varv}=0
for both channels. Expanding near the Cooper and swap solutions, one again obtains
T^{4}\ln T
for the leading term. A concomitant suppression of the optical conductivity for the same geometries was considered in Refs.
[

,
14
,

,

,

,

,

21

]

both in the FL and non-FL regimes.

The main goal of this paper is to see
if this trend still holds for the
magnetoconductivity in the presence of a weak magnetic field. In part, our motivation stems from the fact that the hitherto unexplained
T^{2}
-behavior of the resistivity is often accompanied by anomalous magnetoresistance, which is strong, quasilinear

A priori

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2e^{3}\tau^{2}B\int\frac{d\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{D}}(-n^{\prime}_{\bf k})\oint\frac{da_{\mathbf{k}}}{\varv_{\mathbf{k}}}\varv_{{\bf k},\alpha}u_{\mathbf{k},\beta},

(2a)

\displaystyle\delta\sigma_{\alpha\alpha}(B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2e^{4}\tau^{3}B^{2}\int\frac{d\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{D}}(-n^{\prime}_{\bf k})\oint\frac{da_{\mathbf{k}}}{\varv_{\bf k}}\varv_{{\bf k},\alpha}w_{{\bf k},\alpha},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varv_{{\bf k},x}\frac{\partial\bm{\varv}_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial k_{y}}-\varv_{\mathbf{k},y}\frac{\partial\bm{\varv}_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial k_{x}},

(3a)

(3b)

As we see, unlike for zero field, the magnetoconductivity tensor
contains higher derivatives of the dispersion, namely, the Hall conductivity in Eq. (

(1/m)_{\alpha\beta}=\partial\varv_{\alpha}/\partial k_{\beta}=\partial^{2}\varepsilon_{\bf k}/\partial k_{\alpha}\partial k_{\beta}

\varepsilon_{\bf k}

In a Galilean-invariant system,
ee
interaction does not lead to current relaxation, even in the presence of a magnetic field.
This is a consequence of Larmor’s theorem
[
26
]
:
if all particles have the same charge-to-mass ratio,
the

cancel

thus having no impact on the motion of the center of mass.

A related result in the quantum regime is known as Kohn’s theorem
[

.

In multi-band systems, the condition of equal charge-to-mass ratios is not satisfied.
As a result,

even if the individual bands are approximated as parabolic (see Ref.
[

and references therein.).
The focus of this paper is the effect of

interaction on magneto-transport properties of a single-band, non-Galilean system, i.e., either an isotropic system with a non-parabolic dispersion of charge carriers or an anisotropic system.
We will show that

terms in all components of the magnetoconductivity tensor
in a non-Galilean-invariant system follows the same rules as in the absence of the magnetic field.
Namely, such terms vanish for an isotropic but non-parabolic electron energy spectrum, both in 2D and 3D, and for a convex FS in 2D, while the leading terms behave as
T^{4}\ln T
in 2D and as
T^{4}
in 3D.

The remainder of the paper is
organized as follows. We begin by formulating the problem in terms of the Boltzmann equation (BE) in Sec.
II
.
In Sec.
III
,

with respect to

scattering, which is a valid approximation

we show that, for an isotropic but non-parabolic spectrum, the Hall

and

, respectively, in 2D, and as

and
B^{2}T^{4}
in 3D. In the same section, we show that

In Sec.
III.3
, we extend the analysis to a 2D convex and concave FSs. In Sec.

II
Boltzmann equation: Generalities

We consider a BE with time-independent and spatially uniform electric
\mathbf{E}
and magnetic
\mathbf{B}
fields

-e\;(\mathbf{E}+\bm{\varv}_{\bf k}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial\mathbf{k}}=-I_{\mathrm{ee}}[f_{\mathbf{k}}]-I_{\mathrm{ei}}[f_{\mathbf{k}}],

(4)

Here,
-e
is the electron charge and
f_{\mathbf{k}}
is the distribution function. The collision integrals
I_{\mathrm{ee}}
and
I_{\mathrm{ei}}
on the right-hand side account for the effects of
ee
and electron-impurity (
ei
) interactions, respectively:

I_{\mathrm{ee}}[f_{\mathbf{k}}]=\int_{\mathbf{p}}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{p^{\prime}}}\delta(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}}+\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p^{\prime}}})\\
\times\delta(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k^{\prime}}-\mathbf{p^{\prime}})\times[f_{\mathbf{k}}f_{\mathbf{p}}(1-f_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}})(1-f_{\mathbf{p^{\prime}}})\\
-f_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}f_{\mathbf{p^{\prime}}}(1-f_{\mathbf{k}})(1-f_{\mathbf{p}})],

(5)

and

I_{\mathrm{ei}}=\int_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}R_{\mathbf{k}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}(f_{\mathbf{k}}-f_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}})\delta(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}),

(6)

where
R_{{\bf k}\leftrightarrow{\bf k}^{\prime}}
and
W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{p^{\prime}}}
are the corresponding scattering kernels, and
\int_{\mathbf{k}}
is a short-hand notation for
\int d^{D}k/(2\pi)^{D}
(and the same for other momenta).

For a weak electric field, the driving term
\mathbf{E}\cdot(\partial f_{\mathbf{k}}/\partial\mathbf{k})
is reduced to
\bm{\varv}_{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathbf{E}n^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}
, where
\bm{\varv}_{\mathbf{k}}=\partial\varepsilon_{\bf k}/\partial{\bf k}
is the electron group velocity. As usual, we define a non-equilibrium part of

as follows:

\delta f_{\mathbf{k}}=f_{\bf k}-n_{\bf k}=n_{\mathbf{k}}(1-n_{\mathbf{k}})g_{\mathbf{k}}=-Tn^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}g_{\mathbf{k}}.

(7)

Substituting
Eq. (
7

5
) and linearizing in

yields
[
2
]
:

I_{\mathrm{ee}}[f_{\mathbf{k}}]=\int_{\mathbf{p}}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{p^{\prime}}}\\
\times\delta(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}+\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p^{\prime}}})\times\delta(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k^{\prime}}-\mathbf{p^{\prime}})\\
\times(g_{\mathbf{k}}+g_{\mathbf{p}}-g_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}-g_{\mathbf{p^{\prime}}})\times n_{\mathbf{k}}n_{\mathbf{p}}(1-n_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}})(1-n_{\mathbf{p^{\prime}}}).

(8)

For concrete results, we will assume that electrons interact via a screened Coulomb potential:

U(q)=\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{q+\kappa}\;\text{(in 2D) and}\;U(q)=\frac{4\pi e^{2}}{q^{2}+\kappa^{2}}\;\text{(in 3D)}.

(9)

Recalling the Thomas-Fermi expressions for the inverse screening radius,

\kappa=2\pi e^{2}\nu_{F}

\kappa^{2}=4\pi e^{2}\nu_{F}

\nu_{F}

U(q)=\frac{1}{\nu_{F}}\frac{\kappa}{q+\kappa}\;\text{(in 2D)

(10)

In the first Born approximation, the
ee
scattering kernel is given by

\displaystyle W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{p^{\prime}}}=2\pi U({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime})\left[U({\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}U({\bf p}-{\bf k}^{\prime})\right].

(11)

For a weakly screened Coulomb interaction with

For point-like impurities the

I_{\mathrm{ei}}=-\frac{f_{\mathbf{k}}-\langle f_{\bf k}\rangle}{\tau_{\mathrm{i}}}.

(13)

On the other hand, the collision integral in the relaxation-time approximation (RTA) reads

I_{\mathrm{RTA}}=-\frac{f_{\mathbf{k}}-n_{\mathbf{k}}}{\tau},

(14)

where
\tau
is a phenomenological relaxation time. The difference between Eqs. (
13

[

]
; however, both forms give the same result for the conductivity in a uniform electric field.
In what follows, we will use the RTA form,
I_{\rm ei}=I_{\rm RTA}|_{\tau=\tau_{\rm i}}
; a more detailed justification of such a replacement in given in Appendix
A

a (usually) weak dependence of

on
\varepsilon_{\bf k}

After these simplifications, the BE is reduced to

\displaystyle-e(\mathbf{E}\cdot\bm{\varv}_{{\bf k}})n^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}-e(\bm{\varv}_{\bf k}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot\frac{\partial f_{\mathbf{k}}}{\partial\mathbf{k}}=-\frac{f_{\mathbf{k}}-n_{\mathbf{k}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{i}}}-I_{\mathrm{ee}}[f_{\mathbf{k}}],

Isotropic but non-parabolic spectrum

III.3
Anisotropic Fermi surface in 2D

Now, we turn to the case of a generic anisotropic FS in 2D.
As stated in Sec.
I

T
-dependent corrections to magnetoconductivity.

To see why the

{\bf k}
and
{\bf p}
) and final (
{\bf k}-{\bf q}
and
{\bf p}+{\bf q}

In 2D, this condition
is satisfied only by the Cooper and swap channels, where

and

[
11

3

\Delta\bm{\varv}

22

\Delta{\bf u}
in Eq. (
24

\Delta{\bf w}

To obtain a finite result, one must to expand the vectors
\Delta\bm{\varv}

\Delta{\bf u}

\Delta{\bf w}

For

\max\left\{\Omega^{4}\ln|\Omega|,T^{4}\ln T\right\}

Here, we will also need expansions of

2

2

IV
High-temperatures:

strong electron-electron scattering

IV.1
General results

Up to this point, our analysis has concentrated on the low-temperature limit, where the
ee
contribution to resistivity
is a correction to the
ei
one.
This regime holds up to a temperature
T_{\rm i}
, at which the
ee
and
ei
scattering times become comparable. For
T\gg T_{\rm i}
, frequent but momentum-conserving
ee
collisions establish local equilibrium in the electron system; however, they cannot fix the value of the drift velocity. In the absence of
ei
scattering, electrons as a whole would be still accelerated by the electric field. The role of
ei
scattering is to provide a balancing frictional force, such that a steady state is achieved. The steady-state current in this regime is controlled entirely by impurities (assuming that electron-phonon scattering can still be neglected), and thus the resistivity saturates at some
T
-independent value, which, in general, is different from the residual one
[
31
,
28
]
(for an isotropic system, the low- and high-
T
saturation values coincide
[
17
]
). Presumably, such a saturation was observed in ultraclean samples of aluminum
[
35
]
(although it was attributed to momentum-conserving electron-phonon rather than electron-electron scattering
[
31
]
.)

A method of calculating the resistivity in the high-temperature regime, based on the spectral decomposition of the collision integral, was developed in Refs.
[
3
,
12
]
. Here, we apply this method to magnetoconductivity.
The
ee
collision integral in Eq. (
15
) can be considered as a linear operator
\hat{I}_{\mathrm{ee}}
, acting on the non-equilibrium part of the distribution function
f_{\mathbf{k}}^{(1)}\equiv f_{\mathbf{k}}-n_{\mathbf{k}}

\hat{I}_{\mathrm{ee}}[f_{\mathbf{k}}^{(1)}]=\sum_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\hat{I}_{\mathrm{ee}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})f_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}^{(1)}.

(46)

The non-Hermitian operator
I_{\mathrm{ee}}
can be represented
in terms of its left (
\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{\lambda}_{\gamma}|
) and right (
|\Phi^{\lambda}_{\gamma}\rangle
) eigenstates
as

\hat{I}_{\mathrm{ee}}[f_{\mathbf{k}}^{(1)}]=\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{ee}}^{\star}}\sum_{\lambda,\gamma}\lambda|\Phi^{\lambda}_{\gamma}\rangle\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{\lambda}_{\gamma}|,

(47)

where
\lambda
is the corresponding eigenvalue,
\gamma=x,y,z
, and
\tau_{\mathrm{ee}}^{\star}

is the effective
ee
scattering time, which sets an overall magnitude of the collision integral. The right
and left eigenstates constitute an orthonormal basis:

\langle\tilde{\Phi}^{\lambda}_{\gamma}|\Phi^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{\gamma^{\prime}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\tilde{\Phi}^{\lambda}_{\mathbf{k},\gamma}\Phi^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{k},\gamma^{\prime}}=\delta_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\delta_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}},

(48)

where
\Phi^{\lambda}_{{\bf k},\gamma}=\langle{\bf k}|\Phi^{\lambda}_{\gamma}\rangle
,
\tilde{\Phi}^{\lambda}_{{\bf k},\gamma}=\langle\tilde{\Phi}_{{\bf k},\gamma}|{\bf k}\rangle

and
\mathcal{V}
is the system volume in the
D
-dimensional
space.
A general solution of Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{k_{\mathrm{F}}}\ll\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\rm F}\tau_{\rm i}}\right)^{1/4}\ll 1,

(63)

\kappa\ll k_{\rm F}

IV.2
Specific examples

The low- and high-temperature limits of all the conductivity components differ in the way that the conductivity is averaged over the FS.
Naturally, the two limits align for an isotropic dispersion, which implies that the zero-field conductivity exhibits a minimum at
T\sim T_{\rm i}

[

\sigma_{\alpha\beta}
can be obtained only via numerical solution of the Boltzmann equation, which is beyond the scope of this work. What we can readily calculate
though is the ratio of the low-T/high-T limits, because (at least for pointlike impurities) it
is determined entirely by the FS geometry. In the remainder of this section, we present the results of such a calculation.

V
Conclusions

In this study, we have explored the Hall and diagonal magnetoconductivities arising from electron-electron (
ee
) interactions in a non-Galilean-invariant Fermi liquid. We showed that, at lowest temperatures, a
BT^{2}
correction to the Hall conductivity
\sigma_{xy}
and

B^{2}T^{2}
correction to the diagonal magnetoconductivity
\sigma_{xx}

are absent for an isotropic but non-parabolic spectrum, both in 2D and 3D.
The leading non-zero contributions
in this case behave as
\sigma_{xy}\propto BT^{4}\ln T
and
\sigma_{xx}\propto B^{2}T^{4}\ln T

in 2D
in 2D
and as
\sigma_{xy}\propto BT^{4}
and
\sigma_{xx}\propto B^{2}T^{4}
in 3D.
Although the components of the magnetoconductivity tensor depend on both
B
and
T
, the diagonal magnetoresistance is absent for an isotropic system, as is also the case without
ee
interactions.
The same scaling behavior, i.e,
BT^{4}\ln T
for the Hall and
B^{2}T^{4}\ln T
for the diagonal magnetoconductivity is also found for a convex surface in 2D, while the
BT^{2}
and
B^{2}T^{2}
scaling forms are recovered for a concave Fermi surface.
We also analyzed the high-temperature regime, in which electron-electron scattering dominates over electron-impurity one, leading to a saturation in both zero-field and field-dependent corrections due to
ee
interaction. For an isotropic system, the conductivities
in the high- and low-temperature limits coincide.

Appendix A
Relaxation-time approximation for impurity scattering

The
ei
collision integral for point-like impurities-function
is given by Eq. (
13
), which we copy below for the reader’s convenience

I_{\mathrm{ei}}=\frac{f_{\mathbf{k}}-\langle f\rangle}{\tau_{\mathrm{i}}}.

(65)

Therefore, the solution of the BE,

-e(\bm{\varv}\cdot\mathbf{E})\frac{\partial\langle f\rangle}{\partial\varepsilon_{\bf k}}=\frac{\langle f\rangle-f_{\mathbf{k}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{i}}},

(66)

is defined only up to an arbitrary function of energy,
\langle f\rangle

drops out from the current, but the distribution function itself remains
undetermined. To fix this, we introduce a phenomenological collision integral of the relaxation-time approximation form

I_{\rm RTA}=\frac{n_{\mathbf{k}}-f_{\mathbf{k}}}{\tau},

(67)

where
n_{\mathbf{k}}
is the equilibrium distribution function.
Note that the integral of
I_{\rm RTA}
over the directions of
{\bf k}
at fixed energy is non-zero.
Therefore, such a term cannot come from potential scattering, because the
latter conserves the number of particles at given energy. At the same time
I_{\rm RTA}
leads to energy dissipation, because
\int_{\bf k}\varepsilon_{\bf k}I_{\rm RTA}\neq 0

-e(\bm{\varv}\cdot\mathbf{E})n^{\prime}_{\bf k}=\frac{\langle f\rangle-f_{\mathbf{k}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{i}}}+\frac{n_{\mathbf{k}}-f_{\mathbf{k}}}{\tau},

(68)

where

is expanded
around
n_{\bf k}
, rather than around
\langle f\rangle
. Solving Eq. (

f_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{i}}}+\frac{1}{\tau}}\left[e(\bm{\varv}\cdot\mathbf{E})n^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{\langle f\rangle}{\tau_{\mathrm{i}}}+\frac{n_{\mathbf{k}}}{\tau}\right].

(69)

Averaging the last expression over angles,
we find that
\langle f\rangle=n_{\mathbf{k}}
. Now the solution is unique

f_{\mathbf{k}}=n_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{i}}}+\frac{1}{\tau}}e(\bm{\varv}\cdot\mathbf{E})n^{\prime}_{\mathbf{k}}.

(70)

After this step, we can safely set

, which reproduces Eqs. (
7

Appendix C
Correction to the conductivity due to dynamically screened Coulomb interaction

We now turn to the intermediate range of temperatures,
\varv_{\rm F}\kappa\ll T\ll\varepsilon_{\rm F}
, where,
a priory
, one needs to take into dynamic, rather than static, screening of the Coulomb interaction. We consider the case of zero-field conductivity and deduce the effective current-relaxation time from this quantity. In the isotropic case, the same time enters all the components of the magnetoconductivity tensor.

The only change compared to case of a statically screened interaction is that the scattering probability is now expressed via the dynamically screened one as
[
33
]

\displaystyle W_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{p}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\mathbf{p^{\prime}}}=2\pi|U(q,\omega)|^{2},

(79)

where
{\bf q}={\bf k}-{\bf k}^{\prime}={\bf p}^{\prime}-{\bf p}
, and
\omega=\varepsilon_{{\bf k}}-\varepsilon_{{\bf k}-{\bf q}}=\varepsilon+{{\bf p}+{\bf q}}-\varepsilon_{\bf p}
. In the random-phase approximation,

U(q,\omega)=\frac{U_{0}(q)}{1-U_{0}(q)\Pi(q,\omega)}

(80)

where
U_{0}(q)
is the bare Coulomb potential and
\Pi(q,\omega)

is the polarization bubble. In 2D and for
|\omega|/\varv_{\rm F}\leq q\ll k_{\rm F}
,

\displaystyle\Pi(q,\omega)=-\nu_{\rm F}\left(1+\frac{i\omega}{\sqrt{\varv_{\rm F}^{2}q^{2}-\omega^{2}}}\right).

(81)

Accordingly,

|U(q,\omega)|^{2}=\frac{1}{\nu_{\rm F}^{2}}\frac{\kappa^{2}(q^{2}\varv_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\omega^{2})}{(q+\kappa)^{2}(q^{2}\varv_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\omega^{2})+\kappa^{2}\omega^{2}}.

(82)

Following the same steps as in the main text, we arrive at following form of the correction to the zero-field conductivity

\delta\sigma^{(\mathrm{ee},0)}_{xx}=-\frac{\pi e^{2}\tau_{\mathrm{i}}^{2}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{T\varv_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}}\frac{{{m^{\prime}}_{\rm F}^{2}}}{m^{4}_{\rm F}}\int\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{6}}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\omega\omega^{2}|U({\bf q},\omega)|^{2}\int^{2\pi}_{0}d\theta_{\mathbf{kq}}\int^{2\pi}_{0}d\theta_{\mathbf{pq}}\delta(\omega-\varv_{\mathrm{F}}q\cos\theta_{{\bf k}{\bf q}})\delta(\omega-\varv_{\mathrm{F}}q\cos\theta_{{\bf p}{\bf q}})(k_{x}-p_{x})^{2}\\
\times\int d\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}\int d\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}}n(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})n(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}})[1-n(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\omega)][1-n(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}}+\omega)].

(83)

The integral in the last line yields

\int d\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}\int d\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}}n(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})n(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}})[1-n(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\omega)][1-n(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{p}}+\omega)]=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\sinh^{2}{(\omega/2T)}}.

(84)

In contrast to the statically screened case, one cannot neglect
\omega
in the delta-functions yet.

Next, we approximate
k_{x}=k_{\mathrm{F}}\cos(\theta_{{\bf k}\mathbf{x}})=k_{\mathrm{F}}\cos(\theta_{{\bf k}{\bf q}}+\theta_{{\bf q}{\bf x}})
, and and similarly,
p_{x}=p_{\mathrm{F}}\cos(\theta_{{\bf p}\mathbf{x}})=k_{\mathrm{F}}\cos(\theta_{{\bf p}{\bf q}}+\theta)
, where
\theta=\theta_{{\bf q}\mathbf{x}}
.
Then the integrals over
\theta_{{\bf k}{\bf q}}
and
\theta_{{\bf p}{\bf q}}
yield

\int^{2\pi}_{0}d\theta_{\mathbf{kq}}\int^{2\pi}_{0}d\theta_{\mathbf{pq}}\delta(\omega-\varv_{\mathrm{F}}q\cos\theta_{{\bf k}{\bf q}})\delta(\omega-\varv_{\mathrm{F}}q\cos\theta_{{\bf p}{\bf q}})(k_{x}-p_{x})^{2}=\frac{8k_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{\varv_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}q^{2}}\Theta(\varv_{\mathrm{F}}q-\omega)

(85)

Here,
\Theta
denotes the Heaviside step function. As a result of this simplification, we arrive at the following expression for the interaction-induced Hall conductivity correction:

\delta\sigma^{(\mathrm{ee},0)}_{xx}=-\frac{8\pi^{2}k_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}e^{2}\tau_{\mathrm{i}}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{6}T\varv_{\mathrm{F}}^{6}\nu_{\rm F}^{2}}\frac{{{m^{\prime}}_{\rm F}^{2}}}{m^{4}_{\rm F}}\int^{\infty}_{0}d\omega\frac{\omega^{4}}{2\sinh^{2}(\omega/2T)}F(\omega)

(86)

where

\displaystyle F(\omega)=\nu_{\rm F}^{2}\int_{\omega/\varv_{\mathrm{F}}}^{\infty}\frac{dq}{q}|U(q,\omega)|^{2}.

(87)

Due to the
1/\sinh(\omega/2T)
factor, the integral over
\omega
is dominated by the region
\omega\sim T
. In the lower-temperature limit,
T\sim\omega\ll\varv_{\rm F}\kappa
,
one can put
\omega=0
in the function
F(\omega)
, which reproduces the
T^{4}\ln T
scaling of the conductivity, obtained for a statically screened Coulomb potential.
To analyze the opposite limit of
T\sim\omega\gg\varv_{\rm F}\kappa
, it is convenient to introduce dimensionless variables
x\equiv\varv_{\mathrm{F}}q/\omega
and
y=\kappa\varv_{\mathrm{F}}/\omega
, In terms of these variables,

F(\omega)=y^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x}\frac{x^{2}-1}{(x+y)^{2}(x^{2}-1)+y^{2}}

(88)

The condition
\omega\gg\varv_{\rm F}\kappa
corresponds to

y\ll 1
. In this limit, one can neglect
y
compared in the denominator of Eq. (
88
), upon which it is reduced to

F(\omega)=y^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{3}}=\frac{\varv_{\rm F}^{2}\kappa^{2}}{2\omega^{2}}

(89)

The final step is to perform the integration over
\omega
in Eq. (
86
).
Upon substituting Eq.(
89
) into Eq. (
86
), the
\omega^{2}
in the denominator cancels two powers of
\omega
from the numerator, leaving us with an overall
\omega^{2}
in the integrand, and hence with the
T^{2}
scaling of the conductivity.
The final expression
can be written the form
\delta\sigma^{(\rm ee,0)}_{xx}=-\sigma_{\rm ei}\tau_{\rm i}/\tau_{{\rm ee}}
, where
\sigma_{\mathrm{ei}}
, which defines
the effective current relaxation time,

\tau_{\mathrm{ee}}
,
as given by
Eq. (
37
).

In 3D, the polarization bubble for
|\omega|/\varv_{\rm F}\leq q\ll k_{\rm F}
is given by

\displaystyle\Pi(q,\omega)=-\nu_{\rm F}\left[1-\frac{\omega}{2\varv_{\rm F}q}\ln\frac{\varv_{\rm F}q+\omega}{\varv_{\rm F}q-\omega}\right]-i\nu_{\rm F}\frac{\pi\omega}{2\varv_{\rm F}q}.

(90)

In the limit of
T\sim\omega\gg\varv_{\rm F}q
, the dynamic interaction is reduced to the bare Coulomb potential. Accordingly, Eq. (
87
) is replaced by

\displaystyle F(\omega)\propto\int^{\infty}_{|\omega|\varv_{\rm F}}\frac{dq}{q^{4}}\propto\frac{1}{\omega^{3}},

(91)

which gives
\delta\sigma_{xx}^{(\rm ee,0)}\propto T
.
