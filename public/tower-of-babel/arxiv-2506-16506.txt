Subspace-Boosted Model Merging
Ronald Skorobogat, Karsten Roth, Mariana-Iuliana Georgescu, Zeynep Akata

Abstract
Model merging enables the combination of multiple specialized expert models into a single model capable of performing multiple tasks. However, the benefits of merging an increasing amount of specialized experts generally lead to diminishing returns and reduced overall performance gains. In this work, we offer an explanation and analysis from a task arithmetic perspective; revealing that as the merging process (across numerous existing merging methods) continues for more and more experts, the associated task vector space experiences rank collapse . To mitigate this issue, we introduce Subspace Boosting , which operates on the singular value decomposed task vector space and maintains task vector ranks. Subspace Boosting raises merging efficacy for up to 20 expert models by large margins of more than 10% when evaluated on vision benchmarks. Moreover, we propose employing Higher-Order Generalized Singular Value Decomposition to further quantify task similarity, offering a new interpretable perspective on model merging.

Background.

Given a set of
n
expert models
\{M_{1},M_{2},\dots,M_{n}\}
finetuned for different tasks from the same pretrained model
M_{base}
, the goal of model merging is to obtain the model
M_{m}
that is capable of solving all associated expert tasks. Let
\theta_{base}
be the parameters of
M_{base}
, and
\theta_{i}
be the parameters of each expert
M_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
. Following
[
76
]
, we define the
task vectors
as the weight differential
\Delta_{i}=\theta_{i}-\theta_{base}
,
1\leq i\leq n
. Using this notation, the merged parameters
\theta_{m}
, defined as task arithmetic through linear interpolation, are expressed as:

\theta_{m}=\theta_{base}+\alpha\sum_{i}^{n}\Delta_{i},

(1)

with
\alpha
the scalar merging coefficient. We also define
\Delta_{m}=\Delta_{1}+\dots+\Delta_{n}
as the total task vector.
Subspace Boosting
, as detailed in Sec.
4.1
is applied directly on the merged task vectors
\Delta_{m}
. As a result, it can be integrated directly into any model merging technique that provides the merged task vectors.

Rank Collapse During Merging.

When investigating the weight space, we observe that the weight rank collapses when more tasks are added during the merging process, as illustrated in Figure
1
. To measure the rank collapse, we employ two metrics, namely the
cumulative energy rank
and the
stable rank
. Both utilize a decomposition of matrices into singular values and vectors via the standard SVD. For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times{}n}
, the SVD is defined as:

A=U\Sigma{}V^{T},

(2)

with the orthogonal matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times{}m}
, also denoted as
left
singular vectors,
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times{}n}
the diagonal matrix containing singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{n}\geq 0
, and the
right
singular vectors
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times{}n}
.

Using this decomposition, the
cumulative energy rank

\mathcal{M}_{\text{cer}}
measures how much of the matrix information is captured by the top singular values, or more precisely how many singular values are required to cover
95\%
of the energy
\mathcal{E}
:

\mathcal{E}=\sum_{i}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

(3)

A small cumulative energy rank indicates that most of the information is concentrated in only a few dimensions. This implies that the weight projections operate within a limited subspace.
Meanwhile, the
stable rank

\mathcal{M}_{\text{stable}}
denotes the “effective rank” of the weights, similar to computing the rank of the matrix by ignoring the small singular values:

\mathcal{M}_{\text{stable}}=\frac{\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\max_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}}.

(4)

4.1
Subspace Boosting
for Model Merging

Utilizing the previous insights, which demonstrate that merging models using task arithmetic is associated with a strong rank collapse of task vector weight space, we posit that task arithmetic methods equally reduce the efficacy of the overall merging process and the associated generalization performance.
This occurs because the merged task vectors increasingly rely on a restricted number of dimensions as more and more expert models are merged, requiring more information to be encoded in a decreasing task vector subspace.
Consequently, our aim is to actively intervene in this issue.

To achieve this, we introduce
Subspace Boosting
(with the full algorithm provided in Alg.
1

Subspace Boosting
operates directly on the merged task vectors, ensuring compatibility with any model merging technique that relies on task vectors
[

4.2
Breaking Down Task Vectors with Higher Order Generalized SVD

Our experiments demonstrate that
Subspace Boosting
utilizing SVD effectively mitigates the rank collapse issue in task vectors, and correspondingly improves the merging efficacy. However, the standard decomposition does not allow direct comparison of the weight space between individual task vectors, i.e., expert contributions.
If applied individually, SVD produces expert-specific singular vectors that cannot be easily compared; and if SVD is applied to the final merged model or task vector
\Delta_{m}
, the influence of the individual models cannot be easily separated. However, this information could offer important and meaningful insight into individual expert contributions in the merging process, as well as facilitating potentially improved expert model selection later on.

:
Higher-Order Generalized SVD (HO-GSVD).
Unlike normal Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) which decomposes matrices into individual
A_{i}=U_{i}\Sigma_{i}V_{i}
, HO-GSVD allows for decompositions into shared right singular subspaces
V
.

Higher Order Generalized SVD.

Thus, to enable a straightforward comparison of multiple expert task vectors, we propose to use
Higher-Order Generalized Singular Value Decomposition
(HO-GSVD).
As proposed and described in
[
81
,
31
,
82
,
83
]
, given a set of
N
matrices
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}
, the HO-GSVD algorithm decomposes each matrix
A_{i}
as:

A_{i}=U_{i}\Sigma_{i}V^{T},\quad i=1,\ldots,N,

(5)

resulting in
distinct

U_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}\times n}
,
\Sigma_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and a
shared
subspace
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, identical for all factorizations.
To obtain
V
, we solve the eigensystem
S_{\pi}V=V\Lambda
of the arithmetic mean
S_{\pi}
of all pairwise quotients
D_{i,\pi}D_{j,\pi}^{-1}
:

S_{\pi}=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}(D_{i,\pi}D_{j,\pi}^{-1}+D_{j,\pi}D_{i,\pi}^{-1}),

(6)

with
D_{i,\pi}
defined as:

D_{i,\pi}=A_{i}^{T}A_{i}+\pi A^{T}A,\pi\geq 0,

(7)

where
A=[A_{1}^{T},\ldots,A_{N}^{T}]^{T}
. Following
[
31
]
, we add the previous regularization term
A^{T}A
and scale it by
\pi
to extend HO-GSVD’s applicability to
rank-deficient matrices
. This phenomenon frequently occurs for weight matrices, as particularly visualized for merged weight matrices in Fig.
1
. We set
\pi
to
10^{-2}
by default.
As previously described, HO-GSVD introduces a shared subspace
identical across all inputs
. This enables the
direct comparison of different models
and the identification of common or unique subspaces for different tasks.

Comparison Between Matrix Decompositions via Alignment Matrices and HO-GSVD.
For example, to evaluate the significance of dimension
V_{k}
(of the shared space) for
A_{i}
relative to its significance for
A_{j}
, we take the ratio
\sigma_{i},_{k}/\sigma_{j},_{k}
(from
\Sigma_{i}
and
\Sigma_{j}
, respectively). When
\sigma_{i},_{k}/\sigma_{j},_{k}\gg 0
,
V_{k}
is
significantly more important
for
A_{i}
than
A_{j}
, however,
when the ratio is close to zero
,
V_{k}
is much more significant for the latter matrix.
In cases where
\sigma_{i},_{k}/\sigma_{j},_{k}\approx 1
for all
i
and
j
, it suggests that the corresponding subspace is approximately equally important for all matrices. This defines the
common
subspace, whereas if the ratio is
\sigma_{i},_{k}/\sigma_{j},_{k}\approx 0
for all
i
excluding
j
, then that subspace is
unique
to
A_{j}
. Therefore, the matrices
A_{i}
are formulated as:

A_{i}=\underbrace{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{>}1}\sigma_{i,k}u_{i,k}v_{k}^{T}}_{\textbf{\text{common}}}+\underbrace{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\sigma_{i,k}u_{i,k}v_{k}^{T}}_{\textbf{\text{unique}}},

(8)

where
\mathcal{I}_{>}1
denotes the subspaces that are common for multiple or all matrices, and
\mathcal{I}_{1}
is unique for the given matrix
A_{i}
.
Leveraging the comparative nature of HO-GSVD, we utilize the ratios between pairwise matrices
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
to compare how aligned two matrices are; i.e., how much of the complete subspace
V
overlaps between
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
. We define two matrices as
poorly aligned
if they have a large share of overlapping subspaces, since a large share of the same subspace
V
is important for each individual matrix. Poorly aligned matrices would interfere with each other, whereas
well-aligned
matrices would rely on different subspaces and therefore,
interfere less
.

To calculate the pairwise alignment of each
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
, we define the
Alignment Matrix

\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.
L
is the total number of components to be aggregated, such as the individual layer-wise components of a model.
Then, let
M_{l}
be the number of generalized singular values for component
l
. Let
\sigma_{i,p}^{(l)}
denote the
p
-th generalized singular value of the model
i
for component
l
, and let
\epsilon
be a small constant for numerical stability (e.g.,
10^{-12}
). The element
(i,j)
of the alignment matrix
\mathbf{A}
, which represents the average alignment between model
i
and model
j
, is calculated as:

(\mathbf{{A}})_{ij}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(\frac{1}{M_{l}}\sum_{p=1}^{M_{l}}\left|\log\left(\frac{\sigma_{i,p}^{(l)}+\epsilon}{\sigma_{j,p}^{(l)}+\epsilon}\right)\right|\right)

(9)

The alignment matrix
\mathbf{A}
, in contrast to other methods such as direct weight comparisons, ensures that the comparison is equivalent in decomposed subspaces. This offers a novel perspective on task vector comparison and expert contribution, since it enables the practitioners to evaluate and contrast task vectors across a simplified (decomposed), and yet fundamentally shared projection space. Moreover, we also show how information on subspace alignment can be utilized to select experts from a large pool of contributors by optimizing for minimal subspace overlap, thus reducing interference.

Task Arithmetic

[

proposed the concept of

, and obtained the merged task vectors by simply averaging the task vectors of each individual model.

TIES-Merging

[

mitigated the parameter interference issue by pruning the low-magnitude weights from each task vector independently. The merged task vectors are obtained by averaging the parameters without sign inconsistency.

Consensus Merging

[
75

is a technique that is applied to the merged task vector, removing the weights that are specific to a single task or irrelevant to all tasks.

LiNeS

[
84
]
is a post-processing editing technique applied independently of the model merging method, which scales the parameter updates linearly based on their layer depth in the network to improve generalization and reduce catastrophic forgetting.

5.3
Interpretable
Subspace Boosting
via HO-GSVD

Comparison of Generalized Singular Values.
A key limitation of modern model merging methods is their lack of interpretability. Leveraging HO-GSVD, we aim to provide a novel approach to model merging that enables a more transparent investigation of the merging process. Furthermore, we aim to also enable the comparison of the contribution of individual experts.
Therefore, we illustrate in Fig.
5(a)
the distribution of generalized singular values for an exemplary weight matrix of a ViT-B/16 attention block projection layer. We compare the generalized singular value distribution of 4 individual task vectors of independent tasks (Cars, DTD, Eurosat, GTSRB), against a reference merged task vector over 8 tasks.

As our framework operates over shared decomposition spaces, we can directly compare singular values, and observe that there is a large proportion of singular vectors shared across all tasks, implying high interference for the largest singular vectors (supporting concurrent insights noted in
[
67
]
).
Moreover, unlike SVD, this decomposition illustrates rank collapse clearly without relying on additional metrics, as it facilitates the direct comparison of singular value distributions. We observe that a significant proportion of the generalized singular values in the range 0-200 are near the minimum value for the merged model. However, for independent task vectors, Threshold 1 illustrates the support level for the smallest values. Unlike for the merged model, the generalized singular values in the same range are never near the minimum value for the independent models.
Finally, we observe that the merged task vector is significantly below the others, implying that we need a larger merging coefficient than 1/T (T denoting the number of models) for the closest resemblance to the pre-merged task vectors. This implies that HO-GSVD could be used to
automatically
choose the optimal merging coefficient, opening up new directions for research.

Selecting Optimal Experts For Better Performance.

Another benefit of HO-GSVD is the ability to use its decomposition to compare the relative significance of a certain subspace for matrix
A_{i}
compared to matrix
A_{j}
, as described in Sec.
4.2
. However, choosing the optimal models out of a selection of choices is a combinatorially complex problem (e.g., selecting 6 models out of 20, there are nearly 40,000 possible permutations).
HO-GSVD enables us to break down the problem. We utilize the previously defined alignment matrix, where the higher the value for a pair of models (
A_{i}
,
A_{j}
), the easier it is to merge both models with reduced interference. An example of an alignment matrix is presented in Fig.
5(b)
. Higher scores correspond to models that are more well aligned (easier to merge).
We offer a simple use-case for this property: choosing 8 models (see appendix for more details) out of a pool of 14 candidate models. We perform experiments and report the results in Table
3
, when randomly selecting the models and when optimizing the selection with HO-GSVD. We evaluate the performance across 3 in-distribution tasks (Pool) and 3 out-of-distribution tasks (Ext). We report the mean performance over 10 random draws for random selection. HO-GSVD candidate selection increases the performance of in-distribution tasks (
72.9\%\rightarrow{}75.7\%
), while maintaining performance on external tasks (more experiments are presented in supplementary).

6
Conclusion

In this work, we identified
rank collapse
as a fundamental limitation of task-arithmetic model merging methods
[
29
,
19
,
30
]
. Consequently, we proposed
Subspace Boosting
to mitigate this limitation, thereby achieving significant performance improvements that exceed 10% in a wide range of settings.
Additionally, we proposed
Subspace HOG-Boosting
– an interpretable model merging method based on HO-GSVD to address the black-box nature of modern model merging techniques. Moreover, we demonstrated that
Subspace HOG-Boosting
effectively evaluates the behavior of model merging and offers a strong approach to select a subset of models that achieve higher performance when merged.

Limitations.
Current Task Arithmetic-based methods, including
Subspace Boosting
and
Subspace HOG-Boosting
, require tuning the best merging coefficient. This requires access to a reasonable validation dataset as well as considerable compute in order to tune this hyperparameter. In future work, we consider automating this and removing the necessity for tuning. Another limitation is that as the number of merged models grows, the performance continues to decrease. Therefore, finding optimal methods to prevent this degradation could be another interesting direction for future work.

Appendix F
HO-GSVD: Additional Information & Details

HO-GSVD allows us to perform merging in a more interpretable manner by operating compositions over shared subspaces.
However, unlike standard SVD, the left and right singular matrices are generally not orthonormal
[
81
,
31
]
. In order to perform subspace boosting with this method, we must first orthonormalize the respective matrices. This is achieved by solving the Generalized Orthogonal Procrustes problem
[
83
,
87
]
. Afterwards, the weight matrices can be reconstructed using these newly orthonormal matrices while preserving a shared subspace across all models. Please refer to Algorithm
2
for more details.

\Delta_{m}=\{\Delta_{m_{1}},\dots,\Delta_{m_{N}}\}

updated
merged task vectors for each component in the model.

\lhd

S_{\pi}

k
