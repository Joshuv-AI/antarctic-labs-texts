ChERF017, an ERF transcription factor, confers cadmium stress tolerance in Cerasus humilis
Authors: Xiaolong Ji, Xiaorui Tang, Zhaorong Mi, Ke Ma, Shuguang Liu, Fan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cadmium (Cd) contamination presents a grave threat to the growth and development of plants. One of its fundamental toxicological mechanisms is the disruption of cellular redox homeostasis. To clarify the physiological and molecular mechanisms through which Cerasus humilis responds to Cd stress, this study utilized physiological and biochemical analyses, transcriptomics, and transgenic functional validation methods. The results demonstrate that Cd stress leads to a substantial buildup of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and intensifies membrane lipid peroxidation in C. humilis leaves. This is evidenced by significant elevations in superoxide anion (O₂ ⁻), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. Transcriptome analysis pinpointed a crucial response factor, ChERF017. Heterologous overexpression of this gene in Arabidopsis thaliana significantly enhanced the tolerance to Cd stress, which was manifested as follows: under the stress of 100 µmol/L CdCl₂, the transgenic line displayed less severe chlorophyll degradation, the levels of ROS accumulation were notably reduced, the activity of antioxidant enzymes increased, and the content of glutathione and phytochelatins (PCs) increased. This promoted the transport of free Cd into vacuoles, thereby alleviating their cytotoxic effects on cells. This study verifies that ChERF017 plays a vital role in plant tolerance to Cd by regulating the antioxidant enzyme system and influencing the distribution of Cd within the cell. This finding offers essential theoretical bases and genetic resources for the genetic improvement of Cd resistance in C. humilis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09075-8.

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of industry and agriculture, heavy metal pollution has emerged as a serious global environmental concern. Among these heavy metals, cadmium (Cd) presents one of the most significant threats to ecosystems and human health owing to its high toxicity, strong mobility, and resistance to degradation [1, 2]. The discharge of industrial wastewater, mineral extraction, and the excessive use of Cd-containing phosphate fertilisers have resulted in the continuous accumulation of Cd in soils, which significantly impedes plant growth and development, leading to decreased crop yields and inferior quality. Moreover, it poses risks to human health through the consumption of agricultural products, causing a variety of diseases such as renal damage, osteoporosis, and even cancer [3, 4]. As a result, the remediation of Cd has become a research focus in environmental science and agriculture.

In response to Cd stress, plants have developed a comprehensive and precisely-regulated set of physiological and molecular response mechanisms to guarantee their survival [5–7]. At the physiological level, Cd stress induces an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) in plants, resulting in oxidative stress that damages cell membrane structure, protein function, and nucleic acid integrity. To alleviate oxidative damage, plants activate their antioxidant defense systems. This entails enhancing the activity of key enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT), as well as synthesizing non-enzymatic antioxidants including glutathione (GSH) and phytochelatins (PCs) [8–10]. At the molecular level, plants modulate Cd uptake, transport, and compartmentalization through the coordinated expression of a series of genes. For instance, the altered expression of heavy metal transporters (e.g., HMA and NRAMP families) and genes related to plant chelator synthesis is crucial for sequestering Cd in vacuoles and restricting its transport to the shoot [11–13]. These dynamic changes at physiological, biochemical, and transcriptional levels together form the core defense mechanisms against Cd toxicity in plants.

Within the transcriptional regulatory networks that govern plant responses to abiotic stresses, transcription factors (TFs) act as crucial master switches [14, 15]. The Apetala2/Ethylene Responsive Factor (AP2/ERF) family represents a major superfamily of TFs in plants. Members of the ERF subfamily are extensively engaged in plant responses to drought, high salinity, low temperatures, and heavy metal stress. Research demonstrates that ERF TFs in various plants can respond to Cd stress [16–18]. For example, in model plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa, multiple ERF genes are significantly upregulated under Cd treatment, and their overexpression enhances Cd tolerance in transgenic plants [19, 20]. This implies that ERF TFs may function as key nodes in regulating the Cd stress response network in plants.

Cerasus humilis, a shrubby tree with both ecological and economic value, exhibits strong physiological tolerance and vigorous root activity owing to its high drought resistance and tolerance to infertile soils [21–23]. However, the molecular response mechanism of C. humilis to Cd stress still needs to be confirmed. Here, we analyzed the physiological indicators of C. humilis under different Cd stresses. Through transcriptome analysis, we discovered that ChERF017 actively responds to Cd stress, and it was found that overexpressing ChERF017 lines exhibited significantly higher Cd tolerance than wild-type (WT) plants. Our research findings will contribute to future investigations aimed at elucidating the molecular mechanisms of Cd tolerance in C. humilis and the development of Cd-tolerant varieties.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and Cd treatment

Sixty-day-old seedlings of the C. humilis variety “Nongda No. 4” were transferred to a 1/2 Hoagland nutrient solution for 2 days (16 h light/8 hours night, 22–25°C, and a relative humidity of 65–75%). To determine the antioxidant response to Cd stress, various levels of Cd treatment were chosen, and the seedlings were transferred to solutions containing different concentrations of CdCl₂ (0 µmol/L CK, 10 µmol/L Cd10, 25 µmol/L Cd25, 50 µmol/L Cd50, and 100 µmol/L Cd100) for a period of 7 days (replaced every 3 days), samples were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at -80°C for further analysis. All plant materials were sourced from the School of Horticulture and Landscape Architecture at Henan Institute of Science and Technology.

Determination of chlorophyll content and antioxidant capacity

Experimental materials stored at -80°C were selected for subsequent experiments. The chlorophyll content was determined using the acetone-ethanol extraction method by selecting the second fresh leaf from the ground in each treatment group [24]. The proline content of samples was determined according to Ahammed et al. [25]. Fresh tissue samples (approximately 0.2 g of fresh weight) from 6 seedlings were selected from each group and homogenized in a 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solution. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 4000 g for 10 min at 4°C, and the malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using the thiobarbituric acid method [26]. The activities of SOD and POD were measured in accordance with the method described previously [27, 28]. Leaves (approximately 0.2 g fresh weight) were collected from 6 selected seedlings in each treatment group and processed into a homogenate using 2.5 mL of 0.05 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) under the condition of liquid nitrogen. Subsequently, the homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min, and the supernatant was obtained for the evaluation of enzyme activity. Monitor the activity of CAT and the changes in absorbance at 240 nm per minute using the UV absorption method [29]. Dissolve the dried sample (0.1 g) in HNO3 using a microwave disruptor. Subsequently, conduct the measurement using an Agilent 4210 MP-AES flame atomic absorption spectrometer manufactured in the United States [30, 31].

Determination of ROS content, AsA-GSH cycle, and PC content

The concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and superoxide anion (O₂ ⁻) were determined using kits from Greentest Biotech Co., Ltd. The contents of MDHAR, DHAR, APX, GR, GSSG, GSH, ASA, and DHA were measured according to the kit instructions (Grayce Biotech Co., Ltd.). The PCs content is equivalent to the NPT content minus the GSH content.

Transcriptome analysis and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Based on the results of physiological indicators, transcriptome sequencing was conducted on the leaves of C. humilis under Cd treatment conditions of 0 µmol/L (CK) and 50 µmol/L (Cd50). Total RNA was extracted from the leaves of C. humilis using the MiniBEST Plant RNA Extraction Kit (TAKARA, Japan). Subsequently, the cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (TAKARA, Japan). The integrity of the RNA was precisely evaluated using the Qsep400 Bioanalyzer. The cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina sequencing platform by Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). After the adapter sequences and low-quality reads were filtered out, the clean reads were mapped to the reference genome of C. humilis (PRJCA005423). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified according to the criteria of |log2Fold Change| ≥ 1 and False Discovery Rate < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. Venn diagrams and heatmaps were generated with TBtools software for data visualization. To validate the RNA sequencing results, qRT-PCR analysis was performed on 11 randomly selected genes using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (TAKARA, Japan). All primer sequences used are listed in Table S1.

Subcellular localization of ChERF017

The transient expression of the ChERF017-GFP protein was carried out in tobacco (6 weeks). Subsequently, after 72 h, the subcellular localization of ChERF017 was examined using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 710, Zeiss, Germany). The RFP was employed as a nuclear-cytoplasmic marker.

Generation of transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana

By employing the dipping method, the Agrobacterium tumefaciens solution (GV3101) containing ChERF017 was introduced into Arabidopsis thaliana. Subsequently, resistance screening was carried out using hygromycin to acquire transgenic plants. Pure T3 generation lines were obtained via continuous cultivation. Based on previous studies, when compared with the WT, 60-day-old plants were first pre-cultured in a 1/2 Hoagland solution for 3 days and then transferred to a 1/2 Hoagland solution containing 100 µmol/L CdCl₂ for 7 days. After the samples were collected, they were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at -80°C for subsequent determination.

Phylogenetic analysis

The protein sequences of other species were obtained from the NCBI database for phylogenetic analysis of the ChERF017 protein sequence with homologous proteins from other species. The GenBank accession numbers of protein sequences in the NCBI database were as follows: AtERF062 KAL9286696.1, ZjERF017 XP_015867472.3, PpERF017 XP_007204729.1, MdERF017 NP_001306940.1, FaERF10 AZL19412.1. Multiple alignments of these protein sequences were conducted by ClustalW, and the neighbor-joining tree was constructed using MEGA 11 based on 1000 bootstrap replications.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± standard errors, derived from three biological replicates, with each replicate comprising at least 3 plants. Duncan’s test was employed to evaluate the significance of changes among treatments, and the criterion for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The data were analyzed and visualized using Prism 11 software (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Plant growth and physiological response

The elevation in soil Cd concentration impeded plant growth. As the Cd concentration increased, C. humilis plants exhibited symptoms such as leaf chlorosis, yellowing, brown necrotic spots, leaf curling, and wilting. Concomitantly, as the Cd concentration increased, the Cd content within the plants gradually augmented. To explore the physiological impacts of Cd treatment on C. humilis, physiological parameters, including Cd and MDA content, the H2O2, and O₂ ⁻ production rate were measured in C. humilis (Fig. 1). The findings indicated that under Cd treatment, the free proline content in C. humilis leaves initially increased and subsequently decreased with an increase in Cd concentration. The proline content peaked under the 50 µmol/L Cd treatment. MDA content serves as a crucial indicator reflecting the extent of cell-membrane lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage. When the Cd treatment concentration was 10 µmol/L, the MDA content in the leaves increased only slightly, showing no significant difference compared to the control group. As the stress concentration further escalated, the MDA content increased significantly (P < 0.05) and reached its maximum at 100 µmol/L. In comparison with the control, as the Cd treatment concentration increased, the contents of H₂O₂ and O₂ ⁻ in the leaves of C. humilis exhibited an upward trend.

Plant antioxidant assays

The GSH-ASA cycle represents a fundamental antioxidant biochemical pathway within plant cells, which serves the purpose of scavenging H₂O₂. As the concentration of Cd treatment increased, the activities of the key enzymes in APX, MDHAR, DHAR, and GR demonstrated a pattern of initially increasing and subsequently decreasing. Among these enzymes, the activities of APX, MDHAR, and DHAR reached their peaks in the 50 µmol/L Cd treatment group, whereas the activity of GR attained its maximum at a lower concentration (10 µmol/L) (Fig. 2). Significantly, the ratio of GSH/GSSG in the 10 µmol/L Cd treatment group was markedly higher than that in the control group. However, under higher cadmium stress, this ratio was significantly lower than that in the control group. The metabolic equilibrium of ASA and DHA was also significantly modulated by Cd treatment. Specifically, the ASA content in the 10 µmol/L Cd treatment group rose by 22.58% in comparison to the control group, whereas the DHA content decreased significantly. In the higher Cd treatment groups, the ASA content decreased significantly, and the DHA content increased significantly. Correspondingly, the ASA/DHA ratio also presented a tendency to initially rise and then decline. Additionally, PCs, as crucial molecules for metal chelation in plants, showed a pattern of initially increasing and then decreasing with the increase in Cd concentration. They reached their peak in the 50 µmol/L Cd treatment group (Fig. 2).

Transcriptomic analysis of C. humilis with Cd

This study systematically compared and analyzed the quantitative characteristics and functional differences of DEGs in the leaves of C. humilis under Cd treatment conditions of 0 µmol/L and 50 µmol/L (Fig. 3). In comparison with the control group, 817 DEGs were screened from the leaves of C. humilis in the Cd treatment group, with 450 DEGs up-regulated and 367 DEGs down-regulated (Fig. 3A). To assess the reliability of transcriptomic analysis, 11 DEGs were randomly selected for qRT-PCR (Fig. S1). The gene expression deduced from qRT-PCR is consistent with the FPKM values observed in transcriptomic data, indicating that the transcriptomic data is reliable. To clarify the functional positioning of DEGs, further GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses were conducted to elucidate the core functional categories of DEGs in the leaves of C. humilis in the CK group and the Cd treatment group. The results of GO functional enrichment analysis indicated that most DEGs were concentrated in cellular components such as ADP binding, DNA binding transcription factor activity, cell wall, and cell periphery, as well as GO entries related to biological processes such as cell wall organization or biogenesis and cellular carbohydrate metabolism (Fig. 3C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that DEGs were enriched in 6 key pathways, namely the plant-pathogen interaction pathway (19 DEGs), the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (13 DEGs), the starch and sucrose metabolism pathway (8 DEGs), the ABC transporters pathway (7 DEGs), the plant hormone signal transduction pathway (7 DEGs), and the plant MAPK signaling pathway (6 DEGs) (Fig. 3D, Table S2). The above results suggest that C. humilis may achieve detoxification, fixation, and transport of Cd by regulating key pathways, including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, ABC transporters, and plant hormone signaling.

Stress-related pathways of C. humilis under Cd treatment

Based on the previous analysis and existing research reports, this study verifies that the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, the ABC transporters pathway, the MAPK signaling pathway in plants, and the glutathione metabolism pathway are enriched under Cd treatment conditions (Fig. 4, Table S3). Subsequently, this study carried out a systematic analysis of DEGs associated with the aforementioned pathways. The phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway is a core secondary metabolic pathway exclusive to plants, which can effectively eliminate ROS induced by abiotic stress and maintain the structural integrity and physiological homeostasis of plant cells. Transcriptome data analysis indicated that under Cd treatment, 5 DEGs were up-regulated and 8 DEGs were down-regulated in this pathway. ABC transporters pathway plays an irreplaceable and crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis, 4 DEGs were up-regulated, and 3 DEGs were down-regulated in this pathway. The MAPK signaling pathway in plants, as the core regulatory hub of plant stress response, 4 DEGs in this pathway are up-regulated, and 2 DEGs are down-regulated. The glutathione metabolism pathway is a dynamic metabolic network centered around GSH, encompassing processes such as GSH synthesis and redox conversion, and also participates in the regulation of key physiological functions, 2 DEGs were up-regulated, and 2 DEGs were down-regulated in this pathway.

Analysis of differentially expressed TFs

Under heavy-metal stress, TFs function as crucial regulatory nodes and play a pivotal role when plants are exposed to external stimuli. To clarify the transcriptional regulatory network of C. humilis in response to Cd treatment, this study carried out a systematic identification and differential expression analysis of its TF families. The results indicated that subsequent to the Cd stress treatment, a total of 59 TFs from 21 distinct families showed significant differential expression (Fig. 5A, Table S4). These families all exhibited specific differential expression patterns under Cd treatment, suggesting their potential involvement in the response to Cd stress. Notably, the transcriptome data from this study also identified classical stress-responsive TFs such as heat shock factors (HSFs) (Table S4), further validating that C. humilis experiences extensive and dynamic transcriptomic reprogramming under Cd exposure. Further analysis of the expression characteristics of core TFs indicated that Chumilis14859 from the AP2/ERF family and Chumilis01858 from the WRKY family demonstrated the most pronounced differential expression levels within their respective families (lowest P-value). To investigate the regulatory relationship between key TFs and Cd stress-response pathways, this study established a co-expression network of TFs and differentially expressed genes via gene co-expression network analysis. The results demonstrated that Chumilis14859 was strongly correlated with 14 differentially expressed genes in Cd stress-response pathways (Pearson correlation coefficient ≥ 0.8) (Fig. 5B, C). Overall, these findings imply that the AP2/ERF family TF Chumilis14859 might serve as a core regulatory factor, playing a crucial role in the physiological response of C. humilis to Cd stress.

Characterization of the ChERF017 gene

To conduct a systematic analysis of the biological function of Chumilis14859, this study initially constructed a phylogenetic tree by utilizing the amino acid sequence encoded by it and the sequences of AP2/ERF family TFs from other plant species. Evolutionary analysis indicated that Chumilis14859 clustered in proximity to ERF017 proteins from diverse plant species, displaying high homology (Fig. 6A). Consequently, the gene was formally named ChERF017. Further multiple sequence alignment analysis revealed that ChERF017 shares a highly conserved N-terminal region (amino acid residues 18–80) with MdERF017, which encompasses the characteristic AP2 domain of the AP2/ERF family, in accordance with the traits of its TF family (Fig. 6B). To elucidate the subcellular localization characteristics of the ChERF017 protein, this study constructed a ChERF017-GFP fusion expression vector driven by the CaMV 35S promoter and expressed it in the epidermal cells of Nicotiana benthamiana through Agrobacterium-mediated transient transformation. Confocal microscopy observations demonstrated that the fluorescence signal of the ChERF017-GFP fusion protein was predominantly distributed in the nucleus (Fig. 6C, Fig. S2). At the same time, weak fluorescence signals were also detected in the cytoplasm.

ChERF017 positively regulates Cd tolerance in Arabidopsis thaliana

To further investigate the function of ChERF017 in plant Cd responses, we overexpressed the 35 S::ChERF017 construct heterologously in Arabidopsis thaliana (Fig. 7). In Arabidopsis thaliana, transgenic lines did not exhibit any distinguishable phenotypic differences from WT plants before Cd treatment. After 3 days of hydroponic treatment with 100 µmol/L CdCl₂, all lines endured varying degrees of damage. However, WT plants suffered more severe damage, whereas transgenic Arabidopsis showed milder impairment. Before treatment, the chlorophyll content was similar between transgenic and WT lines. Cd treatment led to a decrease in chlorophyll content in all plants. Nevertheless, transgenic lines maintained higher levels, showing a significantly smaller reduction compared to the WT. Concerning oxidative stress-related indicators, no significant differences were detected between transgenic and WT Arabidopsis before Cd exposure. After Cd treatment, the levels of H₂O₂ and O₂ ⁻ increased in all strains. Nevertheless, the accumulation in the transgenic strain was significantly lower than that in the WT plant.

Owing to the existence of sulphhydryl groups in GSH and PCs, these molecules not only exhibit strong reducing capacity but also efficiently chelate metal ions. Consequently, they play a vital role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and alleviating heavy metal toxicity. When Arabidopsis thaliana was exposed to Cd treatment, the GSH content in its leaves increased notably. The overexpressing lines demonstrated a further 28.13%-33.34% increase in GSH levels. In comparison, there was no significant disparity in the PCs content between untreated overexpressing Arabidopsis leaves and WT plants. Nevertheless, under Cd treatment, the PCs content increased substantially, with the overexpressing lines having a 17.29%-24.27% higher PCs content than WT plants. These results suggest that overexpressing Arabidopsis facilitates the accumulation of GSH and PCs in the plant, thereby enhancing the Cd chelation ability, reducing the toxicity of Cd to plant cells, and ultimately improving the Cd tolerance of Arabidopsis.

Discussion

Cd, a heavy metal with potent physiological toxicity, primarily damages plants by inducing severe oxidative stress [32, 33]. Our findings indicate that Cd treatment significantly disrupts the redox homeostasis of C. humilis leaf cells. With increasing Cd treatment concentrations, the accumulation of superoxide anion and hydrogen peroxide markedly increased, peaking at 100 µmol/L (Fig. 1E, F). This phenomenon aligns with previous findings, where Cd disrupts electron transport pathways and induces leakage by displacing essential metal ions (e.g., Fe²⁺, Mn²⁺) in photosystem II and electron transport chain complexes, subsequently triggering explosive production of ROS [34]. The subsequent sharp increase in MDA, particularly at high Cd concentrations, visually confirms the severe damage that ROS inflict on cell membrane structures. This damage is the direct cause of typical toxic symptoms observed in leaves, including chlorosis, yellowing, and necrosis (Fig. 1).

In response to oxidative stress, plants have evolved a sophisticated antioxidant defense system [35–38]. This study observed that under mild Cd treatment (10 µmol/L), the activities of key enzymes in the AsA-GSH cycle (APX, MDHAR, and DHAR) significantly increased in C. humilis leaves. Concurrently, the contents of AsA and GSH, as well as the AsA/DHA and GSH/GSSG ratios, GSH/GSSG ratios also markedly increased (Fig. 2), indicating effective activation of the cellular antioxidant defense system and efficient operation of the AsA-GSH cycle. As a key non-enzymatic antioxidant in plants, AsA can directly and rapidly scavenge ROS [39, 40]. Within the AsA-GSH cycle, GSH not only serves as a substrate for AsA regeneration and recycling, thereby maintaining stable AsA pools, but also functions as a precursor for PCs. These chelators sequester Cd2+ within vacuoles, thereby reducing the toxicity of free Cd in the cytoplasm [41, 42]. However, when Cd treatment intensity exceeds the cellular regulatory threshold, this defense system tends to collapse. High Cd concentrations caused a decline in the activity of key enzymes in the ASA-GSH cycle, as well as in the ASA/DHA and GSH/GSSG ratios. This indicates that the continuous and intense influx of Cd into plants results in a production rate of ROS that significantly surpasses the clearance capacity of the antioxidant system, disrupting redox balance and rendering the antioxidant metabolic network dysfunctional [43].

Cd that enter plant cells trigger the production of PCs in plants. This enzyme uses GSH as a substrate to catalyze the synthesis of PCs, which then chelate free Cd within the cell to form non-toxic chelates [44–46]. Due to the strong chelating capacity of PCs toward heavy metal ions, this process effectively reduces the concentration of free Cd within plants, thereby mitigating its toxic effects. Furthermore, after being absorbed by plants, Cd is distributed in various forms throughout different tissues and organs, with its mobility restricted, further reducing its physiological toxicity to plants [47]. The subcellular distribution patterns of Cd in plants reveal key aspects of their tolerance and detoxification mechanisms, with cell wall immobilization and vacuolar compartmentalization being two critical pathways. This study further reveals that the Arabidopsis OE-ChERF017 lines enhance Cd tolerance through a dual mechanism. We hypothesize that the OE-ChERF017 lines substantially augmented the biosynthesis of crucial thiol compounds (GSH and PCs), thereby enhancing the cell capacity to chelate cadmium ions and effectively immobilizing toxic ions (Fig. 8). Subcellular fractionation demonstrated that enhanced chelation facilitated the partitioning of Cd into the soluble fraction of leaf cells instead of functional organelles, which indicates an increased vacuolar sequestration of Cd (Fig. S3). Teng et al. noted that GSH forms stable Cd-GSH complexes with free Cd in the cytoplasm, which are then transported to the vacuole via specific transporters, thereby mitigating Cd toxicity to the cytoplasm [48]. Additional studies demonstrate that the overexpression of genes encoding PCs effectively alleviates Cd toxicity in bok choy and poplar [49, 50]. Collectively, these findings confirm the central role of GSH-PCs mediated vacuolar compartmentalization in plant Cd detoxification.

In this study, transcriptomic analysis revealed the molecular networks underlying Cd responses at the systems level. The phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, ABC transporter pathway, plant MAPK signaling pathway, and glutathione metabolism were involved in the response of C. humilis to Cd stress (Fig. 4). Cd stress affects phenylpropanoid synthesis, thereby influencing the content of stress-related metabolites in plants. Studies on plants such as Salix matsudana and Pteris vittata L. similarly revealed that plants respond to Cd stress by regulating the expression of 4CL and F5H genes [51, 52]. Cd transport within plants primarily involves compartmentalization and efflux processes, where the ABC transporter gene family plays a crucial role [34, 53]. Pathway analysis indicates that Cd stress activates the MAPK signaling pathway and ABC transporters. Collectively, these findings reveal a multi-pathway coordinated response mechanism in C. humilis to Cd stress.

TFs are key molecules in gene expression regulation and play a central regulatory role in plant responses to Cd stress [54, 55]. Further analysis of TF expression patterns revealed ChERF017 as a key regulatory hub. To directly validate its function, we constructed a transgenic Arabidopsis line overexpressing ChERF017. Under Cd stress, OE-ChERF017 lines accumulated significantly lower levels of H₂O₂ and O₂ ⁻, but more importantly, exhibited stronger induction of SOD, POD, CAT, and APX activities (Figs. 7 and 8). This finding indicates that ChERF017 mitigates plant damage induced by Cd stress by reducing ROS accumulation and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity. By positively regulating the transcriptional expression of a series of antioxidant enzyme genes, it enhances the overall antioxidant capacity of plants, thereby more effectively maintaining cellular redox homeostasis and mitigating damage to membrane lipids and photosynthetic apparatus (Fig. 9). Recent studies indicate multiple ERF family members directly participate in plant Cd stress responses: AtERF34 and AtERF35 were shown to regulate Cd sensitivity; PvERF104 in common bean, as a metal response element-binding protein, enhances Cd tolerance by modulating downstream gene expression. StAP2/ERF members identified in potato functionally categorized into Cd-accumulating, Cd-reducing, and Cd-detoxifying types. While tomato SlERF1 and durum wheat TdSHN1 exhibit significant regulatory functions under nano-zero-valent iron treatment and Cd stress, respectively [54–57]. In addition, PvERF35, which was cloned from the common bean and heterologously overexpressed in tobacco, demonstrated a significant regulatory association with ABC transporters during the salt-stress response [58]. The LKERF6 of Japanese larch (Larix kaempferi) notably enhanced the drought resistance of transgenic tobacco, and the expression level of ABC transporter protein was significantly upregulated [59]. The aforementioned research provides an important theoretical basis for further exploration of the regulatory relationship between ChERF017 and ABC transporters.

Conclusion

In summary, this study clarifies the physiological and molecular mechanisms through which Cerasus humilis responds to Cd stress. We have successfully identified and functionally verified the crucial role of the ERF in this process. ChERF017 enhances Cd tolerance by increasing the transcriptional levels of antioxidant enzymes. These findings not only enhance our comprehension of the regulatory networks that govern plant responses to Cd stress but also offer valuable candidate genes and theoretical foundations for the development of Cd-tolerant plants via molecular breeding techniques and the promotion of phytoremediation efforts.
