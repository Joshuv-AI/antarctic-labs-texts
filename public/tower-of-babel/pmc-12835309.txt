Thermal evolution of light hydrocarbon fingerprints in biodegraded oils from Ordovician reservoirs, Tabei Uplift, Tarim Basin
Authors: Yuwei Yang, Yuhong Liao, Yueyi Huang, Yijun Zheng, Bin Cheng, Huanyu Lin, Yunpeng Wang, Ping’An Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Within the Tabei Uplift of the Tarim Basin, Ordovician reservoirs in both the northern Halahatang (N-Halahatang) and western Lunnan (W-Lunnan) areas experienced extensive biodegradation during the Late Hercynian (Permian). Subsequent Himalayan (Neogene–Quaternary) tectonism induced divergent burial-thermal histories: the N-Halahatang reservoirs underwent intensive maturation (> 6,500 m depth; 1.02–1.22% Ro), while the W-Lunnan reservoirs experienced milder maturation (< 5,800 m depth; 0.70–0.85% Ro). Despite similar δ13Coil values indicating genetic affinity, the relatively deeply buried biodegraded oils from the N-Halahatang area contain abundant C6–C8 light hydrocarbons (LHs), while the biodegraded oils from the W-Lunnan area exhibit only trace amounts of C6–C8 LHs. To elucidate the evolution of LHs compositions and fingerprints in biodegraded oils under thermal maturation, and to determine whether the more enriched C6–C8 LHs in the N-Halahatang oils can be attributed to enhanced burial-thermal maturation, two relatively shallower-burial biodegraded oils (Well LG40: slight to moderate biodegradation‌; Well LG7: heavy to severe biodegradation) from the W-Lunnan area were artificially pyrolyzed to various maturities. Subsequently, LH parameters of the pyrolyzed oils were compared with those of the naturally matured, deeply buried oils (heavy to severe biodegradation) from the N-Halahatang area. The results indicated that both biodegraded oils generated C6–C8 LHs through thermal cracking, and the more severely biodegraded oil (Well LG7) exhibited a lower LH maximum yield than that from Well LG40. Certain parameters for organic matter type classification (n-C7–DMCP–MCH and 3RP–5RP–6RP diagrams) generally remained applicable during thermal maturation, whereas most parameters for secondary alteration identification and maturity assessment were significantly compromised. Additionally, LH parameters of the N-Halahatang oils (1.02–1.22% Ro) matched those of the LG7 pyrolyzed oils at EasyRo = 1.00–1.20%, confirming that the enriched C6–C8 LHs in the N-Halahatang oils can be attributed to cracking of biodegraded oils (with ‌biodegradation levels equivalent to Well LG7‌) under intense burial-thermal maturation. Furthermore, the potential C6–C13 LHs derived from biodegraded oil cracking constitute 11–16 wt% of N-Halahatang’s liquid hydrocarbon resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33256-4.

Introduction

The Ordovician reservoirs in northern Halahatang (N-Halahatang) and western Lunnan (W-Lunnan) areas, serving as key exploration targets within the Tabei Uplift of the Tarim Basin, are extensively characterized by biodegraded oils1–5. The formation of Ordovician traps, crude oil charge, and biodegradation occurred synchronously in these two areas6,7, but the reservoirs subsequently experienced divergent burial-thermal maturation histories after biodegradation. Specifically, (1) during the Late Hercynian (Permian) period, the Ordovician reservoirs in both areas experienced large-scale crude oil charging8–12‌. Subsequent tectonic uplift reduced the thickness of overlying strata to < 1,200 m‌, with reservoir temperatures below 60°C11–13. These shallow-burial conditions facilitated extensive biodegradation of accumulated oils5,14. (2) After the Late Hercynian period, especially during the Himalayan (Neogene–Quaternary) period, the entire Tabei Uplift entered a stage of continuous subsidence until now. The subsidence of the N-Halahatang area was more significant than that of the W-Lunnan area10,11. Consequently, the deeply buried Ordovician reservoirs in the N-Halahatang area (6,500–7,000 m) currently exhibit higher temperatures‌ (~160 °C) and higher thermal maturity (1.02–1.22% Ro)15,16. In contrast, the relatively shallower W-Lunnan reservoirs (5,100–5,800 m) show lower temperatures‌ (~135 °C) and lower thermal maturity (0.70–0.85% Ro)10,17,18. Furthermore, present-day biodegraded oil reservoirs in the N-Halahatang area contain abundant C6–C8 light hydrocarbons (LHs), whereas those in the W-Lunnan area show only trace amounts of C6–C8 LHs19–21.

Typically, LHs in conventional light oils are generated through ‌thermal cracking of kerogen at high thermal maturity‌ (approximately 1.3–2.0% Ro)22. Additionally, secondary alterations of oil reservoirs, such as thermal cracking of heavy oil fractions, gas washing, and evaporative fractionation, are common mechanisms for the formation of LHs23–27. Although high-maturity hydrocarbon charging occurred during the Himalayan period from the ‌eastern Caohu sag‌ and ‌southern Manjia’er depression‌ into the ‌Halahatang‌ and ‌Lunnan‌ areas, the strike-slip-fault activity that generated fractures conducive to hydrocarbon migration ceased during this period28,29. Consequently, late-stage charging of high-maturity hydrocarbons (liquid/gaseous) was confined to ‌the adjacent‌ southern Halahatang and eastern Lunnan areas‌, while the distal N-Halahatang and W-Lunnan areas were largely unaffected by light oil injection, gas washing, or evaporative fractionation11,13,16,29,30.

The C14+ saturated hydrocarbons, C14+ aromatics, and nitrogen, sulfur, and oxygen-containing (NSO) compounds can all serve as precursors of C6–C13 LHs during normal crude oil cracking, with hydrogen-rich C14+ saturated hydrocarbons being particularly significant31. However, in biodegraded oils, C14+ saturated hydrocarbons, especially alkanes, could be preferentially degraded by microorganisms32. Additionally, microbial degradation products (e.g., long-chain fatty acids/alcohols) can subsequently be incorporated into heavy fractions (resin and asphaltenes). Therefore, with increasing biodegradation intensity, heavy fractions may gradually become the primary precursors for C6–C13 LH generation33–35. This notion is supported by artificial maturation36–38 on biodegraded oils from the Liaohe and Junggar basins, which revealed that thermal cracking of heavy fractions can generate new alkanes (C14+ alkanes, C6–C13 LHs, and C1–C5 gases). Specifically for heavily to severely biodegraded oils: (1) in the EasyRo range of 0.66–0.91%, C14+ alkanes were partially replenished; (2) in the EasyRo range of 0.91–1.41%, the replenished C14+ alkanes were further cracked into C6–C13 LHs; (3) when EasyRo > 1.41%, the C6–C13 LHs were gradually transformed into C1–C5 gases.

Through pyrolysis simulation experiments36 on terrigenous-sourced crude oils with varying biodegradation severities from the Junggar Basin, it has been found that differential microbial consumption of C14+ n-alkanes prior to thermal cracking leads to significant variations in both composition and yield of LH products compared to those from non-biodegraded crude oils. As a result, the diagnostic LH fingerprint parameters (including those for organic matter classification, maturity assessment, and secondary alteration identification) may progressively lose their applicability. In this study, the crude oils from the N-Halahatang area in the Tabei Uplift, Tarim Basin (rich in C6–C8 LHs) represent typical marine-sourced biodegraded oils with identical organic input to those from the W-Lunnan area39–42. Moreover, the adjacent W-Lunnan area hosts relatively shallower-buried, lower-maturity biodegraded oils (lacking C6–C8 LHs), providing a good opportunity to clarify whether the abundant C6–C8 LHs in the N-Halahatang oils result from enhanced thermal maturation. To test this hypothesis, two low-maturity biodegraded oils from the W-Lunnan area were artificially pyrolyzed to various maturities. Then the LH products were analyzed by gas chromatography to monitor yield evolution and LH fingerprint parameter changes with increasing thermal maturity. Ultimately, LH parameters of the naturally matured, deeply buried oils from the N-Halahatang area were compared to evaluate their consistency.

Geological setting

The Tarim Basin, which formed through the superposition of ‌Paleozoic cratonic subsidence‌ and ‌Mesozoic-Cenozoic foreland tectonics‌, represents ‌the most prospective exploration area in Northwest China‌ for deep hydrocarbons (> 4,500 m; Fig. 1a)43. Within this basin, the Halahatang sag and Lunnan uplift, situated on the southern flank of the Tabei Uplift, are bounded by four major structural domains (Fig. 1b, c): the Yingmaili uplift (western boundary), Caohu sag (eastern boundary), Luntai fault-uplift (northern limit), and Manjia’er depression (southern periphery). Structurally, the southeast-dipping slope constitutes the primary hydrocarbon migration pathway for the ‌N-Halahatang‌ and ‌W-Lunnan areas, with hydrocarbons sourced from the Manjia’er depression and Caohu sag generative depocenters2,10.

The petroleum system elements of the Lower Paleozoic succession (Fig. 1d) in the N-Halahatang and W-Lunnan areas and their surrounding regions include: (1) Cambrian marine mudstones and shales‌ serving as the primary source rocks, rich in ‌marine sapropelic organic matter39–42; (2) Middle-Lower Ordovician carbonate units‌, including the Yingshan (O1–2y) and Yijianfang (O2yj) Formations, as principal ‌reservoirs‌ with extensive vuggy–fracture porosity46–48; and (3) Upper Ordovician siliciclastic sequences‌, including the Tumuxiuke (O3t), Lianglitage (O3l), and Santamu (O3s) Formations, serving as regional seals due to their thick evaporite-mudstone layers1,2,29. Moreover, the comparable whole-oil carbon isotope (δ13Coil) values of Ordovician crude oils from these two areas suggest a common organic origin (N-Halahatang: −33.4‰ to −32.3‰; W-Lunnan: −32.9‰ to −32.4‰; Fig. 1c)1,45.

Samples and analytical methods

Sample information (biodegradation severity and maturity)

In this study, eighteen biodegraded oil samples were collected from the Ordovician reservoirs of the W-Lunnan and N-Halahatang areas (Table 1; Fig. 1c). To facilitate academic presentation‌, well designations were adopted as sample codes. Two W-Lunnan samples (LG40 and LG7), recovered from depths of 5,165–5,346 m, lack C6–C8 LHs. In contrast, the sixteen N-Halahatang samples, obtained from deeper intervals between 6,512 and 7,018 m, are rich in C6–C8 LHs (Table 1). Notably, compared to oil LG40 (slight to moderate biodegradation), both oil LG7 and oils from the N-Halahatang area exhibit similar heavy to severe biodegradation (Table 1). Given this similar biodegradation grade, oil LG7 serves as an ideal sample for investigating the potential LH generation mechanisms via biodegraded oil cracking within the N-Halahatang reservoirs.

In carbonate reservoirs of the Tarim Basin, where true higher-plant vitrinite is generally absent, the reflectance of vitrinite-like macerals or reservoir bitumen (BRo) serves as a common proxy for assessing organic matter maturity49,50. The initial BRo-to-Ro conversion model51 was later refined44 based on thermal maturation equilibrium principles, leading to improved accuracy in thermal maturity evaluation for carbonate reservoirs. This enhanced model has been successfully applied in the Tarim Basin44. Using Ro values derived from this model by previous studies16–18, we constructed Ro profiles for the N-Halahatang and W-Lunnan areas (Fig. 1e, f), which were used to estimate the maturity range of the initial crude oil samples: the W-Lunnan samples (0.71–0.75% Ro) and the N-Halahatang samples (1.02–1.22% Ro).

Carbon isotopic analysis of whole oil

Approximately 50 µg of each oil sample was sealed in a tin capsule and loaded into the autosampler carousel of a Thermo Finnigan Delta XL isotope ratio mass spectrometer. The oxidation and reduction reactors were maintained at 950 °C and 650 °C, respectively. The CO2 reference gas was calibrated against the NBS-22 carbonate standard, and black carbon was used as a working standard for system performance verification. Carbon isotope ratios (δ13C) were reported against the Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite (VPDB) standard. Each sample was analyzed at least two times until the analytical precision reached < 0.5‰, with the mean value of replicate measurements accepted as the final result‌.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis

Following an established protocol52, the initial biodegraded crude oil samples were separated into saturate, aromatic, resin, and asphaltene (SARA) fractions. In the primary step, asphaltenes were precipitated by adding excess n-hexane, yielding maltene fractions. Subsequent fractionation of maltenes was performed via silica gel/alumina (4:1, v/v) composite column chromatography with sequential elution: (1) pure n-hexane for saturated hydrocarbons, (2) dichloromethane (DCM)/n-hexane (3:1, v/v) for aromatic compounds, and (3) DCM/methanol (2:1, v/v) for resin isolation.

Saturated and aromatic hydrocarbon fractions were analyzed by GC–MS using a Thermo Scientific Trace GC Ultra system interfaced with a Trace DSQ II quadrupole mass spectrometer.‌ Separation occurred on an HP-1 fused silica capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm film thickness). Ultra-high purity helium carrier gas was maintained at 1.2 mL/min throughout the analysis. The temperature program initiated with a 2-min isothermal hold at 60 °C, followed by a 4 °C/min ramp to 300 °C, culminating in a 25-min final isothermal stage. Mass spectrometric detection featured electron ionization (70 eV) with dual acquisition modes: full-scan monitoring across m/z 50–650 and selected ion monitoring (SIM). The ion source and transfer line temperatures were maintained at 260 °C and 280 °C, respectively, with a scan cycle time of 100 ms.

Pyrolysis experiments and collection of light hydrocarbon products

Two relatively shallower-burial, lower-maturity biodegraded oil samples (LG40 and LG7) from the W-Lunnan area were selected for non-isothermal closed-system pyrolysis experiments in sealed gold tubes, following methodologies established in prior studies36,37. To eliminate organic contaminants, gold tubes (pre-welded at one end; 40 mm length × 5 mm internal diameter × 0.25 mm wall thickness) were pre-cleaned via high-temperature annealing at 800 °C for 2 h. Following a 15-minute argon purge cycle, 10–50 mg oil samples were loaded under inert conditions, with subsequent hermetic sealing achieved through precision tube welding. Pyrolysis experiments utilized stainless-steel autoclaves housing sealed gold tubes, with system pressure maintained at 50 ± 2 MPa during the simulated thermal maturation process. A constant heating rate of 20 °C/h was applied from initial ambient temperature to 522 °C. Thermal evolution was monitored at eleven discrete temperature intervals between 402 °C and 522 °C, spaced at 12 ± 0.5 °C increments (Table 2). Upon reaching each designated temperature, autoclaves were immediately removed from the furnace and quenched in an ice-water bath. Thermal maturation levels of pyrolyzed oils were quantified using the EasyRo vitrinite reflectance model53.

LH products from the two pyrolyzed oil sequences were collected using an optimized cryogenic protocol36,54. The procedure consists of three steps: (1) immersing pre-sterilized gold tubes in liquid nitrogen for 5 min to ensure complete solidification, (2) rapidly fracturing the frozen gold tubes under cryogenic conditions, and (3) immediately transferring the frozen tube contents into pre-chilled 4 mL vials containing 3 mL of n-pentane. For subsequent analysis, the LH components are fully dissolved by ultrasonic agitation in n-pentane for 2 min.

Gas chromatography-flame ionization detection (GC–FID) analysis

The LH components in the two pyrolyzed oil sequences were analyzed by GC-FID. Analyses were performed using an Agilent 7890-B gas chromatograph equipped with an HP-PONA capillary column (50 m × 0.2 mm i.d. × 0.5 μm film thickness). The oven temperature program was: hold at 30 °C for 10 min, increase at 1.5 °C/min to 50 °C, then at 4 °C/min to 290 °C, and finally hold at 290 °C for 20 min. Ultrapure nitrogen was employed as the carrier gas at ‌a‌ constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min‌. Prior to injection, deuterated n-C12D26 was added as an internal standard for quantification of individual LH compounds (Fig. 2). The abbreviations of LH compounds are provided in Table 3, and the corresponding LH parameter data are given in Table S1.

In contrast to the laboratory-controlled collection of LHs from pyrolysis oils, the C6–C8 LH content in the N-Halahatang crude oil samples likely experienced‌ varying degrees of loss (detailed in Sect. "Light hydrocarbons in initial crude oils and pyrolyzed oil sequences"). This loss was attributed to two primary factors: (1) depletion induced by prolonged reservoir production and (2) evaporative losses during sampling and long-term storage55. Therefore, conventional LH parameter data for the N-Halahatang crude oils (Table S2) were primarily derived from previous industrial exploitation studies. To minimize LH evaporative loss, samples were stored at −4 °C prior to analysis20,21.

Results

Basic geochemical characteristics of crude oil samples

Table 1 shows the fractional composition and δ13Coil values of crude oil samples from the W-Lunnan and N-Halahatang areas. The W-Lunnan oils are characterized by lower saturated hydrocarbon content (36.2–54.4%) and higher proportions of heavy fractions (resin + asphaltene: 27.7–38.9%). In contrast, the N-Halahatang oils exhibit higher saturated hydrocarbon content (59.7–76.0%) and lower heavy fractions (6.7–22.0%). Notably, the δ13Coil values of crude oil samples from both areas are comparable (−32.8‰ to −32.6‰ for the W-Lunnan samples vs. −33.4‰ to −32.6‰ for the N-Halahatang samples).

Figure 3a–l shows the total ion chromatograms (TICs) and mass chromatograms (m/z 217 and 191) of crude oils from the W-Lunnan and N-Halahatang areas. The TICs consistently exhibit the coexistence of ‌n-alkanes and unresolved complex mixture (UCM) hump. Specifically, the carbon numbers of n-alkanes in the W-Lunnan oils peak at C18± (Fig. 3a, b), whereas those in the N-Halahatang oils peak at C14± (Fig. 3c, d). The sterane mass chromatograms (m/z 217) are consistently characterized by a predominance of C27 and C29 ααα20R steranes over C28 ααα20R sterane (Fig. 3e–h). In terpane mass chromatograms (m/z 191), tricyclic terpanes range from C19 to C30, with C23–C24 as the dominant components (Fig. 3i–l). Pentacyclic triterpanes dominate C29 17α(H),21β(H)−30-norhopane (H29) and C30 17α(H),21β(H)-hopane (H30), showing a decreasing trend in the relative abundance of C31–C35 homohopane series (Fig. 3i–l). Similarly, the relative abundance of pentacyclic triterpanes to tricyclic terpanes is reduced in the N-Halahatang oils compared to the W-Lunnan oils.

Light hydrocarbon parameters related to classification of organic matter types and depositional environments

The composition of C7 LHs contains critical information about organic matter types and depositional environments of source rocks56,57. Ternary diagrams of n-C7–dimethylcyclopentanes (DMCP)–methylcyclohexane (MCH) and 3RP (isoparaffins)–5RP (dimethylcyclopentane)–6RP (toluene + methylcyclohexane) have been widely used as fingerprints in distinguishing organic matter types or depositional environments55,58,59. Importantly‌, the crude oils from the N-Halahatang and W-Lunnan areas have been confirmed to be sourced from marine and sapropelic organic matter39,41. Figure 6 shows the n-C7–DMCP–MCH and 3RP–5RP–6RP distributions of LHs in the LG40 and LG7 pyrolyzed oil sequences. In the EasyRo range of 0.91–1.26%, both ternary diagrams show that n,i-alkanes (n-C7 and 3RP) are preferentially enriched with increasing thermal maturity, and the enrichment extent of n-C7 and 3RP in the LG7 sequence is lower than that in the LG40 sequence (Fig. 6a, b). However, at EasyRo > 1.26%, most cycloalkanes (DMCP and 5RP) and aromatics (6RP) become progressively enriched with further increases in thermal maturity (Fig. 6a, b). In summary, for LHs derived from biodegraded oil cracking, although there are significant shifts in the distributions of both n-C7–DMCP–MCH and 3RP–5RP–6RP with thermal maturity, the classification of organic matter type based on the n-C7–DMCP–MCH distribution appears to remain valid throughout the entire EasyRo range of 0.91–2.51%‌, and the ternary diagram of 3RP–5RP–6RP may also retain diagnostic utility within a broad EasyRo range of 0.91–2.09% (Fig. 6a, b).

Mango60–64 proposed the steady-state catalytic theory of LHs, and indicated that the (2-MH + 2,3-DMP)/(3-MH + 2,4-DMP) and P3/(P2 + N2) ratios of LHs (see Table 3 for the abbreviations of LH compounds) from the same sedimentary environment remain quite stable. These two ratios were subsequently defined as K1 and K2 values and have been widely applied in oil–oil correlation59,65–67. However, for the LG40 and LG7 pyrolyzed oil sequences‌, both K1 and K2 values show a trend of first decreasing and then increasing with thermal maturity, reaching their minima at EasyRo values of 1.40% and 1.72%, respectively (Fig. 7a, b). Furthermore, although the two pyrolyzed oil sequences exhibit similar variation patterns in both K1 and K2 values, they show notable differences in magnitude at identical thermal maturity levels (Fig. 7a, b). In summary‌, K1 and K2 values in LHs originating from biodegraded oil cracking may undergo significant alterations, potentially limiting their reliability for oil–oil correlation‌.

Light hydrocarbon parameters related to maturity

Typically, the heptane value, isoheptane value, n-C7/MCH ratio and C7 n, i-alkanes/cycloalkanes ratio exhibit monotonic increases with increasing thermal maturity24,57,68,69, leading to their adoption as reliable maturity indicators. However, thermal simulations of the LG40 and LG7 pyrolyzed oil sequences revealed that these maturity-related parameters initially increased with thermal maturity but declined after reaching their peak values (Figs. 8 and 9). Notably, the heptane values of the two sequences peaked at an EasyRo value of ~1.40%, whereas the isoheptane values peaked at a significantly lower EasyRo value of ~0.95% (Fig. 8a, b). The n-C7/MCH ratios of the LG40 and LG7 sequences reached their maxima at EasyRo values of ~1.13% and ~1.55%, while the C7 n, i-alkanes/cycloalkanes ratios of the two sequences reached their maxima at EasyRo values around ~1.01% and ~1.26%, respectively (Fig. 9a, b). Additionally, the differences in heptane value, isoheptane value, and C7 n, i-alkanes/cycloalkanes ratio between the two sequences diminished with thermal maturation, becoming almost identical at EasyRo > 1.2%.

Light hydrocarbon diagrams for identification of secondary alterations

The heptane value versus isoheptane value diagram proposed by Thompson57 has been commonly used to identify thermal maturation and biodegradation of crude oil. Figure 10 shows the plot of heptane value versus isoheptane value for both the LG40 and LG7 pyrolyzed oil sequences. Within the EasyRo range of 0.91–1.90%, the points fall within the ‌supermature oil zone (Zone IV). At higher maturation levels (‌1.90–2.09% EasyRo‌), the points shift to the ‌mature oil zone (Zone III). At even higher maturity (‌2.09–2.51% EasyRo‌), they shift further into the ‌normal and biodegraded oil zones (Zones II and I) (Fig. 10). Critically, the systematic variation ‌trends of heptane value versus isoheptane value in these sequences reverse‌ with increasing thermal maturity, contradicting Thompson’s model57. Additionally, within the EasyRo range of 0.91–1.72%, the variation trends of heptane value versus isoheptane value exhibit significant differences between the two sequences (Fig. 10). In summary, for LHs derived from biodegraded oil cracking, the heptane value versus isoheptane value diagram may have only limited applicability for the identification of secondary alterations.

Thompson24 proposed that the n-C7/MCH versus Tol/n-C7 diagram can be used to identify secondary alteration types of crude oil, including evaporative fractionation, maturation, water washing, and biodegradation. As shown in Fig. 11, the n-C7/MCH ratios in both LG40 and LG7 pyrolyzed oil sequences first increased with thermal maturity and then decreased after reaching a similar maximum value (≈ 4.5) at EasyRo values ~1.13% and ~1.55%, respectively. The variation trends of n-C7/MCH versus Tol/n-C7 in both sequences matched Thompson’s maturation trend24 before reaching this maximum, but deviated from it afterward (Fig. 11a). Instead, the post-maximum trends followed Thompson’s evaporative fractionation trend24. Additionally, within the EasyRo range of 0.91–1.55%, the variation trends of n-C7/MCH versus Tol/n-C7 also exhibited significant differences between the two sequences (Fig. 11). In summary, ‌for LHs derived from biodegraded oil cracking‌, the n-C7/MCH versus Tol/n-C7 diagram remains valid for identifying secondary alteration until the n-C7/MCH ratio reaches its maximum value.

Discussion

Influence of burial-thermal maturation on geochemical characteristics of crude oil samples

Crude oils from the N-Halahatang and W-Lunnan areas exhibit similar distributions of C27–C29 regular steranes, tricyclic terpanes, pentacyclic triterpanes, and δ13Coil values (Table 1; Fig. 3e–l), indicating their close genetic affinity. Given the absence of significant late-stage fresh oil mixing11,16,29,30, the intact n-alkane distributions in biodegraded oils from both areas (Fig. 3a–d) can be attributed to the cracking of biodegraded oils induced by burial-thermal maturation (W-Lunnan: Ro = 0.71–0.75%; N-Halahatang: Ro = 1.02–1.22%; see Fig. 1e, f) during the Himalayan (Neogene–Quaternary) period36–38. Moreover, compared with the W-Lunnan oils (C18±; Fig. 3a, b), the lower peak carbon number of the N-Halahatang oils (C14±; Fig. 3c, d) can be attributed to the systematic shift toward lower values in the peak carbon number of newly generated n-alkanes during the cracking of biodegraded oils under higher thermal maturation36–38 (see details in Sect. “Introduction”).

Similarly, compared with the W-Lunnan oils (Fig. 3e, f, i, j), the reduction in the abundance of C27–C29 regular steranes relative to C21–C22 5α(H)-steranes (Fig. 3g, h) and the decrease in the abundance of pentacyclic triterpanes relative to tricyclic terpanes (Fig. 3k, l) in the N-Halahatang oils can also be attributed to the higher burial-thermal maturation. Specifically, under milder burial-thermal maturation (0.71–0.75% Ro), all sterane and terpane compounds in the W-Lunnan oils maintain thermal stability38. By contrast, under intense burial-thermal maturation (1.02–1.22% Ro), the N-Halahatang oils show preferential thermal degradation of C27–C29 steranes relative to C21–C22 5α(H)-steranes, and more extensive thermal degradation of pentacyclic triterpanes relative to tricyclic terpanes. This differential thermal degradation pattern arises from the relatively lower thermal stabilities of C27–C29 regular steranes versus C21–C22 5α(H)-steranes and pentacyclic triterpanes versus tricyclic terpanes70–73.

Influence of thermal maturation and biodegradation on light hydrocarbon yields through oil cracking

During thermal simulation experiments within the EasyRo range of 0.91–1.55%, new alkanes were generated through continuous cracking of heavy fractions in biodegraded oils, resulting in increased ∑‌C6–C13 LH yields with maturation (Fig. 5h). The accumulation of ‌C6–C8 LHs (Figs. 4 and 5a–g) can be attributed to the evolution of newly generated alkanes from higher to lower carbon numbers with thermal maturity. Moreover, compared with ‌n-C7 and n-C8 (peak yields at EasyRo 1.55%; Fig. 5b, c), n-C6 reaches maximum yield at a higher EasyRo value (1.72%; Fig. 5a), indicating that n-alkanes with lower carbon numbers exhibit higher thermal resistance and thus accumulate over a wider EasyRo range37,74. Similarly, alkanes, cycloalkanes, and alkylbenzenes with equivalent carbon numbers exhibit peak yields at increasing EasyRo values (Fig. 5a, b, d–g), which also reflects their increasing thermal stability.

Critically, compared with the LG40 sequence, the lower maximum yields of ∑C6–C13 LHs and individual C6–C8 LH compounds in the LG7 pyrolyzed sequence (Fig. 5a–h) can be attributed to the more severe biodegradation in oil LG7 (Table 1). This finding aligns with the results of a prior oil pyrolysis study36. Fundamentally‌‌, crude oil cracking is a disproportionation process, gradually yielding carbon-rich components (pyrobitumen) and hydrogen-rich components (LHs and hydrocarbon gases)31,75. For the biodegraded oils LG40 and LG7, the prior microbial action preferentially consumed the hydrogen-rich components, particularly the saturated hydrocarbon fraction (54.4% in slightly to moderately biodegraded oil LG40 vs. 36.2% in heavily to severely biodegraded oil LG7; Table 1), thereby reducing their LH generation potential34,36. Consequently, oil LG7 exhibited significantly lower LH generation potential than oil LG40 (Fig. 5a–h).

Influence of thermal maturation on variation patterns of light hydrocarbon parameters during pyrolysis of biodegraded oils

During thermal simulation experiments, LH parameters in the two pyrolyzed oil sequences (LG40 and LG7) underwent systematic changes that compromised their applicability to varying degrees (see details in Sect. "Light hydrocarbon parameters related to classification of organic matter types and depositional environments"–"Light hydrocarbon diagrams for identification of secondary alterations"). These variations were characterized by two distinct maturation stages. (1) Within the oil-generative window‌‌, alkanes showed increasing enrichment compared to cycloalkanes and aromatic hydrocarbons with identical carbon numbers. This preferential enrichment of alkanes, which have lower thermal stability, is mechanistically explained by their significantly lower activation energy of formation compared to that of the more stable cycloalkanes and alkylbenzenes76. Consequently‌, with the increase in EasyRo, the n-C7/(n-C7 + DMCP + MCH) ratio, 3RP/(3RP + 5RP + 6RP) ratio, and multiple alkanes/cycloalkanes ratios (including the heptane value, isoheptane value, n-C7/MCH ratio, and C7 n, i-alkanes/cycloalkanes ratio) increase at first, while the Tol/n-C7 ratio decreases (Figs. 6, 8, 9, 10 and 11). (2) Beyond the oil-generative window, cycloalkanes and alkylbenzenes were preferentially preserved due to their higher thermal stability, while alkanes with lower thermal stability underwent progressive cracking followed by transformation into cycloalkanes and alkylbenzenes via cycloalkylation and aromatization33,61. Consequently, these LH parameters exhibit trend reversal with further maturation (Figs. 6, 8, 9, 10 and 11).

The evolution of K1 and K2 values (for definitions, see Table 3) is controlled by the differential generation and consumption rates of their constituent compounds. For K1 [(2-MH + 2,3-DMP)/(3-MH + 2,4-DMP)], (3-MH + 2,4-DMP) yields increase more (548–1179%) and decrease more (95–96%) than those of (2-MH + 2,3-DMP) (increase: 384–911%; decrease: 93–94%) across the studied maturity range (Fig. 12a, b). This disparity explains the characteristic decrease (at EasyRo 0.91–1.40%) and subsequent increase (at EasyRo > 1.40%) in K1 (Fig. 7a). K2 [P3/(P2 + N2)] is controlled by a parallel mechanism. The yields of (P2 + N2) show much greater changes than P3, with larger increases (1688–3559% vs. 167–423%) up to EasyRo ~1.72% and larger decreases (96–97% vs. 55–60%) thereafter (Fig. 12c, d). Consequently, K2 similarly decreases and then increases with maturity (Fig. 7b). In summary, the non-parallel generation and depletion of the denominator compounds relative to the numerators underpin the consistent decline followed by an increase in both K1 and K2 parameters.

Comparison of LH parameters in deeply buried oils from the N-Halahatang area and artificially pyrolyzed oils from the W-Lunnan area

The EasyRo-dependent LH parameter curves, derived from thermal simulation experiments on relatively shallower-burial oils from the W-Lunnan area, enable us to study the evolution of LH fingerprints in biodegraded oils and to verify potential LH generation mechanisms via oil cracking within the N-Halahatang reservoirs. By projecting LH parameters of the naturally matured, deep-burial N-Halahatang oils onto these curves, the EasyRo ranges of the N-Halahatang oils were determined and compared with the published Ro values16. Notably, with increasing EasyRo values, most LH parameters in LG40 and LG7 pyrolyzed oil sequences exhibit distinct variation trends (Figs. 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11), which we attribute to the differences in their biodegradation severities (Table 1)‌. As a result, the LG7 pyrolyzed oil sequence provided a more reliable analog for the burial-thermal maturation history of biodegraded oils in the N-Halahatang area, given their comparable biodegradation severities (Table 1). Regarding the multiple sets of projected EasyRo ranges obtained from specific parameters (including the K1 value, heptane value, n-C7/MCH ratio, and C7 n, i-alkane/cycloalkane ratio), the high-maturity ranges (~1.70–1.90% EasyRo) were excluded as unreasonable. This is because, although these LH parameters of the LG7 pyrolyzed oils within this high-maturity range aligned with those of the N-Halahatang oils (Figs. 7a and 8a, and 9a), the LG7 pyrolyzed oils in this interval showed a near-total absence of C13+ alkane responses. This absence is inconsistent with the characteristics of the N-Halahatang oils (Fig. 4).

The EasyRo values obtained by projecting onto the curves for the MCH/(n-C7 + DMCP + MCH) ratio, K1 value, heptane value, and n-C7/MCH ratio all fell within ranges of‌ 1.00–1.10% ‌or 1.00–1.20% (Table 4), which were roughly consistent with the burial-thermal maturation degree (1.02–1.22% Ro) derived from the Ro profile (Fig. 1f)16. However, the EasyRo values obtained by projecting onto the curves for the isoheptane value, 5RP/(3RP + 5RP + 6RP) ratio, K2 value, and C7 n,i-alkane/cycloalkane ratio fell within the ranges of 1.10–1.30%, 1.20–1.30%, 1.20–1.40%, and 1.50–1.70%, respectively, which were higher than the Ro range (1.02–1.22%) (Table 4). These elevated estimates can be attributed to the differential evaporative loss of various LH compounds that constitute these LH parameters during sample collection and storage36,55. For example, for the ‌C7 n, i-alkanes/cycloalkanes ratio, C7 n, i-alkanes have lower boiling points and therefore experience greater evaporative loss than C7 cycloalkanes, leading to an underestimation of the ratio and a corresponding overestimation of the projected EasyRo range (Table 4; Fig. 9b). In contrast, for the K1 value [(2-MH + 2,3-DMP)/(3-MH + 2,4-DMP)], both MH and DMP are C7 chain alkanes with similar boiling points, minimizing differential evaporative loss. Thus‌, the projected EasyRo range of the N-Halahatang oils based on K1 values is reliable (Table 4; Fig. 7a). After excluding evaporative-loss-sensitive LH parameters, LH parameters of the N-Halahatang oils (1.02–1.22% Ro) matched those of the LG7 pyrolyzed oils at EasyRo = 1.00–1.20%. This match confirms that the enriched C6–C8 LHs in the N-Halahatang oils can be attributed to cracking of biodegraded oils under‌ intense burial-thermal maturation.

The deeply buried, naturally matured oils from the N-Halahatang area exhibit a highly dispersed distribution of ∑C6–C13 LH concentrations (59–249 mg/g; Fig. 5h), which likely stems from pronounced heterogeneity in the Ordovician vuggy–fracture reservoirs under geological conditions46,47,77 and greatly complicates LH resource assessment. In contrast, our experiment employed an improved LH collection method36,54 (see Sect. "Pyrolysis experiments and collection of light hydrocarbon products") to precisely quantify the LHs generated from biodegraded oil cracking under EasyRo constraints (Fig. 5). Given that the maturity (Ro) of the Ordovician biodegraded reservoirs in the N-Halahatang area predominantly ranges from 1.02% to 1.22% (Fig. 1f), C6–C13 LHs were estimated to constitute approximately 11–16 wt% of the region’s liquid hydrocarbon resources (Fig. 5h).

Conclusions

Two relatively shallower-burial, lower maturity biodegraded oils from the W-Lunnan area of the Tabei Uplift (Well LG40: slight to moderate biodegradation; Well LG7: heavy to severe biodegradation; both lacking C6–C8 LHs) were pyrolyzed to various maturities. Then LH parameters of these two pyrolyzed oil sequences were compared with each other, and also with those of the naturally matured, deeply buried oils (heavy to severe biodegradation; rich in C6–C8 LHs) from the N-Halahatang area of the Tabei Uplift. The following conclusions were drawn.

(1) During the closed-system thermal simulations, the two biodegraded oils (LG40 and LG7) exhibited significant C6–C8 LH generation, with C6–C13 LH yield peaking at EasyRo ~1.55%. This demonstrates that intense thermal maturation can serve as a dominant mechanism for C6–C8 LH generation in biodegraded oils. Moreover, the crude oil with lower biodegradation had higher LH generation potential.

(2) Regarding LH parameters for organic matter type and depositional environment classification, both n-C7–DMCP–MCH and 3RP–5RP–6RP diagrams maintained applicability within broad EasyRo ranges of 0.91–2.51% and 0.91–2.09%, respectively. For secondary alteration identification, only the n-C7/MCH versus Tol/n-C7 diagram retained validity until the n-C7/MCH ratio peaked at ≈ 4.5. However, the applicability of both oil–oil correlation indices (‌K1 and K2 values) and most maturity-related LH parameters‌ was markedly compromised‌. It should be noted that natural LH concentrations may be affected by evaporative losses or reservoir heterogeneity; however, these factors do not undermine the interpretation trends.

(3) After excluding evaporation-sensitive LH parameters, the LH parameters of ‌naturally matured, deeply buried biodegraded oils‌ from the N-Halahatang area (1.02–1.22% Ro, heavy to severe biodegradation) align closely with those of the LG7 pyrolyzed oil sequence (within the EasyRo range of 1.00–1.20%, heavy to severe biodegradation). This confirms that the enriched C6–C8 LHs in the N-Halahatang oils can be attributed to the cracking of biodegraded oils under‌ intense burial-thermal maturation. The potential C6–C13 LHs derived from biodegraded oil cracking account for 11–16 wt% of the liquid hydrocarbon resources in the N-Halahatang area.
