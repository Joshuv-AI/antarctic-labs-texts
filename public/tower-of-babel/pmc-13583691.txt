Late-life depression and risk of all-cause and cause-specific mortality in an Asian population: the Singapore Chinese Health Study
Authors: Huiqi Li, Sheng Fong, Woon-Puay Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough late-life depressive symptoms were associated with mortality risk, their relationship with cause-specific mortality remained unclear. Hence, we investigated these in older Chinese adults living in Singapore.MethodsWe used data from 16 782 participants aged 61–96 years from follow-up 3 (2014–2016) of the Singapore Chinese Health Study. Depressive symptoms were defined as having ≥5 symptoms on the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale. Mortality data were obtained through linkage with nation-wide death registry till 31 December 2022. We used Cox proportional hazard models and competing risk regression models to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for association with mortalities.ResultsAfter a mean follow-up of 6.9 years, there were 2 960 deaths. Depressive symptoms were significantly associated with total and cause-specific mortality; the multivariable-adjusted HRs (95% CIs) were 1.20 (1.11 to 1.30), 1.38 (1.14 to 1.67), 1.41 (1.16 to 1.70) and 1.91 (1.13 to 3.21) for mortalities from all causes, ischaemic heart disease (IHD), infectious respiratory diseases and chronic, non-malignant respiratory diseases, respectively. The associations were stronger among smokers for all-cause mortality (HR (95% CI) 1.55 (1.25 to 1.92) vs 1.15 (1.05 to 1.26) in non-smokers; Pdifference=0.012) and IHD mortality (2.27 (1.33 to 3.90) vs 1.27 (1.03 to 1.57); Pdifference=0.049) and stronger for IHD mortality among participants aged <75 years (1.89 (1.35 to 2.64) vs 1.16 (0.92 to 1.46) among those aged ≥75 years; Pdifference=0.019).ConclusionsLate-life depressive symptoms were associated with increased risk of all-cause, IHD and respiratory disease mortalities. The associations were stronger among those <75 years old and smokers, highlighting the importance of screening for and addressing depressive symptoms in these at-risk groups.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Late-life depression is associated with increased all-cause mortality across diverse populations. However, evidence on cause-specific mortalities remains limited, and most studies to date have been conducted in Western populations. In light of the growing burden of depressive symptoms among older adults in Asian countries over the past three decades, there is a pressing need to investigate the relationship between late-life depressive symptoms and all-cause and cause-specific mortalities and to evaluate potential effect modification by demographic and behavioural factors in Asian populations.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In this large-scale prospective study of 16 782 participants aged 61–96 years from the Singapore Chinese Health Study, we found that late-life depressive symptoms were independently associated with increased risks of all-cause, ischaemic heart disease, infectious respiratory disease and chronic non-malignant respiratory disease mortalities by 20%, 38%, 41% and 91%, respectively, over a mean follow-up of 6.9 years. Additionally, the depression-mortality associations were stronger among smokers and individuals younger than 75 years, suggesting the importance of identifying high-risk subgroups for early screening and informing future strategies to address late-life depressive symptoms and their adverse health outcomes.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings suggested that depressive symptoms identified through population-based screening may have prognostic relevance for mortality risk in older adults. The stronger depression-mortality associations observed among smokers and individuals aged <75 years emphasise the modifying roles of smoking and age in identifying and managing vulnerable subgroups. Given the persistent underdiagnosis and undertreatment of late-life depression in primary care settings, identifying high-risk individuals, particularly smokers, and integrating smoking cessation with depression management could be beneficial for improving mental health and survival outcomes.

Introduction

Late-life depression has been linked to increased risks of comorbidities,1 cognitive decline and functional impairment,2 all of which contribute to higher mortality among older adults.3 Notably, depression is highly treatable. Emerging evidence has revealed that appropriate treatment of late-life depression could improve quality of life and reduce the risk of mortality among both healthy older adults and those with comorbidities,4 highlighting the importance of early identification and intervention for late-life depression.

Although the association between late-life depression and all-cause mortality has been well documented across various populations,5–7 evidence regarding its relationship with cause-specific mortality is less known. In a recent meta-analysis of 49 cohorts reporting on all-cause mortality and 15 cohorts on cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality, the findings that late-life depression was associated with increased risks of all-cause and CVD mortality were primarily derived from studies conducted in Western populations.8 For CVD mortality, subgroup analysis of the only three cohort studies from Asia reported an increased but statistically insignificant risk. Moreover, evidence pertaining to mortality from respiratory diseases and cancer is scarce.910 Such gaps indicate a pressing need for further research in Asian populations, particularly in light of the growing burden of depressive symptoms among older adults in Asian countries over the recent three decades.11

Furthermore, the association between late-life depression and mortality may vary across population subgroups; several studies have indicated that the mortality risk with late-life depression may differ by age and sex.612 Moreover, despite evidence suggesting that smoking may both contribute to the presence of depressive symptoms and independently increase mortality risk,1314 few studies have assessed the potential effect modification by smoking behaviour. Given the substantial gaps between diagnosis and treatment of late-life depression,11 identifying high-risk groups is particularly important for conducting early screening and implementing targeted management for depression in older adults.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to examine the associations between late-life depressive symptoms and all-cause and cause-specific mortalities in a prospective cohort of an Asian population and to examine factors that may influence these associations. We hypothesised that late-life depressive symptoms could be associated with a higher risk of mortality from all causes, cardiovascular diseases and respiratory diseases, and that these associations might be modified by age and smoking status.

Materials and methods

Study population

We used data from the Singapore Chinese Health Study, which is an ongoing population-based cohort established between 1993 and 1998 with the enrolment of 63 257 participants (27 959 men and 35 298 women) who were aged 45–74 years at that time.15 Although the cohort was initially designed for longitudinal assessment of dietary and other lifestyle determinants related to cancer aetiologies, it has since been expanded in recent follow-up interviews to include a wider range of chronic diseases and ageing-related outcomes. Eligible participants were restricted to Hokkiens and Cantonese, who originated from Fujian and Guangdong provinces, respectively, in Southern China, and formed the two major dialect groups of Chinese in Singapore. Following baseline enrolment, surviving and consenting participants were recontacted for three follow-up visits in 1999–2004 (follow-up 1 by phone), 2006–2010 (follow-up 2 by phone) and 2014–2016 (follow-up 3 by face-to-face interview) for updates on selected lifestyle factors and medical conditions.

During the third follow-up (2014–2016), when surviving participants were aged 61–96 years, ageing-related outcomes, such as depressive symptoms, cognitive function and physical function, were assessed for the first time. They were recontacted sequentially based on the order of their participation in the previous follow-up wave. Although there were potentially 45 109 surviving participants, after successfully revisiting 17 107 participants, the third follow-up was stopped prematurely due to limited funding.

This study used data obtained from the third follow-up interviews comprising 17 107 participants. We sequentially excluded 107 participants who were blind, mute, deaf or living in nursing homes and 218 participants with missing responses for depressive symptoms, leaving 16 782 participants in the current analysis (figure 1).

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Assessment of covariates and depressive symptoms

During the third follow-up, participants answered a structured questionnaire, administered by trained interviewers, including information on demographics (eg, age and marital status), weight, lifestyle factors (eg, smoking habits, sleep duration and diet quality), medical history of comorbidities (eg, hypertension, ischaemic heart disease (IHD), stroke, diabetes, cancer and chronic lung diseases). Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15), a validated and reliable screening tool for identifying late-life depressive symptoms at the population level with demonstrated equivalency across age, gender and ethnicity.16 Total scores on GDS-15 range from 0 to 15, with higher scores indicating an increasing number of depressive symptoms. In the primary analysis, late-life depressive symptoms were defined using the conventional cut-off of GDS-15 ≥5; participants with scores of 0–4 were classified as having no depressive symptoms, whereas those with scores ≥5 were classified as having depressive symptoms. To further evaluate whether mortality risk varied by the severity of depressive symptoms, we also categorised GDS-15 scores into three levels: 0–4 as no depressive symptoms, 5–9 as mild depressive symptoms and ≥10 as moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms.17

Diet quality was assessed using a validated 21-item dietary screener based on the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) score.18 Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m2). For missing BMI (n=844) and DASH score (n=2 141) values, multiple imputation was conducted using the predictive-mean matching methods,19 with detailed description in the online supplemental methods. We also collected data on factors associated with late-life depressive symptoms, including functional limitation, cognitive function, self-rated health and social support, as previously published.20 Specifically, self-rated health was categorised as ‘excellent’, ‘very good’, ‘good’ or ‘fair/poor’ from the responses of participants. Functional limitation was assessed using the Lawton Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale (IADL), and those with limitations in at least one item were considered to have IADL impairment.21 We measured cognitive function using a Singapore-modified version of the Mini Mental State Examination that included 30 items, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance.22 Education-specific cut-off points were applied to define cognitive impairment: <18, <21 and <25 for participants with no formal education, with 1–6 years of education and with >6 years of education, respectively. Social support was measured using the Duke Social Support Scale—details and validity have been published previously.23 Briefly, this scale identifies 10 sources of support from both family and non-family members, as well as a primary person that the respondent can rely on. Scores of this scale range from 0 (no support) to 100 (most support), with participants in the lowest decile being classified to have ‘poor social support’.

Ascertainment of mortality cases

Deaths from all-cause, CVD, respiratory diseases and cancer were coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9, up to 31 December 2011)24 or Tenth Revision (ICD-10, from 2012 to 2020)25 and ascertained through linkage with the nation-wide registry of births and deaths in Singapore until 31 December 2022. Deaths from CVD were further specified as IHD, other chronic heart diseases and stroke. Deaths from respiratory diseases were further specified as infectious respiratory diseases and chronic non-malignant respiratory diseases. The ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes used to define each category of cause-specific mortality were provided in online supplemental table 1.

Statistical analysis

We compared the characteristics of participants at the third follow-up by status of depressive symptoms using χ2 test for categorical variables or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Cox proportional hazards models were fitted to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for the associations of late-life depressive symptoms with all-cause mortality. The Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model was used to estimate cumulative incidence functions for cause-specific mortalities in order to generate curves illustrating the cumulative probability of death from each specific cause and also to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for the associations of late-life depressive symptoms with cause-specific mortality. Late-life depressive symptoms were first analysed as a binary variable using the conventional GDS-15 cut-off, with scores of 0–4 (no depressive symptoms) as the referent group. We then modelled depressive symptom severity as a three-level categorical variable, with no depressive symptoms as the referent group, to examine whether mortality risk increased across higher levels of symptom severity. Tests for trend were performed by modelling the three-level depressive symptom severity variable as an ordinal term.

We conducted sequential adjustment for the estimation. In model 1, we adjusted for the following covariates: age (years), sex, dialect group (Hokkien or Cantonese), marital status (married or otherwise), education level (no formal education, primary school, or secondary school or higher), BMI (<18.5, 18.5–22.9, 23.0–27.4 or ≥27.5 kg/m2), cigarette smoking (never, former or current smokers), sleep duration (≤5, 6–8 or ≥9 hours/day) and DASH score (quintiles). In model 2, we additionally adjusted for medical history of hypertension, IHD, stroke, diabetes, cancer and chronic lung diseases (yes, no). Given the multidimensional nature of health in later life,20 cognitive, functional, physical and social vulnerabilities may be interrelated and may jointly contribute to both depressive symptoms and survival in older adults. We therefore adjusted for these factors in model 3 to evaluate whether depressive symptoms had prognostic relevance for mortality independent of these major ageing-related vulnerabilities. Among these factors, function-limiting pain and self-rated health require more careful consideration because they may be closely related to depressive symptoms, chronic disease burden, frailty and mortality, raising the possibility of overadjustment or collider-related bias. To address this, we constructed an exploratory directed acyclic graph (DAG)26 to evaluate whether function-limiting pain and self-rated health should be included as adjustment variables in the final model. As shown in online supplemental figure 1, self-rated health was considered a potential downstream composite indicator influenced by multiple ageing-related domains and therefore a possible collider; it was therefore not included in the fully adjusted model to minimise potential collider bias. Accordingly, model 3 further adjusted for cognitive impairment (yes, no), IADL impairment (yes, no), function-limiting pain (yes, no) and poor social support (yes, no). The proportionality assumption was tested using the supremum test, and no violation was detected. We found that participants who attended the third follow-up visit (included in this study) were generally younger and had a healthier lifestyle and fewer comorbidities at baseline compared with those who did not attend (not included in this study) (online supplemental table 2). As such, we constructed the inverse probability-weighted regression models to address the potential selection bias caused by loss to follow-up.

We also examined interactions between late-life depressive symptoms and potential modifying factors, including demographic, lifestyle and ageing-related factors. P value for difference across subgroups was calculated using Altman’s methods to evaluate heterogeneity of the depression–mortality associations across the subgroups.27

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis that excluded participants with cognitive impairment measured at the third follow-up (n=2 421), given the potential cooccurrence between depressive symptoms and cognitive vulnerability in later life. We further conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding participants who died within the first 1–2 years of follow-up (n=535) to reduce the possibility that the observed associations were driven by preterminal illness or frailty.

A two-sided P value <0.05 was defined as statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS (V.9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina).

Results

Among 16 782 study participants, 4 440 (26.5%) had late-life depressive symptoms. Median (IQR) and mean (SD) age at the third follow-up were 72.2 (68.0–77.3) years and 73.2 (6.4) years, respectively. Participants with late-life depressive symptoms were older, less educated and more likely to be female and current smokers compared with those without depressive symptoms. They were also more likely to have poorer diet quality, suboptimal sleep duration, had more comorbidities, worse functional status and cognitive health and lower levels of social support (table 1).

A total of 2 960 deaths occurred over a mean follow-up of 6.9 years, including 520 deaths from IHD, 134 deaths from other chronic heart diseases, 169 deaths from stroke, 662 deaths from respiratory diseases and 984 deaths from cancer. Figure 2 shows the cumulative incidence for all-cause and cause-specific mortalities comparing participants with and without late-life depressive symptoms. Compared with individuals without depressive symptoms, those with depressive symptoms had higher cumulative incidence of mortalities from IHD, infectious respiratory diseases and chronic, non-malignant respiratory diseases with increasing follow-up time (χ2=10.582, P=0.001; χ2=12.372, P<0.001; χ2=5.861, P=0.016). After accounting for demographics, lifestyles, comorbidities and ageing-related factors, late-life depressive symptoms were independently associated with a significantly increased risk of all-cause, CVD, IHD, infectious respiratory disease and chronic, non-malignant respiratory disease mortalities (table 2). Compared with those with <5 depressive symptoms, participants with ≥5 depressive symptoms had a HR (95% CI) of 1.20 (1.11 to 1.30) for all-cause, 1.24 (1.06 to 1.44) for CVD, 1.38 (1.14 to 1.67) for IHD, 1.41 (1.16 to 1.70) for infectious respiratory disease and 1.91 (1.13 to 3.21) for chronic, non-malignant respiratory disease mortalities. When depressive symptoms were further categorised into three severity levels, participants with mild and moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms had progressively higher hazards of all-cause and cause-specific mortalities compared with those with no depressive symptoms.

The results remained robust in sensitivity analyses using inverse probability-weighted models to handle survival bias due to attrition (online supplemental table 3), after excluding participants with concurrent cognitive impairment measured at the third follow-up (online supplemental table 4) and after excluding participants who died within the first 1–2 years of follow-up to reduce the potential influence of preterminal illness and frailty (online supplemental table 5).

In stratified analysis (figure 3), stronger associations of late-life depressive symptoms with all-cause mortality were found among smokers than non-smokers. The HR (95% CI) for the association between depressive symptoms and all-cause mortality was 1.55 (1.25–1.92) for smokers but 1.15 (1.05–1.26) for non-smokers (P value for difference=0.012). A similar pattern was observed for IHD mortality, with a stronger association among smokers (HR 2.27; 95% CI 1.33 to 3.90) than non-smokers (HR 1.27; 95% CI 1.03 to 1.57; P value for difference=0.049). Additionally, the association between late-life depressive symptoms and IHD mortality varied by age and was significantly higher for those aged <75 years. The HR (95% CI) for the association between depressive symptoms and IHD mortality was 1.89 (1.35 to 2.64) among those aged <75 years and 1.16 (0.92 to 1.46) among those aged ≥75 years (P value for difference=0.019). Conversely, the association between late-life depressive symptoms and mortality from chronic, non-malignant respiratory diseases was stronger for those aged ≥75 years, with HRs (95% CIs) being 3.10 (1.60 to 6.01) for those aged ≥75 years compared with 0.78 (0.29 to 2.11) for those aged <75 years. We did not find significant interactions between late-life depression and other factors (data not shown).

Discussion

In this large cohort of older Singaporean Chinese adults, we found that late-life depressive symptoms were significantly linked to higher risks of all-cause mortality and mortality from IHD, infectious respiratory diseases and chronic, non-malignant respiratory diseases. Notably, the association between depressive symptoms and mortality was particularly pronounced among smokers. Additionally, the link between late-life depressive symptoms and IHD mortality was stronger in smokers and in those under 75 years of age compared with older participants.

Previous research on the relationship between late-life depression and mortality has produced mixed results across different populations.5–728 For example, a large cohort study in eight low- and middle-income countries, involving 15 991 individuals aged 65 and older, found that subthreshold depression did not significantly increase the risk of all-cause mortality over an average follow-up of 3.9 years (HR 1.06; 95% CI 0.93 to 1.22).28 In contrast, our findings are consistent with a 2019 meta-analysis of 53 cohort studies, mostly from Western countries, which reported that late-life depression was associated with a 34% increase in all-cause mortality and a 31% increase in CVD mortality.8 However, substantial heterogeneity was present across studies regarding population characteristics and covariate adjustments. Specifically, late-life depression raised the risk of CVD mortality by 27% in European populations and 35% in North American populations, while the increase in Asian populations was not statistically significant (HR 1.46; 95% CI 0.89 to 2.40), likely due to a smaller number of studies (only three from Asia). Our study thus addresses this gap by providing evidence from an Asian cohort, showing a 24% increased risk of CVD mortality after comprehensive adjustment. In terms of control for other adverse ageing outcomes, late-life health is multidimensional, and depressive symptoms often cluster with cognitive impairment, functional limitations, chronic diseases and social vulnerabilities in older adults.20 However, these ageing-related vulnerabilities have been inconsistently considered in prior studies, making it difficult to determine whether observed associations between late-life depressive symptoms and mortality reflect prognostic relevance independent of broader health decline. In the present study, we therefore estimated a direct association by adjusting for these ageing-related factors. Among these factors, function-limiting pain and self-rated health required particularly careful consideration because they may be closely interrelated with depressive symptoms, chronic disease burden, frailty and mortality, and adjustment for such variables could potentially introduce overadjustment or collider-related bias. We therefore constructed an exploratory DAG to clarify their assumed relationships with depressive symptoms and mortality. Based on this DAG, function-limiting pain was retained as an adjustment variable, whereas self-rated health was considered a potential downstream composite indicator and was not included in the final multivariable model to minimise the risk of collider bias.

Interestingly, we observed in our study population that the elevated risk of CVD mortality was mainly attributed to increased IHD mortality, with no significant associations found for deaths from other chronic heart diseases or stroke. This may be due to fewer cases in these categories. Our results regarding IHD mortality are in line with a prior meta-analysis of 11 cohort studies involving middle-aged and older adults, which found a 64% higher risk of IHD mortality among those with depression.29 Potential biological mechanisms that may underlie these associations include dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, elevated cortisol, increased systemic inflammation, enhanced platelet activation and imbalances in autonomic nervous system function,30 all of which are relevant to IHD.

There remains a lack of studies specifically focusing on the association between late-life depression and respiratory disease mortality.910 Our study is among the first to demonstrate a significant increase in this risk among individuals with late-life depressive symptoms. For example, a cohort study of 55 946 older adults in Hong Kong, using GDS ≥8 as a cut-off, found no association between late-life depression and respiratory disease mortality over 6 years of follow-up (HR 1.07; 95% CI 0.89 to 1.28).10 Similarly, data from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) involving 4 860 participants aged 50 and above found a non-significant increase in respiratory disease mortality related to episodic depressive symptoms among women (HR 1.47; 95% CI 0.49 to 4.47) over 8 years.9 Differences in findings could stem from variations in cut-offs for definition of depressive symptoms, assessment methods (episodic vs persistent symptoms) and statistical power due to small case numbers (128 cases in ELSA vs 632 in our study).

We also observed that the relationship between depressive symptoms and mortality from chronic respiratory diseases was more pronounced among those aged 75 or older compared with younger participants. This could be related to the advanced or severe stages of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), which accounted for about 76% of chronic respiratory disease deaths in our cohort. Depressive symptoms may exacerbate the physiological and functional impact of COPD, further increasing mortality risk in this older group. However, given the limited number of deaths from chronic respiratory diseases in our analysis, further studies are needed to confirm this observation.

Smoking status emerged as a significant modifier of the association between late-life depressive symptoms and all-cause mortality and IHD mortality, with the relationship being notably stronger in smokers. This suggested that smoking status may help identify a subgroup of older adults in whom depressive symptoms have stronger prognostic relevance for mortality. Since cigarette smoking is a major risk factor for a range of diseases and the detrimental effects of smoking and depressive symptoms may be additive, future studies should evaluate whether addressing both factors could improve health outcomes in older adults. Because late-life depression is often underdiagnosed and undertreated in primary care due to lack of awareness, stigma and concerns about the cost-effectiveness of mass screening,11 identifying high-risk individuals, especially smokers, may help inform more targeted approaches to screening and management.

We further found that among participants with depressive symptoms, those aged 75 or older had a lower risk of IHD mortality than their younger counterparts. Previous work in this cohort showed an inverted J-shaped relationship between age and depressive symptoms, with the highest odds of depressive symptoms seen at age 75 and reduced odds thereafter (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.31 for ages 70–80 and OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.15 for those over 80).31 It is possible that the oldest participants have developed coping mechanisms that decrease their likelihood of experiencing depressive symptoms.32 Similarly, the reduced association between depressive symptoms and IHD mortality in the oldest adults may reflect a ‘resilient survivor’ group, with better psychological strategies and healthier behaviours such as reduced obesity and smoking rates, which buffer against the adverse physiological effects of depressive symptoms. Survival bias could also play a role, with more resilient individuals being more likely to survive into advanced age, thereby weakening the observed association in this group. For example, in our study population, participants aged 75 and above were less likely to be overweight or current smokers compared with younger participants (data not shown).

This study has notable strengths of large sample size, comprehensive assessment of confounders and reliable sources of mortality identification. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, we did not collect information on participants’ access to mental health services or treatment for depressive symptoms, limiting us to investigate whether these factors could have modified the relationship between late-life depressive symptoms and mortality. Second, we only assessed depressive symptoms at a single time point, and any subsequent change in depressive status during follow-up could lead to non-differential misclassification and a consequent underestimation of the risk estimates. Although our primary analysis by the severity of depressive symptom showed consistent findings across increasing GDS-15 score categories, repeated assessments are needed in future studies to evaluate whether persistent, remitted or incident depressive symptoms are differentially associated with mortality risk. Third, potential selection bias should be considered, as the present study only included participants who attended the third follow-up interview. As shown in online supplemental table 2, participants included in the third follow-up were generally younger, had healthier lifestyle profiles and reported fewer comorbid conditions at enrolment (1993–1998) than those who were not included, and such selective bias of including participants with a lower baseline mortality risk could lead to an underestimation of the depression–mortality association. Furthermore, although the results were robust in inverse probability-weighted analyses, such weighting can only account for measured differences related to participation. Residual selection bias due to unmeasured determinants of survival or follow-up participation cannot be excluded. Fourth, although we had adjusted for a wide range of potential confounders, the possibility of residual confounding could not be entirely ruled out. Finally, these findings should be interpreted with caution when extrapolated to other populations, given potential differences in population characteristics.

In conclusion, late-life depressive symptoms were independently associated with higher risks of all-cause mortality and mortality from IHD and respiratory diseases, particularly among adults younger than 75 years and smokers. These findings suggested that depressive symptoms identified through population-based screening have prognostic significance for mortality risk among older adults. Given the increasing prevalence and frequent underdiagnosis of late-life depression, early identification of depressive symptoms may help recognise older adults at elevated mortality risk and inform targeted management to address late-life depressive symptoms and its adverse health outcomes.
