Toward assessment of rupture risk predictors in abdominal aortic aneurysms including intraluminal thrombus based on 3D+t ultrasound images
Authors: Arjet Nievergeld, Judith Fonken, Esther Maas, Jan-Willem Muller, Mirunalini Thirugnanasambandam, Marc van Sambeek, Richard Lopata
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImage-based patient-specific rupture risk analysis for abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs) has shown considerable promise. However, clinical translation has been hampered by the use of invasive and costly imaging modalities. Despite being a promising alternative, ultrasound (US) makes a full analysis, including intraluminal thrombus (ILT), not trivial. This study explored the feasibility of assessing AAA rupture risk parameters, e.g., peak wall stress (PWS) and peak wall rupture index (PWRI), using US-based models of the AAA wall, finally including ILT. Three-dimensional US data were segmented from a group of AAA patients whose CT data were available within 30 days. The segmented vessel wall and ILT boundaries were converted into a mesh including and excluding ILT to evaluate the effect of adding ILT on the model output. US-based rupture risk parameters (PWS and PWRI) were compared to CT-based results. The US-based PWS and PWRI, including ILT, showed good agreement with CT-based results, and the model excluding ILT showed no significant bias in wall stress or rupture index. When including ILT, a lower US-based wall stress and rupture index of 7.2% and 3.8% were found, respectively. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of PWS was 0.60. The highest ICC was found for the PWRI (ICC = 0.86), indicating good absolute agreement. This study showed that PWRI can be estimated with US when including the ILT, yielding comparable results to CT, and good absolute agreement. Future work should focus on improving the contrast of ILT in US, since this will be essential to performing large-scale studies in AAA cohorts.

INTRODUCTION

Abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs) are dilatations in the abdominal aorta that can eventually rupture. Current clinical guidelines of intervention are solely based on AAA diameter.1 However, the AAA diameter is not the only risk factor,2 since rupture has also been associated with, e.g., AAA shape,3 female gender,4 and family history.5 Biomechanical models can be used to improve rupture risk prediction by providing biomechanical markers for rupture potential, for example, peak wall stress (PWS). A three-dimensional (3D) map of the wall stress can be estimated with the finite element method (FEM) based on the patient-specific AAA geometries, typically obtained with imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or more recently, (3D) ultrasound (US).2,6,7

A considerable number of AAAs typically contain intraluminal thrombus (ILT).8 There is a controversy regarding its role on AAA growth and rupture risk. It has been argued that ILT reduces AAA wall strength, because its presence limits oxygen diffusion to the vessel wall.9 On the other hand, finite element studies of AAAs including ILT (based on CT and MRI) showed that mechanically speaking the ILT reduces wall stress, thereby potentially decreasing the rupture risk.10,11

The ILT thickness, and other parameters, such as local AAA diameter, family history, and gender have been used to estimate a measure of wall strength, as proposed by Vande Geest et al.12 This estimated wall strength could be used in combination with the wall stress to determine the so-called peak wall rupture index (PWRI). Multiple studies showed that PWRI is a better rupture risk predictor than both the PWS and the current clinical standard, i.e., the AAA diameter.13–15 Gasser et al. showed that accounting for ILT and the non-homogeneous strength of the AAA wall reinforced statistical differences in PWRI between ruptured and non-ruptured AAAs.16 For clinical translation, the so-called equivalent diameter was introduced, a maximum AAA diameter that is corrected for the PWRI, i.e., a higher PWRI scales the maximum diameter to a higher value that possibly reaches the threshold for surgery. This equivalent diameter provides the clinician with a more straightforward interpretation of the PWRI in relation to the current clinical standard, i.e., the diameter-based guidelines for AAA repair indication.2

The aforementioned studies estimating PWRI are all based on CT imaging. However, CT involves the use of ionizing radiation and nephrotoxic contrast agents. It is therefore unsuitable for frequent follow-up for rupture risk assessment or longitudinal studies on AAA progression.17 These disadvantages have hampered widespread clinical use of CT FEM-based rupture risk assessment. US is a safe, cost-effective, and easy to use imaging modality, making it the preferred imaging modality for monitoring AAA patients. The use of 3D US can boost the clinical applicability of FEM-based rupture risk prediction of AAAs. However, 3D US also has its limitations, such as the limited field-of-view (FOV), the poor visibility of the ILT due to its hypoechoic character,18 and low contrast in general.

Recently, US-based FEM models of AAAs were introduced, already showing a good agreement between US- and CT-based wall stress analyses.6,19 So far, the ILT has not been considered in these US-based PWS estimation studies, which prevents the exploration of PWRI quantification, as well as the analysis of ILT-related parameters. The aim of this study is to investigate the feasibility of creating AAA models including the ILT, based on time-resolved 3D US imaging. In this study, a segmentation algorithm for the AAA geometry including ILT that was previously developed in our group,20 was employed for the creation of the patient-specific models. Resulting anatomical and mechanical model parameters, e.g., ILT thickness, stress, and strain, and rupture risk parameters, such as estimated PWS and PWRI, were validated with CT, and their absolute agreement was assessed.

Statistical analysis

For a more quantitative analysis, the results from all patients were visualized and statistically analyzed using Bland–Altman plots. Figure 6 shows the regression analysis for the stress, strain, and wall expansion for both models with and without ILT. The differences between US and CT were normally distributed for all quantities (pLillie > 0.05), except for the wall stress. For all quantities of the models without ILT, the slope of the regression line was close to 1 (0.98–1.05), and a strong correlation was found (r or ρ = 0.85–0.93). The precision of the stress and strain is comparable, which is reflected in an RPC of 19% and 22%, respectively. The bias of the stress was not significant (p = 0.43), in contrast to strain (p = 0.01). The precision of the expansion was lower (RPC = 42%), compared to the stress and strain, and the bias found was significant (p < 0.01).

Absolute agreement of ultrasound and Computed Tomography-based model parameters

Figure 8 shows rupture risk parameters determined with CT, US, and US corrected for the bias determined in Figs. 6 and 7. The ICC (between scaled US and CT) and its 95th percentile confidence interval showed that the diameter calculated using the 3D geometry has a higher absolute agreement (ICC = 0.67) compared to the one measured in clinic (ICC = 0.28). The 2D US-based diameter is generally higher compared to the one obtained with CT. The ICCs of the PWS and 99th percentile wall stresses are comparable (0.60 and 0.58, respectively). The PWRI has the highest ICC (0.86), which represents good absolute agreement.21 For patients 12 and 16, a larger overestimation was seen in the US-based PWRI. For patient 12, this overestimation resulted from a low lumen-ILT contrast on the anterior side of the AAA and led to differences in ILT thickness between US and CT, which propagated as a lower wall strength in the US-based model. The differences in PWRI for patient 16 resulted from a local overestimation of the US-based wall stress, and a lower wall strength because of the overestimation of low ILT thickness (shown in Fig. 7).

DISCUSSION

The feasibility of US-based wall stress analysis of AAAs was previously shown, with a good agreement with CT.6 However, until now, this analysis lacked the incorporation of ILT, despite the knowledge that ILT affects rupture risk.16 Including ILT in these models allows for stress analysis in which the cushioning effect of the ILT is taken into account, and wall strength estimation in which the wall weakening effect of the ILT is incorporated.12 This study is a first step in showing the feasibility of incorporating ILT in patient-specific models to calculate the US-based PWS and PWRI, which have shown to be promising in AAA rupture risk prediction.13,16

The US-based PWRI found in this study showed good agreement compared to CT (ICC = 0.86).21 This agreement surpasses that of the current clinical criterion, i.e., the anterior–posterior diameter (ICC = 0.28), as well as that of the 3D US-based diameter (ICC = 0.67). The PWS and the 99th percentile wall stress showed a lower agreement between US and CT compared to the 3D diameter (ICC of 0.60 and 0.58, respectively). The lower ICC for the clinical diameter could be explained by the use of 2D-US, which is more prone to error than 3D CT.18

In the US-based segmentations, an overestimation in ILT geometry was observed for small ILT thickness. This overestimation decreased with increasing ILT thickness up to a bias of 0.7% (not significant) for the largest ILT thickness in a sample. A higher US-based ILT thickness further propagates in a lower wall stress and PWRI (median and mean bias of 7.2% and 3.8%, respectively), compared to CT. This bias is not present for the models excluding ILT. Improving ILT segmentation for small ILT thickness, e.g., with deep-learning based segmentation, could reduce this bias. Additionally, large differences in expansion were found between US and CT (RPC = 43%). During this study, it was seen that the expansion highly depends on the smoothness of the segmentation, because of bulging of the geometry.

The median scaled PWS and PWRI were 203 kPa and 0.18, respectively. Other studies report higher values for PWS (mean ranging from 249 to 330 kPa)13,22–24 and PWRI (mean ranging from 0.48 to 1.2). However, these studies included aneurysms with larger diameters (mean ranging from 71 to 82 mm). Siika et al. included AAAs having a comparable diameter and showed a mean PWS and PWRI of 197 kPa and 0.35, respectively.25 Differences in the model characteristics, e.g., inclusion of surrounding tissue26 and different stiffness for ILT and vessel wall, could explain this difference in PRWI. Mainly for the ILT, a wide range of stiffness is found in the literature.27–29 Mechanical characterization of vessel wall, ILT, and surrounding tissue could further personalize rupture risk prediction by combining US speckle tracking with forward or inverse finite element modeling.30,31 Next to the estimation of vascular stiffness on a patient-specific basis,30 speckle tracking could also be used for validation of the simulated tissue motion.

In the equation proposed to estimate wall strength, the family history is required as input.12 The patient files were evaluated for family history, but it was noted that there was no extensive documentation regarding AAA prevalence within the patients' family. Hence, it was chosen not to include this variable in the analysis. An analysis showed that the agreement between US and CT is comparable when including or ignoring a clear family history of AAAs; however, the PWRI is higher when taking this into account (Appendix B). Therefore, for future rupture risk prediction, the patient's AAA family history should be examined and recorded. Additionally, the infrarenal diameter used for the wall strength estimation, was determined using CT. In future research, emphasis should be placed on measuring this parameter using US.

The main limitation is that the poor visibility of the ILT is low due to the hypoechoic character of the ILT,18,20 resulting in lower inclusion numbers. In addition, the (locally) low lumen-ILT contrast makes it challenging to accurately segment the ILT, resulting in discrepancies in wall strength, and wall stress, and therefore also PWRI. The current study is limited to the clinically available data. For future, larger scale studies, improving the visibility of the ILT is crucial, which could be achieved, e.g., with contrast-enhanced US,18 Doppler imaging, or multi-perspective imaging.32 Harnessing the rapidly advancing potential of combining multiple data sources with deep learning could improve the accuracy of the segmentation, and thus rupture risk prediction. Additionally, differences in US- and CT-segmentations for the vessel wall were mainly present at the proximal and distal ends of the AAA, primarily due to the lower contrast, thereby decreasing the visibility of the AAA. CT and US-scans were acquired at different moments in time, and therefore, the geometry of the AAA could have changed. Additional differences in geometry could also be induced by registration errors resulting from fusing multiple US images to overcome the limited FOV in US imaging.

For the modeling framework, a uniform wall thickness of the aortic wall was assumed, which cannot be measured easily from conventional US or CT images. Kazi et al. showed that ILT not only affects wall strength, but also makes the wall thinner.33,34 Additionally, future work could focus on including calcifications in the model. Buijs et al. found that patients with symptomatic or ruptured AAAs treated non-electively had higher calcification scores than those treated electively, suggesting calcification as a potential predictor of rupture and symptoms.35 Calcifications will appear as bright regions in the US acquisition and could possibly be segmented from the images as well.

Future work should focus on improving ILT visibility and further personalizing the models to obtain the patient-specific rupture risk parameters more accurately. In addition, this modeling framework should be tested on larger patient cohorts, to assess its validity and boost clinical acceptance. Future longitudinal studies are crucial to investigate and prove the predictive value of PWRI for AAA rupture risk.

CONCLUSION

This study showed the feasibility of determining the PWRI based on US imaging, revealing comparable results to CT-based analysis. Rupture risk parameters, e.g., PWRI and PWS, were validated against CT imaging and showed a higher absolute agreement than the clinically used maximum diameter, which is represented by the higher ICC reported in this study. Future work should focus on improving the lumen-thrombus contrast in US imaging to improve applicability in large patient cohorts.

METHODS

Population and data acquisition

All US images were obtained from a database of 3D+t US data, which was acquired as a part of an ongoing prospective study on US-based AAA monitoring at the Catharina Hospital Eindhoven. The study was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of the Catharina Hospital Eindhoven (reference number: 2014-62 (CZE)), and all patients gave written informed consent. Inclusion criteria were: the presence of a clearly visible ILT in the US images and the availability of a CT angiography (CTA) scan that was acquired within 30 days of the US exam. US images were acquired using a Philips iU22 (10 patients, 17 datasets) or Philips Epiq Elite (7 patients, 7 datasets) system, both equipped with a Philips 3.5 MHz matrix-array X6-1 probe. The US images were obtained during breath hold.

The CTA images used for validation were acquired for preoperative planning (or other medical reasons) as a part of normal clinical workflow. The CTA data were acquired using a Philips Brilliance 64-channel scanner (N = 11), Philips iCT 256-slice scanner (N = 4), or a Siemens SOMATOM Drive (N = 2), having a slice thickness of 3 mm and an in-plane resolution of 0.63–0.85 mm.

Data processing and segmentation

The diastolic frame in the time-varying ultrasound images was automatically selected by detecting the maxima and minima of the diameter–time curve, with the minima indicating the end-diastolic frame. To increase the FOV, multiple US images were acquired, which were subsequently registered and fused using an image feature-based phase-only correlation method and a wavelet transform, respectively, as described by Sjoerdsma et al.36 The lumen, ILT, and vessel wall were segmented from the (fused) US images with a previously described method comprising of a combined star-Kalman and active contours algorithm.20

CTA scans were semi-automatically segmented using Hemodyn (Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands and the Eindhoven University of Technology, TU/e).37,38 The CT-segmentations were co-registered with the US-segmentations, using an Iterative Closest Point algorithm,39 and the region visible in both image modalities was selected. The exclusion of the bifurcation had previously shown to have no influence on the PWS accuracy36

Modeling

The retrieved geometries of the inner vessel wall and lumen were elongated 3 cm in the proximal direction warping into a vessel with a diameter of 2 cm parallel to the spine. In the distal direction, the AAA was elongated 2 cm in the centerline direction. The resulting vessel geometry was converted into a 15-node two-layered wedge element mesh with a constant wall thickness of 2 mm.30 The surrounding tissue and spine were included in the model as described previously by Petterson et al.26 The surrounding tissue was modeled using 10-node tetrahedral elements, and the ILT was converted into a 10-node tetrahedral mesh, with a minimum ILT thickness of 1.6 mm (equal to the element size). Both ILT and surrounding tissue have shared nodes with the wedge elements of the vessel wall. The proximal and distal ends were restricted to only allow deformation in the radial direction.40 The structural model was solved using Ansys Mechanical APDL (Ansys Inc., Canonsburg, PA, USA, version 2022R2).

Nodal spine displacements were restricted in all directions simulating an infinitely stiff material. The vessel wall and surrounding tissue were modeled as a nearly incompressible neo-Hookean material with shear moduli of 100030 and 20 kPa,41 respectively. For the ILT, a wide range of shear moduli is reported, ranging from less than 1 kPa to more than 100 kPa.27–29 In the current study, a shear modulus of 20 kPa was chosen, which is in agreement with findings of Gasser et al.42 A convergence study (Appendix A) showed that an element size of 1.6 mm for the vessel wall was small enough to reach convergence. The stress present in the diastolic wall geometry was calculated using the backward incremental method (BIM) as described in De Putter et al.43 After the BIM was applied, the AAA was further pressurized to the systolic blood pressure. The diastolic and systolic blood pressures were assumed to be 80 and 140 mm Hg, respectively, based on average clinical values.

Statistical analysis

A comparison of ILT thickness, local wall strength, and local rupture index between US and CT was conducted by analyzing the 25th, 50th, 75th, and 100th percentile values in four regions (anterior, posterior, left, and right aneurysm wall). For stress, strain, and expansion, the higher percentiles were of more interest. Hence, the 95th and 99th percentile stresses were additionally calculated, as was also used by Kok et al.6,19

The distribution of the differences was analyzed with a Lilliefors test.44 When p-values exceeded 0.05, it was assumed that values were normally distributed, allowing the use of parametric tests. The slope of the regression line was analyzed, and the reproducibility coefficient (RPC) was calculated to quantify the accuracy and precision, respectively. The lower the RPC, the higher the precision of the measurement. The parametric RPC was calculated as 1.96 times the standard deviation, and the non-parametric one was defined as 1.45 times IQR. Moreover, the agreement between CT and US was quantified by calculating the Pearson and Spearman coefficient for the parametric and non-parametric tests, respectively. Bland–Altman plots were employed to examine the bias in each measurement, detect patterns in the differences, and evaluate the overall agreement between US and CT.45,46 This bias was statistically tested using a single sample t-test on the differences (parametric) or Wilcoxon signed rank test (non-parametric). The bias determined for the wall stress and rupture index were used to scale the US-based PWS, 99th percentile wall stress, and PWRI.

Absolute agreement of ultrasound and computed tomography-based model parameters

The agreement between US and CT-based AAA assessment was evaluated for five rupture risk predictors: the anterior-posterior maximum diameter based on 2D US and CT measured in the clinic, the diameter based on 3D segmentations, the 99th percentile of wall stress, PWS, and PWRI. The 3D based AAA diameter was estimated by analyzing contours perpendicular to the centerline.47 In addition to the PWS, the 99th percentile wall stress was analyzed, since the PWS is known to be sensitive to small geometric variations, which can be introduced by the segmentation and meshing process.48

To quantify the absolute agreement of CT and US-based AAA parameters, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated. The absolute agreement of the rupture risk parameters was evaluated using the two-way random effects ICC for a single measurement49 and its 95% confidence interval. The ICC provides a powerful tool to analyze whether the total variance is due to variation between subjects or measurement differences between US and CT. In general, ICC values lower than 0.5 indicate a poor absolute agreement, and values larger than 0.9 indicate an excellent absolute agreement.21
