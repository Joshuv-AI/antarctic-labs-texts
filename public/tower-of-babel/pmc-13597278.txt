Wearable low-frequency ultrasound for transdermal drug delivery: from mechanisms to clinical translation☆
Authors: Xinzhu Li, Mingxin Li, Hui Song, Xinyue Liu, Fujin Jia, Yuli Tang, Ping Wu, Qingping Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransdermal drug delivery (TDD) bypasses gastrointestinal degradation and hepatic first-pass metabolism, improving compliance and enabling sustained administration. However, the stratum corneum severely limits the permeation of most hydrophilic and macromolecular drugs. Low-frequency ultrasound (LFU) enhances skin permeability via cavitation and mechanical effects, offering a controllable strategy for overcoming this barrier. Recent advances in flexible electronics have facilitated the development of wearable low-frequency ultrasound patches. This review summarizes the mechanisms, engineering design, applications, and translational challenges of wearable low-frequency ultrasound patches in TDD. A systematic literature search of PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library was used to inform a structured narrative review. The scope encompassed English-language articles published from database inception through May 1, 2026, incorporating both primary and secondary studies to conduct a narrative review. Wearable low-frequency ultrasound patches provide a non-invasive and adjustable enhancement strategy for transdermal delivery, particularly for poorly permeable drugs, although current clinical evidence remains largely limited to small studies. Most mechanistic insights are derived from proximate and analogous evidence. Clinical translation requires standardized safety evaluation, reproducibility validation, scalable manufacturing, and clearer regulatory pathways. Future studies should focus on matching acoustic parameters to drug characteristics and improving the long-term safety and reproducibility of wearable delivery systems.Keywords: Sonophoresis, Phonophoresis, Wearable ultrasound patch, Flexible electronics, Drug delivery

Introduction

Transdermal drug delivery (TDD) can bypass gastrointestinal degradation and hepatic first-pass metabolism while supporting sustained administration, features that are valuable for chronic and localized therapy [1]. Its broader applicability, however, is limited by the stratum corneum, which strongly restricts the passage of hydrophilic compounds, macromolecules, and many charged drugs [2], [3]. Consequently, conventional passive patches remain most suitable for molecules with favorable physicochemical properties, motivating physical strategies that transiently increase skin permeability [2], [3].

Among physical enhancement approaches, ultrasound-assisted TDD is attractive because acoustic exposure can be externally controlled without puncturing the skin [2]. The underlying transport effects are frequency-dependent and include cavitation, bubble-associated microstreaming, acoustic streaming, and other mechanical effects [4], [5]. Importantly, wearability and low operating frequency address different design objectives: a skin-conformal wearable format shortens and stabilizes the source-to-skin acoustic pathway, whereas LFU is emphasized here because cavitation-mediated permeabilization is particularly relevant to opening the stratum corneum [6], [7], [8]. Within the 20–100 kHz range, transient/inertial cavitation is expected to be a major barrier-disrupting phenomenon [5], [7]. Bubble oscillation, microstreaming, and bulk acoustic streaming can additionally promote fluid and solute transport within the coupling domain [9], [10]. At MHz frequencies, transient cavitation is generally more difficult to sustain under conventional therapeutic conditions, whereas distributed acoustic-pressure fields, acoustic streaming, and absorption-related thermal effects may assume greater relative importance [10], [11], [12]. Accordingly, higher-frequency and multi-frequency strategies are treated as complementary rather than competing approaches; dual-frequency and sequential irradiation can exploit distinct acoustic phenomena, and MHz wearable systems demonstrate that sustained sonophoretic transport is feasible over short source-to-skin distances [11], [12], [13], [14].

Advances in flexible electronics and miniaturized transducers have accelerated the transition of ultrasound systems from rigid instruments toward conformal and wearable platforms [8], [15]. Wearable ultrasound patches can integrate an acoustic transducer with a flexible substrate, coupling or drug-containing layer, and compact driving and control electronics at the skin interface [6], [8], [15]. Conformal contact and a short propagation path can improve coupling stability and provide a practical basis for repeated or programmable local exposure, with future potential for sensing and feedback integration [6], [16], [17]. Existing reviews have addressed sonophoresis, physical transdermal enhancement, and wearable ultrasound technologies, but the links among frequency-dependent acoustic phenomena, wearable engineering constraints, and transdermal drug-delivery evidence remain fragmented [2], [8], [15]. In particular, direct clinical evidence for fully integrated wearable LFU drug-delivery systems remains limited, making it necessary to distinguish established sonophoresis mechanisms from engineering precedents and device-specific clinical validation [8], [15].

This review therefore focuses on the mechanistic basis, engineering integration, delivery strategies, and translational challenges of wearable LFU for TDD, while incorporating higher-frequency and multi-frequency studies when they provide relevant mechanistic or engineering comparisons [8], [12], [15]. To maintain thematic focus, this review distinguishes four levels of evidence when discussing wearable low-frequency ultrasound for transdermal drug delivery. Core evidence directly involves wearable LFU or near-LFU ultrasound platforms applied to transdermal delivery. Proximate and comparative evidence includes non-wearable LFU sonophoresis or phonophoresis as well as higher-frequency and multi-frequency transdermal ultrasound studies that inform frequency-dependent mechanisms, parameter selection, and transport behavior, but do not constitute direct validation of wearable LFU devices. Contextual and enabling evidence includes flexible-ultrasound engineering, coupling materials, power and control architectures, and other transdermal technologies used to inform device design or provide comparators rather than to demonstrate wearable LFU efficacy. Finally, analogous evidence from ultrasound interactions with other biological barriers is used only to support mechanistic interpretation and should not be extrapolated as direct evidence for transdermal delivery.

Methods

Literature identification strategy

This narrative review was informed by a systematic literature search conducted to identify relevant studies on wearable low‑frequency ultrasound for transdermal drug delivery. We conducted a systematic search of major literature databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Scopus, covering the period from the inception of each database through May 1, 2026. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free‑text keywords using Boolean operators (AND, OR). Because of the limited number of directly relevant wearable low-frequency ultrasound studies and the substantial heterogeneity in study design, intervention parameters, and outcome measures, this review was conducted as a structured narrative review informed by systematic search principles rather than a formal systematic review.

Study selection and inclusion criteria

Studies were selected using predefined eligibility criteria, and the screening process was reported with reference to PRISMA 2020 [18]. We included studies that met the following criteria: (1) original research articles, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), clinical studies, and clinical trials; (2) involving low‑frequency ultrasound (operationally defined as approximately 20–100 kHz in this review, unless explicitly included as a higher-frequency or multi-frequency comparator) or wearable ultrasound patches for transdermal drug delivery [2]; (3) reporting outcomes related to drug permeation, safety, or device performance [3]; (4) published in English. We excluded conference abstracts, editorials, patents, animal‑only studies without transdermal relevance, and studies focusing solely on diagnostic ultrasound.

Screening process and final inclusion

The literature screening procedure was performed according to the PRISMA 2020 framework to improve the methodological transparency and reproducibility of this narrative review [18]. A total of 40 records were initially identified through searches of PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. After removal of duplicate publications, two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts to exclude studies that were clearly unrelated to wearable low-frequency ultrasound–assisted transdermal drug delivery. Potentially relevant articles then underwent full-text assessment based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements during the selection process were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. Following the sequential screening procedure, nine studies were ultimately retained and served as the primary evidence base for the present review. Data extraction

From each included study, two authors independently extracted the following information: ultrasound frequency, intensity (if reported), duty cycle, exposure duration, delivery mode (pulsed/continuous), drug or formulation, experimental model (including in vitro, animal, translational, and clinical studies depending on study design), main outcome (quantitative or qualitative enhancement), and safety findings. Given the small number of included studies and their clinical heterogeneity, no formal quality assessment or risk‑of‑bias scoring was performed; this is consistent with the narrative review design.

Synthesis approach

Owing to the limited number of included primary studies (n = 9) and the substantial heterogeneity in ultrasound parameters, drug types, experimental models, and outcome measures, a quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) was not appropriate. Therefore, we conducted a qualitative narrative synthesis organized around three themes: mechanisms of permeability enhancement, engineering design and integration, and clinical translation challenges [19]. Where appropriate, we compared findings across studies and highlighted evidence gaps to inform future research. Accordingly, this review should be interpreted as a structured narrative synthesis rather than a formal systematic review and meta-analysis. Fundamentals of Physics and Biomechanics: How Low-Frequency ultrasound enhances skin permeability

Cavitation as the primary mechanism, with thermal effects as a secondary contributor

The primary mechanism by which low-frequency ultrasound enhances skin permeability is acoustic cavitation, with thermal effects playing a supplementary role [27], [28]. Cavitation-bubble oscillation and collapse can generate local shear, shock waves, and microjets that disrupt stratum-corneum lipid bilayers and increase permeability [29]. The biomechanical properties of skin (such as elasticity and texture) have been shown to substantially influence the dynamic behaviour of cavitation bubbles and microjet velocity, providing a mechanical basis for explaining individual variations in TDD efficiency [30]. It should be noted, however, that the contribution of cavitation and the overall enhancement efficiency depend strongly on ultrasound parameters (frequency, intensity, duty cycle), coupling medium, and skin model characteristics [31]. Although the cavitation effect has been shown to overcome biological barriers in brain drug delivery, this finding serves as a mechanistic analogy rather than direct evidence for transdermal application [32], [33]. Human phonophoresis data further indicate that frequency effects can be depth dependent: compared with 1 MHz, 45 kHz ultrasound showed a trend toward higher dexamethasone concentration at 1 mm, whereas no significant between-frequency difference was observed at 4 mm [34]. Engineered gas-containing structures are also being explored in ultrasound-assisted platforms: microsized air pockets have been incorporated into a sonoelectric transdermal patch [35], while porous resins have been used explicitly as cavitation nuclei to increase both cavitation activity and transdermal mannitol transport during LFU [36].

From an acoustic-transmission perspective, these effects arise along a propagation pathway that includes both the coupling medium and the biological interface. In conventional horn-based low-frequency sonophoresis, mechanical vibration generated by the transducer is transmitted through the horn into an aqueous coupling or drug-containing medium, across the liquid-skin interface, and subsequently into the stratum corneum and deeper viable tissue [7], [37], [38]. Within the liquid coupling domain, the alternating acoustic pressure promotes the nucleation, growth, and oscillation of gas cavities. Oscillating bubbles can generate localized microstreaming, whereas sufficiently energetic bubble collapse near the skin surface produces high local shear stresses, microjets, and pressure transients that perturb stratum-corneum lipid organization and contribute to the formation of localized transport regions (LTRs) [7], [9], [38]. In parallel, acoustic streaming within the coupling medium can promote convective fluid and solute motion toward the skin surface and through ultrasound-permeabilized pathways [9], [10]. Accordingly, the coupling layer should be regarded as an active acoustic domain rather than merely a passive spacer, because cavitation activity and acoustic transport depend on frequency, acoustic intensity, dissolved-gas conditions, coupling-medium properties, and the distance between the acoustic source and the skin [5], [7], [37], [38].

Frequency further modifies the spatial distribution of the acoustic field and the relative contributions of these phenomena. Within the 20–100 kHz range emphasized in this review, transient/inertial cavitation is a major contributor to stratum-corneum disruption [5], [7]. The intensity threshold required for detectable permeabilization increases with ultrasound frequency, and lower frequencies around 20 kHz tend to generate more spatially localized transport regions, whereas higher frequencies within the low-frequency range can produce a more dispersed permeabilization pattern [5]. Stable bubble oscillation, bubble-associated microstreaming, and bulk acoustic streaming may coexist with transient cavitation and contribute to fluid and solute transport in the coupling medium [7], [9], [10]. At MHz frequencies, cavitation is not intrinsically absent; rather, its occurrence and relative importance depend strongly on acoustic pressure, available cavitation nuclei, and coupling conditions. Under conventional therapeutic conditions, transient cavitation is generally more difficult to sustain than during LFU, while localized acoustic-pressure fields, acoustic streaming, and absorption-related thermal effects may assume greater relative importance [10], [11], [12]. Therefore, high-frequency ultrasound may complement LFU by promoting transport through permeation pathways that have already been generated by low-frequency cavitation rather than necessarily reproducing the same barrier-disrupting mechanism.

A particularly informative example is the simultaneous dual-frequency configuration reported by Schoellhammer et al. [11]. In this system, a 20 kHz horn and a 1 MHz transducer irradiated the same treatment chamber, so that both acoustic fields propagated through a common aqueous coupling bath containing sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) and Allura red in phosphate-buffered saline before interacting with the skin [11]. The 1 MHz field nucleated bubbles that oscillated over the skin, while simultaneous 20 kHz + 1 MHz exposure enhanced cavitational activity within the shared liquid domain [11]. This behavior is consistent with the strong frequency dependence of cavitation in sonophoresis [7]. Compared with 20 kHz ultrasound alone, simultaneous 20 kHz + 1 MHz exposure generated larger and more permeable LTRs, and the flux of a 4 kDa tracer increased by approximately 3.5- and 7.1-fold after 6 and 8 min of treatment, respectively [11]. Importantly, histological analysis revealed no greater skin disruption than that observed with 20 kHz treatment alone [11]. Thus, the dual-frequency configuration is better interpreted as an interaction of two acoustic fields within a common liquid coupling domain that enhances cavitation-mediated barrier permeabilization, rather than as two independent waves acting separately on the skin.

A different transmission scenario occurs during sequential multi-frequency irradiation. Matsubara et al. applied low-frequency ultrasound at approximately 44 kHz followed by high-frequency ultrasound at approximately 2 MHz to the same drug-skin system [12]. During the first phase, the low-frequency wave propagates through the liquid drug/coupling medium to the skin interface, where cavitation-bubble collapse, associated pressure transients, and microjets disrupt the stratum corneum and establish permeable pathways [9], [12]. During the subsequent high-frequency phase, the MHz wave again propagates through the liquid medium and reaches the pre-permeabilized skin. Rather than functioning primarily as a second barrier-opening step, the high-frequency field produces a more spatially distributed acoustic-pressure field, while ultrasound-driven fluid motion can promote convective transport of the macromolecular solute through the previously generated pathways [10], [12]. Consistent with this interpretation, reversing the irradiation sequence or using only the high-frequency phase did not produce the same enhancement effect [12]. Furthermore, transport efficiency during the MHz phase depended strongly on the transducer-to-skin distance, demonstrating that the geometry of the liquid coupling layer modifies the acoustic field and therefore the resulting transport process [10], [12]. These observations indicate that coupling-layer geometry, source-to-skin distance, transducer configuration, and operating frequency should be considered as coupled acoustic design variables rather than independent parameters.

For wearable implementations, the same physical framework applies, although the propagation distance and sequence of media are determined by the multilayer architecture of the patch [39]. In fluid-coupled conformable devices, acoustic energy generated by the embedded piezoelectric transducer is transmitted through the compliant device structure into a thin liquid drug/coupling reservoir and subsequently to the skin [6]. For example, the conformable ultrasound patch reported by Yu et al. confined cavitation within a fluid coupling medium between the patch and skin, generating localized cavitation pockets and fluid motion close to the stratum corneum [6]. In contrast, when a hydrated polymer or other solid-like coupling layer is positioned between the transducer and skin, ultrasound is transmitted primarily as an elastic wave through the coupling layer, and the transmitted energy is governed by acoustic-impedance matching, interfacial reflection, and attenuation. The calcium-modified silk coupling patch reported by Lee et al., for example, was designed to provide an acoustic impedance close to that of human skin and thereby minimize transmission loss at the wearable interface [16]. Cavitation in such configurations is expected to be favored in adjacent liquid or fluid-containing domains where suitable gas nuclei are available, whereas cavitation within solid-like hydrated coupling layers depends more strongly on the availability of bubble nuclei and the mechanical properties of the matrix [40]. These configurations emphasize that conformal contact and minimization of interfacial air gaps are essential for reproducible acoustic transmission in wearable systems [6], [16]. Collectively, these findings suggest that future wearable systems could retain low-frequency ultrasound as a principal barrier-permeabilizing component while incorporating appropriately designed higher-frequency phases to exploit complementary transport mechanisms and potentially shorten the required low-frequency exposure. However, the currently available multi-frequency studies should primarily be regarded as mechanistic and engineering precedents rather than as direct clinical evidence for fully integrated wearable systems, because clinical validation and device-level translation of wearable ultrasound technologies remain limited [8], [15].

Mechanistically, the improvement in drug transport arises mainly from cavitation-induced disruption of stratum-corneum lipid organization and transient aqueous pathways, while ultrasound-driven fluid motion further assists solute movement through the permeabilized barrier [4], [5], [30], [35]. Direct clinical evidence for wearable LFU patches remains limited, so human phonophoresis trials should be interpreted as proximate clinical evidence rather than device-specific validation [23], [41], [42]. In knee osteoarthritis, Pinkaew et al. reported that P. amarus nanoparticle-gel phonophoresis produced greater VAS pain reduction and greater improvement in 6-min walking distance than ultrasound with nondrug gel, indicating that the clinical improvement comprised both analgesia and increased functional capacity [23]. In cubital tunnel syndrome, chitosan phonophoresis increased ulnar nerve-conduction velocity and reduced pain and QuickDASH disability compared with hand therapy alone, linking benefit to improved neural conduction and function [41]. In subacromial impingement, ibuprofen phonophoresis improved activity-related pain and shoulder function versus sham ultrasound; continuous mode favored early resting-pain relief, whereas pulsed mode favored longer-term quality of life [42]. Thus, the specific effects associated with improvement are upstream barrier permeabilization/convective transport and downstream reductions in pain with better neural or musculoskeletal function, although tissue drug concentrations were not directly measured and a definitive causal link cannot yet be established [23], [30], [35], [41], [42].

Stratum corneum transport pathways and the post-sonication delivery window

Transdermal transport after ultrasound exposure is governed not only by acoustic barrier disruption but also by the intrinsic heterogeneity of skin transport pathways. Passive diffusion through human skin is strongly influenced by the intercellular route, while age-related changes in stratum corneum thickness and microstructure can further alter permeation kinetics [43]. LFU superimposes an additional level of spatial heterogeneity. Cavitation does not necessarily permeabilize the entire exposed skin area uniformly; instead, it can generate localized transport regions (LTRs) characterized by substantial perturbation of stratum-corneum lipid organization and increased aqueous and convective transport, while surrounding regions may exhibit weaker enhancement [4], [7], [9].

This spatial heterogeneity has direct implications for wearable dosing. A wearable ultrasound device should not be viewed as simply switching the skin from a low-permeability state to a fixed high-permeability state. Rather, acoustic exposure establishes a time-dependent transport condition whose magnitude depends on frequency, acoustic pressure or intensity, duty cycle, coupling conditions, and exposure duration [5], [7], [9]. Studies in which sonophoresis was used as a pretreatment before drug application further indicate that acoustic barrier modification and subsequent drug diffusion can be temporally separated, although the magnitude and duration of the useful post-treatment interval depend on the drug, skin model, and pretreatment protocol [44].

Accordingly, the clinically relevant delivery window should be defined by the combination of acoustic barrier opening and subsequent drug transport rather than by ultrasound exposure time alone. For wearable systems, reservoir contact, drug availability, and repeated or pulsed acoustic exposure should therefore be coordinated with the evolving permeability state of the skin [45]. Future development requires assessment of not only immediate flux enhancement but also the duration and spatial uniformity of enhanced permeability, barrier recovery, and the reproducibility of repeated treatment cycles, because these factors will determine whether programmable wearable dosing can be translated into predictable drug exposure [8], [15].

Microbubble dynamics and carrier-mediated enhancement under low-frequency ultrasound

Ultrasound-responsive carriers, including liposomes and gas-filled microbubbles, provide the basis for a carrier–ultrasound dual-driven delivery strategy [46], [47]. For preformed microbubbles, however, their interaction with low-frequency ultrasound (LFU) should be distinguished from conventional resonance-matched excitation because bubble resonance depends strongly on bubble size and shell properties [48], [49]. Representative Definity microbubbles with radii of 1.5–5 μm exhibit resonant responses within approximately 0.5–3.5 MHz, indicating that the 20–100 kHz range emphasized in this review lies well below the principal linear-resonance range of such micron-scale bubbles [48]. Accordingly, LFU–microbubble synergy is better interpreted as sub-resonant or non-resonant cavitation enhancement rather than resonance-matched excitation [48], [49], [50].

Under these off-resonant conditions, added microbubbles can serve as pre-existing gas nuclei and undergo nonlinear radial motion when the acoustic pressure is sufficiently high [50]. Increasing acoustic drive can produce large-amplitude oscillation and ultimately shell disruption, fragmentation, or inertial collapse, with associated microstreaming and localized mechanical stresses [46], [50]. Near the skin–liquid interface, asymmetric bubble activity can generate shear, pressure transients, and microjets that disrupt the stratum-corneum barrier and epidermal junctional integrity, thereby creating transient pathways for enhanced transdermal transport [46], [51]. Thus, resonance mismatch does not preclude enhancement, although the magnitude and safety of the response remain dependent on acoustic pressure, pulse conditions, bubble size and concentration, shell properties, and bubble–skin proximity [48], [49], [50], [51].

Phase-change carriers should be distinguished from preformed gas-filled microbubbles [52]. Perfluoro-n-pentane (PFP)-containing carriers initially contain liquid perfluorocarbon and can undergo ultrasound-triggered vaporization to form microbubbles in situ, followed by bubble expansion, carrier disruption, and payload release [52]. This phase-change pathway can couple ultrasound-triggered drug release with subsequent bubble-mediated mechanical effects, but its activation process and dynamics are distinct from those of pre-existing microbubbles [52].

Technical modules and integrated design

Miniaturized ultrasonic transducers: material and architecture trade-offs and frequency-dependent design

The miniaturization and mechanical compliance of ultrasonic transducers are central to wearability, but they must be balanced against the acoustic amplitude delivered to the skin [53], [54]. Here, amplitude refers to the mechanical displacement of the active element and the resulting acoustic-pressure amplitude at the skin interface, rather than electrical output power alone. Flexible PVDF and P(VDF-TrFE) films provide conformability but typically have lower piezoelectric response and electromechanical coupling than high-performance ceramics, which can limit the displacement and acoustic pressure obtainable from a small radiating area under a given drive condition [53], [54], [55]. PZT- and PMN-PT-based ceramics provide stronger electromechanical conversion but are stiff and brittle [53], [54]. A practical wearable strategy is therefore to balance acoustic output with mechanical compliance by integrating high-performance ceramic elements or ceramic/polymer composites with flexible substrates [53], [54].

This trade-off is amplified at low frequencies. In thickness-mode piezoelectric transducers, lower resonance generally requires a larger active dimension along the propagation direction, whereas flexural-mode resonance depends strongly on diaphragm stiffness, effective mass, and lateral dimensions [53], [54], [56]. As an engineering precedent, the resonance frequency of KNN pMUTs decreased from 241 to 21.2 kHz as diaphragm radius increased from 300 to 1200 μm, while an approximately 100-kHz element produced micrometre-scale out-of-plane displacement under low-voltage excitation [56]. Wearable LFU therefore combines conflicting requirements: low-frequency, high-displacement operation favors a larger or more compliant resonator, whereas skin-mounted systems favor thin, small, flexible structures. Potential solutions include flexural-mode or mechanically amplified resonators, proof-mass or mass-loaded designs for resonance tuning and displacement enhancement, and arrays of miniature elements that recover effective radiating area and acoustic pressure while retaining local conformability [53], [54], [56], [57].

Frequency selection also changes the engineering design space. For the 20–100 kHz range emphasized here, miniaturization must preserve sufficient displacement and acoustic-pressure amplitude to reach cavitation conditions under the intended coupling environment [7], [53]. By comparison, MHz transducers can generally use thinner thickness-mode active elements and facilitate dense array integration [53], [54]. Their use in wearable patches is feasible because the source-to-skin coupling path is short, although frequency-dependent attenuation, absorption, and thermal loading remain relevant design constraints [13], [14], [53]. Wearable 3-MHz sustained acoustic medicine systems have demonstrated prolonged sonophoresis with enhanced diclofenac transport and increased local circulation [13], [14]. Higher frequency is therefore not intrinsically superior to LFU: LFU favors cavitation-oriented barrier opening, whereas MHz operation can favor sustained transport and acoustic-streaming-dominant exposure. Frequency should consequently be co-optimized with piezoelectric material, resonant architecture, radiating area, and the intended acoustic mechanism.

Flexible packaging and coupling interface

For wearable ultrasound, the coupling layer must maintain conformal contact while minimizing acoustic loss between the transducer and skin [16], [58]. Impedance mismatch causes interfacial reflection, whereas attenuation accumulates with propagation distance, making both acoustic impedance and layer thickness key determinants of transmitted pressure [58], [59]. Hydrated polymers are attractive because their water-rich, compliant networks can approximate soft-tissue acoustics and suppress motion-induced air gaps [16], [60]. Calcium-modified silk had an acoustic impedance of 2.15 MRayl versus 1.99 MRayl for human skin and approximately 0.002 dB transmission loss [16]. An AMPS–glycerol hydrogel showed 2.13–2.17 MRayl, attenuation comparable to commercial gel at 0.5–1 mm, and <13% attenuation during long-term testing [59]. Hydrogel–elastomer bioadhesive couplants also maintained stable skin contact for 48 h [60]. Thus, wearable LFU couplants should prioritize tissue-matched impedance, low attenuation, minimal thickness, hydration stability, and conformal adhesion [16], [58], [59], [60]. Direct quantitative data for polymer couplants specifically at 20–100 kHz TDD remain limited, so LFU-specific transmission should be validated under cavitation-relevant conditions [16], [59], [60].

Integrated drug reservoirs

The design of integrated drug reservoirs must balance drug-loading stability, controlled release, and structural compatibility with the ultrasonic module. At the structural level, self-adhesive microcup patches that combine micro-reservoir units with physical enhancers provide a useful precedent for integrating a drug compartment directly within a skin-adherent delivery platform [61]. Formulation strategies based on advanced carriers, including self-emulsifying systems and other nanocarrier approaches, may further improve drug loading, transdermal permeation, and sustained release, particularly for poorly soluble compounds [62], [63], [64].

In this review, the term “lab-on-a-patch” is used in a system-integration sense rather than to describe the drug reservoir alone. The drug reservoir or hydrogel forms the drug-handling layer at the skin interface and must operate together with the ultrasonic transducer, flexible packaging/adhesion, and programmable electronics so that drug availability and acoustic exposure can be coordinated within one wearable platform. Microfluidic or micropump components can be incorporated as optional extensions for on-demand, sequential, or sensor-informed dosing [17], but they are not required to define the basic system-level configuration illustrated here.

Power supply, driving electronics, and control: portability and performance trade-offs

At the system level, portability is constrained by the electrical energy required to sustain the prescribed acoustic exposure rather than by transducer size alone. Once the target acoustic pressure or intensity, exposure duration, and pulse conditions are defined, electroacoustic efficiency and operating duty cycle determine the peak and average electrical demand and, consequently, the required battery capacity, volume, and weight [65], [66], [67]. Direct engineering evidence is available from wearable low-frequency therapeutic systems. A 20-kHz applicator used rechargeable lithium-ion batteries and 25-Hz pulsed excitation at a 50% duty cycle specifically to extend battery life, while a subsequent 20–40-kHz point-of-care system incorporated redesigned, programmable driving electronics into a battery-powered wearable platform [65], [66]. Although these systems were developed for wound therapy rather than sonophoretic drug delivery, they provide the closest direct evidence for power management in wearable LFU. Pulsed or intermittent excitation may therefore reduce average energy consumption and heating, but the duty cycle must remain coupled to the peak acoustic output and cumulative exposure required for the intended therapeutic effect [65], [66].

Power-source selection must also be considered together with the driving electronics and thermal budget [67]. Modern wearable ultrasound TDD systems operating at MHz frequencies provide complementary engineering precedents: compact drivers incorporating microcontroller-based control, MOSFET switching, pulse-width modulation, wireless communication, and lithium-battery power have enabled programmable regulation of ultrasound output [68]. Device-level thermal characterization is equally important because electrical and electromechanical losses ultimately contribute to heat at the skin-mounted interface; for example, a 1.2-MHz wearable ultrasound–microneedle patch showed a temperature increase of approximately 3.2 °C after 30 min of operation at 20 Vpp [69]. Digital regulation of acoustic power and burst duration can further adjust the delivered acoustic dose [70]. Accordingly, no single power architecture is optimal for all wearable LFU applications: intermittent protocols may support battery-powered integrated or closely coupled drivers, whereas higher average-power or longer-duration operation may favor a distributed on-body configuration that keeps the skin-contact module lightweight. Externalizing the energy source is another option when local battery volume becomes limiting, as illustrated by the approximately 100-kHz battery-free photo-elastic patch driven by external optical excitation [25]. Thus, feasible wearable LFU systems require co-optimization of peak acoustic output, average electrical power, duty cycle, driver efficiency, treatment duration, battery burden, and thermal limits rather than maximizing either acoustic performance or miniaturization in isolation [25], [65], [66], [68], [69], [70].

These precedents suggest three practical design pathways rather than a single universally optimal wearable format: (i) battery-powered integrated or closely coupled systems for intermittent or moderate-duty-cycle operation [71]; (ii) distributed on-body architectures that shift part of the power and driver burden away from the skin-contact module during longer or higher-average-power exposure [72]; and (iii) externally powered or battery-free-at-the-patch approaches when local battery volume becomes limiting [73]. The power unit and control circuit in Fig. 5 are therefore shown as functionally linked but modular elements. This representation is intentional because the “lab-on-a-patch” concept depends on coordination among the drug reservoir, acoustic source, coupling/skin interface, and programmable control, whereas the physical placement of the energy source and electronics can be adapted to the portability–performance requirements of a specific dosing scenario.

Delivery strategies from molecules to disease

Delivery configuration: matching patch architecture to the dosing scenario

Wearable ultrasound-assisted TDD does not have a single optimal device architecture. The appropriate configuration depends on whether the intended therapeutic function is short-duration barrier opening, repeated enhancement, or sustained local transport. These different objectives impose different requirements on the acoustic source, coupling or drug-reservoir geometry, treatment area, power supply, and duration of skin contact [6], [13], [14], [24], [25].

Skin-conformal systems illustrate the importance of minimizing the source-to-skin pathway while maintaining a controlled acoustic domain. The conformable ultrasound patch reported by Yu et al. generated cavitation within a thin fluid layer close to the skin, illustrating an architecture in which mechanical conformity and local cavitation are coupled directly to the barrier-opening function [6]. Stretchable sonophoresis platforms have similarly demonstrated that broad-area conformal contact can be maintained on anatomically curved skin surfaces, providing an important precedent for wearable acoustic delivery [24]. The approximately 100-kHz photo-elastic patch reported by Huang et al. further illustrates an alternative near-LFU architecture in which externally supplied optical energy can reduce the need for a local battery while retaining a thin skin-mounted acoustic module [25].

Sustained MHz systems represent a different wearable design strategy. Continuous 3-MHz sustained acoustic medicine has been shown to enhance diclofenac transport and was subsequently evaluated together with a diclofenac patch under prolonged wearable exposure [13], [14]. These studies should not be interpreted as evidence that MHz operation is intrinsically superior to LFU. Rather, they demonstrate that a short wearable source-to-skin pathway can support prolonged high-frequency exposure when the therapeutic objective is sustained transport rather than cavitation-dominated initial barrier opening.

Therefore, wearable architecture should be matched to the intended acoustic mechanism and dosing schedule. LFU or near-LFU systems may prioritize sufficient acoustic amplitude and an appropriate cavitation-supporting coupling domain, whereas sustained MHz systems can prioritize long-duration contact, distributed acoustic exposure, and thermal management. Device modularity is useful only when it serves these mechanism-specific requirements; it should not be treated as an independent indicator of delivery performance [6], [13], [14], [24], [25].

Parameter matching: co-optimizing acoustic dose and drug characteristics

Parameter selection for ultrasound-assisted TDD should be treated as a multidimensional acoustic-dose problem rather than as frequency selection alone. In LFU sonophoresis, the onset and spatial distribution of cavitation-mediated permeabilization depend on frequency, acoustic pressure or intensity, source-to-skin distance, exposure duration, and coupling conditions [7], [9], [12], [37]. Drug molecular size, charge, lipophilicity, formulation, and the desired depth of delivery then determine how effectively the resulting transport pathways can be utilized. Consequently, the same nominal frequency cannot be expected to provide an equivalent delivery effect across different drugs or wearable configurations.

Small-molecule anti-inflammatory agents provide useful examples of this drug–parameter interaction. In an inflammatory arthritis model, 20-kHz low-intensity ultrasound enhanced the therapeutic effect of topically administered betamethasone [26]. Microbubble-assisted ultrasound has also been reported to enhance diclofenac permeation and anti-inflammatory efficacy in an arthritic animal model [22]. These studies support the ability of LFU-associated mechanical effects to improve local delivery, but they do not establish a universal optimal intensity or exposure duration because the formulation, coupling environment, and biological models differed substantially.

Higher-frequency wearable evidence demonstrates a complementary parameter regime. Continuous 3-MHz sustained acoustic medicine increased diclofenac flux under prolonged exposure, while subsequent wearable studies supported sustained NSAID delivery and increased local circulation [13], [14]. These findings are particularly relevant to the short source-to-skin path of wearable devices, but they represent transport-oriented high-frequency evidence rather than direct LFU barrier-opening evidence. For macromolecular delivery, sequential exposure provides a further example of mechanism-specific parameter matching: low-frequency irradiation followed by approximately 2-MHz irradiation enhanced transport more effectively than reversing the sequence or applying the high-frequency phase alone, supporting functional separation between initial barrier permeabilization and subsequent transport enhancement [12].

Temporal parameters are also clinically relevant. A randomized phonophoresis study comparing pulsed and continuous ultrasound reported different patterns of symptomatic and functional improvement, indicating that duty cycle and exposure mode may influence treatment outcomes [42]. However, because local drug concentrations were not directly quantified, these clinical differences should not be assigned to a specific transport mechanism without additional pharmacokinetic evidence.

Overall, matching acoustic parameters to drug characteristics should integrate frequency, acoustic pressure or intensity, duty cycle, treatment duration, coupling-layer geometry, drug formulation, and the intended therapeutic depth. Future wearable studies should report the actual acoustic output at the treatment interface together with drug-specific delivery endpoints rather than relying on nominal device settings alone [4], [5], [7], [37].

Carrier design: overcoming delivery barriers for poorly permeable drugs

Carrier engineering can complement ultrasound when the drug itself has unfavorable physicochemical properties for passive transdermal transport, but the contribution of the carrier should be distinguished from the contribution of the acoustic field. Svenskaya et al. demonstrated ultrasound-assisted transdermal delivery of biodegradable particulate carriers, providing direct evidence that particulate formulation and acoustic enhancement can be combined to improve the transport of otherwise poorly permeable payloads [21]. In a clinical phonophoresis study, a Phyllanthus amarus nanoparticle gel was associated with improved pain and functional outcomes in knee osteoarthritis [23]. However, because the clinical study was not designed as a full factorial comparison of carrier and ultrasound effects, the magnitude of synergy between carrier design and ultrasound cannot be isolated from the therapeutic outcome alone.

Ultrasound-responsive carriers provide a more direct mechanistic coupling between acoustic exposure and formulation behavior. Phase-change transfersomes containing a volatile perfluorocarbon can undergo ultrasound-triggered vaporization followed by bubble formation, carrier disruption, and payload release, thereby linking acoustic activation to both drug release and subsequent bubble-mediated mechanical effects [52]. As discussed in Section 3.3, this mechanism is distinct from the behavior of preformed gas-filled microbubbles and should not be described simply as resonance-matched microbubble enhancement.

Macromolecular payload studies also illustrate the importance of separating “payload feasibility” from “carrier efficacy.” Sweep-frequency ultrasound has enabled non-invasive insulin delivery with measurable glucose-lowering effects in an animal model [20], demonstrating that ultrasound can assist the transdermal transport of a biologically active macromolecule. However, this does not by itself establish that a particular nanocarrier architecture is responsible for the enhancement. Future studies should therefore use study designs capable of separating formulation effects, ultrasound effects, and their interaction, especially when claims of carrier–ultrasound synergy are made.

Challenges and considerations in clinical translation

Although ultrasound-assisted TDD and several wearable ultrasound platforms have demonstrated technical and therapeutic feasibility, direct human evidence for fully integrated wearable LFU drug-delivery systems remains limited. Clinical translation therefore requires a clear distinction between device-specific wearable LFU evidence, proximate phonophoresis evidence, and higher-frequency wearable precedents [8], [15]. The principal translational challenges involve acoustic and material safety, dosing reproducibility, scalable manufacturing, and the generation of a regulatory-grade clinical evidence package.

Biocompatibility and long-term safety

Safety evaluation for wearable LFU should distinguish acoustic bioeffects from material and interface biocompatibility. LFU is intentionally used to perturb the stratum-corneum barrier, and the same cavitation-related phenomena that enable transport can produce excessive local disruption when acoustic exposure is not appropriately controlled. Conversely, prolonged or higher-frequency exposure can increase the relative importance of thermal accumulation. Safety should therefore be evaluated against the complete acoustic dose, including pressure or intensity, duty cycle, exposure duration, frequency, coupling conditions, and repeated-treatment schedule rather than against frequency alone [8], [11], [15].

Available studies provide useful but condition-specific safety evidence. In the simultaneous dual-frequency study of Schoellhammer et al., enhancement of transdermal transport was not accompanied by greater histological disruption than that observed with the corresponding 20-kHz treatment under the tested conditions [11]. Wearable photo-elastic transdermal ultrasound has also been evaluated with attention to thermal safety [25]. By contrast, a 1.2-MHz wearable ultrasound microneedle system showed an approximately 3.2 °C temperature increase during a 30-min exposure, illustrating why device-level thermal characterization remains necessary even when the transducer itself is miniaturized [69]. These observations should not be generalized beyond their individual acoustic protocols but demonstrate the need to characterize both mechanical and thermal effects.

The coupling and adhesive interface introduces an additional safety dimension. Tissue-matched hydrated polymers and bioadhesive couplants can reduce air gaps and stabilize acoustic transmission, but their hydration state, adhesive contact, acoustic attenuation, and skin compatibility must be maintained during repeated wear [16], [59], [60]. Material biocompatibility alone is therefore insufficient to establish the safety of an integrated wearable LFU system. Long-duration studies should evaluate repeated barrier opening and recovery, local temperature, erythema or irritation, coupling-layer stability, and cumulative skin effects under clinically relevant use schedules [8], [15].

Dosing accuracy and reproducibility

Reproducible ultrasound-assisted dosing requires control of both acoustic delivery and biological variability. Frequency-dependent cavitation behavior, transducer-to-skin distance, coupling-layer geometry, acoustic streaming, interfacial reflection, and individual skin biomechanics can all alter the effective exposure received at the skin surface [5], [10], [16], [30], [37]. In a wearable device, variations in adhesion, coupling-layer thickness, hydration, and motion may therefore produce substantial differences in delivered acoustic pressure even when the electronic drive setting remains unchanged [58], [59], [60].

This distinction is important because the electrical setting of a wearable transducer is not itself a measure of transdermal dose. Studies should report the acoustic output at or near the treatment interface together with coupling conditions, treatment duration, duty cycle, and local temperature. Delivery endpoints should likewise be selected according to the intended pharmacological target. Tape-stripping or epidermal sampling can provide information about local drug distribution, whereas tissue concentrations, plasma pharmacokinetics, validated imaging, or pharmacodynamic biomarkers may be required when deeper or systemic delivery is intended [74].

Reproducibility should therefore be evaluated as a complete chain from electrical excitation to acoustic output, barrier modification, drug transport, and pharmacological response [75]. This approach is particularly important for wearable systems, where repeated use outside controlled laboratory conditions will expose the device to greater variability in skin contact and coupling than conventional benchtop sonophoresis [76].

Scalable manufacturing and acoustic quality control

Translation from a laboratory prototype to a reproducible wearable product requires manufacturing control over acoustic, mechanical, and electrical properties. Flexible ultrasound reviews have emphasized that transducer material selection, structural geometry, interconnect design, encapsulation, and system integration all influence wearable performance [8], [15], [53], [54]. For LFU devices, these tolerances are particularly important because small changes in resonator dimensions, stiffness, or mass loading can shift the resonance frequency and acoustic displacement required for cavitation-oriented operation [56], [57].

Recent micromachined transducer studies illustrate this sensitivity. KNN-based pMUTs show strong dependence of resonance frequency and displacement on diaphragm geometry, while mass-loaded suspended pMUT architectures demonstrate how mechanical loading can be used to tune resonance and displacement. These approaches provide useful manufacturing precedents, although their performance must ultimately be validated under the actual coupling and acoustic-loading conditions of a transdermal patch.

Manufacturing control must also extend to the coupling and drug-interface layers. Variations in polymer thickness, hydration, acoustic impedance, attenuation, and adhesive contact can alter the pressure transmitted to the skin even when the transducer itself meets specification [16], [59], [60]. Similarly, driver electronics and power architecture must reliably reproduce the programmed waveform and duty cycle. Existing low-frequency point-of-care and wearable therapeutic ultrasound systems provide useful engineering precedents for integrating programmable electronics with portable power sources [65], [66].

Quality control for future wearable LFU products should therefore include batch-level characterization of resonance frequency, electrical impedance, acoustic-pressure output, electroacoustic efficiency, coupling-layer thickness and transmission, adhesive integrity, and device-level thermal behavior. Establishing such acoustic quality-control metrics is likely to be as important as conventional electrical and mechanical testing for ensuring reproducible transdermal delivery [77].

Clinical evidence and regulatory readiness

Regulatory readiness for wearable ultrasound-assisted TDD will depend on the ability to demonstrate reproducible performance and clinically meaningful benefit for the final integrated system rather than on the technical feasibility of individual components alone. At present, direct clinical evidence for fully integrated wearable LFU drug-delivery platforms remains limited [8], [15]. Wearable 3-MHz sustained acoustic medicine provides an important complementary precedent for prolonged ultrasound exposure and NSAID delivery, but it should not be presented as direct clinical validation of LFU-based devices [13], [14].

Existing phonophoresis trials provide additional proximate clinical evidence. Improvements in pain, functional capacity, nerve conduction, and musculoskeletal function have been reported in different therapeutic settings [23], [41], [42]. However, many such studies quantify symptomatic or functional outcomes without directly measuring local tissue drug concentrations. Consequently, the clinical contribution of enhanced drug transport cannot always be distinguished from the independent therapeutic effects of ultrasound or other co-interventions.

Future clinical development should therefore predefine the acoustic exposure, device and coupling configuration, drug formulation, treatment schedule, and intended pharmacological endpoint. Controlled studies should incorporate appropriate sham or comparator groups, objective delivery or pharmacokinetic measurements when feasible, local and systemic safety assessment, and device-performance monitoring [8], [15], [74]. For repeated-use wearable systems, reliability of acoustic output and coupling should also be evaluated over the intended product lifetime rather than only during a single treatment session.

Because regulatory classification and evidence requirements depend on the final product configuration and intended therapeutic claim, specific regulatory designations should be supported by jurisdiction-specific authoritative guidance rather than inferred from unrelated transdermal patches or ultrasound devices [78]. Establishing the intended product configuration early in development will allow acoustic, pharmaceutical, safety, and human-factors evidence to be generated within a coherent translational framework [79].

Future outlook: toward mechanism-aware and adaptive wearable transdermal systems

The next stage of wearable ultrasound-assisted TDD should move from fixed acoustic protocols toward mechanism-aware control of the complete delivery process. Rather than incorporating sensing simply to increase device functionality, future systems should prioritize variables that directly affect acoustic delivery and transdermal transport, such as skin contact, coupling stability, local temperature, acoustic output, and, where technically validated, recovery of the skin barrier. Wearable ultrasound bioelectronics provides an enabling precedent for integrating flexible electronics, sensing, and miniaturized ultrasound modules [17], while digitally controlled acoustic systems demonstrate that acoustic power and exposure duration can be programmed at the device level [70]. However, these engineering capabilities should be regarded as enabling technologies rather than current evidence for closed-loop LFU drug delivery.

Adaptive frequency selection is a particularly relevant future direction because LFU and higher-frequency ultrasound can perform partially separable functions. Simultaneous 20-kHz and 1-MHz exposure can modify cavitation activity and the resulting transport regions, whereas sequential low-frequency irradiation followed by MHz irradiation can use the first phase primarily for barrier opening and the second phase for subsequent transport enhancement [11], [12]. A future wearable platform could therefore use LFU selectively when barrier permeabilization is required and reduce or terminate LFU exposure once sufficient permeability has been established, while a higher-frequency phase provides continued transport under a different acoustic regime. Whether such strategies reduce exposure time or improve safety in fully integrated wearable systems remains to be demonstrated directly.

Adaptive operation must also respect the constraints linking power demand and portability described in Section 4.4. Low-frequency, high-displacement operation can impose substantial requirements on the transducer, driver, and local energy source, whereas sustained or higher-frequency systems introduce their own average-power and thermal constraints [53], [65], [66]. Battery-powered integrated systems, distributed on-body architectures, and externally powered or battery-free-at-the-patch concepts therefore represent different points within the same design space rather than a simple progression toward increasing integration [25], [65], [66], [68], [69], [70]. Future development should select among these architectures according to the required acoustic dose and treatment duration rather than assuming that maximal local integration is always optimal.

Most importantly, technological sophistication should not outpace clinical evidence. Wearable and sustained high-frequency systems provide useful engineering and translational precedents [13], [14], but direct human validation of wearable LFU-mediated TDD remains limited [8], [15]. Future studies should therefore combine acoustic dosimetry, coupling characterization, drug-specific pharmacokinetic or local-delivery endpoints, repeated-use safety assessment, and device reliability within the same experimental framework. Standardized reporting of frequency, acoustic pressure or intensity, duty cycle, exposure duration, coupling-layer geometry, source-to-skin distance, temperature, and drug formulation would substantially improve comparison across studies and help determine when LFU alone, higher-frequency ultrasound, or multi-frequency strategies provide the most appropriate balance between barrier opening, transport enhancement, safety, and wearability [80].

Conclusion

Wearable LFU for TDD should be understood as the intersection of a skin-conformal delivery geometry and a cavitation-oriented barrier-opening strategy [6], [7], [8]. In thin fluid-containing coupling domains, LFU can promote transient/inertial cavitation near the stratum corneum, generating local shear, microjets, pressure transients, and localized transport regions that increase permeability [7], [9]. Bubble-associated microstreaming and bulk acoustic streaming can further assist convective fluid and solute transport through the permeabilized interface [9], [10]. When hydrated polymer or other solid-like couplants are used, acoustic transmission is governed more strongly by impedance matching, interfacial reflection, attenuation, and layer thickness, with cavitation expected primarily in adjacent fluid-containing domains [16], [59], [60]. Importantly, the short source-to-skin distance of wearable systems also makes higher-frequency exposure technically feasible; under such conditions, distributed acoustic fields, acoustic streaming, and absorption-related thermal effects may provide transport-oriented functions that complement LFU-mediated barrier opening [10], [12], [13], [14].

Direct clinical validation of fully integrated wearable LFU drug-delivery systems remains limited, and much of the present mechanistic and clinical understanding is derived from non-wearable LFU phonophoresis or higher-frequency wearable ultrasound studies [8], [13], [14], [15], [23]. Translation therefore requires standardized reporting of acoustic dose, coupling geometry, duty cycle, exposure duration, thermal response, and drug-specific delivery outcomes, together with repeated-use safety and reproducibility testing [8], [15], [58], [59], [60].

Multi-frequency and adaptive strategies are promising because barrier opening and subsequent transport can be optimized as partially separable acoustic functions, but their benefits must be confirmed in wearable TDD systems rather than inferred from mechanistic precedents alone [11], [12]. Future progress will depend on co-optimizing frequency, transducer architecture, coupling interface, power consumption, and drug formulation while generating direct human evidence for wearable LFU-mediated transdermal delivery [8], [15], [53], [65], [66].
