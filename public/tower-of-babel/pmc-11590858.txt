Implementing group consultations in a lower limb musculoskeletal outpatient service to improve efficiency and maintain positive patient outcomes
Authors: Lisa Wigham, Annette Richardson, Kathryn Moore, Michael Martin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGroup consultations (GCs) in healthcare have been shown to improve clinical outcomes with greater efficiency. This project aimed to develop a more efficient musculoskeletal (MSK) physiotherapy-led GC approach for common lower limb problems. This was to help manage an anticipated increase in demand while maintaining high patient satisfaction and ensuring clinical effectiveness.MethodsThree ‘Plan-Do-Study-Act’ (PDSA) cycles were undertaken to test out the new approach of virtual group consultations (VGCs) and face-to-face GCs with MSK patients. These two approaches were tested and refined on four common lower limb conditions: patellofemoral pain syndrome, Achilles tendinopathy, plantar fasciopathy and knee osteoarthritis. We measured the number of patients seen, staff time, patients’ experience and patient outcome scores.ResultsEach patient used 45 min of physiotherapy time via the traditional one-to-one physiotherapy consultation approach, this was reduced to 22 min in the VGCs and 12 min for the face-to-face GCs. We found a significant increase in EQ-5D scores (PDSA 1: +0.19, p=0.004 and PDSA 2: +0.17, p=0.002) and the Visual Analogue Score for Pain (PDSA 1: −1.95, p=0.001 and PDSA 2: −2.6, p=0.007) from the first to the second virtual GC sessions.ConclusionGCs were shown to be an efficient way of providing physiotherapy to patients with lower limb conditions. Feedback from patients attending GCs has shown a good level of effectiveness and patient satisfaction. This alternative way to review and treat patients has yielded a reduction in physiotherapist contact time per patient, improving the efficiency of our MSK service. We did not compare these groups to the one-to-one face-to-face consultations, therefore whether they are better, is unknown.Keywords: Outpatients, Quality improvement, PDSA, Patient satisfaction, Patient Reported Outcome Measures

Problem

Tyneside integrated musculoskeletal service (TIMS) is an intermediate care service, with a single point of access. It provides expert opinion, diagnosis and treatment services to patients with musculoskeletal (MSK) conditions. Patients can be referred to TIMS if they are registered with one of 65 general practitioners covering a population of approximately 510 000. This service receives on average 40 000 referrals per annum and approximately 40% have lower limb conditions.

All 16 000 lower limb referrals are triaged and categorised as either urgent or routine, and approximately one-third are urgent. The routine lower limb patients (approximately 10 500) are sent information on the management of their condition and asked to contact the service if they require a physiotherapy appointment. During 2019–2020 approximately 3650 patients per annum requested a routine physiotherapist lower limb appointment.

The five most common lower limb conditions presenting for a routine appointment are greater trochanteric pain syndrome, patellofemoral pain syndrome, Achilles tendinopathy, plantar fasciopathy and osteoarthritis (OA) of the knee. During 2019–2020, 1911 patients (52% of routine physiotherapy requests) with one of these conditions requested a routine lower limb appointment. These conditions require a similar management approach and were allocated 45 min for a physiotherapist consultation when delivered in a clinic setting on a one-to-one physiotherapist basis. Each patient consultation involves history taking, examination with clinical management and patient education.

The TIMS was recommissioned in 2018 with a finite income and staffing level. We anticipated an increase in demand for the service with no likelihood of any additional resources. Therefore, TIMS needed to consider modifying and adapting the way the service was delivered to prevent longer waiting times and to cope with the increasing demand. As well as the anticipated increase in demand, we were aware of the repetition of advice and patient education delivered by physiotherapists for some common lower limb conditions. These reasons emphasised the need to look at alternative ways to provide physiotherapy consultations and management of similar lower limb conditions.

Background

MSK conditions are associated with reduced mobility, functional limitations and lower levels of well-being. The number of people living with these conditions is rising rapidly1 2 and within the UK almost one-third of the population is affected by a MSK problem.3 The increase in MSK conditions has emphasised the need for healthcare services to consider how to best deliver their MSK services in the future, by exploring new ways to provide appropriate and timely advice for patients seeking support to manage their conditions.2 4

The incidence of common lower limb conditions is a concern with pain often the dominant symptom. Greater trochanteric pain syndrome is a common cause of lateral hip pain with incidence reported to be approximately 1.8–5.6 patients per 1000/year.5 Patients often have coexisting low back pain, OA, iliotibial band tenderness and obesity.5 Patellofemoral pain syndrome is a common cause of knee pain, with an annual population prevalence of 22.7%, and in adolescents 28.9%.6 Achilles tendinopathy is estimated to affect more than 150 000 people in the UK every year7 and the general population prevalence of plantar fasciopathy is 4%–7%.8 OA is the most common cause of knee pain in those over the age of 45.9 18% of the UK population aged over 45 years have sought treatment for OA of the knee.10

Group consultations (GCs) are a relatively new concept in the UK within primary care and have been one of the many suggested solutions to ease the current primary care waiting times while maintaining effective care.11 It is a method of managing patients presenting with similar problems, such as education and advice to manage their problems.12 GCs, can incorporate clinical interventions such as history taking, examination and clinical management, as well as patient education. During appointments of about 90 min, up to 12 patients can share experiences, interact with facilitating professionals as a group and receive one-to-one care.13 Traditionally GCs were delivered in a clinical setting, however since the COVID-19 pandemic online delivery of virtual group consultations (VGCs) has been developed.11

Evidence supporting the use of VGCs within healthcare is growing substantially.14 A retrospective study looked at managing obesity using GCs. They found supporting weight loss through GCs was effective and there was no difference between a face-to-face or virtual platform.15 Another study implemented VGCs for managing patients with menopausal symptoms and measured quality of life scores, showing a 52% improvement in scores following VGC.16 Results have highlighted the effectiveness and acceptability of the VGC modality. An examination into the effects of VGCs compared with traditional one-to-one appointment in the management of diabetes showed a significant improvement in blood sugar levels and significantly lowered emergency department visits for those managed within VGCs.17 Qualitative data analysis suggested VGCs increased patient’s confidence to self-manage their condition.17

These studies support the effectiveness of VGCs in improving clinical outcomes with potentially greater efficacy compared with traditional care. The evidence highlights the ability of VGCs to promote self-supported care, which is a fundamental aim of the National Health Service long-term plan4 11 and a quality standard of the Chartered Standard of Physiotherapy for MSK practice.18 To date there is no evidence evaluating the implementation of VGCs within lower limb MSK physiotherapy practice.

To summarise the implementation of GCs in healthcare has the potential to improve clinical outcomes with greater efficiency, satisfaction and education. Therefore, this project’s aim was to introduce physiotherapist GCs to an integrated MSK service for patients with lower limb problems, to help manage an anticipated increase in demand while maintaining high patient satisfaction and ensuring clinical effectiveness.

In healthcare, the need to deliver high-value care has been centred on a quadruple aim.19 This includes a framework for improving patient experience of healthcare, lowering costs, improving the health of patients and improving staff experience of providing care. More recently this has been added to with the addition of better staff education, known as the quintuple aim.20 We were keen to provide measures to contribute to these important components of healthcare while changing the delivery approach.

Measurement

Our measurement plan consisted of a count of the number of patients seen, utilisation of staff clinical time, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs).

We used PROMs which are short, self-completed questionnaires designed to capture patient views about health status and are ideally suited to areas such as MSK health, where disease impact is not easily captured using biomarkers. PROMs are increasingly valued for their use in evaluating the performance of MSK services alongside PREMs.21 To capture PROMs, the EQ-5D and Health Thermometer, Visual Analogue Score (VAS) and Oxford Knee Score (OKS) were used. The EQ-5D and Health Thermometer is a well-established, widely used instrument and easy to use for assessing health-related quality of life in normal clinical practice.22 23 It was developed by the EuroQol Group in the 1980s providing a reliable and valid, generic instrument used to measure, compare and value health status across disease areas.22 It can be completed within a few minutes either by self-completion or by interview administration.23 The EQ-5D tool provides a calculated score from 0 to 1. A maximum score of 1 indicates the best health status. The Health Thermometer is a patient self-reported rating of general health status with 100 as the best health status and 0 as the worst. The EQ-5D and Health Thermometer were collected over the telephone by the administration team at the time of allocating an appointment.

The second part of PROMs was the VAS for pain. This is a commonly used unidimensional measure of pain intensity to record patients’ pain progression and compare pain severity between patients with similar conditions. This is best measured by a visual horizontal line and numbers 0–10 marked on the line and the patient nominates a mark on the line to show their pain level 0 the lowest and 10 highest level of pain. VAS has been found to have a good test–retest reliability and validity for the measurement of pain.24 25 The visual view of the 0–10 line was not possible due to the virtual platform capabilities. Instead, the facilitator asked each patient at the beginning of the VGC for a number from 0 to 10 where they would rate their pain on average in the last week. This was repeated at a second session, which took place approximately 6 weeks after the first session, to reassess and see if any improvements had occurred. Each patient’s response was recorded and saved on their electronic patient record.

An additional measure was used for the OA knee group to specifically understand the impact on knee function and pain using a tool validated for OA knee. This was a tool routinely used by orthopaedic surgeons within our organisation to assess outcomes following surgery. Therefore, we were keen to use a familiar tool undertaken at the first and second sessions. This consists of 12 questions about an individual’s level of function, activities of daily living and how they have been affected by pain in their knee(s) over the preceding 4 weeks. The scores are then summed to produce an overall score running from 0 (worst possible) to 48 (best outcome). The lower the score the more the patient is negatively impacted by their knee pain on their function. OKS was shown to be a valid outcome measure with a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 5 points after a total knee arthroplasty.26 27 OKS was also shown to be a valid outcome measure for patients undergoing non-operative OA treatment.26

PREMs were captured using a set of predesigned questions already tested and used for GCs.28 We used five questions using a 0–10 Likert scale developed to capture patient experience. Patients were asked to rate the GC experience, the organisation of the session, the listening ability of the physiotherapist, their understanding of their condition and how well they felt able to manage their condition. There was a section at the end of the questionnaire for open comments. The questionnaire was sent by post after the VGC or given out at the end of a group face-to-face consultation.

Design

At the beginning of 2020, we started the planning process to test the GC approach on the five common lower limb conditions: greater trochanteric pain syndrome, patellofemoral pain syndrome, Achilles tendinopathy and plantar fasciopathy and OA knee. The team involved in planning the new approach included two senior physiotherapists, an associate physiotherapist and two administrators. All staff received training and support on the GC process, which involved two remote sessions for 2 hours each for ongoing support.

In March 2020 when the COVID-19 pandemic restrictions were implemented, virtual delivery was agreed as the most suitable way to implement GCs. The team agreed to remove greater trochanteric pain syndrome at planning stage. This was due to low numbers with accurate diagnosis, many other differential diagnoses and accurate identification at triage. We agreed to start the implementation with GCs on a virtual platform for three lower limb problems: patellofemoral pain syndrome, Achilles tendinopathy and plantar fasciopathy. The OA knee group was excluded initially as the team predicted this group was less likely to be successful, if delivered virtually. This prediction was based on this group having an older average age and the potential for less familiarity with online platforms. Also, this group required a physical examination to ensure correct diagnosis from triage. The OA knee group was to be considered for GC implementation after learning from the VGCs and when face-to-face reviews could be undertaken.

A booking system was developed to manage the allocation of patients to the group sessions following triage. It was coordinated by an administrator. 10 appointments were allocated in the diary for each group. A set of inclusion criteria for VGCs specific to the lower limb conditions was developed for triage, the inclusion criteria was: has a clinical physiotherapy diagnoses of patellofemoral pain syndrome, Achilles tendinopathy or plantar fasciopathy, plus English speaking. If patients contacted the department reporting an inability to use a digital platform their VGC was changed to a traditional one-to-one review. All other patients not meeting the criteria were placed on the traditional one-to-one clinic waiting list.

We identified and provided a facilitator (not a physiotherapist), who provided administrative support for every VGC. The facilitator started the session by sharing a set of slides to explain the VGC process. They covered housekeeping, expected rules for the group, confidentiality, sharing of personal information and the date for a review session.

The second part of the VGC was designed to collect PROMs data. The EQ-5D and Health Thermometer scores were collected before the VGC by the administrative team over the phone to save time during the actual VGC.

The VGC facilitator asked the following three questions from each patient.

In the last week, on average what would you rate your score on a visual analogue scale of 0–10 where 0 represents no pain and 10 is the worst pain you can imagine?

In the last week, on average what would you rate your score on a visual analogue scale of 0–10 where 0 represents no pain and 10 is the worst pain you can imagine?

What is your normal level of activity (including your job, day-to-day duties and sport/exercise) and what is your current level of activity if reduced due to your pain?

What is your normal level of activity (including your job, day-to-day duties and sport/exercise) and what is your current level of activity if reduced due to your pain?

Do you have any questions or concerns you would like the physiotherapist to answer today?

Do you have any questions or concerns you would like the physiotherapist to answer today?

The patient data responses were recorded on a spreadsheet.

The physiotherapist running the VGC was a lower limb clinical specialist in physiotherapy. This was different from the routine patients seen face-to-face on a one-to-one basis in clinic, as these were reviewed by a generic physiotherapist. The specialist physiotherapist running the VGC listened to the patient’s responses to the three set questions. On occasions a junior physiotherapist, as part of their allocated continuous professional development, joined and were introduced as a silent observer on the sessions. They were not required to deliver the service as this was their pure professional learning time.

After patients responded to the questions, the physiotherapist presented the predesigned guidance and advice on the groups’ specific lower limb conditions using a set of slides. The sharing of the advice and guidance was using an evidenced based management approach to help inform patients on the causative factors commonly associated with their lower limb conditions and included how to implement appropriate load management and lower limb strengthening exercises. After the specific guidance was presented, the physiotherapist provided responses to each patient’s individual queries. Guidance was also emailed to each patient after the session.

At the end of the session, a review session date about 6 weeks later was shared with the group. The review session was to assess patient’s response to the VGC and decide if further input was required or if the patient could be discharged.

Strategy

Plan-Do-Study-Act 1

The VGC for three lower limb conditions was tested from August 2020 to June 2021. PROMs, PREMs and team feedback were reviewed continually and guided the changes for the next Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA). The feedback and results indicated a need for more condition specific patient information, and this level of additional information would not be appropriate for all three groups. Therefore, it was suggested that the group should be split into two groups, one for Achilles tendinopathy and plantar fasciopathy and the second for patellofemoral pain syndrome. Further feedback was to enhance the written patient information to help support the management of their condition. Therefore, a lower limb load management leaflet was produced to be provided to patients.

The administrative team provided feedback on the booking system. They reported high non-attenders, when the patients were directly booked into the VGCs from the triaging physiotherapist indicating a change to allow patients some choice for the VGC appointment. The team meetings were used for staff feedback. The team lead described that the group provided an opportunity for more junior members to learn from the specialist by attending the VGCs.

Plan-Do-Study-Act 2

Two separate VGCs, Achilles tendinopathy/plantar fasciitis and patellofemoral pain syndrome were tested from June 2021 to February 2022.

The booking system was amended to an opt-in system at the point of triage by providing patient information, specific to their condition, alongside a VGC invite letter, so patients could choose to self-manage at this point or choose to book into a VGC session. Previously patients were automatically booked into a VGC with a set date and time by the triage staff. However, this was changed due to a high number of non-attenders and some patients reporting VGC was not their expectation and not flexible enough. By changing this to an invite to the VGC with an opt-in system, patients were informed of the VGC content, and expectations and they could also choose the date for their VGC. It was also hoped this change would improve attendance as well as patient satisfaction.

The invite letter explained in more detail what to expect within the VGC. This included how to maintain anonymity and advising on the ability to join as guests with no name if they wished. At the beginning of the VGC, the facilitator advised patients to share as little or as much information within the group, as they felt comfortable. Due to attending virtually, patients were asked to make sure that others could not hear or see the VGC, other than their carer. The facilitator role also expanded on the use of virtual platform, for those unfamiliar and its functions including the chat box, mute button and camera use.

Plan-Do-Study-Act 3

We used our learning from PDSA 1 and 2 to increase our confidence in the effectiveness of VGCs and started a GC for OA knees.

A face-to-face GC process for the OA knee group was implemented in April 2022. A face-to-face appointment allowed for a clinical examination of the knee(s), to ensure the correct clinical diagnosis of OA knee. Two physiotherapists were present, to carry out the assessments and to deliver a 45 min presentation. The presentation included education on understanding OA of the knee and holistic management in line with the Institute for Health and Care Excellence guidelines. While the examination took place, a video was played to patients with more education on OA and management advice. Leaflets were also available for patients to take and read.

Staff feedback from team meetings highlighted, that although many shared an initial reservation, they did gain an opportunity for personal development in educating larger numbers and observed the benefit from patient feedback.

Lessons and limitations

At the start of 2020, we anticipated an increase in demand for our service and our data showed this increase for lower limb problems increased by 47% 3 years later. Our testing of VGCs and GCs on specific groups of patients with lower limb problems (patellofemoral pain syndrome, Achilles tendinopathy, plantar fasciopathy and OA knees) were initially small groups nonetheless with the patients studied we found it to be timely and efficient and effective approach to deliver our physiotherapy-led service. We recognise that the number of patients participating in the VGCs in the first 3 years were small representing a small proportion of the total number of routine patients requesting a physiotherapy appointment. However, the GCs attendance had a higher proportion at 13%.

The efficiency was demonstrated with the ability to see more patients in less time through the group session approach. PROMs data on the VGC and GC demonstrated an increase, suggesting the effectiveness of these sessions. Patient satisfaction following VGC and GC was viewed positively by the team as PREMs data demonstrated patients rated the experience on average as 6.4 or higher on a 0–10 scale. PREMs and PROMs data were not compared with traditional one-to-one face-to-face patient sessions, so it was unable to be determined if one approach was superior.

Time was required to plan and set up the new approach of GCs. We were fortunate to be able to take the opportunity while COVID-19 restrictions were in place and patient consultations were minimal, to invest our time in planning this approach. Most of our time was taken up on receiving training, developing support materials and learning how to use the virtual platform. The early COVID-19 restrictions resulted in outpatient contacts being stopped and this emphasised the need to try a virtual consultation approach first to enable a way of supporting and advising some patients on treatment for their lower limb problems.

We tested both VGCs and GCs using three PDSAs and found this to be a beneficial improvement method to learn and adapt our approach. We are confident that one of the main reasons why the effectiveness of VGCs and GCs was maintained was due to the development and use of evidence-based guidance and advice on the specific lower limb conditions. Specifically, the sharing of the advice in the sessions and the follow-up information sent to patients. This guidance aimed to help inform patients on the causative factors commonly associated with their lower limb condition and how to implement appropriate load management and lower limb strengthening exercises. Another potential reason for the effectiveness of the VGCs and GC approach was due to the physiotherapists being specialists in lower limb problems. Our work supports the national guidance recommending that these groups are run by specialists in their field.11 29

The GC average of 12 min of physiotherapy time per patient was lower than the VGC. This better efficiency was due to the higher number of attenders booking into the GC compared with the VGC and the lower number of DNAs in the GC groups. It may well be that the GC approach is more efficient due to patients’ higher preference for face-to-face over a virtual approach.

We found a high DNA rate with VGCs despite changes to allow patients to choose appointments. In addition, the PREMs question focusing on the patients’ view of how they rated the idea of a VGC, had the lowest score in PDSA 1 and PDSA 2. This finding was similar in other studies15 with a similar level of patient satisfaction with the GCs and VGCs. This highlights the need for further work to focus on understanding the reason for the high DNA.

The OA knee group of patients had an improvement in the OKS using the GC approach. The increase of 3.29 points appears good when compared with a study finding an MCID of 5 as a good outcome following an invasive procedure for the same condition.26

One of the unintended consequences of introducing this new approach was the positive impact on staff delivering the VGCs and GCs. This new approach has allowed junior physiotherapists to join the groups, who reported personal development and learning from observing the delivery.

We used the EQ-5D and the Health Thermometer tool via the telephone which may have introduced some bias as the tool was originally designed as an online or paper instrument. However, this was a practical and quick way of measuring the impact of our new approach during COVID-19 and the associated patient contact restrictions. In addition we used a PREMs questionnaire with a 0–10 Likert scale which may not have accurately assessed patient’s experience, however it was a tool used regularly for GCs elsewhere.

Introducing this new approach has had challenges. Obtaining and recording patient PREMs and PROMs data was a challenge due to the nature of a virtual platform and time constraints for administrative team to collect over the phone prior to the VGC.

A minority of patients were not satisfied that the group met their expectations, and this was managed by offering a one-to-one consultation. Ongoing evaluation is needed to assess if changes we make to increase attendance improve.

We tried to assess the impact of implementing VGCs and GC in our service using a range of measures. This included patient numbers seen, staff time, patients’ experience and patient outcome scores, with the addition of first-hand experience of staff experience. Therefore, we feel this intervention is on the way to meeting healthcare’s quintuple aim.20

Conclusion

We introduced GCs with small groups of patients with lower limb problems, and they were shown to be an efficient way of providing care to lower limb MSK patients. This alternative way to review and treat patients has been shown to use less physiotherapist time per patient so we will continue to use this approach with a future plan to increase the number of patients seen using this approach. The feedback from patients attending VGCs and GCs has shown a good level of effectiveness and patient satisfaction which we feel is acceptable for this new approach. However, as we did not compare these groups to the one-to-one face-to-face consultations, whether they are better, is unknown. We have started to collect effectiveness data on one-to-one face-to-face patients to be able to directly compare results to the VGC and GC.

We plan to evaluate our service with a strategy to continually increase attendance and improve the patient experience of VGCs and GCs. We are planning to explore and test this approach on groups of patients such as chronic lower back pain and upper limb problems. In addition, other local services have observed our approach, with the plan to test it within their own service.
