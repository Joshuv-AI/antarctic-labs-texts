Letter to the Editor Regarding “Adult RSV Vaccination: What is the Role of RSV Subgroup in Disease Prevention?”
Authors: Harish Madhava, Karin Velthove
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article by Williams et al. on the role of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) subgroups in disease prevention [1]. The commentary raises important scientific questions about RSV subgroups and vaccine design. However, several statements and implications would benefit from better contextualization to prevent overstating the strength of the evidence favoring bivalency and to more fully acknowledge critical limitations of the data cited.

The pioneering work by Barney Graham, Jason McLellan, and their groups paved the way for identifying the prefusion (preF) conformation of the RSV fusion (F) protein as the most effective target for vaccines [2]. The F protein is highly conserved across both RSV A and B subgroups, which has been demonstrated beyond reasonable doubt through genotypic surveillance of both current and historic circulating strains [3–5]. Outbreak strains, potentially driven by environmental pressure, can lead to serious public health consequences. However, these are rare events, and significant global efforts are underway to track these changes and to react if needed [6, 7]. Substantial antigenic drift in the F protein that would disrupt neutralizing epitopes could compromise effectiveness irrespective of valency, contrary to what Williams et al. suggest—without any supporting evidence—in their claim that “Including both A and B variants of the F protein in vaccines helps protect against subgroup-specific changes, preventing decreased antibody effectiveness due to other shifts in the virus structure”. Therefore, the argument to favor a bivalent vaccine, like RSVpreF, over vaccines based on RSV A preF, like adjuvanted RSVPreF3 or preF-based mRNA vaccine, does not seem to be supported by the current scientific evidence. This is further corroborated by the fact that licensed products on the market, including long-acting monoclonal antibodies, have demonstrated broad neutralizing activity [4, 8] and efficacy [9] against prevalent circulating strains, and that the adjuvanted RSVPreF3 vaccine has shown robust and sustained efficacy against both RSV A and RSV B disease [10, 11].

Figure 3 in the article, which shows the efficacy estimates of the three available RSV vaccines over time, is an oversimplification of data with inappropriate comparisons. Presenting the data for all three vaccines together in a single figure could mistakenly prompt the reader to compare these efficacies to one another. This is not appropriate because of differences in study design, case definitions for lower respiratory tract illness (LRTI), follow-up times, and study populations. Moreover, the presentation of only selective efficacy point estimates without confidence intervals (CIs) and of seasonal vaccine estimates on a cumulative follow-up time scale may lead to misinterpretation. The authors also did not make any reference to the other co-primary endpoint of the RSVpreF RENOIR study, i.e., vaccine efficacy against first episodes of RSV-LRTI with ≥ 2 signs or symptoms, which was 65.1% (95% CI 35.9, 82.0) in RSV season 1 and 55.7% (34.7, 70.4) in season 2 [12]. For RSV B, an efficacy of 53.8% (5.2, 78.8) was observed for this endpoint in season 1 and a non-statistically significant efficacy of 46.2% (− 7.0, 74.0) in season 2, questioning the hypothetical advantage of a bivalent vaccine.

The real-world evidence (RWE) presented in the article appears to be selective and has been used to fit a narrative rather than to provide a comprehensive assessment of the emerging data across age and risk groups. The Kaiser Permanente Southern California data that the authors refer to are from a pre-print that had not been peer reviewed [13]. Assuming that the referenced pre-print contains the current and accurate representation of the study results, vaccine effectiveness against RSV B for the RSVpreF vaccine is not statistically significant in season 2: 77% (95% CI − 1, 95) for the main endpoint, which is RSV-related LRTI hospitalizations/emergency department visits. It is unclear how these data can support the claim by the authors of a bivalency advantage. The other referenced study in the RWE section evaluated multiple endpoints over two seasons [14]. Williams and colleagues report a difference between adjuvanted RSVPreF3 and RSVpreF in terms of efficacy against subgroup-specific RSV-related acute respiratory illness hospitalization. They state “When evaluating effectiveness by subgroup, the bivalent vaccine appears to provide greater protection than the monovalent vaccine for RSV B cases [81% (95% CI 50–93) and 65% (95% CI 34–82), respectively]” but ignore the fact that the median time since vaccination for both products was markedly different (198 days for RSVpreF and 261 days for adjuvanted RSVPreF3) [14].

The conclusion section of the article is highly speculative and is based on little or no scientific evidence. The questions posed in the commentary are important and merit broad discussion. However, by not more clearly separating hypothesis from established evidence, the current manuscript’s interpretations risk overstating the strength of the available evidence in favor of bivalent formulations.
