Transient cerebral ischemia in pituitary apoplexy–induced internal carotid artery occlusion: illustrative case
Authors: Stanley Xue, Angela Li Ching Ng, Nicholas Chen, Peter Khong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDCerebral ischemia secondary to occlusion or vasospasm of adjacent large vessels is a rare but significant complication of pituitary apoplexy. Misdiagnosis can lead to delays in management and devastating neurological outcomes. This case is the first reported instance of an initial presentation with transient cerebral ischemic symptoms, in addition to a classic pituitary apoplexy syndrome, for this clinical entity of pituitary apoplexy with adjacent large vessel occlusion.OBSERVATIONSA 49-year-old man presented with a classic pituitary apoplexy syndrome of headache, vomiting, and hemianopia, with transient preceding expressive dysphasia and right-hand numbness. Computed tomography (CT) and CT angiography of the brain demonstrated a large apoplectic pituitary tumor and associated occlusion of the left internal carotid artery. Subsequent CT perfusion studies revealed a threatening large ischemic penumbra in the left middle cerebral artery territory. He underwent urgent transsphenoidal resection of the pituitary tumor, resulting in symptom resolution and the prevention of cerebral infarction.LESSONSThe recognition of transient cerebral ischemic symptoms in pituitary apoplexy should encourage the exclusion of any large-vessel complications and resultant cerebral ischemia. Early assessment, particularly with angiography and perfusion studies, can assist in guiding management, which is usually surgical decompression, distinct from traditional treatment for cerebrovascular events. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24566

Pituitary apoplexy arises from hemorrhage or infarction of the pituitary gland and classically presents with headache, visual disturbance, or altered level of consciousness.1 Rarely, pituitary apoplexy is complicated by cerebral infarction secondary to occlusion or vasospasm of adjacent large blood vessels.2 To our knowledge, the following is the first reported instance of an initial presentation with transient cerebral ischemic symptoms (in addition to a classic pituitary apoplexy syndrome) without subsequent progression to cerebral infarction in a case of pituitary apoplexy with adjacent large-vessel complications. We believe that this was due to prompt radiological assessment including angiography and perfusion studies to recognize secondary cerebral ischemia and timely surgical decompression to avoid further deterioration.

Illustrative Case

An otherwise healthy 49-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 5-day history of headache, vomiting, and visual disturbance. This culminated in a 5-minute episode of expressive dysphasia and right-hand numbness that had self-resolved 5 hours prior to hospital presentation. On examination, he was found to have a temporal hemianopia affecting the left eye but no motor, sensory, or speech deficits.

Plain computed tomography (CT) and contrast-enhanced CT of the brain demonstrated a large, expansile, peripherally enhancing, hemorrhagic sellar mass with suprasellar extension and measuring 35 × 30 × 25 mm, consistent with pituitary apoplexy (Fig. 1). No subarachnoid hemorrhage was demonstrated. CT angiography revealed occlusion of the left internal carotid artery (ICA), with no flow demonstrated through its cavernous segment. There was a reconstitution of the left middle cerebral artery (MCA) and left anterior cerebral artery (ACA) more distally by means of the contralateral anterior circulation via the anterior communicating artery and the ipsilateral posterior circulation via the left posterior communicating artery (Fig. 1). CT perfusion (CTP) performed in the context of transient ischemic symptoms demonstrated an associated ischemic penumbra with an increased mean transit time and time to peak with preserved cerebral blood volume and cerebral blood flow in the left MCA territory (Fig. 2). He was treated with intravenous dexamethasone and urgently transferred to our tertiary hospital for further evaluation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain confirmed a large, expansile, hemorrhagic sellar mass with suprasellar extension consistent with pituitary apoplexy. There was near-complete obliteration of the left cavernous ICA flow void as well as elevation and compression of the optic chiasm (Fig. 3). The patient underwent endoscopic transsphenoidal resection of the pituitary tumor the following day. Histopathology demonstrated a gonadotroph pituitary adenoma. Brain CT and MRI the day after his operation demonstrated some residual tumor with ongoing compression of the optic chiasm. There was restoration of the left cavernous ICA flow void. There remained no regions of diffusion restriction to suggest an acute cerebral infarction. His postoperative course was also complicated by a cerebrospinal fluid leak and diabetes insipidus. He underwent insertion of a lumbar drain and repeat endoscopic transsphenoidal debulking of the pituitary tumor with a nasoseptal flap and fat graft. He was started on oral desmopressin for the management of diabetes insipidus. Repeat brain MRI the following day demonstrated a further reduction in the tumor burden, with satisfactory decompression of the left ICA flow void and optic chiasm. There remained no regions of diffusion restriction to suggest an acute cerebral infarction (Fig. 4). The patient made an excellent recovery and was discharged after a total 12-day hospital stay. At the 6-week and 3-month outpatient follow-ups, he remained on oral desmopressin and was started on testosterone replacement. However, he remained well and otherwise asymptomatic with no associated visual or neurological symptoms. Three-month brain MRI demonstrated a small amount of possible residual tumor and continued decompression of the left ICA flow void and optic chiasm (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Observations

Pituitary apoplexy resulting in cerebral ischemia is a rarely reported clinical phenomenon. A recent systematic review by Jiang et al. found only 46 cases of cerebral infarction caused by pituitary apoplexy.2 In addition to the classic pituitary apoplexy syndrome of headache, visual impairment, or altered level of consciousness, the majority of these patients had cerebral ischemic symptoms, such as facial or limb paresis, sensory disturbance, or speech disturbance. The most frequently proposed mechanisms linking pituitary apoplexy and cerebral infarction were mechanical vascular compression in 25 patients (54.3%) and cerebral vasospasm in 17 patients (40%). The main affected blood vessels were the ICA (58.7%), ACA (32.6%), and MCA (25%).

A literature review was undertaken to search for cases of pituitary apoplexy with adjacent large-vessel complications that featured transient cerebral ischemic symptoms as part of their clinical course (Fig. 5). For the literature review, the PubMed database was queried. The string ((“ischemic attack, transient”) OR (“cerebral infarction”) OR (“brain ischemia”) OR (“stroke”)) AND (“pituitary apoplexy”) was entered (date of last search: April 22, 2024). Duplicates were removed. Articles were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Full texts were then retrieved and reviewed. The references of the selected articles were also examined to identify other potentially relevant reports. All original articles in the English language reporting pituitary apoplexy with associated large-vessel complications and documenting either transient cerebral ischemic symptoms as part of their clinical course or the use of CT or MRI perfusion studies as part of their clinical workup were included. Only 3 cases were found to meet these criteria. Ben-Nakhi et al. reported on a 40-year-old man who had presented with transient right hemiparesis in addition to acute confusion and receptive dysphasia after a syncopal episode.3 The patient was treated conservatively with hormonal supplementation and reported significant improvement but residual receptive dysphasia. Akutsu et al. reported on a 29-year-old man who had presented with a classic pituitary apoplexy syndrome.4 He underwent a transsphenoidal resection of the tumor. Postoperatively, he developed transient right hemiparesis that resolved after 3 hours. Subsequent brain CT and MRI demonstrated cerebral infarction in the left Huebner artery and medial lenticulostriate artery territory. Sanno et al. reported on a 55-year-old man who had developed pituitary apoplexy after pituitary function testing.5 On day 7 after the onset of apoplexy, the patient developed transient left hemiparesis, with three-dimensional CT demonstrating vasospasm of the right MCA, likely secondary to the presence of subarachnoid blood. The patient was treated with calcium channel blockers and underwent delayed transsphenoidal resection of the tumor. Thus, our case is the first reported instance of a patient with pituitary apoplexy and adjacent large-vessel complications who, in addition to a classic pituitary apoplexy syndrome, had an initial presentation with cerebral ischemic symptoms that were entirely transient. The relevance of this aspect of the clinical presentation is that even transient cerebral ischemic symptoms in patients with pituitary apoplexy should encourage clinicians to search for associated large-vessel complications and any resultant cerebral ischemia. Vice versa, pituitary apoplexy with large-vessel complications is an important, albeit rare, differential diagnosis for patients presenting with transient cortical ischemic symptoms, necessitating a different management approach compared to traditional transient ischemic attacks.

We argue that angiographic and perfusion studies represent a useful troubleshooting tool to exclude other causes of large vessel occlusion or vasospasm and to guide management, analogous to its role in traditional stroke management. This is especially the case given that the specific management of cerebral ischemia resulting from pituitary apoplexy with large-vessel involvement remains controversial due to the rarity of reported cases and the lack of any prospective studies. Of 41 patients with reported outcomes, Jiang et al. found that 7 patients were conservatively managed, 23 patients underwent resection via a transsphenoidal approach, and 9 patients underwent surgery via craniotomy.2 Those who underwent delayed surgery (at or more than 7 days after presentation) were found to have a better prognosis than those who had undergone urgent surgery (within 7 days of presentation), although this was confounded by the more critical conditions of the patients in the latter group. Some authors also caution against surgical decompression in pituitary apoplexy associated with massive cerebral infarction, given the risk of subsequent reperfusion hemorrhagic transformation.6–8 Banerjee et al. concluded that, regardless of the presence of cerebral infarction, utilizing established indications for the early surgical intervention of pituitary apoplexy (i.e., severe and persistent visual field defects, deteriorating visual field defects, decreased visual acuity, or decreased level of consciousness) is sufficient to determine the need for urgent surgical intervention in patients with pituitary apoplexy associated with cerebral infarction.9 At the time of their review, no case reports had utilized perfusion studies in their assessment of patients with pituitary apoplexy and cerebral ischemia.

In our case, the CTP study, which demonstrated a large ischemic penumbra in the left MCA territory without any established infarction, rationalized urgent surgical decompression to restore cerebral perfusion, in addition to the presence of a visual field defect. A literature review was also undertaken to search for cases of pituitary apoplexy with adjacent large-vessel complications where perfusion studies were utilized during the initial assessment, yielding only 3 other reported cases (Fig. 5). Similar to our case, Sussman et al. demonstrated a large core/penumbra mismatch on CTP in a 46-year-old man with pituitary apoplexy complicated by right ICA compression before proceeding to urgent decompression.10 Ahn et al. reported on a 78-year-old man with pituitary apoplexy complicated by right ICA compression.11 MRI perfusion studies also demonstrated a large right ICA territory diffusion-perfusion mismatch. Elsehety et al. also reported on a 60-year-old woman with pituitary apoplexy complicated by bilateral ICA compression.12 CTP demonstrated a large mismatch volume in the bilateral ICA territories. In the latter 2 cases, perfusion studies prompted urgent balloon angioplasty, which had an unsatisfactory temporary effect only, and patients were required to undergo urgent transsphenoidal resection of the pituitary tumor. Given the time-critical necessity of restoring cerebral perfusion, we believe that urgent surgical decompression should be considered first in the management of these patients, as it can represent a more effective and definitive intervention than endovascular approaches.

In our case, urgent transsphenoidal decompression had a sustained satisfactory outcome in restoring vascular flow. Our patient’s condition did not progress to cerebral infarction, and he had no persistent neurological deficits on discharge, at the 6-week follow-up, or at the 3-month follow-up.

Further study can also help elucidate the incidence and neuroimaging correlates of transient cortical ischemic symptoms in patients with pituitary apoplexy associated with large vessel occlusion or vasospasm. The role of perfusion studies and the degree that core/penumbra mismatch can guide management decisions in pituitary apoplexy with cortical ischemia represent another future direction of investigation.

Lessons

Our case report sheds light on a rare manifestation of pituitary apoplexy presenting with transient cortical ischemic symptoms due to ICA occlusion. Early assessment using CT or magnetic resonance angiography, along with perfusion studies, can be instrumental in recognizing these rare clinical entities and guiding management decisions. Urgent transsphenoidal decompression resulted in a favorable outcome in our patient and should be considered first for effective and definitive management of this time-critical clinical entity. Our case highlights the importance of considering cerebral ischemia as a complication of pituitary apoplexy, necessitating a distinct management approach from traditional cerebrovascular events.
