Elevated risk of infectious diseases in adulthood after prenatal or early postnatal exposure to the Great Chinese Famine
Authors: Qi Li, Philip A. Collender, Hailan Yu, Kristin N. Nelson, Jon Zelner, Xinqiang Wang, Xiaolu Wang, Jiayi Tang, Leonardo Martinez, Justin V. Remais, Changhong Yang, Qu Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificancePrenatal famine exposure increases risks for various noncommunicable conditions, but its impact on infectious diseases remains poorly understood. Using 18 y of surveillance data covering over 4.4 million cases across 11 major notifiable infectious diseases in China, we show that prenatal and early postnatal exposure to the Great Chinese Famine—the most severe famine in human history—is associated with significantly elevated risk of a wide range of infectious diseases in adulthood, as well as in their putative offspring. In light of the global COVID-19 aftermath, an increasing pace of climate-related emergencies, and widening global conflicts that may threaten food security and trigger famines, these findings highlight the need for long-term strategies to mitigate the multigenerational health consequences of famine.

Prenatal exposure to malnutrition has been demonstrated to have long-term health consequences (1, 2), including increasing the risk of noncommunicable diseases in adulthood [e.g., type 2 diabetes (3), metabolic syndrome (4), obesity (5), and hypertension (6) through mechanisms that include interrupted fetal programming (7), abnormal development of some organs (8), and altered metabolism (9)]. These adverse effects may persist for generations, possibly through mechanisms like persistent epigenetic changes established during early embryonic development and passed on to the next generation (7, 10, 11) and unhealthy behaviors observed in directly exposed generation and learned by their offspring (7, 12). Despite these findings on the impact of famine on noncommunicable diseases, the effects of prenatal exposure to famine on infectious diseases remain understudied. Animal studies have yielded potential mechanisms that may link prenatal malnutrition to communicable diseases (13). For example, undernutrition during prenatal stages has been found to lead to abnormal development of the immune system and increased susceptibility to infections in rats (14). However, there are few epidemiologic studies relating prenatal famine exposure to lifetime infectious disease risk in humans.

The Great Chinese Famine, driven by natural disasters and massive institutional and policy changes during the Great Leap Forward (15, 16), is considered to be the most devastating famine in human history, causing an estimated 15 to 43 million excess deaths and a reduction of about 30 million births (17, 18). During 1958–1961, China’s annual grain production decreased from 200 to 140 million tons (19), resulting in widespread undernutrition (17). The severity of the famine varied widely across locations due to differences in local governance, agriculture, climate, and grain supplies (20, 21). The severity of this widespread exposure and its known variation from place to place enable investigation of a dose–response relationship between famine severity and long-term infectious disease risk among exposed populations and their offspring.

Sichuan Province, located in southwest China, is the fourth most populous and fifth largest province by area, consisting of 21 prefectures (22). Compared to other provinces of China, Sichuan has faced a relatively high burden of many infectious diseases, such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection and pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) (23, 24). Sichuan also experienced the most severe famine conditions among all Chinese provinces during the Great Famine, exhibiting the highest total fertility loss, excess mortality, and longest duration of famine from 1958 to 1962 (20, 25).

In the present study, we build upon our previous work, which found significant associations of famine exposure with pulmonary tuberculosis risk in both the generation born during the famine (F1, see definition in Materials and Methods) and their likely offspring (F2) (26). We extend our analysis, incorporating passive surveillance data for a total of 11 notifiable infectious diseases from 2005 to 2022 in Sichuan Province to examine long-term and intergenerational effects of prenatal and early-life famine exposure on the risk of contracting various infectious diseases in adulthood. We combine Bayesian estimation of age, period, and cohort effects with generalized additive models (GAMs) fit to nonfamine cohorts to estimate attributable excess disease risks among the F1 and F2 cohorts, and perform meta-regression at the prefecture level to investigate the potential dose–response relationship of attributable excess disease risk and local famine severity. Notably, we employed a spatiotemporal Bayesian framework here to jointly estimate the prefecture-level age-period-cohort (APC) effects, which offers greater robustness to outlier age-specific incidence rates, particularly in areas with low incidence, compared to the method used in our previous PTB study (26). For consistency across diseases analyzed in this study, we therefore reanalyzed and reported PTB results using this updated framework.

Results

Descriptive Results.

Famine intensity.

The overall migration-adjusted cohort size shrinkage index (CSSI, see details in Materials and Methods) for Sichuan Province was 0.47, indicating that the population size of the famine birth cohort (F1, born between 1958–1962) was 47% smaller than the average population size of the pre- (1953–1957) and postfamine (1963–1967) cohorts. Across the 21 prefectures, migration-adjusted CSSI values ranged from 0.29 (Ganzi Prefecture) to 0.57 (Yaan Prefecture), with a mean of 0.47 and a SD of 0.07 (Fig. 1A). There was remarkable spatial heterogeneity in the severity of famine across prefectures: Yaan (migration-adjusted CSSI: 0.57), Meishan (0.56), Ziyang (0.54), and Leshan (0.54) in the central plain experienced the most severe famine, while Ganzi (0.29), Aba (0.33), Guangyuan (0.42), and Panzhihua (0.43) in the remote mountainous areas experienced the least severe famine (although these absolute effect sizes represent profound losses across all prefectures). To examine whether males and females were differentially affected by the famine, potentially due to favoring of male offspring, we estimated prefecture-level migration-adjusted CSSIs by sex. The migration-adjusted CSSI values for males and females were highly correlated with the estimated migration-adjusted CSSI for the whole population, although females tended to have slightly higher migration-adjusted CSSI values (SI Appendix, Table S2).

Infectious disease incidence.

From 2005 to 2022, a total of 4,437,930 cases for the 11 infectious diseases included in our analysis were reported (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). PTB (1,251,541 cases), hepatitis B (1,098,979 cases), and infectious diarrhea (702,725 cases) contributed the most cases, while hepatitis A (66,116 cases), acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis (AHC) (43,697 cases), and hepatitis E (17,723 cases) contributed the least (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). The relatively more rural and remote western mountainous prefectures, such as Liangshan, Ganzi, and Aba, reported the highest total annual mean incidence rate of the 11 infectious diseases (Fig. 1B). Incidence rates of PTB, bacillary dysentery, and hepatitis A were especially elevated in these prefectures (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Economically developed prefectures in the eastern plain (e.g., Dazhou, Meishan, Chengdu) reported the lowest overall incidence rates (Fig. 1B). Across age groups, the highest incidence rates occurred in individuals <3 y, 15 to 35 y, and ≥60 y old (Fig. 1C). Young children were most affected by infectious diarrhea and bacillary dysentery, while teens and adults were most affected by PTB and hepatitis B (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). The combined annual incidence rate for all 11 infectious diseases decreased from 351.7 per 100,000 persons in 2005 to 242.1 per 100,000 persons in 2015, and then increased to 313.2 per 100,000 persons in 2022 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). Elevated infectious disease risks were observed in the F1 and F2 cohorts when compared to their adjacent birth cohorts for all eligible infectious diseases combined (the upper and lower boxes in Fig. 1C), as well as most diseases individually (SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S10).

Age, Period, and Cohort Effects.

Age effects.

Variations in the incidence rates can be decomposed into three distinct temporal effects, the age, period, and cohort effects (see details in Materials and Methods). After controlling for period and cohort effects, the risk of acquiring any of the 11 studied infectious diseases peaked in children and young adults (Fig. 2A). The highest risk of contracting infectious diarrhea (Fig. 2D), bacillary dysentery (Fig. 2G), hepatitis A (Fig. 2J), and AHC (Fig. 2K) occurred in individuals under 20 y old. The risk of hepatitis C (Fig. 2H) and hepatitis E (Fig. 2L) increased with age, while the risk of bloodborne and sexually transmitted infections, such as hepatitis B (Fig. 2C), syphilis (Fig. 2E), HIV/AIDS (Fig. 2F), and gonorrhea (Fig. 2I), all peaked at the most sexually active ages between 20 and 30. The age effect of PTB also peaked around the same age (Fig. 2B). Age effects stratified by sex showed similar patterns (SI Appendix, Fig. S11).

Period effects.

The period effects of all 11 infectious diseases combined first decreased then increased across the study period (Fig. 3A). Similar trends were observed for hepatitis B (Fig. 3C) and infectious diarrhea (Fig. 3D), while HIV/AIDS showed an opposite trend with an initial increase followed by a decrease (Fig. 3F). Over time, the period effects generally increased for syphilis (Fig. 3E), hepatitis C (Fig. 3H), and hepatitis E (Fig. 3L), decreased for PTB (Fig. 3B), bacillary dysentery (Fig. 3G), gonorrhea (Fig. 3I), and hepatitis A (Fig. 3J), while remaining stable for AHC (Fig. 3K). Notably, outbreaks can be observed for AHC in 2007 and 2010 (Fig. 3K). The period effects for several diseases, particularly chronic infections like syphilis, hepatitis C, and gonorrhea, exhibited slight declines in 2020, likely reflecting both decreased disease transmission due to nonpharmaceutical interventions and pandemic-related disruptions in healthcare access and disease reporting (27, 28). These trends remained consistent when stratified by sex (SI Appendix, Fig. S12).

Although the specific trends of the cohort effects varied with diseases, noticeable increases in disease risk were observed around the F1 and F2 cohorts compared to their adjacent birth cohorts (Fig. 4), especially for all studied diseases in aggregate (Fig. 4A), syphilis (Fig. 4E), and bacillary dysentery (Fig. 4G). These abrupt changes were observed in both sexes (SI Appendix, Fig. S13).

Impact of the Great Chinese Famine on Infectious Disease Risk in the Directly Affected Birth Cohort.

Through comparing the observed number of cases in the F1 cohort with its counterfactual values estimated with APC and GAMs (Materials and Methods), we observed a significant increase in the risk of contracting any of the analyzed diseases among the F1 cohort (Fig. 5A), with a famine-attributable incidence rate ratio (IRR) of 1.13 (95% CI: 1.04 to 1.21). When examining each individual disease, significantly higher F1 risks at the 95% confidence level were observed for almost all studied diseases (Fig. 5A). Diseases where the F1 IRR did not achieve 95% significance did exhibit weaker evidence of elevated risk, including hepatitis B (1.09, 95% CI: 0.98 to 1.22), gonorrhea (1.12, 95% CI: 0.96 to 1.26), and AHC (1.06, 95% CI: 0.91 to 1.23). The maximum F1 IRR was 1.21 (95% CI: 1.13 to 1.29) for bacillary dysentery. There were an estimated 11,860 famine-attributable excess cases (95% CI: 2,445 to 20,545) across the 11 infectious diseases among F1 (SI Appendix, Table S3). Since the differences between the IRRs of males and females were insignificant for all studied diseases (Fig. 5A), we did not stratify the prefecture-level analyses by sex. When stratified by each single birth year, we observed that birth cohorts 1957–1958, who experienced the famine as infants or young children, and the 1963 cohort, who were conceived in the last year of the famine when famine severity was substantially reduced, exhibited lower F1 IRRs than birth cohorts 1959–1962 (SI Appendix, Fig. S14), who were conceived and born during the famine. The 1961 birth cohort exhibited the greatest excess risk, consistent with their highest in utero exposure during the famine’s peak year of 1960 (SI Appendix, Fig. S14).

Using a meta-regression model (see details in Materials and Methods), a dose–response relationship was observed between famine intensity and the risk of acquiring infectious diseases in F1, i.e., F1 IRRs were higher in prefectures with higher migration-adjusted CSSI values (Fig. 5 B and C). When pooling all diseases together, a significant positive association (P < 0.001) between migration-adjusted CSSI and the logarithm of F1 IRR was observed, with an interquartile range increase in migration-adjusted CSSI associated with a 3.95% (95% CI: 1.87 to 6.05%) increase in F1 IRR (Fig. 5B). When stratifying by disease, this positive association persisted, although the estimated association was significant at the 95% level only for PTB and syphilis. The limited statistical power may be attributed to the small sample sizes of only 21 data points for the meta-regression models (Fig. 5C and SI Appendix, Table S4).

Intergenerational Effects of the Great Chinese Famine.

When analyzing all diseases at the provincial level, an IRR of 1.08 (95% CI: 1.00 to 1.15) was observed for the F2 cohort for all diseases in aggregate (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Increased IRRs were observed for almost all diseases studied except hepatitis B, syphilis, AHC, and hepatitis E (SI Appendix, Fig. S15A). However, unlike F1, significant dose–response relationships between prefecture-level famine intensity and infectious disease risks were not observed for F2.

Sensitivity Analysis.

IRRs estimated using the alternative model specifications (see details in Materials and Methods), which decomposed the cohort effects into a smooth trend over time and an abrupt change for the famine-related birth cohorts or using a cohort difference-in-difference model, were comparable with those estimated by the main model (SI Appendix, Text S4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S16). Furthermore, when using the birth cohorts unrelated to famine as negative controls, the 95% credible intervals for the estimated IRRs generally overlapped with the null, suggesting that increased infectious disease risks in famine-related birth cohorts identified under our method were unlikely to have been artifactual (SI Appendix, Fig. S17).

Discussion

While long-term effects of prenatal famine exposure on noncommunicable diseases have been extensively studied, its impact on infectious diseases remains less understood. Using surveillance data on notifiable infectious diseases in Sichuan Province during 2005–2022, we found that individuals born during the Great Chinese Famine had higher risk for a range of infectious diseases in adulthood compared to those in adjacent birth cohorts. The magnitude of excess infectious disease risk was positively associated with a measure of local famine severity. Elevated infectious disease risks appear to extend to the offspring of famine-exposed individuals.

Our findings align with previous studies on prenatal undernutrition that suggest a link with infectious disease risks later in life. For example, individuals born during the wet season (also known as the “hungry” season) in rural Gambia showed elevated levels of malaria and diarrheal disease later in life (29, 30). Similarly, our previous work has found significant associations between prenatal and early-life exposure to famine and an increased risk of tuberculosis across two consecutive generations (26). Prenatal undernutrition has also been associated with a reduced antibody response to typhoid vaccination in adolescents, suggesting a weaker immune response and an increased risk of infection (31). Several studies have found that prenatal or early-life undernutrition may lead to reduced CD4 cells in infants (32) and reduced thymus function in adolescents (33), suggesting early immunological alterations may persist throughout the life course. Notably, in contrast to our work, He et al. failed to identify an impact of prenatal exposure to famine on infectious disease risk in adulthood (34). However, their study exhibits two key limitations: 1) limited representativeness of case data (1,021 cases), as it relied on a sample that was exclusively drawn from disabled individuals; and 2) failure to account for the potential influence of age on disease risk, given that only the postfamine birth cohort was used as the control group.

Although we identified increased IRRs for certain diseases (hepatitis B, gonorrhea, and AHC), their 95% CI overlapped with the null, potentially because of substantial variability in age and period distribution of cases for these diseases (which introduced increased uncertainty in both Bayesian APC posterior estimates and GAM predictions). For example, the AHC outbreaks in 2007 and 2010 resulted in exceptionally large and uncertain period effect estimates from the Bayesian APC model, which propagated and resulted in wider CIs for the estimated IRR.

We found no evidence of dose-response relationships between the prefecture-level famine intensity and infectious disease risks in the F2 cohort. This could potentially reflect a true absence of dose-response relationships, could reflect a lack of statistical power to find such relationships, or a higher chance of exposure misclassification among F2. In addition to a degree of imprecision inherent in identifying the offspring of the famine-exposed generation by birth year, the F2 birth cohort, aged between their 20s to 40s during the study period, was more likely to study or work in a prefecture different from their birthplace (22, 35, 36). Moreover, although HIV/AIDS, hepatitis B, and syphilis can all be transmitted via mother-to-child transmission, their F1 and F2 IRRs exhibited heterogeneity. While all three diseases showed increased or marginally increased IRRs in F1, only HIV/AIDS demonstrated a marginally increased IRR in F2. This heterogeneity is likely due to a combination of independent acquisition of HIV/AIDS and syphilis in F2 and the hepatitis B vaccination program. The F2 cohort was born between 1978 and 1982, whereas the first locally acquired HIV case in Sichuan was not reported until 1991 (37). Syphilis had been effectively controlled through nationwide socioeconomic and public health campaigns, reaching near-elimination by the early 1960 s (38), with fewer than 1,000 cumulative cases reported nationwide between 1979 and 1987 (38, 39). Consequently, mother-to-child transmission was unlikely to be a major contributor to HIV/AIDS or syphilis incidence in F2. For hepatitis B, the introduction of the hepatitis B vaccine may have reduced the estimated IRR in F2 (SI Appendix, Table S5).

Our finding that intergenerational effects exist even for bloodborne and sexually transmitted infections suggests that the mechanisms driving these effects may extend beyond increased contact with the F1 generation. Increased susceptibility to infectious disease among F1 cohort members may be heritable; research in both human and animal studies shows that DNA methylation patterns may be altered by intrauterine nutrient supply and subsequently passed to the next generation (7, 9, 40). Additionally, offspring of parents exposed to adverse prenatal environments often face a range of socioeconomic (41, 42) and educational (43, 44) disadvantages, which may be linked to elevated risks of infectious diseases through crowded households, undernutrition, and poor sanitary conditions (45, 46).

When interpreting the findings of the current study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the lack of detailed individual-level data for assessing famine exposure history, we used year of birth to define the F1 and F2 cohorts and prefecture-level migration-adjusted CSSI to represent the severity of the famine exposure, which may lead to misclassification. However, these temporal misclassifications are nondifferential with respect to disease outcomes and are expected to bias our estimates toward the null. We employed an alternative approach to defining the F2 cohort by estimating the proportion of children born to F1 in each cohort group and observed strong agreement between the two methods (SI Appendix, Fig. S18). Second, we did not explicitly model the impacts of public health policy or environmental changes, but these were implicitly accounted for by the age-period-cohort model, as flexible categorical variables rather than parametric forms were used. Health interventions that affected all age groups simultaneously, such as the nationwide implementation of the directly observed treatment, short-course strategy (DOTS) program for pulmonary tuberculosis (47), were captured by the period effects (e.g., steady decline in the period effects of PTB after 2007, Fig. 3B) and were therefore canceled out in IRR calculations, as they were part of both observed and expected rates. Other cohort-specific interventions that did not affect F1 or F2 were also preserved in the counterfactual cohort effect estimates and thus did not bias the famine-related IRRs. However, health policy changes coinciding with F1 or F2 birth cohorts could, in principle, influence the famine IRRs, since any differences between observed and expected incidence would be attributed to famine. For example, hepatitis B vaccination likely contributed to an overestimation of the counterfactual cohort effects (dashed line in Fig. 4C) and underestimation of the F2 IRR. A summary of the main intervention programs for the included diseases and their potential impacts on F1 and F2 IRRs is provided in SI Appendix, Table S5. In brief, the F2 IRRs for hepatitis B and C may be slightly underestimated, whereas the effects on F1 and on other diseases in F2 are likely minimal. Third, we estimated expected incidence rates in the absence of famine based on both the prefamine and postfamine cohorts. Since individuals born right before the famine may have also suffered from undernutrition during their early childhood, this method may bias the true effects of the famine. However, we chose this approach to better control for infectious disease risks due to biological age differences between the exposed and unexposed cohorts. Fourth, we did not explicitly account for the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on infectious disease occurrence and reporting. However, as these impacts likely affected all birth cohorts equally and were captured by the period rather than cohort effects, they are unlikely to bias our results. This assumption is further supported by sensitivity analyses using only cases diagnosed between 2005 and 2019, which yielded F1 IRRs consistent with those estimated from the full dataset (SI Appendix, Fig. S19). Fifth, we were only able to stratify by prefectures, not counties, due to data availability. This resulted in small sample sizes in meta-regression models which may have limited our statistical power to estimate associations between famine severity and F1 and F2 IRRs. Sixth, our study is limited by its reliance on surveillance data from only one province. Further research is needed to assess the generalizability of these findings to other regions.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that prenatal famine exposure may elevate risk for a wide range of infectious diseases, and these adverse health consequences may extend to subsequent generations. Our study highlights the long-term and intergenerational impacts of large-scale societal shocks that may threaten food security on the risks of infectious diseases. In light of the global COVID-19 aftermath and widening global conflicts that may threaten food security and trigger famines, it is crucial for public health programs and international organizations to acknowledge these long-term consequences and develop strategies to mitigate the adverse health effects of famine that may persist for generations.

Materials and Methods

Disease Data.

We obtained the number of cases for 46 notifiable infectious diseases (SI Appendix, Text S1) reported in Sichuan Province during the period from 2005 to 2022 by age, sex, year of diagnosis, and prefecture of residence from the National Infectious Disease Reporting System (NIDRS), an extensive electronic surveillance system that gathers data from almost all healthcare facilities in China (23). Cases were diagnosed in accordance with case definitions adhering to national guidelines issued by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China (SI Appendix, Table S6).

To ensure sufficient statistical power, we restricted our analyses to diseases for which surveillance data had been collected for at least 5 y, for which at least 150 cases were reported per year, and to those not primarily affecting children (i.e., ≥25% of reported cases occurred in individuals ≥20 y old for all prefectures; Text S1). Since the exposed F1 and F2 cohorts during data collection were more than 20 y old during our study period 2005–2022—with F1 (born 1957–1963) aged 42 to 65 y and F2 (born 1979–1983) aged 22 to 43 y—their risk for infectious diseases that largely impact young children would not be reflected in the available data. To assess the robustness of the age and adult-case proportion thresholds, we performed sensitivity analyses by varying both the age threshold (≥18, ≥20, and ≥22 y) and the proportion of cases occurring above that threshold (≥20%, ≥25%, and ≥30%). Results showed that the set of included diseases remained consistent across all these scenarios (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). A total of 11 diseases met these criteria for our analysis: PTB, hepatitis B, infectious diarrhea, syphilis, HIV/AIDS, bacillary dysentery, hepatitis C, gonorrhea, hepatitis A, acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis (AHC), and hepatitis E (ordered by case count). Specific reasons for exclusion of the other 35 diseases are listed in SI Appendix, Text S1. All data used in this study were anonymized; therefore, ethical approval was not required, as it is exempt under Article 32 of the “Measures for Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Beings”, issued by the National Health Commission of China (48).

Population Data.

We imputed age-specific population sizes at the prefecture level during noncensus years using the population size, age-, and sex-specific death rates, age-specific fertility rates, and sex ratios at birth of the census years. Several methods, including linear interpolation, extrapolation, and forward or reverse cohort component methods, were applied to different birth cohorts and years based on the availability of their specific population data in recent censuses (SI Appendix, Text S2). We also used anonymous individual-level data covering approximately 0.5% of the total population from the 2010 census (35) to adjust for population migration when estimating the prefecture-level famine intensity.

Prefecture-Level Covariates.

We collected prefecture-level population density, GDP per capita, urbanization rate (defined as the share of the resident population living in urban areas) from the Sichuan Statistical Yearbook (2005–2022) (22) and vaccine coverage for Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG, for PTB) and hepatitis B from Sichuan Health Statistical Yearbook (2017–2018) (49), averaged across available years. These covariates were included in the meta-regression models to adjust for regional differences when examining the dose–response relationship between famine severity and increased risks of infectious diseases in the affected population. As historical data for these variables were unavailable, we assumed that the variations in these covariates are highly correlated with their historical values.

Definitions of F1 and F2 Cohorts.

In accordance with our previous work (26), we defined the F1 cohort as individuals born between 1958 and 1962, the famine period in Sichuan, and the F2 cohort as the possible offspring of the F1 cohort. The specific birth year range for the F2 cohort may vary among different prefectures due to differences in the distribution of age at reproduction across prefectures. For example, in 2010, the age groups with the highest fertility rate for Chengdu and Zigong prefectures were 25 to 29 and 20 to 24 y, respectively (35). If these recent differences in peak fertility age were present historically, the F2 cohort for Chengdu would be expected to be born later than the F2 cohort in Zigong. To define the F2 cohort in each prefecture and for Sichuan Province as a whole, we assumed that the F1 cohort was reduced by famine exposure but otherwise had the same average number of offspring and chance of migration as other cohorts and that therefore a noticeable reduction in population size among younger age groups should correspond to the typical birth window of the F2 cohort. The first assumption was supported by the age-specific fertility rates (ASFRs) of women aged 15 to 49 from multiple censuses or surveys (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). The ASFR curves for F1 and adjacent cohorts follow smooth trajectories, without abrupt deviations that would suggest markedly different fertility patterns. The second assumption was supported by the cohort-specific number of emigrants, which did not exhibit unusually high volumes among the defined F2 birth cohorts (SI Appendix, Fig. S21). Therefore, in each prefecture and the province as a whole, we identified the smallest 5-year birth cohort born no earlier than 1970 as the F2 cohort (SI Appendix, Fig. S21). The estimated birth year ranges for F2 for the entire province and for each prefecture ranged from 1976–1980 to 1982–1986 (SI Appendix, Table S7).

Famine Intensity.

We quantified crude famine intensity in each prefecture using the cohort size shrinkage index (CSSI) (11, 50, 51):[1]CSSIp,crude=Nnonfamine,p-Nfamine,pNnonfamine,p,

where Nnonfamine,p is the average birth cohort size of those born during the 5-year period immediately before the famine (1953–1957, prefamine cohort) and the 5-year period immediately after the famine (1963–1967, postfamine cohort) for the pth prefecture, and Nfamine,p is the mean cohort size of those born during the famine (1958–1962). We further adjusted the crude famine intensity to account for migration, recognizing that the residential population in each prefecture comprises individuals born in different prefectures (SI Appendix, Fig. S22). Accordingly, the migration-adjusted CSSI for a given prefecture was calculated as a weighted sum of the crude CSSIs from all contributing prefectures, with weights based on the proportion of the residential population registered in each prefecture, estimated from anonymized individual-level census data. Since birthplace information was unavailable, registered prefecture was used as a proxy. The migration-adjusted CSSI values were highly correlated with the original crude CSSIs with Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.997 (95% CI: 0.973 to 1.000) for F1 and 0.996 (95% CI: 0.967 to 1.000) for F2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S23).

Statistical Analysis.

Estimating age, period, and cohort effects.

Relative infection risks for specific birth cohorts were obtained by decomposing variations in incidence rates into age, period, and cohort effects (52, 53), which refer, respectively, to the differences in disease risk due to different biological ages (54), external factors such as policy interventions or altered diagnostic standards that affect all age groups within a given time period, and historical experiences such as being born during the famine years that affect specific birth cohorts. These effects can be estimated using age-period-cohort (APC) models following various approaches [e.g., Holford parameterization (55), intrinsic estimators (56), smoothing splines (57), or Bayesian hierarchical models (58)]. Among these, Bayesian hierarchical models can incorporate diverse temporal and spatial dependency structures in the data (59, 60). We fitted Bayesian hierarchical models at provincial and prefecture levels for each of the 11 diseases individually, as well as for the total incidence of all 11 diseases combined.

At the province level, the incidence rate of disease d for the ith age, jth period, and kth birth cohort group, λd,ijk, was decomposed as[2]logλd,ijk=μd+αd,i+πd,j+γd,k, Yd,ijk∼NegBinλd,ijkNijk,κd.

where μd is the overall mean incidence rate for disease d; αd,i denotes the ith age effect; πd,j denotes the jth period effect; γd,k denotes the kth cohort effect; Yd,ijk represents the number of cases of disease d for the ith age, jth period and kth birth cohort group; Nijk represents the population size; and κd represents the dispersion parameter of the negative binomial distribution for disease d. We modeled Yd,ijk using a negative binomial distribution to allow for potential overdispersion. The three temporal effects were modeled as first-order random walk (RW1) processes, which assume that the increments between consecutive age groups, periods, and birth cohorts follow zero-mean Gaussian distributions, to account for potential correlations between consecutive groups. Identical age, period, and cohort groups were used for each disease, except for certain diseases with very low incidence rates among young children, in which case age groups and cohorts corresponding to very young populations were aggregated (SI Appendix, Text S3).

At the prefecture level, the incidence rate for disease d for the ith age, jth period and kth birth cohort group in the pth prefecture, λd,ijk,p, was decomposed as[3]logλd,ijk,p=μd,p+αd,i,p+πd,j,p+γd,k,p, Yd,ijk,p∼NegBinλd,ijk,pNijk,p,κd’.

where μd,p,αd,i,p,πd,j,p, and γd,k,p represent the disease- and prefecture-specific intercept, age, period, and cohort effects, respectively. While within a specific prefecture pm, the three temporal effects αd,i,pm,πd,j,pm and γd,k,pm were modeled with the RW1 process, between prefectures, two different model specifications, either independent or spatially structured using a Besag model (61) were tested to account for the potential between-prefecture correlations of these three temporal effects, as well as the intercepts (SI Appendix, Fig. S24). The model with the lowest deviance information criterion (DIC) was retained for further analyses.

Estimating effects of famine on infectious disease risk.

We estimated counterfactual incidence rates in the absence of famine to determine famine-attributable incidence rate ratios (IRRs) for the F1 and F2 generations. After obtaining the cohort effects (solid lines in Fig. 4) via the model described above, we excluded data overlapping with the 1958–1962 famine period (dots in Fig. 4) and interpolated the expected cohort effects in the absence of famine using generalized additive models (GAMs) to infer risk ratios as smooth functions of the central birth year of each cohort (dashed lines in Fig. 4). This approach assumes that cohort effects of prenatal exposure to famine manifest as transient perturbances of otherwise smooth underlying trends in cohort effects caused by unmeasured temporal confounders. It further assumes that any other potential causes of such perturbances are negligible relative to the impacts of famine.

Counterfactual incidence rates in the absence of famine were estimated by summing the interpolated counterfactual cohort effects and the original estimates of the age and period effects (Eq. 2). Famine-attributable IRRs were calculated as the ratio of the observed incidence rates to the counterfactual incidence rates, and the numbers of cases attributable to famine were estimated as the difference between the observed case counts and the estimated counterfactual case counts. The 95% credible intervals (CIs) for these IRRs were obtained with Monte Carlo simulations. Specifically, we randomly drew 1000 sample parameter sets from the posterior estimates of the Bayesian APC models, then fit GAMs to each set as described above and drew 1000 random samples from the predicted distributions of the cohort effects to represent the uncertainty of the GAM predictions. These 1 million sets of predictions were used to estimate distributions of famine-attributable IRRs and obtain their median values, 2.5th, and 97.5th percentiles. To examine the critical developmental window of early-life famine exposure, we further estimated IRRs for each single birth cohort between 1957 and 1963, among which birth cohorts 1957–1958 experienced famine as infants or young children, cohorts 1959–1962 were conceived and born during the famine, and cohort 1963 was conceived in the last year of the famine, when severity was substantially reduced (20, 25), or after the famine. These single-year cohort estimates were derived using the same approach as above, except that counterfactual incidence rates and IRRs were calculated for each single-year birth cohort rather than for the combined 1958–1962 cohort.

Associations between famine intensity and risks of infectious diseases.

The varying famine intensity observed across different prefectures provides an opportunity to examine the association between famine and the risk of infectious diseases in a dose-response manner. After estimating prefecture-level IRRs, we fitted meta-regression models to them, allowing us to estimate associations of migration-adjusted CSSI with disease risk while accounting for uncertainty in IRR estimates across prefectures. To account for potential regional-specific confounding, we further adjusted for population density, GDP per capita, and urbanization rate. We first fitted a pooled model to estimate the common effect of migration-adjusted CSSI across all diseases as[4]logIRRd,p=α+β1Diseased+β2CSSIp+δdXp+εd,p,

where IRRd,p represents the estimated IRR of the dth disease for the pth prefecture, α is the intercept; Diseased is a categorical variable representing the 11 studied infectious diseases; β1 and β2 are coefficients on disease and migration-adjusted CSSI, respectively; Xp denotes the control variables, (i.e., population density, GDP per capita, and urbanization rate); and εd,p is the zero-mean normally distributed error term.

We then fitted a separate meta-regression model for each individual disease. For PTB and hepatitis B, we additionally adjusted for vaccination coverage:[5]logIRRd,p=αd+βdCSSIp+δdXp+εd,p,

where αd is the disease-specific intercept; βd is the disease-specific coefficient; Xp denotes the control variables (i.e., population density, GDP per capita, urbanization rate, and vaccination coverage for PTB and hepatitis B); and εd,p is the zero-mean normally distributed error term.

Sensitivity analysis.

To examine whether the elevated risk observed for the famine cohorts could have arisen due to chance, we used all other 5-year birth cohorts from 1945 to 1993 as negative controls and repeated the analyses after excluding each cohort one at a time. We also explored two alternative model specifications. In the first one, the cohort effects were modeled as arising from a smooth process, represented by a natural cubic spline on time, as well as fixed effects for famine-exposure years; and in the second one, we used a cohort difference-in-difference model to estimate the famine effects. More details about the alternative models can be found in SI Appendix, Text S4.
