. 1.
Introduction

In
[
2
]
the following result was proven:

Propositon 1
.

Given positive integers
d,k,s
, there exists
\tilde{r}=\tilde{r}(d,k,s)
with the following property. Whenever
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
are
k
homogeneous polynomials of the same degree

s
in
d
variables, no two of which are linearly dependent, then
p_{1}^{r},\ldots,p_{k}^{r}
are linearly independent if
r>\tilde{r}
.

The proof is elementary, however it is not entirely obvious how one would go
about removing the dependence of
\tilde{r}
on the degree
s
of the
polynomials. If
\tilde{r}
depends only on the number of polynomials and
variables considered,
[
2
]
show that the interpolation ability of
neural networks with monomial activation functions could be characterized explicitly
(we refer the read to their paper for more details). Thus, they were led to
conjecture the following.

Conjecture 2
.

In the setting of Proposition
1
,
\tilde{r}
may be taken
to depend only on
d
and
k
.

In this short paper, we show that the dependence of
\tilde{r}
on the degree

s
of the polynomials
and
on the number
d
of variables can be
removed. Moreover, it also turns out that the restriction to homogeneous
polynomials can be dropped as well. Before we state our result, we fix
\mathbb{F}
a field of
characteristic
0
. With this, we prove the following result.

Theorem 3
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
be positive integers and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots x_{d}]
non-zero, pairwise linearly independent
polynomials. Then the
r
-th powers
p_{1}^{r},\ldots,p_{k}^{r}
are linearly
independent for any
r>\max\left\{k\binom{k-1}{2},2\right\}
.

Before we present our proof, we mention that
[
4
]
already investigated
this question for powers of elements of integral algebras over algebraically
closed fields in arbitrary characteristic. They found out that given any
k

non-zero elements, there exists some integer
1\leq r\leq k!
such that the

r
-th powers are linearly independent over the base field. The earliest result
we have been able to find related to this problem,
[
1
]
*Theorem 6.2, says
that for
k
polynomials satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
3
,
then their
r
-th powers are linearly dependent for at most
\binom{k-1}{2}
values of
r
, however no general upper bound is given. For
applications of this result to the setting of polynomial neural networks, we
refer the reader to
[
2
]
which first stated the conjecture and to

[
3
]
where a number of (counter-)examples are given.

2.
Proof of Theorem
3

We start by showing that it suffices to consider the univariate case with
relatively prime polynomials over an algebraically closed field. The last two
are easy to see: 1) we can always factor out the greatest common divisor
d

from the polynomials
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
since they are linearly (in)dependent
if and only if their relatively prime factors
p_{1}/d,\ldots,p_{k}/d
are
linearly (in)dependent as well; 2) we can assume that
\mathbb{F}
is algebraically
closed since a counterexample to Theorem
3
when
\mathbb{F}
is not
algebraically closed will certainly carry over when we consider the polynomials
with coefficients in the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}
. The reduction to the
univariate case is harder, and we show how to do it in Section
2.2
.

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
3
for
d=1

We will be using the generalized Mason’s inequality.

Propositon 4

(Generalized Mason’s Inequality
[
5
]
*Theorem
1.3.1)
.

Suppose
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
are polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[x]
such that

(1)

p_{1}+\ldots+p_{k}=0
,

(2)

p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
span a vector space of dimension
k-1

over
\mathbb{F}
,

(3)

p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
have no common zero in
\mathbb{F}
.

Then

\max\{\deg p_{i}\}\leq\binom{k-1}{2}(n_{0}(p_{1}\ldots p_{k})-1).

Here
n_{0}(p_{1}\ldots p_{k})
denotes the radical of
p_{1}\ldots p_{k}
, i.e the
number of distinct roots of the polynomial. Now we can prove our main
theorem.

Theorem 5
.

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{F}[x]
be non-zero,
pairwise linearly independent, relatively prime polynomials. For any
r>\max\left\{k\binom{k-1}{2},2\right\}
,
p_{1}^{r},\ldots,p_{k}^{r}
are linearly
independent.

Proof.

We prove the theorem by induction on
k
. For
k=2
, if one polynomial is a
constant, the assertion follows directly. If both are non-constant, then
assuming their
r
-th powers are linearly dependent, we get that
p_{2}^{r}=\alpha p_{1}^{r}
for some
\alpha\neq 0\in\mathbb{F}
. Hence, they are not
relatively prime; contradiction.

Assume now that
k>2
and that the theorem holds for any
2\leq k^{\prime}<k
.
Suppose that
p_{1}^{r},\ldots,p_{k}^{r}
are linearly dependent for some
r
.
This means that there exist
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{F}
not all zero
such that

\beta_{1}p_{1}^{r}+\ldots+\beta_{k}p_{k}^{r}=0.

We consider two cases:

(1)

q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}
span a vector space of dimension
k-1
, where

q_{i}=\beta_{i}p_{i}^{r}
. We can use
Proposition
4
to bound the degree of the polynomials
(the
q_{i}
’s have no common zeroes since we assumed the
p_{i}
’s
relatively prime). Thus, we get for any
i

\deg q_{i}\leq\binom{k-1}{2}(n_{0}(p_{1}^{r}\ldots p_{k}^{r})-1).

We also have that
n_{0}(p_{1}^{r}\ldots p_{k}^{r})=n_{0}(p_{1}\ldots p_{k})\leq\deg(p_{1}\ldots p_{k})
. Moreover,
\deg q_{1}+\ldots+\deg q_{k}=r\deg(p_{1}\ldots p_{k})
. Hence, summing the inequalities for all
i
’s, we
get

r\deg(p_{1}\ldots p_{k})\leq k\binom{k-1}{2}(\deg(p_{1}\ldots p_{k})-1).

Thus, we see that
r<k\binom{k-1}{2}
.

(2)

If some proper subset of
p_{1}^{r},\ldots,p_{k}^{r}
is linearly
dependent, we will again show that
r<k\binom{k-1}{2}
using the induction hypothesis. Without loss of generality,
assume that
p_{1}^{r},\ldots,p_{n}^{r}
with
2\leq n<k
are
linearly dependent. We can’t directly apply the inductive hypothesis
since
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
may have a common factor. If they don’t,
use the induction hypothesis to show the bound on
r
. If they do,
denote it by
q
. Define
q_{i}=p_{i}/q
. The
q_{i}
’s are
relatively prime, non-zero, and pairwise independent (dividing by a
common factor does not change linear (in)dependence). Apply the
inductive hypothesis on the
q_{i}
’s to get the bound on
r
.

∎

2.2.
Reduction to
d=1
case

The idea is to find a suitable variable
x_{i}
and constants
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
such that evaluating the polynomials

p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
at this tuple leads to
non-zero, pairwise linearly independent
univariate
polynomials
\bar{p}_{1},\ldots,\bar{p}_{k^{\prime}}
. Then, a counterexample to
Theorem
3
with
d>1
, also provides a counterexample when
d=1
, by substitution. We start with a lemma showing that such constants can be found
if
all
polynomials
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
share a common variable.

Lemma 6
.

Let
d,k>0
be positive integers. Given any
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
pairwise linearly independent with
\deg_{x_{1}}p_{i}>0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,
there exists a point
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{F}^{d-1}
such that

\bar{p}_{1}(x_{1})=p_{1}(x_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d}),\ldots,\bar{p}_{k}(x_{1})=p_{k}(x_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
are also pairwise linearly independent.

Proof.

We look at the set of points
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{F}^{d-1}
such
that some pair
\bar{p}_{i},\bar{p}_{j}
is linearly dependent, and we call it
V
. We show that
V
is a variety of dimension
\dim V<k-1
. Since we
assumed
\mathbb{F}
to be of characteristic
0
, it is infinite, hence
it cannot happen that
V=\mathbb{F}^{d-1}
. Thus, we can always find our desired
point.

Take any two polynomials
p_{i},p_{j}
with
i\neq j
. We can then write them as

\displaystyle p_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

\displaystyle=p_{i,m}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})x_{1}^{m}+\ldots+p_{i,0}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d}),

\displaystyle p_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

\displaystyle=p_{j,n}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})x_{1}^{n}+\ldots+p_{j,0}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d}),

and we know that
p_{i,m}
and
p_{j,n}
are not zero. Let
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k})\in\mathbb{F}^{k-1}
and consider three cases:

(1)

\bar{p}_{i}=p_{i}
and
\bar{p}_{j}=p_{j}
, whence they are linearly
independent by assumption.

(2)

\bar{p}_{i}=p_{i}
but
\bar{p}_{j}\neq p_{j}
. Then
p_{i,l}
are
constants for all
l\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Assuming
\bar{p}_{i}
and
\bar{p}_{j}
are linearly dependent we have that either:

(a)

\bar{p}_{j}=0
in which case
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
must
be a solution to the linear system of equations
p_{j,l}=0
for
l\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, i.e.
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in Z(p_{j,1},\ldots,p_{j,n})
. The ideal
(p_{j,1},\ldots,p_{j,n})
cannot be empty since at least one
p_{j,l}
is
non-constant (we assumed
\bar{p}_{j}\neq p_{j}
).

(b)

\bar{p}_{j}\neq 0
in which case
\bar{p}_{j}=\beta\bar{p}_{i}
for some non-zero
\beta\in\mathbb{F}
. Then
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in Z(p_{j,1}-\beta p_{i,1},\ldots,p_{j,n}-\beta p_{i,n})
, which is non-empty.

(3)

\bar{p}_{i}\neq p_{i}
and
\bar{p}_{j}\neq p_{j}
. We have another
two cases:

(a)

\bar{p}_{i}=0
or
\bar{p}_{j}=0
. In this case
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in Z(p_{i,1},\ldots,p_{i,m})\cup Z(p_{j,1},\ldots,p_{j,n})
.

(b)

\bar{p}_{j},\bar{p}_{i}\neq 0
. If they are linearly
dependent, we have
\bar{p}_{j}=\beta\bar{p}_{i}
for some
non-zero
\beta\in\mathbb{F}
. This means that
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in Z(p_{j,1}-\beta p_{i,1},\ldots,p_{j,\max(n,m)}-\beta p_{i,\max(n,m)})
.

In either case, we see that if
\bar{p}_{i}
and
\bar{p}_{j}
are linearly
dependent, then
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
must lie in a variety of
dimension strictly smaller than
d-1
, which we call
V_{i,j}
.

Proceeding similarly for every pair of polynomials, we see that some of the
resulting polynomials will be pairwise linearly dependent only if
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in\bigcup_{i<j}V_{i,j}=V
.
V
is the
finite union of varieties whose dimension is smaller than
d-1
, hence it
is also a variety of dimensions strictly smaller than
d-1
.
∎

Using this lemma, we can now show that Theorem
3
holds for
d>1
as well. Assume Theorem
3
is true for
d=1
and take any
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
non-zero, pairwise linearly independent polynomials such
that their
r
-th powers are linearly dependent for some
r>\max\left\{k\binom{k-1}{2},2\right\}
, i.e. there exist
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{F}
not all zero such that

(2.1)

\beta_{1}p_{1}^{r}+\ldots+\beta_{k}p_{k}^{r}=0.

Consider the sets
S_{i}=\{p_{j}|\deg_{x_{i}}p_{j}\neq 0\}
, i.e. each
S_{i}

contains the polynomials that depend non-trivially on the
i
-th variable. If
the number of elements in each set
|S_{i}|=1
for all
i
, then
Equation (
2.1
) cannot hold since all polynomials are
univariate, with at most one of them being a constant (because of pairwise
linear independence). Hence, there is some
i
such that
|S_{i}|=k^{\prime}>1
.
Without loss of generality, assume
i=1
. Relabel the polynomials in
S_{1}

to
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k^{\prime}}
. Using Lemma
6
on
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k^{\prime}}
, we can find
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
such that
\bar{p}_{1},\ldots,\bar{p}_{k^{\prime}}
are pairwise linearly independent. If
k^{\prime}=k
,
substituting
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
in
Equation
2.1
, we get a contradiction. If
k>k^{\prime}>1
,
evaluating
r
-th powers polynomials
not
in
S_{1}
at
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
and summing the result leads to a constant
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}
. If
\gamma^{\prime}=0
, we are done. Otherwise, take
\gamma
equal to any
r
-th root
of
\gamma^{\prime}
. We have that
\bar{p}_{1},\ldots,\bar{p}_{k^{\prime}},\gamma
are
non-zero, pairwise linearly independent polynomials. Substitution of
(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
in Equation
2.1
leads, again,
to a contradiction.
