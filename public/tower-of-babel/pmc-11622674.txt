Cross-cultural adaptation and validation of the Arabic version of the Stay Independent Brochure as part of the CDC’s STEADI initiative among community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Maha Almarwani, Bashaier Alosaimi, Jennifer L Vincenzo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Stay Independent Brochure (SIB) is part of the CDC’s STEADI initiative and is a fall risk screening self-report tool. This study aimed to translate and cross-culturally adapt the Arabic version of the SIB (AR-SIB) and evaluate its psychometric properties among community-dwelling older adults.MethodsThe translation and cross-cultural adaptation process followed standard guidelines, including forward and backward translation, expert committee review, and pretesting to ensure semantic and conceptual equivalence, clarity, and cultural relevance of the Arabic version of the SIB. Internal consistency was assessed using the Kuder-Richardson formula (KR-20), and test–retest reliability was evaluated with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC2,1). Convergent validity of the AR-SIB was evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (rs) with the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test and the Berg Balance Scale (BBS) and point-biserial correlation coefficients (rpb) with the three key questions. Floor and ceiling effects and the operating characteristic (ROC) curve were also calculated.ResultsA total of 104 community-dwelling older adults participated in the study. The majority of participants were female (52.9%) and had a mean age of 63.77 ± 4.74 years. The AR-SIB demonstrated good internal consistency with an overall KR-20 of 0.73 and excellent test–retest reliability (ICC2,1 = 0.96). The AR-SIB showed moderate correlations with the TUG (rs = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.35 to 0.64) and the BBS (rs = -0.56, 95% CI: -0.69 to -0.41), and a high correlation with the three key questions (rpb = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.65 to 0.82). No floor or ceiling effects were observed. The cutoff point of the AR-SIB was determined to be 4.5.ConclusionsThe AR-SIB is a reliable and valid tool to discriminate falls and screen for fall risk among Arabic-speaking community-dwelling older adults. The AR-SIB can facilitate the implementation of evidence-based fall prevention initiatives tailored to Arabic-speaking older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05592-z.Keywords: Fall prevention, Fall risk, Psychometrics, Screening tool, Translation

Background

Globally, falls among community-dwelling older adults aged 60 and above represent a significant public health challenge, with an annual incidence ranging from 20 to 45% [1–3]. Falls and fall-related injuries constitute a critical and rising public health problem, leading to morbidity and mortality among older adults [4, 5]. The economic burden of falls for older adults is substantial, with an estimated cost to reach $101 billion by 2030 [6]. In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of falls is alarming, with reported rates ranging from 31.6% to 57.7% [7, 8]. Despite the high prevalence of falls among older adults in Saudi Arabia, the lack of comprehensive national data on falls is a critical limitation that hinders our understanding of the extent and nature of this public health issue. This limitation may also impede the implementation of evidence-based fall prevention programs.

The American Geriatrics Society, the British Geriatrics Society [9], and the World Falls Guidelines [10] have established clinical practice guidelines for preventing falls in older adults. Emphasizing evidence-based approaches, these guidelines underscore the importance of fall risk screening, assessment, and interventions to prevent and reduce falls. To facilitate the integration of fall risk prevention into routine healthcare practices, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) launched a national initiative called Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths, and Injuries (STEADI) [11]. The STEADI Initiative is composed of three key components: screening, assessing, and intervening to prevent falls among older adults [12]. Screening is a critical first step, enabling healthcare providers to efficiently identify individuals at increased risk of falling, laying the groundwork for multifactorial fall-risk assessments and tailored interventions [10]. There is currently no standard for fall-risk screening, and clinicians often use various tools to identify individuals at risk of falling as part of their fall prevention strategies [13, 14].

The Stay Independent Brochure (SIB), as part of the CDC’s STEADI initiative, is a self-report screening tool to identify older adults who would benefit from a multifactorial fall-risk assessment [12]. Other commonly used fall-risk screening tools, including the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), and the Five Times Sit-to-Stand (5TSTS) test, are representative examples of assessments that evaluate functional mobility and balance, offering valuable insights into physical performance [15–17]. However, the SIB stands out as a valuable tool due to its integration within the STEADI initiative and its clinical applicability. It offers a simple approach that older adults can complete independently. The SIB is adaptable to both clinical and community settings, where quick and effective screening is essential—an aspect often underrepresented in clinical guidelines [18]. Furthermore, recent evidence supports the use of STEADI screening tools for predicting fall risk, demonstrating their practicality and simplicity over more complex physical assessments, such as ultrasound measurements or physical performance tests among older adults [19].

The SIB has been culturally translated and validated from English to Thai and Portuguese [20, 21]. Recognizing the potential importance of toolkits in integrating best practices into routine care and implementing evidence-based interventions for fall prevention [22], it is imperative to ensure the availability of a translated and culturally adapted fall screening tool in multiple languages, including Arabic. However, limited research has been conducted on falls and fall-related sequelae among community-dwelling older adults in Saudi Arabia. This research gap highlights the need for the translation and cross-cultural adaptation of fall screening tools like the SIB.

Therefore, the present study aims to translate and cross-culturally adapt the SIB into Arabic and evaluate its psychometric properties, including reliability and validity, among community-dwelling older adults. This strategic effort is to align evidence-based practices with the unique sociocultural nuances of Saudi Arabia. The promotion of healthy aging is a goal of Saudi Vision 2030 [23]. Thus, the successful adaptation and validation of the SIB tool can bridge the existing research gap and lay the groundwork to empower healthcare professionals with a culturally sensitive fall-risk screening tool and relevant approach to fall prevention initiatives like STEADI among community-dwelling older adults in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Design and participants

This cross-sectional study adhered to international guidelines for translation, cultural adaptation, and evaluation of content validity for psychometric tests as outlined in the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) [24]. This study was conducted between January 2023 and July 2023. Participants were recruited from the King Salman Social Center in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Eligible participants were 60 years of age or older and were able to walk independently, with or without an assistive device. Exclusion criteria included the presence of uncompensated hearing or visual impairments, cognitive impairments indicated by an inability to discuss the study content after reviewing the consent form.

This study was approved by the College of Medicine Institutional Review Board at King Saud University (IRB #E-22–7306). All participants signed informed consent forms prior to participation.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was estimated in accordance with the Consensus-Based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) reporting guidelines for studies on measurement properties, as a minimum number of 100 subjects is needed to obtain sufficient statistical power to assess internal consistency and validity [24]. For test–retest reliability analysis, a minimum sample size of 50 participants has been suggested [25].

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the Stay Independent Brochure

The Stay Independent Brochure (SIB)

It includes a 12-item self-report fall-risk checklist, designed for easy self-administration by older adults or healthcare professionals. Each checklist item is accompanied by an explanatory statement outlining its significance. Responses of “yes” or “no” on the checklist are assigned numerical values to be tallied. Each item has a score of 0–1 or 0–2, depending on the item assessed. The cut-off point of the SIB is 4 points, which indicates that the individual is at increased risk of falling, and a score of three or less indicates low risk [12]. The SIB demonstrates a sensitivity of 73–80% in discriminating fallers and predicting future falls among community-dwelling older adults in the United States [26].

Translational and adaptation processes

Approval for this translation project was obtained with the permission of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the copyright holder. The original SIB, along with its testing guidelines, was translated from English into Arabic and culturally adapted according to Beaton's guidelines [27] in the following steps: forward translation, synthesis of the translation, backward translation, expert committee, and pretesting. The translation and cultural adaptation of the SIB was done in collaboration with certified healthcare professionals and professional translators. All translators were fluent in both English and Arabic. The steps involved in this process are presented in (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the Stay Independent Brochure (SIB) into Arabic

Step I- Forward Translation

Two bilingual translators (Arabic native speakers) conducted forward translations of the English SIB into Arabic, resulting in two independent Arabic translations. One translator was a physical therapist with 12 years of experience and familiar with the construct of the questionnaire, and the second translator was a professional translator without a medical background.

Step II—Synthesis of the Translation

The two versions of the forward translation were synthesized through a comparison and reconciliation process by a reconciliation group consisting of two translators and three bilingual physical therapists, resulting in the reconciled Arabic version.

Step III- Back Translation

The reconciled Arabic version was translated back into English by two additional independent bilingual translators who were not aware of the original English version of the SIB. Two independent English translations were produced.

Step IV- Expert Committee

All written reports were reviewed by an expert committee comprising ten multidisciplinary healthcare professionals, including geriatricians, physical therapists, occupational therapists, nurses, and health educators who had experience in the area of fall prevention for older adults. This evaluation aimed to assess semantic and conceptual equivalences as well as the clarity of the Arabic version of the SIB (AR-SIB) using the content validity index (CVI). To evaluate the relevance of the AR-SIB, using a paper-based form, the expert committee rated each item of the scale using an ordinal scale from 1 (not relevant) to 4 (highly relevant and clear) [28]. A cut-off score of ≤ 2 for an item was defined as being irrelevant. Comprehensiveness was evaluated by asking the raters to list any shortcomings of the AR-SIB. The clarity of each item was subjectively evaluated using a binary scale (clear or unclear).

Step V- Pretesting

The pre-approval version of the AR-SIB underwent pretesting, administered to a subsample of community-dwelling older adults (n = 25) by the research coordinator, to assess its clarity, comprehensibility, and face validity using a paper-based form. The feedback received from participants was then discussed by the expert committee. Following pretesting, the final Arabic version of the SIB was produced for testing.

Overall study process

Participants attended one session for the initial assessment, during which demographic information such as age, gender, and health status was collected. During the same session, the Stay Independent Brochure (SIB) and additional fall-risk assessments, including the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), and the three key questions of the SIB, were administered. These assessments evaluated functional mobility, balance, and self-reported fall risk. A subset of participants (n = 50) was invited to attend a second session, scheduled one week later, to complete the SIB again for the test–retest reliability assessment. All assessments in both sessions were conducted by trained research personnel in a controlled environment, ensuring standardized administration.

Additional fall risk screening tools and assessments

Three key questions

The three “key questions” are a subset of the full STEADI SIB and are described by the CDC as follows: ‘I have fallen in the past year,’ ‘Sometimes I feel unsteady when I’m walking,’ ‘I am worried about falling’. An individual is identified as being at risk of falls if they answer yes to any of the three questions [12].

Timed Up and Go (TUG)

The Timed Up and Go (TUG) assesses functional mobility and dynamic balance. Participants perform the TUG by getting up from a chair, walking 3 m at a comfortable speed, turning around, walking back to the chair, and sitting down. The time in seconds was recorded from when the examiner verbalized “go” and when the participant’s back touched the backrest of the chair. The average of two experimental trials was used in the analysis [15]. A TUG score of ≥ 12 s identifies older adults as at higher risk of falls [17, 29].

Berg Balance Scale (BBS)

The Berg Balance Scale (BBS) evaluates functional balance through 14 tasks, including sitting to standing, standing unsupported, sitting unsupported, standing to sitting, transfers, standing with eyes closed, standing with feet together, reaching forward with an outstretched arm, retrieving an object from the floor, turning to look behind, turning 360 degrees, placing the foot alternately on a step, standing with one foot in front, and standing on one foot. Scores range from 0 (unable to perform) to 4 (normal performance) for each task, with a maximum total score of 56 indicating normal balance. A total score below 45 suggests a greater risk of falling for older adults [16].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS® version 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. Internal consistency was assessed by the item-total correlations and the Kuder-Richardson formula (KR-20), as the data were dichotomous. Values above 0.7 were considered acceptable [30]. Test–retest reliability was assessed by the intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs) (2, 1 model) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The scores were obtained in the initial test and at the 1-week follow-up. ICCs were interpreted as follows: values between 0.5 and 0.75 indicate moderate reliability; values between 0.75 and 0.9 indicate good reliability; and values above 0.9 indicate excellent reliability [31]. Agreement between the scores of the repeated measures of AR-SIB was evaluated using Bland–Altman methods with a scatter plot of differences and 95% limits of agreement (mean change between scores) [32].

Floor and ceiling effects were presented as the frequency of the lowest (0) or the highest (14) possible score achieved by participants, respectively. If more than 15% of the participants scored the lowest or highest value, a floor or ceiling effect was considered [33].

Given that the data were not normally distributed, as confirmed by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, convergent validity was evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (rs) between the AR-SIB and continuous fall risk assessment measures, including the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test and the Berg Balance Scale (BBS). Additionally, point-biserial correlation coefficients (rpb) were used to assess the relationship between the AR-SIB and the three key questions of the SIB. These measures were selected due to their established reliability and validity in assessing fall risk among older adults [14, 26]. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine specificity and sensitivity. The cutoff score was determined by the maximum value of the Youden index (calculated as sensitivity + specificity-1) [34]. The level of statistical significance was set at α < 0.05.

Results

Participants

A total of 104 community-dwelling older adults (52.9% female, mean age ± SD: 63.77 ± 4.74 years) participated in the study. Approximately 22.1% reported a history of at least one previous fall. Translation and cross-cultural adaptation

The translation and cultural adaptation of the AR-SIB were successfully completed. The translation process of the questionnaires revealed no major problems. Minor linguistic inconsistencies in items 1, 2, 7, and 9 were detected during forward and backward translation and identified and assessed through language comparability and interpretability similarity. A consensus among expert committee members was reached to address these discrepancies.

Face and content validity

The expert committee reviewed all documents of translation with an excellent scale content validity index average of 0.98, a scale content validity index universal of 0.85, and an item-content validity index ranging from 0.90 to 1. The 25 community-dwelling older adults recruited for testing the pre-approval version of the AR-SIB indicated via interview that there are no issues with either the content or structure of the AR-SIB; however, there was a slight modification to the verbatim translation. For example, in item 6, the word “floor” was added to specify standing up from either a chair or floor since sitting on the floor is culturally common [35]. The finalized version of the AR-SIB can be found in Appendix A.

Reliability

The internal consistency of the AR-SIB was good, with an overall KR-20 of 0.73. Item-total correlations ranged from 0.08 to 0.64 (Table 2). A total of fifty participants took part in the test–retest reliability of the AR-SIB within a one-week interval, which demonstrated excellent reliability (ICC2,1 = 0.96, 95% CI = 0.93 to 0.97) (Table 3). No significant differences were observed between week 1 and week 2 in the AR-SIB total score. Examination of the Bland and Altman plot resulted in no systematic bias, or proportional error. The mean difference (SD) of 0.20 (0.12) was plotted near the zero line, and the t score was not statistically significant (1.20, p = 0.23) (Fig. 3). Furthermore, there were no reported changes in the stability of the condition among older adults, as assessed using a global rating of change scale [36].

Convergent validity and floor and ceiling effects

In terms of convergent validity, the AR-SIB had a moderate positive correlation with the TUG (rs = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.35 to 0.64), a moderate negative correlation with the BBS (rs = -0.56, 95% CI: -0.69 to -0.41), and a high correlation with the three key questions (rpb = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.65 to 0.82) (Table 4). No floor or ceiling effects were found in administrations of the AR-SIB score.

Sensitivity and specificity

The AR-SIB showed good accuracy with an AUC of 0.83 (95% CI = 0.75–0.92). The cutoff point of 4.5 was based on 0.58 of the Youden index to discriminate among older adults with and without a history of falls at a sensitivity and specificity of 0.91 and 0.67, respectively (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Our study is the first to translate and cross-culturally adapt, validate, and identify the psychometric properties of the AR-SIB. The Stay Independent Brochure was successfully translated into Arabic through a rigorous standardized translation method that included forward and backward translation, an expert committee, and pretesting. We ensured the integrity of the adaptation process, tailored to Saudi culture. Participants found questionnaires easy to understand and answer, and the time to answer was short. During the pretesting step, some participants commented on item 6, 'I need to push with my hands to stand up from a chair'. They noted that they mainly sit on the floor, reflecting a cultural practice that remains prevalent in some Saudi households. Decades ago, floor-level seating furniture was common in many homes in Saudi Arabia, and this traditional design continues to be maintained in some households today [35]. As a result, we added the word “floor’ to item 6. While several participants highlighted this cultural context, the majority demonstrated a clear understanding of the item. However, it's essential to acknowledge that adding 'floor' to this item may introduce a 'double-barreled' question, where respondents are asked about two distinct abilities: standing up from a chair and standing up from the floor. Moreover, functional floor stand tests and chair stand tests are distinct assessments in clinical evaluations [37, 38], indicating the need for further validation of this modified item. Future research should explore the reliability and validity of this modification to ensure its effectiveness in capturing participants' functional abilities in Saudi cultural contexts.

The main outcomes of our study suggest that the Arabic version of the SIB (AR-SIB) demonstrates good internal consistency, excellent test–retest reliability, and adequate content validity for assessing the risk of falls among community-dwelling older adults. The KR-20 of the scale of 0.73 indicates acceptable internal consistency for the AR-SIB, with no significant change following the deletion of any item. The overall test–retest reliability of the AR-SIB was excellent, which is comparable to previous versions, including the original English, Thai, and Portuguese versions [20, 21, 39].

In terms of convergent validity, the AR-SIB demonstrated a high correlation with the three key questions of the SIB, as well as a moderate positive correlation with the TUG test and a moderate negative correlation with the BBS. These findings were consistent with previous research on the Thai version of the SIB [20]. Notably, these correlations are contingent on TUG and BBS cutoff values, which vary in the literature [17]. The three key questions of the SIB offer an effective and efficient screening tool, reducing screening burden for both patients and healthcare providers [40]. While simplifying the SIB may maximize ease of use, Eckstrom et al. (2017) also argue that the full SIB enables healthcare providers to identify specific fall risks by examining the responses to individual questions that may require additional follow-up. Therefore, further evaluation of both the Arabic full and briefer versions of the Stay Independent Brochure would be valuable to inform clinical practice effectively.

Floor and ceiling effects are critical measurement properties that can significantly affect the validity, reliability, and responsiveness of health questionnaires [25]. The AR-SIB did not present floor or ceiling effects. The cutoff point for the AR-SIB was 4.5, slightly higher than the CDC’s proposed cutoff of 4 out of 14 possible points [12]. These discrepancies could be attributed to the addition of the floor transfer item to the questionnaire, the use of different statistical methods to estimate cutoff values, or specific differences in the characteristics of Saudi community-dwelling older adults [27, 41, 42]. This cutoff value should be viewed as preliminary, emphasizing the need for further research to validate it across a broader range of settings and larger sample sizes. Additionally, the cutoff values for other outcome measures used in this study were derived from Western studies. These values may not fully account for the specific cultural, environmental, and lifestyle factors influencing fall risk in other populations, including those in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, these cutoff values should be interpreted with caution.

The current study found that 52% of community-dwelling older adults were identified as being at high risk of falls based on their score on the AR-SIB. Additionally, the study demonstrated that the AR-SIB total scores were higher in females and in older adults with a fall history. This study contributes additional evidence, emphasizing the common occurrence of fall risk among older adults in Saudi Arabia. The high prevalence of fall risk observed in our study supports the need for accessible, culturally adapted, and validated fall risk assessment tools. They enable proactive screening of fall risk for older adults in both clinical and community settings [43, 44]. Early identification of fall risk could decrease the subsequent morbidity and mortality rates, as well as reduce the financial burdens on healthcare and social services.

Some limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Participants were recruited from a district community center, and self-selected to participate, which may represent a group of relatively active older adults. This could limit the generalizability of our findings to more vulnerable populations. Therefore, future research should examine the psychometric properties of the AR-SIB across more diverse older populations in Saudi Arabia, including clinical settings. Although our study revealed satisfactory results for the psychometric properties evaluated, other aspects require further investigation. Specifically, future studies are needed to evaluate the structural validity of the AR-SIB with a larger sample size to examine its factor structure and dimensionality.

Conclusion

The Arabic version of the Stay Independent Brochure is a reliable and valid tool for discriminating fall history and risk among Arabic-speaking community-dwelling older adults. The AR-SIB can be easily used to screen for fall risk and could facilitate the implementation of evidence-based fall prevention initiatives among Arabic-speaking community-dwelling older adults.
