Performance and incremental value of preoperative multimodal imaging and perioperative clinical characteristics in predicting postoperative major adverse events in type A aortic dissection
Authors: Qing-Tao Wang, Qiang-Qiang You, Fa Shu, Jian Liang, Bing Wang, Bo-Yu Yan, Jun-Ming Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative major adverse events (MAEs) can occur after surgical repair of acute Stanford type A aortic dissection (ATAAD), and conventional clinical assessment may not fully capture the prognostic information provided by preoperative multimodal imaging. This study aimed to develop and externally validate a staged risk prediction model for postoperative MAEs and to evaluate the incremental value of preoperative multimodal imaging relative to conventional clinical and laboratory assessment in the prediction of MAEs.MethodsIn this retrospective, two-center cohort study, patients with surgically treated ATAAD were screened between April 2016 and April 2026. After application of predefined exclusion criteria, 1,960 patients were included, comprising a development cohort from Center I (n=1,498) and an external validation cohort from Center II (n=462). The development cohort was further randomly divided into a training set (n=1,048) and an internal validation set (n=450). Preoperative multimodal imaging and perioperative clinical data were collected, including computed tomography angiography (CTA), transthoracic/transesophageal echocardiography (TTE/TEE), admission laboratory indices, disease-severity indicators, and surgery-related variables. Candidate predictors were screened via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and multivariable logistic regression. Five progressively expanded models were constructed to assess the incremental value of Penn classification, echocardiography, CTA, and intraoperative variables. Model performance was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic analysis, calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsIn the development cohort, postoperative MAEs occurred in 375 patients and did not occur in 1,123 patients. LASSO-based multivariable analysis identified age, lactate level, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), Penn classification, severe true lumen compression, mesenteric artery involvement, and cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time as the core predictors for incorporation into the final nomogram. Model discrimination improved progressively with staged expansion. In the training set, the areas under the curve of models 1–5 were 0.725, 0.768, 0.782, 0.852, and 0.868, respectively; in the internal validation set, they were 0.718, 0.755, 0.776, 0.841, and 0.858; and in the external validation cohort, they were 0.705, 0.742, 0.765, 0.832, and 0.848, respectively. The largest incremental gain was observed after addition of multimodal CTA variables. The final nomogram showed good calibration and favorable clinical utility in both the training and external validation cohorts. Risk stratification in the development cohort demonstrated a marked stepwise increase in observed MAE incidence from 3.7% in the low-risk group to 27.9% in the intermediate-risk group and 74.2% in the high-risk group (P for trend <0.001).ConclusionsA staged prediction model integrating preoperative multimodal imaging and perioperative clinical characteristics provided effective risk stratification for postoperative MAEs among patients with ATAAD. Preoperative CTA contributed the greatest incremental improvement beyond baseline clinical assessment, and the final nomogram demonstrated good discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. This multimodal framework may support more precise perioperative risk assessment and individualized management for patients with surgically treated ATAAD.

Introduction

Among cardiovascular emergencies, Acute Stanford type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is one of the most critical and requires immediate diagnosis and surgical intervention (1,2). Despite the substantial advances achieved in operative techniques and perioperative management, early adverse outcomes are a common occurrence. In the Oxford Vascular Study, the 30-day fatality rate among patients with type A dissection who survived to hospital admission was 47.4% (3). In a large contemporary surgical registry, Conzelmann et al. reported a 30-day mortality of 16.9% among 2,137 surgically treated patients (4). More recent registry-based evidence suggests that early mortality after ATAAD surgery remains clinically substantial. These findings underscore the need for more accurate perioperative risk stratification in this high-risk population (5,6).

The clinical course of ATAAD is markedly heterogeneous, ranging from patients with relatively stable hemodynamics to those presenting with shock, cardiac tamponade, branch-vessel involvement, or end-organ malperfusion at admission. Recent large-scale registry studies have shown that these high-risk clinical presentations, particularly preoperative shock and malperfusion, are closely associated with increased early mortality after ATAAD repair (7,8). Moreover, the prognostic impact of malperfusion varies according to the affected vascular territory, with mesenteric, coronary, and cerebral malperfusion being particularly high-risk patterns (9). These findings suggest that perioperative risk among patients with ATAAD cannot be adequately characterized by general clinical variables alone; rather, anatomical extent, branch-vessel involvement, and ischemic burden should be incorporated into risk stratification and surgical decision-making (10,11).

Several risk assessment tools have been proposed for ATAAD, among which the GERAADA (German Registry for Acute Aortic Dissection Type A) score is one of the most widely recognized disease-specific models (12). The score was derived from 2,537 patients across 56 referral centers and includes 9 preoperative variables for estimating 30-day mortality (13). Although such models have served as useful frameworks for early risk assessment, they largely focus on short-term mortality and are driven predominantly by clinical characteristics (14). External validation studies have also shown that model performance may vary across populations; for example, one validation study reported an observed operative mortality of 14.3% in contrast to predicted mortality rates of 15.6% by GERAADA and 10.6% by EuroSCORE II (European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II) (15). Taken together, these findings indicate that the currently available tools remain clinically valuable but may not fully capture the complexity and transportability of ATAAD risk.

Preoperative multimodal imaging may offer an important approach to improving this situation. computed tomography angiography (CTA) and transthoracic/transesophageal echocardiography (TTE/TEE) can directly characterize true lumen compression, branch-vessel involvement, pericardial effusion, aortic regurgitation, and other structural or hemodynamic abnormalities that are closely related to postoperative deterioration (16,17). Indeed, recent evidence suggests that the addition of imaging markers may meaningfully improve predictive performance. For instance, Pan et al. reported that adding ascending aortic length and pericardial effusion to the GERAADA score improved the area under the curve from 0.683 to 0.832 (18). However, the literature on this subject is fragmented, and relatively few studies have systematically evaluated the incremental prognostic value of integrating baseline clinical variables, disease-severity indicators, echocardiography, CTA findings, and intraoperative characteristics within a staged modeling framework, particularly for postoperative major adverse events (MAE), instead focusing on mortality alone (19,20). Therefore, we conducted a study with the aim of developing and externally validating a staged risk prediction model for postoperative MAEs in patients with surgically treated ATAAD and to assess the incremental value of preoperative multimodal imaging relative to conventional clinical assessment. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1011/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

For retrospective analysis, patients with ATAAD who underwent surgical treatment between April 2016 and April 2026 from two centers were enrolled. Specifically, consecutive patients treated at Beijing Anzhen Hospital of Capital Medical University (Center I) and Xiangyang No. 1 People’s Hospital, Hubei University of Medicine (Center II) were retrospectively screened. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Anzhen Hospital of Capital Medical University (approval No. 2026048x) and by the Ethics Committee of Xiangyang No. 1 People’s Hospital, Hubei University of Medicine (approval No. 2021KYLX61). Given the retrospective design and the use of de-identified clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the respective ethics committees.

Study population

All eligible patients underwent standardized collection of preoperative multimodal imaging and perioperative clinical data, including CTA, TTE/TEE, admission laboratory indices, disease-severity indicators, and surgery-related variables. For outcome ascertainment, patients were followed through discharge from the index hospitalization or postoperative day 30, whichever occurred first. Of the 1,709 patients from Center I and 523 patients from Center II who were initially screened, 1,960 were ultimately included, with patients being excluded for the following reasons: age younger than 18 years; non-acute disease or outside the predefined onset-to-surgery time window; traumatic, iatrogenic, retrograde, or other non-target aortic diseases; missing preoperative CTA raw images or inadequate CTA image quality; missing or uninterpretable key preoperative TTE/TEE parameters; missing key perioperative data or unavailable 30-day outcome data; and duplicate admissions or conflicting records. Center I constituted the development cohort (n=1,498), which was randomly split in a 7:3 ratio into a training set (n=1,048) and an internal validation set (n=450), whereas Center II served as the external validation cohort (n=462). Outcome definition

The primary outcome of this study was postoperative MAEs, defined as the occurrence of any of the following during the index hospitalization or within 30 days after surgery, whichever occurred first: early mortality, permanent stroke, severe acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), re-exploration for postoperative bleeding, and mesenteric ischemia. These components were selected because they represent severe, clinically meaningful, and relatively objectively adjudicable postoperative events that were consistently recorded across both centers. Postoperative pulmonary failure requiring tracheostomy was not included in the primary MAE definition because the indication and timing of tracheostomy might have been strongly influenced by center-specific ventilation or weaning, sedation, neurological, and intensive care protocols in a retrospective multicenter setting.

Early mortality was defined as death from any cause during the index hospitalization or within 30 days after the index operation. Permanent stroke was defined as a new postoperative focal or global neurological deficit of presumed cerebrovascular origin lasting more than 24 hours as indicated by clinical assessment and/or neuroimaging findings that did not completely resolve before discharge or within the 30-day follow-up window. Severe acute kidney injury was defined as postoperative renal dysfunction requiring CRRT. Re-exploration for postoperative bleeding was defined as return to the operating room or an equivalent surgical setting after completion of the index procedure for surgical investigation and/or control of postoperative bleeding or tamponade. Mesenteric ischemia was defined as clinically significant postoperative bowel ischemia diagnosed on the basis of clinical suspicion together with radiographic, intraoperative, or endoscopic evidence and considered severe enough to require urgent intervention or to represent definite ischemic intestinal injury. Outcome events were ascertained according to review of operative notes, intensive care records, laboratory data, postoperative imaging, discharge summaries, and 30-day follow-up documentation.

Sample size determination

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed as a retrospective design was employed. However, the available sample size was considered sufficient for multivariable prediction modeling. The development cohort included 375 MAEs, which provided an adequate events-to-variable ratio for both the multivariable logistic regression analysis of the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-selected candidate variables and the final parsimonious model. In addition, the availability of an independent external validation cohort of 462 patients strengthened the assessment of model transportability and robustness.

Collection of clinical, laboratory, and perioperative data

Clinical and perioperative variables were extracted from the electronic medical records of both centers in accordance with a predefined data collection framework. Baseline variables included age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, current smoking status, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, prior cardiovascular surgery, and Marfan syndrome. Admission variables included time from symptom onset to surgery, shock at presentation, cardiopulmonary resuscitation before surgery, preoperative mechanical ventilation, white blood cell count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), D-dimer, fibrinogen, lactate, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and Penn classification. Penn classification, which was analyzed as a key disease-severity indicator, was categorized as class Aa, class Ab, class Ac, and class Abc.

Preoperative multimodal imaging variables included maximum ascending aortic diameter, primary intimal tear in the ascending aorta, complete false-lumen thrombosis, severe true-lumen compression, pericardial effusion or tamponade, coronary artery involvement, supra-aortic branch involvement, mesenteric artery involvement, renal artery involvement, iliac artery or lower-limb involvement, malperfusion burden score ≥2, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation. Intraoperative variables included root replacement procedure, total arch replacement, frozen elephant trunk implantation, concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) time, aortic cross-clamp time, deep hypothermic circulatory arrest time, and intraoperative red blood cell transfusion.

Preoperative multimodal imaging assessment

All patients underwent preoperative CTA and TTE/TEE as part of routine emergency evaluation before surgery. CTA was used to characterize the extent of dissection, true- and false-lumen morphology, branch-vessel involvement, malperfusion burden, and imaging evidence of impending rupture, including pericardial effusion or hemopericardium. Echocardiography was used to evaluate intimal flap morphology, aortic regurgitation, left ventricular systolic function, pericardial effusion or tamponade, and abnormal flow within the dissected aorta. Imaging reproducibility

To assess the reliability of key imaging measurements, intraobserver and interobserver reproducibility analyses were performed in a randomly selected subset of 55 patients from the study cohort. Maximum ascending aortic diameter, area and diameter of the true lumen, false-lumen area, and malperfusion burden score were re-evaluated. Intraobserver reproducibility was assessed via repeated measurements by Reader 1, whereas interobserver reproducibility was assessed via the comparison on independent measurements obtained by Reader 1 and Reader 2. Agreement was quantified according to intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals, and Bland-Altman analysis was additionally performed to evaluate measurement bias and limits of agreement for representative continuous imaging parameters.

Handling of missing data

Missing data in the development cohort were addressed through multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). Variables with incomplete observations included selected demographic, laboratory, preoperative imaging, and intraoperative variables, such as body mass index, time from onset to surgery, current smoking status, lactate, D-dimer, eGFR, NLR, LVEF, pericardial effusion/tamponade, moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation, malperfusion burden score ≥2, intraoperative red blood cell transfusion, and CPB time. For sensitivity analyses, the distributions of variables in the original complete-case dataset were compared with those in the imputed dataset, and no material differences were observed, indicating acceptable stability of the imputation procedure. Details of the missing data and imputation are shown in Table S1.

Predictor selection and model construction

Candidate predictors associated with postoperative MAEs were first assessed in the development cohort via univariate logistic regression. Variables considered clinically relevant and/or statistically associated with MAEs were then entered into LASSO regression for predictor selection. The LASSO procedure identified nine candidate predictors for subsequent multivariable logistic regression analysis: age, lactate, NLR, D-dimer, Penn classification, severe true-lumen compression, mesenteric artery involvement, malperfusion burden score ≥2, and CPB time. These nine variables were then entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to estimate the adjusted odds ratios and identify the most relevant predictors of postoperative MAE occurrence. On the basis of the multivariable results and model parsimony, a final prediction model was constructed and displayed as a nomogram. The final nomogram incorporated age, lactate, NLR, CPB time, Penn classification, severe true-lumen compression, and mesenteric artery involvement.

Staged model expansion and assessment of incremental value

To quantify the incremental contribution of disease-severity indicators, multimodal imaging, and intraoperative variables beyond baseline clinical assessment, five progressively expanded models were constructed. Model 1 included baseline clinical and laboratory variables, Model 2 included Model 1 plus Penn classification, Model 3 included Model 2 plus TTE/TEE variables, Model 4 included Model 3 plus CTA-derived imaging variables, and Model 5 included Model 4 plus intraoperative variables. This staged framework was designed to determine whether progressive integration of disease-severity status, echocardiography, CTA findings, and surgery-related variables improved the postoperative prediction of MAEs compared to conventional clinical assessment alone.

Model validation and risk stratification

The staged models were evaluated in the training set, internal validation set, and external validation cohort. The final nomogram was developed in the training set and subsequently assessed in the external validation cohort with respect to discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. To evaluate the practical risk-separation ability of the final model, predicted probabilities were further examined in the development cohort according to observed outcome status, and a model-derived cutoff value of 0.28 was used to visualize the separation between lower- and higher-risk patients. In addition, patients were stratified into three predefined risk categories according to predicted probability, including low risk (<20%), intermediate risk (20–50%), and high risk (>50%), and the observed incidence of postoperative MAEs was calculated across these strata.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or as the median and interquartile range, as appropriate according to their distribution, whereas categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed via the independent-samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and via the Chi-squared test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to estimate odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for factors associated with postoperative MAE incidence. Correlations among candidate predictors were evaluated through Spearman correlation analysis. The reproducibility of imaging measurements was assessed according to ICCs and Bland-Altman plots.

Model discrimination was assessed in terms of the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and pairwise differences in AUC were compared via the DeLong test. Calibration was evaluated with calibration curves, and clinical utility was assessed via decision curve analysis (DCA). Overall prediction accuracy in the external validation cohort was additionally quantified by the Brier score. Incremental predictive value across the progressively expanded models was evaluated according to the change in AUC (ΔAUC), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with R software version 4.4.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Specifically, LASSO regression was conducted with the GLMNET package, model discrimination was assessed with the PROC package, nomogram construction and calibration was performed with the RMS package, DCA was performed with the RMDA package, and multiple imputation was conducted with the MICE package.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 2,232 patients with surgically treated ATAAD were initially screened across the two participating centers, including 1,709 patients from Center I and 523 patients from Center II. After exclusion of 272 patients according to the predefined criteria, 1,960 patients were finally included. Center I constituted the development cohort (n=1,498), which was randomly divided into a training set (n=1,048) and an internal validation set (n=450), whereas Center II served as the external validation cohort (n=462) (Figure 1).

Within the development cohort, postoperative MAEs occurred in 375 patients, whereas 1,123 patients did not experience MAEs. There were no statistically significant differences between the MAE and non-MAE groups in terms of male sex, body mass index, hypertension, current smoking status, prior cardiovascular surgery, Marfan syndrome, maximum ascending aortic diameter, root replacement procedure, total arch replacement, frozen elephant trunk implantation, or moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation (all P values >0.05). However, compared with patients in the non-MAE group, those in the MAE group were significantly older and had higher rates of diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, shock at presentation, preoperative cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and preoperative mechanical ventilation (all P values <0.05). The MAE group also showed a significantly higher white blood cell count, NLR, D-dimer level, and lactate level, as well as lower fibrinogen and eGFR values (all P values <0.001). In addition, severe true-lumen compression, pericardial effusion/tamponade, coronary artery involvement, supra-aortic branch involvement, mesenteric artery involvement, renal artery involvement, iliac artery/lower-limb involvement, malperfusion burden score ≥2, concomitant CABG, longer CPB time, longer aortic cross-clamp time, longer deep hypothermic circulatory arrest time, and greater intraoperative red blood cell transfusion were all significantly more frequent in patients who experienced MAEs (Table 1).

Reproducibility of imaging measurements

Reproducibility analysis demonstrated good-to-excellent agreement for the key imaging measurements. The intraobserver ICCs were 0.98 for maximum ascending aortic diameter, 0.95 for the area and diameter of the true lumen, 0.92 for false-lumen area, and 0.93 for malperfusion burden score, whereas the corresponding interobserver ICCs were 0.95, 0.92, 0.88, and 0.89, respectively. Bland-Altman analysis for the true-lumen diameter indicated a mean intraobserver bias of −0.19 mm (95% limits of agreement from −2.18 to 1.81 mm) and a mean interobserver bias of 0.31 mm (95% limits of agreement from −2.95 to 3.58 mm). Taken together, these findings indicate satisfactory reproducibility and measurement reliability of the imaging parameters examined in the study (Figure 3).

Performance of the progressively expanded models

To assess the incremental predictive value of multimodal imaging and intraoperative variables relative to baseline clinical assessment, five progressively expanded models were constructed. In the training set, the AUC increased from 0.725 for Model 1 to 0.768 for Model 2, 0.782 for Model 3, 0.852 for Model 4, and 0.868 for Model 5; a similar pattern was observed in the internal validation set, with corresponding AUCs of 0.718, 0.755, 0.776, 0.841, and 0.858, respectively. In the external validation cohort, the AUCs for models 1–5 were 0.705, 0.742, 0.765, 0.832, and 0.848, respectively, indicating progressive improvement in discrimination with sequential addition of disease-severity, echocardiographic, CTA, and intraoperative variables (Table 5; Figure 5A-5C).

Nomogram construction, calibration, and clinical utility

Based on the final parsimonious model, a nomogram incorporating age, lactate, NLR, CPB time, Penn classification, severe true-lumen compression, and mesenteric artery involvement was constructed for individualized prediction of postoperative MAEs (Figure 6A). Calibration analysis showed good agreement between predicted and observed risks in both the training and external validation cohorts, as the apparent and bias-corrected calibration curves were both close to the ideal reference line (Figure 6B,6C). In addition, DCA demonstrated that the nomogram-based strategy provided a higher net benefit than did either the treat-all or treat-none strategy across a broad range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities in both cohorts, supporting the clinical utility of the final model (Figure 6D,6E).

Distribution of predicted probabilities and risk stratification

The predicted probabilities generated by the final model were further examined in the development cohort according to observed outcome status. Patients without MAEs had predicted probabilities mainly concentrated in the lower-risk range, whereas patients with MAEs exhibited an obvious rightward shift toward the higher-risk range. Although a degree of overlap was observed in the intermediate-risk zone, the overall separation between the two distributions suggested satisfactory discriminatory ability of the final model. The selected risk threshold was 0.28 (Figure 7A).

Risk distribution and stratification performance of the final prediction model. (A) Distribution of predicted probabilities for postoperative MAEs in the development cohort. (B) Observed incidence of postoperative MAE across the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups. MAE, major adverse event.

To further assess clinical risk stratification, we categorized patients in the development cohort into three predefined risk groups according to predicted probability: low risk (<20%), intermediate risk (20–50%), and high risk (>50%). The observed incidence of postoperative MAEs increased progressively across the three strata, from 3.7% (28/748) in the low-risk group to 27.9% (126/452) in the intermediate-risk group and 74.2% (221/298) in the high-risk group, with a significant dose-response trend (P for trend <0.001). These findings indicate that the final model was able to separate patients into clinically meaningful low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories (Figure 7B).

Discussion

In this two-center study of patients with surgically treated acute ATAAD, we developed and externally validated a staged risk model for postoperative MAEs, integrating clinical, laboratory, multimodal imaging, and intraoperative variables. The principal findings were as follows. First, age, lactate, NLR, Penn classification, severe true-lumen compression, mesenteric artery involvement, and CPB time were retained as the most informative variables in the final model. Second, the sequential addition of imaging information meaningfully improved model performance, with the largest gain observed after incorporation of CTA-derived variables. Third, the final nomogram demonstrated good external discrimination (AUC 0.848), favorable calibration, and clinically meaningful net benefit, and it separated patients into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk strata, with observed MAE rates of 3.7%, 27.9%, and 74.2%, respectively. Taken together, these findings support the view that postoperative risk in ATAAD is not determined by any single domain alone but rather emerges from the interaction of systemic illness severity, malperfusion burden, anatomic complexity, and operative stress.

A central conclusion of this study is that clinical severity and malperfusion phenotype remain the backbone of early risk assessment, even in the era of increasingly sophisticated modeling. In our cohort, Penn class Ac and Abc, severe true lumen compression, and mesenteric artery involvement showed the strongest and most consistent adverse associations, while the correlation heatmap further suggested that these variables cluster within a coherent malperfusion-severity domain. This interpretation is supported by contemporary large-scale data. In a recent analysis of 6,437 surgically repaired patients with type A dissection from the International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection, 41% had at least one preoperative malperfusion syndrome, and mesenteric malperfusion carried the highest odds of in-hospital death, exceeding the risk associated with coma, limb ischemia, or coronary malperfusion (21). Similarly, Pacini et al. showed that mortality increased sharply with the number of organ systems involved, rising from 34.7% with malperfusion of a single organ to 61.9% and 85.7% to that of two organ systems and of three or more organ systems, respectively (22). In a more recent study, Penn-class-based outcome stratification again demonstrated a strong gradient, with reported 30-day mortality rates of 5.2%, 10.3%, 15.2%, and 35.3% across Penn classes A, B, C, and BC, respectively (23). Viewed together with our findings, these studies indicate that the burden and pattern of ischemia are not merely descriptive features of ATAAD but are also mechanistically relevant expressions of distal flow compromise and systemic collapse.

Our study also highlights the prognostic importance of admission metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers, particularly lactate and NLR. After multivariable adjustment, both were independently associated with postoperative MAEs. This is biologically plausible: lactate reflects global tissue hypoperfusion, anaerobic metabolism, and the combined effect of circulatory instability and occult malperfusion. Recent surgical data support to this interpretation: in a retrospective series of 166 patients undergoing emergency surgery for ATAAD, a preoperative lactate level of ≥3.7 mmol/L was independently associated with in-hospital mortality and was strongly correlated with Penn classes Ac and Abc (24). NLR, in turn, reflects the intensity of the systemic inflammatory response that accompanies acute aortic wall injury, ischemia-reperfusion, and neurohumoral activation. A 2026 meta-analysis including 1,792 patients found that elevated preoperative NLR was associated with increased mortality among patients with aortic dissection (25). In another study, the optimal NLR cutoff for in-hospital mortality prediction was 9.20, with an AUC of 0.695 and an adjusted odds ratio of 1.084 (26). These observations do not imply that lactate level or NLR is interchangeable with imaging-defined malperfusion but rather suggest that laboratory and imaging signals are complementary: imaging indicates the anatomic source of impaired flow, whereas lactate level and NLR reflect its systemic metabolic and inflammatory consequences.

Another important finding is the persistent relevance of operative burden, especially CPB duration, in determining postoperative risk. In our multivariable model, CPB time remained independently associated with MAE incidence even after preoperative severity and imaging-defined malperfusion were accounted for. This is consistent with the clinical phenomena in which prolonged CPB often reflects a combination of anatomic complexity, technically demanding repair, longer myocardial and end-organ ischemic exposure, and a stronger inflammatory response. A recent multicenter study by Uimonen et al., in which prolonged CPB time during ATAAD surgery was associated with higher 30-day mortality and worse mid-term survival, supports this interpretation (27). Similarly, Yuan et al. found that CPB time >227 minutes, along with supra-aortic vessel involvement and lower-extremity ischemia, predicted higher mortality among patients with ATAAD (28). These results are consistent with earlier surgical series in which CPB duration and operative times were more broadly associated with early death after type A dissection repair (29,30). Within this context, CPB time in our model likely functions both a technical variable and as an integrated surrogate for procedural complexity superimposed on a high-risk physiologic substrate.

A major strength of our study is that it formally quantified the incremental value of multimodal imaging, rather than simply listing imaging findings alongside clinical variables. In the external validation cohort, discrimination improved from 0.705 in the baseline clinical-laboratory model to 0.832 after the addition of CTA variables and to 0.848 after further addition of intraoperative variables; moreover, the largest performance jump occurred at the CTA step (ΔAUC +0.127 relative to Model 1). This pattern is clinically credible. Computed tomography remains the primary modality for defining the anatomy of acute aortic syndromes, and radiologic reviews have emphasized that the most important findings for informing immediate management and prognosis—such as branch-vessel extension, true lumen compromise, pericardial blood, rupture-related features, malperfusion, false-lumen characteristics, and dissection extent—are systematically obtainable on computed tomography (31,32). Consistent with this, other studies have shown that the combined assessment of clinical and computed tomography findings can meaningfully stratify prognosis for patients with surgically treated type A dissection (33,34). The heatmap in our study also showed that severe true-lumen compression and mesenteric artery involvement clustered with malperfusion burden, while Penn class correlated with both lactate level and malperfusion severity, suggesting that multimodal imaging did not simply add redundant variables but captured a structural domain not fully represented by baseline clinical data (35).

The clinical value of the final model lies both in its discrimination ability and its capacity to be translated to bedside risk stratification. The nomogram showed good agreement between predicted and observed risk in both the training and external validation cohorts and yielded meaningful clinical net benefit according to DCA. Importantly, when translated into predefined risk strata, the model identified a low-risk group with an observed MAE rate of only 3.7%, an intermediate-risk group with a rate of 27.9%, and a high-risk group with a rate of 74.2%. This degree of separation is clinically relevant because it may help distinguish two patient types: those for whom standard urgent repair is likely to proceed without excessive perioperative complication and those for whom intensified monitoring, more aggressive organ-protection strategies, and earlier multidisciplinary coordination may be warranted. Recent ATAAD studies have shown that nomogram-based or score-based models can support clinically interpretable postoperative risk stratification and demonstrate useful discrimination, calibration, and clinical net benefit; however, comparison across studies should be done with caution, as their endpoints differ (36,37). A recent study that developed a nomogram for postoperative mortality among patients with ATAAD reported an AUC of 0.8242, which is broadly in line with the performance range achieved by our externally validated model despite our broader composite endpoint (38). This suggests that clinically interpretable models integrating inflammatory biomarkers, severity classification, computed tomography findings, and operative variables can provide useful prognostic performance without compromising transparency (39,40).

Several limitations to this study should be acknowledged. First, we employed a retrospective design, and residual confounding and differences in centers’ management could not be completely excluded. Second, although the model was externally validated in an independent cohort, both centers were from the same county, and broader geographic validation would strengthen the generalizability of the findings. Third, the primary endpoint was a composite of heterogeneous events and did not include postoperative pulmonary failure requiring tracheostomy. Although tracheostomy is a critical complication after ATAAD surgery, its indication and timing might have been influenced by center-specific intensive care practices in this retrospective study of two centers. Therefore, excluding this event might have limited the ability of the MAE endpoint to capture the full burden of postoperative morbidity. Fourth, although the model demonstrated good discrimination and calibration, it should be interpreted as a tool for risk stratification and not as a substitute for clinical judgment or operative decision-making. Another limitation is that excluding patients without available or interpretable preoperative CTA might have underrepresented patients with critically unstable ATAAD requiring immediate surgery, thereby limiting the generalizability of the reported incremental value of CT. Finally, because ATAAD management is highly time-sensitive, the real-world value of any prediction tool depends not only on statistical performance but also on whether its variables are apparent sufficiently early to influence care pathways.

Conclusions

Our study suggests that postoperative risk in ATAAD is most accurately understood as a multidimensional construct shaped by ischemic severity, systemic inflammatory-metabolic disturbance, anatomic malperfusion, and operative complexity. Within this framework, preoperative multimodal imaging appears to provide the largest incremental gain beyond conventional clinical assessment, while lactate level, NLR, Penn classification, severe true-lumen compression, mesenteric artery involvement, and CPB time can identify patients in whom the burden of physiologic and procedural stress is especially high. These findings support the use of integrated, multimodal risk models to refine perioperative stratification and potentially guide more individualized management for patients with ATAAD.
