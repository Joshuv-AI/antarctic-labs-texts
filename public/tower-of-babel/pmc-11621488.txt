A large vaginal stone on top of ureteric stent migration through a poucho-vaginal fistula after radical cystectomy and ileal neobladder reconstruction – A case report
Authors: Omar Hamdy, Eman A Abdelghaffar, Zainab S Alalawi, Ahmed M Eid, Gehad Ahmad Saleh
Published: 2024

Introduction

Urinary stones are among the most frequent urological diseases [1]. Recently, there has been interest in the clinical and molecular relationships between stones and genitourinary cancers [2].

The use of orthotopic neobladder reconstruction is increasing in women with urinary bladder cancer. This technique is associated with a growing number of unique complications. Some complications such as NVF formation are rarely reported [3]. Uretero-ileal anastomotic stricture is another frequently reported complication which can be managed by a stent insertion. The stent may be susceptible to migration, with lower stent diameters being linked to a higher incidence of migration [4].

In this manuscript, we report the presentation of an exceptionally large vaginal stone that developed on top of a migrated stent through a poucho-vaginal fistula in a patient who had a surgical history of radical cystectomy and orthotopic ileal neobladder for urinary bladder cancer.

Case presentation

A 69-year-old lady with no previous medical history and a surgical history of radical cystectomy & ileal neobladder in another center in August 2020 for T3bN0 Grade I bladder squamous cell carcinoma. The operation was complicated with a left ureteric stricture and poucho-vaginal fistula for which she underwent a left ureteric stent and bilateral PCN insertions. She also suffered from an extensive right lower limb deep venous thrombosis (DVT) in January 2021 for which she received anticoagulant therapy. In the same month, the left PCN slipped, and the ureteric stent protruded through the urethra while its upper coil was still in the renal hilum. The stent was pushed back into the pouch by forceps. After this event, the patient did not stick to her follow-up visits to the urology center for three years. She presented to our center with a right subcostal anterior abdominal wall mass in March 2024. The ultrasound described a well-defined hypoechoic solid lesion measuring 10.3 × 4 cm within the right rectus abdominis muscle showing internal vascularity and few calcific foci with few areas of cystic degeneration. The swelling was suggested to be a desmoid tumor or a secondary deposit from the bladder tumor. Ultrasound (US) guided biopsy revealed the picture of a bland spindle cell proliferation, suggestive of a desmoid tumor. Computed tomography (CT) scan (Fig. 1) described an anterior abdominal wall mass of soft tissue density measuring 7 × 4.5 cm with no intra-abdominal extension. The patient underwent wide local excision and abdominal wall reconstruction by double-layered mesh in May 2024. During the pre-operative catheterization, a stony hard object was identified within the vaginal cavity. Examination under anesthesia revealed large stones that had formed upon the migrated stent (Fig. 2) that settled in the vagina through the poucho-vaginal fistula. The stent was extracted with five large stones. The patient was discharged after two days with no post-operative complications and was referred later to the urology center for further management. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case with such a presentation. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [5].

Discussion

Muscle-invasive urinary bladder cancer and recurrent non-muscle-invasive urinary bladder cancer are commonly treated with radical cystectomy (RC) [6,7]. Urinary bladder reconstruction after cystectomy using the ileal neobladder is considered the best surgical choice in comparison with the other techniques of urinary diversion as it provides a better quality of life despite its surgical complexity [8]. Our presented patient underwent RC & ileal neobladder reconstruction 4 years earlier for T3N0 bladder cancer with no subsequent recurrence of distant metastasis.

A study that followed 363 patients for 11 years after ileal neobladder reconstruction has found that the most common late signs associated with ileal neobladder were: neobladder enteric fistula, and stenosis of the uretero-ileal anastomosis. The most frequent endoscopically treated neobladder-related late complications include: anterior urethral stricture, uretero-ileal anastomotic stricture, and calculus development [9]. In another study on 400 patients, it was reported that poucho-vaginal fistulae and pouch stones occurred in 3 and 13 patients, respectively [10]. The presented patient developed two of these complications which are left ureteric stricture as well as a poucho-vaginal fistula. These complications necessitated re-admission for further management. On the other hand, ureteral double J (DJ) stents are also one of the commonly used tools for the management of many urological problems. Nevertheless, the most challenging complication related to ureteral stents is their retention and associated sequalae: encrustation, calcification, and stent fragmentation, Furthermore, restenosis or external compression can lead to stent migration and failure [11,12]. Regarding DJ stent migrations, its incidence is less than 5 % as has been reported, it can happen both distally (in 0.7 %) toward the bladder and proximally (in 0.9 %) into the ureter or pyelocaliceal system. [13,14]

The risk factors that can lead to stent encrustation include bacterial infection, the length of the stent's indwelling time, urinary lithiasis, and other systemic diseases such as chronic kidney disease (CKD), and diabetes mellitus. Other patient-specific risk factors include diet, malabsorptive disorders, and malignancy. These factors all increase the amounts of urinary calcium, oxalate, and uric acid, thus promoting encrustation [15]. Encrustation and stone formation on retained stents should be managed with stent removal and endourological treatments [13]. In the presented case, the stent was left in place for more than three years as the patient lost follow-up. This led to the formation of multiple stones along its distal part. One of them was at least 4 cm in diameter.

About 3.6–10 % of women experience NVF after orthotropic neobladder reconstruction. Poor anterior vaginal wall tissue vascularity, vaginal wall iatrogenic tears, suture line proximity, local tumor relapse, pelvic radiotherapy, and the use of bulking agents in incontinent individuals are risk factors for NVF [16]. To our knowledge, there is only one case of a female patient who underwent ureteral wall stent treatment for radiation-induced stenosis. The stent subsequently migrated into the bladder, causing encrustation and the development of stones. This resulted in the development of a vesicovaginal fistula and bladder perforation [17]. This scenario is relatively like our patient scenario. However, the difference was that the previously reported patient had a normal urinary bladder, not a reconstructed one. Also, the stone was found in the urinary bladder, not the vagina.

Conclusion

We have presented the first-ever reported case of a large vaginal stone that developed on top of a migrated stent through a poucho-vaginal fistula in a patient with a history of radical cystectomy and ileal neobladder for urinary bladder cancer. This highlights the importance of early detection and management of complications after such a complex operation before more complications occur and adds this scenario to the differential diagnosis.
