Switching Rook Polynomials of Collections of Cells: Palindromicity and Domino-Stability
Francesco Navarra, Ayesha Asloob Qureshi, Giancarlo Rinaldo

Abstract
. The rook polynomial is a generating function that enumerates the number of ways to place rooks, with no two in the same row or column, on a collection of cells regarded as a pruned chessboard. In combinatorial commutative algebra, special attention is devoted to its variant, the switching rook polynomial , which is conjectured to coincide with the h h -polynomial of the K K -algebra associated with the given collection of cells. In this context, palindromicity plays a crucial role, as it reflects the algebraic property of Gorensteinness. In this paper, we introduce a new combinatorial property, called domino-stability , and we prove that the switching rook polynomial of a collection of cells 𝒫 \mathcal{P} is palindromic if and only if 𝒫 \mathcal{P} is domino-stable. Building upon this result, we derive new insights into the characterization of Gorenstein K K -algebras arising from collections of cells.

Introduction

Chess is one of the most famous board games in the world, and throughout its history it has inspired a wide variety of combinatorial problems. One of the most classical examples, known as the
rook problem
, involves rooks, i.e., the chess pieces that can attack only horizontally or vertically. This problem consists in determining the
rook polynomial
, that is, the generating function whose
k
-th coefficient is the number of ways to place
k
rooks on a pruned chessboard with at most one rook per row and column. The rook polynomial of a skew diagram is intimately connected with the enumeration of permutations with specific properties, a relation first explored in
[
13
]
and further developed in
[
23
]
. For a comprehensive treatment of permutations with forbidden positions, we refer the reader to
[
26
, Chapter 2]
.

Over the years, rook polynomials have gained prominence due to their applications in various areas (see
[
7
,
8
]
and the references therein), and several variants have been proposed, as in
[
5
]
. One of the most relevant in combinatorial commutative algebra is the
switching rook polynomial
. To provide a working formulation of it, let us consider a collection of cells
\mathcal{P}
, viewed as a pruned
chessboard. We say that one or more rooks are
non-attacking
on
\mathcal{P}
whenever no horizontal or vertical path of edge-adjacent cells in
\mathcal{P}
connects two of them. Furthermore, if
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are two placements of
k
non-attacking rooks on
\mathcal{P}
, we identify
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
whenever
F_{2}
can be obtained from
F_{1}
through a finite sequence of switchings, i.e., by moving pairs of rooks occupying opposite corners of a rectangle entirely contained in
\mathcal{P}
, from the diagonal to the anti-diagonal or vice versa.
Then, the
switching rook polynomial
of
\mathcal{P}
is defined as

\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{r(\mathcal{P})}\tilde{r}_{k}(\mathcal{P})\,t^{k},

where
r(\mathcal{P})
denotes the
rook number
of
\mathcal{P}
, i.e., the maximum number of non-attacking rooks that may be placed on
\mathcal{P}
, and
\tilde{r}_{k}(\mathcal{P})
is the number of equivalence classes of configurations of
k
non-attacking rooks on
\mathcal{P}
under switching operations, for
0\leq k\leq r(\mathcal{P})
.

The significance of the switching rook polynomial arises in combinatorial commutative algebra from its connection with the Hilbert–Poincaré series of the
K
-algebras associated with collections of cells. Starting from a collection of cells
\mathcal{P}
, one may construct the binomial ideal
I_{\mathcal{P}}
generated by the inner
2
-minors of
\mathcal{P}
in the polynomial ring
S_{\mathcal{P}}=K[x_{v}:v\text{ is a vertex of }\mathcal{P}]
, where
K
is a field. The
K
-algebra
K[\mathcal{P}]:=S_{\mathcal{P}}/I_{\mathcal{P}}
is called the coordinate ring of
\mathcal{P}
. This construction was introduced in
[
19
]
as a generalization of the well-known theory of
2
-minor determinantal ideals of an
m\times n
matrix of indeterminates. Refer to
[
2
]
for more details on determinantal ideals. The main focus of this research line is the study of the algebraic invariants of
K[\mathcal{P}]
via the combinatorial geometry of
\mathcal{P}
. Since its introduction, this topic has been extensively investigated, leading to almost forty related publications, which the reader may consult in the recent survey
[
17
]
.

What grants the switching rook polynomial its central relevance in the study of ideals of inner
2
-minors of a collection of cells
\mathcal{P}
is its matching with the
h
-polynomial of
K[\mathcal{P}]
. A first connection was studied for polyominoes, that is, planar figures formed by joining unit squares edge to edge; for a comprehensive overview of polyominoes, see
[
6
]
. It was initially observed in
[
4
]
that the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of
K[\mathcal{P}]
coincides with the rook number of
\mathcal{P}
, when
\mathcal{P}
is an
L
-convex polyomino. Later, it was shown in
[
22
]
that the
h
-polynomial of
K[\mathcal{P}]
coincides with the switching rook polynomial of
\mathcal{P}
, if
\mathcal{P}
is a free-hole polyomino containing no square tetromino. An analogous result was obtained in
[
10
,
14
,
20
]
for polyominoes with at most one hole and possibly containing square tetrominoes. A further step forward was recently made in
[
18
]
, where this equality between the
h
-polynomial and the switching rook polynomial was proven for convex collections of cells with quadratic Gröbner bases. In the same work, the authors also provided an algorithm for computing the switching rook polynomial and, on the basis of extensive computations, conjectured that this equality holds for arbitrary collections of cells (
[
18
, Conjecture 2.4]
). In this setting, the foundational work of Stanley
[
24
]
provides a nice and strong inspiration for analyzing the palindromicity of the switching rook polynomial, as it relates the palindromicity of the
h
-polynomial of a standard graded
K
-algebra to its Gorenstein property. Recall that a polynomial
p(t)=p_{0}+\dots+p_{d}t^{d}
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}[t]
is said to be
palindromic
if
p_{i}=p_{d-i}
for all
i=0,\dots,\lfloor d/2\rfloor
. It is worth mentioning that palindromic polynomials are studied not only in algebraic combinatorics for their elegant symmetries, but also in fields such as analysis and cryptography for their applications (see
[
12
]
); however, no analogous applications have been observed for the switching rook polynomial to date.

In this work, we geometrically characterize all collections of cells with palindromic switching rook polynomial. For this purpose, we introduce a novel combinatorial and geometric property, termed
domino-stability
(see Definition
2.4
), and show that, if
\mathcal{P}
is a collection of cells, then its switching rook polynomial
\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
is palindromic if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is domino-stable (see Theorem
5.1
).

The paper is organized as follows. Section
1
serves as a preliminary section and is divided into two subsections for clarity. In Subsection
1.1
we provide all relevant definitions and known properties of collections of cells, and in Subsection
1.2
we formally define the switching rook polynomial and discuss its properties.

In Section
2
we introduce and investigate the notion of
domino-stability
for a collection of cells (see Definition
2.4
). We first show that every domino-stable collection of cells contains at least one stable square (see Proposition
2.6
and Definition
2.7
). We then examine the switching rook polynomial of a square polyomino, providing an explicit bijection that explains its palindromicity (see Discussion
2.8
), and conclude with Proposition
2.9
, showing that certain cells of a domino-stable collection of cells are aligned horizontally and vertically with some stable squares.

Section
3
is devoted to establishing that domino-stability constitutes a sufficient condition for the palindromicity of the switching rook polynomial. The section begins with Lemma
3.1
, a preliminary yet fundamental result, which shows that for a domino-stable collection of cells
\mathcal{P}
there exists, up to switches, a unique placement of
r(\mathcal{P})
non-attacking rooks on
\mathcal{P}
, obtained by placing all rooks exclusively on the stable squares of
\mathcal{P}
. This key result, together with Discussion
2.8
and Proposition
2.9
, is then employed in Proposition
3.2
, in order to explicitly construct a bijection between the equivalence classes of configurations of
k
non-attacking rooks and those of
r(\mathcal{P})-k
rooks. As a consequence, the switching rook polynomial of
\mathcal{P}
is shown to be palindromic.

In Section
4
, we establish that domino-stability is a necessary condition for the palindromicity of the switching rook polynomial (see Proposition
4.3
). The core of the argument lies in Proposition
4.2
, where we show that if a collection of cells
\mathcal{P}
is not domino-stable and admits, up to switches, a unique configuration of
r(\mathcal{P})
non-attacking rooks on
\mathcal{P}
(equivalently,
\tilde{r}_{d}(\mathcal{P})=1
with
d=r(\mathcal{P})
), then necessarily
\tilde{r}_{d-1}(\mathcal{P})>\tilde{r}_{1}(\mathcal{P})
. In other words, the number of equivalence classes of
(r(\mathcal{P})-1)
non-attacking rooks on
\mathcal{P}
strictly exceeds the number of cells of
\mathcal{P}
.

Finally, in Section
5
, we present the main result of this paper, stated below.

Theorem 0.1
.

(Theorem
5.1
)
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of cells and
\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
be the switching rook polynomial of
\mathcal{P}
. Then,
\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
is palindromic if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is domino-stable.

Notably, the above theorem largely recovers
[
1
, Corollary 5.16]
, which describes the palindromicity of the switching rook polynomial (referred to there as the nested rook polynomial) for skew diagrams.
Furthermore, we present an interesting and unexpected development arising from the above result, that is, a potential characterization of the Gorenstein coordinate rings of collections of cells. Assuming that
[
18
, Conjecture 2.4]
holds and in light of Stanley’s results
[
24
]
, we have the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 0.2
.

(Corollary
5.3
)
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of cells satisfying
[
18
, Conjecture 2.4]
, i.e., such that the
h
-polynomial of
K[\mathcal{P}]
coincides with the switching rook polynomial of
\mathcal{P}
.

(1)

If
K[\mathcal{P}]
is Gorenstein, then
\mathcal{P}
is domino-stable;

(2)

Suppose that
K[\mathcal{P}]
is a Cohen-Macaulay domain, then the converse of (1) is true: that is, if
\mathcal{P}
is domino-stable, then
K[\mathcal{P}]
is Gorenstein.

This naturally motivates the search for supplementary geometric conditions on the structure of collections of cells to be imposed alongside domino-stability, in order to obtain a sufficient criterion for the Gorenstein property, in the non-domain case. The evidence presented below provides substantial support for the belief that domino-stability may, surprisingly, be sufficient to characterize Gorensteinness.

Proposition 0.3
.

(Proposition
5.5
)
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a domino-stable collection of cells with rank less than or equal to
10
or a domino-stable polyomino with rank less than or equal to
12
. Then
K[\mathcal{P}]
is Gorenstein.

The proof of the above result is purely computational and is based on the framework developed in
[
16
]
. This evidence naturally suggests the following conjecture:

Conjecture 0.4
.

(Conjecture
5.6
)
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of cells. Then, the following are equivalent:

(1)

K[\mathcal{P}]
is Gorenstein;

(2)

the
h
-polynomial of
K[\mathcal{P}]
is palindromic;

(3)

the switching rook polynomial of
\mathcal{P}
is palindromic;

(4)

\mathcal{P}
is domino-stable.

This phenomenon is rather surprising. As noted by Stanley in
[
24
, Example 4.5]
, even for relatively simple monomial ideals, weakening the primality assumption shows that palindromicity does not guarantee the Gorenstein property: for example,
K[x,y,z,w]/(xw,yw,xyz)
is a reduced Cohen–Macaulay
K
-algebra with a palindromic
h
-polynomial, yet it is not Gorenstein. In contrast, for ideals of inner
2
-minors of collections of cells, palindromicity alone appears sufficient to ensure the Gorenstein property.

1.
Collections of Cells and Switching Rook Polynomials

In this section, we present the combinatorial preliminaries on collections of cells and provide the definition and basic properties of the switching rook polynomial.

1.1.
Collection of cells.

Let
(i,j),(k,l)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We define the partial order
(i,j)\leq(k,l)
if and only if
i\leq k
and
j\leq l
. Given
a=(i,j)
and
b=(k,l)
in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
a\leq b
, the set
[a,b]=\{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:i\leq m\leq k,\ j\leq n\leq l\}
is called an
interval
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. If
i<k
and
j<l
, the interval
[a,b]
is said to be
proper
, in which case we refer to
a
and
b
as diagonal corners of
[a,b]
, and to
c=(i,l)
and
d=(k,j)
as the anti-diagonal corners. If
j=l
(or
i=k
) then
a
and
b
are in a
horizontal
(or
vertical
)
position
. We denote by
]a,b[
the set
\{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:i<m<k,\ j<n<l\}
. A proper interval
C=[a,b]
where
b=a+(1,1)
is called a
cell
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. The points
a
,
b
,
c
, and
d
are respectively referred to as the
lower left
,
upper right
,
upper left
and
lower right

corners
of
C
. We denote the set of
vertices
and
edges
of
C
by
V(C)=\{a,b,c,d\}
and
E(C)=\{\{a,c\},\{c,b\},\{b,d\},\{a,d\}\}
, respectively.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a non-empty collection of cells in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We define the vertex and edge sets as
V(\mathcal{P})=\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{P}}V(C)
and
E(\mathcal{P})=\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal{P}}E(C)
, respectively. The
rank
of
\mathcal{P}
, denoted by

, is the number of cells in
\mathcal{P}
. We say that

is a

if for any two distinct cells
C
and

in
\mathcal{P}
there exists a sequence of cells

such that

is an edge of
C_{i}
and
C_{i+1}
, for all
i=1,\dots,m-1
. Analogously,
\mathcal{P}
is

if for any two distinct cells in
\mathcal{P}
there exists such a sequence with

, for all
i=1,\dots,m-1
. A

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
of

is a polyomino contained in
\mathcal{P}
which is maximal with respect to the set inclusion, that is if
A\in\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
then
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\cup\{A\}
is not a polyomino.

1.2.
Switching rook polynomial

We now introduce the terminology of non-attacking rooks on a collection of cells and the switching rook polynomial.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of cells. We say that two rooks
R_{1}
and
R_{2}

coincide
or
overlap
if they are located in the same cell of
\mathcal{P}
. Two rooks
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are in
attacking position
or
attacking rooks
in
\mathcal{P}
if they do not overlap and there exists a row or a column of
\mathcal{P}
that contains the cells where
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are placed. In contrast, two rooks are in
non-attacking position
or
non-attacking rooks
in
\mathcal{P}
if they do not overlap and they are not in attacking position. A
j
-rook configuration
in
\mathcal{P}
is a set of
j
rooks arranged in non-attacking positions within
\mathcal{P}
, where
j\geq 0
; for convention, the
0
-rook configuration is
\emptyset
. We say that a
j
-rook configuration in
\mathcal{P}
is
maximal
if there does not exist any
k
-rook configuration in
\mathcal{P}
, with
k>j
, that properly contains it. The
rook number

r(\mathcal{P})
is the maximum number of rooks that can be placed in
\mathcal{P}
in non-attacking positions. In Figures
2
(A) and (B), we illustrate a maximal 5-rook configuration and an
r(\mathcal{P})
-rook configuration in the same collection
\mathcal{P}
of cells, respectively, where
r(\mathcal{P})=7
.

We denote by
\mathcal{R}_{j}(\mathcal{P})
the set of all
j
-rook configurations of
\mathcal{P}
for
j\in\{0,\dots,r(\mathcal{P})\}
, with the convention that
\mathcal{R}_{0}(\mathcal{P})
represents the empty configuration (i.e.,
|\mathcal{R}_{0}(\mathcal{P})|=1
). Note that the collection
\bigcup_{j=0}^{r(\mathcal{P})}\mathcal{R}_{j}(\mathcal{P})
forms a simplicial complex, known as the
rook complex
of
\mathcal{P}
. Two non-attacking rooks in
\mathcal{P}
are said to be in
switching position
if they are placed in the diagonal (respectively, anti-diagonal) corner cells of
\mathcal{R}(I)
, for some inner interval
I
of
\mathcal{P}
, and in such a case we say that the rooks are in a
diagonal
(respectively,
anti-diagonal
) position.

Fix
j\in\{0,\dots,r(\mathcal{P})\}
. Let
F\in\mathcal{R}_{j}(\mathcal{P})
. Consider two switching rooks,
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, within
F
, positioned diagonally (or anti-diagonally) in
\mathcal{R}(I)
for some inner interval
I
. Let
R_{1}^{\prime}
and
R_{2}^{\prime}
be the rooks in the anti-diagonal (or diagonal, respectively) cells of
\mathcal{R}(I)
. Then the set
(F\backslash\{R_{1},R_{2}\})\cup\{R_{1}^{\prime},R_{2}^{\prime}\}
also belongs to
\mathcal{R}_{j}(\mathcal{P})
. This operation of replacing
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
by
R_{1}^{\prime}
and
R_{2}^{\prime}
is called a
switch of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
. This defines an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\mathcal{R}_{j}(\mathcal{P})
:
F_{1}\sim F_{2}
if
F_{2}
can be obtained from
F_{1}
through a sequence of switches. In this case, we say that
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are
equivalent with respect to
\sim
(or
same up to switches
). Figures
2
(C) and (D) show two
4
-rook configurations that are equivalent with respect to
\sim
.

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{j}(\mathcal{P})=\mathcal{R}_{j}(\mathcal{P})/\sim
be the quotient set of equivalence classes. We define
\tilde{r}_{j}(\mathcal{P})=|\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{j}(\mathcal{P})|
for
j\in\{0,\dots,r(\mathcal{P})\}
, with the convention that
\tilde{r}_{0}(\mathcal{P})=1
. The
switching rook polynomial
of
\mathcal{P}
is then defined as the polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}[t]
:

\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{r(\mathcal{P})}\tilde{r}_{j}(\mathcal{P})t^{j}.

For example, for the collection of cells in Figures
2
(C) or (D),
\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)=1+8t+19t^{2}+14t^{3}+3t^{4}.

Remark 1.2

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of cells and
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{P})=\{\mathcal{B}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{B}_{p}\}
be the set of all maximal rectangles of
\mathcal{P}
. Set
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}=\mathcal{B}_{i}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{j\in[p]\setminus\{i\}}\mathcal{B}_{j}\right)

\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\neq\emptyset

\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
consists of a union of axis-aligned rectangles.

Proof.

Observe that if
\mathcal{B}_{i}\cap\mathcal{B}_{k}=\emptyset
for all
k\in[p]\setminus\{i\}

\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}=\mathcal{B}_{i}
, and the claim follows trivially. Otherwise, let

be the smallest index in

\mathcal{B}_{i}\cap\mathcal{B}_{j_{1}}\neq\emptyset

contains a corner of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
.

(1)

Suppose that a corner of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
belongs to
V(\mathcal{B}_{j_{1}})
. Note that at most one corner of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
can lies in the interior of
V(\mathcal{B}_{j_{1}})
, otherwise
\mathcal{B}_{i}
would not be maximal, and that the four corners of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
cannot belong to

at the same time, otherwise
\mathcal{B}_{i}\subset\mathcal{B}_{j_{1}}
so against the maximality of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
. If one of the corners of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
lies in the interior of
\mathcal{B}_{j_{1}}
, then there exist two distinct maximal rectangles of
\mathcal{P}
, one horizontal and one vertical, say
\mathcal{B}_{m_{1}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{n_{1}}
(with

), containing
\mathcal{B}_{i}\cap\mathcal{B}_{j_{1}}

\mathcal{B}_{i}\setminus(\mathcal{B}_{j_{1}}\cup\mathcal{B}_{m_{1}}\cup\mathcal{B}_{n_{1}})
consists of a single rectangle, which we denote by
\mathfrak{R}_{1}
. If no corner of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is in the interior of
\mathcal{B}_{j_{1}}
, then
\mathcal{B}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{j_{1}}
still consists of a single rectangle, which we again denote by
\mathfrak{R}_{1}
.

(2)

Suppose now that no corner of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
is in
V(\mathcal{B}_{j_{1}})
. Then

and

(A)

\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{S}}(t)
is palindromic, meaning that
\tilde{r}_{k}(\mathcal{S})=\tilde{r}_{n-k}(\mathcal{S})
for all
k\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. This symmetry implies the existence of a bijective correspondence between
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(\mathcal{S})
and
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{n-k}(\mathcal{S})
, so, in what follows, we explicitly construct such a bijection.
We may assume that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{R}([(1,1),(n+1,n+1)])
and we denote by
A_{ij}
the cell of
\mathcal{S}
with lower left corner
(i,j)
, for all
i,j\in[n]
. Moreover, we refer to
A_{ii}
for
i\in[n]
as a
diagonal cell
of
\mathcal{S}
. For a non-empty canonical
k
-rook configuration
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{i_{1}j_{1}},\dots,A_{i_{k}j_{k}}\}
, we set
[n]\setminus\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\}=\{i_{1}^{\prime}<\dots<i_{n-k}^{\prime}\}
and
[n]\setminus\{j_{1}<\dots<j_{k}\}=\{j_{1}^{\prime}<\dots<j_{n-k}^{\prime}\}
. Then, we define
\phi_{k}:\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(\mathcal{S})\rightarrow\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{n-k}(\mathcal{S})
as:

\phi_{k}([\mathcal{A}]_{\sim})=\begin{cases}\left[\{A_{ii}:i\in[n]\}\right]_{\sim}&\text{if }k=0\text{ and }\mathcal{A}=\emptyset,\\
\left[\{A_{i_{1}^{\prime}j_{1}^{\prime}},\dots,A_{i_{n-k}^{\prime}j_{n-k}^{\prime}}\}\right]_{\sim}&\text{if }0<k\leq r(\mathcal{S})\text{ and }\mathcal{A}=\{A_{i_{1}j_{1}},\dots,A_{i_{k}j_{k}}\}.\end{cases}

It is an easy exercise to verify that this map is well-defined and bijective. In Figure
7
, we illustrated that, if
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{12},A_{64},A_{76}\}
, then
\phi_{3}([\mathcal{A}]_{\sim})=[\{A_{21},A_{33},A_{45},A_{57},A_{88}\}]_{\sim}
. We provide an intuitive explanation of how the construction of
\phi_{k}([\mathcal{A}]_{\sim})
proceeds, as this may help the reader grasp the underlying philosophy of the arguments throughout the remainder of the paper.

(1)

Given a
k
-rook configuration in
\mathcal{S}
, consider its canonical arrangement namely
\mathcal{A}
.

(2)

Remove the rows and columns of
\mathcal{S}
that contain a rook of
\mathcal{A}
and glue the remaining rows and columns of
\mathcal{S}
to obtain a square
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of size
n-k
.

(3)

Place the rooks on the diagonal cells of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and map these rooks back into
\mathcal{S}
by placing them in the corresponding cells under the gluing.

We conclude this section with the following result, which will be used throughout the paper. It formulates condition (2) of domino-stability for the cells in
\mathcal{B}_{i}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
, when
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
is non-empty.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a domino-stable collection of cells. Let
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{P})=\{\mathcal{B}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{B}_{p}\}
be the set of all maximal rectangles of
\mathcal{P}
and
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}=\mathcal{B}_{i}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{j\in[p]\setminus\{i\}}\mathcal{B}_{j}\right)
, for
i\in[p]
, with
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\neq\emptyset
. Then for every cell
C
of
\mathcal{B}_{i}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
there exist two unique and distinct indices
j,k\in[p]
such that
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j}}
(respectively,
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{k}}
) contains a cell of
\mathcal{P}
in horizontal (respectively, vertical) position with
C
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove the claim when
\mathcal{P}
is a polyomino. Then, suppose
\mathcal{P}
is a polyomino and consider
C\in\mathcal{B}_{i}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
. Let
\mathcal{H}
be the maximal row of
\mathcal{P}
containing
C
, and let
\mathcal{B}_{j}
be the maximal rectangle in
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{P})
containing
\mathcal{H}
. We claim
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\neq\emptyset
. Suppose, for contradiction, that
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j}}=\emptyset
. Then, by condition (2) of Definition
2.4
, there exists a unique index
h\in[p]\setminus\{i\}
such that
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{h}}
contains a cell of
\mathcal{H}
; let us denote this cell by
D
. Since
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{h}}=\mathcal{B}_{h}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{\alpha\in[p]\setminus\{h\}}\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}\right)
, it follows that
D
cannot be in
\mathcal{B}_{j}
, contradicting the fact that
D\in\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{j}
. Hence,
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\neq\emptyset
. We now show that there exists a cell of
\mathcal{H}
which belongs to
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j}}
. Since
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\neq\emptyset
, we may consider a cell in
\overline{B_{j}}
, that we denote by
E
. If
E\in\mathcal{H}
, then we are done. If
E\notin\mathcal{H}
, then consider the column of
\mathcal{P}
which contains
E
, that we indicate by
\mathcal{C}
. We denote by
A
the unique cell in
\mathcal{H}\cap\mathcal{C}
and we claim that
A\in\overline{B_{j}}
. In fact,
\mathcal{C}
is not contained in
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
, for all
\ell\neq i
, otherwise
E
would not belong to
\overline{B_{j}}
; the conclusion same holds for
\mathcal{H}
. Therefore
A\in\overline{B_{j}}
. To prove the uniqueness of the index
j
, suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists an index
j^{\prime}\in[p]\setminus\{i,j\}
such that
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j^{\prime}}}
contains a cell
A^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{P}
in horizontal position with
C
, so
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
. Since
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{j}
and
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j^{\prime}}}=\mathcal{B}_{j^{\prime}}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{\beta\in[p]\setminus\{j^{\prime}\}}\mathcal{B}_{\beta}\right),
then
A^{\prime}
cannot be in
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j^{\prime}}}
, which is a contradiction. The existence and uniqueness of the index
k\in[p]
such that
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{k}}
contains a cell of
\mathcal{P}
in vertical position with
C
can be proven by applying similar arguments as before. This completes the proof of the proposition.

\square

3.
Domino-Stability as a Sufficient Condition for the Palindromicity of the Switching Rook Polynomial

The aim of this section is to prove that domino-stability provides a sufficient condition for the switching rook polynomial to be palindromic. Before establishing this result, we present a preliminary lemma that describes the arrangement of a maximum number of non-attacking rooks in a domino-stable collection of cells, as well as its uniqueness up to switching operations.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a domino-stable collection of cells. Let
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{P})=\{\mathcal{B}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{B}_{p}\}
and
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P})=\{\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{q}\}
be the sets of maximal rectangles and single squares of
\mathcal{P}
, respectively, where
q\leq p
. Then there exists a unique
r(\mathcal{P})
-rook configuration of
\mathcal{P}
, up to switches, and

r(\mathcal{P})=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\sqrt{\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{S}_{i})}.

Proof.

After suitable labeling of the maximal rectangles of
\mathcal{P}
, we may assume that
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\neq\emptyset
, for all
1\leq i\leq q
. Moreover, we set
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\mathrm{G}(\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}})
and
d_{i}=\sqrt{\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{S}_{i})}
, for each
i\in[q]
. Let
\mathcal{T}_{i}
denote the arrangement of
d_{i}
rooks, placed on the cells of
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
, which correspond under the gluing to the diagonal cells of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
, for all
i\in[q]
(see Discussion
2.8
). Finally, define
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{q}
and set
d=\sum_{i=1}^{q}d_{i}=|\mathcal{T}|
. We now prove that
\mathcal{T}
provides the unique way to place the maximum number of non-attacking rooks on
\mathcal{P}
, up to switches.

1) We firstly show that
\mathcal{T}
is a
d
-rook configuration in
\mathcal{P}
. Let
i\in[q]
. Since each rook in
\mathcal{T}_{i}
is placed in a cell of
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
, and by definition
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}=\mathcal{B}_{i}\setminus\bigcup_{j\neq i}\mathcal{B}_{j}
, it follows that all rooks in
\mathcal{T}_{i}
are in non-attacking positions with those in
\mathcal{T}_{j}
for all
j\neq i
. Moreover, by definition of
\mathcal{T}_{i}
, every rook in
\mathcal{T}_{i}
is in non-attacking position with a rook in
\mathcal{T}_{i}
. Hence,
\mathcal{T}
is a
d
-rook configuration in
\mathcal{P}
.

2) Next, we prove that
\mathcal{T}
is maximal. Suppose, by contradiction, that there exists a
k
-rook configuration
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{P}
with
k>d
such that
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
. It is not restrictive to assume that
k=d+1
, that is,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}\cup\{R\}
, where
R
is a rook not in
\mathcal{T}
. Let
C_{R}
be the cell of
\mathcal{P}
where
R
is placed and
\mathcal{B}_{i}
be a maximal rectangle of
\mathcal{P}
containing
C_{R}
, for some
i\in[p]
. Since
\mathcal{P}
is domino-stable, the following holds.

•

If
C_{R}\in\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
, then
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\neq\emptyset
, so
\mathrm{G}(\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}})
is a square by (1) of Definition
2.4
and all the columns and rows of
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
are occupied by a rook of
\mathcal{T}
. Consequently,
R
is in an attacking position with a rook in
\mathcal{T}
, a contradiction.

•

If
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\neq\emptyset
and
C_{R}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
, then by Proposition
2.9
there exist two cells
A
and
B
of
\mathcal{P}
in horizontal and vertical position with
C_{R}
, and only two distinct indices
j,k\in[p]
such that
A\in\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j}}
and
B\in\overline{\mathcal{B}_{k}}
. Hence
R
is necessarily in an attacking position with a rook of
\mathcal{T}
, which is a contradiction

•

On the other hand, if
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}=\emptyset
, then a similar previous contradiction arises from (2) of Definition
2.4
.

In each case, we get a contradiction; therefore, we can conclude that
\mathcal{T}
is maximal.

3) First, observe that if
\mathcal{U}
is a
d
-rook configuration in
\mathcal{P}
, with all rooks placed only on cells of
\bigcup_{i\in[q]}\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
, then
\mathcal{U}\sim\mathcal{T}
by Discussion
2.8
. We now show that if
\mathcal{U}
is an arrangement of
k
non-attacking rooks, not all placed in cells of
\bigcup_{i\in[q]}\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
, then
k<d
. To this end, we decompose
\mathcal{U}
into
q+1
disjoint subsets: for each
i\in[q]
, let
\mathcal{U}_{i}
consist of the rooks placed on the cells of
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
, and let
\mathcal{Q}
consist of those placed on the cells of
\mathcal{P}
that do not belong to
\bigcup_{i\in[q]}\overline{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
. For each
i\in[q]
, let
r_{i}=|\mathcal{U}_{i}|
, so that
0\leq r_{i}\leq d_{i}
. Define
r=\sum_{i=1}^{q}r_{i}
, and note that
\sum_{i=1}^{q}(d_{i}-r_{i})=d-r
. We now claim that
2|\mathcal{Q}|\leq d-r
. Each rook
R
in
\mathcal{Q}
is placed on a cell, denoted by
C_{R}
, of
\mathcal{B}_{j}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j}}
, for some
j\in[p]
. By (2) of Definition
2.4
or Proposition
2.9
, in correspondence of
C_{R}
, there exist two unique and distinct indices
j,k\in[q]
such that
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{j}}
(respectively,
\overline{\mathcal{B}_{k}}
) contains a cell of
\mathcal{P}
in horizontal (respectively, vertical) position with
C_{R}
. For all
i\in[q]
, let
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{i}}
the set of rooks of
\mathcal{T}_{i}
which are in attacking position with some rook of
\mathcal{Q}
. Then
|\overline{\mathcal{T}_{i}}|\leq d_{i}-r_{i}
. Observe that, for each rook
T
in
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{i}}
,
T
attacks a unique rook in
\mathcal{Q}
, which in turn is attacked by a unique rook in
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{j}}
, for some
j\neq i
. Then
2|\mathcal{Q}|=\sum_{i=1}^{q}|\overline{\mathcal{T}_{i}}|\leq d-r
. Hence, it follows that
2|\mathcal{Q}|\leq d-r
, in particular,
|\mathcal{Q}|\leq d-r-|\mathcal{Q}|
. Therefore, since
\mathcal{U}=\left(\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{q}\mathcal{U}_{i}\right)\sqcup\mathcal{Q}
and from the inequality
|\mathcal{Q}|\leq d-r-|\mathcal{Q}|
, we obtain
k=r+|\mathcal{Q}|\leq d-r-|\mathcal{Q}|+r=d-|\mathcal{Q}|<d.

In conclusion,
\mathcal{T}
yields the unique
r(\mathcal{P})
-rook configuration of
\mathcal{P}
, up to switches, thus completing the proof of our claim, since
r(\mathcal{P})=|\mathcal{T}|=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\sqrt{\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{S}_{i})}
.

\square

We are now ready to prove that the switching rook polynomial of a domino-stable collection of cells is always palindromic. The key idea is that, starting from the

-rook configuration described in the Lemma

4.
Domino-Stability as a Necessary Condition for the Palindromicity of the Switching Rook Polynomial

Just as the previous section established a sufficient condition for the palindromicity, we now show that domino-stability is also a necessary condition for a switching rook polynomial to be palindromic. Before presenting the main result, we state a preliminary lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of cells,
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{P})=\{\mathcal{B}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{B}_{p}\}
be the set of maximal rectangles of
\mathcal{P}
and
\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\tilde{r}_{j}(\mathcal{P})
be the switching rook polynomial of
\mathcal{P}
, where
d=r(\mathcal{P})
. Suppose that
\tilde{r}_{d}(\mathcal{P})=1
and let
\mathcal{T}
be the unique canonical
r(\mathcal{P})
-rook configuration in
\mathcal{P}
(with respect to
\mathcal{B}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{B}_{p}
). Then:

(1)

every column of
\mathcal{P}
contains a cell occupied by a rook of
\mathcal{T}
, and the same holds for every row of
\mathcal{P}
;

(2)

if
R
is a rook placed in a cell of
\mathcal{P}
, that does not contain any rook of
\mathcal{T}
, then there exist two unique rooks in
\mathcal{T}
that are in attacking position with
R

Proof.

(1) Assume by contradiction that
C
is a column of
\mathcal{P}
whose cells do not have any rook of
\mathcal{T}
. Now, if there were a row

of
\mathcal{P}
that intersects

\mathcal{T}
, then we could consider
\mathcal{T}\cup\{F\}
, where

is a rook placed in the cell
C\cap L

\mathcal{T}
. Therefore, there must be a rook
T
in a cell of every row of
\mathcal{P}
that intersects
C
. Now, select a rook

in a row

, and consider
(\mathcal{T}\setminus\{T\})\cup\{T^{\prime}\}
, where
T^{\prime}
is placed in the cell

. This provides a

-rook configuration in
\mathcal{P}
that is not equivalent to
\mathcal{T}
, contradicting the assumption
\tilde{r}_{d}(\mathcal{P})=1
. A similar argument shows that every row of
\mathcal{P}
contains a cell occupied by a rook of
\mathcal{T}
.

(2) Suppose that there are no two rooks of
\mathcal{T}
that are in attacking position with
R
. Indeed, if no rook of
\mathcal{T}
is in an attacking position with
R
, then
\mathcal{T}\cup\{R\}
would be a set of non-attacking rooks, which contradicts the maximality of
\mathcal{T}
. Furthermore, suppose that there exists no rook of
\mathcal{T}
which is in the same row (respectively, column) of
R

is in the same column (respectively, row) of a rook

of
\mathcal{T}
. In this case, replacing
T
with
R
yields a new configuration
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{T}\setminus\{T\})\sqcup\{R\}\in{\mathcal{R}}_{d}(\mathcal{P})
. Since
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is obtained from
\mathcal{T}
by swapping a rook in the same column (respectively, row), then
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
are not equivalent with respect to

. Finally, to prove uniqueness, it is enough to observe that we cannot have three rooks of

\mathcal{T}

.

\square

-Minor Ideals

In this section, we present the main theorem of the paper, which provides a complete characterization of the palindromicity of switching rook polynomials in terms of the geometry of the related collections of cells.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of cells and
\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
be the switching rook polynomial of
\mathcal{P}
. Then,
\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
is palindromic if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is domino-stable.

Proof.

This result follows directly from Propositions
3.2
and

.

\square

inner
2
-minor
of
\mathcal{P}
. The ideal
I_{\mathcal{P}}
of
S_{\mathcal{P}}
generated by all inner
2
-minors of
\mathcal{P}
is called the
ideal of
2
-minors
of
\mathcal{P}
. The quotient ring
K[\mathcal{P}]=S_{\mathcal{P}}/I_{\mathcal{P}}
is called the
coordinate ring
of
\mathcal{P}
.

As an application of Theorem
5.1
, combined with Theorem
5.2
, we obtain the following immediate result.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a collection of cells such that
h_{K[\mathcal{P}]}(t)=\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
.

(1)

If
K[\mathcal{P}]
is Gorenstein, then
\mathcal{P}
is domino-stable;

(2)

Suppose that
K[\mathcal{P}]
is a Cohen-Macaulay domain, then the converse of (1) is true: that is, if
\mathcal{P}
is domino-stable, then
K[\mathcal{P}]
is Gorenstein.

To highlight the scope of Corollary
5.3
, we present the following example.

Example 5.4
.

In
[
21
]
it was proven that if
\mathcal{P}
is a simple polyomino (that is, a polyomino without holes), then
K[\mathcal{P}]
is a normal Cohen–Macaulay domain. In particular, for a parallelogram polyomino
\mathcal{P}
(a special class of simple polyominoes that can be viewed as a planar distributive lattice when regarded as a poset)
[
20
, Theorem 3.5]
shows that
h_{K[\mathcal{P}]}(t)=\tilde{r}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)
. Hence, Corollary
5.3
encompasses
[
20
, Theorem 4.10]
. From this perspective, domino stability extends the
S
-property introduced in
[
20
, Definition 4.1]
. For example, the polyomino in Figure
6
(A) is domino-stable (and thus its coordinate ring is Gorenstein), but it does not satisfy the
S
-property. Moreover, parallelogram polyominoes can also be regarded as skew diagrams; in this setting, Theorem
5.1
recovers
[
1
, Corollary 5.16]
.

Furthermore, in
[
10
]
and
[
18
]
the question of Gorensteinness was not initially addressed due to its difficulty. Corollary
5.3
now provides such a description: for convex collections of cells considered in
[
18
]
via
[
18
, Corollary 4.2]
, and for frame polyominoes studied in
[
10
]
via
[
10
, Proposition 3.2 and Theorem 4.7]
. In Figures

The previous corollary motivates us to search for additional geometric conditions on the shape of domino-stable collections of cells that would yield a sufficient criterion for the Gorenstein property. However, the computational evidence suggests a stronger phenomenon: domino-stability alone already appears to be sufficient for the Gorenstein property. By using Theorem
5.1
, we can computationally establish the following result.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a domino-stable collection of cells with rank less than or equal to
10
or a domino-stable polyomino with rank less than or equal to
12
. Then
K[\mathcal{P}]
is Gorenstein.

Proof.

Observe that, if
\mathcal{P}
is a domino-stable collection of cells and
K[\mathcal{P}]
is a Cohen–Macaulay domain, then by (2) of Corollary
5.3
it follows that
K[\mathcal{P}]
is also Gorenstein. Then, it is enough to restrict ourselves to the case where
I_{\mathcal{P}}
is not prime. We implemented scripts in
SageMath

[
25
]
and
Macaulay2

[
9
]
, which are available in
[
16
]
, and here described.

(A) Compute the set
L
of all collections of cells of rank
n
(using
SageMath
). The resulting datasets, that is, plain-text files listing all tested collections of cells and polyominoes, are included in
[
16
,
sage
]
.

(B) We need to verify that, if
\mathcal{P}
is a domino-stable collection of cells and
K[\mathcal{P}]
is not a domain, then
K[\mathcal{P}]
is Gorenstein. For this purpose, we provide a concise
Macaulay2
algorithm (see the file
TestGorenstein.m2
in
[
16
]
), which implements the following steps:

(1)

for a given collection of cells
\mathcal{P}
, it computes the inner
2
-minor ideal of
\mathcal{P}
;

(2)

it discards the non-prime instances by using the function
binomialIsPrime
, provided in the package
Binomials
(
[
11
]
);

(3)

it retains only those with a palindromic
h
-polynomial, using a short preliminary auxiliary function
isPalindromic
;

(4)

it verifies the Gorenstein property of
K[\mathcal{P}]
, by using
isGorenstein
provided in the package
TorAlgebra
(
[
3
]
).

Indeed, filtering out domino-stable collections of cells from the lists of all collections of cells (or polyominoes) of the given ranks reduces to checking the palindromicity of the
h
-polynomial, by
[
18
, Theorem 2.3]
and Theorem
5.1
.

n\leq 8

Conjecture 5.6
.

Let
