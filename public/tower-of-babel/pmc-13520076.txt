Hearing thresholds above age- and sex-based norms and an under-recognized hearing screening gap in short-haul truck drivers in Los Angeles, California: a cross-sectional pilot study
Authors: Rosemelly Jimenez Medal, Donald Jimenez, Sai Venkat Mandalapu, Erica D Walker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo characterize hearing, noise behaviors, and awareness of hearing difficulty in predominantly Hispanic/Latino short-haul truck drivers, and to compare measured thresholds with age- and sex-based norms.MethodsIn this cross-sectional pilot study we studied 50 short-haul truck drivers in Los Angeles, California, USA, in July 2023. Each completed a health and noise survey and automated audiometry from 250 to 8000 Hz; workplace and in-cab sound levels were measured with a smartphone meter. Thresholds were compared with ISO 7029:2017 age- and sex-based normative values, including their position within the normative distribution.ResultsMean age was 51.8 years; 88.6% identified as Hispanic/Latino and 87.0% used no hearing protection. Thresholds exceeded normative medians at every frequency; the sample median fell at or above the 76th percentile across frequencies, most extreme at low frequencies. In an exploratory model, age was associated with the better-ear pure-tone average; years of driving, noise sensitivity, and cardiovascular risk showed no independent association. Among drivers with measured high-frequency loss, 59% reported no perceived decline.ConclusionsShort-haul truck drivers had hearing poorer than age- and sex-based norms across frequencies, rarely used protection, and were often unaware of measurable loss. These findings support accessible hearing screening and protection in this underserved workforce.Keywords: Hearing, Occupational health, Truck drivers, Audiometry, Hearing screening, Health disparities

Introduction

Hearing loss is among the most common chronic conditions worldwide, and much of it is avoidable. The World Health Organization estimates that more than 430 million people live with disabling hearing loss, a number projected to keep rising (World Health Organization, 2021). Occupational noise is a leading preventable contributor: roughly 22 million United States workers are exposed to hazardous noise on the job, and repeated exposure at or above 85 A-weighted decibels can cause permanent sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, and difficulty understanding speech in noise (Themann and Masterson, 2019). Once it develops, noise-induced hearing loss does not recover, which makes prevention and early detection important (Themann and Masterson, 2019; Basner et al., 2014).

Hearing health is a particular concern in trucking. Drivers can encounter persistent noise from engine idling, highway traffic, and radios inside the cab, and from locomotives, ships, and heavy cargo machinery such as forklifts, yard tractors, and cranes at ports and rail yards (Fu et al., 2010; Khoo, 2011). Because drivers can work long on-duty windows, up to 11 h of driving within a 14-h window (Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, 2026a), these exposures can approach or exceed occupational limits such as those in the Occupational Safety and Health Administration Hearing Conservation Program (Occupational Safety and Health Administration, 2026). Studies of truck and bus drivers have reported a high prevalence of hearing loss, often concentrated in the higher frequencies (Mehrotra et al., 2025; Karimi et al., 2010; Pourabdian et al., 2019).

How commercial drivers are screened may also work against early detection. The federal medical standard assesses hearing with a forced whispered-voice test or, when audiometry is used, the average threshold at 500, 1000, and 2000 Hz (Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, 2026b). Those frequencies matter most for speech but are not where noise-induced damage first appears, so a driver can have substantial high-frequency loss and still pass. This gap matters most for workers who already face barriers to care. Hispanic and Latino adults are overrepresented in transportation work, and population data tie hearing loss in this group to lower income and less education, markers of reduced access to screening and protection (Cruickshanks et al., 2015; Daviglus et al., 2012). Awareness compounds the problem: in national data, nearly one in four adults who rated their hearing as good or excellent already had measurable loss on testing (Carroll et al., 2017).

We carried out a cross-sectional pilot study in a predominantly Hispanic/Latino group of short-haul truck drivers in southern California, where the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach make the region a major hub for United States trade (United States Department of Transportation, Bureau of Transportation Statistics, 2024) and a plausible setting for occupational noise exposure. The study was co-created with the community after a concerned member noticed changes in their own hearing. Our aims were descriptive: to characterize drivers' thresholds relative to age- and sex-based norms, to describe their self-reported noise behaviors and sound environment, and to compare measured hearing with drivers' own perception of it. Rather than attributing hearing loss to any single cause, we set out to document how far thresholds depart from normative expectations and how often measurable loss goes unrecognized, two features prior work has rarely combined.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a cross-sectional pilot study of 50 short-haul truck drivers recruited by convenience through community contacts in the Los Angeles metropolitan area in July 2023. Drivers were eligible if currently working and willing to complete a survey and a hearing test. To limit non-occupational contributions to the audiometric findings, individuals who reported a known hearing or ear health condition, including diagnosed ear disease or current hearing-aid use, were not enrolled; this screening was by self-report. Fifty drivers were approached, met these criteria, and completed the protocol; analytic sample sizes vary by measure because of item nonresponse and, for some audiometric summaries, incomplete threshold data, with denominators reported throughout. The protocol was approved by the Brown University Institutional Review Board (IRB #STUDY00000009) on 14 June 2023, and all participants gave written informed consent before any procedures.

Survey

The survey was fielded in English and Spanish and piloted with community stakeholders; the Spanish version was refined with bilingual community members for accuracy and cultural appropriateness. Participants reported age, sex, years of driving, and route type; physician-diagnosed conditions (high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, obesity, heart disease, and stroke); noise sensitivity on a five-point scale; and whether they thought their hearing had changed, had ever had it tested, considered their workplace noisy, used hearing protection, and did anything to reduce noise exposure.

Audiometry

Air-conduction hearing thresholds were measured at 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000, and 8000 Hz in each ear using a calibrated tablet-based automated audiometer (SHOEBOX PureTest™; Shoebox Ltd., Ottawa, ON, Canada) delivered through circumaural (over-ear) RadioEar DD450 headphones on an iPad, using the device's automated modified Hughson-Westlake procedure, which conforms to ANSI S3.6, CSA Z107.6, and IEC 60645-1. Testing was conducted in a quiet room or in the field; the device continuously monitors ambient noise and defers or repeats presentations when background levels are too high, proceeding only when levels are acceptable. Tablet audiometry of this type agrees closely with sound-booth testing, most thresholds falling within 10 dB of booth values, and is validated for use outside a booth (Thompson et al., 2015). For each participant we computed the pure-tone average across 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz and a high-frequency average across 4000 and 8000 Hz, and defined the better and worse ear from the pure-tone average.

Sound level measurements

Workplace and in-cab sound levels were measured with a smartphone sound level meter (the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health Sound Level Meter application, version 1.2.5, on an iPhone 12), which agrees with reference instruments to within about 2 dB across typical occupational exposures, supporting field screening (Kardous and Shaw, 2014). Measurements were taken at two trucking yards, at three port locations, and inside the cab while driving on the Interstate 710 freeway under three conditions: windows up with no music, windows down, and windows up with music. At each location or condition we recorded the one-minute equivalent continuous A-weighted level, the A-weighted maximum, and the C-weighted peak, and averaged approximately five one-minute readings. The operator held the phone at arm's length with the lower microphone pointed toward the sound source and stepped back from the device to avoid blocking or reflecting sound, and before each session the application was checked against a calibrated Class 1 reference meter (Cirrus Optimus, North Yorkshire, United Kingdom). These were short-duration environmental measurements, not personal 8-h time-weighted exposures or dose. For descriptive context only, we expressed each measured A-weighted level as an illustrative allowable daily duration under the recommended exposure limit (85 dB(A) as an 8-h average, 3-dB exchange rate) (National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, 1998); this is not an assessment against the Occupational Safety and Health Administration permissible exposure limit (90 dB(A) limit, 85 dB(A) action level, 5-dB exchange rate).

Comparison with normative thresholds

To place thresholds in the context of normal aging, we compared each measured threshold with the expected distribution for a person of the same age and sex using the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 7029:2017 (International Organization for Standardization, 2017). For every participant and frequency we computed the expected median and defined the excess as the observed threshold minus that median. Because ISO 7029:2017 also specifies the statistical spread of thresholds in an otologically normal population, we additionally expressed each observed threshold as a percentile of that distribution, using the standard's upper standard deviation because the sample lay above the median. We tested whether the mean excess differed from zero at each frequency with one-sample t-tests and report the proportion above the median and the sample percentile position. All audiometric comparisons used participant-level values (binaural means, or the better ear where specified), so each participant contributed one observation and the two ears were not treated as independent. The computation is described in the Supplementary Methods. As the standard notes, normative data cannot separate an individual's age-related and other causes, so the excess should be read as hearing poorer than age and sex would predict, from any cause (International Organization for Standardization, 2017).

Statistical analysis

We summarized characteristics with means and standard deviations or counts and percentages, based on non-missing responses with denominators reported. Left and right thresholds were compared with paired t-tests, and proportions are reported with 95% confidence intervals. Predictors of the better-ear pure-tone average were examined with an exploratory multivariable linear regression including age, years of driving, self-reported noise sensitivity, and any cardiovascular risk factor (high blood pressure, high cholesterol, or diabetes); for comparison we refit the model with the high-frequency average as the outcome. Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidance. Analyses used R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with two-sided p < 0.05 considered significant. This study met the Brown University Human Research Protection Program guidelines for the protection of human subjects with respect to safety and privacy; it was approved by the Brown University Institutional Review Board (IRB #STUDY00000009, approved 14 June 2023), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Results

Participants

The 50 drivers had a mean age of 51.8 years (standard deviation 11.5; range 31 to 70) and a mean of 17.8 years of driving (standard deviation 10.1). Most were male (45 of 48, 93.8%) and Hispanic or Latino (39 of 44, 88.6%), and 93.8% drove short-haul routes. Cardiovascular risk factors were common, with at least one present in 39.6%. Self-reported behaviors pointed to limited prevention and screening: 38.8% had never had their hearing tested, 53.2% described their workplace as noisy, and only 13.0% used hearing protection (87.0% used none). About a third (34.7%) felt their hearing had declined, and 60.9% did nothing to reduce noise exposure (Table 1).

Workplace sound levels

Equivalent A-weighted levels at the yards and ports ranged from 72.7 to 82.0 dB(A). Inside the cab, levels rose with open windows and music, from 79.8 dB(A) (windows up, no music) to 85.5 dB(A) (windows down) and 94.1 dB(A) (windows up, music), with C-weighted peaks up to 115.0 dB. The highest C-weighted peak across all locations, 123.7 dB, was recorded at Port 2. Two of the eight measured conditions were at or above 85 dB(A), both in the cab and involving open windows or music, with the illustrative allowable duration falling to roughly one hour at the highest-level condition (Table 2). Because these were short environmental measurements rather than personal exposures, they describe the sound environment drivers can encounter rather than a measured dose.

Hearing thresholds relative to age- and sex-based norms

Measured thresholds exceeded the ISO 7029 expected median at every frequency, and every comparison was significant (all p < 0.001; Table 3). The excess ranged from about 11 to 19 dB and was largest at 4000 Hz (19.3 dB; 95% CI 14.6, 23.9); the pure-tone average across 0.5 to 4 kHz exceeded its expected value by 14.1 dB (95% CI 11.4, 16.7). Framed within the normative distribution, however, the sample was elevated across the whole range, not only in the high frequencies: the sample median sat at or above the 99th percentile at 250 and 500 Hz and between the 76th and 96th elsewhere (Table 3, Fig. 1). The departure from normal was thus at least as pronounced in the low frequencies as in the high, a pattern not specific to noise. The loss was symmetric: thresholds did not differ between the left and right ears at any frequency (all paired p > 0.10).

Severity and high-frequency loss

By the World Health Organization grades applied to the better-ear pure-tone average, 52% of drivers had no impairment, 44% had mild loss, and 4% had moderate loss, with none in the moderately severe or worse categories (Supplementary Table S1). Any loss in the better ear, defined as a pure-tone average of 20 dB or more, was present in 24 of 50 drivers (48%; 95% CI 35, 61). High-frequency loss of 25 dB or more was present in 28 of 49 better ears (57%; 95% CI 43, 70) and in 35 of 49 worse ears (71%; 95% CI 58, 82).

Predictors of hearing

In the exploratory model for the better-ear pure-tone average, age was associated with hearing (0.33 dB per year; 95% CI 0.04, 0.63). Years of driving, strongly correlated with age (r = 0.71), showed no independent association after adjustment (0.10 dB per year; 95% CI −0.26, 0.45), and none was detected for noise sensitivity or any cardiovascular risk factor (Table 4). Refitting with the high-frequency average as the outcome gave the same pattern, with age again associated with hearing (0.88 dB per year; 95% CI 0.37, 1.40).

The gap between measured and perceived loss

Measured high-frequency loss was more common than the loss drivers reported: while 57% of better ears showed high-frequency loss, only 34.7% felt their hearing had changed. Among drivers with measured high-frequency loss who answered the perception question, 16 of 27 (59%) reported no decline at all, compared with a smaller share of those without measured loss (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S2). With the rarity of hearing protection and the finding that more than a third had never been tested, this describes a workforce largely unaware of a measurable loss.

Discussion

In this pilot study of short-haul truck drivers, hearing thresholds were poorer than age- and sex-based norms at every frequency. Placed within the ISO 7029 distribution, the sample median sat at the upper end of the normal range across frequencies, with the departure at least as large in the low frequencies as in the high. High-frequency loss affected most ears, hearing protection was almost never used, and most drivers with measurable loss did not know they had it. Together these findings describe a consistent picture of underprotection, underscreening, and limited awareness in a workforce rarely studied in the United States, rather than a single dramatic result.

We are deliberately cautious about attributing this excess to occupational noise. Noise-induced hearing loss characteristically begins as a notch in the 3000 to 6000 Hz range and deepens with continued exposure (Themann and Masterson, 2019; Lie et al., 2016). Our data do not match that signature: the elevation was broad and, if anything, most pronounced at the low frequencies, the opposite of what isolated noise would produce (Lie et al., 2016; Lutman et al., 2016). The contrast between the largest raw excess, at 4000 Hz, and the highest percentile positions, at 250 and 500 Hz, partly reflects the ISO 7029 normative distribution itself, whose spread widens at 3000 to 6000 Hz, so that a given elevation corresponds to a lower percentile in that range. These low-frequency findings warrant particular caution, because thresholds at 250 and 500 Hz are especially sensitive to ambient noise, middle-ear conditions, and field-testing artifacts, any of which could contribute to the observed elevation. A departure of this kind is more consistent with a mixture of influences, potentially including field measurement conditions, unassessed middle-ear or other otological factors, and the social and health circumstances of an underserved population, than with occupational noise alone. Because participants were screened only by self-report, we tested only two high frequencies, and the sound levels were short environmental measurements rather than dosimetry, the excess over ISO norms should be read as hearing poorer than expected for age and sex from any cause. This design can document that departure and the accompanying screening and awareness gaps; it cannot estimate the share attributable to occupational noise, and we do not attempt to.

The loss was symmetric, with no left-right difference at any frequency, unlike the asymmetry reported in some driver studies (Mehrotra et al., 2025; Pourabdian et al., 2019), which argues against a strong one-sided effect such as exposure through an open driver-side window.

Age was associated with the pure-tone average, and years of driving did not add to it once age was accounted for, which is unsurprising given how closely the two were correlated and given that this design cannot separate cumulative exposure from aging. The cardiovascular risk factors we examined were not associated with hearing. Occupational noise has been linked to cardiovascular conditions and high-frequency loss in larger datasets (Kerns et al., 2018; Gan and Mannino, 2018); the absence of such relationships here most likely reflects limited power in a sample of 50 rather than evidence against the associations.

The screening and awareness findings have direct prevention implications. Nearly six in ten drivers with measured high-frequency loss perceived no change, echoing national data in which many adults with good self-rated hearing already have measurable loss (Carroll et al., 2017). More than a third had never been tested, and protection use was rare, in line with low use among noise-exposed United States workers and transportation workers in particular (Tak et al., 2009; Masterson et al., 2013). Because the federal standard evaluates hearing only at 500, 1000, and 2000 Hz (Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, 2026b), it is by design insensitive to high-frequency loss, so routine certification will tend to miss early damage regardless of cause. Accessible screening that includes the high frequencies, with ready access to protection and education, would be a reasonable response. Although the regulatory specifics here are those of the United States, the broader problem of unrecognized hearing loss among potentially noise-exposed commercial drivers is likely relevant in many countries with large trucking workforces.

This community-engaged work surfaced a problem that falls disproportionately on an underserved, predominantly Hispanic and Latino workforce, in which hearing loss is tied to lower income and education and access to care is uneven (Cruickshanks et al., 2015; Daviglus et al., 2012). A workforce that is potentially exposed, rarely protected, seldom screened, and largely unaware represents a clear opportunity for prevention rather than an inevitability.

The study combines objective audiometry, on-site sound-level measurement, and a normative comparison in an understudied workforce, and it pairs measured hearing with drivers' own perceptions, which reveals the awareness gap. The limitations shape what can be concluded. The sample was small, convenience-drawn from a single region, and cross-sectional, so it cannot establish prevalence or causality. Participants were screened only by self-report and not otologically examined, so non-occupational causes of the elevation cannot be excluded; the broad across-frequency pattern makes such contributions plausible. We tested only two high frequencies and could not apply notch-based definitions. The smartphone meter and tablet audiometer, though validated for field use (Thompson et al., 2015; Kardous and Shaw, 2014), are not laboratory-grade, and field conditions may have influenced thresholds despite the device's ambient-noise monitoring; the sound measurements characterized the environment, not personal dose. There was no non-driver comparison group, and exposures and behaviors were self-reported. These constraints are typical of pilot work and point to the next step: a larger, controlled study with personal noise dosimetry, full-frequency audiometry, and otologic screening.

Conclusion

In this pilot study, short-haul truck drivers had hearing poorer than age- and sex-based norms across the frequency range, rarely used hearing protection, and were mostly unaware of a measurable loss. Because the departure from normal was broad rather than a high-frequency notch, and because the study could not assess otological or exposure history, the findings should be read as describing a screening and awareness gap in an underserved workforce rather than as evidence of noise-induced injury. Accessible screening that includes the high frequencies, ready access to protection, and targeted education are warranted, and a larger controlled study with personal noise dosimetry and full audiometric testing is the logical next step.
