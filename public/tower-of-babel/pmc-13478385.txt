Burden of incident cardiovascular events in giant cell arteritis using data from a nationwide French cohort
Authors: B. Jauzelon, T. Pereira dos Santos, C. Roubille, C. Dumain, N. Singh, Q. Gomes de Pinho, B. Granel, T. Mura, Radjiv Goulabchand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Giant cell arteritis (GCA) is one of the most common adult vasculitis. As large-scale studies on cardiovascular events in GCA are missing in France, our study examine their incidence using the French national medico-administrative health database. We included patients hospitalized for GCA between January 2013 and December 2022 who received an initial prescription of corticosteroids ≥ 1000 mg over 40 days within one year of the hospitalization. Each patient was matched with three controls by age, sex, and residence. Cardiovascular events and mortality were compared using a multivariate Cox model, adjusting for hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity, sleep apnoea syndrome, chronic kidney disease, and prior cardiovascular conditions. A total of 12,955 GCA patients (67% female) and 33,685 controls were analyzed. Baseline cardiovascular risk factors were more prevalent in patients with GCA. Patients with GCA had a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 1.21 [1.15–1.27]), and major cardiovascular events (1.51 [1.41–1.62]), including myocardial infarction (1.58 [1.36–1.85]), stroke (1.29 [1.14–1.47]), and heart failure (1.41 [1.30–1.52]). A better screening and management of cardiovascular factors could improve overall outcomes in GCA patients. Further studies are needed to determine the impact of treatment strategies, and to better identify at-risk patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55708-1.

Introduction

Giant cell arteritis (GCA) is the most common vasculitis in adults over 501,2, with potential complications including vision loss and aortic aneurysms3. While much of the literature has focused on pathophysiology and treatment strategies, there is a growing need to better understand the overall prognosis of GCA patients, particularly regarding cardiovascular outcomes, which are strongly associated with mortality.

In other chronic inflammatory autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis4 or psoriasis5, an increased risk of cardiovascular events has been well documented. For GCA, data remain heterogeneous. A retrospective study using a US national database reported that 15% of 6-month readmissions in GCA patients were due to cardiovascular events6. European studies have also suggested a higher incidence of major cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure7–10. However, smaller studies11,12 and a meta-analysis13 risk did not report this association on myocardial infarction. Regarding mortality, some French studies tended to show an increased cardiovascular mortality rate in patients with GCA14,15.

There is still a lack of large-scale studies focusing on French GCA patient regarding cardiovascular outcomes. Moreover, the respective roles of patient background, disease characteristics, and treatment strategies in the development of cardiovascular complications remain unclear. Addressing these gaps is essential to improve patient prognosis.

Our study aims to evaluate the incidence of cardiovascular events in French patients diagnosed with GCA, compared to matched controls from the general population, using data from the French National Health Data System (Système national des données de santé, SNDS). Analyzing the extensive and detailed records available in the SNDS, we seek to provide a robust analysis of the cardiovascular risk profile in GCA patients.

Materials and methods

Data source

We performed a historical paired exposed-unexposed cohort study using the French national medico-administrative health database (“Système National des Données de Santé”, SNDS) which includes (1) the French National Hospital discharge database (resulting from the “programme de médicalisation des systèmes d’information”, PMSI database), (2) the French public health insurance database (“Système National d’Information Interrégimes de l’Assurance Maladie”, SNIIRAM database), and (3) the French causes of death database (“ Centre d’épidémiologie sur les causes médicales de décès”, CepiDC database). This database covers nearly 99% of the population (66 million inhabitants in 2015). The available data are age, sex, entry and discharge dates and diagnoses’ codes associated to the hospitalisations (according to International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10)), treatment delivery, prescribed doses of treatment and dates and cause of death.

GCA patients’ group

We searched for all patients over 50 years old hospitalized in France with a diagnosis of GCA between January 1st, 2011 and December 31st, 2022, using International statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) code associated with hospitalisations for GCA+/-Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR) (M315 and M316). Patients whose first code of GCA was identified in 2011 or 2012 were excluded of our analyses to have a minimum of two years of records prior to the identification date to collect medical history. We thus included only patients with incident GCA, with a first hospitalisation between January 1st, 2013 and December 31st, 2022. The date of first hospitalization was defined as the index date.

Because our objective was to identify truly incident GCA cases, we applied several sequential filters. First, we restricted the cohort to patients with GCA listed as the primary diagnosis (DP), to ensure that GCA was the main reason for hospitalization, thereby improving diagnostic specificity and reducing misclassification due to incidental or uncertain coding. Second, we aimed to capture patients receiving an adequate corticosteroid dose in close temporal relationship with the diagnosis. Therefore, only patients who received at least 1000 mg of prednisone-equivalent over a 40-day period were included. This minimal dose aligns with national recommendations for the early phase of GCA management and reflects the minimal expected exposure for newly diagnosed patients. We required that this minimal dose be delivered within one year of the index date, because initiation of prescriptions beyond this period could reflect pre-existing GCA, late relapses, or corticosteroid use for unrelated conditions. Third, individuals with other autoimmune diseases were excluded (codes are detailed in Supplementary Data 1).

Additionally, patients who died within 90 days of the index date were excluded to reduce misclassification of incident GCA, as early death may indicate hospitalization driven by another severe acute condition rather than GCA. The study timeline is detailed in Supplementary Data 2.

Control group

For each GCA patient, three controls were randomly selected from the French SNDS database, matched by age, sex, and area of residence. The control’s index date was defined as that of the matched GCA case. Controls who died before or within 90 days of the index date were excluded to ensure comparability (Fig. 1).

Outcomes

We collected first occurrences of cardiovascular events (CVE): myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, peripheral artery disease, atrial fibrillation, and conduction disorders; cardiovascular risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia (defined as use of hypolipidemic agents), obesity; and associated conditions: obstructive sleep apnoea and chronic kidney disease, along with death and its cause. Outcomes were identified via ICD-10 hospitalization codes and/or treatment delivery (codes are detailed in Supplementary Data 3). Only events occurring after the index date were considered. For each condition, subjects with prior hospitalization for a studied condition, and their matched pairs, were excluded.

We also defined a composite outcome—Major Adverse Cardiovascular Event (MACE)—as the first occurrence of myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, or cardiovascular death.

Death data were exhaustively collected from the CepiDC database, with cause of death available for ~ 90% of cases between 2013 and 2021, based on physician-coded death certificates.

Covariates

We collected potential confounders linked to cardiovascular events, including demographics (age, sex, residence) and medical history prior to GCA identification (based on corticosteroid delivery or hospitalization date), using the same algorithm as for outcomes (codes are detailed in Supplementary Data 3).

For GCA patients, ophthalmologic complications occurring within ± 3 months of the index date were identified via ICD-10 codes (codes are detailed in Supplementary Data 4).

Statistical analyses

The characteristics and medical history of patients at the index date were described by GCA status. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and proportions, and compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Quantitative variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), and compared using the Mann-Whitney rank-sum test. We also separately analyzed the medical history of patients under 65yo.

All-cause mortality between GCA patients and controls was illustrated with a Kaplan-Meier curve and compared using a Log-Rank test. CVE incidences were calculated in each group with 95% confidence intervals (CI), excluding patients with prior history of the studied condition. Cox proportional hazards models, stratified by matched pairs, were used to compare outcome incidence between GCA patients and controls. Follow-up time was defined as the interval between index date and first event or January 1st, 2023, whichever came first. To account for competing risks, patients who died before this date without an event were censored at death16. The same method was applied to cardiovascular mortality, censoring at other causes of death. Multivariate Cox models were adjusted for previously described confounders.

For the risk of death from any cause, we also wanted to study changes in HR over time for patients with GCA. To flexibly model the time-varying effect of the GCA, we used an interaction term between the GCA group and a natural cubic spline function of time. The spline had two degrees of freedom, allowing for a smooth and data-driven representation of the hazard ratio over time. From the fitted model, we extracted time-specific hazard ratios and their 95% confidence intervals, which were then plotted to illustrate the variation in effect over the follow-up period.

We then performed sensitivity analyses, excluding patients and controls who died within the year following hospitalization.

Statistical analyses were performed at the conventional two-tailed α level of 0.05 using R software version 4.3.3.

Ethics, approval and consent

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study was based on pseudonymized data and was carried out under the French regulatory framework for secondary use of health data, in accordance with the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the CNIL (French National Commission for Information Technology and Civil Liberties) reference methodology MR-005. In accordance with French law, individual informed consent was not required as the study relied on previously collected administrative data used for public interest research purposes. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the CNIL (French National Commission for Information Technology and Civil Liberties) waived the need for ethics approval and informed consent.

Results

Population

A total of 35,746 patients were hospitalized in France with a diagnosis code of GCA ± PMR between January 1st, 2013 and December 31st, 2022. After the exclusion of patients without control, those who deceased in the 90 days after index date (cause of deaths for those patients are available in Supplementary data 5), and those without a corticosteroid delivery of at least 1000 mg prednisone-equivalent dose over 40 days within the year around the hospitalization, we selected a cohort of 12,955 patients and 35,333 controls for the main analyses (see Fig. 1).

To explore whether the excess risk of death associated with GCA changed over time, we modelled a time-varying hazard ratio. This analysis showed that mortality risk was significantly increased during the first three years after index date, decreased thereafter, and rose again after approximately seven years of follow-up (Fig. 3).

The causes of death further support this temporal pattern. Indeed, among patients with GCA who died within the first three years (n = 893), cardiovascular causes were predominant (21.4%), compared with infectious (11.7%) and neoplastic causes (12.7%). Among those who died after seven years of follow-up (n = 121), infectious causes became the leading causes (18.3%), closely followed by cardiovascular causes (17.8%) and neoplasms (15%).

Cardiovascular events

Table 2 reports the absolute number of incident cardiovascular events among GCA cases and controls. There were significantly more incident cardiovascular events in patients with GCA for all studied events (p-value < 0.001 for all). Results of both unadjusted and adjusted Cox model for cardiovascular events are also reported. All studied cardiovascular events were significantly increased in patients with GCA than in controls.

The strongest associations were observed for the composite outcome MACE (adjusted HR 1.51 [1.41–1.62]), sleep apnoea syndrome (adjusted HR 2.20 [1.97–2.45]), peripheral artery disease (adjusted HR 2.00 [1.79–2.23]), dyslipidemia/hypolipidemic agents use (1.79 [1.65–1.95]), diabetes (1.64 [1.50–1.80]) and myocardial infarction (adjusted HR 1.58 [1.36–1.85]) (all p-values < 0.001). The excess risk for atrial fibrillation, conduction disorders, or stroke was, by contrast, more modest.

Reported cardiovascular deaths were also increased (adjusted HR 1.32 [1.16–1.51]) in GCA patients.

Sensitivity analyses

12,488 patients and 33,685 controls were part of the sensitivity analyses’ cohort, after excluding patients and controls who died within the year following hospitalization. The results are presented in Supplementary data 7. The results of the adjusted Cox model were statistically significant for all events studied, except for cardiovascular deaths (HR: 1.14 [0.98–1.32], p-value: 0.093), although there was a trend towards an increased risk. It remained significant for all-cause mortality (HR: 1.11 [1.06–1.18], p-value < 0.001).

Discussion

This is currently the largest study focusing on the incidence of cardiovascular events in patients with giant cell arteritis in France, including only patients with incident GCA as Primary diagnosis, and a first hospitalization between January 2013 and December 2022.

Our results demonstrate a significantly increased incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including peripheral artery disease, myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation or conduction disorders, as well as an elevated mortality risk in GCA patients compared to matched controls.

Our findings are consistent with literature data. For instance, Li et al.7 reported similar hazard ratios for myocardial infarction (1.57 vs. 1.58), heart failure (1.46 vs. 1.41), stroke (1.41 vs. 1.26), and peripheral artery disease (1.75 vs. 2.00) in a UK cohort of nearly 10,000 GCA patients. Comparable trends were observed in smaller cohorts from Tomasson et al. and Mohammad et al.9,10(UK and Sweden, respectively), despite limited adjustment for confounders, as did other studies17,18. A meta-analysis also confirmed an increased risk of stroke following GCA diagnosis19.

Our findings reinforce the notion that GCA is associated with a heightened cardiovascular risk profile. This increased risk may have several causes, involving both traditional cardiovascular risk factors and disease-specific mechanisms.

Indeed, at baseline, GCA patients exhibited a higher prevalence of hypertension and use of hypolipidemic agents compared to controls. These data were extracted from outpatient treatment records, minimizing the likelihood of miscoding due to hospitalization. While these traditional risk factors may contribute to the observed cardiovascular burden, as suggested in ANCA-associated vasculitis20, they do not fully explain it. Indeed, multivariate analyses adjusting for these variables still revealed a significant association between GCA and cardiovascular events, suggesting additional underlying mechanisms.

First, chronic inflammation is known to promote metabolic disturbances such as insulin resistance and obesity, which are themselves risk factors for cardiovascular disease21. Moreover, elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines—characteristic of GCA—have been linked to accelerated cardiovascular events22,23. Local vascular inflammation, particularly in large vessels, may accelerate atherogenesis in cerebral and coronary arteries. In older adults, who represent the majority of GCA patients, age-related immune dysregulation further amplifies this inflammatory milieu.

Second, the impact of treatment strategies on cardiovascular outcomes must also be considered. GCA management primarily relies on high-dose corticosteroids, typically tapered over 12 to 18 months. While effective in controlling disease activity24, corticosteroids are associated with numerous metabolic side effects, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, and glucose intolerance. Corticosteroids impact on cardiovascular risk is highly suspected25,26. However, the direct relationship between corticosteroid exposure and cardiovascular outcomes remains debated27. Gale et al., in a study involving GCA patients from the US and UK28, found associations between corticosteroid use and hypertension or bone-related conditions, but did not confirm a statistically significant increase in myocardial infarction or stroke risk with cumulative prednisone exposure. This suggests that while corticosteroids may exacerbate cardiovascular risk factors, their role in triggering major events may be more complex and indirect.

Regarding mortality, we found a moderate increased risk in our cohort among patients with GCA, as suggested in other studies14,15. Interestingly, this risk followed a U-shaped pattern, with peaks in the first three years and again after seven years of follow-up. This temporal distribution mirrors findings from Baslund et al., who observed increased mortality in the first two years and beyond ten years in a Danish cohort29. This shift in mortality causes over time may reflect the evolving impact of GCA and its treatment on patient health. Indeed, in the early stage of the disease, cardiovascular events are the leading causes of death within the first 6 years of follow-up, as suggested by previous study30, while deaths from infectious diseases become more prevalent later.

Beyond the suspected causes of increased cardiovascular burden and mortality, our findings have important clinical consequences.

The elevated hazard ratios for conditions such as sleep apnoea syndrome (HR 2.20) and peripheral artery disease (HR 2.00) highlight the need for proactive screening and management of comorbidities in GCA patients. Sleep apnoea, in particular, is increasingly recognized as a contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and may exacerbate vascular inflammation. Peripheral artery disease, often underdiagnosed, may reflect systemic atherosclerosis and should prompt further cardiovascular screening and preventive strategies.

Overall, our study emphasizes the importance of comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment in patients with GCA. Clinicians should adopt a multidisciplinary approach, integrating rheumatologic, cardiologic, and geriatric expertise to optimize patient outcomes. Early identification and management of traditional risk factors, close monitoring of treatment-related complications, and long-term surveillance for cardiovascular and infectious risks are essential components of care, just like in ANCA-associated vasculitides20. These strategies are particularly relevant given the chronic nature of GCA and the potential for delayed complications.

Our data also provide insights into the epidemiological links between traditional cardiovascular risk factors and the onset of GCA. Notably, patients under 65 years old with GCA showed a higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes compared with matched controls. This finding is striking given that these risks factors are typically less common in younger individuals. This chronological association raises the possibility that early cardiovascular risk factors might contribute to our understanding of GCA pathophysiology. A hypothesis, supported by external literature, is that subclinical atherosclerosis may promote low-grade endothelial inflammation, which in turn could facilitate the development of a broader inflammatory response in large vessels, particularly in the carotid territory. Previous studies described the link between inflammation, aging and atherosclerosis31–33, as well as potential link between atherosclerosis and giant cell arteritis34. However, our large-scale epidemiological data from the SNDS cannot test this mechanistic pathway directly. Rather, our results shed light on an intriguing temporal relationship between cardiovascular risk factors, cardiovascular disease, and the diagnosis of GCA.

Strengths and limitations

This study benefits from the use of the SNDS, one of the world’s largest medico-administrative databases. It covers nearly the entire French population and ensures extensive longitudinal data.

However, limitations include the absence of clinical, laboratory and imaging data, which restricts the precision of certain conclusions. The SNDS also lacks key lifestyle confounders such as smoking status, physical activity, and BMI—all known to influence cardiovascular risk35. These limitations may lead to residual confounding.

To improve the selection of patient with incident GCA disease, we included only patients for whom GCA was coded as the primary diagnosis during hospitalization. This approach increases the likelihood that included patients truly represent newly diagnosed and clinically active GCA cases. However, this methodological choice may also influence the cardiovascular risk profile observed at baseline and after, as patients with more symptomatic or severe disease are more likely to require inpatient management and more likely to present cardiovascular complications. In addition, we excluded patients who died within 90 days of the index date (and within one year in sensibility analysis) to reduce misclassification of incident GCA, as early death may indicate hospitalization driven by another severe acute condition rather than GCA. However, this exclusion may have led to an underestimation of the earliest cardiovascular risk after diagnosis and limits generalizability to the most severe initial presentations.

Our design did not allow us to distinguish the impact of GCA itself from that of corticosteroid therapy on cardiovascular outcomes. Indeed, in GCA, corticosteroids are initiated at diagnosis and lie on the causal pathway between the disease and subsequent cardiovascular events, while also reflecting underlying disease severity. Adjusting for corticosteroid exposure would risk overadjustment and introduce bias. Our estimates therefore capture the overall association between GCA and cardiovascular outcomes. In the same way, while second-line treatments like Tocilizumab and Methotrexate are recommended in cases of steroid dependence or related complications24, we were unable here to evaluate the potential impact of these steroid-sparing therapies. Some national guidelines36 now support the early use of these agents to mitigate steroid-related complications. Larger studies explicitly designed to compare treatment strategies are needed to clarify their influence on cardiovascular risk. The SNDS represents a valuable resource for future research comparing treatment strategies and their cardiovascular impact.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical need for a comprehensive approach to managing patients with GCA, extending beyond disease control to address long-term risks such as cardiovascular events.

Although the increased cardiovascular risk in GCA patients is moderate, it remains clinically significant and potentially modifiable. Enhanced screening and management of cardiovascular risk factors—particularly sleep apnoea and hypertension—could help reduce this burden. In addition, active infection prevention strategies may positively impact long-term mortality.

Finally, our findings suggest a possible interplay between cardiovascular risk factors and the onset of GCA, warranting further investigation into shared pathophysiological mechanisms.
