Long-term clinical outcomes and recurrence factors in intracranial germinoma
Authors: Masashi Higashino, Kazuhiro Tanaka, Takeaki Ishihara, Hiroaki Nagashima, Yuichi Fujita, Yosuke Fujimoto, Masaaki Kohta, Yoichi Uozumi, Hidehito Kimura, Ryohei Sasaki, Takashi Sasayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeIntracranial germinomas have a favorable prognosis, but recurrence is not uncommon. Risk factors for recurrence and optimal long-term follow-up strategies remain undefined. This study assessed long-term outcomes and recurrence-related factors.MethodsPatients with intracranial germinomas treated at a single institution between 1984 and 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. Long-term overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were evaluated. Recurrence factors were analyzed by comparing the recurrence and non-recurrence groups. For patients receiving whole ventricular irradiation (WVI) or whole-brain radiation therapy (WBRT), irradiation plans were re-evaluated to determine whether the entire ventricular system was covered.ResultsForty-three patients were included (median follow-up, 11.0 years). The 10-/15-year OS and PFS rates were 97.4%/81.8% and 86.1%/81.3%, respectively. Eight patients (18.6%) experienced disease recurrence. Univariate analysis showed that atypical tumor location and non-complete response after initial treatment were associated to higher recurrence risk, whereas radiation therapy (RT) including the entire ventricular system was associated with lower risk. Multivariate analysis showed that RT including the entire ventricular system reduced recurrence risk (hazard ratio 0.060; 95% CI 0.012–0.312; p < 0.001). Among the eight recurrence cases, three involved spinal recurrence after prolonged remission at 8.4, 17.5, and 18.1 years, respectively.ConclusionsRT including the entire ventricular system can prolong PFS. Among patients who underwent whole-ventricular RT using opposing portal radiation, some had insufficient radiation field coverage. Even after long-term remission, clinicians should review the initial RT field, and cases with insufficient ventricular irradiation should be monitored closely for recurrence risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11060-026-05665-6.

Introduction

Intracranial germinoma is a rare malignant central nervous system (CNS) tumor that affects children and adolescents. Although its incidence is low worldwide, it occurs more frequently in Japan and other East Asian countries than in Western countries, enabling individual Japanese institutions to accumulate substantial long-term clinical experience. This makes single-institution studies particularly informative despite the rarity of the disease, whereas such experience is often fragmented in multi-institutional studies.

Germinoma is classified as a “good prognosis” tumor among germ cell tumors (GCTs) according to Matsutani’s classification [1, 2]. With appropriate chemoradiotherapy, germinoma is highly radiosensitive, and a complete response (CR) is achieved in most patients, resulting in excellent long-term overall survival (OS). According to reports, the 5-, 10-, 15-, 20-, and 30-year OS rates for germinoma are as follows: 95.4–100%, 92.3%–100%, 87.9%, 80.6–87.0%, and 61.9%, respectively [1, 3–13]. Accordingly, recent strategies have aimed to reduce radiation exposure while maintaining tumor control. The radiation therapy (RT) field size influences recurrence risk, with focal approaches associated with higher distant relapse rates than extended-field irradiation [14, 15]. These observations support whole ventricular irradiation (WVI) as the current standard in both Western and Japanese protocols [16].

Despite favorable survival outcomes, recurrence remains a concern; the 5- and 10-year progression-free survival (PFS) rates are 78.0–91.0% and 74–96.7%, respectively [4, 6, 8–13]. Survival curves show a steady decline over 30 years owing to factors such as recurrence, delayed cerebral infarction, and secondary tumors [3]. Thus, even after remission, the long-term management of patients with germinoma remains challenging. Although recurrence outside the irradiation field occurs, spinal dissemination is extremely rare, making spinal irradiation non-standard [17–19].

Observational studies exceeding 10 years are lacking, particularly those related to long-term outcomes and risk factors for recurrence [8, 10–12, 17]. This study evaluated long-term clinical outcomes and recurrence patterns in patients with intracranial germinoma treated at a single institution over four decades.

Methods

Study design and setting

Medical records were retrospectively reviewed for 56 patients with intracranial GCTs treated and followed up at Kobe University between January 1984 and July 2024. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval no. B250197). Written informed consent was obtained when possible; otherwise, an opt-out consent process was used.

Participants (eligibility, selection, and follow-up)

Patients with pure germinoma were included, whereas those with non-germinomatous GCTs were excluded. Follow-up consisted of regular outpatient visits and medical record reviews to assess OS, PFS, recurrence, and late adverse effects of RT.

Clinical diagnosis

Diagnosis was established using biopsy samples, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology, magnetic resonance (MR) imaging, and tumor marker analysis. Serum markers included alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG), and CSF markers included AFP, HCG, and placental alkaline phosphatase (PLAP). Serum AFP and HCG levels and CSF AFP, HCG, and PLAP levels were evaluated according to the institutional reference ranges at the time of testing. HCG positivity was defined as HCG or HCGβ levels exceeding the normal range.

Variables and data collection

Demographic and clinical data including sex, age at onset, pretreatment Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), tumor site, ventricular or spinal dissemination at onset, tumor size, presence of cysts, and tumor markers (AFP, HCGβ in serum and AFP, HCGβ and cytology in CSF) were collected. Treatment details included surgical procedures (biopsy or removal), chemotherapy regimens [CARE (carboplatin/etoposide-based) and ICE (ifosfamide/cisplatin/etoposide)], and radiotherapy (RT) modalities: involved-field radiotherapy (IFRT), extended local radiation therapy (ELRT), whole ventricular irradiation (WVI), whole-brain radiation therapy (WBRT), and craniospinal irradiation (CSI). “ELRT” referred to irradiation of the tumor plus a 2-cm margin including the ventricles except the lower fourth ventricle [2].

Clinical outcomes included OS and PFS rates at 5, 10, 15, and 20 years. PFS was defined as the time from diagnosis to progression or death, excluding secondary tumors. Late RT complications included secondary tumors and cerebral infarction.

Bifocal germinomas involving both the neurohypophysis and the pineal body were not classified as multiple tumors. The neurohypophysis, pineal body, and bifocal tumors were categorized as typical sites, and the others were categorized as atypical sites.

Dissemination and recurrence were identified based solely on imaging because of potential false-positive CSF cytology [20]. Imaging findings, including dissemination, recurrence, and treatment response, were retrospectively re-reviewed by the study investigators in conjunction with radiology reports issued by board-certified radiologists at our institution.

CARE consisted of carboplatin (450 mg/m², day 1) and etoposide (90 mg/m², days 1–3) every 4 weeks for three cycles, whereas ICE consisted of ifosfamide (900 mg/m², days 1–5), cisplatin (20 mg/m², days 1–5), and etoposide (100 mg/m², days 1–3).

In cases where WVI or WBRT was performed, two neurosurgeons retrospectively reviewed radiation plans and simulation images to assess whether the entire ventricular system, including the lateral ventricles, temporal horns, third ventricle, fourth ventricle and foramen magnum region, was adequately covered by the irradiation field. The sufficient covering cases were defined as RT including the entire ventricular system. Assessments were performed by consensus review.

Treatment response was classified as CR or non–CR. Non-CR was defined as any residual lesion on imaging after first treatment. This category included cases in which scar-like tissue remained and appeared radiologically similar to a small residual tumor after treatment.

Quantitative variables were analyzed continuously; age (0–9, 10–19, 20–29, and ≥ 30 years), KPS (0–40, 50–70, and 80–100), and tumor size (< 40 mm or ≥ 40 mm) were analyzed categorically based on previous studies [13].

Statistical analysis

All consecutive patients with germinomas treated at our institution over a 40-year period were included. The diagnostic accuracy was strengthened by requiring either histological confirmation or combined imaging/tumor marker evaluation reviewed by at least two board-certified neurosurgeons and one radiologist. Misclassification bias in RT coverage was reduced by the retrospective re-evaluation of radiation fields by two neurosurgeons.

The primary outcomes were OS and PFS rates at 5, 10, 15, and 20 years, estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The secondary outcomes included late complications of RT and recurrence patterns. Recurrence risk was analyzed using Cox proportional hazard models, reporting unadjusted and adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variables with clinical relevance or statistical significance in the univariate analyses were subsequently entered into multivariate models to address potential confounding.

Analyses were performed using the available data for each variable; variables with substantial missing data (e.g., CSF AFP) were excluded. Patients lost to follow-up were censored at the time of the last confirmed contact.

All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [21].

Results

Patient characteristics

Of the 56 cases, 13 with nongerminomatous histology were excluded, leaving 43 eligible germinoma patients for analysis; eight were censored due to loss to follow-up. The baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median follow-up was 11.0 years (interquartile range: 5.7–15.3 years). Of these, 31 (72.1%) were male. The median age at diagnosis was 19 years (range, 7–30 years). KPS was 0–40 in 2 patients (4.7%), 50–70 in 7 patients (16.3%), and 80–100 in 34 patients (79.1%). The primary tumor sites were the neurohypophysis (n = 12, 27.9%), pineal body (n = 12, 27.9%), bifocal (n = 13, 30.2%), basal ganglia (n = 3, 7.0%), brainstem (n = 2, 4.7%), and thalamus (n = 1, 2.3%). Disseminated tumors were observed at diagnosis in nine patients (20.9%). Two patients without histological confirmation showed mildly elevated AFP levels (18 and 22 ng/mL), which remained within the International Society of Paediatric Oncology-defined range for germinoma [14].

The initial treatment strategies varied throughout the study period. From 1984 to 1998, nine patients received treatment primarily centered on RT alone. Between 1999 and 2007, 12 patients were treated with a combination of chemotherapy and RT. From 2008 onward, 22 patients were treated according to the current Japanese standard protocol (trial jRCTs031180223, registered in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials [jRCT]), which consists of three cycles of CARE chemotherapy followed by RT: WVI at 23.4 Gy in 13 fractions or WBRT at 23.4 Gy in 13 fractions for basal ganglia and multiple intraparenchymal tumors. Among these patients, nine treated before 2012 received three-dimensional conformal RT, whereas 13 treated after 2013 received intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT).

Consequently, 2 patients (4.7%) received IFRT alone, 3 (7.0%) received ELRT, 24 (55.8%) received WVI, 4 (9.3%) received WBRT, 9 (20.9%) received craniospinal irradiation, and 1 (2.3%) with severely impaired activities of daily living could not undergo RT.

Among patients treated with WBRT, a retrospective review revealed that all patients received an irradiation field extending to the edge of the ventricular wall. However, among those treated with WVI, in four patients treated prior to 2009, the irradiation field did not extend to the foramen magnum (Fig. 1). No such cases were identified in patients who received IMRT. Consequently, 33 patients (76.7%) received RT, including the entire ventricular system, whereas 10 (23.3%) did not.

Initial treatment response

CR was achieved in 35 patients (81.4%), while 8 patients (18.6%) showed residual radiographic lesions. One patient underwent resection, which revealed only fibrous tissue, and one received additional chemotherapy. The remaining six patients were managed with observation alone; during follow-up, one developed local recurrence, and one developed recurrence along the external ventricular drain tract.

Clinical outcomes

At the final follow-up, four patients (9.3%) had died and eight (18.6%) experienced recurrence (Table 1; Fig. 2a). OS rates were 97.4% at both 5 and 10 years, and 81.8% at both 15 and 20 years, while PFS rates were 90.0% at 5 years, 86.1% at 10 years, 81.3% at 15 years, and 58.1% at 20 years.

Late adverse effect of RT

Secondary tumors and cerebral infarctions were detected in the radiation field (Table 1). Meningioma, cavernous angioma, and high-grade glioma were observed in four, three, and one patient, respectively. Cerebral infarction was detected in three patients, with one symptomatic case.

Risk factors for recurrence

Patients were categorized into recurrence (n = 8, 18.6%) and non-recurrence (n = 35, 81.4%) groups to examine the factors related to recurrence (Table 2). Atypical site germinoma had higher HR (HR, 7.25; 95% CI, 1.45–36.20, p = 0.02) than typical sites. RT including the entire ventricular system was associated with lower HR (HR, 0.06; 95% CI, 0.01–0.31, p < 0.001) than cases without such coverage. Patients who did not achieve CR after initial treatment had a higher hazard ratio (HR, 13.24; 95% CI, 2.32–75.58, p = 0.004) than those who achieved CR.

Multivariate analysis was performed using covariates that were significant in the univariate analysis. When the tumor site, RT including the entire ventricular system, and initial treatment response were analyzed together, RT including the entire ventricular system (HR, 0.03; 95% CI, 0.002–0.33, p = 0.004) remained an independent predictor of recurrence. Survival curves for patients with and without RT, including the entire ventricular system, were compared (Fig. 2b). A statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups (log-rank test: χ2 = 8.8, df = 1, p = 0.003).

Recurrence patterns

Detailed data on the eight recurrent cases are presented in Table 3. Among them, one received IFRT, three received ELRT, one received WVI that did not include the foramen magnum, one received WVI and experienced recurrence outside the irradiation field along the external ventricular drain tract, one received WBRT for bilateral basal ganglia germinoma, and one did not receive RT. After the introduction of IMRT in 2013, only one patient experienced recurrence; this patient had not received RT due to poor KPS.

Of the eight recurrences, three were late recurrences occurring > 10 years after the initial treatment. Three patients experienced spinal cord recurrence at 8.4-, 17.5-, and 18.1-years post-treatment, respectively (mean: 14.7 years).

A representative case (case 1 in Table 3) is shown in the Online Resource 1. This patient was retrospectively identified as not having received RT, including the entire ventricular system, and later developed spinal recurrence after the longest observed remission period (18.1 years). A 12-year-old boy diagnosed with a suprasellar germinoma achieved CR after five courses of ICE, WVI (30 Gy in 15 fractions) with local boost (20 Gy in 10 fractions). At 30 years of age, 18.1 years after the initial treatment, he developed gait disturbances, and imaging revealed a spinal cord lesion at the C2–3 level. Rapid progression necessitated emergency decompression surgery, and biopsy confirmed germinoma recurrence. Subsequent treatment included three courses of CARE and local spinal cord RT (45 Gy in 25 fractions), resulting in resolution. At this time, we identified that the initial WVI field did not extend to the foramen magnum. At 1.6 years after treatment, an enhanced lesion appeared on the left lateral ventricular wall. Stereotactic biopsy confirmed recurrence, and three additional courses of ICE achieved resolution. However, multiple recurrences developed in the bilateral frontal horns 1.3 years later. The patient declined further treatment and opted for supportive care. Genetic sequencing was not conducted for either the initial or recurrent tumors.

Discussion

OS, PFS, and late complications of RT

We retrospectively analyzed 40 years of treatment outcomes with evolving protocols. Despite varied patient backgrounds and treatment strategies, OS and PFS were comparable to those in previous reports [3, 4, 6, 8–13]. PFS declined from 81.3% at 15 years to 58.1% at 20 years. Of 43 patients, 8 experienced recurrences, including 3 who experienced recurrence over 10 years after initial treatment, indicating that long-term follow-up is necessary. Two patients showed 17.5 and 18.1 years, the longest-term spinal recurrence in previous reports. The 20-year PFS decline depends on rare late recurrences and the limited number of patients at risk beyond 15 years.

Secondary tumors and cerebral infarctions occurred > 20 years after the initial treatment. These patients had opposing portal radiation as the main therapy, which does not align with the current standard of IMRT combined with chemotherapy; therefore, careful long-term monitoring is warranted.

Necessity of RT including the entire ventricular system

Despite good initial results, recurrence is possible. The RT group including the entire ventricular system showed significantly better PFS than the group not including it. The initial treatment irradiation field is related to prognosis. A study of 28 patients treated with IFRT or ELRT reported four early recurrences (1.5–4.7 years) outside the irradiation field [22]. Large studies from Europe, North America, and Japan highlight the necessity of WVI [2, 14, 15]. Among four patients treated with WVI before 2009, irradiation coverage was insufficient. One patient whose field did not include the foramen magnum experienced spinal recurrence after long-term remission. IMRT introduction eliminated inadequate coverage, enabling precise three-dimensional conformal radiation planning and improving coverage and local control.

The foramen magnum may require inclusion as germinomas can arise in the fourth ventricle and medulla oblongata [23]. Given germinoma’s affinity for the fourth ventricular ependyma, irradiating this region is important. Insufficient coverage may leave the foramen of Magendie unirradiated, reducing tumor control along CSF pathways.

Long-term follow-up management

Only three cases of spinal dissemination occurring more than 10 years after treatment (10-, 15-, and 16-years post-treatment) have previously been reported [17, 24, 25]. Our study identified two cases at 17.5 and 18.1 years, highlighting the potential for very late recurrence (shown in the Online Resource 2). Craniospinal imaging should therefore be considered beyond 10 years, as very late recurrences (> 17 years) were observed in our cohort. Previous reports have also recommended regular spinal MR imaging, particularly for patients who did not undergo craniospinal irradiation [26].

During long-term follow-up, patients may be managed by physicians not involved in the initial treatment. Compared with modern IMRT, opposing portal radiation poses a higher risk of inadequate coverage. Therefore, reviewing the radiation field documented in the patients’ treatment histories is essential.

Limitations, interpretation, and generalizability

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective single-institution design, small sample size, and treatment heterogeneity over four decades limit statistical power and introduce potential confounding. In a supplemental analysis evaluating whether foramen magnum coverage during WVI/WBRT influenced recurrence, complete coverage was confirmed in 33 of 37 patients; although recurrence appeared less frequent in this group, no significant association was detected on Cox regression, likely reflecting the limited number of events. The small sample size also precluded additional sensitivity analyses and raises the possibility of chance findings in subgroup and exploratory analyses. Detailed radiotherapy dose analyses and genetic evaluations were unavailable, limiting the assessment of dose-related effects and distinction between true recurrence and second primary germinoma in late spinal relapse cases. In addition, the non-CR category was defined radiographically and may have included inactive residual tissue, such as fibrosis or treatment-related scar tissue, rather than viable tumor. Therefore, the non-CR classification should be interpreted as reflecting residual radiographic abnormalities rather than histologically confirmed persistent disease, and some degree of misclassification bias may have been present. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Although generalizability to current standardized treatment regimens may be limited, the results reflect real-world clinical practice across evolving treatment eras and remain clinically relevant, particularly because review of initial radiotherapy fields is often feasible in recurrent cases.

Conclusions

RT including the entire ventricular system was strongly associated with improved long-term disease control. Among the patients who underwent WVI using opposing portal radiation, some had insufficient radiation field coverage. Even after long-term remission, careful review of the initial irradiation field is essential. For patients whose historical RT fields did not fully encompass the ventricular system, clinicians should recognize the ongoing risk of late or very late recurrence and consider extended-duration craniospinal surveillance imaging.
