Effects of public hospital clerkship in rural and underserved areas in South Korea on medical students’ perceptions and career intentions: a pre–post study
Authors: Sejin Kim, Sunju Im, Wonjin Lee, Sang-Hwa Lee, Ho Joong Jeong, Sang Yeoup Lee, Jong-Tae Lee, Ji-Hyun Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study examined the short-term effects of a 2-week clinical clerkship program in rural and underserved public hospitals on medical students’ perceptions of public healthcare and their intentions to work in non-metropolitan public hospital settings.MethodsThe Public Hospital Clinical Clerkship Program (PHCCP) was implemented for fifth- and sixth-year medical students from five medical schools in South Korea during 2023 and 2024. The program included clinical observation, mentoring by public hospital physicians, and exposure to community-based healthcare services. Students completed self-administered questionnaires before and after the program assessing perceptions of public hospitals and intentions to work in rural or underserved areas. Post-program satisfaction with program structure and components was also evaluated. Pre–post changes were analyzed using paired t-tests.ResultsPaired pre- and post-program analyses included 24 students from the 2023 cohort and 36 students from the 2024 cohort. Students showed significantly increased intentions to work in public hospitals and rural or small-city settings after the program in both cohorts (all p<0.05). Positive changes were also observed in perceptions of public healthcare roles and support for public hospital careers. Overall satisfaction with the program was above average, particularly for mentoring, program structure, and educational value.ConclusionA structured, short-term clinical clerkship in rural and underserved public hospitals can positively influence medical students’ perceptions of public healthcare and their intentions to work in non-metropolitan settings. Programs such as the PHCCP may represent a feasible educational strategy to enhance exposure to public and regional healthcare within existing medical curricula.

Introduction

Geographically maldistributed physicians and resulting shortages in underserved areas, such as rural regions and small cities, are serious social issues not only in South Korea but also worldwide [1,2]. A recent systematic review illustrates this challenge, reporting that only 10%–20% of medical students in low- and middle-income countries intend to practice in rural areas [3]. Medical education serves as the foundation for shaping future physicians and meeting healthcare workforce demands. In this context, the decision regarding an initial workplace is particularly critical for medical students, as it can profoundly influence their professional development as well as their personal and social lives [4].

Previous studies have identified various factors—including place of origin, age, gender, admission type, and medical school type—as key determinants of medical students’ intentions to practice in rural settings [4]. Nevertheless, relatively few graduates ultimately choose rural practice, and a major barrier to rural recruitment is the lack of exposure to rural communities and rural clinicians during training. This underscores the need for early exposure to rural settings during undergraduate medical education [5]. In response to this challenge, the World Health Organization advocated for the expansion of rural clinical rotations during clinical training to address workforce shortages in rural and remote areas [6]. Consequently, many medical schools worldwide have incorporated rural practice opportunities into their curricula as part of their educational experience [7-10]. Numerous studies have confirmed that such exposure significantly enhances students’ willingness to pursue careers in rural areas [9-14].

In South Korea, although medical school curricula formally include visits to public health centers and rural health posts, transforming these activities into meaningful, hands-on clinical experiences remains challenging. The limited availability of clinical clerkships in rural or underserved public hospitals restricts students’ opportunities to engage in community-based patient care. As a result, most clinical training continues to be concentrated in metropolitan university hospitals.

To address these gaps, we developed a 2-week clinical clerkship program at rural and underserved public hospitals for medical students. The primary objective of this study was to examine changes in students’ perceptions of public healthcare before and after participation in the program. In addition, we explored whether firsthand exposure to rural and underserved public hospitals influenced students’ intentions to practice as healthcare providers in these settings in the future.

Methods

1. Research setting and program participants

In 2023 and 2024, the National Health Insurance Service and the Ministry of Health and Welfare of South Korea launched the ‘Medical Student Training Program in Essential Healthcare’. As part of this initiative, the Busan–Gyeongnam Consortium, comprising five medical schools–Pusan National University, Gyeongsang National University, Kosin University, Dong-A University, and Inje University—implemented the program with technical support from the Korean Association of Medical Colleges.

Seven faculty members with more than a decade of experience in higher education collaborated to design a 2-week clinical clerkship program titled the Public Hospital Clinical Clerkship Program (PHCCP). The PHCCP was implemented in partnership with Masan Medical Center and Tongyeong Red Cross Hospital, both public hospitals located in suburban and rural areas of Gyeongnam Province. The program was designed to provide students with hands-on clinical clerkship experience and mentorship from supervising physicians working in public hospitals. In contrast to traditional clerkships conducted at university hospitals, the PHCCP also allowed students to observe Public Health and Medical Service Office (PHMSO) projects and interact with public health professionals working in the PHMSO (Appendix 1).

Prior to program implementation, 12 physicians from Masan Medical Center and Tongyeong Red Cross Hospital volunteered to serve as supervising physicians and mentors for the PHCCP. These physicians represented a broad range of clinical specialties, including internal medicine, pediatrics, neurology, neurosurgery, orthopedics, emergency medicine, and otolaryngology. To ensure consistent educational quality, all supervising physicians attended a 2-hour orientation session that addressed the program’s objectives, expectations for student supervision, and mentoring strategies specific to public hospital settings. Throughout the clerkship, these physicians provided clinical supervision, mentorship, and professional guidance to participating students.

The PHCCP was offered as an elective course to fifth- and sixth-year medical students who had completed core clinical clerkships at university hospitals. All participating students received academic credit upon program completion. The program was conducted in eight rounds for the 2023 cohort (July 2023 to January 2024) and six rounds for the 2024 cohort (August 2024 to November 2024), with 1–8 students participating in each round. Following an initial orientation on professional conduct, program objectives, and preparation for clinical practice in public hospital settings, students observed both outpatient and inpatient care. A core component of the program consisted of mentor–mentee interactions with supervising physicians, during which students discussed patient care as well as the professional and personal career paths of public hospital physicians. Students maintained clinical logs of observed patients and completed daily reflective notes on their experiences. At the conclusion of the program, students participated in a debriefing session and were evaluated by faculty members at their respective universities.

2. Evaluation of the effects of the Public Hospital Clinical Clerkship Program

The questionnaire consisted of three components to evaluate the PHCCP. First, overall satisfaction with the PHCCP upon program completion was assessed across five domains: planning and guidance, structure and operation, supervision, peer collaboration, and program outcomes (15 items) (Table 1). Second, seven items assessed satisfaction with specific practice components of the PHCCP (Table 2). Third, to assess the program’s effects, 10 items measured students’ perceptions of public hospitals, knowledge of the public sector, and intentions to practice in rural or underserved areas before and after the program (Table 2). All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree or very dissatisfied) to 5 (strongly agree or very satisfied).

Students completed online, self-administered questionnaires before and after the program, as appropriate. To enable paired analysis while maintaining anonymity, students were asked to generate and use their own unique numeric code when completing both the pre- and post-program surveys. Prior to participation, all respondents were provided with detailed information regarding the study objectives and ethical considerations.

3. Research participants

In 2023, 30 students were enrolled in the PHCCP. Of these, 28 completed the clinical clerkship, while two withdrew due to personal reasons. All 28 students who completed the program (100.0%) responded to both the pre- and post-program surveys. However, after excluding four questionnaires with insufficient or inconsistent responses, data from 24 students were included in the paired pre–post analysis. All 28 students completed the post-program satisfaction survey. Among the survey respondents, 42.9% were fifth-year students and 57.1% were sixth-year students; 39.3% were male and 60.7% were female.

In 2024, all 40 students enrolled in the PHCCP completed the clinical clerkship. Among them, 39 students (97.5%) responded to both the pre- and post-program surveys. After excluding three questionnaires with insufficient or inconsistent responses, data from 36 students were included in the paired pre–post analysis. All 40 students completed the post-program satisfaction survey. Among the respondents to the pre- and post-program surveys, 71.8% were fifth-year students and 28.2% were sixth-year students; 38.5% were male and 61.5% were female.

4. Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to summarize sample characteristics using frequencies and percentages. Only students who completed both the pre- and post-program surveys were included in the analyses assessing changes in perceptions and intentions, which were examined using paired t-tests. Paired pre–post analyses were conducted separately for each cohort (2023 and 2024), rather than using pooled data. A two-tailed significance level of 0.05 was applied. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA).

5. Ethics statement

Participation in the survey was voluntary and anonymous. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Gyeongsang National University Hospital (GNUH 2024-07-015). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of enrollment.

Results

1. Satisfactions with the PHCCP and the clerkship components

Overall student satisfaction with the PHCCP exceeded the midpoint in both the 2023 and 2024 cohorts (Table 3). In the domain of planning and guidance, students reported receiving sufficient information regarding program schedules and procedures prior to participation. Notably, satisfaction with schedule planning was higher in 2024 than in 2023, reflecting refinements implemented following feedback from the initial 2023 cohort.

Regarding the structure and operation of the clerkship, students generally perceived the program as well organized, with an appropriate 2-week duration and a manageable workload. Consistently higher satisfaction scores in the 2024 cohort for program organization and structure suggest improved program delivery following these refinements.

In the supervision domain, students valued the practical, needs-aligned guidance provided by supervising physicians, as well as the collaborative nature of the team-based clerkship. Items related to program outcomes also received positive ratings, indicating perceived educational benefits, particularly in communication with patients and understanding of community-based healthcare. Overall, most students indicated that they would recommend the PHCCP to their peers.

Table 1 presents satisfaction scores for individual clerkship components. The pre-program orientation introducing the concept of public healthcare received high satisfaction ratings. Sessions and projects provided by the Medical Services Office were also rated positively for clarifying the roles and functions of public healthcare. Mentoring sessions with supervising physicians were rated favorably as well. Although students reported general satisfaction with outpatient, inpatient, and emergency department rotations, ratings for inpatient ward and emergency department experiences were relatively lower than those for outpatient clinic rotations. A majority of students expressed satisfaction with the case presentation and feedback sessions.

2. Comparing the medical students’ perceptions of public hospitals and intentions to work in the future before and after the program

Pre–post changes in students’ perceptions of public hospitals and public healthcare were assessed using paired t-tests (Table 2). The mean score for intention to work at a public hospital increased from 3.42±0.83 before the program to 3.88±0.74 after the program (p<0.05). Preferences for working in small cities or rural areas rather than metropolitan areas also increased, from 2.92±0.88 to 3.54±0.93 (p<0.05).

Agreement with pursuing a career in public hospitals under conditions of adequate social and policy support increased from 3.79±0.83 to 4.29±0.75 (p<0.05). Agreement with the statement that the government should encourage physicians to work in public hospitals increased from 3.83±0.64 to 4.25±0.61 (p<0.05). In addition, self-reported understanding of the role of the PHMSO increased from 4.33±0.64 to 4.71±0.46 (p<0.05).

For the 2023 cohort, the remaining items—including perceptions of public hospitals as essential healthcare providers, their role during national disasters, perceived quality differences between public and private hospitals, interest in gaining experience with patients at public hospitals, understanding the role of the PHMSO, and perceived contributions to strengthening regional healthcare infrastructure—showed upward trends after the program but did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, for the 2024 cohort, significant improvements were observed in understanding the role of the PHMSO, interest in gaining experience with patients at public hospitals, perceptions of the role of public healthcare in strengthening regional healthcare infrastructure, and perceptions of public hospitals’ responsibilities for national disaster-related tasks, whereas the remaining items showed no statistically significant changes.

Discussion

1. Interpretation and comparison with previous studies

The findings of this study are consistent with prior research indicating that early exposure to rural or underserved clinical settings during undergraduate medical education is associated with increased interest in rural practice. Previous studies have reported that longitudinal rural placements increase the likelihood of graduates pursuing careers in rural medicine [15-19]. In line with these findings, students in the present study also showed greater agreement with working in public hospitals after program completion, particularly when adequate social and governmental support was assumed. Although the PHCCP was shorter in duration than many longitudinal programs, the observed changes suggest that even short-term exposure, when combined with structured clinical participation and mentorship, may be associated with improved students’ perceptions and intentions.

2. Duration of clerkship and educational value

Previous studies have reported a wide range of durations for rural clinical training, ranging from 1-day exposure programs to 9-month longitudinal clerkships [7,9,19]. Short-term programs, such as a 1-day rural exposure initiative at the University of Tsukuba in Japan, have demonstrated significant increases in students’ interest in rural practice [7]. In contrast, longer programs, including the Rural Physician Associate Program at the University of Minnesota Medical School, have shown sustained effects on students’ career choices, with a substantial proportion of participants continuing to practice in rural settings throughout their careers [9].

Within this spectrum, the 2-week duration of the PHCCP appears sufficient to promote student engagement and reflection. Students in the present study reported above-average satisfaction with individual program components, as well as with the overall schedule, duration, and workload. These findings suggest that a well-structured short-term clerkship can strike an effective balance between feasibility and educational value within existing curricula.

3. Role of mentoring and community-based experience

An important feature of the PHCCP was mentoring by physicians working in public hospitals. Prior research has emphasized the role of mentorship in helping students understand the professional identity and daily realities of physicians in rural and public hospital settings [9]. In the present study, mentoring interactions facilitated students’ understanding of the clinical responsibilities and lived experiences of public hospital physicians, complementing their clinical observations. Students also reported satisfaction with case presentation and feedback sessions, highlighting the educational value of guided reflection.

Exposure to community-based healthcare settings may also help explain the observed changes in perceptions. Rural and underserved clinical environments often provide students with opportunities to experience continuity of care, intergenerational patient relationships, and a strong connection between healthcare delivery and community needs [20]. Similar findings have been reported in Japan, where participation in rural clinical practice increased rural self-efficacy even among students with initially low confidence in rural practice [21]. In our study, students reported enhanced communication skills and a deeper understanding of community healthcare concerns, consistent with these observations.

4. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and most participants were from the Busan–Gyeongnam region, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the absence of a control group restricts the ability to attribute the observed outcomes solely to the intervention. Second, participation in the program was elective, raising the possibility of selection bias, as students with pre-existing interest in public or rural healthcare may have been more likely to enroll. In addition, the baseline data available were limited to participants’ school and academic year, as these were the only data that could be provided to the program organizers within the administrative procedures of the project. This limited a more detailed examination of potential selection effects. Other unmeasured factors may also have influenced the findings. Therefore, caution is warranted in interpreting the results and in drawing causal inferences from the observed changes. Third, the study assessed short-term changes in perceptions and intentions using self-reported survey measures and did not examine actual career outcomes. Self-report questionnaires may be influenced by multiple forms of bias, including social desirability bias and response-shift bias. Future studies should incorporate more rigorous assessment tools that allow for a more specific and objective evaluation of program outcomes. Fourth, this study used a questionnaire developed through iterative discussions among the research team. Although the instrument was designed to reflect the objectives and context of the program, no formal validation, including construct validity testing, was conducted. Therefore, the findings based on this questionnaire should be interpreted with caution. Finally, although the program was implemented over a 2-year period, longer-term follow-up is required to determine whether the observed changes are sustained over time.

5. Conclusions

Despite these limitations, this study provides evidence of changes in medical students’ perceptions of public healthcare and their intentions to work in non-metropolitan settings following participation in a structured, short-term clinical clerkship in rural and underserved public hospitals. The high levels of student satisfaction and the observed pre–post changes suggest that programs such as the PHCCP may represent a feasible and effective educational strategy within current medical school curricula. Expanding similar public hospital–based clerkship programs nationwide may provide medical students with valuable opportunities to explore careers in regional and public healthcare, thereby contributing to efforts to address physician maldistribution.
