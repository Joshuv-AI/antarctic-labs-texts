Assessment of a gridded population sample frame for a household survey of refugee populations in Uganda, 2021
Authors: Shannon M Farley, Stephen Delgado, Stephen McCracken, Dana R Thomson, Qixuan Chen, Giles Reid, Sandra Baptista, Sam Biraro, Andrew Kabala, Herbert Mulindwa, David Okimait, Veronicah Mugisha, Hannah Chung, Brittany Gianetti, Sam Sendagala, Jennifer Nel, Dustin Currie, Christine A West, Charles Herbert Matovu, Ronald Nyakoojo, Julius Kasozi, Hillary Mumbere, Wilford Kirungi, Joshua Musinguzi, David Hoos, Wafaa M El-Sadr

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo date, few HIV-related population-based data are available for refugee populations. Household surveys typically require reliable population counts and well-defined geographic areas, which are often not available for refugee settlements. We describe the gridded population sampling approach as an option for conducting such a survey in Uganda and describe its application for a household survey in Uganda and assess its utility among refugee populations.MethodsThe Uganda Refugee Population-based HIV Impact Assessment (RUPHIA) 2021 was a cross-sectional, population-based HIV survey among refugee households in Ugandan settlements, excluding Kampala. We collected shapefiles and population counts for the refugee settlements. These shapefiles from the various geographic areas of interest represented the aggregated refugee settlement zones (including all settlements with available zone shapefiles) and served as the base for creating the sample frame. The sample frame was constructed by disaggregating United Nations High Commission for Refugees population counts from large refugee settlement zones into 100 × 100 m grid cells using WorldPop’s peanutButter-Disaggregate app that uses building footprint information to distribute the population into the grid cells. We then utilized a gridded population sampling approach which redistributed the population into manageable-sized areas of contiguous grid cells based on their estimated population size, forming enumeration area-like sampling units using the publicly available GridEZ algorithm.ResultsThe resulting gridded population dataset had 43,193 100 m x 100 m cells with an estimated mean of 31 people per cell and a range from 2 to 1028. The final gridded population sample frame had 2636 GridEZ units with an average population of 500 ranging from 178 to 1531. The sample frame performed well for survey activities, with few issues encountered in the field, although the size measures for number of households had some inaccuracies, due to issues such as compounds having multiple structures.ConclusionsGridded population sampling was successfully utilized for this refugee study, saving time and money that would have been needed if enumeration of all the refugee settlements had been required. Gridded population sampling is a useful tool when census data are outdated or unavailable or when the population is dynamic, such as with refugees or other mobile or at-risk populations for surveillance or as part of a humanitarian response.Keywords: Satellite-based population modelling, Gridded population sampling, Alternative sample frame, Household survey, Refugee population, Uganda

Background

Uganda hosts the third largest refugee population in the world and the largest in Africa, with over 1.5 million refugees [1]. The majority of refugees come from South Sudan (60.3%), Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) (29.2%), Somalia (4.0%), and Burundi (2.7%) and tend to settle in regions bordering their countries of origin [1]. Uganda’s approach to refugee hosting, as outlined in the 2006 Refugees Act and 2010 Refugees Regulations, has been recognized as an example of progressive refugee response policy [2, 3]. Specifically, Uganda’s refugee protection framework ensures that refugees have property rights and access to land, right to access employment and engage in income-generating activities, right to access public social services, including education and health, freedom of movement and association, and the right to documentation and equality before the law. These policies allow refugees to reside in settlements as opposed to refugee camps, which often do not allow freedom of movement and access to employment and services, and settlements promote integrated strategies for national development planning [4].

Uganda has experienced a generalized HIV epidemic for more than four decades. Four national population-based HIV surveys have documented the magnitude, trends, and heterogeneity of the epidemic in Uganda across the diverse regions and socio-demographic subgroups [5–8]. These surveys revealed that biologically measured HIV prevalence among adults aged 15–49 years was higher in females than males and higher in urban areas than in rural areas. To date, none of the previous population-based HIV surveys in Uganda have explicitly evaluated HIV indicators among the country’s refugee populations. While refugee settlement areas were included in prior population survey sampling frames, HIV-related estimates for refugee populations were not identified separately in the data or in published results.

In general, HIV prevalence in refugee settings reflects the prevalence of their country of origin rather than the local setting in which they have settled, with some studies showing increased risk of HIV transmission in the context of sexual violence associated with displacement [9]. In 2020, HIV prevalence in South Sudan was estimated at 2.5%, and at 0.7% in DRC, but limited data leads to uncertainty in these estimates [10, 11]. A health facility survey in the Nakivale refugee settlement in Southwest Uganda in 2013–2014 found that HIV prevalence was lower among refugees than Ugandan nationals (2% vs 9%), attributing this finding to lower HIV prevalence in refugee’s origin countries and lower rates of sexual risk behaviors among displaced populations [12, 13]. However, there has been a lack of recent, population-based data on HIV prevalence, as well as on HIV testing and treatment for refugees and displaced persons. Moreover, there were concerns that barriers in access to health services for refugees, including heightened stigma or mental health challenges, may negatively affect their HIV-related outcomes [14, 15]. Thus, data on these populations are of paramount importance in order to inform the efforts to address the specific health needs of refugee populations [16].

Typically, household surveys involve a multi-stage sample design, where households are sampled from selected small census enumeration areas (EA) in each sub-national area [17–21]. This type of multi-stage sampling requires a reliable sample frame with population counts and well-defined geographic boundaries at each level. First stage sampling typically involves sampling census EAs with probability proportional to a measure of size such as the number of households from the frame. This is followed by a household listing of dwelling units within each sampled census EA, and second stage sampling of the listed dwellings or households. Detailed maps are required, whether paper or digital (e.g., shapefiles), across the entire area where the populations of interest reside [17, 18].

However, in the event that the sample frame is outdated, nonexistent, or missing well-defined census EA boundaries and associated maps and shapefiles, one alternative is to use a modelled gridded population dataset to construct the sample frame [22]. Gridded population datasets have been available for roughly 15 years and have become more accurate at finer scales because of improvements in computing power and availability of auxiliary datasets (e.g., building footprints from satellite imagery) [23–25]. “Off-the-shelf” gridded population datasets commonly used for gridded population surveys include LandScan (1 × 1 km), WorldPop (100 × 100 m), and GHS-POP (100 × 100 m), all of which use “top-down” methods to disaggregate high-level data from the census or other official population counts from large administrative areas into much smaller grid cells [23]. Grid cells may be sampled directly but are more commonly combined into contiguous EA-like units with approximately similar population numbers. These grid-based EAs are then sampled in the same way as census EAs, though preparing for and implementing fieldwork often requires additional tools and steps because gridded population unit boundaries generally do not align with natural (e.g., rivers) and built features (e.g., roads). Recently, the WorldPop team at University of Southampton and the European Commission Joint Research Centre, both major producers of gridded population datasets, have released open-source tools for generating custom-made gridded population datasets by disaggregating population counts into ~ 100 × 100 m grid cells using auxiliary data (e.g., landcover type, building density and coverage) [22].

The use of spatial epidemiology has a long history in public health, a notable early example being John Snow and the cholera epidemic in London in 1854 [26]. Gridded population datasets have been used for a variety of health surveillance and analysis projects globally, such as creating an electricity access dataset for SSA using gridded datasets for nighttime light, population, and land cover data [27], producing continental scale gridded datasets for the number of birth and pregnancies for all African, Latin America, and Caribbean countries [28], modeling population distributions and densities at a fine spatial resolution to provide an evidence base for refining infectious disease mapping and for planning health service delivery in Somalia [29], to name a few examples. Gridded population datasets have also been used for sampling, for example in the Democratic Republic of Congo, a case study formalized the gridded population estimates as a bi-dimensional random field, and characterized it using spatial trends, spatial autocorrelation, and stratification [24]. Another study in Mozambique used gridded population sampling to create a sampling frame that was representative of the population in two provinces when recent census data was not available [30].

Since 1960, the United Nations High Commission on Refugees (UNHCR) has worked in refugee settlements in Uganda to update official population counts and settlement borders on an ongoing basis at defined administrative and geographical scales [31] Counting refugees is challenging due to mobility and reluctance to be counted, and populations may relocate rapidly as a result of evolving situations in settlements as well as in countries of origin. Settlement population sizes and boundaries can change at a pace that cannot be consistently tracked by standard administrative systems [32]. While reliable population counts for Ugandan refugee settlements were available at all administrative levels, high-quality digital boundaries at the census EA level, the level required for standard household-based survey sampling, were not readily available. Due to these factors, an alternative sampling method was needed to be able to complete the Uganda refugee survey. The objective of this study was to explore the utility of gridded population sampling to conduct a population-based HIV-related survey among refugee populations in Uganda and assess the quality of the sample frame.

Methods

The Uganda Refugee Population-based HIV Impact Assessment (RUPHIA) 2021 was a cross-sectional, population-based HIV survey of refugee households in Uganda, excluding the capital city of Kampala, conducted from October to December 2021. The purpose of the survey was to estimate the prevalence of HIV and HIV-related outcomes among adult refugees aged 15 years and older in Uganda, characterize risk behaviors, and assess the coverage of HIV services to inform re-alignment of resources and programs to address any gaps identified. The survey offered HIV testing and counseling services with same-day return of results. The purpose was to estimate HIV prevalence, viral load suppression prevalence among HIV-positive individuals, and other HIV-related measures.

Study area

The RUPHIA 2021 survey target population was adult refugees living in the designated 31 refugee settlements in the West Nile (West Nile and Mid-North map regions) and Western (Mid-Western and South Western map regions) RUPHIA regions of Uganda.

For the 31 settlements, 18 settlements were combined for the overall Adjumani settlement, two settlements were combined for the overall Rhino settlement, and the remaining settlements were Bidibidi, Imvepi, Palabek, Palorinya, Lobule, Kiryandongo, Kyaka II, Kyangwali, Nakivale, Oruchinga, and Rwamwanja, which were situated in 13 of Uganda’s 135 districts (as of 2021). There were also urban refugee locations in the capital city, Kampala [33]. (Fig. 1) At the time of the survey, approximately 1.5 million refugees were residing in nearly 380,000 households within these settlements (Table 1). Countries of origin for Uganda’s refugee population were: South Sudan (61.6%, 923,565), DRC (28.9%, 433,147), Burundi (3.4%, 51,066), Somalia (3.2%, 47,633), Rwanda (1.2%, 17,750), Eritrea (1.2%, 17,658), Ethiopia (0.2%, 3,476), Sudan (0.2%, 3,429), others (< 0.1%, 718) [34].

Sample design

This cross-sectional, household-based survey utilized a multi-stage cluster sample design. The overall sample size and allocation by subnational units (i.e., regions) were set to achieve a 95% confidence interval (CI) ± 1% for estimated HIV prevalence among persons aged 15 years and older in the combined West Nile and Western regions. The sample comprised 40 selected EAs (all references to EAs are referring to grid-based EAs unless otherwise specified), with proportional allocation of EAs to the Western and West Nile regions based on the refugee population in each region. The resulting sample was expected to yield a 95% CI for the estimate of viral load suppression among HIV-positive refugees aged 15 years and older of ± 11%.

The sample design assumed an HIV prevalence of 3.0% among refugees aged 15 years and older. This assumption was based on a health facility survey in the Nakivale settlement of Southwest Uganda in 2013–2014 [13].

Sample frame

The sample frame was constructed in multiple steps. First, an aggregated shapefile inclusive of all available settlement zone areas was created. Then UNHCR population counts from large refugee settlement zones were disaggregated into 100 × 100 m grid cells using WorldPop’s peanutButter-Disaggregate app. Next, EAs were formed from contiguous grid cells using the publicly available GridEZ algorithm [35, 36]. The UNHCR population counts were field-referenced, up-to-date, and covered the study population of interest, making it an ideal source of sample frame information for this study. The process of transforming UNHCR zone-level population counts into a fine-scale gridded population dataset, and then into a sample frame of spatial units with roughly equal population is described below.

Sample frame coverage

Within each region, UNHCR utilized four levels of administrative area classifications to define geographic boundaries: Level 1 (L1), District; Level 2 (L2), Settlement; Level 3 (L3), Zone; and Level 4 (L4), Village (Table 1). Geographic boundaries (shapefiles) for refugee settlements at the zone level (L3) were available for all settlements, except for Kampala, Oruchinga, and a small number of other zones.

Settlements (L2) were divided into zones (L3), with each zone encompassing approximately 3000 households and 13,000 refugees, on average, although there was substantial variation in the population size and geographic extent of settlement zones. Settlement zones comprised the sample frame coverage, with three exceptions. First, the Kampala area was excluded from the sample frame due to the diffuse nature of the refugee population in the capital city. Second, the Oruchinga settlement, which lacked well-delineated zone-level boundary data at the time of the study, was omitted from the sample frame (0.6% of the refugee population). Third, well-delineated boundary data were unavailable for a subset of zones within study area settlements (26 zones, 7.2% of the refugee population), and these zones were therefore not included in the sample frame. Despite the omission of settlements and zones without shapefiles, collectively they represented less than 8% of the refugee population, which was considered an unfortunate but tolerable loss. Table 2 summarizes sample frame coverage of the overall refugee settlement population of Uganda. All of the available zone geographic boundaries were compiled to create one aggregate shapefile, inclusive of all settlement zones eligible for the sample frame. By creating one aggregate shapefile, boundary simplification was required to encompass all eligible settlement zones together in the one file, this resulted in the inclusion of some non-settlement zone areas in the sample frame. Efforts were made to ensure the included non-settlement areas were as minimal as possible.

Gridded population dataset

To create a bespoke gridded population dataset from UNHCR’s zone-level population counts, WorldPop’s peanutButter-Disaggregate online app was used to proportionally allocate UNHCR zone population counts into 100 × 100 m grid cells. This distribution of the population counts was based on the count of building footprints (outline of space taken up by buildings) per cell within the specified geographic boundaries from the aggregate shapefile [35, 36]. We retained the default settings in the peanutButter app, which did not exclude very small or large buildings (in some contexts, such buildings might be excluded because they are assumed to be uninhabited – e.g., sheds or warehouses). The default setting was retained, due to the potential for any of the buildings to have households. Had they been excluded the population of the grid cells may have been larger than expected. Visual inspection of the building footprint counts in ~ 100 × 100 m cells (used for population allocation) over satellite imagery across all 12 settlements suggested that the building counts were accurate for most types and sizes of structures, with the exception of round buildings that have natural roofs and some very small structures (likely to be latrines, sheds, or kitchens). The 100 × 100 m grid cells were selected instead of finer or coarser grid cell sizes due to the need to aggregate grid cells into approximately EA-sized units with an approximate population of 500 people or 100 households per EA. This size is an average size used for household surveys. This is smaller than the EA size used traditionally by census bureaus, which typically aim for EAs with approximately 300 households.

Gridded population sample frame

To group grid cells into EAs within each of the 12 settlements, we downloaded and used the GridEZ R algorithm [37]. The algorithm requires two inputs: a population raster (the bespoke gridded population dataset) and vectorized strata boundaries that EAs were not allowed to cross (specified as the 12 settlement boundaries in the aggregated shapefile). Additionally, the user specifies the target population and maximum area for each EA. We used the “medium” pre-defined configuration with a maximum area of 3 × 3 km and a target of 500 people per unit. The medium option was selected because the target population size matched the size used for household surveys, as the small option has a target population of 75 people and the large option had a target of 1200 people. The small and large options would have resulted in too few or too many households for a sample of 35 households per EA. The GridEZ algorithm creates rectangular blocks that meet the maximum area requirement, then sub-divides the blocks into smaller units (sometimes reaggregating them) to meet the target population ± 25%. First stage sampling

Sample selection

Once the sample frame is created, the sample of EAs for data collection can be selected. Forty primary sampling units (PSUs) (i.e., EAs) were selected with probability proportional to the estimated population in each PSU, which resulted in an approximately proportional allocation of PSUs to the Western and West Nile regions.

Sample review

The sample frame of gridded EAs were desk-reviewed prior to sample selection to ensure geographic size and population estimates were consistent with targets and segmented prior to listing as needed.

EA replacement

In the field, if a selected EA was determined to be out of scope (if no refugees lived in the selected EA, or it was outside the boundary of the settlement, or it was an enclave of Ugandan nationals), the next EA in the frame that included refugee households was used to replace it.

Household listing

Household listing and mapping was conducted during a one- to two-day period immediately prior to data collection. Household listing teams were equipped with global positioning system (GPS)-enabled tablets, with EA boundary maps loaded onto tablets as GeoPDF files, PDFs that contain georeferencing information. This allowed team members to orient themselves within each EA and ensure that they listed only households within the boundary. During household listing, GPS coordinates of all dwelling units were collected for use during the survey to help field staff identify and locate the selected households during data collection. Listing also included determining the refugee status of each household, households identified as refugees or asylum seekers, or unknown refugee status were considered eligible households for the sample frame. Georeferenced household listing data were reviewed for quality assurance and control in the field as well as in a central office by survey supervisors and data managers.

Second stage sampling

Secondary sampling units (SSUs) consisted of all listed households (with unique serial identifiers) in each PSU. A systematic random sample of 35 households was selected from each PSU. Depending on the household occupancy rate and household interview response rate within each PSU, an average of 32 households per PSU were enrolled for participation. The selected households were visited by the field staff and there were no replacements and no changes of the selected households allowed.

Accuracy of the gridded population sampling

To assess the accuracy of gridded population sampling for refugees in Uganda, exploratory analyses were conducted to compare the sample frame peanutButter population counts with the World Pop building counts, the World Pop building counts with the household listing dwelling unit counts, and the peanutButter population counts with the household listing dwelling unit counts with the purpose of understanding how well peanutButter distributed the estimated population by counts of structures all within the EAs. The fitted lines with confidence intervals were created with linear regression and plotted with Stata’s “graph twoway lfitci” command as part of the exploratory analyses. An assessment of the percent of Ugandan nationals within settlements and on the border or outside the border of the settlements was conducted.

Three of the 40 sampled EAs required replacement. One EA was outside the boundary of the refugee settlement area, which was due to simplification of settlement zone boundaries to create the aggregate shapefile for use within the sampling application. The second EA was a cluster of Ugandan citizen households which pre-dated the refugee settlement. The third EA was replaced due to the peanutButter app identifying garden plots or sheds as structures and distributing population in an uninhabited area.

As determined a priori, replacement EAs were selected as the next eligible EA on the sample frame list. The EA geopdf maps with geofenced boundaries were created for the next five EAs and shared with the field teams to investigate EA eligibility and visited in order. Once a replacement was determined to be eligible-located within a refugee settlement boundary and having refugee households- it was selected.

Data collectors used the same EA geopdf maps with geofencing to ensure they only listed and surveyed households within the EA boundary.

Discussion

A gridded population sampling frame constructed using satellite data and publicly available population estimates was successfully utilized for this Uganda refugee population survey. The gridded population sampling saved the time and cost that would have been required to physically enumerate the settlement areas and create the needed shapefiles. Thus, the survey was not hindered by the lack of shapefiles and census data at the appropriate geographic level [39–43]. These findings in this case study are similar to prior studies that utilized gridded population sampling, supporting its value in avoiding reliance on outdated census information or missing shapefiles to create a useable sample frame. [39–43]

Anecdotal feedback from experienced field teams that were more familiar with working within traditional EA boundaries and survey methods indicated that the process was fairly easy to implement. They found the use of the georeferenced PDFs to be very helpful in determining the new EA boundaries both during training, field practice, household listing and data collection, as it provided them with immediate feedback instead of having to wait for GPS coordinates to be reviewed in the office.

While the peanutButter population estimates did not align well with the ground-truthing of household listing of dwelling units overall, listing and sampling was still successfully completed. We observed differences in the variations between the West Nile and Western regions, with peanutButter overestimating the number of households in the West Nile region and substantially underestimating the number of households in the Western region. This was demonstrated in the comparisons of peanutButter population with both building and dwelling unit ounts as well. This could be due to how the algorithm distributed the population across the building footprint areas. It is possible that more uninhabited or administrative areas were inadvertently incorporated into the shapefiles due to the process of aggregating the separate shapefiles. Although we did not expect the gridded population estimates generated using the aggregated population counts and building footprint data to be very accurate, they proved to be a sufficient replacement for a traditional household sampling frame, allowing for the random selection of EAs. Natural features (such as swamps or mountains) can reduce the size of the EAs, therefore, it is important to preview satellite images for the selected EAs to ensure they do not include a body of water or other inaccessible/non-habitable area. In this study, the WorldPop building counts identified many structures that were not identified as dwelling units in the West Nile region, such as outhouses, animal stalls, sheds, and cooking structures. In the Western region, it was possible that due to building size or shape, the buildings were not recognized, leading to the underestimation. Improvements in satellite building footprint data may help improve the accuracy of building type identification, or an appropriate adjustment factor could be used to account for the proportion of identified structures that are likely to be dwelling units.

There were also several lessons learned from the process. Before selecting the WorldPop’s peanutButter disaggregate web application and the GridEZ R algorithm, several other applications and algorithms were explored to find the best fits for this particular survey. These tools were off-the-shelf and easy to use. It should be noted that the applications selected needed to be adapted and refined, which required individuals with the necessary skills to assist with implementation. While more time could have been spent exploring other options, it would have required more time. Additionally, staff with geospatial skills were required to manipulate and prepare shapefiles needed for creation of the sample frame. Shapefiles defining the survey area at some geographic level were necessary as the sample frame was not the entire country. It was likely that some EAs will need replacement when shapefile boundaries were simplified or generalized as non-intended areas will inevitably end up in the sample. It is important to clearly define the procedures for these processes, including having regular internet access to facilitate implementation.

Disclaimer

In addition to creating a sampling frame for Ugandan refugee populations, gridded population datasets can be used to spatially define refugee populations beyond Uganda as well as other mobile populations such as internally displaced people. In Somalia and Yemen, conflict and the resulting displacement of people created the need for the development of gridded population datasets that could be used to estimate population size and distribution for planning and to reconstruct health sectors [29, 44]. A gridded population sampling project in Iraq, designed to be used in conflict settings, found this method to be faster, feasible with limited financial resources, and more flexible than traditional sampling procedures, provided an experienced GIS person is involved [45]. Using gridded population sampling for humanitarian response allows for improved timeliness, as the gridded population data can be updated more frequently than a national census, flexibility to adjust the aggregation of population data based on shifting boundaries, and the potential to more accurately sample vulnerable or mobile populations that may be hard to find.

Mozambique used gridded population sampling to create a provincial community survey sampling frame to evaluate a health system intervention [30]. Another Population-based HIV Impact Assessment (PHIA) in Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) used a gridded population sample frame as the last census in DRC occurred in 1984 [46]. Traditional household surveys, like PHIA or Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), involve the use of existing samples frames, usually owned by the national statistics organization in each country, and separate processes for first stage sampling of EAs, then household listing, and then a separate second stage sampling for households [17, 18]. Using the existing frames save a lot of time, effort, and cost, as the enumeration process has already been conducted for other purposes, and it would not be feasible to do so for a small survey project. With gridded population sampling, while these steps occur, they can occur on a researcher-defined timeline for the first stage sampling, as in RUPHIA, second stage sampling can occur in the tablet application, expediting second stage sampling, and allowing teams to begin the main survey data collection right away [47]. This also saved on some costs since it involves only one trip to the field for both household listing and main data collection. Of note, our final design effect was not much larger than a traditional PHIA, indicating that the inaccuracies of the sample frame did not substantially impact the precision of the estimates, and can be overcome by planning a slightly larger sample size.

In conclusion, while it is not a replacement for the precision and reliability of a recently completed population census, gridded sampling may be an appropriate alternative when a well-defined sampling frame is not available in the context of the conduct of population surveys among mobile or hard to reach populations. Using a gridded sample frame prioritizes speed and cost-effectiveness over accuracy, while leveraging new technology. As satellite imagery improves, the quality of the gridded sample frames will likely improve too. This type of sample frame creation could be used in any location, when a geographic sample frame is not available and household or other type of geographic sampling is needed. Gridded population sampling is scalable, whether for a few provinces, refugee settlements, or an entire country. It can be used for any type of mobile population including refugees, internally displaced people, or during conflict settings or other humanitarian crises. Finally, the ability to create a sample frame where one did not previously exist, supports the surveillance and public health needs of a jurisdiction with little to no data, often during a crisis situation, to provide up-to-date data for decision-making.
