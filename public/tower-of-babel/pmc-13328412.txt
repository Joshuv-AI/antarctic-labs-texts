Influences of suspending agents on the rheological and sedimentation characteristics of cemented paste backfill
Authors: Yibo Zhou, Bo Pang, Zhuo Shen, Xichun Tian, Xin Xu, Yuan Yan, Pengfei Yang, Haochen Wang, Yu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blockage during the pipeline transport of cemented paste backfill (CPB) causes significant economic losses to mining operations. Suspending agent (SA) addition has been proposed as an easily implementable and low-cost method to mitigate the risk of pipeline blockage caused by sedimentation during the transportation of CPB. However, the effects of SAs on the rheological and sedimentation properties of CPB are not yet fully understood. This study investigated the rheological and sedimentation characteristics of CPB with different types of SAs. CPB mixtures were prepared with three types of SAs (hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC), polyacrylamide (PAM), and xanthan gum (XG)) at concentrations of 1.5, 3.0, and 6.0 g/L. The rheological properties (yield stress and viscosity) and sedimentation characteristics (bleeding and monitored via layered electrical conductivity) were measured over 0–2 h. Additionally, zeta potential and microstructural analyses were conducted. SA addition increased the yield stress and viscosity of CPB. Concurrently, the SAs inhibited bleeding and sedimentation. Furthermore, the SAs appeared to significantly inhibit the sedimentation of cement particles. The findings develop CPB technology utilizing SAs and provide important guidance for ensuring smooth pipeline transportation.

Introduction

Mining activities yield substantial solid wastes1, such as tailings, while extracting metal resources. Long-term storage of these tailings occupies land resources and poses environmental and safety risks2. Paste backfilling has emerged as a critical technological pathway for green and safe mining practices, as well as for the resource utilization of solid waste3,4. This process typically involves preparing a high-density paste slurry by mixing tailings, cementitious materials (e.g., cement), and water in specific proportions. The slurry is then transported via pipelines to mined-out areas5.

Pipeline transportation is one of the most critical steps during on-site backfilling operations. The rheological and sedimentation characteristics of the slurry play a decisive role in ensuring smooth pipeline conveyance6. On the one hand, rheological parameters (e.g., yield stress and viscosity) determine pumping pressure, energy consumption, and the risk of pipeline blockage. On the other hand, bleeding and particle settlement lead to fluctuations in slurry concentration, as well as stratification and segregation, which cause structural inhomogeneity and performance degradation of backfill. Additionally, particle settling leads to the accumulation of solids at the bottom of the pipeline. The hydraulic resistance increases during transport, which induces pipeline blockage. It is particularly pronounced during pump shutdown and restart7,8. Therefore, a key challenge in the design and construction control of paste backfill materials lies in inhibiting bleeding and sedimentation and maintaining good fluidity.

Since backfill paste is a multi-component solid-liquid mixed slurry, each constituent influences the slurry’s rheological and sedimentation characteristics to a certain extent9. As the primary solid skeleton, tailings directly affect the slurry’s rheological behavior and sedimentation through their particle size distribution and morphology. Cement, functioning as a fine-grained cementitious component, provides early structural support and potentially alters the slurry’s structure and rheological response due to its hydration process10. Mixing water, serving as the dispersion medium and flow carrier, determines the slurry’s pumpability as well as the occurrence and progression of bleeding and sedimentation11–13. Consequently, common current measures to address bleeding and sedimentation include optimizing particle size distribution and solid concentration. However, these methods are limited by specific tailings sources and screening conditions, potentially compromising flow performance and creating challenges for engineering applications6,11.

Against this backdrop, introducing an SA into the paste system is considered a more easily operable and cost-effective slurry stabilization strategy. Water-soluble polymers such as HPMC, PAM, and XG are widely used in industrial and everyday applications, offering convenience and accessibility. Theoretically, these agents can alter particle-liquid interactions and system structure through mechanisms, e.g., thickening and water retention, adsorption and film formation, and bridging/entanglement. Therefore, both the rheological behavior and sedimentation process of the paste are influenced14–16.

For the specific engineering system of CPB, a systematic understanding of how SAs influence the coupled response of rheology and bleeding/sedimentation remains lacking. Existing studies have approached this issue by examining different material systems and types of additives. Ma et al. (2018) investigated the effects of the coexistence of HPMC and polycarboxylate-based superplasticizer (PCE) on the rheology and dispersion of cement paste. HPMC increases the yield stress and plastic viscosity while reducing fluidity. Mechanistically, this may be related to interactions between HPMC and Ca²⁺ in the pore solution, as well as competitive adsorption with PCE, which weakens the dispersion effect of PCE17. Jin et al. (2022), focusing on coal gangue skeleton backfill, investigated the rheological control of self-compacting cement-tailings slurry (CTG). Slurry rheology and static stability are optimized by combining thickening/steady-state admixtures (e.g., HPMC and PAM-based agents) with PCE18. Zhang et al. (2024) studied the effects of HPMC on the rheology, stability, and strength of coarse tailings cemented backfill (CTCB). HPMC significantly alters the yield stress and viscosity of fresh slurry while improving bleeding and segregation19. Zou et al. (2024) investigated the synergistic effects of anionic polyacrylamide (APAM) and polycarboxylate ether-based superplasticizer (PCE) on the fresh and hardened properties of cement paste. Increasing the dosage of APAM elevates yield stress and induces shear-thinning behavior, which is associated with the bridging/network structure formed upon adsorption. As hydration progresses, the formation of hydration products further strengthens this network20. Gu et al. (2024) examined the time-dependent fluidity and rheological parameters of fine-grained coal gangue cemented backfill slurry containing HPMC. The fluidity of the slurry varies with time and HPMC dosage, while the yield stress and viscosity parameters exhibit significant time-dependent characteristics21. Feng et al. (2025) studied the influence of HPMC on the rheology and early hydration/phase evolution of C3S and C3A slurries. HPMC significantly increases plastic viscosity, static/dynamic yield stress, and structural recovery ability22. Zhang et al. (2025) investigated the differences in the effects of various polymers (e.g., HPMC and PAM) in fluid-solidified soil materials containing multiple admixtures, which are designed for erosion resistance. HPMC (as well as similar cellulose ethers) is more advantageous in maintaining certain fluidity while enhancing anti-dispersion and anti-scour properties23. Guo et al. (2025) studied the distinct roles of HEMC and HPMC in the early-stage structural development of a PC-CSA composite cementitious system. Both polymers alter moisture distribution and increase the initial yield stress. Compared to HEMC and the reference slurry without cellulose ether, the HPMC system exhibits a more rapid increase in yield stress during the static standing process. The water absorption and thickening effect of cellulose ethers, along with the increased viscosity of the pore solution, are identified as important factors contributing to structural development24.

The majority of the aforementioned studies have found that SA addition in CPB enhances slurry stability while reducing its fluidity. However, these studies have predominantly focused on single SAs and their corresponding macroscopic indices. There is a notable absence of quantitative characterization regarding the internal sedimentation evolution of slurries, and of horizontal comparisons elucidating the differential mechanisms underlying various SAs. That is, many critical questions regarding these SAs remain unanswered. What is the impact of HPMC on the internal stratification and sedimentation evolution of the paste, and how can this be quantitatively characterized? How do PAM and XG influence the flow and sedimentation behavior of the paste over time? Which SA exerts the most significant effect on the flow and sedimentation performance of the paste? How do HPMC, PAM, and XG alter the early-stage microstructural characteristics of the paste, and in what ways do these alterations affect its rheological and sedimentation characteristics? To date, no research has systematically addressed the aforementioned questions concerning the flow and sedimentation characteristics of paste with added SAs. Bridging these research gaps is of paramount importance for ensuring the efficient and reliable transport of backfill slurry.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically investigate the flow and sedimentation properties of CPB containing SAs (HPMC, PAM, and XG) through experimentation.

(3) Additives: SAs.

Three water-soluble polymer SAs were purchased from Tianjin Huasheng Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.: HPMC (viscosity: 200,000 mPa·s; molecular weight: 86,000; density: 0.5 g/cm3), PAM (anionic; molecular weight: 12 million; density: 1.302 g/cm3), and XG (molecular weight: 2 × 106-5 × 106 Da; density: 1.581 g/cm³). Mix proportions and specimen preparation

While keeping the baseline mix proportion constant, comparative experiments were conducted with the type and concentration of the SA as variables. The cementitious material content was 4.5% (percentage of cementitious material relative to the total solid mass), with the water-cement ratio of 7.35 (Table 3). For clarity and conciseness, the time-dependent yield stress and viscosity were investigated using a representative dosage of 3 g/L and a control sample (0 g/L) (Table 3). The effect on bleeding rate was analyzed based on measurements after 1 h of static settling. Stratified conductivity was evaluated for samples with 0 and 3 g/L. During sample preparation, a specified mass of the SA was first added to a measured amount of mixing water and stirred thoroughly until completely dissolved. Then, an SA solution was obtained. Tailings and cement were then weighed according to the mix ratio and dry-mixed for 2 min. Subsequently, the SA solution was slowly added to the dry mixture under continuous stirring. The mixing process was considered complete after stirring for 5 min until the slurry was uniform and free of significant agglomeration. Samples were taken to test rheological characteristics, bleeding, and the evolution of stratified electrical conductivity (EC) of the fresh slurry after mixing (Fig. 2).

Test methods

Vane shear test

The yield stress of fresh slurry was measured using the blade shearing method with instrument model ZY-WF1730/427. The blade was a cross-shaped paddle with a radius of 12.5 mm and a height of 25 mm, equipped with a 1# torsion spring. The slurry was thoroughly stirred before testing. The testing procedure is as follows:

(1) Clean the vane head. Adjust the dial to make the pointer contact the strain indicator. The pointer was aligned with the 0° position on the outer dial.

(2) Rotate the handle counterclockwise to lower the vane. Insert the vane from the central surface of the sample until the top of the vane head is positioned 10–20 mm below the sample surface.

(3) After allowing the sample to stabilize for 30 s, record the pointer reading on the inner dial as the initial angle.

(4) Start the drive motor. Rotate the inner dial at a constant speed of 0.18 rpm.

(5) Observe the relative position between the strain indicator and the pointer. When the strain indicator separated from the pointer, the sample was considered to have failed. Stop the test at this point. Record the pointer reading on the inner dial as the final angle.

The torque angle was obtained by subtracting the initial angle from the final angle. The maximum torque of the slurry was calculated according to the spring calibration manual.

The maximum torque was recorded and converted into yield stress by the accompanying calculation program. Each sample group was tested in triplicate to derive the average.

Viscosity test

The apparent viscosity of fresh slurry was measured using a digital rotational viscometer (DV-E). The RV5 rotor was selected for testing, with the rotational speed set at 50 r/min. The torque percentage shall remain within the effective range of 10–90%. The slurry was thoroughly stirred before testing. Then, the rotor was immersed to the specified marking line. The test was initiated, and readings were taken after standing for approximately 30 s. The viscosity number was recorded once the reading stabilized. Each sample group was tested in triplicate to derive the average.

Bleeding rate test

The freshly mixed slurry was promptly poured into a 100 mL measuring cylinder. The cylinder was then sealed and allowed to stand at room temperature for 60 min.

Layered EC monitoring

Differences in EC at different layers reflect the settlement extent of solid‑liquid slurries19. Therefore, a layered EC monitoring setup is designed to investigate the sedimentation characteristics of the slurry (Fig. 3). The internal dimensions of the container are 10 × 10 × 22 cm, with the slurry height of 20 cm. Three TEROS‑12 EC sensors are arranged along the height direction (top, middle, and bottom layers), with the top sensor placed 2 cm below the slurry surface, an 8 cm spacing between the top and middle sensors, an 8 cm spacing between the middle and bottom sensors, and the bottom sensor positioned 2 cm above the container bottom. The TEROS‑12 sensor has dimensions of 9.4 × 2.4 × 7.5 cm, with a probe length of 5.5 cm. Its measurable conductivity range is 0–20 dS/m, with a resolution of 0.001 dS/m and a measurement frequency of 70 MHz. The freshly prepared slurry is poured into the container. EC data at each position are recorded continuously over time using a ZL6 data logger, with a sampling frequency of once per min. Collected data are then exported to a computer.

Stratified EC monitoring setup.

Zeta potential measurements

The Zeta potential of the sample was measured using a Zetasizer Nano instrument. The test involved conducting an electrophoresis experiment under an applied electric field. The instrument obtained particle electrophoretic velocity through laser doppler velocimetry (LDV) and calculated electrophoretic mobility. The Zeta potential was then derived based on Henry’s equation. Before zeta potential measurement, the sample was diluted with distilled water to prepare a suspension with a concentration of 0.1 g/L. The measurements were performed at 20 °C. Each sample was tested in triplicate to derive the average.

TG-DTG and XRD

Thermogravimetric analysis and X-ray diffraction (XRD) tests were conducted using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA Q5000 V3.15 Build 263) and an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Ultima-IV), respectively. After the cement slurry specimen was mixed, it was cured at 20 °C for a specified duration before microscopic analysis. The sampled material was placed in a 45 °C oven and dried to constant weight to minimize interference from free water on the test results. Subsequently, it was ground into powder for both thermogravimetric analysis and XRD testing.

Heating was performed under a nitrogen-protective atmosphere, with the nitrogen flow rate of 300 mL/min. A temperature range covered from room temperature to 1000 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. For the XRD test, the scanning angle range was set from 5 to 80°, with a scanning speed of 5°/min.

Results and discussions

Influence of SAs on the time-dependent evolution of CPB’s rheological properties

Yield stress

Figure 4 illustrates the evolution of yield stress over time for CPB without any SA (control sample) and those with different types of SAs (HPMC, PAM, and XG). Regardless of whether SAs are added, the yield stress gradually increases within the first 0–2 h. Continuous cement hydration in CPB generates hydration products (e.g., C‑S‑H)27. The internal structure gradually forms and strengthens, which increases the critical stress required to initiate flow as well as yield stress28.

The yield stress of the samples increases upon SA addition, though the extent of improvement varies depending on the type of agent used. The yield stress follows the order within 0–1 h: XG > PAM > HPMC > control. Compared to the control, the higher yield stresses observed for the PAM and HPMC samples are attributed to differences in interparticle repulsive forces. Figure 5 presents the zeta potentials of the four samples. Both PAM and HPMC exhibit lower absolute zeta potentials than the control, indicating weaker electrostatic repulsion between particles. Consequently, reduced interparticle spacing facilitates aggregation29. This promotes particle aggregation and flocculation30 and presents higher yield stress19,31.

Figure 5 shows that XG has the highest absolute zeta potential, which is inconsistent with its highest yield stress observed in Fig. 4. The zeta potential of the XG sample cannot be used to explain its highest yield stress. The strengthening effect of XG on the CPB structure is related to its molecular structural characteristics rather than originating from electrostatic interactions. The relatively long molecular chains and high molecular weight of XG render it highly susceptible to entanglement and bridging effects within the slurry. A continuous spatial network structure forms, significantly enhancing the yield stress of CPB32.

The yield stress of HPMC exceeds that of PAM at 2 h (Fig. 4). In addition to interparticle interactions, the formation of cement hydration products plays a significant role in the evolution of yield stress. Figure 6 displays the thermogravimetric analysis curves of CPB with SAs. Each sample exhibits three distinct weight loss peaks at 60–200, 400–500, and 600–800 °C, corresponding to the dehydration of C-S-H gel, decomposition of Ca(OH)₂, and decomposition of CaCO₃, respectively33. Whether in the first peak (60–200 °C) or the second peak (400–500 °C), HPMC exhibits the highest weight loss. The HPMC sample presents the highest accumulation of hydration products, predominantly C-S-H and CH. The resulting internal structure of CPB becomes denser, which enhances the yield stress observed in the HPMC sample.

Viscosity

The viscosity of all samples gradually increases within 0–2 h, which is primarily attributed to the continuous progress of cement hydration (Fig. 7). On the one hand, the formation of hydration products gradually develops a flocculated network structure in CPB. The enhanced internal cohesion of CPB consequently increases viscosity34. On the other hand, the hydration process consumes free water, which leads to a higher solid volume fraction in CPB and greater interparticle friction. Thus, viscosity rises35,36.

Fig. 7Time-dependent evolution of the viscosity of CPB with/without HPMC, PAM, and XG.

Time-dependent evolution of the viscosity of CPB with/without HPMC, PAM, and XG.

The addition of all three SAs increases the viscosity of CPB at 0–2 h (Fig. 7). Specifically, viscosity increase follows the order at 0–0.25 h: XG > PAM > HPMC. Hydration proceeds slowly in the initial stage, with the limited amount of hydration products. Viscosity is primarily governed by the flocculation state and spatial structure induced by the SAs. The smaller absolute zeta potentials of PAM and HPMC result in weaker electrostatic repulsion between particles. Aggregation and flocculation occur, contributing to higher viscosity. In contrast, the longer molecular chains and higher molecular weight of XG promote entanglement and bridging between chains in CPB. A spatial network structure forms, leading to the highest viscosity.

The extent to which the SAs increase viscosity follows the order at 1–2 h: HPMC > XG > PAM. The viscosity of HPMC-containing CPB exhibits rapid growth at 0–0.25 h. This is attributed to the highest quantity of hydration products (e.g., C-S-H and CH) formed in the HPMC sample. That is, a greater amount of hydration products leads to a higher solid volume fraction and increased viscosity37. The dominant factor governing viscosity gradually shifts from the early-stage flocculation and spatial structural effects to the interparticle friction induced by hydration products in the later stage.

The first peak (60–200 °C) and the second peak (400–500 °C) both show the following order of magnitude: HPMC > Control > XG > PAM (Fig. 6). The amount of hydration products in the samples follows the same order. The overall features of the XRD patterns for the four samples are relatively consistent (Fig. 8). Characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to Ca(OH)2, CaCO3, C2S, C3S, and AFt are detected in all samples, with no new mineral phases observed. SA addition does not alter the fundamental mineral composition of CPB38.

Comparing the CH peaks (400–500 °C) of the four samples33, the HPMC sample exhibits the highest intensity of the CH peak, followed by the control group. However, the PAM and XG systems show lower CH peak intensity. The CH content varies among the four samples39, which is consistent with the thermogravimetric analysis results described earlier.

Based on the thermogravimetric and XRD results mentioned above, SAs do not directly participate in new chemical reactions or alter the fundamental mineral composition of the system. However, they indirectly regulate the formation of hydration products by influencing water distribution and the local hydration environment in CPB40. The differences in hydration product content observed in the thermogravimetric and XRD results indicate that HPMC promotes the hydration reaction, while XG and PAM inhibit it41,42. The promoting effect of highly viscous HPMC on early cement hydration is consistent with Ref.43. HPMC, with its high viscosity, exhibits strong adsorption and retention of free water in CPB, which enhances the overall water-retaining capacity. This temporarily bound water is gradually released and continuously participates in the hydration reaction of cement particles. Therefore, hydration is promoted.

Fig. 8XRD analysis of samples with/without HPMC, PAM, and XG at 1 h.

XRD analysis of samples with/without HPMC, PAM, and XG at 1 h.

The inhibitory effect of XG and PAM on cement hydration is attributed to the functional or polar groups present in their molecular structures. These groups strongly adsorb onto the surface of cement particles, which forms a dense organic film44,45. This film hinders direct contact between cement particles and water molecules. The hydration reaction is physically blocked to reduce the hydration rate27,46.

Impact of SAs on bleeding and sedimentation characteristics of CPB

Bleeding

The bleeding rate of all samples decreases significantly as the dosage of the SA increases from 0 to 6 g/L (Fig. 9). SAs enhance the water retention capacity of CPB and suppress segregation19,47. The bleeding rates of the HPMC, PAM, and XG CPB decrease from 24.5 to 15.2, 18.3, and 19.1%, respectively, in the dosage range from 0 to 1.5 g/L. HPMC results in the greatest reduction in bleeding rate, decreasing by 9.3%, followed by PAM with a reduction of 6.2%, and XG with the smallest reduction of 5.4%. The bleeding rates for CPB with the three SAs are 2.8, 3.5, and 8.6%, respectively, at 3 g/L. The bleeding rates of all CPB approach 0 at 6 g/L, indicating that the inhibitory effect on bleeding saturates at high dosages.

Based on the bleeding rates above, the increased concentration of the SA significantly reduces the bleeding rate. It is closely related to the enhanced internal structure of CPB. The viscosity and structural strength of the slurry improve as the concentration of the SA increases. Free water is more easily trapped within the particle-polymer network. The upward migration of pore water and the settlement of particles are both suppressed, which reduces the bleeding rate. Particularly in the HPMC system, its strong water retention capacity and structural thickening effect result in the most significant inhibitory effect on bleeding43.

Sedimentation characteristics

Continuous monitoring of layered conductivity is conducted in this section. The evolution of conductivity is primarily influenced by two factors. First, the content of insulating solid particles between the probes. The higher solid content indicates the greater resistance to electron conduction between the probes and lower conductivity48,49. Second, the hydration reaction of cement. In the early stages of hydration, an increasing number of ions (e.g., Ca2+ and OH⁻) are generated as the hydration reaction proceeds, which increases conductivity50,51.

Figure 10 shows the conductivity monitoring results for the top, middle, and bottom sections of the control sample. The overall conductivity trend is as follows: top > middle > bottom. Initially, the conductivity is nearly identical, which is attributed to the homogeneous state of the fresh slurry. The evolution of conductivity in different sections shows distinct differences. Both the bottom and middle conductivity curves exhibit a process of first decreasing (approximately 0–5 min) and then increasing (approximately 5–120 min).

The conductive ions generated from cement hydration increase conductivity. Therefore, the decrease observed in the first stage is primarily due to the increased solid particle content, which results from the settling of solid particles (e.g., tailings). An increasing number of solid particles settle at the bottom, which decreases conductivity. Consequently, the first stage can be defined as the rapid settlement stage. The increase in conductivity during the second stage is mainly because the promoting effect of cement hydration on conductivity outweighs the inhibiting effect of settlement on conductivity. Additionally, the conductivity at the bottom decreases more rapidly than that in the middle section during the first stage. More solid particles settle at the bottom compared to the middle.

Conductivity at the top exhibits an initial sharp increase followed by a gradual rise. The sedimentation of solid particles reduces the number of solid particles around the probes at the top, which causes the sharp increase. The subsequent gradual rise is a result of slowing sedimentation and the continued hydration of cement. Compared to the bottom and middle sections, the rate of conductivity increase is slower at the top during the second stage. Sedimentation promotes conductivity at the top, but inhibits conductivity at the bottom and middle. Consequently, the rate of conductive ion generation from cement hydration is lower at the top52. Fewer cement particles remain at the top, with more cement particles settling towards the bottom and middle sections.

The settling of both tailings particles and cement particles adversely affects on-site CPB. On the one hand, sedimentation of tailings or cement particles increases the density of solid particles at the bottom. A higher concentration of solid particles at the bottom leads to greater friction with the pipeline, as well as pipeline transport anomalies. This issue is particularly pronounced during pump restart processes, significantly increasing the resistance to restarting the pipeline54. On the other hand, particle settling enriches the cement content at the bottom, which accelerates the generation of hydration products in this region. More hydration products (e.g., C-S-H) enhance flocculation and linkage between particles, which reduces the fluidity of the slurry at the bottom55,56. Therefore, SA addition positively contributes to pipeline transportation by inhibiting the sedimentation of tailings and cement particles.

Discussion

Combined with the rheological characteristics, bleeding behavior, and layered conductivity monitoring results, different SAs exhibit significant differences in their effects on the flowability and sedimentation of CPB. The underlying reasons are fundamentally related to how these SAs regulate particle interaction and moisture distribution57. Figure 15 illustrates the mechanisms by which the three SAs influence the flow and sedimentation of CPB. The proportion of free water inside the slurry is relatively high in the control sample. Water easily migrates upward through pore channels under gravity, forming a distinct bleeding layer. Meanwhile, tailings and cement particles lack effective constraint, which leads to pronounced particle sedimentation.

Compared with the control sample (without SAs), the addition of the three types of SAs inhibits the sedimentation of solid particles (tailings and cement) in CPB. This inhibition is achieved through the flocculation of particles by the polymeric molecules of SAs and their water-retention effect. HPMC retains water by immobilizing a portion of free water as bound water, which decelerates moisture migration. Furthermore, a continuous, high-viscosity aqueous phase forms within the slurry, which strengthens the pore fluid viscosity and the aqueous film bridging between particles. Consequently, HPMC exhibits the most outstanding performance in inhibiting sedimentation.

PAM primarily exerts its stabilizing effect through the flocculation of its polymer chains. The long-chain structure of PAM can adsorb onto multiple particle surfaces simultaneously, forming dispersed flocculated structures within the slurry. This enhances the solid-phase framework and restricts the relative particle movement58. In contrast, XG works by constructing a flexible, 3D entangled network of bridging structures, which improves the overall structural integrity of the slurry. The network structure enhances the system’s ability to resist sedimentation on a larger scale59.

SA addition negatively influences the flowability of CPB. Firstly, the flocculation or bridging effect of SAs increases the yield stress and viscosity. Secondly, the acceleration or deceleration of hydration reactions caused by SAs affects flowability. For instance, the promoting effect of HPMC on hydration reduces the flowability of CPB.

The above discussion is based on laboratory experimental results. The SAs exert noticeable effects on both sedimentation and flow behavior. Although this study is subject to certain limitations in experimental scale, it still provides valuable guidance for field backfilling operations. On the one hand, the SAs slightly increase the yield stress and viscosity of CPB, which reduces its flowability during pipeline transport. On the other hand, they significantly inhibit particle settling, which mitigates the risk of pipeline blockage during pump shutdown and restart.

Conclusions

Taking tailings-cement paste as the research object, HPMC, PAM, and XG were separately incorporated. A systematic investigation was conducted on their effects on the early-age (0–2 h) rheological properties (yield stress and viscosity), bleeding, and segregation behavior of CPB. Combined with zeta potential, thermogravimetric analysis (TG/DTG), and X‑ray diffraction (XRD) tests, the underlying mechanisms were analyzed. The main conclusions are as follows.

(1) Both the yield stress and viscosity generally increased, which was attributed to the continuous formation of flocculated structures resulting from cement hydration. The addition of the three SAs significantly increased the yield stress and viscosity of CPB. Different SAs enhanced the yield stress and viscosity to varying degrees. SAs promoted particle aggregation and flocculation or induced bridging effects within CPB. Additionally, changes in the quantity of hydration products caused by SAs contributed to the observed variations in the yield stress and viscosity.

(2) According to TG/DTG and XRD results, SA addition influenced the early-age hydration rate of cement rather than forming new phases. The results suggested that HPMC might promote the hydration reaction under the tested conditions, whereas XG and PAM appeared to inhibit it.

(3) The addition of the three types of SAs significantly reduced the bleeding rate of CPB. The bleeding rate gradually decreased as the concentration of SAs increased. The bleeding rate approached zero at a dosage of 6 g/L. The effectiveness of the three SAs in reducing the bleeding rate followed the order: HPMC > PAM > XG.

(4) Based on the monitoring results of the layered EC in the control sample, the decrease or increase in conductivity at the bottom, middle, and top layers during approximately 0–5 min was primarily due to the rapid sedimentation of tailings solid particles. The subsequent conductivity increase from approximately 5 to 120 min was mainly attributed to the increased ions resulting from hydration reactions.

(5) Based on the dispersion of conductivity curves at the bottom, middle, and top layers of the four samples, the SI was calculated. The SI of all four samples gradually decreased at 0–5 min, with a slowed decline rate. Solid particles gradually settled, and the sedimentation rate decreased. SIs of the samples with the three types of SAs were all higher than those of the control sample. SA addition inhibited particle sedimentation, with the effectiveness of sedimentation inhibition following the order: HPMC > PAM > XG.

(6) Based on the results of layered conductivity monitoring, SA addition inhibited the sedimentation of tailings and cement particles. SAs positively contributed to pipeline transport by suppressing the physical settling of particles and reducing the accumulation of hydration products at the bottom.

(7) The increase in the yield stress and viscosity induced by the addition of SAs slightly deteriorated flow performance during pipeline transport. However, their inhibition of particle settling mitigated the risk of pipeline blockage during pump shutdown and restart.

This experimental study provides in-depth insights into the effects of different types of SAs on the rheological and sedimentation characteristics of CPB. The findings offer important reference value for the smooth pipeline transport of CPB.
