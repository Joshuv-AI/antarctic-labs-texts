Peri-prosthetic soft tissue considerations in periodontology and implantology: biological basis, clinical relevance, and contemporary perspectives
Authors: Pavithra R., Vinita A. Boloor, Rajesh K. S., Anupama Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeri-prosthetic soft tissues play a critical role in maintaining biologic stability, functional longevity, and esthetic outcomes of restorative and implant therapy. Rather than acting as passive barriers, these tissues function as dynamic biologic interfaces that respond to restorative procedures, prosthetic contours, and mechanical influences. Contemporary understanding has expanded the traditional concept of gingival biotype into the broader periodontal and peri-implant phenotype, encompassing soft tissue thickness, keratinized mucosa width, and supracrestal tissue dimensions.MethodsTo ensure a comprehensive overview of the literature, a focused electronic search was conducted across the PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases for articles published between January 2000 and March 2026. Search strings utilized combinations of Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms and free-text terms including “peri-prosthetic soft tissue,” “peri-implant phenotype,” “emergence profile,” and “supracrestal tissue attachment”. Selection prioritized randomized controlled trials, prospective clinical studies, and high-quality systematic reviews published in English. Additionally, classic historical landmark papers establishing foundational biological concepts were manually retrieved and included to provide necessary context.ResultsEvidence indicates that soft tissue phenotype significantly influences marginal tissue stability, recession susceptibility, and peri-implant bone remodeling. Prosthetic design variables, including emergence profile, restoration contour, margin placement, and cementation techniques, play a decisive role in maintaining peri-prosthetic tissue health. Adequate soft tissue thickness and keratinized mucosa are associated with improved biologic stability, although some aspects remain subjects of ongoing debate. Recent advances in soft tissue augmentation, biomaterials, digital workflows, and minimally invasive approaches have expanded opportunities for phenotype modification and tissue optimization.ConclusionSuccessful peri-prosthetic tissue management requires integration of periodontal biology, prosthetic design principles, and phenotype-driven treatment planning. Contemporary strategies focused on biologic preservation, soft tissue optimization, and minimally traumatic restorative protocols contribute to long-term peri-prosthetic health, stability, and esthetic success.

Introduction

Long-term success in prosthetic rehabilitation depends not only on mechanical durability and esthetic integration but also on preservation of peri-prosthetic tissue health. The surrounding soft tissues represent biologically active structures that regulate host response, microbial defense, and marginal stability. The traditional term “biologic width” has been superseded by “supracrestal tissue attachment” following the 2018 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions. This terminology more accurately describes the combined junctional epithelium and supracrestal connective tissue attachment coronal to the alveolar crest. Berglundh et al. emphasized that breakdown of this biologic interface frequently precedes structural complications in both tooth- and implant-supported restorations (Berglundh et al. 2018, Jepsen et al. 2018). Distinct structural differences exist between periodontal and peri-implant soft tissues. Around natural teeth, connective tissue fibers insert perpendicularly into cementum, providing enhanced resistance to mechanical and inflammatory challenges. In contrast, Berglundh and Lindhe reported that collagen fibers surrounding implants predominantly align parallel to or circumferentially around the implant surface, resulting in altered tissue resilience (Berglundh et al. 1991). These variations influence tissue stability, susceptibility to remodeling, and response to restorative manipulation. Increasing clinical evidence indicates that many biologic complications originate from prosthetic tissue interactions rather than surgical deficiencies. Consequently, modern restorative therapy requires integration of soft tissue phenotype, prosthetic design, and maintenance considerations. This review synthesizes biologic principles with contemporary clinical perspectives relevant to peri-prosthetic soft tissue management.

Literature search strategy

To provide a comprehensive overview of the available evidence, a focused electronic literature search was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). Articles published between January 2000 and March 2026 were considered, while seminal earlier publications were included where they provided fundamental biological concepts relevant to peri-prosthetic soft tissues. The search strategy incorporated combinations of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms, including “peri-prosthetic soft tissue,” “peri-implant phenotype,” “soft tissue thickness,” “keratinized mucosa,” “emergence profile,” “supracrestal tissue attachment,” “peri-implant mucositis,” “peri-implantitis,” and “soft tissue augmentation.” Greater emphasis was placed on systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials, prospective clinical studies, consensus reports, and landmark publications in the fields of periodontology and implant dentistry. The selected literature was critically evaluated and synthesized to provide a balanced overview of the current biological concepts, clinical evidence, and contemporary approaches to peri-prosthetic soft tissue management.

Biological basis of peri-prosthetic soft tissues

Peri-prosthetic soft tissues should be regarded as dynamic biologic interfaces rather than passive structural barriers. Their principal function involves establishing and maintaining a protective seal that preserves the integrity of the underlying periodontal and peri-implant structures. This biologic interface plays a decisive role in modulating host response, limiting microbial penetration, and maintaining marginal tissue stability. Around natural dentition, this protective architecture is defined by the supracrestal tissue attachment, originally characterized by Gargiulo et al. comprising junctional epithelium and connective tissue attachment (Gargiulo et al. 1961). The connective tissue fibers insert perpendicularly into the cementum, providing enhanced resistance to mechanical forces and inflammatory challenges. Ingber, Rose, and Coslet subsequently emphasized that disruption of this dimension through restorative encroachment results in persistent inflammation and attachment loss (Ingber et al. 1977).

In implant therapy, a comparable peri-implant mucosal barrier develops; however, its structural organization differs fundamentally from that surrounding natural tooth. Berglundh et al. demonstrated that peri-implant tissues exhibit reduced vascularity and collagen fibers predominantly aligned parallel or circumferentially to the implant surface (Berglundh et al. 1991) (Fig. 1: Schematic representation of the peri-implant mucosal seal demonstrating the junctional epithelium, connective tissue zone, and relationship to the alveolar crest. The illustration highlights the biologic soft tissue barrier responsible for peri-implant tissue stability). These characteristics partly explain the diminished capacity of peri-implant tissues to withstand repeated mechanical disruption and inflammatory challenges. These structural distinctions carry important clinical implications. The peri-implant soft tissue interface, characterized by altered fiber orientation and reduced vascular supply, demonstrates increased susceptibility to mechanical trauma, restorative manipulation, and prosthetic-induced inflammation. Clinical procedures such as repeated abutment disconnection, inappropriate emergence profile design, and excessive tissue compression may therefore precipitate inflammatory changes and contribute to crestal bone remodeling.

Importantly, peri-prosthetic soft tissues actively respond to restorative contours and material characteristics. Linkevicius and Apse observed that mucosal adaptation is influenced by prosthetic design, material properties, and biomechanical loading conditions (Linkevicius et al. 2009). Consequently, biologic compatibility and prosthetic-tissue harmony must be regarded as central determinants of long-term tissue stability. Preservation of the supracrestal tissue attachment remains essential for maintaining peri-prosthetic tissue homeostasis. Violation of this biologic zone through subgingival margin placement, over-contoured restorations, or excessive soft tissue compression may initiate chronic inflammatory responses, ultimately leading to attachment loss and marginal instability.

Soft tissue phenotype and clinical implications

The traditional classification of gingival biotype has progressively evolved into the broader and more comprehensive concept of the periodontal and peri-implant phenotype. This contemporary framework recognizes the multidimensional nature of peri-prosthetic tissues, incorporating soft tissue thickness, keratinized tissue width, underlying bone morphology, and supracrestal tissue height. Avila-Ortiz and Wang emphasized that such an integrated assessment more accurately reflects the biologic behavior of peri-prosthetic tissues and their response to surgical and restorative interventions (Avila-Ortiz et al. 2020). Among these parameters, soft tissue thickness has emerged as a critical determinant of biologic stability. Olsson and Lindhe initially demonstrated that thin tissues exhibit an increased tendency toward marginal recession (Olsson and Lindhe 1991). Subsequent investigations by Cook et al. further substantiated that thin phenotypes display greater susceptibility to mechanical trauma and inflammatory alterations (Cook et al. 2011). Clinically, thin tissues are frequently associated with marginal instability, mucosal transparency, and an elevated risk of recession following both restorative and surgical procedures.

Conversely, thick phenotypes are generally characterized by enhanced vascularity, greater resistance to mechanical insult, and improved dimensional stability. These tissues tend to demonstrate more predictable healing patterns and superior resilience following prosthetic manipulation, thereby contributing to improved long-term marginal integrity. In implant therapy, mucosal thickness has gained particular clinical significance. Puisys and Linkevicius reported that thin peri-implant tissues were associated with increased early crestal bone remodeling (Puisys and Linkevicius 2015). These observations were further reinforced by the systematic review conducted by Suárez-López Del Amo et al. which confirmed the relationship between soft tissue thickness and peri-implant bone stability (Suárez-López et al. 2016). Collectively, these findings suggest that soft tissue thickness influences not only mucosal behavior but also underlying bone dynamics.

Importantly, these biologic relationships extend beyond esthetic considerations. Phenotype modification strategies, including soft tissue augmentation, should therefore be interpreted as biologic risk management approaches aimed at enhancing tissue stability and preserving peri-implant structures rather than solely cosmetic interventions. Accurate phenotype evaluation consequently plays a pivotal role in treatment planning. It directly influences clinical decision-making related to implant timing, margin placement, necessity for tissue augmentation, and prosthetic contour design. Incorporation of phenotype assessment into routine clinical protocols thus represents a fundamental component of biologically guided restorative and implant therapy. Soft tissue phenotype and clinical implications

Clinical relevance of peri-prosthetic soft tissues

Peri-prosthetic soft tissues represent a decisive factor governing the long-term stability, health, and esthetic integration of both tooth-supported and implant-supported restorations. Contemporary evidence consistently indicates that biologic complications frequently arise not solely from surgical variables but from the dynamic interaction between prosthetic design and peri-prosthetic tissue response. Prosthetic design variables, including emergence profile, contour, margin location, and material selection, should therefore be evaluated within a biologically guided framework. Recognition of these relationships has shifted contemporary clinical strategies toward preventive tissue management. Soft tissue augmentation procedures, phenotype modification, and digitally guided prosthetic design increasingly serve as biologic risk mitigation approaches rather than purely esthetic enhancements.

Soft tissue thickness and crestal bone stability

Soft tissue thickness has emerged as a primary determinant of marginal stability. Reduced tissue thickness is commonly associated with increased susceptibility to recession, mucosal transparency, and inflammatory alterations. In implant therapy, thin peri-implant mucosa has been correlated with greater early crestal bone remodeling, suggesting that soft tissue dimensions exert a protective influence on underlying osseous structures (Cook et al. 2011, Puisys et al. 2015). Linkevicius et al. demonstrated that thin peri-implant tissues were associated with greater marginal bone loss (Linkevicius et al. 2009). Further studies revealed that soft tissue thickening procedures enhanced crestal bone stability (Linkevicius et al. 2015). These findings underscore the biologic significance of soft tissue volume in preserving peri-implant health. Nevertheless, the available evidence remains heterogeneous. While several clinical studies and systematic reviews have demonstrated an association between increased soft tissue thickness and improved crestal bone stability, variations in implant systems, surgical protocols, measurement techniques, and follow-up duration have contributed to inconsistent findings across studies. Consequently, although soft tissue thickness is widely regarded as an important biologic factor influencing peri-implant tissue stability, its independent contribution should be interpreted within the broader context of patient-, implant-, and prosthesis-related factors.

Keratinized mucosa and tissue stability

The presence and adequacy of keratinized mucosa constitute another clinically relevant parameter. Although peri-implant health may be maintained in sites with limited keratinized tissue, multiple investigations have demonstrated that reduced keratinized mucosa width is frequently associated with increased plaque accumulation, mucosal inflammation, and patient discomfort during oral hygiene procedures. Lang and Loe initially described the association between keratinized tissue width and gingival health (Lang and Löe 1972). More recent systematic reviews by Lin et al. and Perussolo et al. identified the correlation between keratinized mucosa deficiency and increased mucosal inflammation (Lin et al. 2018, Perussolo et al. 2019). Consequently, insufficient keratinized tissue may indirectly compromise peri-implant stability by impairing plaque control efficiency and patient comfort. Nevertheless, the clinical necessity of a minimum width of keratinized mucosa remains a subject of ongoing debate. While several systematic reviews have demonstrated associations between reduced keratinized mucosa and increased plaque accumulation, peri-implant inflammation, and patient discomfort, other studies have reported that satisfactory peri-implant health can be maintained in sites with limited keratinized tissue when effective plaque control and regular maintenance are ensured. Therefore, keratinized mucosa should be considered an important contributing factor rather than an absolute prerequisite for long-term peri-implant tissue stability, with patient-specific risk factors and oral hygiene practices also influencing clinical outcomes.

Prosthetic contours and emergence profile

Prosthetic contouring plays a vital role in modulating peri-prosthetic tissue behavior. Over-contoured restorations create plaque-retentive niches, promoting microbial colonization and chronic inflammatory responses. Conversely, appropriately designed emergence profiles facilitate tissue adaptation and contribute to marginal stability. Restoration contour has therefore been recognized as a critical risk indicator for peri-implant disease development (Fig. 2: Illustration of emergence profile design around an implant-supported restoration showing critical and subcritical contours and their influence on peri-implant soft tissue adaptation). Prosthetic design exerts a direct influence on tissue behavior. Katafuchi et al. identified restoration contour as a risk indicator for peri-implantitis (Katafuchi et al. 2018). Prosthetic factors influencing peri-prosthetic soft tissue stability

Cement-associated biologic complications and metallosis

Cement-associated complications represent a well-documented etiologic factor in peri-implant disease (Wilson 2015). In addition to residual cement, metallic particle release (metallosis) has emerged as a potential contributor to peri-implant inflammatory conditions. Metallic debris generated through implant-abutment wear, corrosion, or mechanical friction may induce macrophage activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine release within peri-implant tissues. Although the precise clinical significance remains under investigation, titanium particle accumulation has been associated with peri-implant inflammation and bone destruction, suggesting that metallosis may act as a cofactor in the development of peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis (Fretwurst et al. 2018) (Fig. 3: Factors associated with peri-implant soft tissue breakdown including plaque accumulation, excess cement, prosthetic overcontouring, mechanical trauma, and metallosis). This recognition has driven increased preference for screw-retained restorations and controlled cementation protocols aimed at minimizing biologic risk. Biologic principles in margin placement

Margin placement further influences peri-prosthetic tissue health. Deep subgingival margins, particularly when violating the supracrestal tissue attachment, may induce persistent inflammation and attachment loss. Careful margin positioning that respects biologic dimensions remain essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis. Preservation of the supracrestal tissue attachment remains fundamental for preventing inflammatory complications. Deep subgingival margins increase the risk of attachment loss, particularly in thin phenotypes. Ingber et al. emphasized that restorative encroachment results in persistent inflammation (Ingber et al. 1977). Periodontal-prosthodontics interrelationship

Periodontal health and prosthetic rehabilitation operate within a biologically interdependent framework. Periodontal stability determines restorative predictability, while prosthetic design directly influences tissue homeostasis. Successful rehabilitation therefore requires integration of biologic and restorative principles. Encroachment upon the supracrestal tissue attachment has been consistently associated with persistent inflammation and attachment loss (Gargiulo et al. 1961, Ingber et al. 1977). Respect for this biologic dimension remains essential during restorative procedures.

Prosthetic morphology critically modulates periodontal response. Restoration contours and emergence profiles influence plaque retention, tissue adaptation, and inflammatory susceptibility, with over-contoured restorations recognized as significant risk factors for gingival inflammation (Katafuchi et al. 2018). Tissue phenotype further governs restorative behavior. Thin phenotypes demonstrate increased susceptibility to marginal recession, whereas thick phenotypes exhibit greater dimensional stability (Avila-Ortiz et al. 2020, Olsson and Lindhe 1991, Cook et al. 2011). These variations necessitate phenotype-guided restorative planning.

Interdisciplinary coordination becomes particularly relevant in surgical site development and phenotype modification. Soft tissue augmentation, when biologically indicated, may enhance peri-prosthetic stability and mitigate complication risk (Thoma et al. 2016, Thoma et al. 2018). Minimally traumatic restorative protocols, characterized by gentle tissue handling and controlled prosthetic manipulation, support tissue stability and reduce inflammatory responses (Linkevicius et al. 2009). Recognition of the periodontal–prosthodontic interface as a dynamic biologic-restorative continuum remains fundamental to achieving long-term clinical success.

Soft tissue augmentation and phenotype modification

Soft tissue augmentation has progressively evolved from a corrective modality to a biologically driven preventive strategy aimed at enhancing peri-prosthetic stability. Contemporary therapeutic objectives extend beyond esthetic refinement to include improvement of tissue thickness, reinforcement of mechanical resilience, optimization of cleansability, masking of restorative materials, and reduction of peri-implant bone remodeling risk (Linkevicius et al. 2009, Suárez-López et al. 2016). Soft tissue thickness is now recognized as a critical determinant of peri-implant tissue stability. Thin phenotypes demonstrate increased susceptibility to marginal recession, mucosal transparency, and early crestal bone remodeling, whereas increased soft tissue volume appears to exert a protective effect on peri-implant bone dynamics (Olsson and Lindhe 1991, Suárez-López et al. 2016). Consequently, phenotype modification should be interpreted as a biologic risk mitigation strategy rather than solely an esthetic intervention.

Autogenous connective tissue grafts (CTG) remain the reference standard for peri-implant soft tissue augmentation due to their predictable volumetric stability and favorable biologic integration (Thoma et al. 2016). However, advancements in biomaterials have expanded clinical options while minimizing patient morbidity. Xenogeneic collagen matrices have demonstrated promising outcomes in soft tissue volume enhancement, offering reduced surgical invasiveness and elimination of donor site complications. Acellular dermal matrices (ADM) and collagen-based scaffolds function as biologic substitutes that facilitate host tissue integration and neovascularization. These materials provide structural support for soft tissue thickening while improving patient comfort and reducing operative morbidity (Thoma et al. 2016, Thoma et al. 2018).

Biologic modifiers, particularly platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), have gained increasing clinical acceptance as adjunctive agents. PRF promotes angiogenesis, enhances soft tissue maturation, and may improve graft stability through sustained release of growth factors (Miron et al. 2017). Minimally invasive surgical approaches further contribute to improved outcomes. Techniques emphasizing vascular preservation and atraumatic tissue handling enhance healing dynamics and reduce postoperative complications (Thoma et al. 2016). Additionally, integration of digital workflows permits precise planning of soft tissue contours and emergence profile optimization, thereby improving prosthetic-tissue harmony. Collectively, contemporary augmentation strategies reflect a paradigm shift toward preventive biologic tissue management designed to enhance long-term peri-prosthetic stability. Contemporary perspectives in peri-prosthetic soft tissue management

Advances in restorative and implant dentistry have progressively shifted peri-prosthetic soft tissue management from reactive correction toward proactive biologic optimization. Contemporary strategies emphasize individualized risk assessment, precision-guided prosthetic design, and minimally invasive therapeutic approaches designed to enhance long-term tissue stability.

Phenotype-driven treatment planning

Recognition of the periodontal and peri-implant phenotype has fundamentally altered treatment planning paradigms (Fig. 4: Phenotype driven treatment planning in peri-implant soft tissue management). Phenotype assessment, incorporating soft tissue thickness, keratinized mucosa width, and supracrestal tissue dimensions, enables identification of biologic risk factors that may predispose to marginal instability and peri-implant bone remodeling (Linkevicius et al. 2009, Suárez-López et al. 2016). Thin phenotypes demonstrate increased susceptibility to recession, mucosal transparency, and early crestal bone remodeling, whereas thick phenotypes exhibit greater dimensional stability and resistance to mechanical disruption (Avila-Ortiz et al. 2020, Olsson and Lindhe 1991, Cook et al. 2011, Puisys and Linkevicius 2015, Suárez-López et al. 2016). Consequently, phenotype evaluation now serves as a determinant for clinical decision-making, influencing implant positioning, timing of placement, margin location, and the necessity for soft tissue augmentation procedures (Olsson and Lindhe 1991, Suárez-López et al. 2016). Importantly, phenotype modification should be regarded as a preventive biologic strategy aimed at enhancing tissue resilience rather than solely an esthetic intervention (Tavelli et al. 2021).

Digital dentistry and soft tissue optimization

Digital technologies have introduced unprecedented precision in peri-prosthetic tissue management. CAD/CAM-fabricated custom abutments, digitally designed emergence profiles, and virtual prosthetic planning permit-controlled modulation of peri-implant mucosal architecture. Digitally guided workflows enable clinicians to harmonize prosthetic contours with biologic tissue requirements, facilitating optimized soft tissue support, improved marginal adaptation, and enhanced esthetic integration. Controlled emergence profile design reduces unintended tissue compression and minimizes inflammatory responses associated with prosthetic misfit. Additionally, digital impression techniques reduce mechanical trauma to peri-prosthetic tissues, thereby contributing to preservation of soft tissue stability. Contemporary evidence increasingly supports the integration of digital planning as a biologically relevant tool rather than merely a technological convenience (Joda et al. 2017).

Minimally invasive approaches

Minimally invasive surgical and restorative techniques represent a cornerstone of modern peri-prosthetic tissue management. Preservation of vascular supply and reduction of surgical trauma contribute significantly to predictable healing and tissue stability. Techniques emphasizing atraumatic tissue manipulation, including tunnel approaches and flap designs minimizing vertical incisions, enhance soft tissue outcomes while reducing patient morbidity. Such strategies are particularly advantageous in esthetic regions and phenotype modification procedures (Misch 2020, Zucchelli et al. 2020). From a restorative perspective, minimally disruptive prosthetic protocols, including controlled abutment manipulation and precision-guided provisionalization, minimize inflammatory reactions and reduce the likelihood of adverse tissue remodeling. Collectively, minimally invasive approaches reflect a broader shift toward biologically respectful treatment strategies designed to maintain peri-prosthetic tissue homeostasis.

Conclusion

Peri-prosthetic soft tissues are fundamental determinants of biologic stability, functional success, and esthetic outcomes in restorative and implant therapy. Contemporary understanding of the periodontal and peri-implant phenotype has highlighted the importance of soft tissue thickness, keratinized mucosa, and supracrestal tissue dimensions in influencing peri-prosthetic tissue behavior and long-term treatment outcomes. In addition, prosthetic design variables, including emergence profile, restoration contour, margin placement, and cementation protocols, play a significant role in preserving peri-prosthetic tissue health.

Advances in soft tissue augmentation procedures, biomaterials, digital workflows, and minimally invasive approaches have expanded opportunities for phenotype modification and tissue optimization. Nevertheless, certain aspects of peri-prosthetic soft tissue management remain subjects of ongoing investigation. The necessity of a minimum width of keratinized mucosa and the magnitude of the influence of soft tissue thickness on crestal bone stability continue to be debated, with variations in study design and clinical methodology contributing to heterogeneity in the available evidence.

Therefore, successful peri-prosthetic tissue management should be guided by a patient-specific, phenotype-driven approach that integrates biologic principles, prosthetic considerations, and individual risk factors. Future well-designed longitudinal studies are needed to further clarify unresolved questions and strengthen evidence-based recommendations for optimizing peri-prosthetic tissue health and long-term clinical outcomes.
