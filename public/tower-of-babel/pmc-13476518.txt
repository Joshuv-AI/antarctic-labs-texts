The use of multi-species probiotic-derived vesicles to encapsulate luteolin for preventing colitis in a mouse model
Authors: Shulin Hou, Haishan Yang, Qian Wang, Xin Zheng, Ruining Li, Huili He, Lei Wang, Taoran Zhao, Qizhi Shuai, Yaojun Sun, Tianru Jin, Zhizhen Liu, Jun Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryProbiotic-derived membrane vesicles (MVs) offer advantages over live probiotics, including lower immunogenicity and enhanced bioavailability of dietary compounds. However, their composition and function depend on culture conditions. In this study, MVs from a single probiotic strain (S-MVs) were compared with those from co-cultured multi-species probiotics (M-MVs). M-MVs showed superior radical scavenging ability due to characteristic antioxidant proteins. Luteolin (Lut), a hydrophobic flavonoid, was loaded into M-MVs to generate Lut-MVs. Compared with Lut or M-MVs alone, Lut-MVs had enhanced water solubility, gastrointestinal stability, and antioxidative capacity. In a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced mouse colitis model, Lut-MVs prevented colitis by alleviating oxidative damage, improving intestinal barrier function, reducing inflammatory responses, and regulating colonic microbial balance. This work provides a method for preparing probiotic-derived vesicles and highlights their potential as carriers for hydrophobic drugs in treating colon diseases.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease, is a chronic inflammation involving dysbiosis of the intestinal microenvironment. Multiple dietary and environmental factors contribute to the risk of IBD development. To date, the incidence rate of UC has been increasing sharply in low- and middle-income countries, while it continues to rise in some high-income countries.1,2,3 Individuals with IBD are experiencing a severe decline in quality of life and an elevated risk of anxiety and depression.4,5,6 Furthermore, patients have an increased susceptibility to colorectal cancer.1 Conventional treatments for IBD, such as antioxidant drugs like 5-aminosalicylates,7,8 anti-inflammatory drugs including corticosteroids, and immunosuppressive agents,9,10 have limited efficacy and are associated with adverse side effects, driving researchers to seek new and effective therapies.

Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota is among the primary causes of IBD. Some probiotics have demonstrated potential in alleviating IBD by restoring intestinal microecological homeostasis. For instance, Bifidobacterium longum (B. longum) BAA2573 was shown to modulate the gut microbiome and metabolites, thereby ameliorating dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis in mice.11 Lactobacillus reuteri (L. reuteri) was shown to improve intestinal disorders not only by regulating gut microbiota and its metabolism but also by protecting the gut barrier, suppressing inflammation and immune responses, and inhibiting oxidative stress.12,13,14 Gut microbiota release membrane vesicles (MVs), which contain various nucleic acids and proteins,15,16,17 facilitating intercellular communication, particularly between bacteria and the host immune system.18

Accumulating evidence indicates that probiotic-derived MVs exhibit effects similar to their parent bacteria and can overcome the potential risks associated with using live probiotics in immunocompromised individuals.19,20 Both Clostridium butyricum (C. butyricum)21 and its derived MVs have been proven effective in attenuating colitis.22 Negatively charged MVs can readily penetrate the mucus layer and reach inflamed areas in the intestines through electrostatic interactions.23 Additionally, MVs from probiotics can be produced on a large scale due to the rapid reproduction rate of bacteria.24 However, the cargo molecules carried by MVs vary depending on environmental factors. For instance, the protein profiles of probiotic-derived MVs differ significantly under varying culture pH conditions.25 Cross-feeding involving the sharing of metabolites between co-cultured26,27 or naturally mixed gut microbes28 might be facilitated by vesicle-mediated communications. This raises the possibility that co-culturing multiple probiotic strains could yield MVs with distinct and potently enhanced functionalities compared with those derived from single strains.

Apart from intestinal flora imbalance, oxidative stress within the inflammatory bowel is regarded as another major factor contributing to the development of IBD.29,30 Mounting evidence indicates that natural flavonoids, such as quercetin,31 luteolin (Lut),32 and apigenin,33 exhibit anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities to alleviate IBD symptoms. Lut was reported to alleviate colitis in mice by suppressing macrophage activation and chemotaxis34 and via the HMGB1-TLR-NF-κB signaling pathway.35 However, its application is hindered by poor chemical stability and solubility. Various drug delivery systems, including polymers and biological particles, have been developed to address these limitations.36,37,38 Among these, biological particles such as probiotic-derived MVs offer distinct advantages over synthetic systems, including inherent biodegradability, cost-effectiveness, low immunogenicity, and the capacity to penetrate physiological barriers.24,39,40

Given that the cargo and biological effects of MVs from mixed strains might be shaped by inter-species interactions, in the current study, we first evaluated the cross-feeding relationship among B. longum, C. butyricum, and L. reuteri, each of which has been shown to alleviate IBD.11,12,13,14,21 These probiotics were then co-cultured to produce mixed-species membrane vesicles (M-MVs). Their characteristics, including particle size, morphology, and protein composition, were further studied and compared with those of MVs derived from the corresponding single strains (B-MVs, C-MVs, and L-MVs). M-MVs displayed superior free radical scavenging ability, likely attributable to their characteristic cargo proteins. This distinct protein profile may arise from cross-feeding interactions during co-cultivation, wherein nutrient exchange and inter-species signaling modulate bacterial metabolism and gene expression, reshaping bacterial proteomes and inducing proteins absent in mono-cultures.41,42 To further enhance therapeutic potential, we loaded M-MVs with luteolin (Lut-MVs) and evaluated their particle characteristics, cytotoxicity, and cellular uptake. Unlike synthetic carriers or single-strain MVs, our multi-species co-culture vesicles leverage bacterial cross-feeding to produce MVs with a distinct protein profile and enhanced functionality, while maintaining inherent biocompatibility and bioactivity. By integrating the intrinsic activity of M-MVs with the pharmacological effects of luteolin (Lut), Lut-MVs function synergistically to improve intestinal barrier function and alleviate oxidative damage and inflammation in colitis, positioning them as promising broad-spectrum agents for treating intestinal diseases.

Results

Preparation and characterization of M-MVs from co-cultured strains

In this study, we cultured each individual strain of the above-mentioned probiotics (B. longum, C. butyricum, and L. reuteri) individually or co-cultured them. Subsequently, MVs derived from co-cultured probiotics were further examined compared with those derived from mono-cultured strains.

We initially explored the mutual interactions among the three strains. As depicted in Figure 1A, viable cell counts in the co-cultured strains increased significantly, particularly for the three-strain co-culture, which was 2- to 3-times higher than that of mono-cultures, indicating no antagonistic effects among these strains. Additionally, viable cell counts also increased in mono-cultures supplemented with the cell-free supernatants (CFS) from the other two strains (Figure 1B). From the supernatant of the co-cultured three strains, we isolated M-MVs by ultracentrifugation (Figure 1C) and further compared them with MVs (B-MVs, C-MVs, and L-MVs) derived from the mono-cultured strains including B. longum, C. butyricum, and L. reuteri, respectively. The morphology and particle sizes of MVs were analyzed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS). All MVs exhibited typical spherical vesicle morphology (Figures 1D–1G). The median particle size of M-MVs was approximately 190 nm, larger than those of other MVs (Figure 1G). The particle sizes of B-MVs, C-MVs, and L-MVs are in the nanoscale, consistent with those reported by several groups previously.44,45,46,47

The protein composition profiles of MVs were determined by SDS-PAGE and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). As shown in the SDS-PAGE (Figure 1H), the protein size distribution of M-MVs was broad, ranging from 10 to 100 kDa, similar to those of B-MVs and C-MVs, while L-MVs had fewer proteins, confined to 30–50 kDa range. Characterization, gastrointestinal stability, and antioxidant activity of Lut-MVs

We then explored the potential of M-MVs as a delivery system. Lut, a flavonoid with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, was loaded into M-MVs via ultrasonic treatment and designated as Lut-MVs.50 As expected, Lut-MVs exhibited higher water solubility than free Lut (Figure 2A), similar to previous observations in which fucoxanthin-loaded MVs dissolved more effectively than free fucoxanthin.51,52 TEM images showed no significant changes in the morphology of Lut-MVs compared with M-MVs (Figures 2B and 2C). DLS analysis indicated a slight increase in vesicle size for Lut-MVs (220 nm) compared with M-MVs (190 nm) (Figures 2D and 2E).

To confirm the successful encapsulation of Lut into M-MVs, UV-vis absorption spectrophotometry and fluorescence microscopy were employed. UV absorption curves (Figure 2F) revealed peaks at 280 nm for the empty M-MVs and at 350 nm for free Lut, while Lut-MVs exhibited both peaks at 280 nm and 350 nm, indicating that Lut was indeed loaded into M-MVs. In the fluorescence microscopy analysis, Lut was substituted by the hydrophobic fluorescent dye Nile Red, while M-MVs were labeled with green membrane dye DiO. Figure 2G displayed the merged signals (yellow) of Nile Red and M-MVs, confirming their successful combination. Additionally, the Lut-loading capacity of M-MVs reached 58%, comparable to a previous report.53

The encapsulation of Lut within M-MVs not only enhanced its water solubility but also improved its gastrointestinal stability. After 3 h of gastric digestion, the retention rates of Lut were 78.3% for free Lut and 91.6% for Lut-MVs (Figure 2H). In the simulated intestinal fluid (Figure 2I), the relative content of free Lut rapidly decreased to 77.5% within 1 h and further declined to 52.2% after 6 h, while Lut-MVs maintained a high content of Lut (exceeding 90%) for 6 h. These findings align with previous studies,53,54 suggesting that probiotic-derived MVs provide a protective interface layer against degradation by pepsin.

Lut has been reported to exhibit significant scavenging properties against superoxide radicals.55,56 In the present study, in addition to evaluating the solubility and stability, we focused on the antioxidant activity of Lut encapsulated within M-MVs. DPPH (Figure 2J) and ABTS (Figure 2K) scavenging assays indicated that, free Lut exhibited more pronounced antioxidant capacity than M-MVs across all tested concentrations, while Lut-MVs demonstrated antioxidant activity comparable to that of free Lut. In conclusion, Lut-MVs display superior antioxidant activity compared to M-MVs, warranting further investigation.

Cytotoxicity and cellular uptake of Lut-MVs

High biocompatibility and efficient cellular uptake are essential for an oral drug to achieve its therapeutic efficacy. In this study, the biocompatibility and cellular uptake of Lut-MVs were evaluated using the macrophage cell line RAW264.7. Biocompatibility was assessed through a CCK8 assay. The results indicated that M-MVs did not show significant cytotoxicity at concentrations below 100 μg/mL, and cell viability was slightly decreased but remained above 80% when the concentration was increased to 200 μg/mL (Figure 3A). Similarly, B-MVs, C-MVs, and L-MVs derived from the mono-cultured strains did not impact cell viability (>80%) (Figures S2A–S2C). To track the distribution of Lut-MVs within RAW264.7 cells, Lut was replaced by Nile Red, and MVs were labeled with green fluorescent dye DiO. As shown in Figure 3C, macrophages treated with M-MVs exhibited clear DiO signals, while those treated with Nile Red-loaded M-MVs displayed merged yellow signals, indicating successful internalization of both empty and Nile Red-loaded M-MVs by the macrophages. The preventative effect of Lut-MVs in DSS-induced IBD mice

Inspired by the excellent gastrointestinal stability and antioxidant activity of Lut-MVs in vitro, as well as their high biocompatibility and efficient cellular uptake, we hypothesized that Lut-MVs could serve as an ideal therapeutic agent for colitis.

In the DSS-induced IBD mouse model (Figure 4A), we evaluated the preventive efficacy of Lut-MVs in comparison to Lut and other MVs. At the end of the experiment, no mortality was observed in the normal control (NC), M-MVs, and Lut-MVs groups, whereas the survival rate of mice in the DSS and Lut groups significantly decreased (Figure S3). During the experiment, mice in the NC group consistently gained weight, while those in the DSS-treated group had a dramatic decrease in body weight. Intervention with Lut, M-MVs, and particularly Lut-MVs, mitigated body weight loss to varying degrees (Figure 4B). Furthermore, mice in the DSS-treated groups exhibited anal bleeding and an elevated disease activity index (DAI) score. Colon atrophy, mainly attributed to dehydration, mucosal disruption, and colonic epithelial damage, is another critical parameter for evaluating the severity of IBD. In the current study, DSS treatment typically induced colonic shortening and an increased colonic mucosal damage index (CMDI), compared with the NC group. However, Lut-MVs and M-MVs significantly alleviated colon shortening and CMDI (Figures 4D–4F) induced by DSS. In comparison with B-MVs, C-MVs, and L-MVs, M-MVs exhibited a more pronounced effect in DSS-induced IBD mice (Figure S4). However, the Lut group exhibited limited efficacy (Figures 4B–4F), which may be attributed to the lower dose of Lut administered in our study.

To further assess the efficacy of the Lut-MVs at the histological level, H&E staining of mouse colon tissue was performed. As shown in Figure 4G, the crypt-villus architecture of the colon tissue in the NC group remained intact, whereas the DSS group displayed severe pathological changes, including thickened colonic walls, disordered villi, crypt loss, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration. In the M-MVs and Lut groups, partial recovery was observed, and the Lut-MVs group demonstrated the most significant improvement in the colonic structural integrity. Repair effect of Lut-MVs on the intestinal mucosal barrier in IBD mice

The results demonstrated that the NC group exhibited a uniformly thick mucosal layer with abundant reddish-purple-stained goblet cells (Figure 6A) and blue-purple mucoproteins (Figure 6B). In contrast, the DSS group showed a significant reduction in both goblet cells and mucoproteins, accompanied by disruption of the mucus layer. MUC2 is a secretory mucoprotein produced by goblet cells and plays a critical role in protecting the mucosal layer.59 Fluorescence analysis revealed that DSS treatment significantly reduced MUC2 levels in colon tissues, whereas M-MVs, Lut, and Lut-MVs markedly prevented this reduction (Figures 6C and 6F). This observation was further supported by RT-qPCR analysis of the gene that encodes MUC2 (Figure 6I).

The tight junction proteins, including ZO-1 and Occludin, are two crucial markers for maintaining epithelial barrier integrity.60,61 Our immunofluorescence and RT-qPCR analyses indicated that both ZO-1 and Occludin levels were significantly decreased in the DSS-induced IBD model, while this effect was notably mitigated in the M-MVs, Lut, and particularly Lut-MVs groups (Figures 6D–6K). In comparison with B-MVs, C-MVs, and L-MVs, M-MVs exhibited a more pronounced repairing effect in IBD mice by significantly increasing the levels of MUC2, ZO-1, and Occludin (Figures S7 and S8). Based on these findings, we suggest that Lut-MVs may effectively alleviate IBD by enhancing the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier.

Effects of Lut-MVs on antioxidation and anti-inflammation in the IBD mouse model

Oxidative stress, characterized by excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), is considered to play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of IBD.62 In vitro studies have shown that Lut and Lut-MVs possess great ROS scavenging capacity (Figures 2J and 2K). Therefore, we evaluated the levels of antioxidative indicators, including myeloperoxidase (MPO), catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione (GSH), malondialdehyde (MDA), and nitric oxide (NO) in intestinal tissue from IBD mice after different treatments. As shown in Figure 7, compared with the NC group, the DSS group exhibited decreased activities of antioxidant enzymes (CAT and SOD) and the non-enzymatic antioxidant GSH, while MPO activity was significantly increased, and there was an accumulation of excess MDA and NO. Treatment with M-MVs, Lut, and particularly Lut-MVs, significantly alleviated these changes in antioxidative indicators (Figures 7A–7F). In summary, these findings suggest that Lut-MVs, with their antioxidant capacities, effectively mitigate inflammation in the IBD mouse model by enhancing the antioxidant capacity of tissues.

Effects of Lut-MVs on intestinal flora regulation in IBD mice

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that dysbiosis of the gut microbiota is a key contributor to IBD.66,67 Therefore, we investigated whether Lut-MVs could modulate gut microbial homeostasis in IBD mice through 16s rRNA sequencing analysis.

The Venn diagram revealed that the unique operational taxonomic unit (OTU) numbers of intestinal flora in the NC, DSS, M-MVs, Lut, and Lut-MVs groups were 430, 641, 501, 376, and 321, respectively (Figure 8A), indicating that DSS treatment enhanced the diversity of the bacterial flora. The phylum level annotation of OTUs in each sample indicated that the dominant bacteria were Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Campylobacterota, and Desulfobacterota, accounting for over 95% of the total abundance across all five groups (Figure 8B). Compared with the NC group, DSS treatment significantly reduced the abundance of Bacteroidota, increased the abundance of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria and raised the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio (Figures 8B–8D). These findings are consistent with previous research, which has shown that the abnormal abundance of Proteobacteria is associated with dysbiosis in the gut microbiota68 and that an increased F/B ratio is implicated in intestinal inflammation.69 At the family level, there were notable increases in the abundances of harmful bacteria in the DSS group, including Desulfovibrionaceae and Enterobacteriaceae (belonging to the phylum Proteobacteria), Marinifilaceae, and Erysipelatoclostridiaceae (Figure 8E), which have been reported to be associated with colon diseases.69,70,71 Additionally, Oscillospiraceae, which is positively associated with leanness,72 exhibited relatively high abundance (Figure 8E), potentially accounting for the significant weight loss observed in the DSS group. However, these phenomena were reversed upon treatment with M-MVs, Lut, and especially Lut-MVs (Figures 8C–8E). Similarly, treatments with C-MVs and L-MVs also reduced the F/B ratio, while B-MVs treatment notably decreased Proteobacteria abundance (Figure S10). To further investigate the consequences of the altered gut microbiota composition in the Lut-MVs group, Spearman correlations were conducted between altered microbes and IBD-related factors. As shown in Figure 8I, the abundance of Proteobacteria (at the phylum level) was significantly negatively correlated with the antioxidant levels (CAT, GSH, and SOD), the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10, and the mucosal barrier protection protein ZO-1, but positively correlated with the inflammatory factor TNF-α. In contrast, the enrichment of the beneficial UCG-010 in the colon presented the opposite trend. Moreover, the abundance of the harmful bacteria, including Desulfovibrionaceae and Enterobacteriaceae, was significantly positively correlated with the accumulation of pro-oxidant factors (MPO and MDA) and the inflammatory factor IL-6 level, but negatively correlated with the expression level of the mucosal barrier-related protein MUC2. However, the beneficial gut bacteria, such as Bacteroides, Blautia, Lachnospiraceae, and Ruminococcus, exhibited the opposite correlation with these factors. These findings indicated that Lut-MVs treatment induces an increase in beneficial bacteria, including Ruminococcaceae, UCG-010, Butyricicoccaceae, Bacteroides, Blautia, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, and Ruminococcus_torques_group, and the decrease in harmful bacteria, including Proteobacteria, Enterobacteriaceae, Desulfovibrionaceae, Marinifilaceae, and Erysipelatoclostridiaceae. This contributes to the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant state as well as to the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier and further protects against the DSS-induced damage in the colon.

Discussion

In this study, we developed Lut-MVs by encapsulating Lut into M-MVs. Lut-MVs significantly alleviated DSS-induced colitis in mice by improving intestinal barrier integrity, alleviating oxidative stress, and modulating inflammatory responses, exhibiting superior efficacy compared with free Lut or blank M-MVs.

The enhanced intestinal barrier restoration conferred by Lut-MVs may involve the synergistic function of Lut and M-MVs. Lut has been reported to directly reinforce epithelial barrier function through the MAPK/NF-κB/MLCK and Nrf2 signaling pathways,77 as well as by restoring the balance of ILC3 subsets to promote intestinal barrier repair.78 Meanwhile, emerging evidence indicates that probiotic-derived vesicles can directly modulate tight junction expression.79,80 For instance, MVs derived from Limosilactobacillus reuteri have been reported to be taken up by intestinal epithelial cells, leading to elevated expression of ZO-1, E-cadherin (Cdh1), and Occludin; reduced intestinal permeability; and protective effects on epithelial tight junction functionality.80 Consistently, in our study, M-MVs alone also enhanced the expression of tight junction proteins, as evidenced by increased levels of MUC2, ZO-1, and Occludin (Figures 6I–6K). Based on these findings, Lut-MVs integrate the direct transcriptional effects of Lut with the intrinsic barrier-protective properties of M-MVs. This dual action likely results in synergistic enhancement of tight junction gene expression, achieving greater efficacy than either component alone, which is consistent with our experimental observations.

In conclusion, we developed a novel method for preparing M-MVs with enhanced antioxidant properties and successfully loaded Lut to generate Lut-MVs. Lut-MVs exhibited improved solubility, stability, and therapeutic efficacy in a colitis model, alleviating intestinal barrier dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation while modulating gut microbiota. This study establishes a novel strategy for preparing multi-species probiotic-derived vesicles and validates Lut-MVs as a promising platform for hydrophobic drug delivery in the treatment of colonic diseases.

Limitations of the study

Despite these promising findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, our study remains primarily descriptive, particularly regarding the mechanistic contribution of MsrA. While proteomic analysis identified MsrA as a protein uniquely enriched in M-MVs, and its known antioxidant function aligns with the observed phenotypic effects, we did not perform direct functional validation to establish causality, such as MsrA knockout or inhibition. Future studies employing genetic ablation of MsrA in probiotic strains and a comparative evaluation of MsrA-deficient vesicles in colitis models will be essential to determine whether MsrA plays a critical role in the observed therapeutic effects.

Second, the study lacks in vivo biodistribution data to confirm intestinal uptake and systemic exposure of Lut-MVs. Although prior reports indicate that bacterial extracellular vesicles can be internalized by intestinal epithelial cells via endocytosis and traverse polarized epithelial monolayers via transcytosis,81,82 we did not experimentally track the fate of Lut-MVs in host tissues. Direct in vivo imaging would provide more definitive evidence regarding their interaction with host cells and their potential function as bioactive participants in host-microbiota crosstalk. Future studies employing fluorescently labeled vesicles will be necessary to address this gap.

Third, the safety evaluation of Lut-MVs remains incomplete. Although no histopathological abnormalities in major organs or adverse effects on body weight and general behavior were observed, these assessments were limited to basic histological examination as a secondary endpoint. While our short-term safety assessment showed no obvious toxicity, long-term safety remains to be investigated. A comprehensive safety evaluation, including blood biochemistry, hematological parameters, and absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) profiling is essential for clinical translation and will be incorporated in future studies.

Additionally, the translational relevance of our findings is constrained by the use of the DSS-induced colitis model. While widely employed for studying acute intestinal inflammation, this model does not fully recapitulate the chronic, relapsing nature of human IBD. Dose optimization, long-term safety, and potential immunogenicity associated with repeated Lut-MV administration also warrant further investigation in more clinically relevant models.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mouse models and husbandry

C57BL/6J male mice (aged 6 weeks, body weight 20-23 g) utilized in this study were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China) and acclimatized for 7 days. Mice were housed under controlled conditions with free access to water and food, maintained at appropriate temperature and humidity levels. All animal experiments were conducted in strict compliance with guidelines approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanxi Medical University (approval no. 2024-112). This study used only male mice, so the potential influence of sex could not be assessed, and the findings may not be generalizable to females.

Microbe strains

The freeze-dried powders of B. longum CICC25033, C. butyricum CICC23847, and L. reuteri CICC6131 (obtained from China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (Beijing, China)) were resuscitated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. B. longum and L. reuteri were cultured anaerobically in MRS broth at 37 °C for 24 h, while C. butyricum was cultured under the same conditions with the addition of 0.1 mg/mL L-cysteine hydrochloride.

Cell lines

The monocyte-derived mouse macrophage cell line RAW264.7 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and glutamine (2 mM) at 37 °C under 5% CO2. The cell line was authenticated by the depositor (ATCC), no additional authentication was performed in our laboratory. All experiments were conducted with cells at low passage numbers (passages <15). Mycoplasma contamination was regularly excluded by PCR assays as suggested previously,84 and all tested batches were negative. Before stimulation, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight.

Method details

Inter-strain synergistic experiment

Each probiotic strain, their pairwise combinations (1:1) and a three-strain combination (1:1:1) were inoculated at 1% into fresh MRS broth and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h, respectively. Groups contained C. butyricum were supplemented with 0.1 mg/mL L-cysteine hydrochloride. Viable cells were determined using the plate counting method.85

Each probiotic strain was cultured in MRS medium supplemented with 1% cell-free supernatant (CFS) from the other two strains, respectively. CFS was obtained by centrifugation (11,000×g, 4 °C, 10 min) after the probiotic strain was cultured for 24 h. To analyze their cross-feeding relationships, viable cell counts were compared between groups with and without added CFS using the plate counting method.

Isolation of probiotic-derived MVs

B. longum, C. butyricum, L. reuteri, and their three-strain combinations were cultured anaerobically in MRS medium at 37 °C for 24 h, respectively. Supernatants were collected by centrifugation (3000×g, 15 min). Membrane vesicles (MVs) were isolated through sequential centrifugation steps as described by Maroto and colleagues previously.86 The supernatant was first centrifuged at 11,000×g for 30 min, then filtered through a 0.22 μm Millex-GP filter (Merck Millipore, Carrigtwohill, Ireland) to remove small cell debris. The filtrate was ultracentrifuged at 150,000×g for 90 min to obtain MV pellets. MVs were resuspended in sterile PBS and stored at -80 °C.

Preparation of Lut-MVs

The encapsulation of Lut was performed according to established methods.53,87 Lut was dissolved in ethanol at 0.2 mg/mL and mixed with M-MVs (0.2 mg/mL, protein concentration) obtained from co-cultured three probiotics in a 1:1 volume ratio. The mixture was sonicated at 200W for 30 cycles (5 s on, 10 s off) in an ice bath, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Unencapsulated luteolin in the mixtures was removed by ultrafiltration (Millipore, USA). The resulting luteolin-loaded M-MVs, designated as Lut-MVs, were concentrated by ultrafiltration to a final protein concentration of 0.2 mg/mL and stored at -80 °C until use.

Characterization of MVs and Lut-MVs

The morphology and particle size of samples were analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, H-7650, Hitachi, Japan) and dynamic light scattering (DLS, Nano ZS90, Malvern Instrument Co., Ltd., England), respectively. Ultraviolet–visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra were measured using a double-beam spectrophotometer (UH5300, Japan).

The loading capacity (LC) of Lut-MVs was measured according to a previous literature by Liang et al.53 Luteolin concentration was calculated based on the absorbance value at 350 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices MT1000, China).55 The BCA protein quantification kit (Solarbio) was employed to determine the protein concentration of Lut-MVs. The LC of Lut-MVs was calculated as follows: LC (%) = MLut/MMVs. Here, MLut is the mass of luteolin in Lut-MVs, and MMVs is the protein content of Lut-MVs.

To investigate the co-localization of M-MVs with luteolin, M-MVs was labeled with the 3,3′-dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine perchlorate (DiO) fluorescent dye, and luteolin was substituted with the hydrophobic fluorescent dye Nile Red. The labeled samples were analyzed by fluorescence microscopy (ECLIPSE Ti2, Nikon, Japan).

Proteomic analysis of MVs

The protein levels of MVs were analyzed using the BCA protein quantification kit (Solarbio) and 10% SDS-PAGE. Protein qualitative analysis of MVs was performed by Applied Protein Technology (Shanghai, China) via liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).

In vitro assessment of the antioxidant activity and stability of MVs

In vitro antioxidant activity was evaluated using 2,2’-azinobis-3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6- sulphonate (ABTS) and 1,1’-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical-scavenging assays, as previously reported.88 Lut-MVs, Lut, and other MVs were prepared at various concentrations. In a 96-well plate, 10 μL of each sample was added to wells, followed by 200 μL of ABTS working solution. After incubating at 30 °C for 6 min in the dark, the absorbance was measured at 734 nm. For the DPPH assay, 10 μL of each sample was mixed with 100 μL of DPPH solution in a 96-well plate. The reaction was carried out at 30 °C for 30 min in the dark, after which the absorbance was measured at 512 nm.

The stability of samples was ascertained in artificial gastric and intestinal fluids. 1 mL Lut-MVs (Lut concentration: 0.15 mg/mL) and Lut (0.15 mg/mL) were respectively added into the artificial gastric or intestinal fluids (5 mL). Subsequently, these mixtures were placed in a shaking incubator (100×g) at 37 °C for a specific period. The Lut content was measured based on the absorbance value at 350 nm.

Cytotoxicity and cellular uptake assay

The cytotoxic effects were determined by CCK8 assay. RAW264.7 macrophages in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 96-well plates with a density of 1×105 cells per well and cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium with 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 24 h. After removing the supernatant, the cells were then treated with 100 μL fresh medium containing different concentrations of MVs for 24 h. Subsequently, the supernatant was replaced by 100 μL of new medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent. After incubation for 1.5 h, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm with a Microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

To track the distribution of MVs in cells, MVs were labeled with fluorescent dye DiO. For Lut-MVs group, Lut was substituted by Nile Red. To determine the cellular uptake of MVs, RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 24-well plates at 1×105 cells per well, and co-cultured with fluorescent labeled MVs samples (500 uL of 1 mg/mL). After incubation for 24 h, a DAPI solution was added for nuclear staining for 10 min. Cell uptake was observed under a fluorescence microscope (ECLIPSE Ti2, Nikon).

Animal experiments

Mice were randomly assigned into five groups: (1) normal control (NC) group, (2) DSS group, (3) M-MVs group, (4) Lut group, and (5) Lut-MVs group. To assess the protective effect of Lut-MVs, mice in the M-MVs, Lut and Lut-MVs groups received daily oral administrations of 0.2 mL of M-MVs, Lut and Lut-MVs at a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL respectively, for 14 days. Mice in the NC and DSS groups were given 0.2 mL of sterile PBS as a control for 14 days. After 14 days, all experimental groups and the DSS group were provided with a 3% DSS (w/w) solution as drinking water for 7 days. Mice in the NC group were not treated with DSS. Daily observations and records were made regarding each mouse’s body weight, fur color, mental state, and fecal morphology. Additionally, daily mortality rates and disease activity index (DAI) scores were calculated for each group. DAI was calculated according to the literature.89

After 21 days of the experiment, all mice were humanely euthanized. Serum samples were collected for ELISA analysis. The dissected colon length was measured, and the colonic mucosal damage index (CMDI) was calculated according to previously published methods.90 Colon tissues were collected for antioxidant analysis, RNA extraction, histological examination and immunofluorescence studies. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were also collected for histological analysis. For microbiota analysis, colon contents were transferred into enzyme-free tubes, immediately frozen using liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C.

In vivo antioxidant activity of colon tissues

The colon tissues in cold saline (w/v 1:10) were homogenized in a tissue grinder (KZ-II, Service-bio, China), and then the homogenate supernatant was collected by centrifugation (12000xg, 10 min). Subsequently, the relevant antioxidant indices of colon samples, including the activities of MPO, SOD and CAT, as well as the levels of MDA, GSH and NO were assayed following the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vivo enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis of colon tissues

Blood samples were collected and allowed to clot at room temperature for 2 hours. Then, the serum samples were obtained by centrifugation at 4 °C, 3500×g for 10 minutes. The levels of cytokines IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α in the serum were quantified by ELISA kit, following the provided instructions.

Histological analysis and immunofluorescence

The Swiss-roll of colon tissue,91 heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were routinely fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, and then embedded in a paraffin blocks. The embedded specimens were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological analysis. Colon sections were also stained with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) or incubated with antibodies to detect tight junction proteins (Occludin, ZO-1, MUC2) by immunofluorescence.

RT-qPCR analysis of colon tissues

Total RNA from the colon tissue was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). cDNA was obtained using a reverse transcription kit (abm, Canada). qPCR analysis was conducted using the abm BlasTaq™ 2X qPCR MasterMix and primers. Primer sequences for genes related to tight junction proteins are shown in the key resources table, including those that encode ZO-1, Occludin and MUC2.

Analysis of gut microbiota

The microbiota of mouse colon contents were analyzed by 16S rDNA sequencing (V3-V4). Total genomic DNA was extracted from the colon content samples. Subsequently, The V3–V4 amplicons of 16S rRNA was amplified using Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs). PCR products were purified by magnetic beads. Sequencing libraries were generated on Illumina platforms (Novogene Genomics Technology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China). Gut microbiota composition, Venn diagram analysis, and species abundance analysis were performed on the cloud platform. Spearman correlation was analyzed by “ggplot2” and “pheatmap” packages of R software to evaluate the association between the gut microbiota and IBD-related metabolic parameters.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experimental data were tested at least three times and presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed by GraphPad Prism 9.0.0. Statistical comparisons among groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA was performed as appropriate, followed by multiple comparisons using the t-test to determine significant differences. A p-value below 0.05 was considered significant and marked with different letters, whereas groups sharing the same letter are not significantly different from each other. Descriptions of statistical analyses can also be found in figure legends. Exact p values are provided in the supplementary table. Raw data used in this manuscript is available from the authors on request.
