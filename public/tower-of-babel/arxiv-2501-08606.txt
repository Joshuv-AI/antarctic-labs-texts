Analysis of quantum mechanics with real-valued Schrödinger equation, single-event quantum-path dynamics, Mauprtuis path in parameter space, and branching paths beyond semiclassics
Kazuo Takatsuka

Abstract
We analyze the Schrödinger dynamics and the Schrödinger function (or the so-called wavefunction), from the viewpoint of conflict and compatibility between a distribution function and dynamical paths. The following four analyses will be made in due order. (1) The Schrödinger equation is reconstructed from scratch in the real field only, without referring to Newtonian mechanics nor optics. Only the very simple conditions such as the space-time translational symmetry and the conservation of flux and energy are imposed on the factorization of the density distribution in configuration space. The real-valued Schrödinger function attained is naturally interpreted as a coherent distribution function of a two-dimensional vector. On returning to the original Schrödinger equation, it is readily understood why and how the imaginary number should arise in the very fundamental equation, and we directly confirm that the Schrödinger equation is physically consistent. (2) The Langevin dynamics gives a path of a Brownian particle in contrast to the Gaussian distribution function subject to the diffusion equation. Likewise in quantum dynamics, we formulate a single-event path dynamics, contrasting with the Schrödinger distribution function. The path thus attained is referred to as one-world path (or quantum stochastic path), which represents, for instance, a path of a singly launched electron in the double-slit experiment that leaves a spot at the measurement board, while many of accumulated spots give rise to the fringe pattern. To formulate the one-world path, we start from the Feynman-Kac formula to draw a relation between a stochastic dynamics and the parabolic differential equations, to one of which the Schrödinger equation is transformed. We thus extract the basic stochastic dynamics for individual single-event (one-world) dynamics. (3) To highlight the roles of the flux and energy conservation as the central pillars for the Schrödinger dynamics, we next study a path dynamics in an infinite dimensional parameter space. We find that the quantum Maupertuis-Hamilton principle reveals manifolds of symplectic structure in the parameter space on which the energy and flux to be conserved. The classical-trajectory-like paths are driven by the coupled ordinary differential equations in a parameter space, which is actually equivalent to propagating the Schrödinger function in space-time. (4) A detailed physical analysis over the space-time propagation of the Schrödinger function is made from the viewpoint of the ultimate roles of classical paths. It is well known that the standard semiclassical theory can be very well represented in terms of classical trajectories. Yet, we show that such trajectory components are demanded to branch into many coherent pieces beyond the semiclassical regime and dissolve into the deep dynamics of genuine full quantum dynamics. We track how the inherent quantum nature like the Huygens-principle-like properties is built.

II.1

It is widely

The “derivation” led Schrödinger himself to an interpretation

[

The notion of the physical wave is not

Instead of tracking the historical progress of quantum mechanics,

[

,
11
,
12
,
9

the imaginary unit

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\delta\phi_{r}&\delta\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)\left(\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\delta\phi_{r}&\delta\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)\left(-\text{{J}}\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m}+\text{{J}}f(q,t)\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right),

which imposes an external condition depending on the choice of
f(q,t)
. A
natural choice is

f(q,t)=-V(q,t)

(80)

with
V(q,t)
being a relevant system potential function. Thus the variational
principle demands the following expression

\hbar\text{{J}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)=\left(\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m}+V(q,t)\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right),

(81)

and the quantum mechanical Hamiltonian is defined as

\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m}+V(q,t),

(82)

and thereby Eq. (
81
) can be rewritten as

\hbar\text{{J}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)=\hat{H}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right),

(83)

or

\hbar\text{{J}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)=\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}+V\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right).

(84)

By comparing this expression with Eq. (
37
), we find

c_{t}=1.

(85)

The Schrödinger equation in the present representation is summarized as
follows.

\hat{H}=\text{{J}}\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}

(86)

and

\hat{p}=-\text{{J}}\hbar\vec{\nabla}

(87)

for the real-valued vector

\bar{\psi}(q,t)=\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}(q,t)\\
\phi_{c}(q,t)\end{array}\right),

(88)

which may be referred to as the Schrödinger vector, and should be
distinguished from the Dirac state vector
\left|\Psi\right\rangle
.
[
1
]

Incidentally, we confirm that

\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\delta\phi_{r}&\delta\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)\left[\left(\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)-\left(\text{{J}}\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m}+\text{{J}}V\right)\right]\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)=0

(89)

is never equivalent to

\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}&\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)\left[\left(\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)-\left(\text{{J}}\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m}+\text{{J}}V\right)\right]\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)=0,

(90)

since the latter holds irrespective of
V(q)
as long as the flux conservation
along with the translational symmetries in space-time for
\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}&\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)^{T}
are satisfied, while Eq. (
89
) demands that
\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}&\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)^{T}
should follow a dynamics on the specified
V(q,t)
.

II.7
The Heisenberg equation

From Eq. (
83
) it follows that for an arbitrary time-independent
operator
\hat{A}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}(q,t)&\phi_{c}(q,t)\end{array}\right)\hat{A}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}(q,t)\\
\phi_{c}(q,t)\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}&\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)\left(\text{{J}}^{-1T}\hat{H}\hat{A}+\hat{A}\hat{H}\text{{J}}^{-1}\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\hbar}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}&\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)\left[\hat{H},\hat{A}\right]\text{{J}}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)

with
\left[\hat{H},\hat{A}\right]=\hat{H}\hat{A}-\hat{A}\hat{H}
, where
the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=

\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m}+V(q)
is assumed to be
Hermitian for square integrable (L
2
) functions
\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}&\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)^{T}.
Equation (
II.7
) gives just the Heisenberg equation
of motion in the real-valued space. Putting
\hat{A}=

\hat{H}\,
, the
conservation of energy is seen.

II.8
The canonical
Schrödinger equation in the complex number field

The symplectic form of two component vectors in the real number field is
essentially equivalent to the complex number scalar field (see Eq.
(
24
)), under setting

\text{{J}}\rightarrow i.

(100)

Then the Hamiltonian, Eq. (
86
), and the momentum operator, Eq. (
87
),
respectively read

\hat{H}=i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}

(101)

and

\hat{p}=-i\hbar\vec{\nabla},

(102)

which are to be operated on a scalar function

\psi(q,t)=\phi_{r}(q,t)+i\phi_{c}(q,t),

(103)

with
\bar{\psi}(q,t)\leftrightarrow\psi(q,t)
. The real-valued vector
Schrödinger equation (
83
) is transformed to the canonical
Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(q,t)

\displaystyle=\hat{H}\psi(q,t)

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}+V\right)\psi(q,t).

(104)

Conversely, the separation of Eq. (

) to the real and imaginary parts

It is obvious that the complex-valued equation of motion, Eq. (

) is
far easier to handle both mathematically and numerically. In particular, the

),

II.9
Normalization

There are basically two ways of normalization. One is to resort to the usual
average (expectation value), for instance

E^{\text{av}}=\left\langle\hat{H}\right\rangle=\frac{\int dq\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}(q,t)&\phi_{c}(q,t)\end{array}\right)\hat{H}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}(q,t)\\
\phi_{c}(q,t)\end{array}\right)}{\int\rho(q,t)dq}.

(105)

The other one is the space-time distribution

\displaystyle E^{\text{local}}(q,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}(q,t)&\phi_{c}(q,t)\end{array}\right)\hat{H}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}(q,t)\\
\phi_{c}(q,t)\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\frac{\hat{H}\psi}{\psi},

(109)

from which the energy eigenvalue problem is already seen. Likewise the
space-time distribution of the velocity is

\displaystyle\vec{v}^{\text{local}}(q,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}(q,t)&\phi_{c}(q,t)\end{array}\right)\frac{\hat{p}}{m}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}(q,t)\\
\phi_{c}(q,t)\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{m\rho}\left(\phi_{r}\left(\vec{\nabla}\phi_{c}\right)-\phi_{c}\left(\vec{\nabla}\phi_{r}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\frac{\vec{\nabla}\psi}{\psi}.

(113)

This class of normalization arises because the physical quantities like

E^{\text{av}}
and
\vec{v}^{\text{local}}(q,t)
should be homogeneous of
degree zero in
\psi
. The notion of quantum velocity is hidden behind

\bar{\psi}
in this manner and is retrieved as above. This form of
\vec{v}^{\text{local}}(q,t)
will be used in the next section (Sec.

III
) for the discussion of the one-world Schrödinger
dynamics. The comparison of Eq. (
109
) and Eq. (
113
)
under setting
\phi_{c}(q,t)\equiv 0
highlights why the real-valued
Schrödinger function should be a two component vector and equivalently why
the original Schrödinger function has to be complex-valued.

II.10
Summary of this section: Meaning of the Schrödinger functions
and vectors

We here summarize the route to the real-valued Schrödinger equation and
what lie behind the procedures.

II.10.1
The process to the real-valued Schrödinger equation
itemized

1) Consider a function
\rho(q,t)
in the Euclidean space-time, which is
positive semidefinite everywhere. (
q
specifies a multidimensional
configuration space.)

2) Factorize
\rho(q,t)
as

\rho(q,t)=\bar{\psi}(q,t)^{T}\bar{\psi}(q,t)
,

with the Schrödinger vector

\bar{\psi}(q,t)=\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}(q,t)&\phi_{c}(q,t)\end{array}\right)^{T}
,

where both
\phi_{r}(q,t)
and
\phi_{c}(q,t)
are real-valued functions.

3) Translational symmetry of
q
-space gives a generator and associated
momentum operator as in

\bar{\psi}(q,t)^{T}(-\hbar
J
\vec{\nabla})\bar{\psi}(q,t)
. The
constant
\hbar
emerges only from comparison with the experimental facts.

4) Translational symmetry of
t
-space gives a generator

\bar{\psi}(q,t)^{T}(\hbar
J
\partial_{t})\bar{\psi}(q,t).

5)
\rho(q,t)
is assumed to induce an incompressible flow satisfying the
equation of continuity

\partial_{t}\rho(q,t)=-\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{j}(q,t)
because
\rho(q,t)
is
assumed to conserve the content, which is actually the number of particles involved.

6) The flux
\vec{j}(q,t)
is found to be

\vec{j}(q,t)=-\left(\hbar/m\right)\bar{\psi}(q,t)^{T}
J
\vec{\nabla}\bar{\psi}(q,t)
,

and the equation of continuity reads

\bar{\psi}(q,t)^{T}\left(\hbar\partial_{t}+\text{{J}}\hat{p}^{2}/2m\right)\bar{\psi}(q,t)=0.

7) The continuity equation is automatically extended to that under a potential
function
V(q,t)
as

\bar{\psi}(q,t)^{T}\left(\hbar\partial_{t}+\text{{J}}\left(\hat{p}^{2}/2m+V\right)\right)\bar{\psi}(q,t)=0
, and thereby the quantum
Hamiltonian is defined as

\hat{H}=\hat{p}^{2}/2m+V
.

8) Among many possible
\rho(q,t)
that satisfy the equation of continuity, we
choose the generic (most probable in this context) one so that
\bar{\psi}(q,t)
behind
\rho(q,t)
satisfies a variational condition

\left(\delta\bar{\psi}(q,t)^{T}\right)\left(\hbar\partial_{t}+\text{{J}}\left(\hat{p}^{2}/2m+V\right)\right)\bar{\psi}(q,t)=0

or simply

\left(\text{{J}}\hbar\partial_{t}-\left(\hat{p}^{2}/2m+V\right)\right)\bar{\psi}(q,t)=0
,

which is equivalent to the original Schrödinger equation that is
decomposed to the real and imaginary parts.

III
Stochastic path dynamics to represent a single quantum incident and
the Schrödinger distribution function

In order to capture the intrinsic physical nature of the Schrödinger
dynamics, we below continue a series of detailed analyses from three different
perspectives of “continuous and smooth distribution
function” versus “path (lay)
dynamics.” In doing do, we need to distinguish the
path-representation of the Schrödinger function and a physical quantum
path, a continuous line along which a point mass may track. This section
discusses the notion of possible physical path, and the two subsequent
sections, Sec.
IV
and Sec.
V
, treat the
path representations of the Schrödinger function.

III.1
Introductory remarks

III.1.1
Distribution and paths in quantum mechanics

The Schrödinger equation and its function do not represent a single
incident (occurrence of an event) but a distribution of coherent superposition
over an ensemble of the possible incidents. Therefore, the Schrödinger
equation is unlikely to be capable of giving direct (unsuperposed) information
for individual one-world experiment. Take an example again from a one-particle
launching in the double slit experiment. It is often misunderstood that a
particle may be able to simultaneously pass through the two slits, in view
that a Schrödinger function
\psi(q,t)
mathematically does. An essential
question about quantum mechanics is therefore whether one can single out a
dynamical path corresponding to an individual event. If yes, it must be
consistent with the prediction by the Schrödinger function. That is, we
need to answer a question on how such an ensemble of independent single event
paths, if any, can create the interference pattern. A single-event path, which
we refer to as one-world path in what follows, serves as a path of a single
dice “God” may cast.
[
9
]

Here in this section we clarify the difference and relationship between the
one-world path and the coherent sum of “many-worlds” reflected in the Schrödinger function.

III.1.2
Paths and flow lines

One of the most beautiful and significant path-representations of the
Schrödinger function is due to Bohm with the so-called Bohmian
trajectories.
[
3
,
41
,
42
]
The Bohmian
trajectories are actually the smooth and continuous flow lines of the
Schrödinger function.
[
42
]
Due to the presence of the
Laplacian in the Schrödinger equation, the Schrödinger function itself
and the associated trajectories are required to be smooth (differentiable
everywhere) in general. This is the intrinsic property of distribution
functions. However, the smoothness is not necessarily applied to the possible
quantum paths. Notably, the Laplacian is translated to the generator of the
Wiener process in the Ito
formula,
[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]

thereby giving birth to indifferentiable paths (an example as such is seen in
Eq. (
133
)).

III.1.3
Feynman path integrals

In discussing the roles of paths in quantum mechanics, it is natural to start
from the Feynman kernel
[
34
,
6
,
35
,
36
]

\displaystyle K(q,t)=\left\langle q\left|\exp\left(\frac{1}{i\hbar}\hat{H}t\right)\right|0\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\int d^{3}q_{1}\cdots\int d^{3}q_{N}\left(\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}\right)^{3(N+1)/2}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\left(\frac{m}{2}\frac{\left(q_{k+1}-q_{k}\right)^{2}}{\Delta t}-V(q_{k})\Delta t\right)\right]

(114)

with

\Delta t=\frac{t}{N+1}.

(115)

As seen in Eq. (
114
), it is expressed in term of the democratic
summation of continuous polylines (broken lines), each connecting two
neighboring positions
q_{k}
and
q_{k+1}
of an infinitessimal distance.
This kernel is somewhat similar to the Wiener path-integrals for the Brownian
motion (shown later in order in Eq. (
125
)). However, the integral
measure is not well defined in Eq. (
114
). As seen explicitly, no
dynamics is imposed on each line or “path”.
Yet, the stationary-phase paths are found to satisfy the Lagrange equation of
motion, thus giving rise to classical trajectories. Otherwise, no specific
dynamics is assumed.

III.1.4
Nagasawa theory

Nagasawa
[
51
,
52
]

established his extensive theory of quantum stochastic path dynamic based on the Kolmogorov and Ito theories, which is
mathematically rigorous: A Markov process having the transition probability
density consisting of the Green function for the following parabolic partial
differential equation (or a backward Fokker-Planck equation)

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\nabla^{2}u+b(q,t)\cdot\nabla u=0

(116)

is associated with the stochastic process satisfying

X_{t}=X_{0}+\sigma B_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}b(X_{s},t)ds,

(117)

with
B_{t}
being the Brownian process. Nagasawa transforms Eq. (
116
)
to a set of

\frac{\partial\psi_{N}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\nabla^{2}\psi_{N}+V(q,t)\psi_{N}=0

(118)

and

-\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}_{N}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\nabla^{2}\tilde{\psi}_{N}+V(q,t)\tilde{\psi}_{N}=0

(119)

each having a pair of real-valued solutions

\psi_{N}(q,t)=\exp(R(q,t)+S(q,t))\text{ \ and \ }\tilde{\psi}_{N}=\exp(R-S),

(120)

where the real-valued functions
R(q,t)
and
S(q,t)
are equivalent to those
in the complex valued Schrödinger function

\psi=\exp\left(R+iS\right),

(121)

(note the position of
R
in Eqs. (
120
) and (
121
)).
Nagasawa calls
\psi_{N}(x,t)
and
\tilde{\psi}_{N}(x,t)
, respectively,
Evolution function and Backward evolution function, neither of which is the
direct solution of the Schrödinger equation. Then, after Eq. (
117
),
he gives the following stochastic process

dX_{t}=\frac{\hbar}{m}\nabla(R+S)dt+\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m}}dW.

(122)

Note again that this process is to represent his
\psi_{N}=\exp(R+S)
of Eq.
(
120
) but not the Schrödinger function itself. Nagasawa
claims that his theory thus formulated has exceeded the Schrödinger
framework in many aspects, including the uncertainty
principle.
[
51
,
52
]
He has thus established
the relationship between stochastic theory and quantum dynamics.

III.2
Stochastic paths and the Schrödinger equation

We below consider a possible one-world Schrödinger dynamics in our own
way, which is based on the relation between the Feynman-Kac formula for
statistical theory and a stochastic differential
equation.
[
53
,
54
]
We first outline the
Feynman-Kac formula and the relevant Green function.

III.2.1
Feynman-Kac formula and stochastic differential equation

To single out a dynamical path from quantum dynamics, we revisit the standard
relation between the stochastic process and the associated forward diffusion
equation under a potential function
V(q,t)
[
53
,
55
]

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(q,t)=D\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial q^{2}}u(q,t)-\lambda V(q,t)u(q,t).

(123)

The Feynman-Kac formula for the Green function of this diffusion equation is

\displaystyle G(q,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\left(q,t:0.0)\right)}\exp\left[-\lambda\int_{0}^{t}V(s,X(s,\omega))ds\right]dP_{W\left(q,t:0.0)\right)}(\omega),

(124)

where
dP_{W\left(q,t:0.0)\right)}(\omega)
is the Wiener measure of the
Brownian motion, and
G(q,t)
can be written more explicitly as

\displaystyle G(q,t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\left(4\pi D\Delta t\right)^{N/2}}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dq_{k}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Delta t\left(\frac{1}{4D}\frac{\left(\Delta q_{k}\right)^{2}}{\Delta t}+\lambda V(q_{k},t_{k})\right)\right]

(125)

with
t_{k}=k\Delta t,

q_{k}=q(t_{k})
,
q_{0}=0
,
q_{n}=q,

\Delta q_{k}=q_{k+1}-q_{k}
, and
D
being the diffusion constant.
G(q,t)
in Eq.
(
125
) looks similar to the Feynman kernel of Eq. (
114
),
but no imaginary number is included here. Behind the distribution function

u(q,t)
and the Green function lies a dynamical equation in the form of the
Ito stochastic differential
equation
[
50
,
47
]

dX_{t}=\alpha(X_{t},t)dt+dW\left(t,\omega\right)

(126)

with

X_{t}=X\left(t,\omega\right)

and
W\left(t,\omega\right)
being the Wiener process.

Consider a transition probability for

\left(t,X_{t}\right)\rightarrow\left(t+\Delta t,X_{t+\Delta t}\right)\text{ with a small }\Delta t.

(127)

From Eq. (
126
) the Wiener process is retrieved out as

W\left(t+\Delta t\right)-W\left(t\right)=X\left(t+\Delta t\right)-X\left(t\right)-\alpha(X_{t},t)\Delta t,

(128)

and since
W\left(t+\Delta t\right)-W\left(t\right)
is subjected to
the transition probability of the Wiener process, we define a probability for
a path starting from
\left(X\left(t\right),t\right)
to arrive at a
place nearby
X\left(t+\Delta t\right)
at time
t+\Delta t
as

\displaystyle P_{X}(\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta x}{\left(4\pi D\Delta t\right)^{1/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{\left(W\left(t+\Delta t\right)-W\left(t\right)\right)^{2}}{4D\Delta t}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta x}{\left(4\pi D\Delta t\right)^{1/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{\left(X\left(t+\Delta t\right)-X\left(t\right)\right)^{2}}{4D\Delta t}\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp[\frac{1}{2D}\alpha(X_{t},t)\left(X\left(t+\Delta t\right)-X\left(t\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4D}\alpha(X_{t},t)^{2}\Delta t].

(129)

Using the transition probability for the pure Wiener process (with
V=0
)

P_{W}(\Delta t)=\frac{\Delta x}{\left(4\pi D\Delta t\right)^{1/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{\left(W\left(t+\Delta t\right)-W\left(t\right)\right)^{2}}{4D\Delta t}\right],

(130)

P_{X}(\Delta t)
is further rewritten in a compact form as

\displaystyle P_{X}(\Delta t)=P_{W}(\Delta t)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\frac{\alpha(X_{t},t)}{2D}\left(X\left(t+\Delta t\right)-X\left(t\right)\right)^{2}-\frac{\alpha(X_{t},t)^{2}}{4D}\Delta t\right].

(131)

Here, as usual, we introduce a function
A(q,t)
that satisfies

\frac{\partial A(q,t)}{\partial q}=\alpha(q,t).

(132)

Then the Ito formula for
A
reads

\displaystyle dA(X,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}dt+\frac{\partial A}{\partial X}dX+\frac{\partial^{2}A}{\partial X^{2}}Ddt

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}+D\frac{\partial^{2}A}{\partial X^{2}}\right)dt+\alpha(X,t)dX,

(133)

in which

dXdX=2Ddt

(134)

has been used. Hence

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\alpha(x,t)dX

\displaystyle=A(T)-A(0)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}+D\frac{\partial^{2}A}{\partial x^{2}}\right)dt

(135)

and the probability for a stochastic path to pass through the possible range

\Phi
is

\displaystyle P_{X}(\Phi)=\int_{\Phi}\exp\left[\frac{1}{2D}\left(A(T)-A(0)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{4D}\left(2\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}+2D\frac{\partial^{2}A}{\partial X^{2}}+\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial X}\right)^{2}\right)dt\right]

\displaystyle\times dP_{W}(\omega).

(136)

Now by setting

2\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}+2D\frac{\partial^{2}A}{\partial q^{2}}+\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial q}\right)^{2}=4\lambda V(q,t).

(137)

we reach the Feynman-Kac formula of Eq. (
124
).

Further, defining a real-valued function
\psi(q,t)
as

\psi_{f}(q,t)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2D}A(q,t)\right),

(138)

Eq. (
137
) is transformed to a backward diffusion equation

\frac{\partial\psi_{f}(q,t)}{\partial t}=\left(-D\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial q^{2}}+\lambda V(q,t)\right)\psi_{f}(q,t).

(139)

With further changes of variables,
[
55
]
we after all reach the standard
Feynman-Kac formula, Eq. (
124
), which is followed by the Green function
of a forward diffusion equation

\frac{\partial G(q,t)}{\partial t}=\left(D\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial q^{2}}-\lambda V(q,t)\right)G(q,t)

(140)

with

G(q,0)=\delta(q),

(141)

which is to be used for propagation of a forward diffusion function
\psi_{f}(q,t)
from time zero to the future (
t>0
) such that

\psi_{f}(q,t)=\int_{0}^{t}dsG(q,s)\psi_{f}(q,s).

(142)

The Markov process of Eq. (
126
) is therefore followed by a stochastic
dynamics

\displaystyle dX_{t}

\displaystyle=\alpha(X_{t},t)dt+dW\left(t,\omega\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial A(X_{t},t)}{\partial x}dt+dW\left(t,\omega\right)

\displaystyle=2D\frac{\nabla\psi_{f}}{\psi_{f}}dt+dW\left(t,\omega\right),

(143)

which sets a foundation on which to consider the stochastic paths in harmony
with the Schrödinger dynamics. We thus have seen a relation between a
stochastic process and the parabolic partial differential equation. We next
consider the similar treatment for the Schrödinger dynamics.

It is interesting to compare the velocity drift term of Eq. (
143
)

\alpha(X_{t},t)=2D\frac{\nabla\psi_{f}}{\psi_{f}}

(144)

and the quantum local velocity of Eq. (
113
)

\frac{1}{\rho}v(q,t)=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\frac{\nabla\psi}{\psi},

(145)

where
\psi

Quantum stochastic paths driven by the Schrödinger velocity field

We intend to implant the information of the Schrödinger equation into the

III.2.3

The dynamics of the momentum

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}&\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)\hat{p}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\

\phi_{c}\end{array}\right),

(notice however that the integration over the

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{c}dX_{t}=\frac{1}{m\rho}P_{X_{t}}dt+\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m}}dW_{0}\left(t,\omega\right)\\

\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ }\end{array}\right.

(184)

III.3
No direct interaction but indirect correlation among the
one-world paths

We stress again that the one-world paths are supposed to run on different
occasions, because each of them is launched independently. In this
regard, we recall that each path involved in the Brownian process represents
an individual sample of a single Brownian motion in real space. An infinite
number of possible Brownian paths are theoretically supposed to run on
different occasions in a manner one after another one. (We are not discussing
the collision among the Brownian particles.) Therefore, no mutual direct
interaction among the paths is expected.

On the other hand, the Feynman path integral is subtle in that each path in it
is not subject to any dynamics and is associated with the imaginary
“weighting factor”. Since they are
democratically superposed in the kernel, each of the paths may be regarded as
a “basis function” to expand the
kernel.
[
37
]
The simultaneous interference among the
paths through the coherent summation is the very core of the theory.

Nevertheless, a one-world path can “indirectly” correlate with other one-world paths through the
velocity drift term, Eq. (
III.2.2
), which is a function of the
Schrödinger function. We mean by indirect correlation that (i) we have no
machinery through which two or more one-world paths, launched on different
occasions, mechanically interact each other, but (ii) as long as a same
Schrödinger function lies behind the drift velocity of Eq.
(
III.2.2
), not only they conform to the same global trend but also
each is mathematically responsible to form the
\alpha(X_{t},t)
through the
Schrödinger function. Therefore, even a slight change of the surroundings
like an experimental setting in the level of the one-world path can affect the
Schrödinger function, and thereby it can modulate the way of correlation
among the paths. Therefore
\alpha(X_{t},t)
may (yet doesn’t have to) be
regarded as a nonlocal hidden variable, and it varies in general depending on
experimental contexts such as the initial conditions, boundary conditions,
switching the measurement channels, and so on. The one-world paths are thus
supposed to run under the mutual indirect correlation, which is nonlinear in
the sense of self-referring. We will be back to this aspect later with a
precise discussion.

III.3.1
Interference fringe intensity pattern in the double slit experiment

Each path can pass through only one of the two slits in the double-slit
experiment. On the other hand, a single Schrödinger function can bifurcate
and pass through the two slits simultaneously as a coherent distribution
function. This is well represented by the Feynman path integrals, since the
packet consists of many independent geometrical paths in the sense of Eq.
(
114
). Note that it is not
\left|\psi(q,t)\right|^{2}

that physically makes a set of spots on the measurement board, but each
one-world path one by one with an amplitude
\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}(X_{0},0)&\phi_{c}(X_{0},0)\end{array}\right)^{T}
. Nevertheless, each one-world
path is “driven and guided” by the drift
velocity term, which is composed of the relevant Schrödinger function

\psi(q,t)
. It is therefore not very mysterious that the interference pattern
is shaped after many frequencies of launching of single particles. It is a
great mystery how nature manages to materialize the nonlinear relation between
the parts (one-world paths) and the whole (the Schrödinger function).

III.3.2
A tie to and difference from the Bohm trajectory

It is interesting in the present context to recall the idea of de Broglie and
Bohm that the Schrödinger function should serve as a pilot wave that
guides the particle motion.
[
21
,
22
]

As a matter of fact, the Schrödinger function serves in the drift term as
in Eq. (
III.2.2
), but not as a direct “wave” to guide. We need to time-propagate the
Schrödinger function simultaneously or before integration of the
stochastic equation. Then we have quantum Hamilton equations of motion, Eq.
(
184
), in which the velocity drift term is installed. On the other
hand, the Bohm representation of the Schrödinger
function
[
32
]
reads in the form

\psi\left(q,t\right)=R(q,t)\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}S_{B}\left(q,t\right)\right),

(187)

and the quantum Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) equation

\frac{\partial S_{B}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2m}\left(\nabla S_{B}\right)^{2}+V-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\nabla^{2}R}{R}=0,

(188)

is derived along with the equation of continuity for
R(q,t)^{2}
. If

\psi\left(q,t\right)
is given beforehand, the quantum velocity is given
by

v_{B}=\frac{1}{m}\nabla S_{B}=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\frac{\nabla\psi}{\psi}.

(189)

Therefore
v_{B}
is exactly the same as the drift velocity of Eq.
(
III.2.2
), as long as a common Schrödinger function is resorted
to (see also Eq. (
113
)). Hence, the mathematical difference
between the Bohmian trajectory and the one-world path is the presence of the
Wiener process or not. Meanwhile, it is known already that the interference
(fringe intensity) pattern in the double-slit experiment is numerically
realized by the set of the Bohmian trajectories,
[
56
]

and therefore a sufficient large set of the one-world paths should reproduce
the similar interference pattern. Physically, however, the Bohmian
trajectories do not represent a one-world physical path but only flow-lines of
the
S_{B}
-plane.
[
42
]
Mathematically they are a set of
integral curves of Eq. (
188
). Since the Bohmian representations, Eq.
(
187
), Eq. (
188
), and the equation of continuity for

R(q,t)^{2}
, are all equivalent to the Schrödinger equation itself, it
must be logically hard for the Bohm theory to make an essentially novel
interpretation beyond the limit of the Schrödinger equation. Yet, it sheds
a new light on the hidden properties of the Schrödinger dynamics as in Eq.
(
189
).

Recalling how the real-valued Schrödinger equation has been made up in
Sec.
II
, we notice that there is no theoretical mechanism
that can bring about stochasticity into the Schrödinger dynamics. To
single out a one-world path, therefore, we made use of the Feynman-Kac formula
as a starting point, and the stochastic nature of a one-world path has been
identified in such a process. Therefore, the Bohmian trajectories have no way
to take account of the Wiener process.
S_{B}
-plane is similar to the
classical action plane
S_{cl}
, and indeed in the limit of
\hbar\rightarrow 0
,
S_{B}\rightarrow S_{cl}
. Nonetheless, this
convergence does not warrant that the Bohmian paths at
\hbar\neq 0
represent
the one-world quantum dynamics, because of the lack of stochasticity. Also,
Eq. (
188
) claims that the continuous set of Bohmian paths run
simultaneously, not one after another. This is therefore not realistic as a
physical path but each is regarded as a probe of the
flow-lines.
[
42
]

III.4
Classical limit of the quantum Hamilton canonical equations

Let us consider the classical limit (
\hbar\rightarrow 0
) in the quantum
canonical equations of motion in Eq. (
184
). First, since the Wiener
process is simply linear in
\hbar
, it can be simply reduced to zero as

\hbar\rightarrow 0
. We then rewrite Eq. (
184
) in the polar coordinate.
It simply follows that

P_{X_{t}}=\hbar\rho\nabla\theta(X_{t},t)

(190)

and

\displaystyle\left[\vec{\nabla}V\right]_{X_{t}}

\displaystyle=\left.\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}&\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)\left(\vec{\nabla}V\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)\right|_{X_{t}}

\displaystyle=\rho(X_{t},t)\vec{\nabla}V(X_{t},t).

(194)

Since the Wiener process is nullified, a one-world path is now a smooth path
that does not have a chance of branching in its direction.
This implies that
X_{t}
is determined uniquely as soon
as
X_{0}
is prepared under an initial velocity field. Since there is no path
branching, we may expect that magnitude of
\rho(X_{0},0)
is to be maintained
constant throughout the propagation as

\rho(X_{t},t)=\rho(X_{0},0)\equiv\rho_{0}.

(195)

Then we have

P_{X_{t}}=\hbar\rho_{0}\nabla\theta(X_{t},t).

(196)

in Eq. (
190
). (Note that
\hbar\rightarrow 0
should not be taken in this
stage, because
\nabla\theta(X_{t},t)
gives a term proportional to

\hbar^{-1}
.) Further, we define

\hbar\nabla\theta(X_{t},t)=p(X_{t},t)

(197)

and

P_{X_{t}}=\hbar\rho_{0}\nabla\theta(X_{t},t)=\rho_{0}p(X_{t},t).

(198)

Insertion of Eq. (
198
) into the first equation of Eq. (
184
) gives

dX_{t}=\frac{1}{m}p(X_{t},t)dt.

(199)

On the other hand, the second equation in Eq. (
184
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle dP_{X_{t}}

\displaystyle=-\left[\vec{\nabla}V\right]_{X_{t}}dt=-\rho_{0}\vec{\nabla}V\left(X_{t}\right)dt

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}dp(X_{t},t)

(200)

and therefore

dp(X_{t},t)=-\vec{\nabla}V\left(X_{t}\right)dt.

(201)

It is obvious to see the coupled equations of (
199
) and (
201
) be
just the classical Hamilton canonical equations by identifying
p(X_{t},t)
of
Eq. (
197
) as the classical momentum. Because there is no stochastic
term in these expressions, we can take the simple limit
dX_{t}\rightarrow 0

and
dp(X_{t},t)\rightarrow 0
as
dt\rightarrow 0
and thereby have the
Hamilton canonical equations of motion

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{c}\frac{dX_{t}}{dt}=\frac{1}{m}p(X_{t},t)\text{ \ \ }\\
\\
\frac{dp(X_{t},t)}{dt}=-\vec{\nabla}V\left(X_{t}\right).\end{array}\right.

(202)

It is well known that the Schrödinger equation converges to (more
precisely, correspond to) the Hamilton-Jacobi equation through WKB
theory
[
5
,
4
]
(see also Sec.
V
) and also
through the Bohm representation as in Eq. (
188
). The equation of
motion for the Wigner phase-space distribution function is reduced to the
classical Liouville
equation.
[
44
,
45
,
57
]
These
classical limits are seen in the distribution functions. On the other hand,
since we are treating the quantum path dynamics, its classical limit should be
the Hamilton canonical equations of motion. This indicates that the classical
trajectories are a classical approximation to the one-world paths.

Incidentally, the canonical equations of Eq. (
202
) are readily
reduced to the Newtonian equation

m\frac{d^{2}X_{t}}{dt^{2}}=-\vec{\nabla}V\left(X_{t}\right).

(203)

On the other hand, the quantum canonical equations, Eq. (
184
), are
reduced to

\displaystyle d^{2}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m\rho}dP_{X_{t}}dt+\frac{1}{m\rho}P_{X_{t}}d^{2}t+\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m}}d^{2}W_{0}\left(t,\omega\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{m\rho}\left[\vec{\nabla}V\right]_{X_{t}}\left(dt\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{m\rho}P_{X_{t}}d^{2}t+\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m}}d^{2}W_{0}\left(t,\omega\right)

(204)

under an assumption that
\rho
is nearly constant at
(X_{t},t)
.

The study of the classical limit of the one-world path dynamics highlights the
intrinsic quantum effects.

1. The Wiener process is found to be indeed critical to quantum mechanics. Equation
(
195
) and those following it have shown that the one-world path
is immediately fallen down to a classical path, indicating that
“if a one-world path could be tracked deterministically due
to the absence of stochasticity, it should be subject to the law of classical
mechanics.” The contraposition of this statement is that
“if a one-world path does not satisfy classical mechanics, it
cannot be tracked in a deterministic manner due to the presence of
stochasticity.” This last statement is consistent with why
Nelson began his theory with the stochastic equations.
[
58
,
59
]

2. The quantum effects are expected to appear more significantly when the
condition of Eq. (
195
) is violated and
\rho(X_{t},t)
has a
broader spatial distribution around
X_{t}
, which indicates that the
stochastic path can wander about the area covered by
\psi(q,t)
. This aspect
is critical in semiclassical mechanics and will be discussed in Sec.

V
.

3. By the stochastic dynamics is activated the main quantum effect on the
one-world paths, which arises from the fact that the drift velocity is a
function of
\psi(q,t)
. Besides, the time derivative of the velocity term
itself depends on
\rho(X_{t},t)
in the term of
\left[\vec{\nabla}V\right]_{X_{t}}
as in Eq. (
184
) and (
200
). Meanwhile,
the one-world paths in turn contribute to the formation of
\rho(X_{t},t)
. Therefore, it should be stressed again that there is
a self-referring nonlinear relation between the parts (the one-world path) and
whole (
\rho(X_{t},t)
and
\psi(q,t)
). By quantum nonlocality we mean the
present nonlinear relation and the resultant correlation among one-world paths
on separated occasions, which was discussed in Sec.
III.3
.
Hence, the presupposition for the Bell inequality that has been figured out to
test the existence of the nonlocal hidden variables behind quantum dynamics is
unlikely to be applied to the present theoretical structure. Bell
assumed
[
60
]
that a correlation between two observed
quantities
a
and
b
can be represented in the following simple expression

C(a,b)=\int d\lambda A\left(a,\lambda\right)\rho_{B}(\lambda)B\left(b,\lambda\right),

(205)

where
\rho_{B}(\lambda)
is a probability distribution function for a
possible hidden parameter
\lambda
, satisfying
\int\rho_{B}(\lambda)d\lambda=1
. He proved that

S=C(a,b)-C(a,b^{\prime})+C(a^{\prime},b)+C(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})

(206)

should satisfy

\left|S\right|\leq 2.

(207)

It is quite unlikely that the simple structure of Eq. (
205
) can well
take account of the self-referring nonlinearity between the parts and the
whole, besides the effect of switching of the experimental circuit to
distinguish, for example,
(a,b)
and
(a,b^{\prime})
(the context of the
measurement). However, this aspect is beyond the scope of this article and
will be discussed elsewhere.

III.5
Summary of this section: Single-event quantum path

The Schrödinger function
\psi(q,t)
is supposed to represent a

coherent
sum of numerous physical events, each of which cannot be
singled out from it in general. The fringe (interference) pattern in the
double-slit experiment made up with an ensemble of the singly launched
electrons can be reproduced (by destruction of the quantum phase) in terms of

\left|\psi(q,t)\right|^{2}=\rho(q,t)
on the measurement board.
Meanwhile, we have formulated in this section the quantum stochastic path
(one-world path) for each of electron, which connects
\rho(X_{0},0)
to

\rho(X_{t},t)
. It seems that many of confusion in the interpretation of the
Schrödinger dynamics emerges from the lack of the discussion of the
quantum stochastic path (one-world path) and instead resorting to
\psi(q,t)

alone to explain everything.

The dynamics of the one-world paths is given as

dX_{t}=-\frac{\hbar}{m\rho}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}&\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)\mathbf{J}\nabla\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)dt+\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m}}dW_{0}\left(t,\omega\right),

(208)

which includes the two conflicting factors: The drift term Eq.
(
III.2.2
)

\bar{v}(q,t)\equiv-\frac{\hbar}{m\rho}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\phi_{r}&\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)\text{{J}}\nabla\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)

(209)

offers a particle velocity taken out of the smooth Schrödinger
distribution, and the Wiener process resulting in

\left\langle\left(\Delta q\right)^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{m}\Delta t,

(210)

refuses the classical definition of the velocity

v=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\Delta q}{\Delta t}.

(211)

Thus the paths are indifferentiable almost everywhere. The presence of

\psi(q,t)
in
\bar{v}(x,t)
and the Wiener process are both the origins of
the quantum nature in the one-word path dynamics: If the velocity drift term

\bar{v}(q,t)
is a local quantity, Eq. (
208
) is reduced merely to a
classical stochastic motion like the Brownian motion. If the Wiener process is
removed from Eq. (
208
), the path equation is eventually reduced to the
classical Hamilton canonical equations of motion due to the property of Eq.
(
195
). Thus each one-world path is regarded as a
“physical path”, and the assumption of
sudden collapse of the Schrödinger function (wave packet) has no place to
play a role.

A serious question remains on how the individual one-world paths can capture
the information of the Schrödinger vector
\bar{\psi}(q,t)
involved in
the velocity drift term of Eq. (
208
), in spite of the fact that the
total
\rho(q,t)
is supposed to be a sum of all the information from those
individual stochastic paths and
\bar{\psi}(q,t)
is a factorization of

\rho(q,t)
. How does nature resolve such a nonlinear relationship between the
parts and the whole? This is identified to be the deepest mystery in quantum
mechanics. However, if we
deem that nature creates
\bar{v}(q,t)
of Eq. (
209
) as a (classical)
field depending on the physical context, the question will be somewhat
relaxed. Besides, it would be exciting if the quantum Hamilton equations of
motion Eq. (
184
) inspires a new development of semiclassical ideas.
This aspect will be discussed elsewhere.

Before closing this section, we would like to touch upon the
characteristics of the dices God may play with. As for our ordinary dices, one
can roll a large set of dices at a time, or single dice many times. The law of
large numbers shows that the probability for a given role to appear should
converge to 1/6 in both ways. In quantum dynamics we can imagine an ensemble
of experimental events, but we cannot practically perform those experiments at
a time. (A double slit experiments in which to launch many electrons at a time
is different from the experiment to launch electrons one by one.)
Nevertheless, the Schrödinger equation can mathematically realize the
situation as a coherent superposition of the events. Meanwhile, we can run a
one-world path one after another separately. However, in contrast to the
ordinary dices, the one-world paths indirectly correlate with one another
through the velocity field made up with the Schrödinger function. Besides,
the density (or the density matrix) carried by all those one-world paths is expected to converge to the density
(density matrix) predicted by the relevant Schrödinger function. This is
the rule of the quantum dice play.

IV
Path dynamics on the manifolds of symplectic structure in parameter
spaces to time-evolve the Schrödinger functions

Here in this section and Sec.
V
, we study the space-time
propagation of the Schrödinger function from the view point of path
dynamics. The physical meaning of the paths to be considered below is totally
different from that of the one-world stochastic paths studied above.

IV.1
Introductory remarks: The Schrödinger partial differential
equation to coupled ordinary differential equations

As we saw in Sec.
II
, the real-valued Schrödinger
dynamics stands only on space-time translational symmetry, and the equation of
continuity (also referred to as flux conservation), along with a variational
principle of Dirac-Frenkel type.
[
61
]
No classical
mechanics was referred to in the derivation. Nevertheless we see a formal
proximity between the real-valued vector Schrödinger equation (
83
)
and the Hamilton canonical equations of motion such that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)=-\text{{J}}\frac{\hat{H}}{\hbar}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\phi_{r}\\
\phi_{c}\end{array}\right)

(212)

and

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}q\\
p\end{array}\right)=-\text{{J}}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\partial/\partial q\\
\partial/\partial p\end{array}\right)H_{cl}(q,p),

(213)

where
\hat{H}
and
H_{cl}(q,p)
are the quantum and classical Hamiltonian.
We also recall the quantum Hamilton equations of motion, Eqs. (
184
).
The prominent difference between Eq. (
212
) and Eq. (
213
) is that the
former is partial differential equation with its solutions extending in
space-time, while the latter is composed of coupled ordinary differential
equations (ODE) having ray solutions (paths, trajectories) that are supposed
to run on the Lagrangian manifolds of the symplectic property in phase-space.

J
in Eq. (
213
) highlights such a symplectic property. The
symplectic property is closely relevant to the conservation of total energy as
readily seen in Eq. (
213
) and moreover to the conservation of phase-space
volume elements as highlighted by the Poincaré-Cartan
theorem.
[
25
]
The Schrödinger equation of Eq. (
212
) is also
characterized by
J
, which suggests that symplectic property should be
relevant to quantum dynamics, too. In fact, de
Gosson
[
46
,
32
]
has extensively exhibited the
symplectic structure particularly of phase-space quantum mechanics. Such
phase-space quantum
mechanics,
[
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
,
45
,
57
]

the dynamics of the Wigner phase-space distribution
function
[
44
]
being the first and most well-known example,
has a beautiful reduction to semiclassical solutions, which are represented in
terms of classical trajectories. However, the exact equation of motion for
the Wigner phase-space functions turns out to be a high order partial
differential equation, which is ironically far more complicated than the
Schrödinger equation itself is.
[
44
,
45
]

In order to study another aspect of the symplectic property of the
Schrödinger dynamics, we track the space-time deformation of the
Schrödinger function
\psi(q,t)
by mapping it onto an (infinitely) many
dimensional parameter space in such a way that
\psi(q,t)\rightarrow

\psi(q,t:\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
or
\psi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))
. The
dynamics of those paths
(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))
is found to emerge
from the quantum mechanical Maupertuis-Hamilton variational
principle
[
66
]
with the functionals
\left\langle\psi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\psi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))\left|i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|\psi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))\right\rangle
. The
trajectories
(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))
are actually driven by coupled
ordinary differential equations for
(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))
and are
supposed to run on the manifolds of symplectic property. Thus the present path
dynamics reveals another mathematical and physical structure of quantum
Schrödinger dynamics.

IV.2
Variational principles in quantum mechanics

Before presenting the quantum mechanical Maupertuis-Hamilton variational
principle, we briefly mention to other variational methods developed for
quantum mechanics. The variational principles are inevitable for practical
studies in quantum mechanics: The Rayleigh–Ritz principle dominates the
eigenvalue problems for the stationary-sate Schrödinger equation,
particularly in molecular electronic structure
theory.
[
67
,
68
,
69
]
In the
stationary-state quantum scattering problems, the so-called Kohn-type
variational principles based on the Schrödinger
equation
[
70
,
71
]
and Schwinger principle
based on the Lippmann-Schwinger
equation
[
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
]

have been actively studied.

As a variational principle devised for the time-dependent Schrödinger
dynamics, we briefly outline the theory of Kan.
[
76
]
It starts from

\delta\int\left\langle\psi(\mathbf{\alpha})\left|\left(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\hat{H}\right)\right|\psi(\mathbf{\alpha})\right\rangle dt=0,

(214)

where
\mathbf{\alpha}
is a vector of
real-valued
parameters. The
Euler-Lagrange principle immediately gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{\alpha}}\mathcal{F}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{\alpha}}\mathcal{F}=0,

(215)

with

\mathcal{F}=\left\langle\psi(\mathbf{\alpha})\left|\left(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\hat{H}\right)\right|\psi(\mathbf{\alpha})\right\rangle.

(216)

After some manipulation, coupled ODEs for
\mathbf{\alpha}(t)
are given as

\frac{d\alpha_{k}}{dt}=\sum_{j}B_{kj}^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\left\langle\psi(\mathbf{\alpha})\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi(\mathbf{\alpha})\right\rangle,

(217)

with

\mathbf{B}_{jk}=i\hbar\left(\left\langle\left.\frac{\partial\psi(\mathbf{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\right|\frac{\partial\psi(\mathbf{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha_{k}}\right\rangle-\left\langle\left.\frac{\partial\psi(\mathbf{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha_{k}}\right|\frac{\partial\psi(\mathbf{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha_{j}}\right\rangle\right).

(218)

In this study Kan concludes
[
76
]
that there is no Legendre transformation
that allows
d\alpha_{k}/dt
to be treated as independent variables as momenta
in classical mechanics, and thereby the theory is regarded to be closed. This
is because the right hand side of Eq. (
217
) does not contain
momentum-like variables like
d\alpha_{k}/dt

[
76
]
. Matrix
\mathbf{B}

in Eq. (
218
) can be singular in practice. The Kan variational principle
emerges from a family of the similar ideas.

[
77
,
78
,
61
,
79
]

Being beautiful, the variational principles of the above kinds do not seem to
reveal the symplectic structure behind the Schrödinger dynamics. We
therefore consider below another class of the variational theory.

IV.3
Maupertuis-Hamilton principle in quantum parameter space

In what follows we consider trial Schrödinger functions in a form of

\psi\left(\mathbf{u,v}\right)
, in which a pair of real-valued vectors

\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
having pairwise components
\left(u_{i},v_{i}\right)
are supposed to serve as parameters such that

\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[c]{cccc}u_{1}&\cdots&u_{k}&\cdots\\
v_{1}&\cdots&v_{k}&\cdots\end{array}\right).

(219)

We here consider only time-independent Hamiltonian, yet it is straightforward
to extend to time-dependent problems.

As in the classical Maupertuis least action principle
[
25
]

\delta\int pdq=\delta\int p\dot{q}dt=0,

(220)

we consider the similar variational principle in the parameter space

\delta\int\mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{u}=\delta\int\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{\dot{u}}dt\mathbf{=}0

(221)

under the following constraints.

IV.3.1
Variational principle under energy conservation

A variational functional that conserves the energy can be readily made up in
analogy to the classical least action principle by simply writing
as
[
66
]

S_{H}=\int_{\mathit{C}}\left(\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}}-\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle\right)dt,

(222)

where
\mathit{C}
indicates an arbitrary time interval under study, and

\int_{\mathit{C}}\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}}dt=

\int_{\mathit{C}}\mathbf{v\cdot}d\mathbf{\dot{u}}
is the reduced action. The variation of
S_{H}
gives

\displaystyle\delta S_{H}=\left[\mathbf{v\cdot}\delta\mathbf{u}\right]_{end1}^{end2}

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathit{C}}\left(-\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}-\frac{\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\right)\mathbf{\cdot}\delta\mathbf{u}dt

\displaystyle\mathbf{+}\int_{\mathit{C}}\left(\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}-\frac{\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\right)\mathbf{\cdot}\delta\mathbf{v}dt=0

(223)

under the fixed boundary condition

\left[\mathbf{v\cdot}\delta\mathbf{u}\right]_{end1}^{end2}=0.

(224)

It immediately follows that

\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}=\frac{\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{v}}

(225)

and

\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}=-\frac{\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{u}}.

(226)

Since both
\delta\mathbf{u}
and
\delta\mathbf{v}
in Eq. (
223
) are
individually arbitrary,
\delta S_{H}=0
requires Eqs. (
225
) and
(
226
) to identically hold. Thus we have

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{u}\end{array}\right)=\text{{J}}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\partial/\partial\mathbf{v}\\
\partial/\partial\mathbf{u}\end{array}\right)\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle

(227)

which is mathematically of the same form as Eq. (
213
).

Along the flow lines
\left(\mathbf{u}(t)\mathbf{,v}(t)\right)
thus
determined, the energy conservation is readily ensured by Eq. (
227
).
An advantage of this simple method is that quantum effects such as the
zero-point energy along the path are naturally taken into account, and yet
their resultant paths can be similar to those of the classical counterpart.
Yet, it is obvious that the energy conservation alone is not enough to
determine the correct Schrödinger dynamics. In other words,
\delta S_{H}=0
is not sufficient.

IV.3.2
Variational principle under flux conservation

As in the energy conservation, we need to consider the flux conservation (the
equation of continuity) in terms of
\left\langle\psi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|\psi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))\right\rangle
as an independent condition.
The energy conservation and flux conservation are mutually independent as we
saw in Eqs. (
II.6
) and (
81
) and the discussion above them. We
therefore consider the least action principle under the condition of flux
conservation with a variational functional

\displaystyle S_{W}(\mathbf{u,v})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathit{C}}\left(\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}}-\left\langle\psi\left|i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|\psi\right\rangle\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathit{C}}\left(\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}}-i\hbar\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\phi}\right\rangle\right)dt

(228)

demanding

\delta S_{W}(\mathbf{u,v})=0,

(229)

which gives rise to another coupled ODE, that is

\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}=i\hbar\frac{\partial\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{v}}

(230)

and

\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}=-i\hbar\frac{\partial\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{u}},

(231)

or

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{u}\end{array}\right)=\text{{J}}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\partial/\partial\mathbf{v}\\
\partial/\partial\mathbf{u}\end{array}\right)\left\langle\psi\left|i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|\psi\right\rangle.

(232)

A flux conservation law follows directly as

\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle=\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}\cdot\frac{\partial\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{v}}+\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}\cdot\frac{\partial\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{u}}=0.

(233)

In Sec.
II
we have stressed the critical importance of the
flux conservation arising from the incompressible flow that
\left|\psi(q,t)\right|^{2}
induces in the
(q,t)
space. Likewise, we need to
impose the flux conservation in the
\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{\,}t\right)
space in addition to the energy conservation of Eq.
(
227
). It is not clear if the variational functional of Eq.
(
214
) conserves the norm in any
\mathbf{\alpha}
-parameter space unconditionally.

We analyze
S_{W}
in a little more precisely, resuming from

i\hbar\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle=\frac{i\hbar}{2}\left(\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle-\left\langle\left.\dot{\psi}\right|\psi\right\rangle\right),

(234)

which is real-valued. Rewrite the right hand side as

\displaystyle\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle-\left\langle\left.\dot{\psi}\right|\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}\cdot\int\left(\psi^{\ast}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\mathbf{u}}-\psi\frac{\partial\psi^{\ast}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\right)d\mathbf{q}

(235)

\displaystyle+\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}\cdot\int\left(\psi^{\ast}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\mathbf{v}}-\phi\frac{\partial\psi^{\ast}}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\right)d\mathbf{q,}

(236)

in which the fluxes in the parameter space have manifested themselves. For
instance,

\int\left(\psi^{\ast}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\mathbf{u}}-\psi\frac{\partial\psi^{\ast}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\right)d\mathbf{q}

(237)

corresponds to the gradient of the field of fluid in
\mathbf{u}
-direction.
Let us recall the definition of quantum mechanical flux (current of
probability density)
[
5
]
which is defined as

\mathbf{j}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)\mathbf{=}\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\psi^{\ast}\nabla\psi-\psi\nabla\psi^{\ast}\right),

(238)

with scaling the mass to
m=1
. By analogy, we may define fluxes (probability
current) in the parameter space in the
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}

directions, respectively, such that

\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{u}}\equiv\frac{i\hbar}{2}\int\left(\phi^{\ast}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\mathbf{u}}-\phi\frac{\partial\psi^{\ast}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\right)d\mathbf{q}=-\hbar\operatorname{Im}\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\right\rangle,

(239)

and

\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{v}}\equiv\frac{i\hbar}{2}\int\left(\psi^{\ast}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\mathbf{v}}-\psi\frac{\partial\psi^{\ast}}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\right)d\mathbf{q}=-\hbar\operatorname{Im}\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle.

(240)

After all we have

i\hbar\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle=\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{u}}+\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{v}},

(241)

which gives an alternative interpretation of
\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle
in terms of the flow in the
parameter space. Flux of such mathematical form exists in classical mechanics, and therefore Eq.
(
241
) represents a genuine quantum nature.

Back to Eqs. (
230
)-(
231
), they are rewritten as

\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\left(\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{u}}+\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{v}}\right)

(242)

and

\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\left(\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{u}}+\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{v}}\right).

(243)

Since
d\mathbf{u/}dt
and
d\mathbf{v/}dt
appear in both Eqs. (
242
)
and (
243
), they are essentially nonlinear and hard to integrate in these
forms. We return to this aspect later.

IV.3.3
A simple example bringing back to classical
dynamics

For a later purpose, we here show a simple example from the so-called coherent
state wavepacket

\psi\left(q,t\right)=N\exp\left(-\alpha\left(q-q_{0}(t)\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}(t)\left(q-q_{0}(t)\right)\right).

(244)

with
\alpha>0
being deliberately fixed. This is frequently used in practice
as an expansion basis (see for instance ref.
[
80
]
).
Set
q_{0}(t)\rightarrow\mathbf{u}(t)
and
p_{0}(t)\rightarrow\mathbf{v}
. Noting

\left\langle\psi\left(q,t\right)\left|\frac{d^{2}}{dq^{2}}\right|\psi\left(q,t\right)\right\rangle=-\alpha-\left(\frac{p_{0}(t)}{\hbar}\right)^{2}

(245)

we see

\frac{dq_{0}}{dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{0}}\left\langle\psi\left(q,t\right)\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\left(q,t\right)\right\rangle=\frac{p_{0}}{m}.

(246)

On the other hand, since
N^{2}\exp\left(-2\alpha\left(q-q_{0}(t)\right)^{2}\right)\rightarrow\delta(q-q_{0}(t))
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle\left\langle\psi\left(q,t\right)\left|V\right|\psi\left(q,t\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=N^{2}\int dqV(q)\exp\left(-2\alpha\left(q-q_{0}(t)\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow V(q_{0}(t))\text{ \ \ as \ }\alpha\rightarrow\infty

(247)

and

\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{0}}\left\langle\psi\left(q,t\right)\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\left(q,t\right)\right\rangle\rightarrow\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{0}}V(q_{0}(t))\text{ \ \ as \ }\alpha\rightarrow\infty.

(248)

Thus the combination of Eqs. (
246
) and (
248
) brings us back to the
canonical equations Eq. (
213
) in this limit. The above feature is well
known in the studies of practical application of the coherent
states.
[
81
,
80
]

Hence, it turns out that this simple example bring us back to the classical
Maupertuis-Hamilton principle

\delta\int\left(p_{0}d\dot{q}-H_{cl}(q_{0},p_{0})\right)dt=0,

(249)

which is the simplest example of
\delta S_{H}=0
only. In more general cases,
we need to consider
\delta S_{W}=0
.

Next we see the flux in the parameter space according to Eqs. (
239
) and
(
240
). The results are simple

\mathbf{j}_{q_{0}}=-\hbar\operatorname{Im}\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial q_{0}}\right\rangle=p_{0},

(250)

and

\mathbf{j}_{p_{0}}\equiv-\hbar\operatorname{Im}\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial p_{0}}\right\rangle=0.

(251)

There is no flux in the
p_{0}
space. On the other hand,
\mathbf{j}_{q_{0}}

in Eq. (
250
) seems natural and is consistent with the quantum momentum average

\left\langle p\right\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{i}\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial q}\right\rangle,

(252)

This trivial-looking example will show later why the flux-conservation
condition
\delta S_{W}(\mathbf{u,v})=0
is not necessary in this particular case.

IV.4
Intersection of two independent manifolds of symplectic structure

We now have two variational conditions
\delta S_{H}\left(\mathbf{u}\left(t\right)\mathbf{,v}\left(t\right)\right)=0
and
\delta S_{W}\left(\mathbf{u}\left(t\right)\mathbf{,v}\left(t\right)\right)=0
, which
are mathematically independent from each other. For them to physically
represent the Schrödinger dynamics, we need to combine them.

IV.4.1
Dual least action principle with the symplectic structure in
the parameter space

It is obvious that two conditions both should be fulfilled simultaneously such
that

\delta S_{W}(\mathbf{u,v})=\delta S_{W}(\mathbf{u,v})=0,

(253)

which naturally brings us back

\delta\int_{\mathit{C}}\left\langle\psi(\mathbf{u,v})\left|\left(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\hat{H}\right)\right|\psi(\mathbf{u,v})\right\rangle dt=0.

(254)

This one is obviously weaker a condition than that of Eq. (
253
). To
summarize, we need to study the simultaneous variational principles

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{c}\delta S_{H}\left(\mathbf{u}\left(t\right)\mathbf{,v}\left(t\right)\right)-\delta S_{W}\left(\mathbf{u}\left(t\right)\mathbf{,v}\left(t\right)\right)=0\\
\delta S_{H}\left(\mathbf{u}\left(t\right)\mathbf{,v}\left(t\right)\right)=0\\
\delta S_{W}\left(\mathbf{u}\left(t\right)\mathbf{,v}\left(t\right)\right)=0,\end{array}\right.\text{ \ \ \ }

(255)

which is a dual least action principle.

An obvious reason why the functional
S_{W}
appears to be necessary in
addition to
S_{H}
is because
S_{H}
of Eq. (
222
) alone does not
warrant the Schrödinger dynamics, because they are physically independent
as already stressed in Sec.
II
. As another obvious example
assume a hypothetical (or Klein-Gordon) dynamics

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\psi=\hat{H}\psi.

(256)

Then it should be required that

S_{W}(\mathbf{u,v})=\int_{\mathit{C}}\left(\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}}-\left\langle\psi\left|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\right|\psi\right\rangle\right)dt

(257)

even if the same Hamiltonian operator is adopted. Hence the condition
\delta S_{W}=0
determines the way of propagation of the flow arising from
\psi
,
while
\delta S_{H}=0
is
mainly
responsible for energetics.

IV.4.2
Working equations coupling the energy and flux conservations

Equations (

)-(

implant

and

)-(

and

\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\left(\frac{\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{u}}-\frac{\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{v}}\right).

(259)

)-(

Incidentally, the factor in Eqs. (

rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{u}}-\frac{\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle/\partial\mathbf{u}&\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle/\partial\mathbf{v}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\mathbf{0}&-\mathbf{I}\\
\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{u}}\\
\mathbf{j}_{\mathbf{v}}\end{array}\right),

which is a symplectic inner product between the Hamiltonian derivative vector

\psi

have prepared all the necessary quantities to propagate the Schrödinger

IV.4.3
Connecting piecewise trajectories

An exact solution of the Schrödinger equation having a proper set of
infinite number of parameters
\psi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))
should
naturally satisfy the two dynamical equations, Eqs. (
225
)-(
226
)
from the Hamilton dynamics and Eqs. (
242
)-(
243
) (or Eqs.
(
258
)-(
259
)) from the flow dynamics, which is obvious by construction.

As for our non-exact trial functions, we need to consider a technical aspect
in a little more systematic manner. Suppose we carry out a short time (
\Delta t
) propagation in Eqs. (
225
)-(
226
), which is formally written
as

\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(t+\Delta t)\\
\mathbf{v}(t+\Delta t)\end{array}\right)_{H}=\mathbf{F}\left(t;\Delta t\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(t)\\
\mathbf{v}(t)\end{array}\right)_{H}

(265)

and likewise from Eqs. (
258
)-(
259
) we have

\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(t+\Delta t)\\
\mathbf{v}(t+\Delta t)\end{array}\right)_{W}=\mathbf{G}\left(t;\Delta t\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(t)\\
\mathbf{v}(t)\end{array}\right)_{W}.

(266)

The suffices
H
and
W
indicate the Hamilton dynamics and the flow dynamics,
respectively. Finite time solution of each is to be formally given as

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(t)\\
\mathbf{v}(t)\end{array}\right)_{H}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}\left(t-\Delta t;\Delta t\right)\cdots\mathbf{F}\left(\Delta t;\Delta t\right)\mathbf{F}\left(0;\Delta t\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(0)\\
\mathbf{v}(0)\end{array}\right)

and

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(t)\\
\mathbf{v}(t)\end{array}\right)_{W}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}\left(t-\Delta t;\Delta t\right)\cdots\mathbf{G}\left(\Delta t;\Delta t\right)\mathbf{G}\left(0;\Delta t\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(0)\\
\mathbf{v}(0)\end{array}\right).

If
\psi(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t))
was exact globally in the parameter
space, it should hold that
\mathbf{F}\left(t;\Delta t\right)=\mathbf{G}\left(t;\Delta t\right)
under the same initial conditions and

\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(t)\\
\mathbf{v}(t)\end{array}\right)_{H}=\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(t)\\
\mathbf{v}(t)\end{array}\right)_{W}.

(275)

However, in our practical approximations, these two solutions at a finite time

t
can deviate from each other, depending on the physical conditions given.
We therefore assume that the simultaneous variational principles
\delta S_{H}=0
and
\delta S_{W}=0
are satisfied only for a very short time
segments
\Delta t
. The finite time solutions are to be obtained by
connecting the short-time segment solutions of
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{G}

in such a way that

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(t)\\
\mathbf{v}(t)\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\mathbf{G}\left(t-\Delta t/2;\Delta t/2\right)\mathbf{F}\left(t-\Delta t;\Delta t/2\right)\cdots

\displaystyle\times\mathbf{G}\left(\Delta t/2;\Delta t/2\right)\mathbf{F}\left(0;\Delta t/2\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\mathbf{u}(0)\\
\mathbf{v}(0)\end{array}\right).

Incidentally, we note the higher-order symplectic integrators can be applied
in practical calculations.
[
82
]

IV.4.4
A condition for the path dynamics to be well-posed

In order for
d\mathbf{u}/dt
and
d\mathbf{v}/dt
of Eqs. (
225
)-(
226
) and Eqs. (
230
)-(
231
) (in place of Eqs. (
258
)-(
259
) for shorter notation) to be mathematically consistent and for
the time-propagation by means of these sets of ordinary differential equations
to be well-posed, the following condition needs to be fulfilled

\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle/\partial\mathbf{u}&\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle/\partial\mathbf{v}\\
\partial\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle/\partial\mathbf{u}&\partial\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle/\partial\mathbf{v}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}d\mathbf{u}/dt\\
d\mathbf{v}/dt\end{array}\right)=0

(280)

(see Eq. (
233
) and its counterpart for energy conservation). This is a
Poisson bracket of
\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle
with respect to the variables
\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right)
. And further, for Eq. (
280
) to give nontrivial
solutions for the vector
\left(d\mathbf{u}/dt,d\mathbf{v}/dt\right)
, the
relevant determinant should vanish, that is,

\left|\begin{array}[c]{cc}\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle/\partial\mathbf{u}&\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle/\partial\mathbf{v}\\
\partial\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle/\partial\mathbf{u}&\partial\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle/\partial\mathbf{v}\end{array}\right|=0.

(281)

Likewise, the choice of initial conditions
(\mathbf{u}(0),\mathbf{v}(0))

should practically satisfy

\det\left|\begin{array}[c]{cc}\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle/\partial u_{i}&\partial\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle/\partial v_{i}\\
\partial\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle/\partial u_{i}&\partial\left\langle\left.\psi\right|\dot{\psi}\right\rangle/\partial v_{i}\end{array}\right|=0

(282)

on each pair of the initial components
(u_{i}(0),v_{i}(0))
prior to the
integration. Otherwise, the parameter setting is to be judged ill-conditioned.
Choice of the linear dependent and/or inconsistent parameters can be avoided
by monitoring Eq. (
281
).

IV.5
Conditions for the energy conservation alone to give correct

Back to the simplest case of the coherent state dynamics, Sec.

Schrödinger dynamics (approximately). There are mathematical conditions to

for the flow dynamics is fulfilled automatically.
Some are discussed below. Otherwise, both

The flow-dynamics variational functional

\displaystyle S_{W}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}-}\left\langle\psi(\mathbf{u,v})\left|i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|\psi(\mathbf{u,v})\right\rangle\right)dt

(283)

when

(284)

(285)

of Eq. (

(

[

In the case of the coherent state in Sec.

already had Eq. (

(

\displaystyle\psi\left(q,t\right)

IV.6
Integral invariance for the quantum reduced action in parameter
space

We note that
\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}}
has been introduced to the variational
functionals in order to find the stationary paths and invariances in the
parameter spaces in analogy to classical mechanics but not to induce the
Legendre transformation. We nevertheless identify
\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}}
as
a quantity related to an absolute invariance in the parameter space

[
25
]
as follows. Under an assumption that both of the two sets of
Eqs. (
225
)-(
226
) and Eqs. (
230
)-(
231
) are
simultaneously satisfied, it is straightforward to see that the divergence of
the vector field
\left(d\mathbf{u}/dt,d\mathbf{v}/dt\right)
be zero

[
25
]
, that is,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\cdot\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{v}}\cdot\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}=0,

(289)

which claims that
\left(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t)\right)
forms an
incompressible flow in parameter space. This is similar to incompressible
phase flow in classical dynamics, in which the integral invariances are
captured.
[
25
]
Similarly, we may take a continuous area
\Omega

encircled by its boundary
\partial\Omega
, which is to be carried by the flow
lines of
\left(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t)\right)
. Then we have an
integral invariance, along with the Stokes theorem

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}}dt

\displaystyle=\int\int_{\Omega}d\mathbf{v\wedge}d\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle\mathbf{=}\mathbf{-}\int\int_{\Omega}d\mathbf{u\wedge}d\mathbf{v=-}\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf{u\cdot\dot{v}}dt,

(290)

This quantity is conserved for an area
\Omega
to be carried along
\left(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{v}(t)\right)
by the unique and incompressible
flow.
[
25
]
This is one of the geometrical characteristics of
Schrödinger dynamics.

IV.7
Summary of this section: Variational principle in parameter
space

We have studied the time-dependent variational principle behind the
Schrödinger dynamics by splitting

\delta\int\left\langle\psi\left|\left(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\hat{H}\right)\right|\psi\right\rangle dt=0

(291)

into the set of two coupled Maupertuis-Hamilton dynamics

\delta S_{H}=\delta\int\left(\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}}-\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle\right)dt=0

(292)

and

\delta S_{W}=\delta\int\left(\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}}-\left\langle\psi\left|i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|\psi\right\rangle\right)dt=0.

(293)

These two variational principles represent different physics in themselves. It
seems miracle to the present author that Schrödinger abruptly combined
them into a single partial differential equation. Equation (
292
)
corresponds to the classical Hamilton least action principle, while the latter
is responsible for the flow dynamics. The conservation laws emerge not only
for energy but for the so-called “probability” current. By eliminating the term

\delta\int\mathbf{v\cdot\dot{u}}dt

(294)

from Eqs. (
292
)-(
293
) we attain Eq. (
291
) after all.

We have to go back to Sec.
II
to see the physical basis on
how the energy functional
\left\langle\psi\left|\hat{H}\right|\psi\right\rangle
and probability flow functional
\left\langle\psi\left|i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|\psi\right\rangle
should have those functional forms. Recall that we formulated there the
minimal Schrödinger equation from scratch by imposing the flux
conservation and the energy conservation in addition to the space-time
translational symmetry.

V
Roles of the classical paths beyond the semiclassical
mechanics

We further proceed to analyze how the Schrödinger function is to be
practically propagated in the present configuration space.

V.1
Introductory remarks: Deep into the Schrödinger dynamics

It is well known that quantum mechanics converges (not absolute convergence
though) to classical mechanics as
\hbar\rightarrow 0
. On the other side of
large
\hbar
, the Schrödinger functions bear the
Huygens-like
[
83
]
(wave-like) properties like diffraction,
bifurcation, and so on. To comprehend this strange property of the
Schrödinger dynamics, it is natural to compare the Schrödinger
equation with the Hamilton-Jacobi equation, which has a wave-like solution
over an ensemble of classical trajectories. Another standard method of
asymptotic analysis is due to Brillouin, Wentzel and Kramers (WKB) and
Jeffreys in which
W
in

\psi=A\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}W\right)

(295)

is expanded in powers of
\hbar
.
[
5
,
4
]
The hierarchical
transport equations by Maslov provides with another fundamental
method.
[
84
]
Also, a variety of theories of similar semiclassical
mechanics have been
proposed.
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]

In these expansions, while the lowest order term exhibits classical mechanics
immediately, it is not straightforward to climb the power of
\hbar
to the
high orders. It is not therefore easy to track how the classical nature in the
Schrödinger function is gradually modulated and ends up with the full
quantum nature after all. The genuine quantum nature must be identified at
some stage on the way from classical to quantum feature.

In the operator algebra of quantum mechanics, the transition from classical to
quantum mechanics is clearly proclaimed by replacement of scalar quantities to
operators such as
E\rightarrow\hat{H}=i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t},

p\rightarrow\hat{p}=-i\hbar\vec{\nabla}
, along with changing the Poisson
bracket to the commutator
[
1
]
and so on. No ambiguity
exists in this type of quantization. However, we do want to understand what is
the continuous and/or discontinuous relationship between classical and quantum dynamics.

We therefore analyze the space-time propagation of the Schrödinger
function from the viewpoint of path dynamics in the following three steps.
First, by factorizing the Hamilton-Jacobi component out of a Schrödinger
equation, we proceed to describe semiclassical mechanics in our
way.
[
85
]
There are two stages in the semiclassical mechanics, in
both stages of which the semiclassical Schrödinger function is propagated
along classical paths.
[
86
]
And beyond the semiclassical stage, we
see the successive branching of classical paths, thereby bringing about the
Huygens-like (wave-like) property into the
dynamics.
[
87
]
As the quantum nature becomes deeper,
those branching paths are seen to dissolve into the sea of wave-like nature of
the Schrödinger function.

V.2
Separation of the primitive classical dynamics from the
Schrödinger function

Let us begin with the Malov-type representation of a Schrödinger
function.
[
84
]
For a short time interval
\left[t,t+\Delta t\right]
, the total Schrödinger function can be written as

\psi\left(q,t\right)=F(q,t)\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}S_{cl}\left(q,t\right)\right),

(296)

on a multidimensional coordinate
q
in configuration space, with
S_{cl}

locally satisfying the classical Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) equation

\frac{\partial S_{cl}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2m}\left(\nabla S_{cl}\right)^{2}+V=0,

(297)

which defines relevant classical trajectories during the interval, with
m

being the mass.
[
88
,
89
,
90
,
85
,
86
]
We assume
that the complex amplitude function
F(q,t)
is compact enough in comparison
with the range of a potential function
V(q)
under study. Thus the primitive
classical component has been factored out from
\psi\left(q,t\right)
,
leaving the quantum component
F(q,t)
behind. We refer to
F(q,t)
as Action
Decomposed Function (ADF),
[
88
]
which is found to satisfy a liner
dynamical equation

\frac{\partial F(q,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{m}\left(-p\cdot\nabla-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\cdot p)\right)F(q,t)+\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla^{2}F(q,t),

(298)

where
p
is a classical momentum at
\left(q,t\right)
as
p=\nabla S_{cl}\left(q,t\right)
. It is natural to expect that the simple form of
Eq. (
296
) becomes less valid in a long-time and wide-space dynamics,
where
\psi\left(q,t\right)
is to experience a large variation of the
qualitative nature of the potential function.

As seen in Eq. (
298
), the classical momentum
p
is left behind in the
dynamics of
F(q,t)
. The reason for this comes from the property of the
Laplacian in the Hamiltonian such that

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\left[F(q,t)\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}S_{cl}\left(q,t\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=[F\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\left(\nabla S_{cl}\left(q,t\right)\right)^{2}

(299)

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{\hbar}F\nabla\cdot\nabla S_{cl}\left(q,t\right)+2\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\nabla S_{cl}\left(q,t\right)\right)\cdot\nabla F

(300)

\displaystyle+\nabla^{2}F(q,t)]\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}S_{cl}\left(q,t\right)\right).

(301)

The term of Eq. (
299
) is absorbed into Eq. (
297
). The last term Eq.
(
301
) is responsible for the pure quantum diffusion, and the term of Eq.
(
300
) couples the classical and pure quantum terms as Eq. (
298
)
and serves as a semiclassical interaction.

Notice the difference of the Maslov-type representation Eq. (
296
) from
the Bohm one
[
3
,
41
,
42
]

\psi\left(q,t\right)=R(q,t)\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}S_{B}\left(q,t\right)\right),

(302)

where both
R(q,t)
and
S_{B}\left(q,t\right)
are required to be
real-valued functions, and the so-called quantum potential
-\frac{\hbar}{2m}\frac{\nabla^{2}R}{R}
enters the dynamics for
S_{B}
. In contrast, Eq.
(
298
) for
F(q,t)
is linear, but as a price it is complex in general.
We use the mass weighted coordinates throughout, in which all the masses (
m
)
is scaled to unity
m=1
, and thereby
p
turns numerically equivalent to the
corresponding velocity
v
. Therefore, Eq. (
298
) is rewritten in a
simpler form as

\frac{D}{Dt}F(q,t)=\left[-\frac{1}{2m}(\nabla\cdot p)+\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla^{2}\right]F(q,t),

(303)

where the Lagrange picture in fluid dynamics is adopted by defining the
derivative as
\frac{D}{Dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v\cdot\nabla
(since

m=1
). Thus,
F(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right),t)
is to be carried along a
classical trajectory
q_{cl}\left(t\right)
serving as a fluid line. Then
the Trotter decomposition
[

]
valid for a very short time–interval

\Delta t
gives

\displaystyle F(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right),t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle\simeq\exp\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\Delta t\nabla^{2}\right]\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2m}(\nabla\cdot p)\Delta t\right]F(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right),t)

\displaystyle\simeq\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2m}(\nabla\cdot p)\Delta t\right]\exp\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\Delta t\nabla^{2}\right]F(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right),t)

(304)

and represents the quantum effects in a product between the momentum gradient
(
\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2m}(\nabla\cdot p)\Delta t\right]
) and the quantum
diffusion (
\exp\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\Delta t\nabla^{2}\right]
).

V.3
A semiclassical view of the Schrödinger function as quantum

To proceed, we first integrate the momentum gradient propagator

[

\displaystyle\left.F_{sc}(q-q\left(t+\Delta t\right),t+\Delta t)\right|_{q=q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right)}

\displaystyle=\left.\left(\frac{\sigma\left(t\right)}{\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)}\right)^{1/2}F_{sc}(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right),t)\right|_{q=q_{cl}\left(t\right)},

the reference path
q_{cl}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle\left.\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)^{1/2}F_{sc}(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right),t+\Delta t)\right|_{q=q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right)}

\displaystyle=\left.\sigma\left(t\right)^{1/2}F_{sc}(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right),t)\right|_{q=q_{cl}\left(t\right)},

(307)

The Planck constant does not yet appear in this level.

Equation (

to

\displaystyle\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)\left|F_{sc}(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right),t+\Delta t)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(t\right)\left|F_{sc}(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right),t)\right|^{2},

(308)

which is valid up to the normalization, we have

\displaystyle\int dq\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)\left|F_{sc}(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right),t+\Delta t)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int dq\sigma\left(t\right)\left|F_{sc}(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right),t)\right|^{2},

(309)

which is further rewritten as

\displaystyle\int dq_{t+\Delta t}\left|F_{sc}(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right),t+\Delta t)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int dq_{t}\left|F_{sc}(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right),t)\right|^{2},

(310)

where
dq_{t}
and
dq_{t+\Delta t}
are the time-and-space dependent volume
elements for the integration. Comparison of Eqs. (
309
) and (
310
)
leads to

dq=\frac{dq_{t}}{\sigma\left(t\right)}=\frac{dq_{t+\Delta t}}{\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)}

(311)

To clarify further the physical meaning of
F_{sc}
, rewriting Eq.
(
307

element
[
90
]
for semiclassical integrals (or the half-density)

dq_{t}=dq_{t}^{1/2}dq_{t}^{1/2\ast}=\left|dq_{t}\right|,

(312)

we rewrite Eq. (

as
[
90

F_{sc}\left(q{{}_{t+\Delta t},t}+\Delta t\right)dq_{t+\Delta t}^{1/2}=F_{sc}\left(q{{}_{t},t}\right)dq_{t}^{1/2},

(313)

where the square root of
dq_{t}
is defined by

dq_{t}^{1/2}\equiv\exp\left[{{\frac{{i\pi}M\left(q_{0},q_{t}\right)}{2}}}\right]\left|dq_{t}\right|^{1/2},

(314)

with the Maslov index
M\left(q_{0},q_{t}\right)
being the Maslov index
counting the number of the change of sign of
\sigma\left(t\right)
of Eq.
(
306

[

,

The physical meaning of the above conservation law, or the rescaling rule of
the length in
q
-space is described as follows: Suppose a time propagation of
a phase-space volume element (
\Delta q_{t}\Delta p_{t}
) in classical
dynamics, which is conserved by the Liouville theorem, that is,
\Delta q_{t+\Delta t}\Delta p_{t+\Delta t}=\Delta q_{t}\Delta p_{t}
by setting a
phase space distribution function
\Gamma(q,p,t)=1
within the area
\Delta q_{t}\Delta p_{t}
and
\Gamma(q,p,t)=0
otherwise. Reading
\Delta q_{t}=dq_{t}
as a tiny volume element in configuration space (for
t+\Delta t
as well), and comparing this conservation law with another conservation law
in semiclassics, Eq. (
313

F_{sc}\left(q{{}_{t},t}\right)^{2}

in
F_{sc}\left(q{{}_{t},t}\right)^{2}dq_{t}
serve as a classical
distribution density function (without respect to the Maslov phase).
Therefore,
F_{sc}\left(q{{}_{t},t}\right)dq_{t}^{1/2}
can be regarded as a
distribution covering the tiny variable space
dq_{t}^{1/2}
, and
F_{sc}
and

\psi
as well can be regarded as “a quantum (coherent)
distribution function” per
dq_{t}^{1/2}
.

V.4
Further quantum dynamics manifesting in a complex Gaussian
Schrödinger function

Beyond the above semiclassics, we next proceed to the deeper quantum level,
which is the quantum diffusion
\exp\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\Delta t\nabla^{2}\right]
in Eq. (
304
). An integral representation of this
operator is
[
35
]

\displaystyle\left\langle q\left|\hat{K}_{dif}\right|f\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar\Delta t}\right)^{1/2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\exp\left[\frac{im(q-y)^{2}}{2\Delta t}\right]f\left(y\right).

(315)

Choosing
f\left(y\right)
to be a complex Gaussian function, Eq.
(
315
) proceeds analytically. The Gaussian function thus chosen should
reflect the stochastic motion of a one-world path considered in Sec.

III
. Recall that the kernel in Eq. (
315
) is
mathematically equivalent to that of the Wiener process if we regard

im/2\hbar
as a diffusion constant
-1/4D
, and the normalization constant

\left(m/2\pi i\hbar\Delta t\right)^{1/2}
as
\left(1/4\pi D\Delta t\right)^{1/2}
. Therefore
\hat{K}_{dif}
in Eq. (
315
) reflects
the hidden Brownian motion of the diffusion constant
D=-i2m/\hbar
.

V.4.1
Semiclassics for the Gaussian

We resume the dynamics for the action decomposed function
F(q,t)
by assuming
a Gaussian function of the compact form

G(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right),t)=g\left(t\right)\exp\left[-\gamma\left(t\right)(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right))^{2}\right],

(316)

where
g\left(t\right)
is a normalization constant.
g\left(t\right)

is important, since the semiclassical mechanics considered above is about the
rescaling of the configuration space.

A semiclassics for the Gaussian function was extensively studied by Heller,
who assumed that the potential function covered by the Gaussian is locally
approximated by a quadratic form.
[
91
,
92
]
Here we are taking a
different approach. The momentum gradient part can be formally treated for the
Gaussian function too as

\displaystyle G^{mg}\left(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right),t+\Delta t\right))

\displaystyle=g^{mg}\left(t+\Delta t\right)\exp(-\left[\frac{\sigma\left(t\right)}{\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)}(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right))\right]^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\gamma^{mg}\left(t\right)\right)\left[\frac{\sigma\left(t\right)}{\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)}(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right))\right]),

(317)

where
g^{mg}\left(t+\Delta t\right)
renormalizes the function. The
result is simpler written as

\displaystyle G^{mg}\left(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right),t+\Delta t\right))=g^{mg}\left(t\right)\left(\frac{\sigma\left(t\right)}{\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\left(\gamma^{mg}\left(t+\Delta t\right)\right)(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right))^{2}\right],

(318)

with

\gamma^{mg}\left(t+\Delta t\right)=\gamma\left(t\right)\left(\frac{\sigma\left(t\right)}{\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)}\right)^{2}

(319)

and

g^{mg}\left(t+\Delta t\right)=g\left(t\right)\left(\frac{\sigma\left(t\right)}{\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)}\right)^{1/2},

(320)

where suffix
mg
on
\gamma
and
g
indicates that the effects of the
momentum gradient
only
have been taken into account in this stage.

V.4.2
Modulation of the complex Gaussian exponent by the quantum
diffusion

Dynamics of the exponents

Further a direct application of (
315
) on Eq. (
318
) gives

\displaystyle G(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right),t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\int dyK_{dif}\left(q,y,\Delta t\right)G^{mg}(y-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right),t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=g\left(t\right)\left(\frac{\sigma\left(t\right)}{\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)}\frac{1/\gamma^{mg}}{1/\gamma^{mg}+i/b}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\frac{1}{1/\gamma^{mg}+i/b}\left(q-q_{cl}\left(t+\Delta t\right)\right)^{2}\right]

(321)

with
b=m/2\hbar\Delta t.

To make the expressions better visualized, we write
G
with the following form

G(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right),t)=g\left(t\right)\exp\left[-\frac{1}{c\left(t\right)+id\left(t\right)}(q-q_{cl}\left(t\right))^{2}\right],

(322)

with

g\left(t\right)=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}\left[c\left(t\right)\right]^{1/4}\left[c\left(t\right)+id\left(t\right)\right]^{-1/2},

(323)

making the correspondence with Eq. (
316
) with
\gamma\left(t\right)^{-1}=c\left(t\right)+id\left(t\right)
, where both
c\left(t\right)
and
d\left(t\right)
are real-valued. It is found that the
parameters
c\left(t\right)
and
d\left(t\right)
satisfy the
following dynamical equations, respectively,
[
86
]

\displaystyle c\left(t+\Delta t\right)

\displaystyle=c\left(t\right)\left(\frac{\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)}{\sigma\left(t\right)}\right)^{2}\text{ \ or \ }

\displaystyle\dot{c}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=2\frac{\dot{\sigma}\left(t\right)}{\sigma\left(t\right)}c\left(t\right)

(324)

and

\displaystyle d\left(t+\Delta t\right)

\displaystyle=d\left(t\right)\left(\frac{\sigma\left(t+\Delta t\right)}{\sigma\left(t\right)}\right)^{2}+\frac{2\hbar\Delta t}{m}\text{ \ or \ }

\displaystyle\dot{d}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=2\frac{\dot{\sigma}\left(t\right)}{\sigma\left(t\right)}d\left(t\right)+\frac{2\hbar}{m}.

(325)

Therefore,
c\left(t\right)
[the real part of
1/\gamma\left(t\right)
] is responsible only for the semiclassical flow, and
d\left(t\right)

[the imaginary part of
1/\gamma\left(t\right)
] is responsible for
quantum diffusion along with its coupling with the semiclassical flow.

The Planck constant finally appears in this stage but only in the dynamics of

d\left(t\right)
. The effects of the momentum gradient and the quantum
diffusion are mixed up when
c\left(t\right)
and
d\left(t\right)

appear together in
\gamma\left(t+\Delta t\right)
. We refer to the
present normalized-Gaussian representation of ADF as ADF-NVG in what follows.

V.4.3
Experimentally uncontrollable nature of the complex Gaussian

The physical implication behind the complex exponent
\left(c\left(t\right),d\left(t\right)\right)
is as follows. Defining a quantity

\zeta(t)
as

\zeta(t)=d(t)/\sigma\left(t\right),

(326)

we find the following Wronskian relation
[
86
]
taken out of Eq.
(
325
)

\sigma\left(t\right)\dot{\zeta}(t)-\dot{\sigma}\left(t\right)\zeta(t)=\left|\begin{array}[c]{cc}\sigma\left(t\right)&\dot{\sigma}\left(t\right)\\
\zeta(t)&\dot{\zeta}(t)\end{array}\right|=\frac{2\hbar}{m}.

(327)

Therefore a vector
(\sigma\left(t\right),\zeta(t))
is basically rotating
on
\sigma-\zeta
plane with a relevant “angular
momentum”, which is directed vertical to the plane and is
conserved. This Wronskian relation is valid for the “rotational motion” in the
\sigma-\zeta
space, which is
realized for vibration and libration. This is not the case for the rotational
motion of a pendulum and free particle motion, because
\sigma\left(t\right)
does not oscillate.

We thus have faced an experimentally uncontrollable parameter
d(t)
emerging
from the quantum diffusion, and the Planck constant begins to play its role in
this stage. Further, there is no way to choose the initial exponent
1/(c+id)

experimentally. Therefore, even if one can track the center of the single
Gaussian in terms of the classical paths, the Gaussian width is of nature far
from classical dynamics.

V.4.4
Limitation and breakdown of the Gaussian average

In an analogy to the classical stochastic dynamics, the simple Gaussian itself
suggests the presence of a stochastic motion around the center (average) path.
And, so far, we have assumed that the classical motion of the Gaussian center
can well average over the fast stochastic quantum dynamics. However, it is
obvious that the single Gaussian representation is a bad approximation in case
where the potential range is shorter than or equal to the width of the
Gaussian, particularly in those systems where a Schrödinger function is
expected to undergo bifurcation (branching). In such a case, we actually need
to explicitly track the individual stochastic paths, since those possibly fast
stochastic paths can trigger to deviate the average motion of the Gaussian. We
therefore proceed to this aspect.

V.5
Splitting of a single Gaussian and classical
paths

As the genuine quantum nature is deepened, the notion of classical dynamics is
getting lost gradually (continuously and not abruptly), and eventually we find
that classical paths have to split into many pieces and each dissolves in to a
distribution function deformed from the simple Gaussian. We below consider how
such a Gaussian deformation and branching can be represented and be tracked
through.

V.5.1
The Weierstrass transform

Consider a case in which a Gaussian wavepacket sits on a potential function,
the range of spatial variation of which is shorter than the width of the
Gaussian. Then the different parts within the extent of the Gaussian are
supposed to be driven by the different force fields. In such a case it is
necessary to re-express the Gaussian in a set of smaller (short-ranged)
Schrödinger functions. Among the many possible ways for the
re-representation mathematically, we here adopt a method which rigorously
allows to split a parent Gaussian to a continuous set of daughter
Gaussians.
[
87
]
(We note that the aim of this study
is not to seek for a convenient numerical approximation.)

We resume with a Gaussian wavepacket of the Maslov type or the coherent-state
type at a time
t
of the form

G(q-q_{0}\left(t\right),t)=\exp\left(-\gamma_{1}\left(q-q_{0}\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}\left(q-q_{0}\right)\right),

(328)

where
\left(q_{0},p_{0}\right)
is the center, and the exponent

\gamma_{1}
is complex as
\gamma_{1}\equiv\gamma_{1}^{r}+i\gamma_{1}^{c},

where
\gamma_{1}^{r}
(
>0
) and
\gamma_{1}^{c}
are the real and imaginary
parts, respectively. We thus have two phase terms in the exponent of Eq.
(
328
), which are

\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[p_{0}\left(q-q_{0}\right)-\hbar\gamma_{1}^{c}\left(q-q_{0}\right)^{2}\right]\right).

(329)

Aside from these phase parts, we apply the following integral transform only
for the real part
\exp\left(-\gamma_{1}^{r}\left(q-q_{0}\right)^{2}\right)
in such a way that

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\gamma_{1}^{r}\left(q-q_{0}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\int d\Omega\exp\left(-\gamma_{2}^{r}\left(q-q_{0}-\Omega\right)^{2}\right)\exp\left(-A\Omega^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{A+\gamma_{2}^{r}}}\exp\left[-\left(\gamma_{2}^{r}-\frac{\left(\gamma_{2}^{r}\right)^{2}}{A+\gamma_{2}^{r}}\right)\left(q-q_{0}\right)^{2}\right]

(330)

under the condition
A>0
. This is essentially the Weierstrass transform
(W-transform for short). The exponents in Eq. (
330
) can be chosen as

\gamma_{1}^{r}=\gamma_{2}^{r}-\frac{\left(\gamma_{2}^{r}\right)^{2}}{A+\gamma_{2}^{r}}

(331)

or in more compact form

\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}^{r}}\equiv\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{r}}-\frac{1}{A}.

(332)

Thus the single Gaussian at the left hand side of Eq. (
330
) is
transformed to an continuous set of other Gaussians of a common exponent

\gamma_{2}^{r}
, each being centered at
q=q_{0}+\Omega
. We refer to these
Gaussians containing
\Omega
as
\Omega
-Gaussian. Since
A>0
,
\gamma_{2}^{r}>\gamma_{1}^{r}
in Eq. (
332
), and the newly born Gaussians
are sharper and narrower. (
\gamma_{2}^{r}
may be relaxed to a smaller value
in the subsequent dynamics.) The magnitude of
\gamma_{2}^{r}
can be chosen
such that the potential function can fit well in the potential range.

Equation (
328
) is then rewritten as

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\gamma_{1}\left(q-q_{0}\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}\left(q-q_{0}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{A+\gamma_{2}^{r}}{\pi}\right)^{1/2}\int d\Omega\exp\left(-A\Omega^{2}\right)\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}\Omega-i\gamma_{1}^{c}\Omega^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(-\gamma_{2}\left(q-q_{\Omega}\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{\Omega}\left(q-q_{\Omega}\right)\right)

(333)

with the new centers of the split Gaussians

q_{\Omega}\equiv q_{0}+\Omega,

(334)

and

p_{\Omega}\equiv p_{0}-2\hbar\gamma_{1}^{c}\Omega,

(335)

with

\gamma_{2}\equiv\gamma_{2}^{r}+i\gamma_{1}^{c}.

(336)

(Notice
\gamma_{2}^{c}\equiv\gamma_{1}^{c}
). Thus the center of each
daughter Gaussian is located at (
q_{\Omega},p_{\Omega}
) ready for the next
propagation. All these split Gaussians are kept coherently connected to each
other through
\int d\Omega
in Eq. (
333
). The role of the imaginary
part of the exponent
\gamma_{1}^{c}
on
p_{\Omega}
(see Eq. (
335
)) is quite critical.

We here detour back to Eq. (
113
) for the local velocity, which is
rewritten for the local momentum as

p^{\text{local}}(q,t)=\hbar\operatorname{Im}\frac{\vec{\nabla}\psi}{\psi}.

(337)

For the mother Gaussian in Eq. (
333
)

\exp\left(-\gamma_{1}\left(q-q_{0}\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}\left(q-q_{0}\right)\right),

(338)

the local momentum
p^{\text{local}}(q,t)
is

-2h\left(\operatorname{Im}\gamma_{1}\right)\left(q-q_{0}\right)+p_{0}

(339)

and the daughter Gaussians shift their central position according to Eq.
(
334
), or
q_{0}=q_{\Omega}-\Omega,
which also shifts the local
momentum of Eq. (
339
) as

-2h\gamma_{1}^{c}\left(q-q_{\Omega}\right))-2h\gamma_{1}^{c}\Omega+p_{0}.

(340)

(
\operatorname{Im}\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{1}^{c}
). Thus each daughter Gaussian
has a shifted momentum
p_{\Omega}
of (
335
) at its central
position
q_{\Omega}
(
334
).

Incidentally, one may want to discretize the continuous transformation such
Eq. (
333
) with a quadrature such that

\displaystyle\int d\Omega\exp\left(-A\Omega^{2}\right)f\left(\Omega\right)

\displaystyle\simeq\sum_{k}w_{k}\exp\left(-A\Omega_{k}^{2}\right)f\left(\Omega_{k}\right),

(341)

where
\Omega_{k}
indicates the quadrature point with their weighting factor

w_{k}
.

V.5.2
Recursive processes of ADF-NVG dynamics and proliferation of
daughter packets

We next repeat the W-transform as a branched packet passes through another
deep quantum region. From an initial single squeezed Gaussian at time
t_{0}

\displaystyle G(q-q_{0}\left(t_{0}\right),t_{0})

\displaystyle=n_{\gamma_{0}}(t_{0})\exp\left[-\gamma_{0}(t_{0})(q-q_{0}(t_{0}))^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}(t_{0})\left(q-q_{0}(t_{0})\right)\right],

(342)

where
n_{\gamma}
is the normalization factor, let us propagate it from

t_{0}
to
t_{1}=t_{0}+\Delta t
(with
\Delta t
being sufficiently small,
if necessary. Yet, it can be practically elongated to a finite length

carried out from
t_{0}
to
t_{1}
without splitting such that

\displaystyle\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}(t_{1}-t_{0})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{\gamma_{0}}(t_{1})}{n_{\gamma_{0}}(t_{0})}\exp\left[-\gamma_{0}(t_{1})(q-q_{0}(t_{1}))^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}(t_{1})\left(q-q_{0}(t_{1})\right)\right]

(343)

Then we perform a W-transform at time

\displaystyle\exp[-\gamma_{0}(t_{1})\left(q-q_{0}(t_{1})\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}\left(t_{1}\right)\left(q-q_{0}\left(t_{1}\right)\right)]

\displaystyle\times\int d\Omega_{1}\exp\left(-A_{1}\Omega_{1}^{2}\right)\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}(t_{1})\Omega_{1}-i\gamma_{0}^{c}(t_{1})\Omega_{1}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\gamma_{1}(t_{1})\left(q-q_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1})\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1})\left(q-q_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1})\right)\right]

(344)

with an appropriate choice of the exponent
\gamma_{1}
. Following the above
rule,
A_{1}
is to be determined by

\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}^{r}(t_{1})}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{0}^{r}(t_{1})}-\frac{1}{A_{1}},

(345)

and

\gamma_{1}^{c}(t_{1})=\gamma_{0}^{c}(t_{1}),

(346)

with

q_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1})=q_{0}(t_{1})+\Omega_{1}

(347)

and

p_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1})=p_{0}(t_{1})-2\hbar\gamma_{1}^{c}(t_{1})\Omega_{1}.

(348)

We can further propagate the
\Omega
-Gaussians from
t_{1}
to
t_{2}=t_{1}+\Delta t
such that

\displaystyle\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}(t_{2}-t_{1})\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp[-\gamma_{0}(t_{1})\left(q-q_{0}(t_{1})\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}\left(t_{1}\right)\left(q-q_{0}\left(t_{1}\right)\right)]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{A_{1}+\gamma_{1}^{r}(t_{1})}{\pi}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\int d\Omega_{1}\exp\left(-A_{1}\Omega_{1}^{2}\right)\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}(t_{1})\Omega_{1}-i\gamma_{1}^{c}(t_{1})\Omega_{1}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}(t_{2}-t_{1})\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\gamma_{1}(t_{1})\left(q-q_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1})\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1})\left(q-q_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1})\right)\right].

(349)

Note that each of the
\Omega
-Gaussians can be propagated independently,
since ADF-NVG dynamics is linear. We thus can rewrite the right hand side of
Eq. (
349
) with use of Eq. (
343

\displaystyle\left(\frac{A_{1}+\gamma_{1}^{r}(t_{1})}{\pi}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\int d\Omega_{1}\exp\left(-A_{1}\Omega_{1}^{2}\right)\exp\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{0}\Omega_{1}-i\gamma_{0}^{c}\Omega_{1}^{2}\right)\frac{n_{\gamma_{1}\Omega_{1}}(t_{2})}{n_{\gamma_{1}\Omega_{1}}(t_{1})}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\gamma_{1}(t_{2})\left(q-q_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{i}{\hbar}p_{\Omega_{1}}\left(t_{2}\right)\left(q-q_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{2}\right))\right],

(350)

where
n_{\gamma_{1}\Omega_{1}}(t)
is the normalization constant for the
coherent state of

In the ADF-NVG dynamics, its Gaussian center
\left(q_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1}),p_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1})\right)
proceeds classically as

\left(q_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1}),p_{\Omega_{1}}(t_{1})\right)\text{
}\underrightarrow{\text{ \ classical \ }}\text{ }(q_{\Omega_{1}}\left(t_{2}\right),p_{\Omega_{1}}\left(t_{2}\right))

(351)

during this time interval. And of course the Gaussians at
(q_{\Omega_{1}}\left(t_{2}\right),p_{\Omega_{1}}\left(t_{2}\right))
are subject to
another W-transform, if necessary.

V.6
Path integrals over many coherently branching paths

Multiple applications of the W-transform can be performed to proliferate the

\Omega
-Gaussians of the next generations with different exponents. As an

to
t_{4}
via
t_{1}
,

t_{2}
, and
t_{3}
, staring from the initial squeezed Gaussian of Eq.
(

We in this article regard that the Schrödinger function should be a

V.8
Summary of this section: Path branching in deep quantum dynamics

We have studied the mechanism of time-propagation of the Schrödinger
function from the view point of a relationship between the quantum
distribution function and classical paths and their branching. First we have
separated the classical component that satisfies the Hamilton-Jacobi equation
out of a Schrödinger function and factored what we call the action
decomposed function (ADF). With ADF we revisited semiclassical mechanics, in
which the rescaling of the configuration space along with the emergence of
Maslov phase are highlighted. To proceed further in analytic manner, we chose
a complex exponent Gaussian function as an ADF. Although the Gaussian center
runs along a classical path, its complex exponents undergo a very
characteristic dynamics under the coupling of the configuration-space
rescaling and the quantum diffusion. Those hidden dynamics dominate the
dynamics of the Gaussian exponents.

The quantum diffusion has already brought a Gaussian deep into the quantum
realm. However, the single Gaussian representation of the Schrödinger
function is far from sufficient to reproduce the wave-like behavior of the
coherent distribution function, which are bifurcation, diffraction, and those
that arise from the Huygens principle in quantum
mechanics.
[
83
]
We hence introduce a mathematical mechanism
to transform a Gaussian into a continuous set of daughter Gaussians having
different exponents. With this transform a Gaussian can split to smaller
Gaussians, and each local path is branched to infinitely many pieces. A
Schrödinger function is thus time-propagated by a series of branching
paths, each carrying a piece of complex Gaussian. Thus, as the quantum nature
of the system becomes deeper, the paths dissolves into the quantum sea as
finer pieces. The manner of the entire propagation can be represented in the
form of path integrals over those branching pieces.

VI
Concluding remarks

We have studied the nature of the Schrödinger dynamics and Schrödinger
functions: First in Sec.
II
, we factorized the distribution
function of particles
\rho(q,t)
to a two component real-valued vector (the
Schrödinger vector), and imposing the space-time translational symmetry
and the equation of continuity (flux conservation) under a given Hamiltonian.
Then the variational condition applied to the equation of continuity chooses
the Schrödinger vector as a real-valued function that gives the most
robust (probable) distribution among those satisfying the equation continuity.
The real-valued Schrödinger vector is readily transformed to the ordinary
complex-valued counterpart. The physical meaning of the Schrödinger
function is therefore regarded as a distribution function by definition. It
was also found that the Schrödinger equation is internally consistent. The
present reconstruction of the Schrödinger equation covers only the minimal
arena of non-relativistic quantum theory. Yet, we believe we have come to the
basic understanding and interpretation of the Schrödinger dynamics.

It is well known that the Schrödinger function does not describe the
dynamics of the individual single incident (event) each, but it represents a
coherent superposition of an infinite (and inseparable) ensemble of possible
physical events. In the double-slit experiment electrons are launched
one-by-one and the accumulated spots made by each electron form a fringe
pattern on a measurement board. The fringe pattern is predicted by the
Schrödinger function (the absolute square thereof), while we have
formulated in Sec.
III
a quantum path named one-world
stochastic path that each individual electron is supposed to run on. Both the
Schrödinger function and one-world paths are extremely sensitive to the
external perturbation. The one-world quantum paths (indifferentiable almost
everywhere) are taken out of the Feynman-Kac formalism and the Schrödinger
equation. The stochastic motion seems to be essential and inherent in quantum
dynamics. In fact, it has been shown that the scaling law with an appropriate
diffusion constant naturally leads to the Bohr quantization condition for
hydrogen atom. We also have shown that the one-world paths loses the quantum
nature and the quantum canonical equations of motion for them are reduced to
the Hamilton (classical) canonical equations of motion as soon as the Wiener
process is removed. In order to drive the one-world quantum dynamics, each
path requires the information of the drift velocity, which is only to be
provided by the Schrödinger function in turn. Hence, the individual
one-world path runs in a stochastic manner and guided by the drift velocity
given by the Schrödinger function. The nonlinear relation between the
one-world dynamics (local information) and the Schrödinger dynamics
(coherent sum of the possible global events) has been identified as the very
deepest origin of the mystery inherent to quantum mechanics.

In the last half of this article from Sec.
IV
and Sec.

V
, we have analyzed the physical and mathematical processes of
time-propagation of the Schrödinger function from the view point of two
classes of path dynamics.

Classical trajectories run on a Lagrange manifold having the symplectic
structure, thereby conserving not only the energy by phase-space volume
elements (Poincaré-Cartan). Having an anticipation that quantum mechanics
should have the similar structure, we have developed the quantum mechanical
Maupertuis-Hamilton variational principle by mapping the Schrödinger
function on a parameter space. Yet, quantum mechanics demands another
variational functional emerging from the principle of flux conservation (or
the equation of continuity). The deterministic paths run on these manifolds of
symplectic structure as a solution of the resultant ordinary differential
equations. Thus the time-propagation of the Schrödinger distribution
function turns out to be compatible with the path-dynamics in the parameter
space. The similarity between classical and quantum dynamics on the manifold
of symplectic property comes from their common principle of the conservation
laws. In contrast, the most significant difference between the two is that
quantum mechanics requires the flow dynamics and the relevant variational
functional. Another difference is that even a single trajectory in the
parameter space allows the Schrödinger function to simultaneously pass
through two slits in the double-slit experiment.

As the last step, we have studied the mechanism of time-propagation of the
Schrödinger function through the intricate relationship between quantum
and classical dynamics. By investigating the necessary role and limitation of
the classical paths, we have captured how the space-time distribution of the
Schrödinger function is materialized. Semiclassical mechanics based on the
complex Gaussian function reveals hidden variables in the dynamics.
Furthermore, a Schrödinger function running on a non-smooth potential
function of short reach-range shows the wave-like properties based on the
Huygens-like principle, such as diffraction, bifurcation, tunneling, and so
on. For a Gaussian to materialize those dynamics, it needs to split and
proliferate to smaller pieces. This is the final end of the roles of classical
trajectory. We have studied this aspect in terms of the Weierstrass transform.
Such branching paths and Gaussians are summarized in term of a path integral.
Thus the classical paths repeat branching and dissolve into the deep sea of
the genuine quantum dynamics, ending up with full quantum dynamics.

The Schrödinger equation allows for physically interesting applications
through approximate solutions to the partial differential equations, even
without fully understanding its essence, the meaning of the wave function, the
physical consistency within the theory, or why imaginary numbers should appear
in its basic structure. The 100 years of quantum mechanics has thus worked
very efficiently. Nevertheless, in reality, many individual scientists still
face fundamental questions on quantum mechanics. The author hopes that the
present article could share a part of answers to those general questions.
