Efficient Sample-optimal Learning of Gaussian Tree Models via Sample-optimal Testing of Gaussian Mutual Information
Sutanu Gayen, Sanket Kale, Sayantan Sen

Abstract
Learning high-dimensional distributions is a significant challenge in machine learning and statistics in this era of big data. Classical research has mostly concentrated on asymptotic analysis of such data when the underlying distribution exhibits structured patterns like product distributions, tree structures, or Bayesian networks. While existing works [Bhattacharyya et al.: SICOMP 2023, Daskalakis et al.: STOC 2021, Choo et al.: ALT 2024] focus on discrete distributions, the current work addresses the tree structure learning problem for Gaussian distributions, providing efficient algorithms with solid theoretical guarantees. This is crucial as real-world distributions are often continuous and differ from the discrete scenarios studied in prior works. In this work, we first design a conditional mutual information tester for Gaussian random variables that can test whether two Gaussian random variables are independent, or their conditional mutual information is at least ε \varepsilon , for some parameter ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \varepsilon\in(0,1) using 𝒪 ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}) samples which we show to be near-optimal. In contrast, an additive estimation would require Ω ⁡ ( ε − 2 ) \Omega(\varepsilon^{-2}) samples. Our upper bound technique uses linear regression on a pair of suitably transformed random variables. Importantly, we show that the chain rule of conditional mutual information continues to hold for the estimated (conditional) mutual information. As an application of such a mutual information tester, we give an efficient ε \varepsilon -approximate structure-learning algorithm for an n n -variate Gaussian tree model that takes Θ ~ ​ ( n ​ ε − 1 ) \widetilde{\Theta}(n\varepsilon^{-1}) samples which we again show to be near-optimal. In contrast, when the underlying Gaussian model is not known to be tree-structured, we show that Θ ~ ​ ( n 2 ​ ε − 2 ) \widetilde{{{\Theta}}}(n^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}) samples are necessary and sufficient to output an ε \varepsilon -approximate tree structure. We also extensively perform experiments for our mutual information testing and structure learning algorithms. Our experiments corroborate our theoretical convergence bounds for the empirical (conditional) mutual information estimators. Finally, we also compare our tree-structured learning result with Graphical Lasso (GLASSO) and Constrained ℓ 1 \ell_{1} Inverse Covariance Estimation (CLIME), two widely used models in the literature, and show that our algorithm outperforms both for learning tree-structured distributions.



1
Introduction

Learning high-dimensional distributions has been one of the cornerstone problems in the field of machine learning and statistics. In this era of big data, data is being generated in massive amounts, and traditional methods of analyzing data are often not feasible. As a result, over the past two decades, there has been significant interest in developing efficient algorithmic techniques to analyze high dimensional data more efficiently, which also have sound theoretical guarantees. Although learning an arbitrary high dimensional distribution is hard in general, often these tasks become easier when these distributions have some structure. Examples include when these unknown distributions over
n
-dimensional Hamming cube is a
product distribution
,
tree-structured distribution
, or
bounded degree Bayesian networks
.

Probabilistic graphical models
(see
[
33
]
) were proposed to address the above situation of succinctly modeling high-dimensional probability distributions. Concrete examples of such models include the class of
Ising models
, Bayesian networks, and Gaussian graphical models. The focus of this work is the Gaussian graphical model. Since its introduction, it has been used to model important datasets across various scientific disciplines such as modeling
brain connectivity networks
:
[
30
,
54
]
,
gene regulatory networks
:
[
11
,
41
,
48
,
57
]
etc.

A
Gaussian graphical model (GGM)
is simply a multivariate Gaussian distribution
X\sim N(\mu,\Sigma)
, with the mean vector
\mu
and the covariance matrix
\Sigma
, over the sample space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Its probability density is given by:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}[X=x]=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\det(\Sigma)\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}}\exp\left(-\left(x-\mu\right)\Sigma^{-1}\left(x-\mu\right)^{\top}\right)

We can think of the random variable
X
to be consisting of
n
(possibly correlated) component random variables
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
.
The inverse of the covariance matrix
\Theta=\Sigma^{-1}
, called the
precision matrix
, encodes the conditional independence relations of the component random variables as follows. First, construct a dependency graph
G_{\Theta}
on the
n
vertices:
[n]
, whose
(i,j)
-th entry of the adjacency matrix
A
is
1
iff
\Theta_{i,j}\neq 0
. Let
\mathrm{nbr}(i)
be the set of nodes adjacent to
i
in
G_{\Theta}
. Then, any particular component
X_{i}
, conditioned on the set of neighboring components
X_{\mathrm{nbr}(i)}:=\{X_{j}:j\in\mathrm{nbr}(i)\}
is independent of all other components:

\displaystyle X_{i}\bot X_{\mathrm{nbr}(i)}\mid X\setminus\left(\{X_{i}\}\cup X_{\mathrm{nbr}(i)}\right).

Specifically, in this paper, we consider the set of
tree-structured Gaussian graphical models
where the graph
G_{\Theta}
is
tree-structured
, namely it does not have any cycles. Perhaps, this is the simplest class of GGMs one can think of apart from the class of product distributions where
\Sigma
is diagonal and the graph
G_{\Theta}
does not have any edges.
Tree-structured graphical models are used in Biology to model
phylogenetic networks

[
31
, Page 53]
,
[
4
]
and references therein.
[
18
]
in a classic work have shown that when the underlying GGM is tree-structured, the underlying tree structure
G_{\Theta}
coincides with the maximum spanning tree of the complete graph whose weight of the edge
(i,j)
is given by the mutual information
I(X_{i};X_{j})
between the component random variables
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
for every
i\neq j\in[n]
. More generally in the non-realizable case, even if the distribution
P
is not tree-structured, its closest tree-structured distribution
P^{*}
in terms of the reverse-KL divergence to
P
,

\displaystyle P^{*}=\arg\min\limits_{\mbox{tree structured}\ Q}\mathsf{D_{KL}}(P||Q),

is also given by the same maximum spanning tree, where the reverse KL divergence is defined by
\mathsf{D_{KL}}(P||Q)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}dx
. A tree
\widehat{T}
is said to be
\varepsilon
-approximate tree
for
P
if
\mathsf{D_{KL}}(P||P_{\widehat{T}})\leq\mathsf{D_{KL}}(P||P^{*})+\varepsilon
. A tree-structured distribution is said to be
T
-structured
if the underlying tree is
T
. In the
structure learning problem
, we are interested in approximately recovering the best tree structure for the unknown distribution
P
from random samples. Therefore, a natural approach to estimating the best tree structure is to estimate the pairwise mutual informations and simply return the estimated maximum spanning tree. This algorithm, which we refer to as the
Chow-Liu algorithm
, has been extensively studied from asymptotic viewpoints where the goal is to accurately recover the underlying tree structure as the number of samples tends to infinity. In
[
50
]
, the authors studied the “error exponent” for the maximum-likelihood estimator of the tree structure,
[
49
]
studied the error exponents for composite hypothesis testing of Markov forest distributions,
[
52
]
considered learning two tree graphical models to classify future observations into one of two categories,
[
51
]
studied the problem of learning forests for discrete graphical models by removing edges from
the Chow–Liu tree. See the PhD thesis of
[
31
, Chapter 7]
for several related discussions and references.

In contrast, a series of recent papers have looked at the question of
prediction-centric learning
, where the goal is not to recover the exact tree structure but to only
approximately
recover the underlying tree structure such that the best distribution on the recovered tree structure is close to the true one in some distance measure such as KL divergence or variation distance, see
[
10
,
4
,
9
,
24
,
21
]
and the references therein.
The idea is that such an approximate structure should be good enough for downstream inference tasks. Despite the simplicity and usefulness of tree-structured GGMs, a finite sample guarantee of approximately learning these models from samples has remained elusive so far.

Our current paper initiates the study of prediction-centric learning of Gaussian tree models in KL divergence and settles down its sample complexity using efficient algorithms.
Specifically, we characterize the minimum number of samples needed to learn an
\varepsilon
-approximate tree in both realizable and non-realizable cases, up to small factors by giving almost tight sample-complexity upper and lower bounds. We show that the Chow-Liu algorithm always takes a near-optimal number of samples in terms of the approximation error (
\varepsilon
) and the number of component random variables (
n
), to achieve a desired approximation guarantee for structure learning in both the realizable and non-realizable cases. Moreover, there is a quadratic gap for the optimal sample complexity of the realizable and the non-realizable cases:
\widetilde{\Theta}(n\varepsilon^{-1})
versus
\widetilde{\Theta}(n^{2}\varepsilon^{-2})
for any algorithm.

1.1
Our results

We will start with the result of additive estimation of the mutual information (MI) between two arbitrary-mean Gaussian random variables. Given two arbitrary-mean Gaussian random variables
X
and
Y
, we show that if we take
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2}+\log 1/\delta)
samples, the empirical mutual information
\widehat{I}(X;Y)
is
\varepsilon
-close to the true mutual information
I(X;Y)
with probability at least
1-\delta
. We also prove that the dependence on
\varepsilon
is tight. This result might be known in the community. However, as we will use this result for structure learning (to be discussed later), we give a formal proof for completeness.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two real-valued Gaussian random variables, and
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. If we take
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2}+\log 1/\delta)
samples, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\left|\widehat{I}(X;Y)-I(X;Y)\right|\leq\varepsilon
holds, where
\widehat{I}(X;Y)
is the empirical mutual information between
X
and
Y
obtained from
Algorithm

2
with
m=\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2}+\log 1/\delta)
. Moreover, the dependence of
\varepsilon
on the sample complexity is tight, and the time complexity is
\mathcal{O}(m)
.

Now, instead of estimating the mutual information additively, we design an unconditional mutual information tester. It turns out that this problem is quadratically easier compared to the additive estimation problem. Here, given parameters
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, and two jointly distributed arbitrary-mean Gaussian
(X,Z)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, our goal is to distinguish between the cases if
I(X;Z)=0
or
I(X;Z)\geq\varepsilon
. We show that the empirical mutual estimator is sufficient to distinguish between these two cases when taking
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon\log 1/\delta)
samples. The formal statement of our result is as follows:

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
(X,Z)
be jointly distributed as an arbitrary-mean Gaussian. Fix any
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. Then the empirical mutual information estimator
\widehat{I}(X;Z)
satisfies the following guarantees with probability at least
(1-\delta)
whenever
m\geq\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\delta^{-1})
: (i)
I(X;Z)=0
implies
\widehat{I}(X;Z)\leq\varepsilon/20
, (ii)
I(X;Z)\geq\varepsilon
implies
\widehat{I}(X;Z)\geq\varepsilon/8
. Moreover, the time complexity is
\mathcal{O}(m)
.

Similar result also holds for conditional mutual information (CMI) tester. We show that
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon\log 1/\delta)
samples are sufficient to distinguish
I(X;Y\mid Z)=0
from
I(X;Y\mid Z)\geq\varepsilon
. In fact, we prove that similar to the unconditional mutual information setting, empirical conditional mutual information tester is sufficient for this purpose.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
(X,Y,Z)
be jointly distributed as an arbitrary-mean Gaussian, and
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. Then the empirical conditional mutual information estimator satisfies the following with probability at least
(1-\delta)
whenever
m\geq\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon\log 1/\delta)
: (i)
I(X;Y\mid Z)=0
implies
\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z)\leq\varepsilon/20
, (ii)
I(X;Y\mid Z)\geq\varepsilon
implies
\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z)\geq\varepsilon/8
. Moreover, the time complexity is
\mathcal{O}(m)
.

Chain rule of conditional mutual information is a celebrated result in the machine learning and information theory community. However, here, we will be estimating and testing the (conditional) mutual information between Gaussian random variables by using regression. So, it is not immediately clear if the chain rule will continue to hold. Fortunately for us, we show that the chain rule of empirical mutual information remains true. We will later crucially use this result in the correctness proof of our realizable structure learning result.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
\widehat{I}(X;Z)
,
\widehat{I}(X;Y),\widehat{I}(X;Z),\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z)
and
\widehat{I}(X;Z\mid Y)
be our estimations from
Algorithm

2
using
m>3
samples. Then

\widehat{I}(X;Z)+\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z)=\widehat{I}(X;Y)+\widehat{I}(X;Z\mid Y)
.

We also show that our upper bound result on unconditional mutual information tester is tight, by showing a lower bound of
\Omega(1/\varepsilon)
samples for distinguishing between whether
I(X;Y)=0
from
I(X;Y)\geq\varepsilon
. Note that this also shows that our upper bound result for the conditional mutual information tester is tight. Moreover, any lower bound proven for zero-mean Gaussian random variables will continue to hold for arbitrary-mean Gaussian random variables.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
X,Y
be two zero-mean Gaussian random variables.
\Omega(1/\varepsilon)
samples are necessary to distinguish if
I(X;Y)=0
or
I(X;Y)\geq\varepsilon
with probability at least
2/3
.

We would like to note that we first study these problems under the assumption that the distributions are zero-mean Gaussian for the brevity of our calculations. However, there is nothing special about the zero-mean assumption, these results continue to hold for the case when the distributions have arbitrary-mean. Later, we present a reduction that translates the arbitrary mean assumption to the zero-mean assumption and requires only twice the number of samples as compared to the zero-mean setting.

Now we are ready to present our structure learning results. We start with the structure learning result for the non-realizable setting, where the unknown distribution

Additional Related works

As mentioned in the introduction, learning high dimensional distributions from samples is intractable in general (see
[
38
]
). As a result, several structural assumptions are made to design efficient algorithms for learning high dimensional distributions such as Bayesian networks, Markov Random Fields etc. Unfortunately, even in these settings, learning the best structure is known to be NP-hard (
[
17
,
39
]
). Interestingly, Chow-Liu is one of the very few algorithms that are efficient. As a result, there have been several works to understand the applicability of this algorithm in various settings such as in
bounded treewidth graphs
:
[
47
,
43
]
,
mixture of trees
:
[

,
3
]
,
polytrees
:
[
22
]
etc. However, these works mostly focus on the asymptotic regime of the sample complexity. Only recently, researchers have been looking into the performance of these algorithms in the non-asymptotic regime.
[
9
]
and
[
24
]
were the one of the first few works to study the non-asymptotic behavior of Chow-Liu algorithm. Very recently
[
21
]
studied the problem of learning polytree when the underlying skeleton is known.
There have also been many interesting recent works on Ising model:
[
12
,
10
,
4
,
32
,
24
,
28
,
35
]
.

One of the crucial components of our algorithm for structure learning is a novel conditional mutual information tester of Gaussian random variables, which is closely related to the independence testing of two Gaussian random variables. On a general note, this problem falls in the purview of the area of
distribution testing
, where the goal is to infer some property of an unknown probability distribution, with only sample access from it. See the surveys
[
45
,
14
,
13
]
for an introduction to this field. Formally, given sample access to an unknown joint distribution
D
on variables
(X,Y)
, the goal is to distinguish with probability at least
2/3
, if
X
and
Y
are independent or far from being independent under some suitable distance measure. This problem has been extensively studied in the distribution testing community, see
[
8
,
2
,
23
]
. Recently, the conditional variant of independence testing has also been studied (
[
15
,
44
,
36
,
42
,
37
]
). There have also been some works for Bayesian networks (
[
16
,
7
]
).

However, all these works are mostly focused on the case when the unknown distribution is discrete. There have been very few works for the continuous setting: see for example:
[
46
,
44
]
.
However, most of these works are studied in the asymptotic setting, namely the performance of the algorithms when the number of samples tends to infinity. One important work in this scenario is
[
34
]
, where the authors studied the problem of exact structure learning (not necessarily tree) and gave certain parameterized sample-complexity bounds.
In this work, we study these problems in the non-asymptotic finite sample regime and make significant progress in this direction for unconditionally recovering an approximate tree structure.

Deviation from
[
9
]

As mentioned before, the work of
[
9
]
also follows a similar line, where the idea is to reduce the structure learning problem to conditional mutual information testing as reported by
[
9
]
. However, there are several crucial differences between this work and
[
9
]
. We will now briefly discuss them.

1.

In
[
9
]
, the authors studied the problem when the distributions were discrete. However, we are considering Gaussian distributions, so their techniques do not immediately translate here.

2.

In the correctness proof of the conditional mutual information tester, the authors in
[
9
]
showed that if
I(X;Y\mid Z)\geq\varepsilon
, then
\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z)\geq C\cdot I(X;Y\mid Z)
for some absolute constant
C
.
Instead, here we show an alternative guarantee that if
I(X;Y\mid Z)\geq\varepsilon
, then
\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z)\geq C\cdot\varepsilon
, for some suitable constant
C
, which is sufficient for our analysis. This requires some modifications in the structure learning proof of
[
9
]
, which we present in
Section

4.4
.

3.

The chain rule of mutual information is crucially used for the correctness analysis for the structure learning problem in the realizable scenario in
[
9
]
. In their case, they estimated the (conditional) mutual information as the (conditional) mutual information of the observed distribution. Therefore the chain rule was immediate for them.
However, in this work, we are using regression to estimate the unconditional and conditional mutual information.
As a result, it is not immediately clear that the chain rule will continue to hold for the empirical conditional mutual information as well.
One of the crucial contributions of this work is to show that similar results continue to hold in our setting.

4.

The authors in
[
9
]
designed a conditional mutual information tester using averaging arguments. However, this technique can not be extended for the Gaussian setting since the probability density at a particular point is
0
. In this work, we designed a novel tester for this problem using regression on a suitably transformed data.

5.

The sample complexity of the conditional mutual information tester for the discrete setting is
\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\varepsilon^{-1})
, as shown in
[
9
]
for constant
\delta
. However, interestingly, here in the Gaussian setting, our conditional mutual information tester requires only
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
samples for constant
\delta
. So, there is a logarithmic improvement in the sample complexity as compared to
[
9
]
. Similar improvements also hold for structure learning.

6.

Our lower bounds are technically different from that of
[
9
]
. In a broad sense, they use Le Cam’s method for their proofs, whereas we use Fano’s inequality along with coding theoretic arguments to construct the hard instances of our lower bounds.

Comparison with
[
56
]
:

In a parallel work, Wang et al.
[
56
]
have looked at the problem of Gaussian polytree learning. They have independently established the same optimal sample complexity bounds for tree structure learning by the Chow-Liu algorithm in both realizable and agnostic cases. However, there are some important difference between the two papers that are worth highlighting. Their conditional mutual information tester and its analysis directly replaces certain suitable regression coefficients with its empirical counterpart. Whereas, our mutual information tester is based on the regression between some suitably transformed data. We also give a detailed proof of the empirical chain rule of Gaussian mutual information which is not straightforward and is missing in their paper. Finally, the experiments across the two papers are very different. We compare the Chow-Liu algorithm with GLASSO and CLIME algorithms for structure recovery tasks on the hard instances given by our lower bounds; whereas they compare Chow-Liu algorithm with PC and GES algorithms for random trees. Additionally, we perform experiments to confirm the theoretical convergence rates of the mutual information testers on the hard instances given by our lower bounds. Their paper also gives a result for learning Gaussian polytrees.

Overview of our results

In this section, we present overviews of our results. We start with our results for estimating and testing the mutual and conditional mutual information, and then proceed to the structure learning results for both realizable and non-realizable settings. For the brevity of the calculations, we will be working with the zero-mean Gaussian random variables, and finally extend them to arbitrary-mean setting using reductions, which require only twice the number of samples as that of the zero-mean setting. We present a general-purpose algorithm (
Algorithm

2
) for (conditional) mutual information (MI) estimator and tester for the zero-mean setting, called with a suitable number of samples (
m
). The formal proofs are presented in
Section

4
.

Description of
Algorithm

2

Given
m
samples depending upon the problem,
Algorithm

2
first computes the correlation coefficients (either
\widehat{\rho}_{X,Z}
or
\widehat{\rho}_{\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y}}

\widehat{I}(X;Z)
and

3.1
Overview of mutual information testers

Additive estimation of mutual information

We start with our result of additive estimation of mutual information. Refer to the algorithm presented in
Algorithm

2
and the notations therein, called with
m=\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2}+\log 1/\delta)
. Given two zero-mean Gaussian random variables
X
and

\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2}+\log 1/\delta)
samples, the empirical mutual information
\widehat{I}(X;Y)
is
\varepsilon

with probability at least

and

\widehat{\rho}_{X,Y}:=\frac{\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Y}\right)}{\sqrt{\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}}\sqrt{\bar{Y}\cdot\bar{Y}}}

and then we estimate
\widehat{I}(X;Y)=-1/2\log(1-\widehat{\rho}_{X,Y}^{2})

\varepsilon
for additive estimation is tight.
We now present the hard instances for our lower bound. Let us assume
U_{0},V_{0},U_{1},V_{1}\sim N(0,1)
be four iid random bits. The null hypothesis
H_{0}
is defined as: (i)
X_{0}\leftarrow U_{0}
, (ii)
Y_{0}\leftarrow(1/2+\varepsilon)\cdot X_{0}+V_{0}

is defined as: (i)
X_{1}\leftarrow U_{1}
, (ii)
Y_{1}\leftarrow(1/2-\varepsilon)\cdot X_{1}+V_{1}

Mutual information tester

Now we present our result for the mutual information tester.
Given a zero-mean Gaussian joint distribution

and

I(X;Z)=0
or

Z\sim N(0,a^{2}),V\sim N(0,b^{2})

. Suppose we have taken

Conditional mutual information tester

Now we discuss our result of the conditional mutual information testing of three zero-mean Gaussian random variables
X,Y,Z
. More formally, given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we want to distinguish between
I(X;Y\mid Z)=0
from
I(X;Y\mid Z)\geq\varepsilon
. One crucial ingredient of our analysis is the fact that
I(X;Y|Z=z)=I(X;Y|Z=0)
.
Thus, from
Observation

2.16
, we consider the following linear system, where
Z\sim N(0,a^{2}),V\sim N(0,b^{2}),U\sim N(0,c^{2})
,
X\leftarrow\alpha Z+V
and
Y\leftarrow\beta X+\gamma Z+U
. Thus, to distinguish
I(X;Y\mid Z)=0
from
I(X;Y\mid Z)\geq\varepsilon
, it is sufficient to estimate the correlation coefficient between

X-\alpha Z
and
Y-(\alpha\beta+\gamma)Z
and threshold it appropriately as in the case of the unconditional tester discussed earlier. However, as we do not know the parameters
\alpha,\beta
and
\gamma
, we again apply regression techniques to first estimate
\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\beta}
and
\widehat{\gamma}
, and then estimate the correlation coefficient between
X-\widehat{\alpha}Z
and
Y-(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma})Z
. We also prove that the total error in this procedure is not too much again using Bernstein’s concentration inequality. This requires
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon\log 1/\delta)
samples in total and is presented in
Algorithm

2
, called with
m=\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon\log 1/\delta)
.

Chain rule of empirical conditional mutual information

Now we discuss another crucial ingredient of our technical results: the chain rule of empirical conditional mutual information. Given three random variables
X,Y
, and
Z
, it is a very well-known and useful fact that

I(X;Y)+I(X;Z\mid Y)=I(X;Z)+I(X;Y\mid Z).

However, as we will be estimating the (conditional) mutual information
\widehat{I}(X;Y),\widehat{I}(X;Z)
, as well as
\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z)
and
\widehat{I}(X;Z\mid Y)
using regression, it is not immediately clear if the chain rule continues to hold for the estimated mutual and conditional mutual information between
X,Y
and
Z
. We show that the chain rule remains true in this scenario, that is:

\widehat{I}(X;Z)+\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z)=\widehat{I}(X;Y)+\widehat{I}(X;Z\mid Y)

Specifically, we show that our MI and CMI estimators can be seen as that of a linear Gaussian model whose coefficients are such that its covariance matrix coincides with the empirical covariance matrix of the samples. Therefore, the empirical chain rule immediately follows from the chain rule of the aforesaid linear model. We will crucially use this result for structure learning in the realizable setting (
Theorem

1.8
).

Note that in contrast with the additive estimation of mutual information, this is quadratically easier. Interestingly, this lower bound construction also translates to a similar lower bound for the conditional mutual information tester and shows that the dependence on
\varepsilon

3.2
Overview of structure learning result

Now we will discuss our structure learning results. Let us start with the non-realizable setting.

Non-realizable structure learning

Here the distribution
P
from which we are obtaining samples may not be a tree-structured distribution and our goal is to output an
\varepsilon
-approximate tree
T
of
P
.
We use a black-box reduction from the work of

\mathcal{A}
for estimating the mutual information between two random variables using

samples, then there exists an algorithm for learning the
\varepsilon
-approximate tree

that takes
m(\varepsilon/n,\delta/n^{2})
samples from
P
. We also follow the same route. We will use our additive estimator of the mutual information between any two zero-mean Gaussian random variables from

1.1

\varepsilon
-approximate tree of
P
. This requires
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}+\log n/\delta)
samples from
P
.
We would like to point out that the authors in
[
9
]
studied the problem for the discrete distribution setting.
As a result, the additive mutual information tester that we use in our setting is different from that of
[

]
.

Realizable structure learning

Next, we consider the realizable setting, when the unknown distribution
P
is promised to be a tree-structured distribution. Similar to non-realizable case, we follow the same route of

[
9
]
. There it was shown that if there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that takes

samples to distinguish between the cases if

9

Transfer theorem for the non-zero mean case

We start by presenting a transfer theorem that gives mutual information estimation/testing results for the non-zero-mean setting assuming such results for the zero-mean setting.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(X_{1},Y_{1},Z_{1})
and
(X_{2},Y_{2},Z_{2})
be two independent and identically distributed 3-variate Gaussians. Let
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime})=(X_{1}-X_{2},Y_{1}-Y_{2},Z_{1}-Z_{2})

be the covariance matrices for
(X_{1},Y_{1},Z_{1})

M^{\prime}=2\cdot M

2.

I(X_{1};Z_{1})=I(X^{\prime};Z^{\prime})

3.

\displaystyle\longleftarrow\beta X+\gamma Z+U

We have
I(X;Y\mid Z)=I(X;Y\mid Z=0)
from
Observation

2.17
. Note that conditioned on
Z=0
, the above system reduces to the following simpler system:

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle\sim N(0,b^{2})

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\sim N(0,c^{2})

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\longleftarrow V

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\longleftarrow\beta V+U

The latter system has the same behavior as that of
(X-\alpha Z)
and
(Y-(\alpha\beta+\gamma)Z)
. In particular, we get:

I(X;Y\mid Z)=I\left((X-\alpha Z,Y-(\alpha\beta+\gamma)Z\right).

Therefore, if somebody gives us
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, we can test
I(X;Y\mid Z)
very easily by running the tester from
Section

4.3
on the two transformed random variables:
(X-\alpha Z)
and
(Y-(\alpha\beta+\gamma)Z)
i.e. by performing ordinary least squares on the dataset
(\bar{X}-\alpha\bar{Z})
and
(\bar{Y}-(\alpha\beta+\gamma)\bar{Z})
. However, we do not have the true values of
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
. Therefore, we take a two-step approach. First we estimate
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
using the ordinary least squares on the dataset
(\bar{X},\bar{Y},\bar{Z})
to get
\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\beta},\widehat{\gamma}
. Then we perform another ordinary least squares on the transformed dataset
(\bar{X}-\widehat{\alpha}\bar{Z})
and
(\bar{Y}-(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma})\bar{Z})
using the regression coefficients obtained in the first step. This is exactly what
Algorithm

2
performs.

We start by showing the following fact.

Fact 4.5
.

Let
\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\beta},\widehat{\gamma}
be the estimated regression coefficients between the random variable pairs
(X,Z),(Y,X),(Y,Z)
, respectively in
Algorithm

2
. Then the following equality holds:

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\right)=\frac{\left(\bar{Y}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}.

Proof.

Note that
Algorithm

2
uses simple linear regression to estimate the aforesaid correlation coefficients. Therefore,

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{X}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\gamma}\\
\widehat{\beta}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle={\begin{bmatrix}\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}&\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\\
\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}&\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}\end{bmatrix}}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Y}\\
\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle={\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Y}\right)-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)^{2}}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}\right)-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)^{2}}\\
\frac{-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Y}\right)+\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}\right)-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)^{2}}\end{bmatrix}}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{X}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}\left(-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Y}\right)+\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)\right)+\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Y}\right)-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)^{2}}{\left(\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)^{2}\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Y}\right)/\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)+\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Y}\right)}{\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Y}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}

∎

Now we are ready to prove our result on conditional mutual information tester.

1.3

Proof.

As before, we will prove this above result under the assumption that the Gaussian random variables are zero-mean. Then using
Lemma

4.2
, we will able to extend this result to arbitrary-mean Gaussian random variable at the cost of doubling the sample complexity.

Without loss of generality, we assume
\Pr[X,Y,Z]
factorizes as
\Pr[Z]\Pr[X\mid Z]\Pr[Y\mid X,Z]
. In that case, following
Observation

2.16
, we can say that
(X,Y,Z)
follows the following equations :

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle\sim N(0,a^{2})

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle\sim N(0,b^{2})

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\sim N(0,c^{2})

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\longleftarrow\alpha Z+V

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\longleftarrow\beta X+\gamma Z+U

We take
m
samples from
(X,Y,Z)
such that

\begin{bmatrix}\bar{Z}&\bar{V}&\bar{U}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}&V_{1}&U_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
Z_{m}&V_{m}&U_{m}\end{bmatrix}

\begin{bmatrix}\bar{Z}&\bar{X}&\bar{Y}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}&X_{1}&Y_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
Z_{m}&X_{m}&Y_{m}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
\alpha&1&0\\
\beta&\gamma&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{Z}&\bar{V}&\bar{U}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

Then our estimator will be as follows.
We first estimate the regression coefficients between
(X,Z),(Y,X)
and
(Y,Z)
as
\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\beta}
and
\widehat{\gamma}
, respectively using the empirical estimator. Finally, we estimate the regression coefficient between
\left(X\mid Z=0,Y\mid Z=0\right)
as that of the pair
\left(X-\widehat{\alpha}Z,Y-\left(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\right)Z\right)
which we again estimate using the empirical estimator.

Since
Algorithm

2
uses linear regression, we have the following using

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}

\displaystyle=\alpha+\frac{\left(\bar{V}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Y}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha\beta+\gamma\right)+\frac{\left(\left(\beta\bar{V}+\bar{U}\right)\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}.

From Bernstein’s inequality (
Lemma

2.5
), we can say that whenever
m\geq\max\left(\frac{64\log\frac{6}{\delta}}{9c\lambda^{2}},\frac{8\log\frac{3}{\delta}}{3c\lambda}\right)
, the following three statements hold together with probability at least
(1-\delta)

\displaystyle\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)

\displaystyle\in ma^{2}\left(1\pm\lambda\right)

\displaystyle\left(\bar{V}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)

\displaystyle\in\pm mab{\lambda}

\displaystyle\left(\left(\beta\bar{V}+\bar{U}\right)\cdot\bar{Z}\right)

\displaystyle\in\pm ma\lambda\sqrt{\beta^{2}b^{2}+c^{2}}

Therefore,

\displaystyle X-\widehat{\alpha}Z

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha-\widehat{\alpha}\right)Z+V

\displaystyle=V-\frac{\left(\bar{V}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}Z

\displaystyle=V+\eta_{\alpha}Z

where we have:

\displaystyle-\frac{b}{a}\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)\leq\eta_{\alpha}\leq\frac{b}{a}\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right).

Similarly,

\displaystyle Y-\left(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\right)Z

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\alpha\beta+\gamma\right)-\left(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\right)\right)Z+\left(\beta V+U\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\beta V+U\right)-\frac{\left(\left(\beta\bar{V}+\bar{U}\right)\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}Z

\displaystyle=\left(\beta V+U\right)+\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}Z

where,

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{\beta^{2}b^{2}+c^{2}}}{a}\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)\leq\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\beta^{2}b^{2}+c^{2}}}{a}\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\rho_{\left(X-\widehat{\alpha}Z\right),\left(Y-\left(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\right)Z\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{{\bf Cov}}\left(\left(X-\widehat{\alpha}Z\right),\left(Y-\left(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\right)\right)Z\right)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}\left(X-\widehat{\alpha}Z\right)\operatorname{{\bf Var}}\left(Y-\left(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\right)\right)Z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{{\bf Cov}}\left({\left(V+\eta_{\alpha}Z\right),\left(\beta V+U\right)+\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}Z}\right)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}{\left(V+\eta_{\alpha}Z\right)}\operatorname{{\bf Var}}\left(\left(\beta V+U\right)+\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}Z\right)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{{\bf Cov}}\left(\left(\beta V+U\right),V\right)+\operatorname{{\bf Cov}}\left(V,\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}Z\right)+\operatorname{{\bf Cov}}\left(\eta_{\alpha}Z,\beta V+U\right)+\operatorname{{\bf Cov}}\left(\eta_{\alpha}Z,\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}Z\right)}{{{\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(V)+\eta_{\alpha}^{2}\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(Z)}\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}\left(\beta V+U\right)+\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}^{2}\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(Z)}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{{\bf Cov}}\left(\left(\beta V+U\right),V\right)+\eta_{\alpha}\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}\operatorname{{\bf Var}}\left(Z\right)}{{{\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(V)}\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(\beta V+U)}\sqrt{1+\eta_{\alpha}^{2}\frac{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(Z)}{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(V)}}\sqrt{1+\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}^{2}\frac{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(Z)}{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}\left(\beta V+U\right)}}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\eta_{\alpha}^{2}\frac{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(Z)}{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(V)}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}^{2}\frac{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(Z)}{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}\left(\beta V+U\right)}}}\left(\rho_{\left(\beta V+U\right),V}+\frac{\eta_{\alpha}\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}\operatorname{{\bf Var}}\left(Z\right)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(V)}\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(\beta V+U)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\eta_{\alpha}^{2}\frac{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(Z)}{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(V)}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}^{2}\frac{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(Z)}{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}\left(\beta V+U\right)}}}\left(\rho_{\left(X\mid Z=0\right),\left(Y\mid Z=0\right)}+\frac{\eta_{\alpha}\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}\operatorname{{\bf Var}}\left(Z\right)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(V)}\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(\beta V+U)}}\right)

Note that

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)\leq\frac{\eta_{\alpha}\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(Z)}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(V)}}\leq\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)\leq\frac{\eta_{\alpha\beta+\gamma}\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(Z)}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(\beta V+U)}}\leq\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right).

We subsequently consider (we may reuse previous samples)
m^{\prime}\geq\Omega(\lambda^{-2}\log{\delta^{-1}})
samples from
X^{\prime}=X-\widehat{\alpha}Z
and
Y^{\prime}=Y-\left(\widehat{\alpha}\widehat{\beta}+\widehat{\gamma}\right)Z
to estimate
\widehat{\rho}_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}
using the empirical estimator. From
Equation

4
, we can say that, with probability at least
(1-\delta)
, the following holds:

\rho_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{X^{\prime}}\eta_{Y^{\prime}}}\right)-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{\eta_{X^{\prime}}\eta_{Y^{\prime}}}\leq\widehat{\rho}_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}\leq\rho_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{X^{\prime}}\eta_{Y^{\prime}}}\right)+\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{\eta_{X^{\prime}}\eta_{Y^{\prime}}}

where
\eta_{X^{\prime}},\eta_{Y^{\prime}}\in[\sqrt{1-\lambda^{\prime}},\sqrt{1+\lambda^{\prime}}]
.

We define
\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z):=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\widehat{\rho}_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}^{2}}\right)
as our estimated conditional mutual information. Henceforth, we will fix
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{50}}
and assume
\varepsilon<c
for some suitable constant
c
.

Now we consider the two cases below:

Part (i).
Firstly, assume the case
I(X;Y\mid Z)=0
. Then,
\rho_{\left(X\mid Z=0\right),\left(Y\mid Z=0\right)}=0
and hence
\left|\rho_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}\right|\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\left(1-\lambda\right)^{2}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{\prime}}-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{1-\lambda^{\prime}}\leq\widehat{\rho}_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{\prime}}+\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{(1-\lambda^{\prime})}\leq 0.7

and
\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z)\leq\widehat{\rho}_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{\prime}}+\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{(1-\lambda^{\prime})}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{20}
.

Part (ii).
Now, consider the case when
I(X;Y\mid Z)\geq\varepsilon
. Then,
\rho_{\left(X\mid Z=0\right),\left(Y\mid Z=0\right)}^{2}=1-e^{-2I(X;Y\mid Z)}\geq 1-e^{-2\varepsilon}>25\lambda^{2}
. Suppose,
\rho_{\left(X\mid Z=0\right),\left(Y\mid Z=0\right)}>5\lambda
, then

\rho_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}\geq\frac{1}{\left(1+\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)}\left(\rho_{\left(X\mid Z=0\right),\left(Y\mid Z=0\right)}-\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)

and therefore

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}\geq\frac{1}{\left(1+\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)}\left(\rho_{\left(X\mid Z=0\right),\left(Y\mid Z=0\right)}-\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{\prime}}\right)-\left(\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{1-\lambda^{\prime}}\right).

Otherwise,
\rho_{\left(X\mid Z=0\right),\left(Y\mid Z=0\right)}<-5\lambda
, then

\displaystyle\rho_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}<\frac{1}{\left(1+\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)}\left(\rho_{\left(X\mid Z=0\right),\left(Y\mid Z=0\right)}+\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)

and therefore

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}<\frac{1}{\left(1+\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)}\left(\rho_{\left(X\mid Z=0\right),\left(Y\mid Z=0\right)}+\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{\prime}}\right)+\left(\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{1-\lambda^{\prime}}\right)

Combining the two cases and setting
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{50}}
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\rho}_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{\left(1+\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)}\left(\left|\rho_{\left(X\mid Z=0\right),\left(Y\mid Z=0\right)}\right|-\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\right)-\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}}\right)\left|\rho_{\left(X\mid Z=0\right),\left(Y\mid Z=0\right)}\right|-\left(\frac{\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\right)}{{\left(1+\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}\right)}}+\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle={\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{4}},

Therefore,
\widehat{I}(X;Y\mid Z)\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{1-0.25\varepsilon}\geq\varepsilon/8
.

The total number of samples is
\max\left\{m,m^{\prime}\right\}=\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon\log 1/\delta)
and the total failure probability is
2\delta
. We re-scale it to
\delta
, however, that would only change the sample complexity only by a factor of
2
. This completes the proof of the lemma. The proof of the time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(m)
is direct and omitted.
∎

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}{\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Y}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)\left(\bar{Y}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)}{\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\bar{Y}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}\right)-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Y}\right)}{\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{X}\right)\left(\bar{Z}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)-\left(\bar{X}\cdot\bar{Z}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}

Learning Tree Structured Distributions: realizable case

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(I(e_{i})-I(f_{i})\right)>2\varepsilon

and

6.3
Chow-Liu and GLASSO Comparison

In this subsection, we compare the results of our Gaussian Chow-Liu algorithm with Graphical Lasso (GLASSO), a popular algorithm used in the literature for estimation of covariance matrices. Let us start by briefly describing GLASSO.

6.3.1
Graphical Lasso (GLASSO)

The Graphical Lasso (GLASSO) is an algorithm for estimating sparse inverse covariance matrices in Gaussian graphical models, especially useful for high-dimensional data where the number of variables
p
exceeds the number of observations
n
. It estimates the precision matrix
\Theta=\Sigma^{-1}
, revealing conditional dependencies between variables.
The precision matrix
\Theta
is estimated by solving the optimization problem stated in
[
26
]
:

\hat{\Theta}=\underset{\Theta\succ 0}{\text{argmin}}\left\{\text{tr}(S\Theta)-\log\det(\Theta)+\lambda\|\Theta\|_{1}\right\}

Here,
S
is the sample covariance matrix,
\|\Theta\|_{1}
is the element-wise
\ell_{1}
-norm of
\Theta
, and
\lambda
is a regularization parameter controlling sparsity. The
\ell_{1}
-penalty induces sparsity in
\Theta
.

Conclusion

In this work, we designed novel (conditional) mutual information testers for Gaussian random variables and used them to design efficient structure learning algorithms for Gaussian tree models. There are several open problems.
An interesting open problem here is the
robust learning
problem, where adversarially corrupted samples from the unknown distribution are obtained. Another open problem is to study this problem for continuous models other than the Gaussian model.
