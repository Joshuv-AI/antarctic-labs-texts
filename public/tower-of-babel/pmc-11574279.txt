BCL3, GBP1, IFI16, and CCR1 as potential brain-derived biomarkers for parietal grey matter lesions in multiple sclerosis
Authors: Hua Guo, Zhaocheng Li, Yanqing Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple Sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease of the central nervous system, progressing from Relapsing–Remitting MS (RRMS) to Secondary Progressive MS (SPMS) in many cases. The transition involves complex biological changes. Our study aims to identify potential biomarkers for distinguishing SPMS by analyzing gene expression differences between normal-appearing and lesioned parietal grey matter, which may also contribute to understand the pathogenesis of SPMS. We utilized public datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), applying bioinformatics and machine learning techniques including Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), and Random Forest (RF) for predictive model construction. Our study also included analyses of immune cell infiltration. The study identified 359 DEGs, with 105 up-regulated and 254 down-regulated. WGCNA identified 264 common genes, which were subjected to KEGG and GO enrichment analyses, highlighting their role in immune response and viral infection pathways. Four genes (BCL3, GBP1, IFI16, and CCR1) were identified as key biomarkers for SPMS, supported by LASSO regression and RF analyses. These genes were further validated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, demonstrating significant predictive potential for SPMS. Our study provides a novel set of biomarkers for SPMS from lesioned grey matter of SPMS cases, offering potential for diagnosis and targeted therapeutic strategies. Introduction

Multiple Sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune, inflammatory disease that affects the central nervous system (CNS). It occurs when the immune system mistakenly attacks the myelin sheath, the protective covering of nerve fibers, leading to disrupted communication between neurons1. This degeneration results in a range of symptoms, including optic neuritis, cognitive impairments, fatigue, dizziness, motor dysfunction, and issues with the digestive and urinary systems2. Globally, MS affects approximately 2.8 million people, with its prevalence continuing to rise3. The disease can be classified into two main types based on its clinical progression: Relapsing–Remitting Multiple Sclerosis (RRMS) and Secondary Progressive Multiple Sclerosis (SPMS). RRMS is characterized by acute episodes of symptom exacerbation, termed relapses, followed by periods of partial or complete recovery, known as remissions. In contrast, SPMS involves a gradual, continuous progression of symptoms, which may or may not be accompanied by relapses and remissions. The transition from RRMS to SPMS varies widely, with the median time from the initial relapse to SPMS onset being approximately 20 years, although it can range from 1 to 51 years4. Most patients initially diagnosed with RRMS will eventually develop SPMS5, which is marked by steady symptom progression.

Diagnosing SPMS remains challenging compared to RRMS. It is generally defined as a deterioration persisting for at least six months following an initial relapsing–remitting course, independent of relapses6. However, there is no standardized, objective definition universally accepted, and clinical practice often assumes a minimum level of cumulative disability before confirming an SPMS diagnosis, though this threshold is not formally specified7. Consequently, the progression in early RRMS can accumulate silently over time, often going unnoticed by both the patient and physician due to the slow and subtle loss of function, a phenomenon known as “silent progression”8,9. RRMS and SPMS are part of a disease continuum without a distinct boundary, complicating the clinical transition. Furthermore, this transition is often mired in diagnostic uncertainty, which can last nearly three years6. Therefore, developing methods to identify SPMS earlier is crucial to improving patient outcomes and tailoring treatment strategies.

In our study, we analyzed data from parietal grey-matter tissue samples to identify potential biomarkers for SPMS. We employed a combination of bioinformatics tools and machine learning technologies, including Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), and Random Forest (RF). Our findings revealed four diagnostic biomarkers (BCL3, GBP1, IFI16, and CCR1) for SPMS lesions and enabled us to develop a risk model to distinguish the lesions and normal brain region. Furthermore, by exploring the molecular and genetic bases of SPMS through biomarker analysis, we have identified novel potential therapeutic targets. Our study also included analyses of immune cell infiltration, enhancing our understanding of the pathological processes of SPMS and potentially guiding future clinical interventions.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

We obtained RNA expression data of the parietal cortex from patients with SPMS from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/)10. We utilized the GSE131282 dataset, which includes 42 control gray matter tissue samples, 105 normal-appearing grey matter tissue samples from MS cases, and 37 cortical grey matter lesion tissue samples from MS cases11. In the original study, lesions were defined as areas where the staining for anti-MOG (myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein) was entirely absent or exhibited diminished myelin density that was distinctly separated from surrounding normal-appearing tissue. Normal-appearing grey matter was characterized by no staining loss of MOG, neuronal nuclei, or oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2 compared with control cases, and no elevated level of CD68 compared with controls11. To ensure data reliability, the original study implemented several measures for adjustment and exclusion of probes or samples. These measures included immunohistochemical characterization, RNA isolation and quality assessment, removal of “bad quality probes,” selection of unique gene-level probes, exclusion of samples with mismatched sex based on Y-chromosome linked genes, and principal component analysis11. For data processing and subsequent analyses, we used R software (version 4.3.2) and TBtools software (version 2.030)12.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

We conducted a differential analysis of mRNA expression comparing normal-appearing parietal grey matter (NAPGM) with parietal grey matter lesions (PGML) in patients diagnosed with SPMS. Using the R package “limma” (version 3.54.2), we identified DEGs based on the criteria of an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (log2FC) > 1. For visualization, we employed the R packages “pheatmap” (version 1.0.12) to generate heatmaps and “ggplot2” (version 3.4.4) to produce volcano plots of the DEGs.

WGCNA and screening of common genes

WGCNA is a widely used tool for exploring relationships between genes within modules, between different modules, or between modules and disease outcomes13. We employed the “WGCNA shiny” plugin in TBtools software (version 2.030)12 to conduct WGCNA. This analysis groups genes with highly correlated expression into co-expression modules, the correlations between each module and phenotype were measured by correlation coefficient and p-value simultaneously. We applied WGCNA to data from both the NAPGM and PGML groups to explore clustering relationships within the dataset. To identify modules significantly associated with the phenotypes, we looked for a correlation coefficient greater than 0.7 and a p-value less than 0.05. We then intersected genes in these significant modules with DEGs using the R package “VennDiagram” (version 1.7.3)14. The genes found at this intersection were termed “common genes.”, which embodied the DEGs showing relative strong correlation with parietal grey matter lesions.

Functional enrichment analysis of common genes

KEGG is a comprehensive knowledge base that systematically analyzes gene functions within gene and molecular networks15–17. The GO consortium offers a dynamic, controlled vocabulary that describes gene and protein interactions within cells across all eukaryotes18. To further explore the biological functions of the identified common genes, we conducted KEGG and GO enrichment analyses, considering p-values less than 0.05 as statistically significant. Our GO enrichment analysis included biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories for the common genes. These enrichment analyses were facilitated by the R package “clusterProfiler” (version 4.6.2)19.

PPI network

We used the online tool STRING (version 12.0, http://string-db.org) to construct a PPI network for the common genes identified in our study, setting the minimum required interaction score to 0.4 (medium confidence). The resulting data were then imported into Cytoscape (version 3.10.1) for further analysis. Within Cytoscape (version 3.10.1)20, we utilized the “cytoHubba” plugin to visualize the interactions21. The “Stress” parameter was selected to prioritize the common genes in the network.

Machine learning and candidate genes

To identify candidate genes for predicting the occurrence of SPMS, we utilized LASSO and Random Forest (RF). We chose to focus on the intersection of genes identified by both methods to increase the robustness of our selection. LASSO focuses on feature selection and regularization, producing a sparse model by shrinking less important feature coefficients to zero22,23. This method is particularly useful for identifying a subset of the most relevant features while handling multicollinearity. Random Forest, an ensemble learning method, emphasizes robust and diverse predictions through multiple decision trees22. RF can capture non-linear relationships and interactions between features, providing a different perspective on feature importance. For LASSO, we input 50 genes (obtained from the PPI network) and identified six genes (BCL3, GBP1, XRCC4, IFI16, CCR1, and KAT2B) when the lambda value was set to lambda.min, which provides the minimum mean cross-validated error. For RF, we selected the top 20 genes based on their Mean Decrease in Gini (MDG) importance. This decision was informed by previous research showing that MDG ranking reaches a stable overlap of around 75% for top 25 lists, while Mean Decrease in Accuracy (MDA) showed complete instability24. Additionally, we considered the importance values themselves. In our study, the MDG of the 20th gene was 0.24 (Supplementary file 3), which we deemed a reasonable cutoff based on similar thresholds used in other studies22,25. Both analyses were conducted using the R packages “glmnet” (version 4.1-8) for LASSO and “randomforest” (version 4.7-1.1) for RF. Ultimately, the genes identified at the intersection of these two methods were designated as “candidate genes.”

Construction of risk models based on key genes

Furthermore, we validated candidate genes using receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Genes with an area under the curve (AUC) greater than 0.8 were considered to have credible predictive value. Based on these findings, we used the R package “rms” (version 6.7-1) to construct nomograms that predict the occurrence of SPMS. Calibration curves were also plotted to assess the accuracy of these nomograms. Key genes were identified based on their AUC and calibration results.

Immune cell infiltration and enrichment analysis

To further explore the roles of various cells in SPMS, we used the xCell web tool, which profiles 64 cell types including lymphoid cells, stem cells, myeloid cells, stromal cells, and others. xCell provides reliable gene signatures based on data from multiple sources and accounts for dependencies between different cell types26. The relative immune cell infiltration was quantified using the xCell score. This score represents the relative abundance of each immune cell type rather than an absolute count or percentage. Higher xCell scores indicate greater enrichment of a particular cell type in the sample. In addition to individual cell type scores, we calculated an overall Immune score based on the xCell scores of different immune cell types. The Immune score was computed as follows: Immune score = (sum of adjusted scores for [B-cells, CD4+ T-cells, CD8 + T-cells, Dendritic cells, Eosinophils, Macrophages, Monocytes, Mast cells, Neutrophils, NK cells]) / 1.5. This calculation is based on the xCell algorithm’s source code (https://github.com/dviraran/xCell/blob/master/R/xCell.R). Statistical analyses were performed to compare xCell scores and Immune scores between NAPGM and PGML groups, with p-values calculated to determine the significance of observed differences. We visualized the correlation heatmaps and violin plots using the R packages “corrplot”, “vioplot”, and “ggplot2”. Additionally, we assessed the relationship between key gene expressions and the abundance of immune cells, considering a p-value < 0.05 as statistically significant.

WGCNA and common genes

We applied WGCNA to build a co-expression network. The topology analysis did not reveal a soft threshold that achieved the desired R2 cut-off of > 0.8 (Fig. 3A and B). Therefore, based on the sample size, we selected a soft threshold of 14, which resulted in an R2 of 0.69. We set the minimum module size at 30 and the module cut-tree height at 0.25, leading to the identification of ten co-expression modules (Fig. 3C). We then assessed the correlation between these modules and the clinical phenotype (Fig. 3D). For further analysis, we focused on modules with a correlation coefficient (rho) > 0.7 and a corresponding p-value < 0.05. Specifically, genes within the MEmagenta module (R = 0.79, p = 0.000000039) and MEturquoise module (R = 0.7, p = 0.0000068) were cross-referenced with the DEGs to identify common genes (Fig. 3E). This process identified 12 overlapping genes between the MEmagenta module and DEGs, and 252 between the MEturquoise module and DEGs, resulting in a total of 264 common genes for subsequent analysis (Supplementary File 1).

Gene functional enrichment analysis of common genes

KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were conducted to characterize the function of common genes. This result indicated that genes in the parietal grey matter of patients with SPMS were primarily enriched in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, cAMP signaling pathway, and Hedgehog signaling pathway (Fig. 4A). Additionally, these genes are also involved in pathways related to Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus infection and various cancers, including basal cell carcinoma, melanoma, pancreatic cancer, and chronic myeloid leukemia. Consistent with the KEGG results, viral infection pathways were also prominent in the BP enrichment (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, potential roles were suggested for T cell apoptosis and organic hydroxy compound metabolism in SPMS. For CC, abnormalities in protein and lipid metabolism, critical components of biological membranes, were noted. In MF, guanyl-nucleotide-binding protein (G-protein) related signaling pathways, key players in signal transduction, were implicated in the pathogenesis of SPMS.

Machine learning and candidate genes

We imported the top 50 genes, ranked by “Stress” scores from the PPI network, into machine learning analyses. In the LASSO regression analysis, six genes—BCL3, GBP1, XRCC4, IFI16, CCR1, and KAT2B—were identified as having associations with SPMS when the lambda value was set as lambda. min (Fig. 5B and C). Additionally, we extracted the top 20 important genes using the RF algorithm, evaluated by mean decrease accuracy (MDA) and mean decrease Gini (MDG) (Fig. 6A). Genes that appeared in both the LASSO results and the top 20 important genes from RF were designated as candidate genes. Ultimately, four candidate genes—BCL3, GBP1, IFI16, CCR1—were identified (Fig. 6B).

Risk model and validation

We evaluated the four candidate genes—BCL3, GBP1, IFI16, and CCR1—using ROC curves. The AUC values for BCL3, GBP1, IFI16, and CCR1 were 0.992, 0.948, 0.996, and 0.909, respectively (Fig. 7A). These results strongly suggest that these genes have potential for use in a predictive model for SPMS. Further analysis through a nomogram indicated that SPMS was associated with significantly elevated GBP1 expression and reduced expressions of CCR1, BCL3, and IFI16 (Fig. 7B). To assess the accuracy of the nomogram, we plotted a calibration curve (Fig. 7C). With a mean absolute error of 0.015, a mean squared error of 0.00054, and a 0.9 quantile of absolute error at 0.037, the calibration curve demonstrated that this risk model reliably predicts the occurrence of SPMS. Therefore, we identified BCL3, GBP1, IFI16, and CCR1 as key predictive genes for SPMS.

Immune cell infiltration and stromal cell enrichment analysis

Given the significant role of immune cells in MS progression, we conducted an analysis of immune cell infiltration (lymphoid and myeloid cells). Using the xCell algorithm, we estimated the relative abundance of various immune cell types. Our findings showed statistically significant higher proportions of basophils, CD4+ central memory T-cells (CD4+ Tcm), CD8+ naïve T-cells, class-switched memory B-cells, natural killer T-cells (NKT), regulatory T-cells (Tregs), and immature dendritic cells (iDC) in the PGML group. Conversely, the NAPGM group exhibited a higher infiltration of CD4+ memory T-cells, CD8+ central memory T-cells (CD8+ Tcm), macrophages, macrophages M1, monocytes, NK cells, activated dendritic cells (aDC), conventional dendritic cells (cDC), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC) (Fig. 8A and B).

We also examined the relationship between four key genes and immune cells. BCL3 correlated positively with CD8 + naïve T-cell (p = 0.017) and Th1 cell (p = 0.037) populations. It also showed negative associations with mast cells (p = 0.028) and CD4+ Tcm (p = 0.026) (Fig. 8C). CCR1 was positively associated with plasmacytoid dendritic cells (p = 0.022) but negatively with iDC (p = 0.037), NKT (p = 0.037), and cDC (p = 0.030) (Fig. 8D). GBP1 was suggested to downregulate iDC (p = 0.032), aDC (p = 0.021), and cDC (p = 0.007) (Fig. 8E). IFI16 showed no significant regulatory relationship with inflammatory cells.

Discussion

The transition from RRMS to SPMS typically occurs 10–15 years after the initial RRMS diagnosis and is considered a critical turning point in MS progression27,28. Previous studies have identified potential biomarkers in blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)—including TNF-alpha, IL-4, interferon-gamma, IL-17, TGF-β1, HGF, EGF, CCL11, CCL4, YKL-40, and CXCL13—that could potentially differentiate RRMS from SPMS29–31. However, these fluid biomarkers have not been effective in facilitating early diagnosis of SPMS, and their relationship to brain tissue pathology remains unclear. Recent insights suggest that while neurodegenerative processes become more prominent in SPMS, inflammatory effects are relatively reduced compared to RRMS32,33. This shift in disease mechanisms may explain the reduced efficacy of immunomodulatory therapies in SPMS. We observed a downregulation of certain immune-related genes (BCL3, GBP1, IFI16, and CCR1) in SPMS lesions compared to normal-appearing grey matter, which aligns with the concept of reduced inflammatory activity in SPMS lesions.

In our study, we identified 359 DEGs (105 up-regulated and 254 down-regulated) between normal-appearing and lesioned parietal grey matter in SPMS patients. We also performed WGCNA, identifying 264 common genes present in both DEGs and two optimal WGCNA modules. Subsequent KEGG and GO enrichment analyses of these genes suggested enrichment in pathways related to immune responses and inflammatory processes. While some of these enriched pathways included terms associated with viral responses, it’s important to note that such enrichment analyses should be interpreted cautiously and not taken as direct evidence of viral involvement in SPMS pathology. Our study on these common genes also suggested their potential involvement in various biological processes, including some associated with cancer pathways. Further analysis using PPI networks and assessment of node “Stress” led to the selection of four genes—BCL3, GBP1, IFI16, and CCR1—through LASSO regression and RF analyses. These genes were integrated into a model that demonstrated potential to distinguish parietal grey matter lesions in post-mortem SPMS brain tissue from SPMS controls, as evidenced by AUC values and calibration curves. While this model shows promise in differentiating SPMS lesions in post-mortem tissue, it’s important to note that its predictive value for early SPMS diagnosis in living patients requires further investigation and validation.

BCL3 is part of the inhibitor of NF-κB (IκB) family, which has been associated with T cell function and survival34,35. Its downregulation in our samples might reflect altered T cell dynamics in chronic lesions. Similarly, GBP1, a member of the guanylate binding protein (GBP) family of interferon-inducible GTPases36, is prevalent in cells activated by IFNγ and plays diverse roles, including limiting infection by directly targeting intracellular microbes and inducing autophagy, pyroptosis, or apoptosis37. It is reduced expression in our study, potentially indicating a shift in the innate immune response in SPMS lesions. The downregulation of these immune-related genes in SPMS lesions aligns with the concept of reduced inflammatory activity in SPMS compared to RRMS, as proposed by Lassmann et al.5. However, it’s crucial to note that reduced expression doesn’t necessarily equate to reduced importance. These genes might play subtle yet critical roles in maintaining a chronic inflammatory state or in modulating neurodegenerative processes.

The interferon-inducible protein IFI16 has been implicated in both innate immunity and DNA damage responses38–40. Its altered expression in SPMS lesions could potentially link immune responses to neurodegenerative processes, a connection that merits further exploration. CCR1 has been extensively studied for its involvement in chemokine-driven pathologies within the CNS, including MS and Alzheimer’s disease41. While traditionally linked to inflammatory processes42,43, our findings suggest a potential role for CCR1 in MS pathology. The observed decreased expression of CCR1 in our samples may reflect changes in inflammatory processes, though its specific relevance to SPMS and potential as a biomarker requires additional research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study contributes to the growing body of evidence suggesting a complex interplay between immune responses and neurodegenerative processes in SPMS. The transition from RRMS to SPMS likely involves subtle shifts in multiple pathways rather than a single definitive change. As we continue to unravel these complexities, integrating findings from genetics, immunology, and neurobiology will be crucial in developing more effective diagnostic tools and treatments for SPMS.

Limitations

While our study provides valuable insights into the molecular landscape of SPMS lesions, it’s important to acknowledge its limitations. Our findings are based on post-mortem tissue, which represents the end-stage of the disease. Longitudinal studies in living patients would be necessary to understand how these gene expression changes evolve over time and potentially contribute to the transition from RRMS to SPMS. Moreover, the heterogeneity of MS lesions is well-documented. Our focus on parietal grey matter lesions provides a specific perspective, but complementary studies in other brain regions and in white matter lesions would offer a more comprehensive understanding. The identification of these genes opens new avenues for therapeutic exploration. While current SPMS treatments primarily aim to slow disease progression, understanding the roles of BCL3, GBP1, IFI16, and CCR1 in SPMS pathology could potentially lead to more targeted interventions. For instance, if future research confirms a protective role for any of these genes in limiting neurodegeneration, they could become targets for gene therapy approaches.
