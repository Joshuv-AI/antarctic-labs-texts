Exploring medical and nursing students’ perceptions of death and dying: a qualitative study using photo-elicitation interviews
Authors: Wei How Darryl Ang, Ming Marcus Chua, Jin Wei Fan, Daniel Zhi Yu Ramos Oon, Jung Jae Lee, Shek Nam Marques Ng, M Kamala Devi, Laura Tham Schmidt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn individual’s perception of death and dying is influenced by multidimensional factors, including societal, relational and personal aspects. When factors such as cultural taboos and fears dominate, they can negatively shape one’s perception of death and dying. Consequently, inexperienced medical and nursing students may be more susceptible to the negative impacts of dealing with death and dying.AimTo explore perceptions of death and dying among medical and nursing students.MethodA descriptive qualitative study using participant-selected photo-elicitation interviews was conducted at a medical school situated in Singapore. A purposive sample based on a maximum variation technique was used to select 32 medical and nursing students. Individual semi-structured audio and video-recorded interviews were conducted via Zoom. The interviews were transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were subjected to thematic analyses. Photos were used to complement the interview narratives.ResultsA total of three themes were derived from the analysis, (1) interpretations of death and dying, (2) navigating through cultural and spiritual beliefs, and (3) encountering deaths in everyday life. While participants viewed death as a celebration of a life’s journey, they also feared uncertainties about what happens after death. Their perceptions were shaped by cultural norms, taboos, and spiritual beliefs. Additionally, participants’ perceptions of death were shaped by the proximity and strength of their relationship with the deceased.ConclusionsThe findings of this study can contribute to the broader literature by providing an in-depth understanding of medical and nursing students’ perception of death. The recommendations to the medical and nursing curriculum through the introduction of death conversations, pastoral care, and resilience training can help support students as they navigate their way through their academic journeys.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05125-6.Keywords: Death, Dying, Qualitative, Photo-elicitation interviews, Nursing, Medical, Students

Introduction

Death is regarded as a natural and inevitable aspect of life yet initiating conversations about death and dying remains a challenging and sensitive topic [1, 2]. Studies have revealed that one’s ability to communicate about death and dying can be influenced by personal perceptions and beliefs regarding the topic [3, 4]. Among healthcare professionals, the inability to initiate or sustain such difficult conversations can potentially limit one’s capacity to provide effective care for patients with life-limiting conditions [2, 5, 6]. This is significant as a large part of initiating care for patients with life-limiting conditions commonly involves an in-depth discussion of patients’ preferences through advance care planning (ACP) [7]. Carrying out ACP conversations often entails sensitive discussions of death and dying [8, 9]. For this reason, it is important to understand healthcare professionals’ outlook towards death and dying as it may negatively impact their responses (e.g., emotional and psychological stress) when dealing with end-of-life situations [10]. This may, in turn, result in burnout and poorer job satisfaction in healthcare professionals [11, 12].

Background

An individual’s perception of death and dying can be influenced by various macro factors such as societal and cultural reasons, as well as micro factors including personal beliefs [1, 13]. Historically, each generation espouses different beliefs and customs relating to death and dying [14]. From a cultural perspective, several notable changes have transpired over the past few centuries. Since the beginning of the late 19th century, discussing death has been considered a cultural taboo [15, 16]. Taboos are defined as ‘forbidden’ words or expressions that can apply to any prohibitions [17]. For example, some people believe that discussing death and dying may lead to ill-fated consequences [2, 18]. Changes in beliefs towards death have evolved, primarily due to advancements in healthcare. While death was once perceived as a peaceful and intimate event at home, it is now often viewed as a frightening occurrence in the hospital [15].

Beyond cultural beliefs, an individual’s perception of death and dying may also be influenced by personal factors such as age, health condition, and religion [19, 20]. A study by Okechi (2017) reported that older individuals experience higher levels of thanatophobia (i.e., fear of death or dying) and thus have a negative outlook towards death [20]. From a health perspective, individuals with poorer health conditions were found to fear death more [21]. Although religion is often a protective factor that helps individuals cope with death by providing meanings [22], Lockhart and colleagues [21] concluded that both extremely religious as well as irreligious individuals have lower levels of thanatophobia.

Understanding healthcare professionals’ perceptions of death and dying carries significant implications. Firstly, evidence suggests that healthcare providers who are more comfortable with conversations about death and dying can provide better care and make timely referrals to palliative care services [23, 24]. Palliative care is a specialized medical speciality that focuses on relieving symptoms and stress of serious illness with the goal of improving quality of life for patients and families [25]. Additionally, negative attitudes toward death can contribute to increased burnout among healthcare professionals [26, 27]. Medical and nursing students may not frequently encounter individuals receiving palliative and end-of-life care. This lack of exposure and experience can leave them inadequately prepared to handle difficult conversations during their clinical rotations. Consequently, they may be more susceptible to the negative impacts of death and dying [28, 29]. Students with poor perceptions of death and dying may not initiate end-of-life planning for patients, leading to inadequate or delayed end-of-life planning (e.g., advance care planning conversations). Furthermore, these students may struggle to offer clear and compassionate communication [30, 31]. Therefore, exploring medical and nursing students’ perceptions of death and dying is imperative in devising supportive educational strategies to improve their mental well-being.

To date, a considerable amount of palliative care research has focused on understanding death and dying from the perspectives of patients and caregivers [32–34]. In line with the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) on ensuring good health and well-being [35], it is crucial to enhance the capacities of medical and nursing students to provide care for patients with life-limiting conditions by first understanding their perceptions of death and dying.

Methods

A descriptive qualitative study [36] was conducted to explore the perception of death and dying among undergraduate medical and nursing students, acknowledging the unique cultural and contextual influences on each individual’s approach to these topics. This study was reported following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [37].

Participants and setting

Participants were recruited from a medical school situated in Singapore. To be eligible, participants had to be students above 18 years of age and pursuing a full-time undergraduate degree in medicine or nursing. To capture a wide range of multi-ethnic perceptions and experiences related to the death and dying, a maximum variation sampling approach (a form of purposive sampling) was used [38]. This approach took into consideration participants’ sociodemographic profiles (e.g., age, gender, ethnicity and religion) and academic characteristics (e.g., course of study and seniority) to ensure sample diversity. In both medical and nursing programs, medical and nursing students undergo a module on palliative care and as their advanced coursework and clinical experience would provide them with a deeper understanding and consideration of death and dying. Hence, it is important to consider the perspectives of all students regardless of their knowledge on palliative care. Recruitment continued until information redundancy was achieved at the 32nd participant [39]. Throughout data collection, the researchers met regularly to review the interview transcripts and emerging codes. Redundancy was considered to have been reached when several consecutive interviews produced no new codes or concepts and the existing themes were judged to be sufficiently rich and well developed.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted over two months, from August to September 2023. A research poster was disseminated via the university’s social media platforms (e.g., Instagram and Telegram). A total of 96 potential participants indicated their interest in an online Qualtrics form. Two researchers (DA, and MC) screened for participation eligibility and then invited the participants based on the purposive sampling approach. To ensure maximum variation, the following socio (e.g., age, gender, and ethnicity) and academic variables (e.g., degree program, and year of seniority) were used in the purposive selection process. Next, the researcher (MC) scheduled an online individual interview with each of the participants according to their availability. Participants provided informed consent online via Qualtrics before the interviews commenced.

By conducting individual interviews with a respectful and thoughtful approach towards death, the study acknowledged the cultural nuances and sensitivities surrounding the topic in Singapore, where death is frequently considered a taboo subject [2, 40]. All interviews were conducted once via the Zoom videoconferencing platform. The audio- and video-recorded interviews were conducted by two male research assistants who received formal training in qualitative research (MC, DO). Both interviewers recorded the fieldnotes after the interview completed. There were no other individuals present during the interviewers to ensure participants’ privacy.

Debriefing sessions were conducted after every interview to ensure consistency and to identify any biases that may have arisen during the interviewing process. The interviewers did not have any dependent relationships with the participants. An interview guide with semi-structured questions (Table 1) was developed based on a review of existing literature on death and dying [29, 41]. The interview guide was disseminated to all members of the research team and revised until a consensus on the questions was reached. Pilot interviews were conducted with two participants to ensure that the questions were comprehensible. In addition, the pilot interviews provided the interviewers with an opportunity to evaluate their skills in engaging with the interviewees. They also assessed whether the interview questions were effective in eliciting the rich, detailed data that was sought. As there were minimal changes to the final interview guide, the data from the pilot interviews were included in the analyses. The average duration of the interview was 46 min (Ranging from 41 to 63 min). Each participant received SGD20 (≈ USD 15) as an incentive for participation in the study.

Participant-selected photo-elicitation interviewing [42] is a qualitative method that integrates photographs or other visual images into interviews to facilitate participants’ expression and exploration of their experiences, and perspectives [43, 44]. Given the sensitive and potentially difficult-to-articulate nature of death and dying, the use of images may provide participants with an alternative means of expression and a concrete stimulus for reflection and discussion, complementing conventional verbal questioning. Furthermore, as visual forms of communication are increasingly integrated into how contemporary students communicate and express ideas [45], the use of images provided an additional and potentially familiar medium through which participants could convey and discuss their perceptions of death and dying.

Prior to the interview, each participant was invited to select one photograph or image that represented or supported their description of death and dying. Participants could provide either a self-taken photograph or an image sourced from the internet and were instructed not to select images containing offensive content. Given the sensitive and abstract nature of death and dying, including concepts that may be difficult to represent through personal photographs (e.g., heaven or the afterlife), several participants were unable to provide self-taken photographs. These participants were therefore guided to select copyright-free images (e.g., Unsplash, https://unsplash.com/) that reflected their perceptions of death and dying.

During the interview, participants shared their selected image through the Zoom chat function. The interviewer downloaded and displayed the image using screen sharing, after which participants were invited to describe its meaning and narrate the experiences, beliefs, or perceptions that it represented. Participants provided consent for the research team to retain copies of the selected images for data analysis and publication. All images were stored on a password-protected laptop and were accessible only to members of the research team. Any potentially identifying information within participant-generated photographs was redacted, and additional consent was obtained from participants before such images were considered for publication. To further protect participants’ anonymity, only copyright-free images were reproduced in the present publication.

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim and assigned pseudonyms (e.g., P1) to ensure participant anonymity. The transcripts were subsequently checked for verbatim accuracy by one researcher (MC) before data analysis. A six-step inductive thematic analysis approach [46] was undertaken independently by two researchers (DA and JW). First, the researchers familiarised themselves with the data through multiple readings of all interview transcripts and review of the accompanying photographs. The photographs were not analysed independently using a separate visual analytic framework. Rather, they were considered alongside participants’ verbal descriptions and interpretations of the images to provide contextual and interpretive insights into their perceptions of death and dying. Particular attention was given to the meanings that participants attributed to their selected images and how these meanings related to, reinforced, or extended their interview narratives.

Second, inductive open manual coding was performed manually on the first five transcripts to generate initial codes. Coding was primarily grounded in participants’ verbal accounts, including their descriptions and interpretations of the selected photographs. The two researchers developed their initial codes independently before comparing and consolidating their respective codebooks. The resulting codebook, comprising 68 codes, was reviewed and discussed by both researchers. Following consensus on the coding framework, the researchers proceeded to code the remaining interviews. Next, codes reflecting similar overarching meanings were compared for similarities and differences using the constant comparative method [47]. Throughout this process, the photographs and participants’ associated narratives were revisited to support interpretation and to examine how the visual representations corresponded with or added contextual depth to the developing patterns of meaning. The photographs therefore served as supplementary contextual data that informed interpretation and theme development rather than as independently coded visual data. A coding tree was developed to illustrate the relationships among the codes, subthemes, and themes (Fig. 1). When disagreements arose, a third researcher (MC) was involved in the deliberation and consensus process.

Coding tree

The developed themes and subthemes, together with selected photographs illustrating participants’ perceptions, were subsequently presented to a group of participants who were willing to provide feedback as part of member checking. This process was undertaken to assess whether the interpretations and thematic findings adequately reflected participants’ perceptions of death and dying [48]. Finally, the themes and subthemes were presented narratively, with selected photographs used to complement and illustrate the participants’ accounts.

Rigor

This study ensured rigor by focusing on credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [49]. Conducted by interviewers who did not have dependent relationships with the participants (e.g., authority figures between students and teacher), this study ensured that students were not subjected to any undue influence, minimizing the likelihood of socially desirable responses. In addition, the use of pilot interviews, member checking, and debriefing with interviewers were strategies used to enhance credibility [50]. Furthermore, the inclusion of two independent data analysts demonstrated credibility and dependability. Next, the maintenance of an audit trail documenting the entire study process from data collection to data analysis ensured confirmability. Finally, in-depth descriptions of the contexts in which the study was conducted was provided to demonstrate transferability.

Ethical considerations

Ethics approval was obtained on 1st August 2023 from the university’s institutional review board (NUS-IRB-2023-256). The study conformed to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [51]. All participants provided documented informed consent and were told that their participation was voluntary. The participants also provided documented consent for the publication of their photos. The interview transcripts were coded using pseudonyms and no identifiable information was linked to the research data.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 32 undergraduate medical and nursing students participated in this study. There were no dropouts. The mean age of the participants was 22.3 years old (SD: 2.64; Range: 19 to 33 years). The majority of the participants were Chinese (n = 25; 78%) and were individuals who did not conform to any religious beliefs (n = 10; 31%), and had a prior exposure to the death of another person (n = 24; 75%). The students’ socio-demographic information is detailed in Table 2. A total of three themes were elucidated from participants’ narratives and photos, namely (1) interpretations of death and dying, (2) navigating through cultural and spiritual beliefs, and (3) encountering deaths in everyday life.

Theme 1: Interpretations of death and dying

Based on prior exposures, the participants had varying interpretations, resulting in some form of ambivalence towards death and dying. To begin, the participants viewed death and dying as a “celebration” of life’s milestones. Despite acknowledging the celebratory nature of death, the participants also verbalized fears associated with the afterlife.

Subtheme 1.1: Celebrating the end of a journey

The participants viewed death positively and analogized their lives as a journey. Through this outlook, the students view death as a celebratory affair, as it is deemed as the completion of their milestones in life.

There should be something happy celebrated about, cause if you see it [referring to death and dying] as like, perhaps the end of life … a marathon is celebrated (P30, Male, Chinese, Freethinker, Medicine, Year 3)

When asked about the reason for this positivity toward death, the participants likened death and dying to an inevitable and natural occurrence. Drawing on a river as a metaphor (Supplementary Fig. 1), a medical student explained that by going along with the flow, one can change their outlook towards death and achieve acceptance of death.

It [referring to death] is something you can’t avoid … it’s something that is just like a natural occurrence in a way, like the river… I remember this saying, you can’t beat the river into submission … you have to change your outlook and go with the flow of it. I think that’s how you ride out death and dying (P29, Male, Chinese, Christian, Medicine, Year 3)

Subtheme 1.2: Experiencing uncertainties about the afterlife

Through the participants’ narratives, several uncertainties about the afterlife surfaced. Primarily, the uncertainties relate to the fears of the unknown after death.

I do feel there is a certain amount of fear and, but it’s result from uncertainty (P27, Male, Malay, Muslim, Nursing, Year 2)

Primarily, the uncertainties stem from the participants’ inability to foresee what happens in the afterlife. A student likened the path towards the afterlife to one walking through a vortex (Supplementary Fig. 2), and the inability to see what lies ahead was described as one form of uncertainty about the afterlife.

No one knows what happened after you passed on. So, I guess, you can’t see what’s in front but seeing how this man is walking alone himself [photo of man walking alone into a vortex]. You come to this world alone by yourself so when you pass on you will also go by yourself. … I believe that it is the beginning of something though we are not sure what is it about (P2, Female, Chinese, Buddhist, Nursing, Year 3)

Theme 2: Navigating through cultural and spiritual beliefs

This theme presents the various conditions that influence students’ perceptions of death and dying. From a macro perspective, as a result of cultural and societal norms, students professed death and dying as a taboo topic that is often avoided in everyday life. Furthermore, students also alluded to the importance of spirituality and their past encounters with death and dying in playing a role in influencing their perceptions of death and dying.

Two subthemes detail the influences on participants’ perceptions towards death and dying: (1) Overcoming cultural norms and taboos, and (2) connecting with a higher power.

Subtheme 2.1 Overcoming cultural norms and taboos

Within a multicultural Asian context, participants verbalized that topics about death and dying continue to be considered taboo and, to some degree, a form of disrespect when conversing with the older generation.

You don’t even talk to the old people about whether if they die, like the possibility, because they will think of it as a taboo or disrespect, especially with the elder generation (P1, Male, Malay, Muslim, Nursing, Year 2)

However, this was not the same for all individuals. For one participant, his recollection of his deceased grandfather’s openness towards discussing death was the facilitator of his advance care planning.

He [referring to his grandfather] has been talking about death a lot…recently he has gotten advance care planning done (P5, Male, Chinese, Christian, Nursing, Year 2)

Subtheme 2.2: Connecting with a higher power

Beyond cultural norms and taboos, participants with strong religious faiths found that connections with their spirituality shaped their perceptions of death and dying. Some participants, steadfast in their faith, viewed death with a fatalistic mindset, professing death and dying as being pre-determined.

It’s just the fate that He [referring to God] has written out for you and you get to spend time with Him in heaven after you have completed your time here on Earth (P25, Female, Eurasian, Christian, Nursing, Year 1)

Among students with religious faiths, they found comfort in knowing that there is a place (i.e., heaven) to go to in the afterlife. This strong faith and belief was essential in reducing one’s fear and uncertainty about death and dying. a medical student described:

If you know where you’re going after you die, then there’s a lot of comfort in that. And then it takes away the uncertainty and the fear (P24, Male, Chinese, Christian, Medicine, Year 3)

Theme 3: Encountering death in everyday life

This final theme depicts how students’ encounters with death and dying shaped their perceptions. Through the narratives, students associated death with a wide spectrum of emotions. These emotions were largely dependent on the proximity and strength of the relationships and the contexts in which death occurred. Beyond the death of significant people in their lives, time spent during the students’ clinical placements has led to several patient death encounters. This theme is further extrapolated into three subthemes: (1) Confronting the death of a significant other, (2) encountering the death of a patient, and (3) the dying context.

Subtheme 3.1 Confronting the death of a significant other

This subtheme describes participants’ responses to the death of a family member. It is noteworthy that the proximity and strength of the relationship influences one’s perception of death and dying. For instance, among participants who shared strong bonds with their grandparents, there were demonstrations of sadness and shock.

So initial emotions were a bit shocked and at a loss but because I had some time to process it by the time I went there I was like calm and still quite sad but there’s nothing I could do about it but yeah, I felt quite sad that I couldn’t be there during her [referring to grandmother] last moments (P10, Male, Chinese, Christian, Medicine, Year 1)

However, the magnitude of emotions was expressed differently when the death relates to a distant relative.

It’s like a distant relative… my cousin’s brother’s dad I didn’t have any negative feelings or feel sad for a long period… didn’t feel an impact on my life or anything (P6, Female, Chinese, Buddhist, Nursing, Year 4)

Subtheme 3.2 Facing the death of a patient

As aspiring doctors and nurses, many participants perceived death negatively when faced with the death of a patient. Broadly, descriptors such as failure, guilt, helplessness, sadness, and shock were used when patients under their care passed on. Among the students who expressed grieving emotions, it was younger patients with whom they developed better rapport.

You just feel very helpless as a healthcare professional. in the healthcare team, you feel very helpless, useless, … sense of guilt and failure (P29, Male, Chinese, Christian, Medicine, Year 3)

I think in this case because the patient was a bit younger and because we got to know her and there was some rapport established, we got to know her personality as well. I mean first, there was the shock and then there was a bit of sadness also to lose this patient… it was mostly shock and a bit of sadness (P9, Female, Chinese, Freethinker, Medicine, Year 5)

Participants coped by thinking that their patient has gone to a better place. One student provided an image illustrating a patient reaching out to an angel (Supplementary Fig. 3), symbolizing how patients find peace and move on to the afterlife.

He [referring to his patient] is trying to hold an angel’s hand, like telling the angel that, ‘Hey, take me away to a better place’ … He’s not suffering, he’s not sad, but he’s more like happy to go to the place. He accepts that the phase, the fact that he’s going to heaven (P16, Male, Chinese, Buddhist, Nursing, Year 1)

Subtheme 3.3: The dying context

The contexts in which death can take place also affected the participants’ perception of death and dying. For instance, when death occurs due to sudden unforeseen events or accidents, participants may experience a surge of negative emotions ranging from shock to numbness.

Because it was an accident, so it was sudden. So, I think I feel like throughout, even from receiving the message about it, from going to the wake, it was just, it felt very, almost numbing and it was a very detached experience because of how … yeah because of how shocking it was (P9, Female, Chinese, Freethinker, Medicine, Year 5)

Participants commonly associated (and perhaps even expected) death with older adults. Consequently, students may not experience strong emotional responses when they encounter death among the older population as it is seen as a normal occurrence and a sign of their longevity.

Whenever I think about death, I associate it with old people and old couples (P15. Female. Chinese, Buddhist, Medicine, Year 2)

My parents also say ‘70s, 80s is a very old age, your grandparents have lived very long’. Yeah. So, to me, it didn’t feel like it was super tragic or bad that they were passing away, at least not of a personal capacity. (P11, Female, Chinese, Freethinker, Medicine, Year 4)

Discussion

This study explored the perceptions of death and dying through the narratives of medical and nursing students. Using a combination of open-ended questions and photo-elicitation interviews, the findings illustrate that the participants viewed death and dying ambivalently, with some perceiving it as a celebration of a journey, while others experiencing uncertainties towards the afterlife. The participants’ perceptions were potentially shaped by multidimensional factors such as cultural norms, taboos, spiritual beliefs, context and the proximity of the relationship.

Firstly, with regards to participants’ interpretations of death and dying, this study found that students viewed death in a positive light, likening it to a celebration of one’s life journey, a finding echoed in several studies [29, 52, 53]. This positivity is an interesting finding as young adults often have some form of aging anxiety and a fear of death [54, 55]. Nonetheless, it is plausible that this positivity could be attributed to the perception of the inevitable nature of death, which was described by the participants as a natural occurrence [29].

On the other hand, several uncertainties about the afterlife also surfaced amongst participants. Corresponding to the wider literature on death research [56, 57], the findings of this study unveiled several distinct fears of death, such as fear of uncertainty of the afterlife. It is noteworthy that senior medical and nursing students (third year students and above) in this study undergo formal palliative care training within their respective curriculums. However, there are inconsistent findings of the impacts of these formal palliative care trainings in alleviating participants’ fear of death [58, 59].

Secondly, this study found several factors that influenced the students’ meanings and perceptions of death and dying. Notably, the students have alluded to the presence of cultural taboos surrounding the discussions of death and dying as one factor that influenced their perception. This was corroborated in earlier studies [1, 2]. As death is considered a taboo topic, this may have inevitably led to students developing some form of negative perceptions towards it (i.e., fears and uncertainties). The results of this study show that junior participants (first and second year students) were observed to have interpreted death and dying using descriptors such as uncertainty and fear. From an educational system perspective, this is expected as junior students have relatively fewer clinical postings, which may in turn reduce their exposures to death and dying. In addition, these students have not been formally introduced to palliative care training. Nevertheless, it may be premature to conclude that seniority and exposure to death and dying can have direct implications on their perceptions.

From a macro cultural perspective, the existence of taboos relating to death and dying, have further limited the participants’ opportunities for any discussions with the elderly population in the community (e.g., at home with family members). Specifically, taboos surrounding death and dying among the Asian population have often prevented healthcare professionals from engaging in advance care planning due to superstitions of ill-foreboding effects [2, 18]. For this reason, it may have resulted in limited opportunities for the students to discuss death and dying, which may have consequently led to their perception of uncertainties towards death.

The participants of this study also alluded to the presence of spiritual factors such as religion as an influence on death and dying. Several studies have examined the role of religion in influencing one’s perceptions of death [60–62]. Although some studies have affirmed that religious individuals are more likely to have lesser death anxiety [61, 62], this finding remains relatively inconsistent [60]. From a theoretical perspective, the terror management theory [63] posits that individuals experience lesser death anxiety as they draw strength from their religion through beliefs in the afterlife [60]. On the other hand, it has been proposed that social support acquired through memberships in religious congregations may offer an additional buffer against death anxieties [64].

Thirdly, the findings of this study also highlighted how students’ perceptions were shaped by the proximity and strength of the relationship with the deceased and the contexts in which death has taken place. These findings are not unexpected and were similarly reported in numerous studies [65–67]. One factor unique to healthcare students is patient contact, with students using descriptors such as guilt and failure when they witnessed the death of patients under their care. The negative emotions associated with patients’ death are common [28, 68]. The most salient note throughout the narratives was the students’ experience of dealing with unexpected deaths or the death of younger patients. Specifically, there was more emotional distress when dealing with the death of a younger patient [69, 70]. This could be due to the students’ association of death with older patients [70]. This is an important finding for educators as these young students, with presumably lesser experience, and coping abilities, may experience greater distress when they encounter the death of the patients under their charge [28, 68].

Implications for practice and research

The findings of this study can make meaningful contributions to the review of healthcare education. Considering that the participants perceive death with an ambivalent outlook fraught by uncertainties, it may be necessary to broaden the extent of the existing palliative care education framework by introducing conversations about death and dying within the medical and nursing curriculums. By hosting death conversations in the form of death café conducted within a classroom setting for modules related to communication, geriatric and palliative care, educators can introduce these informal dialogues, which can serve as potential entry points for students to engage discussions about death and dying. These conversations (e.g., death cafés) can provide unique opportunities for students to engage in dialogues around mortality within a safe environment [71]. Specifically, discussions about handling special contexts of death (e.g., the death of a young patient or family member) may be useful in helping participants acquire greater literacy in death and grief [72]. This may ultimately translate to enhanced coping ability with death and dying [73].

On the other hand, students may experience difficult emotions during their clinical rotations, especially in postings such as emergency, trauma, or paediatrics where students may be at higher risks of exposure to emotionally challenging deaths (i.e., death of young patients, or sudden deaths). Healthcare and educational institutions should deliberate on the provision and evaluation of resilience and practice readiness programs to enhance students’ fortitude when faced with challenging situations. In addition, regular pastoral care and supervision by clinical preceptors and supervisors could be considered for those at risk of emotional distress. More work using randomized controlled trials will be necessary to investigate the impact of the introduction of informal death conversations (e.g., death café) and resilience programs on students’ abilities to cope with as well as discuss death and dying.

Limitations

The findings of this study have to be examined in light of several limitations. Firstly, this study was conducted in one university and its results may not be representative of students from other universities within or beyond Singapore. Nevertheless, an exhaustive purposive sampling approach was adopted to recruit participants of different sociodemographic (e.g., gender and ethnicity) and academic backgrounds (i.e., course and seniority). Secondly, due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, it was not possible to explore any of the students’ changing perceptions towards death and dying as they progress in their personal and professional journeys. It may be worthy to use a longitudinal approach to detect any plausible changes in students’ perceptions towards death and dying across their clinical journeys.

Conclusions

This study explored the perspectives of medical and nursing students towards death and dying through a multi-ethnic viewpoint. The students perceived death and dying in both positive (i.e., a celebration of their life journey) and negative lights (i.e., fearing the uncertainties of the afterlife). Their perceptions were shaped by various factors, such as cultural norms and spirituality. Students’ responses to death and dying were influenced by the contexts and the strength of their relationship towards the deceased. For these reasons, it may be worthwhile for hospitals and schools to consider the addition of informal death conversations (e.g., death café) to the formal palliative care curriculum to promote students’ exposure and comfort towards end of life conversations. Further research using a longitudinal design across different institutions of higher learning will help in providing an enhanced overview of medical and nursing students’ perceptions of death and dying.
