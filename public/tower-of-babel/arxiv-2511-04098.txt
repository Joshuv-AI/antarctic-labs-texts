Spectrum for a non-unitary one-dimensional two-state quantum walk with one defect
Takako Endo, Yohei Matsumoto, Hiromichi Ohno, Akito Suzuki

Abstract
Existence of the eigenvalues of the discrete-time quantum walks is deeply related to localization. Also, for the study of open quantum systems, non-Hermitian systems have attracted much attention. As mathematical models for such systems, non-unitary quantum walks with the chiral symmetry are essential for the study of the topological insulator. In this paper, we give the whole picture of the eigenvalues of a non-unitary one-dimensional two-state quantum walks with one defect and the chiral symmetry.

Introduction

In recent years, the quantum walk , which is regarded as the quantum mechanical analogue of the classical random walk, has attracted considerable attention
[
1
,
2
,
9
,
10
,
23
,
26
]
.

Quantum walks, which exploit quantum superposition and interference effects, exhibit propagation speeds surpassing those of classical diffusion and have been extensively studied from both algorithmic and physical perspectives
[
19
]
.
They have attained universality as a general computational model, demonstrating that any quantum circuit can be efficiently simulated on a suitably constructed graph
[
5
,
6
,
18
]
.
Building upon this universality, research has advanced toward designing new quantum algorithms that leverage the structural properties of quantum walks
[
3
,
25
]
.
At the same time, quantum walks have been applied to the modeling of physical systems, enabling the simulation of topological phase transitions and interface states, thereby contributing to quantum simulations of topological materials
[
11
,
22
]
. Furthermore, quantum walks form a unified framework for quantum simulation itself, establishing a direct connection between discrete-time dynamics and continuous-space quantum evolution
[
28
]
.
Moreover, non-unitary quantum walks have been employed for simulating natural phenomena, providing a quantum-mechanical explanation for the high efficiency of excitonic energy transfer in photosynthetic complexes
[
21
]
.
Thus, quantum walks continue to evolve as a central concept in quantum information science, encompassing computational theory, algorithmic design, and the simulation of natural processes.

One important application of (discrete-time) quantum walk is Grover’s search algorithm
[
7
]
. Shenvi
et al.

[
24
]
demonstrated that one of the models of quantum walk is equivalent to Grover’s search algorithm. Moreover, quantum walks have the following two properties, they are considered useful for generalizing Grover’s search algorithm. One is that the spread speed of quantum walk is fast. Indeed, while the width of the probability distribution in a classical random walk diverges as
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{t})
, the width of quantum walks diverges linearly with time
t

[
14
]
. The other is localization. Localization is defined as the phenomenon in which the probability of finding the walker at a certain position does not converge to zero even in the long-time limit.
It is known that localization occurs when the time-evolution operator of the quantum walk possesses eigenvalues
[
8
]
.

Many studies have investigated both the enhancement of search speed and the emergence of localization.
In the following, we briefly review previous works related to localization and the eigenvalue problem. Konno
[
17
]
studied a quantum walk model with a perturbation at the origin, referred to as a one defect unitary quantum walk, and proved the occurrence of localization using a path-counting method. Wójcik
et al.

[
27
]
introduced a one defect quantum walk model:

\displaystyle C(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{bmatrix}&(x=\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots),\\[10.0pt]
\dfrac{\omega}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{bmatrix}&(x=0),\end{cases}

where
\omega=e^{2i\pi\phi}
and
\phi\in(0,1)
.
This model describes a globally unitary system with a local perturbation
\omega
at the origin. Their analytical results showed that localization can arise even from such a minimal phase defect.
They also demonstrated that the existence of localization depends strongly on the initial coin state.
Following this, Endo
et al.

[
16
]
used the generating function method, and derived the candidate eigenvalues of this system. Furthermore, Endo
et al.

[
15
]
generalized this result to a two-phase quantum walk with one defect and obtained candidate eigenvalues.
Later, Kiumi
et al.

[
13
]
extended this model to a more general class of two-phase quantum walks with one defect and derived a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of eigenvalues using the transfer matrix method.
They also obtained explicit expressions for eigenvalues and eigenvectors in special cases.
This transfer matrix approach provides a powerful framework for including eigenvalues that could not be accessed by the generating function method.

Recent studies have also investigated non-unitary quantum walks, whose time-evolution operators are no longer unitary.
For instance, Mochizuki
et al.

[
20
]
introduced a model of non-unitary quantum walk and analyzed the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry of this non-unitary quantum walk. Furthermore, Asahara et al.
[
4
]
derived the Witten index and essential spectrum for this model. Additionally, Kiumi et al.
[
12
]
derived the eigenvalues of the two-phase quantum walk, a special case of this model, using the transfer matrix method.

Motivated by these developments, the present work aims to solve completely the eigenvalue problem of a non-unitary quantum walk model that possesses a one defect and chiral symmetry, using the transfer matrix method.
This model can be regarded as a non-unitary extension of the unitary model studied in
[
16
,
27
]
.
In the unitary case, the spectrum lies on the unit circle; however, due to the non-unitarity in our model, the spectrum extends beyond the unit circle, exhibiting a spiderweb-like structure.

The structure of this paper is as follows:
Section 2 introduces the definition of our quantum walk model.
Section 3 presents the main results.
Section 4 defines the transfer matrix and provides the lemmas and proofs needed to establish the main theorem.

2
Definition of our non-unitary model

A quantum walk considered in this paper is a one-dimensional two-state quantum walk with one defect.
Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{2})=\Biggl\{\Psi:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}\penalty\ \Bigg|\penalty\ \sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\Arrowvert\Psi(x)\Arrowvert^{2}_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}<\infty\ \Biggr\}

be the Hilbert space of states.
\mathcal{H}
is naturally identified with
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\oplus\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\bigoplus_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}^{2}
. For a vector
\Psi\in\mathcal{H}
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote

\displaystyle\Psi(x)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Psi_{L}(x)\\
\Psi_{R}(x)\\
\end{array}\right]\ \in\mathbb{C}^{2}.

The time-evolution operator
U
of a one-dimensional two-state quantum walk is defined as the product
U=SC
of two operators
S
and
C
on
\mathcal{H}
. Here,
S
is a shift operator defined by

\displaystyle S=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}L&0\\
0&L^{*}\\
\end{array}\right]

on
\mathcal{H}
identified with
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\oplus\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, where
L
is the shift operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
defined by
(Lf)(x)=f(x+1)

(x\in\mathbb{Z})
for
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. The operator
C
is a coin operator defined by

\displaystyle C=\bigoplus_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}C(x)

on
\mathcal{H}
identified with
\bigoplus_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}^{2}
, where
\{C(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a family of two by two matrices. In this paper, we take
C(x)
as

\displaystyle C(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}&(x=\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots),\\
\\
\dfrac{\omega}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}&(x=0)\\
\end{array}\right.

with a parameter
\omega\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
, and we denote the time-evolution operator
U
as

\displaystyle U=U_{\omega}\quad(\omega\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\})

to emphasize the dependence of the parameter
\omega
. When
\omega=1
, we write
C=C_{1}
. Since

\displaystyle C_{1}(x)=\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\end{array}

for all
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
the time-evolution operator
U_{1}
is homogeneous. When
\omega\neq 1
, the coin operator

\displaystyle C(x)=\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\end{array}+(\omega-1)\delta_{0}(x)\begin{array}[]{ll}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\end{array}

has a one defect only at the origin, where
\delta_{0}(0)=1
and
\delta_{0}(x)=0\penalty\ (x\neq 0)
. Here,
|\omega-1|
represents the strength of perturbation from the homogeneous time-evolution operator
U_{1}
. Moreover, when
\omega\neq\pm 1
,
C(0)
is not unitary and
U_{\omega}
becomes a non-unitary time-evolution operator.
Thus, our evolution
U_{\omega}
defines a non-unitary one defect quantum-walk model.

3
Main results

Let

\displaystyle R_{\pm}(\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{\pm\omega(\omega-1)^{2}+\sqrt{\omega^{2}((\omega-1)^{4}+(\omega^{2}-\omega+1)^{2})}}{4(\omega-\frac{1}{2})^{2}+1}}

(16)

be functions defined for
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
. In the following, for a complex number
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
expressed in the polar form as
z=re^{i\theta}
with
-\pi<\theta\leq\pi
, the square root
\sqrt{z}
is defined by

\displaystyle\sqrt{z}=z^{\frac{1}{2}}=r^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{i\theta}{2}}.

Here, since
\omega^{2}((\omega-1)^{4}+(\omega^{2}-\omega+1)^{2})\geq 0
and
\pm\omega(\omega-1)^{2}+\sqrt{\omega^{2}((\omega-1)^{4}+(\omega^{2}-\omega+1)^{2})}>0
, it follows that
R_{\pm}(\omega)>0
.

The following is the main result of this paper.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
\omega\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0,1\}
.

(i)

U_{\omega}
has four eigenvalues which are expressed as

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=R_{-}(\omega)+iR_{+}(\omega),\quad\lambda_{2}=-\bar{\lambda_{1}}=-R_{-}(\omega)+iR_{+}(\omega),

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}=-\lambda_{1}=-R_{-}(\omega)-iR_{+}(\omega),\quad\lambda_{4}=\bar{\lambda_{1}}=R_{-}(\omega)-iR_{+}(\omega).

(ii)

When
\lambda=R_{-}(\omega)\pm i\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)R_{+}(\omega)
, the corresponding eigenvector
\Psi(x)
is

\displaystyle\Psi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\begin{bmatrix}\mp iz_{-}^{x}\Bigl(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)\\
\pm iz_{-}^{x-1}\end{bmatrix}&(x\geq 1),\\
\\
\begin{bmatrix}\mp i\Bigl(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)\\
\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix}&(x=0),\\
\\
\begin{bmatrix}z_{+}^{-|x+1|}\\
z_{+}^{-|x|}\Bigl(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)\end{bmatrix}&(x\leq-1)\end{array}\right.

up to a scalar multiple, where
z_{+}=\frac{\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}
and
z_{-}=\frac{\lambda+\lambda^{-1}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}
.
When
\lambda=-R_{-}(\omega)\pm i\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)R_{+}(\omega)
, the corresponding eigenvector
\Psi(x)
is

\displaystyle\Psi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\begin{bmatrix}\mp iz_{+}^{x}\Bigl(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)\\
\pm iz_{+}^{x-1}\end{bmatrix}&(x\geq 1),\\
\\
\begin{bmatrix}\mp i\Bigl(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)\\
\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix}&(x=0)\\
\\
\begin{bmatrix}z_{-}^{-|x+1|}\\
z_{-}^{-|x|}\Bigl(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)\end{bmatrix}&(x\leq-1)\end{array}\right.

up to a scalar multiple.
In particular,
{\rm dimker}(U_{\omega}-\lambda_{j})=1\quad(j=1,2,3,4).

4
Proof of Main results

In this section, we introduce the transfer matrix for our
U_{\omega}
, the main tool of our study. We note that
0
is not an eigenvalue of
U_{\omega}
, since the inverse
U_{\omega}^{-1}=C^{-1}S^{-1}
exists and is bounded for any
\omega\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Put
T_{\lambda\infty}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{2}\lambda&1\\
1&\sqrt{2}\lambda^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\in SL(2,\mathbb{C})=\{M\in M(2,\mathbb{C})|\det M=1\}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and define the transfer matrix of
U_{\omega}
as

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}T_{\lambda\infty}&(x=\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots),\\
\\
\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{2}\lambda}{\omega}&1\\
1&\frac{\sqrt{2}\omega}{\lambda}\end{bmatrix}&(x=0).\end{array}\right.

Then
x\mapsto T_{\lambda}(x)
is the map from
\mathbb{Z}
to
SL(2,\mathbb{C})
.

To solve the eigenvalue problem
U_{\omega}\Psi=\lambda\Psi
, we transform the vector
\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{L}(x)\\
\Psi_{R}(x)\end{bmatrix}

into
\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{L}(x-1)\\
\Psi_{R}(x)\end{bmatrix}

by the unitary operator

J=L^{*}\oplus I_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

on
\>\mathcal{H}\simeq\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\oplus\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})

and rewrite the eigenvalue problem in term of the transfer matrix.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
\Psi\in\mathcal{H}
. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

\Psi\in{\rm ker}(U_{\omega}-\lambda)
.

(ii)

\Psi
satisfies

\displaystyle(L\oplus L)J\Psi=T_{\lambda}J\Psi,

(33)

where
T_{\lambda}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
is defined as
(T_{\lambda}\Phi)(x)=T_{\lambda}(x)\Phi(x)
for
\Phi\in\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

We first prove that (
\mathrm{i})
implies (
\mathrm{ii}
). Let
(U_{\omega}-\lambda)\Psi=0
, then

\displaystyle\lambda\Psi_{L}(x-1)=\frac{\omega_{x}}{\sqrt{2}}(\Psi_{L}(x)-\Psi_{R}(x)),

(34)

\displaystyle\lambda\Psi_{R}(x+1)=\frac{\omega_{x}}{\sqrt{2}}(\Psi_{L}(x)+\Psi_{R}(x)),

(35)

for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
\omega_{x}=\omega
when
x=0
, and
\omega_{x}=1
when
x\neq 0
. Subtracting Eq.(
34
) from Eq.(
35
), we have the equation

\displaystyle\Psi_{R}(x+1)=\Psi_{L}(x-1)+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\lambda}\omega_{x}\Psi_{R}(x).

(36)

Hence, from Eq.(
34
) and Eq.(
36
), we obtain

\displaystyle((L\oplus L)J\Psi)(x)=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{L}(x)\\
\Psi_{R}(x+1)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{2}\lambda}{\omega_{x}}\Psi_{L}(x-1)+\Psi_{R}(x)\\
\Psi_{L}(x-1)+\frac{\sqrt{2}\omega_{x}}{\lambda}\Psi_{R}(x)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{2}\lambda}{\omega_{x}}&1\\
1&\frac{\sqrt{2}\omega_{x}}{\lambda}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{L}(x-1)\\
\Psi_{R}(x)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T_{\lambda}J\Psi)(x)

which leads to (
\mathrm{ii})
.

Next, we prove that (ii) implies (i). Assume
(L\oplus L)J\Psi=T_{\lambda}J\Psi
, then the equation

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{L}(x)\\
\Psi_{R}(x+1)\end{bmatrix}=((L\oplus L)J\Psi)(x)=(T_{\lambda}J\Psi)(x)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{2}\lambda}{\omega_{x}}\Psi_{L}(x-1)+\Psi_{R}(x)\\
\Psi_{L}(x-1)+\frac{\sqrt{2}\omega_{x}}{\lambda}\Psi_{R}(x)\end{bmatrix}

holds. This equation leads to the Eq.(
34
) and Eq.(
35
).
Consequently, we have
\Psi\in\ker(U_{\omega}-\lambda)
.
∎

Here Eq.(
33
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle(J\Psi)(x+1)=T_{\lambda}(x)(J\Psi)(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{Z}.

Hence, for
\Psi\in\ker(U_{\omega}-\lambda)
, multiplying
T_{\lambda}
to
J\Psi
is equivalent to applying the left shift to
J\Psi
. This is why we call
T_{\lambda}
the transfer matrix. On the other hand, by Lemma 4.1 and the iteration of the above equation,
\Psi\in{\rm ker}(U_{\omega}-\lambda)
holds if and only if
J\Psi
satisfies

\displaystyle(J\Psi)(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}T_{\lambda\infty}^{x-1}T_{\lambda}(0)(J\Psi)(0)&(x\geq 1),\\
\\
T_{\lambda\infty}^{x}(J\Psi)(0)&(x\leq-1).\end{array}\right.

To calculate
T_{\lambda\infty}^{x}
, we need to show the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
T_{\lambda\infty}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

(i)

The eigenvalues of
T_{\lambda\infty}
are

\displaystyle z_{+}=\frac{\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}},\quad z_{-}=\frac{\lambda+\lambda^{-1}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}.

(41)

Here,
z_{+}
and
z_{-}
can be possibly the same.

(ii)

The eigenvector of
T_{\lambda\infty}
associated with
z_{\pm}
is a constant multiple of

\displaystyle\chi_{\pm}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}\pm\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

When
z_{+}
and
z_{-}
are the same,
\chi_{+}
and
\chi_{-}
are also the same, and the dimension of the eigenspace is one.

Proof.

Solving the quadratic equation

\det(\mu-T_{\lambda\infty})=\mu^{2}-\big(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\lambda}+\sqrt{2}\lambda\big)\mu+1=0
, we obtain (
\mathrm{i}
). One can check (
\mathrm{ii}
) by direct calculation.
∎

Remark 1
.

Noting
\det T_{\lambda\infty}=1
, we observe

z_{+}=\frac{1}{z_{-}}.

(42)

To consider the convergence of
\|J\Psi\|
, it is important to know the value
|z_{+}|
. To do this, we define the following sets:

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{\lambda=e^{i\theta}\colon\theta\in\Bigl[\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{3\pi}{4}\Bigr]\cup\Bigl[\frac{5\pi}{4},\frac{7\pi}{4}\Bigr]\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}

\displaystyle=\{e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}},e^{i\frac{3\pi}{4}},e^{i\frac{5\pi}{4}},e^{i\frac{7\pi}{4}}\},

\displaystyle\Xi_{+}

\displaystyle=(\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon\Re z>0\}\cup\{it\colon t\in(-1,0)\cup(1,\infty)\})\backslash\Sigma,

\displaystyle\Xi_{-}

\displaystyle=(\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon\Re z<0\}\cup\{it\colon t\in(-\infty,-1)\cup(0,1)\})\backslash\Sigma.

Then, we have the next lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

(i)

z_{+}=z_{-}
holds if and only if
\lambda\in\Sigma_{0}
.

(ii)

|z_{+}|=1
holds if and only if
\lambda\in\Sigma
.

(iii)

|z_{+}|>1
holds if and only if
\lambda\in\Xi_{+}
, while
|z_{+}|<1
holds if and only if
\lambda\in\Xi_{-}
.

We postpone the proof of Lemma
4.3
to Appendix.

Lemma 4.4
.

(i)

When
\lambda\in\Xi_{+}
, a non-zero solution
\Psi
of Eq.(
33
) in Lemma
4.1
exists in
\mathcal{H}
if and only if
T_{\lambda}(0)\chi_{+}
and
\chi_{-}
are linearly dependent. In this case, the solution
\Psi
is unique up to a constant factor.

(ii)

When
\lambda\in\Xi_{-}
, a non-zero solution
\Psi
of Eq.(
33
) in Lemma
4.1
exists in
\mathcal{H}
if and only if
T_{\lambda}(0)\chi_{-}
and
\chi_{+}
are linearly dependent. In this case, the solution
\Psi
is unique up to a constant factor.

(iii)

When
\lambda\in\Sigma
,
{\rm ker}(U_{\omega}-\lambda)=\{0\}
.

Proof.

First, we assume
\lambda\notin\Sigma_{0}
so that
z_{+}\neq z_{-}
. Let
\Psi
be a solution of Eq.(
33
) or equivalently
\Psi\in\ker(U_{\omega}-\lambda)
. Since
\{\chi_{+},\chi_{-}\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, there exist scalars
a_{\pm},b_{\pm}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}(0)(J\Psi)(0)=a_{+}\chi_{+}+a_{-}\chi_{-},\quad(J\Psi)(0)=b_{+}\chi_{+}+b_{-}\chi_{-}.

(43)

We have from Eq.(
4
)

\displaystyle J\Psi(x)

\displaystyle=T_{\lambda\infty}^{x-1}T_{\lambda}(0)(J\Psi)(0)

\displaystyle=z_{+}^{x-1}a_{+}\chi_{+}+z_{-}^{x-1}a_{-}\chi_{-}\quad(x\geq 1)

and

\displaystyle J\Psi(x)

\displaystyle=T_{\lambda\infty}^{x}(J\Psi)(0)

\displaystyle=z_{+}^{x}b_{+}\chi_{+}+z_{-}^{x}b_{-}\chi_{-}\quad(x\leq 0).

Hence, the norm of
\Psi
is calculated by

\displaystyle\|\Psi\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|J\Psi\|^{2}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\|J\Psi(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\geq 1}\|z_{+}^{x-1}a_{+}\chi_{+}+z_{-}^{x-1}a_{-}\chi_{-}\|^{2}+\sum_{x\leq 0}\|z_{+}^{x}b_{+}\chi_{+}+z_{-}^{x}b_{-}\chi_{-}\|^{2}.

(i) When
\lambda\in\Xi_{+}
,
|z_{+}|>1
and
|z_{-}|<1
by Lemma
4.3
and Eq. (
42
). Therefore, the necessary condition for
\|\Psi\|
to converge is
a_{+}=0
and
b_{-}=0
. This is because, if
a_{+}\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle\|z_{+}^{x-1}a_{+}\chi_{+}+z_{-}^{x-1}a_{-}\chi_{-}\|\geq|z_{+}|^{x-1}|a_{+}|\|\chi_{+}\|-|z_{-}|^{x-1}|a_{-}|\|\chi_{-}\|

which implies that the expression diverges as
x\to\infty
. Since the norm does not tend to zero, the corresponding series cannot be convergent. An analogous conclusion holds for
b_{-}
based on the second sum. This means, from Eq.(
43
),

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}(0)b_{+}\chi_{+}=a_{-}\chi_{-}.

Hence, if Eq.(
33
) has a non-zero solution,
T_{\lambda}(0)\chi_{+}
and
\chi_{-}
are linearly dependent. Furthermore, the solution
\Psi
of Eq.(
33
) is unique up to a constant factor. Conversely if
T_{\lambda}(0)\chi_{+}
and
\chi_{-}
are linearly dependent, the vector
\Phi
defined by
J\Phi(0)=\chi_{+}
and Eq.(
4
) is a non-zero solution of Eq.(
33
).

(ii) It can be shown by performing calculations similar to those in (i).

(iii) Assume
\lambda\in\Sigma\backslash\Sigma_{0}
and
\Psi
is a non-zero solution of Eq.(
33
).
Noting
|z_{+}|=|z_{-}|=1
, for
x\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\|(J\Psi)(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|z_{+}^{x-1}a_{+}\chi_{+}+z_{-}^{x-1}a_{-}\chi_{-}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=|a_{+}|^{2}\|\chi_{+}\|^{2}+|a_{-}|^{2}\|\chi_{-}\|^{2}+2\Re\bar{z_{+}}^{x-1}\bar{a_{+}}z_{-}^{x-1}a_{-}\langle\chi_{+},\chi_{-}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\|a_{+}\chi_{+}\|^{2}+\|a_{-}\chi_{-}\|^{2}-2|\langle a_{+}\chi_{+},a_{-}\chi_{-}\rangle|=:\epsilon.

Here,
\epsilon
is independent of
x
.
By Schwartz’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq\|a_{+}\chi_{+}\|^{2}+\|a_{-}\chi_{-}\|^{2}-2\|a_{+}\chi_{+}\|\cdot\|a_{-}\chi_{-}\|

\displaystyle=(\|a_{+}\chi_{+}\|-\|a_{-}\chi_{-}\|)^{2}.

Since
\Psi
is not zero,
(a_{+},a_{-})\neq(0,0)
. Thus, the equality holds true only when
\chi_{+}
and
\chi_{-}
are linearly dependent. However, this contradicts to the fact that
\chi_{+}
and
\chi_{-}
are the eigenvectors of different eigenvalues.
Hence,
\epsilon
is strictly positive and

\displaystyle\inf_{x\geq 1}\|J\Psi(x)\|^{2}\geq\epsilon>0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\Psi\|^{2}=\|J\Psi\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\|J\Psi(x)\|^{2}\geq\sum_{x=1}^{N}\|J\Psi(x)\|^{2}\geq N\epsilon\rightarrow\infty\quad(N\rightarrow\infty).

Thus,
\Psi\notin\mathcal{H}
, which indicates
\ker(U_{\omega}-\lambda)=\{0\}
.

Finally, we assume
\lambda\in\Sigma_{0}
and
\Psi
is a solution of Eq.(
33
) and therefore
\Psi
satisfies Eq.(
4
). Considering the Jordan decomposition, there exists
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
that satisfies

\displaystyle T_{\lambda\infty}\xi=z_{+}\xi+\chi_{+}.

Since the vectors
\xi
and
\chi_{+}
form a basis,
T_{\lambda}(0)(J\Psi)(0)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}(0)(J\Psi)(0)=a\chi_{+}+b\xi.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\geq 1}\|(J\Psi)(x)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\geq 1}\|T_{\lambda\infty}^{x-1}T_{\lambda}(0)(J\Psi)(0)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\geq 1}\|(az_{+}^{x-1}+b(x-1)z_{+}^{x-2})\chi_{+}+z_{+}^{x-1}b\xi\|^{2}.

When
b\neq 0
, the above expression diverges. The reason is that

\displaystyle\|(az_{+}^{x-1}+b(x-1)z_{+}^{x-2})\chi_{+}+z_{+}^{x-1}b\xi\|\geq|b|(x-1)\|\chi_{+}\|-|a|\|\chi_{+}\|-|b|\|\xi\|

which diverges as
x\to\infty
. Thus,
b
must be zero. Moreover, since
|z_{+}|=1

\displaystyle\sum_{x\geq 1}\|(J\Psi)(x)\|^{2}\geq\sum_{x=1}^{N}\|(J\Psi)(x)\|^{2}=N|a|^{2}\|\chi_{+}\|^{2}\to\infty\quad(N\to\infty).

Therefore,
a=0
. This means that Eq.(
33

\ker(U_{\omega}-\lambda)=\{0\}

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

(i)

When

T_{\lambda}(0)\chi_{+}

(ii)

\chi_{+}

Proof.

(
\mathrm{i}
)
T_{\lambda}(0)\chi_{+}
and
\chi_{-}
are linearly dependent if and only if
\det\begin{bmatrix}T_{\lambda}(0)\chi_{+}&\chi_{-}\end{bmatrix}=0
. Since
\left(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{-1}=-\left(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\det\begin{bmatrix}T_{\lambda}(0)\chi_{+}&\chi_{-}\end{bmatrix}=\det\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{2}\lambda}{\omega}+\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}&1\\

\displaystyle=-2-\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\omega}{\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)\right)-\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\omega}{\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)\right)^{-1}.

\Lambda_{1}:=\frac{\omega}{\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)
,

is equivalent to

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{1}^{-1}+\sqrt{2}=0.

Solving this equation for
\Lambda_{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}=\frac{-1\pm i}{\sqrt{2}}

which follows

\displaystyle\frac{\omega}{\lambda}\left(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\frac{-1\pm i}{\sqrt{2}}.

\chi_{+}

\Lambda_{2}:=\frac{\lambda}{\omega}\Bigl(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigr)

Solving this equation for

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}=\frac{1\pm i}{\sqrt{2}}

Lemma 4.7
.

(i)

When
\lambda\in\Xi_{+}

\frac{\omega}{\lambda}\left(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\frac{-1\pm i}{\sqrt{2}}

(ii)

\lambda\in\Xi_{-}

\frac{\lambda}{\omega}(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}})=\frac{1\pm i}{\sqrt{2}}

\lambda=-R_{-}(\omega)\pm i\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)R_{+}(\omega)

Proof.

(
\mathrm{i}
) We solve the following equation with respect to
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\frac{\omega}{\lambda}\left(\frac{-\lambda+\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=\frac{-1\pm i}{\sqrt{2}}.

By computing and simplifying this equation, we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}=(-1\pm i)\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\omega}+\lambda-\lambda^{-1}.

(49)

Squaring both sides of this equation and simplifying, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\omega^{2}-\omega\pm i\omega)(-\omega\mp i(\omega-1))}{2\omega^{2}-2\omega+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega(-\omega^{2}+2\omega-1)\mp i\omega(\omega^{2}-\omega+1)}{2\omega^{2}-2\omega+1}.

We divide the problem into two cases. We first consider the case of

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}=\frac{\omega(-\omega^{2}+2\omega-1)-i\omega(\omega^{2}-\omega+1)}{2\omega^{2}-2\omega+1}.

Since
\omega^{2}-\omega+1
is positive for any
\omega\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
, we observe that

\displaystyle\Im\lambda^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm positive}&{\rm if}\quad\omega<0,\\
{\rm negative}&{\rm if}\quad\omega>0.\end{array}\right.

By Lemma
4.6
and the fact that
\Re\lambda\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\lambda=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}R_{-}(\omega)-iR_{+}(\omega)&\omega>0,\\
R_{-}(\omega)+iR_{+}(\omega)&\omega<0.\end{array}\right.

The obtained expression for
\lambda
can be written in the following form using
\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)
:

\displaystyle\lambda=R_{-}(\omega)-i\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)R_{+}(\omega).

We next consider the case of

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}=\frac{\omega(-\omega^{2}+2\omega-1)+i\omega(\omega^{2}-\omega+1)}{2\omega^{2}-2\omega+1},

where

\displaystyle\Im\lambda^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm positive}&{\rm if}\quad\omega>0,\\
{\rm negative}&{\rm if}\quad\omega<0.\end{array}\right.

Similarly as above, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda=R_{-}(\omega)+i\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)R_{+}(\omega).

We need to verify whether the obtained values of
\lambda
realy satisfy Eq.(
49
),
because we square Eq.(
49
) to solve the equation. Since
\Re\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\lambda^{-2}}\geq 0
, it is enough to see that
\Re\Bigl((-1\pm i)\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\omega}+\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\Bigr)>0
for
\lambda=R_{-}(\omega)\mp i\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)R_{+}(\omega)

Substituting
\lambda

\displaystyle(-1\pm i)\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\omega}+\lambda-\lambda^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{R_{-}(\omega)\mp i\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)R_{+}(\omega)}{\omega}+\frac{\pm iR_{-}(\omega)+\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)R_{+}(\omega)}{\omega}

\displaystyle+R_{-}(\omega)\mp i\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)R_{+}(\omega)-\frac{R_{-}(\omega)\pm i\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)R_{+}(\omega)}{R_{-}(\omega)^{2}+R_{+}(\omega)^{2}}.

By extracting the real part from both sides, we have

\displaystyle\Re\Bigl((-1\pm i)\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\omega}+\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\Bigr)=-\frac{R_{-}(\omega)}{\omega}+\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)R_{+}(\omega)}{\omega}+R_{-}(\omega)-\frac{R_{-}(\omega)}{R_{-}(\omega)^{2}+R_{+}(\omega)^{2}}.

Considering
R_{-}(\omega)>0
, we only need to show

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\omega}+\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)\frac{1}{\omega}\frac{R_{+}(\omega)}{R_{-}(\omega)}+1-\frac{1}{R_{-}(\omega)^{2}+R_{+}(\omega)^{2}}>0.

(53)

Since

,

\displaystyle\frac{R_{+}(\omega)}{R_{-}(\omega)}=\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)(\omega-1)^{2}+\sqrt{(2\omega^{2}-2\omega+1)(\omega^{2}-2\omega+2)}}{\omega^{2}-\omega+1}.

Note that
2\omega^{2}-2\omega+1>0
and
\omega^{2}-2\omega+2>0
for any
\omega\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Then, we find

\displaystyle R_{-}(\omega)^{2}+R_{+}(\omega)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)\omega\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{2}-2\omega+2}{2\omega^{2}-2\omega+1}},

and hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R_{-}(\omega)^{2}+R_{+}(\omega)^{2}}=\mathrm{sgn}(\omega)\frac{1}{\omega}\sqrt{\frac{2\omega^{2}-2\omega+1}{\omega^{2}-2\omega+2}}.

Substituting these results into Eq.(
53
) and

\displaystyle=\frac{(\pm i\omega^{2}-\omega\mp i\omega)(1-\omega\pm i\omega)}{2\omega^{2}-2\omega+1}

We divide the problem into two case. We first consider the case of

4.6

We next consider the case of

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}=\frac{\omega(-\omega^{2}+2\omega-1)+i\omega(\omega^{2}-\omega+1)}{2\omega^{2}-2\omega+1},

\displaystyle\Im\lambda^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm positive}&{\rm if}\quad\omega>0,\\
