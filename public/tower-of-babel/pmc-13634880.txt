Readiness for Peritoneal Dialysis Implementation in Ethiopia: A National Survey of Nephrologists
Authors: Wubshet Jote Tolossa, Ayantu Tesfaye Lemma, Helen Gebremedhin Gebreegziabhier, Talerngsak Kanjanabuch
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) requires less facility-based infrastructure than hemodialysis and can achieve comparable outcomes when supported by trained teams, reliable supplies, and clinical oversight.1 Yet PD accounts for only about 11% of maintenance dialysis worldwide and remains uncommon across sub-Saharan Africa, which has approximately 16% of the global population but less than 1% of the global dialysis population.2 Successful expansion of home dialysis requires coordinated policy, financing, procurement, workforce development, and clinical governance.3 In Ethiopia, maintenance dialysis is limited to hemodialysis and concentrated in Addis Ababa; no chronic PD program exists.4 The readiness of the nephrology workforce to introduce PD has not been assessed.

Between June 1 and 10, 2026, we conducted a census-based cross-sectional survey of all nephrologists practicing in Ethiopia (Supplementary Methods). Of 50 eligible nephrologists, 41 responded (82%). Most were adult nephrologists (88%) and worked in government hospitals (88%); 21 (51%) practiced in Addis Ababa, and 44% had practiced nephrology for 1 to 5 years (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S1).

Exposure to PD was limited. Only 22% rated their PD knowledge as good, and 59% had received no structured PD training. Four respondents (10%) managed patients receiving PD; all were pediatric nephrologists, representing 4 of the 5 pediatric nephrologists surveyed. None had cared for a patient receiving chronic PD for kidney failure. High-barrier ratings clustered around health-system capacity: supply-chain and logistics problems (81%), absence of local PD supply manufacturers (76%), lack of proper training (69%), supply cost (69%), and limited PD catheter availability (64%). Patient-related factors were less often rated as high barriers, including preference for hemodialysis (10%), fear of infection or peritonitis (13%), and religious or cultural beliefs (13%; Figure 1).

Perceived barriers to implementing peritoneal dialysis among nephrologists in Ethiopia, 2026. Bars represent the percentage of respondents (N = 41) who rated each factor as a high barrier.

Despite limited experience, support for PD was broad. Thirty-three respondents (81%) believed that PD could expand dialysis access, including 32 (78%) who strongly held this view (Supplementary Figure S2). Thirty-two (78%) favored government hospitals as pilot sites, and 40 (98%) were willing to participate in a national PD task force (Supplementary Table S1).

Ethiopia’s immediate PD readiness gap lies primarily in modifiable health-system constraints rather than provider opposition. Experience from public-sector PD-first programs suggests that these barriers can be addressed through government stewardship, stable procurement, and trained multidisciplinary teams.5 The survey also identifies a platform for implementation—broad support for public-sector pilots and near-universal willingness to join a national task force. A phased program should begin in selected government hospitals and prioritize structured training, PD catheter access, uninterrupted dialysate supply, sustainable financing, and clinical governance (Supplementary Discussion; see the Supplementary References,S1–S27).
