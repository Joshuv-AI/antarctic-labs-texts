Sesamin alleviates drug-induced hepatotoxicity via autophagy enhancement and ferroptosis inhibition
Authors: Jingyi Ren, Ziyi Wang, Yadong Zhang, Huanting Pei, Rui Wen, Zhenao Zhang, Siqi Zhu, Xiaoya Sun, Bowen Yin, Shenghe Li, Yuxia Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Acetaminophen (APAP) overdose is the leading cause of drug-induced acute liver failure. Sesamin (Ses), a natural polyphenolic compound, possesses diverse pharmacological properties. This study was to explore the effects of Ses on APAP-induced liver injury and the underlying molecular mechanism.Methods:In vivo, C57BL/6J mice were treated with different doses of Ses for 28 days. After overnight food deprivation, mice were intraperitoneally injected with a single dose of APAP. In vitro, the AML12 and HepG2 cells were pre-treated with Ses and then treated with APAP.Results:Ses dramatically alleviated APAP-induced hepatocellular injury in vitro and in vivo. Proteomic analysis suggested that the differentially expressed proteins were enriched in ferroptosis, autophagy, and FoxO signaling pathways between the APAP group and the Ses-treated groups. We subsequently found that ferroptosis was significantly inhibited, and autophagy was significantly enhanced in the Ses-treated group. Consistently, Ses obviously reduced the expression of Foxo1. However, hepatocyte-specific knockout Foxo1 abolished the effect of Ses on hepatocyte autophagy and ferroptosis caused by APAP in mice.Conclusions:Our findings demonstrate that Ses attenuates APAP-induced hepatotoxicity through Foxo1, which mediates both the activation of autophagy and the inhibition of ferroptosis. It provides a new strategy for the prevention and treatment of drug-induced liver injury.KEYWORDS: Sesamin, acetaminophen, liver injury, autophagy, ferroptosis, Foxo1, proteomic analysis, drug-induced hepatotoxicity

Introduction

Acetaminophen (APAP) is a commonly used antipyretic and analgesic drug worldwide. It is safe at therapeutic doses, but can lead to severe acute liver injury or even acute liver failure when overdosed [1, 2]. The majority of APAP undergoes metabolism in the liver via glucuronosyltransferase or phenolsulfotransferases, resulting in the formation of non-toxic compounds that are excreted in the urine. Approximately 10% of APAP is metabolized by cytochrome P450 to produce the highly reactive intermediate metabolite N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI) [3]. NAPQI then reacts immediately with glutathione (GSH) form a hazard-free metabolite [4]. APAP overdose results in the depletion of GSH within hepatic cells, subsequently inducing the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and thereby causing damage to the liver [5, 6]. Currently, N-acetylcysteine is commonly used to treat the toxic effects caused by APAP in clinical practice, but its application is limited due to its adverse effects and narrow therapeutic time window [6–8]. Thus, more effective therapeutic drugs still urgently need to be discovered.

Recently, several studies revealed that the mechanism of hepatocellular damage caused by APAP is related to various forms of regulated cell death (RCD), such as necroptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, and apoptosis [9]. Ferroptosis is a novel form of programmed cell death characterized by cellular free iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation [10]. It exhibits morphological, biochemical, and genetic characteristics distinct from other forms of cell death [11]. The accumulation of lipid peroxides within cells is a key contributing factor to the induction of ferroptosis [12]. After excessive intake of APAP, a significant increase in lipid peroxides was observed in the liver of mice, accompanied by the accumulation of intracellular iron [13]. Studies published recently suggested that ferroptosis was closely related to the occurrence and development of APAP-induced liver injury [14]. And pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis may alleviate hepatocellular injury induced by APAP [15]. Autophagy is a widely distributed cellular process that can be induced as a protective mechanism to preserve or restore intracellular homeostasis [16]. It can facilitate cell renewal by eliminating undesired cytoplasmic components. The administration of a high dose of APAP induces the activation of autophagy, and the subsequent enhancement of autophagic activity contributes to the restoration of hepatic cells by eliminating damaged mitochondria [17]. It is interesting that the degree of hepatocellular damage is aggravated when autophagy is inhibited [18]. Therefore, activating autophagy may be an effective approach for the prevention and treatment of APAP-induced liver injury.

Sesamin (Ses) is a polyphenol lignan obtained from sesame seeds, it has strong anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antihypertensive effects [19–21]. Previous studies showed that Ses plays a protective role in nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), liver fibrosis, and liver cancer [22–24]. Study has found that the inhibition of nickel-induced apoptosis by Ses can be attributed, in part, to its antioxidant properties and its capacity to regulate the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway [25]. Another study found that Ses can significantly attenuate hepatocyte pyroptosis with the regulation of the PKCδ/nod-like receptor family CARD domain-containing protein 4/caspase-1 axis in in vivo and in vitro [22]. Although Ses has been proven to have a protective effect on multiple liver diseases, its protective effect and mechanism against acute liver injury caused by APAP still need further exploration.

In summary, this study aimed to explore the protective effect and potential mechanism of Ses on acute liver injury caused by APAP, in order to discover simpler and more effective treatment strategies for the prevention and treatment of APAP-induced hepatotoxicity.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Sesamin (Ses, purity ≥ 98% by HPLC, S171302) and acetaminophen (APAP, purity > 98.5%, A105808) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Carboxymethylcellulose (CMC, C8621) was purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Tissue iron content assay kit (A039-2-1), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, A020-2) assay kit, superoxide dismutase (SOD, A001-3) assay kit, alkaline phosphatase (AKP, A059-2) assay kit, malondialdehyde (MDA, A003-1) assay kit, cell malondialdehyde (MDA, A003-4-1) assay kit, glutathione (GSH, A006-2-1) assay kit, aspartate transaminase (AST, C010-2-1) assay kit, alanine transaminase (ALT, C009-2-1) assay kit, triglyceride (TG, A110-1-1) assay kit and total bilirubin (TBIL, C019-1-1) assay kit were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Cell iron content assay kit (BC5310) was purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). ELISA kits for interleukin-1β (IL-1β, ml098430), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, ml106471) and interleukin 6 (IL-6, ml098430) were purchased from Mlbio (Shanghai, China). The CCK-8 kit (K009-500) was derived from Zeta life (San Francisco, USA). Primary antibodies for β-actin (AC026, 1:50000), COX2 (SC56-06, 1:3000), SLC7A11 (A2413, 1:2000), GPX4 (A1933, 1:1500) were obtained from ABclonal Biotech Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). Anti-phospho-Foxo1 antibodies (AP1379, 1:3000) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Massachusetts, USA). Antibodies against p62 (CY5546, 1:7000) were purchased from Abways Technology (Shanghai, China) and antibodies against LC3 (14600-1-AP, 1:3000) were obtained from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). The secondary antibody (AB0101, 1:20000) was derived from Abways Technology (Shanghai, China).

Animals and treatments

Wild type male C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Hepatocyte-specific knockout Foxo1 (Hko-Foxo1) mice were derived from Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Suzhou, China) by mating Flox mice with albumin-Cre mice. All animals were kept in the animal room (room temperature at 22 ± 2°C; 12 h light/dark cycle), and the humidity was held constant. All animals were adaptively fed for a week before the onset of the experiments. Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for Experimental Animals and approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Hebei Medical University (IACUC-Hebmu-2024052). Male C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6 each): (1) control, (2) APAP (300 mg/kg), (3) APAP (300 mg/kg) + Ses-L (50 mg/kg), (4) APAP (300 mg/kg) + Ses-H (100 mg/kg), (5) Ses-H (100 mg/kg). For the Ses-treated groups, mice were given Ses by gavage daily for 4 weeks. Ses was suspended in 0.5% CMC. The Ses dosage was chosen based on previous studies to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing potential toxicity [25, 26]. For the control group and APAP group, mice were given the solvent at the same dose.

To set up the liver injury model, the mice were fasted overnight and received a single intraperitoneal injection of 300 mg/kg APAP on the 29th day. The mice in the control group and Ses-treated group were given an equal amount of physiological saline. Male Hko-Foxo1 mice were randomly divided into three groups as follows (n = 6): (1) control, (2) APAP (300 mg/kg), (3) APAP (300 mg/kg) + Ses-H (100 mg/kg). Cre-Foxo1-negative but flox/flox-positive mice were also classified into three groups that were used as the control. The intervention method was the same as the wild-type. All of the mice were euthanized 24 h after the final intervention. All mice were anesthetized with 2–3% isoflurane and placed supine on a dissection tray. Approximately 500 µL of blood was collected from the abdominal aorta. The animals were then euthanized by cervical dislocation. Serum and liver samples were collected and stored at −80°C.

Cell culture and drug intervention

AML12 (CL-0602) and HepG2 (CL-0103) cell lines were obtained from Pricella (Wuhan, China). AML12 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin, while HepG2 cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) containing 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin in a sterile environment at 37°C and 5% CO2. The two types of cells were both divided into five groups: (1) control, (2) APAP (20 mM), (3) Ses (40 μM) + APAP (20 mM), (4) Ses (40 μM) + APAP (20 mM) + 3-MA (5 mM), (5) Fer-1 (2 μM) + APAP (20 mM). Subsequent relevant indicators were measured on this basis.

Viability assay

The viability of AML12 cells and HepG2 cells was detected with the CCK-8 assay. AML12 cells and HepG2 cells were prepared into a cell suspension with a concentration of 1× 104 cells/mL. Two kinds of cells were both seeded onto 96-well plates and incubated for 12 h at 37°C, 5% CO2 cell incubator. Cells in each group were treated differently according to grouping requirements and cultured again for 24 h. Subsequently, the configured volume of 100μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and then the plate was cultured at 37 °C for 1 h. Survival rate of cells in each group was detected by measuring the OD value at 450 nm using an enzyme-labeling instrument.

Morphological and immunostaining analyses

Mouse liver tissues were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde overnight, embedded in paraffin, and sliced in 4 µm sections. Tissues were stained with hematoxylin eosin (H&E) according to standard histopathological procedures.

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were immunohistochemically stained to measure the protein expression of GPX4, p62, and LC3. Briefly, paraffin sections underwent deparaffinization and rehydration, followed by antigen retrieval and serum sealing. The sections were then placed in a humidified chamber at 4 °C overnight for incubation with a primary antibody. Subsequently, the section was incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 50 min at room temperature.

Measurement of serum ALT and AST levels

Liver function was evaluated by measuring AST and ALT in serum. The blood obtained from mice was placed at room temperature for coagulation, then was centrifuged at 3500 r/min for 15 min to separate the serum samples from the blood. The levels of ALT and AST in serum were determined with commercial assay kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Measurement of LDH, SOD, AKP, TG and TBIL contents

Liver tissue from each experimental group was grinded in a cold 0.9% (w/v) saline solution (NS) using a high-speed homogenizer to obtain a 10% weight/volume (w/v) homogenization. Afterwards, the homogenate was centrifuged at 8000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant for analysis. The HepG2 and AML12 cells were scraped from the culture bottle into the lysis solution and were shook with a mixer, then were made a cell suspension by using a high-speed homogenizer. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 8000 r/min for ten minutes at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. Based on the manufacturer's instructions, the activity of LDH, SOD and AKP in liver tissue homogenate supernatant and cell lysate were detected by corresponding assay kits. The levels of TG and TBIL in liver tissue homogenate supernatant were detected by TG assay kits and TBIL assay kits.

Determination of iron

According to the instructions, the iron content in liver tissue homogenate supernatant and cell lysate was respectively measured with tissue iron assay kits and cell iron assay kits.

Assays of MDA and GSH levels

MDA and GSH levels in liver homogenate supernatant and cell lysate were measured with MDA and GSH assay kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. The content of MDA and GSH in liver tissues and cells were calculated based on the method described in the manual.

Assays of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels

Levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in liver homogenate were determined with TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 ELISA kits based on the OD value read on the microplate reader. All measurement results are standardized based on protein concentration.

ROS and JC-1 assay using flow cytometer

The level of ROS in the animal tissues and cells was measured using a ROS assay kit containing dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). Fluorescent probe JC-1 is used to detect mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). Freshly isolated mouse livers were cut and divided into small pieces, and then were digested with trypsin in a constant temperature shaker for 20 min. After digestion, it was stopped with fetal bovine serum. The sediment was filtered through 70 μm cell filters, centrifuged at 1200 rpm/min for 10 min to obtain the organizational sediment. The obtained sediment suspension was divided into two parts. One was added to the ROS fluorescent probe and was incubated in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm/min for 10 min, and then leaved the precipitate and resuspended with PBS, repeating the above steps three times. Finally, the sediment was added to an appropriate amount of PBS and was detected by flow cytometry. The other was added to JC-1 probe and was incubated in a 37 °C constant temperature incubator for 20 min. After incubation, it was centrifuged at 450 g/min for 3 min, and then leaved the precipitate and resuspended with JC-1 buffer. The subsequent steps were similar to ROS. Each group of HepG2 cells and AML12 cells were subjected to different treatments for 24 h. The operation method was similar to the organizational process, and the final measurement results were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Proteomics analysis

Liver tissues were promptly harvested from mice and underwent enzymatic digestion for proteomic analysis. A total of 12 samples were assessed through data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomics. The analyses were conducted using LC-MS with a UPLC M-Class system (Waters, USA) and a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Proteins showing significant expression changes between different groups, defined as identifying and quantifying with at least 2 unique peptides, a p value <0.05, and an expression change value >1.2-fold (with <0.83 indicating downregulation and >1.2 indicating upregulation), were selected for further analysis. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were selected in R software (R version 4.0.3). Volcano plots, Gene Ontology (GO) terms, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were visualized by an online platform for data analysis and visualization (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn).

Western blotting

The liver tissues were placed into a cold RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors and were ground to prepare tissue homogenate using a high-speed homogenizer. The protein concentration of the obtained tissue homogenate was quantified by using the Lorry method, then it was standardized to the same concentration and immersed in boiling water for 10 min for denaturation. Finally, the prepared samples were stored at −20 °C for further analysis. Equal amount of protein samples (20 µg) was separated by using 8-12% (w/v) SDS-PAGE and transferred the protein onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were sealed with 5% (w/v) skim milk at room temperature for 2 h or overnight at 4 °C. Then, the membranes were washed with TBST three times. Afterwards, the membranes were incubated with different primary antibodies prepared in antibody diluent overnight at 4 °C, and then were washed with TBST three times. Next, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 40 min. Finally, the membrane was uniformly immersed in an enhanced chemiluminescence solution for detection and quantified using Tanon digital imaging analysis software.

Docking analysis

Molecular docking was performed using tools, including AutoDock Tools (version1.5.6), AutoDockVina (version1.1.2), and PyMOL. The crystal structure of Ses was downloaded from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and that of Foxo1 was gained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). 3D structure of Foxo1 was acquired from Protein Data Bank (PDB) database (http://www.rcsb.org/). Then the ligand and protein were prepared by AutoDock software. Molecular docking was completed in the AutoDockVina software, and the results was visualized by PyMOL software.

Statistical analyses

The experimental data results in this article were expressed as mean ± SEM and performed statistical analysis using SPSS statistical software. Data were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc test was used when assumptions were met; otherwise, non-parametric tests were applied.

Results

Ses reduces APAP-induced liver injury in mice

Mouse body weights were recorded every four days, with the growth curve depicted in Figure S1A. Following APAP intervention, the body weight of the model group mice decreased significantly. However, Ses pretreatment effectively attenuated the adverse effects of APAP exposure on body weight (Figure S1B). Similarly, Ses significantly suppressed the increase in the liver-to-body weight ratio in APAP-exposed mice (Figure S1C). Mice were treated with APAP and/or Ses according to the experimental procedures as showed in Figure 1A. Serum aminotransferases, specifically ALT and AST, are highly sensitive markers of hepatocyte injury. These enzymes are predominantly localized in hepatocytes and are released into the bloodstream in response to various insults such as infection, toxicity, inflammation, and necrosis. Our results showed that the levels of ALT and AST were significantly increased in the APAP group, and different doses of Ses apparently decreased serum activities of ALT and AST (Figure 1B,C). In addition, Ses reversed the decrease of SOD activities and inhibited the production of LDH and AKP in the livers of APAP-treated mice (Figure 1D-F). In addition, the levels of TG and TBIL in mouse liver also showed similar results (Figure S2A, B). HE staining were employed to observe the pathological damage of liver tissues. As shown in Figure 1G, obvious histopathological changes and large areas of necrosis were observed in APAP group and Ses-treated groups. However, the pathological morphology of the damaged liver in Ses-treated groups were significantly improved compared to the APAP group (Figure S3A). APAP-induced liver injury is related to a series of inflammatory changes. In our study, we also detected the levels of the inflammatory factors (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) in mice liver tissue. The results suggested that Ses obviously decreased the levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α of mice treated with APAP (Figure 1H–J).

Proteomics analysis of the liver

There were 1713 up-regulated DEGs and 2325 down-regulated DEGs in mice liver treated with APAP compared with the control group (Figure S4A). KEGG pathway enrichment analyses was used to further explore the signaling pathways related to APAP hepatotoxicity. The results revealed that the DEGs were majorly involved in Autophagy, FoxO signaling pathway, Ferroptosis, TNF signaling pathway, and C-type lectin receptor signaling pathway (Figure S4B). From PharmMapper, Swiss Target Prediction, BATMAN-TCM, and TCMSP, A total of 60 targets was obtained after removal of the partially overlapped targets (Figure S4C). As showcased in Figure S4D, there is much overlap between DEGs and targets for Ses. Based on the above findings, we therefore speculate that Ses may had a protective effect on APAP-induced liver injury. To systemically elucidate how Ses prevents APAP-induced liver unjury, we performed proteomics analysis on the livers. A total of 12 samples with 4 samples in each group were subjected to sequence. The Pearson correlation analysis revealed that each group of the samples had good biological repeatability (Figure 2A). Unsupervised PCA and two-dimensional hierarchical clustering clearly separated the samples into two clusters (Figure 2B,C). We then compared the gene expression in three experimental groups. Compared with the control group, there were 266 up-regulated DEPs and 276 down-regulated DEPs (Figure 2D). In comparison to the APAP group, the Ses-treated group exhibited 488 DEPs, with 340 up-regulated and 148 down-regulated genes (Figure 2D). To identify key functions and signaling pathways of the hub target genes of DEPs, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed. The results of KEGG analysis on the DEPs between the control group and the APAP group indicated that the DEPs were majorly involved in Phagosome, Cholesterol metabolism, Chemical carcinogenesis – DNA adducts, Efferocytosis, Ferroptosis, and so on (Figure 2E; Table S1). In addition, the DEPs between the APAP group and the Ses-treated group were mainly enriched in Chemical carcinogenesis – reactive oxygen species, Autophagy, Fatty acid metabolism, FoxO signaling pathway, Ferroptosis, and so on (Figure 2F; Table S2). It is worth noting that proteomic results indicate that the adverse effects of APAP on the liver and the protective effect of Ses on liver injury are both related to ferroptosis. This is consistent with the results predicted by network pharmacology. Subsequently, we performed GO analysis to identify DEPs participating in biological processes (BPs), cellular components (CCs), and molecular functions (MFs). The results of the GO analysis suggested that GO terms, such as autophagy (GO:0006914), process utilizing autophagic mechanism (GO:0061919), oxidoreductase complex (GO:1990204), 2 iron, 2 sulfur cluster binding (GO:0051537), oxidoreductase activity, acting on the aldehyde or Foxo1 group of donors (GO:0016903), oxidoreductase activity, acting on a sulfur group of donors (GO:0016667) were enriched after Ses treatment (Figure 2G; Table S3). Based on the above results, we speculate that the protective effect of Ses on APAP-induced liver injury is related to the Autophagy, FoxO signaling pathway, and, Ferroptosis.

Ses alleviates the damage effect caused by APAP in AML12 and HepG2 cells

In order to understand the underlying mechanism of Ses against APAP-induced liver injury, we preformed cell experiments in HepG2 and AML12 cell lines. The experimental workflow was shown in Figure 3A. The CCK8 assay was conducted to select appropriate concentrations of Ses (range from 0 to 80 µM) and APAP (range from 0 to 40 mM) treatment (Figure 3B-E). We found that AML12 cell viability linearly over the Ses concentration range of 0–40 μM (Figure 3B) and APAP induced significant cytotoxicity in AML12, with IC50 of 20 mM (Figure 3D). The results of cell viability experiments in HepG2 cells are similar to those in AML12 cells (Figure 3C, E). Therefore, we chose the optimal concentration of Ses (40 µM) and APAP (20 mM) for use in the subsequent experiments. According to our previous proteomic results, autophagy and ferroptosis may play important roles in reducing liver damage with Ses. We subsequently applied autophagy inhibitor 3-MA and ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 to investigate the role of autophagy and ferroptosis in APAP induced liver injury. As shown by the experimental results, treatment with Fer-1 significantly decreased the observed decline in cell viability induced by APAP in both AML12 and HepG2 cells. In contrast, autophagy inhibitor 3-MA had no effect on APAP-induced decrease in cell viability (Figure S5A, B). Based on previously published articles, phytochemical compounds can alleviate the toxic effects of drugs on the liver by activating autophagy and inhibiting ferroptosis [27]. Therefore, we explored the possible mechanisms by which Ses exerts protective effects through co-treated with 3-MA and Ses. As shown in Figure 3F,G, in AML12 and HepG2 cells treated with 40 μM Ses and ferroptosis inhibitors Fer-1 demonstrated a clear increase in cell activity following exposure to APAP. However, the protective effect of Ses was neutralized by the addition of 3-MA (Figure 3F,G). SOD is an important biomarker of antioxidant defense capacity and oxidative injury. The results showed that Ses and Fer-1 significantly increased the SOD activity of APAP-treated mice (Figure 3H, I). LDH release is an important indicator for assessing cellular injury. We found that the levels of LDH were significantly increased in the APAP group, compared with the control group. In contrast, treatment with Ses and Fer-1 substantially decreased LDH release induced by the APAP treatment (Figure 3J, K). However, 3-MA can significantly reverse the protective effect of Ses. These results indicated that Ses may ameliorate APAP-induced liver injury by inhibiting ferroptosis and activating autophagy in both AML12 and HepG2 cells.

Ses inhibits APAP-induced ferroptosis in vivo

Next, we embarked to investigate the effect of Ses on APAP-induced liver ferroptosis. Iron plays an important role in APAP-induced liver injury and initiation of ferroptosis [28]. Therefore, we detected the changes in tissue iron content and found APAP injection caused significant increases in liver iron content (Figure 4A). However, Ses significantly inhibited that elevation (Figure 4A). Ferroptosis is characterized by lipid peroxidation, and the level of lipid peroxidation was then estimated via determining the levels of MDA. The results showed that the administration of APAP notably augmented the levels of MDA in the liver (Figure 4B). However, Ses prominently reversed this effect (Figure 4B). GSH is an antioxidant enzyme, and it is consumed during detoxification of lipid peroxides. As showed in Figure 4C, after administration of APAP, the activity of GSH in liver tissues was decreased strongly and the Ses treatment effectively inhibited the reduction in GSH. Also, the process of cell ferroptosis is also accompanied by ROS accumulation and the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). The results indicated that compared to control group, APAP treatment markedly increased ROS accumulation and decreased MMP (Figure 4D, E). Nevertheless, Ses treatment obviously exhibited a significant reversal of this effect (Figure 4D, E). We also examined the expression of ferroptosis-related protein, including COX2, SLC7A11, and GPX4. Western blot assay showed that Ses treatment significantly down-regulated the hepatic expression of COX2 in APAP – exposed mice, whereas it up-regulated the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4 in APAP – exposed mice (Figure 4F-I). Immunohistochemical staining for GPX4 was completely consistent with western blot results (Figure 4J-K). In summary, Ses exerted a beneficial effect on APAP-induced liver injury by inhibiting ferroptosis.

Ses enhances autophagy in APAP-treated mice

Autophagy is a catabolic cytoprotective mechanism in the cellular defense against stress [29]. Mounting evidence suggesting that autophagy is triggered in specific liver zones in response to APAP overdose, and that pharmacological induction of autophagy confers protection against APAP-induced liver damage [30]. Proteomics results indicated that the protective mechanism of Ses against APAP-induced liver injury was connected with autophagy signaling pathway. Therefore, we evaluated the levels of autophagy in different groups. the autophagy-related protein LC3II/LC3I in the Ses-treated group was increased compared with that APAP group, while the protein expression of p62 was decreased (Figure 5A-C). Immunohistochemistry for LC3 and p62 further confirm the results pertaining to the protein levels of autophagy-related protein in each group (Figure 5D-F). The above results suggested that Ses treatment enhanced the production of autophagy in APAP-treated mice.

Ses inhibits ferroptosis in APAP-treated AML12 and HepG2 cells

We found that both Ses and ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 significantly decreased the levels of iron and MDA and increased GSH activity in APAP-treated AML12 and HepG2 cells (Figure 6A-F). However, the protective effect of Ses was counteracted by the addition of 3-MA. Similar results were observed in detection of ROS and MMP. As shown in Figure 6G-J, Ses treatment strikingly suppressed ROS generation and increased MMP level in APAP-treated AML12 and HepG2 cells. Nevertheless, addition of 3-MA offset Ses-elicited protective effects on APAP-triggered liver injury. Moreover, we also measured the expression of pivotal ferroptosis proteins, COX2, GPX4 and SLC7A11, which are key proteins in ferroptosis regulation. Our results showed that following APAP treatment, not only the expression of COX2 was upregulated, but also the expression of GPX4 and SLC7A11 was downregulated in AML12 and HepG2 cells (Figure 6K-R). However, these alterations were significantly reversed by Ses treatment. Interestingly, addition of 3-MA counteracted the Ses-related effects. These results confirmed that Ses treatment can alleviate APAP-induced hepatic injury by inhibiting in AML12 and HepG2 cells.

Hepatocyte-specific knockout Foxo1 eliminates the effect of Ses on hepatocyte autophagy and ferroptosis in APAP-treated mice

According to the results of proteomics results, the protective mechanism of Ses is related to the FOXO signaling pathway. The FOXO family contains Foxo1, Foxo3, Foxo4, and Foxo6. According to the previous literature, Foxo1 is essential in autophagy regulation [31, 32]. Molecular docking is a useful approach to study the probable binding mode of ligands and proteins. The binding between Ses and Foxo1 protein was evaluated by the binding energy to predict their affinity, and the binding energy was −7.6 kcal/mol (Figure 7A). Binding energy less than −5 kcal/mol represented a good binding ability, and less than −7 kcal/mol represented a strong binding ability [33]. We further evaluated the protein expression of Foxo1 in animals. Compared with control group, the protein expression of Foxo1 increased significantly in APAP group, while Ses treatment strongly reduced Foxo1 protein expression (Figure 7B,C). To further investigate the role of Foxo1 in reducing APAP-induced liver injury with Ses, we established a hepatocyte-specific knockout Foxo1 gene knockdown mice. Mouse body weights were recorded every four days, with the growth curve presented in Figure S6A. Following liver-specific knockout of Foxo1, we observed that the protective effect of Ses pretreatment against the adverse effects of APAP- induced on body weight was abolished (Figure S6B). Likewise, the inhibitory effect of Ses on the increased liver/body weight ratio in APAP-induced mice was also eliminated (Figure S6C). We first examined the effect of Ses on ferroptotic events, such as iron accumulation (Figure 7D), MDA production (Figure 7E), GSH depletion (Figure 7F), intracellular ROS accumulation (Figure 7G), and MMP drop (Figure 7H), and we found that Ses treatment decreased the occurrence of those events induced by APAP in wild-type mice. However, Ses-induced protective effects were diminished when Foxo1 was knocked out in mice hepatocytes. As shown in Figure 7L-O, western blot assay showed that Ses obviously decreased the protein expression of COX2 and increased the protein expression of GPX4 and SLC7A11 in wild-type mice, but these effects were nearly abolished in hepatocyte-specific knockout Foxo1 mice. Also, we investigated the effects of Ses intervention on autophagy-related protein expression. The protein expression of p62 and LC3II/LC3I indicated that the protective effect of Ses on protein expression changes of these proteins was almost abrogated in hepatocyte-specific knockout mice (Figure 7I-K).

Discussion

APAP is a widely utilized antipyretic analgesic globally and reportedly the most common leading cause of acute liver failure (ALI) [34]. It is estimated that 40%–70% of ALI cases in the UK and Europe are attributed to APAP, and 46% of ALI cases in the United States are linked to APAP [35, 36]. Therefore, it is imperative to develop preventive measures for ALI induced by APAP, with a preference for preferably nonpharma-cological methods. Natural products are becoming increasingly popular in drug discovery and the management of complex diseases, mainly because of their numerous benefits such as minimal potent biological activity, low toxicity, and side effects. Ses is the major bioactive compound extracted from sesame which is reported to possess free-radical-scavenging and antioxidative activities. Here, we for the first time provided both in vitro and in vivo evidence that Ses alleviated excessive APAP induced liver injury, as evidenced by alteration in the biochemical indices of hepatocytes (AML12 and HepG2 cell lines) and mouse livers. Our proteomics results revealed the protective mechanism of Ses on APAP-induced liver injury may be related to Autophagy signaling pathway, FoxO signaling pathway, and, Ferroptosis signaling pathway. Then, we found Ses can attenuate acute liver injury caused by excessive APAP by inhibiting ferroptosis and enhancing autophagy. Further, we identified Foxo1 as the target of Ses action through molecular docking and knockout animal models, whereby Foxo1 activation promoted hepatocyte autophagy while suppressing ferroptosis.

Toxicological studies have shown that excessive APAP have various toxic effects on the body, such as hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and reproductive toxicity [37–39]. Multiple studies have explored the mechanism of excessive APAP-induced liver injury. Leng et al. [40] demonstrated that overdose of APAP can trigger the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to inflammatory damage. In a separate study, Guo et al. [41] observed that excessive APAP intake markedly diminished the liver's antioxidant capacity and exacerbated oxidative stress within the body. Our research results are consistent with previous research findings. The main manifestation was morphological alterations, the infiltration of inflammatory cells, and increased oxidative stress level after APAP administration, as evidenced by the histopathological observations of stained sections and the kit test results. Despite decades of studies have been launched, the precise mechanisms underlying the progression of APAP-induced liver injury and the processes involved in liver regeneration and recovery remain incompletely understood. It is very important to get insights into the underlying mechanisms and develop effective measures to protect against APAP overdose-induced liver damage.

More and more evidence suggested that targeted inhibition of ferroptosis can prevent acute liver injury induced by APAP [42, 43]. Ferroptosis is a novel form of programmed cell death that results from the accumulation of iron-dependent lipid peroxide. Li et al. [44] showed that overdose APAP upregulated GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression and led to acute liver injury. Shan et al. [45] also found that the absorption of ultrasmall poly (acrylic) acid-coated Mn3O4 nanoparticles primarily occurs through macropinocytosis, leading to the inhibition of ferritinophagy-mediated iron mobilization and the preservation of mTOR activation. These effects collectively contribute to the significant inhibition of ferroptosis in APAP-induced acute liver injury in mice. Autophagy is an intracellular degradation system that transports intracellular substances to lysosomes for digestion. Chao et al. [30] found that autophagy was activated in specific liver regions under the action of excessive APAP, and pharmacologically enhancing autophagy can prevent APAP induced liver injury. All the findings above suggested targeting autophagy and ferroptosis may be an effective strategy for treating and preventing acute liver injury.

Ses is a natural lignin compound and has many pharmacological and physiological activities [46]. There is a lot of evidence to prove that Ses has a protective effect on various liver related diseases [23, 47], which may be attributed to the liver is the main metabolism organ of Ses [48]. However, the protective mechanism of Ses against APAP-induced liver injury still need to be further elucidated. Our research results indicated that Ses can significantly alleviate oxidative stress and inflammatory damage in liver caused by excessive APAP. Meanwhile, we investigated the potential mechanisms and targets of the action of Ses. Previous studies showed that the protective effect of Ses on liver injury may be related to its regulation of multiple regulatory cell death modes, such as apoptosis, ferroptosis, and autophagy [49–51]. In our study, we found that Ses significantly inhibited the occurrence of ferroptosis in liver induced by excessive APAP, mainly manifested by reducing intracellular ROS and the increase in MMP, as well as inhibiting the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins in vitro and in vivo. In addition, we also measured the effect of Ses on the expression of autophagy related proteins in APAP-induced liver injury model. The results suggested that Ses can increase the protein expression of LC3II/LC3I and decrease p62 expression. However, measuring autophagy at a single time point does not capture dynamic changes in autophagic flux, thereby limiting the interpretation of the results. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the absence of ultrastructural validation by TEM remains a limitations, and future studies will incorporate these approaches to further substantiate the mechanistic role of autophagy. Additionally, given the time-dependent nature of APAP-induced liver injury, time-course experiments are needed to pinpoint Ses’ s specific contributions at critical stages.

The forehead box transcription factor class O (FOXO) family represents a set of transcription factors that are involved in many stress-related transcription programs, including antioxidant response, cell apoptosis, and autophagy [52]. Foxo1 is a member of the FOXO family and has been implicated in the development of multiple hepatic illnesses [53]. However, there is still some controversy over the role of Foxo1 in regulating liver diseases. The study by Pan and colleagues revealed that the expression of Foxo1 increased in CCL4-induced fibrosis model and hepatic Foxo1 deletion alleviated CCL4-induced hepatic inflammation and reduced TGF-β1 mRNA and protein levels [54], concluding that Foxo1 is a promoting factor for liver injury. In a high-fat diet-induced nonalcoholic fatty liver model, mice were administered a high-fat diet exhibited elevated levels of Foxo1, which correlated with the induction of endoplasmic reticulum stress and necroptosis. However, the suppression of Foxo1 resulted in the mitigation of both ER stress and necroptosis [55]. Contradictorily, Pan and colleagues found that hepatic Foxo1 deficiency led to the expression of inflammatory cytokines and fibrotic genes in NAFLD model, suggesting that Foxo1 played a beneficial role in preventing high-fat diet-induced liver disease [56]. In a hepatic ischemic reperfusion (IR) injury model, the protein expression of Foxo1 increased markedly following ischemic IR-stress, and upregulation of Foxo1 reversed the Th17/Treg cytokine imbalance and attenuated liver inflammation [57]. In our study, we found that the protein expression of Foxo1 considerably elevated in APAP-treated mice and Ses treatment notably reduced Foxo1 expression. It was reported that Foxo1 regulated liver autophagy and lipid metabolism through autophagy-related gene 14 in a high-fat diet model [58]. Thus, it is speculated that targeted regulation of Foxo1 may be an effective method for improving liver injury. To explore the mechanism of the protective effect of Ses on APAP-induced liver injury, we constructed hepatocyte-specific knockout of Foxo1 mice. A recent study reported that Ses ameliorates APAP-induced hepatotoxicity by deactivating the HMGB1/TLR4/NF-κB pathway [26]. Our findings build on this research by providing deeper mechanistic insights. Previous studies have shown that, in liver injury models, hepatocyte ferroptosis promotes inflammation via the HMGB1/TLR4/NF-κB pathway [59]. In summary, these results confirm that Ses indirectly regulates ferroptosis and autophagy through Foxo1.

Ses is a promising agent for the prevention and treatment of drug-induced liver injury, yet several translational challenges remain. Its poor aqueous solubility and extensive first-pass metabolism markedly limit dissolution, release, and oral bioavailability [60, 61]. Formulation strategies such as nanoparticle encapsulation or liposomal delivery have shown potential to improve its solubility, stability, and absorption [62, 63]. Currently, N-acetylcysteine is the specific antidote for acetaminophen toxicity, but its efficacy is time-dependent and associated with adverse effects. By contrast, Ses has demonstrated health benefits at safe oral doses of 50–200 mg/day [20]. Thus, further studies are needed to enhance its metabolic stability and bioavailability and to define its therapeutic window, thereby increasing its translational value.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our present study demonstrated that Ses significantly alleviated APAP-induced liver injury via activating autophagy and inhibiting ferroptosis. Mechanistically, Ses modulated cell autophagic and anti-ferroptosis phenotypes of hepatocytes through Foxo1 pathway. Thus, Ses may be a promising lead compound for combating APAP overdose. These findings establish a preliminary theoretical basis for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies to prevent and treat drug-induced liver injury.
