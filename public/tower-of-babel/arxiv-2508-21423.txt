Introduction

Given an
m\times n
matrix
{\cal B}
with columns
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
having at most unit
\ell_{2}
-norm,
the problem of
discrepancy minimization
seeks to obtain a coloring
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
which minimizes
\|{\cal B}x\|_{\infty}
.
The question of finding a low-discrepancy
\pm 1
-coloring has been of interest to computer scientists and mathematicians since
the early 1980’s. There have been several algorithmic proofs of discrepancy bounds in the past decade and a half,
employing a variety of techniques, from semidefinite duality theory, Gaussian random walks and martingale concentration, to ideas in convex geometry
and functional analysis.

In this work, we consider the related problem of
prefix discrepancy minimization
, also known as the
signed series problem
,
in the
\ell_{2}
-norm.
Given a set of
n
vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
having
\ell_{2}
norm at most
1
, the problem asks to find a collection of signs
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
,
which minimizes

\max_{i\in[n]}\|\sum_{j=1}^{i}x(i)v_{i}\|_{2}.

The problem, which is closely connected to – and harder than – the century-old
Steinitz problem
, has been studied for a long time
[
14
]
.
In this problem, studied by Steinitz
[
24
]
and asked even earlier by Riemann, we are given a norm
\|.\|
over
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
together with
n
vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which sum to zero and have at most unit norm,
and asked to find the minimum bound
B_{S}
, such that there exists a permutation
\pi
of
n
for which the norms of the partial sums
\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}v_{\pi(i)}\|
,
k\in[n]
,
are bounded by
B_{S}
. Grinberg and
Sevast’yanov
[
18
]
showed that the bound for the Steinitz problem in any norm is at most
d
, improving Steinitz’s
[
24
]
earlier bound of
2d
.
Chobanyan
[
15
]
gave
a general reduction from the Steinitz problem to the signed series problem, showing that
the bound for the Steinitz problem is at most the bound for the signed series problem. It has been conjectured that
the actual bound for both the Steinitz and the signed series problems is
O\left(\sqrt{d}\right)
. Both these problems have found several applications in graph theory,
integer programming, scheduling, and related areas
[
13
,
22
,
11
]
. In 2012,
Banaszczyk
[
2
]
gave a non-constructive bound of

for both

[

showed that Chobanyan’s reduction can be made

version of the signed series problem to the

in Machine Learning, achieving a bound of
O\left(\sqrt{d}\log^{5/2}n\right)
.
The best constructive bound known so far, was given by Bansal and Garg

of

Discrepancy Minimisation and The Bansal-Garg Algorithm

The signed series as well as the Steinitz problems are closely related to the larger framework of
discrepancy minimisation
.
The central open problems in this area are the
Beck-Fiala
conjecture and more
generally the
Komlos
conjecture, where given a set of
n
vectors in the unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, the
goal is to assign signs (also called “colors”) to the vectors so as to bound the
\ell_{\infty}
norm of the final signed sum, by a constant independent of
m
and
n
.
Since the seminal work of Beck and Fiala
[
12
]
, and the non-constructive bounds proved by Spencer
[
23
]
, Gluskin
[
17
]
, Banaszczyk
[
26
]
and others,
the question
of
algorithmic discrepancy minimisation
was open for a long time, until Bansal’s remarkable breakthrough
[
4
]
, which showed Spencer’s bound could be achieved constructively
in polynomial time. Since then there have been several algorithmic results on discrepancy minimisation,
which achieved Spencer’s or Banaszczyk’s bounds algorithmically, though the question of making further progress on the Komlos problem remained open.
Very recently, Bansal and Jiang
[
9
]
have solved the
Beck-Fiala conjecture for
k\geq\log^{2}n
, and also improved the bound on the Komlos conjecture to
O\left(\log^{1/4}n\right)
.

A large class of discrepancy minimisation algorithms operate on the framework of
multi-dimensional random walks on the relaxed version of the problem where the space of candidate solutions is relaxed
to
[-1,1]^{n}
in the intermediate steps, and the final solution is rounded off, to
lie in
\{-1,1\}^{n}
. In this context, the algorithmic framework of Bansal and Garg
[
6
]
is relevant as it achieves an
O\left(\sqrt{d\log n}\right)

bound on the signed series and Steinitz
problems. The idea is to conduct each step of the random walk using a random vector with a specially constructed covariance matrix.
Subsequently they show that each component of the discrepancy vector evolves as a subgaussian random variable with bounded variance.
Given a problem instance as a matrix
M
and an initial coloring vector
x
, together with an increment
\Delta x
,
the increment of the squared
\ell_{2}
discrepancy
\|M(x+\Delta x)\|_{2}^{2}-\|Mx\|_{2}^{2}
, can be partitioned into a linear component
\Delta L:=\langle Mx,M\Delta x\rangle
and a quadratic component

\Delta Q:=\|M\Delta x\|_{2}^{2}
. The analysis of Bansal and Garg
[
6
]
(see also Bansal, Garg and Dadush
[
5
]
)
utilizes the fact that the quadratic component behaves as a sub-exponential random variable, while the linear component is subgaussian with variance
O(d)
. This gives a tail bound
inversely exponential in the multiplicative
deviation, which together with a union bound over all
i\in[n]
gives a bound on the quantity
\max_{i\in[n]}\|M_{i}x\|_{2}
, where
M_{i}
is the matrix containing the first
i
columns of
M
and
having the other columns replaced by zeroes. The crucial new development in their analysis is the construction of the covariance matrix, which they call Universal Vector
Colorings (UVCs). Using this they show that choosing
\Delta x=Ur
, where
U
is a UVC and
r
is a subgaussian random vector, ensures that the variance of
M\Delta x

along any coordinate axis
e_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, i.e.
e_{i}^{\top}Mx
, is of the
order of the sum of the variances of
\Delta x(j)
,
j\in[n]
, weighted according to the entries in the
i
-th row of
M
. This allows them to argue that if the squared of the norm of the
discrepancy vector, i.e.
\|Mx\|_{2}^{2}
, increases, then the corresponding sum of variances of the coloring vector
x
also increases, and so with high probability
the process has already reached a vertex of the unit hypercube.

1.1
Our Contribution

Our main result is a constructive bound for the signed series problem, that comes close to Banaszczyk’s non-constructive version. Specifically, we show the following.

Theorem 1
.

Given vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
having at most unit
\ell_{2}
norm, there exists a constant
K_{1}>0
, independent of
n
and
d
, and a randomized algorithm with runtime
polynomial in
n
and
d
, which outputs signs

x(i)\in\{-1,1\}
,
i\in[n]
, such that

\max_{i\in[n]}\|\sum_{j=1}^{i}x(i)v_{i}\|_{2}\leq K_{1}\sqrt{d+\log^{2}n}=O\left(\sqrt{d}+\log n\right).

In particular, this is the first constructive bound that achieves additive rather than multiplicative error. As in the case of the Bansal-Garg algorithm, by the constructive reduction of the Steinitz to the signed series problem by Harvey and Samadi
[
19
]
,
we also get a constructive bound of
O\left(\sqrt{d+\log^{2}n}\right)
for the
\ell_{2}
version of the Steinitz problem.

Overview of Proof

Our idea is firstly to use the existing Bansal-Garg framework, in order to use the properties of UVCs, which will be crucial to bound the variances of the
increments
M\Delta x
along each coordinate axis in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as before.
We firstly observe that the quadratic component of the increment
\Delta Q
is – after appropriate centering – a
(sub)-gaussian chaos
. Therefore, concentration inequalities for
chaoses can be utilized to achieve stronger tail bounds for
\|Mx\|_{2}^{2}
. Although this is implicit in the work of Bansal and Garg
1

1

Indeed, their concentration inequality for the
quadratic component would yield a bound matching Banaszczyk’s. However, the bound on the quadratic variation of the quadratic is stated using, “it can be computed that”, and as far as
our computations show, it seems to be quite larger. Proving the Bansal-Garg claimed bound on this term, in combination with our bounds on the quadratic variation of the
linear component
L_{t}
, would yield an
\ell_{2}

O\left(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log n}\right)

, yet the formulation of the chaos concentration
inequality in terms of matrices, also allows us to design some
spectral constraints during the construction of the universal vector coloring, which we use to bound large singular values of submatrices, as well as to maintain near-orthogonality
of the discrepancy increment vector with the current discrepancy vector. This enables us to bound the quadratic variation of the linear as well as the quadratic components of the
increments.

Similarly, in order to bound the quadratic variation of the linear component, we need to ensure that the discrepancy increment vector
M_{i}\Delta x_{t}
is orthogonal,
or near-orthogonal to the existing discrepancy vector
M_{i}x_{t}
. This is again established using an extra set of discrepancy-orthogonal constraints.

Finally, we also give a slight generalization of the Freedman concentration inequality, which would allow us to work with general subgaussian vector distributions such as gaussian,
rather than only with distributions having bounded support. Though the original Freedman inequality suffices for our case, neverthelss we use the generalization for
convenience.

In order to state our result on the Komlos problem, first we need to define the notion of

. Given a matrix

k
is a collection of

M
, where each subset has at least
k
rows, and every row of
M
occurs in some subset in the collection. Let
e_{i}
,

, denote the canonical basis vectors
for
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. For row covers of polynomial size and width at least

, we show the following.

Theorem 2
.

There exists an algorithm
{\cal A}
such that, given an
m\times n
real matrix
M
with columns having at most unit
\ell_{2}
-norm, and a row cover
{\mathcal{F}}
of

\log n

\sum_{i\in F}(e_{i}^{\top}Mx)^{2}\leq K_{2}(\log n+|F|),

where
K_{2}
is independent of

and

.

Thus, for each subset of rows

.

2.2
Linear Constraints for Signed Series Problem

We fix
\beta:=\delta:=1/4
and
\delta^{\prime}=1/16
. At each time
t
, the
active variables
A(t)
shall be only the
\min\{|N(t)|,d/\delta^{\prime}\}
least-indexed alive variables of the input matrix
{\cal B}
. In addition to the
linear constraints on the row-wise discrepancies, there will be two additional types of linear constraints, thus making three types in total.

Row discrepancy constraints
If
|A(t)|=d/\delta^{\prime}
, then for each row with more than
d/\delta^{\prime}
coordinates corresponding to alive variables, we set a
constraint for the row vector restricted to the
d/\delta^{\prime}
coordinates with indices in
A(t)
.
This gives us
d
constraints. (These are similar to the constraints in
[
6
]
, except that we replaced

by the larger number
d/\delta^{\prime}
.) If
|A(t)|:=k<d/\delta^{\prime}
, then we do not set any row constraint.

Singular vector constraints
Our next set of constraints correspond to certain right singular vectors of submatrices of
{\cal B}
.
Let
k:=|A(t)|
. We partition the set of active indices into
\alpha_{1}\sqrt{d}
contiguous blocks
(\alpha_{1}:=1/16)
, each of size
k/\alpha_{1}\sqrt{d}
. Thus if the active elements at time
t

are
A(t)=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
, we form blocks
L_{1}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\sqrt{d}/\alpha_{1}\delta^{\prime}}\}
,
L_{2}=\{i_{\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\alpha_{1}\delta^{\prime}}+1},\ldots,i_{2\sqrt{d}/\alpha_{1}\delta^{\prime}}\}
,
etc.
For a block
L_{j}
, consider the prefix submatrix
B_{L_{j}}
, having the columns of
{\cal B}
with indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{jk/\alpha_{1}\sqrt{d}}
,
with entries in other columns being set to zero. Since the columns of
{\cal B}
have
\ell_{2}
-norm at most
1
,
the square of the Frobenius norm of the matrix
B_{L_{j}}
, which is also the sum of its squared singular values, is at most the number of columns in
B_{L_{j}}
, i.e.
k
, and
so by an averaging argument, there are at most
k/\sqrt{d}
squared singular values greater than
\sqrt{d}
. In other words,
if
v_{i}
denotes the
i
-th column of
B_{L_{j}}
, and
\sigma_{l}
is the
l
-th singular value of
B_{L_{j}}
, then

\|B_{L_{j}}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{l}\sigma_{l}^{2}=\sum_{i\leq jk/\alpha_{1}\sqrt{d}}\|v_{i}\|_{2}\leq k,

and therefore

\sum_{l:\;\sigma_{l}^{2}>\sqrt{d}}1\leq k\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}=\frac{k}{\sqrt{d}}.

For each prefix matrix
B_{L}
, and for each singular vector
q
of
B_{L}
corresponding to a singular value greater than
\sqrt{d}
, we add the
vector
q
to the set
Y(t)
of constraints. This gives us
\alpha_{1}\sqrt{d}\cdot\frac{k}{\sqrt{d}}=\alpha_{1}k
constraints.

Discrepancy vector orthogonality constraints
Finally, we shall need an additional set of constraints to ensure that at every step,
the increment in the discrepancy vector
{\cal B}\Delta x_{t}
will be almost orthogonal to the current discrepancy vector.
Let
k:=|A(t)|
. We partition the set of active indices into
\alpha_{2}k
contiguous blocks
\alpha_{2}:=1/8
, each of size
1/\alpha_{2}
.
For a block
S_{j}
, consider the
d\times n
prefix submatrix
B_{S_{j}}
, having the columns of
{\cal B}
with indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{jk/(\alpha_{2})}
,
with other columns being set to zero.
For each such prefix matrix

, we add the vector
w=B_{S_{j}}^{\top}B_{S_{j}}x_{t-1}
to the set
Y(t)
of constraints. Thus we shall ensure that
w^{\top}U_{t}=0
.
Note that here we only have one constraint per block. This gives us
\alpha_{2}k
constraints.

Ratio of constraints to active variables
Thus from the above discussion, when
|A(t)|=d/\delta^{\prime}
, we have a total of
d/\delta^{\prime}
variables,
and at most
d(1+\alpha_{1}/\delta^{\prime}+\alpha_{2}/\delta^{\prime})
constraints, so that the ratio of constraints to active variables satisfies

\frac{|Y(t)|}{|A(t)|}\leq\frac{d+\alpha_{1}d/\delta^{\prime}+\alpha_{2}d/\delta^{\prime}}{d/\delta^{\prime}}=\frac{1+\alpha_{1}/\delta^{\prime}+\alpha_{2}/\delta^{\prime}}{1/\delta^{\prime}}=\delta^{\prime}+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=\delta.

When the set of active variables has size

, we have

variables and at most
\alpha_{1}k+\alpha_{2}k
constraints, i.e.

\frac{|Y(t)|}{|A(t)|}\leq\frac{k(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})}{k}=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}<\delta.

Proof Outline and Back-of-Envelope Estimates

The idea of the proof can be summarized as follows. The total change in the squared
\ell_{2}
-discrepancy
can be split into a quadratic component
Q_{T}
, which is the sum of subgaussian chaoses, together with a subgaussian linear component
L_{T}
.
The analysis shall be split into two cases, based on whether the quadratic variation of the increments is small or large. In each case, we shall
handle the linear and quadratic components separately:

Bounded Quadratic Variation
: First, we consider
the situation when the sum of the variances of the increments in
Q_{T}
, i.e. the
path variance
of
Q_{T}
, is bounded.
As mentioned earlier, in this case we shall bound
the maximum deviation of the sum
Q_{T}
from its expected value, using the concentration inequality (Theorem
8
)
for filtration-dependent sums of subgaussian chaoses, proved in
Section
3
. We shall apply Theorem
8
, allowing an additive deviation of
\approx(d+\log^{2}n)
from the expected value. Thus we obtain a failure probability inversely
exponential in
(d+\log^{2}n)/\left(\max_{t}\|BU_{t}\|^{2}\right)
. Here we shall need an upper bound on the operator norm of the matrices
(BU_{t})_{t\leq T}
, which we show
to be
O\left(d^{1/4}\right)
in Section
5
.
With this, the negative exponent in the failure probability becomes
\approx(d+\log^{2}n)/\sqrt{d}
, which yields an inverse polynomial (in
n
) failure probability for
d\geq\log^{2}n
.

For
L_{T}
, i.e. the sum of the subgaussian linear terms, we shall show that the bound on the path variance of
Q_{T}
implies a
bound of

on the path variance of
L_{T}
.
In this case, we shall simply allow a deviation of

. From the bound on the variance and the allowed deviation, it can be seen that the

\exp\left(-\Omega(s^{2}/v_{L})\right)=\exp\left(\Omega(\log n)\right)
, which is
already inverse polynomial in
n
. With the above parameters, we get that
L_{T}
is at most
O\left(\left((d+\log^{2}n)\log n\right)^{1/2}\right)
, and
Q_{T}
is
O\left(d+\log^{2}n\right)
,
with probability at least
1-1/n^{\Omega(1)}
.
Observe that this implies the net increase in the squared
\ell_{2}
-discrepancy

, will be at most

, since for

and

, we have

, and so the squared
\ell_{2}
-discrepancy satisfies

.

Large Quadratic Variation
: This leaves us with the case when the path variance is large. Our strategy shall be to show that in this case the norm of the coloring vector
x_{T}
must also become
large with high probability, which means
x_{T}\not\in[-1,1]^{n}
w.h.p. Since this is impossible by the construction of the random walk, this implies that the probability of the quadratic variation
becoming large is quite small.

In order to show this, we need an important consequence of having UVCs as the covariance matrices of
\Delta Q_{t}

which was also crucial in the analysis of Bansal and Garg:
the path variance of

.
Thus, in this case the path variance of

also becomes large.

Here we observe that the path variance is also the expected value

.
Now Theorem

comes to our rescue, showing that w.h.p.

becomes large in this case.
This implies that

must also be very large, since otherwise the coloring vector

would go outside the unit hypercube

,
which is impossible by our construction of the random walk (see Remark

shows that with high probability, it cannot be too large either, and therefore the probability of the path variance being large, is

Thus with high probability, the path variance is small, and so the squared
\ell_{2}
-discrepancy is
O\left(d+\log^{2}n\right)
.

\tau:=T
. Let
Z_{t}:=\Expected_{t-1}\left[Y_{t}\right]
. Note that
Z_{t}=\gamma^{2}{\rm Tr}\left(BU_{t}U_{t}^{\top}B^{\top}\right)
, which is

{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}
-measurable. Let
V_{t}:=\gamma^{2}\|A_{t}\|_{F}^{2}
, and
{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}:=\sum_{t\leq T}V_{t}=\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}\|A_{t}\|_{F}^{2}
.
By Lemma
14

(i)
, we get that the condition on
M_{t}
is satisfied for

\displaystyle c^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle b_{op}\cdot\|U_{t}\|^{2}\;\;=\;\;\frac{4\sqrt{d}}{\alpha_{1}\delta^{\prime}}\|U_{t}\|^{2}\;\;\leq\;\;\frac{2\sqrt{d}}{\alpha_{1}\delta^{\prime}\beta},

(5)

where we used Lemma
12
in the second inequality.
From Lemma
14
, we get that if
{\mathcal{V}}_{B}=\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\leq m_{bd}
, then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}\|U_{t}^{\top}B^{\top}BU_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\;\;\leq\;\;\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}\|U_{t}^{\top}B^{\top}\|^{2}\cdot\|BU_{t}\|_{F}^{2}

(6)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}b_{op}\|U_{t}\|^{2}\cdot\frac{\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}}{\beta}

(7)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}\frac{b_{op}}{\beta^{2}}\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{b_{op}{\mathcal{V}}_{B}}{\beta^{2}}

(8)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{b_{op}m_{bd}}{\beta^{2}},

(9)

where in (
6
), we used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, in (
7
), we used Lemma
14

(i)
to bound the operator norm of the
first term in the product, and applied Lemma
13
, to bound
\|BU_{t}\|_{F}^{2}
.
Lastly in (
8
), we used Lemma
12
.

Finally we set the parameters
b:=b_{op}m_{bd}/\beta^{2}=\frac{4\sqrt{d}K_{0}(d+\log^{2}n)}{\alpha_{1}\delta^{\prime}\beta^{2}}
and
a:=m_{bd}=K_{0}(d+\log^{2}n)
. Thus we get

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(|Q_{T}-\sum_{t\leq T}Z_{t}|\geq a\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq m_{bd}\right)\right]

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(|Q_{T}-\sum_{t\leq T}Z_{t}|\geq a\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\leq b\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq m_{bd}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\min\left(\frac{a^{2}}{4Cb},ca/(c^{\prime})^{2}\right)\right),

(11)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-c_{3}\cdot\min\left(\frac{K_{0}^{2}(d+\log^{2}n)^{2}}{4C\cdot 4\sqrt{d}K_{0}(d+\log^{2}n)/(\alpha_{1}\beta^{2}\delta^{\prime})},\frac{cK_{0}(d+\log^{2}n)}{8\sqrt{d}/(\alpha_{1}\beta\delta^{\prime})}\right)\right)

(12)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-c_{4}K_{0}(d+\log^{2}n)/\sqrt{d}\right),

where in (
10
) we used (
9
), since
{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq m_{bd}
implies
{\mathcal{V}}_{Q}\leq b_{op}{\mathcal{V}}_{B}/\beta^{2}\leq b_{op}m_{bd}/\beta^{2}
;
in (
11
) we applied Theorem
8
;
and in (
12
),
we substituted the values of
a,b
and used
c^{\prime}
defined in (
5
). In the last step,
c_{4}
denotes a constant independent of
n
and
d
.
Observe that for each
t\leq T
,

Z_{t}=\gamma^{2}\|BU_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\beta}\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2},

by applying Lemma
13
, so that the sum
\sum_{t\leq T}Z_{t}\leq\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{B}}{\beta}
. Under our condition that
{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq m_{bd}
, we get

\sum_{t}Z_{t}\leq m_{bd}/\beta
.
Thus we get

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(Q_{T}\geq(1+1/\beta)m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq m_{bd}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-c_{4}K_{0}\left(\frac{d+\log^{2}n}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\right),

and so for a sufficiently large
K_{0}
, we get that with probability at least
1-1/(4n^{3})
,

\displaystyle Q_{T}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+1/\beta)m_{bd}\;\;=\;\;(1+1/\beta)K_{0}(d+\log^{2}n).

Bounding
L_{t}
:

Next, we come to the first term on the RHS of (
4
). This can be handled as follows.

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(\exists t\;\|Bx_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\geq(3+1/\beta)m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left(\forall t\;Q_{t}\leq(1+1/\beta)m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq m_{bd}\right)\right]

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(\exists t\;\left(|L_{t}|\geq m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left(\forall t^{\prime}<t\;\|Bx_{t^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}<(3+1/\beta)m_{bd}\right)\right)\wedge\left(\forall t\;Q_{t}\leq(1+1/\beta)m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq m_{bd}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(\exists t\;\left(|L_{t}|\geq m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left(\forall t^{\prime}<t\;\|Bx_{t^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}<(3+1/\beta)m_{bd}\right)\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq m_{bd}\right)\right]

In the above, (
) follows by considering the first time
t
when the squared
\ell_{2}
discrepancy

\|Bx_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
became large, (i.e. for all
t^{\prime}<t
,
\|Bx_{t^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}
remained
small) together with the fact that
\|Bx_{t}\|_{2}^{2}=Q_{t}+2L_{t}
.
Indeed, since
\|Bx_{t}\|_{2}^{2}=Q_{t}+2L_{t}
, if
Q_{t}
always remains small, and
t^{*}
is the first time when
\|Bx_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
becomes large, it must be that

L_{t}
has become large at time
t^{*}
. Further, by our definition of
t^{*}
, for all
t^{\prime}<t^{*}
,
\|Bx_{t^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}
was small.
The last line (
14
), follows simply by removing the condition on
Q_{t}
.

The probability of the event in (
14
), can be bounded by applying Freedman’s inequality, as shown below.
Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{L}
denote the sum of the path variances of the increments of
L_{t}
, i.e.

{\mathcal{V}}_{L}:=\sum_{t\leq T}\Expected_{t-1}\left[\langle Bx_{t-1},B\Delta x_{t}\rangle^{2}\right].

By Lemma
15
and the definition of
{\mathcal{V}}_{B}
, we get that

{\mathcal{V}}_{L}\leq\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}\frac{C_{L}\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}}{\beta}=C_{L}{\mathcal{V}}_{B}/\beta.

Now we shall apply Freedman’s inequality to
L_{t}
with the allowed deviation
a:=m_{bd}
and the path variation
{\mathcal{V}}_{L}
bounded
by
C_{L}{\mathcal{V}}_{B}/\beta
.
We get

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(\exists t\;\left(|L_{t}|\geq m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left(\forall t^{\prime}<t\;\|Bx_{t^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}<(3+1/\beta)m_{bd}\right)\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq m_{bd}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(\exists t\;|L_{t}|\geq m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{L}\leq C_{L}K_{0}(d+\log^{2}n)/\beta\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{m_{bd}^{2}}{4C_{L}m_{bd}/\beta}\right)\;\;\leq\;\;2\exp\left(-c_{5}K_{0}\left(d+\log^{2}n\right)\right)\;\;\leq\;\;1/4n^{3},

for sufficiently large
K_{0}
, and
d\geq\log^{2}n
.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Large Path Variance Case

Getting back to the case when the path variance is large, recall that we defined

\tilde{Q}_{t}:=\sum_{t^{\prime}\leq t}\|I_{S}\Delta x_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

to denote the sum of the quadratic terms in the expansion of the squared
\ell_{2}
norm
\|I_{S}x_{T}\|_{2}^{2}
,
and

\tilde{L}_{t}:=\sum_{t^{\prime}\leq t}\langle I_{S}x_{t-1},I_{S}\Delta x_{t}\rangle,

to denote the sum of the linear terms.
Thus, starting from an initial position
x_{0}=0
, we have

\|I_{S}x_{t}\|_{2}^{2}=\tilde{Q}_{t}+2\tilde{L}_{t}.

Proof of Lemma
11
.

Define
N_{k}:=(2^{k-1}m_{bd},2^{k}m_{bd}]
. The event
\{{\mathcal{V}}_{B}>m_{bd}\}
can be decomposed as

\Probability\left[{\mathcal{V}}_{B}>m_{bd}\right]=\sum_{k\geq 1}\Probability\left[{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}\right].

We shall now focus on bounding the probability on the right-hand side, for a fixed
k
.

\displaystyle\Probability\left[{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(\tilde{Q}_{T}<\frac{1}{4}2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}\right)\right]+\Probability\left[\left(\tilde{Q}_{T}\geq\frac{1}{4}2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}\right)\right]

We shall show that the probabilities of each of the events on the left hand side above, decay (super-)exponentially as
k
increases.
In the first case, when
\tilde{Q}_{T}
is small and
{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}
, we’ll use Theorem
8
to bound the probability.
When
\tilde{Q}_{T}
is large, this implies that the sum of the cross-terms
\tilde{L}_{T}
must also be large, since by our construction of the
random walk (Remark
1
), we always have
x_{T}(i)^{2}\leq 1
for each
i\in[n]
. Here, we shall use Freedman’s
inequality on
\tilde{L}_{T}
, to show that the probability of this event will also decay exponentially with
k
.

Consider the first case, i.e. when
\tilde{Q}_{T}
is small. Recall that
\tilde{Q}_{T}=\sum_{t}\|I_{S}\Delta x_{t}\|_{2}^{2}=\gamma^{2}\sum_{t}r_{t}^{\top}U_{t}^{\top}I_{S}^{\top}I_{S}U_{t}r_{t}
,
which is the sum of subgaussian chaoses. We shall apply the bound for the lower tail from Theorem
8
in this case. From
Lemma
12
, we get that
\max_{t}\|U_{t}^{\top}\|^{2}\leq 1/\beta
, so that we can take
c^{\prime}:=1/\sqrt{\beta}
, where
c^{\prime}
is defined in
Theorem
8
.

Let

\tilde{Y}_{t}:=\Expected_{t-1}\left[\|I_{S}\Delta x_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\gamma^{2}\Expected_{t-1}\left[\|I_{S}U_{t}r_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right],

denote the conditional expectation of the increment in the squared norm. We have

\sum_{t\leq T}\tilde{Y}_{t}=\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}{\rm Tr}(I_{S}U_{t}U_{t}^{\top}I_{S}^{\top})=\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}\|I_{S}U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}={\mathcal{V}}_{B}.

Further, let
{\mathcal{V}}_{N}=\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}\|U_{t}^{\top}U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}
. We have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{N}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}\sum_{t\leq T}\|U_{t}^{\top}\|^{2}\cdot\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\;\;\leq\;\;\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\beta}\sum_{t\leq T}\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}=\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{B}}{\beta}.

(15)

The probability of the event that the squared norm is small while
{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}
, can be bounded from above as follows.

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(\tilde{Q}_{T}<\frac{1}{4}2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(|\tilde{Q}_{T}-{\mathcal{V}}_{B}|>\frac{1}{4}2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}\right)\right]

(16)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(|\tilde{Q}_{T}-{\mathcal{V}}_{B}|>\frac{1}{4}2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq 2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\right]

(17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(|\tilde{Q}_{T}-{\mathcal{V}}_{B}|>\frac{1}{4}2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{N}\leq 2^{k}m_{bd}/\beta\right)\right]

(18)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\cdot\exp\left(-c_{2}\min\left(1/4^{2},{\varepsilon}_{1}\right)2^{k}m_{bd}/\beta\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\cdot\exp\left(-c_{6}2^{k}m_{bd}\right),

where in the last step,
c_{6}:=c_{2}\cdot\min\left(1/16,{\varepsilon}_{1}\right)
is a constant independent of
n
and
d
. In the above, in (
16
), we used
that
{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}
implies
{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\geq 2^{k}m_{bd}/2
, so that the event
\{\tilde{Q}_{T}<2^{k}m_{bd}/4\}
is contained in the event
\{|\tilde{Q}_{T}-{\mathcal{V}}_{B}|>2^{k}m_{bd}/4\}
.
(
17
) also follows by relaxing the condition on the variance, i.e.
{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}\implies{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq 2^{k}m_{bd}
.
The next step (
18
) follows again by relaxing the condition on the variance, since from (
15
),

{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq 2^{k}m_{bd}\implies{\mathcal{V}}_{N}\leq 2^{k}m_{bd}/\beta
.
At this point, we have a centered sum of subgaussian chaoses, since
\Expected_{t-1}\left[\Delta\tilde{Q}_{t}\right]=\gamma^{2}\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}
,
so that
\sum_{t\leq T}\Expected_{t-1}\left[\Delta\tilde{Q}_{t}\right]={\mathcal{V}}_{B}
.
We can therefore apply Theorem
8
, to get (
19
).

Finally, we come to the case when
\tilde{Q}_{T}
is large, while
{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}
. By Remark
1
, for all
t\geq 0
,
and for all
i\in[n]
, it holds that
|x_{t}(i)|^{2}\leq 1
. By our choice of protected sets, there are at most
d/\delta^{\prime}
corrupted columns in
B
, whose norm we
are estimating. Lastly
\tilde{Q}_{T}+2\tilde{L}_{T}=\|x_{T}\|_{2}^{2}
. Thus, for a sufficiently large
K_{0}
,
m_{bd}=K_{0}(d+\log^{2}n)>8d
, and the event

\|\tilde{Q}_{T}\|_{2}^{2}>\frac{1}{4}2^{k}m_{bd}
, can occur only when
|\tilde{L}_{T}|>\frac{1}{8}2^{k}m_{bd}
. We get

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(\tilde{Q}_{T}>\frac{1}{4}2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(|\tilde{L}_{T}|>\frac{1}{8}2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{B}\leq 2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\right]

(20)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Probability\left[\left(|\tilde{L}_{T}|>\frac{1}{8}2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\wedge\left({\mathcal{V}}_{N}\leq 2^{k}m_{bd}/\beta\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{(2^{k-3}m_{bd})^{2}}{2(2^{k-3}m_{bd}+2^{k}m_{bd}/\beta)}\right)

(21)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\cdot\exp\left(-c_{7}(2^{k}m_{bd})\right),

(22)

where
c_{7}\geq 2^{-9}
is independent of
n
and
d
. In the above, (
20
) follows from (
15
),
and (
21
) follows from an application of Freedman’s inequality (Theorem
5
).
The probability bound obtained, (
22
), again decays exponentially with increasing
k
.
Combining the bounds on the probabilities of the cases corresponding to large
\tilde{Q}_{T}
and small
\tilde{Q}_{T}
, we get that
for each
k\geq 1
,

\Probability\left[{\mathcal{V}}_{B}\in N_{k}\right]\leq\exp\left(-c_{7}2^{k}m_{bd}\right).

Now summing the above bounds over all
k\geq 1
and substituting
m_{bd}:=K_{0}(d+\log^{2}n)
, gives that

\Probability\left[{\mathcal{V}}_{B}>m_{bd}\right]\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}2\cdot\exp\left(-c_{7}2^{k}m_{bd}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{8}(d+\log^{2}n)\right).

Thus, choosing the constant
K_{0}
sufficiently large gives that
\Probability\left[{\mathcal{V}}_{B}>m_{bd}\right]\leq 1/2n^{3}
, which
completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

5
Bounds on Step Variances

In this section, we shall prove some auxiliary bounds on norms of certain matrices that were crucial in the analysis in the previous section.
First, let us bound the operator norm of
U_{t}^{\top}
, an easy consequence of the properties of UVCs.
Recall that
B^{*}
denotes the submatrix of
{\cal B}
, which has non-zero columns corresponding to the elements of
[n]
active at time
t
.

Lemma 12
.

For all
t\leq T
,
\|U_{t}^{\top}\|^{2}=\|U_{t}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
.

Proof of Lemma
12
.

Let
x
be a unit vector in the column space of
U_{t}
. Then we have

\|U_{t}x\|_{2}^{2}\leq x^{\top}U_{t}^{\top}U_{t}x\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j}x(j)^{2}\|u_{t}(j)\|_{2}^{2},

where
u_{t}(j)
is the
j
-th row of
U_{t}
.
Now since for each
j
,
\|u_{t}(j)\|\leq 1
, we get

\|U_{t}x\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j}x(j)^{2}=\frac{1}{\beta}.

Therefore we get

\|U_{t}^{\top}\|^{2}=\|U_{t}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Next, we give a lemma to bound the Frobenius norm of
BU_{t}
, for each time step
t\leq T
.
Given
\kappa\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
C
be a
\kappa\times n
matrix, where each column of
C
has
\ell_{2}
-norm at most
1
.
A column of
C
is
relevant
if it is non-zero and the corresponding row of
U_{t}
is also non-zero.
Let
I_{C}
be the
n\times n
diagonal matrix whose
j
-th diagonal entry is
1
if and only if the corresponding column of
C
is relevant.

Lemma 13
.

\|CU_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\|I_{C}U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}.

Proof of Lemma
13
.

To bound
\|CU_{t}\|_{F}^{2}
, it is sufficient to focus on the relevant columns of
C
, since the other columns of
C
are either zero,
or the corresponding rows of
U_{t}
are zero. Now, observe that
\|CU_{t}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i\in\kappa}\|r_{i}U_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
, where
r_{i}
is the
i
-th row of
C
. By
Theorem
3
,
(ii)
, for each
i\in\kappa
, we have
\|r_{i}U_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j\in[n]}r_{i}(j)^{2}\|u_{t}(j)\|_{2}^{2}
, where
r_{i}(j)
is the entry in
the
j
-th column of
r_{i}
, and
u_{t}(j)
is the
j
-th row of
U_{t}
. Summing over all
i\in[\kappa]
, we get

\displaystyle\|CU_{t}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\in\kappa}\sum_{j\in[n]}r_{i}(j)^{2}\|u_{t}(j)\|_{2}^{2}

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j\in[n]}\|u_{t}(j)\|_{2}^{2}\sum_{i\in[\kappa]}r_{i}(j)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j\in N_{C}}\|u_{t}(j)\|_{2}^{2}

(24)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\|I_{C}U_{t}\|_{F}^{2},

which is the bound stated in the lemma. Here, in (
23
), we changed the order of summation, and in (
24
), we used
the fact that the
\ell_{2}
-norm of each non-zero column of
C
is at most
1
. The last line follows simply from the definition of
I_{C}
, as the indicator matrix
of the relevant columns of
C
.
Finally, the second inequality in the statement of the lemma follows since each entry of
I_{C}
is at most
1
.
∎

For the remainder of this section, we recall the constants
\beta=\delta=1/4
,
\delta^{\prime}=\alpha_{1}=1/16
, and
\alpha_{2}=1/8
defined in Section
2
.

Lemma 14

(Bound on subgaussian norm of
\Delta Q_{t}
)
.

For all
t\leq T
,

(i)\>\>\|U_{t}^{\top}B^{\top}\|^{2}=\|BU_{t}\|^{2}\leq b_{op}\cdot\|U_{t}\|^{2},

where
b_{op}:=:=\frac{4\sqrt{d}}{\alpha_{1}\delta^{\prime}}
.
Consequently, for all
0\leq t\leq T
,

(ii)\>\>\Expected_{t-1}\left[\Delta Q_{t}\right]\leq\left(\gamma^{2}\lambda^{2}C\frac{b_{op}}{\beta^{2}}\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\right).

Proof of Lemma
14
.

We have
\Delta Q_{t}=\gamma^{2}\|BU_{t}r_{t}\|_{2}^{2}=\gamma^{2}r_{t}^{\top}U_{t}^{\top}B^{\top}BU_{t}r_{t}
, which is
a subgaussian chaos. By Lemma
6
, for all
0\leq\lambda\leq c/\|U_{t}^{\top}B^{\top}BU_{t}\|=c/\|BU_{t}\|^{2}
, the
subgaussian norm of this chaos is at most

C\gamma^{2}\|U_{t}^{\top}B^{\top}BU_{t}\|_{F}^{2}
, which is at most
C\gamma^{2}\|U_{t}^{\top}B^{\top}\|^{2}\cdot\|BU_{t}\|_{F}^{2}
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
Applying Lemma
13
, the second term
\|BU_{t}\|_{F}^{2}
is at most
\frac{1}{\beta}\|I_{S}U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}
, which
is at most
\frac{1}{\beta}\|U_{t}\|_{F}^{2}
.

To bound the first term
\|U_{t}^{\top}B^{\top}\|^{2}
, we shall use the constraints on the singular values of submatrices that we defined in Section
2.2
.
Recall that
B^{*}
is column-selected from
{\cal B}
with non-zero columns having indices in
A(t)
. From Section
2.2
, the non-zero columns of
B^{*}
were
partitioned into contiguous blocks.
Let
B_{L}
be the column-selection of
B
given by the maximal number of blocks which are completely contained in the prefix
set
{\rm corr}_{[i]}(t)
, and let
B_{r}
be
B-B_{L}
. We have

\displaystyle\|BU_{t}\|^{2}=\|B_{L}U_{t}+B_{r}U_{t}\|^{2}\leq 2\|B_{L}U_{t}\|^{2}+2\|B_{r}U_{t}\|^{2}.

(25)

From our construction of the blocks,
B_{r}
has at most
\sqrt{d}/\alpha_{1}\delta^{\prime}
non-zero columns, each having
\ell_{2}
-norm at most
1
. The second
term
\|B_{r}U_{t}\|^{2}
can therefore be bounded simply as below.

\displaystyle\|B_{r}U_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|B_{r}\|^{2}\cdot\|U_{t}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\alpha_{1}\delta^{\prime}}\|U_{t}\|^{2}=\frac{b_{op}}{\alpha_{1}\delta^{\prime}}\cdot\|U_{t}\|^{2}.

(26)
