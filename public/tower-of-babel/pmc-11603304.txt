Glycosphingolipids and their impact on platelet activity in a murine model of fabry disease
Authors: Adam J Kanack, Eve Prodoehl, Mayumi Ishihara-Aoki, Kazuhiro Aoki, Nancy M Dahms
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFabry disease is an X-linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by deficiency of the lysosomal enzyme ⍺-galactosidase-A (⍺-Gal A), resulting in widespread accumulation of terminal galactose-containing glycosphingolipids (GSLs) and the impairment of multiple organ systems. Thrombotic events are common in Fabry patients, with strokes and heart attacks being significant contributors to a shortened lifespan in patients of both genders. Previously, we developed an ⍺-Gal A-knockout (KO) murine model that recapitulates most Fabry symptomologies and demonstrated that platelets from KO males become sensitized to agonist-mediated activation. In the current report, we used mass spectrometry, platelet-based assays and histology to define further the mechanisms linking GSL accumulation with thrombotic phenotypes in both sexes. Sera and platelets from ⍺-Gal A-KO females have elevated levels of Fabry-associated GSLs relative to wild-type females, but accumulated less of these GSLs than KO males. Correspondingly, KO females demonstrate a less severe thrombotic phenotypes than KO males. Notably, treatment of platelets from wild-type animals with globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) increased baseline platelet activation and aggregation. In contrast, several control GSLs did not stimulate platelet responses. These data suggest that chronically high concentrations of the Fabry-associated GSL, Gb3, contributes to the prothrombotic phenotypes experienced by Fabry patients by directly stimulating platelet activation.Subject terms: Glycobiology, Glycomics

Introduction

Fabry disease (MIM 301,500) is an inherited X-linked disorder caused by pathogenic variations in the GLA gene (MIM 300,644, EC3.2.1.22) that decrease the activity of the lysosomal hydrolase α-galactosidase A (α-Gal A) and result in the accumulation of terminal galactose-containing substrates in the glycosphingolipid (GSL) metabolic pathway1–5. Most prominently, the GSLs globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) and its deacylated derivative globotriaosylsphingosine (lyso-Gb3) accumulate, but tissue deposition of these GSLs varies with the relative rates of sphingolipid metabolism in individual cell and tissue types and with the level of residual α-Gal A activity3,5,6.

Recent epidemiological studies based on genetic screening suggest Fabry disease is the most prevalent of lysosomal storage disorders, and significantly more common than previously appreciated. For example, of approximately 200,000 newborns screened in Illinois Fabry disease occurred most frequently (1 in 8454 infants)7. In additional studies, GLA mutations associated with a classical presentation of the disease were present in approximately 1 in 50,000 males. In comparison, mutations associated with an atypical presentation or later-onset disease were present in approximately 1 in 2,000 to 1 in 8,000 males and 1 in 6,000 to 1 in 40,000 females8–12. As newborn screening techniques continue to identify novel mutations in GLA that attenuate α-Gal A activity, the estimated prevalence of Fabry disease will likely continue to increase.

Classically, manifestations of Fabry disease begin in childhood with the appearance of corneal opacities, sweating abnormalities, and neuropathic or limb pain13–18. During adolescence, dermatologic abnormalities (e.g., telangiectasias and angiokeratomas1,4) and gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., diarrhea or constipation, abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting19,20) often begin, while kidney dysfunction and hearing loss frequently develop during the succeeding decades21,22. Notably, cardiovascular (e.g., hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, cardiac arrhythmias, valvular disease23,24) and cerebrovascular complications (e.g., transient ischemic attacks, ischemic strokes25) occur at a high rate among Fabry patients and account for the majority of deaths in patients of both genders26.

While enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) is a standard treatment, some patients may not respond due to the development of neutralizing antibodies or the inability of replacement ⍺-Gal A to be trafficked to all affected tissues. Apart from ERT, chaperone-based therapy is available for a subset of Fabry patients with amenable GAL missense mutations [e.g., mutations that display a ≥ 1.2-fold increase in activity over baseline when treated with a pharmacological chaperone in a GLP-validated amenability assay]27. For amenable mutations, chaperone treatment can reduce ⍺-Gal A misfolding and improve ⍺-Gal A lysosomal targeting and activity. However, even with ERT or chaperone-based therapies, the manifestations of Fabry disease still significantly reduce the quality of life and lifespan for both male and female patients, with most patients still requiring symptomatic treatment28,29.

α-Gal A substrates accumulate in the vascular endothelium, vascular smooth muscle, and patient plasma, suggesting that several candidate pathways may contribute to an increased thrombotic risk6,30,31. Recently, we developed a murine model of Fabry disease that closely recapitulates most human phenotypes, including ocular, cardiovascular, and neuropathic manifestations and the accumulation of GSLs in the plasma32–34. Using this Fabry rat model lacking detectable ⍺-Gal A enzyme activity, we investigated the possible mechanisms contributing to prothrombotic phenotypes in Fabry patients. In a recent study, we describe data demonstrating that knockout (KO) male rats accumulate ⍺-Gal A substrates in their platelets and plasma and show a proinflammatory cytokine profile compared to normal animals. In addition, KO males had significant increases in several circulating white blood cell types, and their platelets were more responsive to activation by the platelet agonist ADP35.

The current report expands on our previous study to examine how sex differences impact the pathology of Fabry disease by including ⍺-Gal A-KO female and heterozygous female animals. Additionally, these studies investigate the direct effect of GSLs on the activation state of resting platelets and agonist responsiveness. To achieve this, platelets from male and female animals with normal ⍺-Gal A activity were treated with Fabry-associated GSLs before assessing platelet activation markers at resting state or after agonist treatment. Overall, the combination of mass spectrometry, functional platelet assays, and histological studies performed here suggest that the GSL globotriaosylceramide significantly contributes to prothrombotic platelet phenotypes in Fabry disease.

Results

Animals lacking α-Gal A activity accumulate terminal galactose-containing GSLs and have increased CD3-positive T lymphocytes and CD68-positive monocytic cells in the bone marrow

The bone marrow is a crucial regulator of hematopoietic cell generation and development, and glycans and glycosphingolipids within the marrow help regulate differentiation36–38. Additionally, changes in glycosphingolipid content in the marrow have been correlated with several types of cancers39,40. In a previous study, we discovered that α-Gal A KO male rats had significant changes in the bone marrow environment. Males deficient in α-Gal A accumulated substantial amounts of terminal-galactose-containing molecules compared to wild-type males in the bone marrow, as assessed using Griffonia simplicifolia isolectin B4 (IB4), a lectin with high affinity for terminal α-galactose moieties, and consistent with the accumulation of terminal α-galactose-containing GSLs (e.g., Gb3 and lyso-Gb3) in the bone marrow environment. The accumulation of α-galactose-containing molecules in the bone marrow of KO males also correlated with significant increases in T lymphocytes and myeloid-derived monocytic cells in the bone marrow, as assessed using CD3 and CD68 specific antibodies.

As Fabry disease is an X-linked disorder, the present study aimed to evaluate the accumulation of terminal α-galactose-containing molecules in the bone marrow of α-Gal A wild-type, heterozygous, and homozygous-KO females to define sex differences involved in the pathology of Fabry disease. Our results reveal that both heterozygote and homozygous-KO females exhibit significant increases in total areas of IB4 and CD3-staining, suggestive of an accumulation of α-galactose-containing GSLs (Figs. 1A-B) and an increase in the number of T lymphocytes in the bone marrow compared to wild-type females (Figs. 1C-D). However, relative to wild-type females, only α-Gal A-KO females display increased CD68-staining in the bone marrow, indicative of an increase in monocytic cells, whereas α-Gal A heterozygous females did not (Figs. 1E-F). In contrast, no differences are seen with CD41 staining, indicating the number of megakaryocytes within the bone marrow is similar among wild-type, heterozygous, and KO females (Fig. S1).

Like the bone marrow environment, α-Gal A-KO female animals had elevated levels of several circulating white blood cell types, including lymphocytes and monocytes, relative to age-matched animals with normal α-Gal A activity (Fig. 2A). Lymphocyte and monocyte counts increased in an age-dependent manner, with lymphocyte counts peaking between 25–50 weeks, and monocyte counts peaking between 50–75 weeks. In contrast, α-Gal A heterozygote females did not have increases in the number of circulating white blood cells (Fig. 2A).

Fabry patients have an increase in the concentration of the proinflammatory cytokines tissue necrosis factor (TNF) and interleukin-6 (IL-6)41. Correspondingly, we found that α-Gal A-KO males had increases in circulating TNF, IL-13, IL-17A, and IL-2235. To ascertain whether sex differences impact the cytokine profiles of male and female α-Gal A-deficient rats, we simultaneously quantified the abundance of several proinflammatory cytokines in male and female animals (Fig. 2B-C, Fig. S3, S4). Overall, we confirm that KO males had significantly higher concentrations of TNF in their circulation than males with normal α-Gal A activity (Fig. 2B). α-Gal A KO females also display an increase in circulating TNF levels, however this increase did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2C; p = 0.0655). In contrast, IL-6 concentrations were not elevated in female or male α-Gal A-KO rats.

Sera from α-Gal A knockout animals of both sexes accumulate higher concentrations of α-Gal A substrates relative to wild-type animals, but knockout males accumulate several α-Gal A substrate GSLs to a greater extent than enzyme-deficient females

To evaluate the extent that increases in the number of circulating white blood cells and proinflammatory cytokines correlate with the abundance of terminal α-galactose-containing GSLs in the circulation, GSLs were isolated from the sera of α-Gal A homozygous, heterozygous, and KO females and wild-type and hemizygous KO males. Isolated GSLs were then quantified by mass spectrometry (Fig. 3A). Increases in the abundance of several terminal α-galactose-containing GSLs (e.g., Gb3, lyso-Gb3, and B-group antigen) were observed in α-Gal A-KO females but not heterozygous animals. (Fig. 3B). KO males also show significant increases in α-Gal A substrate GSLs relative to normal males (Fig. 3C). Prothrombotic platelet phenotypes correlate with the relative concentrations of α-Gal A substrate accumulation in platelets

In our previous study of male animals, platelets from α-Gal A-KO males were found to be hyper-responsive to agonist-mediated activation of PY-family receptors stimulated by ADP but not by agonist-mediated stimulation of PAR or GPVI receptors by thrombin or convulxin35. In the current study, we directly compared male and female α-Gal A-deficient animals for differences in agonist-stimulated platelet activation using light transmission aggregometry (Fig. 4A). Female animals were also assessed for thrombotic platelet phenotypes using flow cytometry-based functional (Fig. 4B) and microfluidic platelet adhesion (Fig. 4C) assays. Although α-Gal A-KO male rats exhibit increased aggregation in response to ADP, neither heterozygous nor KO females show increased aggregation after ADP stimulation (Fig. 4A and S6). Similarly, heterozygote and KO females did not demonstrate a significant difference from wild-type females in a flow-cytometry-based functional assay (Fig. 4B) or a microfluidic-based fibrinogen adhesion assay (Fig. 4C) when platelets were stimulated by ADP or convulxin. No differences in P-selectin expression levels or annexin V binding were detected between younger and older wild-type, heterozygous or KO females (Fig. S7).

Due to the sex differences observed when comparing α-Gal A-KO male and female responses in platelet-based functional assays, platelet GSL content from α-Gal A-deficient animals of both sexes was quantified by mass spectrometry to determine whether the accumulation of α-Gal A terminal-galactose-containing substrates correlated with the sex differences observed in platelet-based functional assays (Fig. 5A). α-Gal A-KO male and female animals accumulate significantly more of the terminal-galactose-containing GSLs Gb3, and lyso-Gb3 than age and sex-matched wild-type animals (Figs. 5B-C). Strikingly, however, the accumulation of the α-Gal A substrate Gb3 was nearly two-fold higher in α-Gal A-KO males (mean; 391.19 fmol) than females (mean; 203.81 fmol). In comparison, lyso-Gb3 accumulated to a similar extent in α-Gal A-KO deficient male (mean; 233.56 fmol) and female (mean; 199.08 fmol) animals (Fig. S5). Interestingly, platelets from α-Gal A heterozygote females did not accumulate significantly higher levels of terminal-galactose-containing GSLs than wild-type females with normal α-Gal A activity (Figs. 5B-C). These findings suggest that phenotypic differences observed in α-Gal A-deficient male and female animals are caused by the accumulation of Gb3 above a threshold level achieved only by α-Gal A-KO males, but not α-Gal A-KO female animals.

Globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) alters platelets in a prothrombotic manner by increasing baseline platelet activation and sensitizing platelets to agonist-mediated activation

To determine whether the Fabry disease-associated GSL Gb3 was capable of directly eliciting thrombotic platelet phenotypes, platelets isolated from male and female animals with normal α-Gal A activity were treated with pairs of acylated and deacylated GSLs that either included Gb3 (e.g., Gb3 + lyso-Gb3) or did not include Gb3 [e.g., glucosylceramide (GlcCer) and glucosylsphingosine (lyso-GlcCer)]. GlcCer was utilized as a control due to its similar ceramide-based structure. However, whereas GlcCer terminates with a single glucose molecule, Gb3 contains a trisaccharide that terminates with galactose (Fig. S8). GSL-treated platelets were then assessed for platelet activation in a flow cytometry-based functional platelet assay (Fig. 6A). Strikingly, the combination of Gb3 and lyso-Gb3 elicited a dose-dependent activation of resting platelets as assessed by fibrinogen binding (10 µM Gb3/2 µM Lyso-Gb3, fold change in fibrinogen binding, males, 5.13-fold increase; Females, 5.18-fold increase), and sensitized platelets to ADP-mediated activation relative to platelets treated with the combination of GlcCer and lyso-GlcCer (Fig. 6B). Moreover, when platelet activation assays were performed using male and female platelets isolated from wild-type animals and then treated with individual GSLs, only the addition of Gb3 significantly increased baseline platelet activation (10 µM Gb3, fold change in fibrinogen binding, Males, 6.47-fold increase; Females; 6.81-fold increase) and responsiveness to ADP (Figs. 6C, and S9). Similar results were observed when platelets with normal α-Gal A activity were treated with Gb3, or control GSLs, in the presence or absence of ADP and assessed by light-transmission aggregometry (Fig. 7A). In these experiments, the addition of Gb3 to platelets isolated from male or females with normal α-Gal A activity significantly increased the aggregation of resting platelets relative to platelets treated with the control GSL glucosylceramide (Figs. 7B-C, Fig. S10).

Discussion

Our previous studies used a Fabry rat model to identify possible mechanisms contributing to prothrombotic phenotypes in Fabry patients. In these studies, male rats lacking the enzyme ⍺-Gal A accumulated GSL substrates in their platelets, serum, and bone marrow. They also displayed a proinflammatory cytokine profile compared to animals with ⍺-Gal A activity. In addition, KO males had significant increases in several circulating white blood cell types, and their platelets were more responsive to activation by the platelet agonist ADP33,35..

Here, we expanded our previous studies in two directions. First, we characterized the extent to which ⍺-Gal A heterozygote and ⍺-Gal A-KO female rats experience prothrombotic phenotypes. Second, we define the extent to which Fabry disease-associated GSLs directly impact platelet biology to increase thrombotic risk.

In the first aspect of the study, ⍺-Gal-A-deficient female and male animals demonstrated similar disease manifestations and indicators of chronic inflammation. The histological analysis of the bone marrow from 52-week-old females stained with Griffonia simplicifolia isolectin B4, a lectin with high specificity for terminal-galactose-containing GSLs, revealed striking accumulations of glycosphingolipids in the bone marrow of females with attenuated ⍺-Gal A activity, similar to ⍺-Gal A-KO males (Fig. 1A). Both male and female Fabry animals also had chronically high levels of white blood cells in their bone marrow (Figs. 1A-B) and circulation, and displayed significantly elevated lymphocyte and monocyte counts compared to age- and sex-matched cohorts with normal ⍺-Gal A activity (Fig. 2A). Additionally, both male and female ⍺-Gal A-KO animals had elevated tumor necrosis factor concentrations in plasma, suggestive of chronic inflammation (Fig. 2B). These data are consistent with clinical studies reporting elevated TNF concentrations in Fabry patients, and studies reporting the enhancement of a feedback loop whereby elevated levels of Gb3 lead to the increased expression of tumor necrosis factor, which, in turn, further stimulates sphingolipid production31,42,43. Notably, TNF antagonists have been FDA-approved for treating several inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis and Crohn’s disease, and could be assessed for their ability to dampen systemic inflammation and attenuate similar symptomologies experienced by Fabry patients44–47..

In this study we also report that female ⍺-Gal A-KO rats had drastically different GSL profiles in their sera and platelets compared to females with normal ⍺-Gal A activity (Figs. 3 and 5). However, compared to male ⍺-Gal A-KO rats, KO females, on average, accumulated less of several Fabry-associated GSLs in their sera and platelets than age-matched males (Figs. 3, 5, and Fig. S5). Correspondingly, platelets isolated from ⍺-Gal A-deficient females were not sensitized to agonist-mediated activation or aggregation, unlike platelets isolated from hemizygous KO males (Fig. 4 and Fig. S6), suggesting that a threshold level of GSL accumulation must be met to elicit these thrombotic platelet phenotypes.

Although ⍺-Gal A heterozygote and KO female animals accumulated lower concentrations of Fabry-associated GSLs than males in our study, the thrombotic phenotypes experienced by affected heterozygote female Fabry patients may strongly depend on factors that impact residual ⍺-Gal A activity and the resultant accumulation of GSLs. For example, patterns of X-chromosome inactivation in various cell and tissue types in heterozygotic Fabry females may play a prominent role in determining the severity of clinical manifestations of the disease. Plasma concentration Gb3 ranges have been reported between approximately 3–11 µM in hemizygous males and between 1–4 µM in heterozygous female Fabry patients. Although, on average, Gb3 concentrations are lower among heterozygous females, a significant overlap exists among hemizygous male and heterozygous female cohorts in the 3–4 µM range. Additionally, plasma Gb3 concentrations in these studies did not significantly differ based on ERT treatment status48,49. Thus, retrospectively examining the correlation between plasma Gb3 levels and the incidence of thrombotic events among subgroups of Fabry patients may help to evaluate further the strength of the relationship between plasma Gb3 concentrations and thrombotic risk.

To assess the ability of Gb3 and lyso-Gb3 to directly impact platelet biology, platelets were isolated from healthy animals with normal ⍺-Gal A activity and then treated with GSLs at concentrations found among Fabry patients with classical disease presentation48,49 (10 µM Gb3 and 2 µM lyso-Gb3, or 10 µM Gb3 alone) or with control GSLs (10 µM glucocerebroside and 2 µM lyso-glucocerebroside) at the same concentrations. Strikingly, only the Fabry-associated GSL, Gb3, was sufficient to stimulate increased baseline platelet activation as assessed by fibrinogen binding (Fig. 6C) and platelet aggregation (Fig. 7B-C). Adding Gb3 alone also increased platelet responsiveness to ADP in functional platelet assays assessing fibrinogen binding (Fig. 6B). These data are remarkable in that they suggest that chronically high concentrations of the Fabry disease-associated GSL, Gb3, can directly stimulate platelet activation and increase platelet responsiveness to agonist-mediated activation.

Apart from the data presented here, gangliosides and glycosphingolipids have been shown to be capable of modifying cellular responses to extracellular signals. For example, the oligosaccharide portion of the ganglioside GM1, can transduce extracellular signals through the plasma membrane when the oligosaccharide portion of GM1 interacts with the transmembrane protein complex TrkA-NGF50, whereas, the ganglioside GM3 has been demonstrated to inhibit human epidermal growth factor receptor51. Notably, interactions between the GSL lactosylceramide and the tyrosine protein kinase Lyn have been shown to negatively regulate FcγRIIa receptors on the surface of immune cells52. Finally, Gb3 itself has been shown in vitro, to interact with the glycosphingolipid binding motif of Fas, a TNF-receptor family member important for the induction of apoptosis53. In this current study, the data suggest that the terminal and penultimate galactose residues of Gb3 are essential for eliciting prothrombotic platelet changes, as the closely related GSL glucosylceramide, which shares an identical ceramide-based structure with Gb3, but which terminates with a single glucose moiety, failed to elicit similar platelet changes.

Overall, these findings underscore the direct role of Gb3 in eliciting thrombotic platelet phenotypes, shedding a light on its potential contribution to the heightened risk of thrombosis observed in Fabry disease patients and providing further rationale to evaluate the effectiveness of platelet inhibitors in reducing thrombotic burden and the incidence of cerebrovascular and cardiovascular complications in female and male Fabry patients.

Our observations have led us to hypothesize several potential mechanisms by which Gb3 may impact platelet biology. One possibility is that Gb3 directly interacts with platelet receptors such as FcγRIIa or PY-family receptors, resulting in downstream signaling that favors platelet activation. Alternatively, as we have noted that Gb3 increases baseline platelet activation and sensitizes platelets to activation by ADP, another hypothesis is that Gb3 may alter the composition of platelet membranes, resulting in conformational changes in platelet membrane components that lead to activation signaling and increased sensitization to agonist-mediated activation. In future studies, it will be crucial to further define the mechanisms by which Gb3 affects platelet activation and to include evaluating the impact of Gb3 on platelets obtained from healthy human donors.

Methods

Animals

Male and female dark agouti (DA) rats were initially acquired from Taconic Biosciences, Inc. (Rensselaer, NY). The Fabry rat model (Dark Agouti background), completely deficient in α-galactosidase A activity, was generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, as we described previously33. The wild-type (WT), knockout (KO), and heterozygous female (HET) rats were obtained from the existing Fabry rat colony at the Medical College of Wisconsin. The rats were provided free access to chow (Purina, diet 5001) and drinking water and were maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle. All animal procedures were carried out following the recommendations in The National Research Council Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Medical College of Wisconsin (Protocol Number: AUA00004621). For this study, male and female rats 15–75 weeks-of-age were used. Rats were euthanized by compressed CO2 and bilateral pneumothorax. All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines54.

Femur isolation

Rats were euthanized, and hind-limb femurs were removed intact from the acetabulum and below the knee. Connective tissue and muscle were removed from dissected femurs, and the isolated femurs were utilized for histology and immunohistochemistry.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Rat femurs were fixed in 10% (v/v) neutral buffered formalin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 24–72 h as previously described32,33. Following fixation, femurs were treated with ethanol (Decon Laboratories, Prussia, PA, USA) for dehydration, cleared with xylene (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Hesse, Germany), and infiltrated and embedded with paraffin (Sakura, Finetek, Tokyo, Honshu, Japan). Paraffin blocks were cut into 4 μm sections before being mounted on poly-L-lysine–coated slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Antigens were visualized using streptavidin–biotin detection. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating slides to 95 °C, addition of BOND epitope retrieval solution two (AR9640; Leica, Buffalo Grove, Il, USA), and a 12-min cooldown. For lectin staining, slides were incubated with biotinylated Griffonia simplicifolia isolectin B4 (IB4, 5 μg/ml; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA), and then incubated with streptavidin-HRP and 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (Agilent, Madison, WI, USA) to detect α-galactosyl glycoconjugates. For antibody staining, slides were incubated with anti-CD68 (ED-1 clone, MAB1435, 1:100; MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA), anti-CD3 (A0452, 1:200; Agilent, Madison, WI, USA), or anti-CD41 (ab181582, 1:500; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) antibodies and areas of staining were visualized by streptavidin–biotin detection using biotinylated secondary antibodies, streptavidin-HRP, and 3,3’diaminobenzidine detection. High-resolution images were obtained with a NanoZoomer 2.0-HT digital slide scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Honshu, Japan). For immunohistology quantification, three independent sections from each animal were analyzed at 20X magnification and quantified using Image J2 version 2.14.0/1.54f. Build: c89e8500e455; https://imagej.net/.

Whole blood isolation

Whole blood (900 μL) was drawn from the tail artery using a 25-gauge needle (BD, East Rutherford, NJ, USA) into sterile syringes containing 3.2% (w/v) sodium citrate (100 μL). The blood was inverted seven times and added to sterile microfuge tubes. Following the blood draws, whole blood or blood components were immediately used for downstream assays.

Complete blood counts

Whole blood was diluted 1:7 (v/v) in DCL buffer (Sysmex, Kobe, Hyogo, Japan). Following dilution, whole blood samples were counted by Sysmex XP-300 hematological analyzer to obtain blood count values.

Platelet isolation

Whole blood was centrifuged at 250 × g for 8 min at 25 °C, and platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was transferred to a new tube. A second centrifugation was then performed at 1000 × g for 5 min at 25 °C and platelets were resuspended in 500 μL of modified Tyrode’s Solution (20 mM Hepes, 135 mM NaCl, 6 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 5.6 mM D-glucose, pH 7.40) Platelets were counted by a hematological analyzer as above, resuspended in Tyrode’s buffer at a concentration of 300,000 platelets/μL, and immediately used in platelet assays. An additional centrifugation step and resuspension of platelets in an appropriate buffer was performed as above to obtain washed platelets for subsequent biochemical analyses or cell-based assays.

Cytokine quantification

Cytokines were quantified based on manufacturer recommendations using a LegendPlex assay for rat cytokines (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA). In brief, plasma for cytokine quantification was obtained by centrifugation of whole blood for 10 min at 1,000 × g at room temperature, and plasma was immediately used to quantify circulating cytokine levels. A 96-well V-bottom polypropylene plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was then blocked (LegendPlex assay kit, Biolegend). Sample wells were then aspirated to remove buffer, and 25 μL of assay buffer and 25 μL of plasma were added to individual sample wells. Following plasma addition, antibody-coated beads were vortexed for 30 s, and 25 μL of mixed beads were added to each sample well with gentle mixing. The plate was then sealed, wrapped with aluminum foil, and placed on a shaker to incubate for two hours at 25 °C. Following primary incubation, the plate was centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 min at 25 °C. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and beads were resuspended in wash buffer and centrifuged again at 1,000 × g for 5 min at 25 °C. After removing the wash buffer, 25 μL of detection antibodies were added to each sample well. The plate was then sealed, wrapped with aluminum foil, and placed on a shaker at 800 RPM for one hour at 25 °C. Following incubation, 25 μL of SA-PE reagent was added to each well for fluorescent detection of cytokines, and the plate was placed on a shaker at 800 RPM for 30 min at 25 °C. Using a multichannel pipet, 200 μL of wash buffer was added to each well, and the plate was centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 min at 25 °C. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, beads were resuspended with 150 μL wash buffer, and flow cytometry was performed using an Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lake, NJ, USA).

Platelet activation assay

A 96-well V-bottom polypropylene plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was blocked with 1% BSA (w/v) in modified Tyrode’s Solution, and 3 × 105 platelets in modified Tyrode’s Solution with 1% BSA were added to each assay well. Activation assays were adapted from the work of Nylander and Lindahl56. Platelets were incubated with AF488 fluorescently labeled fibrinogen (Thermo Fisher Scientific, F12191) for 30 min and activated with agonist for 15 min using 30 μM ADP (Sigma-Aldrich, A2754), 5 mg/mL convulxin (Santa Cruz), or 1% BSA in modified Tyrode’s Solution as a negative control. Following platelet activation, fibrinogen binding was analyzed using an LSRII flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Platelet activation with glycosphingolipids

Following centrifugation steps during platelet isolation, platelets were resuspended in modified Tyrode’s Solution. A 96-well V-bottom polypropylene plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was blocked using modified Tyrode’s Solution. Platelets were incubated with AF488 fluorescently labeled fibrinogen (Thermo Fisher Scientific, F12191) and specified glycosphingolipid (GSL) concentration or DMSO (Thermo Fischer Scientific) vehicle for one hour at 25 °C. Following the initial incubation, 3 × 105 platelets in modified Tyrode’s Solution were added to each assay well, and platelets were activated with agonist for 15 min using 30 μM ADP (Sigma – Aldrich, A2754) or modified Tyrode’s Solution. Following platelet activation, platelets were analyzed with an LSRII flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Platelet aggregation assay

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was isolated from whole blood by centrifugation at 250 × g for eight minutes at 25 °C and removing the top layer post-centrifugation. Platelet-poor plasma (PPP) was obtained from whole blood by performing a second centrifugation of PRP at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove residual cells. PPP was used as a negative control in aggregometry studies and to baseline the aggregometer. For PRP, platelet concentration was determined using a hematological analyzer and adjusted to 300 × 103 platelets/μL with modified Tyrode’s Solution. A total of 300 μL of PRP or PPP as a negative control, was added to glass cuvettes and placed in a Chrono-log Model 700 Whole-blood/Optical Lumi-aggregometer (Chrono-log, Havertown, PA, USA). Light transmittance was recorded for 30 s before platelet-rich plasma, and PPP channels of the aggregometer were simultaneously zeroed to baseline using the PPP. ADP (30 μM) (Sigma – Aldrich, A2754) was then added to each cuvette, and light transmission was recorded for a minimum of five minutes at 37 °C.

Platelet aggregation assay with the addition of glycosphingolipids

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and platelet-poor plasma (PPP) were obtained as detailed above and adjusted to 300 × 103 platelets/μL with modified Tyrode’s Solution. A total of 300uL of PRP or PPP + DMSO (Thermo Fischer Scientific) vehicle was added to glass cuvettes and placed in a Chrono-log Model 700 Whole-blood/Optical Lumi-aggregometer (Chrono-log, Havertown, PA, USA). Light transmittance was recorded for 30 s before platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and PPP channels of the aggregometer were simultaneously zeroed to baseline using the PPP. 10 μM of designated glycosphingolipid (GSL) was then added to the PRP cuvette, and light transmission was recorded for a minimum of 10 min at 37 °C. After 10 min, 30 μM ADP (Sigma – Aldrich, A2754) was added to each cuvette, and light transmission was recorded for an additional 5 min at 37 °C.

Generation of stock solutions of commercially obtained glycosphingolipids

Glycosphingolipids (GSLs), ceramide trihexoside (Gb3), lyso-ceramide trihexoside (lyso-Gb3), glucocerebrosides, buttermilk (GC), and glucosylsphingosine, buttermilk (lyso-GC) were all obtained from Matreya (Matreya, catalog No: 1067, 1520, 1521, and 1306). Each lyophilized GSL was resuspended in DMSO (Thermo Fischer Scientific) as a 1.0 mg/mL stock solution and stored at −20 °C. For experimental use, stock solutions of GSLs were further diluted using DMSO (Thermo Fischer Scientific) as needed.

Assessment of platelet adhesion under flow conditions

A microfluidic adhesion assay was adapted from Manz et al., and we have previously detailed this assay35,57. In brief, fibrinogen (341,576; MilleporeSigma) was immobilized by adding 20 μL of fibrinogen diluted in Hepes buffer to a final concentration of 50 μg/mL in each microfluidic channel of an Ibidi μ-slide VI 0.4 chip (80,601; Ibidi, Fitchburg, WI, USA). Microfluidic chips were then incubated overnight at 4 °C inside of a 100 mm petri dish wrapped in parafilm to prevent evaporation. Following fibrinogen immobilization, each microfluidic channel was blocked for two hours at room temperature with 200 μL of 1% BSA in Hepes buffer. Whole blood was then isolated as described above and, following isolation, was incubated with 50 μM Mepacrine (Q3251; MilliporeSigma) at 25 °C for 15 min to fluorescently label platelet dense granules. To assemble the microfluidic flow system, two 16-inch sections of 0.8 mm I.D. silicone tubing (10,841, Ibidi) were connected to a straight female luer lock connector (10,825; Ibidi) on one side, and an elbow male luer lock connector (10,802, Ibidi) on the opposite side. To prevent clotting, 1% BSA in Hepes buffer was drawn through the silicone tubing and into a 20 mL BD syringe (309,661; BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The silicone tubing was then connected to the microfluidic slide using the elbow luer connectors, and the syringe was inserted into a syringe pump apparatus (New Era Pump 4000; New Era Pump Systems, Farmingdale, NY, USA). For microfluidic assays, the syringe pump was adjusted to a 25 mL/hr flow rate, and the syringe diameter setting was adjusted to 19.05 mm. Whole blood was then diluted 1:1 with 1% BSA in Hepes buffer and recalcified with 10 μM CaCl2/MgCl2. Following dilution and recalcification, whole blood was activated with 30 μM ADP, and flow through the microfluidic chamber was started immediately following platelet activation. For imaging of platelet adherence on immobilized fibrinogen, one image was collected every 10 s using an EVOS inverted microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 20X magnification for five minutes. The number of adherent platelets was then analyzed at 20X magnification and quantified using Image J software55.

Quantification of lyso-glycosphingolipids

We have previously developed a method to quantitatively profile lyso-type glycosphingolipids following N-acetylation of the free amine group of sphingosine33. Briefly, purified glycosphingolipids were N-acetylated, dried, and then subjected to permethylation. The N-acetylated and O-permethylated glycosphingolipids containing lyso-glycosphingolipids were then infused for nanospray ionization mass spectrometry (NSI-MS) with reference to the standard for quantitative analysis. This approach was used to compare glycosphingolipids and lyso-GSLs isolated from plasma or platelets. To detect lyso-glycosphingolipids species in the rat samples, TIM profiles were filtered for neutral loss of C18-sphingosine (Δ mass = 351, singly charged) to evaluate the concentration of lyso-GSL species33.

Mass spectrometry

According to the previously described method of Anumula and Taylor, 1–3 µg of tC18-purified GSLs were permethylated with 12C-methyliodide before MS analysis58. Maltotetraose oligosaccharide (Dp4) was permethylated with 13C-methyliodide and spiked into permethylated GSLs as a reference standard for GSL quantification59. Permethylated GSLs were reconstituted in methanol/2-propanol/1-propanol/water (16:3:3:2 by volume) containing 1 mM NaOH for direct infusion into the mass spectrometer. Ten picomoles of 13C-permethylated Dp4 standard was added into the reconstituted permethylated GSL samples, and the mixture was analyzed by direct infusion into an ion trap mass spectrometer (Orbitrap Velos; Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a nanoelectrospray source, syringe flow rate of 0.6 mL/min and temperature set to 210°C60–62. The instrument was tuned in positive ion mode using permethylated GSLs and Dp4 standards. For fragmentation by collision-induced dissociation (CID) in MS/MS and MSn, a normalized collision energy of 35% was used. Detection and absolute quantification of GSLs were accomplished using total ion mapping (TIM) and neutral loss scan (NL scan) functionalities of the Xcalibur software package version 2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), as previously described60,63. For TIM, the m/z range from 600 to 2000 was scanned in 2.4 mass unit windows with an overlap of 0.4 mass units. GSLs were identified as singly, doubly, and triply charged, sodiated species [M + Na] in positive mode, and peak intensities for all charge states were deconvoluted and summed for quantification. The major MS/MS fragment ions produced by CID of GSL preparations correspond to neutral loss of the ceramide moiety, leaving an intact GSL oligosaccharide ion for subsequent fragmentation in MSn. Therefore, an NL scan workflow was designed to acquire MSn fragmentation when an MS/MS profile contained a fragment ion with an m/z equivalent to losing the most prevalent ceramide moiety. After the data-dependent acquisition, the workflow returned to the full MS, excluding the parent ion, which was just fragmented, and chose the peak of the next highest intensity for MS/MS and MSn analysis. Using this data-dependent acquisition workflow, GSL glycan profiles and MSn sequencing were rapidly acquired for complex mixtures of GSLs63. Permethylated GSLs were analyzed from multiple biological replicates from plasma (n = 3 for WT male and female, n = 3 for KO male and female, and n = 3 homozygous female) along with platelets (n = 3 for WT male and female, n = 3 for KO male and female, and n = 3 homozygous female). For all samples, GSLs were characterized by MS. The MS-based glycomics data generated in these analyses and the associated annotations are presented in accordance with the MIRAGE standards, the Athens Guidelines, and the broadly accepted SNFG symbol nomenclature for glycans64–66. The MS raw data is deposited into the publicly available data repository tool, GlycoPost67; the accession number is (GPST000428).
