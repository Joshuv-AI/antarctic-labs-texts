Low-Mach-number limit of a compressible two-phase flow system with algebraic closure
Cassandre Lebot

Abstract
We analyse a bi-fluid isentropic compressible Navier-Stokes system with barotropic pressure laws in a two-phase framework with equal pressure and single velocity. We focus on the rigorous analysis of the low Mach number limit under well-prepared initial data. Our main result shows that, as the Mach number tends to zero, the partial densities converge to constant states while the velocity field converges to a divergence-free vector field, and we recover the incompressible non-homogenous fluid system. The volume fractions remain nontrivial and are transported by the limit flow. Our method is based on the introduction of suitable modulated quantities and a novel relative entropy functional adapted to the two-phase structure. The key novelty lies in an original comparison of L 1 L^{1} and L 2 L^{2} norms of the partial densities, exploiting the special structure of the bi-fluid system, together with a variant of Csiszar-Kullback-Pinsker inequality. It allows us to rigorously justify the convergence of weak solutions of the compressible system toward solutions of the corresponding incompressible limit system in the low Mach number regime.

Introduction

Compressible multiphase flow models are a fundamental class of nonlinear systems that arise in fluid mechanics and engineering applications. In many formulations, each phase satisfies its own balance laws, and additional closure relations are required to determine thermodynamic quantities and interfacial effects. Classical two-fluid models developed by Ishii and Hibiki
[
22
]
, as well as the hyperbolic model introduced by Baer and Nunziato
[
1
]
, exemplify this framework. Depending on the modeling assumptions, closures may be differential, introducing additional evolution equations, or algebraic, in which the pressure is expressed as a function of partial densities, volume fractions, or other state variables. In particular, algebraic isentropic closures induce a nontrivial coupling between the phases, which has important implications for the analytical structure of the system. From a mathematical perspective, these models have been studied in the compressible PDE community; see, for instance, the handbook chapter by DD. Bresch, B. Desjardins, J.M. Ghidaglia, E. Grenier and M. Hillairet
[
3
]
, and the references therein. These works provide a mathematical formulation of the models, establish their main structural properties, and contain some of the first analytical results, marking the emergence of sustained interest in such systems within the theory of compressible PDEs.

The growing interest in compressible two-fluid flows with viscosity and weak-regularity data has led to significant mathematical advances, particularly in the viscous framework. Fundamental results on the existence of finite-energy weak solutions were established by Bresch, Mucha, and Zatorska
[
6
]
as well as by Vasseur, Wen, and Yu
[
45
]
, highlighting suitable dissipation mechanisms. In the same spirit, Novotný and Pokorný
[
39
]
and Novotný
[
38
]
developed a weak solution theory of compressible bi-fluid models, while Jin, Kwon, Nečasová, and Novotný
[
24
]
introduced dissipative turbulent solutions and investigated their stability. Weak-strong uniqueness properties were further analyzed by Jin and Novotný
[
25
]
and by Li and Zatorska
[
35
]
. Moreover, Li and Zatorska
[
34
]
addressed weak solutions to the compressible inviscid two-fluid model, emphasizing the additional analytical difficulties arising in the absence of viscosity, whereas Piasecki and Zatorska
[
41
]
obtained maximal regularity results in the viscous setting. The large-time behavior of solutions with large initial data was studied by Li, Sun, and Zatorska
[
33
]
. Finally, the recent work of Li, Wang, and Zhang
[
31
]
established the non-existence of classical solutions in the presence of vacuum, further illustrating the analytical complexity of such models. Note that recently a preprint by Li, Lukáčová-Medvid’ová, Pokornỳ and Zatorska (see
[
32
]
) related to weak-strong uniqueness for two-phase systems has been posted on ArXiv independently of ours. These contributions, among others, form a coherent and evolving theory for compressible two-fluid flows.

In this work, we consider a compressible isentropic two-phase model with an algebraic closure and a single common velocity field for both phases. This assumption simplifies the structure of the system while retaining the essential coupling induced by the pressure law. After nondimensionalization, the Mach number
\varepsilon
appears explicitly in front of the pressure gradient, producing a singular perturbation as
\varepsilon\to 0
. We are interested in the rigorous justification of the corresponding low Mach number limit under well-prepared initial data.

For single-phase flows, the low Mach number limit is by now well understood. For the compressible Euler and Navier–Stokes equations, convergence toward incompressible dynamics under well-prepared initial data has been established in various functional settings; see, for instance, the works of Klainerman and Majda
[
27
]
, and subsequent developments in the context of symmetric hyperbolic systems and viscous flows.

Without aiming at exhaustiveness, we mention the following representative contributions. In the framework of weak solutions, the low Mach number limit has been justified in
[
36
]
,
[
15
]
,
[

]
, and
[

]

[

[

]
. A comprehensive treatment of the compressible Navier–Stokes system with constant viscosities, with particular emphasis on the relative entropy method as a stability and convergence tool, is given in
[
18
]
. For more complex systems, such as compressible Navier–Stokes equations with density-dependent viscosities, the notion of relative entropy had to be revisited and adapted; see, for instance,
[
7
]
and
[
5
]
. We also mention works developing the relative entropy framework in related contexts, such as
[
17
]
and
[
43
]
.

In contrast, significantly fewer results are available for multiphase flow models, even in simplified configurations. Only recently has attention been devoted to the formal derivation of low Mach number limits for various two-phase systems, including models with algebraic or PDE closures, as well as formulations involving one or two velocities. In these works, asymptotic expansions are typically performed as the Mach number tends to zero; see, for instance,
[
44
]
and
[
29
]
. We may also refer to Fanelli et al
[
16
]
for related incompressible limits in a reduced compressible MHD system.

In the present paper, in the two-phase setting with equal pressure, we introduce several relevant modulated quantities and propose a relative entropy functional based on a suitable combination of them. This approach requires the development of appropriate comparison tools in order to strengthen certain convergence properties.

Our main result establishes that, in the low Mach limit and under well-prepared initial data, the partial densities converge to constant values, while the velocity field converges to a divergence-free vector field. The volume fractions remain variable and are transported by the limit flow, so the mixture density evolves through advection of the phase fractions. This leads to a variable-density incompressible system in which the mixture density depends only on the transported volume fractions.

1.1
Bi-fluid compressible system with algebraic closure

In the present paper, we analyse a bi-fluid isentropic compressible Navier-Stokes system for barotropic pressures.
More precisely, denoting the phases by
+
and
-
, we are considering the following system.

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}u)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}((\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})u)+\operatorname{div}((\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})u\otimes u)+\nabla(\alpha_{+}p_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}p_{-}(\rho_{-}))\\
&\quad=\mu\Delta u+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}u,\\
&p_{i}(\rho_{i})=(\rho_{i})^{\gamma_{i}},\qquad\rho_{i}\geqslant 0,\qquad i=+,-,\\
&p_{+}(\rho_{+})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}),\\
&\alpha_{+}+\alpha_{-}=1,\qquad 0\leqslant\alpha_{+}\leqslant 1,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

where
\rho_{i}
,
p_{i}
,
\alpha_{i}
the density, pressure and volume fraction of phase
i
. The real numbers
\mu
and
\lambda
are the constant shear and bulk viscosities verifying, as usual,

\mu>0\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda+2\mu>0.

Let us notice that, thanks to the algebraic closure, the pressure term in the momentum equation can be simplified:

\nabla(\alpha_{+}p_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}p_{-}(\rho_{-}))=\nabla(\alpha_{+}p_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}p_{+}(\rho_{+}))=\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+}).

In this system, we restrict ourselves to the case of a common velocity field, denoted by
u
. Physically, this corresponds to a regime of rapid mechanical relaxation between the phases, where pressure equilibrium is quickly achieved. Mathematically, such models can be rigorously derived as asymptotic limits of more general two-velocities, two-pressures systems, using homogenization (see, e.g., Bresch et al.
[
2
]
) or pressure-relaxation arguments (see
[
9
]
,
[
8
]
). This assumption considerably simplifies the structure of the equations and is standard in the literature, see for instance
[
39
]
. Allowing for two distinct velocities would lead to a substantially more intricate coupling mechanism and raises major analytical difficulties; the rigorous study of such models remains a challenging and largely open problem.

The system is endowed with the Dirichlet boundary condition

u(t,x)\lvert_{\partial\Omega}=0.

(1.2)

on
(0,T)\times\partial\Omega
, where
\Omega
is a sufficiently smooth bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
T>0
is arbitrary large.

Remark 1.1
.

The analysis can also be extended to periodic domains or to two-dimensional settings, possibly under additional assumptions on the initial data.

Let us denote by
\varepsilon
the Mach number. The incompressible limit is the limit where the speed of the fluid becomes negligible compared to the speed of sound. Thus we reset the flow speed to the scale by replacing
u
with
\varepsilon u^{\varepsilon}
. A particle will move a distance of order
1/\varepsilon
during a time of order
1/\varepsilon^{2}
, meaning that the relevant time scale is
1/\varepsilon^{2}
. This is why we can use a change of variable like
t^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{2}t
and
x^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon x
, with
t^{\varepsilon}
and
x^{\varepsilon}
of order
1
(see
[
12
]
). Thus, we scale
u
and
\rho
in the following way

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\varepsilon u^{\varepsilon}(t^{\varepsilon},x^{\varepsilon}),\quad\rho(t,x)=\rho^{\varepsilon}(t^{\varepsilon},x^{\varepsilon}).

We can rewrite System (
1.1
) in the following form:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon})=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon})=0,\\
&\partial_{t}((\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon})u^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}((\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon})u^{\varepsilon}\otimes u^{\varepsilon})+\boldsymbol{\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}}\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon})\\
&\hskip 199.16928pt=\mu\Delta u^{\varepsilon}+(\mu+\lambda)\nabla\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon},\\
&p_{i}(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon})=(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon})^{\gamma_{i}},\qquad\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geqslant 0,\qquad i=+,-\\
&p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}),\\
&\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}=1,\qquad 0\leqslant\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\leqslant 1,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.3)

still considering the Dirichlet boundary condition (
1.2
).

As usual in the mathematical studies of bi-fluid models, we introduce the fractional masses
R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}:=\alpha_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}
, the mixture density by
\rho^{\varepsilon}:=R_{+}^{\varepsilon}+R_{-}^{\varepsilon}
and the momentum by
m^{\varepsilon}:=\rho^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}
.

Remark 1.2
.

For
0\leqslant R_{+}^{\varepsilon}\leqslant\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}
and
m^{\varepsilon}
given, if
\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}>0
, we may define
\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}=R_{+}^{\varepsilon}/\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}\in[0,1]
. Otherwise, we set
\alpha_{+}=1/2
on
\{\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}=0\}
. Next we define
\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}=1-\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\in[0,1]
,
\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}=(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon})^{\gamma_{-}/\gamma_{+}}\geqslant 0
(by the equality of pressures),
R_{-}^{\varepsilon}=\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}
and
\rho^{\varepsilon}=R_{+}^{\varepsilon}+R_{-}^{\varepsilon}
. If
\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}>0
, we get that
\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon},\rho^{\varepsilon}>0
and we may define the velocity
u^{\varepsilon}=m^{\varepsilon}/\rho^{\varepsilon}
. Otherwise, we set
u^{\varepsilon}=0
on
\{\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}=0\}
.

For any functions
(\rho_{+,0}^{\varepsilon},R_{+,0}^{\varepsilon},m_{0}^{\varepsilon})\in L^{\gamma_{+}}\times L^{\gamma_{+}}\times L^{1}
satisfying the following compatibility conditions

0\leqslant R_{+,0}^{\varepsilon}\leqslant\rho_{+,0}^{\varepsilon}\text{\ a.e.},\quad\sqrt{\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}

(1.4)

where we have defined by the previous remark
\alpha_{\pm,0}^{\varepsilon}\in[0,1]
,
\rho_{-,0}^{\varepsilon}\geqslant 0
,
\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}\geqslant 0
and
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}
, we consider a global weak solution “à la Leray”, see Definition
2.1
for the precise meaning.

System (
1.3
) is a particular case of the system studied by
[
39
]
for which they obtain the existence of global-in-time finite-energy weak solutions for large initial data, but adding some conditions on
\gamma_{\pm}
. Note that
[
6
]
also gives a proof of the existence of global-in-time weak solutions for a semi-stationary bifluid system when
\gamma_{+}\neq\gamma_{-}
.

1.2
Bi-fluid incompressible system

Let us now investigate the Mach-number limit. As shown in
[
29
]
, when we let the Mach number go to zero in (
1.3
), we obtain formally the following non-homogeneous viscous incompressible Navier-Stokes equations:

\begin{cases}\rho_{+}=C_{0}^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}}},\qquad\rho_{-}=C_{0}^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{-}}},\\
\operatorname{div}u=0,\\
\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}+(u\cdot\nabla)\alpha_{+}=0,\\
\left(\rho_{+}\alpha_{+}+\rho_{-}\alpha_{-}\right)\left(\partial_{t}u+(u\cdot\nabla)u\right)+\nabla\Pi=\mu\Delta u,\\
\alpha_{+}+\alpha_{-}=1,\end{cases}

(1.5)

where
C_{0}>0
is a real number and
u
satisfies the Dirichlet boundary condition (
1.2
).
Let us notice that the first line implies the pressure equality:

p_{+}(\rho_{+})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}).

The densities
\rho_{\pm}
are now constant, and the continuity equations on
(\alpha_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{\pm}^{\varepsilon})
became a transport equation on
\alpha_{\pm}
by a divergence free vector field. It is then clear that the initial assumption
\alpha_{\pm,0}\in[0,1]
remains true for all time
t>0
.

Denoting again the mixture density by
\rho:=\rho_{+}\alpha_{+}+\rho_{-}\alpha_{-}
we get the classical non-homogeneous viscous incompressible Navier-Stokes equations where
\rho
satisfies the transport equation by
u
.

In the case of nonhomogeneous incompressible flows with constant viscosities, the existence of weak and strong solutions has been investigated in several works. The existence of weak solutions was first established by Kazhikhov
[
26
]
. Concerning strong solutions, Ladyzhenskaya and Solonnikov
[
28
]
proved local existence, together with global existence for small initial data (and global solvability in two space dimensions). In sufficiently smooth domains, Lemoine
[
30
]
obtained local strong regular solutions, which are global for small regular data. Okamoto
[
40
]
proved existence and uniqueness of local weak solutions and obtained global solutions in three dimensions under smallness assumptions on the initial data. Simon
[
42
]
established the existence of global-in-time weak solutions under suitable weak assumptions on the initial data, and proved global strong solvability provided the initial density is not too small (and sufficiently regular). The solvability of nonstationary problems for nonhomogeneous incompressible fluids, as well as convergence in the vanishing viscosity limit, was studied by Itoh
[
23
]
. Choe and Kim
[
10
]
established local existence and uniqueness of strong solutions in three dimensions, including situations where the initial density vanishes on an open subset of the domain. More recently, Danchin
[
13
]
developed a well-posedness theory in critical Besov spaces and proved the existence of global-in-time unique solutions under suitable smallness assumptions on the initial data.

Among the vast existing literature, let us state a result of local existence of strong solutions of non-homogeneous viscous incompressible Navier-Stokes equations.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
be some initial data such that
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{s}(\Omega)
,
\operatorname{div}u_{0}=0
in
\Omega
and
\rho_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, with
\overline{C}\geqslant\rho_{0}\geqslant\underline{C}>0
for some constants
\overline{C},\underline{C}
.
Then there exists
T>0
and a (unique) solution
(\rho,u,\Pi)
of (
1.5
) such taht
0<\underline{C}\leqslant\rho\leqslant\overline{C}
that satisfy

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap H^{s}(\Omega)),\qquad\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\nabla^{s}u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega)),\qquad\nabla^{s+1}u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{3/2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\rho\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),\qquad\sqrt{\rho}u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\nabla\Pi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega)\cap W^{s-1,3/2}(\Omega)).

Remark 1.4
.

When we consider
(\rho,u)
a regular solution to non-homogeneous viscous incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, it is clear that we may construct a solution of (
1.5
). Indeed, solving
\rho=\rho_{+}\alpha_{+}+\rho_{-}\alpha_{-}
and
1=\alpha_{+}+\alpha_{-}
, we simply define

\alpha_{+}=\frac{\rho-\rho_{-}}{\rho_{+}-\rho_{-}}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{-}=-\frac{\rho-\rho_{+}}{\rho_{+}-\rho_{-}}

which is well defined for
\gamma_{+}\neq\gamma_{-}
, as
C_{0}>0
. We observe that
\alpha_{\pm}
satisfy the transport equation. Assuming initially
\alpha_{\pm,0}\in[\underline{a},\bar{a}]\subset(0,1)
, such a property then remains true. Let us notice that we can express
\underline{C}
and
\overline{C}
in terms of
\underline{a},\overline{a},C_{0},\gamma_{\pm}
.

Remark 1.5
.

We will consider
s=3
. The existence result stated above is not optimal in terms of regularity for our subsequent arguments. For our purposes, a lower level of regularity would suffice, but this is not relevant here.

1.3
Main theorem

We can now state the main result of this paper, where we denote the fractional masses of the limit system by
R_{\pm 0}=\alpha_{\pm,0}\rho_{\pm,0}

Let

\gamma_{\pm}\geqslant 2

\alpha_{\pm,0}

\gamma_{+}\neq\gamma_{-}
and
0<\underline{a}\leqslant\alpha_{\pm,0}\leqslant\overline{a}<1

(\alpha_{\pm,0},u_{0})

1.3

For each

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}R_{\pm,0}^{\varepsilon}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}R_{\pm,0}\,dx>0.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}|u^{\varepsilon}_{0}|^{2}\Big)(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{+,0}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+,0}^{\varepsilon\gamma_{+}}(x)}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{+}-1)}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{-,0}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-,0}^{\varepsilon\gamma_{-}}(x)}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{-}-1)}\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant C,

\varepsilon\to 0

Relative entropy

In this section, we introduce the relative entropy functional that will be our main tool to prove Theorem

2.1
Definition

We derive a relative entropy between a weak solution
(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{+},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{-},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{+},u^{\varepsilon})

1.3
), in the sense of Definition
2.1
below, and any other state
(\rho_{+},\rho_{-},\alpha_{+},u)

denoted by
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{+},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{-},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{+},u^{\varepsilon}|\rho_{+},\rho_{-},\alpha_{+},u)
and defined as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{+},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{-},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{+},u^{\varepsilon}|\rho_{+},\rho_{-},\alpha_{+},u\Big)=&\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{+}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{+}+\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{-}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{-})|u^{\varepsilon}-u|^{2}\\
&+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}H(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}|\rho_{+})+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}H(\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}|\rho_{-}),\end{split}

(2.1)

with, for

\displaystyle H(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}|\rho_{i}):=\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}-1}\left((\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon})^{\gamma_{i}}-\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}-\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-1}(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{i})\right).

(2.2)

In order to control the
L^{1}
norm of the partial densities and close the Gronwall argument required for the proof of Theorem

, we introduce the following functional

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{+},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{-},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{+},u^{\varepsilon}|\rho_{+},\rho_{-},\alpha_{+},u\Big)=\int_{\Omega}R_{+}^{\varepsilon}\ln_{+}\left(\frac{R_{+}^{\varepsilon}}{R_{+}}\right)+\int_{\Omega}R_{-}^{\varepsilon}\ln_{+}\left(\frac{R_{-}^{\varepsilon}}{R_{-}}\right).\\
\end{split}

(2.3)

and we set
\ln_{+}s=\max(0,\ln s)
.

Finally, we define the total relative entropy functional:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{+},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{-},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{+},u^{\varepsilon}|\rho_{+},\rho_{-},\alpha_{+},u\Big)=({\mathcal{E}}_{1}+{\mathcal{E}}_{2})\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{+},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{-},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{+},u^{\varepsilon}|\rho_{+},\rho_{-},\alpha_{+},u\Big).

In this definition, we always set
\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}=1-\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}
and
\alpha_{-}=1-\alpha_{+}

The goal of the next subsection is to study the evolution of this entropy for the weak solution of System (
1.3

Definition 2.1
.

is a finite energy weak solution of the compressible two-phase Navier-Stokes system (

associated with the initial data

\displaystyle\rho_{\pm}^{\varepsilon},\ \alpha_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\in C_{\text{weak}}([0,T];L^{\gamma_{\pm}}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\gamma_{\pm}}(\Omega)),

the continuity equation:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\phi)(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}(\alpha_{i,0}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{i,0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\phi(0,x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{t}\phi+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\phi)\Big)(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s,

for a.e.

,

the momentum equation:

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}\phi+\rho^{\varepsilon}(u^{\varepsilon}\otimes u^{\varepsilon}):\nabla\phi+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon})^{\gamma_{+}}\operatorname{div}\phi\Big)(s,x)dxds

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(t)-{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(0)=&E(t)-E(0)\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}|u|^{2}\Big)(t,\cdot)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}|u|^{2}\Big)(0,\cdot) \\
&-\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\cdot u\Big)(t,\cdot)+\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\cdot u\Big)(0,\cdot)\\
&-\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{i}-1)}\Big(\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}+\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-1}(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{i})\Big)(t,\cdot)\\
&+\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{i}-1)}\Big(\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}+\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-1}(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{i})\Big)(0,\cdot).\end{split}

(2.7)

Let us consider mass equations with
\phi=\frac{|u|^{2}}{2}
and add the two, we get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}|u|^{2}\Big)(t,\cdot)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}|u|^{2}\Big)(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\varepsilon}\left(\partial_{t}|u|^{2}+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla|u|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\varepsilon}u\cdot\Big(\partial_{t}u+(u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)u\Big).

Let us notice that we need, here and below, to check that all terms are well defined by using the regularity properties of the functions depending on
\varepsilon
(see Definition
2.1
) and the smooth functions (see Theorem
1.3
).

Let us now use the momentum equation by taking
\phi=u
, we get:

-\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\cdot u\Big)(t,\cdot)+\int_{\Omega}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\cdot u\Big)(0,\cdot)=-\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\cdot(\partial_{t}u+(u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)u)\\
-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon\gamma_{+}}\operatorname{div}u+\mu\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\nabla u^{\varepsilon}:\nabla u+(\mu+\lambda)\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\left(\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon}\right)(\operatorname{div}u),

where we can replace

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon\gamma_{+}}\operatorname{div}u

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon})\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon\gamma_{+}}\operatorname{div}u

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon})+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}))\operatorname{div}u.

We can also rewrite

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{t}u+(u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)u)\cdot(u-u^{\varepsilon})=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{t}u+(u\cdot\nabla)u)\cdot(u-u^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle+\rho^{\varepsilon}\Big(((u^{\varepsilon}-u)\cdot\nabla)u\Big)\cdot(u-u^{\varepsilon}).

By the equality of the pressures and the fact that
\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}=1
, we have

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{i}-1)}\Big(\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}-\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-1}\rho_{i}\Big)=\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}}

hence

-\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{i}-1)}\Big(\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}-\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-1}\rho_{i}\Big)(t,\cdot)+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{i}-1)}\Big(\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}-\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-1}\rho_{i})\Big)(0,\cdot)\\
=\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon})\partial_{t}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}}=\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma-1}\partial_{t}\rho_{i}

For
i=+,-
, remark now that the mass equation with
\phi=\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-1}
gives

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{i}-1)}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-1}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{i}-1)}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-1}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big(\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+(u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla)\rho_{i}\Big).

We then have obtained

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{i}-1)}\Big(\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}+\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-1}(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{i})\Big)(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{i}-1)}\Big(\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}+\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-1}(\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{i})\Big)(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\gamma_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-2}(\rho_{i}-\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon})\partial_{t}\rho_{i}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-2}(\alpha_{i}\rho_{i}u-\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon})\cdot\nabla\rho_{i}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-2}\alpha_{i}\rho_{i}u\cdot\nabla\rho_{i}

Using again that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\gamma_{i}\rho_{i}^{\gamma_{i}-2}\alpha_{i}\rho_{i}u\cdot\nabla\rho_{i}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\alpha_{i}u\cdot\nabla p_{i}(\rho_{i})=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}p_{+}(\rho_{+})\operatorname{div}u

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}p_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}p_{-}(\rho_{-}))\operatorname{div}u

where we have used that

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(t)-{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(0)&\leqslant-\mu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t-\left(\lambda+\mu\right)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}\right)(\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u)\cdot(u-u^{\varepsilon})\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}\right)(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\cdot\nabla u\cdot(u-u^{\varepsilon})\\

\end{split}

Proposition 2.4
.

If
(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{+},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{-},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{+},u^{\varepsilon})

We now study the evolution of the entropy component (

Proposition 2.5
.

If
(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{+},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{-},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{+},u^{\varepsilon})
is a weak solution of System (
1.3
), defined as in Definition (
2.1
), and if
(\rho_{+},\rho_{-},\alpha_{+},u)
is a solution of System (
1.5
), verifying the regularity properties of the limit solution, see Theorem

, then there exits
C>0
depending on
\|\nabla R_{i}\|_{L^{3}}
such that

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}R_{i}^{\varepsilon}\ln\Big(\frac{R_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{R_{i}}\Big)(t)-\int_{\Omega}R_{i}^{\varepsilon}\ln\Big(\frac{R_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{R_{i}}\Big)(0)&\leqslant C\sum_{i=+,-}\int_{0}^{t}\|R_{i}^{\varepsilon}-R_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\

(2.10)

Proof.

We want to take
\phi=\ln R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}
in the continuity equation of Definition
2.1
. To justify the use of a possibly non-admissible test function, we appeal to the theory of renormalized solutions. Since

u^{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

we regularize the continuity equation by convolution.
At the regularized level, the quantities are smooth enough to perform the computations rigorously. This procedures is classical, in particular, it is used by P.-L. Lions in the barotropic compressible Navier–Stokes system to obtain compactness of the density. It requires

\rho\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

if one wishes to avoid the truncation–regularization method of E. Feireisl.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(R_{i}^{\varepsilon}\ln R_{i}^{\varepsilon})(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}R_{i,0}^{\varepsilon}(x)\ln R_{i,0}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(R_{i}^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{t}\ln R_{i}^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\ln R_{i}^{\varepsilon})\Big)(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s,

for a.a.

and
i=+,-
. Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(R_{i}^{\varepsilon}\ln R_{i}^{\varepsilon})(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}R_{i,0}^{\varepsilon}(x)\ln R_{i,0}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\Big(\int_{\Omega}R_{i}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}R_{i,0}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}R_{i}^{\varepsilon}\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon}(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s=0.

(2.11)

We multiply now the mass equation of
R_{\pm}
by
R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}/R_{\pm}
and we can write, for
i=+,-
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}R_{i}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}R_{i,0}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{R_{i}^{\varepsilon}}{R_{i}}\Big(\partial_{t}R_{i}+u\cdot\nabla R_{i})\Big)(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.

Therefore

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}R_{i}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}R_{i,0}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}R_{i}^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{t}\ln R_{i}+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\ln_{+}R_{i})(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(R_{i}^{\varepsilon}(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\nabla\ln R_{i}\Big)(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(R_{i}^{\varepsilon}\operatorname{div}u\Big)(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

Then we use the mass equation of
R_{i}^{\varepsilon}
in the weak sense taking
\phi=\ln R_{i}
, we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}R_{i}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}R_{i,0}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&=\int_{\Omega}(R_{i}^{\varepsilon}\ln R_{i})(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}R_{i,0}^{\varepsilon}(x)\ln R_{i,0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(R_{i}^{\varepsilon}(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\nabla\ln R_{i}\Big)(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(R_{i}^{\varepsilon}\operatorname{div}u\Big)(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

(2.12)

Adding (
2.11
) and (

By symmetry, we have the similar result for

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\gamma>0
. For all

(3.4)

\displaystyle d_{2}a_{2}^{\gamma}+(a_{2}-1)=0.

Moreover, there exists a constant

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

For each

, we define the function

F_{d_{i}}(x):=d_{i}x^{\gamma}+x-1.

The function
F_{d_{i}}
is strictly increasing with respect to

. Moreover, we have

F_{d_{i}}(0)=-1
and
F_{d_{i}}(1)=d_{i}>0
.
By continuity, there exists a unique solution

.
We denote this solution by
a_{i}
for
i=1,2

We then define the function
g

for all

with respect to

g
is decreasing with respect to
d_{i}

First, consider the case where

sufficiently large such that

Moreover, since

|a_{1}-a_{2}|\leqslant 1\leqslant C|d_{1}-d_{2}|.

Now consider the case where

is decreasing, it follows that

. We then introduce the compact set

and and define the function

For any

Hence, we have shown that there exists a constant

3.2
Csiszár-Kullback-Pinsker-type inequality

We will now present an inequality close to the Csiszar-Kullback-Pinsker inequality.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
f
a nonnegative and
g
a positive real functions in
L^{1}
such that
\int f=\int g
. Then

\|f-g\|_{L^{1}}\leqslant\int f\ln_{+}\frac{f}{g}

and

\int f\Big|\ln\frac{f}{g}\Big|\leqslant 2\int f\ln_{+}\frac{f}{g}

Proof.

As

\int_{\{g<f\}}f-g=-\int_{\{g\geqslant f\}}f-g

we have

\|f-g\|_{L^{1}}=\int_{\{g<f\}}f-g+\int_{\{g\geqslant f\}}g-f=2\int_{\{g<f\}}f-g=2\int_{\{g<f\}}g\Big(\frac{f}{g}-1\Big).

By convexity of
h(r)=r\ln r
we have
r-1\leqslant r\ln r
for all
r>0
, hence

\|f-g\|_{L^{1}}\leqslant 2\int_{\{g<f\}}g\frac{f}{g}\ln\frac{f}{g}=2\int f\ln_{+}\frac{f}{g}

which gives the first result.

For the second inequality, we compute

-\int_{\{g\geqslant f\}}f\ln\frac{f}{g}=\int_{\{g\geqslant f>0\}}f(\ln g-\ln f)\leqslant\int_{\{g\geqslant f>0\}}f\frac{1}{f}(g-f)\leqslant\|f-g\|_{L^{1}}

and we end this proof by applying the first inequality.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

We will apply the previous proposition for

4.4
)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\varepsilon}(u-u^{\varepsilon})\cdot\nabla u\cdot(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant c\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{\varepsilon}\left\lvert u^{\varepsilon}-u\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant c\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\Big(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{+},\rho^{\varepsilon}_{-},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{+},u^{\varepsilon}|\rho_{+},\rho_{-},\alpha_{+},u\Big).

Now we focus on the term (
4.3
). Recalling that
\rho^{\varepsilon}-\rho=\sum_{i=+,-}R_{i}^{\varepsilon}-R_{i}
, we use Hölder inequality (
1/2+1/3+1/6=1
), to get

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\Omega}\frac{R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}-R_{\pm}}{\rho}\Delta u(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\lVert\frac{1}{\rho}\Delta u\rVert_{L^{3}(\Omega)}\lVert u-u^{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{6}(\Omega)}\left(\int_{\Omega}\lvert R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}-R_{\pm}\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Therefore, using Young’s inequality with an arbitrary
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mu\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}-\alpha_{\pm}\rho_{\pm}}{\rho}\Delta u(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{\delta}\lVert\frac{1}{\rho}\Delta u\rVert_{L^{3}(\Omega)}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\lvert R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}-R_{\pm}\rvert^{2}+C\delta\lVert u-u^{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Using Sobolev injections and Poincaré inequality for the second term

\displaystyle\lVert u-u^{\varepsilon}\rVert_{L^{6}(\Omega)}^{2}\leqslant C\lVert\nabla(u-u^{\varepsilon})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Finally, using Proposition
3.3
and Corollary
3.6
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mu\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}-\alpha_{\pm}\rho_{\pm}}{\rho}\Delta u(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{\delta}\varepsilon^{2}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}+\frac{C}{\delta}{\mathcal{E}}_{2}+\delta C\lVert\nabla(u-u^{\varepsilon})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

We end by finding an estimate for the term (
4.5
). We have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\Omega}-\frac{\rho^{\varepsilon}}{\rho}\nabla\Pi(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho-\rho^{\varepsilon}}{\rho}\nabla\Pi(u-u^{\varepsilon})-\nabla\Pi(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\frac{\rho-\rho^{\varepsilon}}{\rho}\nabla\Pi(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert+\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla\Pi(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert.

For the first term we can use the same method as we just did previously to find an estimate for (
4.3
), where now
\nabla\Pi
replaces
\Delta u
. We have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\frac{\rho-\rho^{\varepsilon}}{\rho}\nabla\Pi(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{C}{\delta}\varepsilon^{2}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}+\frac{C}{\delta}{\mathcal{E}}_{2}+\delta C\lVert\nabla(u-u^{\varepsilon})\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Then for the second term we just use the Poincaré inequality:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla\Pi(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\lVert\nabla\Pi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\delta C\lVert\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Therefore, we have the following inequality for
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(t)-{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(0)

\displaystyle+\mu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\right\rvert^{2}+(\lambda+\mu)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\operatorname{div}(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\int_{0}^{t}({\mathcal{E}}_{1}+{\mathcal{E}}_{2})+C\delta\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla\Pi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(4.6)

4.3
Uniform bound for
{\mathcal{E}}

Putting together the estimates for
{\mathcal{E}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
, see (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), we conclude that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(t)-{\mathcal{E}}(0)

\displaystyle+\mu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\right\rvert^{2}+(\lambda+\mu)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\operatorname{div}(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(\delta_{0},\delta_{2})\int_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{E}}+\delta_{0}\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

(4.7)

\displaystyle\qquad+\delta_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\operatorname{div}(u-u^{\varepsilon})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C(\delta_{0})\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla\Pi\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Setting
\delta_{0}=\mu/2
and
\delta_{2}=(\lambda+\mu)/2
, a Gronwall-type argument allows us to state that
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}(t)
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{2}(t)
are uniformly bounded on
[0,T]
.

4.4
Convergence of the densities and volume fractions

As all the problem is symmetric in terms of
\gamma_{+}
,
\gamma_{-}
, we can assume without loss of generality that
\gamma_{+}>\gamma_{-}
. Alternatively, we can exchange
\alpha_{\pm},\rho_{\pm}
by
\alpha_{\mp},\rho_{\mp}
in this subsection. Hence
\gamma=\gamma_{+}/\gamma_{-}>1
. The equality of pressures gives

\rho_{-}=\rho_{+}^{\gamma},

so that we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{+}-1)}\lvert\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{+}\rvert^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{-}-1)}\lvert\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{-}\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{+}-1)}\lvert\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{+}\rvert^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{-}-1)}\lvert(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon})^{\gamma}-\rho_{+}^{\gamma}\rvert^{2}.

For all
x\in\Omega
such that
\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}<\rho_{+}/2
, we have

|\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon\gamma}-\rho_{+}^{\gamma}|=\rho_{+}^{\gamma}-\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon\gamma}\geqslant\rho_{+}^{\gamma}-(\rho_{+}/2)^{\gamma}\geqslant(1-2^{-\gamma})\frac{\rho_{+}^{\gamma}}{\rho_{+}}|\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{+}|.

because
|\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{+}|=\rho_{+}-\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}\leqslant\rho_{+}

Otherwise, for all
x
such that
\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}>\rho_{+}/2
, we have by the mean value theorem applied to
F(x)=x^{1/\gamma}

|\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{+}|\leqslant\sup_{x>(\rho_{+}/2)^{1/\gamma}}F^{\prime}(x)|\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon\gamma}-\rho_{+}^{\gamma}|=\frac{1}{\gamma}(\rho_{+}/2)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}-1}|\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon\gamma}-\rho_{+}^{\gamma}|

Hence, we obtain the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle\geqslant C\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{+}-1)}\lvert\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{+}\rvert^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma_{-}-1)}\lvert\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{+}\rvert^{2}.

for some constant
C>0
.

Since
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is uniformly bounded, we deduce that

\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}\to\rho_{+}\quad\text{in }L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega))\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

Doing a similar type of analysis using the equality of pressures, we also obtain

\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}\to\rho_{-}\quad\text{in }L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega)).

Furthermore, since
\alpha_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\leqslant 1
, the sequences
(\alpha_{\pm}^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
are uniformly bounded in
L^{1}(\Omega)
and
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Hence, up to extraction of a subsequence (still denoted by
\alpha_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}
), there exist limits
\bar{\alpha}_{\pm}
such that

\alpha_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\bar{\alpha}_{\pm}\quad\text{weakly-$*$ in }L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega)),

with

\bar{\alpha}_{+}+\bar{\alpha}_{-}=1.

Finally, using the product of a strongly convergent sequence and a weakly convergent sequence, we deduce that, for
i=+,-
,

R_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\bar{\alpha}_{i}\rho_{i}\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

4.5
Strong convergence of the partial densities
R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}
and weak convergence of
\operatorname{div}(u^{\varepsilon})

We recall that
u^{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
by the energy estimate.
By Sobolev embeddings in space dimension
d=3
, we have for
p\in[2,6]

H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{p}(\Omega),

so that, taking
p=6
,
u^{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))
.

Since
R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
,
Hölder’s inequality implies that
R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{3/2}(\Omega))
.

Consequently,
\operatorname{div}(R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon})
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;W^{-1,3/2}(\Omega))
in the sense of distributions.

Using the mass conservation equations, we deduce that
\partial_{t}R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(0,T;W^{-1,3/2}(\Omega))
.
Applying a version of the Aubin–Lions lemma together with the compact and continuous embeddings

L^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow H^{-1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{-1,\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega),

we infer that, up to the extraction of a subsequence,

R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\to\bar{R}_{\pm}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega)).

By weak strong uniqueness of the limit, we conclude that

\bar{R}_{\pm}=\bar{\alpha}_{\pm}\rho_{\pm}.

Moreover, since
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is uniformly bounded and

R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
,
Poincaré’s inequality ensures that the quantity

R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}|u^{\varepsilon}-u|^{2}

is uniformly bounded in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.
In view of the uniform bounds on
R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}
,
we therefore infer that the sequence

(u^{\varepsilon}-u)_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded in

L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
.
Since
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
is reflexive, there exists a function

v\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
and a subsequence (not relabeled) such that

u^{\varepsilon}-u\rightharpoonup v\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega).

Passing to the limit in the mass equations, we obtain, for
i=+,-
,

\partial_{t}(\bar{\alpha}_{i}\rho_{i})+\operatorname{div}\big(\bar{\alpha}_{i}\rho_{i}(u+v)\big)=0,

which yields

\partial_{t}\bar{\alpha}_{i}+\operatorname{div}\big(\bar{\alpha}_{i}(u+v)\big)=0.

Summing the two equations for
i=+,-
, and using
\bar{\alpha}_{+}+\bar{\alpha}_{-}=1
together with
\operatorname{div}u=0
, we deduce

\operatorname{div}v=0.

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup 0\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega).

(4.8)

Using a standard compactness argument for Navier Stokes, we also get a strong convergence of
\rho^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
, which allows to pass at the limit in the momentum equation for any test function divergence free. This allows to prove that
(\overline{R}_{+}+\overline{R}_{-},u+v)
satisfies the limit system (
1.5
) in the weak sense. By weak strong uniqueness, we have that
\overline{R}_{+}+\overline{R}_{-}=\rho
and
v=0
. By Remark
1.4
this means that
\overline{\alpha}_{\pm}=\alpha_{\pm}
and
\overline{R}_{\pm}=R_{\pm}
.

5
End of proof

We will now find a new estimate for
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
and use the convergences found above to conclude.

We first find a new bound for the term (
4.3
). Recalling that
\rho^{\varepsilon}-\rho=\sum_{i=+,-}R_{i}^{\varepsilon}-R_{i}
, we use Cauchy-Schwartz in dual spaces
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
H^{-1}(\Omega)
, to get

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\Omega}\frac{R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}-R_{\pm}}{\rho}\Delta u(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lVert\frac{1}{\rho}\Delta u(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lVert R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}-R_{\pm}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\lVert\Delta u\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lVert u-u^{\varepsilon}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lvert R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}-R_{\pm}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}.

Then, to find a new estimate for the term (
4.5
), we write

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}-\frac{\rho^{\varepsilon}}{\rho}\nabla\Pi(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}\left\lvert\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho-\rho^{\varepsilon}}{\rho}\nabla\Pi(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert+\left\lvert\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\Pi(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert.

and similarly, we get for the first term

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho-\rho^{\varepsilon}}{\rho}\nabla\Pi(u-u^{\varepsilon})\right\rvert\leqslant C\lVert\nabla\Pi\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lVert u-u^{\varepsilon}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lvert R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}-R_{\pm}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}.

Therefore, we deduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(t)-\mathcal{E}_{1}(0)+\mu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\rvert^{2}-(\lambda+\mu)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\lvert\operatorname{div}(u^{\varepsilon}-u)\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant C\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{E}_{1}(s)\,ds+C\int_{0}^{t}\lVert u-u^{\varepsilon}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lvert R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}-R_{\pm}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\,ds+\Big\lvert\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Pi\operatorname{div}(u-u^{\varepsilon})\,dx\,ds\Big\rvert.

Applying Gronwall’s lemma, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(t)\leqslant e^{Ct}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}(0)+C\int_{0}^{t}\lVert u-u^{\varepsilon}\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lvert R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}-R_{\pm}\rVert_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\,ds+\Big\lvert\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Pi\operatorname{div}(u-u^{\varepsilon})\,dx\,ds\Big\rvert\right].

Assuming that the initial energies satisfy

\mathcal{E}_{1}(0)\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0,

and recalling that

R_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\to R_{\pm}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega)).

and

\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup 0\quad\text{in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega),

we immediately deduce from the previously established Gronwall estimate that

\mathcal{E}_{1}(t)\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0.

Combining the energy estimates and the previously established bounds, we conclude the convergence of the approximate solutions
(\alpha_{\pm}^{\varepsilon},\rho_{\pm}^{\varepsilon},u^{\varepsilon})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

From the Gronwall estimate and the hypothesis that the initial energies satisfy

\mathcal{E}_{1}(0)\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0,

we deduce that the velocity converges strongly:

u^{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow u\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

The gradients
(\nabla u^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
are uniformly bounded in
L^{2}
, so that up to extraction,

\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\nabla u\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega),

and in particular, the divergence satisfies

\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup 0\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega),

which shows that the limit velocity
u
is incompressible.

In summary, the sequence of approximate solutions converges to the expected limit

(\alpha_{+},\alpha_{-},\rho_{+},\rho_{-},u)

with the convergence properties

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow u

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\nabla u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\nabla u

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega),

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup 0

\displaystyle\text{weakly in }L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega),

\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\rho_{i}

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle\text{weak-$*$ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle R_{i}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow R_{i}

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega)),\quad i=+,-.

This convergence framework is standard in the context of singular limits for compressible Navier–Stokes equations and allows the identification of the limit solution as the physically relevant incompressible two-phase flow.
