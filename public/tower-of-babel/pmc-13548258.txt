Experiences of pregnant women staying in a maternity waiting home at Ari zone public health facilities, Ethiopia: a phenomenological qualitative study
Authors: Worku Mimani Minuta, Abera Gezume, Ermias Wabeto Wana, Getamesay Aynalem Tesfaye, Getachew Nigussie Bolado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundMaternity Waiting Homes (MWHs) are a key strategy for improving maternal health by bringing expectant women closer to health facilities and skilled birth attendants. Despite their potential benefits, a woman’s stay is shaped by various factors, including emotional, social and logistical aspects.ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the lived experiences of pregnant women using maternity waiting homes in the Ari zone public health facilities, Ethiopia.DesignA qualitative phenomenological study, grounded in a descriptive (Husserlian) phenomenological orientation and analysed using Colaizzi’s seven-step framework.SettingPublic health facilities providing MWH services in the Ari zone, southern Ethiopia, ranging from a zonal hospital to primary health centres.Participants15 pregnant women, who had stayed in an MWH for at least 1 week during their current pregnancy, selected using purposive sampling until data saturation was reached.MethodsWe conducted in-depth interviews from 15 June 2025 to 15 July 2025 to match the Study Area and Period section. The data were transcribed, translated and thematically analysed with Open Code software V.4.02 to identify key themes and subthemes. The rigour of the study was ensured by applying Guba’s trustworthiness criteria, with a focus on credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability.ResultsBased on the findings, the experiences of women in maternity waiting homes centred on four main themes. Emotionally, women faced both isolation and anxiety from being away from home but also felt comfort and relief from being near medical care. Socially, they found support from other women and were influenced by the attitudes of health staff while also carrying the burden of family and cultural concerns. Practical challenges included significant financial burdens, lack of food and inadequate facilities. Despite these difficulties, women ultimately valued the homes for the increased confidence and improved access to skilled care they provided for a safer birth.ConclusionsA woman’s experience in an MWH is shaped by a mix of emotional, social, infrastructural and cultural factors. To improve the use of MWHs and maternal health outcomes, it is crucial to enhance the quality of these facilities, offer psychosocial support and involve communities. Future intervention studies should test whether targeted psychosocial and food-security support measurably improve women’s experiences and MWH uptake.Keywords: Midwifery, Maternal medicine, OBSTETRICS

Introduction

Maternal mortality remains a major global health issue, particularly in developing countries with limited access to skilled birth attendants and emergency care. According to the WHO, around 810 women die daily from preventable causes related to pregnancy and childbirth, with 94% of these deaths occurring in low-resource areas.1

Maternity waiting homes are residential facilities, located near a qualified medical centre, where pregnant women from remote areas can await their delivery.2 The primary goal of an MWH is to bridge the geographical gap between a woman’s home and a health facility, thereby increasing her access to skilled birth attendants and reducing the risk of a complicated delivery without professional medical help.3 By staying at an MWH, women can be monitored by healthcare professionals, ensuring that they receive timely intervention should an obstetric emergency arise.

Ethiopia has nearly four decades of experience with maternity waiting homes (MWHs), with the first facilities established in a few hospitals during the 1980s. Over the last decade, in partnership with the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), Ethiopia’s Ministry of Health has been working to expand and improve these MWHs across the country. This effort is a key strategy to encourage more women to give birth in a health facility and use other maternal health services. To achieve this, the UNFPA has been actively supporting government initiatives by providing training for healthcare workers and supplying necessary equipment and supplies.4

Despite the clear public health rationale, the lived experiences of women who use these facilities are complex and multifaceted.1 Research has often focused on the effectiveness of MWHs in reducing maternal mortality and morbidity, but less attention has been given to the social, psychological and cultural dimensions of a woman’s stay.5 The decision to leave one’s home and family to stay in an unfamiliar environment, often for an extended period, can be emotionally and financially challenging. Women may face feelings of isolation, loneliness and anxiety about their family’s well-being in their absence.2 Furthermore, the social dynamics within the MWH, the quality of the facility and the interaction with healthcare staff all contribute to a woman’s overall experience.6

Maternal mortality is a global health crisis, with an estimated 295 000 women dying annually from preventable causes related to pregnancy and childbirth, with sub-Saharan Africa accounting for nearly 70% of these deaths.7 In Ethiopia, the maternal mortality ratio was estimated at 401 deaths per 100 000 live births, highlighting the urgent need for effective interventions.8

Maternal mortality is particularly acute in low- and middle-income countries, where a combination of factors, including poor health infrastructure, poverty and geographical barriers, creates a ‘three delays’ model that contributes to a high number of preventable deaths.9 The first delay is the time it takes to decide to seek care; the second is the time taken to reach a health facility; and the third is the time from arrival to receiving adequate care.10–12 While the establishment of MWHs has been a strategic response to the second delay, the lived experiences of the women who use them have not been adequately explored. This creates a significant gap in the literature and potentially hinders the effectiveness of these interventions.13

MWHs are designed to address the geographical barrier to healthcare for women in remote areas.14 However, the assumption that a physical proximity to a health facility is sufficient to ensure good health outcomes overlooks the complex human element. Women’s decisions to use these homes are influenced not only by physical distance but also by social, psychological and cultural factors.15 16 For many, staying in an MWH means leaving behind their children, husbands and daily responsibilities, which can lead to feelings of stress, anxiety and guilt. The financial burden, including transportation costs and the cost of staying, can also be a significant deterrent.17 18

Furthermore, the quality of life within the MWH itself is a critical, yet often unexamined, factor. Conditions may vary widely, from well-equipped, comfortable facilities to basic, crowded shelters. A woman who feels isolated, uncared for or unsafe may not benefit from the MWH as intended and may be less likely to recommend it to others. This can undermine the community-level buy-in that is essential for the long-term success of such a public health intervention.18–20

In the rugged terrain of rural Ethiopia, a pregnant woman’s journey to childbirth is often a race against time and distance.21 For many, a maternity waiting home is their best hope for a safe delivery, but this decision comes with its own set of anxieties and sacrifices.17 22 While the public health value of these homes is clear in reducing mortality and morbidity, the lived experience of the women who use them is a complex and often overlooked story. Understanding these lived experiences is critical, as they directly influence a woman’s decision to use an MWH for her current and future pregnancies, as well as her willingness to recommend it to others in her community. Exploring these personal narratives is essential for designing and improving maternity waiting homes to be more effective, culturally sensitive and supportive environments. This qualitative inquiry seeks to uncover these experiences, providing a nuanced perspective that goes beyond quantitative health metrics. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the multifaceted experiences of women using MWHs in the Ari zone of Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study area and period

This study was conducted in selected public health facilities with active MWHs in the Ari zone, Ethiopia. The Ari zone is mostly rural, with a difficult landscape that often limits access to healthcare. The zone is located about 750 kilometers south of Addis Ababa, at a latitude and longitude of 5°47′N 36°34′E, with an elevation of 1490 m above sea level. Its residents are semi-agrarian with different cultural backgrounds and the total area of the zone is 22 360.76 km2. According to a 2014 Ethiopian fiscal year estimate by the Central Statistical Agency, the zone has a total population of 821 405 with an equal male-to-female ratio. There are five woredas (districts) in the zone, and most of the residents rely on raising animals and farming crops (maize, lentils) and livestock (cattle, goats and sheep). Regarding health services, the zone has one zonal hospital, one district primary hospital and several health centres and health posts. Overall, there are six health institutions in the zone that provide MWH services. The study was carried out from 15 June 2025 to 15 July 2025.

Study design

A qualitative phenomenological study design was used to gain an in-depth understanding of the lived experiences, perceptions and meanings pregnant women attributed to their stay in the MWHs. The study was grounded in a descriptive (Husserlian) phenomenological perspective, which seeks to describe a phenomenon as it is directly experienced by participants rather than to interpret it through a pre-existing theoretical lens. Consistent with this orientation, the research team aimed to bracket their own assumptions and prior clinical/professional knowledge of MWHs during data collection and analysis and adopted Colaizzi’s descriptive phenomenological method to guide the interview approach, coding and interpretation of findings, as detailed in the Data management and analysis section below.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were involved in the design or conduct of this study. The findings were subsequently disseminated through a validation workshop and a conference presentation hosted at Jinka University, with participation from government representatives, private-sector stakeholders and academic scholars.

Study population

The study population consisted of pregnant women who had stayed in a maternity waiting home for at least 1 week during their current pregnancy.

Sampling technique and sample size

A purposive sampling technique was used to select participants who could provide rich, relevant information about their experiences, with attention to maximising diversity in parity, gestational age and length of stay at the MWH. The sample size was guided by data saturation, defined as the point at which additional interviews no longer generated substantively new codes, categories or information relevant to the study objectives. Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. Following every three interviews, the research team reviewed the transcripts and compared emerging codes and categories with those identified from earlier interviews. Recruitment continued until successive interviews failed to provide substantively new information or codes relevant to the developing analysis. Saturation was achieved after 15 in-depth interviews. During the final stage of analysis, the research team reviewed the most recent interviews and confirmed that they did not generate substantively new codes or themes. Consequently, participant recruitment was discontinued after the 15th interview.

Data collection methods

Data were collected through face-to-face, in-depth interviews using a semi-structured interview guide (see online supplemental file 1). The guide was developed in English, translated into the local language (Amarigna) and pre-tested for clarity. It covered key areas including emotional experiences, social interactions, facility conditions, cultural concerns, motivations and perceived benefits.

The interviews were conducted by four researchers, all of whom held master’s-level qualifications and had professional backgrounds in midwifery, public health and nursing, with prior experience in qualitative research and qualitative data collection. Before data collection, the interviewers were oriented to the study objectives, interview guide, ethical procedures, confidentiality and non-directive interviewing techniques. Interviews were conducted in a private setting within the health facility using open-ended and neutral questions to encourage participants to describe their experiences freely and in their own words. None of the interviewers had a prior personal relationship with the participants or was directly involved in providing their clinical care to the interviewed participants. This separation was maintained to minimise potential power dynamics and reduce the possibility that participants would feel obliged to provide socially desirable responses or hesitate to discuss negative experiences.

Each interview lasted approximately 30–45 min and was audio-recorded with participants’ permission. Detailed field notes were also taken to document relevant contextual observations and non-verbal cues.

The research team recognised that their professional backgrounds could influence the collection and interpretation of data. Interviewers used open-ended and non-directive questioning and avoided introducing personal opinions or expectations during the interviews. Throughout the analysis, the research team reviewed emerging interpretations against the original participant narratives and used peer discussion to critically examine assumptions and ensure that the developing findings remained grounded in participants’ accounts.

Data management and analysis

Data collection was carried out simultaneously with the analysis. The participants’ audio-recorded interviews and all expressions, behaviours and actions, such as laughter or crying, were transcribed to be immersed in the data and translated verbatim into English by the PI. The interview transcripts were analysed using Colaizzi’s seven-step descriptive phenomenological method23 : Step 1: Familiarisation (reading the transcripts and field notes repeatedly, listening to the interviews and considering key statements or descriptions in the participants’ narratives were used to immerse in the data and to ensure that the transcribed data is clear and consistent). Step 2: Identifying significant statements (we identified statements that were interesting segments of text and directly related to the study’s objective). Step 3: Formulating meanings (meanings for the statements that were related to the phenomena by considering significant statements and holding the researcher’s set aside presumptions or presuppositions) was done. Step 4: Clustering themes (we clustered the identified meanings/codes and grouped them into subthemes and themes. Comparative analysis was done by moving back and forth between texts, codes, subthemes and themes). Step 5: Developing an exhaustive description (we wrote a full and inclusive description of the phenomena under study by incorporating the subthemes and themes that were produced from the previous step). Step 6: Producing the fundamental structure of the phenomenon (themes and subthemes were developed from the interview data, which provided a detailed understanding of the perspective of the participants towards the phenomenon in question, thereafter the themes and subthemes were supported by relevant quotations of the participants’ expressions). Step 7: Member checking. Following the initial analysis, concise summaries of the emerging findings were shared with a subset of participants to assess whether the interpretations adequately reflected their experiences and perspectives. Participants’ feedback was considered when refining the final themes and interpretations.

Two researchers independently coded the transcripts. The remaining researchers reviewed the coding, categories, subthemes and themes. Differences in interpretation were discussed by the research team and resolved through consensus, with relevant transcript sections revisited when necessary. OpenCode software V.4.02 was used to organise, label, retrieve and compare coded segments of text and to facilitate systematic management of the qualitative dataset. The analytical hierarchy showing the progression from the 132 initial codes to 12 subthemes and four overarching themes is presented in online supplemental figure 1).

An audit trail was maintained throughout the analytic process through a coding log and analytic memos documenting how codes were grouped into subthemes and themes, allowing the analytical decisions underlying table 1 to be traced back to the original transcripts. Negative cases and contrasting experiences, such as instances where participants reported entirely positive views of staff behaviour or contrasting perceptions of facility conditions, were actively identified and examined against prevailing patterns. These accounts were integrated into the analysis to nuance and refine the boundaries of the themes rather than being treated as outliers. Where participant counts are reported alongside a subtheme (eg, indicating how many of the 15 women described a given experience), these figures are intended purely as descriptive indicators of how widely a particular experience was shared across the sample, rather than as statistical estimates. They do not substitute for the interpretive, meaning-focused analysis central to phenomenological inquiry.

Regarding their educational background, five pregnant women had no formal education, five had completed primary school, three had completed secondary school, one had a diploma and one had a bachelor’s degree. All pregnant women were married and twelve were housewives. Their gestational age at the time of the interviews ranged from 28 to 38 weeks and their stay in the MWH varied from two to 7 weeks. Experiences of pregnant women staying in a maternity waiting home

A total of 132 codes, 12 subthemes and four overarching themes were identified from the interviews regarding the experiences of pregnant women staying in maternity waiting homes. The four themes were: emotional and psychological experiences, social and cultural dynamics, practical challenges and barriers and perceived benefits and outcomes. The complete list of codes and their grouping into subthemes and themes is provided in the supplementary codebook (online supplemental file 3). For readability, only the themes and subthemes are presented in table 1.

Theme 1: emotional and psychological experiences

The emotional experiences of pregnant women staying in MWHs reflected a constant tension between the distress of separation from family and social support and the reassurance of being close to emergency obstetric care. Pregnant women experienced an emotional tension in which vulnerability and uncertainty were partly alleviated by the reassurance of being close to healthcare professionals and emergency care.

Subtheme 1.1: feelings of isolation and loneliness

The feeling of isolation within the MWH is more than just physical; it is a profound feeling of being disconnected from their community and the rhythm of their daily lives. The absence of familiar social rituals and daily interactions with neighbours creates an emotional void that can lead to significant distress.

Here, we are just waiting. There is no laughter, no coffee ceremony with my neighbors… It feels empty and not like our village life. (P2, 24 years old pregnant woman)

Sometimes I feel lonely being away from my family… I miss my children, but I know I need to stay here for my safety. (P11, 32 years old pregnant woman)

The days are very long here. At home, I am always busy with my children, cooking, and farming. Here, I just sit and think. I count the days until I can go back. (P5, 29 years old pregnant woman)

Subtheme 1.2: feelings of anxiety and fear

The impending event of childbirth, along with worries about the health of both themselves and their babies, is a constant source of anxiety for women. This apprehension is heightened by being in an unfamiliar environment, away from their traditional support networks.

I was worried about my baby and myself, wondering if everything would go well after staying here…Sometimes I can't sleep because I keep thinking about what might happen during labor. (P8, 20 years old pregnant woman)

This is my first pregnancy. I hear stories from other women about painful labor. I am scared of the pain and that something bad will happen to me or the baby. (P14, 22 years old pregnant woman)

When the night comes, my heart gets full of worry thinking about the labor. (P4, 27 years old pregnant woman)

Subtheme 1.3: sense of comfort and relief

Despite these fears, women find a significant sense of comfort and relief in being close to healthcare professionals and the availability of emergency care. This reassurance provides a counterpoint to their anxiety, validating their difficult decision to leave home.

Being here makes me feel safer because I know help is nearby if anything happens …Knowing that doctors and nurses are close by makes me less anxious about giving birth. (P10, 26 years old pregnant woman)

At home, if a problem comes, it is far to the hospital. The road is bad. Here, my mind is quiet. I have put my worry down. (P15, 29 years old pregnant woman)

My mind is at peace now because the clinic is right here, and I don't worry about transport in the night. (P12, 29 years old pregnant woman)

Collectively, these three subthemes reveal that women’s emotional experience of the MWH is not unidirectional. The same distance from home that engenders feelings of loneliness and anxiety also supplies the comfort that ultimately renders the stay endurable. Furthermore, the emotional weight of separation and the emotional lift of being close to care are sustained throughout the stay rather than replacing one another over time.

Theme 2: social and cultural dynamics

Social interactions within the MWH provide pregnant women with an important source of emotional support, helping to reduce the anxiety associated with staying in an institutional setting. By sharing experiences, offering encouragement and spending time together, pregnant women create a sense of community that makes the MWH feel less like a clinical facility and more like a familiar and supportive social space. These peer relationships also help pregnant women cope with the emotional strain of being away from their families and managing competing responsibilities at home. At the same time, support from other pregnant women can help them deal with the uncertainty and, at times, intimidating attitudes they may encounter from healthcare providers.

Subtheme 2.1: importance of peer support

Despite initial feelings of isolation, women in the MWH quickly find a new, though temporary, community among their peers. This peer support becomes a vital coping mechanism.

Being with other women who are pregnant… we share our stories, laugh, and support each other. It helps me feel less anxious. (P3, 31 years old pregnant woman)

Talking with others who are going through the same thing makes me feel stronger. We are like sisters here. We share our food, our worries, and our hopes. When one feels sad, we comfort her. We have become each other’s family for this time. (P9, 26 years old pregnant woman)

Sitting together and talking takes our minds off the waiting. When we share what we are going through, it feels like our burden is shared too. (P6, 25 years old pregnant woman)

Subtheme 2.2: perceived attitude of health staff

The behaviour and attitude of health staff significantly influence a woman’s perception of her care. While some women reported positive interactions, highlighting kindness and respect, others recounted moments of fear and intimidation.

Some of the nurses are kind and respectful. But sometimes, when you are in pain, they are rude and shout at you. This makes you afraid to go to the hospital. (P13, 33 years old pregnant woman)

The nurses are kind and always check on us, which makes me feel cared for. Their support makes me feel more comfortable and confident about giving birth here. (P10, 26 years old pregnant woman)

One midwife speaks gently and holds my hand. But others look at you like a burden, making you feel small and afraid to ask for help. (P4, 27 years old pregnant woman)

Subtheme 2.3: cultural and family concerns

Leaving their families and daily responsibilities is one of the most significant challenges for women. This decision is not made lightly, as it disrupts their traditional roles and places a heavy burden on the family members left behind.

My older children have to take care of the younger ones. It is a heavy burden for them, and I worry about what will happen without me. (P11, 32 years old pregnant woman)

Cultural expectations make it difficult to stay away from my family, but I know I need to be here for the baby’s safety. (P5, 29 years old pregnant woman)

My husband does not know how to cook for the children. I feel guilty that they are eating poorly because I am not there. A mother should be with her children. (P2, 24 years old pregnant woman)

Across these subthemes, pregnant women’s social experiences within the MWH are shaped by two contrasting forces. On one hand, relationships with other residents provide a sense of companionship, solidarity and emotional support. On the other hand, interactions with healthcare staff and ongoing family responsibilities can create uncertainty and emotional strain. The support pregnant women receive from their peers helps reduce the sense of isolation described in Theme 1, giving them a stronger sense of connection while staying at the MWH. However, unresolved family and cultural concerns continue to draw their thoughts and emotions back home, making it difficult for women to fully disconnect from their everyday responsibilities and relationships.

Theme 3: practical challenges and barriers

Women’s experiences during their stay at the MWH are also shaped by a range of practical and logistical challenges. Although maternity services are intended to be free, families may still face considerable out-of-pocket expenses related to food, transportation and other basic needs. Limited access to adequate food, uncomfortable living conditions and financial difficulties can make the stay particularly challenging, especially for families with limited resources. As a result, what is intended as a preventive health measure may become a significant economic and emotional burden for already vulnerable households.

Subtheme 3.1: financial burden and sacrifices

The financial costs associated with staying at an MWH, although sometimes unexpected, can be immense for a low-income family. The expenses for transport, food and other supplies can be crippling.

Transportation is a burden; my husband had to sell one of our goats to pay for the transport and things I need here. (P4, 27 years old pregnant woman)

We had to choose between buying fertilizer for our farm or paying for my transport to come here. We chose me. Now I worry if we will have enough food next season. (P10, 26 years old pregnant woman)

Everything requires money here, and coming to this place has finished what little cash we had saved. (P8, 20 years old pregnant woman)

Subtheme 3.2: lack of food support

The absence of a consistent food supply at the MWH is a major practical challenge. This issue is not just about hunger; it’s a constant worry that forces families to redirect their already limited resources.

….but there is no food. We have to bring all our own food from home, and for a poor family, this is very difficult. We have to make a choice between feeding the family at home and bringing enough food for my stay here. (P7, 28 years old pregnant woman)

They told us there would be food, but there is nothing here. My family has to bring me food from far away, and sometimes they cannot come. It is very hard to stay here when you are hungry. (P1, 21 years old pregnant woman)

I am eating less so the food my husband brought will last longer. I am hungry sometimes, but I don't want to be a problem for my family. A pregnant woman should eat well, but here it is difficult. (P5, 29 years old pregnant woman)

Subtheme 3.3: inadequate facilities and lack of privacy

The physical conditions of the MWH and the availability of basic resources are critical components of the women’s experience. A lack of essential amenities, overcrowding and constant disruptions transform the stay into a struggle for comfort and dignity.

The facility like toilet room, water is not enough for all of us. This makes it very difficult to keep ourselves and our environment clean, which is especially worrying when we are about to give birth. (P12, 29 years old pregnant woman)

We are too many women in one room. The beds are close together. There is no curtain. When I change my clothes, I feel shy. There is no private place. (P5, 29 years old pregnant woman)

The facility lacks privacy. Health workers and visitors are always coming in and out of our room, which disturbs our rest. (P4, 27 years old pregnant woman)

Subtheme 3.4: gaps in accessing medicines and care

Women reported critical gaps in the availability of promised services, particularly essential medicines. This system failure forces them to bear unexpected costs, negating the principle of free care and adding a layer of financial and emotional stress.

Sometimes when we are sick or feel uncomfortable, if a medicine is prescribed, it is supposed to be free here. But it is often not available. We are then forced to purchase from a private pharmacy. For my family, who already sacrificed to get me here, this extra cost is sometimes devastating. (P1, 21 years old pregnant woman)

The doctor prescribed medicine for me, but the clinic had none. We had to find money to buy it outside. (P6, 25 years old pregnant woman)

They told us everything here is free, but when you need drugs, you are told to go buy from town. (P13, 33 years old pregnant woman)

These practical challenges point to broader structural vulnerabilities that may affect the sustainability and effectiveness of the MWH programme. Although the cost of staying at the MWH may be waived, women and their families often continue to bear indirect expenses, including transportation, food, maintaining privacy and purchasing medications when they are not readily available. These costs can place a particularly heavy burden on low-income households, sometimes forcing families to make difficult choices between meeting immediate maternity-related needs and maintaining their livelihoods at home. In this way, the promise of free maternal healthcare is only partly realised in women’s everyday experiences. These unmet practical needs also stand in contrast to the emotional and social support women may gain from staying at the MWH, highlighting the gap between the intended benefits of the programme and the realities of accessing and sustaining care.

Theme 4: perceived benefits and outcomes

Despite the logistical challenges and difficulties associated with staying at the MWH, women consistently described the experience as valuable for protecting their health and that of their babies. Many recognised the risks associated with pregnancy and childbirth and viewed the MWH not simply as a place where they were required to stay, but as an important source of preparation, reassurance and protection. Participants described how staying at the MWH increased their confidence, brought them closer to skilled maternity care and helped them feel physically and emotionally prepared for childbirth. Overall, their experiences suggest that MWHs can provide women with a greater sense of safety and readiness, particularly when complications arise and timely access to skilled care is needed.

Subtheme 4.1: motivations and expectations

The primary motivation for a woman to stay at an MWH is the belief that it will lead to a safer delivery and a healthier baby. The proximity to a health facility and skilled medical staff provides a sense of security that is often absent in their rural communities.

I decided to stay here because I was told it is safer for me and my baby. Being close to the hospital helps me feel more confident about delivering safely. (P10, 26 years old pregnant woman)

I thought it would be more comfortable, but it’s okay because I am closer to the hospital. It’s not perfect, but I am glad I am near medical help when I need it. (P12, 29 years old pregnant woman)

My last delivery had a problem and we almost lost the baby. That is why I came here early this time; I will not take that risk again. (P5, 29 years old pregnant woman)

Subtheme 4.2: increased confidence and improved access to care

Staying at the MWH helps women feel more prepared and confident about childbirth. The proximity to medical staff and the constant readiness for delivery alleviate their anxieties and empower them.

Staying here helped me prepare mentally and physically for childbirth. I feel more confident because I know help is close if needed. (P9, 26 years old pregnant woman)

Knowing that doctors are nearby gives me peace of mind…Having skilled attendants close by makes me feel secure about giving birth. (P10, 26 years old pregnant woman)

They checked my blood pressure and the baby’s position every day. They explained what will happen during delivery. I feel prepared now. I am not coming to the hospital as a stranger. (P15, 29 years old pregnant woman)

Women’s descriptions of the MWH suggest that its benefits go beyond simply avoiding the difficulties they experienced during their stay. For many, being close to a health facility and having timely access to skilled care provided a strong sense of security that made the challenges of staying at the MWH worthwhile. Even when women experienced emotional, social or financial difficulties, they continued to value the protection and reassurance that the MWH offered. Staying closer to skilled care helped reduce fears about possible complications and allowed women to feel more prepared for childbirth. In this sense, the MWH appears to fulfil an important role in reducing the risks associated with distance from health facilities and helping women approach childbirth with greater confidence and a sense of preparedness.

Discussion

This study investigated the experiences of pregnant women within MWHs in the Ari zone, focusing on emotional, social, physical and motivational aspects.

Our findings reveal a complex emotional state for women staying in MWHs. Consistent with earlier research in which women expressed anxiety and fear related to childbirth,3 24 however, this study adds a deeper understanding by highlighting the often-unreported emotional struggles of loneliness and isolation, particularly at night. This finding is similar to studies that underscore the need for proactive psychosocial support. The emotional distress caused by leaving their families and abandoned responsibilities was a significant barrier, mirroring findings reported elsewhere.17 24 This sense of guilt and worry over household duties and children represents a key Delay 1 (the delay in deciding to seek care), as it can discourage women from leaving their homes in the first place.

These emotional experiences are best understood in light of the specific sociocultural setting in which they occurred. In much of rural southern Ethiopia, including the ethnically diverse Ari zone, a woman’s daily value and identity are closely bound to her role within the household; cooking, child-rearing and farm labour are rarely delegated to others and a prolonged absence is felt by the whole family rather than by the woman alone. This helps explain why guilt over abandoned responsibilities and worry about children left behind were such prominent and recurring concerns in our data, consistent with the household-responsibility barriers identified among MWH users in Southwest Ethiopia18 and with description of the social and logistical pressures shaping women’s MWH experiences nationally.24 The financial trade-offs we identified are similarly rooted in local economic life: several participants described competing directly between transport or MWH-related costs and agricultural inputs, a tension consistent with the broader economic pressures documented among agrarian and pastoralist households in South Ari and Benatsemay woredas of the same zone.25

In response to these challenges, peer support emerged as a powerful, self-organised coping mechanism. Shared experiences among women fostered resilience and reduced anxiety17, demonstrating that promoting a supportive community environment is an unintended but vital benefit of the MWH model.

Several of our findings closely echo prior Ethiopian and sub-Saharan African literature, while also extending it in specific ways. Food insecurity emerged as a dominant practical concern in our participants’ accounts, consistent with findings that women in Southwest Ethiopia may discontinue an MWH stay altogether if food is unavailable,18 and with a facility-based qualitative study conducted in the same South Omo Zone as the present study, which documented food insecurity as a persistent, community-wide challenge in South Ari and neighbouring woredas.25 Similarly, the mixed staff-attitude experiences we identified, women describing both reassurance and, in other cases, feeling like a burden align with Hagaman et al’s qualitative account of Ethiopian women’s facility-based maternity care, in which respectful treatment coexisted with instances of disrespect within the same health system.15 Our findings are also broadly consistent with descriptions of MWH-related challenges in rural Ethiopian health centres.26 Where our findings diverge somewhat from this literature is in the overall balance of experience: despite considerable practical and interpersonal difficulty, our participants did not describe MWH use as something to be avoided, but rather as a cost worth bearing for proximity to skilled care—a more integrated, ambivalent stance than the largely barrier-focused framing of much of the existing access literature.15 18

The physical conditions of MWHs significantly influence women’s comfort and their perception of care. While MWHs provide safe shelter, overcrowding, lack of privacy and poor sanitation were notable concerns, consistent with observations reported elsewhere. These issues can compromise a woman’s well-being and satisfaction, potentially undermining the facility’s purpose.18 27 28 Addressing basic needs like food security is not a secondary concern but is central to the ethical and effective operation of an MWH.

Financially, while MWH accommodation is often free, the cost of transportation remains a significant barrier. This financial burden can be a critical factor in ‘Delay 2’ (the delay in reaching a health facility), as families may be forced to make difficult economic trade-offs, potentially delaying or deterring a woman from seeking care.

Much of the existing Ethiopian MWH literature, including influential account of women’s experiences and challenges in rural health centres24 and the national integrative review by Gurara et al,29 is structured around cataloguing distinct barriers and benefits as largely separate categories. Our study instead captures how these forces are experienced together, within the same women, over the course of a single stay. Rather than characterising MWH use as a straightforwardly positive or negative experience, our findings show emotional distress and perceived safety, isolation and peer support, and financial hardship and confidence in skilled care are held simultaneously rather than sequentially resolved. This ambivalence and the specific cultural mechanisms that produce it, particularly disrupted caregiving roles and competing household economics within the Ari zone context, is, to our knowledge, articulated in more depth here than in prior Ethiopian MWH literature, and represents the principal contribution of this study beyond its geographic setting alone.

Despite the emotional, social and physical challenges, the overwhelming motivation for women to stay at an MWH is the perceived benefit of safety. This powerful belief in the MWH’s ability to provide a safer delivery and a healthier baby acts as a strong counterpoint to all the difficulties. This core finding is well-documented in the literature.29 It is this sense of security and trust in skilled care that primarily drives women’s decisions and validates the significant sacrifices they make. However, the effectiveness of this safety net can be undermined by ‘Delay 3’ (the delay in receiving adequate care) once at the facility. Our findings indicate that negative interactions with staff where women felt like a burden and were hesitant to ask for help directly contributed to this delay.18 30 Conversely, positive staff interactions built trust and ensured timely care. This highlights the crucial role of staff training and a patient-centred approach in reinforcing the primary objective of MWHs.

Read critically, our findings suggest that the three-delays framework,31 while useful for locating where barriers to obstetric care arise, does not fully capture how a single structural factor can act across more than one delay simultaneously, nor what happens within the waiting period itself. Financial hardship in our data did not only shape women’s initial decision to travel to an MWH (Delay 2); it resurfaced during their stay, through ongoing concerns about food costs and the price of medicines that should have been free. Consistent with qualitative applications of the three-delays model elsewhere,9 this points to an under-recognised delay in receiving adequate rather than merely accessible care during the waiting period itself, sitting between the conventional second and third delays. Likewise, our data suggest that even the anticipation of possible disrespect (women describing hesitancy to bother staff) may independently shape how women experience their stay, regardless of whether disrespect is ultimately experienced. This suggests that MWH quality should be understood not only through direct measures of mistreatment, but through the anticipatory anxiety that women bring into the facility.28

Implications of the study

The findings of this study have important, actionable implications for improving maternity waiting homes across four audiences. For frontline practice, our results suggest that integrating psychosocial support, such as training providers in compassionate care and facilitating peer-support groups, is essential to address the emotional distress women experience. For facility management, the study suggests an urgent need to secure a reliable food supply and improve infrastructure to ensure privacy and reduce overcrowding. For policy, the financial burdens identified suggest that policymakers must explore strategies like transportation subsidies to reduce out-of-pocket costs for families. Finally, for communities, the findings suggest that targeted awareness programmes are needed to engage families and community leaders, building a more supportive environment that encourages MWH use and shares domestic responsibilities.

For future interventions and research, our findings suggest a need to move beyond utilisation-focused study designs towards longitudinal or comparative qualitative work that follows women from the decision to attend an MWH through their stay and subsequent birth outcome, in order to establish whether the ambivalent balance of cost and benefit observed here shifts over time, or differs for women who leave an MWH early. Programme-theory approaches to understanding MWH uptake and scale-up may offer a useful framework for testing whether the psychosocial and financial interventions proposed above meaningfully shift this balance in practice.

Recommendations

Based on our findings, we recommend the following actions. First, zonal health departments and facility managers should prioritise providing a consistent food supply, improve infrastructure for privacy and implement training for healthcare providers on compassionate, respectful maternity care. Second, regional and federal policymakers should develop nationally standardised and funded guidelines for MWHs and explore financial support mechanisms, such as transportation vouchers, to reduce the economic burden on families. Third, community health workers should conduct targeted awareness campaigns to educate families and community leaders about the benefits of MWHs and encourage shared household responsibilities. Lastly, researchers should conduct intervention studies to test the effectiveness of providing food support or psychosocial services and further explore the full economic impact of MWH use on families.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several strengths and limitations. Its main strengths include the use of a qualitative design, which allowed for an in-depth exploration of women’s lived experiences and the achievement of data saturation, which enhanced the credibility of the findings. Furthermore, the use of member checking ensured the interpretations reflected participants’ realities and the detailed methodology allows for transferability to similar settings.

Regarding transferability, the participating facilities in the Ari zone ranged from a zonal hospital to primary health centres, all operating in a semi-agrarian, ethnically diverse rural context with limited road infrastructure. MWHs were typically staffed by frontline midwives and clinical nurses with limited medical supplies, while facility conditions featured shared, crowded rooms, communal outdoor cooking and intermittent water and sanitation amenities. Local cultural practices, such as traditional dietary regimens, reliance on informal peer networks and household labour dynamics requiring alternative childcare arrangements further shape women’s extended stays. Readers working in comparable rural, low-resource contexts may find these findings more directly applicable than readers in urban or better-resourced settings.

However, the study also has limitations. The findings are not statistically generalisable to all of Ethiopia due to the qualitative design and focus on one zone. The perspectives of women who chose not to use an MWH are missing, which might reveal additional barriers. Because participants were recruited from among women already resident in an MWH, the sample may also be subject to self-selection bias, favouring women who found the experience acceptable enough to remain for the duration of their stay; women who left an MWH early or avoided using one altogether may hold different, potentially more negative, views that are not represented here. Interviews took place within the health facility itself, which despite efforts to ensure a private setting may have encouraged socially desirable responses, particularly regarding staff attitudes and social desirability bias may have led some participants to underreport negative experiences with healthcare staff. Together, these factors suggest that the credibility of our findings for the experiences that were reported is likely to be higher than the confirmability of the absence of more negative accounts; future research using anonymous surveys or interviews conducted away from the facility could help address this.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found that women staying in maternity waiting homes experience a complex mix of challenges and benefits. They face significant emotional distress, including isolation, anxiety and guilt, alongside practical barriers like financial strain and food insecurity. However, they also find immense value in the proximity to skilled care, which provides relief and confidence and they develop vital peer-support networks. Therefore, for MWHs to fully reach their potential and become truly supportive environments, programmes must evolve to address these holistic emotional, social and practical needs, not just the need for physical proximity to a health facility.
