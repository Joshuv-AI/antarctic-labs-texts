Predicting differential ratings of perceived exertion (dRPE) in youth soccer using decision tree models
Authors: Robert Śliwowski, Tomasz Górecki, Michał Włodarczyk, Thierry Paillard, Teresa Zwierko, Jakub Marynowicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the relationship between external training load metrics obtained from GPS technology and differentiated ratings of perceived exertion (dRPE) in elite youth soccer players, and evaluated the predictive value of decision tree models for estimating exertion across multiple domains. Data were collected over a 17-week in-season period, yielding 537 training observations from 15 players. External load variables included total distance, moderate- and high-speed running, accelerations, decelerations, distances covered during acceleration and deceleration, and impacts, all normalized per minute of training. Internal load was assessed using a modified Borg CR-10 scale with separate ratings for overall exertion, lower-limb effort, breathlessness, and technical-cognitive demand. Decision tree models were developed for each dRPE dimension. Total distance emerged as the strongest predictor of overall exertion, muscular effort, and technical-cognitive strain, while breathlessness was primarily explained by moderate-speed running (14.4–19.8 km·h⁻¹). The models showed satisfactory session-level predictive accuracy within the monitored squad under repeated-session monitoring, with root mean square error values between 0.96 and 1.19 across domains. These results confirm the multidimensional nature of exertion in soccer and indicate the value of combining perceptual measures with interpretable machine learning models to improve individualized monitoring and understanding of training responses in elite youth players.Keywords: Perceived exertion, Training load, Youth soccer, GPS monitoring, Decision tree modeSubject terms: Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Monitoring training load in soccer is essential for maximizing physical performance, preventing injury and illness, and minimizing the risk of non-functional overreaching by ensuring adequate recovery. A comprehensive assessment of training load includes both external load (EL) and internal load (IL) measures1.

External load refers to the objective work completed by the athlete, regardless of their internal responses2. One of the most widely used and reliable tools for tracking player activity in running-based team sports is the Global Positioning System (GPS)3.

In contrast, internal load reflects the physiological and psychological responses to external stimuli. It is typically monitored using indicators such as the rating of perceived exertion (RPE), heart rate, or biochemical markers4. Several studies have demonstrated strong correlations between session RPE (sRPE) and physiological variables such as heart rate and blood lactate concentration during intermittent team sports like soccer4–7.

Importantly, RPE integrates both physiological strain and psychological effort, offering a comprehensive perspective on the demands imposed during training and match play8. A more specific variation, known as differentiated RPE (dRPE), enables separate assessment of distinct exertional domains, such as leg muscle effort (RPE-L), breathlessness (RPE-B), and technical or cognitive exertion (RPE-T)9. This method has been validated in team sport contexts9,10. The theoretical model of dRPE suggests that different perceptual signals, including central, peripheral, and cognitive components, are associated with specific physiological pathways11. Given the heterogeneous nature of training loads in team sports, a single global rating of perceived exertion may lack sufficient sensitivity to detect modality-specific internal load responses. Therefore, the assessment of dRPE, which has demonstrated sensitivity to variations in exercise type and intensity by partitioning central (e.g. respiratory) and peripheral (e.g. muscular) perceptual inputs, provides a more nuanced and sensitive approach to internal load monitoring12. As a result, dRPE may serve as a sensitive tool for capturing domain-specific training load13,14.

These subjective measures reflect the athlete’s perceived effort, which integrates both physical and psychological responses, offering a comprehensive understanding of training and match demands8. Moreover subjective measures can be collected for other modes of training such as resistance training and therefore allows the monitoring of cumulative load and change in load over the course of time15.

Integrating internal and external load measures offers a holistic perspective on players’ exertion levels, enabling precise workload management to optimize performance and recovery while minimizing the risk of overtraining or underloading. Consequently, predictive models that link internal responses to external loads are essential for optimizing training strategies and reducing injury risk16,17.

The relationship between external and internal load has been extensively examined in soccer. Multiple studies have reported moderate to very strong correlations between GPS-derived external load metrics and subjective internal load indicators, such as sRPE4,6,18–21.

More recently, de Dios-Álvarez et al.22 found that RPE-based indicators were moderately to strongly correlated with external load metrics in youth soccer players (age: 18.4 ± 0.7 years), supporting the applicability of RPE in this population. In contrast, Marynowicz et al.23 reported only small to moderate within-individual correlations between RPE and intensity-related measures. It is important to note that most existing studies have examined these relationships using group-level linear models.

To address this limitation, recent research has begun to explore machine learning approaches for modeling internal-external load relationships. Decision tree models (DTMs), in particular, have demonstrated effectiveness in predicting RPE based on external load metrics in a range of athlete populations, including professionals24–26, semi-professionals6, and youth players27,28. For example, Clemente27 reported moderate to large correlations between RPE and GPS metrics such as accelerations, PlayerLoad, sprint distance, and total distance during small-sided games. Marynowicz et al.28 showed that high-speed running (HSR) was the most important predictor of RPE for half of the youth players analyzed. Their decision tree approach provided transparent and visual insight into data structure.

Given the complexity of factors shaping differential ratings of perceived exertion, machine learning models such as decision trees offer promising potential to capture multidimensional relationships. Their ability to model non-linear patterns and identify variable-specific thresholds makes them particularly valuable in applied sport settings. Such predictive approaches may enhance individualized training prescriptions by tailoring workload to the athlete’s unique perceptual and physiological responses29.

Therefore, the aim of this study was twofold: to examine the relationship between GPS-derived external load metrics and dRPE components in youth soccer players, and to evaluate the application of decision tree models for predicting dRPE responses based on objective external training variables.

Materials and methods

Participants and data collection

Participants

Fifteen youth soccer players (age 13.76 ± 0.34 years, height 161.84 ± 9.98 cm, body mass 48.85 ± 8.34 kg) participated in the investigation. The players surveyed came from leading soccer academies in Poland and were all actively participating in high-level soccer competitions within their age category. Over 70% of the surveyed players represented Regional Football Associations in their particular age categories and over the period of data collection. In accordance with the Participant Classification Framework proposed by McKay et al.14, the athletes in the present study were classified as Tier 3: Highly Trained/National Level. This classification was based on the fact that the players received structured and periodized training, developed their soccer-specific skills within a professional academy environment, and competed in the first autonomous division. No players sustained injuries during the investigation. Goalkeepers were not included in the study due to the different physical demands of their position. Although the data obtained for this analysis are part of the athletes’ daily monitoring routine, the study was conducted and fully approved before the start of the assessments by the Health Research Ethics Committee of the institution where the research was conducted. All participants or their parents/ guardians were informed of the risks and signed an informed consent form before the investigation.

Design

Training load data was collected over a 17-week in-season competition period. This timeframe was selected to minimize variability in players’ physical fitness levels. Only field-based soccer training sessions, including warm-ups performed on the pitch, were considered for analysis. Individual rehabilitation or fitness sessions were excluded. Data was analyzed exclusively from training microcycles that included one competitive match per week. Microcycles involving multiple competitions were excluded due to the atypical and highly variable external loads associated with congested match schedules, which may confound the interpretation of weekly GPS-derived training load metrics. A typical microcycle consisted of four training sessions, all conducted at the same time of day on the same outdoor artificial grass pitch. Training sessions took place in the afternoon (between 4:00 pm and 7:00 pm), with at least 24 h of recovery between sessions. Players were allowed to hydrate freely during scheduled rest intervals.

Over the 17-week period, a total of 537 training session observations, nested within 15 players, were recorded, with a mean session duration of 79 ± 11 min. The number of observations per player ranged from 28 to 43, with a mean of 35.8 ± 4.86. The average ambient temperature during training was (10.2 ± 6.2 °C).

Both internal load (RPE) and external load (GPS-derived) metrics were collected as part of routine player monitoring. Only data from players who completed each training session in its entirety were included in the final analysis.

Methodology

The players’ external load during each training session was monitored using a portable, non-differential 10 Hz global positioning system (GPS) integrated with a 400 Hz triaxial accelerometer and a 10 Hz triaxial magnetometer (PLAYERTEK, Catapult Innovations, Melbourne, Australia). These GPS units have been shown to be valid and reliable for use in team sports and have been widely applied in soccer-related research30,31. Each player consistently wore the same device throughout the data collection period to reduce inter-unit variability32. Devices were positioned between the scapulae using a tight vest and were activated 15 min prior to the session in line with manufacturer guidelines to ensure optimal satellite signal acquisition. Following each session, data were downloaded and analyzed using dedicated GPS software.

External measures of intensity

Eight external load variables were collected and analyzed. These included: total distance (TD, m), moderate-speed running distance (MSR, m; defined as distance covered 14.4–19.8 km·h⁻¹), high-speed running distance (HSR, m; defined as distance covered > 19.8 km·h⁻¹), number of impacts (n; defined as accelerometer events > 3 g), acceleration (ACC, n; ≥2 m·s⁻² maintained for at least 0.5 s), deceleration (DEC, n; ≥2 m·s⁻² for ≥ 0.5 s), and distance covered during acceleration and deceleration phases (AccD and DecD, m). All variables were normalized per minute of session duration to obtain intensity values (e.g., TD/min, HSR/min). Impact data were derived from the triaxial accelerometers and included only significant events (e.g., collisions), excluding normal foot contacts during walking or running. The selected GPS and accelerometry-based variables are widely recognized in the literature and provide a comprehensive representation of both locomotor demands and mechanical load in elite soccer contexts33.

Internal training load was assessed using the modified Borg CR-10 scale, as described by Foster et al.34. Each player’s rating of perceived exertion (RPE) was collected individually (in isolation), separately for global exertion (RPE), breathlessness (RPE-B), leg muscle exertion (RPE-L) and cognitive/technical demands (RPE-T) approximately 20 min after the end of each training session. This time frame was selected to reduce the influence of the final part of the session and to minimize peer pressure effects on during reporting. Players were first asked the standardized question: “How hard was your session?” (1 = very, very easy; 10 = maximal exertion) and were then prompted to “please rate your (muscular, respiratory, cognitive/technical) perception of exertion. All players were thoroughly familiarized with the scale prior to the start of the study.

Statistical analysis

Decision trees were analyzed to examine the collected data. These widely utilized statistical models, which are non-parametric and non-linear, play a key role in machine learning applications. Tree-based models offer several benefits, including ease of interpretation, the ability to account for interactions among predictors, efficient training and prediction, and reliable modelling. Breiman35 provided a more detailed comparison with linear models. When comparing regression trees to neural networks, which have been employed in similar studies36, the former yields more precise predictions and allows for model visualization. This feature enables further expert evaluation, akin to linear models.

The regression trees were developed using the CART algorithm37. We used the Recursive PARTitioning (rpart) implementation38. The splitting criterion was the mean squared error (MSE), a widely employed criterion in regression tasks. Feature importance is calculated as the decrease in the mean squared error assigned to each feature at every split, and the total is computed. Additionally, the best alternative variables are tracked at each split, as some influential variables might not be selected for that split.

Since the analytical approach relied on CART-based predictive modelling, a classical a priori power analysis was not applicable. Instead, model adequacy was addressed by controlling tree complexity and evaluating generalization performance. All regression trees were constrained to a maximum depth of 3 and a limited number of terminal nodes, ensuring a favorable ratio between sample size (537 observations) and model complexity. Model tuning and pruning were conducted using cross-validation within the caret39 framework, and minimum terminal node sizes were imposed to prevent overfitting driven by small subsamples.

For each outcome (RPE, RPE-B, RPE-L, RPE-T), RMSE was computed from out-of-sample predictions obtained via cross-validation, providing an estimate of predictive accuracy on unseen data rather than training error. The nesting of observations within players was acknowledged in the interpretation of predictive performance and explicitly discussed as a methodological limitation.

Although observations were nested within players, standard CART models were applied at the session level with the primary aim of prediction rather than inference, and with strict control of model complexity to reduce overfitting.

Results

Table 1 presents descriptive statistics for internal and external training load variables. The mean values of RPE and its components (RPE, RPE-L, RPE-B, RPE-T) ranged between 4.5 and 5.9, indicating moderate to high perceived exertion across training sessions. Standard deviations of about 1.3–1.4 reflect substantial inter-individual variability in subjective responses. Among external measures, total distance (65.4 m/min) showed the highest average values, confirming its central role as a global indicator of training intensity, though with considerable variability (SD = 16.4). High-speed running was relatively low (2.0 m/min, SD = 3.1), suggesting that very intense efforts occurred less frequently within the microcycles. The number and distance of accelerations and decelerations (approximately 2.1 per minute and 2.2–2.9 m/min, respectively) underscore the significance of mechanical load in soccer-specific activity patterns. Overall, the descriptive data highlight the heterogeneous structure of training demands, with total distance and moderate-speed running emerging as the dominant contributors to external load, providing a solid basis for the subsequent predictive modeling of dRPE components.

As a baseline comparison, multiple linear regression models including the same set of external load predictors were trained and evaluated using the identical cross-validation procedure. Across all outcomes (RPE, RPE-B, RPE-L, RPE-T), CART models yielded lower RMSE values than linear regression, with an average improvement of approximately 3–7%. This comparison indicates superior predictive performance of tree-based models and supports the presence of non-linear relationships between external load metrics and perceived exertion.

The CART models also demonstrated satisfactory predictive performance across all dRPE components, with root mean square error (RMSE) values ranging from 0.958 for overall RPE to 1.190 for RPE-T, indicating stable and reliable session-level predictions.

Discussion

It should be emphasized that the present findings are based on observational data and predictive modelling; therefore, the identified relationships reflect associations and predictive contributions rather than causal effects.

This study investigated the relationship between GPS-derived external load variables and dRPE in youth soccer players. Additionally, it evaluated the predictive performance of decision tree models for estimating dRPE components based on external load metrics. The findings of this study highlight the effectiveness of decision tree models in predicting dRPE among youth soccer players. Particular attention was given to variables such as TD and MSR, which were the strongest predictors of dRPE components.

The study suggests that TD was the strongest predictor of overall RPE (relative importance: 30.4%) and two dRPE components: RPE-L (31.2%) and RPE-T (28.2%). TD is a widely used variable to quantify workload during both training sessions and competitive matches in elite-level soccer3. Our findings are consistent with previous research on adult sub-elite football players6, where sRPE was strongly associated with TD covered (r = 0.74). At the youth level Marynowicz et al.23 showed large within-individual correlations between TD and sRPE (r = 0.70). TD is also the variable that showed the strongest association with internal load and intensity indicators during team sport training and competition21, which highlights the importance of TD as a key load indicator in professional soccer.

On the other hand, MSR (14.4–19.8 km·h⁻¹) emerged as the strongest predictor of RPE-B (32.5%), indicating its dominant predictive importance for the perception of cardiovascular effort among youth soccer players. McLaren et al.7 reported “Unclear” relationships between sRPE and high speed running distance (≥ 13.1–15.0 km·h⁻¹) per minute in team-sport athletes during training and competition. Gaudino et al.18 found that sRPE-TL was significantly associated with high-speed distance (> 14.4 km·h⁻¹; r = 0.61) and showed a weaker association for RPE and high-speed distance per minute (r = 0.26) among adult soccer players. Previous studies on youth soccer players have consistently highlighted the significance of high-speed running (HSR) in monitoring training load and player response. Using Principal Component Analysis Maughan et al.40 reported that HSR (19.8–24.98 km·h⁻¹) had one of the highest loadings on component 2 during days when two principal components were identified. The relevance of HSR in the dose-response relationship has been well-documented. In a group analysis, high-speed running distance (> 19.8 km·h⁻¹) was the strongest predictor of perceived exertion (RPE) for half of the players28. Moreover, it was the only metric to show a strong correlation with post-match changes in both biochemical and neuromuscular markers at the senior level41. Despite the fact that another threshold was used authors concluded that such activities (MSR and HSR) were associated with substantial metabolic demands, which are particularly relevant in youth soccer, where the interplay between mixed (aerobic-anaerobic) effort plays a crucial role in their performance and development1.

The studies cited above predominantly employed traditional statistical methods, which use linear modeling approaches to examine the relationships between internal and external training loads, offering foundational insights. However, these methods are constrained by predefined assumptions and have a limited capacity to capture complex, nonlinear interactions. The decision tree models used in this study allowed for the identification of hierarchical and nonlinear relationships between external load variables and perceptual exertion. Unlike traditional linear approaches, decision trees are capable of modeling threshold effects and interactions among predictors, while remaining interpretable for practitioners. The tree structures revealed distinct variable importance profiles for each dRPE component, reinforcing the conceptual framework proposed by Robertson and Noble11, which suggests that perceived exertion emerges from the integration of multiple physiological and task-related inputs.

The results of this study indicate that decision tree models constitute an effective framework for predicting differentiated Ratings of Perceived Exertion (dRPE) in youth soccer. Beyond overall predictive capability, the models revealed distinct variable importance profiles across dRPE components, highlighting that different perceptual domains were associated with partially unique external load characteristics. This finding aligns with the conceptual framework proposed by Robertson and Noble¹¹, which suggests that perceived exertion emerges from the integration of multiple physiological, mechanical, and task-related inputs rather than from a single dominant stimulus.

Taken together, these results indicate that, compared with linear regression approaches, CART models offer both improved predictive accuracy and interpretable non-linear thresholds, which are particularly relevant for applied monitoring in youth soccer.

Additionally, the visual representation of decision trees simplifies result interpretation, enhancing their applicability in practice. Each model (RPE, RPE-B, RPE-L, RPE-T) was designed with five leaf nodes and a depth = 3, revealing nuanced insights into player exertion levels. The structured representation of decision trees, with distinct leaf nodes, provided granular insights into the complex interplay between external load variables and dRPE components. Thus, the segmentation observed in leaf nodes highlighted thresholds for critical predictors, such as MSR and TD per minute, which were associated with higher predicted values of perceptual responses.

Our findings are consistent with and further supported by a growing body of research utilizing machine learning approaches to predict RPE based on external training load metrics in soccer players24,26,28,42. Across these studies, various machine learning techniques have highlighted the importance of external load indicators (ELIs) as key predictors of RPE, while also underscoring the nuanced and individualized nature of these relationships. For instance, Marynowicz et al.28 indicated the utility of decision tree models in capturing the relationship between RPE and external load metrics in youth soccer players. HSR was the dominant predictor of RPE (relative importance = 0.61), but other variables, such as impacts per minute and accelerations per minute, played significant roles for certain individuals. Interestingly, while HSR was the primary predictor for most players, total distance per minute was the strongest predictor for only one player, reflecting variability in individual responses to training load. This aligns with findings from Jaspers et al.24, who used artificial neural networks (ANNs) and LASSO models to explore RPE prediction in soccer. Their results confirmed the importance of decelerations as a key external load indicator, highlighting the role of sudden intensity changes in shaping internal load.

Adding further depth to these insights, Geurkink et al.42 employed Gradient Boosting Machines and emphasized the predictive power of total distance as a central external load indicator. Their findings also highlighted the importance of supplementary variables, such as individual player markers and group-based predictions, which enhanced model accuracy. This resonates with both Marynowicz et al.‘s28 emphasis on individualized predictors and Jaspers et al.‘s24 group-level findings, illustrating the complementary nature of these approaches. Although we did not incorporate supplementary variables in our study, the demonstrated potential for refining predictions through supplementary data offers an intriguing avenue for future research.

From a more metabolic oriented perspective, Rossi et al.26 provided additional insights into the relationship between external training workloads and both RPE and sRPE in elite soccer players. Their findings revealed that acute workloads from the preceding week were the strongest predictors of RPE, with metabolic variables, such as time spent in specific power zones, being more influential than kinematic or mechanical ones. For sRPE, daily workload features, particularly those capturing mechanical demands like distance in acceleration zones, played a dominant role. Interestingly, while their predictive models achieved strong correlations (e.g., r = 0.70 for RPE and r = 0.84 for sRPE), they tended to underestimate high values and overestimate low ones -.

a systematic pattern also observed in other machine learning studies. Despite its conceptual advantages and its ability to capture the specific demands of accelerations and decelerations43, the validity of the metabolic power approach in intermittent exercise remains highly contentious44. Previous validation studies have indicated that GPS-derived models may substantially underestimate energy expenditure during sport-specific intermittent protocols when compared to criterion measures of oxygen uptake (VO2), with reported discrepancies reaching up to 52%45–47. Furthermore, significant differences exist between metabolic power estimates obtained via microtechnology and those derived from the direct measurement of VO248. Despite its ability to capture the energetic demands of accelerations and decelerations43, the metabolic power approach was originally developed to estimate the cost of accelerated running49. Its validity appears most consistent in protocols that closely reflect these locomotor assumptions, such as running-based circuits performed on grass with professional players, as demonstrated by Savoia et al.48, where high agreement with oxygen uptake was reported. In contrast, discrepancies have been observed in tasks involving complex, technical, and non-locomotor actions - such as dribbling, passing, or collisions - which extend beyond the original theoretical framework of the model45,46,50. These findings suggest that the validity of the metabolic power approach is context-dependent and may be reduced in ecologically valid, game-based training environments characterized by multidirectional and task-integrated activity patterns. Given that the training sessions analyzed in the present study involved a substantial proportion of such technical and non-locomotor activities, the metabolic power approach was intentionally excluded. This decision ensures methodological consistency by focusing on conventional locomotive metrics with established reliability and interpretability for training prescription in this specific context.

Unlike previous studies that have examined overall perceived exertion in relation to global or velocity-based metrics, this study extends the analysis by incorporating differentiated RPE components (RPE-L, RPE-B, RPE-T). By modelling perceptual responses across distinct physiological and cognitive domains, the findings offer a more nuanced understanding of how external training variables relate to specific types of perceived strain. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply decision tree models for predicting differentiated exertion components based on GPS-derived metrics in youth soccer players. This approach advances the field in two ways: first, by indicating that different load variables dominate in different perceptual domains, and second, by confirming that interpretable, non-linear models can successfully capture these domain-specific relationships. As such, the study provides both a theoretical contribution to the understanding of exertional mechanisms in team sport settings and a practical framework for individualized training monitoring using dRPE.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. A key methodological limitation arises from the longitudinal, nested structure of the dataset, with repeated and unbalanced observations collected within individual players. Standard CART models assume independence of observations and do not explicitly account for within-player correlation. As a result, prediction error estimates may be partially optimistic due to shared individual-specific patterns across sessions. Although model complexity was constrained and predictive performance was evaluated using cross-validation, future investigations should consider subject-wise validation strategies or mixed-effects tree-based models to explicitly address within-player dependency. From an applied perspective, however, the present models remain useful for within-squad monitoring, where repeated observations per player are an inherent feature of the training process. A further limitation concerns the scope and generalizability of the findings. Generalizability of the findings is limited by the specific characteristics of the sample, including the young age of the players and the single-academy context. These characteristics may constrain the extent to which the observed relationships can be extrapolated to other populations, such as female athletes, older players, or those competing in different competitive and organizational environments. In addition, the predictive models did not account for several contextual and developmental factors, including tactical role, biological maturation, and competition exposure, all of which may influence both external load accumulation and perceptual responses to training. The absence of these variables may reduce contextual sensitivity and should be addressed in future studies to enhance model generalizability and ecological relevance. Although internal load was quantified using the structured and validated dRPE framework, the lack of complementary physiological or biochemical measures (e.g., heart rate-derived indices or blood-based markers) limited the possibility of fully triangulating subjective exertion with objective internal load responses. Finally, while decision tree models offer transparency and interpretability, they remain sensitive to data variability and are limited in their ability to capture complex temporal dynamics and longer-term training history effects. These methodological considerations highlight the need for future research combining longitudinal designs, richer contextual information, and advanced modelling strategies to more fully represent the multidimensional nature of perceived exertion in youth soccer.

In summary, the findings of this study confirm that decision tree models can effectively predict differentiated perceived exertion responses based on GPS-derived external training load metrics. TD and MSR were the most influential predictors, although their relative importance varied across perceptual domains. These outcomes support the practical use of perceptually differentiated RPE and data-driven methods for individual monitoring of training responses in youth soccer.

Conclusions

Based on these findings, several practical strategies can be recommended for optimizing training design, monitoring, and individualization in youth soccer. First, total distance (TD), identified as the strongest predictor of overall RPE and key dRPE components (RPE-L and RPE-T), underscores its central role in managing training intensity. Coaches should use this metric to monitor and regulate workload, ensuring balanced distribution throughout the microcycle in order to prevent overtraining and promote adequate recovery, particularly on the days leading after matches (MD + 2 and MD + 1). Second, MSR, identified as the strongest predictor of RPE-B, emphasizes the importance of cardiovascular effort in youth soccer. The findings indicate that MSR exposure/density can be interpreted as a practical surrogate for cardiorespiratory perceptual load within the intermittent activity profile characteristic of soccer51. To ensure training specificity and capture the multidimensional nature of soccer demands, we emphasize that MSR-derived metrics should be integrated with other external load indicators (e.g., high-speed running, accelerations, and decelerations) rather than used as a standalone measure. Third, the decision tree models offer an intuitive and interpretable framework for prescribing training loads based on repeated-session monitoring within players. These models enable coaches to identify critical thresholds in TD and MSR, allowing for precise adjustments to training content. Such an approach supports data-informed planning aimed at improving training efficiency while reducing the risk of injury and excessive exertion.
