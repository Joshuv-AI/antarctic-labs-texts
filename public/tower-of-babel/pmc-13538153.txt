Radiographic outcomes of CT-based robotic-assisted versus manual direct anterior total hip arthroplasty with an acetabular-only registration workflow: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zhiming Yu, Haoran Gong, Run Liu, Hui Wang, Qi Sun, Changcheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLimited exposure through the direct anterior approach may restrict robotic registration to the acetabulum. We compared postoperative radiographic outcomes of CT-based robotic-assisted DAA THA performed with an acetabular-only bony registration workflow with manual DAA THA. This retrospective cohort included 111 primary direct anterior total hip arthroplasties in 108 patients: 57 robotic-assisted and 54 manual procedures performed from October to December 2025. Both groups targeted 40 degrees of radiographic inclination and 15 degrees of radiographic anteversion. The robotic workflow used 30 registration points exclusively on acetabular bone. Co-primary outcomes were absolute postoperative inclination and anteversion target deviations. Generalized estimating equations accounted for bilateral hips and adjusted for baseline covariates. Robot-reported and postoperative angles were assessed using Bland-Altman analysis and intraclass correlation coefficients. Absolute inclination deviation was lower with robotic assistance (2.89 ± 2.39 degrees vs. 4.83 ± 3.53 degrees; adjusted difference, -1.40 degrees; 95% confidence interval [CI], -2.51 to -0.29; Holm-adjusted P = 0.027). Absolute anteversion deviation did not differ significantly (5.09 ± 3.82 degrees vs. 6.02 ± 4.49 degrees; adjusted difference, -0.92 degrees; 95% CI, -2.44 to 0.60; Holm-adjusted P = 0.234). Combined angular error was lower with robotic assistance (adjusted difference, -1.65 degrees; 95% CI, -3.12 to -0.19; P = 0.027). Both angles were within the Lewinnek zone in 86.0% and 72.2% of hips, respectively (adjusted odds ratio, 2.20; 95% CI, 0.76–6.33; P = 0.145). In the robotic group, intraoperative-to-postoperative bias was − 0.05 degrees for inclination and − 0.38 degrees for anteversion, but 95% limits of agreement were − 9.08 to 8.98 degrees and − 10.14 to 9.38 degrees; corresponding intraclass correlation coefficients were 0.319 and 0.491. Robotic assistance increased operative time by 15.02 min (P < 0.001). CT-based robotic-assisted DAA THA using an acetabular-only registration workflow was associated with a modest reduction in postoperative inclination error and combined angular error compared with manual placement, but not with lower anteversion error or a higher adjusted Lewinnek-zone rate. Because the study did not include a robotic comparator using a conventional registration strategy, the observed differences cannot be attributed specifically to acetabular-only registration. Wide individual limits of agreement further indicate that robot-reported and postoperative angles are not interchangeable, and the clinical relevance of the small radiographic differences remains uncertain.Keywords: Robotic-assisted total hip arthroplasty, Direct anterior approach, Acetabular-only registration, Cup orientation, CT-based planning

Background

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a highly successful operation, but reproducible acetabular component orientation remains technically demanding [1]. Cup malposition has been associated with instability, impingement, altered biomechanics, accelerated wear, and revision risk [2, 3]. The conventional Lewinnek zone centers on 40 degrees of inclination and 15 degrees of anteversion, although later work has shown that no universal static safe zone can fully account for patient-specific stability [3, 4].

Robotic and computer-assisted systems were developed to improve the accuracy and reproducibility of implant placement. CT-based systems use preoperative three-dimensional planning, intraoperative registration, and tracked instrumentation to relate the surgical field to the virtual pelvic model. Previous studies have generally shown reduced variability or fewer orientation outliers with robotic assistance [5–7]. However, accuracy depends not only on robotic execution but also on registration quality, intraoperative pelvic motion, impaction, and the relationship between the system coordinate frame and postoperative measurement methods [6, 7].

The direct anterior approach (DAA) preserves posterior soft tissues and facilitates early recovery, but its limited operative field may restrict access to the bony landmarks commonly used for registration. Prior robotic DAA studies have specifically recognized that limited access to the anatomy required for bony registration could affect accuracy [5]. In our practice, all registration points are acquired from the exposed acetabular bony surface, without extra-acetabular landmarks. The radiographic performance of this acetabular-only workflow has not been well characterized for the CT-dependent robotic system used in this cohort. Importantly, comparison with manual THA can evaluate the performance of the overall robotic workflow but cannot isolate the specific contribution of the registration strategy.

The primary objective of this study was to compare postoperative radiographic inclination and anteversion target deviations between CT-based robotic-assisted DAA THA performed with an acetabular-only registration workflow and manual DAA THA. The secondary objectives were to assess agreement between intraoperative robot-reported cup orientation and postoperative measurements, compare conventional safe-zone rates, and evaluate restoration of leg length, offset, hip center height, and operative time. We hypothesized that the robotic-assisted workflow would reduce postoperative cup-orientation error compared with manual placement.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-center retrospective cohort study was reported in accordance with the STROBE recommendations [8]. We screened 148 consecutive primary DAA THA procedures performed between October and December 2025. Eligible procedures required complete postoperative imaging suitable for the predefined measurements. Revision THA, active hip infection, tumor, severe metabolic bone disease, severe developmental dysplasia, acetabular bone loss, old fracture deformity, previous ipsilateral hip surgery, or pelvic, spinal, or lower-limb deformity that prevented reliable measurement were excluded.

Thirty-seven hips were excluded because the required postoperative imaging was unavailable or the images did not satisfy the radiographic quality criteria, including excessive pelvic rotation. Image eligibility was determined jointly by two observers. The final cohort comprised 111 hips in 108 patients: 57 robotic-assisted and 54 manual procedures. Three patients underwent bilateral THA; each hip was treated as a separate procedure and patient-level clustering was addressed in the statistical analysis. The treatment method was selected by the patient after discussion of both options; robotic assistance incurred an additional charge. The study flow is shown in Fig. 1.

Surgical procedure

All procedures were primary cementless press-fit THAs performed through the DAA with the patient in the lateral decubitus position. The pelvis was stabilized using fixation posts mounted on a perforated positioning board, with anterior and posterior supports applied at the pubic symphysis and sacrum, respectively. The lateral decubitus position was maintained throughout acetabular exposure, reaming, and cup implantation without intraoperative repositioning. The skin incision started approximately 1 cm posterolateral to the anterior superior iliac spine and extended approximately 10 cm toward the fibular head. In the robotic group, after the periacetabular soft tissues were cleared and the acetabular bony surface was adequately exposed, the pelvic tracking array was fixed to the ipsilateral iliac crest and acetabular registration was performed with the patient maintained in the same lateral decubitus position. Robotic guidance was used during acetabular preparation and cup implantation. After cup placement and verification of the final cup orientation, the robotic-assisted equipment was removed. A representative photograph of the operative positioning and skin-incision orientation is shown in Fig. 2. A single surgeon who had passed the learning curve for robotic-assisted THA performed all operations. The incision, exposure, and soft-tissue management were identical in the two groups, and intraoperative fluoroscopy was not used. Although DAA THA is frequently performed in the supine position, it can also be performed in the lateral decubitus position, and prior randomized evidence has reported satisfactory clinical and radiographic outcomes with both setups [9].

Robotic registration and cup placement

In the robotic-assisted group, preoperative CT-based planning was performed with the JianJia robotic-assisted THA system (Arthrobot Hip; Hangzhou Jianjia Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). The predefined target orientation was 40 degrees of radiographic inclination and 15 degrees of radiographic anteversion. The pelvic tracking array was fixed to the ipsilateral iliac crest. Specific model identifiers for the implant components, conventional alignment guide, CT and radiographic equipment, and image-analysis software were not consistently retrievable from the retrospective records and are therefore not reported.

Because of the limited exposure available through the DAA, registration was restricted entirely to the acetabulum. Thirty registration points were collected from exposed acetabular bony surfaces, including the acetabular rim, lunate surface, acetabular fossa, and bony regions adjacent to the transverse acetabular ligament. No extra-acetabular bony landmarks were registered. Acetabular reaming proceeded only when the system-displayed registration error was 0.5 mm or less. Registration was then checked at five verification points specified by the system.

After final cup implantation, a tracked probe was sequentially placed on multiple points along the opening rim of the acetabular component. The system-reported radiographic inclination and radiographic anteversion were recorded as the intraoperative robot-reported cup orientation.

Manual cup placement

In the manual group, the acetabular component was implanted with the same target orientation of 40 degrees of inclination and 15 degrees of anteversion. Cup alignment was determined using the mechanical alignment guide attached to the cup impactor together with the surgeon’s intraoperative assessment.

Postoperative imaging and measurements

Postoperative CT and standardized anteroposterior pelvic radiographs were obtained on the first or second postoperative day. On axial CT, the outer rim of the acetabular component was identified to define the cup opening plane. Three-dimensional pelvic reconstruction was performed, and the pelvic coordinate system was corrected to the anterior pelvic plane. Anatomical anteversion was measured as the angle between the cup opening plane and the sagittal plane. Radiographic inclination was measured on the standardized anteroposterior pelvic radiograph as the angle between the cup opening line and the inter-teardrop line.

Radiographic anteversion was derived from anatomical anteversion and radiographic inclination according to Murray’s definition [10].

RA = arctan[tan(AA) × sin(RI)]

where RA is radiographic anteversion, AA is anatomical anteversion, and RI is radiographic inclination.

On the postoperative anteroposterior pelvic radiograph, the pelvic midline, hip centers of rotation, inter-teardrop line, femoral shaft axes, and apices of the lesser trochanters were identified. Acetabular offset was the horizontal distance from the pelvic midline to the hip center of rotation. Femoral offset was the perpendicular distance from the femoral shaft axis to the hip center of rotation. Combined offset was the sum of acetabular and femoral offsets. Hip center height was the perpendicular distance from the hip center of rotation to the inter-teardrop line. Leg length was the perpendicular distance from the apex of the lesser trochanter to the inter-teardrop line. Absolute side-to-side differences were used to quantify restoration. Outcome definitions

The co-primary outcomes were the absolute postoperative deviations from the predefined target radiographic inclination and anteversion: |RI − 40 degrees| and |RA − 15 degrees|. A two-dimensional combined angular error was calculated as the square root of [(RI − 40 degrees)² + (RA − 15 degrees)²]. The conventional Lewinnek zone was defined as an inclination of 30–50 degrees and an anteversion of 5–25 degrees [3].

Secondary outcomes included postoperative RI and RA, the proportions within the inclination, anteversion, and combined Lewinnek zones, absolute leg-length discrepancy, absolute acetabular offset difference, absolute femoral offset difference, absolute combined offset difference, absolute hip-center height difference, and operative time. In the robotic group, agreement outcomes included signed bias (intraoperative minus postoperative), mean absolute error, root-mean-square error, 95% limits of agreement, absolute-agreement intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and the proportions of hips with errors within 2, 3, and 5 degrees.

Measurement reliability

Two observers who were blinded to the treatment group independently completed all postoperative measurements. The mean of their initial measurements was used for the main analysis. One observer repeated all measurements after a 2-week interval. Interobserver reliability was assessed from the two initial measurement sets, and intraobserver reliability was assessed from the first and repeated measurements. Two-way absolute-agreement, single-measure ICCs with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were reported [11].

Data management and statistical analysis

The source data were checked for blank records, range errors, duplicate procedure rows, missingness, and internal arithmetic consistency. The three repeated patient identifiers represented bilateral procedures rather than duplicate records. The calculated radiographic anteversion values reproduced the Murray transformation, and the main analysis variables were complete. Intraoperative robot-reported values were structurally unavailable in the manual group and were therefore restricted to robotic-group agreement analyses. No imputation was performed.

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and median with interquartile range; categorical variables were summarized as number and percentage. The hip was the primary unit of analysis. Between-group effects were estimated using generalized estimating equations with an exchangeable working correlation and robust standard errors, with patient as the clustering variable [12]. Gaussian identity-link models were used for continuous outcomes and binomial logit-link models for binary outcomes. Models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, height, and operative side. Effects are reported as adjusted mean differences for continuous outcomes and adjusted odds ratios for binary outcomes, each with 95% confidence intervals.

The family-wise error rate for the two co-primary outcomes was controlled using Holm’s sequential procedure [13]. Other analyses were considered secondary or exploratory and used two-sided P < 0.05 without multiplicity adjustment. Agreement between intraoperative and postoperative measurements was assessed using Bland-Altman analysis [14], mean absolute error, root-mean-square error, and two-way absolute-agreement, single-measure ICC. Bootstrap confidence intervals were based on 10,000 resamples. Sensitivity analyses repeated the adjusted models after retaining one hip per patient; all eight possible selections for the three bilateral patients were evaluated. No formal sample-size calculation was performed because all eligible procedures during the study period were included.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University (approval no. [2026] Research Ethics Review No. Y027). The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design.

Results

Cohort and baseline characteristics

Of 148 screened hips, 37 were excluded because of unavailable or inadequate postoperative imaging, leaving 111 hips in 108 patients for analysis (Fig. 1). The robotic-assisted group included 57 hips and the manual group included 54 hips. There were no missing values in the variables used for the comparative analyses.

Mean age was 44.30 ± 7.50 years in the robotic group and 46.83 ± 6.71 years in the manual group. Men accounted for 77.2% and 75.9% of the groups, respectively; mean body mass index was 26.23 ± 3.52 and 26.11 ± 3.94 kg/m²; and right-sided procedures accounted for 54.4% and 42.6%. Mean height was greater in the robotic group (1.706 ± 0.059 m vs. 1.663 ± 0.079 m, P = 0.002), supporting covariate adjustment in the main models. Other measured baseline characteristics were not statistically different (Table 1).

Primary cup-orientation outcomes

Mean postoperative RI was 40.51 ± 3.74 degrees in the robotic group and 37.32 ± 5.37 degrees in the manual group. Mean postoperative RA was 17.54 ± 5.86 degrees and 14.50 ± 7.53 degrees, respectively.

The absolute RI target deviation was 2.89 ± 2.39 degrees in the robotic group and 4.83 ± 3.53 degrees in the manual group. After adjustment, robotic assistance was associated with a 1.40-degree lower absolute RI deviation (adjusted mean difference, -1.40 degrees; 95% CI, -2.51 to -0.29; unadjusted-for-multiplicity P = 0.014; Holm-adjusted P = 0.027).

The absolute RA target deviation was 5.09 ± 3.82 degrees in the robotic group and 6.02 ± 4.49 degrees in the manual group. The adjusted difference was − 0.92 degrees (95% CI, -2.44 to 0.60; Holm-adjusted P = 0.234). Thus, the co-primary outcome was significant for inclination but not for anteversion.

The combined angular error was also lower in the robotic group (6.38 ± 3.71 degrees vs. 8.36 ± 4.69 degrees; adjusted mean difference, -1.65 degrees; 95% CI, -3.12 to -0.19; P = 0.027) (Table 2).

Lewinnek-zone placement

Radiographic inclination was within the Lewinnek range in 56 of 57 robotic hips (98.2%) and 48 of 54 manual hips (88.9%). Radiographic anteversion was within range in 50 of 57 (87.7%) and 41 of 54 hips (75.9%), respectively. Both angles were within range in 49 of 57 robotic hips (86.0%) and 39 of 54 manual hips (72.2%). Adjusted between-group differences were not statistically significant: the adjusted odds ratio for both angles being within range was 2.20 (95% CI, 0.76–6.33; P = 0.145) (Table 2).

Agreement between intraoperative robot-reported and postoperative angles

In the robotic group, mean intraoperative robot-reported RI was 40.45 ± 4.12 degrees and mean postoperative RI was 40.51 ± 3.74 degrees. The signed bias was − 0.05 degrees (95% CI, -1.28 to 1.17), with 95% limits of agreement from − 9.08 to 8.98 degrees. Mean absolute error was 3.36 degrees (bootstrap 95% CI, 2.60–4.21), root-mean-square error was 4.57 degrees, and the absolute-agreement ICC was 0.319 (bootstrap 95% CI, -0.031 to 0.574). RI error was within 2, 3, and 5 degrees in 42.1%, 54.4%, and 80.7% of hips, respectively.

Mean intraoperative robot-reported RA was 17.16 ± 3.75 degrees and mean postoperative RA was 17.54 ± 5.86 degrees. The signed bias was − 0.38 degrees (95% CI, -1.70 to 0.94), with 95% limits of agreement from − 10.14 to 9.38 degrees. Mean absolute error was 3.93 degrees (bootstrap 95% CI, 3.18–4.71), root-mean-square error was 4.95 degrees, and the ICC was 0.491 (bootstrap 95% CI, 0.288–0.645). RA error was within 2, 3, and 5 degrees in 29.8%, 42.1%, and 70.2% of hips. Both RI and RA errors were within 5 degrees in 36 of 57 hips (63.2%) (Table 4; Fig. 4).

Secondary radiographic outcomes and operative time

Absolute leg-length discrepancy was 3.10 ± 2.29 mm in the robotic group and 3.68 ± 2.93 mm in the manual group; the adjusted difference was − 0.38 mm (95% CI, -1.37 to 0.60; P = 0.444). Absolute hip-center height difference was also similar (adjusted difference, -0.06 mm; 95% CI, -1.03 to 0.91; P = 0.905).

Absolute acetabular offset difference was lower in the robotic group (5.15 ± 4.01 mm vs. 6.95 ± 4.55 mm; adjusted difference, -1.78 mm; 95% CI, -3.49 to -0.06; P = 0.042), as was absolute femoral offset difference (3.47 ± 3.44 mm vs. 5.82 ± 4.52 mm; adjusted difference, -2.27 mm; 95% CI, -3.82 to -0.73; P = 0.004). The adjusted difference in absolute combined offset was − 2.34 mm (95% CI, -4.69 to 0.02; P = 0.052). These secondary findings were exploratory and were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Mean operative time was 87.05 ± 17.47 min with robotic assistance and 74.41 ± 17.78 min with manual surgery. The adjusted increase associated with robotic assistance was 15.02 min (95% CI, 8.71–21.32; P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Measurement reliability and sensitivity analyses

Interobserver ICCs ranged from 0.800 for RA to 0.988 for leg-length difference, and intraobserver ICCs ranged from 0.828 for hip-center height difference to 0.957 for RI. All prespecified imaging measures therefore demonstrated good to excellent reliability according to commonly used ICC interpretation thresholds (Table 4) [11].

Across all eight sensitivity datasets that retained one hip per bilateral patient, the adjusted robotic effect remained significant for absolute RI deviation (effect range, -1.51 to -1.32 degrees; P range, 0.009–0.021) and combined angular error (effect range, -1.77 to -1.62 degrees; P range, 0.021–0.034). Conclusions for absolute RA deviation and combined Lewinnek-zone placement were unchanged.

Discussion

The principal finding was that CT-based robotic-assisted DAA THA performed with an acetabular-only registration workflow was associated with a smaller postoperative inclination error than manual DAA THA. The adjusted between-group difference in absolute inclination deviation was 1.40 degrees and remained statistically significant after control of the family-wise error rate. However, the magnitude of this difference was modest, absolute anteversion error was not significantly reduced, and combined Lewinnek-zone placement was not significantly higher after adjustment. Because no clinically important threshold for angular error was prespecified and no patient-reported or other clinical outcomes were evaluated, the clinical importance of the 1.40-degree radiographic difference cannot be determined from this study.

The study design also limits attribution of the observed difference to acetabular-only registration itself. The comparator was manual THA rather than robotic THA using a conventional registration strategy; therefore, the between-group effect reflects the complete robotic workflow, including CT-based planning, tracking, registration, guided execution, and intraoperative assessment. The results should consequently be interpreted as a characterization of a robotic-assisted DAA workflow that uses acetabular-only registration, not as proof that acetabular-only registration is superior to or equivalent to other robotic registration strategies. A direct robotic-to-robotic comparison would be required to isolate the contribution of the registration method.

The method-comparison results further support a cautious interpretation. Mean intraoperative-to-postoperative biases were close to zero for both RI and RA, indicating little systematic overestimation or underestimation at the group level. In contrast, the 95% limits of agreement spanned approximately +/-9-10 degrees, and absolute-agreement ICCs were only 0.319 for RI and 0.491 for RA. Thus, small average bias masked substantial patient-level variability. Similar discrepancies between intraoperative navigation or robotic readings and postoperative imaging have been described previously [7, 15]. The observed variation may reflect registration uncertainty, pelvic motion, cup movement during final impaction, probe acquisition at the cup rim, and differences between the intraoperative anterior-pelvic-plane reference and postoperative imaging geometry. Robot-reported angles should therefore not be used as interchangeable substitutes for postoperative measurements in an individual hip.

The effect was clearer for inclination than for anteversion. Anteversion is particularly sensitive to pelvic tilt and to the selected definition and reference plane [6, 10]. In this study, postoperative RA was derived from CT-based AA and radiographic inclination using Murray’s transformation. Although measurement reliability was good, uncertainty in both source angles contributes to the derived RA. Lateral decubitus positioning and intraoperative pelvic motion may further alter the relationship between the planned anterior pelvic plane and the postoperative reference. These factors may partly explain why the robotic-assisted workflow reduced inclination error but did not produce a statistically significant reduction in anteversion error.

The conventional Lewinnek zone was retained because both groups targeted its center and because it remains a familiar descriptive benchmark. However, static cup orientation does not capture contemporary concepts of functional stability. Spinopelvic mobility, patient-specific functional cup orientation, femoral version, soft-tissue tension, and implant factors were not assessed in this cohort [4]. Accordingly, the Lewinnek-zone analysis should be interpreted only as a descriptive radiographic benchmark and not as evidence of improved stability or reduced dislocation risk.

Robotic assistance was associated with smaller acetabular and femoral offset differences in exploratory analyses, whereas leg-length discrepancy and hip-center height restoration were similar. Because these outcomes were secondary and were not multiplicity-adjusted, they should be considered hypothesis-generating. Robotic procedures required approximately 15 additional minutes after adjustment. This time cost likely reflects tracking-array placement, registration, verification, and probe-based assessment and should be weighed against the relatively small improvement in inclination error.

Technical variation within DAA THA also affects the context in which these findings should be interpreted. DAA can be performed with different patient positions and operating-table strategies. A recent network meta-analysis comparing traction-table and standard-table DAA found differences in selected perioperative outcomes, while most radiological, functional, and complication outcomes were similar [16]. More broadly, network meta-analysis comparing DAA with other THA approaches has shown short-term differences among approaches without consistent superiority across all clinically relevant outcomes [17]. These data reinforce that a statistically significant radiographic difference in a specific DAA setup should not be assumed to translate into overall clinical superiority.

This study has several strengths. All operations were performed by one experienced surgeon during a narrow period, both groups used the same approach and soft-tissue workflow, no intraoperative fluoroscopy was used, postoperative measurements were blinded and highly reliable, bilateral hips were addressed using clustered models, and the conclusions were stable in one-hip-per-patient sensitivity analyses. Nevertheless, the limitations are important. The retrospective, nonrandomized design is susceptible to residual confounding. Treatment selection was patient-driven and robotic assistance required an additional charge; therefore, socioeconomic status, health literacy, treatment expectations, or other unmeasured characteristics may have influenced group allocation and cannot be fully addressed by adjustment for age, sex, BMI, height, and operative side. The study was conducted at a single center by one surgeon using a specific lateral-decubitus DAA workflow, which limits generalizability. Thirty-seven of 148 screened hips were excluded because postoperative imaging was unavailable or unsuitable, creating an additional risk of selection bias. Most importantly, the study did not include a robotic comparator using a conventional registration strategy, so the effect of acetabular-only registration cannot be separated from the effect of robotic assistance as a whole. The analysis evaluated early radiographic outcomes rather than patient-reported outcomes, complications, dislocation, revision, or implant survival; therefore, it cannot establish long-term clinical benefit. Postoperative RI was obtained from a standardized anteroposterior radiograph and RA incorporated a CT-based transformation, and these measurements are not identical to the intraoperative robotic coordinate system. Finally, the study could not isolate the contribution of registration itself from subsequent pelvic motion, reaming, impaction, probe acquisition, and postoperative measurement. Prospective studies directly comparing registration strategies and incorporating functional spinopelvic assessment, patient-reported outcomes, complications, and longer-term follow-up are required.

Conclusions

CT-based robotic-assisted DAA THA performed with an acetabular-only registration workflow was associated with a modest reduction in postoperative radiographic inclination error and combined angular error compared with manual cup placement, but not with a statistically significant reduction in anteversion error or a higher adjusted Lewinnek-zone rate. Because the study did not include a robotic comparator using a conventional registration strategy, the observed differences cannot be attributed specifically to acetabular-only registration. The clinical relevance of the approximately 1.4-degree reduction in inclination error remains uncertain in the absence of patient-reported, complication, and long-term outcomes. Near-zero mean bias between intraoperative and postoperative angles coexisted with wide individual limits of agreement and low-to-moderate ICCs, indicating that robot-reported values should not be treated as interchangeable with postoperative measurements for individual hips. These findings characterize the early radiographic performance of this specific robotic workflow rather than establish superiority in overall component positioning or clinical outcome.
