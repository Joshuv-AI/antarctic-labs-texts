Fluctuations of the Boundary-Driven Symmetric Zero-Range Process from the NESS
Patrícia Gonçalves 1 , Adriana Neumann 2 , Maria Chiara Ricciuti 3

Abstract
We study the non-equilibrium stationary fluctuations of a symmetric zero-range process on the discrete interval { 1 , … , N − 1 } \{1,\ldots,N-1\} coupled to reservoirs at sites 1 1 and N − 1 N-1 , which inject and remove particles at rates proportional to N − θ N^{-\theta} for any value of θ ∈ ℝ \theta\in\mathbb{R} . We prove that, if the jump rate is bounded and under diffusive scaling, the fluctuations converge to the solution of a generalised Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation with characteristic operators that depend on the stationary density profile. The limiting equation is supplemented with boundary conditions of Dirichlet, Robin, or Neumann type, depending on the strength of the reservoirs. We also introduce two notions of solutions to the corresponding martingale problems, which differ according to the choice of test functions.

1  Introduction

Understanding the macroscopic evolution of conserved quantities in interacting particle systems from their microscopic dynamics is a cornerstone of nonequilibrium statistical mechanics. In recent decades, substantial progress has been made in establishing hydrodynamic limits, that is, laws of large numbers describing the (typically deterministic) evolution of macroscopic quantities such as particle densities at diffusive or superdiffusive scales. Once the macroscopic behaviour has been characterised, a natural next question concerns the behaviour of the random fluctuations around this deterministic limit. The study of these fluctuations provides insight into the emergence of macroscopic stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) and, more broadly, into universal features of the microscopic models.

Among the classical systems in this area, the
zero-range process
plays a distinguished role. In this system, each lattice site
x
may host an arbitrary number
\eta(x)
of indistinguishable particles, and a particle at site
x
jumps to a site
y
with probability
p(x,y)
scaled by a factor
g(\eta(x))
(with the function
g
satisfying mild assumptions to ensure well-defined dynamics). The model was introduced by Spitzer in 1970
[
Spi70
]
and has since served as a canonical example of a conservative interacting particle system with unbounded state-space, and has been extensively studied both on the discrete
d
-dimensional torus and on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Another widely-studied model is the
exclusion process.
In this system, particles are constrained by the exclusion rule, meaning that each site may contain at most a finite number of particles. The dynamics are defined by allowing a particle at site
x
to attempt a jump to a site
y
at rate
p(x,y)
, with the jump being suppressed if
y
has already attained its maximum value. Despite its simple microscopic definition, this process displays a remarkably rich macroscopic behaviour, and it has served as a fundamental testing ground for the development of techniques in hydrodynamic limits, fluctuation theory and large deviations. Owing to its simple structure and its well-behaved analytical properties, the exclusion process has been extensively analysed and remains one of the most thoroughly understood interacting particle systems.

When such a microscopic system evolves on a finite interval
I_{N}:=\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
and is coupled to stochastic reservoirs at the boundary, particles are injected and removed at random, breaking the conservation of mass and inducing a
nonequilibrium steady state (NESS)
. For both the exclusion and the zero-range process evolving on the torus or
\mathbb{Z}
, stationary measures are translation invariant and of product form. However, when coupled with reservoirs, the NESS of the exclusion process becomes substantially more intricate. In general, the invariant measure is no longer of product form and exhibits non-trivial spatial correlations, even in one dimension. These long-range correlations, which persist uniformly in the system size, reflect the presence of a particle current induced by the boundary reservoirs, and except for some special cases, the explicit characterisation of the NESS remains unknown; see, for example,
[
DEHP93
,
FC23
]
. On the other hand, remarkably, for the boundary-driven zero-range process, the NESS is still of product form, but its parameters vary spatially, encoding the macroscopic density gradient generated by the boundary dynamics; see
[
LMS05
]
.

As previously mentioned, the hydrodynamic limit of such boundary-driven systems describes the deterministic evolution of the macroscopic density profile. This problem has been extensively studied for the exclusion process (see, for example,
[
BMNS17
,
BGJ19
,
Gon19
,
BGJ21
,
GS22
,
BCGS23
,
GJMN20
]
). In this context, the interplay between bulk dynamics and boundary reservoirs leads to a rich collection of limiting behaviours, with the parameter
\theta
governing the effective strength of the boundary interaction in the macroscopic limit. Depending on the regime, the hydrodynamic equation exhibits different boundary conditions: ranging from strong reservoirs imposing Dirichlet boundary conditions, to critical regimes yielding Robin-type conditions, and to weak reservoirs resulting in Neumann boundary conditions. These results reveal how microscopic injection and removal mechanisms translate into macroscopic boundary laws, and they play a crucial role in the analysis of stationary profiles, nonequilibrium fluctuations, and large-deviation principles for the associated empirical measures.

For the zero-range process, the hydrodynamic limit has long been well-known both on the discrete torus and on
\mathbb{Z}
, and in particular, under diffusive scaling, the empirical density evolves according to the nonlinear diffusion equation
\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(u)=\Delta\Phi(\rho_{t}(u))
, where the flux function
\Phi
is determined by the expectation of the jump rate
g
under the invariant measure. In the boundary-driven case, instead, the hydrodynamic behaviour was partially analysed in
[
FMN21
]
and then only recently rigorously established in
[
AGNR25
]
for the symmetric, nearest-neighbour case (that is, for
p(x,y)=\boldsymbol{1}_{\{x\pm 1\}}(y)
) in the regimes
\theta\geq 1
and under some mild assumptions on the jump rate
g
. Therein, it was shown that the empirical density evolves according to the same nonlinear diffusion equation (but where the flux function
\Phi
is now given by the expectation of
g
under the NESS) supplemented with boundary conditions of Robin type for
\theta=1
and Neumann type for
\theta>1
. The result for
\theta<1
remains an open problem, but it is conjectured to be described by the same equation with boundary conditions of Dirichlet type.

In the present article, we answer a question left open in
[
AGNR25
]
and analyse the fluctuations around the stationary state of this system. Specifically, we study the sequence of processes describing the spacetime fluctuations of the empirical density field under the stationary measure in the diffusive scaling. Our main result shows that, when the jump rate
g
is bounded from above and from below by a positive constant, these fluctuations converge, as
N\to\infty
, to the solution of the
generalised Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation

\partial_{t}\mathscr{Y}_{t}=\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\Delta\mathscr{Y}_{t}+\nabla\mathscr{\dot{W}}_{t}
, where
\bar{\rho}_{\theta}
is the stationary density profile and
\mathscr{\dot{W}}_{t}
denotes a space-time white noise. This limiting equation is supplemented with boundary conditions whose nature – Dirichlet, Robin, or Neumann – depends on the strength of the boundary reservoirs, namely on the value of
\theta
. Our result covers all values
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, and thus provides a complete description – in the case of
g
bounded – of the stationary fluctuations of the boundary-driven zero-range process in all regimes of boundary intensity.

The study of fluctuations for boundary-driven systems has a long history. For the symmetric exclusion process, Gaussian fluctuations around the stationary state were established in
[
FGN17
,
FGN19
]
, yielding Ornstein-Uhlenbeck limits with boundary conditions depending on the reservoir strength. Beyond the stationary regime, the time-dependent nonequilibrium fluctuations have also been analysed (see
[
GJMN20
]
), revealing spacetime covariance structures different from their equilibrium counterparts. These works highlight the sensitivity of fluctuation behaviour to the scaling of the boundary dynamics and the resulting macroscopic boundary conditions. In contrast, for the zero-range process, much less is known. On the torus or infinite lattice, the equilibrium fluctuations have long been well understood (see
[
KL99
]
), but results in the open-boundary setting were lacking. Our work fills this gap by providing the first rigorous characterisation of equilibrium fluctuations in the boundary-driven setting, including the identification of the limiting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and its boundary conditions.

Structure of the Proof.

Our strategy combines elements from the equilibrium fluctuation theory of
[
KL99
]
with adaptations to the non-translation-invariant, boundary-driven setting. The starting point is the construction of an appropriate martingale decomposition for the fluctuation field, obtained via Dynkin’s formula applied to chosen test functions. A key point is the identification of this space of test functions, which needs to be chosen in such a way that the boundary terms of the equation do not diverge and all terms of the martingale equation are well defined, yet rich enough to yield uniqueness of the martingale problem. In the case
\theta\geq 0
, our chosen space of test functions is indeed large enough, and we establish uniqueness by making use of results about regular Sturm-Liouville problems; see Appendix
A
. For
\theta<0
, instead, we need to impose that derivatives of all orders of these test functions vanish at the boundary. While this space is too small to guarantee uniqueness, similar to what was done in
[
BGJS22
]
, we prove two additional conditions on the fluctuation field which then guarantee uniqueness; see Proposition
2.16
. This means that we obtain two martingale problems in the case of Dirichlet boundary conditions.

We are also able to provide a new martingale problem for the Robin and Neumann cases, for a large space of test functions that do not satisfy any boundary condition. This new martingale problem contains an additional term with respect to the aforementioned one, reflecting the fact that no boundary conditions are imposed on the test functions. This manifests itself at the martingale level as an extra term appearing in the form of an additive functional, which depends on the function
H
through its value at the boundary. Since the space of test functions in this case is larger than the previous one, which already guarantees uniqueness, we immediately conclude uniqueness in law for the solution of this new martingale problem. We also observe that this new finding is not restricted to the zero-range process, but also appears in other models, such as the exclusion process, although a proof of this fact has never been established. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that a new formulation of the martingale problem of the Robin-Neumann regimes is provided, which may be useful for both linear and nonlinear particle systems.

In all the regimes, one of the main difficulties of the proof consists in establishing a
Boltzmann-Gibbs principle
, namely Theorem
3.1
, which allows us to close the martingale equation in terms of the density fluctuation field. Our method follows the approach of the original result of
[
BR84
]
(see also
[
KL99
]
), but requires substantial adaptations due to the lack of reversibility and translation invariance of the stationary measure. Indeed, due to the non-reversibility, when localising the generator to finite boxes, these are not mean-zero: to get around this, we introduce an appropriate correction of the generator which makes it locally centred with respect to the stationary measure, and then bound this correction via the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. The non-translation invariance, on the other hand, means that we cannot automatically reduce the proof to a single finite-size box. To this end, we define an appropriate weighted translation operator and then apply a trick inspired by the Boltzmann-Gibbs principle of
[
Lab18
]
, which takes advantage of the fact that the stationary measure is “smooth in space”. This result (and its local version at the boundary for
\theta<0
) is the only instance where we need the boundedness assumption for the jump rate
g
; everything else holds in full generality, as long as
g
satisfies standard assumptions to guarantee well-defined dynamics.

Another difficulty in the proof is the derivation of the boundary conditions in the martingale problems. We are able to replace the boundary contributions by macroscopic averages and, from these, deduce the corresponding macroscopic terms in the martingale problem. As a consequence, we obtain two different martingale problems in each regime of
\theta
, both of which admit unique (in law) solutions.

Future Work.
Several natural extensions of the present work remain open. First, it would be of interest to relax the boundedness assumption on the jump rate
g
, which is only used in the proof of the Boltzmann-Gibbs principle, and to develop techniques allowing the argument to be carried out under more general growth conditions.

Another interesting question is whether our results, in the specific case
g(k)=\boldsymbol{1}_{[1,\infty)}(k)
, can be transferred to the exclusion process, for instance by means of coupling arguments.

Finally, an important direction for future research is the study of the weakly asymmetric version of the model and the derivation of the crossover to the stochastic Burgers equation, as in
[
GJ14
]
. Moreover, since our analysis is already performed at the level of the NESS, it would be of interest to extend these results to the non-equilibrium scenario, following the approach of
[
JM18
]
.

2  Framework and Results

2.1  The Model

Given a positive integer
N
, let
I_{N}:=\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
. We consider a system of indistinguishable particles moving on the set
I_{N}
, which we call
bulk
, and we will denote by
\eta(x)
the occupation variable at
x\in I_{N}
. The system we consider is known as the
zero-range process
and it imposes no restriction on the total number of particles allowed per site, so that our state space is the set
\Omega_{N}:=\mathbb{N}^{I_{N}}
. The rates of the process are defined via a function
g:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
.

Assumptions on the Jump Rate
\boldsymbol{g}
. Throughout this work, we assume that
g
satisfies
g(0)=0
(which reflects the fact that, if there are no particles at a site, then the jump rate from that site is null), and that it is strictly positive on the set of positive integers. Moreover, we assume that it has bounded variation, in the sense that

g^{*}:=\mysup_{k\geq 1}|g(k+1)-g(k)|<\infty.

(2.1)

The condition above ensures that the process is well defined; see
[
Lig05
]
for details. In addition, the proof of our main result will require the following boundedness condition for the jump rate
g
:

0<m_{g}:=\myinf_{k\geq 1}g(k)\leq\mysup_{k\geq 1}g(k)=:M_{g}<\infty.

(2.2)

In fact, this condition will be used exclusively in Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
, and we will indicate precisely where it is required in their proofs; no other result relies on this hypothesis.

We consider the Markov process whose infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}^{N}
is given by

\mathcal{L}^{N}:=\mathcal{L}^{N}_{l}+\mathcal{L}^{N}_{0}+\mathcal{L}^{N}_{r},

with
\mathcal{L}^{N}_{l},\mathcal{L}^{N}_{0}
and
\mathcal{L}^{N}_{r}
acting on functions

via

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{N}_{l}f(\eta):=\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\left[f(\eta^{1+})-f(\eta)\right]+\frac{\lambda g(\eta(1))}{N^{\theta}}\left[f(\eta^{1-})-f(\eta)\right],

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{N}_{0}f(\eta):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in I_{N}\\
|x-y|=1\end{subarray}}g(\eta(x))\left[f(\eta^{x,y})-f(\eta)\right],

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{N}_{r}f(\eta):=\frac{\beta}{N^{\theta}}\left[f(\eta^{(N-1)+})-f(\eta)\right]+\frac{\delta g(\eta(N-1))}{N^{\theta}}\left[f(\eta^{(N-1)-})-f(\eta)\right].

To ease the notation, we only index these generators in
N
; however, note that the boundary generators
\mathcal{L}^{N}_{l}
and
\mathcal{L}^{N}_{r}
also depend on the parameters

Here,
\eta^{x,y}
denotes the configuration obtained from

\eta^{x,y}(z):=\begin{cases}\eta(z)-1&\mbox{if }z=x,\\

and
\eta^{x\pm}
denotes the one obtained after a particle is added or removed at site

\eta^{x\pm}(z):=\begin{cases}\eta(z)\pm 1&\mbox{if }z=x,\\
\eta(z)&\mbox{if }z\neq x.\end{cases}

The dynamics can be informally described as follows. In the bulk, particles jump from a site

\eta(x)

2.2  The Invariant Measure

A remarkable property of the zero-range process is that its invariant measure can be explicitly computed even when in contact with stochastic reservoirs, and it is of product form. In particular, let

\bar{\nu}_{N}\{\eta(x)=k\}:=\frac{1}{Z(\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x))}\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)^{k}}{g(k)!},

(2.3)

where
g(k)!:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}g(j)
,
\bar{\varphi}_{N}:I_{N}\to[0,\infty)
is a map to be determined, and
Z
is the partition function

Z(\varphi):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi^{j}}{g(j)!}.

(2.4)

Let
\varphi^{*}
denote the radius of convergence of
Z
. The following result characterises the stationary measure of
\{\eta_{t}^{N},t\geq 0\}
and can be found in
[
LMS05
, Equation 20]
(or
[
AGNR25
, Lemma 2.1]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Define the map
\bar{\varphi}_{N}:I_{N}\to[0,\infty)
as

\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x):=\frac{-(\alpha\delta-\beta\lambda)(x-1)+\alpha\delta(N-2)+(\alpha+\beta)N^{\theta}}{\lambda\delta(N-2)+(\lambda+\delta)N^{\theta}}.

(2.5)

For
\alpha,\beta,\lambda,\delta\geq 0
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying

\mymax\left\{\frac{\alpha\delta(N-2)+(\alpha+\beta)N^{\theta}}{\lambda\delta(N-2)+(\lambda+\delta)N^{\theta}},\frac{\beta\lambda(N-2)+(\alpha+\beta)N^{\theta}}{\lambda\delta(N-2)+(\lambda+\delta)N^{\theta}}\right\}<\varphi^{*},

(2.6)

the measure (
2.3
) with fugacity profile
\bar{\varphi}_{N}
is the unique invariant measure of
\{\eta_{t}^{N},t\geq 0\}
.

The condition (
2.6
) comes from the fact that, in order for the measure (
2.3
) to make sense, we need to have
\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)<\varphi^{*}
uniformly in
x\in I_{N}
and
N\geq 1
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that, if
\alpha,\beta,\lambda,\delta>0
, the asymptotic fugacity profile
\bar{\varphi}_{\theta}:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
is given by:

1.

{\theta<1}
:
\bar{\varphi}_{\theta}(u)=\frac{-(\alpha\delta-\beta\lambda)u+\alpha\delta}{\lambda\delta}
;

2.

\theta=1
:
\bar{\varphi}_{\theta}(u)=\frac{-(\alpha\delta-\beta\lambda)u+\alpha\delta+\alpha+\beta}{\lambda\delta+\lambda+\delta}
;

3.

\theta>1
:
\bar{\varphi}_{\theta}(u)=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\lambda+\delta}
;

see Figure
2
. In fact, it is easy to see that not all parameters
\alpha,\beta,\lambda,\delta
need to be strictly positive in order for the discrete and asymptotic fugacity profile to be well-defined; however, for simplicity, throughout we will assume
\alpha,\beta,\lambda,\delta>0
.

Let now
R:[0,\varphi^{*})\to[0,\infty)
be the function defined as the power series

R(\varphi):=\frac{1}{Z(\varphi)}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{j\varphi^{j}}{g(j)!},

(2.7)

where
\varphi^{*}
is again the radius of convergence of the partition function
Z
. Then, denoting by
E_{\nu}[\,\cdot\,]
the expectation with respect to a measure
\nu
on
\Omega_{N}
, we can rewrite the expected value of the occupation variable at site

under
\bar{\nu}_{N}
as

E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}[\eta(x)]=R(\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)),\qquad x\in I_{N}.

Remark 2.3
.

Under the assumptions of Remark
2.2
, the limit density profile
\bar{\rho}_{\theta}:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
is given
\bar{\rho}_{\theta}(u)=R(\bar{\varphi}_{\theta}(u))
. In particular, a linear fugacity profile does
not
, in general, imply a linear density profile.

The following result can be found in
[
AGNR25
, Lemma 3.6]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\bar{\nu}_{N}
denote the invariant measure from Lemma
2.1
. For each positive integer
\ell
, we have that

\mysup_{N}\mysup_{x\in I_{N}}E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}[(\eta(x))^{\ell}]<\infty.

Throughout, we will assume that
\mylim_{\varphi\uparrow\varphi*}Z(\varphi)=\infty
. Then, as highlighted in
[
KL99
, Section 2.3]
, the range of
R
is
[0,\infty)
, and
R
is strictly increasing; then the map

\Phi:=R^{-1}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\varphi^{*})

(2.8)

is well defined, and a strictly increasing function. Calling
\rho_{N}(x):=E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}[\eta(x)]
for

, a simple computation shows that

E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}[g(\eta(x))]=\Phi(\rho_{N}(x))=\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x).

(2.9)

In fact, by
[
AGNR25
, Lemma 3.3]
, the following holds.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
\ell=1,2
, we have that

\mysup_{N}\mysup_{x\in I_{N}}E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}[g(\eta(x))^{\ell}]<\infty.

(2.10)

Remark 2.6
.

Although we will only require (

) for

, define

2.3  Hydrodynamic Limit

The hydrodynamic limit of this model for
\theta\geq 1
was studied in
[
AGNR25
]
under the following assumptions on the jump rate
g
.

Assumption 1
:
g
is non decreasing;

Assumption 2
: denoting by
\text{gap}(j,\ell)
the spectral gap of the generator of a symmetric zero-range process on
\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
with
j
particles and jump rate
g
, there exists
C=C(g)>0
such that
\text{gap}(\ell,j)\geq\frac{C}{\ell^{2}(1+\frac{j}{\ell})^{2}}
for all
\ell\geq 2
and
j\geq 0
.

We note that the hydrodynamic limit in
[
AGNR25
]
requires additional assumptions on
g
. In this work, we do not assume any of the above conditions, but instead impose the purely technical assumption (
2.2
), whose removal is left for future work.

Let
C^{m,n}([0,T]\times[0,1])
denote the set of continuous functions on
[0,T]\times[0,1]
which are
m
times differentiable in the first variable and
n
in the second, with continuous derivatives, where
m
and
n
are positive integers. Moreover, let
\mathcal{H}^{1}
denote the Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{1}(0,1)
, equipped with the norm

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}^{2}:=\|\cdot\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,1))}+\|\nabla\cdot\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,1))},

and by
L^{2}([0,T],\mathcal{H}^{1})
the set of measurable functions
f:[0,T]\to\mathcal{H}^{1}
with
\int_{0}^{T}\|f_{t}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}^{2}\de t<\infty
. Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
be a measurable and integrable function, and consider the non-linear PDE with boundary conditions

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(u)=\Delta\Phi(\rho_{t}(u))&\mbox{ for }u\in(0,1)\mbox{ and }t\in(0,T],\\
\partial_{u}\Phi(\rho_{t}(0))=\boldsymbol{1}_{\{1\}}(\theta)(\lambda\Phi(\rho_{t}(0))-\alpha)&\mbox{ for }t\in(0,T],\\
\partial_{u}\Phi(\rho_{t}(1))=\boldsymbol{1}_{\{1\}}(\theta)(\beta-\delta\Phi(\rho_{t}(1)))&\mbox{ for }t\in(0,T],\\
\rho_{0}(u)=\gamma(u)&\mbox{ for }u\in[0,1],\end{array}\right.

(2.12)

where the function
\Phi
was defined in (
2.8
).

Definition 2.7

\displaystyle x\in I_{N}.

\displaystyle\phantom{N^{2}\mathcal{L}_{0}^{N}\mathscr{Y}^{N}(H)=}+\frac{N}{\sqrt{N}}g(\eta(1))\nabla_{N}^{+}H\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)-\frac{N}{\sqrt{N}}g(\eta(N-1))\nabla_{N}^{-}H\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right),

(3.3)

3.2  The Boltzmann-Gibbs Principle

For a positive integer
k
, let
\tau_{k}
denote the shift by
k
modulo
N
, namely
\tau_{k}\eta(x):=\eta([\![x+k]\!])
, where
[\![\,\cdot\,]\!]
denotes the congruence class in
\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
modulo
N
. We define the map
V_{N}:\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
via

V_{N}(\eta):=g(\eta(1))-\Phi(\rho_{N}(1))-\Phi^{\prime}(\rho_{N}(1))\left[\eta(1)-\rho_{N}(1)\right],

as well as its
k
-shifts

\tau_{k}V_{N}(\eta):=g(\eta(k+1))-\Phi(\rho_{N}(k+1))-\Phi^{\prime}(\rho_{N}(k+1))\left[\eta(k+1)-\rho_{N}(k+1)\right]

(3.13)

for positive integers
k
.

Theorem 3.1

(Boltzmann-Gibbs Principle)
.

If (
2.2
) holds, then for every continuous function
G:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
and every
t>0
,

\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=2}^{N-2}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\tau_{x}V_{N}(\eta_{s})\de s\right)^{2}\right]=0.

(3.14)

This theorem is classical in interacting particle systems; however, our setting presents some peculiarities, as we lack translation invariance and reversibility of the invariant measure
\bar{\nu}_{N}
.

The next result is a local version of the one above and will be needed to show that, for
\theta<0
, the field satisfies item ii) of Proposition
2.16
. Note that, unlike in Theorem
3.1
, the test function is no longer continuous; however, the proof of the theorem above can be readily adapted to cover this case.

Theorem 3.2

(Local Boltzmann-Gibbs Principle)
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, define the map
\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0}:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
as in Proposition
2.16
, namely
\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0}(u):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,\varepsilon]}(u)
. If (
2.2
) holds, then for every
t>0
,

\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{N}\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0}\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\tau_{x}V_{N}(\eta_{s})\de s\right)^{2}\right]=0.

The same result holds with
\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0}
replaced by
\iota_{\varepsilon}^{1}
, where
\iota_{\varepsilon}^{1}(u):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{1}_{[1-\varepsilon,1)}(u)
.

3.2.1
\boldsymbol{L^{2}}
Bounds and Dirichlet Forms

Lemma 3.3
.

Let (
2.2
) hold. If
f\in L^{2}(\bar{\nu}_{N})
, then for each
x,y\in I_{N}
we have that the map
\eta\mapsto f(\eta^{x,y})
is also in
L^{2}(\bar{\nu}_{N})
.

Proof.

We start by noting that, for all
x,y\in I_{N}
,

\frac{\bar{\nu}_{N}(\eta^{y,x})}{\bar{\nu}_{N}(\eta)}=\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)}{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y)}\frac{g(\eta(y))}{g(\eta(x)+1)}.

(3.15)

Hence, we can write

\displaystyle E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[f(\eta^{x,y})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{N}}f(\eta)^{2}\frac{\de\bar{\nu}_{N}(\eta^{y,x})}{\de\bar{\nu}_{N}(\eta)}\de\bar{\nu}_{N}(\eta)=\int_{\Omega_{N}}f(\eta)^{2}\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)}{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y)}\frac{g(\eta(y))}{g(\eta(x)+1)}\de\bar{\nu}_{N}(\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M_{g}\left\{\mysup_{N}\mysup_{z\in I_{N}}\bar{\varphi}_{N}(z)\right\}}{m_{g}\left\{\myinf_{N}\myinf_{z\in I_{N}}\bar{\varphi}_{N}(z)\right\}}E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[f(\eta)^{2}\right],

which completes the proof.
∎

Now, given a function
f\in L^{2}(\bar{\nu}_{N})
, we define its
Dirichlet form
with respect to the invariant measure
\bar{\nu}_{N}
by

\mathscr{D}_{N}(f):=-\langle f,\mathcal{L}^{N}f\rangle_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}.

(3.16)

Moreover, define

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{N}^{0}(f):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in I_{N}\\
|x-y|=1\end{subarray}}g(\eta(x))\left[f(\eta^{x,y})-f(\eta)\right]^{2}\de\bar{\nu}_{N}

and, for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{N}^{l}(f):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\left\{\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\left[f(\eta^{1+})-f(\eta)\right]^{2}+\frac{\lambda g(\eta(1))}{N^{\theta}}\left[f(\eta^{1-})-f(\eta)\right]^{2}\right\}\de\bar{\nu}_{N},

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{N}^{r}(f):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\bigg\{\frac{\beta}{N^{\theta}}\left[f(\eta^{(N-1)+})-f(\eta)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{\mathscr{D}_{N}^{r}(f):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\bigg\{}+\frac{\delta g(\eta(N-1))}{N^{\theta}}\left[f(\eta^{(N-1)-})-f(\eta)\right]^{2}\bigg\}\de\bar{\nu}_{N}.

Lemma 3.4
.

For each
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and any function
f\in L^{2}(\bar{\nu}_{N})
, we have that

\mathscr{D}_{N}(f)=\mathscr{D}_{N}^{l}(f)+\mathscr{D}_{N}^{0}(f)+\mathscr{D}_{N}^{r}(f).

Proof.

We start by writing

\mathscr{D}_{N}(f)=-\int_{\Omega_{N}}f(\eta)\left\{\mathcal{L}^{N}_{l}f(\eta)+\mathcal{L}^{N}_{0}f(\eta)+\mathcal{L}^{N}_{r}f(\eta)\right\}\de\bar{\nu}_{N}.

(3.17)

Now, note that

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega_{N}}f(\eta)\mathcal{L}^{N}_{l}f(\eta)\de\bar{\nu}_{N}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{N}}f(\eta)\left\{\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\left[f(\eta^{1+})-f(\eta)\right]+\frac{\lambda g(\eta(1))}{N^{\theta}}\left[f(\eta^{1-})-f(\eta)\right]\right\}\de\bar{\nu}_{N}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{N}}\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\frac{\left[f(\eta)-f(\eta^{1+})\right]}{2}\left[f(\eta^{1+})-f(\eta)\right]\de\bar{\nu}_{N}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\int_{\Omega_{N}}\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\frac{\left[f(\eta)+f(\eta^{1+})\right]}{2}\left[f(\eta^{1+})-f(\eta)\right]\de\bar{\nu}_{N}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\int_{\Omega_{N}}\frac{\lambda g(\eta(1))}{N^{\theta}}\frac{\left[f(\eta)-f(\eta^{1-})\right]}{2}\left[f(\eta^{1-})-f(\eta)\right]\de\bar{\nu}_{N}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\int_{\Omega_{N}}\frac{\lambda g(\eta^{1})}{N^{\theta}}\frac{\left[f(\eta)+f(\eta^{1-})\right]}{2}\left[f(\eta^{1-})-f(\eta)\right]\de\bar{\nu}_{N}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{D}_{N}^{l}(f)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\mathcal{L}^{N}_{l}f^{2}(\eta)\de\bar{\nu}_{N}.

Hence, by performing a similar computation for the terms with
\mathcal{L}^{N}_{0}
and
\mathcal{L}^{N}_{r}
in (
3.17
),
we get that

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{N}(f)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{D}_{N}^{l}(f)+\mathscr{D}_{N}^{0}(f)+\mathscr{D}_{N}^{r}(f)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\left\{\mathcal{L}^{N}_{l}+\mathcal{L}^{N}_{0}+\mathcal{L}^{N}_{r}\right\}f^{2}(\eta)\de{\bar{\nu}_{N}}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{D}_{N}^{l}(f)+\mathscr{D}_{N}^{0}(f)+\mathscr{D}_{N}^{r}(f)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\mathcal{L}^{N}f^{2}(\eta)\de{\bar{\nu}_{N}}.

But now, since

is invariant for the dynamics, we have that
\int_{\Omega_{N}}\mathcal{L}^{N}h(\eta)\de{\bar{\nu}_{N}}=0
for every

∎

3.4  Tightness

In the section, we show the following result.

]
)

Proposition 3.8

(Aldous’s Criterion
[

]
)
.

A sequence of real-valued processes
\{X_{t}^{N},

, by performing a Taylor expansion, for each positive integer
m
we see that

\eqref{eq:qv_boundaries}\lesssim\frac{tN^{1-\theta}}{N^{2m}}\hskip-1.99997pt\left\{(\partial_{u}^{m}H(x_{0}))^{2}\big(\alpha+\lambda\bar{\varphi}_{N}(1)\big)+(\partial_{u}^{m}H(x_{1}))^{2}\big(\beta+\delta\bar{\varphi}_{N}(N-1)\big)\right\}

for some
x_{0}\in[0,\frac{1}{N}]
and
x_{1}\in[\frac{N-1}{N},1]
. Choosing
m>\frac{1-\theta}{2}
, we see that the last display vanishes as
N\to\infty
;

2.

0\leq\theta<1
: since
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
, we have that

\eqref{eq:qv_boundaries}\lesssim\frac{tN^{1-\theta}}{N^{2}}\left\{(\partial_{u}H(0))^{2}\big(\alpha+\lambda\bar{\varphi}_{N}(1)\big)+(\partial_{u}H(1))^{2}\big(\beta+\delta\bar{\varphi}_{N}(N-1)\big)\right\},

which vanishes as
N\to\infty
;

3.

\theta=1
: recalling that
H(0)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\partial_{u}H(0)
and

, we see that

\displaystyle\mylim_{N\to\infty}\eqref{eq:qv_boundaries}

\displaystyle=t\left\{\big(\alpha+\lambda\Phi(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}(0))\big)H(0)^{2}+\big(\beta+\delta\Phi(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}(1))\big)H(1)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=t\left\{\frac{\alpha}{\lambda^{2}}+\frac{\Phi(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}(0))}{\lambda}\right\}(\partial_{u}H(0))^{2}+t\left\{\frac{\beta}{\delta^{2}}+\frac{\Phi(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}(1))}{\delta}\right\}(\partial_{u}H(1))^{2};

4.

\theta\geq 1
: in this case,

\eqref{eq:qv_boundaries}\lesssim tN^{1-\theta}\left\{H(0)^{2}\big(\alpha+\lambda\bar{\varphi}_{N}(1)\big)+H(1)^{2}\big(\beta+\delta\bar{\varphi}_{N}(N-1)\big)\right\},

so it automatically vanishes as
N\to\infty
.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.15
.

For any
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
and any
t\in[0,T]
,

\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\left(\langle\mathscr{M}^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\langle\mathscr{M}^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}\right]\right)^{2}\right]=0.

Proof.

By (
3.58
), the stationarity of
\bar{\nu}_{N}
and a standard convexity inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\left(\langle\mathscr{M}^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\langle\mathscr{M}^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}\right]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\left(\frac{2}{N}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{N-2}\left\{g(\eta_{s}(x))-E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[g(\eta(x))\right]\right\}\nabla_{N}^{+}H\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}\de s\right)^{2}\right]

(3.62)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\left(\lambda N^{1-\theta}\int_{0}^{t}\left\{g(\eta_{s}(1))-E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[g(\eta(1))\right]\right\}H\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)^{2}\de s\right)^{2}\right]

(3.63)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\left(\delta N^{1-\theta}\int_{0}^{t}\left\{g(\eta_{s}(N-1))-E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[g(\eta(N-1))\right]\right\}H\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^{2}\de s\right)^{2}\right].

(3.64)

Now, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that
\bar{\nu}_{N}
is a product measure,

\eqref{eq:martingale_bulk}\lesssim\frac{t^{2}}{N^{2}}\sum_{x=1}^{N-2}\Var_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}(g(\eta(x))),

which vanishes as
N\to\infty
by Lemma
2.5
. As for (
3.63
), again by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

1.

\theta<0
: by performing a Taylor expansion of
H
around
0
, for each positive integer
m
we see that

\eqref{eq:martingale_left}\lesssim t^{2}N^{2-2\theta-4m}(\partial_{u}^{m}H(x_{0}))^{2},

for some
x_{0}\in[0,\frac{1}{N}]
. Choosing
m>\frac{1-\theta}{2}
, we see that the last display vanishes as
N\to\infty
;

2.

0\leq\theta<1
: since
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
and by Lemma
3.5
,

\eqref{eq:martingale_left}\lesssim t^{2}N^{-2\theta}(\partial_{u}H(0))^{2}N^{\theta-2},

which vanishes as
N\to\infty
;

3.

\theta\geq 1
: by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
3.5
,

\eqref{eq:martingale_left}\lesssim t^{2}N^{2-2\theta}H(0)^{2}N^{\theta-2},

which again vanishes as
N\to\infty
.

The argument for (
3.64
) is identical, hence the claim follows.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.11
.

By Lemmas
3.13
,
3.14
and

, for each

, the sequence of martingales

with

. Calling

3.5  Characterisation of the Limit Point

We finally conclude the proof of Theorem
2.19
by completing the characterisation of the limit point of the density fluctuation field.

What is mainly left to show is that, in the case
\theta<0
, any limit path
\mathscr{Y}
of the density fluctuation field satisfies items i) and ii) of Proposition
2.16
. We start by showing item i), which follows immediately.

Lemma 3.16
.

For each
\theta<0
, any limit point
\mathcal{Q}
of
\{\mathcal{Q}^{N}\}_{N}
is concentrated on paths
\mathscr{Y}\in\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\theta})
which satisfy item i) of Proposition
2.16
.

Proof.

Recall (
2.20
). It suffices to show that
\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}[\mathscr{Y}_{t}^{N}(H)^{2}]\lesssim\|H\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
for each
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
. But now,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{t}^{N}(H)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}H\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}[\bar{\eta}(x)^{2}]\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}H\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2},

where we used Lemma
2.4
.
∎

The proof of item ii) is more involved. Since the functions
\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0}
and
\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota_{\varepsilon}^{1}
do not belong to the space of test functions
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
, we first have to define the quantities involved in condition ii). To this end, following
[
BGJS22
, Section 5]
, let
\mathscr{H}
denote the Hilbert space of real-valued, progressively measurable processes
\{x_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
in
L^{2}([0,1])
, equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{H}}
defined via

\|x\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}|x_{t}|^{2}\de t\right].

(3.65)

Lemma 3.17
.

Assume that the stochastic process
\mathscr{Y}\in\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\theta})
satisfies condition i) of Proposition
2.16
. For any
H\in L^{2}([0,1])
, we can define in a unique way a stochastic process on
\mathscr{H}
, which we will still denote by
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H),t\in[0,T]\}
, such that it coincides with
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H),t\in[0,T]\}
for
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
. Moreover, condition i) also holds for any
H\in L^{2}([0,1])
.

Proof.

Let
H\in L^{2}([0,1])
and let
\{H_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
be a sequence of functions in
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
converging to
H
in
L^{2}([0,1])
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and condition i), the sequence of real-valued processes
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H_{\varepsilon}),t\in[0,T]\}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathscr{H}
, and thus it converges to a process (in
\mathscr{H}
) which we denote by
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H),t\in[0,T]\}
. It is easy to show that the limiting process depends only on
H
and not on the approximating sequence
\{H_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
, justifying the notation. Condition i) trivially holds for this process.
∎

Throughout, even if
\varepsilon N
is not an integer, we will write
\varepsilon N
for
\lfloor\varepsilon N\rfloor
. The result below follows the approach of
[
BGJS22
, Lemma 5.2]
. The proof of this result relies on the local Boltzmann-Gibbs principle (Theorem
3.2
), and hence we require the boundedness condition (
2.2
).

Lemma 3.18
.

Assume (
2.2
). For each
\theta<0
, any limit point
\mathcal{Q}
of
\{\mathcal{Q}^{N}\}_{N}
is concentrated on paths
\mathscr{Y}\in\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\theta})
which satisfy item ii) of Proposition
2.16
.

Proof.

We will only show the result for
\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0}
, as the proof for
\iota_{\varepsilon}^{1}
is completely analogous. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
\{H_{\varepsilon}^{m}\}_{m\geq 1}
be a sequence of functions in
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
such that
\mylim_{m\to\infty}\|H_{\varepsilon}^{m}-\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=0
. By condition i) of Proposition
2.16
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0})\de s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim T^{2}\left\|\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})(H_{\varepsilon}^{m}-\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0})\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\mathbb{E}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})H_{\varepsilon}^{m})\de s\right)^{2}\right].

Note now that, if we equip
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathcal{S}_{\theta}^{\prime})
with the uniform topology, for any
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
, the map

\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathcal{S}_{\theta}^{\prime})\ni\mathscr{Z}\mapsto\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Z}_{s}(H)\de s\right)^{2}\in[0,\infty)

is continuous, and in particular it is lower semi-continuous and bounded from below. Hence, by the convergence of
\{\mathscr{Y}^{N}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
to
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
in distribution, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\hskip-1.00006pt\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})H_{\varepsilon}^{m})\de s\right)^{2}\right]\hskip-1.99997pt\leq\myliminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\hskip-1.99997pt\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\hskip-1.00006pt\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})H_{\varepsilon}^{m})\de s\right)^{2}\right].

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the stationarity of
\{\mathscr{Y}^{N}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
with respect to
\bar{\nu}_{N}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})H_{\varepsilon}^{m})\de s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim T^{2}\left\|\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})(H_{\varepsilon}^{m}-\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0})\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{\lesssim}+\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0})\de s\right)^{2}\right].

Then, by the linearity of
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}(\cdot),t\in[0,T]\}
, a standard convexity inequality and Lemma
3.17
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0})\de s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim T^{2}\|\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\|_{\infty}^{2}\|H_{\varepsilon}^{m}-\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\,\myliminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0})\de s\right)^{2}\right].

Sending
m\to\infty
, we get that it suffices to show that

\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0})\de s\right)^{2}\right]=0.

To this end, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0})\de s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\Phi^{\prime}\left(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\right)\bar{\eta}_{s}^{N}(x)\de s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\left\{\Phi^{\prime}\left(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\right)-\Phi^{\prime}(\rho_{N}(x))\right\}\bar{\eta}_{s}^{N}(x)\de s\right)^{2}\right]

(3.66)

\displaystyle\phantom{\lesssim}+\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\left\{\Phi^{\prime}(\rho_{N}(x))\bar{\eta}_{s}^{N}(x)-\big(g(\eta_{s}(x))-\varphi_{N}(x)\big)\right\}\de s\right)^{2}\right]

(3.67)

\displaystyle\phantom{\lesssim}+\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\big(g(\eta_{s}(x))-\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)\big)\de s\right)^{2}\right].

Now, for each
x\in I_{N}
,
\left|\Phi^{\prime}\left(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\right)-\Phi^{\prime}(\rho_{N}(x))\right|
goes to zero as
N\to\infty
(by definition of
\bar{\rho}_{\theta}
), and hence (
3.66
) vanishes as
N\to\infty
. By the local Boltzmann-Gibbs principle (Theorem
3.2
), (
3.67
) also vanishes as
N\to\infty
. Thus, it only remains to show that

\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\big(g(\eta_{s}(x))-\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)\big)\de s\right)^{2}\right]=0.

This is achieved by Lemma
3.19
below.
∎

Lemma 3.19
.

For each
\theta<0
, we have that

\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\big(g(\eta_{s}(x))-\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)\big)\de s\right)^{2}\right]=0.

Proof.

By a standard convexity inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\big(g(\eta_{s}(x))-\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)\big)\de s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\big(g(\eta_{s}(x))-g(\eta_{s}(1))+\bar{\varphi}_{N}(1)-\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)\big)\de s\right)^{2}\right]

(3.68)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+2\,\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\big(g(\eta_{s}(1))-\bar{\varphi}_{N}(1)\big)\de s\right)^{2}\right].

(3.69)

By Lemma
3.5
, we immediately see that

\eqref{eq:rl_term}=2\,\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\sqrt{N}\int_{0}^{t}\big(g(\eta_{s}(1))-\bar{\varphi}_{N}(1)\big)\de s\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}N^{\theta-1},

which vanishes as
N\to\infty
for any
\theta<0
.

Let now
\bar{g}(\eta(x)):=g(\eta(x))-\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)
for each
x\in I_{N}
(so that
E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}[\bar{g}(\eta(x))]=0
). It is not hard to check that we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\big(g(\eta(x))-g(\eta(1))+\bar{\varphi}_{N}(1)-\bar{\varphi}_{N}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}\left\{g(\eta(y))-\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y)}{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y+1)}g(\eta(y+1))\right\}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}\bar{g}(\eta(y+1))\left\{\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y)}{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y+1)}-1\right\}.

Hence, by the same convexity inequality as above, it only remains to bound

\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}\left\{g(\eta_{s}(y))-\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y)}{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y+1)}g(\eta_{s}(y+1))\right\}\de s\right)^{2}\right]

(3.70)

and

\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}\bar{g}(\eta_{s}(y+1))\left\{\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y)}{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y+1)}-1\right\}\de s\right)^{2}\right].

(3.71)

Now, by the Kipnis-Varadhan inequality
[
KLO12
, Lemma 2.4]
, we have that

\begin{split}\eqref{eq:kv_term}\lesssim T\mysup_{f\in L^{2}(\bar{\nu}_{N})}\Bigg\{&\int_{\Omega_{N}}\frac{2}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}\left\{g(\eta(y))-\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y)}{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y+1)}g(\eta(y+1))\right\}f(\eta)\de\bar{\nu}_{N}\\
&-N^{2}\mathscr{D}_{N}(f)\Bigg\},\end{split}

(3.72)

with
\mathscr{D}_{N}(f)
defined in (
3.16
). We now write

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{N}}\frac{2}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}g(\eta(y))f(\eta)\de\bar{\nu}_{N}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{N}}\frac{2}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}g(\eta(y))\left[f(\eta)-f(\eta^{y,y+1})\right]\de\bar{\nu}_{N}

(3.73)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{\Omega_{N}}\frac{2}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}g(\eta(y))f(\eta^{y,y+1})\de\bar{\nu}_{N}.

(3.74)

By Young’s inequality, for each
A>0
we see that

(
3.73
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}\int_{\Omega_{N}}g(\eta(y))\left[f(\eta)-f(\eta^{y,y+1})\right]^{2}\de\bar{\nu}_{N}

(3.75)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\frac{1}{A\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}\int_{\Omega_{N}}g(\eta(y))\de\bar{\nu}_{N}.

(3.76)

Choosing
A=N^{3/2}
, we get
\eqref{eq:dir_term}\leq N^{2}\mathscr{D}_{N}(f)
. Moreover, since
E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}[g(\eta(y))]
is uniformly bounded in
N
and
y\in I_{N}
, this choice yields
\eqref{eq:vanishing_term}\lesssim\frac{(\varepsilon N)^{2}}{N^{3/2}\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}=\varepsilon
, which vanishes as
N\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0
.

As for (
3.74
), by performing the change of variable
\eta\mapsto\eta^{y+1,y}
and using the identity

\frac{\bar{\nu}_{N}(\eta^{y+1,y})}{\bar{\nu}_{N}(\eta)}=\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y)}{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y+1)}\frac{g(\eta(y+1))}{g(\eta(y)+1)}

(which follows immediately from (
2.3
)), we see that

\eqref{eq:cancellation_term}=\int_{\Omega_{N}}\frac{2}{\varepsilon\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}g(\eta(y+1))f(\eta)\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y)}{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y+1)}\de\bar{\nu}_{N}.

But then, this term cancels with the remaining contribution in the supremum in (
3.72
), so that this all yields
\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mylim_{N\to\infty}\eqref{eq:kv_term}=0
.

Finally, it remains to treat (
3.71
): by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, since
\bar{\nu}_{N}
is a product measure and by Lemma
2.5
, we have that

\eqref{eq:cs_term}\lesssim\frac{T^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}N}\sum_{x=1}^{\varepsilon N}\sum_{y=1}^{x-1}\sum_{z=1}^{\varepsilon N}\left\{\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y)}{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y+1)}-1\right\}^{2}E_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}[\bar{g}(\eta(y))^{2}]\lesssim\frac{T^{2}(\varepsilon N)^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}N\cdot N^{2}}=T^{2}\varepsilon,

where we used
\left|\frac{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y)}{\bar{\varphi}_{N}(y+1)}-1\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{N}
. Sending
\varepsilon\to 0
completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to conclude the proof of Theorem
2.19
.

.

By the arguments given in Section

, together with Lemmas

2.20

The proof of this proposition follows the same steps as the proof of Theorem
2.19
presented in the previous section. We therefore do not repeat it in full, but only highlight the terms that require additional arguments. These are precisely the terms
\mathscr{Z}_{t}^{\theta,i}(H)
appearing in (

Proof of Proposition
2.20
.

Recall (
3.7
). We present the proof only for the case
\theta=1
, since the case
\theta>1
is completely analogous and, in fact, simpler. Observe that the space of test functions is now
\widetilde{S}
, and therefore we cannot use boundary conditions to simplify the expression of the martingale. When
\theta=1
, the terms that require further analysis are those appearing in the second through fourth lines of (

). We focus on the left boundary terms, as the right boundary terms can be treated in the same way. We thus need to analyse

\sqrt{N}[g(\eta_{s}(1))-\bar{\varphi}_{N}(1)]\left\{\nabla_{N}^{+}H\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)-\lambda H\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\right\}.

We first claim that

\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\Big\{\sqrt{N}[g(\eta_{s}(1))-\bar{\varphi}_{N}(1)]-\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0})\Big\}\de s\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim\varepsilon(T^{2}+1).

To prove this claim, we apply a standard convexity inequality and bound the expectation above by the sum of the terms in (
3.66
), (
3.67
), and (
3.68
). Collecting these bounds yields the desired estimate. By the triangle inequality and for
\delta<\varepsilon

\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\nu}_{N}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\Big\{\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota_{\varepsilon}^{0})-\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota_{\delta}^{0})\Big\}\de s\right)^{2}\right]\lesssim\varepsilon(T^{2}+1).

Throughout, we will denote by

Lemma A.1

For each

Proof.

Recalling the definition of
\Phi

\Phi^{\prime}(\rho)=\frac{1}{R^{\prime}(\Phi(\rho))}

(A.1)

\varphi\in[0,\varphi^{*})

denote the probability distribution on

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(\varphi)

which, by (

A.1  Sturm-Liouville Problems

Let
A
be a smooth, positive function on
[0,1]
and consider the following
Sturm-Liouville (SL) problems
on
[0,1]
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}A(u)\psi^{\prime\prime}(u)+\gamma\psi(u)=0&\mbox{ for }u\in(0,1),\\
\psi(0)=\psi(1)=0,&\end{array}\right.

(A.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}A(u)\psi^{\prime\prime}(u)+\gamma\psi(u)=0&\mbox{ for }u\in(0,1),\\
\psi^{\prime}(0)=\lambda\psi(0),&\\
\psi^{\prime}(1)=-\delta\psi(1),&\end{array}\right.

(A.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}A(u)\psi^{\prime\prime}(u)+\gamma\psi(u)=0&\mbox{ for }u\in(0,1),\\
\psi^{\prime}(0)=\psi^{\prime}(1)=0&\end{array}\right.

(A.5)

(with
\lambda,\delta>0
), which we will refer to as
\textsc{SL}_{0}(A)
,
\textsc{SL}_{1}(A)
and
\textsc{SL}_{2}(A)
, respectively; see
[
BR89
, Chapter 10]
for a detailed exposition of the topic.

Lemma A.2
.

For each
j=0,1,2
, the system
\textsc{SL}_{j}(A)
satisfies the following:

1.

the spectrum is real and an ordered sequence
\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}<\ldots\to\infty
;

2.

the sequence of normalised eigenfunctions
\{\psi_{n}\}_{n}
forms a complete, orthonormal basis of the
A^{-1}
-weighted Hilbert space

L^{2}([0,1],A(u)^{-1}\de u):=\left\{f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}:\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1],A^{-1})}<\infty\right\},

(A.6)

where
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}([0,1],A^{-1})}
denotes the norm associated to the inner product

\langle f,h\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1],A^{-1})}:=\int_{0}^{1}f(u)A(u)^{-1}h(u)\de u;

3.

the sequence
\{\psi_{n}\}_{n}
defined above satisfies the bound
\|\psi_{n}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}\vee 1
.

Proof.

All three systems
\textsc{SL}_{0}(A),\textsc{SL}_{1}(A)
and
\textsc{SL}_{2}(A)
are
regular
SL problems; see
[
BR89
, Section 10.1]
. Standard results of the theory, then, immediately yield items i) and ii), so we only need to verify item iii).

Recalling (
2.20
), we start by noting that

\|\psi_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\psi_{n}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\psi_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}.

(A.7)

Since
A
is smooth and positive, the spaces
L^{2}([0,1])
and
L^{2}([0,1],A(u)^{-1}\de u)
coincide, and the norms
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}([0,1],A^{-1})}
are equivalent. Then, since
\{\psi_{n}\}_{n}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([0,1],A(u)^{-1}\de u)
, we immediately get a uniform upper bound for
\|\psi_{n}\|_{L^{2}}
. In order to estimate
\|\psi_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}
, performing an integration by parts and using
A(u)\psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u)+\gamma_{n}\psi_{n}(u)=0
for
u\in(0,1)
, we write

\|\psi_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\psi_{n}\psi_{n}^{\prime}\big|_{0}^{1}-\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u)\psi_{n}(u)\de u=\psi_{n}\psi_{n}^{\prime}\big|_{0}^{1}+\gamma_{n}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\psi_{n}(u)^{2}}{A(u)}\de u.

(A.8)

But now, for both problems (
A.3
) and (
A.5
), the boundary conditions imply that
\psi_{n}\psi_{n}^{\prime}\big|_{0}^{1}=0
; nonetheless, for the problem (
A.4
) we get

\psi_{n}\psi_{n}^{\prime}\big|_{0}^{1}=-\delta\psi_{n}(1)^{2}-\lambda\psi_{n}(0)^{2}\leq 0.

Hence, since
\myinf_{u\in[0,1]}A(u)>0
, combining (
A.7
) and (
A.8
) completes the proof.
∎

Below, for two continuous functions
A,\widetilde{A}
on
[0,1]
, we write
A\leq\widetilde{A}
if
A(u)\leq\widetilde{A}(u)
for (almost) every
u\in[0,1]
.

Lemma A.3
.

For each
j=0,1,2
, the following holds. If
A\leq\widetilde{A}
, then the sequences of eigenvalues
\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n},\{\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}\}_{n}
of
\textsc{SL}_{j}(A)
and
\textsc{SL}_{j}(\widetilde{A})
, respectively, satisfy
\gamma_{n-1}\leq\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}
for each
n\geq 2
.

Proof.

Fix
j\in\{0,1,2\}
and consider the differential equations on
(0,1)

\psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u)+A(u)^{-1}\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}\psi_{n}(u)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\psi}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u)+\widetilde{A}(u)^{-1}\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(u)=0.

Since
A(u)^{-1}\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}\geq\widetilde{A}(u)^{-1}\widetilde{\gamma}_{n}
for each
u\in(0,1)
, by the Sturm Comparison Theorem
[
BR89
, Theorem 10.6.3]
, the number of zeroes of any solution to the first equation is at least the number of zeroes of any solution to the second one. Moreover, the same theorem implies that the number of zeroes of any solution to the differential equation

\psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u)+A(u)^{-1}\gamma\psi_{n}(u)=0

is a non-decreasing function of
\gamma
. But now, by
[
BR89
, Theorem 10.8.5]
, any eigenfunction
\psi_{n}
of
\textsc{SL}_{j}(A)
corresponding to
\gamma_{n}
has exactly
n-1
zeroes in the open interval
(0,1)
. This completes the proof.
∎

A.19
) as

\begin{split}&\mathscr{Y}_{t_{2}}\left(P_{S-t_{2}+\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{n}}^{\theta}H\right)-\mathscr{Y}_{t_{1}}\left(P_{S-t_{1}}^{\theta}H\right)\\
&+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(\mathscr{Y}_{s_{j}^{n}}\left(P_{S-s_{j-1}^{n}}^{\theta}H-P_{S-s_{j}^{n}}^{\theta}H\right)-\int_{s_{j}^{n}}^{s_{j+1}^{n}}\mathscr{Y}_{r}\left(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}P_{S-s_{j}^{n}}^{\theta}H\right)\de r\right),\end{split}

(A.21)

where we used the linearity of
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
in the test function. Now, by Corollary
A.6
, we can further rewrite the sum in (
A.21
) as

\begin{split}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\Bigg(&\int_{s_{j}^{n}}^{s_{j+1}^{n}}\left\{\mathscr{Y}_{s_{j}^{n}}\left(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}P_{S-s_{j}^{n}}^{\theta}H\right)-\mathscr{Y}_{r}\left(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}P_{S-s_{j}^{n}}^{\theta}H\right)\right\}\de r\\
&+\mathscr{Y}_{s_{j}^{n}}\left(o\left(\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{n},S-s_{j}^{n}\right)\right)\Bigg).\end{split}

By Lemma
A.4
, for each fixed
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
, the map
t\mapsto\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}P_{t}^{\theta}H
is uniformly continuous on
[0,T]
. Moreover, since
\mathscr{Y}\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathcal{S}_{\theta}^{\prime})
, the map
(s,t)\mapsto\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}P_{t}^{\theta}H)
is uniformly continuous on the compact set
[0,T]\times[s,T]
. This implies that the last display vanishes as
n\to\infty
, which in turn implies that
\mylim_{n\to\infty}\eqref{eq:nonexp_sum}=\mathscr{Y}_{t_{2}}(P_{S-t_{2}}^{\theta}H)-\mathscr{Y}_{t_{1}}(P_{S-t_{1}}^{\theta}H)
almost surely. Recalling (
A.20
), we have thus proved that

\mylim_{n\to\infty}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathscr{X}_{s_{j+1}^{n},s_{j}^{n}}\left(P_{S-s_{j}^{n}}^{\theta}H\right)=e^{\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left\|\nabla P_{S-r}^{\theta}H\right\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}\de r+i\big(\mathscr{Y}_{t_{2}}\left(P_{S-t_{2}}^{\theta}H\right)-\mathscr{Y}_{t_{1}}\left(P_{S-t_{1}}^{\theta}H\right)\big)}

almost surely, which in turn is equal to
\frac{\mathscr{Z}_{t_{2},S}(H)}{\mathscr{Z}_{t_{1},S}(H)}
almost surely.

Let now
G
be a bounded,
\mathcal{F}_{t_{1}}
-measurable function, where
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\{\mathscr{Y}_{s}(H):s\leq t,H\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta}\}
. By the Dominated Convergence Theorem, the convergence above takes place in
L^{1}
, and thus

\mathbb{E}\left[G\frac{\mathscr{Z}_{t_{2},S}(H)}{\mathscr{Z}_{t_{1},S}(H)}\right]=\mylim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[G\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathscr{X}_{s_{j+1}^{n},s_{j}^{n}}\left(P_{S-s_{j}^{n}}^{\theta}H\right)\right].

(A.22)

Since
\{\mathscr{X}_{t,s}(H),t\in[s,T]\}
is a martingale, taking conditional expectations with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{s_{n-1}^{n}}
yields

\mathbb{E}\left[G\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathscr{X}_{s_{j+1}^{n},s_{j}^{n}}\left(P_{S-s_{j}^{n}}^{\theta}H\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[G\prod_{j=0}^{n-2}\mathscr{X}_{s_{j+1}^{n},s_{j}^{n}}\left(P_{S-s_{j}^{n}}^{\theta}H\right)\right].

Iterating this identity inductively, by (
A.22
) we deduce that

\mathbb{E}\left[G\frac{\mathscr{Z}_{t_{2},S}(H)}{\mathscr{Z}_{t_{1},S}(H)}\right]=\mathbb{E}[G]

for any bounded,
\mathcal{F}_{t_{1}}
-measurable function
G
. This shows that
\{\mathscr{Z}_{t,S}(H),t\in[0,S]\}
is indeed a martingale. But then, since
\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{Z}_{t,S}(H)|\mathcal{F}_{s}]=\mathscr{Z}_{s,S}(H)
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\big\|\nabla P_{S-r}^{\theta}H\big\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}\de r+i\mathscr{Y}_{t}\left(P_{S-t}^{\theta}H\right)}\;\Big|\;\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]=e^{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{s}\big\|\nabla P_{S-r}^{\theta}H\big\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}\de r+i\mathscr{Y}_{s}\left(P_{S-s}^{\theta}H\right)},

which in turn yields

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\mathscr{Y}_{t}\left(P_{S-t}^{\theta}H\right)}\;\Big|\;\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}\big\|\nabla P_{S-r}^{\theta}H\big\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}\de r+i\mathscr{Y}_{s}\left(P_{S-s}^{\theta}H\right)}.

Note that
P_{S-s}^{\theta}H=P_{t-s}^{\theta}P_{S-t}^{\theta}H
, and hence, writing
h:=P_{S-t}^{\theta}H
, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{i\mathscr{Y}_{t}\left(h\right)}\;\Big|\;\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}\big\|\nabla P_{t-r}^{\theta}h\big\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}\de r+i\mathscr{Y}_{s}\left(P_{t-s}^{\theta}h\right)}.

This means that, for each
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
and conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{s}
, the random variable
\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H)
has a Gaussian distribution of mean
\mathscr{Y}_{s}(P_{t-s}^{\theta}H)
and variance
\int_{s}^{t}\|\nabla P_{t-r}^{\theta}H\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}\de r
. But then, since the time-zero distribution is determined by (
2.19
), a standard Markov argument yields the uniqueness of finite-dimensional distributions of
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H),t\in[0,T]\}
, which in turn ensures the uniqueness in distribution of the stochastic process
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
.
∎

A.4  Proof of Proposition
2.16

Finally, we conclude by showing uniqueness of the martingale problem in Theorem
2.19
for
\theta<0
. Our proof follows the approach of
[
BGJS22
, Section 5.1.2]
. Throughout, we will assume
\theta<0
and we will call
\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
(since this space of test functions has in fact no dependence on
\theta
), which we recall is given by

\mathcal{S}=\left\{f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]):\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{u}^{k}f(0)=0,\\
\partial_{u}^{k}f(1)=0\end{array}\text{for all integers}\ k\geq 0\right\}.

Moreover, we will denote by
\mathcal{S}_{\theta,\Dir}
the space of test functions given in Definition
2.10
but for
0\leq\theta<1
, namely

\mathcal{S}_{\theta,\Dir}:=\left\{f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]):\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}^{k}f(0)=0,\\
\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}^{k}f(1)=0\end{array}\text{for all integers}\ k\geq 0\right\}.

By the arguments given in the previous sections, we have already proved the existence of a solution to the martingale problem stated in Proposition
2.16
. The idea is then the following: take any
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
-valued solution to this martingale problem, show that, for any time
t\in[0,T]
, there exists an
\mathcal{S}_{\theta,\Dir}^{\prime}
-valued solution
\mathscr{\tilde{Y}}
which extends it, and then show that the extension belongs to the space of continuous paths
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathcal{S}_{\theta,\Dir}^{\prime})
. Since we already know (from the argument given to show Proposition
2.15
) that solutions to the martingale problem in Proposition
2.15
are unique in law, this would then imply uniqueness of the
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
-valued limit point.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,1])
denote the Sobolev space of square integrable and twice differentiable functions
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\partial_{u}f
and
\partial_{u}^{2}f
are also square integrable, equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}
defined via

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}^{2}:=\int_{0}^{2}f(u)^{2}\de u+\int_{0}^{2}(\partial_{u}f(u))^{2}\de u+\int_{0}^{2}(\partial_{u}^{2}f(u))^{2}\de u.

We define the “intermediate” space of test functions

\mathcal{\tilde{S}}:=\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,1]):\begin{array}[]{ll}f(0)=\partial_{u}f(0)=0,\\
f(1)=\partial_{u}f(1)=0\end{array}\right\}.

This space is a closed vector subspace of
\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,1])
and contains
\mathcal{S}
.

Recall the definition of the space
L^{2}([0,1])
in Section
2.5
. The following approximation result can be found in
[
BGJS22
, Lemma A.4]
.

Lemma A.7
.

Let
H\in\mathcal{\tilde{S}}
. There exists a sequence of functions
\{H_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
in
\mathcal{S}
such that, for
k=0,1,2
,
\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\partial_{u}^{k}H_{\varepsilon}=\partial_{u}^{k}H
in
L^{2}([0,1])
, namely
\{H_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges to
H
in
\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,1])
.

Let now
\mathscr{B}
denote the Banach space of real-valued processes
\{x_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
with continuous trajectories, equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{B}}
defined by

\|x\|_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}|x_{t}|^{2}\right].

Recalling the definition of
\mathscr{H}
in Section
3.5
and the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{H}}
in (
3.65
), we see that
\mathscr{B}\subseteq\mathscr{H}
, and moreover
\|x\|_{\mathscr{H}}\leq\sqrt{T}\|x\|_{\mathscr{B}}
. In particular, if a sequence of processes
\{x^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges to
x
in
\mathscr{B}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, then the convergence also holds in
\mathscr{H}
.

We will keep the notation
\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}
to denote the extension of the operator
\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}
to
\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,1])
, and similarly for the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2,\theta}}
defined in (
2.14
), which we recall is simply given by

\|H\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}=2\int_{0}^{1}\Phi(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}(u))H^{2}(u)\de u

for
\theta<0
. The result below follows
[
BGJS22
, Lemma 5.3]
.

Lemma A.8
.

Let
\mathscr{Y}
be an
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
-valued stochastic process satisfying (
2.18
) and item i) of Proposition
2.16
for each
H\in\mathcal{S}
. Then, for any
H\in\mathcal{\tilde{S}}
, the process
\{\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(H)_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
defined via

\mathscr{\tilde{M}}_{t}(H):=\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H)-\mathscr{Y}_{0}(H)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H)\de s

(A.23)

is a martingale with continuous trajectories and with quadratic variation given by

\langle\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(H)\rangle_{t}=t\|\partial_{u}H\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}.

(A.24)

Moreover, the process
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H),t\in[0,T]\}
has continuous trajectories.

Remark A.9
.

Since, for
H\in\mathcal{\tilde{S}}
, the functions
H
and
\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H
may not be in
\mathcal{S}
, the terms on the right-hand side of (
A.23
) are to be understood in the sense of Lemma
3.17
.

Proof of Lemma
A.8
.

Given
H\in\mathcal{\tilde{S}}
, by Lemma
A.7
we can approximate
H
by a sequence
\{H_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
in
\mathcal{S}
such that, for
k=0,1,2
,
\|\partial_{u}^{k}H_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{u}^{k}H\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, so that in particular
\|\partial_{u}H_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{u}H\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}\leq 2\|\Phi(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\|_{\infty}\|\partial_{u}H_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{u}H\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Using (
2.18
), the process
\{\mathscr{M}_{t}(H_{\varepsilon}),t\in[0,T]\}
defined via

\mathscr{M}_{t}(H_{\varepsilon}):=\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H_{\varepsilon})-\mathscr{Y}_{0}(H_{\varepsilon})-\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H_{\varepsilon})\de s

(A.25)

is a martingale with continuous trajectories and with quadratic variation
\langle\mathscr{M}(H_{\varepsilon})\rangle_{t}=t\|\partial_{u}H_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}
.

We claim that the sequence of real-valued martingales
\{\mathscr{M}_{t}(H_{\varepsilon}),t\in[0,T]\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges in
\mathscr{B}
to a martingale, denoted by
\{\mathscr{\tilde{M}}_{t}(H),t\in[0,T]\}
, with continuous trajectories and with quadratic variation given by (
A.24
). To this end, for any
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
note that, by Doob’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{M}(H_{\varepsilon})-\mathscr{M}(H_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\|_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\mathscr{M}_{t}(H_{\varepsilon})-\mathscr{M}_{t}(H_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\big)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\mathscr{M}_{t}(H_{\varepsilon}-H_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\big)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=T\|\partial_{u}H_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{u}H_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2},

and the last term converges to
0
as
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0
. Hence,
\{\mathscr{M}(H_{\varepsilon})\}_{\varepsilon}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathscr{B}
, and thus it converges to a process in
\mathscr{B}
, which we denote by
\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(H)
. One can easily check that the limit only depends on
H
and not on the approximating sequence
\{H_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
, so that the notation is justified. Moreover, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{\tilde{M}}_{t}(H)^{2}\right]=\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{M}_{t}(H_{\varepsilon})^{2}\right]=t\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|\partial_{u}H_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}=t\|\partial_{u}H\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2},

so that the quadratic variation of
\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(H)
is given by (
A.24
).

Now, by Lemma
3.17
and since
\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}
, the processes
\mathscr{Y}(H_{\varepsilon})
and
\mathscr{Y}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H_{\varepsilon})
converge in
\mathscr{H}
to
\mathscr{Y}(H)
and
\mathscr{Y}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H)
, respectively. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and item i) of Proposition
2.16
, the sequence of real-valued processes
\{\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H_{\varepsilon})\de s,t\in[0,T]\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathscr{B}
, which follows from the fact that

\|\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H_{\varepsilon}-\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\partial_{u}^{2}H_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{u}^{2}H\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\to 0

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Thus, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, this sequence converges to a process
\{\mathscr{Z}_{t}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H),t\in[0,T]\}
in
\mathscr{B}
(where, again, the limiting process is independent of the approximating sequence), and the convergence holds also in
\mathscr{H}
.

Hence, from (
A.25
) we get that in
\mathscr{H}
, and consequently almost everywhere in time and
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely, the equality

\mathscr{\tilde{M}}_{t}(H)=\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H)-\mathscr{Y}_{0}(H)-\mathscr{Z}_{t}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H)

holds. Since
\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(H)
and
\mathscr{Z}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H)
have continuous trajectories, this implies that the same holds for the process
\mathscr{Y}(H)
. Finally, it is easy to show that
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely we have the equality

\mathscr{Z}_{t}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H)\de s,

which completes the proof.
∎

Following
[
BGJS22
, Lemma 5.5]
, we are now going to construct a function which is neither in
\mathcal{S}_{\theta,\Dir}
nor in
\mathcal{\tilde{S}}
for which we can properly define the martingale problem of Proposition
2.16
. This intermediate step will allow us to define the martingale problem for any test function
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta,\Dir}
.

Let
a:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the map defined via

a(u):=ce^{-\frac{1}{u(1-u)}}\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,1)}(u),\quad\text{where }c:=\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\frac{1}{u(1-u)}}\de u\right)^{-1},

and define
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\phi(u):=1-\int_{0}^{u}a(t)\de t.

(A.26)

For
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha>\frac{1}{1-\beta}
, let
\psi_{\alpha,\beta}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined via

\psi_{\alpha,\beta}(u):=u\,\phi(\alpha(u-\beta))

(A.27)

(so that, in particular,
\psi_{\alpha,\beta}(u)=u
for
u\in[0,\beta]
and
\psi_{\alpha,\beta}(u)=0
for
u\in[\beta+\tfrac{1}{\alpha},1]
; see Figure
3(a)
). We set
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta}:=\psi_{\alpha,\beta}\circ i
, where
i:[0,1]\mapsto[0,1]
is given by
i(u):=1-u
(so that
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta}
is simply the reflection of
\psi_{\alpha,\beta}
with respect to the axis
x=\frac{1}{2}
).

Lemma A.10
.

Let
\mathscr{Y}
be an
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
-valued stochastic process satisfying (
2.18
) and items i) and ii) of Proposition
2.16
for each
H\in\mathcal{S}
. Fix
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha>\frac{1}{1-\beta}
. For
\psi\in\{\psi_{\alpha,\beta},\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta}\}
, the process
\{\mathscr{\tilde{M}}_{t}(\psi),t\in[0,T]\}
defined via

\mathscr{\tilde{M}}_{t}(\psi):=\mathscr{Y}_{t}(\psi)-\mathscr{Y}_{0}(\psi)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}\psi)\de s

(A.28)

is a martingale with continuous trajectories and with quadratic variation given by

\langle\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(\psi)\rangle_{t}=t\|\partial_{u}\psi\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}.

(A.29)

Moreover, the process
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}(\psi),t\in[0,T]\}
has continuous trajectories.

Proof.

Since the proof for
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta}
is completely analogous, we will only show the result for
\psi=\psi_{\alpha,\beta}
. For
0<\varepsilon<1
, define the map

h_{\varepsilon}(u):=\begin{cases}\frac{u^{2}}{2\varepsilon},&\text{ for }u\in[0,\varepsilon],\\
u-\frac{\varepsilon}{2},&\text{ for }u\in[\varepsilon,1],\end{cases}

which belongs to
\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,1])
, and let
{\phi}_{\alpha,\beta}(u):=\phi(\alpha(u-\beta))
(with
\phi
given in (
A.26
)). Recalling (
A.27
), it is not hard to show that
\psi_{\varepsilon}:=h_{\varepsilon}{\phi}_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathcal{\tilde{S}}
; see Figure
3(b)
.

Moreover, defining the Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{1}([0,1])
in analogy to
\mathcal{H}^{2}([0,1])
, the sequence
\{\psi_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges in
\mathcal{H}^{1}([0,1])
to
\psi
as
\varepsilon\to 0
: indeed, a simple computation shows that

\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\int_{0}^{1}(\psi_{\varepsilon}(u)-\psi(u))^{2}\de u+\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{u}\psi_{\varepsilon}(u)-\partial_{u}\psi(u))^{2}\de u\right)=0.

Now, for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, by Lemma
A.8
we have that the process
\{\mathscr{\tilde{M}}_{t}(\psi_{\varepsilon}),

t\in[0,T]\}
defined via

\mathscr{\tilde{M}}_{t}(\psi_{\varepsilon}):=\mathscr{Y}_{t}(\psi_{\varepsilon})-\mathscr{Y}_{0}(\psi_{\varepsilon})-\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}\psi_{\varepsilon})\de s

is a real-valued martingale with continuous trajectories and quadratic variation given by
\langle\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\rangle_{t}=t\|\partial_{u}\psi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}
, and moreover the process
\mathscr{Y}(\psi_{\varepsilon})
has continuous trajectories. Also, since
\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|\partial_{u}\psi_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{u}\psi\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}=0
, the sequence of martingales
\{\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(\psi_{\varepsilon})\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges in
\mathscr{B}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
to a martingale, which we denote by
\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(\psi)
, which has continuous trajectories and quadratic variation given by (
A.29
). Moreover, we have that
\mathscr{Y}(\psi_{\varepsilon})
converges to
\mathscr{Y}(\psi)
in
\mathscr{H}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This can be seen as in the proof of Lemma
A.8
.

Setting now

x^{\varepsilon}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}{\mathscr{Y}}_{s}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}\psi_{\varepsilon})\de s,

it only remains to show the convergence of the sequence
\{x^{\varepsilon}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}_{\varepsilon>0}\subseteq\mathscr{B}
to the process
\{x_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
given by
x_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}\psi)\de s
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}\psi_{\varepsilon}(u)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}(u))\partial_{u}^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}(u)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}(u))\phi_{\alpha,\beta}(u)\partial_{u}^{2}h_{\varepsilon}(u)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}(u))2\partial_{u}\phi_{\alpha,\beta}(u)\partial_{u}h_{\varepsilon}(u)+\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta}(u))h_{\varepsilon}(u)\partial_{u}^{2}{\phi}_{\alpha,\beta}(u).

The function given by the bottom line of the last display converges in
L^{2}([0,1])
to
\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\partial_{u}^{2}\psi

=\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}\psi
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which is easy to see from the identity
\partial_{u}^{2}\psi(u)=2\partial_{u}\phi_{\alpha,\beta}(u)+u\,\partial_{u}^{2}\phi_{\alpha,\beta}(u)
for any
u\in[0,1]
. At the same time, we have that
\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\phi_{\alpha,\beta}\,\partial_{u}^{2}h_{\varepsilon}=\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\iota^{0}_{\varepsilon}
, and hence, by condition ii) of Proposition
2.16
, we have that

\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\mysup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{\theta})\phi_{\alpha,\beta}\,\partial_{u}^{2}h_{\varepsilon})\de s\right)^{2}\right]=0.

Hence, we get that
\{x^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges to
x
as
\varepsilon\to 0
in
\mathscr{B}
.
∎

From Lemmas

Corollary A.11
.

Let
\mathscr{Y}
be an
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
-valued stochastic process satisfying (
2.18
) and items i) and ii) of Proposition
2.16
for each
H\in\mathcal{S}
. Then, the process
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
can be extended to a process
\{\mathscr{\tilde{Y}}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
belonging to
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathcal{S}_{\theta,\Dir})
and solution to the martingale problem of Proposition
2.15
(with
\theta<0
and with
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
substituted with
\mathcal{S}_{\theta,\Dir}
).

Proof.

For any
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\alpha>\frac{1}{1-\beta}
, we can decompose any function
H
in
\mathcal{S}_{\theta,\Dir}
as

H=\partial_{u}H(0)\psi_{\alpha,\beta}-\partial_{u}H(1)\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta}+(H-\partial_{u}H(0)\psi_{\alpha,\beta}+\partial_{u}H(1)\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta}).

By the properties of
H
,
\psi_{\alpha,\beta}
and
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta}
, it is easy to check that the map

G_{\alpha,\beta}:=H-\partial_{u}H(0)\psi_{\alpha,\beta}+\partial_{u}H(1)\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta}

belongs to
\mathcal{\tilde{S}}
: indeed, both
G_{\alpha,\beta}(0)=G_{\alpha,\beta}(1)=0
and
\partial_{u}G_{\alpha,\beta}(0)=\partial_{u}G_{\alpha,\beta}(1)=0
follow immediately.

Also, by the linearity of the martingale problem, the maps
\partial_{u}H(0)\psi_{\alpha,\beta}
and
\partial_{u}H(1)\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta}
are suitable for Lemma
A.10
. This implies that there exists a continuous martingale, which we denote by
\{\mathscr{\tilde{M}}^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(H),t\in[0,T]\}
, and given by

\mathscr{\tilde{M}}^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(H):=\partial_{u}H(0)\mathscr{\tilde{M}}_{t}(\psi_{\alpha,\beta})-\partial_{u}H(1)\mathscr{\tilde{M}}_{t}(\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta})+\mathscr{\tilde{M}}_{t}(G_{\alpha,\beta}),

(with
\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(\psi_{\alpha,\beta}),\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta})
and
\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(G_{\alpha,\beta})
defined via (
A.28
) and (
A.23
)) which has continuous trajectories and satisfies

\mathscr{\tilde{M}}^{\alpha,\beta}_{t}(H)=\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H)-\mathscr{Y}_{0}(H)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\mathfrak{A}_{\theta}H)\de s.

Moreover, for any
H\in\mathcal{S}_{\theta,\Dir}
, the real-valued process
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H),t\in[0,T]\}
has continuous trajectories, which implies that the process
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}(H),t\in[0,T]\}
belongs to
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathcal{S}_{\theta,\Dir}^{\prime})
.

We now claim that, by choosing
\beta=\frac{1}{\alpha}
, the sequence of martingales
\{\mathscr{\tilde{M}}^{\alpha,\beta}(H)\}_{\alpha>0}
converges in
\mathscr{B}
, as
\alpha\to\infty
, to a martingale
\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(H)
whose quadratic variation is given by
\langle\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(H)\rangle_{t}=t\|\partial_{u}H\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}
. It is easy to check that, if
\beta=\frac{1}{\alpha}
, then

\mylim_{\alpha\to\infty}\|\partial_{u}\psi_{\alpha,\beta}\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}=\mylim_{\alpha\to\infty}\|\partial_{u}\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta}\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}=0,

since the supports of
\psi_{\alpha,\frac{1}{\alpha}}
and
\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\frac{1}{\alpha}}
converge to the empty set as
\alpha
goes to infinity. This implies that

\mylim_{\alpha\to\infty}\|\partial_{u}G_{\alpha,\beta}-\partial_{u}H\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}=0.

Moreover, recalling that
\langle\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(\psi_{\alpha,\beta})\rangle_{t}=t\|\partial_{u}\psi_{\alpha,\beta}\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}
,
\langle\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta})\rangle_{t}=t\|\partial_{u}\tilde{\psi}_{\alpha,\beta}\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}
and
\langle\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(G_{\alpha,\beta})\rangle_{t}=t\|\partial_{u}G_{\alpha,\beta}\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2}
, by Doob’s inequality we conclude that
\{\mathscr{\tilde{M}}^{\alpha,\beta}\}_{\alpha>0}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathscr{B}
. Thus, this sequence converges to a martingale
\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(H)
in
\mathscr{B}
whose quadratic variation satisfies

\langle\mathscr{\tilde{M}}(H)\rangle_{t}=\mylim_{\alpha\to\infty}\langle\mathscr{\tilde{M}}^{\alpha,\beta}(H)\rangle_{t}=t\|\partial_{u}H\|_{L^{2,\theta}}^{2},

as required.
∎

This completes the proof of Proposition
2.16
.
