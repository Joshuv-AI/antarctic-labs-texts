Abstract
. This article deals with functions with a prefixed range and their inclusions in Hardy and weighted Bergman spaces. This idea was originally introduced by Hansen for Hardy spaces, and it was recently taken into weighted Bergman spaces by Karafyllia and Karamanlis. We provide a new characterization for the Hardy number of a domain in terms of its Green function. Based on this, we present a class of domains for which the Hardy number and the Bergman number coincide. However, in general, we show that the Hardy number and the Bergman number of a domain are not equal; even for domains which are regular for the Dirichlet problem.

Primary: 30H10, 30H20; Secondary: 31A15.

1.
Introduction

This article is devoted to analyze the Hardy and weighted Bergman spaces to which holomorphic functions with a prefixed range belong. To introduce these ideas, let us recall that the Hardy space
H^{p}
for
0<p<+\infty
is defined as the space of holomorphic functions
f
defined on the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
for which

\sup_{0<r<1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{p}d\theta<+\infty.

In the case
p=+\infty
,
H^{\infty}
stands for the space of bounded holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{D}
. An introduction to these spaces can be found in
[
6
]
.

It is known
[
6
, p. 2]
that
H^{q}\subset H^{p}
whenever
0<p\leq q\leq+\infty
. The Hardy number of a function
f
that is holomorphic on
\mathbb{D}
is defined as

\h(f)=\sup(\{0\}\cup\{p>0:f\in H^{p}\})\in[0,+\infty].

In
[
10
]
, Hansen introduced the Hardy number of a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\h(D)

\displaystyle=\sup(\{0\}\cup\{p>0:\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D},D)\subset H^{p}\})

\displaystyle=\inf\{\h(f):f\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D},D)\}\in[0,+\infty].

Here,
\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D},D)
denotes the set of all holomorphic functions
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
.
There are some recent works in which this topic is studied. Some of them (see
[
8
,
13
,
18
]
) estimate the Hardy number of general domains using the harmonic measure or the hyperbolic distance. Some others (see
[
5
,
14
]
) analyze the Hardy number of domains with specific geometric properties.

In
[
8
]
, Essén related the Hardy number of a domain with certain harmonic measures. Later, Kim and Sugawa
[
18
]
provided a formula for the Hardy number of a domain using Essén’s theorem; see Subsection
2.4
. The first result of this article deals with the Hardy number of a domain and its Green function. To introduce it, we recall some tools: If the complement (in
\mathbb{C}
) of a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
contains at least two points, then
D
is a
hyperbolic domain
and there exists a universal covering map
f_{D}\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
; see
[
19
, Chapter 15]
. If
D
is a domain and its complement is non-polar, then
D
possesses a Green function; see Subsection
2.2
. Such domains are called
Greenian
. In particular, Greenian domains are also hyperbolic. Without loss of generality, we will sometimes assume that
0\in D
and that the universal covering map
f_{D}
is such that
f_{D}(0)=0
. In such a case, if
g_{D}
is the Green function of
D
, we extend it so that
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}\ni z\mapsto g_{D}(z,0)
is defined to be
0
whenever
z\not\in D
. Under the latter setting, we define the function

(1.1)

\psi_{D}(r)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}g_{D}(re^{i\theta},0)d\theta,\quad 0<r<+\infty.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a Greenian domain. Assume that
0\in D
, and let
f_{D}\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
be a universal covering map with
f_{D}(0)=0
. Let
\psi_{D}
be as in (
1.1
). Then,

\h(D)=\h(f_{D})=\liminf_{r\to+\infty}\left(-\dfrac{\log\psi_{D}(r)}{\log r}\right).

We recall that if a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
is not Greenian, then
\h(D)=0
; see Subsection
2.4
.

The function
\psi_{D}
in (
1.1
) can also be used to connect the Hardy number and the Bergman number of a domain. To present this, we recall that the weighted Bergman space
A^{p}_{\alpha}
for
0<p<+\infty
and
\alpha>-1
is the space of holomorphic functions
f
on
\mathbb{D}
for which

\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left|f(z)\right|^{p}(1-\left|z\right|)^{\alpha}dA(z)<+\infty,

where
A
denotes the (Lebesgue) area measure. An exposition regarding these spaces can be found in
[
7
]
.

In
[
3
]
, Betsakos, Karafyllia, and Karamanlis analyzed the weighted Bergman spaces to which a given conformal map belongs. Later, Karafyllia and Karamanlis
[
16
]
introduced the Bergman number of a holomorphic map
f
on
\mathbb{D}
, that is,

\b(f)=\sup\left(\{0\}\cup\left\{\dfrac{p}{\alpha+2}:p>0,\,\alpha>-1,\,f\in A^{p}_{\alpha}\right\}\right)\in[0,+\infty].

They showed that
\h(f)=\b(f)
for all conformal maps on
\mathbb{D}
in contrast to the general case; see Remark
2.11
.

In
[
15
]
, Karafyllia also introduced the Bergman number of a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
, that is,

(1.2)

\b(D)=\inf\{\b(f):f\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D},D)\}\in[0,+\infty].

To continue with these ideas, we introduce Bergman numbers depending on the weight: For
\alpha>-1
and a holomorphic map
f
on
\mathbb{D}
, set

\b_{\alpha}(f)=\sup(\{0\}\cup\{p>0:f\in A^{p}_{\alpha}\})\in[0,+\infty].

Similarly, for a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
, we also define

(1.3)

\displaystyle\b_{\alpha}(D)

\displaystyle=\sup(\{0\}\cup\{p>0:\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D},D)\subset A^{p}_{\alpha}\})

\displaystyle=\inf\{\b_{\alpha}(f):f\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D},D)\}\in[0,+\infty].

It follows from the work of Karafyllia and Karamanlis
[
16
]
that
\b(D)=\h(D)
for every simply connected domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
. In the present article, we further investigate about this equality for multiply connected domains. For instance, we construct some domains for which the Hardy number and the Bergman number differ. In particular, one of these examples disproves a previous claim in
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

(a)
For every
p\in[0,+\infty)
there exists a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
such that
\h(D)=p
and
\b(D)=\b_{\alpha}(D)=+\infty
,
\alpha>-1
.

(b)
There exists a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
which is regular for the Dirichlet problem but
\h(D)=1/2
and
\b(D)=\b_{\alpha}(D)=+\infty
,
\alpha>-1
.

In contrast, we also show that the equality between the Hardy number and the Bergman number holds for a class of multiply connected domains. This class is given as follows:

Definition 1.3
.

A domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
belongs to the class
\mathcal{D}
, if it has the following properties:

(a)
D
is unbounded.

(b) Let
F
be the union of all bounded components of
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\setminus D
. The set
F
is bounded.

(c) Consider the simply connected domain
\Omega=D\cup F
. For all sufficiently large
r>0
, the set
\Omega\cap\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=r\}
has exactly one component.

A half-strip or a sector having a finite number of holes are typical domains in the class
\mathcal{D}
. Also,
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n}\}
is a domain in
\mathcal{D}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\alpha>-1
,
D\in\mathcal{D}
and let
\Omega
be the simply connected domain associated with
D
in Definition
1.3
. Then

\h(D)=\b(D)=\h(\Omega)=\b(\Omega)=\dfrac{\b_{\alpha}(D)}{\alpha+2}=\dfrac{\b_{\alpha}(\Omega)}{\alpha+2}.

If, in addition,
D
is a hyperbolic domain with
0\in D
, we have

\h(f_{D})=\b(f_{D})=\dfrac{\b_{\alpha}(f)}{\alpha+2},

where
f_{D}\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
is a universal covering map with
f_{D}(0)=0
.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
we provide some preliminaries needed throughout the paper. Next, in the subsequent sections, we prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. In Section
5
we provide some properties of the class
\mathcal{D}
. Finally, in Section
6
, we present the proof of Theorem
1.4
. We end with some open problems in Section
7
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Harmonic measure

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{C}
and let
E
be a Borel subset of
\partial\Omega
. The harmonic measure of
E
with respect to
\Omega
is the solution of the generalized Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian in
\Omega
with boundary function
\chi_{E}
; see
[
22
, Theorem 4.2.6]
. For
z\in\Omega
, we are going to use the standard notation
\omega(z,E,\Omega)
for the harmonic measure of
E
with respect to
\Omega
, evaluated at
z\in\Omega
. We will use the same notation even if
\Omega
is only an open set; in this case, we consider the component of
\Omega
containing the point
z
.

Note that the harmonic measure, as a function of
E
, is a Borel probability measure on
\partial\Omega
.
The basic properties of harmonic measure are the maximum principle and the conformal invariance. We briefly review now some additional properties of harmonic measure that we will need.

The first property we state is the
Strong Markov Property
. Let
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega_{2}
be domains and
E
be a Borel subset of
\partial\Omega_{1}\cap\partial\Omega_{2}
. Then, for all
z\in\Omega_{1}
, by
[
21
, p. 88]
,

(2.1)

\omega(z,E,\Omega_{2})=\omega(z,E,\Omega_{1})+\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}}\omega(\zeta,E,\Omega_{2})\;\omega(z,d\zeta,\Omega_{1}).

We will also need a polarization result of Solynin. We state here a simple version of
[
24
, Theorem 2]
. Let
\Omega
be a planar domain and let
\ell
be a straight line dividing the plane into two open half-planes
H^{+},H^{-}
. Set
\Omega^{+}=\Omega\cap H^{+}
and
\Omega^{-}=\Omega\cap H^{-}
. Let
*
denote the reflection in
\ell
.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
24
, Theorem 2]

Let
E
be a Borel subset of
\partial\Omega
. Assume that
(\Omega^{-})^{*}\subset\Omega^{+}
.

(a)
If
z\in\ell\cap\Omega
,
E\subset\partial\Omega\cap\partial\Omega^{-}
,
and
E^{*}\subset\partial\Omega
, then

\omega(z,E,\Omega)\leq\omega(z,E^{*},\Omega)
.

(b)
If
z\in\Omega^{-}
and
E=E^{*}
, then
\omega(z,E,\Omega)\leq\omega(z^{*},E,\Omega)
.

2.2.
Green function and hyperbolic metric

A Borel set
E\subset\mathbb{C}
is called polar if

\int_{\mathbb{C}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log\left|z-w\right|d\mu(z)d\mu(w)=-\infty

for all Borel probabilities
\mu
whose support is a compact set lying on
E
. A property is said to hold for
nearly every
point of a set
A\subset\mathbb{C}
(briefly,
n.e.
) if there exists a polar subset
E\subset A
such that the property holds on
A\setminus E
.
For more details on this concept we refer to
[
22
, Sections 3.2 and 5.1]
.

A Green function for a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
is a map
g_{D}\colon D\times D\to(-\infty,+\infty]
such that, for all
w\in D
, the following properties hold:

(i) The function
z\mapsto g_{D}(z,w)
is harmonic on
D\setminus\{w\}
and bounded outside every neighbourhood of
w
.

(ii)
g_{D}(w,w)=+\infty
; moreover,
g(z,w)=-\log\left|z-w\right|+\mathrm{O}(1)
as
z\to w
.

(iii) For nearly every
\xi\in\partial D
,
g_{D}(z,w)\to 0
as
z\to\xi
.

A domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
possesses a (necessarily unique) Green function if and only if
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}
is not a polar set; see
[
22
, Theorem 4.4.2]
. As stated before, such domains are called Greenian. In particular, if
D
is a Greenian domain, then
g_{D}(z,w)>0
for all
z,w\in D
; see
[
22
, Theorem 4.4.3]
. Thus, the function
\psi_{D}
defined on (
1.1
) is non-negative. In the case of the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
, its Green function is given by

g_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z)=\log\frac{1}{|z|},\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{D}.

We will use the following relation with universal covering maps:

Theorem 2.2
.

(see e.g.
[
19
, Theorem 15.6]
)

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a domain with Green function
g_{D}
and universal covering map
f_{D}\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
. Then, for all
a\in\mathbb{D}
and all
w\in D
,
w\neq f(a)
, it holds

g_{D}(f(a),w)=\sum_{j}g_{\mathbb{D}}(a,z_{j}(w)),

where
\{z_{j}(w)\}\subset\mathbb{D}
are all the preimages of
w
by
f_{D}
.

Another basic property of the Green function is its domain monotonicity: If
D,\Omega
are Greenian domains and
D\subset\Omega
, then

g_{D}(z,w)\leq g_{\Omega}(z,w),\;\;\;z,w\in D.

A more precise relation between
g_{D}
and
g_{\Omega}
is established by the Strong Markov Property (see
[
21
, p. 54]
): For
z,w\in D\subset\Omega
,

(2.2)

g_{\Omega}(z,w)=g_{D}(z,w)+\int_{\partial D\cap\Omega}g_{\Omega}(\zeta,w)\omega(z,d\zeta,D).

We will need some basic properties of the hyperbolic metric. We refer to
[
4
]
and
[
11
]
for detailed accounts. First we recall the definition of the hyperbolic distance for the unit disk.
If
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{D}
, then

\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})}{1-\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})},\;\;\;\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})=\left|\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{1-\overline{z_{1}}z_{2}}\right|.

For a hyperbolic domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
, the hyperbolic distance
\rho_{D}
is defined as follows: Let
\zeta,w\in D
. Consider a universal covering map (for
D
)
f_{D}:\mathbb{D}\to D
with
f_{D}(0)=\zeta
. Let
z_{j}(w),j=1,2,\dots
, be the preimages of
w
under
f_{D}
with
|z_{1}(w)|\leq|z_{2}(w)|\leq\dots
. Then

(2.3)

\rho_{D}(\zeta,w):=\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{1}(w))=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{D}\\
f(z)=w\end{subarray}}\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z).

The following lemma presents some relations between Green function and hyperbolic distance.

Lemma 2.3
.

(a)
Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a simply connected domain. Then for
z,w\in D,\;z\neq w
,

(2.4)

g_{D}(z,w)=\log\frac{1+e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}}{1-e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}}.

(b)
Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a simply connected domain. If
z,w\in D
and
\rho_{D}(z,w)\geq\frac{\log 2}{2}
, then

(2.5)

g_{D}(z,w)\leq 4\,e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}.

(c)
If
D\subset\mathbb{C}
is a Greenian domain, then for
z,w\in D,\;z\neq w
,

(2.6)

g_{D}(z,w)\geq e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}.

Proof.

(a) If
D=\mathbb{D}
and
z=0
, then (
2.4
) is an easy consequence of the explicit formulas for the Green function and the hyperbolic distance on
\mathbb{D}
. The general case follows from conformal invariance.

(b) Because of (
2.4
) and the inequality
\rho_{D}(z,w)\geq\frac{\log 2}{2}
, we have

(2.7)

\displaystyle g_{D}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\frac{1+e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}}{1-e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}}=\log\left(1+\frac{2e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}}{1-e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}}{1-e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}}\leq 4\,e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}.

(c) Let
z,w\in D,\;z\neq w
. Consider a universal covering map
f_{D}\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
with
f_{D}(0)=z
. Let
z_{j}(w)
,
j=1,2,\dots
, be the preimages of
w
under
f_{D}
with
\left|z_{1}(w)\right|\leq\left|z_{2}(w)\right|\leq\dots
. By (
2.2
) and (
2.4
),

(2.8)

\displaystyle g_{D}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}g_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{j}(w))\geq g_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{1}(w))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\frac{1+e^{-2\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{1}(w))}}{1-e^{-2\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{1}(w))}}.

Using the elementary inequality
\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}\geq x
,
0<x<1
, and (
2.3
), we infer from (
2.7
) that

(2.9)

\displaystyle g_{D}(z,w)\geq e^{-2\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{1}(w))}=e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)}.

∎

2.3.
Littlewood-Paley formulas.

In order to relate the Hardy and Bergman number of a domain with its Green function, the following classical characterizations will be used.

Theorem 2.4
.

[
25
, Theorem 1]

Let
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function. For
0<p<+\infty
,
f\in H^{p}
if and only if

\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left|f(z)\right|^{p-2}\left|f^{\prime}(z)\right|^{2}\log(1/\left|z\right|)dA(z)<+\infty.

Remark 2.5
.

The integrability condition of the latter result is not the one stated in
[
25
, Theorem 1]
, but it is easy to see that they are equivalent.

Theorem 2.6
.

[
23
, Lemma 2.3]

Let
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function. For
0<p<+\infty
and
\alpha>-1
,
f\in A^{p}_{\alpha}
if and only if

\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left|f(z)\right|^{p-2}\left|f^{\prime}(z)\right|^{2}\log(1/\left|z\right|)^{\alpha+2}dA(z)<+\infty.

2.4.
The Essén-Kim-Sugawa formula.

Kim and Sugawa
[
18
, Lemma 3.2]
, based on an older theorem of Essén
[
8
, Lemma 1]
,
proved the following result: Let
D
be a domain containing the origin. For
R>0
,
let
\Delta_{R}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<R\}
and
D_{R}
be the connected component of
D\cap\Delta_{R}
containing
0
. Set

\omega(R)=\omega(0,\partial D_{R}\cap\partial\Delta_{R},D_{R})
. Then

(2.10)

\h(D)=\liminf_{R\to+\infty}\left(-\frac{\log\omega(R)}{\log R}\right).

In particular, if a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
is not Greenian (i.e.,
\mathbb{C}\setminus D
is polar), then
\h(D)=\h(\mathbb{C})=0
; see
[
18
, Theorem 2.4.(i)]
. This also follows from an older result due to Nevanlinna.

2.5.
Inclusions among Hardy and weighted Bergman spaces.

Inclusions between Hardy spaces are simple and very well known
[
6
, p. 2]
:
H^{q}\subset H^{p}
if and only if
0<p\leq q\leq+\infty
. This is, indeed, what motivates the definition of the Hardy number. There are also classical results relating Hardy and Bergman spaces. For example:

Theorem 2.7
.

(see
[
6
, Theorem 5.11]
)

Let
p,q>0
and
\alpha>-1
. If
q\geq p/(\alpha+2)
, then
H^{q}\subset A^{p}_{\alpha}
. In particular,
H^{p}\subset A^{p}_{\alpha}
for all
p>0
,
\alpha>-1
.

However, the reversed inclusions completely fail; see
[
7
, p. 80]
for a discussion and
[
6
, Theorem 5.10]
for a wider setting:

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
p>0
,
\alpha>-1
. The space
A^{p}_{\alpha}
contains functions that are not in the Nevanlinna class, and therefore they are not in any Hardy space.

For a definition of the Nevanlinna class, its properties, and the inclusion of Hardy spaces in it, we refer to
[
6
, Section 2.1]
.

The inclusions between mixed-norm spaces have been completely characterized in
[
2
]
. In particular, this yields a similar characterization for the case of Bergman spaces. Indeed, the following result is a consequence of
[
2
, Theorems 1 and 2]
:

Theorem 2.9
.

Consider
p,q>0
and
\alpha,\beta>-1
.

(a)
Suppose that
p=q
. Then,
A^{p}_{\alpha}\subset A^{q}_{\beta}
if and only if
\alpha\leq\beta
.

(b)
Suppose that
p>q
. Then,
A^{p}_{\alpha}\subset A^{q}_{\beta}
if and only if

\dfrac{\alpha+1}{p}<\dfrac{\beta+1}{q}.

(c)
Suppose that
p<q
. Then,
A^{p}_{\alpha}\subset A^{q}_{\beta}
if and only if

\dfrac{\alpha+2}{p}\leq\dfrac{\beta+2}{q}.

2.6.
Initial remarks on Hardy and Bergman numbers.

The inclusions in the latter subsection impose several relations among the Hardy number
\h
and the Bergman numbers
\b
and
\b_{\alpha}
. Some of them have been previously noticed in
[
15
, Lemmas 2.1 and 2.2]
:

Lemma 2.10
.

(a)
Let
f
be a holomorphic map on
\mathbb{D}
. Then
\h(f)\leq\b(f)
.

(b)
Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a domain. Then
\h(D)\leq\b(D)
.

(c)
If
\mathbb{C}\setminus D
is bounded, then there exists a holomorphic function
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
such that
f\not\in A^{p}_{\alpha}
for all
p>0
,
\alpha>-1
. In particular,
\b(D)=0
.

Remark 2.11
.

It follows from Theorem
2.8
that the reverse inequality in Lemma
2.10
(a) fails: if
f\in A^{p}_{\alpha}
is such that
f\not\in H^{q}
for all
q>0
, then
\b(f)>0
and
\b_{\alpha}(f)>0
, but
\h(f)=0
. However, as already noted,
\h(f)=\b(f)
for a conformal map
f
; see
[
16
, Eq. (1.5)]
.

In a similar fashion, we can also give the following inequality:

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a domain. For all
\alpha>-1
,

\h(D)\leq\frac{\b_{\alpha}(D)}{\alpha+2}.

Proof.

If
\h(D)=0
, the inequality holds. Therefore, let us assume that
\h(D)>0
. In that case, choose
0<q<\h(D)
and a function
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
. By the definition of the Hardy number,
f\in H^{q}
. But then it follows from Theorem
2.7
that
f\in A^{p}_{\alpha}
whenever
q\geq p/(\alpha+2)
. This implies that
q\leq\b_{\alpha}(D)/(\alpha+2)
. The result follows by taking the limit as
q\to\h(D)
.
∎

The Hardy number of a hyperbolic domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
coincides with the Hardy number of its universal covering map
f_{D}\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
. A proof can be found in
[
18
, Lemma 2.1]
. The same equality holds in the Bergman case:

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a hyperbolic domain with universal covering map
f_{D}\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
. Then, for all
\alpha>-1
,

\h(D)=\h(f_{D}),\;\;\;\b(D)=\b(f_{D}),\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\b_{\alpha}(D)=\b_{\alpha}(f_{D}).

Proof.

Since both cases (
\b
and
\b_{\alpha}
) are similar, let us reason with the Bergman number
\b
. First, notice that
\b(D)\leq\b(f_{D})
by definition. Therefore, it suffices to prove that
\b(f_{D})\leq\b(g)
for all holomorphic maps
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
. This is a consequence of Littlewood Subordination Theorem
[
6
, Theorem 1.7]
and the expression of the weighted Bergman norm as a radial integral (see, for example,
[
7
, p. 77]
).
∎

There are also some relations between both Bergman numbers:

Lemma 2.14
.

(a)
Let
f
be a holomorphic map on
\mathbb{D}
. Then, for all
\alpha>-1
,

\frac{\b_{\alpha}(f)}{\alpha+2}\leq\b(f).

(b)
Let
D
be a domain in
\mathbb{C}
.
Then, for all
\alpha>-1
,

\frac{\b_{\alpha}(D)}{\alpha+2}\leq\b(D).

Proof.

(a) Arguing by contradiction, suppose that
\b(f)<\b_{\alpha}(f)/(\alpha+2)
. In that case, choose
p>0
such that
\b(f)<p/(\alpha+2)<\b_{\alpha}(f)/(\alpha+2)
. Since
\b(f)<p/(\alpha+2)
, it follows that
f\not\in A^{p}_{\alpha}
. But this contradicts the fact that
p<\b_{\alpha}(f)
.

(b) This follows from (a) and the definitions given in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
).
∎

Remark 2.15
.

The inequality in the latter result is, in general, strict. For example, consider the spaces
A^{2}_{0}
and
A^{5}_{2}
. If follows from Theorem
2.9
that
A^{5}_{2}\not\subset A^{2}_{0}
. Therefore, choose
f\in A^{5}_{2}\setminus A^{2}_{0}
. Since
f\in A^{5}_{2}
, it follows that
\b(f)\geq 5/(2+2)=5/4
. Since
f\not\in A^{2}_{0}
, we have that
\b_{0}(f)\leq 2
. So,
\b_{0}(f)/(0+2)\leq 1
. That is,
f
is an example where
\b_{0}(f)/(0+2)<\b(f)
.

2.7.
The Bloch space.

Recall that the Bloch space
\mathcal{B}
is the collection of all holomorphic maps
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
with

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}(1-\left|z\right|^{2})\left|f^{\prime}(z)\right|<+\infty.

The following result is classical. Here we use the notation
D(z,r)=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:\left|w-z\right|<r\}
for
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
r>0
.

Theorem 2.16
.

(see
[
1
]
and
[
20
, p. 35]
)

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a hyperbolic domain, and consider a universal covering map
f_{D}\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
. The following are equivalent:

(a)

\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D},D)\subset\mathcal{B}
,

(b)

f_{D}\in\mathcal{B}
,

(c)

\sup_{z\in D}R(z)<+\infty
, where
R(z)=\sup\{r>0:D(z,r)\subset D\}
.

Due to its importance, we say that a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
is a Bloch domain if it satisfies the geometric property given in condition (c). Since
\mathcal{B}\subset A^{p}_{\alpha}
for every
0<p<+\infty
and every
\alpha>-1
(see e.g.
[
7
, p. 44]
), the following result is obtained.

Corollary 2.17
.

If
D\subset\mathbb{C}
is a Bloch domain, then
\b(D)=\b_{\alpha}(D)=+\infty
for all
\alpha>-1
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section,
D\subset\mathbb{C}
denotes a Greenian domain with
0\in D
, and
f_{D}\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
denotes a universal covering map with
f_{D}(0)=0
. We will also use the integral form
\psi_{D}
of the Green function, given in (
1.1
).
We first show that
\psi_{D}
is a decreasing function (we use this term in the sense of non-strict monotonicity). This turns out to be a key property of
\psi_{D}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The function
\psi_{D}
is decreasing on
(0,+\infty)
.

Proof.

Assume first that
D
is regular for the Dirichlet problem. Then, the extended function
g_{D}(\cdot,0)
is continuous and subharmonic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. It follows (see
[
12
, Theorem 2.12]
) that
\psi_{D}
is a convex function of
\log r
,
0<r<+\infty
. Therefore, either
\psi_{D}
is decreasing or there exists
r_{0}>0
such that
g
is decreasing on
(0,r_{0})
and increasing on
(r_{0},\infty)
.

We will show that the latter case cannot occur: The point at
\infty
is either a non-boundary point of
D
(i.e.,
\infty\not\in\overline{D}
in the topology of
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}
) or a regular boundary point of
D
. Therefore, (see
[
22
, Theorem 4.4.9]
)
\lim_{z\to\infty}g_{D}(z,0)=0
. So, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem,

\lim_{r\to\infty}\psi_{D}(r)=0.

Hence
\psi_{D}
cannot be increasing on any interval
(r_{0},\infty)
. We proved that the second case cannot occur and thus
\psi_{D}
is decreasing.

We now remove the assumption that
D
is regular. Suppose only that
D
is a Greenian domain. Let
D_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be an increasing sequence of regular domains with
0\in D_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{n}=D
(a standard construction can be found, for example, in
[
17
, Section XI.14]
). Let
g_{D_{n}}
be the Green function of
D_{n}
and set
\psi_{n}(r):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}g_{D_{n}}(re^{i\theta},0)d\theta
. By
[
22
, Theorem 4.4.6]
,
\lim_{n}g_{D_{n}}(z,0)=g_{D}(z,0)
,
z\in D
. The Monotone Convergence Theorem yields
\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}(r)=\psi_{D}(r)
,
r\in(0,\infty)
. By the first part of the proof, each
\psi_{n}
is decreasing, and so
\psi_{D}
is decreasing.
∎

We now derive a characterization for the Hardy spaces to which a universal covering map belongs (cf.
[
15
, Theorem 3.1]
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
0<p<+\infty
. The following are equivalent:

(a)

f_{D}\in H^{p}
.

(b)

\int_{0}^{+\infty}r^{p-1}\psi_{D}(r)dr<+\infty.

(c)

\int_{D}\left|w\right|^{p-2}g_{D}(0,w)dA(w)<+\infty
.

Proof.

For
w\in D
, let
z_{j}(w),\;j=1,2,\dots
, be the preimages of
w
by
f_{D}
.
By Theorem
2.2
and a non-univalent change of variables (see
[
9
, Theorem 3.9]
),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left|f_{D}(z)\right|^{p-2}\left|f_{D}^{\prime}(z)\right|^{2}\log\frac{1}{\left|z\right|}dA(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}\left|w\right|^{p-2}\sum_{j}\log\frac{1}{\left|z_{j}(w)\right|}dA(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}\left|w\right|^{p-2}\sum_{j}g_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{j}(w))dA(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}\left|w\right|^{p-2}g_{D}(0,w)dA(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}r^{p-1}\psi_{D}(r)dr.

The result is, then, a direct consequence of Theorem
2.4
.
∎

As a consequence of the latter characterization, we now prove one of our main results (cf.
[
15
, Theorem 1.2]
).

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The equality
\h(f_{D})=\h(D)
can be found in
[
18
, Lemma 2.1.(5)]
.

For the rest of the proof, in order to ease the notation, set

p=\liminf_{r\to+\infty}\left(-\dfrac{\log\psi_{D}(r)}{\log r}\right).

We start by showing that
p\leq\h(D)
. If
p=0
, this is trivial. Suppose that
p>0
and choose
0<q<p
and
\epsilon>0
. By the definition of
p
there exists
R>0
such that

-\dfrac{\log\psi_{D}(r)}{\log r}\geq p-\epsilon,\quad\text{for all }r>R.

In that case,
\psi_{D}(r)\leq r^{\epsilon-p}
for all
r>R
. Therefore,

\int_{0}^{+\infty}r^{q-1}\psi_{D}(r)dr\leq\int_{0}^{R}r^{q-1}\psi_{D}(r)dr+\int_{R}^{+\infty}r^{q-p+\epsilon-1}dr.

In the right hand term, the first integral converges because
\psi_{D}(r)+2\pi\log(r)
remains bounded as
r\to 0^{+}
, which follows the definition of the Green function
g_{D}
. The second integral converges if
0<q<p-\epsilon
. As a result, it follows from Lemma
3.2
that
f_{D}\in H^{q}
for all
0<q<p-\epsilon
. Letting
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, we deduce that
p\leq\h(D)
.

To complete the proof, we argue by contradiction. Suppose
p<\h(D)
. Choose some
p<q<\h(D)
. By definition,
f_{D}\in H^{q}
. Therefore, by Lemma
3.2
,

\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{q-1}\psi_{D}(t)dt<+\infty.

In particular, by Lemma
3.1
, for
r>0
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\dfrac{r^{q}}{q}\psi_{D}(r)

\displaystyle=\psi_{D}(r)\int_{0}^{r}t^{q-1}dt\leq\int_{0}^{r}t^{q-1}\psi_{D}(t)dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{q-1}\psi_{D}(t)dt<+\infty.

This means that there exists
C>0
such that
r^{q}\psi_{D}(r)\leq C
for all
r>0
. Thus,

\liminf_{r\to+\infty}\left(-\dfrac{\log\psi_{D}(r)}{\log r}\right)\geq q,

which yields
p\geq q
, a contradiction.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Let us commence with the proof of the first part of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.(a).

Assume first that
0<p<+\infty
. By
[
5
, Theorem 5.2]
, there exists a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
such that
\h(\Omega)=p
. Set
A=\{m+in:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Consider the domain
D=\Omega\setminus A
. Since
A
is a polar set (see
[
18
, Theorem 2.4]
),
\h(D)=\h(\Omega)=p
.

On the other hand,
D
does not contain arbitrarily large disks, and therefore it is a Bloch domain; see Theorem
2.16
. Hence, from Corollary
2.17
, we have that
\b(D)=\b_{\alpha}(D)=+\infty
,
\alpha>-1
.

If
p=0
, we repeat the same argument as above with
\Omega=\mathbb{C}
.
∎

Now, for the proof of the second part of Theorem
1.2
, we construct a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
with the following three properties:
(i)
D
is regular, (ii)
\b(D)=\b_{\alpha}(D)=+\infty
,
\alpha>-1
, and (iii)
\h(D)=1/2
.
The domain
D
is of the form
D=\mathbb{C}\setminus((-\infty,-1]\cup C)
, where
C
is a collection of non-trivial circular arcs (i.e., none of them is a singleton). In particular, by
[
22
, Theorem 4.2.2]
,
D
is a regular domain (notice that infinity, considered as a point on the boundary of
D
, is also regular). The arcs on
C
will be located so that
D
is a Bloch domain; see Theorem
2.16
. Once more, by Corollary
2.17
, this assures that
\b(D)=\b_{\alpha}(D)=+\infty
,
\alpha>-1
, at once. The main technical work comes from showing that the length of the arcs of
C
can be chosen small enough so that
\h(D)=1/2
(recall that
\h(\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,-1])=1/2
).

For the construction, we will need some lemmas for harmonic measure. For instance, we will strongly use the following continuity argument as a way to obtain lower estimates for harmonic measure.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
R>0
, and consider the domain
\Omega=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|<R\}\setminus(-R,-1]
. Let
K\subset\Omega
be a compact set such that
\Omega\setminus K
is a regular domain for the Dirichlet problem. Let
\{K_{\delta}\}_{\delta\in(0,1]}
be a family of compact subsets of
\Omega\setminus K
with the following properties:

(i)
The family is increasing. That is,
K_{\delta}\subset K_{\delta^{\prime}}
if and only if
\delta\leq\delta^{\prime}
.

(ii)

K^{*}:=\cap_{\delta\in(0,1]}K_{\delta}
is a finite set.

For
\delta\in(0,1]
, consider the domain
\Omega_{\delta}:=\Omega\setminus(K\cup K_{\delta})
, and define

\omega_{\delta}(z):=\omega(z,C_{R},\Omega_{\delta}),\qquad z\in\Omega_{\delta},

where
C_{R}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|=R\}
. Then, for every
z\in\Omega^{*}:=\Omega\setminus(K\cup K^{*})
we have that

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\omega_{\delta}(z)=\omega(z,C_{R},\Omega\setminus K).

Proof.

Let
\delta>0
, and assume that
z\in\Omega_{\delta}
. By the maximum principle we see that
\omega_{\delta_{1}}(z)\geq\omega_{\delta_{2}}(z)
whenever
0<\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{2}\leq\delta
. Therefore, since the harmonic maps
\omega_{\delta}
are uniformly bounded by
1
, we see that the function

(4.1)

\omega^{*}(z)=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\omega_{\delta}(z)

is well-defined and it is harmonic on
\Omega_{\delta}
.

Moreover, since
\delta>0
is arbitrary, we see that
\omega^{*}
is well-defined and harmonic on
\Omega^{*}
. But, since it is bounded by above and below, the singularities of
\omega^{*}
on
K^{*}
are removable. Therefore, we can define
\omega^{*}
on
K^{*}
so that it is a harmonic map on
\Omega\setminus K
.

By the boundary properties of harmonic measure given in
[
22
, Theorem 4.3.4]
and the regularity of
\Omega\setminus K
it is easy to see that

\lim_{z\to\xi}\omega^{*}(z)=1,\qquad\xi\in C_{R}\setminus\{-R\},

\lim_{z\to\xi}\omega^{*}(z)=0,\qquad\xi\in\partial(\Omega\setminus K)\setminus C_{R}.

Therefore, by the uniqueness property in
[
22
, Theorem 4.3.4]
,

(4.2)

\omega^{*}(z)=\omega(z,C_{R},\Omega\setminus K),\qquad z\in\Omega\setminus K.

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
0<R_{1}<R
and
S=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|=R,\,\mathrm{Re}(z)\geq 0\}
.
There exists a constant
C=C(R_{1},R)>0
with the following property: If
R_{2}>R
,
\Omega=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:R_{1}<\left|z\right|<R_{2}\}\setminus(-R_{2},-R_{1})
,
T=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|=R_{2}\}
, and
z\in S
, then

(4.3)

\omega(z,T,\Omega)\geq\dfrac{C}{\sqrt{R_{2}}}.

Proof.

Assume first that
R_{2}<2R
.
Set

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{z\in\mathbb{C}:R_{1}<\left|z\right|<2R\}\setminus(-2R,-R_{1}),

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\frac{R_{1}}{2R}<\left|z\right|<1\right\}\setminus\left(-1,-\frac{R_{1}}{2R}\right).

By a polarization inequality (see Theorem
2.1
.(b)), the maximum principle, and the conformal invariance of harmonic measure, for every
z\in S
,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\omega(z,T,\Omega)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\omega(iR,T,\Omega)\geq\omega(iR,\{|z|=2R\},\Omega_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega(\frac{i}{2},\partial\mathbb{D},\Omega_{2}).

It follows that
\omega(z,T,\Omega)
is bounded below by a positive constant depending
only on
R
and
R_{1}
; so (
4.3
) holds.

Suppose next that
R_{2}\geq 2R
. By Harnack’s inequality
[
22
, Corollary 1.3.3]
and the domain monotonicity of Harnack’s constant
[
22
, Corollary 1.3.7]
, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
depending only on
R
and
R_{1}
so that

(4.5)

\omega(z,T,\Omega)\geq\omega(iR,T,\Omega)\geq C_{1}\;\omega(R,T,\Omega),\;\;\;z\in S.

Set

\displaystyle\Omega_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\setminus(\{|z|\leq R_{1}\}\cup(-\infty,-R_{1})),

\displaystyle\Omega_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}\setminus(-1,0).

By the maximum principle and conformal invariance,

(4.6)

\omega(R,T,\Omega)\geq\omega(R,(-\infty,-R_{2}],\Omega_{3})=\omega\left(\frac{R_{1}}{R},\left[-\frac{R_{1}}{R_{2}},0\right],\Omega_{4}\right).

To estimate the latter harmonic measure, we use the Koebe function and a square root map. It follows easily that there exists a constant
C_{2}=C_{2}(R_{1},R)
such that

(4.7)

\omega\left(\frac{R_{1}}{R},\left[-\frac{R_{1}}{R_{2}},0\right],\Omega_{4}\right)\geq\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{R_{2}}}.

Combining (
4.5
)-(
4.7
), we see that (
4.3
) is true.
∎

We will also use the following result dealing with symmetry.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
R>1
, and consider the domain
\Omega=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|<R\}\setminus(-R,-1]
. Let
K\subset\Omega
be a compact set which is symmetric with respect to the imaginary axis. Assume
0\notin K
. Define
C_{R}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|=R\}
and
C_{R}^{+}=C_{R}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{Re}(z)>0\}
. It holds that

\omega(0,C_{R}^{+},\Omega\setminus K)\geq\dfrac{1}{2}\omega(0,C_{R},\Omega\setminus K).

Proof.

The result follows from a polarization argument; we use Theorem
2.1
(a) to obtain

\omega(0,C_{R}^{-},\Omega\setminus K)\leq\omega(0,C_{R}^{+},\Omega\setminus K),

from which the result follows easily.
∎

The following lemma will be one of the main arguments in our construction.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
0<R_{1}<R
and
K\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|\leq R_{1}\}
be a compact set which is symmetric with respect to the imaginary axis. There exists a constant
C=C(R_{1},R)>0
with the following property: If
R\leq r\leq R_{2}
and

\Omega_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|<r\}\setminus((-r,-1]\cup K),\qquad C_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|=r\},

then

\omega(0,C_{R_{2}},\Omega_{R_{2}})\geq\dfrac{C}{\sqrt{R_{2}}}\omega(0,C_{R},\Omega_{R}).

Proof.

The proof is a combination of the Markov Property for harmonic measure (
2.1
) and Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
.

More precisely, by the Markov Property (
2.1
),

(4.8)

\displaystyle\omega(0,C_{R_{2}},\Omega_{R_{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{C_{R}}\omega(\alpha,C_{R_{2}},\Omega_{R_{2}})\omega(0,d\alpha,\Omega_{R})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{C_{R}^{+}}\omega(\alpha,C_{R_{2}},\Omega_{R_{2}})\omega(0,d\alpha,\Omega_{R})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{\alpha\in C_{R}^{+}}\omega(\alpha,C_{R_{2}},\Omega_{R_{2}})\omega(0,C_{R}^{+},\Omega_{R}),

where
C_{R}^{+}=C_{R}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{Re}(z)>0\}
. From Lemma
4.3
we see that

\omega(0,C_{R}^{+},\Omega_{R})\geq\dfrac{1}{2}\omega(0,C_{R},\Omega_{R}).

Let us also consider

\Omega=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:R_{1}<\left|z\right|<R_{2}\}\setminus(-R_{2},-R_{1}).

Using the Maximum Principle we see that

\inf_{\alpha\in C_{R}^{+}}\omega(\alpha,C_{R_{2}},\Omega_{R_{2}})\geq\inf_{\alpha\in C_{R}^{+}}\omega(\alpha,C_{R_{2}},\Omega).

From this, the proof follows immediately using Lemma
4.2
.
∎

We are now ready to present the construction in Theorem
1.2
.(b). The domain
D
is given by

D=D(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\ldots)=\mathbb{C}\setminus\left((-\infty,-1]\cup\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}C(n,\alpha_{n})\right),

where

C(n,\alpha)=\bigcup_{j=0}^{2n-1}I(n,\alpha,j),\qquad I(n,\alpha,j)=\{np(n,j)e^{i\theta}:\left|\theta\right|\leq\alpha\},

In other words, the complement of the domain
D
is given by the half-line
(-\infty,-1]
and by a collection of circular arcs. More precisely, given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the complement of
D
contains
2n
circular arcs of radius
n
and center zero. The midpoint of these arcs are uniformly distributed following the
2n
-th roots of unity, and its angular width is controlled by the coefficient
\alpha_{n}
.

Domain
D
for some choice of the coefficients

.

From this, we see that

.

Proof of Theorem

.(b).

We introduce a useful notation. Given

D(r;\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}):=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|<r\}\setminus\left((-r,-1]\cup\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}C(k,\alpha_{k})\right).

As usual, we will also use the notation
C_{r}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|=r\}

.

The coefficients
\{\alpha_{n}\}
are constructed inductively. Namely, let

be such that

\omega(0,C_{2},D(2;0))=\omega(0,C_{2},\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|z\right|<2\}\setminus(-2,-1])=\dfrac{A}{\sqrt{2}\log(2)}.

Using Lemma
4.1
(with
K=\emptyset
and

) we can find
\alpha_{1}>0
so that

\omega(0,C_{2},D(2;\alpha_{1}))\geq\dfrac{A}{2\sqrt{2}\log(2)}.

Now, assume that we have found
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}>0
and

so that

\omega(0,C_{r},D(r;\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}))\geq\dfrac{A}{2\sqrt{r}\log(r)}.

Using Lemma
4.4
we see that there exists
B=B(n,r)>0
such that

\omega(0,C_{R},D(R;\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}))\geq\dfrac{B}{\sqrt{R}},\qquad R\geq r.

In particular, it is clear that

\lim_{R\to\infty}\log(R)\sqrt{R}\omega(0,C_{R},D(R;\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}))=+\infty.

Thus, we can find
R>n+1
such that

\omega(0,C_{R},D(R;\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}))\geq\dfrac{A}{\sqrt{R}\log(R)}.

In that case, let
m=\sup(\{k\in\mathbb{N}:k<R\})
, and notice that
m\geq n+1
. Using Lemma
4.1
(here, we use

K=\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}C(k,\alpha_{k}),\qquad K_{\delta}=\bigcup_{k=n+1}^{m}C(k,\delta))

we can find
\alpha_{n+1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}>0
such that

\omega(0,C_{R},D(R;\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}))\geq\dfrac{A}{2\sqrt{R}\log(R)}.

By the construction, it is clear that we can find
\{\alpha_{n}>0\}
and
\{r_{n_{k}}>0\}
with
n_{k}<r_{n_{k}}\leq n_{k}+1
such that

(4.9)

\omega(0,C_{r_{n_{k}}},D(r_{n_{k}};\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n_{k}}))\geq\dfrac{A}{2\sqrt{r_{n_{k}}}\log(r_{n_{k}})}.

Let
D:=D(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\ldots)
. We know that
\h(D)\geq 1/2
. By (
4.9
) and the Essén-Kim-Sugawa formula (see Subsection
2.4
), we conclude that
\h(D)=1/2
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Let
p\in[1/2,+\infty)
. Modifying the geometry of the latter proof (i.e., replacing
C\setminus(-\infty,-1]
by some angular region), one could argue that there exists a regular Bloch domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
for which
\h(D)=p
.

5.
Properties of domains in the class
\mathcal{D}

In this section, for a domain
D
in the class
\mathcal{D}
, we will use the notation set in Definition
1.3
:
F
is the union of the bounded components of
\mathbb{C}_{\infty}\setminus D
and
\Omega
is the simply connected domain
D\cup F
.

To begin, we notice the following fact: if
\Omega=\mathbb{C}
, the set
\mathbb{C}\setminus D
is bounded. So, by Lemma
2.10
,

\h(D)=\h(\Omega)=\b(D)=\b(\Omega)=0
.
It follows from Lemma
2.14
that
\b_{\alpha}(D)=0
. Since
\Omega=\mathbb{C}
, we also have
\b_{\alpha}(\Omega)=0
.

From now on, we assume that
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{C}
.
For
r>0
, we set
D_{r}=\{w\in D:|w|>r\}
and
A_{r}=\{w\in D:|w|=r\}
. It follows from Definition
1.3
and the assumption
\Omega\subsetneq\mathbb{C}
that there exists
R=R(D)>0
such that

(i)
F\subset\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|w|<R-1\}
,

(ii) for every
r\geq R
,
\partial D\cap\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|w|=r\}\neq\varnothing
,

(iii) for every
r\geq R
,
A_{r}
is a single arc of the circle
\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|w|=r\}
.

In the sequel,
R
will be this constant associated with the domain
D
. Now, we will prove several properties concerning those domains
D\in\mathcal{D}
for which
\Omega\neq\mathbb{C}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{D}
with
\Omega\neq\mathbb{C}
. There exist constants
\sigma>\rho>R
with the following properties:

(a)
If
a\in A_{\rho}
, then
\omega(a,A_{R},D_{R})<\frac{1}{4}
.

(b)
If
\zeta\in A_{\rho}
and
w\in A_{\sigma}
, then
\rho_{\Omega}(\zeta,w)>\frac{\log 2}{2}
.

Proof.

(a) By a classical symmetrization theorem of Baernstein
[
11
, Theorem 9.5]
, for every
r>R
and every
a\in A_{r}
,

\omega(a,A_{R},D_{R})\leq\omega(r,\{z:|z|=R\},\Omega^{*}),

where
\Omega^{*}=\mathbb{C}\setminus(\{z:|z|\leq R\}\cup(-\infty,-R))
. Since

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\omega(r,\{z:|z|=R\},\Omega^{*})=0,

we can find
\rho>R
large enough so that for
a\in A_{\rho}
,
\omega(a,A_{R},D_{R})<\frac{1}{4}
.

(b) Let
\rho
be the constant we found in (a). By a classical symmetrization theorem of Weitsman
[
11
, Theorem 9.16]
, if
s>\rho
,
\zeta\in A_{\rho}
and
w\in A_{s}
, then
\rho_{\Omega}(\zeta,w)\geq\rho_{\tilde{\Omega}}(\rho,s)
, where

\tilde{\Omega}=\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,-R]
. Since

\lim_{s\to+\infty}\rho_{\tilde{\Omega}}(\rho,s)=+\infty,

we can find
\sigma>\rho
large enough so that

\rho_{\Omega}(\zeta,w)>\frac{\log 2}{2},\;\;\;\zeta\in A_{\rho},\;w\in A_{\sigma}.

∎

In the sequel,
\sigma,\rho
will be these constants associated with the domain
D
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{D}
with
\Omega\neq\mathbb{C}
. There exists a closed arc
K\subset A_{\rho}
with the following property: If
\zeta,w\in\Omega
,
|\zeta|\leq R
,
|w|\geq\sigma
, and
\gamma
is the hyperbolic geodesic arc for
\Omega
connecting
\zeta
and
w
, then
\gamma\cap A_{\rho}\subset K
.

Proof.

First we note that every hyperbolic geodesic arc
\gamma
for
\Omega
connecting the points
\zeta,w\in\Omega
,
|\zeta|\leq R
,
|w|\geq\sigma
, has a closed subarc in
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:R\leq|z|\leq\sigma\}\cap\Omega
connecting two points
\zeta_{1}\in A_{R}
,
w_{1}\in A_{\sigma}
.

Next, suppose that the conclusion of the lemma is not true. Then we can find sequences
(\zeta_{n})
,
(w_{n})
in
\Omega
with the properties:

(a)
|\zeta_{n}|=R
,
|w_{n}|=\sigma
,

(b) If
\gamma_{n}
is the hyperbolic geodesic arc for
\Omega
connecting
\zeta_{n}
with
w_{n}
,
n=1,2,\dots
, then there exists
a_{n}\in\gamma_{n}\cap A_{\rho}
with
a_{n}\to a_{\infty}\in\partial\Omega
.

Let
f
be a Riemann map of
\mathbb{D}
onto
\Omega
. Let
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
,
\tilde{\zeta}_{n}
,
\tilde{w}_{n}
,
\tilde{a}_{n}
,
\tilde{A}_{R}
,
\tilde{A}_{\rho}
,
\tilde{A}_{\sigma}
be the images of
\gamma_{n}
,
\zeta_{n}
,
w_{n}
,
a_{n}
,
A_{R}
,
A_{\rho}
,
A_{\sigma}
, respectively, under
f^{-1}
.
By a classical theorem for conformal maps (see e.g.
[
4
, Proposition 3.3.3]
), the curves
A_{R}
,
A_{\rho}
,
A_{\sigma}
are crosscuts of
\mathbb{D}
having six distinct endpoints on
\partial\mathbb{D}
. By passing to subsequences, we may assume that

(5.1)

\tilde{a}_{n}\to\tilde{a}_{\infty}\in\partial\mathbb{D},\;\;\tilde{\zeta}_{n}\to\tilde{\zeta}_{\infty}\in\overline{\mathbb{D}},\;\;\tilde{w}_{n}\to\tilde{w}_{\infty}\in\overline{\mathbb{D}},\;\;\hbox{as}\;\;n\to\infty.

The curves
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
are arcs of circles orthogonal to
\partial\mathbb{D}
and they join the points
\tilde{\zeta}_{n}
,
\tilde{w}_{n}
. It follows that

\tilde{\gamma}_{n}\to\tilde{\gamma}_{\infty}
(in the Hausdorff metric), where

\tilde{\gamma}_{\infty}
is an arc of a circle orthogonal to
\partial\mathbb{D}
joining the points
\tilde{\zeta}_{\infty}
,
\tilde{w}_{\infty}
. Since

\tilde{a}_{n}\to\tilde{a}_{\infty}
and
\tilde{a}_{n}\in\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
, we have
\tilde{a}_{\infty}\in\tilde{\gamma}_{\infty}\cap\partial\mathbb{D}
. Therefore, either
\tilde{\zeta}_{\infty}\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and
\tilde{a}_{\infty}=\tilde{\zeta}_{\infty}
or
\tilde{w}_{\infty}\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and
\tilde{a}_{\infty}=\tilde{w}_{\infty}
. It follows that in the first case
\tilde{\zeta}_{\infty}
is an endpoint of
\tilde{A}_{R}
and in the second case that
\tilde{w}_{\infty}
is an endpoint of
\tilde{A}_{\sigma}
. Noting that

\tilde{a}_{\infty}
is an endpoint of
\tilde{A}_{\rho}
and recalling that all these endpoints are distinct, we arrive at a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{D}
with
\Omega\neq\mathbb{C}
. Let
a
be the midpoint of the arc
A_{\rho}
. There exists a constant
C=C(D)
such that for every
w\in D_{\sigma}
,
g_{\Omega}(a,w)\leq C\,g_{D_{R}}(a,w)
.

Proof.

Let
w\in D_{\sigma}
. Let
\zeta_{w}\in A_{R}
be such that

(5.2)

g_{\Omega}(\zeta_{w},w)=\max_{\zeta\in A_{R}}g_{\Omega}(\zeta,w).

Let
a_{o}
be a point on
A_{\rho}
such that

(5.3)

\omega_{o}:=\omega(a_{o},A_{R},D_{R})=\max_{z\in A_{\rho}}\omega(z,A_{R},D_{R}).

Note that
\omega_{o}
is a constant depending only on
D
and that
\omega_{o}<1/4
, by Lemma
5.1
.
Consider the hyperbolic geodesic arc
\gamma_{w}
for
\Omega
joining
\zeta_{w},w
. Let
a_{w}
be a point on
A_{\rho}\cap\gamma_{w}
. By the Strong Markov Property (
2.2
), (
5.2
), and (
5.3
),

(5.4)

\displaystyle g_{\Omega}(a_{w},w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{D_{R}}(a_{w},w)+\int_{A_{R}}g_{\Omega}(\zeta,w)\omega(a_{w},d\zeta,D_{R})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle g_{D_{R}}(a_{w},w)+\omega_{o}\;g_{\Omega}(\zeta_{w},w).

Because of Lemma
2.3
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle g_{\Omega}(\zeta_{w},w)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4e^{-2\rho_{\Omega}(\zeta_{w},w)}\leq 4e^{-2\rho_{\Omega}(a_{w},w)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4g_{\Omega}(a_{w},w).

Hence (
5.4
) gives

(5.6)

g_{\Omega}(a_{w},w)\leq C_{1}\,g_{D_{R}}(a_{w},w),\;\;\;C_{1}=C_{1}(D).

By Lemma
5.2
, the point
a_{w}
lies on a compact set
K=K(D)\subset A_{\rho}
. Therefore, by Harnack’s theorem, there exist constants depending only on
D
so that

(5.7)

\displaystyle g_{\Omega}(a,w)\leq C_{2}g_{\Omega}(a_{w},w)\leq C_{3}g_{D_{R}}(a_{w},w)\leq C_{4}g_{D_{R}}(a,w).

∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{D}
with
\Omega\neq\mathbb{C}
such that
0\in D
. There exists a constant
C=C(D)
such that for every
w\in D_{\sigma}
,
g_{D}(0,w)\leq C\,e^{-2\rho_{D}(0,w)}
.

Proof.

Let
a
be the midpoint of the arc
A_{\rho}
. By the domain monotonicity of the Green function, Harnack’s theorem, Lemma
5.3
, Lemma
2.3
, and the domain monotonicity of the hyperbolic distance, there exist constants depending only on
D
so that for every
w\in D_{\sigma}
,

(5.8)

\displaystyle g_{D}(0,w)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle g_{\Omega}(0,w)\leq C_{1}g_{\Omega}(a,w)\leq C_{2}g_{D_{R}}(a,w)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{3}e^{-2\rho_{D_{R}}(a,w)}\leq C_{3}e^{-2\rho_{D}(a,w)}.

Using the triangle inequality
\rho_{D}(0,w)\leq\rho_{D}(0,a)+\rho_{D}(a,w)
, we obtain

(5.9)

g_{D}(0,w)\leq C_{3}e^{2\rho_{D}(0,a)}e^{-2\rho_{D}(0,w)}.

Since
e^{2\rho_{D}(0,a)}
is a constant depending only on
D
, the proof is complete.
∎

As a result of the latter lemmas, we provide the following property.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{D}
with
\Omega\neq\mathbb{C}
such that
0\in D
. Let
f_{D}
be a universal covering map of
\mathbb{D}
onto
D
with
f_{D}(0)=0
. For
w\in D_{\sigma}
, denote by
z_{j}(w),j=1,2,\dots
the preimages of
w
under
f_{D}
with
\left|z_{1}(w)\right|\leq\left|z_{2}(w)\right|\leq\dots
. Then there exists a positive constant
C
depending only on
D
such that

g_{D}(0,w)\leq C\,g_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{1}(w)).

Proof.

By Lemmas
5.4
and
2.3
,

(5.10)

g_{D}(0,w)\leq Ce^{-2\rho_{D}(0,w)}=Ce^{-2\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{1}(w))}\leq C\,g_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{1}(w)).

∎

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a Greenian domain with
0\in D
, and let
f_{D}\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
be a universal covering map with
f_{D}(0)=0
. Assume that
D
satisfies the following property: there exist
R=R(D)>0
and
C=C(D)>0
such that

(6.1)

g_{D}(0,w)\leq C\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{D}\\
f(z)=w\end{subarray}}g_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z),\qquad\text{for all }w\in D\text{ with }\left|w\right|>R.

Notice that, by Proposition
5.5
, domains
D\in\mathcal{D}
with
\Omega\neq\mathbb{C}
satisfy (
6.1
). For these domains, the following result holds.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a domain satisfying (
6.1
). Let
0<p<+\infty
and
\alpha>-1
, be such that
f_{D}\in A^{p}_{\alpha}
. Then

(6.2)

\int_{0}^{+\infty}r^{p-1}\psi_{D}(r)^{\alpha+2}dr<+\infty.

Proof.

Let
R=R(D)>0
and
C=C(D)>0
be the constants in (
6.1
). Let
D_{R}=\{z\in\mathbb{D}:\left|z\right|>R\}
. By Theorem
2.6
, arguing as in the proof of Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle+\infty

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left|f_{D}(z)\right|^{p-2}\left|f_{D}^{\prime}(z)\right|^{2}\log(1/\left|z\right|)^{\alpha+2}dA(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}\left|w\right|^{p-2}\left(\sum_{j}\log(1/\left|z_{j}(w)\right|)^{\alpha+2}\right)dA(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}\left|w\right|^{p-2}\left(\sum_{j}g_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{j}(w))^{\alpha+2}\right)dA(w)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{D}\left|w\right|^{p-2}g_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{1}(w))^{\alpha+2}dA(w),

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{D_{R}}\left|w\right|^{p-2}g_{\mathbb{D}}(0,z_{1}(w))^{\alpha+2}dA(w),

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C^{\alpha+2}}\int_{D_{R}}\left|w\right|^{p-2}g_{D}(0,w)^{\alpha+2}dA(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C^{\alpha+2}}\int_{R}^{+\infty}r^{p-1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}g_{D}(0,re^{i\theta})^{\alpha+2}d\theta dr,

where
\left|z_{1}(w)\right|\leq\left|z_{2}(w)\right|\leq\ldots
are the preimages of
w
by
f_{D}
.

Since
\alpha+2>1
, the function
t\mapsto t^{\alpha+2}
is convex. Jensen’s inequality
[
22
, Theorem 2.6.2]
assures that for
r>0
,

\left(\dfrac{\psi_{D}(r)}{2\pi}\right)^{\alpha+2}=\left(\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}g_{D}(0,re^{i\theta})d\theta\right)^{\alpha+2}\leq\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}g_{D}(0,re^{i\theta})^{\alpha+2}d\theta.

It follows that

(6.3)

\int_{R}^{+\infty}r^{p-1}\psi_{D}(r)^{\alpha+2}dr<+\infty.

By the definition of the Green function, there exists a constant
M=M(D,R)>0
such that

g_{D}(0,w)\leq\log\frac{1}{|w|}+M,\;\;\;w\in D,\;\;|w|\leq R.

Hence

(6.4)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}r^{p-1}\psi_{D}(r)^{\alpha+2}dr\leq\int_{0}^{R}r^{p-1}(\log\frac{1}{r}+M)^{\alpha+2}dr<+\infty.

All in all, (
6.2
) follows from (
6.3
) and (
6.4
).
∎

The latter lemma assures some equalities between Hardy numbers and Bergman numbers.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a Greenian domain satisfying (
6.1
). Then,

\h(D)=\h(f_{D})=\b(D)=\b(f_{D})=\dfrac{\b_{\alpha}(D)}{\alpha+2}=\dfrac{\b_{\alpha}(f_{D})}{\alpha+2},\qquad\alpha>-1.

Proof.

By Lemmas
2.10
,
2.12
,
2.13
, and
2.14
, it suffices to show that
\b(f_{D})\leq\h(D)
. Notice that the inequality trivially holds if
\b(f_{D})=0
. In other case, suppose that
f_{D}\in A^{p}_{\alpha}
. By Lemma
6.1
,

\int_{0}^{+\infty}r^{p-1}\psi_{D}(r)^{\alpha+2}dr<+\infty.

By Lemma
3.1
, similarly as in (
3.1
), it is possible to deduce that there exists
C>0
such that
r^{p}\psi_{D}(r)^{\alpha+2}\leq C
for all
r>0
. Then, by Theorem
1.1
,

\h(D)=\liminf_{r\to+\infty}\left(-\dfrac{\log\psi_{D}(r)}{\log r}\right)\geq\dfrac{p}{\alpha+2}.

This yields, by definition,
\h(D)\geq\b(f_{D})
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

Notice that (
6.1
) is trivially satisfied whenever
D
is a simply connected domain, since Green functions are conformally invariant. Therefore, the latter result is a generalization of the result for simply connected domains due to Karafyllia and Karamanlis in
[
16
]
.

We are now in position to prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

In the beginning of Section
5
we justified that, if
\Omega=\mathbb{C}
, then

\h(D)=\b(D)=\h(\Omega)=\b(\Omega)=\dfrac{\b_{\alpha}(D)}{\alpha+2}=\dfrac{\b_{\alpha}(\Omega)}{\alpha+2}=0.

If
D
is hyperbolic, by Lemma
2.13
, the latter equalities imply that

\h(f_{D})=\b(f_{D})=\dfrac{\b_{\alpha}(f_{D})}{\alpha+2}=0.

Let us now assume that
\Omega\neq\mathbb{C}
. Then,
D
is a Greenian domain. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
0\in D
. By Proposition
5.5
,
D
satisfies (
6.1
). In particular, from Lemma
6.2
we know that

\h(D)=\b(D)=\b(f_{D})=\dfrac{\b_{\alpha}(D)}{\alpha+2}=\dfrac{\b_{\alpha}(f_{D})}{\alpha+2},\qquad\alpha>-1.

Our next goal is to prove the inequality

(6.5)

\h(D)\leq\h(\Omega).

Let
0<p<+\infty
, and let
f_{D}\colon\mathbb{D}\to D
be a universal covering map with
f_{D}(0)=0
. Assume that
f_{D}\in H^{p}
. Then Lemma
3.2
implies that

(6.6)

\int_{D}\left|w\right|^{p-2}g_{D}(0,w)dA(w)<+\infty.

We will use the positive constants
R,\rho,\sigma
associated with
D
, which we have previously defined in Section
5
. Let
a
be the midpoint of the arc
A_{\rho}
. By Harnack’s inequality, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
(depending only on
D
) such that

(6.7)

g_{D}(a,w)\leq C_{1}g_{D}(0,w),\;\;\;w\in D_{\sigma}.

So the inclusion
D_{R}\subset D
and (
6.6
) yield

(6.8)

\int_{D_{\sigma}}\left|w\right|^{p-2}g_{D_{R}}(a,w)dA(w)<+\infty.

By Lemma
5.3
, there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
(depending only on
D
) such that for every
w\in D_{\sigma}
,
g_{\Omega}(a,w)\leq C_{2}\,g_{D_{R}}(a,w)
.
So (
6.8
) gives

(6.9)

\int_{D_{\sigma}}\left|w\right|^{p-2}g_{\Omega}(a,w)dA(w)<+\infty.

Using again an argument we have used in the proof of Lemma
6.1
(for the Green function near its pole), we infer that

(6.10)

\int_{\Omega}\left|w\right|^{p-2}g_{\Omega}(a,w)dA(w)<+\infty.

By Harnack’s inequality, this implies that

(6.11)

\int_{\Omega}\left|w\right|^{p-2}g_{\Omega}(0,w)dA(w)<+\infty.

Therefore (see Lemma
3.2
),
f_{\Omega}\in H^{p}
.
So, we have proved that
f_{D}\in H^{p}
implies
f_{\Omega}\in H^{p}
. It follows from the definition of the Hardy number that
\h(f_{D})\leq\h(f_{\Omega})
. Then, by Lemma
2.13
,
\h(D)=\h(\Omega)
and (
6.5
) has been proved.

Note that, since
\Omega
is simply connected, we have
\h(\Omega)=\b(\Omega)
.
Hence, we obtain

(6.12)

\b(\Omega)\leq\b(D)=\h(D)\leq\h(\Omega)=\b(\Omega).

The proof is completed by using (
6.12
) and Lemmas
2.12
and
2.14
.
∎

7.
Open problems

A basic open problem concerning the Hardy number and the Bergman number is the following:

Problem 7.1
.

Find geometric-topological conditions on a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}

that are necessary and sufficient for the equality
\b(D)=\h(D)
.

Proposition
5.5
states that domains
D\in\mathcal{D}
with
\Omega\neq\mathbb{C}
satisfy the condition
6.1
. This turns out to be fundamental in the proof of Theorem
1.4
. For this reason, we state the following:

Problem 7.2
.

Find geometric-topological conditions on a Greenian domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
that are necessary and sufficient for the condition (
6.1
), or for the equivalent inequality

(7.1)

g_{D}(z,w)\leq C(D)\,e^{-2\rho_{D}(z,w)},\;\;\;\;\;\;z,w\in D,\;\rho_{D}(z,w)\geq 1.

The examples of domains we studied suggest that the Hardy and Bergman numbers depend on the geometry of the domain near infinity. Here is a related problem:

Problem 7.3
.

Let
D_{1},D_{2}\subset\mathbb{C}
be domains. Suppose that there exists
R>0
such that

D_{1}\cap\{z:|z|>R\}=D_{2}\cap\{z:|z|>R\}.

Is it true that
\h(D_{1})=\h(D_{2})
? Is it true that
\b(D_{1})=\b(D_{2})
? For
\alpha>-1
, is it true that
\b_{\alpha}(D_{1})=\b_{\alpha}(D_{2})
?

To end, we ask for a construction of a domain whose Bergman number is finite and different from its Hardy number.

Problem 7.4
.

Is there a domain
