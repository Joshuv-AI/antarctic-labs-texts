RETRACTED ARTICLE: A novel approach integrating topological deep learning from EEG Data in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Miriam Esteve, Alejandro Martinez-Gracia, Jesús J Rodríguez-Sala, Jorge R Brotons-Mas, Antonio Falcó
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-throughput analysis of EEG data has significantly contributed to understanding neural dynamics in Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis. However, the complexity and high dimensionality of EEG signals pose challenges for traditional classification methods, which often fail to capture intricate patterns. To address this, we propose a hybrid approach integrating Topological Deep Learning (TDL) with machine learning models-including Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Neural Networks (NN), and Logistic Regression (LR)-for Alzheimer’s disease classification. By leveraging TDL, our method extracts topological and neural features from EEG data, enhancing the identification of disease-specific patterns that conventional models may overlook. The dataset consists of EEG recordings from 88 individuals, categorized into AD patients, FTD patients, and CN, providing a robust foundation for model evaluation. Our findings demonstrate that NN augmented by TDL achieve the highest classification accuracy, reaching up to 90% in distinguishing AD, FTD, and CN cases. These results highlight the potential of TDL-enhanced deep learning models in clinical applications, offering a more accurate and detailed tool for Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis and differentiation from other neurodegenerative conditions. This work is presented as a proof-of-concept demonstrating that persistence-based topological descriptors can enhance EEG classification; multicenter validation on larger, diverse cohorts will be required to confirm generalizability.Subject terms: Computational models, Machine learning

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a growing epidemic that affects more than 50 million people worldwide and is expected to triple by 20501. AD is an age-related neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a progressive decline in cognitive abilities and the accumulation of amyloid-beta (A) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles of tau protein2,3. AD begins with the appearance of early biomarkers, such as amyloid burden, typically detected by amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) and plasma biomarkers, such as tau 2174,5. While the presence of a single biological indicator may be used as a diagnostic criterion, approximately 30% of elderly individuals without dementia symptoms exhibit high or intermediate A/tau levels without signs of synaptic loss or neurodegeneration6, a phenomenon known as AD resilience. This suggests that, under certain conditions, the central nervous system (CNS) can adapt and respond physiologically to AD-related damage, highlighting significant variability in the progression of AD. The multiple clinical scenarios in AD necessitate the use of diverse biomarkers. EEG techniques that capture both functional and structural aspects of NN, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)7, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)8, EEG, and magnetoencephalography (MEG)9, can aid in more accurate diagnosis and provide insights into the complex changes occurring in the brain during disease progression and resilience10. EEG and MEG are particularly valuable for revealing functional brain networks, as they capture electrical and magnetic brain activities with high temporal resolution. Through advanced analytical methods, such as connectivity analysis, researchers can identify functional networks that reveal dynamic interactions between brain regions and highlight altered communication patterns in individuals with AD11,12.

EEG, a relatively cost-effective and non-invasive technique, provides reliable information about brain activity by recording electrical oscillations with high temporal resolution13. These brain oscillations result from interactions between excitatory and inhibitory neurons and establish a precise temporal structure across neuronal populations within brain circuits14. This oscillatory activity serves as a “brain syntax” essential for information processing, neuroplasticity, and cognitive performance15. Abnormalities in oscillatory activity are evident in various neuropsychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia and AD16,17. Pathological A and tangles disrupt pre- and postsynaptic mechanisms, which likely underlie oscillatory disturbances in AD, leading to synaptic disruption, neuronal loss, excitatory-inhibitory imbalance, and altered brain oscillations, including epileptogenic activity18,19. Thus, EEG-based techniques have strong potential in both AD diagnosis and research17,20. This is supported by the fact that multiple investigations have reported that EEG measures correlate with neuropsychological tests such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Global Deterioration Scale (GDS), and Addenbrooke Cognitive Examination (ACE)21.

A variety of analytical and computational methods have been developed for using EEG in AD diagnosis, including visual assessment, wavelet coherence, fractal dimension, quadratic entropy, wavelet energy, and visibility graphs. Traditional spectral analysis techniques, such as Fourier and wavelet analysis, have identified specific changes in power across frequency bands-most notably a decrease in alpha (8–15 Hz) and beta (16–31 Hz) bands, along with an increase in theta (4–8 Hz) and delta (0.5–4.5 Hz) bands22,23. Moreover, these changes correlate with anatomical alterations, including white and gray matter changes across various brain regions24–27. Despite the effectiveness of these analytical methods, further advances are needed to capture the intricate network changes that characterize Alzheimer’s pathology. Recent work has emphasized both spectral and connectivity features of EEG in AD and MCI28, as well as deep learning approaches based on Graph Neural Networks and Transformers29–31, highlighting the need for methods that go beyond traditional metrics. Similar efforts using optimized spectral–temporal features and convolutional neural networks have also been reported in recent EEG-based AD studies32–34.

This need for advanced methods introduces Topological Deep Learning (TDL)-a mathematical framework designed to analyze and quantify complex shapes and relationships within data. Applying TDL to EEG datasets allows researchers to explore altered connectivity patterns, structural changes, and the spatial distribution of pathological markers like A plaques and tangles35–37. Persistent homology has recently been applied to EEG to reveal altered connectivity in AD38, but has not yet been integrated into deep learning pipelines capable of both classification and interpretability.

The novelty of our study lies in the first integration of persistence images-stable descriptors derived from persistent homology-into a TDL framework for EEG-based classification of AD, Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD), and Cognitively Normal (CN) subjects. By embedding topological features directly into neural models, our approach captures higher-order connectivity patterns that are overlooked by conventional spectral or graph-based methods and links them to known disruptions of functional networks in AD. To validate our approach, we utilized an OpenNeuro dataset39, demonstrating that TDL not only improves classification accuracy but also enhances biomedical interpretability.

Our results indicated notable performance improvements across various deep learning models when classifying AD, FTD, and CN groups. Specifically, our NN achieved an accuracy of 0.89 for AD, 0.86 for FTD, and 0.92 for CN, outperforming other models in sensitivity and specificity. The F1 scores for AD, FTD, and CN were 0.86, 0.83, and 0.88, respectively, while the Area Under the Curve (AUC) values reached 0.93 for AD, 0.90 for FTD, and 0.94 for CN. These findings underscore the potential of our method to enhance classification accuracy in Alzheimer’s research and provide new insights into disease-specific alterations of brain networks.

Overall, TDL offers a promising avenue for advancing our understanding of Alzheimer’s disease by bridging the gap between conventional analytical approaches and the complex topological characteristics of EEG data. As TDL continues to evolve, its integration into neurodegenerative disease research is likely to yield transformative insights and contribute to the development of more effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Data

The EEG dataset used in this study was obtained from the OpenNeuro repository and originally provided by researchers led by40. It comprises recordings from a total of 88 participants, categorized into three groups: (A) 29 healthy control subjects, (B) 36 patients diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and (C) 23 patients with frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Clinical diagnoses were made according to standardized criteria relevant to neurodegenerative disorders, as reported in the original study. Each participant’s demographic information, including age and gender, is included in the metadata, along with diagnostic labels and disease stage classifications (early, moderate, or advanced), though no further sub-grouping based on cognitive impairment level was applied. EEG signals were recorded using the international 10–20 system of electrode placement, a clinically validated standard for neurophysiological monitoring. Electrode sites included Fp1, Fp2, F3, F4, C3, C4, P3, P4, O1, O2, among others, ensuring comprehensive cortical coverage (see Fig. 1). The signals were captured across multiple channels and organized as multi-dimensional arrays, with annotations for each epoch and accompanying timestamps to facilitate temporal analysis. While the precise sampling frequency and preprocessing steps (e.g., artifact removal or band-pass filtering) are not explicitly documented in the OpenNeuro entry, the dataset structure is compatible with standard preprocessing pipelines for clinical and research applications. This dataset provides a valuable open-access resource for exploring electrophysiological characteristics of Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal dementia, supporting biomarker discovery and the development of diagnostic tools.

Methods

This section provides a detailed overview of the methodologies used for EEG data analysis, including preprocessing, artifact removal using ICA, and feature extraction through PSD and persistence images. These methods are crucial for capturing the spectral and topological properties of EEG signals. Additionally, TDL models are applied to evaluate the extracted features across various test datasets and validation settings, highlighting their significance in the interpretation and classification of EEG data. This overview aims to equip readers with the essential knowledge required for further exploration and implementation of these techniques.

Data preprocessing

EEG signals are highly sensitive and often contaminated with artifacts arising from non-neural sources such as eye movements, muscle contractions, and electrical interference from acquisition hardware. Preprocessing is therefore a critical step to isolate brain-related electrical activity and ensure the reliability of downstream analyses.

The preprocessing pipeline began by discarding the initial seconds of each EEG recording to eliminate transient artifacts typically introduced during the onset of data acquisition. Subsequently, digital filtering was applied to confine the signal to the frequency range of interest. A high-pass filter removed low-frequency drifts (e.g., from sweat or slow head movements), while a low-pass filter eliminated high-frequency noise not associated with neural activity.

A key component of preprocessing was the removal of artifacts using ICA, a powerful statistical method for separating multivariate signals into statistically independent sources. ICA is especially effective for disentangling EEG signals into components representing distinct neural and non-neural activity, such as eye blinks, muscle movements, and line noise41–43. While ICA is effective in removing common artifacts, it assumes statistical independence of sources and may require manual inspection, which introduces limitations for scalability and full automation.

It is particularly useful for capturing the connectivity and relationships between data points, called simplices. Persistence images

While persistence diagrams are a foundational output of topological data analysis (TDA), their structure as multisets of points in the extended plane makes them less amenable to direct use in statistical and machine learning pipelines. To address this, we leverage persistence images (PIs), a method that transforms persistence diagrams into fixed-size, vectorized representations suitable for integration with standard classifiers and deep learning models.

Persistence images convert topological features into a smooth, grid-based format by mapping each birth-death pair from a persistence diagram to a Gaussian distribution centered at the transformed coordinates , where encodes the persistence (lifetime) of the feature. The use of persistence in the vertical axis ensures that longer-lived (more significant) topological features have a greater influence on the final image.

Persistence images preserve the stability properties of persistence diagrams: small perturbations in the data lead to small changes in the image, making the representation robust to noise. This is particularly important in biomedical applications such as EEG analysis, where measurements are inherently noisy. Unlike raw persistence diagrams, persistence images provide a fixed-dimensional, noise-robust representation that can be directly integrated into machine learning pipelines, enabling systematic comparison across subjects.

Persistence images provide a functional summary of persistence diagrams and are well-suited for integration into deep learning workflows. They capture both global and local topological characteristics, enabling rich feature representations that can enhance classification performance.

Across groups, AD patients exhibit a marked reduction in high-persistence H1 features, reflecting the loss of stable loops in brain connectivity, which is consistent with a breakdown of long-range network integration. Comparison of EEG processing pipelines

To evaluate the effectiveness of different EEG preprocessing strategies for the classification of AD, FTD, and CN individuals, we compared our proposed methodology based on TDL via persistence images with two established toolkits: EEGLAB44 and Brainstorm55. Each approach was assessed using multiple performance metrics, including Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, F1 Score, Cohen’s Kappa, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC).

As presented in Table 2, our TDL-based pipeline consistently achieved superior results across all diagnostic categories and metrics. In particular, it reached the highest AUC scores (0.83 for AD, 0.79 for FTD, and 0.80 for CN), reflecting greater discriminatory power. It also demonstrated improved Sensitivity and F1 Score, suggesting more accurate identification of both disease and control cases. These improvements stem from the integration of persistent homology through persistence images, which capture multiscale spatial and temporal patterns in EEG signals that are often inaccessible to traditional processing techniques.

In contrast, EEGLAB44 and Brainstorm55 represent conventional preprocessing frameworks primarily based on spectral, statistical, and time-domain analyses. EEGLAB provides an ICA-driven pipeline that emphasizes artifact rejection and spectral decomposition, while Brainstorm focuses on sensor-level and source-space analysis using filtering, epoching, and time-frequency representations. Although both are widely adopted and offer robust standard pipelines, their feature representations lack the geometrical and topological depth introduced by our TDL approach.

Interestingly, spectral features alone (e.g., alpha/theta power) achieved lower discriminative power, consistent with recent studies28. This finding reinforces the limitations of using frequency-based descriptors in isolation, which capture local oscillatory dynamics but not higher-order connectivity. In contrast, persistence-based topological features encode multiscale structural properties of brain networks, yielding superior discriminative performance. We recognize, however, that hybrid pipelines combining spectral and topological descriptors may exploit complementary strengths, integrating frequency-specific and structural information. Although developing such hybrid models was beyond the scope of this proof-of-concept study, we now highlight it as a promising avenue for future research.

Overall, the results underscore the methodological advantage of incorporating topological descriptors into EEG processing. Our TDL framework enriches the feature space, enabling more expressive and robust classification performance across complex neurodegenerative conditions. Moreover, persistence images not only provide discriminative features but can also be linked to well-documented alterations in AD, such as reduced alpha-band coherence and impaired global efficiency, thereby enhancing the interpretability and clinical relevance of the classification outcomes.

Discussion

Traditional EEG-based classification methods in Alzheimer’s disease diagnosis face several well-recognized limitations. They often depend on handcrafted spectral or connectivity features, such as band power or coherence, which may fail to capture the full complexity of EEG signals and rely on expert-driven choices that limit generalizability. Moreover, EEG is inherently noisy and subject to artifacts (e.g., muscle activity, eye blinks, electrode placement), and linear preprocessing pipelines with fixed statistical thresholds may not adequately account for inter-subject variability or subtle disease-related patterns. Conventional classifiers such as support vector machines or linear discriminants also struggle with the nonlinear, high-dimensional structure of EEG recordings and rarely exploit their temporal–spatial dynamics, which are critical in neurodegenerative disorders. Finally, while some approaches are relatively transparent, many provide limited interpretability with respect to the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying classification. This lack of explanatory insight hinders clinical acceptance, as understanding why a subject is classified as AD or CN is often as important as the classification accuracy itself. These limitations motivate the integration of topological deep learning, which can capture global structural properties of brain dynamics in a way that is both robust to noise and more informative for clinical interpretation.

Our findings also clarify the role of canonical EEG frequency bands. In line with previous studies, we observed that alterations in the alpha band (8–12 Hz) provided the strongest discrimination between AD and controls, with AD patients showing a marked reduction in alpha activity that reflects impaired functional connectivity and network efficiency. Theta activity was increased but less specific, while beta and gamma contributed minimally to group separation. These results confirm that alpha alterations remain the most robust electrophysiological marker of AD, although spectral features alone achieved lower performance than when combined with persistence-based topological descriptors. The integration of TDL therefore not only improved accuracy but also offered a more interpretable characterization of higher-order network disruptions not captured by band power metrics.

The results of our study highlight the effectiveness of various deep learning models in classifying AD, FTD, and CN. Among the models evaluated, the Neural Network consistently achieved the highest performance metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, and AUC. This exceptional performance underscores the model’s robustness and its capability to distinguish between neurodegenerative conditions with greater precision. Its high sensitivity and AUC make it particularly valuable for clinical diagnostics where accurate differentiation between AD and FTD is critical.

The Random Forest model also performed notably well, especially in terms of sensitivity and AUC, demonstrating its effectiveness in identifying positive cases and distinguishing between the groups. While it did not reach the Neural Network’s level of accuracy and AUC, its strong performance suggests it is a viable alternative, particularly in contexts where computational resources are limited. Conversely, the SVM and LR models showed lower performance across the metrics. The SVM, while offering good overall performance, lagged behind in sensitivity and AUC, which are crucial for precise classification. The LR model exhibited the lowest performance, indicating its reduced effectiveness in distinguishing between AD, FTD, and CN.

The integration of TDL significantly contributed to these outcomes. By embedding persistence-based descriptors into deep learning pipelines, our method captured multiscale topological patterns associated with AD and FTD, reinforcing the biological interpretability of the classification. These topological features can be linked to well-established findings of disrupted alpha-band coherence and altered network efficiency in AD, providing an additional layer of clinical relevance. Nevertheless, our dataset was relatively small (88 participants) and lacked demographic diversity, which may restrict generalizability. Larger multicenter validation cohorts will be necessary to confirm robustness. Future work should also benchmark TDL against Graph Neural Networks and Transformers on the same data and investigate multimodal integration (EEG, fMRI, DTI, MEG) to further improve diagnostic accuracy and biological plausibility.

Overall, this study underscores the potential of combining advanced deep learning techniques with topological data analysis to advance our understanding of brain dynamics in neurodegenerative diseases. The results provide a promising proof-of-concept that supports the utility of TDL in this context, while also highlighting the need for further validation. Although cross-validated results indicate consistent gains, confirmation on larger, independent, multicenter datasets remains essential. Future work will therefore focus on replicating the approach across multi-site EEG repositories and evaluating its robustness under domain shift, with the ultimate goal of enhancing diagnostic capabilities and improving patient outcomes in neurodegenerative disease management.
