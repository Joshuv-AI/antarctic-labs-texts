Artistic style classification in traditional cultural heritage using convolutional neural networks
Authors: Bo Xu, Xiaomeng Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The automatic recognition of artistic styles is an important part of the management, analysis, and access to the huge digital cultural heritage archives. Nevertheless, the aesthetic complexity and minor distinctions between works of art become serious obstacles to this process. To overcome this, this study introduces a multi-stage, novel technique that, in comparison to current techniques, intelligently derives and combines local and global features to obtain a more comprehensive style representation. In the suggested approach, initially, the image is split into visual parts with the help of the K-means algorithm, and the local characteristics of each part are obtained with the help of specific CNNs. Simultaneously, a more detailed CNN obtains global features of the whole picture. The two sets of features are then intelligently fused with an attention mechanism. In the third step, an Autoencoder (AE) is employed to downsize the dimensions of the extracted features and eliminate noise and redundant data because of the large dimensionality of the extracted features. Lastly, a SoftMax classifier is used to perform classification. The proposed method was assessed using the Metropolitan Museum of Art (MET) Public Archives dataset in this study. Our approach on the MET dataset had an 89.07% accuracy and F-measure of 0.8867, which proves it to be superior.

Introduction

Computerized recognition of artistic styles is important in the management, analysis, and accessibility of the huge repositories of digital cultural heritage1. With the digitization of museums and galleries, researchers have been presented with databases of hundreds of thousands of unclassified or poorly-cataloged artworks2. Art historians have a subjective method of manual classification, which cannot scale to this challenge, and is time-consuming. Hence, the creation of powerful, computational art analysis models is not just an academic endeavor, but a necessary one to the preservation and interpretation of our shared cultural past3,4.

The most common solution to this issue has been the use of deep learning, specifically Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Initial and pioneering studies showed that conventional CNN models (such as VGG, ResNet, and Inception), typically trained on large-scale datasets such as ImageNet) could be used to obtain decent classification performance on artworks5–7. These models are very good at learning global features-the overall make up, the preponderant color scheme, and the overall structure of an image.

Nonetheless, such dependence on global characteristics is also a major flaw. These models tend to ignore the small, fine-grained details and local effects that are the hallmarks of an artistic style, like brushwork of a certain type, a certain pattern of texture, or the way light is cast on a surface8,9. This causes confusion of styles that have common compositions in the world, but they vary in local performance (e.g., Impressionism and Post-Impressionism). Although there has been some work that has tried to solve this by emphasizing hand-crafted texture features10,11, hierarchical approaches12, or small pieces13, a strong way of integrating local detailed information with a global perspective has been a major problem. Most systems did not have a good way to dynamically integrate these local and global representations and they were usually considered equally valuable14.

In order to fill this gap, this study introduces a new, multi-step method of proper classification of artistic styles. Specifically, this study focuses on the classification of nine distinct art styles (Romanticism, Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, Cubism, Surrealism, Baroque, Renaissance, Neoclassicism, and Art Deco). We base our approach on the premise that a more precise classification could be attained by smartly deriving and combining local as well as global characteristics. The suggested framework works in a number of major steps. The first step is to divide the image into visual segments with the use of the K-means algorithm and enable specific CNNs to identify fine-grained local features in each of the individual parts. Simultaneously, a deeper CNN obtains global features of the whole image. Second, the two sets of features are intelligently integrated with the help of a self-attention mechanism that learns to balance the relative importance of each feature. Third, the fused vector is dimensionally reduced to eliminate noise and redundant data with the help of an Autoencoder (AE). Lastly, a SoftMax classifier is used to determine the artistic style. The introduction of an attention mechanism to combine features of segmented regions is a major contribution, which provides a more complete and efficient representation of the artwork. Related works

With the development of deep learning, the literature on the analysis of computational art and style classification has expanded dramatically. The study can be broadly divided into a number of major themes, which we will discuss here to put our proposed approach into perspective.

CNNs and transfer learning for global feature extraction

The most common method of art style classification is based on the use of standard CNN architectures and transfer learning15. The pre-trained models, including VGG, Inception, and ResNet, have been widely compared by researchers to establish their efficacy on art datasets16. For instance, Menai and Babahenini6 compared six CNN for style classification of paintings on the basis of deep learning for image processing. Their results indicated that InceptionResNetV2, when optimized using an Adam optimizer with the learning rate of 1e-4, was the best-performing model to classify style.

Zhao et al.7 compared seven transfer learning models on three datasets for art classification. They concluded that model structure optimization improved the performance significantly. The models performed at the state of art with regard to each task and their visualization was used to reveal the complexity of art classification. This has been expanded by others, such as Orfeas et al.5, who have investigated art style recognition with deep networks and have compared eight architectures on two art datasets. They have come up with a stacking ensemble approach that averaged the first-stage classifiers using a meta-classifier. It attained state-of-the-art accuracy on WikiArt’s largest dataset of art, and the research also explored the effects of data and art styles. Also, Pancaroglu17 used FaceNet for classifying artists, years, and styles in fine-art paintings, from a dataset of over 80,000 paintings by 1000 artists. Accuracy was proved to be 58.8, 63.7 and 81.3 respectively and mean purities were 63.1, 72.4 and 85.9 respectively.

These papers, together with18 and19, provide a solid foundation, which proves that global characteristics represented by deep CNNs are very informative in style. Specifically, Guo and Hao18 employed CNN for oil painting style characterization both aesthetically and historically. Two style spaces were built independently and distance maps were generated, ultimately checking their alignment with actual art history to 70.7% accuracy. Also, Yang19 suggested the classification of art painting styles using CNN. The approach used the VGG-19 hybrid transfer learning model, which identified three classes of painting styles and was more suitable for classification than visual inspection using the naked eye. However, as noted in introduction, these works are insufficient for distinguishing between fine-grained styles.

Beyond global features: local, hierarchical, and feature-based methods

Other studies have identified that global features have their limitations, and they examine more detailed visual information. A number of studies have been done on traditional, hand-crafted features. Liu et al.10 proposed novel features based on color distance and texture. They employed Modified Color Distance, ColorRatio Feature, and Weber’s Law Based Texture Feature in their solution. It outperformed previous approaches in this field and showed acceptable accuracy. Nunez-Garcia et al.11 used a combination of color and texture features to train a neural network. Such methods confirm the significance of local patterns yet they are fragile and less generalizable as compared to learned features. A more sophisticated approach is hierarchical classification, as proposed by Mohammadi and Rustaee12, which first classifies super-styles and then specific sub-styles. According to their method, the styles were initially classified into super-styles (represented as parents) and subsequently utilized one parent classifier and multiple child classifiers. This contributed greatly to the average F1 score of the DenseNet121 network. In the same way, Elkin et al.13 developed a style prediction framework of image fragments, which shows the strength of local information in ancient art.

Advanced deep learning techniques

In more recent studies, scholars have used more sophisticated deep learning approaches to art. The self-supervised learning that does not require huge labeled datasets has been promising. Both Joshi et al.20 and Zhang et al.21 came up with self-supervised approaches to learn subtle stylistic features, which outperform most supervised approaches. The indexing of large-scale art databases was an important advance in the field of art database indexing, with the work in20 outperforming previous approaches by almost 20 percent on a WikiArt dataset of 27 art styles. In contrast, the work in21 suggested a multi-task self-supervised style feature learning algorithm for computational aesthetics to address the issue of expensive aesthetic style labels. Their algorithm took into account compositional rules, luminance and color factors and eventually performed better than most supervised learning algorithms.

At the same time, data augmentation has become essential, as there is comparative dearth of quality labeled art. Recently, Moyano et al.22 investigated how Diffusion Models can be used to create new and synthetic pieces of art, thus improving the performance of classifiers by increasing the size of the training set. The procedure was meant to enhance the performance of machine learning models in predicting pictorial styles in artworks by generating new samples from the current dataset.

Li23, employed modified CNN model for classification of artistic styles. The author developed a modified CNN model to recognize art styles based on related characteristics like compositions, textures, and color patterns. The study focuses on classification of 5 major art styles and achieved the accuracy of 93% which outperforms well-known CNN models such as VGG16 and ResNet50. However, overlapping features among styles, limited recognizable classes, and bias concerns in training data can be considered as the major limitations of this research.

Applications and surveys in digital heritage

In addition to the simple classification, ML models are finding their way into digital heritage in general. Abidin24 employed two machine learning models for art genre classification. The findings showed that metadata-related characteristics, including nationalities and artist periods, contributed to the accuracy of the classification to a significant extent, confirming the capability of artificial intelligence to cope with similar tasks on artworks. Castellano and Vessio25 and Valencia et al.26 present extensive literature reviews of the area, including the opportunities and challenges of deep learning application to visual arts. The article in25 investigated the use of deep learning in the extraction and identification of patterns in visual arts, namely painting and drawing. Their article emphasized the potential of computer science researchers to promote access and cultural awareness in this field. On the other hand, paper26 reviewed the use of machine learning models in artistic style prediction and found a growing trend in this area. They observed that the most popular technique was neural networks but noted that most studies were on historical styles which showed a gap in research. Other papers concentrate on particular applications, including the CLEOPATRA system of frescos reconstruction27 or image-only authentication of modern art28.

Research gap

The general, global-feature models have a definite tendency to more specific and fine-grained approaches, as can be seen in this review. Although these approaches (e.g., feature-based, hierarchical, fragment-based) prove the significance of local details, not many studies have suggested an end-to-end framework that explicitly divides artworks to obtain local features and dynamically combines them with global features. Our model fills in this particular methodological gap by combining K-means segmentation and a self-attention mechanism into a single model that learns what to attend to (attention) both where (local segments) and what (global context).

Materials and methods

This section first describes the specifications of the dataset used and how its samples are organized, followed by the proposed framework for identifying artistic styles in artworks.

Database

The dataset used in this research was meticulously compiled from the public archive of the Metropolitan Museum of Art (MET)29 in New York. Being one of the world’s most renowned art museums, the MET boasts a freely available collection of over 492,000 high-resolution images. The extent, image quality, and open availability of this vast collection render the MET an ideal source for art style classification research, especially given its unparalleled diversity of artworks. For this study, a randomly sampled collection of 15,000 images composed of various paintings, sculptures, drawings, photographs, and decorative art works were utilized. The artwork was strictly screened to consist only of pieces produced in the last three centuries, from 1700 up to the current time, to remain dedicated to distinct and better-documented art movements. Those images with a resolution of below 256×256 pixels were rejected, and all the selected images were resized into a uniform size of 256×256 pixels for model input. The variety of media (paintings, sculptures, drawings, photographs, and decorative art works) is a calculated decision aimed at challenging the strength of the model. This cross-media heterogeneity poses a major problem, because stylistic indicators are different in every medium. For example, ‘Impressionism’ is defined by brushwork in painting but by surface texture and form in sculpture. We have modeled our learning accordingly to learn the high-level, abstract visual patterns and compositional elements that bring a style together across these diverse forms, and not just to use medium-specific attributes.

Given the absence of precise artistic style labeling in the original MET collection, a manual labeling approach based on specific art history movements was adopted. This process was carried out by three art history experts, and the majority vote method was used to reach a final consensus. Ambiguous cases or artworks that were difficult to categorize were discussed and reviewed among the experts to assign the correct label. Each filtered artwork was carefully visually examined and assigned to one of nine predefined art style categories. These categories and the number of images corresponding to each are as follows: 1- Romanticism: 2100 images, 2- Impressionism: 2500 images, 3- post-Impressionism: 1800 images, 4- Cubism: 1200 images, 5- Surrealism: 1000 images, 6- Baroque: 1400 images, 7- Renaissance: 1300 images, 8- Neoclassicism: 1500 images, and 9- Art Deco: 2200 images.

For instance, the paintings containing visual elements such as clear brushstrokes, focus on color and light, and everyday scenes were tagged under "Impressionism." Similarly, the paintings containing geometric shapes, multiple perspectives, and form analysis were tagged as belonging to "Cubism," and those paintings containing strong emotions, drama, and focus on individuality were tagged as belonging to "Romanticism." This painstaking and time-consuming manual tagging guaranteed the validity and integrity of our dataset and made it a sound foundation for art style classification model training and testing.

Proposed method

This section will present the proposed framework for solving the problem of automated art style classification in traditional cultural heritage, considering the existing challenges in terms of the diversity and visual complexity of artworks. In the present research, a multi-stage architecture has been developed for the accurate identification and differentiation of art styles, inspired by the capabilities of CNN in extracting rich features and hybrid learning approaches. The overall proposed strategy is based on the intelligent combination of local features (extracted from distinct components of the image) and global features (extracted from the entire image) to achieve a comprehensive and accurate model for solving the discussed problem. The feature extraction and combination step are the core of the visual information extraction process from artworks. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the artwork’s artistic style, it is necessary to consider both the subtle local details of the work and its overall structure and composition. To meet these requirements, the proposed framework suggests two parallel pathways for feature extraction. For this purpose, local features are extracted using the K-means segmentation strategy and separate CNN models for each segment. Since artworks often contain distinct visual regions that provide crucial information for style identification, segmentation will be an appropriate strategy to improve the description of style-relevant features. The reason for choosing K-means is its simplicity, speed, and ability to cluster pixels based on visual features such as color and texture, which leads to the creation of segments with clear boundaries. This method is long-standing in image analysis, where segmentation is applied to divide an image into meaningful parts, and a more granular region-based feature extraction can be performed30,31. Our model can store localized stylistic information -the brushwork of a single part of a work or the color range of another- that is central to the identification of complex art styles by breaking down an artwork into its constituent parts (e.g., figure, background, foreground)32.

After segmentation, each of these regions is processed by a dedicated CNN to obtain specialized models for learning unique features corresponding to intensity in each image. This approach allows the model to extract specific visual details and patterns present in distinct regions of the image more deeply and purposefully. In addition to local features, general image features such as overall composition, dominant color palette, and visual flow of the artwork also play a significant role in describing an artistic style. The proposed strategy for extracting these global and abstract features uses a CNN (with a more complex structure than local CNNs).

Once parallel feature sets have been extracted, they have to be integrated to form a single representation. Simple concatenation is a common method, but this method assumes that all features are equally important, which is a serious weakness. To address this, the proposed framework uses an attention-based fusion mechanism33. The attention mechanisms are similar to the human visual perception in that they learn to place weight of importance on various features. This enables the model to dynamically choose between local detail (e.g., segment 1) or a global pattern to be more informative in identifying a particular style, a technique that has been shown to be very effective in a number of computer vision tasks, including fine-grained classification34,35. This fusion mechanism allows the model to assign different importance weights (attention) to each of the local features and the overall image feature. With this capability, the model will be able to identify the importance of each piece of information (e.g., a specific region or a specific subset of global image features) relative to other parts. This approach not only can improve classification accuracy but also increases the interpretability of the model, as it can be observed which parts of the image the model has focused on more.

Since the merged feature vector will be high-dimensional, intelligently reducing it will be essential. This is because high-dimensional features, in addition to increasing computational load, are prone to noise and redundant information that affect the final performance of the model. Accordingly, in the second step, an AE is used to reduce the dimensionality of the features. By utilizing AE networks, the most meaningful information can be extracted from the original feature vector and compressed into a lower-dimensional space. This process leads to noise and redundancy reduction, contributing to increased computational efficiency of the model and reduced complexity of the final classifier.

In the last phase of the suggested approach, the reduced feature vector is sent to a SoftMax-based meta-learner. This classifier can calculate the probability of each sample belonging to each of the predefined art styles and, ultimately, suggest the style with the highest probability as the final output of the model for that artwork. Feature extraction and combination

In the first step of the proposed method, the processes of extracting and then combining image features are performed using deep learning models. In this step, local and global image features are first extracted in parallel. Local features are extracted based on the segmented image and CNN specific to each cluster; while global features are extracted by a CNN with a more complex structure. At the end of this step, the features are merged using an attention mechanism. The remainder of this section is dedicated to explaining the details of image feature extraction and combination.

Local feature extraction.

Local attributes derived from segmented parts of images are utilized as well to create a rich and unique presentation of the artwork’s stylistic contents. This allows the visual features to be described more accurately in different parts of the image, as opposed to the whole image. Extraction of such features involves two significant steps. The k-means segmentation technique is used to separate the inputs into a group of non-overlapping segments once they have all been transformed to grayscale. This segmentation aims to divide the image into visually cohesive areas that most likely correspond to various surfaces or objects in the artwork. For instance, in a painting, there may be face, landscape, or object part regions, each painted with some technique and style. In the proposed method, the wanted number of regions per image is set to the empirical value of k=3. By predefining the number of clusters, the number of image segments is thus always maintained constant, and therefore the dimension of the ultimate feature vector is always kept constant, which is essential for the operational viability of the model. In particular, the K-means algorithm works with the grayscale intensity of the pixels as the main attribute of clustering. The squared Euclidean distance is used as the similarity measure in order to assign each pixel to a cluster. Global feature extraction

In parallel with the local feature extraction process, there will be a need to capture higher-level, more abstract, and more semantic features of the artwork, which is also achieved using a CNN model with a more complex architecture. The CNN model used for global feature extraction includes 5 convolutional layers. The input to this model is the original 256x256 RGB image, and the convolutional filters have 8, 16, 24, 32, 48, and 128 filters, respectively. After each convolutional filter, the ReLU function acts as an activator, followed by a max pooling layer with dimensions of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2\times 2$$\end{document} and a unit stride. At the end of this architecture, a fully connected layer with 100 dimensions is used to extract image features.

For the sake of achieving optimal efficiency in the extraction of features, the hyperparameters of both the global and local CNN models have been tuned through a grid search technique. The procedure involved trying the performance of the model with different configurations of parameters related to network architecture as well as training procedure. At the time of searching, the search interval of the filter dimension hyperparameter was defined as [+2,+12]. Furthermore, the hyperparameter of filter count was searched from [+8,+128] with a step size of 1. Different activation functions like {ReLU, PReLU, LeakyReLU} and different pooling layers like {Max, Min, Global, Average} were tried in the search process.

The configuration search was performed based on the validation error criterion and using 20% of the experimental samples. It should be noted that based on the grid search results, using the SGDM optimizer for local CNNs and the Adam optimizer for global CNNs resulted in the best training performance. At the end of the first step, and after extracting local and global features, these feature vectors are merged using an attention layer.

Feature combination based on attention mechanism

The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k+1$$\end{document} feature vectors (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=3$$\end{document} local and 1 global) are combined with the help of a self-attention mechanism as presented in Section "Proposed Method". This process learns to place weights of importance to every feature vector, and the model is able to dynamically weight the most salient information to be classified. The mathematical description of this process of self-attention, the computation of Query, Key, and Value vectors, and the weighted integration in the end have been described in detail in Appendix A to enhance readability. The result of this step is one, context-sensitive feature vector, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}^{U}$$\end{document}, which is then fed into the Autoencoder network.

It should be mentioned that the proposed architecture does not depend on the numbering or indexing of the three clusters produced by K-means. This autonomy is obtained in two design decisions, first, the three local CNNs are configured with the same architectural structure and weight-initialization logic, so that they extract features with the same degree of granularity irrespective of the segment they are acting on. Second, the attention mechanism processes the input feature vectors as a collection of vectors and not as a sequence. Since the attention weights are calculated using the Query-Key (Q-K) similarity (content-based relevance), the resulting fused representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}^{U}$$\end{document} is also calculated using the visual significance of the features themselves, and the model is resistant to an arbitrary permutation of the cluster indices.

Feature dimensionality reduction by autoencoder network

Given the high dimensionality of the features extracted in the first step of the proposed framework and the presence of redundancy and noise in these features, it is necessary to use an efficient technique to address this challenge. Although dimensionality reduction can be done by traditional linear approaches such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), the approach tends to miss the complex, non-linear correlation between deep features generated by art37. Autoencoders (AEs) as a non-linear dimensionality reduction method are much more appropriate to this task. They are good at learning a latent representation (encoding) of the input data, which is compressed, and have been extensively applied in pipelines of deep learning to denoise features and extract the most informative content38.

An AE network consists of two parts: an encoder and a decoder. An AE receives the extracted features for each sample in the form of a vector X and, using its encoder part, compresses the input dimensions to describe the sample in n steps. Then, using its decoder part, it attempts to reconstruct the input X with the least difference and only by relying on the representation created by the encoder part. This process is unsupervised and, similar to early neural networks, is done through an iterative process of updating the weight values of the network’s neurons. Specifically, the encoder can be represented as a function f that maps the input X to the hidden representation Y. This process is formulated as follows39:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y=f\left(X\right)={s}_{f}\left(WX+{b}_{X}\right)$$\end{document}

In the above relation; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{f}$$\end{document} is the nonlinear activation function defined as hyperbolic tangent sigmoid in the proposed AE model, and W is the weight matrix of the encoder part of the neural network. Also, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b}_{X}$$\end{document} will represent the bias vector. In the decoder part, the weight matrix values will be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W}^{\prime}={W}^{T}$$\end{document} and its bias vector will be described as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b}_{Y}$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b}_{X}\ne {b}_{Y}$$\end{document}). In the proposed method, the encoder part of the AE will be used to create a compressed representation of the extracted features. For this purpose, the network output in the last layer of the encoder part (Y) is calculated for each input vector X, and these features will be used as descriptive characteristics of the image style in the next step. To train this network, the mean squared error function has been used. AE training involves finding optimal values for the parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta =\left(W,{b}_{X},{b}_{Y}\right)$$\end{document} such that using these parameters, the mean squared error resulting from the reconstruction of each sample like X is minimized. The training process can be formulated as follows39:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\propto =m{in}_{\theta }L\left(X,{X}^{\prime}\right)=m{in}_{\theta }L\left(X,g\left(f\left(X\right)\right)\right)$$\end{document}

In the above relation; g and f are, respectively, the activation functions of the decoding and encoding parts of the network. The structure of the AE network used in the proposed method has 3 layers in its encoding part. The number of neurons in the first layer is equal to the number of features extracted in the first step, and the number of neurons in each subsequent layer is equal to ½ of the neurons in the layer before it. Thus, a feature vector with length n will be reduced in dimensionality to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{n}{4}$$\end{document}. In the determined configuration for the proposed method, the reduced length of the feature vector will be 25. In the third step, these features are processed using a SoftMax classifier to identify the artistic style of the artwork.

Art style classification

The final step of the proposed method is to classify artworks based on an art style label according to the optimized and compressed features extracted and dimensionally reduced in the previous steps. In this step, a SoftMax classifier is used to carry out the multi-class classification. The reason why the SoftMax classifier is used is that it is a generalization of logistic regression to multi-class classification and it is preferred because it can provide interpretable probabilities of all classes40.

Compressed and reduced features are passed as input to this classifier. The classification begins with calculating a score for each art style class. These are calculated as a linear combination of input features and learnable weights of the classifier. Last, the SoftMax function transforms these scores to probabilities in the range [0,1] such that the sum of probabilities across all classes is 1. These probabilities give the confidence of the model in a sample belonging to a given style. The probability calculation of each class (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{j}$$\end{document}) is given by41:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\left(y={C}_{j}|z;{W}_{s},{b}_{s}\right)=\frac{{e}^{z.{w}_{j}+{b}_{j}}}{{\sum }_{k=1}^{{N}_{c}}{e}^{z.{w}_{k}+{b}_{k}}}$$\end{document}

In the above relationship, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\left(y={C}_{j}|z\right)$$\end{document} denotes the probability of the sample belonging to the j-th class, given that the input feature vector is z, and z is the reduced feature vector obtained from the AE network. Also, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W}_{s}$$\end{document} represents the learnable weight matrix of the SoftMax classifier, whose dimensions will be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{c}\times f$$\end{document}, where f is the number of input features and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{c}$$\end{document} is the total number of art style classes. In addition, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{j}$$\end{document} is the weight vector corresponding to the j-th class, which corresponds to the j-th column of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W}_{s}$$\end{document} matrix, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b}_{j}$$\end{document} represents the bias corresponding to the j-th class.

The SoftMax classifier is trained to minimize the cross-entropy loss function. This loss function measures the difference between the probability distribution predicted by the model and the actual probability distribution (which is a unique distribution for the correct class). The training process is performed through SGD optimization. After training, to classify a new sample, the model extracts and reduces the dimensions of its feature vector through the previous steps and then applies it to the SoftMax classifier. The class that obtains the highest probability is determined as the predicted art style for that artwork.

Training strategy of the proposed model

The training of the entire framework is based on a modular optimization strategy instead of a single end to end approach. This design decision is to guarantee that every component (feature extraction, dimensionality reduction, and classification) is optimally configured to its goal without the interference of the vanishing gradient issue, which commonly afflicts hybrid designs with intricate attention-based fusion.

In this protocol, the local and global CNNs (defined in Section "Feature Extraction and Combination") are optimized by the Cross-Entropy loss first. The 100-dimensional feature vectors that are obtained are the ultimate latent representations of the stylistic markers of the artwork. The Autoencoder is then trained separately to minimize the reconstruction loss with Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss.

This step will help to denoise features by reducing the 100 dimensional inputs to a 25 dimensional space and preserving the most salient data. Lastly, the SoftMax classifier is trained with Cross-Entropy loss to generate these refined features to the nine art style categories. This piecewise optimization is more numerically stable and has the benefit of being able to analyse the features extracted at each stage of the pipeline in a more granular way.

Computational cost

The multi-stage, hybrid structure of our proposed approach, although proven to be effective, adds an additional computational cost to that of a single end-to-end network. This expense is mostly focused on the training stage which entails a number of independent optimization processes:Preprocessing: This is a one-time cost of executing K-means segmentation on the entire training data.CNN training: CNNs require the most computationally expensive part to be training four different CNN models (the three local-feature CNNs and the deeper global-feature CNN).AE training: The Autoencoder needs to be trained to learn the compressed feature-space using the fused CNN outputs, which is a less-intensive training phase.Classifier Training: Lastly, the SoftMax classifier is trained on the 25-dimensional features that are reduced, and this is computationally insignificant.

The cost is additive in the process of inference. We assume that this accepted trade-off in the computational complexity is justified by the large 8.67% accuracy improvement (as described in Section "Results and Discussion").

Results and discussion

The experiments were conducted using MATLAB 2020a software and with a cross-validation approach with 10 repetitions. In each repetition, the data was divided into 90/10: 90% of the data was used for Training and Validation and the remaining 10% for Test. In the training and validation section, 90% of the total data was used, 80% for model training and 10% for validation. This process was repeated ten times and finally, the average of the results from these ten repetitions was reported as the final results.

Conclusion

The main issues of automatic artistic style identification that we discussed in this paper are the visual complexity and the inability of the traditional CNNs to extract the necessary local information. We introduced a new multi-step system, which is a synergistic combination of local and global visual characteristics to represent the complete stylistic image. We use K-means segmentation to extract local features using dedicated CNNs and extract global features using a deep CNN. These sets of features are then combined through an attention mechanism and refined and compressed with an Autoencoder to optimize dimensions and eliminate noise. The final style categorization is done by a SoftMax classifier. The proposed method was tested on our own dataset based on the MET Public Archives and showed good results with an accuracy of 89.07 and F-measure of 0.8867. This paper emphasizes the centrality of judicious integration of multi-level characteristics to achieve strong classification of art styles, which provides a sound solution to the management and access of large repositories of digital cultural heritage.

In spite of the promising outcomes, this study has a number of limitations that leave the future research open:Computational complexity: The suggested multi-stage architecture that involves the use of several CNNs, an attention layer, and an AE is computationally intensive. In spite of the fact that we employed rather shallow CNNs, further research should be conducted on optimization and pruning methods of models to minimize the computational burden without affecting accuracy, making it more practical to implement in real-time.Segmentation strategy: The fact that K-means segmentation uses a fixed number of clusters (k=3) and still makes the model easy to use might not be the best strategy to use on all artworks. Minimalist designs may need less segments, whereas Baroque scenes may need more. Future studies need to explore adaptive or dynamic segmentation algorithms that are able to find the optimum number of regions depending on image content.Dataset scope and diversity: Our dataset is curated but has a limitation in the scope. The scalability and generalizability of the framework should be tested in future research, where the framework will be applied to other, larger cultural heritage data that covers a broader range of time and a greater number of style categories. Moreover, since our dataset is heterogeneous in media, one way forward in future research would be to come up with media-specific models (e.g., sculpture vs. painting) to understand how the stylistic cues vary and cross-media.Task granularity: The existing structure is planned to be used in the classification of the style. This model could also be useful to be extended to more granular tasks, including artist-specific classification or sub-style identification which are still important in the area.
