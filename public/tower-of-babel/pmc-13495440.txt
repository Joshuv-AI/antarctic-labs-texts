The predictive value of nurse-led preoperative frailty assessment for postoperative delirium in liver transplant recipients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yi Jiang, Juan Du, Jiajun Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative delirium (POD) is a severe and frequent complication after liver transplantation (LT), associated with increased morbidity and healthcare utilization. Frailty represents a clinically measurable state of diminished physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors. The predictive value of a structured, nurse-led frailty assessment for POD in LT recipients requires rigorous evaluation.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled adults who underwent first-time liver transplantation from 2020 to 2024 at a single center. Patients with severe pre-existing cognitive impairment or missing data were excluded. Preoperative frailty was assessed by trained transplant nurse coordinators using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) and Liver Frailty Index (LFI), with frailty defined as CFS ≥ 5 or LFI ≥ 4.5. The primary outcome was POD incidence within 7 days post-LT, diagnosed using the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU).ResultsAmong 156 patients, 46 (29.5%) were frail. The incidence of POD was markedly elevated in frail patients compared to their non-frail counterparts (52.2% vs. 18.2%, P < 0.001). Following multivariable analysis, preoperative frailty was independently associated with POD (aOR = 4.32, 95% CI: 2.15–8.68). A significant positive correlation was observed between frailty severity (CFS score) and delirium severity (CAM-ICU-7 score; ρ = 0.42, P < 0.001). Frail patients also received prolonged mechanical ventilation (median 28 vs. 18 h, P = 0.002), ICU length of stay (6 vs. 4 days, P < 0.001), and hospital length of stay (22 vs. 17 days, P = 0.001). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed significantly lower delirium-free survival in frail patients (Log-rank P < 0.001).ConclusionsA nurse-led preoperative frailty assessment is associated with POD and resource use in LT recipients in this retrospective cohort. These findings suggest that integrating this feasible screening into routine nursing practice may facilitate early identification of high-risk patients, supporting targeted preventive strategies.

Introduction

Liver transplantation, while potentially life-saving for end-stage liver disease, constitutes a major physiological insult. Postoperative delirium (POD) is an acute and fluctuating disturbance in attention, awareness, and cognition, representing a form of acute brain dysfunction that typically develops within the first postoperative week. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), delirium is characterized by (1) a disturbance in attention and awareness, (2) an acute change from baseline that tends to fluctuate in severity, and (3) an additional disturbance in cognition. Clinical subtypes include hypoactive, hyperactive, and mixed forms, each with distinct presentations and prognostic implications. This condition is exceedingly common in this population, with incidence rates reported between 20% and 50% [1]. The ramifications of POD extend far beyond the immediate postoperative phase, as it is strongly associated with a spectrum of adverse outcomes such as extended mechanical ventilation, longer ICU and hospital stays, increased institutionalization, accelerated cognitive decline, and higher mortality [2, 3]. This significant burden necessitates the identification of modifiable preoperative risk factors to guide effective prevention.

The pathogenesis of POD is best understood through the interaction of patient vulnerability with perioperative stressors [4]. LT recipients possess a unique vulnerability profile due to the pathophysiology of cirrhosis, which often includes systemic inflammation, metabolic derangements, and varying degrees of hepatic encephalopathy, all of which can compromise cerebral resilience [5]. The perioperative period adds numerous precipitating factors, such as hemodynamic swings, significant transfusion requirements, exposure to neurotoxic immunosuppressants like tacrolimus, and electrolyte imbalances [6]. While factors like age are non-modifiable, targeting potentially malleable conditions such as frailty presents a critical opportunity for risk reduction [7].

Frailty is a clinically recognized multidimensional syndrome marked by decreased physiological reserve across multiple organ systems and heightened vulnerability to acute stressors. It is conceptually distinct from comorbidity and disability, reflecting a state of reduced homeostatic capacity that increases the risk of adverse outcomes following physiological challenges such as surgery or critical illness [8]. Distinct from mere comorbidity or disability, it represents a holistic measure of biological aging. Its value in predicting adverse surgical outcomes, including mortality and complications, is well-established across various specialties [9]. The link between frailty and POD is biologically plausible, mediated through pathways such as a diminished capacity to buffer neuroinflammation, altered cerebral autoregulation, increased sensitivity to sedative and analgesic medications, and reduced cognitive reserve [10, 11]. Importantly, patients with cirrhosis are particularly susceptible to a physical phenotype of frailty, often termed sarcopenia, due to chronic metabolic disturbances and anabolic resistance, making its preoperative assessment highly relevant in LT candidates [12].

Despite the high stakes, systematic preoperative assessment of physiological reserve via frailty metrics is not yet standard in many LT programs. Common tools like the Karnofsky Performance Status offer a global functional estimate but are less sensitive than validated frailty instruments for capturing multisystem vulnerability [13]. This creates a critical gap in risk stratification for POD. To fill this gap, a feasible and reliable assessment method must be identified. Nurses, by virtue of their frontline role, sustained patient contact, and expertise in holistic assessment, are the logical clinicians to operationalize such screening within existing preoperative workflows [14]. Two pragmatic tools suitable for nursing administration are the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), a rapid global assessment, and the Liver Frailty Index (LFI), a performance-based measure specifically validated in patients with cirrhosis [15, 16]. While frailty has been linked to adverse transplant outcomes [17, 18], robust evidence specifically supporting the predictive value of a standardized, nurse-led protocol combining these tools for POD in LT recipients remains lacking.

To address this gap, we conducted this retrospective cohort study. We hypothesized that a preoperative frailty assessment, performed by trained nurses using the CFS and LFI, would be a strong and independent predictor of POD in adult LT recipients. We aimed to quantify this association and assess its impact on key clinical and resource utilization outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Liver Transplantation Center of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval No.: 2025 − 429), which operates in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, with informed consent waived due to its retrospective design, and adhered to STROBE guidelines.

Study population

This study specifically focused on recipients of deceased-donor liver transplantation. We screened the electronic medical records of all consecutive adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) who underwent this procedure at our center between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024. Living donor liver transplant recipients were not included in this analysis to ensure homogeneity in perioperative physiological stress, as deceased-donor transplantation is associated with different ischemic conditions, hemodynamic profiles, and surgical complexity that may influence both frailty expression and delirium risk independently. The exclusion criteria were: (1) re-transplantation or combined multi-organ transplantation; (2) documented severe cognitive impairment or dementia; (3) pre-operative coma or minimal conscious state; (4) absence of a documented preoperative nurse-led frailty assessment; and (5) incomplete medical records precluding accurate outcome assessment. Among the 29 patients excluded due to missing frailty assessment, reasons included emergency transplantation without pre-assessment window (n = 12), hemodynamic instability precluding performance-based testing (n = 9), and logistical or administrative omissions (n = 8). The final study cohort comprised 156 eligible patients.

Data collection and variables

Data were systematically extracted from the hospital’s electronic health record system using a standardized data collection form. Collected variables included demographics and clinical characteristics, such as age, sex, BMI, and primary liver disease etiology (e.g., viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease). Preoperative Severity and Laboratory Data: Laboratory Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score, serum sodium, albumin, creatinine, and the need for pre-operative renal replacement therapy (RRT). Comorbidities: Data on the presence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus were extracted from medical records; other comorbidities (e.g., coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) were documented but not systematically included in the primary analysis due to low prevalence and inconsistent documentation. Surgical Parameters: Cold ischemia time (minutes) and total operative time (minutes). Preoperative Frailty Assessment (Exposure): The key exposure variable was preoperative frailty status. As per institutional protocol initiated in 2020, all LT candidates were assessed by a trained transplant nurse coordinator within 48 h prior to surgery. Nurses underwent a structured training program consisting of a 2-hour didactic session on frailty concepts and tool administration, followed by supervised assessments on five patients, with competency confirmed by a certified geriatrician. Formal inter-rater reliability was not assessed during the study period, which represents a limitation. Consequently, we cannot quantify the extent of potential measurement error in frailty assessments, and such error could either attenuate or distort the observed association with POD. Future prospective work should incorporate structured inter-rater reliability testing for the CFS and LFI in this clinical context to support the operationalization of nurse-led screening models. The same nursing staff performed both preoperative frailty assessment and postoperative CAM-ICU evaluations; therefore, knowledge of frailty status may have influenced delirium documentation, potentially introducing information bias and overestimating the observed association.

Limitations of Frailty Assessment: While the CFS and LFI are well-validated instruments in broader and cirrhosis-specific populations respectively, this study did not include a formal re-assessment of inter-rater reliability or criterion validity within our specific cohort of nurses and liver transplant candidates. The application of these tools followed a standardized training protocol but was not subjected to independent validation in this retrospective context.

Assessment included: Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS): A 9-point pictorial and descriptive scale ranging from 1 (very fit) to 9 (terminally ill) was selected for its brevity, feasibility in clinical settings, and established predictive validity for adverse outcomes in surgical and critically ill populations. Although multidimensional tools such as the Groningen Frailty Indicator offer broader domain coverage, the CFS was prioritized to minimize assessment burden and align with routine nursing workflows [15]. The nurse assigned a score based on a holistic clinical assessment. Liver Frailty Index (LFI): A performance-based measure comprising three tests: dominant handgrip strength (kg) measured with a Jamar dynamometer, timed chair stands (seconds for 5 rises), and balance testing (seconds holding three positions) [16]. The results were entered into a validated calculator to derive a continuous score. Patients were classified as “Frail” if they had a CFS score ≥ 5 or an LFI score ≥ 4.5. The CFS cut-off of ≥ 5 was selected based on its established association with adverse outcomes in surgical and critically ill populations [15], while the LFI cut-off of ≥ 4.5 reflects the validated threshold for predicting waitlist mortality in patients with cirrhosis [16, 17]. A “union” approach (CFS ≥ 5 or LFI ≥ 4.5) was prioritized to maximize sensitivity in identifying physiological vulnerability, aligning with the pragmatic goal of screening all at-risk patients in a nurse-led workflow. In cases where frailty classification was discordant (n = 12; frail by one criterion only), patients were included in the frail group to maintain a conservative risk-stratification strategy. All other patients were classified as “Non-frail.”

Outcome Measures: Primary Outcome: Incidence of POD within the first 7 postoperative days. POD was diagnosed using the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU), which has high sensitivity and specificity for delirium in critically ill patients [19]. Per ICU protocol, trained nurses performed CAM-ICU assessments twice daily (morning and evening) for all patients. During the study period, ICU sedation followed a standardized protocol targeting a RASS score of -2 to 0 using propofol or dexmedetomidine as first-line agents, with intermittent boluses of fentanyl for analgesia. Based on available records, we compared the types of sedative agents used and the presence or absence of protocol adherence, but detailed cumulative doses or daily sedation duration were not consistently documented. Sedation practices did not differ significantly between frail and non-frail patients according to these crude indicators. However, meaningful differences in depth, duration, or cumulative exposure to sedatives and opioids could still exist and contribute to residual confounding, which we acknowledge as a limitation. POD was defined as at least one positive CAM-ICU assessment during the observation period. Secondary Outcomes: POD subtype (hypoactive, hyperactive, mixed) based on nurse documentation and Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) patterns. POD severity, represented by the highest CAM-ICU-7 score (a 7-point severity scale derived from CAM-ICU) recorded during the delirium episode. Duration of invasive mechanical ventilation (hours). ICU length of stay (days). Total postoperative hospital length of stay (days). All-cause 30-day mortality.

Statistical analysis

Analyses used SPSS v28.0 and R v4.3.0. Continuous and categorical variables were compared using t-/Mann-Whitney U and Chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests, respectively. Candidates for the final multivariable logistic regression model were identified via univariate analysis (P < 0.1). Potential confounders considered a priori included age, sex, MELD score, diabetes, hypertension, pre-operative RRT, and primary liver disease etiology. Variables such as baseline hepatic encephalopathy, intraoperative transfusion volume, and specific sedative protocols were not consistently documented and could not be included. Before constructing the final model, multicollinearity among predictor variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a threshold of VIF < 5 considered acceptable. Given 47 POD events, the final model included five covariates (frailty, age, MELD, diabetes, pre-op RRT), maintaining an events-per-variable ratio of > 9, which is acceptable for logistic regression. The final model reported adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The robustness of the primary finding (association between frailty and POD) was evaluated through a sensitivity analysis by forcing all clinically relevant baseline variables (Age, Sex, MELD, Diabetes, Hypertension, Pre-op RRT) into an alternative multivariable model, regardless of their univariate P value.

Delirium-free survival was analyzed via Kaplan-Meier (Log-rank test). Subgroup analyses (age ≥ 65, MELD ≥ 25, cholestatic) assessed the frailty-POD association. Significance was set at P < 0.05. To explore a potential dose-response relationship between the severity of frailty and POD severity, the correlation between the ordinal CFS score and the highest recorded CAM-ICU-7 score was evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

During the study period, 215 patients underwent LT. After applying exclusion criteria (re-transplantation, n = 12; severe preoperative cognitive impairment/coma, n = 18; missing frailty assessment, n = 29), 156 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The median age was 58 years (IQR 52–65), and 105 (67.3%) were male. The most common etiology of liver disease was hepatitis B virus-related cirrhosis (36.5%), followed by alcoholic liver disease (24.4%). Based on the nurse-led assessment, 46 of 156 patients (29.5%) were classified as frail, defined as a CFS score ≥ 5 or an LFI score ≥ 4.5.

Patients excluded due to missing frailty assessment (n = 29) had higher median MELD scores (26 vs. 21, P = 0.014) and were more likely to undergo emergency transplantation (41.4% vs. 12.8%, P < 0.001) compared to the included cohort, but did not differ significantly in age or sex distribution. The exclusion of these higher-acuity patients may have resulted in an underestimation of the frailty-POD association, as the most vulnerable patients were not represented in the final analysis.

Postoperative outcomes

Postoperative outcomes are detailed in Table 2. The overall incidence of POD was 30.1% (47/156). The incidence was more than twofold higher in the frail group (52.2% [24/46]) compared to the non-frail group (18.2% [20/110], P < 0.001). Hypoactive was the most common POD subtype in both groups, but the frail group had a significantly higher proportion of mixed-type delirium (33.3% vs. 5.0%, P = 0.028). The median highest CAM-ICU-7 score was also significantly higher in frail patients (4 vs. 3, P = 0.012). Frail patients experienced significantly longer durations of mechanical ventilation (median 28 vs. 18 h, P = 0.002), ICU stay (median 6 vs. 4 days, P < 0.001), and total hospital stay (median 22 vs. 17 days, P = 0.001). The 30-day mortality rate was numerically higher in the frail group (6.5% vs. 1.8%) but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.129). Exploratory analysis of 90-day mortality showed a similar pattern (frail: 10.9% vs. non-frail: 3.6%, P = 0.072), although the study was not powered for mortality outcomes. To assess the robustness of the primary association to the composite frailty definition, we performed sensitivity analyses using alternative frailty classifications. The association between frailty and POD remained significant when frailty was defined by CFS ≥ 5 alone (aOR = 3.89, 95% CI: 1.92–7.88) and when defined by LFI ≥ 4.5 alone (aOR = 4.01, 95% CI: 1.98–8.12). In a three-level sensitivity analysis (non-frail, n = 110; discordant frail, n = 12; concordant frail, n = 34), the aOR for POD was 3.92 (95% CI: 1.53–10.04) in the discordant group and 4.71 (95% CI: 2.01–11.05) in the concordant group, compared with non-frail patients.

Dose-response relationship between frailty and delirium severity

To further elucidate the association, we examined whether a gradient of frailty was linked to a gradient of delirium severity. Analyzing the entire cohort, a significant positive correlation was found between the preoperative CFS score (as an ordinal measure of frailty severity) and the highest postoperative CAM-ICU-7 score (ρ = 0.42, P < 0.001). The observed gradient between frailty severity and delirium severity is compatible with a biological dose-response relationship, but, given the retrospective design and potential residual confounding, it cannot establish causality.

Risk factor analysis for postoperative delirium

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses for POD are presented in Table 3. In univariate analysis, preoperative frailty (OR = 4.88, 95% CI: 2.37–10.05), older age (per year increase, OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02–1.09), higher MELD score (per point increase, OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02–1.10), diabetes (OR = 2.58, 95% CI: 1.27–5.25), and pre-op RRT (OR = 2.71, 95% CI: 1.02–7.18) were significantly associated with POD. In the multivariate model adjusting for these significant univariate factors, preoperative frailty remained the strongest independent predictor (aOR = 4.32, 95% CI: 2.15–8.68). Age (aOR = 1.05 per year, 95% CI: 1.01–1.09) and diabetes (aOR = 2.40, 95% CI: 1.11–5.19) also retained independent significance, while MELD score and pre-op RRT were no longer significant. In the forced-entry sensitivity analysis adjusting for age, sex, MELD, diabetes, hypertension, and pre-operative RRT, frailty remained independently associated with POD (aOR = 4.10, 95% CI: 1.98–8.48, P < 0.001). Age (aOR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.00-1.08, P = 0.042) and diabetes (aOR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.05–4.95, P = 0.037) also remained significant predictors in this model.

Subgroup and survival analyses

Subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of the association between frailty and POD across various patient strata (Table 4). The point estimates for the aOR of frailty were consistently above 4.0 in all subgroups, including older (≥ 65 years) and younger patients, those with high (≥ 25) and low MELD scores, and across different disease etiologies. No significant interaction was found between frailty and these subgrouping variables (P for interaction > 0.05 for all). Kaplan-Meier analysis for delirium-free survival is shown in Fig. 2. The curve for frail patients diverged early and remained significantly lower than that for non-frail patients throughout the 30-day observation period (Log-rank test, χ² = 23.1, P < 0.001). The median delirium-free survival time was substantially shorter in frail patients (4 days; 95% CI: 2–6 days) compared to non-frail patients (30 days; 95% CI: 30–30 days), with the latter group not reaching the median survival within the observation period.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study suggests that a preoperative frailty assessment, conducted by trained transplant nurse coordinators using simple, validated tools, may be independently associated with POD in adult liver transplant recipients. The principal finding-a greater than fourfold increased odds of POD in frail patients, persisting after rigorous adjustment for confounders-highlights the critical role of physiological reserve in determining neurological resilience post-LT. Furthermore, frailty was associated with a more severe delirium phenotype and substantially increased consumption of critical care and hospital resources. The preliminary contribution of this work lies in its pragmatic demonstration that a nurse-led screening protocol may be feasibly integrated into the standard pre-transplant evaluation workflow, suggesting a potential pathway for translating the research concept of frailty into a routine clinical risk indicator. However, this study does not compare the nurse-led approach with physician-led or mixed models, nor does it test whether implementing nurse-led frailty screening improves clinical outcomes. Randomized or quasi-experimental studies will be required to evaluate whether frailty-informed care bundles triggered by such screening can reduce the incidence or severity of POD. This observation suggests a potential shift of frailty assessment from a sporadic, specialist-led activity to a systematic, frontline nursing function, although this remains to be confirmed in prospective designs.

The primary contribution is the operationalization and validation of this workflow in a real-world setting. While the prognostic importance of frailty is increasingly recognized [8, 9], barriers to its clinical implementation often include perceived complexity, time constraints, and uncertainty about who should perform the assessment [20]. The results are consistent with the possibility that transplant nurse coordinators, after targeted training, might administer these assessments with adequate fidelity. This is significant because nurses have sustained patient contact, perform holistic assessments, and are pivotal in care coordination. From a health system perspective, leveraging existing nursing personnel to conduct frailty screening adds no additional variable cost beyond training, making it a resource-efficient strategy that avoids the expense of dedicated specialist-led assessments [14]. Embedding frailty screening into nursing practice ensures it is performed consistently for all candidates. The generated “frailty flag” can then trigger a standardized, multidisciplinary response. Although the pathophysiological basis of POD in frail patients is multifactorial-centered on diminished neurocognitive reserve and amplified neuroinflammatory responses-emerging evidence supports that protocolized interventions such as sedation minimization, early mobilization, and targeted medication management can reduce delirium incidence across surgical populations [21, 22], with frail patients potentially deriving greater absolute benefit due to their heightened baseline vulnerability. Thus, the findings offer preliminary insights that could inform the design of frailty-guided perioperative care bundles aimed at POD mitigation, though such applications require prospective validation.

Our findings extend the growing literature on frailty and poor outcomes in transplantation. Prior studies in LT have predominantly linked frailty to mortality and resource use [17, 18]. We confirm and quantify its strong association with the specific, debilitating outcome of POD. The magnitude of the association (aOR > 4) is substantial and suggests frailty may be an important risk factor for POD in the LT population. This could reflect the convergence of two vulnerability pathways: the systemic dysregulation inherent to the frailty syndrome (e.g., chronic inflammation, sarcopenia) and the unique metabolic and inflammatory milieu of end-stage liver disease [5, 12, 23]. This synergy may create a “perfect storm,” overwhelming the brain’s capacity to cope with the substantial stress of transplantation.

The biological mechanisms linking frailty to POD in LT recipients are multifactorial. Frailty is characterized by dysregulation across multiple systems, including a pro-inflammatory state (“inflammaging”) and endocrine dysfunction [8, 24]. LT surgery acts as a profound second hit, triggering a massive release of inflammatory cytokines that may lead to an exaggerated and sustained neuroinflammatory response in frail individuals. A frail individual’s diminished physiological reserve may lead to an exaggerated and sustained neuroinflammatory response, disrupting key neurotransmitter systems (notably cholinergic and dopaminergic pathways) and precipitating neuronal dysfunction manifesting as delirium [10, 25]. These mechanistic hypotheses remain inferential, and the observational design of this study precludes definitive causal conclusions. Moreover, frailty, especially its sarcopenic component common in cirrhosis, alters the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of essential perioperative medications, including sedatives, opioids, and tacrolimus, potentially increasing central nervous system exposure and neurotoxicity-a known trigger for delirium. This mechanistic link is supported by studies demonstrating that sarcopenia reduces drug clearance and volume of distribution for lipophilic agents, while cirrhosis further impairs hepatic metabolism, collectively predisposing to heightened drug sensitivity and delirium risk [26]. The observed higher rate of mixed-type delirium in frail patients, coupled with the identified dose-response relationship between CFS score and CAM-ICU-7 severity (ρ = 0.42, P < 0.001),strengthens the argument for a dose-dependent pathophysiological link by demonstrating that increasing frailty severity correlates with greater delirium severity, consistent with a biological gradient that supports causality. These findings suggest that the degree of physiological dysregulation in frailty may proportionally compromise the brain’s resilience, leading to not only a higher incidence but also a more severe and complex phenotype of delirium following the major physiological insult of transplantation. It is important to note that interventions such as sedation minimization and early mobilization are not exclusive to frail patients; rather, they represent evidence-based practices that benefit the broader surgical population. However, frail patients may be particularly susceptible to the neurotoxic effects of sedatives and opioids due to altered pharmacokinetics and reduced physiological reserve, making adherence to these protocols especially critical in this subgroup. The generalizability of these findings to other transplant populations warrants consideration. Our cohort was predominantly composed of patients with hepatitis B virus-related cirrhosis (36.5%) and alcoholic liver disease (24.4%), reflecting the etiological distribution in the region. In Western populations where non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) is increasingly prevalent, frailty phenotypes may differ due to associated metabolic comorbidities, and the predictive performance of the CFS and LFI may vary. Additionally, listing criteria, perioperative care pathways, and ICU sedation practices differ across centers, which may influence both frailty prevalence and POD incidence. Therefore, external validation in diverse clinical settings is essential before broad implementation.

Although the 30-day mortality rate was numerically higher in the frail group (6.5% vs. 1.8%), this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.129). As the study was designed and powered to assess the association with POD rather than mortality, conclusions regarding survival should remain cautious. This finding warrants careful interpretation. The lack of significance is likely multifactorial. First, our study may have been underpowered to detect a difference in mortality, as it was primarily designed and powered to assess the association with POD. Second, the relatively short 30-day follow-up period may not capture the full impact of frailty and POD on longer-term survival, as the deleterious effects of these conditions often manifest over months [3]. Third, intensive perioperative support in a specialized transplant ICU might have mitigated early fatal complications in frail patients, potentially masking an underlying mortality risk that could become apparent with longer observation. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are needed to definitively characterize the frailty-mortality relationship in this population.

This study has limitations inherent to its design. First, the retrospective, single-center nature introduces risks of selection bias and unmeasured confounding, including subclinical hepatic encephalopathy, specific drug exposures, and detailed sedation or anesthesia protocols, which may influence both frailty classification and POD risk independently. Second, findings may not be fully generalizable to all transplant centers. Third, while we used validated tools, the combined frailty definition (CFS ≥ 5 or LFI ≥ 4.5), though clinically pragmatic, requires further validation. Future research should focus on: (1) prospective, multicenter validation of this nurse-led model; (2) evaluation of whether interventions triggered by frailty screening (e.g., a “frailty-informed perioperative bundle”) can reduce POD incidence and severity; and (3) exploration of underlying biomarkers to refine risk prediction and elucidate mechanisms.

Based on the findings and limitations of this study, several directions for future research are warranted. First, multicenter prospective validation of the nurse-led frailty assessment workflow is needed, including formal inter-rater reliability testing for the CFS and LFI. Second, intervention studies should be designed to test whether frailty-informed perioperative care bundles-such as sedation minimization, early mobilization, and tailored medication protocols-can reduce the incidence or severity of POD in liver transplant recipients. Third, exploration of additional biomarkers or neurocognitive measures may help refine risk stratification and elucidate underlying mechanisms linking frailty to POD.

Conclusion

This study suggests that a nurse-led preoperative frailty assessment using the Clinical Frailty Scale and Liver Frailty Index may represent a potentially useful correlate of postoperative delirium in liver transplant recipients, though further validation is needed. Our findings demonstrate feasibility and potential clinical relevance of this workflow but do not validate its superiority over other models or establish its effect on patient outcomes. Frail patients are at markedly increased risk for early and severe delirium, leading to prolonged critical care support and hospitalization. These findings suggest that integrating this low-cost, feasible screening into the standard preoperative nursing evaluation for all LT candidates may represent a potentially useful approach for early risk stratification. If confirmed in future studies, such identification could help inform the development and implementation of targeted, multidisciplinary preventive care pathways, with the ultimate goal of improving neurological outcomes and recovery in this vulnerable population.
