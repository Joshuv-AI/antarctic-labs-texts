Immunopeptidomics of blood coagulation factor IX: a core peptide derived from the protease domain is promiscuously presented on HLA-DR
Authors: Michela Leoni, Margot Dierickx, Mariarosaria Miranda, Pieter Langerhorst, Carmen van der Zwaan, Erik Mul, Arie J Hoogendijk, Karin Fijnvandraat, Jan Voorberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHemophilia B is an X-linked bleeding disorder that results in a qualitative or quantitative factor IX (FIX) deficiency. A subset of hemophilia B patients treated with FIX replacement therapy develop antibodies against FIX. The development of FIX inhibitors can present as allergic or anaphylactic reactions in some patients. It is currently unknown why a subset of hemophilia B patients develop inhibitors. CD4+ T cells recognizing FIX-derived peptides on major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II on antigen-presenting cells are crucial for the development of class-switched, high-affinity anti-FIX antibodies.ObjectivesIn this study, we explored MHC class II presentation of FIX-derived peptides by antigen presenting cells.MethodsWe incubated purified FIX with monocyte derived dendritic cells of healthy, human leukocyte antigen-typed donors. MHCII peptide complexes were immunopurified, followed by peptide elution and mass spectrometry-based analysis of eluted peptides.ResultsWe analyzed 12 healthy donors; of them, 8 presented FIX-derived peptides. The majority of FIX peptides were derived from the serine protease domain of FIX. With the exception of 1 donor, all of them presented at least one peptide containing the same core sequence, “NVIRIIPHHN” (residues 295-306 of FIX).ConclusionsTaken together, we identified a promiscuously presented peptide derived from the protease domain of FIX. Based on its frequent presentation in multiple donors, we speculate that this peptide may be recognized by FIX-specific CD4+ T cells that develop in hemophilia B patients with inhibitors. Peptides identified in our study can be used for inhibitor risk stratification and development of novel therapies to induce tolerance.Keywords: blood coagulation, hemophilia B, HLA-DR, peptides, mass spectrometry

Introduction

Hemophilia B is an X-linked bleeding disorder, characterized by genetic defects within the F9 gene that results in either qualitative or quantitative factor IX (FIX) deficiency [1]. The incidence of hemophilia B is 1 in 30,000 males; historically, hemophilia B patients have been treated with infusion of purified plasma or recombinant FIX to prevent joint bleeds. Depending on the remaining FIX activity, patients are classified as severe (<1%), moderate (1%-5%) and mild (5%-40%). The circulatory half-life of FIX ranges from 16 to 18 hours, and to maintain therapeutic levels, 2 to 3 weekly infusions of FIX are needed [2]. Recently, extended half-life products composed of PEGylated FIX, FIX-Fc, and FIX-albumin fusions with a 3- to 4-fold prolonged half-life have been developed, allowing for once weekly dosing of FIX [3]. Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene therapy for FIX has been successfully developed and has now been approved by the European Medicines Agency and the US Food and Drug Administration for clinical use in patients with hemophilia B [4,5]. Both AAV5-based as well as AAV8-based vectors are being used; the naturally occurring gain-of-function FIX Padua (R338L) variant has been used to boost expression of functional FIX approximately 5-fold [6]. In contrast to the decline in FVIII expression in gene therapy trials employing AAV5, current data show sustained and very stable levels of FIX in patients treated with both AAV5- and/or AAV8-based gene therapy vectors in which the FIX Padua variant is expressed under the control of a liver-specific promoter [7,8].

FIX is one of the vitamin K-dependent blood coagulation factors, a group that also includes protein C, protein S, prothrombin, FVII, and FX. FIX is composed of different domains: the Gla domain at the N-terminus contains multiple Ca2+ binding ɣ-carboxy glutamic acid residues, 2 epidermal growth factor (EGF)-like domains, the first of which contains a Ca2+ binding site [9]. The EGF domains are followed by an activation peptide that is released during activation of FIX by FXIa or the tissue factor/activated FVII (FVIIa) complex [10]. The carboxy terminal serine protease domain of FIX contains a metal ion-binding site as well as the active site composed of a catalytic triad composed of Ser195, His57, and Asp102 (chymotrypsin numbering) [11,12].

Similar to patients with hemophilia A, patients with hemophilia B can develop antibodies against FIX that interfere with treatment. These so-called FIX inhibitors occur in 1% to 4% of patients with hemophilia B [13,14]. Patients who develop FIX inhibitors tend to develop allergic or anaphylactic reactions prior to or during inhibitor formation for reasons that are incompletely understood. One study suggested that anaphylactic shocks are due to relatively high dosage of FIX used for standard infusions when compared with FVIII [15]. Additionally, diffusion of FIX in the extracellular space has been suggested to contribute to the risk of anaphylactic shocks and allergic reactions [15]. IgE-mediated reactions directed toward FIX have also been proposed as a potential mediator of allergic reactions in patients with hemophilia B [16]. The risk of inhibitors is strictly associated with the severity of hemophilia and with the type of F9 mutations [17,18]. Inhibitors develop in 0.4% of patients carrying missense, splice site, or promoter mutations, whereas inhibitors develop in 5% of patients with nonsense and frameshift mutations [19,20]. Importantly, the risk of inhibitors in hemophilia B patients with large F9 deletions extending beyond 50 base pairs is relatively high at 27% [20].

FIX inhibitors are not as well characterized as those for hemophilia A, but previous studies showed that the epitopes recognized by inhibitory antibodies localize to the Gla and the serine protease domains of FIX [17]. The EGF domains and the activation peptide do not seem to contain any binding sites for inhibitory antibodies [17]. Subclass analysis revealed that FIX inhibitors are primarily composed of IgG1 and IgG4; in some patients, anti-FIX antibodies of subclass IgE have been described that may be linked to anaphylactic reactions and allergic responses in patients with hemophilia B [21].

Currently, there is limited information on the processing and presentation of FIX-derived peptides by antigen-presenting cells. Saini et al. [22] reported on genetic determinants of immunogenicity to FIX in 5 hemophilia B patients with inhibitors. In silico major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II peptide binding studies suggested that the ability of MHC class II alleles to present FIX-derived peptides was linked to the efficacy of immune tolerance induction in a pair of brothers with hemophilia B.

In this study, we explored the repertoire of FIX-derived peptides that is presented on human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DR upon pulsing of monocyte-derived dendritic cells (DCs) with FIX. The DCs used were at the immature stage (iDCs) when pulsed with FIX to allow for optimal endocytosis of FIX and subsequently maturated with lipopolysaccharide for optimal peptide presentation on MHC class II [[23], [24], [25]]. Our findings indicate that a peptide derived from the protease domain of FIX is promiscuously presented on multiple HLA-DR alleles.

Methods

Reagents

Reagents included Ficoll-Paque PLUS (Cytiva), anti-CD14 magnetic MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec), CellGenix GMP DC Medium (Sartorius), 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution: 100 IU/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Sigma), granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, interleukin-4 (PeptroTech), Nonafact (ultrapure, monoclonal antibody-purified factor IX concentrate; Sanquin [26]), Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen), Amicon ultracentrifugal filter, 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff (Millipore), anti-CD209 IgG2a phycoerythrin, clone FAB1621P (R&D Systems), and Alexa Fluor 647 (AF647) antibody labelling kit (Thermo Fisher).

HLA-DR peptide enrichment and sample preparation for mass spectrometry

Cells were detached from plates with scrapers and washed twice with CellGenix medium. The cells were collected and lysed in 1.5 mL of lysis buffer composed of 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 0.25% n-octyl-β-d-glucopyranoside (Sigma), 1% desoxycholate (bioPLUS), and 10 mM EDTA (UltraPure 0.5 M EDTA pH 8.0; Invitrogen). Lysates were incubated end-over-end for 30 minutes at 4 °C. The lysate was cleared by centrifugation at18,000g for 20 minutes at 4 °C. To isolate the HLA-DR-peptide complexes, the cell lysate was incubated together with CNBr-activated SepFast MAG 4HF magnetic beads (BioToolomics) coupled to anti-human HLA-DR antibody L243 (final concentration 2 mg/mL; source InVivoMAb, Bio X Cell) overnight at 4 °C with end-over-end rotation. Each sample was divided into triplicate, over 300 μL of L243 conjugated beads. Beads were washed twice with lysis buffer and 5 times with 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0. Peptides were eluted from the beads using 500 μL 10% acetic acid (LC-MS LiChropur, Merck). The supernatant was collected and incubated for 15 minutes at 70 °C and then frozen at −30 °C until mass spectrometry analysis.

Mass spectrometry analysis

Mass spectrometry analysis was performed as described previously [27]. Briefly, we loaded samples on Evotip PureTips (Evosep), which were eluted onto an EV1137 Performance column (Evosep), and the gradient was set to 15 samples per day. TimsTOF HT Mass Spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) was used for acquisition and operated in ddsPASEF mode.

HLA-DR peptide search and identification

The mass-spectrometry files were analyzed using MSFragger (v 3.8) in the FragPipe (v20) pipeline as described previously [27,28]. Peptides and proteins were identified using the filtered human Swissprot database (released April 22, 2021) with 50% added decoys. The peak precursor mass tolerance was set at −20 to +20 ppm with a fragment mass tolerance of 10 ppm. The peptide mass range was set to 600 to 3000 and the peptide length to 8 to 30 amino acids, and protein digestion was set as nonspecific. The allowed modifications were γ-carboxylation of glutamic acids, N-terminal acetylation, methionine oxidation, pyroglutamylation, and cysteinylation. Peptides were quantified using IonQuant (v1.9.8) using default settings without allowing match between runs.

ImageStream

FIX (Nonafact) was dissolved in 10 mL water and concentrated with an Amicon spin column; FIX was labelled with AF647 following manufacturer’s instructions. iDCs were incubated with 100 nM labeled FIX for 1 hour and stained for a membrane marker (CD209) and Hoechst 33342. Images were acquired on an imaging flow cytometer (Amnis ImageStream Imaging Flow Cytometer) as described previously [29].

Results

Immunopeptidomics of FIX by antigen-presenting cells

As a first step toward the identification of HLA-DR-presented FIX peptides, we monitored the internalization of FIX by antigen-presenting cells. We employed iDCs that were incubated with 100 nM AF647-labeled FIX; uptake was monitored by ImageStream (Figure 1). AF647-labeled FIX was incubated with iDCs for 1 hour at 37 °C. At this time point, most of the iDCs contained AF647-labeled FIX; representative images obtained are shown in Figure 1. Fixed cells were also stained for CD209, which is highly expressed on the surface of iDCs. Together these findings indicate that FIX is internalized by iDCs.

Uptake of FIX by dendritic cells. Internalization of FIX was monitored by ImageStream. Representative images are shown. Brightfield and fluorescence images of iDCs after incubation with 100 nM of AF647-labeled FIX (pseudocolored to red); iDCs are also stained for DC-SIGN (CD209, in yellow); nuclei are stained with Hoechst (violet). FIX, factor IX; iDC, immature dendritic cell.

Having shown that FIX is readily endocytosed by antigen-presenting cells, we subsequently purified peptide-MHC class II complexes from DCs pulsed with 100 nM FIX employing monoclonal antibody L243, which specifically binds to HLA-DR. Subsequently, HLA-DR-bound peptides were eluted from HLA-DR using acetic acid and analyzed by mass spectrometry. For donor 1 (HLA-DRB1∗01/13), the number of peptides eluted from HLA-DR ranged from 2227 to 2488 (Figure 2A, B). Peptides presented on HLA-DR were derived from 511 to 544 unique proteins (Figure 2C). The number of peptides derived from the isotype control immunoprecipitations ranged from 577 to 939 peptides derived from 200 to 250 unique proteins (Figure 2B, C). The length distribution of peptides derived from the isotype control ranged between 8 and 30 residues, whereas the majority of L243-derived peptides had a length of 15 to 25 amino acid residues (Figure 2D). HLA-DR-presented peptides were mostly derived from intracellular proteins present in the cytosol, plasma membrane, and endolysosomal compartments; this also included peptides derived from MHC class II itself (Figure 3A). For donor 1, a total of 10 peptides derived from human FIX were detected for the 3 replicates (Figures 2A, 3C). Strikingly, all 10 peptides contained the core sequence NVIRIIPHHNYNA that corresponds to amino acids 295 to 307 of human FIX (Figure 3C). This indicates that this specific FIX peptide, which is derived from the protease domain of FIX, is preferentially presented in HLA-DR. The panel of HLA-DR-presented FIX peptides contained a number of larger peptides with amino-terminal and carboxy-terminal extensions. This is consistent with the typical ‘proteolytic signature’ of MHC class II-presented peptides, in which the core peptide is protected from proteolysis by virtue of its presence in the binding pocket of MHC class II. Amino-terminal and carboxy-terminal extensions of core peptides are subjected to processing by endosomal and lysosomal proteases after their loading on MHC class II [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]].

Analysis of FIX-presented peptides on MHC class II

To further characterize the repertoire of FIX-derived peptides presented on HLA-DR, we used DCs of 11 additional healthy donors with a known HLA-DRB1 genotype. In agreement with the results obtained for donor 1, the number of peptides detected using the isotype control was much lower than obtained using L243-coupled beads (Supplementary Table 1). Similar to donor 1, the length of peptides derived from the isotype control ranged between 8 and 30 amino acids, whereas the HLA-DR-presented peptides displayed were primarily between 14 and 18 amino acid residues (Supplementary Figure 1). As expected, we did not observe any FIX-derived peptides in DCs that were not pulsed with FIX (Supplementary Table 1). The total number of HLA-DR-presented peptides observed for the different donors ranged between 1459 and 4619 (Figure 4A, B), which were derived from 381 to 1028 unique proteins (Figure 4A, C). The number of FIX-derived peptides was consistent among the 3 replicates and ranged from 0 FIX-derived peptides for donors 2, 6, 10, and 11 and up to 14 FIX-derived peptides for donor 8 (Figure 4A). In 8 of the 12 donors analyzed, we detected at least 1 FIX peptide (Figure 4A).

Number of HLA-DR-presented peptides and proteins of a cohort of 12 donors. (A) Number of peptides, proteins, and FIX-derived peptides from a panel of 12 donors. For each donor, 3 replicates were performed. (B, C) Graphical representation of the number of peptides and proteins recovered for each of the 12 donors. Dark green bars represent the number of peptides and light green bars represent the number of proteins obtained after immunoprecipitations employing HLA-DR antibody L243. FIX, factor IX; HLA, human leukocyte antigen.

Strikingly, 6 of 12 donors presented peptides containing the core sequence NVIRIIPHHNYN (residues 295-306). Inspection of the HLA-DRB1 genotype (Supplementary Table 2) suggests that peptides containing this core sequence can be presented by multiple HLA-DRB1 alleles. We employed NetMHCIIpan-4.1 [35] for the in silico prediction of FIX-derived peptides presented on HLA-DRB1 alleles. Bioinformatic analysis predicted that core sequence 292-QKRNVIRIIPHHNYNAAINK-306 was indeed presented by multiple HLA-DRB1 alleles. Peptides derived from peptide sequence 292 to 306 were predicted as being weak to strong binders for almost all HLA-DRB1 alleles (Supplementary Figure 2 and 3). The prediction of presented peptides derived from other regions of FIX did not fully align with our experimental data; some of the predicted peptides were not identified in our donors, and some of the peptides that we found were not predicted to be strong binders by NetMHCIIpan-4.1 (Supplementary Figure 2).

Donor 12 presented a unique WIVTAAHCVETGVK (residues 261-275) peptide that was not detected in other donors. This peptide has been detected with a cysteinylation (+119.0041 C); this modification can either occur in vivo before peptide loading or after the immunoprecipitation of the MHC II-peptide complex during sample preparation [36,37]. Interestingly, donor 8 (DRB1∗16/16) presented multiple peptides derived from the FIX protease domain (Figure 4). Apart from the core peptide NVIRIIPHHNYN (residues 295-306), 3 additional peptides derived from the protease domain of FIX were presented on HLA-DRB1∗16 of this specific donor. The data obtained suggests that multiple peptides derived from amino acid region 312 to 340 can be presented by DCs derived from the HLA-DRB1∗16 homozygous donor (Figure 5). In addition, a series of carboxy-terminal trimmed peptides derived from amino acid residues 443 to 459 were presented by DCs derived from donor 8. For 4 of the 12 donors, no FIX peptide presentation was detected. One of these 4 donors (donor 6) was homozygous for DRB1∗04, which may suggest that FIX-derived peptides are not efficiently presented by DRB1∗04. Despite the lack of detected FIX-derived peptides, a large repertoire of peptides derived of endogenous proteins was found to be presented on DCs derived from donors 2, 6, 10, and 11. This finding suggests that the lack of presentation of FIX-derived peptides on HLA-DR was not linked to a general defect in peptide presentation in these donors.

Discussion

The development of inhibitory antibodies toward FIX in patients with hemophilia B is a rare event occurring in 1% to 5% of patients, usually within 50 exposure days [20,38,39]. The risk of development of FIX inhibitors is strongly related to the F9 gene mutation; hemophilia B patients with inhibitors almost exclusively have large F9 deletions and nonsense mutations [20,40]. The frequent occurrence of missense mutations and large deletions suggest that lack of endogenous FIX contributes to the risk of inhibitor development in patients with hemophilia B [13]. Nevertheless, inhibitors only develop in 27% to 30% of hemophilia B patients carrying high-risk mutations, suggesting that other genetic and/or treatment-related risk factors may contribute to inhibitor development in hemophilia B [20].

In the present study, we showed that a FIX-derived peptide with the core binding sequence NVIRIIPHHNYN (residues 295-306) was presented by multiple donors. In silico prediction employing NetMHCIIpan-4.1 revealed that peptides derived from this region were predicted to bind with moderate to strong affinity to almost all HLA-DRB1 alleles (Supplementary Figures 2 and 3). Based on our data, we propose that CD4+ T cells recognizing this peptide may contribute to inhibitor development in patients with hemophilia B. Interestingly, 295-NVIRIIPHHNYNA-306 containing peptides were not detected in all donors. A link between MHC class II genotype and inhibitor development in hemophilia B has not yet been established. A large international study on the natural history of hemophilia B (the B-Natural study) has recently been initiated, which aims to explore a potential relationship between MHC class II genotype and inhibitor development in hemophilia B [41]. It will be of interest to see whether HLA-DR molecules that can present FIX-derived peptides containing the 295-NVIRIIPHHNYNA-306 core sequence are more prevalent in hemophilia B patients with inhibitors when compared with patients with high-risk mutations without inhibitors.

Donor 8 (DRB1∗16/16) presented an unusually rich peptide repertoire from the FIX protease domain. None of the hemophilia B patients with inhibitors reported in a previous study was positive for DRB1∗16 [22]. Interestingly, DRB1∗16 has been associated with acquired hemophilia A as well as other autoimmune disorders [42]. Based on the large number of FIX peptides that are presented on DRB1∗16, we expect that the presence of the HLA-DRB1∗16 allele in hemophilia B patients with a large deletion in the F9 gene may provoke inhibitor development. Genetic analysis of large cohorts of patients is needed to address whether HLA-DRB1∗16 is a risk factor for inhibitor development in hemophilia B patients with large deletions.

We also found a peptide that contains Arg384, the amino acid that is modified in the Padua variant of FIX, commonly referred to as R338L (alternating numbering caused by inclusion of 46 residues derived of the signal and propeptide included in the UniProt numbering used in this study). This may be relevant because FIX Padua is the variant used for gene therapy in hemophilia B. However, based on our analysis, amino acid Arg384 is not part of the core peptide presented by donor 8, as shown in Figure 5. Formal immunopeptidomic studies employing the FIX Padua variant would be needed to address potential immunogenicity of FIX Padua. So far, no FIX inhibitors have been reported in AAV gene therapy trials that employ FIX Padua [43]. Together, this suggests that it is unlikely that Leu384-containing peptides (FIX Padua-derived peptides) are presented on MHC class II.

Apart from hemophilia B, inhibitory antibodies may also develop in patients with FVII deficiency upon initiation of replacement therapy [44]. Interestingly, in patients with FVII deficiency, inhibitor development is also linked to the large deletions and nonsense mutations in the F7 gene [44]. FVIIa has been successfully developed as a bypassing agent for treatment of hemophilia A patients with inhibitors [45]. Bioengineered FVIIa variants with improved catalytic activity have been designed [46]. Upon in vivo testing of these bioengineered variants, anti-drug antibodies were developed in a subset of patients [47,48]. The engineered FVIIa variant (designated vatreptocog alfa) contained 3 amino acid substitutions when compared with wild-type FVIIa (V158D, E296V, and M298Q) [49]. Immunopeptidomics revealed that a peptide containing the E296V and M298Q substitution was preferentially presented by DCs of 12 out of 17 donors [49]. Subsequent analysis showed that the modified peptide with amino acid sequence 351-GATALVLQVLNVPR-365 (modified residues in bold/underlined) was capable of inducing proliferation of CD4+ T cells derived from normal donors [47]. These data show that changes in the protease domain of bioengineered FVIIa allowed for presentation of a neo-epitope on MHC class II, which promoted development of an immune responses in recipients of this bioengineered FVIIa variant [49]. Alignment of the FIX and FVII sequences did not show overlap between the FVII peptide sequence and the 295-NVIRIIPHHNYN-306 FIX core sequence that was identified in this study. It is interesting to note that both FVII- and FIX-presented peptides are derived from the protease domain.

Similar to other vitamin K-dependent proteases, FIX is extensively modified by posttranslational modifications [50]. Vitamin K-dependent coagulation proteases can bind to phospholipids by virtue of their ɣ-carboxyglutamic acid domain (Gla domain), which contains glutamic acid residues that are modified by a vitamin K-dependent ɣ-glutamyl carboxylase [51]. The Gla domain of FIX contains 12 ɣ-carboxyglutamic acid residues [50]. In addition to ɣ-carboxylation, residue Asp64 is modified by β-hydroxylation. Residues Tyr155 and Ser158 in the activation peptide of FIX are modified by tyrosine sulfation and phosphorylation, respectively [50]. O-glycosylation sites are present at Thr159, Thr169, Thr172, Thr179, Ser53, and Ser61, and N-glycans are linked to Asn157 and Asn167 [50]. Interestingly, posttranslational modifications of FIX are located in the Gla domain, the 2 EGF domains, and the activation peptide; no posttranslational modifications have been identified in the serine protease of FIX. In our data processing, we took into account potential posttranslational modifications such as ɣ-carboxylation and β-hydroxylation of presented peptides with the FragPipe software used for the analysis of the presented peptide. The detection of ɣ-carboxylated peptides is challenging due to spontaneous loss of CO2 during mass fragmentation [52]. In addition, application of polarity switching positive and negative ionization modes has been recommended for optimal detection of ɣ-carboxylated peptides [52]. Implementation of this specific protocol for the detection of ɣ-carboxylated peptides can potentially extend the findings documented in the current study [48,49]. Potentially, also due to their inherent heterogeneity, O-linked glycan-containing peptides derived of the EGF1 and AP domains may have been missed by the data processing applied in this study [53,54]. In this respect, it is interesting to note that O-linked glycan-containing peptides have been shown to be presented on MHC class I [31].

Several methods for mass spectrometry-based identification of MHC class II-presented peptides have been described in the literature [31,55]. Mild acid elution-based methods utilizing living cells to directly elute peptides from surface-expressed MHC class I provide an elegant approach for the isolation of MHC class I-presented peptides [34,56]. Immunopeptidomics of MHC class II involves purification of MHC class II–peptide complexes employing monoclonal antibodies directed toward HLA-DR, HLA-DQ, or HLA-DP [55]. In this and previous studies, we have successfully used L243 to explore HLA-DR-presented peptide repertoires of human monocyte-derived DCs pulsed with blood coagulation FVIII and ADAMTS13 [57,58]. MSFragger-based analysis of the resulting spectra consistently yielded 2000 to 5000 different MHC class II-presented peptides, which included commonly HLA-DR-presented FIX peptides with core sequence NVIRIIPHHNYN. It should be noted that a considerable number of peptides were also recovered from isotype controls (Figure 2), and we hypothesize that these peptides correspond to non–HLA-DR-binding peptides that are derived from proteolytic degradation of endogenous cellular proteins. Inspection of the peptide lengths that were recovered from the isotype control did not reveal amino- and carboxy-terminal trimming, which is a specific feature of MHC class II-presented peptides. We are currently optimizing our purification protocol for MHC class II–peptide complexes to further reduce the background within our isotype controls. We did not recover any FIX-derived peptides from the isotype controls, and therefore we hypothesize that our data on the presentation of FIX-derived peptides are not affected by the relatively high background observed in the isotype controls.

Overall, our findings highlight the presence of an immunodominant peptide derived from the protease domain of FIX that can bind to multiple MHC class II alleles. Future studies employing analysis of CD4+ T cell responses should reveal whether FIX peptides containing the core sequence 295-NVIRIIPHHNYN-306 contribute to the development of inhibitors in patients with hemophilia B. Additionally, our results suggest that MHC class II genotype could also be relevant for the risk of inhibitor development in hemophilia B. Potentially the identification of a promiscuously presented peptide, as reported in this study, can be exploited for the design of novel peptide-based approaches for treatment of inhibitors in patients with hemophilia B.
