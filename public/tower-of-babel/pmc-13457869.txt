Effect of Ni and Mg incorporation methods into Li₄SiO₄ derived from natural zeolite on CO₂ methanation performance
Authors: M M Mambetova, G Y Yergaziyeva, L K Myltykbayeva, N M Makayeva, M M Anissova, Kh Avchukir, K Dossumov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study investigates the effect of Ni and Mg oxide incorporation methods into Li₄SiO₄ synthesized from natural zeolite of the Taizhuzgen deposit on the structure and catalytic performance in CO₂ methanation. Ni/Li₄SiO₄, Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ prepared by capillary impregnation (CI), and Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ synthesized via solution combustion (SC) at a Li: Si ratio of 1.5:1 were evaluated in the CO₂ + 4 H₂ → CH₄ + 2 H₂O reaction at 250–550 °C. XRD, SEM–EDS, H₂-TPR, CO₂-TPD, XPS, TEM/HRTEM, and TGA analyses revealed correlations between phase composition, reducibility of Ni species, basicity, and catalytic behavior. Li₄SiO₄ formed predominantly as orthosilicate (~ 93 wt%) and acted as an effective support for Ni and Ni–Mg catalysts. The Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst, characterized by uniformly dispersed Ni particles and balanced medium and strong basic sites, achieved 75–78% CO₂ conversion and 87–88% CH₄ selectivity at 450–500 °C. In contrast, the more porous Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst with predominantly strong basic sites reached complete CO₂ conversion but showed lower CH₄ selectivity (20–50%) due to enhancement of the RWGS pathway. Stability tests showed higher coke formation for the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst, whereas the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst exhibited lower carbon deposition and greater stability.Keywords: Natural zeolites, Ni–Mg-containing catalysts, CO₂ methanation, DecarbonizationSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

One of the key challenges of modern chemical technology is the reduction of atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO₂), the major greenhouse gas responsible for climate change1–3. The atmospheric CO₂ concentration is currently increasing at a rate of approximately 1.5–3 ppm per year, reaching a level of ~ 412 ppm4,5. In this context, increasing attention has been devoted to carbon capture and utilization (CCU) technologies, which not only reduce CO₂ emissions but also enable its conversion into value-added products3.

Among CCU approaches, CO₂ methanation (the Sabatier reaction) has attracted significant interest due to its high CH₄ selectivity, compatibility with existing gas infrastructure, and potential for energy storage in the form of synthetic natural gas. These features make it a promising route for both emission reduction and renewable energy storage in chemical form6.

A substantial body of research has focused on the development of Ni-based catalysts, which combine high catalytic activity with relatively low cost, making them attractive for practical applications7–10. It has been established that their catalytic performance is strongly influenced by the nature of the support, Ni dispersion, reducibility of the active phase, and surface basicity, which governs CO₂ adsorption and activation11.

Among solid inorganic materials, lithium orthosilicate (Li₄SiO₄) has attracted particular attention as an effective high-temperature CO₂ sorbent and a promising support for catalytic systems12,13. Li₄SiO₄ exhibits strong basicity, high thermal stability, and good compatibility with transition metals, making it a suitable platform for the development of Ni-based methanation catalysts14,15. However, most studies have focused either on CO₂ capture or on improving the sorption capacity of Li₄SiO₄, while the influence of metal incorporation methods and alkaline-earth promoters on the structure and catalytic performance of Li₄SiO₄-based catalysts remains insufficiently explored16,17.

The use of natural mineral resources as silica precursors for Li₄SiO₄ synthesis offers additional advantages in terms of cost reduction and the development of specific structural and textural properties. Recent studies have shown that Li₄SiO₄ derived from natural silicates can exhibit improved textural and sorption properties compared to materials synthesized from commercial SiO₂18. Nevertheless, studies addressing Li₄SiO₄ derived from natural sources specifically as a support for Ni- and Ni–Mg-based CO₂ methanation catalysts remain limited. Therefore, the development of Ni- and Ni–Mg-based catalysts supported on Li₄SiO₄ derived from natural zeolite, as well as the investigation of the influence of metal incorporation methods on phase composition, morphology, reducibility of Ni species, distribution of basic sites, and ultimately catalytic activity and selectivity in CO₂ hydrogenation to methane, represents a relevant and timely research objective.

The aim of this study was to develop and comprehensively characterize Ni- and Ni–Mg-based catalysts supported on Li₄SiO₄ derived from natural zeolite for CO₂ methanation, and to establish correlations between their structural, morphological, redox, and acid–base properties and their catalytic activity and selectivity in CO₂ hydrogenation to methane.

The scientific novelty of this work can be summarized as follows. The feasibility of synthesizing Li₄SiO₄ as the dominant phase from Tayzhuzgen natural zeolite at a Li: Si molar ratio of 1.5:1 is demonstrated, and its application as a support for Ni- and Ni–Mg-based CO₂ methanation catalysts is validated. A comparative study of two methods for Ni and Mg incorporation (incipient wetness impregnation and solution combustion) into the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ system is presented, revealing their impact on phase composition, morphology, Ni reducibility, and the distribution of surface basic sites. This approach enabled the identification of key parameters governing methane selectivity.

It is shown that the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst, characterized by an optimal balance between medium- and strong-strength basic sites and highly reducible Ni⁰ species, exhibits the highest CH₄ selectivity and predominantly follows a carbonate–formate reaction pathway. In contrast, the excess of strong basic sites in the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst promotes the CO-mediated pathway (RWGS), leading to reduced methane selectivity.

Experimental part

Materials

Natural zeolite from the Tayzhuzgen deposit (Kazakhstan) was used as a silica precursor. Lithium nitrate (LiNO₃, ≥ 99.0%), nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O, ≥ 99.0%), and magnesium nitrate hexahydrate (Mg(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O, ≥ 99.0%) were purchased from Laborfarm (Almaty, Kazakhstan) and used as received. Urea was employed as a fuel (reducing agent) in the solution combustion synthesis. Distilled water was used for the preparation of all aqueous solutions.

Preparation of sorbent–catalyst materials

Li₄SiO₄ was synthesized from natural zeolite and used as a support for catalyst preparation. The zeolite was mixed with LiNO₃ at Li: Si molar ratios of 1.5:1, 2:1, and 4:1, homogenized in a ball mill (300 rpm, 10 min), and calcined at 750 °C for 4 h. The sample prepared at Li: Si = 1.5:1 was selected for further modification.

Three catalysts were prepared: Ni/Li₄SiO₄, Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI), and Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC).

Ni and Ni–Mg catalysts obtained by impregnation (Ni/Li₄SiO₄ and Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI)) were prepared by incipient wetness impregnation using aqueous solutions of Ni(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O or a mixture of Ni(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O and Mg(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O. After impregnation, the samples were kept at room temperature for 12 h, followed by drying at 300 °C for 2 h and calcination at 500 °C for 3 h.

The Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst was synthesized via the solution combustion method. Nickel and magnesium nitrates were dissolved in a minimal amount of distilled water, and urea was added in a stoichiometric ratio relative to the total nitrate content. The resulting solution was mixed with Li₄SiO₄ powder to form a homogeneous suspension, aged for 12 h, and then subjected to thermal treatment at 300 °C for 2 h followed by calcination at 500 °C for 3 h. In all samples, the NiO loading was fixed at 10 wt%, while the MgO content was 2 wt%.

Catalytic testing in CO₂ methanation

Catalytic tests for CO₂ methanation were carried out at atmospheric pressure using a continuous flow catalytic unit (FCU-1). The experiments were performed in a fixed-bed quartz reactor (25 cm in length, inner diameter 9–9.5 mm), loaded with 2 mL (1,1 g) of catalyst granules (2–3 mm). The catalysts were heated under a programmed temperature ramp from 250 to 550 °C at a rate of 5 °C min⁻¹ under a CO₂/H₂ gas mixture (1:4), corresponding to the stoichiometry of the Sabatier reaction. The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) was maintained at 1000 h⁻¹. The reaction temperature was monitored using K-type thermocouples positioned at the inlet and outlet of the catalytic bed. Prior to catalytic testing, the samples were reduced in an H₂/Ar flow (20 mL min⁻¹) at 700 °C for 3 h. The outlet gas composition (H₂, CO₂, CO, and CH₄) was analyzed using a Chromos GC-1000 gas chromatograph equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and a flame ionization detector (FID). Materials characterization

Structural, morphological, thermal, and surface properties of the synthesized samples were investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR), and CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD).

The phase composition and crystalline structure of the samples were determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with Cu Kα radiation. Diffraction patterns were recorded in the 2θ range of 3–120° with a step size of 0.01–0.02° and a counting time of 0.3–0.5 s per step. A nickel filter was used to suppress Cu Kβ radiation, and phase identification was performed using the PDXL2 database.

The surface morphology and elemental composition of the samples were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a Thermo Scientific Apreo 2 S LoVac microscope (Waltham, MA, USA). High-resolution images were used to analyze particle shape, degree of agglomeration, and surface texture, with a spatial resolution down to 500 nm. Elemental analysis was carried out by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) integrated into the SEM system.

The surface basic properties of the samples were evaluated by CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD) using a UNISIT USGA-101 chemisorption analyzer (Moscow, Russia). Approximately 0.06 g of powdered sample was placed in a quartz reactor and fixed with quartz wool. Prior to CO₂ adsorption, the samples were pretreated at 500 °C for 2 h under a helium flow. CO₂ adsorption was carried out at room temperature for 30 min with a gas flow rate of 20 mL min-1, followed by desorption under helium flow with programmed heating.

For H₂ temperature-programmed reduction (H₂-TPR) experiments, a sample (0.1–0.2 g) was first degassed at 150 °C for 2 h. The reduction was then performed in a 10% H₂/Ar gas mixture (25 mL min-1) over the temperature range of 50–800 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C min-1 to evaluate the reducibility of the samples.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out using a STA 449 F3 Jupiter thermal analyzer (Netzsch, Germany). Approximately 20–25 mg of sample was heated from room temperature to 900 °C at a heating rate of 20 °C min⁻¹. The measurements were performed under flowing nitrogen (50 mL min⁻¹) and oxygen (20 mL min⁻¹) atmospheres.

XPS analysis was performed using a Kratos Axis Ultra-DLD spectrometer equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα radiation source (1486.6 eV, 150 W) and a delay line detector. The measurements were conducted under normal emission mode with a pass energy of 40 eV. Surface charging was compensated using a charge neutralizer, while all binding energies were referenced to the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) analyses were performed using a Hitachi HF5000 FE-S/TEM microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Prior to analysis, the catalyst powders were dispersed in ethanol by ultrasonication and deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids. Results and discussion

Phase composition of the obtained sample

X-ray diffraction analysis shows that when natural zeolite is used as a silica source, Li₄SiO₄ is the dominant crystalline phase in all samples. For the sample synthesized at a Li: Si molar ratio of 1.5:1, the lithium orthosilicate content reaches 93.0 wt%, indicating that the solid-state reaction between lithium nitrate and the zeolite proceeds nearly to completion at 750 °C (Table 1). Minor amounts of SiO₂ (1.0 wt%) and Li₂SiO₃ (6.0 wt%) can be attributed to residual silica from the zeolite and partially incomplete reaction of the lithium precursor with the silicate matrix19.

With increasing lithium content (Li: Si = 2:1 and 4:1), a progressive decrease in the Li₄SiO₄ fraction to 69.0 and 29.0 wt%, respectively, is observed, accompanied by the formation of Li₂SiO₃, Li₂CaSiO₄, and LiAlO₂ phases. The appearance of calcium- and aluminum-containing silicates is associated with the participation of CaO and Al₂O₃ impurities present in the natural zeolite, which react with excess Li₂O formed during lithium nitrate decomposition. This behavior is consistent with literature reports on lithium silicate synthesis from mineral-based precursors20. As shown in the XRD patterns (Fig. 2), reflections of Li₄SiO₄ dominate in the Li: Si = 1.5:1 sample, whereas at higher lithium ratios their intensity decreases significantly, accompanied by the growth of peaks corresponding to Li₂SiO₃, Li₂CaSiO₄, and LiAlO₂ phases. This phase redistribution suggests that excess lithium shifts the reaction equilibrium toward the formation of more complex lithium and alkaline-earth silicates, which may, in turn, influence the CO₂ sorption behavior and textural properties of the resulting materials18.

X-ray diffraction spectra analysis of samples.

As revealed by XRD analysis, at a Li: Si molar ratio of 1.5:1, the obtained material is predominantly composed of Li₄SiO₄ (93.0 wt%), while the amounts of secondary phases such as SiO₂ and Li₂SiO₃ remain minimal. Such phase purity and the dominance of lithium orthosilicate make this composition a promising support for the development of Ni-based CO₂ methanation catalysts15,21,22.

In the next step, Ni-containing catalysts based on Li₄SiO₄ were synthesized, and XRD analysis was employed to evaluate the preservation of the lithium silicate matrix as well as the distribution of NiO and MgO active phases. The XRD patterns of the fresh Ni/Li₄SiO₄, Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI), and Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalysts are shown in Fig. 3.

X-ray diffraction spectra of fresh catalysts.

For the Ni/Li₄SiO₄ sample, weak and broadened reflections are observed in the regions of 2θ ≈ 21–22°, 26–27°, 32–33°, and 36–43°, which can be attributed to Li₄SiO₄ (PDF 01–084-7600) and SiO₂ (PDF 00–033-1161) phases. The low intensity and broadening of the peaks indicate a partially amorphous lithium silicate matrix and a highly dispersed Ni-containing phase. The weak NiO reflections suggest either strong interaction between NiO and the support or the presence of very small crystallites, leading to diminished diffraction signals.

For the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst, distinct reflections are observed at 2θ = 21.7°, 22.5°, 26.4°, 26.7°, 33.2°, 34.0°, 36.0°, 37.1°, 38.4°, 42.5°, 43.2°, and 62.9°.

The reflections at 2θ = 21.7°, 22.5°, 33.2°, and 34.0° are assigned to the Li₄SiO₄ phase (PDF 01–084-7600), confirming the preservation of the lithium silicate matrix. The peaks at 26.4° and 26.7° correspond to SiO₂ (PDF 00–033-1161) and are associated with residual silica originating from the natural zeolite. Additional reflections at 33.2°, 34.0°, and 38.4° indicate the presence of the intermediate Li₂SiO₃ phase (PDF 00–030-0766), while peaks at 36.0°, 37.1°, 43.2°, and 62.9° can be attributed to NiO (PDF 00–073-0450). The overlap of reflections in the range of 42.5–43.2° with the characteristic positions of MgO (PDF 00–004-0829), in the absence of distinct MgO peaks, suggests the possible formation of a Ni1−хMgₓO solid solution, which is typical for NiO–MgO systems and is consistent with literature reports23.

For the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst synthesized via the solution combustion method, a broader set of reflections is observed, while the main phases (Li₄SiO₄, Li₂SiO₃, NiO, and a probable Ni1−хMgₓO phase) are retained24. The partial broadening of diffraction peaks compared to the impregnated sample indicates smaller crystallite size and the presence of an amorphous component. Such features are typical of materials obtained via solution combustion synthesis and are generally associated with enhanced dispersion of the active phase.

Morphology of catalysts

The surface morphology and particle distribution of the catalysts were investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and the results are presented in Fig. 4.

SEM images, surface particle distribution, and Ni mapping (EDS, Ni-K) of Ni/Li₄SiO₄, Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI), and Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalysts.

SEM micrographs of the Ni/Li₄SiO₄ sample reveal a surface composed of large, irregularly shaped aggregates. The particles, predominantly spherical and angular, form dense clusters with moderately developed interparticle porosity. Elemental mapping (Ni-K) indicates a localized distribution of nickel, with the formation of distinct Ni-rich regions, suggesting particle agglomeration and limited dispersion of the active phase. Such morphology is typical of Ni-based systems with weak metal–support interaction and is often associated with reduced dispersion and catalytic performance, as reported by Wei et al.25. The introduction of Mg leads to a significant modification of the morphology. SEM images of Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) show finer surface domains distributed more uniformly over the catalyst surface instead of smaller particles forming aggregates of approximately 1–2 μm. Ni-K mapping reveals a more homogeneous distribution of nickel across the surface, with the absence of large Ni-rich domains. This behavior is consistent with literature reports indicating that Mg incorporation promotes the formation of Ni–MgO solid solutions through Ni–O–Mg interactions and stabilizes highly dispersed NiO species, with particle sizes below 5 nm reported for similar Ni–Mg-containing systems26.

For the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) sample, a foam-like porous morphology is observed, consisting of interconnected pores and thin walls. Elemental mapping shows an almost continuous distribution of Ni over the entire surface, indicating a relatively uniform distribution of Ni-containing species instead of a high degree of dispersion and the absence of localized agglomeration. Such structures are characteristic of catalysts prepared via the solution combustion method, where intense gas evolution during synthesis promotes the formation of a porous framework and restricts structural coarsening during synthesis instead of limits particle growth. As a result, a more homogeneous distribution of the Ni-containing phase is observed instead of a uniformly dispersed Ni phase is obtained, in agreement with previous reports on similar systems27. In addition, the presence of MgO likely contributes to the stabilization of Ni species by suppressing their migration and sintering.

The H₂-TPR profile of the Ni/Li₄SiO₄ sample is characterized by a main intense peak at 473 °C, along with weak high-temperature maxima at 605, 714, and 830 °C. The peak at 473 °C is attributed to the reduction of the majority of NiO species moderately interacting with the Li₄SiO₄ matrix28. The peaks at 605 and 714 °C correspond to Ni species more strongly interacting with the silicate matrix, while the high-temperature maximum around 830 °C indicates the presence of hardly reducible Ni species, likely incorporated into the silicate structure or forming mixed oxide phases29.

Upon Mg incorporation, the reduction behavior of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) sample changes significantly. Instead of the peak at 473 °C, a broad maximum centered at 531 °C is observed. The shift of the main peak to higher temperature suggests a stronger interaction between NiO and the support induced by Mg addition, while the peak broadening indicates a distribution of Ni²⁺ species with varying interaction strength, including Ni–O–Mg and Ni–O–Li–Si structures30. It is well established that MgO can modify the electronic structure of NiO and promote the formation of NiO–MgO solid solutions or strongly interacting NiO–MgO interfaces, which enhance the stability and dispersion of the Ni phase, but simultaneously decrease the fraction of easily reducible Ni species9,31.

For the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) sample, the H₂-TPR profile exhibits four reduction peaks at 466, 522, 652–653, and 818 °C, indicating the presence of multiple types of Ni-containing species. The peak at 466 °C is attributed to the reduction of the most accessible and relatively weakly bound NiO species, likely located on the surface of the porous framework. The maximum at 522 °C corresponds to more strongly interacting Ni²⁺ species associated with the Mg- and Li-containing matrix.

The high-temperature peaks at 652–653 and 818 °C indicate the presence of the most stable Ni-containing species, which are likely associated with mixed oxide structures such as Ni–O–Mg and/or Ni–O–Si, or located in strongly interacting interfacial regions. The reduction of these species occurs only at elevated temperatures9,10.

Characterization of surface basicity of the catalysts by CO₂-TPD

To evaluate the strength of CO₂ binding and the distribution of basic sites, CO₂ temperature-programmed desorption (CO₂-TPD) profiles were analyzed for the Ni/Li₄SiO₄, Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI), and Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalysts (Fig. 6), while the corresponding integrated areas and site distributions are summarized in Table 3. Based on the desorption temperature, surface basic sites are commonly classified into three categories: weak (50–200 °C), medium-strength (200–400 °C), and strong (> 400 °C) sites32,33.

All samples exhibit basic sites of different strengths. Weak basic sites are mainly associated with surface hydroxyl groups, whereas medium-strength sites are typically attributed to metal–oxygen pairs and are involved in the formation of bicarbonate and carbonate species upon CO₂ adsorption34,35.

For the pristine Ni/Li₄SiO₄ sample, the CO₂-TPD profile exhibits desorption peaks at 272, 604, and 712 °C, corresponding to medium- and strong-strength basic sites. The peak at 272 °C is attributed to medium-strength basic centers, whereas the peaks at 604 and 712 °C indicate the presence of strong basic sites. According to the quantitative data (Table 3), the fractions of weak, medium, and strong basic sites are approximately 9%, 16%, and 75%, respectively, indicating a dominant contribution of strong basic sites and the formation of thermally stable carbonate species. However, the relatively low fraction of weak and medium-strength sites may limit the availability of active sites for initial CO₂ adsorption36,37.

For the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst, the CO₂-TPD profile changes significantly, with the main desorption peak shifting to lower temperature (246 °C), indicating an increased contribution of medium-strength basic sites. At the same time, the high-temperature peaks (521–721 °C) associated with strong basic sites become less pronounced, suggesting a reduced contribution of strong sites, while the desorption intensity in the low- and medium-temperature regions increases. This is consistent with the increase in the fraction of weak (20%) and medium (31%) basic sites and a decrease in strong sites to 49%. According to literature reports38,39 MgO promotes the formation of additional O²⁻ sites, surface hydroxyl groups, and metal–oxygen pairs, thereby increasing the proportion of medium-strength sites and facilitating CO₂ adsorption.

For the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) sample, the CO₂-TPD profile shows desorption peaks at 269 and 615 °C, corresponding to medium- and strong-strength basic sites, respectively. The high-temperature peak at 615 °C dominates, while the fractions of weak and medium-strength sites are limited to approximately 3% and 7%, respectively, and the contribution of strong sites reaches ~ 90%. This distribution indicates the formation of a surface enriched in strong basic sites, capable of stabilizing CO₂ in the form of thermally stable carbonate species and promoting strong CO₂ adsorption on the catalyst surface.

XPS characterization of the catalysts

XPS analysis was performed to investigate the surface chemical states and interaction between Ni- and Mg-containing species in the prepared catalysts (Fig. 7). XPS analysis revealed noticeable differences in the surface electronic state of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ catalysts depending on the preparation method.

XPS spectra of catalysts: (a) Ni 2p; (b) Mg 1 s; (c) O 1s.

In particular, the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst exhibited a slight positive shift of the Ni 2p₃/₂ signal from ~ 854.8 eV to ~ 855.1 eV together with a less pronounced shake-up satellite contribution compared with the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) sample. Such behavior indicates modification of the electronic state of Ni species due to stronger interaction with neighboring Mg-containing oxide phases and partial redistribution of electron density at the Ni–Mg oxide interface. Similar changes in the electronic state of Ni species associated with enhanced metal–oxide interaction have previously been reported for Ni-based methanation catalysts prepared via different synthesis approaches40,41. A similar tendency was also observed in the Mg 1 s spectra. Compared with the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst, the Mg 1 s signal of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) sample became narrower and more symmetric, indicating a more homogeneous distribution of Mg-containing oxide species on the catalyst surface. Such behavior additionally suggests the formation of a more structurally coupled Ni–Mg oxide environment in the catalyst prepared by the capillary impregnation method.

The O 1 s spectra of both catalysts were dominated by lattice oxygen species centered near 528–529 eV together with a weaker contribution of surface oxygen-containing groups at higher binding energies. In comparison with the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst, the O 1 s profile of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) sample appeared narrower and more symmetric, indicating a more ordered surface oxide structure and stronger interfacial interaction between the oxide components. Similar correlations between surface oxygen organization, metal–oxide interaction and catalytic behavior have also been reported for Ni-based methanation catalysts synthesized via different preparation methods42,43.

TEM/HRTEM characterization of the catalysts

TEM and HRTEM analyses were additionally carried out to obtain deeper insight into the structural organization of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ catalysts prepared by different synthesis methods (Fig. 8). The TEM images of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst revealed a relatively porous morphology composed of aggregated nanosized oxide particles with a relatively weakly ordered structure. Such morphology is characteristic of combustion-derived materials, where rapid gas evolution during the solution combustion process suppresses extensive crystal growth and promotes the formation of highly dispersed oxide domains41.

TEM images of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst (a, b) and TEM/HRTEM images of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst (c, d).

A noticeably different microstructure was observed for the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst. In this case, the oxide phase was distributed more uniformly throughout the catalyst surface, while the aggregates themselves appeared denser and locally more ordered. The HRTEM images of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) sample revealed well-resolved lattice fringes with an average interplanar spacing of approximately 0.21–0.22 nm, indicating the presence of nanocrystalline mixed oxide regions. At the same time, partial distortion and broadening of the lattice fringes were observed, which may be associated with strong interaction between Ni- and Mg-containing oxide species. Similar features have previously been reported for Ni–Mg oxide systems, where strong interaction between NiO and Mg-containing oxide species was found to modify the local lattice environment and contribute to the formation of strongly interacting Ni–Mg–O oxide domains44–46.

Compared with the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst, the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) sample exhibited a higher degree of local structural ordering, which correlates well with the XPS and H₂-TPR results indicating stronger interaction between Ni and Mg oxide phases.

Catalytic characteristics of samples

The catalytic performance of the synthesized Ni/Li₄SiO₄, Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI), and Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) materials was evaluated in the CO₂ hydrogenation reaction using a model gas mixture (CO₂:H₂ = 1:4) over the temperature range of 250–550 °C, following prior reduction of the samples in an H₂/Ar flow at 700 °C for 3 h. The temperature-dependent CO₂ conversion and CH₄ selectivity are presented in Fig. 9a, b.

CO₂ conversion (a) and CH₄ selectivity (b) over Ni/Li₄SiO₄, Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) and Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalysts under model reaction conditions.

For the Ni/Li₄SiO₄ catalyst, CO₂ conversion ranges from 30 to 43%, reaching a maximum of 43% at 500 °C, followed by a decrease to 35% at 550 °C, while CH₄ selectivity remains low over the entire temperature range. This behavior is consistent with the XRD, SEM, and H₂-TPR results, which indicate a limited number of accessible reduced Ni⁰ sites and weak interaction with the reactant mixture, resulting in low catalytic activity of the unmodified sample.

The introduction of Mg and the use of the incipient wetness impregnation method significantly enhance both activity and selectivity. For the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst, CO₂ conversion increases from 28% at 250 °C to 78% at 450 °C, while CH₄ selectivity rises from 3% to 87%. At 500 °C, the conversion reaches 75% with a maximum CH₄ selectivity of 88%. This improved performance correlates well with the H₂-TPR and CO₂-TPD data, indicating a high fraction of accessible surface Ni⁰ sites and a predominance of medium-strength basic sites, which facilitate efficient CO₂ adsorption, activation, and subsequent hydrogenation of intermediates to CH₄11.

In contrast, the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst, synthesized via the solution combustion method, shows an increase in CO₂ conversion from 23% at 250 °C to 100% at 550 °C; however, CH₄ selectivity remains significantly lower than that of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) sample, ranging from 1 to 4% at 250–350 °C to 47–50% at 450–500 °C, and decreasing to 20% at 550 °C. Despite achieving complete CO₂ conversion, the predominance of strong basic sites and less reducible Ni species, as evidenced by CO₂-TPD and H₂-TPR analyses, likely leads to strong adsorption of CO₂ and carbonate intermediates, promoting the reverse water–gas shift (RWGS) reaction and thereby limiting selective methane formation47,48.

Thus, the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst exhibits the most favorable combination of CO₂ conversion and CH₄ selectivity, which can be attributed to a balanced distribution of accessible Ni⁰ sites and medium-strength basic centers. In contrast, the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) sample, despite its developed porous structure and high overall conversion, is characterized by an excess of strong basic sites that shift the reaction pathway toward CO formation.

According to the H₂-TPR results, the total hydrogen consumption of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst reached 1242 µmol g⁻¹, corresponding to approximately 93% reduction of NiO to metallic Ni according to the stoichiometric reaction NiO + H₂ → Ni + H₂O, considering the nominal loading of 10 wt% NiO (theoretical value: 1340 µmol NiO g⁻¹). Accordingly, the amount of reduced nickel was estimated to be 1.25 × 10⁻³ mol Ni g⁻¹. Using this value together with the CO₂ consumption rate at 450 °C (12.7 mmol CO₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹), the turnover frequency (TOF), defined as the number of CO₂ molecules converted per Ni atom per unit time, was calculated. The obtained TOF value for the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ CI catalyst was 2.8 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ under a CO₂/H₂ ratio of 1/4.

For the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst, the total hydrogen uptake during the H₂-TPR experiment was 968 µmol g⁻¹, corresponding to approximately 72% reduction of NiO to Ni⁰ under the same theoretical value of 1340 µmol g⁻¹ for 10 wt% NiO loading. Therefore, the amount of reduced nickel for this sample was estimated to be 9.6 × 10⁻⁴ mol Ni g⁻¹. Based on the CO₂ consumption rate at 450 °C (10.2 mmol CO₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹), the TOF value for the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst was calculated to be 2.9 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹, which is of the same order of magnitude as that of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst despite the significantly lower CH₄ selectivity observed for the (SC) catalyst. This finding suggests that the differences in catalytic performance between the two samples are primarily associated with differences in the distribution of basic sites and the CO₂ conversion pathway rather than fundamentally different intrinsic activities of the nickel active centers.

Catalytic stability and carbon deposition

Stability tests showed that both catalysts maintained nearly constant CO₂ conversion over 50 h; however, their methane selectivity changed differently (Fig. 10a). For the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst, a moderate decrease in CH₄ selectivity was observed, whereas the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) sample exhibited more stable selectivity. This difference is likely associated with both the distribution of basic sites and the different tendency of the catalysts to accumulate carbonaceous deposits.

According to the CO₂-TPD results, the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst is characterized by the predominance of strong basic sites, which likely promotes a greater contribution of the RWGS reaction and, consequently, results in lower but more stable CH₄ selectivity, consistent with previous studies reporting enhanced CO formation over catalysts possessing strong basicity at elevated temperatures49. In contrast, the higher initial selectivity of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst is associated with a larger fraction of weak and medium-strength basic sites, which are considered more favorable for the formation and subsequent hydrogenation of formate intermediates to methane on interfacial Ni⁰ basic sites50.

Thermogravimetric analysis of the spent samples revealed significant differences in the nature of carbon deposits (Fig. 10b).

For the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst, a more pronounced weight loss was observed in the 450–650 °C region, with a total weight loss of approximately 2.6–3.0 wt%, indicating the formation of a larger amount of relatively stable carbon-containing species. In contrast, the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst exhibited a significantly lower weight loss of about 0.5 wt%, suggesting a substantially lower tendency toward coking.

Thus, the decrease in methane selectivity for Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) is apparently associated with the gradual accumulation of carbonaceous deposits and/or stable carbonate species, which partially block the most effective interfacial Ni⁰–basic sites50,51. As a result, the contribution of the methanation pathway decreases, whereas the role of side pathways, including RWGS, becomes more pronounced. In contrast, the more porous and less ordered structure of the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst likely facilitates the removal of surface carbon-containing intermediates and provides more stable catalytic behavior over time52.

Mechanistic insights into CO₂ methanation

The mechanism of CO₂ methanation over Ni-based catalysts has been widely discussed in the literature and is strongly dependent on the nature of the support, metal dispersion, and reaction conditions. According to previous studies, H₂ dissociation occurs on metallic Ni sites, whereas CO₂ activation may take place either on the basic sites of the support or at the metal–support interface, where carbonate, bicarbonate, and formate surface species are formed28.

For Ni-based catalysts with high metal dispersion and well-developed interfacial contact, an increased number of accessible Ni sites and metal–support interfaces promotes both hydrogen activation and stabilization of surface intermediates, preventing their premature desorption prior to complete hydrogenation to CH₄. The high CO₂ conversion combined with high CH₄ selectivity suggests efficient formation and subsequent hydrogenation of formate intermediates. This behavior can be attributed to the optimal balance between accessible Ni⁰ sites and medium-strength basic centers. The presence of Mg likely contributes to the formation of such sites, while maintaining sufficient Ni accessibility for effective H₂ dissociation and surface hydrogen coverage, thus favoring methane formation.

In the present case, the predominance of strong basic sites in the SC sample likely leads to stronger adsorption of CO₂-derived species, hindering their further hydrogenation and increasing the contribution of the RWGS pathway, which results in lower methane selectivity.

Therefore, CO₂ methanation over the studied catalysts can be described predominantly by a carbonate–formate mechanism occurring at interfacial sites in the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) system, where Ni⁰ sites facilitate H₂ dissociation and medium-strength basic sites promote CO₂ adsorption and activation. In contrast, for the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst, the higher fraction of strong basic sites shifts the reaction pathway toward RWGS, in agreement with the observed high conversion and reduced CH₄ selectivity.

Conclusion

A comprehensive study of Ni- and Ni–Mg-containing catalysts based on Li₄SiO₄ synthesized from natural zeolite was carried out to establish the influence of the Ni and Mg incorporation method on their structural characteristics and behavior in the CO₂ methanation reaction. It was shown that at a Li: Si molar ratio of 1.5:1, a support with a dominant Li₄SiO₄ phase (~ 93 wt%) and minimal content of secondary phases is formed, making it a suitable basis for the development of methanation systems. Comparison of Ni/Li₄SiO₄, Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI), and Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalysts demonstrated that Mg addition and the method of Ni/Mg incorporation determine the phase composition, morphology, dispersion degree of the active phase, reducibility of nickel species, and distribution of surface basic sites. The Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst forms uniformly distributed Ni nanoparticles within the Li₄SiO₄–MgO matrix, contains a significant fraction of easily reducible Ni⁰ species, and possesses a balanced distribution of medium and strong basic sites. This provides high CO₂ conversion (75–78%) and CH₄ selectivity of 87–88% at 450–500 °C with comparable TOF values, indicating the predominance of a carbonate–formate mechanism on interfacial Ni⁰–basic sites.

The Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC) catalyst synthesized by the solution combustion method is characterized by higher porosity, smaller crystallite sizes, and a significantly larger fraction of strong basic sites; however, it contains fewer easily reducible Ni species. As a result, complete CO₂ conversion is achieved at elevated temperatures, but CH₄ selectivity remains limited (20–50%) due to the predominance of the CO-mediated pathway involving the reverse water–gas shift reaction. Similar TOF values for the CI and SC samples indicate that the differences in selectivity are primarily governed by acid–base and interfacial properties rather than by the intrinsic activity of Ni centers. Stability tests and TGA analysis of spent catalysts revealed that, at comparable CO₂ conversion, the Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (CI) catalyst is prone to the accumulation of a larger amount of stable carbon deposits (2.6–3.0 wt% versus ~ 0.5 wt% for SC), leading to a gradual decrease in CH₄ selectivity. For Ni–Mg/Li₄SiO₄ (SC), the more porous structure and predominance of strong basic sites provide lower susceptibility to coking and a more stable but less methanation-selective surface state.

Thus, the method of Ni and Mg incorporation into Li₄SiO₄ derived from natural zeolite represents an effective tool for tuning the balance between activity, selectivity, and stability of CO₂ methanation catalysts. Capillary impregnation provides an optimal balance of easily reducible Ni⁰ centers and medium-strength basic sites, resulting in maximum methane selectivity, whereas the solution combustion method forms a system with enhanced CO₂ conversion but a stronger RWGS contribution and lower CH₄ selectivity. These results demonstrate the potential of naturally derived Li₄SiO₄ as a support material and provide directions for the rational design of Ni–Mg catalysts for CO₂ methanation and decarbonization technologies.
