Parent perspectives on neonatal research without prior consent: a mixed-methods interview study
Authors: Stephanie A Kraft, Abigail Brickler, Abril Beretta, Rishabh Jain, Andrea Kelsh, Kaashif A Ahmad, Justin T Clapp, Neal W Dickert, Rebecca A Dorner, Katherine F Guttmann, Anup C Katheria, Stephanie K Kukora, Benjamin A Mooso, Elliott Mark Weiss, FAM-CONNECT Collaborative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecruitment for clinical research in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) is difficult, which may lead to low enrollment rates, systematically skewed samples, and nongeneralizable results. Research without prior consent (RWPC), defined as obtaining consent only after infants have been randomized to a treatment arm, may improve enrollment but raises ethical challenges. There is a need to better understand how parents with lived experience of having an infant in the NICU consider RWPC’s impact on critical ethical values related to informed consent, including decision-making, respect, and trust. This mixed-methods study aimed to describe parental perceptions of the acceptability and ethical implications of RWPC for neonatal clinical research.MethodsInterviews were conducted with parents of infants recently admitted to the NICU across 6 US locations. Parents were asked a series of Likert-scale questions followed by open-ended questions with interactive probes about the acceptability of hypothetical study vignettes using either prospective informed consent or RWPC. Likert-scale responses were analyzed using mixed-effects linear regression models. Open-ended responses were inductively coded and thematically analyzed through a constructivist lens.ResultsParents (n = 38) scored the acceptability of RWPC vignettes lower than prospective informed consent vignettes on mean adjusted total score and items about perceived control, values-aligned decision-making, and respect for babies and families. Four themes were identified as impacting parents’ views on acceptability of RWPC: (1) equipoise as the foundation for acceptability of consent approaches, (2) informational preferences for consent approaches, (3) complex interplay between consent approaches and research relationships, and (4) consent approaches as shaping perceived control.ConclusionsParents’ perspectives illustrate key considerations for the potential use of RWPC as well as broader reflections on engagement about clinical research in the NICU setting. Situational factors in the NICU raise barriers to consent, and RWPC may be differentially acceptable depending on study specifics. Thoughtful communication about equipoise and attention to trust are essential when considering alternative methods of consent. Future research should further examine ethical trade-offs involved in RWPC and varied perceptions across study features.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01455-x.Keywords: Informed consent, Waiver, Deferred consent, NICU, Neonatal, Clinical trials, Interviews

Background

Research recruitment in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) is challenging. Clinically urgent situations may limit time for discussions, and parents may be navigating stress, heightened emotions, and physical recovery from childbirth [1–3]. Low enrollment rates can lead to underpowered studies or prolonged times to completion; this wastes resources and can produce unreliable data. Further, systematic underrepresentation associated with skewed enrollment patterns can create biased samples that contribute to non-generalizable results [4].

Alternative methods of consent, such as research without prior consent (RWPC), offer a potential pathway for trials to proceed in emergent settings and enhance clinically representative inclusion [5–7]. Globally, these alternative methods may be referred to by terms such as “deferred consent” or “waiver of consent” and may include slightly different procedures, but they share the feature of delaying or waiving participants’ prospective informed consent before beginning their participation in research. In the US regulatory context, a study may be granted a waiver of consent either if it is determined to be no greater than minimal risk, not practicable to carry out without the waiver or any identifiable data that are used, without adverse impact on participants’ rights and welfare, and to include pertinent information after participation [8], or if it is conducted in an emergent setting and meets criteria, including community consultation, for an exception from informed consent [9]. Here, the term RWPC is used to describe the process of obtaining consent for continued participation and data use only after a patient’s initial allocation to a treatment arm.

A growing number of NICU trials have used RWPC and other alternative methods of consent [10–13]. However, because alternative methods of consent involve allocating infants to a study arm before obtaining parental permission, they warrant ethical scrutiny to ensure they do not unjustifiably limit parental decision-making, fail to respect families, or undermine trust [14, 15]. Several global studies have examined parental attitudes about the acceptability of these approaches. Surveys and interviews with parents enrolled in studies using alternative methods of consent in Australia, England, and the US have found that most were satisfied, though some stated they would have preferred prospective consent [16–18]. Conversely, a survey of NICU parents in Ireland, not conducted in connection with a trial using alternative methods of consent, found greater agreement with prospective consent over RWPC, though agreement with RWPC increased when it was justified as a time-sensitive study [19]. Collectively, these prior data suggest parents may find RWPC acceptable in at least some cases but that this can vary based on context and framing.

Few studies have explored nuanced considerations for implementing RWPC across neonatal research settings, including the extent to which it aligns with ethical goals of the informed consent process. In-depth exploration of the implications of RWPC for consent-relevant factors such as parents’ perceived decision-making control, values alignment of participation, and trust with the research team can help contextualize the ethically appropriate use of RWPC. To better understand these topics, this mixed-methods interview study explored US-based parents’ perceptions of the acceptability of RWPC as compared to prospective consent for clinical research in the NICU. Because US regulations require studies to involve no more than minimal risk to allow for waiver of consent, parents were asked to consider hypothetical comparative effectiveness studies that could be determined to be minimal risk studies, as examples of realistic situations where RWPC might be considered in the NICU.

Methods

Overview

Interviews were conducted with parents of infants who were recently admitted to the NICU. Parents were not recruited in connection with a study involving RWPC. The primary study team included experts in neonatology and bioethics and was supported by a broader group including NICU parent representatives, neonatal clinical trialists, IRB representatives, and research ethicists. The team drew on this breadth of expertise throughout the study, engaging in reflexive practices to remain attentive to how their respective viewpoints shaped study decisions. This study was approved by the Seattle Children’s Hospital IRB.

Recruitment

Clinical collaborators at 6 US NICUs referred participants. Sites included Kansas City, MO; New York, NY; Philadelphia, PA; San Antonio, TX; San Diego, CA; and Seattle, WA. English- or Spanish-speaking parents (one per infant) were eligible after their infant’s 5th day of life.

Interviews

The team developed a semi-structured interview guide Supplementary Material 1 that included hypothetical study vignettes followed by questions about the degree to which the consent approach described in the vignette fulfilled different functions of informed consent, as well as general questions and self-reported demographic questions. Cases were based on real neonatal comparative effectiveness studies and represented a range of trials for which RWPC might be considered. The three studies compared (1) room air versus oxygen provision, (2) meconium suctioning versus resuscitation, and (3) two anti-seizure medications with comparable risk–benefit profiles. Each was described with both a prospective consent and RWPC approach, which included the provision of flyers or handouts with an opt-out option before allocation followed by a later consent discussion for ongoing participation. Figure 1 shows an example of how each approach was described; full vignettes are provided in Supplementary Material 2. Participants were assigned two of three vignettes in random order, one with prospective consent and the other RWPC, using stratified randomization by site to assign vignettes. Participants were asked to respond to the same questions after each vignette. Questions drew on Dickert and colleagues’ framework of functions of consent [20]. Likert-scale responses were ranked from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”).

Trained interviewers conducted interviews via Microsoft Teams between February and November 2024. Participants provided verbal consent. Spanish-language interviews were conducted by a fluent Spanish speaker on the study team. Evaluation for thematic saturation occurred throughout analysis, and recruitment stopped when saturation was reached [21]. Interviews were audio recorded, professionally transcribed, translated into English (if necessary), de-identified, and uploaded to the cloud-based qualitative analysis platform Dedoose [22] to facilitate analysis.

Analysis

For the 7 Likert-scale questions, a composite total score was calculated by summing responses across all questions (range: 7–35). Analyses followed a three-stage approach. First, exploratory data analysis examined response distributions across questions and vignettes. Second, paired t-tests assessed unadjusted mean differences between consent approaches for individual questions and total scores. Third, mixed-effects linear regression models estimated adjusted mean differences between consent approaches, incorporating fixed effects for gender, race, ethnicity, and Medicaid status, with random intercepts accounting for repeated measures within participants. To examine consent approach effects within individual studies, separate linear regression models were fitted for each study type using the same covariates as before (mixed-effects modeling was unnecessary as participants never evaluated both consent approaches within the same study). Study type effects were assessed using mixed-effects linear regression with identical covariate structure. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between study types employed Tukey's method for multiple comparison adjustment. Additional analyses included Fisher's exact tests comparing dichotomized responses (high vs. low) between consent approaches within each study for each individual question, using cutpoints of ≥ 3 and ≥ 4. All analyses used R statistical software with significance set at α = 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals reported.

Open-ended responses were analyzed using thematic analysis, guided by a constructivist approach centering participants’ lived experiences in data interpretation [21]. The team developed an initial codebook using deductive and inductive methods after reviewing initial transcripts. Codes and definitions were iteratively revised through test applications until all relevant concepts were captured and could be applied consistently. Two coders independently coded all transcripts then reviewed them in consensus meetings, with a third team member as a tiebreaker when needed. Coded data were summarized by code and evaluated for distinctions based on vignette and consent approach, then grouped into themes through iterative team discussion. Draft themes were reviewed with the broader group to ensure face validity and refined based on group feedback.

Equipoise as the foundation for acceptability of consent approaches (Table 4)

Participants’ perceptions of whether both treatment arms were standard of care and whether there might be evidence supporting use of one over the other were foundational to their views on acceptability of the consent approach. Some accepted the premise of the study description that it was unknown which treatment would produce better outcomes, emphasizing they would receive a standard of care regardless of whether they enrolled. Some participants wanted to know how hospitals determined which approach to use and the supporting evidence to inform their enrollment decision.

Others did not accept that there was equipoise between treatment arms. Some questioned whether there might be additional information not provided in the vignettes or noted the vignette assumed all patients were similarly situated. Several participants suggested clinicians may have a clear preference between treatment arms based on a patient’s unique attributes. Some participants desired a medical professional’s recommendation about the appropriate treatment, in at least one case preferring an opinion from a clinician with whom they had a prior relationship. Several said they would not want the study to dictate their clinician’s informed decision about the best treatment for their child.

Informational preferences for consent approaches (Table 5)

Considerations regarding quantity, delivery, timing, and function of information shaped participants’ views about acceptability of the consent approach. Several participants desired more information in general; some specifically wanted more about current practice and differences in standard of care. Participants viewed information as valuable for learning about the study, facilitating their own process of gathering additional information, and/or supporting informed discussions with trusted providers.

When participants responded to vignettes using RWPC, some reflected on the value of being notified about the study via flyers or handouts. Some said the presence of flyers supported the value of transparency, at least to a degree, but others raised concerns that some parents might be too preoccupied to read a flyer thoroughly or might miss it altogether. Some said receiving flyers earlier (e.g., prenatally) would be preferable. Many participants highlighted the value of having a conversation about the study, either instead of or in addition to a flyer, with some saying flyers alone would give insufficient control over enrollment.

Participants also discussed the timing of information but had sometimes conflicting perspectives. Many said they would prefer to discuss the study as early as possible and before any study intervention to support understanding. However, some said people might not be ready to engage with information delivered before it was clear their child would be eligible to participate. Some discussed needing to balance the desire for information with what is realistic given the clinical context (e.g., making an informed decision immediately after delivery). Participants’ views varied regarding when they would want to receive information in relation to a clinically urgent or stressful event. Most supported the notion that receiving study information during a stressful time would not be ideal, preferring discussions to happen earlier or later.

Complex interplay between consent approaches and research relationships (Table 6)

Participants described their views on consent approaches as both shaped by and shaping perceptions of the research and clinical teams’ trustworthiness, respectfulness, and transparency. Some said their trust in healthcare providers in general, their chosen provider specifically, and/or their healthcare institution influenced their views. Participants identified positive interactions with the research team, prospective consent discussions, and thorough study descriptions as demonstrating respect and building trust during the consent process. Perceived transparency during the research approach was also discussed in tandem with respect and trust; actions that participants identified as demonstrating transparency included obtaining prospective consent, posting study information in flyers, discussing information with families, and being clear about the study’s design, rationale, and plan for random allocation.

Impact of consent approach on control (Table 7)

Participants discussed the impact of prospective consent versus RWPC on control. Many desired the choice whether to enroll, which treatment arm to be allocated to, and/or whether to continue participating after initial allocation; having one or more of these choices provided a sense of control and, for some, influenced their willingness to enroll. When participants discussed choice and control, they often noted these supported other ethical values, including trustworthiness, respect, and alignment with one’s values. Alternatively, some participants noted that parental control may not always be desired or optimal.

Participants expressed variable views on the importance of prospective control and the extent to which prospective research discussions would feel appropriate. Some said not having the opportunity to decide whether to enroll when RWPC was used meant they did not have enough control, while others said the ability to decide whether to continue participating was adequate. A few, however, said having the choice about enrollment was undesirable, particularly at a time of clinical urgency. Some of these participants said they would prefer to avoid having research discussions during a stressful time or that participation in research was not a priority in a time of crisis; others wanted a choice despite the stressful context. Some linked the appropriate method of consent to the perceived risk to the infant from being in the study. Many discussed contextual challenges of the NICU setting, including ongoing stress even after a clinically emergent situation passed, limited time to make enrollment decisions, and barriers to robust prospective conversations about research.

Discussion

NICU parent interviews highlighted considerations that may inform the acceptability of RWPC in neonatal clinical trials. Many interviewed parents expressed a preference for prospective consent over RWPC, though many also indicated high agreement that each approach fulfilled different functions of consent. Views were variably shaped by prior experiences and perspectives, as well as different interpretations of study vignettes. This variability illustrates the challenges of hypothetical vignette-based studies of ethical issues that involve complex trade-offs and highlights opportunities for further examination of ethical and practical considerations for RWPC. Nevertheless, these findings offer important insights into how parents perceive research engagement in the NICU and thus may better contextualize the potential use of RWPC, including key regulatory considerations such as whether research without a waiver of consent is practicable and how RWPC might affect participants’ rights and welfare. Key implications of our findings relate to situational factors in the NICU, differential acceptability depending on study specifics, communication about equipoise, and attention to trust.

First, situational factors in the NICU such as clinical urgency, stress, and short decision-making timeframes make consent challenging and raise questions about the practicability of obtaining prospective informed consent. These are well-recognized challenges [1, 23, 24]. This study’s findings highlight how some parents may adjust their preferences for learning about a study when external factors preclude their ideal approach; this willingness to adjust one’s preferences suggests that there may be circumstances when RWPC does not adversely affect one’s rights and welfare as it relates to study communication. Interviewed parents expressed variable perspectives on when RWPC might be preferable in a clinically urgent context. Other studies of parent preferences regarding consent during emergency situations have described similar variability [25]. Alternatives, such as short oral consent followed by a later full consent discussion, were not explored in this study but may be a preferred solution; their reception should be assessed in future research [25]. These findings also generally cohere with data from adult contexts that suggest both a preference for prospective involvement in decisions where possible and a recognition that there may be times where that is not possible [26–28].

Second, the nature of a study’s interventions may contribute to acceptability. This study’s findings suggest differing perceptions of consent approaches across vignettes; for example, some parents may have perceived higher risk in the meconium suctioning vignette—a comparison of an intensive intervention to non-intervention—in contrast to vignettes with more qualitatively similar interventions. At the same time, however, only 29% of parents indicated high agreement that they would enroll their child in the meconium suctioning vignette when presented with prospective informed consent, as compared to 50% when presented with RWPC, which suggests complexity in the relationship between parents’ perceptions of the functions of consent and the ultimate enrollment decision. Other scholars have suggested that differences in the invasiveness of study arms may increase a comparative effectiveness study’s risk and consequently affect its required approach to consent [29]. Future research is needed to clarify parents’ understanding, rationale, and preferences for similar studies, particularly in emergent situations when tradeoffs must be made. Nevertheless, these findings emphasize the importance of understanding parents’ perceptions of treatment arms when engaging with them about a research study.

Third, these findings illustrate the importance of clear communication about the presence of equipoise between trial arms. Parents’ acceptance or non-acceptance of equipoise shaped their understanding of the study information and preferences for consent approach. Often, responses revealed a belief that one’s doctor always knows the right answer for their child, even if the field doesn’t. This raises an important challenge: How ought researchers and clinicians convey clinical uncertainty and the need for research without undermining parents’ trust in doctors? Communication about equipoise at the time of trial recruitment is challenging [30] and may warrant interventions such as focused training for recruiters or earlier education about uncertainty in clinical practice.

Finally, demonstrating trustworthiness must be central when considering alternative methods of consent. A majority of interviewed parents indicated high agreement that both prospective informed consent and RWPC vignettes achieved critical relational functions including promoting respect, trustworthiness, and transparency, but some differences were still observed. RWPC has potential to impact critical relational constructs including trustworthiness, respect, and transparency; these constructs intersect with families’ perceptions of equipoise and extent to which received treatment is within the standard of care, what information they seek and how they interpret it, and how they experience and exercise control. Perceived trustworthiness of research can also affect attitudes toward clinical care [31]. If researchers consider using alternative methods of consent, they must be attentive to potential impacts on the relationship between families and their clinical and research teams. For example, if a child experiences complications after being allocated to a treatment arm in a study using alternative methods of consent, the clinical and research teams must be prepared to navigate any subsequent loss of trust. Intentionally designing strategies to enhance respectful interpersonal communication after enrollment may help demonstrate trustworthiness [32] and could be incorporated into best practices for interacting with families about research [24].

Although this study’s recruitment strategy was designed to reach a diverse group of NICU parents, some viewpoints may not be represented. Importantly, only families willing to engage in research were included; others may have more negative perceptions about research overall. This study’s results are different than those from studies involving parents who have participated in RWPC, which is expected given its hypothetical nature: parents in this study were asked to imagine themselves in a position that they were not actually in, whereas responses from those with direct experience may reflect actual outcomes. Nevertheless, this design allowed exploration of multiple potential factors that might shape people’s perspectives. Future hypothetical studies could include parents who have previously experienced RWPC. Additionally, the brief vignettes were designed to provide sufficient information without overburdening parents; many participants, however, wanted additional information. While interviewers were trained to respond to indications that the participant may have misunderstood the vignettes and/or questions, it is possible that misunderstandings informed some responses. This highlights the importance of continuing to explore these topics with families to foster mutual understanding and co-production of ethical guidance.

Conclusions

Interviews with US-based parents of infants treated in the NICU provided nuanced and varied insight into parental perspectives on research engagement and informed consent, which may inform ethical considerations for the use of RWPC in neonatal clinical research. Future research should explore parents’ views on challenging trade-offs between ethical values and should examine variability across study goals, design, and clinical contexts to better understand how to ensure alternative methods of consent are maximally respectful and attentive to families’ needs.
