LLMThinkBench : Towards Basic Math Reasoning and Overthinking in Large Language Models
Gaurav Srivastava, Aafiya Hussain, Sriram Srinivasan, Xuan Wang

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable performance on complex mathematical benchmarks, yet often struggle with simple arithmetic tasks and exhibit a tendency toward over-explaining or "overthinking" answers. To systematically assess this phenomenon, we introduce LLMThinkBench , 1 1 1 LLMThinkBench is released under the MIT License and is available as a pip-installable package (pip install llmthinkbench) , ensuring broad accessibility for research and commercial use. a modular benchmarking framework that enables researchers to evaluate basic math reasoning and overthinking in LLMs. The framework provides 14 configurable math tasks with randomized test data generation and robust parsing strategies. Researchers can quantify overthinking using our Overthinking Score metric, which captures accuracy-verbosity tradeoffs through harmonic mean formulation. The tool offers flexible evaluation with a scalable vLLM/Transformers backend, multi-GPU support, and full configurability. Users can extend the tool with custom tasks, reproduce experiments with seeding, and generate detailed efficiency reports. Distributed as a pip-installable package with CLI and API access, LLMThinkBench provides researchers and practitioners an accessible, cost-effective alternative to expensive LLM-as-a-judge methods for diagnosing basic reasoning capabilities and efficiency analysis.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive performance on various mathematical benchmarks
Srivastava et al. 2025a
such as GSM8K
Cobbe et al. 2021
, GSMPlus
Li et al. 2024b
, and HARDMath
Fan et al. 2024
. These datasets include different types of problems ranging from grade-school arithmetic to competition-level math olympiad questions. However, recent research
Srivastava et al. 2025b
,
Yan et al. 2025
,
Xu and Ma 2024
has revealed a concerning disconnect: while LLMs can achieve high scores across these complex benchmarks, they often fail to demonstrate similar performance on basic arithmetic tasks, such as adding and sorting numbers.

Poor performance on simple numerical tasks raises fundamental questions about the generalizability of LLMs on basic arithmetic. Recent studies suggest that LLM reasoning may be attributed to pattern matching rather than genuine reasoning
Shojaee*† et al. 2025
; however, concerns have also been raised regarding the experimental designs of these studies
Lawsen 2025
. The disconnect between benchmark performance and basic arithmetic capabilities suggests that current evaluation methods may not adequately capture genuine mathematical understanding.

Moreover, LLM responses are often wrapped in long, unnecessary explanations, resulting in computational inefficiency. While it is intuitive to think that a complicated question would require more steps, hence more tokens to reach a solution, recent works show that model responses tend to be lengthy even for simple arithmetic
Chen et al. 2025
, and LLMs often get stuck in "inefficient overthinking"
Shojaee*† et al. 2025
. Long reasoning chains are also observed to lead to incorrect answers
Ha 2025
. To mitigate this issue, recent approaches address overthinking by limiting the number of reasoning steps or redesigning prompts
Sui et al. 2025
. The enormous amount of work in this area calls for a robust and efficient evaluation and benchmarking tool that can effectively diagnose overthinking.

To the best of our knowledge, no existing tool provides a comprehensive and extensible framework for evaluating LLMs on basic mathematical reasoning without relying on static benchmark datasets. This is crucial because: first, the mathematical reasoning tasks we focus on are fundamental operations rather than complex problem solving, unlike MATH
Hendrycks et al. 2021
or HLE
Phan et al. 2025
benchmarks; second, we avoid static benchmark datasets, which prevents memorization and provides a clean assessment of reasoning capabilities; and third, our evaluation framework is designed to be fast and flexible for practical deployment. Furthermore, commonly used evaluation methods, such as LLMs as a judge, are costly and do not generalize well across tasks
Gu et al. 2025
. Most existing frameworks focus exclusively on accuracy metrics while overlooking computational efficiency and reasoning verbosity, thereby failing to capture the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff that is essential for practical deployment.

To address these gaps, we present
LLMThinkBench
, a comprehensive benchmarking and diagnostic framework designed to stress-test basic mathematical reasoning and overthinking in LLMs. Our tool provides researchers and practitioners with: 1)
dynamic test generation
capabilities that create mathematical problems on-the-fly across 14 fundamental task types, preventing data contamination and ensuring fresh evaluation scenarios; 2)
robust answer parsing
through task-specific parsers that combine structured prompting and regular expressions, iteratively refined through analysis of responses across 29 models from major families; 3)
efficiency-aware evaluation metrics
that quantify overthinking through accuracy-vs-token-length tradeoffs, enabling identification of models that balance performance with computational efficiency; 4)
flexible backend support
integrating a fast vLLM-based
Kwon et al. 2023
backend with auto-fallback to HuggingFace Transformers
Wolf et al. 2020
, ensuring broad model compatibility while maintaining high-throughput evaluation; and 5)
full configurability
allowing users to configure experimental and system hyperparameters, extend with custom tasks, and reproduce experiments with optional seeding for consistent results.

LLMThinkBench
is distributed as a
pip-installable package
with both
CLI and API access
, making it easily accessible for researchers and developers. The framework addresses critical limitations in current evaluation practices by: 1) providing
cost-effective evaluation
through task-specific parsers rather than expensive LLM-as-a-judge approaches; 2) generating
reproducible datasets
while avoiding contamination concerns; and 3) offering
diagnostic capabilities
essential for understanding basic math reasoning patterns that remain invisible upon evaluating on traditional benchmarks.

Related Works

Math Reasoning

LLMs achieve high performance on datasets such as GSM8K, GSMPlus, and HARDMath, but recent work has shown that they are unable to perform on basic mathematical tasks at a similar level
Srivastava et al. 2025b
;
Yan et al. 2025
;
Xu and Ma 2024
. Recent benchmarks comprising simple numerical problems evaluate the generalizability of LLMs on basic math tasks
Li et al. 2025a
;
Rahman 2025
. This has led to several works on improving the mathematical reasoning of LLMs. Graph of Thoughts
Besta et al. 2024
models the information generated by an LLM as an arbitrary graph, reporting improvement on sorting tasks. LogicPuzzleRL
Wong et al. 2025
fine-tunes LLMs through reinforcement learning on a suite of custom logic puzzles, showing improvement over algebraic, geometric, and combinatorial tasks. However, these works do not provide comprehensive evaluation tools for basic mathematical tasks.

LLM Overthinking

Recent works demonstrate that LLMs tend to provide lengthy reasoning chains to very simple questions
Chen et al. 2025
. Overthinking can be mitigated using methods such as model-based, reasoning output-based, and input prompts-based efficient reasoning
Sui et al. 2025
. Works such as ThoughtTerminator
Pu et al. 2025
and Self-Braking Tuning
Zhao et al. 2025
have been shown to reduce overthinking. However, existing approaches focus on mitigation rather than systematic evaluation and diagnosis of overthinking behavior.

LLM Reasoning Evaluation

There has been an increase in the number of frameworks that evaluate different aspects of LLM reasoning. Think-Bench
Li et al. 2025b
evaluates thinking efficiency and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) quality in Large Reasoning Models (LRM) on mathematics, physics, and chemistry questions. LegalAgentBench
Li et al. 2024a
evaluates reasoning in the Chinese Legal domain. Despite their strengths, existing toolkits lack an extensible and efficient framework for diagnosing basic math reasoning and overthinking in deterministic tasks. Most frameworks also rely on static datasets, which may suffer from data contamination or expensive LLM-as-a-judge evaluation methods. Addressing this gap,
LLMThinkBench
presents a focused benchmarking platform with 14 tasks, task-specific parsers, and on-the-fly data generation while explicitly avoiding costly LLM-as-a-judge evaluation.

3.1
Task Curation and Dataset Generation

The dataset generation module generates reproducible mathematical evaluation datasets across 14 operations. To ensure comprehensive coverage, our framework organizes tasks into four categories as shown in Table

Given user-specified parameters
(N,F,[r_{\min},r_{\max}],\mathcal{L})
where
N
represents sample count,
F

\mathcal{L}=\{l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{k}\}
specifies list sizes, the system generates randomized test instances using seeded random number generators. For each task
\tau_{i}
and fold
f_{j}
, we sample data points
\mathcal{D}_{i,j}=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{N}\}
ensuring reproducibility across different experimental runs. The generation process adapts to task-specific requirements. For list-based tasks like sorting and statistical operations, we sample
n

[r_{\min},r_{\max}]
where
n\in\mathcal{L}
. For pair-based tasks like comparison and division, we generate two numbers with appropriate constraints (e.g., non-zero denominators for division). This systematic approach ensures comprehensive coverage while maintaining mathematical validity across all generated problems.

3.2
Model Inference

Once task generation is completed, the generated problems are passed to the model inference module for evaluation. The inference module implements a dual-backend architecture that prioritizes performance while ensuring broad compatibility across different model architectures. The system first attempts to use vLLM for high-throughput batched inference, automatically detecting model compatibility and hardware requirements. For each data point
d_{k}\in\mathcal{D}_{i,j}
, the system generates structured prompts following the format: "
Task description
+
Problem instance
+
Your final answer must be in the format \boxed
{
answer
}". The model then generates response
r_{k}=M(\text{prompt}(d_{k});\theta)
where
\theta=\{T,p,\text{max\_tokens}\}
represents configurable inference parameters.

When vLLM encounters compatibility issues or hardware constraints, the system seamlessly falls back to HuggingFace Transformers without requiring user intervention. This dual-backend approach ensures that evaluation can proceed regardless of model architecture or system limitations. The module also implements token counting using a tiktoken module, model-specific tokenizers when available, and word-based estimation as fallback, ensuring accurate efficiency measurements across different tokenization schemes. The inference stage supports multi-GPU tensor parallelism with configurable memory utilization, enabling efficient evaluation of large models while providing detailed progress tracking and error handling throughout the process.

3.3
Answer Extraction

After model inference is completed, the system transitions to the answer extraction stage, where a robust parsing module automatically extracts final answers from diverse model response formats. This stage addresses one of the most critical challenges in automated LLM evaluation: reliably identifying correct answers despite significant variation in response formatting across different model families. Through systematic analysis of response patterns from 29 models across major families (Qwen-7, LLaMA-5, Phi-4, Mistral-6, Others-15+), we developed a hierarchical parsing system that handles over 50 distinct answer formats. The parsing pipeline implements priority-ordered extraction methods:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(r_{k})=\begin{cases}\text{Extract}_{box}(r_{k})&\text{if }\backslash\text{boxed}\{\}\text{ found}\\
\text{Extract}_{exp}(r_{k})&\text{if explicit found}\\
\text{Extract}_{ctx}(r_{k})&\text{if markdown found}\\
\text{Extract}_{fall}(r_{k})&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(1)

Each extraction method implements task-specific validation logic to ensure extracted answers represent final results rather than problem restatements. For example, the sorting task parser validates that extracted lists contain the same elements as input with correct ordering:
\text{Valid}_{sort}(\mathcal{P}(r),d)=\text{sorted}(\text{Input}(d))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\mathcal{P}(r)
. Similarly, arithmetic tasks filter out input numbers to prevent extraction of problem components rather than solutions.

3.4
Overthinking Analysis

The final stage processes extracted answers to compute comprehensive efficiency metrics that quantify the accuracy-verbosity tradeoff. This module implements our
Overthinking Score
metric, which addresses critical limitations in traditional accuracy-only evaluation approaches. The Overthinking Score uses harmonic mean formulation to balance accuracy and computational efficiency:

\mathcal{O}_{i}=\frac{2\cdot A_{i}\cdot E_{t,i}}{A_{i}+E_{t,i}}

(2)

where accuracy
A_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}[\mathcal{P}(r_{k})=\text{GroundTruth}(d_{k})]
and token efficiency

E_{t,i}=1-\frac{\bar{T}_{i}-T_{\min}}{T_{\max}-T_{\min}}
where
\bar{T}_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|r_{k}|
.

The harmonic mean formulation is motivated by F1-score principles
Yacouby and Axman 2020
, addressing the inherent imbalance between accuracy and efficiency metrics. Unlike arithmetic means that can be dominated by either component, the harmonic mean penalizes extreme imbalances:
\lim_{E_{t}\to 0}\mathcal{O}=0
and
\lim_{A\to 0}\mathcal{O}=0
. This ensures that models achieving high accuracy through excessive verbosity receive lower efficiency scores than models with balanced performance.

The analysis module aggregates results across multiple folds using mean and standard deviation calculations, providing statistical robustness and confidence intervals for reported metrics. The final reporting system generates efficiency rankings, detailed performance breakdowns, and JSON exports for downstream analysis. This comprehensive approach provides actionable insights for model selection and deployment decisions in resource-constrained environments.

Results and Discussion

We evaluated 53 language models across multiple families using
LLMThinkBench
, revealing
striking contradictions
between standard benchmark performance and basic math reasoning. These findings expose systematic inefficiencies in modern LLMs and challenge core assumptions about model scaling and reasoning enhancement.

4.1
Performance on Basic Math Reasoning

Models that excel on complex benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH often struggle with basic arithmetic tasks,
suggesting they rely more on pattern recognition than genuine mathematical understanding. We observed significant different between models’ reported performance on standard benchmarks and their capabilities on basic arithmetic tasks. While many of these models achieve impressive scores on GSM8K
Srivastava et al. 2025b
;
Srivastava et al. 2025a
and similar benchmarks

The Qwen3 family shows particularly interesting scaling behavior. The 14B model achieves 86.52% accuracy with strong efficiency (0.725), but the larger 32B variant surprisingly drops to 84.13% accuracy (results in Appendix Table
3
). This non-monotonic scaling suggests that larger models don’t always improve on fundamental reasoning tasks, possibly due to overfitting to complex patterns while losing sight of basic operations.
LLMThinkBench’s
evaluation across fundamental mathematical operations exposes these gaps that traditional benchmarks miss.

4.2
The Overthinking Problem

Reasoning models generate
\sim
18x more tokens than standard models while achieving worse accuracy, and their performance collapses when token generation is constrained.
We discovered systematic overthinking behavior across model families, especially in reasoning-enhanced variants as shown in Figure
2
(b). The Phi-4-reasoning models
Abdin et al. 2025
exemplify this issue, generating 6,780 tokens on average compared to 378 tokens for standard Phi-4, while achieving lower accuracy (69.54% vs 78.92%). When we constrained reasoning models to 1,024 tokens, their performance dropped dramatically. Phi-4-reasoning fell from 72.23% to 53.48% accuracy, while Phi-4-reasoning-plus dropped from 69.54% to 44.33% (see Appendix Table
5
). Figure
2
shows this relationship across different reasoning models. These results suggest that reasoning models have developed a dependency on verbose exploration rather than efficient problem-solving. They seem to "think out loud" extensively without necessarily thinking more effectively.

4.3
Quantization Effects on LLM Reasoning

Math reasoning capabilities are robust to quantization, with larger models maintaining performance even under aggressive 4-bit compression while smaller models are more vulnerable.
Our quantization analysis reveals surprising resilience in mathematical reasoning. Most models maintain performance well under 8-bit quantization. The Qwen2.5-32B model achieved 73.08% accuracy under 8-bit quantization compared to 72.90% at full precision (see Appendix Table
4
). However, 4-bit quantization shows more complex patterns. Larger models (32B, 72B) handle aggressive quantization remarkably well, while smaller models suffer more degradation. The 0.5B model drops from 21.31% to 12.77% accuracy under 4-bit quantization. Figure
2
illustrates these size-dependent effects. This size-dependent resilience suggests that larger models have more redundant pathways for mathematical reasoning, making them more robust to precision reduction. This finding has important implications for deploying efficient models in resource-constrained environments.

4.4
Model Size, Verbosity, and Accuracy Relationships

Larger models don’t always perform better on basic reasoning tasks, and the most efficient models balance accuracy with response conciseness rather than simply scaling up parameters.
Our analysis reveals complex relationships between model size, response length, and accuracy that challenge simple assumptions about model performance. Figure
2
(a) and also figure
6
in Appendix
C
shows these relationships across different model families. Interestingly, some smaller models achieve better efficiency scores than their larger counterparts. The Llama-3.1-70B model achieves strong efficiency (0.691) despite its size. The overthinking score metric we introduce helps identify models that balance accuracy with response conciseness. Models with high efficiency scores solve problems correctly without unnecessary verbosity, suggesting they have learned more direct reasoning pathways.

5
Conclusion

We developed LLMThinkBench, a robust evaluation framework for assessing basic mathematical reasoning and overthinking behavior in language models. Using
LLMThinkBench
, we evaluated 53 model which revealed fundamental gaps between benchmark performance and basic mathematical reasoning capabilities.
LLMThinkBench
addresses critical limitations in existing evaluation frameworks and provides essential diagnostic capabilities for understanding fundamental reasoning patterns invisible to traditional benchmarks, enabling more informed model development and deployment decisions.

Limitations

1)

LLMThinkBench
focuses exclusively on basic mathematical operations and may not capture more complex reasoning required in real-world scenarios.
2)
The automated evaluation enables scalable assessment, though human studies could provide additional insights into metric interpretability.
3)
While dynamic test generation prevents memorization, models may still exploit learned mathematical patterns.
4)
The framework’s effectiveness depends on task-specific parsers that require updates as model response formats evolve.

Ethics Statement

LLMThinkBench
is designed as an evaluation tool that does not generate harmful content or pose direct ethical risks. The framework evaluates publicly available language models on mathematical tasks without collecting personal data or requiring human annotations that could raise privacy concerns. All evaluated models are used according to their respective licensing terms, and our tool respects model usage policies. The evaluation results are intended to provide objective insights into model capabilities and should not be used to make discriminatory decisions about individuals or groups. We encourage responsible use of evaluation results and acknowledge that performance on basic mathematical tasks represents only one dimension of model capability.

Broader Impact Statement

LLMThinkBench
contributes to the responsible development and deployment of language models by providing transparent evaluation of fundamental reasoning capabilities. By identifying models that achieve high accuracy without excessive computational overhead, our framework supports more sustainable AI deployment practices. The tool’s ability to detect overthinking behavior can help researchers develop more efficient reasoning approaches, potentially reducing the environmental impact of large-scale model inference. For researchers,
LLMThinkBench
enables informed model selection based on accuracy-efficiency tradeoffs, promoting cost-effective deployment in resource-constrained environments. The open-source nature of our tool democratizes access to comprehensive LLM evaluation, supporting broader participation in AI research and development across academic and industry settings.

Appendix E
Parsing Robustness

LLMThinkBench’s parsing layer plays a critical role in enabling accurate evaluation of LLMs. Given the variability in LLM outputs—ranging from concise answers to verbose multi-step reasoning with inconsistent formatting—naïve parsing methods often fail, misclassifying correct answers due to minor stylistic differences.

To address this, we developed a multi-layered parsing architecture based on advanced regular expressions and task-specific heuristics. Through systematic analysis of outputs from diverse model families, we identified common formatting patterns and response structures. The parser employs a hierarchical strategy, prioritizing explicit formats such as and markdown blocks, with fallbacks to phrases like “The answer is X,” LaTeX expressions, scientific notation, and final-line inference.

The system also normalizes numeric formats, filters embedded input data, and distinguishes between calculated results and extraneous text. This design improves extraction reliability, reduces false negatives, and minimizes manual post-processing, enabling scalable and robust benchmarking across a wide range of LLMs.

Appendix F
Dataset generation Details

We use a structured, programmatic approach to generate data on the fly within the LLMThinkBench framework. This avoids the risk of data contamination from benchmarks used during training, which can otherwise lead to inflated performance. By generating new examples each time, we ensure that evaluations accurately reflect the model’s actual reasoning ability, rather than its memorization.

The framework allows precise control over key parameters such as numerical ranges (
min_val
,
max_val
), list sizes (
list_size
), and the number of samples (
num_samples
). This flexibility supports a wide range of tasks in basic math, statistics, and logic. Task-specific rules ensure meaningful and balanced inputs. For example,
DivisionTask
avoids zero denominators, while
ComparisonTask
includes an equal mix of “greater than,” “less than,” and “equal to” cases to prevent bias.

Lastly, to support reproducibility, we include a random seed option that allows users to regenerate the same test data across different models or runs.

Appendix G
Challanges in Parsing

Parsing outputs from large language models (LLMs) poses significant challenges due to their variability in syntax, style, and semantics—even under explicit formatting instructions. Unlike structured data, LLM responses often embed answers within verbose or inconsistent natural language, use varied numerical formats, and apply diverse enclosures. As a result, simple pattern-matching approaches frequently yield false negatives by misinterpreting correct answers, especially when reasoning steps obscure the final result. Reliable answer extraction thus requires a careful balance between tolerance to diverse formats and precision to avoid false positives.

To address this, we designed a robust, multi-layered parsing system tailored specifically for LLM outputs. The development of this system required an iterative refinement process grounded in empirical analysis of model outputs across diverse LLM families. Initial parsing rules were designed based on common output patterns; however, as evaluation expanded to 40+ models, we observed a wide range of response formats, inconsistencies, and edge cases. To address these issues, we systematically reviewed misparsed outputs from 29 models, categorizing failure cases (e.g., embedded input reuse, non-standard syntax, and ambiguous formatting). We incrementally updated the parsing logic with additional regular expressions, validation checks, and fallback strategies. Each iteration was evaluated against a curated set of challenging cases to ensure improved robustness without introducing overfitting or false positives. This continuous feedback loop enabled us to generalize across model behaviors and ensured high extraction accuracy even as new LLM variants were introduced.
