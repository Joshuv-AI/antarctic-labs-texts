Reproducibility in the Control of Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand Systems
Xinling Li, Meshal Alharbi, Daniele Gammelli, James Harrison, Filipe Rodrigues, Maximilian Schiffer, Marco Pavone, Emilio Frazzoli, Jinhua Zhao, Gioele Zardini

Abstract
Autonomous Mobility-on-Demand (AMoD)systems, powered by advances in robotics, control, and Machine Learning (ML), offer a promising paradigm for future urban transportation. AMoDoffers fast and personalized travel services by leveraging centralized control of autonomous vehicle fleets to optimize operations and enhance service performance. However, the rapid growth of this field has outpaced the development of standardized practices for evaluating and reporting results, leading to significant challenges in reproducibility. As AMoDcontrol algorithms become increasingly complex and data-driven, a lack of transparency in modeling assumptions, experimental setups, and algorithmic implementation hinders scientific progress and undermines confidence in the results. This paper presents a systematic study of reproducibility in AMoDresearch. We identify key components across the research pipeline, spanning system modeling, control problems, simulation design, algorithm specification, and evaluation, and analyze common sources of irreproducibility. We survey prevalent practices in the literature, highlight gaps, and propose a structured framework to assess and improve reproducibility. Specifically, concrete guidelines are offered, along with a “reproducibility checklist”, to support future work in achieving replicable, comparable, and extensible results. While focused on AMoD, the principles and practices we advocate generalize to a broader class of cyber-physical systems that rely on networked autonomy and data-driven control. This work aims to lay the foundation for a more transparent and reproducible research culture in the design and deployment of intelligent mobility systems.



I
Introduction

The rapid urbanization over the past decades has led to a significant rise in private automobile use.
While private vehicles offer convenient, door-to-door service at any time, their proliferation has resulted in widespread traffic congestion, increased emissions, and a substantial loss of productive hours
[
schrank20112011
]
.
In response,
Mobility-on-Demand (MoD)services have emerged as a compelling alternative.
By dynamically allocating vehicles based on real-time demand,
MoDsystems provide personalized travel options while reducing dependence on private car ownership.
As such, they retain many of the advantages of private vehicles, offering a more sustainable and scalable mobility solution.
With advances in autonomous driving technologies,
AMoDhas gained traction as a key enabler of next-generation transportation systems
[
Pavone2015
]
.

AMoDsystems (i.e., fleets of robo-taxis performing on-demand mobility tasks) autonomously manage vehicle fleets to respond to passenger requests and satisfy transportation demand effectively.
Such systems are inherently complex: they involve high degrees of interaction and require coordination across spatial and temporal scales
[
zardini2022analysis
]
.
As a result, different studies often focus on distinct operational challenges, each adopting specific assumptions regarding system dynamics and passenger behavior.
Such methodological diversity complicates efforts to ensure research reproducibility and benchmark comparability.
Nevertheless, reproducibility remains a cornerstone of rigorous scientific practice.
It increases credibility, accelerates progress, and enables meaningful comparisons across research contributions
[
munafo2017manifesto
]
.
In disciplines such as computational science, robotics, and management science, reproducibility is widely promoted via standardized evaluation procedures, common benchmarks, and the routine of sharing code and data
[
peng2011reproducible
,
leitner2017acrv
,
baines2024need
,
fivsar2024reproducibility
]
.
By contrast, while the importance of reproducibility is gaining recognition in transportation research
[
wu2024reproducibility
,
riehl2025revisiting
]
, consistent practices and community standards are still scarce.

This paper aims to address this gap by identifying and analyzing the key components that determine the reproducibility of
AMoDcontrol research.
We propose that these components, ranging from modeling choices to experimental setup, be explicitly documented in research outputs.
While the focus is on
AMoD, the principles discussed are applicable to a broad range of networked control and optimization problems, including those in aviation, maritime transport, and other complex cyber-physical systems.

The term
reproducibility
has been used to describe varying degrees of rigor in how scientific findings are communicated and validated
[
goodman2016does
,
national2019reproducibility
]
. At its most basic level, reproducible research requires sufficient transparency and methodological clarity to enable others to recreate the results and verify the underlying logic. This minimal standard typically assumes access to the same input data and requires that the described methods and conditions are detailed enough to reproduce the original outcomes. A more rigorous standard is achieved when independent researchers, using comparable techniques and assumptions and not necessarily the same data, can arrive at similar findings and inferences. This higher level, which is sometimes referred to as inferential reproducibility or replicability, is critical not only for confirming the robustness of scientific claims but also for enabling future research to build upon and extend the original work. In this article, we strive to provide recommendations that strengthen both levels of reproducibility of
AMoDresearch, though we do not draw a strict distinction between the terms.

AMoDsystems operate fleets of

Users request trips via mobile applications, and the system must coordinate vehicles in real time to deliver a high-quality service.
The
AMoDcontrol problem, therefore, centers on optimizing fleet management, with performance metrics including profitability, service rate, passenger waiting time, and environmental impact.

To define an

The system model provides a tractable mathematical representation of the real-world
AMoDnetwork and serves as the foundation for problem formulation.
Based on such a model, researchers define the operation of interest, typically some aspect of fleet control constrained by infrastructure properties (e.g., road capacity), and select an appropriate objective function, which often reflects operational efficiency, profitability, or sustainability.
Crucially, any proposed solution is meaningful only in the precise context of its assumptions.
These include both explicit modeling assumptions (e.g., demand elasticity, congestion models, penetration rates of new technologies), and implicit design choices (e.g., simulation fidelity).
These assumptions directly affect the applicability, generalizability, and replicability of the results.
Once a solution is developed, it should be evaluated through experiments that benchmark performance, demonstrate robustness, and assess computational tractability.
Clear reporting of all steps in this pipeline is essential to support reproducible research.

II
Mathematical Modeling AMoD Systems

The essence of modeling
AMoDsystems is to capture the dynamic interplay between passengers, vehicles, and their interactions within the transportation network.
The system model serves as the foundation for any resource allocation strategy, as it formally defines the entities involved, the resources (
AVs), the consumers (passengers), and the logic governing their interactions.
In the existing
AMoDliterature, most system models can be broadly categorized into three main types: queueing-theoretic models, network flow models, and vehicle-centric (agent-based) ones.
Each modeling paradigm is built upon distinct assumptions and is tailored to highlight different operational characteristics of
AMoDsystems.
This section introduces these three modeling approaches, focusing on their respective assumptions, advantages, and limitations.
A clear understanding of these trade-offs is critical for both selecting an appropriate modeling framework and interpreting the applicability and reproducibility of derived results.
We close the section by encouraging future research to explicitly specify the chosen system model, including its assumptions and structural limitations.
The goal of this section is not only to survey the dominant modeling strategies, but also to establish a reference that enables transparent communication and comparability across studies.

II-A

Queueing-theoretic models

Queueing theory provides a mathematical framework for analyzing waiting lines and service systems.
In the context of
AMoD, queueing-theoretic models typically represent trip requests as stochastic arrival processes acting as servers, with vehicles being served when they pick up passengers.
Locations in the network are modeled as queues, and system dynamics are described by the flow of vehicles and passengers between them
[
zhang2018analysis
,
zhang2016control
]
.

II-A
1
Assumptions

First, these models represent passengers and vehicles as
discrete
entities.
Customers are assumed to arrive according to an exogenous stochastic process (e.g., a Poisson process), reaching the origin station at a
fixed
rate and selecting their destination based on probabilities
[
iglesias2019bcmp
,
braverman2019empty
]
.

To cast the
AMoDproblem within the queueing framework, specific assumptions about passenger arrivals, travel times, and passenger behavior are needed.
Specifically, common assumptions include: demand following a time-invariant Poisson distribution, travel times between stations following an exponential distribution, and passengers immediately leaving the system if not matched with a vehicle
[
zhang2016control
,
zhang2018analysis
,
iglesias2019bcmp
]
.
Such assumptions simplify associated problems while preserving accuracy, as demonstrated in
[
larson1981urban
]
, where reasonable deviations were shown to have negligible impact on performance.

II-A
2
Benefits

The queueing-theoretic model preserves the discrete nature of passenger arrivals and allows the direct application of queueing theory to compute key performance metrics, such as node throughput
[
shortle2018fundamentals
]
, which corresponds to vehicle movement and availability in
AMoDsystems.
It explicitly accounts for system randomness and ensures performance in expectation.
The derived policies maintain system equilibrium, preventing queues and waiting times from growing indefinitely
[
iglesias2019bcmp
,
zhang2016control
,
zhang2018analysis
]
.

II-A
3
Limitations

As discussed in
Section

II-A1
, queueing-theoretic models rely on strong assumptions regarding passenger arrival rates and travel times.
Such assumptions are necessary to derive analytical results and ensure tractability.
In many formulations, arrival rates are further assumed to be time-invariant to facilitate steady-state analysis.
However, such simplifications may not accurately reflect the dynamics of real-world transportation systems, particularly in long-term planning scenarios where demand patterns are non-stationary and influenced by external factors.

II-B

Network flow models

Network-flow models are among the most widely adopted approaches in
AMoDresearch.
Such models represent the transportation system as a directed graph, where nodes correspond to physical or logical locations, and arcs to possible vehicle or passenger movements.
System dynamics are captured by flows of vehicles and passengers across the network.

II-B
1
Assumptions

When paired with fluid dynamics
[
pavone2012robotic
]
, network flow models treat vehicle and passenger flows as continuous variables.
Although this abstraction deviates from the discrete nature of individual trips and vehicles, it enables significant computational simplifications.
For instance, problems originally formulated as integer programs can often be relaxed into linear programs, allowing for more scalable optimization even when considering large networks. In fact, when studying large transportation networks, a recent study showed that the difference between using a microscopic simulation or a (continuous) network flow model is marginal when analyzing effects on a system-level
[
molter2024public
,
lienkamp2024column
]
, which further motivates the use of network flow models.

II-B
2
Benefits

These models align naturally with many foundational ideas in transportation science, such as demand modeling via
Origin-Destination (OD)pairs and network equilibrium analysis.
The graph-based structure offers an intuitive and modular framework for capturing fleet movements, demand fulfillment, and operational constraints.
When a sufficient number of nodes is used, network flow models can effectively approximate free-floating
AMoDsystems with high fidelity and minimal discretization errors
[
spieser2016shared
]
.

II-B
3
Limitations

Despite their widespread use and conceptual clarity, network flow models exhibit limitations when transitioning from abstract flows to actionable control policies.
In most formulations, control decisions are expressed as aggregate flows between nodes rather than explicit vehicle trajectories.
As a result, translating these flows into path-level instructions for
AVsrequires an additional step of path reconstruction.
While this step is mostly bypassed in simulation studies, it becomes critical in real-world implementations, where routing feasibility, vehicle-level state, and road network constraints (e.g., capacity) must be respected
[
wollenstein2021routing
]
.

II-C

Vehicle-centric models

Unlike network flow or queueing-theoretic models that abstract away vehicle identities in favor of aggregate flows, this approach explicitly tracks the state and control actions of every vehicle in the system.

II-C
1
Assumptions

Vehicle-centric models assume that system dynamics can be described and controlled at the level of individual vehicles.
The state of each vehicle typically includes location, occupancy, battery level, and assigned task (e.g., serving a customer or rebalancing)
[
enders2023hybrid
,
alonso2017demand
,
boewing2020vehicle
]
.
Control actions, such as dispatching, routing, and rebalancing, are determined independently for each vehicle, often based on local or partially observed information.

II-C
2
Benefits

This modeling paradigm enables direct and interpretable decision-making for each vehicle, which simplifies implementation in real-world fleet management systems.
In contrast to network flow models that require a path reconstruction step to translate abstract flows into individual actions, such models yield executable plans by design.
Moreover, they naturally accommodate heterogeneous environments: fleets, supporting operational features such as vehicle-specific capacities, preferences, or constraints.

II-C
3
Limitations

Under centralized control, the main limitation of vehicle-centric models lies in their scalability.
Because decisions are made at the individual vehicle level, the number of control variables grows linearly with the fleet size, unlike queueing or network flow models, which typically scale with the number of
ODpairs.
This fine-grained formulation significantly increases the computational burden, especially for large-scale systems, and can limit the tractability of centralized optimization approaches. While distributed algorithms can help maintain the scalability, they may compromise the quality of the decisions and overall fleet performance.

II-D

Reproducibility considerations

Modeling choices play a fundamental role in determining the reproducibility and comparability of
AMoDcontrol research.
Each modeling paradigm carries implicit assumptions that shape the problem formulation, solution methods, and interpretation of results.
However, these assumptions are not always made explicit in published work, making it difficult for future researchers to replicate studies or fairly compare approaches.
For instance, queueing-based models may obscure how time-varying demand is handled, network flow models often omit details of path reconstruction logic, and vehicle-centric ones require precise specification of vehicle states and constraints.
To promote reproducibility, researchers should clearly document the chosen modeling framework, including its structural assumptions, abstractions, and any simplifications made for tractability.

The deployment of

The primary goal of the designed policies is to
maximize

Such operations are subject to various system-specific constraints.
In this section, we introduce the key operational tasks and constraints governing
AMoDsystems.
Rather than providing a comprehensive review of existing solutions, our focus is on defining these tasks to establish a clear context for future references.

Matching

Given the set of passengers and vehicles available, the matching problem focuses on the assignment of the vehicles to the passengers (

).
In the one-to-one passenger-to-vehicle assignment setting, the matching problem can be formulated as a
Bipartite Matching Problem (BMP)
[

,
woywood2024multi
,
hu2020artificial
]

III-A
2
Rebalancing

Vehicle rebalancing, or relocation, refers to the process of redistributing available vehicles to areas where supply and demand are imbalanced (
Fig.

2(b)
).
By optimizing fleet distribution, rebalancing improves service rates, improves utilization, and can reduce average wait times and delays
[
wallar2018vehicle
]
.
Its benefits are particularly significant in scenarios where vehicle supply is scarce
[
li4798020learning
]
.

III-A
3
Vehicle routing

The vehicle routing problem focuses on optimizing the schedule to visit stops given a set of tasks (
Fig.

2(c)
).
A comprehensive review on this topic can be found in
[
schiffer2019vehicle
]
.
In
AMoDsystems, routing occurs at two levels.
The first is the level of the road network, where, given an
ODpair, the goal is to determine the optimal route for vehicles to navigate the road network efficiently
[
lazar2021learning
]
. In this context, algorithmic solutions may go beyond computing naive shortest paths. Particularly in an
AMoDsetting, there is a huge potential for fleet optimal routing by either balancing the respective flows over alternative paths
[
jalota2023online
]
or staggering them temporally
[
coppola2024staggered
]
to avoid congestion bottlenecks.
The second is the vehicle operation level, which involves sequencing the order in which vehicles visit stations based on rebalancing, dispatch, or a combination of both.
In the
AMoDliterature, the term “routing” usually refers to the vehicle operation level.
