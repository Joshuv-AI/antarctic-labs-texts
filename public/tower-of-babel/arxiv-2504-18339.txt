Optimal Control of Sensor-Induced Illusions on Robotic Agents
Lorenzo Medici, Steven M. LaValle, Başak Sakçak

Abstract
This paper presents a novel problem of creating and regulating localization and navigation illusions considering two agents: a receiver and a producer. A receiver is moving on a plane localizing itself using the intensity of signals from three known towers observed at its position. Based on this position estimate, it follows a simple policy to reach its goal. The key idea is that a producer alters the signal intensities to alter the position estimate of the receiver while ensuring it reaches a different destination with the belief that it reached its goal. We provide a precise mathematical formulation of this problem and show that it allows standard techniques from control theory to be applied to generate localization and navigation illusions that result in a desired receiver behavior.



I
INTRODUCTION

Humans experience illusions when sensory organs are exposed to stimuli that, when processed by the nervous system, result in a perceptual experience that does not coincide with the reality.
For example, the famous Ponzo illusion results from two identical lines being perceived as having different length
[
1
,
2
]
.
Analogous to the human sensory organs and nervous system are the sensors and the processor of a robotic agent. The sensors provide measurements of the agent’s body and the physical world, and a processor
condenses these measurements into an internal state that represents the state of the physical world. Therefore, robotic agents are susceptible to illusions similar to how humans are.

In this work, we consider robotic agents that can sense and act. We are interested in controlling the perception of a robotic agent, that is, controlling its internal state.
Typically, it is not possible to alter the internal state directly, therefore, appropriate sensor measurements must be generated by altering the physical world that lead to a desired internal state. The agent determines its actions based on its internal state. Therefore, by altering its internal state, a desired behavior can be achieved. An important issue is to ensure that the generated perception is plausible, that is, it is consistent with the agent’s model of the physical world.

Many of the ideas in this work will build upon
[
3
]
, in which a general mathematical formulation was introduced to model the process of altering the perceptual experience of an agent. In this work, we show how these ideas can be applied to pose and solve robotics problems.
The concrete problem that we address in this paper is creating localization and navigation illusions for an agent, a receiver, moving on a plane and localizing itself by trilateration using the intensity of signals emitted from three towers measured at agent position (see Fig.
1
). The agent sensor model is based on inverse-square law parametrized with signal intensity at tower position used for calibration.
Based on this model, the agent can uniquely determine its position as the intersection of the level circles corresponding to measured intensities. However, setting the signal intensity at tower base different than the calibrated value results in a position estimate that is different than the agent’s actual position.
We consider a receiver agent with this type of localization following a state-feedback policy that leads the agent to its goal under correct localization. We address the problem of designing an agent, a producer, which controls the signal intensities of the towers. The goal of the producer is to ensure that the receiver reaches a goal that is different than its own while believing that it has reached its destination.
We model the receiver dynamics together with its internal state, that is, the position estimate and design a regulator (producer) that stabilizes this system while considering plausibility constraints.

II
Preliminary Definitions

In this section, we briefly review the relevant background and define notions central to the paper.

II-A

Agents in a universe

We model an agent capable of sensing and acting in an environment shared with other agents as two coupled systems named
internal
and
external
systems, similar to previous work
[
20
]
. An internal system is seen as a centralized computation component that processes sensor observations and actions. Consequently, an external system is the physical world. Although our notion of an internal system is general, for the scope of this paper, we will consider an internal system which maintains a representation of the external system state.
To this end, we assume that the internal system of the agent is
model-based
coinciding with the use of the term in robotics and control-theory. We call this model an
intrinsic model
of the external system since it describes the external system from the agent’s perspective.

Definition 1

(Intrinsic model)
.

An intrinsic model of the external system corresponding to the sets of actions
U
and observations
Y
is a tuple
{\mathcal{M}}_{U\rightarrow Y}=(X,f,h)
consisting of

•

Set of states
X
.

•

A nondeterministic X-state transition function (XTF)
f:X\times U\rightarrow\pow(X)\setminus\emptyset
, in which,
\pow(X)
denotes the power set of
x
, such that
f(x_{k},u_{k})
is the set of possible states that can be reached at stage
k+1
when an action
u_{k}
is taken at state
x_{k}
at stage
k
.

•

A sensor-mapping
h:X\rightarrow\pow(Y)
such that
y_{k}\in h(x_{k})
is an observation that an agent receives when at state
x_{k}
at stage
k
.

We use
U\rightarrow Y
in the subscript of
{\mathcal{M}}_{U\rightarrow Y}
to emphasize that the model captures the input-output relationship between the actions applied to and observations received from the external system.
In case the images of
f
and
h
are always singletons,
{\mathcal{M}}_{U\rightarrow Y}
is a deterministic model of the external system. In this paper, we will only consider nondeterministic models together with deterministic models as a special case.
However, probabilistic formulations are also possible, see for example
[
3
,
20
]
.

The next definition makes the term
agent
precise within this model-based context, which corresponds to an internal system and an intrinsic model of the external system. The internal system state is an
information state
(I-state) according to the definition introduced in
[
21
, Chapter 11]
which is based on the von Neumann-Morgenstern notion of information for games with imperfect information.

Definition 2

(Agent)
.

An agent
\mathcal{A}
is the tuple
\mathcal{A}=({\mathcal{M}}_{U\rightarrow Y},{\mathcal{I}},U,Y,\phi,\pi)
, in which

•

{\mathcal{I}}
is the set of information states.

•

U
and
Y
are the sets of actions and observations, respectively.

•

Transition from an I-state
{\iota}_{k-1}
to the next one
{\iota}_{k}
with the receipt of
y_{k}
is described by the information-transition function (ITF)
\phi:{\mathcal{I}}\times Y\rightarrow{\mathcal{I}}
so that
{\iota}_{k}=\phi({\iota}_{k-1},y_{k})
.

•

A policy
\pi:{\mathcal{I}}\rightarrow U
determines an action to take at each I-state.

An intrinsic model is an approximation of the external system, that is, it does not necessarily coincide with the “reality”. However, to define illusions, we need to rely on a third-person or godlike perspective.
The reality for an agent is described as a model as well, which we call the
extrinsic model
.
Let
\Omega
be the set of all possible states of the external system, containing all the agents, from this godlike perspective, named the
universe space
.

Definition 3

(Extrinsic model)
.

An extrinsic model for
\mathcal{A}^{(i)}
is a tuple

\left(\Omega,{\bar{f}},U,{\bar{h}}^{(i)},Y^{(i)}\right)

consisting of:

•

A set of
universe states

\Omega
.

•

A set of
actions

U=U^{(1)}\times U^{(2)}\times\dots\times U^{(N)}
, in which
U^{(n)}
,
n=1,\dots,N
, is the action set of
n^{\text{th}}
agent.

•

A
universe transition function
(UTF)
{\bar{f}}:\Omega\times U\rightarrow\Omega
such that

\omega_{k+1}={\bar{f}}(\omega_{k},u_{k}),

in which the subscripts indicate the stage indices.

•

A sensor mapping
{\bar{h}}^{(i)}:\Omega\rightarrow Y^{(i)}
and set of observations
Y^{(i)}
.

II-B

Producer and receiver agents

In this work, we consider two special agents: a
producer
and a
receiver
.
A producer agent delivers a targeted perceptual experience to a receiver agent.

Definition 4

(Receiver)
.

A receiver is an agent
\mathcal{A}^{r}=({\mathcal{M}}_{U^{r}\rightarrow Y^{r}},{\mathcal{I}}^{r},U^{r},Y^{r},\phi^{r},\pi^{r})
. An extrinsic model for the receiver is the tuple
(\Omega,{\bar{f}},U,{\bar{h}}^{r},Y^{r})
, in which
U=U^{p}\times U^{r}
, and
U^{p}
is the set of producer actions.

We will consider an omniscient producer which means that its intrinsic model coincides with the extrinsic model and its sensor mapping is a bijection revealing the full state of the universe. The next definition makes this precise.

Definition 5

(Producer)
.

Let
(\Omega,{\bar{f}},U,{\bar{h}}^{p},Y^{p})
be an extrinsic model for the producer such that
U=U^{p}\times U^{r}
,
Y^{p}=\Omega
and
{\bar{h}}^{p}
is an identity mapping, that is,
y^{p}={\bar{h}}^{p}(\omega)=\omega
.
A producer is an agent
\mathcal{A}^{p}=({\mathcal{M}}_{U^{p}\rightarrow Y^{p}},{\mathcal{I}}^{p},U^{p},Y^{p},\phi^{p},\pi^{p})

such that the intrinsic model
{\mathcal{M}}_{U^{p}\rightarrow Y^{p}}=(X^{p},f^{p},h^{p})
satisfies that
X^{p}=\Omega
,
f^{p}={\bar{f}}
, and
h^{p}={\bar{h}}^{p}
.

Fig.
2
shows these two agents coupled to the same universe. Altering the perception of a receiver implies determining actions
u^{p}
which alter the universe state and causing a change in receiver I-state. A receiver behavior that is different than its intended one can be achieved by a careful selection of producer actions that result in receiver actions through receiver I-states and policy.

II-C

Defining plausibility and illusions

Illusions occur if the intrinsic model of the receiver does not coincide with its extrinsic model. In
[
3
]
, we have introduced a general way to model the correspondence between these models from a third-person perspective.
Let
\mathcal{A}^{r}=({\mathcal{M}}_{U^{r}\rightarrow Y^{r}},{\mathcal{I}}^{r},U^{r},Y^{r},\phi^{r},\pi^{r})
be a receiver and let
(\Omega,{\bar{f}},U,{\bar{h}}^{r},Y^{r})
be its extrinsic model.

Definition 6

(Correspondence)
.

Let
C\subset X^{r}\times\Omega
be called a
correspondence relation
. If
(x^{r},\omega)\in C
, then we say that
x^{r}
corresponds to
\omega
. If
C
is one to many and onto, then there exists a correspondence function
\alpha:\Omega\rightarrow X^{r}
such that
(\alpha(w),w)\in C
for all
\omega\in\Omega
. Thus,
x^{r}=\alpha(\omega)
.

Fig. 2
:
Producer and receiver agents coupled to the same universe.

Because we are considering model-based internal systems, the I-state reached by interacting with the external world should be consistent with the model, that is, it should be plausible. This is captured by the next definition.

Definition 7

(Plausibility)
.

Let
M\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}^{r}\times X^{r}
be a
model relation
. An I-state
{\iota}^{r}
is called
plausible
if there exists a
x^{r}\in X^{r}
such that
({\iota}^{r},x^{r})\in M
. An I-state is called
implausible
if it is not plausible.

An illusion occurs when the receiver I-state does not correspond to the reality. This correspondence relation is defined by composing the model and correspondence relations.

Definition 8

(Reality)
.

Let
R\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}^{r}\times\Omega
be a reality relation. A pair
({\iota}^{r},\omega)\in R
if there exists a
x^{r}\in X^{r}
such that
({\iota}^{r},x^{r})\in M
and
(x^{r},\omega)\in C
.

Finally, the notion of illusion can be defined through the reality relation.

Definition 9

(Illusion)
.

A pair
({\iota}^{r},\omega)\in{\mathcal{I}}^{r}\times\Omega
is an illusion if
{\iota}^{r}
is plausible and
({\iota}^{r},\omega)\not\in R
.

To make these notions more concrete, consider the example given in Figure
1(a)
for which
X^{r}=\Omega^{r}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
{\mathcal{I}}^{r}=\pow(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. We define the correspondence and model relations as
C=\{(x^{r},\omega)\in X^{r}\times\Omega^{r}\mid x^{r}=\omega\}
and
M=\{({\iota}^{r},x^{r})\in{\mathcal{I}}^{r}\times X^{r}\mid x^{r}\in{\iota}^{r}\}
. In the example,
{\iota}^{r}_{k}
is a plausible I-state according to the agent’s model since there exists an
x^{r}\in X^{r}
such that
x^{r}\in{\iota}^{r}_{k}
(note that
{\iota}^{r}_{k}
is a singleton in the figure). However, the respective
x^{r}
does not correspond to the actual state of the universe, that is,
(x^{r},\omega_{k})\not\in C
since
x^{r}\not=w_{k}
. Therefore, the pair
({\iota}^{r}_{k},\omega_{k})
an illusion.

III
Optimal Control of Sensor-Induced Illusions

In this section we introduce the problem that we address in this paper, that is, the optimal control of localization and navigation illusions for a receiver, which entails determining an optimal policy for the producer. Consequently, we describe our proposed solution considering two receiver models.

III-A

Problem Description

We consider a receiver moving on a plane and localizing itself using the signal intensity measurements obtained from three signal emitting towers. The producer does not have an embodiment but it can control the signal intensity of towers.

We start with defining the extrinsic model of the receiver. The universe space is
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(0,\infty)^{3}

The universe state transitions are defined as

w^{t}_{k+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bar{f}}^{r}(\omega^{r}_{k},u^{r}_{k})\\
{\bar{f}}^{t}(\omega^{t}_{k},u^{p}_{k})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{r}_{k}+u^{r}_{k}\\
\omega^{t}_{k}+u^{t}_{k}\end{bmatrix},

(1)

in which

and

are receiver and producer actions at stage
k
, respectively. Whereas receiver actions affect on its position, producer actions correspond to changing the signal intensity of the towers.
Consequently,
{\bar{f}}=({\bar{f}}^{r},{\bar{f}}^{t})
.
We assume that each tower has a fixed position.
The extrinsic sensor-mapping for the receiver is

{\bar{h}}^{r}(\omega_{k})=y_{k}=\left(r_{k}^{(1)},r_{k}^{(2)},r_{k}^{(3)}\right),

(2)

in which

is the intensity of the signal transmitted by tower

at stage

.
Finally, the extrinsic model of the receiver is given as
(\Omega,{\bar{f}},U,{\bar{h}}^{r},Y^{r})

with

denoting non-negative reals and

The intrinsic model of the receiver is

Proposed Solution

Our proposed solution relies on a producer intrinsic model that is equivalent to (
1

{\iota}_{k}^{r}\not=\emptyset
, there exists a bijective map between the receiver I-state
{\iota}_{k}^{r}
and the tower signal intensities
\omega^{t}_{k}=(s^{(1)}_{k},s^{(1)}_{k},s^{(1)}_{k})

{\iota}_{k}^{r}\mapsto\omega^{t}_{k}

(8)

Then, the system in (
1

\displaystyle\omega^{r}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\omega^{r}_{k}+u^{r}_{k}

(9)

\displaystyle\iota^{r}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\iota^{r}_{k}+u^{p}_{k},

in which

is the producer action corresponding to a change in the receiver I-state and relates to

similarly through (

be the set of all possible producer actions

is unbounded as well, then

Proposition 2

There exists

\displaystyle\omega^{r}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\omega^{r}_{k}+K_{r}(x_{G}^{r}-{\iota}_{k}^{r})

(10)

\displaystyle\iota^{r}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\iota^{r}_{k}+u^{p}_{k}.

Consequently, a change of variables by considering the error in the receiver’s I-state with respect to its goal

\displaystyle\omega^{r}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x^{r}_{k}+K_{r}e_{k}

\displaystyle e_{k+1}

\displaystyle=e^{r}_{k}-u^{p}_{k}

is an identity map. Considering the omniscient producer defined in Section
III-A
under this new universe formulation, we wish to find a producer policy
\pi^{p}:{\mathcal{I}}^{p}\rightarrow U^{p}
with
{\mathcal{I}}^{p}=\Omega^{\prime}

that solves Problem
1
. Because any element of
U^{p}
can be uniquely mapped to an element of
U^{t}
, we chose not to introduce new notation and not to rewrite the problem.

To simplify the notation used, without loss of generality, we assume that the producer goal is
\omega^{\prime p}_{G}=[0,0]^{T}
.
The selected cost formulation
is given by

J=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{r}_{k}&e_{k}\end{bmatrix}Q\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{r}_{k}\\
e_{k}\end{bmatrix}+u^{pT}_{k}Ru^{p}_{k},

(13)

in which
Q
and
R
are symmetric positive-definite weight matrices determining the relative importance of each cost component.

The producer policy
\pi^{p}
that minimizes the cost given in (
13
) is

\pi^{p}(\omega^{\prime}_{k})=-K_{p}\omega^{\prime}_{k},

(14)

with

K_{p}=(B^{T}PB+R)^{-1}B^{T}PA,

in which
P
is the solution to the Discrete Algebraic Riccati Equation:

P=Q+A^{T}\left(P-PB(R+B^{T}PB)^{-1}B^{T}P\right)A.

Given that the pair
(A,B)
is controllable and that the sensor mapping
{\bar{h}}^{\prime}
is an identity map so that the system is observable, the closed-loop system with policy
\pi^{p}
is asymptotically stable.
Therefore, minimizing the cost in (
13
), the resulting universe-state trajectory will converge to
(\omega_{N}^{r}=x_{G}^{p},{\iota}^{r}_{N}=x^{r}_{G})
as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Furthermore, any I-state along the resulting trajectory
\tilde{{\iota}^{r}}=({\iota}_{1},{\iota}_{2},\dots)
is plausible by definition. Then,
\pi^{p}
is an optimal solution to Problem
1
.

Let
C\subset X^{r}\times\Omega
be a correspondence relation (Definition
6
) defined as
(x_{r},\omega)\in C
if
x^{r}=\omega^{r}
. Then
(x_{G}^{p},x^{r}_{G})
is an illusion if
x_{G}^{p}\not=x_{G}^{r}
according to Definition
9
.

III-C

Extending to advanced receivers

The receiver formulation in Section
III-A
allows any producer action as long as the next I-state is a singleton. Therefore, it allows large “jumps” in receiver’s I-state such that the distance between two consecutive position-estimates can be arbitrarily large.
In this section, we consider advanced receivers which explicitly take a motion model into account in their ITF.

We consider nondeterministic disturbances, which come from a compact set
\Theta
with no associated statistical information.
The intrinsic model of the receiver
\mathcal{M}_{U^{r}\rightarrow Y^{r}}=(X^{r},f^{r},h^{r})
is defined similarly in Section
III-A
with the exception that

f^{r}(x_{k}^{r},u_{k}^{r})=\{x^{r}_{k}+\theta\mid x^{r}_{k}\in X^{r}\land\theta\in\Theta\},

(15)

in which
\Theta=[\theta_{min},\theta_{max}]

is a set of disturbances.
Informally, this motion model means that given the current state
x^{r}_{k}
of the receiver, the next state
x^{r}_{k+1}
is constrained to a box centered at
x^{r}_{k}
indicating uncertainties in the motion model and limits on receiver actions.

Recall that
{\mathcal{I}}^{r}\subseteq\pow(X^{r})
.
The intrinsic model with XTF given in (
15
) can be taken into account within the receiver ITF by modifying (
5
) such that

{\iota}^{r}_{k}=\phi^{r}({\iota}^{r}_{k-1},y^{r}_{k})=(h^{r})^{-1}(y_{k}^{r})\cap\{{\iota}_{k-1}^{r}+\theta\mid\theta\in\Theta\},

(16)

assuming
{\iota}_{k-1}^{r}
is plausible, that is, it is not empty. Similar to Section
III-A
, we take
{\iota}^{r}_{k}=\{x^{r}_{k}\}
as
{\iota}^{r}_{k}=x^{r}_{k}
if it is not empty, and by Proposition
1
it is a singleton. If
{\iota}_{k-1}=\emptyset
, then
{\iota}_{i}=\emptyset
for all
i>k-1
.

Whereas it was possible to generate any plausible
{\iota}_{k}^{r}
in Section
III-B
(see Proposition
2
), this is no longer possible with the receiver ITF given in (
16
).
However, the plausibility can be taken into account by introducing limits on producer actions by imposing that the producer actions need to satisfy for all
k=1,2\dots
that
u_{k}^{p}\in\Theta
.
The resulting constrained optimal control problem can be solved in a receding horizon manner. To this end, we define the following constrained optimization problem at stage
k
, that is, C-OPT
(k,N)
:

\displaystyle\min_{u^{p}_{k},\dots,u^{p}_{k+N}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=k}^{k+N}\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{r}_{i+1}&e_{i+1}\end{bmatrix}Q\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{r}_{i+1}\\
e_{i+1}\end{bmatrix}+(u^{p}_{k})^{T}Ru^{p}_{k},

(17)

\displaystyle\textrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}_{i+1}=A\omega^{\prime}_{i}+Bu^{p}_{i}\quad\text{starting from $\omega^{\prime}_{k}$}

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{min}}\leq u^{p}_{i}\leq\theta_{\mathrm{max}}

\displaystyle\forall i=k,\dots,k+N.

Notice that C-OPT
(k,N)
is a quadratic program which can be solved efficiently with the available tools.
Now, the producer policy
\pi^{p}
corresponds to an algorithm given in (Algorithm
1
). Starting from an initial state
\omega^{\prime}_{1}
, the policy dictates solving C-OPT
(k,N)
until a termination condition is reached. A termination condition could be that
\omega^{\prime}_{k}
is sufficiently close to the goal.

Producer policy

k\leftarrow 1

while
!Termination Condition
do

(u_{k},\dots,u_{k+N})\leftarrow\text{Solution to {C-OPT}$(k,N)$}

Apply
u_{k}

k\leftarrow k+1

end

while

V
CONCLUSION

In this work, we have considered designing a producer that is able to regulate the perceptual experience of a receiver agent so that the latter’s actions achieve the producer’s goal rather than its own. The novel mathematical formulation of the interaction between a receiver and producers as a control system, and posing the problem of regulating the receiver perceptual experience (its I-state) as a control problem enabled the use of standard methods.A first extension of the proposed models comes in the form of trajectory tracking: rather than driving the state of the system to the origin, the objective can be to have it follow a reference trajectory
(\omega^{\circ}_{1},\omega^{\circ}_{2},\dots,\omega^{\circ}_{N+1})
with as little error as possible. This can be accomplished by a simple modification in the cost function (
13
).

Our formulation is general and allows considering different receiver and producer agents. Future work includes considering more advanced receivers that potentially take into account more accurate motion models or even potential producers within their intrinsic model. Therefore, further constraining the plausible set of I-states and consequently, producer actions. Another direction is to take sensing uncertainties into account in the receiver model, in this case, the preimage of an observation coming from a tower is an annulus instead of a circle.
Extensions to probabilistic I-states is also possible, and already considered in
[
3
]
. However, probabilistic models introduce additional complications in terms of determining what is plausible especially if the I-states correspond to a probability distribution over an unbounded set resulting in any one of those to be plausible. In this case, introducing thresholds or considering distributions with bounded support can be explored. Some progress along this direction was made in
[
23
]
.

Another interesting direction is to consider weaker producers, that is, producers that are not omniscient. In this case, the intrinsic model of the producer differs from its extrinsic model, leading to a partially observable system. Therefore, determining a producer policy with limited information and analyzing the stability of the closed-loop system opens up interesting areas to explore.
