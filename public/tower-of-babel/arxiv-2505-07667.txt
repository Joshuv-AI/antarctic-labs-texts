1.
Introduction

Given a couple
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}
, the Baumslag-Solitar group of parameter
(m,n)
is the group defined by the following presentation

(1)

\mathrm{BS}(m,n)=\langle b,t\mid tb^{m}t^{-1}=b^{n}\rangle.

Baumslag-Solitar groups were introduced in
[
2
]
to give the first examples of two-generated, finitely presented non-Hopfian groups. They have been widely studied in relation to various properties, that strongly depend on the parameters
(m,n)
: residual finiteness (
[
12
]
), classification up to quasi-isometry (see
[
7
]
and
[
14
]
), classification up to measure equivalence (announced by the authors of
[
9
]
)…The group
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
acts on its
Bass-Serre tree

\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
(
i.e.
the infinite oriented tree all of whose vertices have
m
incoming edges and
n
outgoing edges), with a single orbit of vertices and a single orbit of edges, and the vertex and edge stabilizers are infinite cyclic.

In this article, we pursue the study of the space of subgroups of Baumslag-Solitar groups, which was initiated in
[
4
]
. Endowed with the Chabauty topolology, the set of subgroups
\mathrm{Sub}(\Gamma)
of any infinite countable group
\Gamma
is a closed subset of the Cantor space
\{0,1\}^{\Gamma}
. A particular subset of
\mathrm{Sub}(\Gamma)
that we are interested in is the
perfect kernel

\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
,
i.e.
the largest closed subset without isolated point. This subset is invariant under
\Gamma
-conjugation. We are interested in the dynamics induced by this action. More precisely, we are interested in finding subsets of the perfect kernel on which the action is highly topologically transitive, or even topologically
\mu
-mixing. Recall that an action of a group
\Gamma
on a topological space
X
is
highly topologically transitive
(HTT) if for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
and for every non-empty open subsets
U_{1},...,U_{r}
,
V_{1},...,V_{r}
, there exists
g\in\Gamma
such that
g\cdot U_{i}\cap V_{i}\neq\emptyset
for every
i\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
. The authors of
[
10
]
introduced a strenghtening of this notion, called topological
\mu
-mixing. Given a probability measure
\mu
on a countable group
\Gamma
, an action of
\Gamma
on a topological space
X
is called
topologically
\mu
-mixing
if for every non-empty open subsets
U,V\subseteq X
, denoting by
(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of independently
\mu
-distributed random variables and by
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=(S_{1}\cdots S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
the random walk on
\Gamma
with step distribution
\mu
, one has

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(G_{k}\cdot U\cap V\neq\emptyset\right)=1.

The authors of
[
1
]
and
[
10
]
studied independently the space
\mathrm{Sub}(\Gamma)
in the case where
\Gamma
acts on a hyperbolic space with "vanishing" stabilizers. More formally, in the case where the hyperbolic space is a tree
\mathcal{T}
(and the action is minimal and irreducible), one application of their results we are interested in is the case where the action of
\Gamma
on
\mathcal{T}
is
acylindrical
,
i.e.
there exists
R>0
such that the stabilizer of any path of length larger than
R
is trivial. The following statements are applications of their results to this particular setting. In
[
1
]
, the authors proved that in this case, the perfect kernel of
\Gamma
contains the closure of the set
\mathrm{Sub}_{|\bullet\backslash\mathcal{T}|_{\infty}}(\Gamma)
of subgroups
\Lambda
of
\Gamma
satisfying that the quotient graph
\Lambda\backslash\mathcal{T}
is infinite:

\overline{\mathrm{Sub}_{|\bullet\backslash\mathcal{T}|_{\infty}}(\Gamma)}\subseteq\mathcal{K}(\Gamma).

Moreover, this subset is invariant under conjugation and the action of
\Gamma
on
\overline{\mathrm{Sub}_{|\bullet\backslash\mathcal{T}|_{\infty}}(\Gamma)}
is HTT. Still in the particular context of acylindrical actions on trees, the results of
[
10
]
lead to the study of the set
\mathrm{Sub}_{\infty}^{cc}(\Gamma\curvearrowright\mathcal{T})
, namely the set of infinite index
\mathcal{T}
-convex cocompact subgroups. Recall that a subgroup
\Lambda\leq\Gamma
is called
\mathcal{T}
-convex cocompact
if it acts properly on
\mathcal{T}
(
i.e.
with finite vertex stabilizers), with quasi-convex orbits (
i.e.
for any vertex
v\in\mathcal{T}
, there exists
\eta>0
such that the reduced edge path connecting two vertices of
\Lambda\cdot v
remains at distance
<\eta
from
\Lambda\cdot v
). In the setting of actions on simplicial trees, this subset is exactly the set of finitely generated subgroups of infinite index that act properly on
\mathcal{T}
. If we assume moreover that the action is acylindrical, this is a subset of
\mathrm{Sub}_{|\bullet\backslash\mathcal{T}|_{\infty}}(\Gamma)
:

\mathrm{Sub}_{\infty}^{cc}(\Gamma\curvearrowright\mathcal{T})\subseteq\mathrm{Sub}_{|\bullet\backslash\mathcal{T}|_{\infty}}(\Gamma).

On the closure of this subset, they proved that the action by conjugation is even topologically
\mu
-mixing for every measure
\mu
on
\Gamma
whose support is bounded (
i.e.

\{g\cdot v,g\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)\}
is finite for any vertex
v
of
\mathcal{T}
), symmetric (
i.e.
stable under inversion), and generates
\Gamma
.

A Baumslag-Solitar group
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
is a typical example whose action on its Bass-Serre tree
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
is
not
acylindrical, because the stabilizer of every finite edge path is infinite cyclic. The authors of
[
4
]
and
[
8
]
proved that this leads to a very different situation for the dynamics induced by the action by conjugation on the perfect kernel. In the case where
\min(|m|,|n|)>1
, they proved that the perfect kernel exactly consists of the set
\mathrm{Sub}_{|\bullet\backslash\mathcal{T}_{m,n}|_{\infty}}(\mathrm{BS}(m,n))
, and they uncovered a countably infinite invariant partition of the perfect kernel
\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{BS}(m,n))=\bigsqcup_{P\in\mathcal{Q}_{m,n}}\mathcal{K}_{P}
(where
\mathcal{Q}_{m,n}
is an infinite subset of
\mathbb{N}^{*}\sqcup\{\infty\}
that contains
\infty
) such that

•

\mathcal{K}_{P}
is open for every finite
P\in\mathcal{Q}_{m,n}
(and also closed iff
|m|=|n|
);

•

\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
is closed;

•

the action by conjugation on
\mathcal{K}_{P}
is HTT for every
P\in\mathcal{Q}_{m,n}
.

Notice that the existence of disjoint invariant open subsets prevents the action on
\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{BS}(m,n))
from being HTT. However, the last item may make us wonder if the action is also topologically
\mu
-mixing on each piece. Our first result shows that this is false in general:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\min(|m|,|n|)>1
. Let us assume that
|m|\neq|n|
. Then, there exists a probability measure
\mu
whose support is finite, symmetric and generates
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
, such that for every finite
P\in\mathcal{Q}_{m,n}
, the action by conjugation of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
on
\mathcal{K}_{P}
is not topologically
\mu
-mixing.

Notice that, in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, though the support of
\mu
is symmetric, we will construct
\mu
in such a way that
\mu(t)\neq\mu(t^{-1})
.

However, we have the following positive result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let

such that
\min(|m|,|n|)>1
and let
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}

\mathrm{BS}(m,n)

\mu

\mathrm{BS}(m,n)

(1)

2.
Preliminaries and notations

We denote by
\mathcal{P}
the set of prime numbers. For every integer
N
and every prime number
p
, we denote by
|N|_{p}
the
p
-adic valuation of
N
, that is, the largest
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p^{n}
divides
N
. For every integers
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\smallsetminus\{0\}
we denote by
m\wedge n
their greatest common divisor, that is, the largest integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
dividing both
m
and
n
. By convention,
\infty\wedge n=n\wedge\infty=|n|
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. By "countable" we mean finite or in bijection with
\mathbb{N}
. For any finite set
F
, we denote by
|F|
its cardinality. We use the same convention as in
[
13
]
about graphs. We denote by
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G})
the set of vertices of an oriented graph
\mathcal{G}
and by
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})
its set of edges. We denote by
\mathcal{E}^{+}(\mathcal{G})
(
resp.

\mathcal{E}^{-}(\mathcal{G})
) its set of positive (
resp.
negative) edges, and by
\mathbf{s}:\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G})
and
\mathbf{t}:\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})\to\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G})
the source and target maps, respectively. For any edge
e
, we denote by
\overline{e}
the reversed edge. If
\mathcal{G}
is a graph, we denote by
d_{\mathcal{G}}
the induced metric on the set of vertices. If
v\in\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{G})
and
R\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we denote by
B_{\mathcal{G}}(v,R)
the closed ball of center
v
and radius
R
in
\mathcal{G}
(for the metric
d_{\mathcal{G}}
). For any subgraph
\mathcal{G}_{0}
of
\mathcal{G}
and any
R\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we denote by
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{G},R}(\mathcal{G}_{0})
the
R
-neighborhood of
\mathcal{G}_{0}
in
\mathcal{G}
,
i.e.
the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
induced by the set of vertices at distance less than
R
from
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. By the half-graph of an edge
e
, we mean the connected component of
\mathcal{G}\smallsetminus\{e\}
that contains
\mathbf{t}(e)
. If this half-graph is a tree, we call it a half-tree. If
\mathcal{T}
is a tree and
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
is a subtree of
\mathcal{T}
and
e\in\mathcal{E}\left(\mathcal{T}\smallsetminus\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right)
, we say that
e
points towards
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
if the half-tree of
e
contains
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
.

2.1.
Space of subgroups

Given an infinite countable group
\Gamma
, one can endow its set of subgroups
\mathrm{Sub}(\Gamma)
with the
Chabauty topology
, which comes from the natural inclusion
\mathrm{Sub}(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow\{0,1\}^{\Gamma}
of
\mathrm{Sub}(\Gamma)
into the Cantor space. A basis of neighborhoods is given by the following family of clopen sets

\mathcal{V}(O,I)=\{\Lambda\leq\Gamma\mid I\subseteq\Lambda\text{ \ and \ }\Lambda\cap O=\emptyset\}

(where
I
and
O
are finite subsets of
\Gamma
).

One has a correspondence between subgroups of
\Gamma
and isomorphism classes of transitive right actions of
\Gamma
on pointed countable sets, which yields a correspondence between conjugacy classes of subgroups of
\Gamma
and isomorphism classes of transitive right actions of
\Gamma
on countable sets, where the action by conjugation amounts to changing the base point. It is given by the bijection

(2)

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\{\text{isomorphism classes of pointed transitive right $\Gamma$-actions}\}&\to&\mathrm{Sub}(\Gamma)\\
(X,x_{0})\curvearrowleft^{\alpha}\Gamma&\mapsto&\mathrm{Stab}_{\alpha}(x_{0})\\
\end{array}

whose inverse is given by

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathrm{Sub}(\Gamma)&\to&\{\text{isomorphism classes of pointed transitive right $\Gamma$-actions}\}\\
\Lambda&\mapsto&(\Lambda\backslash\Gamma,\Lambda)\curvearrowleft\Gamma\\
\end{array}.

The Chabauty topology can be defined on the set of isomorphism classes of pointed transitive right
\Gamma
-actions thanks to
Schreier graphs
. Given a symmetric generating set
S
of
\Gamma
and a right
\Gamma
-action
\alpha
on a pointed countable set
(X,x_{0})
, one can define the (rooted) Schreier graph of
\alpha
as follows: its set of vertices is
X
and, for every
x\in X
and
s\in S
, there is a positive edge labeled
s
with source
x
and target
x\cdot s
, whose opposite edge is labeled
s^{-1}
(and has source
x\cdot s
and target
x
). Its root is the point
x_{0}
. The set of isomorphism classes of pointed transitive right
\Gamma
-actions can be endowed with the following topology. A basis of neighborhoods of a pointed transitive right action
(X,v)\curvearrowleft^{\alpha}\Gamma
is given by the set of actions whose Schreier graph has the same
R
-ball around the origin (for
R>0
):

\displaystyle V_{R}=

\displaystyle\{(X^{\prime},v^{\prime})\curvearrowleft^{\beta}\Gamma,(B_{\mathrm{Sch}(\beta)}(v^{\prime},R),v^{\prime})\text{ and }(B_{\mathrm{Sch}(\alpha)}(v,R),v)

\displaystyle\text{ are isomorphic (as pointed labeled graphs)}.\}

When
S
is finite, this is exactly the Chabauty topology (
via
the aforementioned identification between actions and subgroups). We refer to
[
3
, Section 2]
for more details. From now on, we will freely identify the set of subgroups of
\Gamma
with the set of isomorphism classes of pointed transitive right actions of
\Gamma
.

By a theorem of Cantor-Bendixson, there exists a unique decomposition

\mathrm{Sub}(\Gamma)=\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\sqcup C

where
C
is countable and
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
is a closed subset without isolated point, called the
perfect kernel
of
\Gamma
. This is the largest closed subset of
\mathrm{Sub}(\Gamma)
without isolated points, or equivalently, the set of subgroups all of whose neighborhoods are uncountable. See
[
11
, Section 6]
for more details.

2.2.
Baumslag-Solitar groups; preactions and
(m,n)
-(Schreier) graphs

In this section, we provide a quick reminder on Bass-Serre theory and we recall the main tools that were introduced in
[
4
]
to study
\mathrm{Sub}(\mathrm{BS}(m,n))
. Let
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\min(|m|,|n|)>1
. The Baumslag-Solitar group of parameters
(m,n)
is the group
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
defined by the presentation (
1
).

2.2.1.
Some background on Bass-Serre theory

As an HNN-extension, the group
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
acts on its
Bass-Serre tree

\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
(on the left): this is the infinite oriented tree all of whose vertices have
m
incoming edges and
n
outgoing edges. This tree arises as follows: it is obtained from the (right) Cayley graph of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
(with respect to the generating set
\{b,b^{-1},t,t^{-1}\}
) by shrinking all the
\langle b\rangle
-orbits. There is a natural map
p:\mathrm{Cay}(\mathrm{BS}(m,n))\to\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
(applying
[
4
, Definition 3.10]
to the free action of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
on itself); it sends

•

the vertex
\gamma
of the Cayley graph to the vertex
\gamma\langle b\rangle
of the Bass-Serre tree;

•

the positive edge
(\gamma,\gamma t)
of the Cayley graph to the positive edge
\gamma\langle b^{n}\rangle
of the Bass-Serre tree;

•

the negative edge
(\gamma,\gamma t^{-1})
of the Cayley graph to the negative edge
\gamma\langle b^{m}\rangle
of the Bass-Serre tree;

•

the edges of the form
(\gamma,\gamma b^{k})
to the single vertex
\gamma\langle b\rangle
.

The action of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
on the set of (positive) edges of
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
is defined by:
\gamma\cdot g\langle b^{n}\rangle=\gamma g\langle b^{n}\rangle
. The action of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
on its Bass-Serre tree has a single orbit of vertices and the quotient graph
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)\backslash\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
is a loop. The vertex
p(1)=\langle b\rangle
is the root of the Bass-Serre tree. See
[
4
, Section 2.3, Section 3]
for more details.

2.2.2.
Normal forms

A
word
of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
is a finite sequence of elements of
\{b,b^{-1},t,t^{-1}\}
.
The element associated to such
s=(u_{0},\ldots,u_{k_{0}})
in
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
is the product
\mathfrak{s}=u_{1}\cdots u_{k_{0}}
. A
subword

s^{\prime}
of
s
is a sequence of the form
s^{\prime}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{l_{0}})
, for some
l_{0}\leq k_{0}
. By an abuse of notation, we will freely identify the word
s
and the element
\mathfrak{s}
of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
(keeping in mind that the writing of
\mathfrak{s}
as a product of elements of
\{b,b^{-1},t,t^{-1}\}
is not unique!). In particular, the set of subwords of an element of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
strongly depends on the chosen writing of this element as a product of elements in
\{b,b^{-1},t,t^{-1}\}
.

Example 2.1
.

For instance, the subwords of the word
tbbt^{-1}b^{-1}b^{-1}b^{-1}
in
\mathrm{BS}(2,3)
are
\emptyset
,
t
,
tb
,
tbb
,
tbbt^{-1}
,
tbbt^{-1}b^{-1}
,
tbbt^{-1}b^{-1}b^{-1}
,
tbbt^{-1}b^{-1}b^{-1}b^{-1}
(though this is the trivial element of
\mathrm{BS}(2,3)
).

As a particular case of
[
13
, Chapter 1, Section 5.2]
(which yields the normal form for HNN-extensions), any element
g
of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
can be uniquely written as

g=b^{n_{1}}t^{\varepsilon_{1}}b^{n_{2}}\cdots b^{n_{r}}t^{\varepsilon_{r}}b^{n_{r+1}}

where
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for every
i\in\llbracket 1,r+1\rrbracket
, and
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{1,-1\}
for every
i\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
, and

•

if
\varepsilon_{i}=1
, then
n_{i+1}\in\left\llbracket 0,|m|-1\right\rrbracket
;

•

if
\varepsilon_{i}=-1
, then
n_{i+1}\in\left\llbracket 0,|n|-1\right\rrbracket
;

•

there is no subword of the form
t^{\varepsilon}b^{0}t^{-\varepsilon}
.

This is the
normal form
of
g
. If
g
is written in its normal form, we say that
g
is
reduced
. The
height
of an element
g=b^{n_{1}}t^{\varepsilon_{1}}b^{n_{2}}\cdots b^{n_{r}}t^{\varepsilon_{r}}b^{n_{r+1}}\in\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
written in its normal form is the integer

\mathfrak{h}(g)=r.

Remark 2.2
.

This is also the distance

\mathfrak{h}(g)=d_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,g\cdot v),

where
v=\langle b\rangle
is the root of the Bass-Serre tree
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
. More specifically, if
g=b^{n_{1}}t^{\varepsilon_{1}}b^{n_{2}}\cdots b^{n_{r}}t^{\varepsilon_{r}}b^{n_{r+1}}
is written in its normal form, then the geodesic between
v
and
g\cdot v
is of the form
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
, where the orientation of
e_{i}
is given by the sign of
\varepsilon_{i}
.

Schreier

-graph

of a preaction
\alpha

and whose (positive) edges are either of the form
(x,x\cdot\beta)
, or of the form
(x,x\cdot\tau)
(for

). Every path

is a group morphism, and the image of this map is called the

of the point

for the preaction
\alpha

\bm{\mathrm{Stab}_{\alpha}}\textbf{($\bm{x_{0}}$)}

(
\bm{m,n}
)-graph

of a preaction

is the Schreier

-graph of

the target and source maps are defined by

Remark 2.4
.

Let us assume that
\Lambda\leq\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
is a finitely generated subgroup whose graph of groups (induced by its action on
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
) is infinite (that is to say,
\Lambda\backslash\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
is infinite). Let
\pi:\mathcal{T}_{m,n}\to\Lambda\backslash\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
be the projection. Then, for any connected finite subgraph
\mathcal{F}
of this graph of groups such that
\Lambda
is the fundamental group of
\mathcal{F}
(in the formalism of Bass Serre theory), the preimage
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{F})
is a proper invariant subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
. Let us explain why. Notice that the complement of
\mathcal{F}
in the graph of groups of
\Lambda
is a forest; otherwise, any edge path with source and target in
\mathcal{F}
that is not fully contained in
\mathcal{F}
would yield an extra edge generator

by Bass-Serre theory. And if
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{F})
was not connected, then any edge path of
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
connecting two distinct connected components of
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{F})
would be mapped by
\pi
to an edge path with source and target in
\mathcal{F}
that is not fully contained in
\mathcal{F}
, contradicting the previous observation.

An abstract
(
\bm{m,n}
)-graph
is then an oriented labeled graph all of whose vertices are labeled by an integer or
\infty

(1)

every vertex labeled
N

outgoing edges and at most

(2)

(Transfer Equation)
for every positive edge with source labeled
N
and target labeled

(3)

\frac{N}{N\wedge n}=\frac{M}{M\wedge m}.

It is called

if equalities hold for every vertex in the first item.

We will make use of the following lemma that gives the existence of a particular extension of a given preaction. A proof can be found in

For any transitive and non-saturated preaction
\alpha

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}

Notice that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{o}=

. If

2.3.
Random walks on groups

The definitions of this section come from
[
10
]
. Let
\Gamma
be a countable group. Let
\mu:\Gamma\to[0,1]
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
. A
random walk on
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
with step distribution
\bm{\mu}
is a random sequence
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=(S_{1}\cdots S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\Gamma
where the
S_{i}
’s are independently
\mu
-distributed random variables.

Let
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a random walk on a group
\Gamma
with step distribution
\mu
. A
\Gamma
-action on a Polish space
X
is said
topologically
\bm{\mu}
-mixing
if for any non-empty open subsets
U,V\subseteq X
, the following holds:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(G_{k}\cdot U\cap V\neq\emptyset)=1.

Notice that any topologically
\mu
-mixing action is also
l
-topologically transitive
for every
l\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
: for every non-empty open subsets
U_{1},\ldots,U_{l},V_{1},\ldots,V_{l}\subseteq X
, there exists an element
g\in\Gamma
such that

g\cdot U_{i}\cap V_{i}\neq\emptyset,\ \forall i\in\llbracket 1,l\rrbracket

(see
[
10
, Proposition 1.2]
).

Given a probability measure
\mu:\Gamma\to[0,1]
, we denote by
\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)
its
support
, that is to say, the set

\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)=\{g\in\Gamma\mid\mu(g)>0\}.

We say that the support of
\mu
is
generating
if
\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)
generates
\Gamma
as a semi-group, and that it is
symmetric
if
\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)
is stable under inversion.

Given a probability measure
\mu
on
\Gamma
, we denote by
\mu^{-1}
the probability measure defined by:
\mu^{-1}(g):=\mu(g^{-1})
for every
g\in\Gamma
.

If
\Gamma
acts isometrically on a metric space
X
, we say that the support of
\mu
is
bounded
(with respect to this action) if
\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)(x)
is a bounded subset of
X
for some (equivalently for all)
x\in X
.

Remark 2.12
.

If
\mu
has one of the following properties: generating support, symmetric support, or bounded support (with respect to some isometric action of
\Gamma
), then so does
\mu^{-1}
.

Remark 2.13
.

Let
\Gamma=\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
, that acts on its Bass-Serre tree
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
and let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
. Then, by Remark
2.2
the support of
\mu
is bounded if and only if the set of integers

\{\mathfrak{h}(g),g\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)\}

is finite. We draw the attention of the reader to the fact that in this case, the support of
\mu
need not be finite.

Throughout this paper, random variables are always denoted by capital letters.

2.4.
Phenotype of a subgroup of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)

In this section, we recall the computation of the perfect kernel and the construction of the decomposition of
\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{BS}(m,n))
obtained in
[
4
]
. The authors proved the following result:

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
(m,n)\in\left(\mathbb{Z}\smallsetminus\{0\}\right)^{2}
such that
|m|\neq 1
and
|n|\neq 1
. Then

\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{BS}(m,n))=\{\Lambda\leq\mathrm{BS}(m,n)\mid\text{ \ the $(m,n)$-graph of $\Lambda$ is infinite}\}.

To understand the dynamics induced by the action by conjugation, the authors introduced the notion of
phenotype
. This is a function
\mathrm{Ph}_{m,n}:\mathbb{N}^{*}\sqcup\{\infty\}\to\mathbb{N}^{*}\sqcup\{\infty\}
which is constant on the set of labels of any connected
(m,n)
-graph. It is defined by

(5)

\mathrm{Ph}_{m,n}(N):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P},|m|_{p}=|n|_{p}\text{ \ and \ }|N|_{p}>|n|_{p}}p^{|N|_{p}}&\mbox{if }N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\\
\infty&\mbox{if }N=\infty.\end{array}\right.

The phenotype of a connected
(m,n)
-graph
\mathcal{G}
is defined as follows: denoting by
N
any label of
\mathcal{G}
, then
\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}\left(\mathcal{G}\right)=\mathrm{Ph}_{m,n}(N)
.

The phenotype
\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}(\Lambda)
(
resp.

\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}(\alpha)
) of a subgroup
\Lambda\leq\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
(
resp.
of a preaction
\alpha
) is then the phenotype of its
(m,n)
-graph. As the labels of the
(m,n)
-graph of
\Lambda
encode the intersections of the conjugates of
\Lambda
with
\langle b\rangle
, one also has
\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}(\Lambda)=\mathrm{Ph}_{m,n}\left(\left[\langle b\rangle:\Lambda\cap\langle b\rangle\right]\right)
(as defined in (
5
)).

The authors of
[
4
]
proved that this quantity is invariant under conjugation and that the set
\mathcal{Q}_{m,n}=\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}(\mathrm{Sub}(\Gamma))
is infinite. Thus, we get an infinite countable invariant partition of

\mathrm{Sub}(\mathrm{BS}(m,n))=\bigsqcup_{P\in\mathcal{Q}_{m,n}}\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}^{-1}(P),

which yields a partition of the perfect kernel

\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{BS}(m,n))=\bigsqcup_{P\in\mathcal{Q}_{m,n}}\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{BS}(m,n))\cap\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}^{-1}(P).

Notation 2.15
.

For any
P\in\mathcal{Q}_{m,n}
, we denote by
\mathcal{K}_{P}
the intersection
\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{BS}(m,n))\cap\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}^{-1}(P)=\{\Lambda\in\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}^{-1}(P)\mid\Lambda\backslash\mathcal{T}_{m,n}\text{ is infinite}\}
.

Moreover, they proved the following result:

Theorem 2.16
.

Let
(m,n)\in\left(\mathbb{Z}\smallsetminus\{0\}\right)^{2}
such that
|m|\neq 1
and
|n|\neq 1
. Then, for any
P\in\mathcal{Q}_{m,n}
:

•

if

, the piece
\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}^{-1}(P)
is open;

•

it is also closed if and only if
|m|=|n|
;

•

the piece
\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}^{-1}(\infty)
is closed;

•

\mathcal{K}_{P}
is non-empty and the action by conjugation of
\mathrm{BS}(m,n)
on it is topologically transitive.

In
[
8
]
, the same authors even proved that the action of

by conjugation on

is highly topologically transitive if

.

Remark 2.17
.

In the case where
|m|=|n|

Let

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

To prove Theorem

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\in\{b,b^{-1},t,t^{-1}\}

\mathfrak{h}_{i}^{+}:=\left|\{j\in\llbracket 1,i\rrbracket\mid s_{j}=t\}\right|

). Let

•

•

Then, for every

of the

-orbit of

satisfies

(where

and

Proof.

We proceed by induction on

Base case

The result is clear for

.

Induction step

Let us assume that

and that

We distinguish three cases:

1st case:

In this case, the
\langle b\rangle
-orbits of

and

. By the Transfer Equation (
3

, which implies that

By the induction hypothesis

\displaystyle|N_{i}|_{p}

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{h}_{i}^{+}-\mathfrak{h}_{i}^{-})(|m|_{p}-|n|_{p})+|N|_{p}

\displaystyle\geq|N|_{p}\text{ \ by the assumption made on $\mathfrak{h}_{i}^{+},\mathfrak{h}_{i}^{-}$ and the assumption made on $p$}

\displaystyle>|m|_{p}\text{ \ by the assumption made on $N$}

\displaystyle>|n|_{p}\text{ \ by the assumption made on $p$}.

Thus by Equation (

\displaystyle|N_{i+1}|_{p}-\min(|N_{i+1}|_{p},|m|_{p})

\displaystyle=|N_{i}|_{p}-|n|_{p}

\displaystyle>0

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{h}_{i}^{+}-\mathfrak{h}_{i}^{-}+1)(|m|_{p}-|n|_{p})+|N|_{p}

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{h}_{i+1}^{+}-\mathfrak{h}_{i+1}^{-})(|m|_{p}-|n|_{p})+|N|_{p},

which concludes the induction step in the case where

3rd case:

In particular,

. This last case is very similar, so we leave it to the reader.

Before proving Theorem

, let us recall some basic facts about one dimensional random walks.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of independently and identically distributed random variables, valued in
\{-1,0,1\}
, such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=1)>\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=-1)
. Let us denote by
Z_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
(where
Z_{0}=0
), and by
p_{+}:=\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=1)
and
p_{-}:=\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=-1)
. Then

(1)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}Z_{n}=p_{+}-p_{-}\right)=1
;

(2)

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0,\ \forall n>0)=p_{+}-p_{-}
.

Proof.

The first item results from the strong law of large numbers.

For the second one, notice that, denoting by
p_{k,l}
the probability that
Z_{n}
reaches
l
, starting from
k>l
, one has

\displaystyle p_{1,0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=-1)+\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=0)p_{1,0}+\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=1)p_{2,0}

As any realization of
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
starting from
2
and reaching
0
has to reach
1
before
0
, and
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Markov chain, one has
p_{2,0}=p_{2,1}p_{1,0}
. Noticing that
p_{k,l}=p_{k-l,0}
for any
k>l
, one deduces that
p_{1,0}
satisfies the following equation:

p_{+}p_{1,0}^{2}-(p_{+}+p_{-})p_{1,0}+p_{-}=0

which leads to
p_{1,0}=\frac{p_{-}}{p_{+}}
or
p_{1,0}=1
. Thus

(7)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0,\ \forall n>0)&=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=1)(1-p_{1,0})\\
&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}p_{+}-p_{-}&\mbox{if }p_{1,0}=\frac{p_{-}}{p_{+}}\\
0&\mbox{if }p_{1,0}=1.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

We want to show that
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0,\ \forall n>0)=p_{+}-p_{-}
.
The first item of the proposition implies that

\mathbb{P}(\exists n_{0}\in\mathbb{N},Z_{n_{0}}=0\text{ \ and \ }Z_{n}>0,\forall n>n_{0})=1.

In particular, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n_{0}}=0\text{ \ and \ }Z_{n}>0,\forall n>n_{0})>0.

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0,\forall n>0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}>0,\forall n>n_{0}\mid Z_{n_{0}}=0\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(Z_{n_{0}}=0\text{ \ and \ }Z_{n}>0,\forall n>n_{0})}{\mathbb{P}(Z_{n_{0}}=0)}

\displaystyle>0.

Thus, Equation (
7
) implies that

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0,\ \forall n>0)=p_{+}-p_{-},

which concludes the proof. ∎

Up to exchanging

Let

-1&\mbox{if \ }S_{i}=t^{-1};\\

and notice that the variables

•

\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=1)=\mu(t)

•

\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=-1)=\mu\left(t^{-1}\right)

•

\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=0)=\mu(b)+\mu(b^{-1})=1-\left(\mu(t)+\mu\left(t^{-1}\right)\right).

For every

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{1}}_{\{S_{i}=t\}}-\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=t^{-1}\}}

\displaystyle=\left|\{i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\mid S_{i}=t\}\right|-\left|\{i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\mid S_{i}=t^{-1}\}\right|.

(1)

(2)

(1)

for every

(2)

and let

For any subgroup
\Lambda\leq\Gamma
such that

g_{k}^{-1}\Lambda g_{k}\cap\langle b\rangle=\left\langle b^{N_{k}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle|N_{k}|_{p}

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{h}_{k}^{+}-\mathfrak{h}_{k}^{-})(|m|_{p}-|n|_{p})+|N|_{p}\text{ \ by the first condition and Proposition \ref{evanescent}}

\displaystyle>k\frac{\mu(t)-\mu\left(t^{-1}\right)}{2}\text{ \ for $k\geq k_{0}$ large enough by the second condition}

k_{0}
only depends on

For any

.
∎

In fact, a stronger statement holds: in the setting of the previous lemma, the random walk
G_{k}\cdot v
escapes
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}

and never returns
almost surely. This is encoded by the following proposition:

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\Lambda
be a subgroup of
\Gamma
that acts cocompactly on a proper subtree
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
of
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
. Then, the random walk
(G_{k}\cdot v)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges almost surely to a random end
\xi\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{m,n}\smallsetminus\partial\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
.

To prove Proposition
3.6
, we will make use of the following result, which is a consequence of the main theorem of
[
5
]
:

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
\min\left(|m|,|n|\right)>1
. Then, the sequence
(G_{k}\cdot v)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges almost surely to a random end
\xi\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
.

Theorem
3.7
relies on the fact that
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a regular random walk on the automorphism group
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{T}_{m,n})
of the infinite locally finite tree
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
and that
\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)
is not contained in any amenable closed subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{T}_{m,n})
. Thus, the hypotheses of the main theorem of
[
5
]
are satisfied. For more details, see
[
6
, Lemma 4.8]
.

Hence, Theorem
3.7
gives the existence of
\xi\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
in Proposition
3.6
, so what we need to prove is that, almost surely,
\xi\notin\partial\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
. Before entering into technical details, let us give an intuition about the proof. Lemma
3.4
implies that the random walk starting from any element
w
of
\Gamma
escapes
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
almost surely. We will prove that, when it reaches a vertex outside a suitable neighborhood of
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
, the probability of never returning into
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
is bounded below by a strictly positive probability (Lemma
3.10
). Then, a standard argument using stopping times will show that, in fact, the random walk escapes
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
and never returns almost surely, which ensures that
\xi\notin\partial\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
almost surely. Here, the main subtlety comes from the fact that the sequence
(S_{1}\cdots S_{k}\cdot v)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is not a Markov chain: because of the non-triviality of the vertex stabilizers (for the action of
\Gamma
on
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
), the image
S_{1}\cdots S_{k+1}\cdot v
does not only depend on
S_{1}\cdots S_{k}\cdot v
, but on the element
S_{1}\cdots S_{k}
of
\Gamma
. Thus, when bounding probabilities by below, we have to condition on the realization of the random walk in the group at a previous step, not on the image of this realization in
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
.

Now we give some technical lemmas to prove Proposition
3.6
. Before that, let us introduce some notations. Using the fact that
\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)
is bounded, let us denote by

M:=\max_{\gamma\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)}\mathfrak{h}(\gamma).

Let
F_{0}
be the (finite) set of reduced words
g=b^{n_{1}}t^{\varepsilon_{1}}b^{n_{2}}\cdots b^{n_{r}}t^{\varepsilon_{r}}b^{n_{r+1}}
that satisfy
n_{1}=0
and
\mathfrak{h}(g)\leq M
. In particular, the set of words of height less than
M
is precisely
\bigcup_{g\in F_{0}}\langle b\rangle g
; and by Remark
2.2
, this is also the set of elements
w\in\Gamma
that satisfy
w\cdot v\in B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)
.

Lemma 3.8
.

There exists some
f\in F_{0}
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(fG_{k}\cdot v\notin B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)\ \forall k>0\right)>0.

Proof.

Towards a contradiction, let us assume that, for every
f\in F_{0}
, one has

(9)

\mathbb{P}\left(fG_{k}\cdot v\notin B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)\ \forall k>0\right)=0.

Let us define by induction the sequence of random variables
(\tau_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\tau_{1}

\displaystyle=\inf\{k>0\mid G_{k}\cdot v\in B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)\};\text{ and, for every $i\geq 1$}

\displaystyle\tau_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\inf\{k>\tau_{i}\mid G_{k}\cdot v\in B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)\}.

For every
i
, notice that
\tau_{i}
is a stopping time with respect to the Markov chain
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Applying Equation
9
to
f=1
, we get that
\tau_{1}<\infty
almost surely. Assuming by induction that
\tau_{i}<\infty
almost surely, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+1}<\infty\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\exists k>\tau_{i},\ G_{k}\cdot v\in B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\in F_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists k>\tau_{i},S_{1}\cdots S_{k}\cdot v\in B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)\text{ and }S_{1}\cdots S_{\tau_{i}}\in\langle b\rangle f\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\in F_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists k>\tau_{i},f\cdot S_{\tau_{i}+1}\cdots S_{k}\in B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)\text{ and }S_{1}\cdots S_{\tau_{i}}\in\langle b\rangle f\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\in F_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists k>\tau_{i},f\cdot S_{\tau_{i}+1}\cdots S_{k}\in B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)\right)\mathbb{P}\left(S_{1}\cdots S_{\tau_{i}}\in\langle b\rangle f\right)

(by independence of the
S_{k}
’s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f\in F_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{1}\cdots S_{\tau_{i}}\in\langle b\rangle f\right)

(as the
S_{j}
’s are identically distributed and using (
9
))

\displaystyle=1.

This proves that, almost surely,
G_{k}\cdot v\in B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)
for infinitely many
k
’s which contradicts the fact that
(G_{k}\cdot v)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges almost surely to a random end.
∎

Before proving that the random walk
G_{k}\cdot v
never returns to a subtree
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
as soon as it escapes a suitable neighborhood of
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
with non-zero (uniform) probability, we need the following deterministic result:

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
be a subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
. Fix a sequence
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)^{\mathbb{N}}
and some
w_{0}\in\Gamma
such that
w_{0}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
and let us denote by
e
the edge with source
w_{0}\cdot v
that points towards
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
. Also assume that there exists some
l_{0}>0
such that

(1)

w_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{l_{0}}\cdot v\in B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(w_{0}\cdot v,M)
; and

(2)

w_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v\notin B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(w_{0}\cdot v,M)
for every
k>l_{0}
; and

(3)

there exists some
m_{0}>l_{0}
such that
w_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{m_{0}}\cdot v\in\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
.

Let

\eta_{e}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}t^{-1}bt&\mbox{if $e$ is positive}\\
tbt^{-1}&\mbox{if $e$ is negative}\end{array}.\right.

Then, for every
k>l_{0}
, the vertex
w_{0}\cdot\eta_{e}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v
does not belong to
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
.

Proof.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{T}_{e}
the half-tree of
e
. As it contains
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
, one has
w_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{m_{0}}\cdot v\in\mathcal{T}_{e}
. Thus, as
w_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v
never returns in
B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(w_{0}\cdot v,M)
for
k>l_{0}
, the fact that
d(w_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v,w_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{k+1}\cdot v)\leq M
for every
k
(by definition of
M
) implies that the sequence of vertices
(w_{0}s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v)_{k>l_{0}}
remains in
\mathcal{T}_{e}
. Let us assume that
e
is positive. In particular, the geodesic between
w_{0}\cdot v
and
w_{0}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v
begins with the positive edge
e
for every
k>l_{0}
. Thus, the geodesic between
v
and
s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v
begins with the positive edge
w_{0}^{-1}\cdot e
for every
k>l_{0}
. So by the “more specifically” part of Remark
2.2
, the normal form of
s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
begins with
b^{r}t
for some
r\in\mathbb{Z}
and for every
k>l_{0}
. Consequently, the normal form of
t^{-1}bts_{1}\cdots s_{k}
begins with
t^{-1}
, which implies (using again Remark
2.2
) that the geodesic that connects
v
and
t^{-1}bts_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v
begins with a negative edge for every
k>l_{0}
. Thus, the geodesic that connects
w_{0}\cdot v
and
w_{0}\cdot t^{-1}bts_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v
begins with a negative edge, hence
w_{0}t^{-1}bts_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{T}_{e}
so
w_{0}\eta_{e}s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
for every
k>l_{0}
. If
e
is negative, the same argument shows that the normal form of
tbt^{-1}s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
begins with
t
and consequently, that
w_{0}\eta_{e}s_{1}\cdots s_{k}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
for every
k>l_{0}
.
∎

Now we provide a uniform lower bound on the probability of never returning to some subtree
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
knowing that the random walk has escaped a suitable neighborhood of
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

There exist
L>0
and
q>0
such that, for every
w_{0}\in\Gamma
that satisfies
w_{0}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right)
, one has

\mathbb{P}\left(\ w_{0}G_{k}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\ \forall k>0\right)\geq q.

Proof.

Using the fact that
\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)
generates
\Gamma
as a semi-group, let us fix a writing

g=\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{g}\cdots\mathfrak{g}_{N_{g}}^{g},

for every
g\in F_{0}\cup\{tbt^{-1},t^{-1}bt\}
, where
\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{g},\ldots,\mathfrak{g}_{N_{g}}^{g}\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)
. Let us also define

q_{0}=\min_{(\eta,g)\in\{tbt^{-1},t^{-1}bt\}\times F_{0}}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{\eta}}\mu\left(\mathfrak{g}_{i}^{\eta}\right)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{N_{g}}\mu\left(\mathfrak{g}_{i}^{g}\right).

Let

\kappa=\max_{(\eta,g)\in\{tbt^{-1},t^{-1}bt\}\times F_{0},(i,j)\in\llbracket 1,N_{\eta}\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,N_{g}\rrbracket}\left\{\mathfrak{h}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{i}\mathfrak{g}_{k}^{\eta}\right),\mathfrak{h}\left(\eta\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{j}\mathfrak{g}_{k}^{g}\right),\mathfrak{h}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j}\mathfrak{g}_{k}^{g}\right)\right\}

and let us take

L=\kappa+1.

Now let
w_{0}\in\Gamma
such that
w_{0}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right)
. Let us denote by
e
the edge with source
w_{0}\cdot v
that points towards
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
.

Using Lemma
3.8
, let us fix some
f\in F_{0}
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(fG_{k}\cdot v\notin B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)\ \forall k>0\right)>0.

We denote by
p
this probability. Notice that, as
\Gamma
acts on
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
by isometries, one also has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(w_{0}fG_{k}\cdot v\notin B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(w_{0}\cdot v,M)\ \forall k>0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(fG_{k}\cdot v\notin B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(v,M)\ \forall k>0\right)

(10)

\displaystyle=p.

The definition of
\kappa
implies that, for every
(\eta,g)\in\{tbt^{-1},t^{-1}bt\}\times F_{0}
:

•

for every
i\in\llbracket 1,N_{g}\rrbracket
, the vertex
u_{i}:=w_{0}\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{g}\cdots\mathfrak{g}_{i}^{g}\cdot v
satisfies
d(w_{0}\cdot v,u_{i})\leq\kappa
;

•

for every
i\in\llbracket 1,N_{g}\rrbracket
, the vertex
v_{i}:=w_{0}\eta\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{g}\cdots\mathfrak{g}_{i}^{g}\cdot v
satisfies
d(w_{0}\cdot v,v_{i})\leq\kappa
;

•

for every
i\in\llbracket 1,N_{\eta}\rrbracket
, the vertex
w_{i}:=w_{0}\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{\eta}\cdots\mathfrak{g}_{i}^{\eta}\cdot v
satisfies
d(w_{0}\cdot v,w_{i})\leq\kappa
.

In particular, the triangle inequality implies that, if
x
is one of the
u_{i}
’s,
v_{i}
’s,
w_{i}
’s, then

\displaystyle d\left(\mathcal{T}^{(0)},x\right)

\displaystyle\geq d(\mathcal{T}^{(0)},w_{0}\cdot v)-\kappa

\displaystyle\geq L-\kappa

\displaystyle=1,

thus
x\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
. Together with Lemma
3.9
, this implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(w_{0}G_{k}\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)},\forall k>0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\bigg(S_{1}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{\eta_{e}},\ldots,S_{N_{\eta_{e}}}=\mathfrak{g}_{N_{\eta_{e}}}^{\eta_{e}},S_{N_{\eta_{e}}+1}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{f},\ldots,S_{N_{\eta_{e}}+N_{f}}=\mathfrak{g}_{N_{f}}^{f}

\displaystyle\text{ and }w_{0}fS_{N_{\eta_{e}}+N_{f}+1}\cdots S_{k}\cdot v\notin B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(w_{0}\cdot v,M),\forall k>N_{\eta_{e}}+N_{f}

\displaystyle\text{ and }\exists k>N_{\eta_{e}}+N_{f},w_{0}fS_{N_{\eta_{e}}+N_{f}+1}\cdots S_{k}\cdot v\in\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq q_{0}\mathbb{P}\left(w_{0}fG_{k}\cdot v\notin B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(w_{0}\cdot v,M),\forall k>0\text{ and }\exists k>0,w_{0}fG_{k}\cdot v\in\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right),

the last inequality resulting from the fact that the
S_{i}
’s are independent and identically distributed. Likewise, using again the previous remark:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(w_{0}G_{k}\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)},\forall k>0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(S_{1}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{f},\ldots,S_{N_{f}}=\mathfrak{g}_{N_{f}}^{f}\text{ and }w_{0}fS_{N_{f}+1}\cdots S_{k}\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\forall k>N_{f}\right)

\displaystyle\geq q_{0}\mathbb{P}\left(w_{0}fG_{k}\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\forall k>0\right).

Decomposing

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(w_{0}fG_{k}\cdot v\notin B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(w_{0}\cdot v,M)\forall k>0\text{ and }\exists k>0,w_{0}fG_{k}\cdot v\in\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(w_{0}fG_{k}\cdot v\notin B_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n}}(w_{0}\cdot v,M)\forall k>0\text{ and }w_{0}fG_{k}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)},\forall k>0\right)

we thus get

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{q_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left(w_{0}G_{k}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)},\forall k>0\right),

i.e.

\mathbb{P}\left(w_{0}G_{k}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)},\forall k>0\right)\geq\frac{pq_{0}}{2}.

Hence, setting
q:=\frac{pq_{0}}{2}
concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Now we are able to prove Proposition
3.6
.

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

The fact that the random walk
G_{k}\cdot v
converges almost surely to a random end
\xi\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
results from Theorem
3.7
. So it remains to show that
\xi\notin\partial\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
almost surely.

Let
L
and
q
be provided by Lemma
3.10
. As
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right)
is still a proper
\Lambda
-invariant subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
on which
\Lambda
acts cocompactly, Lemma
3.4
tells us that:

(11)

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists k\geq 0,wG_{k}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right)\right)=1\text{ for every $w\in\Gamma$.}

Let us define inductively the random variables
\tau_{i},\sigma_{i}
which are the (possibly infinite) indices of the
i
-th exit from
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right)
(
resp.
return to
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
):

\displaystyle\tau_{1}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{k>0,G_{k}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right)\right\};

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{k>\tau_{i},G_{k}\cdot v\in\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right\};

\displaystyle\tau_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{k>\sigma_{i},G_{k}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right)\right\}.

Let us define the event
\Sigma_{i}:=\left\{\sigma_{i}<\infty\right\}
. Equation (
11
) implies that

\left\{G_{k}\cdot v\in\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\text{ for infinitely many $k$'s}\right\}=\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\Sigma_{i},

thus we want to prove that
\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\Sigma_{i}\right)=0
. As
\Sigma_{i+1}\subseteq\Sigma_{i}
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, one has
\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\Sigma_{i}\right)=\lim_{i\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\Sigma_{i}\right)
. For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Sigma_{i+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+1}<\infty\text{ and }\sigma_{i+1}<\infty\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{0}\in\Gamma,w_{0}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}(\mathcal{T}^{(0)})}\mathbb{P}\big(\tau_{i+1}<\infty\text{ and }S_{1}\cdots S_{\tau_{i+1}}=w_{0}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ and }\exists\sigma>\tau_{i+1},S_{1}\cdots S_{\sigma}\cdot v\in\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{0}\in\Gamma,w_{0}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}(\mathcal{T}^{(0)})}\mathbb{P}\big(\tau_{i+1}<\infty\text{ and }S_{1}\cdots S_{\tau_{i+1}}=w_{0}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{ and }\exists\sigma>\tau_{i+1},w_{0}S_{\tau_{i+1}+1}\cdots S_{\sigma}\cdot v\in\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{0}\in\Gamma,w_{0}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}(\mathcal{T}^{(0)})}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+1}<\infty\text{ and }S_{1}\cdots S_{\tau_{i+1}}=w_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\sigma>\tau_{i+1},w_{0}S_{\tau_{i+1}+1}\cdots S_{\sigma}\cdot v\in\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right),

the last equality resulting from the independence of the
S_{k}
’s and the fact that
\tau_{i+1}
is a stopping time with respect to the random walk with step distribution
(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. As the
S_{k}
’s are identically distributed, we thus get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Sigma_{i+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{0}\in\Gamma,w_{0}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}(\mathcal{T}^{(0)})}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+1}<\infty\text{ and }S_{1}\cdots S_{\tau_{i+1}}=w_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\sigma^{\prime}>0,w_{0}S_{1}\cdots S_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cdot v\in\mathcal{T}^{(0)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-q)\sum_{w_{0}\in\Gamma,w_{0}\cdot v\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{T}_{m,n},L}(\mathcal{T}^{(0)})}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+1}<\infty\text{ and }S_{1}\cdots S_{\tau_{i+1}}=w_{0}\right)

(by Lemma
3.10
)

\displaystyle=(1-q)\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+1}<\infty\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-q)\mathbb{P}(\Sigma_{i}).

A straightforward induction thus implies that
\mathbb{P}\left(\Sigma_{i}\right)\leq(1-q)^{i-1}
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, which proves that
\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\Sigma_{i}\right)=0
.
∎

Now we come back to preactions. The previous result has the following translation in the setting of Theorem
1.2
:

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
\alpha
be a transitive and non-saturated preaction on a pointed countable set
(X,x)
whose
(m,n)
-graph is finite and let
\beta
be its maximal forest saturation action (defined on a pointed countable set
(X^{\prime},x)
that contains
X
). Let us assume that

•

either
|m|=|n|
; or

•

\alpha
has infinite phenotype.

Then, the sequence
p_{\beta}(x\cdot G_{k})
converges almost surely to a random end of
\mathcal{G}_{\beta}
.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda=\mathrm{Stab}_{\alpha}(x)
and let
\pi:\mathcal{T}_{m,n}\to\Lambda\backslash\mathcal{T}_{m,n}=\mathcal{G}_{\beta}
be the projection. Let
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}=\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{G}_{\alpha})
. By Remark
2.4
,
\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
is a proper
\Lambda
-invariant subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
on which
\Lambda
acts cocompactly (the quotient graph
\Lambda\backslash\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
being
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}
).
So by Proposition
3.6
, the sequence
\left(G_{k}\cdot v\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges almost surely to a random end
\xi\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{m,n}\smallsetminus\partial\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
. Let
e\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{T}_{m,n})
such that
\mathbf{s}(e)\in\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
,
\mathbf{t}(e)\notin\mathcal{T}^{(0)}
and
\xi
belongs to the half-tree
\mathcal{T}_{e}
of
e
. For
k
large enough, the sequence
G_{k}\cdot v
remains in
\mathcal{T}_{e}
. Furthermore,
\pi
induces a graph isomorphism between
\mathcal{T}_{e}
and
\pi(\mathcal{T}_{e})
by Proposition
2.8
applied to
f=\pi(e)
. Thus, the sequence

\displaystyle p_{\beta}(x\cdot G_{k})

\displaystyle=(\Lambda G_{k})\langle b\rangle

\displaystyle=\Lambda(G_{k}\langle b\rangle)

\displaystyle=\pi(G_{k}\cdot v)

converges almost surely to the random end
\pi\left(\xi\right)
in
\pi\left(\mathcal{T}_{e}\right)
.
∎

3.2.2.
Pasting preactions

The proof of Theorem
1.2
now relies on the following key deterministic result:

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
be transitive non-saturated preactions on pointed countable sets
(X_{i},x_{i})
(
i\in\{1,2\}
) whose
(m,n)
-graphs share the same phenotype
P
. Assume that this phenotype is infinite if
|m|\neq|n|
. For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
\beta_{i}
be the maximal forest saturation action of
\alpha_{i}
(defined on a pointed coutable set
(Y_{i},x_{i})
that contains
X_{i}
).

Let us consider reduced words
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}
such that:

(1)

for every subword
w
of
g_{2}g_{3}
, one has
p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot g_{1}w)\notin\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{1}}
;

(2)

for every subword
w
of
g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}^{-1}
, one has
p_{\beta_{2}}(x_{2}\cdot g_{3}^{-1}w)\notin\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{2}}
;

(3)

one has

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}(p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot g_{1}),p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot g_{1}g_{2}))

\displaystyle\geq d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}\left(\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{1}},p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot g_{1})\right)

\displaystyle+d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{2}}}\left(\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{2}},p_{\beta_{2}}\left(x_{2}\cdot g_{3}^{-1}\right)\right)+2.

Then, there exists an action
\alpha
, whose
(m,n)
-graph is infinite, that extends both
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
, and such that
x_{1}\cdot g_{1}g_{2}g_{3}=x_{2}
.

Proof.

Let us define the subset

X_{1}^{\prime}=X_{1}\cup\{p_{\beta_{1}}^{-1}(p_{\beta_{1}}\left(x_{1}\cdot w)\right)\mid\text{$w$ subword of $g_{1}$}\}

of
Y_{1}
and the subset

X_{2}^{\prime}=X_{2}\cup\{p_{\beta_{2}}^{-1}\left(p_{\beta_{2}}(x_{2}\cdot w)\right)\mid\text{$w$ subword of $g_{3}^{-1}$}\}

of
Y_{2}
. For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let us denote by
\beta_{i}^{\prime}
the restriction of
\beta_{i}
defined on
X_{i}^{\prime}
.

As
P=\infty
or
|m|=|n|
, the following arguments will take place in a half-tree of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{i}}^{c}
(for
i\in\{1,2\}
), which is isomorphic to a half-tree of
\mathcal{T}_{m,n}
by Proposition
2.8
; in particular, an edge path of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{i}}^{c}
that derives from a vertex of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{i}}^{c}
and a reduced word
\gamma
is reduced, and its length is the height
\mathfrak{h}(\gamma)
of
\gamma
.

Especially, the edge path deriving from
(g_{2},x_{1}\cdot g_{1})
is reduced in
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{1}}^{c}
. Thus, one can write the normal form of
g_{2}
as
g_{2}=uv
, where all the subwords of
u
are defined on
x_{1}\cdot g_{1}
and no nonempty subword of
v
is defined on
x_{1}\cdot g_{1}u
(for the preaction
\beta_{1}^{\prime}
). One has:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}(u)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}(p_{1}(x_{1}\cdot g_{1}),p_{1}(x_{1}\cdot g_{1}u))

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}(p_{1}(x_{1}\cdot g_{1}),\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{1}}).

Likewise, one can write the reduced normal form of
g_{2}
as
g_{2}=u^{\prime}v^{\prime}
, where all subwords of
v^{\prime-1}
are defined on
x_{2}\cdot g_{3}^{-1}
and no nonempty subword of
u^{\prime-1}
is defined on
x_{2}\cdot g_{3}^{-1}v^{\prime-1}
(for the preaction
\beta_{2}^{\prime})
. One has

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}(v^{\prime})

\displaystyle=d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{2}}}\left(p_{\beta_{2}}\left(x_{2}\cdot g_{3}^{-1}\right),p_{\beta_{2}}\left(x_{2}\cdot g_{3}^{-1}v^{\prime-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{2}}}\left(p_{\beta_{2}}\left(x_{2}\cdot g_{3}^{-1}\right),\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{2}}\right).

Thus, the third assumption implies that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}(g_{2})

\displaystyle=d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}(p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot g_{1}),p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot g_{1}g_{2}))

\displaystyle\geq\mathfrak{h}(u)+\mathfrak{h}(v^{\prime})+2,

so the initial subword
u
of
g_{2}
is in fact an initial subword of
u^{\prime}
, and one can write the normal form of
g_{2}
as
g_{2}=uu^{\prime\prime}v^{\prime}
, where
\mathfrak{h}(u^{\prime\prime})\geq 2
.

Let us write
u^{\prime\prime}=t^{\varepsilon}\mathfrak{m}t^{\eta}
for some reduced word
\mathfrak{m}
, where

•

\varepsilon,\eta\in\{1,-1\}
;

•

t^{\varepsilon}
is not defined on
x_{1}\cdot g_{1}u
(for the preaction
\beta_{1}^{\prime}
);

•

t^{-\eta}
is not defined on
x_{2}\cdot g_{3}^{-1}v^{\prime-1}
(for the preaction
\beta_{2}^{\prime}
).

Let us denote by
b^{n_{1}}t^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots b^{n_{r}}t^{\varepsilon_{r}}b^{n_{r+1}}
the reduced form of
\mathfrak{m}
. By induction on
r
, we build a preaction
\gamma
defined on a countable set
S

•

whose
(m,n)
-graph is an edge path
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
, such that the orientation of the edge
e_{i}
is the sign of
\varepsilon_{i}
;

•

all of whose
\langle b\rangle
-orbits share the same cardinal
C
, which is the common label of the vertices of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha_{i}}^{c}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
by Remark
2.17
(and which is infinite if
P
is);

•

such that there exist
y_{1},y_{2}\in S
such that

–

t^{-\varepsilon}
is not defined on
y_{1}
;

–

t^{\eta}
is not defined on
y_{2}
;

–

y_{2}=y_{1}\cdot\mathfrak{m}
.

Finally, we merge the preactions
\beta_{1}^{\prime}
,
\gamma
and
\beta_{2}^{\prime}
into a single preaction defined on
X_{1}^{\prime}\sqcup S\sqcup X_{2}^{\prime}
by defining

•

x_{1}\cdot g_{1}u\cdot t^{\varepsilon}=y_{1}
;

•

y_{2}\cdot t^{\eta}=x_{2}\cdot g_{3}^{-1}v^{\prime-1}
.

-graphs is provided in Figure

.

For this new preaction
\delta
we get

\displaystyle x_{2}\cdot g_{3}^{-1}v^{\prime-1}

\displaystyle=y_{2}\cdot t^{\eta}

\displaystyle=y_{1}\cdot\mathfrak{m}\cdot t^{\eta}

\displaystyle=x_{1}\cdot g_{1}ut^{\varepsilon}\mathfrak{m}\cdot t^{\eta}

\displaystyle=x_{1}\cdot g_{1}uu^{\prime\prime}

which implies that

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=x_{1}\cdot g_{1}uu^{\prime\prime}v^{\prime}g_{3}

\displaystyle=x_{1}\cdot g_{1}g_{2}g_{3}

and
\delta
extends both
\alpha_{1}^{\prime}
and
\alpha_{2}^{\prime}
. The
(m,n)
-graph of
\delta
consists of

•

the

;

•

the

the edge path
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
(with
\mathbf{s}(e_{1})\in\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}^{\prime}}

Thus, as

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.2
:

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
be two subgroups of
\Gamma
whose
(m,n)
-graphs are infinite. Let us assume that

•

either
\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}(\Lambda_{i})=\infty
for
i\in\{1,2\}
; or

•

|m|=|n|
and
\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}(\Lambda_{1})=\bm{\mathrm{Ph}}_{m,n}(\Lambda_{2})
,

and let us denote by
P
the common phenotype of
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let us denote by
\gamma_{i}
the associated transitive right action on a pointed countable set
(X_{i},x_{i})
. Let us fix
R>0
and let
\mathcal{F}_{i}
be the
R
-ball of
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma_{i}}
around
p_{\gamma_{i}}(x_{i})
. We denote by

\displaystyle U_{i}^{(R)}=\big\{

\displaystyle\gamma\text{ transitive and saturated preaction on a pointed countable set }(W_{\gamma},w_{\gamma})\mid

\displaystyle\mathrm{Stab}_{\gamma}(w_{\gamma})\in\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\text{ and }\left(p_{\gamma}^{-1}\left(B_{\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}}(p_{\gamma}(w_{\gamma}),R)\right),w_{\gamma}\right)\simeq\left(p_{\gamma_{i}}^{-1}(\mathcal{F}_{i}),x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\text{ (as pointed labeled graphs)}\big\}.

Identifying subgroups with pointed, transitive and saturated preactions, we recall that the sets
\left(U_{i}^{(R)}\right)_{R>0}
form a basis of neighborhoods of
\Lambda_{i}
for the topology
\mathcal{T}_{sat}
on the set of pointed transitive actions defined in Subsection
2.2.3
induced on
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
. Moreover,
U_{i}^{(R)}
is included in
\bm{\mathrm{Ph}_{m,n}}^{-1}(P)
.

Let
\alpha_{i}
be the subpreaction of
\gamma_{i}
defined on a subset
X_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq X_{i}
containing
x_{i}
and whose
(m,n)
-graph is
\mathcal{F}_{i}
. For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
\beta_{i}
be the maximal forest saturation action of
\alpha_{i}
given by Lemma
2.5
(defined on a countable set
Y_{i}
that contains
X_{i}^{\prime}
).

Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
. Using the fact that
\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)
is bounded, let us denote by
M:=\max_{\gamma\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)}\mathfrak{h}(\gamma)
. By Corollary
3.11
applied to
\beta_{1}
and a sequence
(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of independently
\mu
-distributed random variables on the one hand, and to
\beta_{2}
and a sequence
\left(S^{\prime}_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of independently
\mu^{-1}
-distributed random variables (legit by Remark
2.12
), denoting by
(G_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=(S_{1}\cdots S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(G_{k}^{\prime})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=\left(S_{1}^{\prime}\cdots S_{k}^{\prime}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, we get that

•

there exists some
k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(12)

\mathbb{P}\left(p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot G_{k})\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}},M}(\mathcal{F}_{1}),\ \forall k\geq k_{1}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon;

•

there exists some
k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(13)

\mathbb{P}\left(p_{\beta_{2}}\left(x_{2}\cdot G_{k}^{\prime}\right)\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{2}},M}(\mathcal{F}_{2}),\ \forall k\geq k_{2}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

The choice of
M
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot G_{k_{1}}U)\notin\mathcal{F}_{1}\text{ \ for every subword $U$ of $S_{k_{1}+1}\cdots S_{k}$ and every $k\geq k_{1}$}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot G_{k})\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}},M}(\mathcal{F}_{1}),\ \forall k\geq k_{1}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\varepsilon\text{ by \eqref{k1m}}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(p_{\beta_{2}}\left(x_{2}\cdot G^{\prime}_{k_{2}}U\right)\notin\mathcal{F}_{2}\text{ \ for every subword $U$ of $S_{k_{2}+1}^{\prime}\cdots S_{k}^{\prime}$ and every $k\geq k_{2}$}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(p_{\beta_{2}}\left(x_{2}\cdot G_{k}^{\prime}\right)\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{2}},M}(\mathcal{F}_{2}),\ \forall k\geq k_{2}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\varepsilon\text{ by \eqref{k2m}.}

We draw the attention of the reader to the fact that
S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime}
’s may not be one of the standard generators
b,b^{-1},t,t^{-1}
, and that the term
subword
has to be understood in the sense of Section
2.2.2
,
i.e.
with respect to the standard generators.

Let us fix some
M^{\prime}
such that, for
i\in\{1,2\}
:

M^{\prime}>\max_{\left(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k_{i}}\right)\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)^{k_{i}}}\mathfrak{h}(h_{1}\cdots h_{k_{i}}).

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let us also define

C_{i}=\max_{\left(h_{1},\ldots,h_{k_{i}}\right)\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)^{k_{i}}}d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{i}}}\left(p_{\beta_{i}}\left(x_{i}\cdot h_{1}\cdots h_{k_{i}}\right),\mathcal{F}_{i}\right).

By Corollary
3.11
, there exists
k_{0}>k_{1}+k_{2}
such that, for any
k\geq k_{0}
:

(16)

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}\left(p_{1}(x_{1}),p_{1}\left(x_{1}\cdot S_{1}\cdots S_{k-k_{2}}\right)\right)\geq C_{1}+C_{2}+2M^{\prime}+2\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Now we fix
k\geq k_{0}
and, for every
i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
, we let
S_{i}^{\prime}=S_{k-i+1}^{-1}
. Let us introduce the events

A:=\left\{p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot S_{1}\cdots S_{k_{1}}U)\notin\mathcal{F}_{1}\text{ \ for every subword $U$ of $S_{k_{1}+1}\cdots S_{k}$}\right\};

B:=\left\{p_{\beta_{2}}(x_{2}\cdot S_{1}^{\prime}\cdots S_{k_{2}}^{\prime}U)\notin\mathcal{F}_{2}\text{ \ for every subword $U$ of $S_{k_{2}+1}^{\prime}\cdots S_{k}^{\prime}$}\right\};

C:=\left\{d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}\left(p_{1}(x_{1}),p_{1}\left(x_{1}\cdot S_{1}\cdots S_{k-k_{2}}\right)\right)\geq C_{1}+C_{2}+2M^{\prime}+2\right\}.

By Equations (
14
), (
15
) and (
16
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A\cap B\cap C\right)\geq 1-3\varepsilon.

Let
(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathrm{Supp}(\mu)^{\mathbb{N}}
satisfying these three conditions,
i.e.

•

p_{\beta_{1}}\left(x_{1}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k_{1}}u\right)\notin\mathcal{F}_{1}
for every subword
u
of
s_{k_{1}+1}\cdots s_{k}
;

•

p_{\beta_{2}}\left(x_{2}\cdot s_{k}^{-1}\cdots s_{k-k_{2}+1}^{-1}v\right)\notin\mathcal{F}_{2}
for every subword
v
of
s_{k-k_{2}}^{-1}\cdots s_{1}^{-1}
;

•

d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}\left(p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}),p_{\beta_{1}}\left(x_{1}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k-k_{2}}\right)\right)\geq C_{1}+C_{2}+2M^{\prime}+2
.

Then:

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}\left(p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k_{1}}),p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k_{1}}\cdot s_{k_{1}+1}\cdots s_{k-k_{2}})\right)

\displaystyle\geq d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}\left(p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}),p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k})\right)-d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}\left(p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}),p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k_{1}})\right)

\displaystyle-d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}\left(p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k-k_{2}}),p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k})\right)\text{\ (by the triangle inequality)}

\displaystyle\geq\left(C_{1}+C_{2}+2M^{\prime}+2\right)-M^{\prime}-M^{\prime}

\displaystyle=C_{1}+C_{2}+2

\displaystyle\geq d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{1}}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{1},p_{\beta_{1}}(x_{1}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k_{1}})\right)+d_{\mathcal{G}_{\beta_{2}}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{2},p_{\beta_{2}}\left(x_{2}\cdot s_{k}^{-1}\cdots s_{k-k_{2}+1}^{-1}\right)\right)+2,

which implies that the preactions
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
and the reduced forms
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}
of the three elements
s_{1}\cdots s_{k_{1}}
,
s_{k_{1}+1}\cdots s_{k-k_{2}}
and
s_{k-k_{2}+1}\cdots s_{k}
of
\Gamma
satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
3.12
. Thus, by Lemma
3.12
, there exists a saturated preaction
\alpha
defined on a pointed countable set
(X,x_{1})
that contains
X_{1}^{\prime}
and
X_{2}^{\prime}
as disjoint subsets, whose
(m,n)
-graph is infinite, and such that

•

\alpha
extends both
\alpha_{1}^{\prime}
and
\alpha_{2}^{\prime}
;

•

x_{1}\cdot s_{1}\cdots s_{k}=x_{2}
.

We proved that for every

.∎
