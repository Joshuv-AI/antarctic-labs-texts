Screening and evaluation of potential histone deacetylase inhibitors against Babesia infections
Authors: Xinxin Zhang, Yubin Li, Jin Che, Junwei Wang, Qindong Liang, Yuting Zhang, Yanan Bai, Shuaiyang Zhao, Hong Yin, Jinming Wang, Guiquan Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Babesia spp., a group of blood protozoa transmitted via the bites of tick vectors, cause enormous economic losses for livestock industry and pose significant risk to public health. Currently, the drugs available for treating babesiosis are limited and possess certain toxicity, making the discovery of new and safer drug candidates an urgent priority. Inhibitors targeting histone deacetylase (HDACs) have attracted increasing attention for its antiparasitic effects as a drug targeting epigenetic regulatory enzymes. However, little is known about the effects of HDAC inhibitors on Babesia parasites. Here, 69 compounds targeting HDAC were evaluated for inhibiting Babesia xinjiangensis (B. xinjiangensis) growth in vitro. Among them, 7 compounds (at 2 μM) exhibited high growth inhibition (≥50%) and the cytotoxicity on MDOK cells of the compounds was assessed subsequently. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of the seven compounds for MDOK cells are greater than 3 μM. Two compounds, namely panobinostat and belinostat were chosen for in vivo assays. In our study, panobinostat and belinostat demonstrated outstanding activity in vitro at the nanomolar level. The IC50 values of panobinostat and belinostat were determined to be 30(±2) nM and 235(±21) nM at 48 h, respectively, and both exhibited low cytotoxicity, with IC50 values of 3 (±0.4) μM and 20 (±3) μM respectively. In addition, panobinostat effectively and safely inhibited B. microti growth in BALB/c mice. Our findings support that panobinostat is a potential alternative drug to control babesiosis.

Introduction

Babesia, a tick-borne intraerythrocytic protozoan belonging to the phylum Apicomplexa, poses a significant threat to human and animal health. While sharing this phylum with other well-known parasitic genera such as Theileria, Plasmodium, Toxoplasma, and Cryptosporidium, it is distinguished by its unique mode of transmission and host interaction (Homer et al., 2000; Vannier et al., 2015). Babesia has evolved complex and subtle survival strategies to achieve persistence within the tick vector through transstadial and transovarial transmission (Yabsley and Shock, 2013; Jalovecka et al., 2019). This mode of tick transmission, together with the global distribution of tick vectors, has greatly contributed to Babesia becoming the second most common blood parasite after trypanosomes (Schnittger et al., 2012). Babesiosis is prevalent in many mammals such as cattle, horses, sheep, pigs, cats and dogs, severely affecting the livestock economy (Schnittger et al., 2012; Malgwi et al., 2023). Global climate change, increased travel activity, tick activity and blood transfusions have led to a rise in human babesiosis, which has become a serious public health concern (Sanchez et al., 2016; Westblade et al., 2017; Lukavská and Kybicová, 2023). Clinical manifestations of babesiosis range from asymptomatic and mild infections to fulminant disease resulting in death (Vannier et al., 2015). Karshima et al. summarized the global prevalence, distribution, and species diversity of zoonotic Babesia spp., revealing an overall global prevalence rate of 2.23% (Karshima et al., 2022). Overall, Babesia poses significant threats to public health security and development of livestock industry.

Immunization and chemical treatment are currently the primary measures for controlling Babesia infections. Compounds are preferred over vaccines due to the limitations of the latter, such as the limited number of species covered, the risk of reversion to a high level of virulence, and the high cost of production and transportation of the vaccine, all of which prevents large-scale application of the vaccines. Despite the serious side effects and the potential for resistance of Babesia to diminazene aceturate (DA) (Mosqueda et al., 2012), DA and imidocarb remain the first choice for the treatment of animals babesiosis (Kumar et al., 2003; Yamasaki et al., 2017). Human babesiosis is typically treated with atovaquone plus azithromycin for mild to moderate cases (Krause et al., 2000; Sanchez et al., 2016). However, prolonged use of these medications often leads to drug resistance, resulting in disease recurrence and chronic infections (Simon et al., 2017). Another therapy is clindamycin plus quinine, but treatment is often interrupted due to severe toxic reactions (Vannier et al., 2015; Sanchez et al., 2016). Thus, it is critical and urgent to develop novel anti-Babesia drugs that are low in toxicity and well-tolerated.

Epigenetic regulation is a promising target for the development of novel anti-parasitic drugs. Histone deacetylase (HDAC), an important epigenetic modulators, has been preliminarily investigated for its function in some protozoa, and it has been demonstrated that histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) have a favorable antiprotozoal effect (Gurboga et al., 2021; Vanheer and Kafsack, 2021). The antiparasitic activity of vorinostat against Plasmodium, Leishmania, Cryptosporidium, etc. other parasites has been demonstrated (Herrera-Martínez et al., 2020). Huang et al. showed that the optimized compound JX21108 is highly active against various stages of P. falciparum, with an antiparasitic mechanism similar to that of the reference HDAC inhibitor suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA) (Huang et al., 2020). Mouveaux et al. identified a potential HDACi, MC1742, that inhibits Toxoplasma gondii tachyzoite growth with nanomolar concentration and profoundly interferes with gene expression profiles (Mouveaux et al., 2022). According to a report by Jublot et al., the synthetic HDACi JF363 can suppress the growths of Toxoplasma and Plasmodium in vitro (Jublot et al., 2022). In conclusion, HDACi shows considerable potential for antiparasitic drug development. However, their efficacy against Babesia parasites remains unknown.

In this study, we screened 69 HDACi and found that 7 of them effectively inhibited Babesia xinjiangensis (B. xinjiangensis) (Guan et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2025) growth in vitro. Two promising compounds, panobinostat and belinostat, were further tested for their efficacy in inhibiting Babesia growth in vivo to assess their potential therapeutic window. Importantly, we demonstrated that panobinostat is effective and safe against B. microti infection in mice. These findings provide new possibilities for the development of novel and effective anti-Babesia drugs.

Materials and methods

Chemical compounds

All of the 69 HDACi and diminazene aceturate (DA) were purchased from MedChemExpress LLC (MCE, NJ, USA) (supplementary material) and Lanzhou IS Abundant Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (Lanzhou, China), respectively. The 69 HDACi include 10 from the hydroxamic acids class, 6 from the benzamides class, 1 from the cyclic tetrapeptide class, 2 from the short-chain fatty acids class, and the remainder are unclassified. All HDACi were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to 10 mM, while DA were dissolved in sterilized water to 10 mg/ml. All dissolved solutions were stored at −20 °C until use.

Parasites and animals

B. xinjiangensis was first isolated from a splenectomized sheep infested by ticks collected from cattle and sheep in Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China, and was subsequently confirmed to be transmitted by Hyalomma anatolicum anatolicum (Guan et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2025). B. xinjiangensis was maintained in Vectors and Vector-Borne Diseases (VVBD) Laboratory, Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute (LVRI) of Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS) (Guan et al., 2012). Babesia microti (Franca) Reichenow (PRA-99) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen. Female BALB/c mice were obtained from Laboratory Animal Center, LVRI. All animals were bred in a clean environment under uniform conditions.

In vitro culture of B. xinjiangensis

As described by Guan et al., B. xinjiangensis were cultured in 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, U.S.A) of RPMI 1640 medium (Lonza, Belgium) (complete culture medium, CM) and supplemented with 5% fresh red blood cells (RBCs) collected and isolated from the jugular vein of sheep at 37 °C in 5% CO2 (Guan et al., 2012).

Preliminary screening and evaluating of HDACi inhibiting B. xinjiangensis growth in vitro

B. xinjiangensis-infected RBCs were distributed into 96-well plates and inhibition rates were calculated after incubation (48 h) with HDACi at a final concentrations of 2 μM. Compounds with an inhibition rate exceeding 50% were selected for IC50 and cytotoxicity assays in vitro. When B. xinjiangensis parasitemia reached 1% in 24-well plates, inhibitors diluted to different concentrations with CM were added to the wells (each concentration with 3 replicate wells), followed by in vitro cultivating in cell incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h. The detailed drug dilution concentrations are as follows: panobinostat (270 nM, 135 nM, 67.5 nM, 33.75 nM, 16.875 nM, 8.4375 nM); belinostat (720 nM, 360 nM, 180 nM, 90 nM, 45 nM, 22.5 nM); trichostatin A, pracinostat and resminostat hydrochloride (1 μM, 500 nM, 250 nM, 125 nM, 62.5 nM, 31.25 nM); vorinostat and mocetinostat (10 μM, 5 μM, 2.5 μM, 1.25 μM, 625 nM, 312.5 nM). DMSO was used as a negative control. The growth inhibition rate was calculated by detecting parasitemia in Giemsa-stained blood smears using the following formula: parasitemia in control wells - parasitemia in wells treated with inhibitors/parasitemia in control wells × 100%.

Cytotoxicity assay in host cells

The toxicity of HDACi on Madin-Darby Ovine Kidney (MDOK) cells (KL-1633, ATCC) was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Baisai, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2500 cells/100 μL per well and incubated for 12 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After 12 h, the medium was replaced with different concentrations of inhibitors (100 μM to 1.28 nM) diluted in a 5-fold gradient using complete medium. The cells were cultured for 48 h. The medium was replaced with the same drug concentration every 24 h interval. Subsequently, CCK-8 (10 μL/100 μL) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. The absorbance was detected at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan Flash, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). DMSO served as a negative control. Independent experiments with technical replicates were performed.

In vivo efficacy of HDACi on the growth of B. microti in infected mice

B. microti preserved in liquid nitrogen were revived and injected into mice until the parasitemia reached 25%–30%. Then twenty 6-8 weeks female BALB/c mice that had been injected intraperitoneally with diluted RBC containing approximately 1 × 107 B. microti were randomly divided into 4 groups (5 BALB/c/group). As we found some non-infectious events after inoculation with B. microti -infected erythrocytes, inhibitor treatment of mice was initiated as soon as the parasites were observed in the Giemsa-stained blood smears, with the following protocol: Panobinostat (25 mg/kg.bw) and Belinostat (25 mg/kg.bw) were administered by oral gavage twice a day (Chua et al., 2017); DA (20 mg/kg.bw) was administered by intraperitoneal injection once a day; and 100 μL/dose of vehicle control (DMSO/PBS (v/v) = 1:1) was administered by intraperitoneal injection once a day. Mice were treated with the drugs for 5 consecutive days. Blood smears were prepared daily and parasitemia was observed and counted. Ultimately, the mice were euthanized by inhalation of an overdose of isoflurane (Orbiepharm, Qingdao, China) in a Small Animal Anesthesia Machine (Yuyan Instruments, Shanghai).

In vivo safety evaluation of panobinostat

6-8 weeks BALB/c mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 4 per group): a control group (DMSO/PBS) and a therapeutic dose group (25 mg/kg bw). According to the grouping, mice received a daily oral administration of panobinostat or an equivalent volume of the vehicle solvent for five consecutive days. Clinical manifestations, including activity, responsiveness, and respiration, were monitored daily. Body weight was recorded each morning prior to dosing. 24 h after the last administration, mice were anesthetized by inhalation of excessive amounts of isoflurane (Orbiepharm, Qingdao, China), and blood samples were collected via the retro-orbital plexus. Each blood sample was divided into two equal portions. A portion of the blood was placed in EDTA-K2-coated tubes for hematological analysis. And the remaining blood was centrifuged at 3500 rpm at room temperature for 10 min to separate serum for biochemical analysis. After blood sampling, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation.

Statistical analysis

Parasitemia (PI) was determined by counting infected erythrocytes (iRBC) in 2000 to 4000 RBCs (total RBC, tRBC) on thin Giemsa-stained blood smears: PI = iRBCs/tRBCs × 100%. The curves between the inhibitor concentrations and inhibition rates were fitted using GraphPad Prism 7.0. Disparities among different groups were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and multiple comparisons were performed by the post hoc Tukey-Kramer test. A p value below 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Molecular docking

The 3D structure available for B. microti HDAC (BmHDAC) predicted by AlphaFold (accession No. I7I9T7) and the ligands structure file [panobinostat (ID-6918837)] were downloaded from UniProt and PubChem databases, respectively. The protein and ligands were optimized using the OPLS3e force field in the Protein Preparation Wizard module and LigPrep module, followed by docking the ligand into the generated receptor grid using Schrodinger Maestro (version 12.2). The obtained docked project was saved in PDB format and imported into PyMOL for analyzation and visualization.

Results

A preliminary screening for HDACi against B. xinjiangensis in vitro

A total of 69 reported HADCi were selected and evaluated for their ability to inhibit the growth of B. xinjiangensis in vitro (supplemental material 1). As depicted in Fig. 1A, we conducted a screen at 2 μM compound concentrations, incubated for 48 h, and assessed the inhibition rate by comparing the number of infected erythrocytes with DMSO-treated control samples. The result showed that 7 compounds exhibited a growth inhibition rate exceeding 50% against B. xinjiangensis and were therefore selected for further testing. Among them, the performance of panobinostat and belinostat (Fig. 1B) was particularly remarkable, with the inhibition rates exceeding 75%. Of the remaining five compounds, apart from the benzamide class compound mocetinostat exhibiting a 50% inhibition rate against Babesia, the inhibition rates for the hydroxamic acids class compounds trichostatin A, resminostat hydrochloride, pracinostat, and vorinostat were 73%, 70%, 69%, and 66%, respectively. Additionally, 10 compounds exhibited inhibition rates between 25% and 50%, while 11 compounds showed inhibition rates below 25%. The remaining 41 compounds demonstrated no significant anti-Babesia activity (inhibition rate 0%). DA, as a positive control, still exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect on B. xinjiangensis proliferation, with an IC50 of 9 nM. Panobinostat was also impressive, with an IC50 of 30 nM, followed by belinostat at 235 (±21) nM, which demonstrates that panobinostat and belinostat are potential inhibitors of B. xinjiangensis growth in vitro. The IC50 values of vorinostat (878 ± 97 nM) and mocetinostat (2078 ± 187 nM) were at least 25- and 70-fold higher than that of the panobinostat (Table 1). HDACi effects on B. microti infected BALB/c mice in vivo

The promising results of panobinostat and belinostat in the in vitro antiproliferation assay encouraged us to further estimate their in vivo efficacy as a single therapy against B. microti-infected BALB/c mice. As depicted in Fig. 2C, peak infection rates in the panobinostat-treated group 25 mg/kg reached an average of 2.79% at day 9 post-inoculation (pi). Moreover, parasitemia levels were effectively reduced to less than 5% in mice treated with panobinostat and reached zero level at day 12 pi, showing an in vivo effect on the proliferation of B. microti almost comparable to that of DA (p > 0.05). However, the belinostat group showed little inhibition of B. microti growth, with the mean parasitemia exceeding 20% comparing to 30% in the control group. These results indicated that panobinostat may be a promising compound for treating Babesia infection.

In vivo safety evaluation of panobinostat

To evaluate the in vivo safety profile of panobinostat at the therapeutic dosage, we conducted a systemic toxicity study in BALB/c mice. Hematological and biochemical analyses revealed no significant differences between the panobinostat-treated group and the control group. The complete blood count and serum biochemical parameters, including red blood cells, white blood cells, platelet counts, hemoglobin, hematocrit, neutrophile, urea and aspartic aminotransferase showed no significant differences between the two groups. Furthermore, the body weight of mice in the panobinostat group did not decrease significantly compared to that of the control group (Fig. 3). Discussion

Babesia spp. are intraerythrocytic protozoan parasites of animals and humans that cause babesiosis. Novel therapeutic agents are urgently needed for the treatment of babesiosis due to limited drug choices, emerging drug resistance, and toxicity issues (Mosqueda et al., 2012). Repurposing “old” drugs is an attractive strategy because it can shorten the time to market, reduce costs and risks compared to de novo drug discovery. Small molecules that act on epigenetic regulatory proteins, such as HDACi, which are currently clinically approved for cancer treatment, are now being investigated for their potential application in treating various parasitic diseases, including malaria, toxoplasmosis, schistosomiasis, leishmaniasis, trypanosomosis (Hailu et al., 2017; Di Bello et al., 2022; Jublot et al., 2022; von Bredow et al., 2022) and theileriosis (Barman et al., 2021). These encouraging findings prompted us to carry out the present study to investigate the effects of HDACi against Babesia parasites both in vitro and in vivo.

Here, the comparative anti-parasitic profiles of 69 HDACi were assessed in vitro against the B. xinjiangensis (Fig. 1). Two of the compounds were also examined in vivo in a murine model of B. microti. We identified and compared the HDAC amino acid sequences of B. xinjiangensis and B. microti and found that the HDACs of the two species share certain conserved regions (supplemental material 2). This finding helps to address for the limitation of this study regarding the use of different species in in vivo and in vitro experiments—a limitation that stems from the technical challenge of establishing continuous in vitro cultivation B. microti. Furthermore, this result provides additional support for the efficacy of the test compounds against Babesia. According to the results of anti-Babesia efficacy evaluation of the compounds in vitro, both panobinostat and belinostat exhibited relatively outstanding efficacy on inhibiting B. xinjiangensis growth, with inhibition rates exceeding 80% at concentrations below 0.15 μM and 0.5 μM, respectively. It has been reported that panobinostat and belinostat were potent inhibitors of P. knowlesi, with IC50 values of 9 nM and 190 nM, respectively. However, both compounds exhibit no activity and weak activity against L. amazonensis promastigotes and free L. donovani promastigotes (inhibiting 20–35% at 20 mM) (Chua et al., 2017). In the present study, both panobinostat and belinostat exhibited potent in vitro activity against B. xinjiangensis, with IC50 values below 0.3 μM, and panobinostat demonstrated superior potency (IC50 < 0.05 μM). These findings are consistent with a recent report showing that panobinostat is highly active against B. duncani (EC50 = 8.4 nM) in vitro, (Dews et al., 2025), suggesting that panobinostat may be a broad-spectrum anti-Babesia agents with nanomolar potency. However, when comparing with published data on other apicomplexan parasites, notable species-specific differences emerge. For instance, belinostat and vorinostat show potent activity against T. annulata (IC50 values of 69 nM and 103 nM, respectively), while panobinostat did not significantly inhibit the growth of T. annulata even at an IC50 of 20 μM (Barman et al., 2021). This striking reversal in potency—where the most potent anti-Babesia compound is the least potent anti-Theileria compound—indicates that the molecular determinants of HDACi susceptibility are not conserved across these apicomplexan genera. In addition, vorinostat exhibited moderate activity against B. xinjiangensis (IC50 = 878 nM) (Table 1), which is approximately three-fold higher than its reported IC50 against T. gondii gondii (260 nM) in vitro (Zeng et al., 2025). It is worth noting that although panobinostat, belinostat, and vorinostat all belong to the hydroxamic acids class of HDACi sharing a zinc binding group (ZBG) moiety, the observed differences in antiparasitic activity due to the variations in the cap and linker regions (Chen et al., 2016) likely reflect their distinct selectivity profiles towards specific HDAC isozymes. Additionally, apicomplexan parasites possess multiple HDAC enzymes with distinct substrate specificities and structural features that may vary across species (Hailu et al., 2017). To sum up, future investigations, including isoform profiling of Babesia HDACs and structure-activity relationship (SAR) analyses of HDAC inhibitors, will be essential to rationalize these differential activities and to guide the development of optimized HDAC-targeting therapies for babesiosis.

Although both compounds show good activity against Babesia parasites, cytotoxicity must be assessed before moving panobinostat and belinostat into clinical trials as anti-babesia compounds. The concentrations at which panobinostat and belinostat affect cellular activity are significantly higher than their IC50 values for inhibiting Babesia growth. Notably, the SIs of panobinostat and belinostat (100 and 85, respectively) are far greater than previously published SI for Plasmodium parasites (2-18 and 11-40, respectively) indicating greater selectivity for Babesia parasites (Engel et al., 2015).

Given the in vitro activity of panobinostat and belinostat against B. xinjiangensis, we extended our findings to evaluate, for the first time, the in vivo anti-B. microti efficacy of two compounds using a mouse model. As expected, compared with the vehicle control-treated group, panobinostat still performed well, with an inhibitory effect similar to that of the DA treatment group (P > 0.05), while belinostat treatment failed to inhibit Babesia (Fig. 2C), despite its relatively good in vitro inhibitory effect. This striking divergence in in vivo efficacy between two structurally related hydroxamic acid-based HDAC inhibitors prompted us to consider potential pharmacokinetic determinants of their differential performance. To critically evaluate whether the administered belinostat dose is expected to achieve plasma concentrations compatible with its in vitro potency, we examined published pharmacokinetic data for belinostat in mice. Studies have shown that belinostat is characterized by rapid systemic clearance and relatively short half-life period following intravenous injection. Based on reported pharmacokinetic parameters, a 25 mg/kg twice-daily regimen would be expected to produce peak plasma concentrations that may exceed the in vitro IC50 transiently; however, the rapid clearance could result in subtherapeutic trough concentrations between doses. This intermittent exposure profile may be insufficient to sustain the antiparasitic effect required for parasite clearance. In contrast, panobinostat is known to possess more favorable pharmacokinetic properties in mice, including slower clearance and higher bioavailability. (Giri et al., 2017). These differences in pharmacokinetics may explain the observed differences in in vivo efficacy. Future studies incorporating optimization of dosing regimens and exposure-response analyses, such as LC-MS/MS quantification of plasma drug concentrations, are warranted to definitively establish the pharmacokinetic determinants of HDAC inhibitor efficacy against B. microti. Importantly, the superior in vivo performance of panobinostat aligns with its documented activity against other apicomplexan parasites and supports its further evaluation for babesiosis treatment. In Plasmodium, Chua et al. showed that panobinostat is the most potent among belinostat, vorinostat, romidepsin and chloroquine, exhibiting good activity against both P. falciparum 3D7 and P. knowlesi A1H.1, and in vivo efficacy in P. berghei-infected mice. Mechanistically, panoninostat induce hyperacetylation of Plasmodium histone H4, thereby directly or indirectly inhibiting the activity of HDAC. Zhang et al. disclosed panobinostat causes severe ultrastructural damage to T. gondii by diffusing across the host cell plasma membrane and the parasitophorous vacuole and reduces retinal inflammation in infected mice (Zhang et al., 2022). This study provides the first in vivo evidence of panobinostat efficacy against Toxoplasma. El-Sayed et al. demonstrated the efficacy of FLLL-32 against multiple Babesia species, showing that parasitemia was inhibited significantly and reached zero at 16 days post-infection (dpi) in mice infected with B. microti after treatment with FLLL-32 (El-Sayed et al., 2023), which is similar to the anti-B.microti effects of panobinostat observed in our study. Furthermore, hematological and biochemical datas further support the safety and therapeutic potential of panobinostat in vivo (Fig. 3). It is worth noting that future studies employing longer follow-up periods and more sensitive detection methods (e.g., nested PCR or subinoculation into naive animals) are warranted to confirm the sterilizing activity of panobinostat. Additionally, molecular docking analysis predicted that panobinostat can bind to the AlphaFold-predicted structure of BmHDAC, suggesting that HDAC inhibition may represent a plausible mechanism of action. However, simulated docking alone does not constitute direct evidence of targeted binding. To elucidate the underlying mechanism, further functional validation is required, such as measurement of HDAC enzymatic activity in parasite lysates, assessment of histone acetylation levels following treatment, or genetic confirmation of HDAC dependency. In summary, panobinostat exhibits inhibitory effects against Plasmodium, Toxoplasma, B. ducani, B. xinjiangensis, and B. microti, suggesting its potential as a broad-spectrum antiparasitic drug.

In conclusion, we conducted an HDACi-based screening assay and identified panobinostat as a promising compound against B. xinjiangensis in vitro with outstanding parasiticidal effects and mild cytotoxicity. Its ability to inhibit the growth of B. microti in mice and the safety profile, together with molecular docking analysis further suggests that panobinostat may be a promising anti-Babesia drug for the treatment of clinical babesiosis. Future research should focus on improving its efficacy against Babesia by synthesizing more potent derivatives of panobinostat or using it in combination with other anti-Babesia drugs, as well as investigating the underlying mechanisms to identify specific molecular targets.
