Nurses' and Nursing Students' Experiences With Generative Artificial Intelligence in Educational and Clinical Settings: A Scoping Review
Authors: Ming Wei Jeffrey Woo, Adrian Heng Tsai Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis scoping review follows the Arksey and O'Malley five‐stage framework: (1) identifying research questions, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) selecting studies, (4) extracting, mapping, and charting data, and (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting findings. The review seeks to identify the extent and type of literature and gain insights into nurses' and nursing students' experiences with using Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI). A comprehensive search was conducted across five databases. The search was limited to studies published between January 2020 and July 2025. The systematic screening process identified 20 papers for inclusion in this review. Nurses' experiences with GenAI were mainly positive, while nursing students had mixed perceptions, though mostly positive. Thematic analysis revealed that various factors underpinning their attitudinal, normative, and control beliefs are critical determinants of nurses' and students' overall experiences with GenAI and their intentions to use GenAI technologies. An overarching analysis using the theory of planned behavior mapped these factors and experiences, yielding predictions of behavioral intentions and actions regarding GenAI use. Nurse leaders are vital in shaping institutional policies and ethical guidelines to encourage responsible use of GenAI.Keywords: clinical practice, generative artificial intelligence, nursing education, scoping review, technology adoption

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is progressively being incorporated into multiple sectors, with its use in educational contexts emerging as a notable area of interest. Although its origins date back to 1956 (Cristianini 2016), AI systems have demonstrated advancements in task performance previously associated with human cognition, including providing support to innovation, reasoning, decision‐making, and problem‐solving (Sharma and Sharma 2023). In higher education, AI has driven a paradigm shift over the past 5 years (Chu et al. 2022). This is especially true for healthcare education, where emerging technologies offer AI‐enabled solutions such as predictive analytics and AI teaching bots (Chan and Zary 2019; Liaw et al. 2023). This integration of AI, however, carries several educational and clinical implications.

In contemporary nursing practice, two recent state‐of‐the‐art AI technologies are Predictive AI and Generative AI (Harrington 2023; Lansdowne et al. 2026). Predictive AI uses existing data, such as patient information, to forecast future events or patterns. In contrast, Generative AI (GenAI) draws on learned patterns from large datasets to generate new textual, visual, or audio content when prompted by users (Gunawan, Aungsuroch, Marzilli, et al. 2024; Lansdowne et al. 2026). The advent of GenAI has led to a multitude of new technologies such as Wordtune, Scribe, and Claude AI. Among these, Chat Generative Pretrained Transformer (ChatGPT) is the archetypal GenAI tool, with human‐like intelligence that generates content based on existing data (Tam et al. 2023). Its debut on 30 November 2022 has since popularized AI technologies across academic and clinical settings (Hawk et al. 2025; Tam et al. 2023). Presently, these GenAI tools are easily accessible to the general public, with some offered at no cost. The continued evolution of these tools is expected to refine our interactions with information and technology.

Notwithstanding its promising applicability, controversies surround GenAI use in academia and clinical settings. Judicious use of GenAI supports nursing clinicians in their clinical duties, such as decision‐making, diagnostic queries, and care planning, thereby optimizing work efficiency and clinical outcomes (Nilsen 2024; O'Connor et al. 2023). Moreover, GenAI‐based technologies not only aid students in scholarly research and academic writing but also enable pedagogical approaches such as simulation‐based learning, improving their clinical competencies and development of soft skills such as language and innovative thinking (Castonguay et al. 2023; Fawaz et al. 2025; Sun and Hoelscher 2023). Such potential benefits accordingly form the basis for the advocacy of some nursing educators and clinicians to integrate GenAI into nursing education and clinical practice (Hawk et al. 2025; Park et al. 2024). Despite these benefits, GenAI use is not without concerns. The misuse of GenAI may lead to adverse repercussions that outweigh its benefits, as underscored by moves by some universities to ban students from using them (Deshpande and Szefer 2023; Lund and Wang 2023).

Several reviews have examined GenAI and ChatGPT in nursing education and practice. These reviews focus on their integration and impacts on learning and clinical outcomes (Gunawan, Aungsuroch, and Montayre 2024; Park et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024). Nonetheless, they reflect mainly the perspectives of experts from faculties and the healthcare sector. No comprehensive reviews have explored the experiences of nurses and nursing students with GenAI. It is thus timely to conduct a scoping review to address this gap and gain insights into their experiences. Insights from our review are expected to support interventions that integrate GenAI, enhance user experiences and adoption readiness, and inform future research. As such, this scoping review seeks to identify the extent and type of literature and gain insights into nurses' and nursing students' experiences with using GenAI. Before conducting this review, the principal investigator and first author (M.W.J.W) conducted a preliminary search of the existing literature on this topic to guide the formulation of three research questions that underpin this scoping review:

What is the nature and extent of published evidence examining nurses' and nursing students' experiences of using generative artificial intelligence?

Methods

Design

This scoping review was guided by the five‐stage framework of Arksey and O'malley (2005). This process consists of: (1) identifying research questions; (2) identifying relevant studies; (3) selecting studies; (4) extracting, mapping, and charting data; and (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting findings (Arksey and O'malley 2005). These five stages outline the core principles of a scoping review, and each will be elaborated in detail in the following sections. The Arksey and O'malley (2005) scoping review framework provides a structured, systematic approach to searching and mapping current evidence to synthesize findings, identify areas of knowledge gaps, and clarify key concepts in the emerging field, making it suitable for exploring the new phenomena of interest, which aligns with the focus of this study (Munn et al. 2018).

The sixth step, the consultation phase, was excluded because it is optional in the Arksey and O'malley (2005) framework and it is not mandatory in the PRISMA‐ScR (Tricco et al. 2018). The review aimed to synthesize available empirical evidence on the experiences of nurses and nursing students in the use of GenAI, and the rigor of the method is ensured by the systematic application of the 5‐stage framework. The review was also guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses Statement for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) (Figure 1). The protocol for the scoping review was developed and registered in the Open Science Framework registry on 23 January 2025 (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8YF24).

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for new systematic reviews, which included searches of databases, registers, and other sources.

Search Methods

A preliminary search was conducted across databases such as PROSPERO, CINAHL, Medline, Scopus, and JBI Evidence Synthesis, as well as search engines such as Google Scholar, which provides a supplementary search system (Cantrell et al. 2024; Haddaway et al. 2015). To the best of our knowledge, no existing reviews have been published on our topic of interest. Accordingly, it is timely to undertake a scoping review to explore key concepts, research gaps, and potential research questions regarding not only nurses' and nursing students' experiences with GenAI, but also factors that govern their perceptions (Munn et al. 2018; Peters et al. 2020).

Upon consultation with the University library information specialist, our literature search proceeded on 31 July 2025 across four major electronic databases: CINAHL, Medline (Ovid), Scopus, and Web of Science. ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global was also searched for gray literature, such as dissertations and theses, and supplemented by hand searching Google Scholar and the reference lists of the retrieved papers. This is to retrieve all possible available literature on this topic to ensure a comprehensive review. Our search period of interest spanned from January 2020 to July 2025. Publications from 2020 were included to capture the substantial developments in GenAI that unfolded before ChatGPT's public debut, notably the rapid evolution of machine learning algorithms, especially neural networks, and the emergence of foundation models (Ferrara 2024; Waqas et al. 2023). The inclusion of papers up to 2025 aims to examine contemporary study findings. Initial search keywords were identified based on our preliminary database search and reading of relevant literature. A faculty librarian verified and validated the proposed keywords and search strategies for each database to ensure accuracy. Boolean Operators (AND/OR), truncation symbols (*), and quotes around keywords were used to expand the search. Table 1 presents the search terms and strategies.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The population, concept, and context (PCC) framework guided the formulation of the key search terms and selection criteria of our scoping review (Peters et al. 2020). The ‘population’ of interest comprised nurses (regardless of career stage, level, or clinical specialization) and nursing students (regardless of academic level or year of study). The ‘concept’ related to the nurses' and nursing students' experiences, perceptions, attitudes, views, or opinions on the use of GenAI. Lastly, the ‘context’ related to primary studies in English, in peer‐reviewed scholarly journals or gray literature (such as dissertations or theses), that examined nurses working in any setting or nursing students at any academic level worldwide. Eligible records were screened against the inclusion and exclusion criteria as guided by the PCC framework.

Search Outcomes

The 1831 papers from our preliminary search across five databases were imported into EndNote 21, and 756 duplicates were removed. The rest were exported to Covidence, an online review software for screening. Both authors independently conducted the title‐ and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 991 irrelevant papers. Inter‐rater reliability for the title and abstract screening stage was evaluated using Cohen's kappa, yielding substantial agreement (κ = 0.83) and strong consistency between the authors. Full texts of the remaining 84 papers were then independently reviewed by the authors (M.W.J.W & A.H.T.T) based on the eligibility criteria for inclusion, and disagreements were resolved by consensus of these, 65 papers were excluded for the following reasons (Figure 1: PRISMA‐ScR diagram), whereas 19 papers were included. Another 11 papers were retrieved from Google Scholar and hand‐searching of references, where only 1 met the inclusion criteria upon full‐text screening. This led to the final inclusion of 20 papers for quality appraisal.

Quality Appraisal

Evaluating the quality of the included studies is optional in scoping reviews (Arksey and O'malley 2005). Nonetheless, both authors opted to do so because quality appraisal is critical to enhancing the transparency and rigorousness of this study (Daudt et al. 2013; Woo et al. 2023). Critical appraisal of the included studies was performed using three Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists for cross‐sectional studies (N = 7), quasi‐experimental studies (N = 2), and qualitative research (N = 11) (Joanna Briggs Institute 2020). The first author (M.W.J.W) assessed the risk of bias for all 20 papers, while the second author (A.H.T.T) counterchecked them for accuracy. The critical appraisal scores for the included studies are presented in Table 2. No papers were excluded.

Data Analysis and Synthesis

Data analysis of the 20 included studies was underpinned by the six‐stage thematic analysis framework of Braun and Clarke (2006). In stage one, the authors (M.W.J.W & A.H.T.T) read the papers iteratively to become familiar with the data. In stage two, M.W.J.W. generated initial codes by identifying pertinent findings and common patterns of meaning across the papers. In stage three, provisional subthemes were developed by identifying shared patterns of meaning across the codes. Stage four involved iterative review of the themes against the coded data set (Level 1) and the complete data set (Level 2), enabling comparison of findings across the included studies to examine relationships and inconsistencies. In stage five, the authors worked collectively to refine, finalize, and name the themes. Lastly, in stage six, findings from the 20 studies were narratively synthesized by integrating thematic interpretation with evidence mapping under three overarching themes. Both authors independently conducted the thematic analysis, followed by detailed discussions to share their thoughts about the thematic process, resolve disagreements, and finalize the themes. These critical steps support reflexivity and rigor (Braun and Clarke 2021; Tricco et al. 2018). NVivo 15 was used to perform coding and analysis.

Results

A total of 20 studies were included in this scoping review of these, most (N = 19) were retrieved through electronic database search, while one was retrieved through forward and backward citation searching. The selection stage is systematically presented in the PRISMA flow chart (Figure 1). All included papers (N = 20) were primary studies published in peer‐reviewed journal articles. The characteristics of the included articles are presented in Table 3. Although dissertations or theses (N = 25) of empirical studies were considered for inclusion, all were excluded after screening and found unsuitable.

Study Participants and Settings

All 20 eligible papers involved an aggregate of 3851 participants. Fourteen studies whose population of interest was nursing students had 2578 participants. The sample sizes of individual papers ranged from 12 to 1788 students. Most studies (N = 11) were conducted in a single setting, while three studies were conducted across multiple settings (Luo et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2025; Salama et al. 2025). As for educational settings, most studies (N = 9) recruited university students, while three recruited students from other higher education settings (Benfatah et al. 2024; Gunawan, Aungsuroch, Marzilli, et al. 2024; Han et al. 2025). One study was conducted across multiple educational institutions nationwide, including universities and colleges (Luo et al. 2023; Salama et al. 2025). Conversely, among the six studies whose population of interest was nurses, the total number of participants was 1273. The sample sizes of individual papers ranged from 24 to 330 nurses. All six studies involved recruiting nurses across multiple healthcare settings.

Geographical Locations

Of 20 papers included in this review, most (N = 13) were conducted within the continent of Asia, which consists of China (N = 4), Saudi Arabia (N = 2), Taiwan (N = 2), Turkey (N = 2), the United Arab Emirates (N = 1), South Korea (N = 1), and Indonesia (N = 1). The remaining seven papers were from Spain (N = 2), the United States (N = 2), Australia (N = 1), Morocco (N = 1), and Palestine (N = 1). These figures highlight vast regions that have not been studied further, underscoring the significant lack of empirical research in this emerging area of interest.

Type of Research Methods and Study Characteristics

In exploring nurses' and nursing students' experiences with GenAI, 20 studies employed diverse methodologies, with close to equal numbers of quantitative (N = 9) and qualitative papers (N = 11) (Table 3). Most nurse‐related studies (N = 4) have quantitatively examined nurses' experiences with GenAI. In contrast, the remaining study was qualitative and offered only limited insights into nurses' experiences, as it focused on consolidating experiences across various healthcare professionals. Conversely, most nursing student‐related studies (N = 9) have quantitatively explored students' experiences.

The qualitative studies (N = 11) yielded richer data, despite their smaller sample sizes. Of these 10 qualitative studies, most (91%, N = 10) adopted a generic descriptive design, while one employed a descriptive phenomenological design (N = 1). Data collection methods included physical or online semi‐structured interviews (N = 4), focus group discussions (N = 3), video‐recorded interviews with asynchronous online discussion forums (N = 1), open‐ended questionnaires (N = 2), and reflective essays (N = 1).

Conversely, the quantitative studies (N = 9) yielded relatively sparse data, despite their larger sample sizes. Of these nine quantitative studies, most (78%, N = 7) adopted a cross‐sectional descriptive design, while the others (N = 2) employed a quasi‐experimental design. Data‐collection methods consist of: surveys using validated questionnaires such as the TAME‐ChatGPT questionnaire (N = 2), the General Attitudes to Artificial Intelligence Scale (N = 1), and the AI Awareness‐Demand Questionnaire (N = 1); surveys using researchers' self‐constructed questionnaires (N = 4); and reflective essays (N = 1).

The studies have mostly focused on either the nurses' or nursing students' perceptions of using ChatGPT (N = 14). One paper examined nurses' perceptions of using a ChatGPT‐powered AI‐Chatbot (Makhlouf et al. 2024). Six papers examined users' perceptions of using DeepFakes (Martínez et al. 2024), ChatGAi and Ernie Bot (Kong et al. 2024), Large Language Models (Ma et al. 2025), and general GenAI (Han et al. 2025; Summers et al. 2024; Tong et al. 2025).

Thematic Presentation of Findings

The findings of all included quantitative and qualitative studies (N = 20) will be summarized in narrative texts and presented in themes to address research questions 2 and 3. Thematic analysis of the findings revealed that nurses and nursing students' experiences of using GenAI resulted in the emergence of three themes: (1) perceived attitude towards using GenAI, (2) perceived norms of using GenAI, and (3) perceived ability to use GenAI. The three themes will be discussed in the next sections.

Perceived Attitude Towards Using GenAI

The first theme examines the nurses' and nursing students' attitudes towards the use of GenAI, which constituted a critical area of their overall experiences. Among the nursing student‐related studies (N = 14), more than half (N = 9) reported their mixed attitudes towards GenAI (Ahmed et al. 2025; Gunawan, Aungsuroch, and Montayre 2024; Han et al. 2025; Hawk et al. 2025; Martínez et al. 2024; Salama et al. 2025; Shin et al. 2023; Summers et al. 2024; Tong et al. 2025). This reflected the ambivalence among a subset of students who were mindful of GenAI benefits and the risks associated with their misuse. Conversely, the other studies (N = 6) reported the students' perceptions of the benefits of using GenAI and their positive attitudes (Benfatah et al. 2024; Gonzalez‐Garcia et al. 2025; Karaçay and Yaşar 2024; Kong et al. 2024; Luo et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2025). On the other hand, among the nurse‐related studies (N = 5), the majority (N = 4) found that nurses who were cognizant of the benefits of GenAI tended to exhibit positive attitudes (Alanzi 2023; Lin et al. 2024; Makhlouf et al. 2024; Tuncer and Tuncer 2024). One study reported that nurses' anxiety, perceived risks, and low socio‐technological impacts from GenAI were associated with lower satisfaction (Chang et al. 2024).

Perceived Benefits of Using GenAI

This subtheme is further represented by two categories that explore how nurses and nursing students viewed the benefits of GenAI from both educational and clinical perspectives.

GenAI as an Effective Assistive Learning Tool

Nursing students have generally recognized the promising potential of GenAI‐powered software to support their educational journey by enhancing academic productivity and performance, as delineated across 12 studies. Eight studies found that nurses' and students' positive attitudes stemmed from the ability of GenAI tools to guide and support them in their academic undertakings and scholarly research (Ahmad et al. 2023; Gonzalez‐Garcia et al. 2025; Gunawan, Aungsuroch, and Montayre 2024; Han et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2025; Salama et al. 2025; Summers et al. 2024; Tong et al. 2025). Moreover, five qualitative studies further posited that some students attributed improved efficiency, productivity, and timesaving in academic writing to the user‐friendliness and remarkable speeds of GenAI (Ahmed et al. 2025; Gunawan, Aungsuroch, and Montayre 2024; Han et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2025; Summers et al. 2024). Two studies revealed how students shared additional benefits of using GenAI, having been convinced of the functionalities of the tools to support their learning with their ability to check for grammatical errors in academic writing, generate definitions and simplify abstract concepts through providing clear explanations and supportive visual aids which leads to them perceiving Gen AI tools to be more effective than traditional textbooks (Han et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2025).

Given their ability to generate instant responses, GenAIs are also well received by nursing students as a valuable educational resource to complement their learning, as reported across 11 studies. This was demonstrated by nursing students who turned to GenAI tools for consultation, highlighting several benefits, as reflected across five studies. Such benefits include students being fascinated by the ability of the GenAI system to generate comprehensive responses to address their questions (Han et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2023) and to be demonstrably accurate as scholarly resources (Hawk et al. 2025). Others have opined that GenAI‐generated outputs not only support them in better understanding concepts by summarizing learning points (Gonzalez‐Garcia et al. 2025) but also provide model answers to confirm their understanding of the learning (Hawk et al. 2025; Karaçay and Yaşar 2024; Summers et al. 2024). According to Shin et al. (2023), these benefits were further augmented by the increasing accessibility of the GenAI tools. Moreover, many students appreciated GenAI's ability to provide alternative learning perspectives. In this regard, the opportunity to critically analyze and appraise the reliability of academic sources, which enhanced their analytical and critical thinking skills, was highly valued by nursing students (Karaçay and Yaşar 2024; Summers et al. 2024) and nurses (Tong et al. 2025). This finding was paralleled by Makhlouf et al. (2024), who reported that nurses perceived ChatGPT‐powered AI Chatbot as superior to traditional lectures in improving nursing students' critical thinking skills.

The pedagogical utility of GenAI has also been evidently demonstrated by its progressive deployment in nursing curricula. To facilitate discussions during self‐directed learning group activities, various GenAI tools ranging from the combination of ChatGAi and Ernie Bots to ChatGPT were integrated into classroom lessons for nursing students, many of whom perceived this to foster better teamwork and collaboration while reducing the intensity of their lessons (Kong et al. 2024; Karaçay and Yaşar 2024). Benfatah et al. (2024) further highlighted that some students advocated incorporating ChatGPT into simulation‐based learning class activities, where GenAI‐enabled engagement with virtual patients was deemed to improve their interaction skills and enhance their overall clinical learning experiences. Likewise, Chang et al. (2024) observed that nurses who were cognizant of ChatGPT's benefits were more likely to use it for digital self‐directed learning.

Gen AI as an Effective Clinical Supporting Tool

The benefits of using the Gen AI system to drive improvements in clinical training and practices, as perceived by nursing students and nurses, are documented across 8 studies. Martínez et al. (2024) reported that nursing students who have used DeepFake, a GenAI system, found it saved time and resources. It facilitated nursing care, supported staff training, and simplified diagnoses. Three studies noted that nursing students believed GenAI systems offered benefits and viewed them as valuable tools for ethical decision‐making and clinical reasoning. They also supported improvements in patient safety practices in clinical settings, such as performing precise medication dose calculations. Other benefits included handling routine administrative duties and supporting research initiatives for better patient outcomes (Han et al. 2025; Shin et al. 2023; Summers et al. 2024). Nurses also recognized benefits in integrating ChatGPT to improve nursing care, patient safety, and clinical outcomes (Makhlouf et al. 2024; Tuncer and Tuncer 2024). Further, nurses and students described Gen AI tools as user‐friendly resources for retrieving detailed information and enhancing decision‐making and problem‐solving. These systems assisted with nursing skills like health assessment and clinical diagnosis, and improved medical knowledge through explanations of diseases and anatomy (Han et al. 2025; Makhlouf et al. 2024; Salama et al. 2025). Additional reported benefits included improved online communication with patients, reduced care burdens through automated medical recommendations, efficient documentation via keywords, and better nurse–patient interactions through multilingual support (Alanzi 2023).

Perceived Risks of Using GenAI

This subtheme is represented by two categories that focus on identifying various factors from the perspectives of nurses and nursing students that undermine their attitude towards GenAI.

Concerns Over GenAI Misuses

As described in six studies, nursing students highlighted various concerns surrounding the academic misuse of GenAI tools. Some students have expressed caution over the excessive use of GenAI, which might lead to their over‐dependence in the future, resulting in loss of critical thinking as revealed in five studies (Ahmed et al. 2025; Han et al. 2025; Hawk et al. 2025; Summers et al. 2024; Tong et al. 2025). Expanding on these concerns, two studies have highlighted the potential for academic misuse of ChatGPT, which could lead to dishonesty, such as plagiarism (Gunawan, Aungsuroch, Marzilli, et al. 2024; Han et al. 2025). Likewise, Summers et al. (2024) explained that students were concerned about ethical transgressions involving GenAI, such as contract cheating, plagiarism, and academic misconduct, and many felt that misusing GenAI as an expedient shortcut to graduation without completing coursework themselves would significantly devalue their academic credentials. Additionally, a minority of students perceived the use of GenAI‐enabled language translation in written assignments by less fluent peers as an unfair violation of academic integrity (Summers et al. 2024).

To address such breaches, nurses and nursing students have shared their views on how to better integrate GenAI tools in the future, as reflected across four studies. Firstly, they recommended that faculties set out appropriate guidelines on plagiarism and educate students on the responsible and ethical use of GenAI, rather than prohibiting it (Ahmed et al. 2025; Gunawan, Aungsuroch, Marzilli, et al. 2024; Tong et al. 2025). Secondly, to uphold academic integrity, they recommended that faculties should invest in building robust AI‐detection systems to ensure originality (Ahmed et al. 2025). Lastly, to ensure equitable GenAI use, they felt that all students should be granted equal access to GenAI and that a fair marking system should be used, with assessments written by students using GenAI graded differently from those not using it (Summers et al. 2024).

Another concern raised by nurses and nursing students surrounds the malicious misuse of GenAI tools. Tuncer and Tuncer (2024) found that some students have viewed the use of GenAI and ChatGPT as dangerous. Other challenges expressed by students were also highlighted by Martínez et al. (2024), including the malicious use of DeepFakes, such as information hoaxes, cyberbullying, identity theft, and breaches of patients' privacy and confidentiality.

Concerns of GenAI Inadequacies

The accuracy and reliability of GenAI‐generated content were identified as the most critical inadequacy by nurses and nursing students. Three studies reported low reliability in GenAI outputs (Ahmed et al. 2025; Gunawan, Aungsuroch, and Montayre 2024; Shin et al. 2023). Recent studies (Han et al. 2025; Hawk et al. 2025; Karaçay and Yaşar 2024; Ma et al. 2025; Salama et al. 2025) show that, despite GenAI's value as a learning resource, students remain cautious and mistrustful. They also view GenAI as cumbersome and time‐consuming because they must spend additional time verifying the credibility of the output through scholarly research (Hawk et al. 2025; Karaçay and Yaşar 2024), driven by a fear of penalties for inaccuracies (Ma et al. 2025).

Another inadequacy of GenAI is its inability to support nursing care, as shown by two scenarios across four studies. Martínez et al. (2024) detailed nursing students' cautious stance on the accuracy of DeepFake‐generated outputs for health applications. Three qualitative studies indicated that nursing students believed GenAI tools lacked the fundamental values of healthcare and the human proficiency required for nursing, and concluded that they could not replace nurses in delivering holistic patient care (Gunawan, Aungsuroch, Marzilli, et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2025; Summers et al. 2024). Nursing students also contended that GenAI could not accurately interpret patients' non‐verbal cues and stressed that therapeutic communication requires physical interaction with nurses, not GenAI (Gunawan, Aungsuroch, and Montayre 2024; Salama et al. 2025; Summers et al. 2024; Gunawan, Aungsuroch, Marzilli, et al. 2024; Salama et al. 2025; Summers et al. 2024).

Perceived Norms of Using GenAI Tools

The second theme examines the impact of subjective norms on nursing students' perceptions and adoption of GenAI. Two studies found that social media was an influential normative factor in shaping and amplifying the benefits and risks of GenAI, critically shaping nursing students' beliefs and decisions to employ such tools (Ma et al. 2025; Martínez et al. 2024). Peer pressure represented another normative factor. Ma et al. (2025) reported that some nursing students were more motivated to adopt GenAI upon noticing practical applications, as well as the value and success of using it as showcased by their classmates, faculty staff, and friends, while others exhibited the ‘fear of missing out (FOMO)’ on the potential benefits of such tools. Conversely, institutional disapproval from faculties, teachers, medical research centres, and hospitals has deterred the use of GenAI (Ma et al. 2025). Despite the organization openly enforcing a ban on use of GenAI, some students still use it (Ma et al. 2025). Nonetheless, acknowledging the growing acceptance of GenAI, students have opined that faculty should stay abreast of contemporary educational practices by integrating GenAI into their curricula to enhance the quality of teaching and learning (Gunawan, Aungsuroch, Marzilli, et al. 2024; Han et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2025).

Perceived Ability to Use GenAI Tools

The third theme, as synthesized from eight studies, focuses on how nurses' and nursing students' perceived capability with GenAI tools influenced their acceptance and intention to use them. Seven studies reported nursing students' positive perception towards user‐friendly designs of GenAI tools, which crucially promoted the ease and convenience of adoption among users (Ahmed et al. 2025; Han et al. 2025; Karaçay and Yaşar 2024; Lin et al. 2024; Luo et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2025; Shin et al. 2023). Cost was another pivotal determinant of students' access to and use of GenAI tools (Han et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2025), with some reporting that access at no or minimal cost had spurred their adoption, and vice versa (Ma et al. 2025). Accordingly, many emphasized the need for faculty financial support to subscribe to GenAI tools (Han et al. 2025). Conversely, barriers to the use of GenAI tools among nursing students included limited access due to geographical restrictions and digital illiteracy (Ma et al. 2025; Kong et al. 2024). Digital illiteracy refers to students' perceived deficiency in understanding the functionality of GenAI tools, which impedes their use (Ma et al. 2025). Kong et al. (2024) found that some nursing students gradually grew to embrace GenAI tools (ChatGAi and Ernie Bots), despite their initial resistance owing to technological unfamiliarity.

Discussion

This paper is the first review study to explore and gain a collective insight into the experiences of nurses and nursing students using GenAI. The increasing pervasiveness of GenAI presents a compulsion to comprehend and explain the experiences stemming from behaviors using such emergent technology in the interest of nursing education and practice (Ajzen 2020; Park et al. 2024). As such, our scoping review serves as an important focus of research, providing existential evidence on the experiences of using GenAI among nurses and nursing students and offering insights into the factors that influence these experiences.

The review reveals growing interest in empirical research on nurses' perceptions and experiences with GenAI. Most included papers (N = 11) were qualitative studies, which is unsurprising given the suitability of qualitative methodologies for exploring nurses' and nursing students' experiences with emerging GenAI‐enabled technologies (Jeanfreau and Jack Jr 2010). Conversely, the number of quantitative studies (N = 9) retrieved for this review indicates value in quantitative approaches for examining experiences. Because GenAI is an emerging and evolving field, future studies should incorporate mixed‐methods research designs, which are currently lacking. Both approaches provide scale and depth to deepen understanding of nurses' and nursing students' experiences using GenAI. This is especially useful while this area remains a work in progress. Nonetheless, much remains to be explored regarding the experiences of nurses and nursing students using GenAI, as gaps remain in the literature. Most empirical studies have focused on nursing students' perceptions and experiences with ChatGPT for learning and assessment, leaving few studies examining practicing nurses and other GenAI tools. Research addressing these gaps could support the development of GenAI tailored to nursing practice and education. Regarding nurses' experiences with GenAI, it may be useful to explore differences between early‐career and senior nurses, as generational differences may affect acceptance of GenAI, as with any other technology. Research on attitudinal experiences with GenAI could also examine the moderating effects of demographic factors, including age, gender, education level, nursing experience, nursing role, and work culture.

This review also highlighted that nurses' experiences with GenAI were mainly positive, while nursing students reported mixed perceptions, though more positive. Studies have shown that a positive attitude is a key determinant of higher acceptance of GenAI among users (Adiguzel et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2024). As revealed by this review, their positive attitude stemmed from the perceived value that GenAI had served as an assistive learning and clinical support tool, enhancing their productivity and performance in their studies and clinical practices. This is in line with earlier studies that observed a relationship between perceived benefits of GenAI and positive attitudes towards it, contributing to increased acceptance and adoption of GenAI (Tiwari et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2021). Having been convinced of its usefulness, accessibility, and convenience, nurses and nursing students in this review leveraged GenAI to generate information and manage searching and retrieving information for clinical work and academic assignments, respectively. These findings echoed previous studies, which also observed that students actively sought GenAI as a source of academic support enabling individualized learning experiences and better learning outcomes (Abujaber et al. 2023; Sun and Hoelscher 2023).

This review and previous studies also collectively suggest that both nurses and nursing students found GenAI a reliable and efficient tool, and support the idea that, when effectively integrated into both nursing education and clinical practice, can generate nurses' and nursing students' overall positive experiences. Given the increased popularity of GenAI and its role in educational and clinical settings, it is impractical to ban its use. Hence, contemporary strategies call on faculties and healthcare institutions to consider incorporating GenAI tools, such as ChatGPT, Microsoft Co‐Pilot, and Claude AI, into classroom learning and clinical education. These recommendations concurred with those of previous studies (Ahmad et al. 2023; Hassan Mekawy et al. 2020). Educational and healthcare organizations could collaborate with digital vendors to curate ‘tailored’ or ‘protected’ versions of GenAI tools, integrating them into routine coursework activities and assessments for information search, improvement of sentence structure, or correction of language errors.

Conversely, this review also reports several factors that undermine nurses' and nursing students' attitudinal beliefs towards the use of GenAI. As highlighted in this review and consistent with the literature (Abujaber et al. 2023), their disinclination is driven by concerns about the accuracy and reliability of GenAI‐generated content, overreliance, plagiarism, academic integrity, ethical implications, and the repercussions associated with its abuse and misuse. While it is reassuring that both groups showed greater awareness of these risks, these findings could indicate reduced exploration of GenAI, thereby reducing overreliance and misuse. Nonetheless, this cautious attitude reflects disciplined professionals and critical consumers of GenAI (Castonguay et al. 2023). However, Adler‐Milstein et al. (2024) argued that demonstrating awareness and caution regarding potential misuse is generally insufficient and increasingly challenging. Hence, along with other studies, this review proposes that faculties and healthcare institutions curate appropriate training programs to enhance nurses' and nursing students' digital awareness and competencies in the ethical, responsible, and reasonable use of GenAI tools to achieve beneficial integration (El‐Sayed et al. 2025; Fernandes et al. 2026).

This review revealed that normative beliefs, shaped by social media, peers, and organizational pressure, are key determinants of nursing students' experiences and intention to adopt GenAI. Shahzad et al. (2025) asserted that social media platforms have transformed educational approaches and how students interact with media‐generated information and their environment; therefore, they are influential in shaping beliefs. This review shows that nursing students reported positive and negative social media messages shaped their perceptions and commitment to using GenAI. Given the expected increase in GenAI use in the coming years, incorporating GenAI into nursing education and practice has become crucial to nurses' and students' learning processes and daily activities (Alqahtani et al. 2023), underscoring the role of social media platforms in educating users on safe and responsible use. The review also highlighted peer pressure as a critical determinant of their perception and adoption of GenAI, resonating with previous studies (Abdaljaleel et al. 2024; Bond et al. 2018) and reinforcing concerns that GenAI is increasingly popular and ubiquitous, leaving individuals worried about feeling left out or failing to meet standard practice. As nursing students became more convinced of the benefits of GenAI, they were concerned that not using it might compromise their academic performance. Accordingly, peer influence cannot be overlooked. The review also observed that institutional disapproval of GenAI use deterred some students' intention to use it, while others continued to engage with it. Such paradoxical behavior can be explained by the ‘forbidden fruit effect’ which implies that restricting something can heighten curiosity and desire, especially when the perceived benefits of using GenAI outweigh the risks and consequences (Spindel and Ackerman 2024). In this context, openly enforcing the ban on GenAI can be counterproductive, as it may prompt them to use it to satisfy their curiosity and desires (Khalaf 2025). As GenAI has been embedded in daily life, this review supports the argument that nurse leaders in educational and healthcare institutions must acknowledge and accept its use and effectively use it to support the delivery of nursing education and clinical practice.

The review further observed that nursing students' perceptions of their ability to use GenAI are crucial in determining their intention to use. Cost is a decisive factor for nursing students in determining whether they can access GenAI, the type of GenAI they can access (free or subscription mode), and, eventually, their intention to use it. Such findings are consistent with previous studies (Al‐Abdullatif and Alsubaie 2024; Wutz et al. 2023). Kim et al. (2007) argued that evaluating one's willingness to adopt technology involves weighing the trade‐off between perceived benefits (usefulness and enjoyment) and perceived drawbacks and sacrifices (such as costs and risks). Lastly, this review observed that digital literacy is critical for enabling or preventing nursing students' ability to engage with GenAI. These findings concurred with those of earlier studies, which argued that possessing relevant digital competencies is the key enabling factor for students to use GenAI (Ciampa et al. 2023; Martzoukou et al. 2024). As digitalization continues to evolve, nursing leaders in educational and healthcare institutions must incorporate digital literacy education and training for both nurses and students to strengthen their technological knowledge and directly address this barrier.

Through an overarching analysis of this review, it was possible to postulate that the findings and discussion may be mapped to Ajzen's theory of planned behavior (Ajzen 1991, 2020). Figure 2 illustrates the mapping of factors and experiences of nurses and nursing students regarding the use of GenAI. The mapping suggests that the findings from our review could be used to predict nurses' and nursing students' behavioral intentions and actions regarding GenAI, which can help shape perceptions of GenAI in nursing education and practice. As such, the theory suggests that the use of GenAI is intended and that nurses and nursing students need to be aware of the consequences of their intentions when using GenAI.

Limitations do exist in this review. First, only five electronic databases were used to search for peer‐reviewed journal papers and gray literature. Using more databases would have generated a more detailed search and may have retrieved other relevant studies. Second, while this review aimed to include findings from primary studies' gray literature, none were included because they were irrelevant (N = 25). Third, given the search criteria applied only to peer‐reviewed English‐language papers published between January 2020 and July 2025, studies published in other languages or outside this period could be excluded. Finally, while this review summarizes the most recent research, the results are difficult to apply to healthcare settings due to varied study designs, outcome measures, and contextual factors. Clinicians and practitioners should carefully interpret the results and consider how they fit into their own practices. Future studies should bridge the gap between empirical findings and practical clinical recommendations.

Relevance to Clinical Practice

The findings of this scoping review highlight key implications for clinical nursing practice, particularly in shaping regulatory policies and organizational culture to support the effective integration of GenAI. As catalysts of change, nursing leaders in both educational and healthcare institutions are pivotal in shaping organizational culture by leading and driving initiatives to integrate GenAI. Nursing leaders in both educational and healthcare institutions play a pivotal role by establishing clear policies and ethical guidelines, communicating expectations to foster responsible use. In education, they should lead training initiatives and provide resources to strengthen nurses' and students' knowledge, competencies, and ethical awareness. In practice, nursing leaders must work closely with stakeholders to integrate GenAI into workflows and streamline processes to improve clinical care and service quality. Finally, ongoing dialogue through forums, retreats, and feedback sessions is essential to solicit nurses' and students' constructive feedback about the adoption of GenAI, including their challenges, to inform targeted interventions that drive meaningful adoption of GenAI.

Conclusion

This scoping review mapped the empirical literature on nurses' and nursing students' experiences with GenAI in nursing education and clinical practice, and the factors affecting their adoption, addressing a notable gap in the literature. This review clarifies the experiential landscape of GenAI in nursing by offering insights into how the theory of planned behavior (TPB), underpinned by attitudinal, normative, and control beliefs, shapes users' experiences with GenAI and is closely linked to their intentions to use these technologies in educational and clinical contexts. As GenAI becomes increasingly popularized, gaining collective insights into this critical information provides a structured overview of the factors influencing GenAI engagement among key nursing stakeholders. This serves as a valuable evidence base to inform the development of institutional policies, educational strategies, and leadership initiatives to drive effective and responsible integration of GenAI in educational and clinical settings. Doing so would advance and enhance the quality of nursing education and patient care.
