Efficient Learning of POMDPs with Known Observation Model in Average-Reward Setting
Alessio Russo, Alberto Maria Metelli, Marcello Restelli

Abstract
Dealing with Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes is notably a challenging task. We face an average-reward infinite-horizon POMDP setting with an unknown transition model, where we assume the knowledge of the observation model. Under this assumption, we propose the Observation-Aware Spectral (OAS) estimation technique, which enables the POMDP parameters to be learned from samples collected using a belief-based policy. Then, we propose the OAS-UCRL algorithm that implicitly balances the exploration-exploitation trade-off following the optimism in the face of uncertainty principle. The algorithm runs through episodes of increasing length. For each episode, the optimal belief-based policy of the estimated POMDP interacts with the environment and collects samples that will be used in the next episode by the OAS estimation procedure to compute a new estimate of the POMDP parameters. Given the estimated model, an optimization oracle computes the new optimal policy. We show the consistency of the OAS procedure, and we prove a regret guarantee of order 𝒪 ⁡ ( T ​ log ⁡ ( T ) ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T\log(T)}) for the proposed OAS-UCRL algorithm. We compare against the oracle playing the optimal stochastic belief-based policy and show the efficient scaling of our approach with respect to the dimensionality of the state, action, and observation space. We finally conduct numerical simulations to validate and compare the proposed technique with other baseline approaches.

Introduction

In Reinforcement Learning (RL)
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
)
, an agent interacts with an unknown or partially known environment to maximize the expected long-term reward. This class of approaches has been successfully used in a large variety of problems
(
Mnih et al. 2016
;
Schulman et al. 2017
;
Casas 2017
)
. Most existing results consider learning under
fully observable
systems where the underlying state of the system is visible to the learner. However, many real-world problems present a
partially observable
nature, further complicating the task since an agent needs to implement a memory mechanism and keep a belief over the system state. Some applications include autonomous driving
(
Levinson et al. 2011
)
, medical diagnosis
(
Hauskrecht and Fraser 2000
)
, resource allocation
(
Gilbert and Clark 2005
)
.

Dealing with Partially Observable MDPs (POMDP)
(
Sondik 1978
)
is both statistically and computationally intractable in general
(
Mossel and Roch 2005
;
Mundhenk et al. 2000
)
.

Some recent works have focused on considering a class of POMDPs where sample-efficient algorithms can be devised.

Azizzadenesheli, Lazaric, and
Anandkumar (2016)
and
Guo, Doroudi, and Brunskill (2016)
extend to the decision-making problem the use of techniques based on
spectral decomposition
(SD), which have been initially devised for estimation of latent variable models such as HMMs
(
Anandkumar et al. 2014
)
.
Jin et al. (2020)
presents a sample-efficient algorithm for undercomplete POMDPs, where the number of observations is larger than the number of states. All these classes fall under the richer family of
weakly-revealing
POMDPs, which have been lately defined in the work of
Liu et al. (2022a)
.

This paper considers a POMDP setting where the
observation model is known
, and the agent needs to learn the
unknown transition model
. The assumption of having knowledge of some portions of the system has been previously used in problems with partial observability. Examples are the works on Latent Bandits
(
Maillard and Mannor 2014
;
Hong et al. 2020a
)
and Switching Latent Bandits
(
Author 2023
)
, where the reward distributions of each bandit are known, but the identity of the active bandit (latent state) is hidden to the learner.

In many real-world applications, situations where the observation model is known emerge. This happens, for example, when the learning phase of the process is decoupled into an offline phase (used for learning the observation model) and an online phase where the knowledge of the dynamics of the underlying state is hidden. Examples of this type are applications that initially learn through simulators
(
Thananjeyan et al. 2021
;
Marco et al. 2017
)
.
Furthermore, this setting is of practical interest in non-stationary environments where changes do not affect the entire environment but only the dynamics of the system. In these cases, previous knowledge of the observation model can be retained and employed for faster learning of the system dynamics. Analogously, this condition is applicable in the field of
transfer learning
where the knowledge of some domain can be used to help learning on a similar domain.

Contributions.

The contributions of the paper are summarized as follows:

•

We propose a consistent procedure that estimates the transition model of a POMDP in the average-reward infinite-horizon setting under the assumption of knowing the observation model. In particular, by assuming the ergodicity of the Markov chain induced by the belief-based policy used to collect samples, we prove that our OAS procedure provides an estimation rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
while also showing a desirable dependency on the problem parameters;

•

We plug this approach into a regret minimization algorithm and, by exploiting the obtained convergence result, we establish that our OAS-UCRL algorithm achieves a regret bound of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)

under the set of stochastic belief-based policies;

•

We provide numerical simulations validating both our OAS estimation procedure and showing the effectiveness of the OAS-UCRL algorithm over state-of-the-art approaches.

Related Works

Learning in the fully observable RL setting has been extensively studied in the last few years. Some works considered the episodic finite-horizon setting
(
Azar, Osband, and Munos 2017
;
Jin et al. 2018
;
Zanette et al. 2020
)
, while a different line of works studied the non-episodic undiscounted setting
(
Bartlett and Tewari 2009
;
Jaksch, Ortner, and Auer 2010
;
Ortner and Ryabko 2012
)
. Differently, the study of the partially observable RL setting has been relatively less explored.

Learning in the general POMDP setting is known to be statistically hard
(
Krishnamurthy, Agarwal, and Langford 2016
)
, and the hard instances are generally those where observations do not contain useful information for identifying the system dynamics.

Efficient Learning in POMDPs.

Some recent works have focused on specific POMDP classes where the observation model is full-rank or have considered its robust version called
\alpha
-weakly revealing where
\alpha
intuitively quantifies the amount of information that observations provide when inferring the latent states. These conditions rule out the pathological POMDP instances that can lead to hardness results.

A first line of work focuses on the
episodic
setting
(
Guo, Doroudi, and Brunskill 2016
;
Jin et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2022a
;
Liu et al. 2022b
)
.
Jin et al. (2020)
propose an algorithm with optimal sample complexity
(1/\epsilon^{2})
for finding an
\epsilon
-optimal policy for the undercomplete case, where the number of latent states must not be greater than the number of observations (
S\leq O
), while
Liu et al. (2022a)
present a new generic algorithm based on
Maximum Likelihood Estimation
that reaches a
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{K})
regret for the undercomplete setting, with
K
being the number of episodes. They are also able to handle the more difficult overcomplete case (
S>O
), for which they show a regret of order
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(K^{2/3})
.

A second line of works instead focuses on the
non-episodic
average reward setting
(
Xiong et al. 2022
;
Jafarnia Jahromi, Jain, and Nayyar 2022
;
Azizzadenesheli, Lazaric, and
Anandkumar 2016
)
. Among them,
Xiong et al. (2022)
employ assumptions similar to ours and devise an algorithm alternating exploration and exploitation phases, they make use of spectral decomposition techniques to learn the POMDP parameters from samples collected during the exploration phase. Their algorithm suffers a regret of
\mathcal{O}(T^{2/3})
with respect to the optimal POMDP policy.

Jafarnia Jahromi, Jain, and Nayyar (2022)
propose a posterior sampling-based algorithm for which they prove a Bayesian regret of order
\mathcal{O}(T^{2/3})
under some technical assumptions on the belief state approximation and the transition parameters estimation. Differently,
Azizzadenesheli, Lazaric, and
Anandkumar (2016)
adapt spectral decomposition techniques for learning the POMDP parameters under stochastic memoryless policies and present an algorithm suffering a
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
regret bound. However, they compare against an oracle using the best stochastic memoryless policy, which is weaker than the optimal POMDP oracle used in the previously mentioned works.

For a thorough comparison with the most relevant works, we refer the reader to Table
1
in Appendix
A
.

Settings with Known Observation Model.

Having partial knowledge of the model is a relatively common assumption in RL:
(
Azar, Osband, and Munos 2017
)
for example, assume to know the reward model characterizing the environment. Concerning instead the partial observable setting, this assumption has been largely used in MAB problems, such as Latent Bandits
(
Maillard and Mannor 2014
;
Hong et al. 2020a
)
where the learning agent knows reward distributions of arms conditioned on an unknown discrete latent, or its non-stationary variants
(
Hong et al. 2020b
;
Author 2023
)
where the reward distributions are still known but the latent state keeps changing according to an unknown Markov chain. Analogously to our case, for the POMDP setting,
Jafarnia Jahromi, Jain, and Nayyar (2022)
develop a posterior sampling-based algorithm under the assumption of knowing the observation model.

Preliminaries

In this section, we provide the necessary backgrounds and
notations that will be used throughout the rest of the article.

Controlled Markov Process.

A Controlled Markov Process (CMP) is a tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathbb{T},\mu)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is a finite state space (
|\mathcal{S}|\eqqcolon S
),
\mathcal{A}
is a finite action space (
|\mathcal{A}|\eqqcolon A
),
\mathbb{T}=\{\mathbb{T}_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
is a matrix of dimension
SA\times S
and represents a collection of transition matrices
\mathbb{T}_{a}
with size
S\times S
such that
\mathbb{T}_{a}(\cdot|s)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
denotes the distribution of the next state when the agent takes action
a\in\mathcal{A}
in state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, and
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the distribution of the initial state.
2
2

2

We denote with
\Delta(\mathcal{X})
the simplex over a finite set
\mathcal{X}
.

Policies and State Distributions in CMPs.

A policy
\pi
defines the behavior of an agent interacting in an environment. It is characterized by a sequence of decision rules
\pi:=(\pi_{t})_{t=0}^{\infty}
. Each of them maps a history
h\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
into distributions over actions
\pi_{t}:\mathcal{H}_{t}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A})
such that
\pi_{t}(a|h)
defines the conditional probability of taking action
a\in\mathcal{A}
having observed history
h\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
. We denote as
\mathcal{H}
the space of histories of arbitrary length. When interacting with a CMP the history can be defined as
h:=(s_{j},a_{j})_{j=0}^{t}\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
.

We denote with
\Pi
the set of all the policies. A policy
\pi\in\Pi
interacting with a CMP induces a state visitation distribution
d_{t}^{\pi}(s)\coloneqq P(S_{t}=s|\pi)

(
Puterman 2014
)
, also defined as
d_{t}^{\pi}(s)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}d_{t-1}^{\pi}(a^{\prime},s^{\prime})\mathbb{T}_{a^{\prime}}(s|s^{\prime})
,
where
\bm{d}_{t}^{\pi}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
is the
t
-step action-state visitation distribution and is defined as
d_{t}^{\pi}(a,s)\coloneqq P(A_{t}=a,S_{t}=s|\pi)
. The fixed point of the defined temporal relation (when it exists) is
d_{\infty}^{\pi}(s)\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}d_{t}^{\pi}(s)
and is called
stationary state distribution
. If the limiting distribution
d_{\infty}^{\pi}(s)
exists and
d_{\infty}^{\pi}(s)>0
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, then this distribution is unique. Analogous considerations hold for the stationary action-state distribution
\bm{d}_{\infty}^{\pi}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
.

From now on, we will use vector notation
\bm{d}^{\pi}_{S}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
for the stationary state distribution and
\bm{d}^{\pi}_{AS}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
for the stationary action-state distribution.
3
3

3

The subscript represents the support size of the distribution.

Partially Observable MDP.

A Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)
(
Åström 1965
)
is described by a tuple
\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},\mathbb{T},\mathbb{O},\mu,r)
where
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathbb{T}
,
\mu
are denoted as in the CMP.
\mathcal{O}
denotes the finite space of observation (
|\mathcal{O}|\eqqcolon O
);
\mathbb{O}=\{\mathbb{O}_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
denotes the set of emission matrices of size
O\times S
such that
\mathbb{O}_{a}(\cdot|s)\in\Delta(\mathcal{O})
gives the distribution over observations when the agent takes action
a\in\mathcal{A}
conditioned on the hidden state
s\in\mathcal{S}
;
r:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow[0,1]
is the known reward function that deterministically maps each observation to a finite reward such that
r(o)
is the received reward when the agent observes
o\in\mathcal{O}
.
In a POMDP, states are hidden, and the agent can only see the observations and its actions. The interaction proceeds as follows. At each step
t
, the agent takes an action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
and receives an observation
o_{t}\in\mathcal{O}
conditioned on the hidden state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
according to the law
\mathbb{O}_{a_{t}}(\cdot|s_{t})
. Finally, the action played makes the POMDP transition into a new hidden state
s_{t+1}
according to the distribution
\mathbb{T}_{a_{t}}(\cdot|s_{t})
.

From POMDP to Belief MDP.

By knowing the transition and emission matrices
\mathbb{T}
and
\mathbb{O}
respectively, and from the observed history at time
t-1
,
h_{t-1}:=(a_{j},o_{j})_{j=0}^{t-1}
, it is possible to build a belief vector
b_{t}\in\mathcal{B}
with
\mathcal{B}:=\Delta(\mathcal{S})
defined as a simplex over the state space. At time
t
, we define
b_{t}(s):=P(S_{t}=s|h_{t-1})
. From the agent’s point of view, a POMDP can be seen as a belief MDP
(
Krishnamurthy 2016
)
. The update rule of the belief
b_{t+1}
is determined by Bayes’s theorem as follows:

\displaystyle b_{t+1}(s)=\frac{\sum_{s^{\prime}}b_{t}(s^{\prime})\mathbb{O}_{a_{t}}(O_{t}=o_{t}|S_{t}=s^{\prime})\mathbb{T}_{a_{t}}(s|s^{\prime})}{\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}\mathbb{O}_{a_{t}}(O_{t}=o_{t}|S_{t}=s^{\prime\prime})b_{t}(s^{\prime\prime})}.

(1)

The average reward of the infinite-horizon belief MDP given an initial belief
b\in\mathcal{B}
is defined as:
\rho_{b}^{\pi}:=\lim\sup_{T\to\infty}(1/T)\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}r(o_{t})|b_{1}=b]
. If the underlying MDP is weakly communicating,
Bertsekas (1995)
showed that
\rho^{*}:=\sup_{\pi}\rho_{b}^{\pi}
is independent of the initial belief
b
and the following Bellman optimality equation can be defined:

\displaystyle\rho^{*}+v(b)=\underset{a\in\mathcal{A}}{\max}\;\left[g(b,a)+\int_{\mathcal{B}}P(\,db^{\prime}|b,a)v(b^{\prime})\right],

(2)

where
g(b,a)
represents the expected instantaneous reward obtained when taking action
a\in\mathcal{A}
having belief
b\in\mathcal{B}
such that
g(b,a)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}b(s)\mathbb{O}_{a}(o|s)r(o)
. Instead,
v:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
is the bias function that commonly appears in the average reward MDP setting
(
Mahadevan 1996
)
.

4
Problem Formulation

We consider a non-episodic POMDP setting, as the one described in Section
3
. Specifically, we focus on
undercomplete
POMDPs
(
Jin et al. 2020
)
, where the number of states is less than or equal to the number of observations
S\leq O
. Similarly to
Jafarnia Jahromi, Jain, and Nayyar (2022)
, we assume knowledge of the emission matrices
\mathbb{O}=\{\mathbb{O}_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
, while we learn the transition model
\mathbb{T}=\{\mathbb{T}_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
.

We focus on stochastic belief-based policies which map the space
\mathcal{B}
of belief over the states to a distribution over actions, such that
\pi:\mathcal{B}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A})
. We denote by
\mathcal{P}
the set of all stochastic belief-based policies having probability at least
\iota
(with
\iota>0
) of exploring all actions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\coloneqq\{\pi:\underset{b\in\mathcal{B}}{\min}\>\underset{a\in\mathcal{A}}{\min}\>\pi(a|b)\geq\iota\}.

(3)

In the following, we denote the assumptions that we enforce for our setting.

Assumption 4.1

(
Minimum Value Transition Matrices
)
.

The smallest value appearing in the transition matrices is given by
\epsilon:=\underset{s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}}{\min}\mathbb{T}_{a}(s^{\prime}|s)>0
.

This assumption is necessary in our setting for multiple purposes. From a technical point of view, it ensures geometric ergodicity
(
Krishnamurthy 2016
)
under each policy
\pi
, thus allowing it to reach its stationary distribution exponentially fast. Furthermore,
Xiong et al. (2022)
showed that under this assumption the diameter
D
of the belief MDP can be bounded and this allows Equation (
2
) to be well defined.
Most importantly, this assumption is fundamental in the theoretical analysis to bound the error in the belief computed using the estimated transition matrices, using the result appearing in Proposition
H.3
(see the appendix). Despite seeming a strong assumption, it is satisfied in relevant POMDP applications involving information gathering as detailed in
(
Guo, Doroudi, and Brunskill 2016
)
. Furthermore, this assumption has been commonly used in works facing settings with partial observability
(
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Author 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Xiong et al. 2022
)
.

Assumption 4.2

(
\bm{\alpha}
-weakly Revealing Condition
)
.

There exists
\alpha>0
such that
\underset{a\in\mathcal{A}}{\min}\;\sigma_{S}(\mathbb{O}_{a})\geq\alpha
.

Here, we denote with
\sigma_{S}(\mathbb{O}_{a})
the
S
-th singular value of matrix
\mathbb{O}_{a}
.
This second assumption is related to the identifiability of the POMDP parameters and has been largely used in its
full-rank
version in works using spectral decomposition techniques
(
Azizzadenesheli, Lazaric, and
Anandkumar 2016
;
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Hsu, Kakade, and Zhang 2012
)
. This condition allows excluding hard POMDP instances and has been employed to define the tractable subclass of
weakly revealing
POMDPs
(
Jin et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2022a
;
Liu et al. 2022b
)
(see Section
2
). Moreover, it directly implies that
S\leq O
, which is a common scenario in many real-world applications. Just to name a few, in spoken dialogue systems, the number of observations is much larger than the state (meaning) of the conversation
(
Png, Pineau, and Chaib-Draa 2012
)
; or medical applications where the state (physical condition) of a patient generates a large number of different observations
(
Hauskrecht and Fraser 2000
)
.

Assumption 4.3

(
Policy Set
)
.

The policy
\pi
interacting with the POMDP belongs to
\mathcal{P}
.

This assumption guarantees that each action
a\in\mathcal{A}
is constantly chosen and is commonly used in spectral-based approaches. Indeed, we will see soon that our estimation procedure resembles SD methods.

Azizzadenesheli, Lazaric, and
Anandkumar (2016)
use a similar assumption when defining their policy class. In particular, they focus on a less powerful class that denotes the set of all stochastic memoryless policies having non-zero probabilities for all actions.
4
4

4

When using a memoryless policy, the choice of each action is conditioned only on the last received observation rather than the whole history.

Learning Objective.

Having defined all the relevant elements for our setting, we are ready to reformulate the Bellman equation in (
2
) as follows:

\displaystyle\rho^{*}+v(b)=\underset{\pi\in\mathcal{P}}{\max}\underset{a\sim\pi(\cdot|b)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(b,a)+\int_{\mathcal{B}}P(\,db^{\prime}|b,a)v(b^{\prime})\right],

(4)

where the maximization is over the policy class
\mathcal{P}
. Under Assumption
4.1
, this equation is always verified (see Proposition
H.1
in the appendix).
Determining the optimal policy for the POMDP model is generally computationally intractable
(
Madani 1999
)
. In this work, we do not focus on solving this planning problem for a known model. Instead, we assume access to an optimization oracle capable of solving Equation (
4
) and providing the optimal average reward
\rho^{*}
and the optimal stationary policy
\pi
for a given POMDP instance
\mathcal{Q}
.
Our learning objective is to minimize the total regret after
T
periods. It is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}:=T\rho^{*}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}^{\pi}(o_{t}),

(5)

with
r_{t}^{\pi}(o_{t})
being the reward obtained from the observation received while following policy
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
.

5
OAS Estimation Procedure

The core idea behind the presented approach is to exploit the known relation between the observations and the underlying latent state. In the following, we will use the term ergodic to denote a Markov chain induced by a policy
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
having stationary distribution
\bm{d}_{S}^{\pi}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
such that the chain is irreducible and aperiodic and
d_{S}^{\pi}(s)>0
for all states
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Before proceeding, we provide the following result, whose proof is reported in Appendix
C
:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let us assume that a policy
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
induces an ergodic Markov chain with stationary distribution
\bm{d}^{\pi}_{S}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
when interacting with a POMDP instance
\mathcal{Q}
. Then, there exists a unique stationary distribution
\bm{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}^{\pi}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{2}\times\mathcal{S}^{2})
over consecutive action-state pairs.
5
5

5

Formally,
d_{A^{2}S^{2}}^{\pi}(a,a^{\prime},s,s^{\prime}):=\lim_{t\to\infty}d_{t}^{\pi}(a,a^{\prime},s,s^{\prime})
with
d_{t}^{\pi}(a,a^{\prime},s,s^{\prime}):=P(A_{t}=a,A_{t+1}=a^{\prime},S_{t}=s,S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}|\pi)
.

Clearly, because of the partially observable nature of the considered setting, we are not able to directly estimate the induced distribution
\bm{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}
. However, we show how they are related to the received observations and how to use this information for estimation. For this purpose, we first need to define an analogous distribution
d_{t}^{\pi}(a,o):=P(A_{t}=a,O_{t}=o|\pi)
defined over action-observation pairs and its doubled version
d_{t}^{\pi}(a,a^{\prime},o,o^{\prime}):=P(A_{t}=a,A_{t+1}=a^{\prime},O_{t}=o,O_{t+1}=o^{\prime}|\pi)
considering consecutive action-observation pairs. We denote as well their limit versions as
\bm{d}_{AO}^{\pi}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{O})
and
\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}^{\pi}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{2}\times\mathcal{O}^{2})
respectively.

The relations holding between these quantities can be characterized as follows:

\displaystyle d_{AO}^{\pi}(a,o)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{O}_{a}(o|s)d_{AS}^{\pi}(a,s)\quad\forall a\in\mathcal{A},o\in\mathcal{O}.

Similarly,
\forall a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
and
\forall o,o^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}
, we have:

\displaystyle d_{A^{2}O^{2}}^{\pi}(a,a^{\prime},o,o^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\qquad\sum_{s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{O}_{a}(o|s)\mathbb{O}_{a^{\prime}}(o^{\prime}|s^{\prime})d_{A^{2}S^{2}}^{\pi}(a,a^{\prime},s,s^{\prime}).

(6)

These equations link the probability of action-state pairs with that of action-observation pairs and turn out to be relevant for the estimation of the distribution
\bm{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}^{\pi}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{2}\times\mathcal{S}^{2})
, as will be shown in the following subsection.

For the remainder of this section, we show how the estimates of the transition model
\widehat{\mathbb{T}}_{a}
can be computed using the estimated
\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}S^{2}}^{\pi}
. We first need to marginalize over the second component such that:

\displaystyle\widehat{d}_{AS^{2}}^{\pi}(a,s,s^{\prime})=\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\widehat{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}^{\pi}(a,a^{\prime},s,s^{\prime}),

(7)

where
\widehat{\bm{d}}_{AS^{2}}^{\pi}
is an estimate of
\bm{d}_{AS^{2}}^{\pi}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}^{2})

representing the stationary distribution over the tuple
(a,s,s^{\prime})
. Since it is immediate to see that
\mathbb{T}_{a}(s^{\prime}|s)\>\propto\>d_{AS^{2}}^{\pi}(a,s,s^{\prime})
, we use this relation to obtain
\widehat{\mathbb{T}}_{a}
from
\widehat{\bm{d}}_{AS^{2}}^{\pi}
.
Indeed, the final step results in normalizing the obtained quantities such that each row of the estimated transition matrix
\widehat{\mathbb{T}}_{a}
sums to one. For any
(a,s,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}^{2}
, we get:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{T}}_{a}(s^{\prime}|s)=\frac{\widehat{d}_{AS^{2}}^{\pi}(a,s,s^{\prime})}{\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\widehat{d}_{AS^{2}}^{\pi}(a,s,s^{\prime\prime})}.

(8)

Proposed Approach and Theoretical Guarantees

Let us now introduce the quantities that will be useful for estimating the stationary distribution of consecutive action-state pairs
\bm{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}^{\pi}
induced by a policy
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
. First of all, for each pair of actions

OAS-UCRL Algorithm

In this section, we present the Observation-Aware Spectral UCRL (OAS-UCRL) algorithm. It is obtained by combining Algorithm
1
with a UCRL-like algorithm
(
Jaksch, Ortner, and Auer 2010
)
, designed to optimally balance the exploration-exploitation trade-off.

During the first episode
k=0
, a uniform policy over the actions is arbitrarily chosen to collect samples for the first estimation. At the beginning of each episode
k>0
, a new POMDP
\mathcal{Q}^{(k)}
is computed using Algorithm
1
from samples collected in episode
k-1
. From this estimated quantity, the algorithm builds a confidence region
\mathcal{C}_{k}(\delta_{k})
with a confidence level
1-\delta_{k}
where
\delta_{k}\coloneqq\delta/k^{3}
. The confidence region contains the true POMDP instance
\mathcal{Q}
in high probability, namely
P(\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}(\delta_{k}))\geq 1-\delta_{k}
. As previously specified, we assume the existence of an oracle computing the optimal policy corresponding to the most optimistic POMDP in
\mathcal{C}_{k}(\delta_{k})
, as prescribed by the optimistic principle also used in UCRL. More formally, the oracle computes:

\displaystyle\pi^{(k)}=\arg\underset{\pi\in\mathcal{P}}{\max}\;\;\underset{\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}(\delta_{k})}{\max}\;\;\rho(\pi,\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}),

where we used
\rho(\pi,\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}})
to clarify the dependence of the average reward
\rho
from the employed policy
\pi
and the considered POMDP instance
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
, as evidenced in Equation (
4
).

Each time a new POMDP is estimated, the belief vector is updated starting from the first sample up to the current time step
7
7

7

Since the induced Markov chain is ergodic and the belief converges geometrically fast, it would also be sufficient to use a subset of the last samples for the recomputation of the belief.
. New samples are then collected using the new policy
\pi^{(k)}
, while the belief is updated using the estimated
\widehat{\mathbb{T}}_{k}
.

Since from an episode to the successive one, the employed policy is updated, the initial and stationary state distribution will not coincide, thus preventing us from directly using the results of Lemma
5.2
. However, we report in Lemma
D.1
(see the appendix) an extension of Lemma
5.2
, which considers the case of a chain not starting from its stationary distribution and that can be applied in the context of the OAS-UCRL algorithm.

The OAS-UCRL algorithm proceeds in episodes of increasing length by doubling the number of samples collected at each episode. Dy denoting with
N_{0}=T_{0}
the length of the first episode, the subsequent episodes have length
N_{k}=T_{0}2^{k}
. Unfortunately, the OAS estimation procedure is not able to reuse past samples since the estimated values depend on the distribution
\bm{d}_{AS}^{\pi^{(k)}}
induced by the policy, which changes across episodes. The pseudocode of the approach is reported in Algorithm

OAS Estimation Error.

In this experiment, a belief-based policy

As expected, the error of the estimated transition model keeps decreasing when the number of collected samples increases. We can see that the presented procedure makes good estimates even with a limited number of samples, as can be observed from the drop in the error after the first checkpoints. Regret Comparison.

The second set of experiments shows the result in terms of regret of the OAS-UCRL algorithm when compared with the SEEU algorithm from
Xiong et al. (2022)
and the PSRL-POMDP algorithm from
Jafarnia Jahromi, Jain, and Nayyar (2022)
. We recall that, like the OAS-UCRL algorithm, the PSRL-POMDP works under the assumption of knowing the observation model. Differently, the SEEU algorithm estimates both the transition and the observation model. To make a more fair comparison, we modified the spectral estimation used in the SEEU algorithm to help it by using the knowledge of the observation model (refer to Appendix
J
for details). We focus on a small-scale problem following the previous works by
Azizzadenesheli, Lazaric, and
Anandkumar (2016)
and
Xiong et al. (2022)
and report the results in Figure
2
.

Our OAS-UCRL algorithm shows a reduction in terms of regret compatible with an order of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
outperforming both baseline approaches. Indeed, the SD technique used in the SEEU algorithm requires a much larger amount of data, even for small problem instances. This aspect translates into higher estimation errors in the model parameters, inevitably leading to a higher regret. Instead, the PSRL-POMDP algorithm enjoys better regret guarantees than the SEEU algorithm, which is also favored by the knowledge of the observation model. However, the PSRL-POMDP algorithm is, in practice, unable to provide an improved estimate of the transition model since it does not come with a consistent procedure able to estimate the model parameters. This fact can be observed by the higher regret experienced with respect to the OAS-UCRL algorithm.

8
Conclusions and Future Works

We introduced a novel estimation procedure for the POMDP parameters under the knowledge of the observation model. To the best of our understanding, this is the first algorithm working in the Partially Observable setting that can use samples collected by a belief-based policy
\pi
to learn the transition parameters of the model. Then, we plugged this approach into a regret minimization algorithm (OAS-UCRL) that uses an optimistic approach to handle the exploration-exploitation trade-off.
We proved theoretical guarantees for both the OAS estimation procedure and the proposed OAS-UCRL algorithm. We demonstrated the efficiency of the OAS approach over standard SD techniques while achieving a regret
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
for the proposed algorithm.

Here, we highlight some interesting aspects that can be tackled in future works.

Sample Reuse.
The OAS-UCRL algorithm is not able to reuse past samples but only employs those coming from the last episode since each policy induces a different distribution over the action-state pairs. It may be interesting to study under which conditions all the samples can be reused. This problem is also present in SD techniques
(
Azizzadenesheli, Lazaric, and
Anandkumar 2016
)
.

Stronger Oracle.
Solving the first point may also be helpful when considering a stronger oracle, such as the optimal deterministic belief-based policy. It is still an open question whether it is possible to achieve a regret of order
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
under this oracle in the infinite-horizon setting.

Differently from their work, we devise the new OAS estimation procedure and show how this approach leads to consistent estimates.

•

Comparison of OAS with Spectral Decomposition Approaches

In this section, we highlight the main differences between our approach and the SD procedure employed in
Azizzadenesheli, Lazaric, and
Anandkumar (2016)
.

We recall that SD techniques have been commonly used to learn the transition and the observation model in HMM jointly, and their use has also been extended to learning POMDP models. To begin with, SD techniques require invertibility of the transition matrix
\mathbb{T}_{a}
associated with each action, while this is not required for our approach.

By taking into account the dependency on the problem parameters, their estimation error scales with an overall dependency of
S^{3/2}
with respect to states, compared against the dependence of order
S^{1/2}
experienced by our approach, as shown in Lemma
5.2
. The estimation error of the transition matrices in
Azizzadenesheli, Lazaric, and
Anandkumar (2016)
also depends on terms that cannot be directly mapped into our setting. However, by only considering the common terms and by comparing them directly, we can see that they scale with a dependence of
1/(\alpha^{4}\iota^{2}d_{\min}^{3/2})
, while Lemma
5.2
shows a dependency of
1/(\alpha^{2}\iota d_{\min})
that improves over all the considered terms.

The last aspect we would like to highlight is that SD techniques are generally used under fully explorative policies, such as uniform policies or round-robin approaches
(
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Xiong et al. 2022
;
Hsu, Kakade, and Zhang 2012
)
.
Azizzadenesheli, Lazaric, and
Anandkumar (2016)
managed to adapt their use to samples collected from memoryless policies. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no estimation procedures based on SD techniques that use samples collected from belief-based policies to estimate the POMDP parameters. Our OAS procedure is, instead, the first that can be used under any policy
\pi
that induces an ergodic Markov chain on the POMDP.

\bm{d}^{\pi}_{AO^{2}}(a,o,o^{\prime})=\lim_{t\to\infty}d_{t}^{\pi}(a,o,o^{\prime})
.

Thus, the terms with the apex will refer to a successive timestamp (
t+1
) with respect to those without the apex (
t
).

C
Proof of Proposition
5.1

In this section, we will present the proof of Proposition
5.1
. We report here for clarity the statement of the proposition.
See
5.1

Proof.

The objective of the proof is to show that given the condition on the ergodicity (the induced chain is thus irreducible and aperiodic) there exists a limiting action-state distribution
\bm{d}^{\pi}_{AS}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
where the apex
\pi
denotes that the distribution is induced by the belief-based policy
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
. From the existence of this distribution, we can easily extend it to the existence of a limiting distribution on consecutive action-state pairs
\bm{d}^{\pi}_{A^{2}S^{2}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{2}\times\mathcal{S}^{2})
.

When all states are reachable under the used policy
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
, the following Bellman Expectation Equation holds:

\displaystyle\rho^{\pi}+v_{\pi}(b)\;=\underset{a\sim\pi(\cdot|b)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(b,a)+\int_{\mathcal{B}}P(\,db^{\prime}|b,a)v_{\pi}(b^{\prime})\right].

We will now define a quantity useful for the following analysis. We denote the expected reward of an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
while being in state
s\in\mathcal{S}
as:

\displaystyle\mu(s,a)=\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}r(o)\mathbb{O}_{a}(o|s)=\bm{r}^{\top}\mathbb{O}_{a}(\cdot|s),

(12)

where the last term represents the vector form of the expression and
\bm{r}
is a vector of size
O
containing the deterministic reward associated with each observation.
By using the Bellman Expectation Equation and quantity
\mu(s,a)
just defined, we can also express the average reward induced by policy
\pi
as:

\displaystyle\rho^{\pi}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}d^{\pi}(a,s)\mu(s,a).

Let us now consider a specific
a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
and a state
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
and let us define the reward function as
\mu(s,a)=\mathds{1}[s=s^{\prime},a=a^{\prime}]
.
By applying this reward function, we get that
\rho^{\pi}=d^{\pi}(a^{\prime},s^{\prime})
, which ensures as well the existence of
d^{\pi}(a^{\prime},s^{\prime})
since
\rho^{\pi}
is ensured to exists from the Bellman Equation. By extending this consideration to all other pairs of actions and states and choosing the reward function accordingly, we can show a unique stationary distribution
\bm{d}^{\pi}_{AS}
on action-state pairs exists. From this result, the existence of a stationary distribution
\bm{d}^{\pi}_{A^{2}S^{2}}
on consecutive action-state pairs directly follows, thus concluding the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma

Before presenting the proof of Lemma
5.2
, we report here the statement.

See

Proof.

Let us first recall from the notation that we denote with
\mathbb{T}
the transition matrix having size
SA\times S
where each row
\mathbb{T}({\cdot|s,a})\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
denotes the probability distribution of reaching the next state, given the current state
s
and the chosen action
a
. While we denote with
\mathbb{T}_{a}
the transition matrix having size
S\times S
where the probabilities are conditioned on having taken action
a
. Furthermore, we recall that
\bm{d}^{\pi}_{A^{2}S^{2}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{2}\times\mathcal{S}^{2})
denotes the vector of size
A^{2}S^{2}
representing the stationary distribution over the tuples
(a,a^{\prime},s,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}^{2}\times\mathcal{S}^{2}
, while
\bm{d}^{\pi}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{2}\times\mathcal{O}^{2})
denotes the vector of size
A^{2}O^{2}
representing the stationary distribution over the tuples
(a,a^{\prime},o,o^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}^{2}\times\mathcal{O}^{2}
. We define their estimated quantities as
\widehat{\bm{d}}^{\pi}_{A^{2}S^{2}}
and
\widehat{\bm{d}}^{\pi}_{A^{2}O^{2}}
respectively.

From now on we will omit apex
\pi
when referring to the vectors of induced stationary distribution, thus having
\bm{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}
and
\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}
.

The proof of the presented bound can be broken down into two main parts. The former part bounds the estimation error of the vector representing the stationary distribution of consecutive action-state pairs
\bm{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}
, while the latter part bounds the estimation error of the transition matrix
\mathbb{T}
with respect to the estimation error of
\bm{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}
.

We will start by proving the first result. We have that:

\displaystyle\|\bm{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}S^{2}}\|_{2}=

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{B}^{\dagger}(\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{B}^{\dagger}\|_{2}\lVert\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbb{B})}\lVert\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\rVert_{2}

(13)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\lVert\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\rVert_{2},

(14)

where the first equality follows from Equation (
11
), while the first inequality follows by the consistency property of matrices: we bound the 2-norm on the first line with the product of the 2-norm of the vector of differences and the spectral norm of matrix
\mathbb{B}^{\dagger}
.

The second inequality derives by definition of the block matrix
\mathbb{B}
, that is composed by submatrices
\{\mathbb{O}_{a,a^{\prime}}\}_{(a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}^{2}}
for which it holds that
\sigma_{\min}(\mathbb{O}_{a,a^{\prime}})\geq\alpha^{2}
for all
(a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}^{2}
. For the properties of block diagonal matrices, it also follows that
\sigma_{\min}(\mathbb{B})\geq\alpha^{2}
.

We will now focus on bounding the last term appearing in
14
representing the estimation error of the stationary distribution of consecutive action-observation pairs
\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}
. We recall that this vector represents the parameters of a categorical distribution on the consecutive action-observation couples. If the samples used to estimate this distribution were independent, we could easily apply the standard version of McDiarmid’s inequality
(
McDiarmid 1989
)
for categorical distributions.

However, in our case, we have a dependency among samples since they originated from the policy
\pi
interacting with the environment. Indeed, the tuples
(a,a^{\prime},o,o^{\prime})
observed every 2 steps are realizations of stochastic functions that depend on the underlying tuple
(a,a^{\prime},s,s^{\prime})
. The sequence of these action-state tuples, in turn, depends on the Markov chains induced by policy
\pi
.

Let us now see how these Markov chains are characterized.
For any time
t
, there is a Markov chain determined by a transition matrix
\mathbb{M}_{t}
with size
SA\times SA
and such that
\mathbb{M}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}|s,a)
represents the joint probability of reaching
S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}
and taking action
A_{t+1}=a^{\prime}
under policy
\pi
, after having taken action
A_{t}=a
in state
S_{t}=s
. We have that
\mathbb{M}(\cdot,\cdot|s,a)\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})
. We will refer to them as state-action transition matrices.

These matrices
(\mathbb{M}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
change at every time instant, and this inhomogeneity is due to the non-markovian nature of the policy that updates the belief at every time step, thus continuously changing the action probabilities.
Despite their inhomogeneity, the ergodicity assumption ensures that these chains converge to an invariant measure represented by the stationary action-state distribution
\bm{d}_{AS}
induced by policy
\pi
.

Fan, Jiang, and Sun (2021)
show that the speed of convergence of a sequence of Inhomogeneous Markov chains depends on
\lambda_{\max}
, which is defined as:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}:=\underset{t\geq 1}{\max}|\lambda(\mathbb{M}_{t})|,

where
|\lambda(\mathbb{M}_{t})|
denotes the modulus of the second largest eigenvalue of matrix
\mathbb{M}_{t}
. Since, by assumption, the sequence of chains is ergodic, it holds that
\lambda_{\max}<1
, where the value of
\lambda_{\max}
depends on the model characterizing the POMDP and the properties of policy
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
.

Given these considerations, we have all the information to bound the quantity in Equation (
14
). We will do it by using the result formulated in Lemma
G.1
that bounds the estimation error of probability distributions of samples coming from Markov chains. Using Lemma
G.1
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{\Big(\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{1-\lambda_{\max}}\Big)\frac{2+5\log(1/\delta)}{n}}.

(15)

While in Lemma
G.1
we have a dependency
\sqrt{1/2n}
, here we only have
\sqrt{1/n}
. The 2 term is removed since the meaning of the quantity
n
is different in the two lemmas: this Lemma uses
n
as the number of interaction time steps, while Lemma
G.1
uses
n
to refer to the tuple
(a,a^{\prime}o,o^{\prime})
collected in 2 consecutive time steps.

By combining the results just obtained, we can finally conclude the first part of the proof having that:

\displaystyle\|\bm{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}S^{2}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\sqrt{\Big(\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{1-\lambda_{\max}}\Big)\frac{2+5\log(1/\delta)}{n}},

(16)

holding with probability at least
1-\delta
.

We proceed now with the second part of the proof which consists in bounding the error of the transition matrix
\mathbb{T}
that derives from the estimation error of vector
\bm{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}
. In the following, we will use variable
i\in[AS]
to map any couple
(a,s)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
thus denoting the rows of the transition matrix
\mathbb{T}
. Furthermore, we will use notation
\mathbb{T}(i,j)
with the same meaning of
\mathbb{T}(j|i)
, with

and

specified as above.

The proof goes on by transforming vector
\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}S^{2}}
into vector

\displaystyle\widehat{d}_{AS^{2}}(a,s,s^{\prime})=\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\widehat{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}(a,a^{\prime},s,s^{\prime})\quad\forall a\in\mathcal{A},\forall s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\|\bm{d}_{AS^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{AS^{2}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{A}\|\bm{d}_{A^{2}S^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}S^{2}}\|_{2},

As done above, we will refer to each element

Extension of Lemma
5.2

The objective of this subsection is to present a different version of Lemma
5.2
that is more suitable for our setting since it does not assume that the chain starts from its stationary distribution. It will be used to prove the result of Theorem
6.1
. Let us first state the new extended Lemma and then we will proceed with the proof.

Lemma D.1
.

Let us assume to have a POMDP instance
\mathcal{Q}
with a finite number of states
\mathcal{S}
and a finite number of actions
\mathcal{A}
. Let Assumptions
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
hold. By denoting with
\bm{\nu}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
the initial distribution and with
\bm{d}_{AS}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
the stationary distribution induced by the policy
\pi
, by using the estimation procedure defined Algorithm
1
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds:

\|\mathbb{T}-\widehat{\mathbb{T}}\|_{F}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}(\epsilon\iota)^{3/2}}\sqrt{\frac{2C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})+5\log(C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})/\delta))}{n}},

where
C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})
is defined as
C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})=\left\|\frac{\bm{\nu}}{\bm{d}_{AS}}\right\|_{\infty}
and
\frac{\bm{\nu}}{\bm{d}_{AS}}
is a vector of size
AS
containing the element-wise ratio of the elements of the two vectors.

Proof.

The proof of this lemma largely follows from that of Lemma
5.2
. The main difference is that the process may not start from its stationary distribution. Furthermore, since Assumption
4.1
holds, we are able to better characterize the contraction coefficient of the chain and, thus, its related absolute spectral gap. We will start by analyzing this last point.

We refer to the concept of state-action transition matrices
(\mathbb{M}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
defined in the proof of Lemma
5.2
.
Let us show that if Assumption
4.1
holds, it is always true that the modulus of the second largest eigenvalue
|\lambda|
is strictly smaller than 1.

Under Assumption
4.1
and assuming that
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
(Assumption
4.3
), it follows that we can lower bound the minimum value associated with each matrix
\mathbb{M}_{t}
. In particular, by having variables
i,j\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, we have that:

\displaystyle\tau:=\underset{i,j}{\min}\;\mathbb{M}_{t}(i|j)\geq\epsilon\iota>0\qquad\forall t\geq 1,

where
j
and
i
encode respectively the index of the row and of the column of the state-action transition matrix
\mathbb{M}_{t}
.

In order to better characterize the second largest eigenvalue of the induced Markov chains, we will use the notion of Dobrushin coefficient
(
Krishnamurthy 2016
)
and exploit its properties. The Dobrushin coefficient is a contraction coefficient that controls the speed of convergence of the Markov chain induced by the sequence
(\mathbb{M}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
towards the stationary distribution. In particular, for any matrix
\mathbb{D}
,
Krishnamurthy (2016)
show that the contraction coefficient
\rho(\mathbb{D})\in[0,1]
satisfies the following contraction equation:

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{D}^{\top}\nu-\mathbb{D}^{\top}\bar{\nu}\|_{1}\leq\rho(\mathbb{D})\|\nu-\bar{\nu}\|_{1},

with
\nu
and
\bar{\nu}
being any two initial distributions, and
\mathbb{D}^{\top}
being the transpose of matrix
\mathbb{D}
. We defer to Appendix
E
for a thoughtful presentation of its properties.

For our setting, it is relevant to know that when the elements of matrix
\mathbb{D}
are all positive, the associated
\rho(\mathbb{D})
coefficient is strictly smaller than 1. In particular, we have:

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbb{M}_{t})

\displaystyle=1-\min_{i,j\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\min\{\mathbb{M}_{t}(l|i),\mathbb{M}_{t}(l|j)\}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\sum_{l\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\tau=1-SA\tau

\displaystyle\leq 1-SA\epsilon\iota,

where the first equality derives from Property
1
in Appendix
E
, while the last two inequalities follow from the definition of
\tau
.

Since the Dobrushin coefficient is known to be an upper bound on the modulus of the second largest eigenvalue (Property
2
in Appendix
E
),
\forall t
we have that:

\displaystyle|\lambda(\mathbb{M}_{t})|\leq\rho(\mathbb{M}_{t})\leq 1-SA\epsilon\iota.

From now on, we will thus use:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}:=1-SA\epsilon\iota

(24)

to denote the maximum magnitude
\lambda(\mathbb{M}_{t})
can have for any
t
.

We can now tackle the problem of the process not starting from its invariant distribution. The additional factor influencing the estimation error in this case is how fast the Markov chain transitions from the initial distribution to the stationary one. For this purpose, we will use Lemma
G.3
appearing in Appendix
G
, which bounds the estimation error of a categorical distribution from samples coming from a Markov chain not starting from the stationarity. If we denote with
\bm{\nu}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
the starting action-state distribution and with
\bm{d}_{AS}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
the stationary distribution induced by the used policy, Lemma
G.3
allows us to write that, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds:

\displaystyle\|\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\Big(\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{1-\lambda_{\max}}\Big)\frac{2C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{S})+5\log(C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{S})/\delta)}{2n}},

(25)

where
C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})
is defined as
C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})=\left\|\frac{\bm{\nu}}{\bm{d}_{AS}}\right\|_{\infty}
and
\frac{\bm{\nu}}{\bm{d}_{AS}}
is a vector of size
AS
containing the element-wise ratio of the elements of the two vectors. We can easily see that when
\bm{\nu}=\bm{d}_{AS}
, we have
C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})=1
thus resorting to the result appearing in Lemma
G.1
.

Summarizing the obtained result and including those from Lemma
5.2
, we get with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{T}-\widehat{\mathbb{T}}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}d_{\min}\iota}\sqrt{\Big(\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{1-\lambda_{\max}}\Big)\frac{SA(2C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})+5\log(C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})/\delta))}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}\epsilon\iota}\sqrt{\Big(\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{1-\lambda_{\max}}\Big)\frac{SA(2C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})+5\log(C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})/\delta))}{n}}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}(\epsilon\iota)^{3/2}}\sqrt{\frac{2C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})+5\log(C(\bm{\nu},\bm{d}_{AS})/\delta))}{n}}

(27)

where in line (
26
) we simply used that
\epsilon\leq d_{\min}
, while in the last step we used
1+\lambda_{\max}\leq 2
at the numerator and we substituted the definition of
\lambda_{\max}
appearing in (
24
) at the denominator.
This leads to removing the explicit dependence on
S
and
A
from the bound at the cost of having a further
\sqrt{\epsilon\iota}
term in the final result. This last step concludes the proof.
∎

5
) appearing in the main paper:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}:=T\rho^{*}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}^{\pi}(o_{t})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\rho^{*}-\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[r(O_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}])+\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[r(O_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]-r(o_{t})),

(28)

where the expectation
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}
is taken w.r.t. the true transition matrices
\mathbb{T}
, the true observation matrices
\mathbb{O}
and the stochasticity of the policy
\pi
. We can observe that the second term in the summation is a martingale. Indeed, by defining a stochastic process as:

\displaystyle X_{0}=0,X_{t}=\sum_{l=1}^{t}(\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[r(O_{l})|\mathcal{F}_{l-1}]-r(o_{l})),

we can easily see that
X_{t}
is a martingale. This allows us to apply the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality
(
Azuma 1967
)
and obtain that with probability at least
1-\delta/4
we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[r(O_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]-r(o_{t}))\leq\sqrt{2T\log(4/\delta)}.

(29)

We will now define some quantities that will be useful for the following analysis. We recall here the definition reported in Equation (
12
) of the expected reward of an action
a_{t}
assuming to be in state
s_{t}
as:

\displaystyle\mu(s_{t},a_{t})=\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}r(o)\mathbb{O}_{a_{t}}(o|s_{t})=\bm{r}^{\top}\mathbb{O}_{a_{t}}(\cdot|s_{t}).

Therefore, we can define the expected reward given a belief state
b_{t}
at time
t
when taking action
a_{t}
as:

\displaystyle g(b_{t},a_{t})=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s,a_{t})b_{t}(s)=\bm{\mu}(a_{t})b_{t}=\bm{r}^{\top}\mathbb{O}_{a_{t}}b_{t},

(30)

where the last equalities define the expression in matrix notation, with
\bm{\mu}(a_{t})
being a vector of dimension
S
containing the quantity
\mu(s,a_{t})\>\>\forall s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Since action
A_{t}
is adapted to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[\mu(S_{t},A_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=g(b_{t},A_{t}),

where the belief
b_{t}
is computed using the true transition and observation matrices and actions are taken according to policy
\pi
. We will instead denote with:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{k}[\mu(S_{t},A_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=g(b_{t}^{k},A_{t})

the expected instantaneous reward assuming to have computed the belief using the estimate of the transition probability employed during the
k
-th episode. We refer to this estimate as
\mathbb{T}_{k}
.

Given the defined quantities, we can rewrite the first term of (
28
) as:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\rho^{*}-\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[r(O_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}])=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\rho^{*}-\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[\mu(S_{t},A_{t})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}])=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\rho^{*}-g(b_{t},A_{t})).

(31)

Given that the algorithm proceeds in episodes of increasing length, we can rewrite the previous expression by denoting with
[N_{k}]
the steps belonging to the
k
-th phase:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}(\rho^{*}-g(b_{t},A_{t})),

(32)

where from this summation, we exclude the episode
k=0
that is used to initialize the POMDP parameters and for which the suffered regret corresponds to the length of episode
N_{0}=T_{0}
. At the beginning of each episode, the proposed algorithm defines a confidence
\mathcal{C}_{k}(\delta_{k})
such that the real POMDP instance
\mathcal{Q}
is contained in high probability in the region, namely
P(\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}(\delta_{k}))\geq 1-\delta_{k}
, with
\delta_{k}\eqqcolon\delta/k^{3}
. We assume that the provided oracle is able to return the value function
v_{k}
, the average reward
\rho^{k}
, and the transition matrix
\mathbb{T}_{k}
corresponding to the most optimistic POMDP instance
\mathcal{Q}^{(k)}
belonging to the confidence region
\mathcal{C}_{k}(\delta_{k})
.

We take into account now two possible events: the
good event
considers the case where for all episodes
k
, the true POMDP is contained in the confidence sets
\{\mathcal{C}_{k}(\delta_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
; the
failure event
denotes the complementary event.

We can thus derive:

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{Q}\notin\mathcal{C}_{k}(\delta_{k}),\text{for some k})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\delta_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\delta}{k^{3}}\leq\frac{3}{2}\delta.

From this relation, with probability at least
1-\frac{3}{2}\delta
, the
good event
holds. When the
good event
holds, we have that the
\rho^{*}\leq\rho^{k}
for any
k
since the optimal average reward is taken from the optimistic POMDP
\mathcal{Q}^{(k)}
.

We can now bound the regret under the
good event
during the
k
phases.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}(\rho^{*}-g(b_{t},A_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}(\rho^{k}-g(b_{t},A_{t}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}(\rho^{k}-g(b_{t}^{k},A_{t}))+(g(b_{t}^{k},A_{t})-g(b_{t},A_{t})).

(33)

We will proceed by initially bounding the first term of (
33
) and then the second one. The first term can be bounded by using the Bellman expectation equation (see Equation (
63
)) for the optimistic belief MDP
\mathcal{Q}^{(k)}
. Before going on, we introduce the vector
U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})=P_{\mathbb{T}_{k}}(b_{t+1}\in\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})
which defines the probability of each possible future belief
b_{t+1}
conditioned on the actual belief
b_{t}^{k}
, on the action taken
A_{t}
and with transition kernel given by the estimated
\mathbb{T}_{k}
. We rewrite the Bellman expectation equation under policy
\pi^{(k)}
as follows:

\displaystyle\rho^{k}+v_{k}(b_{t}^{k})

\displaystyle=\underset{a\sim\pi^{(k)}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k})}{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(b_{t}^{k},a)+\int_{b_{t+1}\in\mathcal{B}}v_{k}(b_{t+1})U_{k}(\,db_{t+1}|b_{t}^{k},a)\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{a\sim\pi^{(k)}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k})}{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(b_{t}^{k},a)+\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},a),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\right]

(34)

Given that the value function
v_{k}
satisfies the Bellman expectation Equation, the same would hold for a shifted version
v_{k}+c\bm{1}
. From this consideration, we can assume that

\|v_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\text{span}(v_{k})/2
. By using the result in Proposition
H.1
taken from
Zhou et al. (2021)
, we are able to bound the span of
v_{k}
, where the span is defined as
span(v_{k}):=\max_{b\in\mathcal{B}}v_{k}(b)-\min_{b\in\mathcal{B}}v_{k}(b)
. The span is bounded by a finite quantity
D
representing the diameter of the POMDP. We can thus write:

\displaystyle\|v_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\text{span}(v_{k})}{2}\leq\frac{D}{2}.

(35)

Taking into account the previous considerations, we can write for the first term of (
33
):

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}(\rho^{k}-g(b_{t}^{k},A_{t}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(-v_{k}(b_{t}^{k})+\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\right)+\left(\underset{a\sim\pi^{(k)}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k})}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},a),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle)\right]-\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left(\underset{a\sim\pi^{(k)}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k})}{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(b_{t}^{k},a)\right]-g(b_{t}^{k},A_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(-v_{k}(b_{t}^{k})+\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(\underset{a\sim\pi^{(k)}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k})}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},a),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle)\right]-\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(\underset{a\sim\pi^{(k)}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k})}{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(b_{t}^{k},a)\right]-g(b_{t}^{k},A_{t})\right),

(36)

where the first equality is obtained by substituting
\rho^{k}
as appears in the Bellman Equation in line (
34
) and by adding and subtracting the quantity
\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle
for all the time steps. From the last equality, we observe three different blocks of summations. We focus on the last two: it can be shown that they are both martingale sequences (see Proposition 4 in
Zhou et al. (2021)
for the second term), that can be bounded as follows.

For the second term in (
36
), we have with probability at least
1-\delta/4
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(\underset{a\sim\pi^{(k)}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k})}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},a),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle)\right]-\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\right)\leq D\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}.

(37)

Analogously, for the third term:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(\underset{a\sim\pi^{(k)}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k})}{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(b_{t}^{k},a)\right]-g(b_{t}^{k},A_{t})\right)\leq\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}.

(38)

Let us now focus on the first term appearing in (
36
). We can rewrite it as:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(-v_{k}(b_{t}^{k})+\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\Big(-v_{k}(b_{t}^{k})+\langle U(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\Big)+\Big(\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})-U(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\Big),

(39)

where we recall that
U(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})
represents the probability distribution over the belief at the next step
t+1
under the true transition matrix
\mathbb{T}
, while
U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})
represents the same probability distribution under the estimated transition matrix
\mathbb{T}_{k}
.

For the first term of (
39
), we can write:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(-v_{k}(b_{t}^{k})+\langle U(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\right)=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(-v_{k}(b_{t}^{k})+v_{k}(b_{t+1}^{k})\right)+\left(-v_{k}(b_{t+1}^{k})+\langle U(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\right)

(40)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}-v_{k}(b_{s_{k}}^{k})+v_{k}(b_{e_{k}+1}^{k})+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[v_{k}(b_{t+1}^{k}|\mathcal{F}_{t})]-v_{k}(b_{t+1}^{k}),

(41)

where the first term in the last equality is a telescopic summation, thus resulting in the first value
v_{k}(b_{s_{k}}^{k})
and the last value
v_{k}(b_{e_{k}+1}^{k})
of the bias function
v_{k}
in the exploitation phase
k
. The second term in the last equality is instead obtained by showing that:

\displaystyle\langle U(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle=\int_{b_{t+1}\in\mathcal{B}}v_{k}(b_{t+1})U(\,db_{t+1}|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[v_{k}(b_{t+1}^{k}|b_{t}^{k})]=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[v_{k}(b_{t+1}^{k}|\mathcal{F}_{t})].

By recalling Proposition
H.1
to bound the span of the bias function, we get:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}-v_{k}(b_{s_{k}}^{k})+v_{k}(b_{e_{k}+1}^{k})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}D=KD.

(42)

By applying instead analogous considerations as those used for bounding (
37
), we can state that with probability at least
1-\delta/4
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}[v_{k}(b_{t+1}^{k}|\mathcal{F}_{t})]-v_{k}(b_{t+1}^{k})\leq D\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}.

(43)

By putting together the previous considerations, we can bound the first term appearing in (
39
) as:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(-v_{k}(b_{t}^{k})+\langle U(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\right)\leq KD+D\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}.

(44)

We can proceed now in bounding the second term of (
39
). Before that, we introduce functions
H(b_{t},a_{t},o_{t})
and
H_{k}(b_{t},a_{t},o_{t})
which deterministically return the belief at the next time step
b_{t+1}
given the current belief
b_{t}
, the action taken
a_{t}
and the received observation
o_{t}
using the real
\mathbb{T}_{a_{t}}
and the estimated transition matrix
\mathbb{T}_{a_{t}k}
, respectively.

Let us analyze each quantity appearing in the summation of the second term of (
39
):

\displaystyle\langle U_{k}(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})-U(\cdot|b_{t}^{k},A_{t}),v_{k}(\cdot)\rangle\leq\;

\displaystyle\Bigg\lvert\int_{\mathcal{B}}v_{k}(b^{\prime})U_{k}(\,db^{\prime}|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})-\int_{\mathcal{B}}v_{k}(b^{\prime})U(\,db^{\prime}|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})\Bigg\rvert

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\Bigg\lvert\sum_{o_{t}\in\mathcal{O}}v_{k}(H_{k}(b_{t}^{k},A_{t},o_{t}))P(o_{t}|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})-\sum_{o_{t}\in\mathcal{O}}v_{k}(H(b_{t}^{k},A_{t},o_{t}))P(o_{t}|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})\Bigg\rvert

(45)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\Bigg\lvert\sum_{o_{t}\in\mathcal{O}}\left[v_{k}(H_{k}(b_{t}^{k},A_{t},o_{t}))-v_{k}(H(b_{t}^{k},A_{t},o_{t}))\right]P(o_{t}|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})\Bigg\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\sum_{o_{t}\in\mathcal{O}}\Big\lvert v_{k}(H_{k}(b_{t}^{k},A_{t},o_{t}))-v_{k}(H(b_{t}^{k},A_{t},o_{t}))\Big\rvert P(o_{t}|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\sum_{o_{t}\in\mathcal{O}}\Big\lvert\frac{D}{2}\big(H_{k}(b_{t}^{k},A_{t},o_{t})-H(b_{t}^{k},A_{t},o_{t})\big)\Big\rvert P(o_{t}|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})

(46)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\sum_{o_{t}\in\mathcal{O}}\frac{D}{2}\left(L\|\mathbb{T}_{A_{t}}-\mathbb{T}_{A_{t}k}\|_{F}\right)P(o_{t}|b_{t}^{k},A_{t})

(47)

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{DL}{2}\|\mathbb{T}_{A_{t}}-\mathbb{T}_{A_{t}k}\|_{F},

(48)

where in line (
45
) we have explicitly decoupled the stochasticity induced by the observation from the deterministic update of the belief
b^{\prime}
at the next step through the
H
and
H_{k}
functions. Line (
46
) is obtained from the bound on the bias function as defined in (
35
) and Holder’s inequality, while line (
47
) is obtained from Proposition
H.3
, which has been derived from the original result of
De Castro, Gassiat, and Le Corff (2017)
.

Given the results obtained so far, we are able to bound the first term of (
33
). By combining the bounds on the martingale sequences (
37
), (
38
), (
43
) with (
42
) and (
48
), we get:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}(\rho^{k}-g(b_{t}^{k},A_{t}))\leq KD+2D\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}+\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\frac{DL}{2}\|\mathbb{T}_{A_{t}}-\mathbb{T}_{A_{t}k}\|_{F}

(49)

We can now proceed in analyzing the second term of (
33
):

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}(g(b_{t}^{k},A_{t})-g(b_{t},A_{t}))=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}(\bm{r}^{\top}\mathbb{O}_{A_{t}}b_{t}^{k}-\bm{r}^{\top}\mathbb{O}_{A_{t}}b_{t})

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\bm{r}^{\top}\mathbb{O}_{A_{t}}(b_{t}^{k}-b_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\|\bm{r}^{\top}\mathbb{O}_{A_{t}}\|_{\infty}\|b_{t}^{k}-b_{t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\|b_{t}^{k}-b_{t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}L\|\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}_{k}\|_{F}

where in the first equality, we use the definition of the function
g
in (
30
). In the first inequality we use Holder’s inequality, in the second inequality we consider that
\|\bm{r}^{\top}\mathbb{O}_{A_{t}}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. The final inequality derives from the bound on the belief of Proposition
H.3
, where we used that
\|\mathbb{T}_{a}-\mathbb{T}_{ak}\|_{F}\leq\|\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}_{k}\|_{F}
holds for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

We can finally put all the results together, thus having:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}(\rho^{*}-g(b_{t},A_{t})\leq\;

\displaystyle KD+2D\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}+\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\frac{DL}{2}\|\mathbb{T}_{A_{t}}-\mathbb{T}_{A_{t}k}\|_{F}+L\|\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}_{k}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle KD+2D\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}+\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(1+\frac{D}{2}\right)L\|\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}_{k}\|_{F},

(50)

where in the last inequality we used again that
\|\mathbb{T}_{A_{t}}-\mathbb{T}_{A_{t}k}\|_{F}\leq\|\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}_{k}\|_{F}
.

Let us now consider the last term appearing in line (
50
). The estimation error of the transition model could be bounded by using Lemma
D.1
that uses Assumption
4.1
and also does not assume that the process starts from its invariant distribution.

We can thus rewrite the last term in (
50
) as follows, holding with probability at least
1-\frac{3}{2}\delta
(since it is the probability of the
good event
):

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\left(1+\frac{D}{2}\right)L\|\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}_{k}\|_{F}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\frac{L\left(2+D\right)}{\alpha^{2}(\epsilon\iota)^{3/2}}\sqrt{\frac{2C_{k}+5\log(C_{k}/\delta_{k})}{N_{k-1}}},

(51)

where
N_{k-1}
represents the number of samples collected during the
(k-1)
-th episode, while
C_{k}=C(\bm{\nu}_{k},\bm{d}_{AS,k})
where
\bm{\nu}_{k}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
and
\bm{d}_{AS,k}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
are respectively the action-state distribution at the beginning of the
k
-th episode and the action-state stationary distribution induced by policy
\pi^{(k)}
.

Let us recall now how the OAS-UCRL Algorithm handles the switch from one episode to the next one: it happens when the number of samples collected during the
k
-th episode reaches
N_{k}=T_{0}2^{k}
. By considering a total of
K+1
episodes (including
k=0
), they would last for a total number of steps given by:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{K}N_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{K}T_{0}2^{i}=T_{0}(2^{K+1}-1).

(52)

By considering a total horizon of length
T
, we have that:

\displaystyle K+1\leq\left\lceil\log_{2}\left(\frac{T}{T_{0}}+1\right)\right\rceil.

(53)

Let us consider now the formulation appearing in (
51
) in terms of the length of each episode:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\frac{L\left(2+D\right)}{\alpha^{2}(\epsilon\iota)^{3/2}}\sqrt{\frac{2C_{k}+5\log(C_{k}k^{3}/\delta)}{N_{k-1}}}\leq\;\frac{L\left(2+D\right)}{\alpha^{2}(\epsilon\iota)^{3/2}}\sqrt{2C_{\max}+5\log(C_{\max}K^{3}/\delta)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{k-1}}}

(54)

where we defined the quantity
C_{\max}:=\max_{k\in[K]}C_{k}
and reorganized the expression to isolate the constant terms.
Let us focus now on the summation appearing in (
54
). We have:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in[N_{k}]}\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{k-1}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}T_{0}2^{k}\sqrt{\frac{1}{T_{0}2^{k-1}}}=\sqrt{2T_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{2}^{k}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2T_{0}}}{\sqrt{2}-1}\sqrt{2}^{K+1}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2T_{0}}(1+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{\frac{T}{T_{0}}+1}

(55)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2T_{0}}(1+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{\frac{2T}{T_{0}}}

\displaystyle\leq 6\sqrt{T},

(56)

where the relations easily follow from simple algebraic manipulations while line (
55
) uses the expression in (
53
).

Wrapping up the previous results and combining them with (
29
), we are finally able to write the formulation of the regret, holding with probability at least
1-\frac{5}{2}\delta
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq KD+2D\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}+2\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}+T_{0}+\frac{6L\left(2+D\right)}{\alpha^{2}(\epsilon\iota)^{3/2}}\sqrt{\big(2C_{\max}+5\log(C_{\max}K^{3}/\delta)\big)T}

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}(T)D+4D\sqrt{2T\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}+T_{0}+\frac{6L\left(2+D\right)}{\alpha^{2}(\epsilon\iota)^{3/2}}\sqrt{\big(2C_{\max}+5\log(C_{\max}/\delta)+15\log(\log T)\big)T}

(57)

where we introduced the quantity
T_{0}
related to the regret suffered in the first episode (
k=0
) and we used that
K\leq\log(T)
which can be derived from (
53
).
By rearranging the bound and expressing it in a more compact form highlighting the most relevant terms, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\frac{S\left(2+D\right)}{\alpha^{2}(\epsilon\iota)^{3/2}}\sqrt{T\log(T/\delta)}+C_{2},

(58)

where we make explicit the linear dependence on the number of states
S
derived from the definition of
L
and define constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, which are obtained by simplifying the expression in (
57
).

This last expression completes the proof.
∎

E
Geometric ergodicity and Dobrushin coefficient for Markov Chains

The objective of this section is to introduce the concept of geometric ergodicity for Markov chains and characterize the speed of convergence towards the stationary distribution.

We will focus here on Markov chains with finite state space
|X|=\mathcal{X}
and such that their corresponding transition matrix
\mathbb{T}
satisfies Assumption
4.1
, thus having that each cell of the matrix is lower bounded by a quantity
\epsilon
.

A key element that we need to introduce is the Dobrushin coefficient
(
Krishnamurthy 2016
)
. Let us consider two distributions
\nu,\bar{\nu}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
and a transition matrix
\mathbb{T}
on
\mathcal{X}
.
The Dobrushin coefficient
\rho(\cdot)
gives the following bound:

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{T}^{\top}\nu-\mathbb{T}^{\top}\bar{\nu}\|_{1}\leq\rho(\mathbb{T})\|\nu-\bar{\nu}\|_{1},

(59)

where
\mathbb{T}^{\top}
represents the transpose of matrix
\mathbb{T}
. The inequality above says that the one-step evolution of two probability vectors induced by the same kernel
\mathbb{T}
can be bounded by the quantity on the right where scalar
\rho(\mathbb{T})\in[0,1]
represents the Dobrushin coefficient. If
\rho(\mathbb{T})
is strictly smaller than 1, by iteratively applying the inequality, it is possible to ensure geometric convergence of the initial distance.

Among the relevant properties associated with this quantity (we refer to
Krishnamurthy (2016)
for more details), we report the ones that will be relevant for our purposes:

1.

\rho(\mathbb{T})=1-\min_{i,j\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{X}}\min\{\mathbb{T}(l|i),\mathbb{T}(l|j)\}
.

2.

|\lambda(\mathbb{T})|\leq\rho(\mathbb{T})
. That is, the Dobrushin coefficient upper bounds the modulus of the second largest eigenvalue of matrix
\mathbb{T}
.

Under Assumption
4.1
, we can apply Property
1
to set an upper bound on the Dobrushin coefficient
\rho(\mathbb{T})
:

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbb{T})

\displaystyle=1-\min_{i,j\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{X}}\min\{\mathbb{T}(l|i),\mathbb{T}(l|j)\}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\sum_{l\in\mathcal{X}}\epsilon=1-X\epsilon

where the inequality derives from the assumption.

Since we have set an upper bound on
\rho(\mathbb{T})
, from Property
2
, it also follows that this is an upper bound also for the second largest eigenvalue:

\displaystyle|\lambda(\mathbb{T})|\leq\rho(\mathbb{T})\leq 1-X\epsilon

The considerations above show that the Dobrushin coefficient is useful when it is strictly less than 1. On the contrary, for sparse matrices,
\rho(\mathbb{T})
is typically equal to 1, thus only providing trivial upper bounds for
|\lambda(\mathbb{T})|
.

F
OAS Estimation Procedure under Continuous Observations

The objective of this section is to present how the OAS estimation procedure described in the main text should be adapted when the observation space is no longer finite.

When the observation space is continuous and known, we can as well apply the OAS estimation procedure but we first need to discretize the observation space. Analogously to what is suggested in
Author (2023)
, continuous distributions can be converted into multinomial ones. The discretization process goes on by defining a number
U
of distinct consecutive intervals and the split points characterizing the partition of the observation space. The split points should be the same for any continuous distribution
P(\cdot|a,s)
in the observation space, for each
a\in\mathcal{A},s\in\mathcal{S}
. After the discretization step, each observation matrix
\mathbb{O}_{a}
has dimension
U\times S
and the value contained in a cell
\mathbb{O}_{a}(u|s)
will be:

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}_{a}(u|s)=P(u\in\mathcal{I}_{ab}|s,a)=\int_{u_{a}}^{u_{b}}P(do|s,a)do,

where
u_{a}<u_{b}
are the extremes of interval
\mathcal{I}_{ab}
.
If the integrals can be correctly computed, no discretization errors will be introduced with this procedure. The newly generated observation matrices will need to satisfy Assumption
4.2
.

It is not an easy problem to find the splitting points in the observation space that allow defining observation matrices with a high minimum singular value
\sigma_{\min}
; however, it is possible to devise an optimization procedure that proceeds over multiple runs.

For each run, a set of splitting points is randomly chosen, and the new observation matrices are computed together with their minimum singular value. At the end of the trials, the splitting points of the run showing the highest
\sigma_{\min}
will be chosen.

Finally, during the estimation procedure in Algorithm
1
, the vector of counts
\bm{N}
has dimension
U^{2}A^{2}
and, for each tuple
(a,a^{\prime},u,u^{\prime})
, it will increment by 1 the right cell, based on the intervals to which
u
and
u^{\prime}
belong.

Estimation Error Bounds of Stationary Distributions induced by Markov Chains

In this section, we are going to show the main concentration results that are used in the proof of Lemma

.

Lemma
G.1
shows the convergence guarantees when the estimation comes from a stationary chain (the initial distribution coincides with the stationary one) while Lemma
G.3
considers the case when the chain starts from an arbitrary distribution.

Lemma G.1

(
Stationary Distribution under Stationary Chain
)
.

Let us assume to have a POMDP instance
\mathcal{Q}
with a finite number of states
\mathcal{S}
and a finite number of actions
\mathcal{A}
. Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
be the belief-based policy used on
\mathcal{Q}
and let us assume that the induced stationary distribution
\bm{d}_{AS}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
is ergodic, thus having that
\bm{d}_{AS}(a,s)>0\;\forall a\in\mathcal{A},s\in\mathcal{S}
. Let Assumption
4.2
hold. If the process starts from its stationary distribution
\bm{d}_{AS}
, then by using the estimation procedure defined in Algorithm
1
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\|\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\Big(\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{1-\lambda_{\max}}\Big)\frac{(2+5\log(1/\delta))}{2n}},

where
\lambda_{\max}
denotes the maximum spectral gap associated with the inhomogeneous chain, while
n
denotes the number of tuples of the form
(a,a^{\prime},o,o^{\prime})
collected during the interaction with the environment.

Proof.

Since the chain is stationary, it also follows that the initial distribution
\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}
coincides with the stationary one.

To prove this lemma, we combine the results on the difference of probability vectors presented in
Kontorovich and Weiss (2012)
(see their Section 2.4) with the new concentration results of
Fan, Jiang, and Sun (2021)
and obtain the following:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\|\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\|_{2}>\mathbb{E}\|\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\|_{2}+\epsilon\big)\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{1-\lambda_{\max}}{1+\lambda_{\max}}2n\epsilon^{2}\Big).

(60)

To proceed with this part, we need to bound
\mathbb{E}\|\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\|_{2}
. This is done in Lemma
G.2
that shows that the following relation holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{n(1-\lambda_{\max})}}.

The presented result allows us to rewrite the relation in (
60

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg(\|\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\|_{2}>\sqrt{\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{n(1-\lambda_{\max})}}+\epsilon\Bigg)\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}2n\epsilon^{2}\Big).

By setting the right-hand side of the bound equal to
\delta
, and rearranging the terms, we are able to get the final result of the Lemma.
∎

We report here the claim and the proof of Lemma
G.2
which is used to demonstrate the lemma above.

Lemma G.2
.

Under the assumption of starting from a stationary distribution
\bm{d}_{AS}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\bm{d}_{A^{2}O^{2}}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{A^{2}O^{2}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{n(1-\lambda_{\max})}}

Proof.

The proof largely follows from
Kontorovich and Weiss (2012)
. To reduce clutter, we will omit the pedices
A^{2}O^{2}
when present. By Jensen’s inequality, we have:

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}\|\bm{d}-\widehat{\bm{d}}\|_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|\bm{d}-\widehat{\bm{d}}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{y\in[A^{2}O^{2}]}|\bm{d}_{y}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{y}|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in[A^{2}O^{2}]}\mathbb{E}\big[(\bm{d}_{y}-\widehat{\bm{d}}_{y})^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in[A^{2}O^{2}]}var\big[\widehat{\bm{d}}_{y}\big],

(61)

\bm{d}_{y}

y

\bm{d}

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{1-\lambda_{\max}}\Big)n\bm{d}_{y}(1-\bm{d}_{y}),

with
\lambda_{\max}

From this consideration, it also follows that:

\displaystyle var\big[\widehat{\bm{d}}_{y}\big]\leq\Big(\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{1-\lambda_{\max}}\Big)\frac{\bm{d}_{y}}{n},

that slightly improves over the result in
Kontorovich and Weiss (2012)
. Reusing this relation in line (

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}\|\bm{d}-\widehat{\bm{d}}\|_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y\in[A^{2}O^{2}]}var\big[\widehat{\bm{d}}_{y}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y\in[A^{2}O^{2}]}\Big(\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{1-\lambda_{\max}}\Big)\frac{\bm{d}_{y}}{n}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1+\lambda_{\max}}{1-\lambda_{\max}}\Big)\frac{1}{n},

where the last equality follows since

∎

H
Useful Results for Belief MDPs

In this section, we will present a relevant result reported in
Zhou et al. (2021)
that bounds the bias span in a belief MDP. Then, we will present another core result coming from
De Castro, Gassiat, and Le Corff (2017)
that allows bounding the belief error with respect to the estimation error of an HMM model.

We start by reporting the average reward Bellman expectation equation for belief MDPs under a belief-based policy
\pi

\displaystyle\rho^{\pi}+v_{\pi}(b)\;=\underset{a\sim\pi(\cdot|b)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[g(b,a)+\int_{\mathcal{B}}P(\,db^{\prime}|b,a)v_{\pi}(b^{\prime})\right].

(63)

We report as well the average reward Bellman optimality equation for belief MDPs:

\displaystyle\rho^{*}+v(b)\;=\underset{a\in\mathcal{A}}{\max}\left[g(b,a)+\int_{\mathcal{B}}P(\,db^{\prime}|b,a)v(b^{\prime})\right].

(64)

Various conditions allow the two equations to be satisfied. One condition requires the MDP to be weakly communicating
(
Bertsekas 1995
)
. In particular, this condition is verified under Assumption
4.1
, as also shown in
Zhou et al. (2021)
. In particular, they derive a result that bounds the span of the bias function under this assumption:

Proposition H.1

(Uniform bound on the bias span from
Zhou et al. (2021)

holds, then for

\displaystyle D(\epsilon):=\frac{8\Big(\frac{2}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}+(1+\alpha)\log_{a}\big(\frac{1-\alpha}{8}\big)\Big)}{1-\alpha},\qquad\text{with}\qquad\alpha=\frac{1-2\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\in(0,1).

For all

associated with the estimated optimistic belief MDP

is bounded by

uniformly in

It is possible to see that the bound stated in Proposition

The following result instead shows the relation between vectors which are obtained by the aggregation of higher-dimensional ones.

Lemma I.2

(Aggregation Lemma)
.

Let
\bm{M}
be a matrix of dimension
X\times Y
and have positive values. Let
\widehat{\bm{M}}
be an estimation of
\bm{M}
. Let now
\bm{c}
be a vector of dimension
X
obtained by summing all the elements of
\bm{M}
along the second dimension, such that
\bm{c}(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\bm{M}(i,j)
and let
\widehat{\bm{c}}
be a vector obtained with the same procedure from
\widehat{\bm{M}}
. Then we will have:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{Y}\|\widehat{\bm{M}}-\bm{M}\|_{F}

Proof.

Let us first consider the vectorized version of matrix
\bm{M}
and denote it with
\bm{m}
having size
X\cdot Y
. Analogously, we define
\widehat{\bm{m}}
.
Let us now define the transformation from
\bm{m}
to
\bm{c}
using vector notation. We will have:

\displaystyle\bm{c}=\bm{A}\bm{m},

where
\bm{A}
is a matrix of size
X\times X\cdot Y
and it is defined as
\bm{A}:=\mathcal{I}_{X}\otimes\mathds{1}_{Y}^{\top}
where
\mathcal{I}_{X}
is the identity matrix of size
X
,
\mathds{1}_{Y}
is a vector of ones of dimension
Y
, while the symbol
\otimes
represents the Kronecker product between the two.
This allows us to write:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\bm{c}}-\bm{c}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{A}(\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{m})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{A}\|_{2}\|\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{m}\|_{2}

(65)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A})\|\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{m}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\max}(\mathcal{I}_{X})\sigma_{\max}(\mathds{1}_{Y})\|\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{m}\|_{2}

(66)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}^{1/2}(\mathds{1}_{Y}^{\top}\mathds{1}_{Y})\|\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{m}\|_{2}=\sqrt{Y}\|\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{m}\|_{2}

(67)

where the first inequality in line

\bm{A}

\sigma_{\max}^{2}(\mathds{1}_{Y})=\lambda_{\max}(\mathds{1}_{Y}^{\top}\mathds{1}_{Y})

Simulation Details

In this section, we provide details on the experiments reported in the main paper. All the reported experiments have been run on an notebook with CPU Intel i7-11th and 16G RAM using Ubuntu as OS.

Estimation Error of Transition Matrix

The number of states, actions, and observations of the instances used for the experiments of the estimation error of the transition model are reported in the legend of Figure
2
. Given each instance considered in the experiment, the transition and observation matrices have been generated as follows. An initial version of transition and observation matrices is generated with random elements and, subsequently:

1.

regarding the transition matrix, the experiments are reported for instances having transition matrices where the minimum transition probability satisfies
\epsilon=1/5S
, with
S
being the number of states.

2.

regarding the observation matrix, for each pair of states and actions, we choose a specific observation that will be drawn with higher probability, in order to avoid having too much stochasticity in the reward distributions and ensure a diverse observation distribution among states. We require that the generated observation matrix has a minimum singular value lower bounded by a quantity
\alpha>=0.001
, thus satisfying our Assumption
4.2
.

3.

The policy class is characterized by a minimum action sampling probability
\iota=1/10A
with
A
being the number of actions.

The experiment is conducted in the following way. A POMDP instance is generated by giving as input the number of states, number of actions, and number of observations and by following the procedures highlighted in points 1. and 2.

Samples are collected using a belief-based policy
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
that updates its belief using the real observation matrix and a different transition matrix randomly generated, as specified in point 1. This transition matrix is not updated but kept fixed during the whole experiment. We highlight that this transition matrix is only used by the belief-based policy to induce a specific action-state distribution
\bm{d}_{AS}
and is indeed independent of the real transition model that needs to be estimated.

The minimum action probability of the belief-based policy
\pi
is defined as in point 3.

The policy collects samples and updates its belief based on its internal transition matrix, while the underlying process follows the dynamics induced by the real transition model. The collected samples are then provided to the OAS algorithm (Algorithm
1
) which gives as output an estimate of the real transition model.

Regret Experiments

The experimental results on the regret have been conducted as described in the following.

Because of the computational complexity of finding the optimal policy that maximizes the long-run average reward in a known environment, we conducted the experiment by discretizing the belief space, allowing us to apply common techniques used for finite state spaces. We are thus able to solve the optimal Bellman equation appearing in
4
, where the computation of the optimal policy and the optimistic transition model is done by adapting the Extended Value Iteration algorithm
(
Jaksch, Ortner, and Auer 2010
)
to the discretized state space.

The uncertainty coming from the estimates of the transition model is defined by the theoretical results obtained for the OAS algorithm. As commonly done in many practical approaches, the theoretical bounds are replaced by much smaller values. We recall that using scaled values rather than those suggested by theory mostly translates into a regret with bigger multiplicative constants or holding with smaller probability. This approach is common when performing experimental comparisons in these settings (as observed in Remark 3 in
Azizzadenesheli, Lazaric, and
Anandkumar (2016)
).

SEEU algorithm
(
Xiong et al. 2022
)

In the experiments, we do not use the classical Spectral Decomposition approach, but we help the estimation procedure in the following way:

•

The matrices used by the Spectral Decomposition approach are provided with more information with respect to what is commonly done. In particular, instead of updating the matrices based on the observation seen when pulling an arm, we directly provide the matrix with the probabilities defining the observation distribution of the pulled arm. This caveat helps the estimation of the transition matrix and that of the observation model since vanishes the noise given by the realizations of the observations.

•

The computation of the optimistic policy for the SEEU algorithm is done by using the estimate of the transition model but is provided with the real observation model, as also done for the OAS-UCRL algorithm.

The parameters used for the SEEU algorithm are
\tau_{1}=20000
and
\tau_{2}=65000
which are used to determine the length of the exploration and the exploitation phase respectively.

PSRL-POMDP
(
Jafarnia Jahromi, Jain, and Nayyar 2022
)

Since the authors present a computationally intractable update of the model parameters, we opted for an implementation that makes use of the particle filter approach, commonly used in the Bayesian setting. We recall that the authors provide theoretical guarantees for this algorithm under the assumption that the employed estimator is consistent. However, in practice, there are no provably consistent estimators based on a Bayesian approach.

We implement their algorithm 1 by using the particles to represent the prior distributions and, referring to the parameters of the PSRL-POMDP algorithm, we set:

•

SCHED(t_{k},T_{k-1})=t_{k}+T_{k-1}
with
t_{k}
representing the length of the
k
-th episode, as suggested in their work.

•

\widetilde{m}_{t}(s,a)=n_{t}(a)
with
\widetilde{m}_{t}(s,a)
being an upper bound to the expected number of times the pair
(s,a)
has been encountered up to time
t
.
n_{t}(a)
instead counts the number of times action
a
has been pulled up to time
t
. This choice of
\widetilde{m}_{t}(s,a)
is suggested in their work.

•

N=100
particles have been used in the experiments, while updates of the particles are triggered when the
effective sample size
(ESS) associated with their weights goes below
30
.

The choice of these values is performed by testing different hyperparameters both for the number
N
of particles and the effective sample size (ESS). This combination has the best results while also not requiring extensive computational time, which is a typical problem of Particle-based approaches when the number of used particles gets high.
