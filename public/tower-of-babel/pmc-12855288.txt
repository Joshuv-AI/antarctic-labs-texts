A novel chromatography method for high purity production of Recombinant human parathyroid hormone from E. coli
Authors: Farhad Mollasoltani, Milad Adak, HomaSadat Esfahani, Elaheh Roshani Yasaghi, Sirwan Khanchezar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman parathyroid hormone (hPTH) shows great potential as a therapeutic option for osteoporosis in patients who do not respond sufficiently to conventional therapies. We utilize recombinant DNA technology to enhance the drug development process and introduce a state-of-the-art purification protocol for the first 34 residues of human parathyroid hormone (hPTH), as these residues are responsible for the majority of its hormonal activity. Typically, the purification of recombinant human parathyroid hormone has relied on His-Tag fusion partners using Ni²⁺-chelating chromatography. This is the first time ion exchange chromatography has been used for the soluble PTH form, and no reports of ion exchange chromatography for this purpose have been documented. SUMO-tag technology and a three-step chromatography process were used to purify recombinant human parathyroid hormone [rhPTH1–34 on a large scale from Escherichia coli. The rhPTH (1–84) construct was cloned into E. coli host cells and expressed at fermenter scale with a 5-liter volume. The SUMO-hPTH1–34 fusion protein was extracted and purified using a Capto™ adhere column. The N-terminus of SUMO-hPTH was specifically cleaved with SUMO protease, after which rhPTH was further purified through sequential chromatography on Capto adhere and Capto SP columns. Our purification procedure for rhPTH from E. coli achieved over 98% purity, as confirmed by SDS-PAGE and RP-HPLC. This study demonstrates that the purified rhPTH1–34 meets the purity standards for commercial drug specifications. We propose that our three-step chromatography process effectively purifies active rhPTH1–34 derived from E. coli. Compared with IMAC and RPC, the purification steps used in this article are more cost-effective and environmentally friendly. This methodology has the potential to be scaled up in an industrial setting.Keywords: Recombinant human parathyroid hormone [hPTH] (1–34), SUMO-tag protein, Purification, Ion exchange chromatography, Large-scale preparationSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology

Introduction

Osteoporosis is characterized by low bone mass and abnormal skeletal architecture, which leads to impaired bone strength and an increased risk of fragility fractures1,2. Approximately 20% of men and 50% of women over the age of 50 are at high risk of fractures caused by osteoporosis3. In Iran alone, an estimated 154,530 osteoporotic fractures occurred in 2020, resulting in 3,554 deaths4. The demand for pharmacological treatments for osteoporosis is significant due to these statistics and the potential for improved outcomes with new medications.

To address the challenges posed by osteoporosis, various pharmacological interventions have been developed. Teriparatide, a recombinant form of human parathyroid hormone, has been approved in Iran for the treatment of osteoporosis. Teriparatide increases bone mass and improves bone microarchitecture, thereby enhancing bone density and strength. It significantly reduces fracture risk by stimulating new bone formation5–7.

This peptide drug consists of the first 34 N-terminal amino acids of the full-length parathyroid hormone (84 amino acids), which is the minimal segment required for the hormone’s biological activity8–11. Teriparatide1–34 is produced using a genetically modified strain of E.coli through recombinant DNA technology12. Nowadays, recombinant drugs are produced in a variety of hosts13,14, with E. coli being the most widely used due to its rapid growth, high yield, cost-effectiveness, and scalability15.

However, several challenges arise when producing recombinant hPTH (rhPTH) in E. coli, including degradation by host intracellular proteases, the addition of methionine at the N-terminus due to the host’s translation system, and low solubility of the product at higher concentrations. Additional obstacles include the formation of insoluble inclusion bodies, challenges in achieving proper disulfide bond formation, susceptibility to proteolytic cleavage during purification, and the need for rigorous removal of endotoxins to meet pharmaceutical standards16–18.

To address these production hurdles, innovative strategies such as fusion protein technology have been developed, significantly improving the yield and activity of the hormone. These approaches not only improve expression levels in E. coli but also simplify downstream purification and recovery of bioactive rhPTH16,19. Incorporating a SUMO (Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier) tag at the N-terminus of the coding sequence can improve protein solubility and facilitate purification. Importantly, kinetic analyses have demonstrated that SUMO protease exhibits markedly higher catalytic efficiency than commonly used proteases such as TEV, with up to ~ 25-fold higher turnover20. This high activity translates into substantially lower enzyme requirements, with efficient cleavage reported at protease-to-substrate ratios as low as 1:500021.

In contrast to many proteases, which often leave residual amino acids on the N-terminus after cleavage, SUMO protease recognizes the tertiary structure of the SUMO domain and cleaves precisely at its natural di-glycine (GG) motif. This ensures highly specific processing and the release of the target protein with a native N-terminus and no extra residues (scarless cleavage)22. In addition, SUMO protease can cleave efficiently under a wide range of conditions, including pH, temperature, and ionic strength23. Taken together, these features make SUMO fusion technology as a practical option for large-scale production of sensitive therapeutic peptides such as hPTH1–34.

After the production phase, the focus shifts to purifying the recombinant protein effectively. Developing an efficient purification strategy is essential for recovering biologically active products. The required purity level varies based on the final application of the recombinant protein, and large-scale production often necessitates tailored parameters and standardizations24,25. There is no universal method for recombinant protein purification; the choice of purification technique depends on the specific properties of the protein.

Several chromatographic methods are employed to achieve the necessary purity for therapeutic purposes, including affinity chromatography, ion exchange, hydrophobic interaction, and size exclusion chromatography25,26. In particular, affinity chromatography using Ni-NTA (Nickel-Nitrilotriacetic Acid) resin is widely utilized for purifying rhPTH1-3413,27–29. However, using nickel columns (Ni-NTA) for purifying rhPTH1-34 can present several challenges: (1) Unwanted proteins or contaminants may bind to the nickel resin, reducing the purity of the rhPTH1-34. (2) Efficiently eluting rhPTH1-34 from the nickel resin without affecting its activity can be challenging. (3) Nickel-based resins, such as Ni-NTA, generate strong oxidizing agents under light irradiation, which can have several effects on protein purification. Oxidizing agents can modify amino acids, leading to structural changes in the protein. In addition, this photocatalytic activity can lead to degradation of the target protein30–32.

In this study, we utilized a genetically modified strain of E. coli to produce hPTH as a fusion protein through recombinant DNA technology. We added a SUMO tag to enhance protein solubility and stability, as well as to facilitate effective purification. The main focus of this work is the development and optimization of a downstream purification protocol tailored for the soluble fusion protein. The downstream purification process for the soluble fusion protein included several key steps: clarification, capture using multimodal chromatography resin, ultrafiltration/diafiltration, enzymatic digestion, intermediate purification with multimodal chromatography resin, and a polishing step with a strong cation exchanger for the drug formulation.

Figure 1 schematically illustrates our workflow. These steps (Three-Step Chromatography Method) aim to mitigate the nonspecific binding of untagged proteins, thereby enhancing the purity and specificity of the target protein isolation. Our study presents a valuable and cost-effective method for purifying hPTH, which could significantly benefit industrial-scale production of this essential protein.

Materials and methods

Materials

EDTA, L-methionine, PolyDADMAC, Triton X100, Acetonitrile, and NaCl were purchased from Merck Chemical Co. SUMO protease was produced in-house by Ronak Pharmaceutical Co.

Other chemicals used throughout the experiments were also supplied by Merck Chemical Co.

Sequence information

The recombinant construct was designed to facilitate efficient expression, purification, and precise removal of fusion tags. The expression vector pET-24b(+) containing a T7 promoter was used, and expression was induced with rhamnose-PTG (RPTG) as the inducer. The construct encoded an N-terminal hexahistidine (His) tag fused directly to the Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier (SUMO) tag. The SUMO domain terminates with its natural di-glycine (GG) motif, which serves as the recognition and cleavage site for SUMO protease. This arrangement enables specific proteolytic cleavage, releasing the native target protein without any additional residues. Following cleavage, the final recombinant hPTH1–34 product corresponds exactly to the native sequence: SVSEIQLMHNLGKHLNSMERVEWLRKKLQDVHNF.

Figure 2 shows a schematic representation of the fusion construct used for recombinant hPTH1–34 production. The construct contains an N-terminal His-tag fused to the SUMO tag, which ends with its natural di-glycine (GG) residues serving as the SUMO protease cleavage site. Cleavage at this site releases the native hPTH1–34 sequence without any additional residues.

The peptide sequence exhibits the following properties: Molecular Weight: 4117.76 Da, Theoretical Isoelectric Point (pI): 8.29, and Grand Average of Hydropathicity (GRAVY): −0.671.

The complete amino acid sequence, including the Histidine and SUMO tags, has the following properties: Grand Average of Hydropathicity (GRAVY): −0.880, Molecular Weight: 17138.35 Da, and Theoretical Isoelectric Point (pI): 6.25. Properties of the SUMO Tag Alone: Molecular Weight: 13038.61 Da and Theoretical Isoelectric Point (pI): 5.87.

Batch fermentation of E. coli BL21(DE3)

For large-scale expression, E. coli BL21(DE3) cells harboring the recombinant construct were cultivated in a 5 L fermenter containing M9 minimal medium. The fermentation was carried out in a fed-batch at 32 °C with continuous agitation and aeration to maintain dissolved oxygen above 30%. The pH of the culture was controlled at 6.7 ± 0.1 using ammonium solution 25% for pH adjustment. Under these conditions, a biomass yield of approximately 700–800 g wet weight was obtained per 4.5 L batch culture.

Chromatography resins and columns

The chromatography experiments were performed on an ÄKTA pure™ 150 chromatography system with Unicorn 7.0 version software (https://www.cytivalifesciences.com/en/us/shop/cell-therapy/software/unicorn-7-p-05649?psmenu=0, Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden). Capto™ Adhere Impres resin (Catalog Number: [17371502], Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) was employed for capture and intermediate steps. The resin was packed into an XK 26/20 column (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden), which was equipped with a bed height of 13 cm, resulting in a total column volume of 69 ml. Capto™ SP Impres resin (Catalog Number: [17546803], Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) was utilized for the polishing step. The resin was packed into an XK 26/20 column (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) with a bed height of 13 cm, resulting in a column volume of 69 mL.

Cell disruption and clarification of fusion protein

The bacterial biomass prepared in the upstream unit was submerged in a lysis buffer (PBS, 9 mM; NaCl, 150 mM; EDTA, 1 mM; L-methionine, 1 mM; pH 7.3), at a ratio of approximately 1 g wet cell mass per 10 mL of buffer (biomass/buffer ratio 1:10, w/v) for 30 min at a temperature of 4 °C. Cell walls were disrupted using a homogenizer (APV 1500, USA) at 900 ± 50 bar. To remove cell debris from the supernatant, PolyDADMAC was added from a 10% stock solution to the lysis buffer, resulting in a final concentration of 0.05%. The suspension was incubated for one hour at 4 °C, then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 40 min at a temperature of 4 °C (Sigma 6–16KS).

Capturing step of hPTH purification

The supernatant was loaded into a Capto™ adhere column (XK 26/20) equilibrated with lysis buffer. A wash buffer (PBS, 9 mM; EDTA, 1 mM; NaCl, 150 mM; L-methionine, 1 mM; Triton X-100, 0.1%; pH 7.3) was used to remove non-specifically bound proteins. An Elution buffer (PBS 9 mM; EDTA 1 mM; NaCl, 400 mM; L-methionine 1 mM; pH 7.3) was used to collect the target protein. The flow rate for all the steps was maintained at 156 cm/h. The eluted protein was analyzed using SDS-PAGE.

Buffer exchanging and concentrating of the purified SUMO hPTH fusion protein

Buffer exchange of the Capto™ adhere eluate was first concentrated to 1/2 volume and then diafiltrated 7 times against equilibration buffer-EQ buffer (PBS, 9 mM; EDTA, 1 mM; L-methionine, 1 mM; pH 7.3) using CYTIVA-AKTA Flux 6 Tangential-Flow Filtration (TFF) with a Sartocon Slice PESU 10 kDa filter. To cleave hPTH from the fusion segment, the Ultrafiltration/diafiltration (UF/DF) fusion protein was treated with SUMO protease (protein ratio of 1/50 w/w) and stirred for 10 min. Intermediate chromatography purification step of the Recombinant hPTH

Following enzymatic cleavage, the sample was loaded onto a Capto™ adhere column (XK 26/20) in flowthrough mode, equilibrated with PBS (9 mM), EDTA (1 mM), and L-methionine (1 mM), pH 7.3, at 4 °C. The target rhPTH1–34 was collected in the flowthrough, while major impurities remained on the column and were eluted with an elution buffer containing 2 M NaCl. The flow rate for all steps was set to 156 cm/h. The collected flowthrough was analyzed using SDS-PAGE.

Polishing chromatography purification step of the Recombinant hPTH

To further enhance purity, the flowthrough was loaded onto an equilibrated Capto™ SP column (XK 26/20) using an equilibration buffer (PBS, 9 mM; EDTA, 1 mM; L-methionine, 1 mM; pH 7.3). The highly purified recombinant hPTH1–34 peptide was eluted using an elution buffer (PBS, 9 mM; EDTA, 1 mM; L-methionine, 1 mM; NaCl, 200 mM; pH 7.3). The flow rate was maintained at 156 cm/h, and fractions were collected and stored at 4 °C for further analysis.

Purity analysis

To assess the purity of the Capto™ SP fractions, RP-HPLC analysis was performed using a Hawach C18 Universal HPLC column. The RP-HPLC chromatogram of hPTH1–34 was obtained using a linear gradient from 100% Solution A to 100% Solution B over 40 min at a flow rate of 1 ml/min. Solution A contained acetonitrile in a buffer solution (10:90), while Solution B contained acetonitrile in a buffer solution (50:50). The purified rhPTH1–34 and hPTH standards were diluted in a dilution buffer (222 mM anhydrous sodium sulfate, pH 2.3) to a final concentration of 0.7 mg/ml. Subsequently, 40 µl of each diluted sample was applied to the RP-HPLC column. Protein purity was determined using a UV detector at 214 nm at 40 °C.

Results

Extraction of SUMO-Tagged Recombinant hPTH and Capto™ adhere column purification

The Fusion protein was obtained in a soluble form. Cell Disruption was carried out as described in the Materials and Methods section. Briefly, the harvested cells were resuspended in lysis buffer and homogenized. During this step, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) was employed to prevent protease activity. Antioxidants like L-methionine were added to prevent the oxidation of methionine 8 and 18 in the PTH structure. Therefore, the use of nickel affinity resin is not recommended under these conditions, as both the chelating effect of EDTA and the oxidative sensitivity of methionine residues pose significant challenges to protein integrity. Here, we directly loaded the clarified product onto the column without the need for additional buffer exchange.

Polydiallyldimethylammonium chloride (polyDADMAC), a cationic polyelectrolyte, was utilized in the initial clarification process to remove host cell debris by promoting the formation of larger flocculi that help separate the negatively charged cell debris from the supernatant. Following the disruption of bacterial cells, soluble cell fractions were isolated using centrifugation and then loaded onto a multimodal chromatography Capto adhere column (as shown in Fig. 3-A). Next, SDS-PAGE was used to analyze both samples (Fig. 3-B).

Capto™ SP column Polishing step and purity analysis

Various cation exchange resins were utilized in our study to assess their effectiveness in removing impurities (data not shown). The Capto™ SP column was used (Fig. 5-A) to separate small proteins due to its two advantages. First, the Capto™ SP with a particle size of approximately 36 μm provides excellent resolution for the separation of small proteins and allows for a high flow rate and minimal non-specific interactions. Second, there is no need to re-equilibrate the column between two consecutive steps, resulting in greatly improved efficiency.

Discussion

The main purpose of the downstream process is to produce a pharmaceutical product with the appropriate level of efficacy, purity, and unit cost33. This stage is essential to ensuring the suitability of drug production from the extraction to the final product, free of impurities. Depending on the type of production, specific procedures and methods are required. The extraction and purification processes for recombinant human parathyroid hormone (rhPTH) from E. coli have evolved considerably, with numerous techniques developed to enhance yield and purity28,31,34. Nevertheless, several challenges remain with these methods, including low levels of recombinant protein expression, environmental contamination, the use of expensive chemicals and enzymes like Kex2 and enteropeptidase or two proteases for rhPTH cleavage from fusion partners. Due to its low molecular weight and lack of stable tertiary structure, hPTH1–34 is highly prone to proteolytic degradation and aggregation in E. coli. These properties lead to poor solubility and frequent inclusion body formation, complicating its purification. Formation of inclusion bodies and the need for complex protein refolding steps remain significant bottlenecks in achieving high recovery and bioactivity of the final product.

One of the primary strengths of our strategy is that hPTH is expressed as a soluble SUMO fusion, completely avoiding inclusion body formation. To our knowledge, this is the first report of directly loading the soluble fusion protein onto an anion-exchange chromatography column—bypassing chaotrope-based solubilization (e.g., urea or guanidine hydrochloride) and complex refolding protocols. In contrast to previously reported methods employing anion-exchange chromatography—such as those described by Abbaszadeh et al., and Al-Badran et al.29,31—which rely on inclusion body formation, denaturing solubilization, and in vitro refolding before purification, our strategy maintains the native conformation and bioactivity of the protein, shortens processing time and reduces costs, and significantly enhances scalability and batch-to-batch consistency. This innovation underscores the feasibility of our method for cost-effective and environmentally friendly production of hPTH, especially in industrial settings where minimal processing steps are highly desirable.

In the study by Wang et al.35, the soluble but non-fusion form was also used. A key difference between our methodology and that of Wang et al. is the variation in the type of resin employed. In our study, we employed two types of resins, whereas Wang et al. used three distinct resins, which significantly increases the cost at an industrial scale. Furthermore, in their intermediate and polishing steps, they utilized hydrophobic interaction and size-exclusion chromatography columns. These methods are less favorable for large-scale production due to their high operational costs. Hydrophobic columns require high salt concentrations, which further drive up expenses, and size-exclusion chromatography is generally not preferred in industrial settings due to its limited scalability and throughput. A brief note should be made regarding the use of a bicistronic expression system in Wang et al.‘s study, which may introduce limitations in downstream gene expression and protein stability. While this aspect is noteworthy, it falls outside the scope of our current work, which is primarily focused on optimizing the purification protocol—but it remains important to keep in mind.

In contrast to the protocol described by Abbaszadeh et al. and E. Al-badran et al., where protein expression leads to inclusion body formation requiring denaturing lysis with 8 M urea and chemical additives such as lysozyme and β-mercaptoethanol, our system yields soluble PTH, eliminating the need for harsh solubilization and refolding steps. Their workflow involves sonication in a denaturing buffer, followed by multi-step washing, which can compromise protein integrity and bioactivity. Similar to many previous studies, their purification strategy begins with Ni–NTA affinity chromatography (Abbaszadeh et al. via Ni–NTA affinity chromatography and E. Al-badran et al. via g His LinkTM Protein Purification Resin) —a widely used method for His-tagged proteins. However, as discussed in detail elsewhere in this work, the use of affinity resins presents several drawbacks, including potential metal contamination, non-specific binding, and challenges in downstream polishing. Furthermore, in the subsequent purification steps, their protocol incorporates ion exchange chromatography under which differs fundamentally from our approach. To better demonstrate the advantages of our protocol, we included a comparative analysis (Table 3) focused on studies that utilized ion-exchange chromatography columns. This aims to clearly highlight our process’s practical benefits.

The other critical parameter influencing the total yield of the purification process is the proteolytic cleavage of the fusion protein. Therefore, it is important to emphasize that the hPTH fusion protein must be protected from nonspecific digestion. To overcome the solubility and degradation challenges of rhPTH1–34, we utilize the SUMO fusion system. The SUMO tag not only enhances the solubility and stability of the recombinant peptide during expression in E. coli, but also serves as a precise protease recognition site, allowing efficient cleavage and recovery of native hPTH with a correct N-terminal sequence36,37. The production of the target protein with a native N-terminus is facilitated by the effective removal of the SUMO tag by SUMO protease under in vitro conditions. The advantage of this technique is further enhanced by the availability of naturally occurring SUMO proteases22. Importantly, the SUMO protease used in this study is much more cost-effective compared to commonly employed enzymes such as Enterokinase, both in terms of market price per unit and catalytic efficiency. Beyond its relatively high cost, Enterokinase presents several limitations that restrict its use in biopharmaceutical applications. Despite being a commonly used enzyme for tag removal in recombinant protein purification28,29,31, its nonspecific proteolytic activity poses a major challenge, often resulting in unintended cleavage of internal peptide bonds within the target protein. Additionally, secondary substrate preferences have been observed in Enteropeptidases from different species, which can lead to off-target hydrolysis beyond the canonical (Asp)₄-Lys recognition site. These factors compromise product integrity and reduce purification efficiency, making Enterokinase less suitable for sensitive therapeutic peptides like rhPTH1–34,38–41. Looking ahead, our results also emphasize the importance of selecting efficient fusion partners and purification strategies to overcome long-standing limitations in downstream processing. In this context, emerging affinity platforms such as Protein Select™, iCapTag™, and intein-based self-cleaving systems represent a promising evolution in purification technology. By enabling the controlled and spontaneous release of native proteins without the need for exogenous proteases, these systems could eliminate the enzymatic cleavage step. Such approaches share the same objectives demonstrated in our work, reducing process complexity, shortening purification workflows, and lowering production costs, and may, in the future, offer even more streamlined and scalable solutions for industrial bio-manufacturing.

Another notable advantage of our strategy lies in the flexibility it offers for selecting an appropriate chromatography method during purification. Affinity chromatography, commonly employed to separate protein tags from host cell proteins, was initially considered due to its efficiency on a laboratory scale25,42,43. However, when producing proteins on an industrial scale, affinity chromatography, especially Ni2+ resin, can be expensive. Ni2 + column large-scale application is often limited by the higher cost of resins and the need for stripping with EDTA and recharging with Ni²⁺ ions after only a few runs (3to 5 runs). By contrast, ion-exchange resins can be reused for many more runs with lower cost, which is why in industrial practice, ion-exchange chromatography is generally preferred over affinity systems for large-scale protein purification.

Moreover, one problem with using polyhistidine affinity tags is the nonspecific binding of untagged proteins44. In our study, nonspecific binding and digestion of the target protein were observed when we examined the nickel resin’s efficiency. O. Audu and colleagues have also reported non-specific interactions of the peptide rhPTH1–34 (4 kDa) with the Ni2+ column45. In a study by E. Al-Badran et al., the affinity chromatography capturing step using HisLink™ Protein Purification Resin (Ni-chelating resin) resulted in some fusion protein loss due to the washing buffer, indicating that nickel IMAC resin is not a good choice for the rhPTH capture step29. Considering these limitations, we chose an alternative approach that employed Capto™ adhere chromatography to reduce nonspecific interactions in the initial purification stage. Based on the absorption principle, Capto™ adhere has a high sample loading capacity and is suitable for capturing steps as the main method in the primary step of protein purification46. There are numerous benefits to using this method: First, Capto™ adhere simplifies purifying hPTH fusion protein with SUMO tag, Second, it reduces nonspecific hydrophobic interactions of rhPTH fusion protein with the matrices.

We employed multimodal chromatography and strong cation exchanger chromatography for large-scale preparation in the next steps. Capto adhere chromatography was the critical step in the procedure. SUMO protease, the SUMO tag, and uncleaved fusion protein were captured by the Capto™ Adhere resin, while most hPTH was obtained in the flow-through, thereby purifying hPTH from the reaction mixtures. Utilizing Capto adhere in both capture and intermediate steps increases efficiency and optimizes workflow. The Capto™ SP High Performance medium was chosen to separate residual impurities from hPTH in the final purification step. The Capto SP ImpRes base matrix creates excellent pressure and flow characteristics, which, in turn, deliver high-resolution results in polishing. The final yield of rhPTH, after purification, was 42%, higher than in the most efficient fusion protein systems. Nevertheless, there is still room for further optimization to improve recovery.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the optimization of the purification process for recombinant human parathyroid hormone (rhPTH) has been successfully achieved through the strategic use of Capto™ Adhere chromatography and SUMO fusion technology. These methods have demonstrated significant advantages over traditional affinity chromatography, notably in reducing nonspecific interactions and enhancing the yield of the target protein. By utilizing the solubility benefits of the SUMO tag and employing cost-effective SUMO proteases for tag removal, we have enhanced both the efficiency and purity of rhPTH production. Furthermore, the combination of multimodal chromatography and strong cation-exchanger chromatography contributed to a streamlined and effective workflow, minimizing the challenges typically associated with affinity chromatography using Ni-NTA and large-scale protein purification. We believe methods developed here can be effectively applied by the biopharmaceutical industry to the purification of recombinant human parathyroid hormone from E. coli, and they are also worth investigating with other recombinant proteins in bacterial systems.
