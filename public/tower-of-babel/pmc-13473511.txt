Prevalence of sarcopenia and population attributable fraction of related factors in Iranian older adults from the IMOS‑2021 study
Authors: Arash Ghazbani, Amirhossein Aghakhani, Kazem Khalagi, Narges Zargar Balajam, Mohammad Javad Mansourzadeh, Mahnaz Sanjari, Sepideh Hajivalizadeh, Elahe Hesari, Noushin Fahimfar, Afshin Ostovar, Ramin Heshmat, Gita Shafiee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecognizing the growing impact of sarcopenia among older adults in Iran, this study aimed to estimate its prevalence and quantify the population-attributable fraction (PAF) of potential risk factors using data from the Iranian Multicenter Osteoporosis Study (IMOS-2021). A total of 705 participants aged ≥ 60 years completed the IMOS examinations. Sarcopenia was defined as the coexistence of low muscle mass and low muscle strength, and severe sarcopenia as low muscle mass, low muscle strength, and low physical performance. Complex survey analysis was used to calculate the weighted prevalence, and multivariable Poisson regression models were applied to identify associated risk factors and subsequently, their PAFs. The mean age of participants was 67.2 years, and 50.4% were women. The weighted prevalence of sarcopenia was 13.8% (11.1–17.0%), increasing significantly with age (P for trend < 0.001). A body mass index (BMI) < 25 showed a strong association [APR = 5.80 (2.40–13.97)] and the highest PAF (51.0%) for sarcopenia, followed by high fat mass [APR = 2.31 (1.47–3.64); PAF = 42.39%]. Other possible contributing factors were low physical activity (PAF = 28.71%), and small calf circumference (PAF = 16.55%). Sarcopenia is relatively prevalent among Iranian older adults. Low BMI, high adiposity, and low physical activity are key modifiable targets, highlighting the need for prompt nationwide interventions.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Population attributable fraction, Prevalence, Survey, IranSubject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a progressive, generalized skeletal muscle disorder characterized by declines in both muscle mass and function. From around the fifth decade of life, muscle mass and strength decrease annually by approximately 0.8% and 1–3%, respectively1,2. Regarding the significance of this condition, sarcopenia was allocated an ICD-10 code in the year 20163.

The burden of conditions attributed to sarcopenia has raised due to its relatively high prevalence and its association with both short-term and long-term complications4. However, prevalence estimates highly depend on the definition used. A recent meta-analysis reported sarcopenia prevalence ranging from 10% using the revised European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP-2) criteria to 27% when based solely on overall muscle mass5. Moreover, considering the increasing global population of older individuals, the prevalence of sarcopenia is anticipated to rise, rendering it a recognized global public health concern.

As a developing country experiencing a growing elderly demographic, Iran is not exceptional either. While several studies have investigated sarcopenia in Iran, there is a need for nationally representative estimates using consistent, gold-standard methodology6,7. Existing studies have varied considerably in sample sizes, ranging from small single center studies to modest regional samples, and have employed diverse diagnostic criteria, which complicates the accurate estimation of prevalence across different regions and demographic subgroups and limits the generalizability of their findings. Recognizing the impact of musculoskeletal disorders in Iran, this study addresses a key gap by assessing the prevalence of sarcopenia and the population-attributable fraction (PAF) of its associated risk factors. Leveraging data from the Iranian Multicenter Osteoporosis Study (IMOS-2021), it provides valuable clinical and public health insights into the burden of this condition among the country’s older adults.

Methods

Study population, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

The present study was conducted on data from the Iranian Multi-center Osteoporosis Study (IMOS-2021). The complete protocol for IMOS-2021 has been previously detailed in another publication8. IMOS-2021 is a population-based survey conducted in Iran to estimate the prevalence of osteoporosis and sarcopenia, along with their potential associated factors.

Eligible IMOS participants were aged ≥ 50 years, in sufficient physical and mental health to participate, and of both genders. Physical activity levels were assessed by calculating the metabolic equivalent of task (MET) scores using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ). Sufficient physical health is required for participants to engage in low physical activity. Low physical activity was defined as ≤ 600 MET-adjusted minutes per week (see Definition of terms section below for details). Exclusions include weight > 150 kg, conditions severely affecting bone or muscle metabolism (e.g., cystic fibrosis), contraindications to DXA, and contrast-enhanced X-ray imaging within the previous two weeks.

Sampling

The sample size was calculated to estimate the prevalence of osteoporosis and sarcopenia. This part previously described in the article of the study protocol of IMOS8. In the multi-stage random sampling method was used in the survey. Based on geographical region and the prevalence of key osteoporosis risk factors (e.g., calcium intake, sunlight exposure) derived from prior national surveys, the 31 provinces of Iran were stratified into five homogeneous strata using Finite Gaussian Mixture modeling. From each stratum, one or two provinces were randomly selected with probability proportional to size (PPS) of the ≥ 50 years population of that stratum. The eight included provinces were: Tehran, Isfahan, Mazandaran, West Azerbaijan, North Khorasan, Fars, Kermanshah, and Khuzestan. This design aimed to capture national diversity.

Individuals aged ≥ 50 years who had participated in STEPs-2021 were contacted via phone and then invited to participate in IMOS-20219. The STEPs survey is a WHO-endorsed, national cross-sectional study monitoring non-communicable disease (NCD) risk factors through questionnaires, physical measurements, and biochemical assessments in different countries. So far, eight studies have been conducted in Iran from 2005 to 2021, and in the present study, STEPs-2021 data is used9.

Since the aim of the present study is the prevalence of age-related sarcopenia (Primary sarcopenia), therefore, based on the definition of sarcopenia by the European (EWGSOP) and Asian Working Group (AWGS) on sarcopenia, elderly individuals, aged 60 years and older, from the IMOS study were included in this study10,11.

Data collection and definitions

The IMOS measurements consisted of variables assessed through the STEPs study as well as those specifically collected within the IMOS protocol.

Data collection combines:

STEPs study variables Standardized WHO questionnaires and examinations covering demographics, socioeconomic status, medical history, lifestyle factors (smoking, physical activity), physical measurements (blood pressure, anthropometry), and lab tests (lipid profile, Fasting Plasma Glucose (FPG), Hemoglobin A1C, etc.)8,9.

STEPs study variables Standardized WHO questionnaires and examinations covering demographics, socioeconomic status, medical history, lifestyle factors (smoking, physical activity), physical measurements (blood pressure, anthropometry), and lab tests (lipid profile, Fasting Plasma Glucose (FPG), Hemoglobin A1C, etc.)8,9.

IMOS-specific variables The IMOS-specific measurements were conducted through a comprehensive methodological framework encompassing three complementary approaches: questionnaires, physical examinations, and BMD assessments. The IMOS-specific questionnaire package collected information on general bone and muscle health, a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), and medication history. Following the development of the initial questionnaire, its validity and reliability were evaluated8. The IMOS-specific physical examinations were conducted by trained interviewers following the completion of participant interviews at the BMD centers. Body composition was assessed during the same session as BMD measurements using whole-body dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). Specialized lab tests (e.g., vitamin D, PTH) performed on stored serum samples from the STEPs study at designated BMD centers.

Physical activity levels were assessed by calculating the metabolic equivalent of task (MET) scores using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) instrument. GPAQ is a suitable and acceptable instrument for monitoring physical activity in population health surveillance systems12.

Dietary intake was assessed using a 168-item semi-quantitative FFQ, which has been previously validated for use in the country13. The FFQ listed commonly consumed Iranian foods with standard serving sizes, and participants reported intake frequency over the past year. Trained interviewers administered the questionnaire to ensure validity and reliability. Frequencies were converted to daily intake estimates, adjusted for seasonal foods, and entered into Nutritionist IV software for analysis. Subsequently, an analysis of the dietary components, including vitamin D, protein, energy, and fat intake was conducted.

Measurements

Participants’ heights and weights were measured using a fixed stadiometer and a digital scale, following standard protocol with shoes removed and participants dressed in light clothing. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by height squared (m2). Waist circumference was assessed halfway between the lowest rib and hip, almost crossing the umbilicus, in standing upright and at the end of a normal expiration14,15. Although waist circumference was not a primary variable in the present study, it was measured as part of the comprehensive IMOS protocol for potential secondary analyses examining the relationship between central adiposity and musculoskeletal outcomes such as sarcopenia. Calf circumference was measured at the widest level while the participant was standing upright. All measurements were read to the nearest 0.1 cm. Following 15 min in the sitting position, two measurements of systolic and diastolic blood pressure were taken on the right arm, and the mean of the two measurements was considered as the participant’s blood pressure.

As mentioned earlier, DXA scanner (discovery and horizon model, manufactured by HOLOGIC) was used to determine body composition for each person. Participants were instructed to lie in a supine position and to remain motionless throughout the imaging procedure. According to the DXA results, appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) was measured for each participant as the sum of upper and lower limb muscle mass (kg)16. The skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) was then calculated as ASM divided by squared height (m2)17.

Muscle strength was measured by handgrip strength, using a digital dynamometer. The participant is seated with the elbow positioned at the side at a 90° angle and the hand in a neutral position. Grip strength was measured twice for each hand, with the maximum grip strength determined by selecting the highest value from both hands7,18.

Muscle performance of participants was assessed using a 4.57-meter gait speed test. Each participant was instructed to walk at their usual pace across the 4.57-meter distance while the time was recorded in seconds using a chronometer7,18.

lipid profile and FPG were measured by assessing venous samples, drawn after overnight fasting. Using the enzymatic colorimetric method with cholesterol esterase and cholesterol oxidase, total cholesterol was determined. Details for the measurements of FPG, lipid profile were reported elsewhere9.

Definition of terms

Sarcopenia was detected by the presence of low muscle strength with low muscle mass, and severe sarcopenia was considered when low muscle strength, low muscle mass, and low physical performance were all detected7,10. In this study, both sarcopenic and severe sarcopenic individuals were classified as sarcopenia patients. According to the cutoff points recommended by the prior research in Iran, SMI below 5.4 kg/m2 for women and 7.0 kg/m2 for men were classified as low18. The cut-off points for low muscle strength were defined as less than 26 kg for men and less than 18 kg for women18. A gait speed of less than 0.8 m/s was classified as low muscle performance for both men and women10.

One MET is defined as the energy expenditure of one kilocalorie per kilogram per hour while at rest, which corresponds to oxygen consumption of 3.5 ml/kg/min. Low physical activity was classified as having ≤ 600 MET-adjusted minutes per week19.

Current smokers were defined as participants who were smoking cigarettes daily or occasionally as well as those who used water pipe or pipe.

Hypertension was defined as the presence of at least one of the following conditions: (a) systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg; (b) diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg; and consumption of anti-hypertensive medications20.

Hypercholesterolemia was characterized by levels of cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dl21. High-fat mass was characterized as total body percent fat > 30 for men and > 40 for women22. Low calf circumference was defined as < 34 cm for men and < 33 cm for women11.

Dietary protein intake adjusted for energy was categorized into tertiles (1st tertile: <74.12 gr/day − 2nd tertile: 74.12–84.29 gr/day − 3rd tertile: ≥84.30 gr/day).

Statistical analysis

In the present study, IMOS participants aged 60 years and older were included in the final analysis, as the prevalence and clinical manifestations of sarcopenia are more pronounced in this age group. For national prevalence estimates, the final weight was calculated by multiplying W3 with W1 and W2.

We used Poisson regression with robust variance to estimate the adjusted prevalence ratio (APR) and PAF of possible risk factors. This approach was selected because logistic regression, though commonly used, can substantially overestimate associations when the outcome is common, complicating interpretations. Poisson regression, by directly modeling prevalence ratios, provides estimates that are statistically appropriate and more easily interpreted in cross-sectional studies. Univariable and multivariable regression was performed to investigate the factors associated with sarcopenia. Variables were included in the multivariable model if they had a P-value of 0.2 or lower in the univariable model or strong prior biological plausibility from the literature review. In the multivariable analysis, results were reported as an APR and 95% CI. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Furthermore, multicollinearity was assessed using the generalized variance inflation factor. All variables in the final model had a generalized variance inflation factor below 5. Furthermore, no statistically significant interaction was observed between sex and the identified factors (all P for interaction > 0.200). The choice between categorical and continuous forms of variables was guided by model performance.

PAF is an epidemiological measure commonly employed to evaluate the public health impact of specific exposures within populations. PAF is defined as the proportion of all cases of a specific disease or adverse condition in a population that can be attributed to a particular exposure23. Ethics approval and consent to participate

The ethics committee of the Iranian National Institute for Medical Research Development (NIMAD) approved the IMOS-2021 on February 2nd, 2019 (Approval ID: IR.NIMAD.REC.1398.056). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, which included consent for the publication of analyzed data in scientific articles in both domestic and international journals. The study was conducted in full accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Of the 2650 eligible individuals who completed the three steps of the STEPs study, 1,450 individuals participated in IMOS-2021. In the present study, the data of a total of 705 participants aged ≥ 60 were analyzed. The geographic distribution of participants was as follows: Isfahan (22.1%, n = 156), Tehran (16.3%, n = 115), Fars (15.9%, n = 112), Mazandaran (15.0%, n = 106), North Khorasan (11.1%, n = 78), Khuzestan (9.4%, n = 66), West Azerbaijan (5.8%, n = 41), and Kermanshah (4.4%, n = 31).

The mean participant’ age was 67.2 ± 6.0 years; women identification comprised 50.4% of the participants. Notably, 14.1% of the participants fell above the age of 75. Additionally, 73.0% of the participants resided in urban settings. Table 1 reports the full characteristics of participants according to sarcopenia status. A significant difference was observed between participants classified as sarcopenic and those classified as non-sarcopenic for BMI categories. Furthermore, a significant difference was observed between participants classified as sarcopenic and those classified as non-sarcopenic for dietary protein intake in the first and third tertiles. This significant difference was also observed in the term of low physical activity (P = 0.004), and low calf circumference (P < 0.001).

In this descriptive analysis, the following PAF estimates represent associations rather than causal effects: BMI < 25 had the highest APR [5.80 (95% CI 2.40-13.97)] and PAF [51.0% (95% CI 40.80-61.14)] for sarcopenia. Furthermore, the APR of high-fat mass to sarcopenia was 2.31 (95% CI 1.47–3.64) and the PAF of high-fat mass to sarcopenia was 42.39% (95% CI 32.20–52.29). Additionally, the APR of low physical activity and low calf circumference to sarcopenia were 1.71 (95% CI 1.10–2.66) and 1.64 (95% CI 1.06–2.55) with the PAF of 28.71% and 16.55%, respectively.

Discussion

In the present study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of sarcopenia prevalence and associated risk factors among Iranian adults aged 60 years and older. The weighted prevalence of sarcopenia was estimated to be 13.8%. Our findings revealed that a lower BMI was associated with an elevated risk of sarcopenia, while higher fat mass, low calf circumference and low physical activity were likewise observed to be potential factors contributing to the condition.

Previous studies utilizing the EWGSOP-2 criteria, adjusted for Iranian cut-offs, indicated that the prevalence of sarcopenia and severe sarcopenia in Iran is 10.5% and 12.7% among men, and 7.1% and 16.5% among women, respectively7. Additionally, the SARIR study revealed that the prevalence of pre-sarcopenia, sarcopenia, and severe sarcopenia in Iran were 52.7%, 20.7%, and 6% among men, and 25.3, 15.3, and 5.3% among women, respectively6. The controversies surrounding the prevalence of sarcopenia primarily stem from inconsistencies in definition, diagnostic criteria, and population-specific factors4,7,25,26. The latest meta-analysis on the global prevalence of sarcopenia corroborates this statement, estimating the prevalence of sarcopenia to range between 10 and 27%, depending on the different definitions and cut-points5. In this term, standardization methods can improve the accuracy of prevalence estimates and enhance the development of effective interventions.

The increasing trend in sarcopenia prevalence with age contrasts with studies suggesting lower prevalence among older Asian populations27,28. Although the prevalence of sarcopenia was higher in men over 75 years old compared to women of the same age, this difference was not significant, challenging previous findings6. However, it has been scientifically proved that in older men, the rate of muscular atrophy is higher than in women29,30.

A higher BMI indicates better nutrition intake and relatively higher muscle mass31. Recent studies indicated that obesity defined by BMI is a protective factor against sarcopenia in both men and women32. The mentioned results were consistent with our results. However, weight gain or increased BMI in individuals without monitoring body composition is inadvisable, as older adults typically have reduced lipid turnover and are at higher risk for metabolic diseases33.

The exceptionally high prevalence ratio for low BMI likely reflects a subset of older adults with significant catabolism or chronic energy deficiency, a state strongly conducive to muscle wasting. Although BMI and fat mass are correlated, their inclusion together suggests that both low lean mass (reflected by low BMI) and high adiposity contribute independently to the risk profile. The PAF for BMI < 25 related to sarcopenia was estimated to be 51.0%, suggesting that nutritional interventions and strategies to promote healthy weight gain could potentially have a substantial impact on sarcopenia prevalence. While this large effect size is striking, it should be interpreted with caution. The cross-sectional design precludes determination of whether low BMI precedes sarcopenia or results from underlying catabolic processes that also drive muscle wasting. Furthermore, residual confounding by chronic diseases or nutritional deficiencies cannot be entirely excluded. Therefore, this estimate should be viewed as an association that requires confirmation in prospective studies.

Related evidence suggest the protective role of dietary protein intake in older adults to preserve lean body mass and prevent frailty, accepted lifestyle intervention targets for reducing the risk of sarcopenia34. Our results also suggested that low protein consumption may represent a potential risk factor. Consistent with these findings, data from the Health, Aging, and Body Composition study reported an inverse relationship between dietary protein intake and loss of lean mass among older community-dwelling adults35. A recent meta-analysis of four studies found that older adults with sarcopenia consumed significantly less protein compared to their peers without sarcopenia36. The bioavailability of amino acids plays a pivotal role in facilitating muscle protein synthesis and maintaining the integrity of skeletal muscle mass. Aging correlates with reduced responsiveness of muscle protein synthesis to essential amino acids. Additionally, older adults often encounter anabolic resistance, leading to increased frailty, functional decline, and depleted muscle mass preservation, which facilitates the need for increased dietary protein intake to increase skeletal muscle mass37. Promoting adequate dietary protein consumption through tailored nutrition plans or supplementation could play a supportive role in reducing sarcopenia risk. Combining dietary interventions with physical activity programs, particularly resistance training, may maximize the prevention and management of sarcopenia in vulnerable populations.

It is well established that a sedentary lifestyle is associated with increases in fat mass and body weight38. The present results also indicated the significant effect of low physical activity on sarcopenia. Our results were consistent with Merchant et, al. study who reported that individuals with the highest fat mass index and fat mass-to–fat-free mass ratio were less likely to meet WHO physical activity recommendations39. Weight gain and fat mass are associated with slow gait speed which can lead to a downward spiral in physical activity and eventually, sarcopenia40,41. Moreover, the PAF for low physical activity related to sarcopenia suggested that nearly one-third of sarcopenia cases could be attributed to insufficient physical activity, which underscores the importance of promoting active lifestyles, especially among older adults to mitigate muscle deterioration. However, assuming a causal relationship, which requires prospective validation.

Sarcopenic muscles exhibit a decrease in the quantity of stem cells, commonly referred to as satellite cells, as well as a decline in fully developed myofibers. Moreover, type II glycolytic myofibers become hypertrophic with reduced diameter, and the muscles become infiltrated with adipose tissue. In later stages, fibrotic tissue infiltration occurs within the muscles. Fatty infiltration into both muscle and bone, along with the redistribution of subcutaneous fat to the intra-abdominal (visceral) area, leads to a reduction in overall strength and functionality. This, in turn, increases the risk of falls and fractures and may contribute to a higher incidence of morbidity4. Our findings also suggested 42.39% of sarcopenia cases could be attributed to high-fat mass. Whether this represents a causal relationship remains to be established in longitudinal studies. Given the significant contribution of high-fat mass to sarcopenia, interventions aimed at weight management, particularly strategies targeting fat loss while preserving muscle, are crucial.

Regarding calf circumference, it is important to highlight that we found a marginally significant relationship between low calf circumference and sarcopenia. Older adults with smaller calf circumferences are at a higher risk of developing sarcopenia, whereas larger calf muscle size may present as a protective effect against the condition. It is important to recognize that calf circumference is physiologically correlated with appendicular muscle mass and therefore shares conceptual overlap with the diagnostic criteria for low muscle mass; this inherent association should be considered when interpreting its predictive value. The observed association should be interpreted with the understanding that it partly reflects the same underlying construct of low muscle mass42,43. Borges et al. conducted a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of a prediction model based on calf circumference in distinguishing between sarcopenic and non-sarcopenic patients. The study focused on hospitalized patients with hip fractures, and the model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.82, indicating a strong predictive performance43. Moreover, Endo et al. conducted a ROC analysis to diagnose sarcopenia based on calf circumference among hospitalized patients with chronic liver disease in Japan. They concluded that it seemed a useful and simple surrogate tool for screening sarcopenia in these types of patients, especially for those who are under 70 years old with an AUC of 0.94 for both men and women44. While low calf circumference is not directly modifiable, it serves as a proxy for underlying factors like poor nutrition, inactivity, or other health conditions contributing to muscle loss.

The present results have to be interpreted within the context of potential limitations and strengths. The study design was cross-sectional and limits our ability to draw certain conclusions about causal relationships and PAFs. However, our study is the first to describe the PAF of important risk factors for sarcopenia in Iran. While we report PAFs to estimate the population-level association of risk factors, reverse causality (e.g., sarcopenia leading to reduced physical activity or weight loss) remains plausible. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these associations and establish temporality. This pioneering effort is precious for informing and guiding the development of effective prevention policies. On the other hand, the studied population was sampled from both urban and rural areas, ensuring a diverse and representative cross-section of the community. This comprehensive sampling approach enhances the external validity of our research.

Conclusion

Recognition of sarcopenia as an important health condition in both clinical and research contexts remain relevant for aging populations. Based on data from the IMOS 2021 study, sarcopenia appears to be relatively common among Iranian adults, highlighting its potential public health relevance. The observed associations between sarcopenia and factors such as lower BMI, higher fat mass, and insufficient physical activity suggest that these characteristics may be important correlates within this population. However, given the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and the reported PAFs should be interpreted as descriptive measures of association rather than estimates of preventable. Nevertheless, these findings may help inform hypothesis generation and guide future longitudinal and interventional studies. From a public health perspective, strategies that support healthy body composition and physical activity across the lifespan may be worth consideration within broader healthy aging frameworks. Incorporating sarcopenia assessment into routine health evaluations could also improve population monitoring and facilitate earlier identification of at-risk individuals.
