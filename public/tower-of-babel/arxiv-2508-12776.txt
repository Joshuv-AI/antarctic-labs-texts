Randomized PCA Forest for Outlier Detection
1 st Muhammad Rajabinasab 1 , 2 nd Farhad Pakdaman 2 , 3 rd Moncef Gabbouj 2 , 4 th Peter Schneider-Kamp 1 , 5 th Arthur Zimek 1

Abstract
We propose a novel unsupervised outlier detection method based on Randomized Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Inspired by the performance of Randomized PCA (RPCA) Forest in approximate K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) search, we develop a novel unsupervised outlier detection method that utilizes RPCA Forest for outlier detection. Experimental results showcase the superiority of the proposed approach compared to the classical and state-of-the-art methods in performing the outlier detection task on several datasets while performing competitively on the rest. The extensive analysis of the proposed method reflects it high generalization power and its computational efficiency, highlighting it as a good choice for unsupervised outlier detection.

It is one of the most important and fundamental tasks in data mining and machine learning with applications in intrusion detection

, fault detection

, fraud detection

and others

In recent years, many methods have been proposed to carry out the outlier detection task

[

or Local Outlier Factor (LOF)
[

Challenges in outlier detection, such as the encompassment of every instance of normal behavior, imprecise boundaries between normal samples and outliers, presence of noise in normal data, and the unavailability of labeled data for training
[

are still addressed more thoroughly by classical methods like KNN and LOF that arguably remain state-of-the-art

Recently proposed algorithms might offer better performance for a specific domain or application, but they do not constitute a generalizable solution for outlier detection in the same way that KNN and LOF do. A comprehensive evaluation on a broad range of datasets must be conducted on novel outlier detection algorithms to investigate different aspects of their performance and reveal where their strengths and, more importantly, where their weaknesses lie

Related Work

Outlier detection is a fundamental machine learning task grounding in statistics and many solutions have been proposed for this task, addressing various challenges

[

KNN
[
31
]
uses the KNN distance directly for outlier detection. There are several variations of the KNN algorithm for outlier detection. For example, KNN-Weight (KNNW)
[
2
]
uses the sum of distances
to an object’s KNNs in order to reduce variation in scores and make the score robust against the change of the parameter
k
. Outlier Detection Using Indegree Number (ODIN)
[

utilizes the KNN graph and defines outlierness as a low number of in-adjacent edges. One of the classical and most well-known outlier detection methods is LOF. LOF is based on a local reachability density, estimated from the the K-nearest neighborhood of each point, and a comparison of these local models to the local models of the K-nearest neighborhood. LOF shows how a point differs from other observations in its vicinity
There are many variants of LOF available, such as Simplified LOF (SLOF)
[
33
]
which uses KNN distance in place of the local reachability density and hence, avoids one level of neighborhood computation.

Another fundamental and important method for outlier detection is Isolation Forest (IForest)
[
24
]
. IForest is a tree-based method which randomly selects one feature for each tree and tries isolating data points (keeping only one data point in a leaf) based on the values of that feature. The method is built on the idea that outliers are isolated faster (in a lower depth) compared to the inliers. Other variants of IForest include the Extended Isolation Forest (EIF)
[
16
]
, which suggests a solution for issues with inconsistent assignment of anomaly score to given data points by IForest in special cases, and the Deep Isolation Forest
[
40
]
which introduces a new representation scheme by utilizing casually initialized neural networks and mapping original data into random representation ensembles, where random axis-parallel cuts are subsequently applied to perform the data partition.

Despite the critical revision of the recent advancements in outlier detection and discussion on how they do not generalize well, there are still a variety of methods proposed recently for outlier detection. Dimensionality-Aware Outlier Detection (DAO)
[
1
]
utlizies Local Intrinsic Dimensionality (LID)
[
19

There are also other approaches for addressing the outlier detection problem. K+1-way classifier is utilized and used for outlier detection
[
9
]
by formulating a novel outlier score as an ensemble of in-distribution uncertainty and the posterior of the negative class. Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) is also utilized for outlier detection
[
10
]
alongside with an autoencoder. The main idea is that GANs tend to favor the majority (normal) class in order to minimize the error. The generated normal samples are used for data augmentation and training the autoencoder. The reconstruction error of the autoencoder determines the outlierness score for data samples which are fed to it. High-Density Iteration Outlier Detection (HDIOD)
[
42
]
is a recent method for outlier detection which integrates a Gaussian kernel function with the K-nearest neighbors algorithm to determine the local kernel density of each sample. It compares this density to that of the sample’s K-neighbors, iteratively selecting the neighbor with the highest density in case it exceeds the density of the sample itself. The K-neighbors of all such objects form the extended K-neighbor set. The ratio of the maximum local kernel density within this set to the sample’s density is then computed as a notion for the sample’s outlierness degree. A scaled Minimum Spanning Tree (MST)
[
23
]
is employed for detecting cluster-based outliers. This is done by computing a scaled MST and iteratively cutting the current longest edge to obtain clusters and applying a new medoid selection method which takes into account the effect of noise in order to improve the quality of cluster-based outlier identification.

Many methods are based on some use of the K-nearest neighborhood, and finding the K-nearest neighbors for every point is the computational bottleneck for all these methods
[
21

Examples include the use of locality sensitive hashing (LSH)

As discussed by Okkels et al.

Many methods use the approximate neighborhood search as a module that is replacing the exact search, not considering the internal structure of the search method (“blackbox”). Some others make use of internal structures, such as the graph underlying some HNSW instance

Our method follows the “whitebox” strategy.

III
Proposed Method

In this paper, we propose a novel unsupervised outlier detection method based on RPCA forests
[
30
]
. We are inspired by the performance of the RPCA forest in approximate KNN search which motivates its adaptation for the outlier detection task. RPCA forest is an ensemble of RPCA trees employing randomized PCA
[
14
]
in their design. The construction of a tree is done by the following process: First, in the root of the tree, the entirety of data points are contained. Then, randomized PCA is employed to project the data points into a lower-dimensional subspace in which most of the informative value of the original feature space is preserved. Randomized PCA offers a faster and more efficient alternative compared to the traditional PCA by using a randomized Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) solver, as its complexity is tied to the number of principal components being calculated and not the dimensionality of the data
[
14
]
. A unique splitting criterion is then generated to divide the data points into the left and the right child by their coordinates in the principal components space. This process continues until the stopping criterion is met. The stopping criterion is based on the (approximate) neighborhood size (e.g., maximum size of the tree leaves). Outlier detection using RPCA forest stands on the idea that, first, the outliers are more likely to appear inside the leaf nodes which are located in the lower depths of the tree, and second, their average distance in the original feature space from the other data points in the same leaf node is higher compared to the inliers. In the remainder of this section, we discuss each part of the outlier detection process separately.

III-A

RPCA Tree

RPCA tree is the backbone of RPCA forest which constructs the forest by forming an ensemble. Alg.
1
describes the process of fitting an RPCA tree. Let
\mathit{root}
be the root node of the RPCA tree
\mathcal{T}
which contains all of the data points. Let
p
be the number of principal components used in
\mathcal{T}
and
k
be the (approximate) neighborhood size.
k
is used for the stopping criterion by setting the maximum leaf size to be less than or equal to
k
. As there might be fewer data points in the leaf node, the neighborhood size is approximate. The fitting process of the tree starts with all the data points in the root node. Then, data points are projected into
p
corresponding principal components using randomized PCA to form a lower-dimensional subspace in which most of the informative value of the features is preserved. A splitting point is sampled for each principal component from a Laplace distribution over the subspace. Then, by employing majority voting, a decision is made to assign data points to the left or right child. The fitting process continues for each child node until every branch reaches the stopping criterion, which is the approximate neighborhood size or the maximum size for a node to be considered a leaf. An illustration of a Laplace distribution is shown in Fig.

. It is evident that using a Laplace distribution is a good choice as it assigns higher probability to the values close to the mean, leading to balanced splits for different datasets and faster fitting process.

III-B

Outlier Score

We can utilize an RPCA forest for outlier detection by defining, as a first property, an outlier probability criterion based on the depth of the trees. Let
T
be the set of RPCA trees
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathbf{q}
the query point for outlier detection. Let
L_{\mathbf{q}}
be the leaf node of
\mathcal{T}
that includes
\mathbf{q}
if we traverse the tree using
\mathbf{q}
based on the projections and splitting criterion calculated during the fitting process. The outlier probability of
\mathbf{q}
is defined as

P(\mathbf{q})=\frac{\sum_{\mathcal{T}\in T}\left({1-\frac{\Depth(L_{\mathbf{q}})}{\Depth(\mathcal{T})}}\right)}{|T|}

(1)

where
\Depth(L_{\mathbf{q}})
is the depth of the leaf node in which
\mathbf{q}
lies and
\Depth(\mathcal{T})
is the depth of the tree (maximum depth among all of the leaf nodes).

The outlier probability is based on the idea that outliers are more likely to reach a leaf node earlier (higher up) in the tree as they are different from other observations, i.e., their depth will be a smaller value. The benefit of this outlier probability is that it is comparable across different datasets and settings. However, in order to have a precise outlier probability,
k
must be set to 1. If
k
is set to a larger value, data points with different coordinates will share the same outlier probability, which would contradict some desirable properties of an outlier probability
[
32
]
. On the other hand, setting
k
to 1 poses another limitation. As we are using PCA in each node and the number of principal components being calculated can not be larger than
\min(n_{\text{instances}},n_{\text{features}})
, we will have to set
p
to 2, which limits the performance of the algorithm. These limitations demonstrate the need for a more robust and granular outlier detection criterion.

To address the shortcoming of the outlier probability, we introduce a more precise and granular criterion for outlier detection, the mean of the distances of a query point to every point in its corresponding leaf:

\mu_{\mathit{dist}}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{\sum_{\mathcal{T}\in T}{\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in L_{\mathbf{q}}}{d(\mathbf{q}},\mathbf{x})}{|L_{\mathbf{q}}|}}}{|T|}

(2)

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Euclidean distance.

The final outlier score for a point
\mathbf{q}
is then the mean distance of
\mathbf{q}
within its leaf node weighted by its depth-based probability:

\mathit{RPCAForest}_{\mathit{Score}}(\mathbf{q})=P(\mathbf{q})\mu_{\mathit{dist}}(\mathbf{q})

(3)

By incorporating the distance in the original feature space and the outlier probability in the calculation of the outlier score, we make sure that even if, based on the representation in the principal component space, there are some inliers mixed with the outlier in the same leaf node, they are well separated as the mean of the distances to the data points in the leaf is expected to be higher for outliers compared to the inliers.

III-C

Computational Complexity

The computational complexity of constructing an RPCA tree is determined by the number of data points
n
and the number of principal components
p
. The construction process entails recursively partitioning the dataset using randomized PCA. The expected computational complexity for building an RPCA tree with
n
data points is
O(\log^{2}(n)p+\log(n)p^{3})
.

For search phase, the average time complexity is
O(\log(n))
, corresponding to the traversal from the root to a leaf node. However, the search cost is also influenced by the approximate neighborhood size, which sets the maximum number of points stored in a leaf node. A larger neighborhood size reduces the tree’s depth, potentially decreasing the traversal time but increasing the computational cost of processing the leaf node’s contents. This trade-off enables the tree’s structure to be tuned based on specific application requirements.

Furthermore, RPCA trees are well-suited for parallelization. Since each tree is constructed independently, multiple trees can be built concurrently, leveraging parallel computing resources to significantly reduce the overall construction time. This property is particularly advantageous in multi-tree scenarios, such as those encountered in randomized forest-based methods.

V
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we introduced a novel outlier detection method leveraging RPCA forest. Drawing inspiration from the robust performance of RPCA forest in approximate KNN search, we adapted this machine learning model for unsupervised outlier detection, exploiting its ability to capture complex data structures efficiently. We conducted extensive experiments to evaluate the proposed method across multiple datasets, comparing its performance against several established outlier detection methods. The experimental results clearly showed that the proposed method outperforms the competitors using the same parameter choices. For the competing methods, we also performed a comprehensive hyperparameter tuning to optimize their performance. In contrast, for the proposed method, we explored only two hyperparameters, yet it consistently demonstrated superior or competitive performance. The results underscored the method’s high generalization power, achieving exceptional performance on several datasets and competitive results on others, even without exhaustive hyperparameter optimization. This highlights the method’s robustness and efficiency, making it a promising approach for outlier detection tasks.

The proposed method’s computational efficiency and its ability to perform effectively across diverse datasets and experimental settings are its key strengths. Its success with limited hyperparameter tuning suggests that the RPCA forest inherently captures meaningful patterns in data, reducing the need for extensive configuration. However, to further establish its superiority, a more thorough hyperparameter tuning process is warranted. By systematically exploring a broader range of hyperparameter combinations, we can identify optimal settings for each dataset, potentially amplifying the method’s performance. Such an analysis would enable a more robust comparison with competing methods, providing deeper insights into the conditions under which the proposed method excels. We anticipate that with comprehensive tuning, the method’s performance could be significantly enhanced, leading to clearer evidence of its advantages over existing approaches.

Looking ahead, the RPCA forest’s versatility offers several avenues for future research. As a machine learning model, it has proven effective in both approximate KNN search and unsupervised outlier detection, owing to its ability to handle high-dimensional data effectively and efficiently. One promising direction is to develop additional novel outlier score criteria tailored to the RPCA forest’s structure. The outlier score is the cornerstone of any outlier detection method, as it quantifies the degree to which a data point deviates from expected patterns. By designing new scoring mechanisms that leverage the unique properties of RPCA forest, we can potentially improve detection accuracy. Additionally, we plan to investigate alternative methods for computing outlier scores, exploring how different formulations interact with the RPCA forest’s architecture. These efforts could uncover new ways to enhance the model’s sensitivity to outliers, particularly in challenging datasets with complex distributions. Furthermore, extending the RPCA forest to incorporate adaptive mechanisms, such as dynamic neighborhood sizes across different trees in the forest or weighted decision making for the splits instead of majority voting, could further boost its performance, making it a more effective tool for outlier detection and other related machine learning and data mining tasks.
