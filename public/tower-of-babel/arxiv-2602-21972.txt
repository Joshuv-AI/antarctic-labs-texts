Particle, kinetic and hydrodynamic models for sea ice floes. Part II: Rotating floes with nonlinear contact forces
Quanling Deng, Seung-Yeal Ha, Jaemoon Lee

Abstract
This paper extends the multiscale modeling framework introduced in Part I (Deng and Ha, Physica D: Nonlinear Phenomena 483 (2025) 134951 ) for sea-ice floe dynamics with non-rotating floes to the case with rotational floes and nonlinear contact interactions. Building on the particle–kinetic–hydrodynamic hierarchy developed for non-rotating floes, we generalize the particle model to describe ice floes as rigid bodies characterized by position, linear velocity, angular velocity, size, and moment of inertia. The interaction rules now include nonlinear contact forces and torques arising from short-range compression, restitution, and tangential friction laws, together with hydrodynamic drag that couples translational and rotational motions. These particle descriptions lead to an enriched Vlasov-type kinetic equation posed on an extended phase space, whose moments yield a hydrodynamic system for mass, momentum, and angular-momentum balances. Compared with Part I, the resulting macroscopic equations feature additional stress contributions, rotational transport, and dissipative mechanisms stemming from nonlinear collisions. The proposed framework provides a more realistic description of sea-ice floe dynamics and offers a systematic pathway toward multiscale modeling of sea-ice rheology under complex environmental forcing.



Keywords:

Sea ice floe dynamics, Hertz contact mechanics, energy dissipation, mean-field approximation, hydrodynamic limit.

Introduction

Sea ice plays a fundamental role in Earth’s polar climate system by regulating heat exchange, momentum transfer, and biogeochemical processes at the ocean–atmosphere interface. Its dynamics emerge from the complex interplay between environmental forcing, including wind, ocean currents, and waves, and mechanical interactions among heterogeneous floes (c.f.,
[

,

,

,

,
61
,

,

). In the Marginal Ice Zone (MIZ), where the ice cover is highly fragmented, individual floe-floe interactions dominate the mechanical behavior of the ice pack, often giving rise to phenomena that are more naturally described by particle floes (c.f.,
[
5
,
6

Continuum-based models, such as elastic-plastic
[
16
]
, viscous–plastic
[
42
,
63
]
, elastic–viscous–plastic
[
45
]
, and Maxwell-type rheological models
[
19
]
, have been developed for simulating the large-scale behavior of compact ice packs (for ice sheets and glaciers, we refer to, e.g.,
[
57
]
). However, the continuum assumption can break down in both large and small scales in the MIZ or other areas
[
68
]
, where collisions, heterogeneity, and anisotropic deformation fields produce granular-like behavior that cannot be adequately represented by homogenized constitutive laws. These limitations have motivated the development of particle-based (discrete element) approaches
[
43
,
52
,
69
,
20
,
18
,
54
,
4
]
, which explicitly represent sea ice as a collection of rigid bodies and naturally capture processes such as collisions, ridging, rafting, and fracture patterns.
In earlier model developments, Gudkovich
et al.

[
34
]
studied the dynamics of an individual floe in 1963, based on the observations of Nansen
[
56
]
in 1902, and developed a model to describe the speed and direction of drift depended on floe size and shape.
Future directions for sea ice modelling are also discussed here
[
47
,
7
,
29
]
.

A key challenge in particle-based modeling lies in accurately representing contact interactions. Nonlinear contact mechanics, including Hertzian-type normal forces, velocity-dependent restitution, tangential friction laws, and torque transfer at contact points, play a critical role in determining floe trajectories and rotational dynamics. Such nonlinear collision models, widely used in granular and rigid-body simulations
[
48
,
17
]
, have recently been applied to sea ice floes
[
39
]
. Rotational degrees of freedom further enrich the dynamics: collisions generate torques, frictional impulses modify angular momentum, and ocean drag acts simultaneously on translational and rotational motions. These effects contribute to complex behaviors such as spin alignment, rotational clustering, collision-induced jamming, and enhanced dissipation, all observed in high-resolution DEM studies
[
39
]
.

In Part I of this series
[
23
]
, we have developed a particle–kinetic–hydrodynamic hierarchy for non-rotating cylindrical floes governed by linear contact forces. That formulation established a systematic pathway from Newtonian-type particle models to Vlasov-type kinetic descriptions and further to hydrodynamic equations based on velocity moments. While the non-rotating framework provides conceptual clarity, it neglects nonlinear contact mechanics and rotational effects that are essential for accurately capturing sea-ice behavior in fragmented regimes.

The present paper extends the multiscale framework of Part I by incorporating rotational degrees of freedom and nonlinear contact forces into the particle, kinetic, and hydrodynamic descriptions. This provides a pathway to couple particle and continuum models within a multiscale framework
[
24
]
, together with their integration into data assimilation schemes
[
21
,
14
,
15
]
, enabling more accurate predictions. At the particle level, each floe is modeled as a rigid body characterized by its position, orientation, linear velocity, angular velocity, size, and moment of inertia. Collisions generate both forces and torques, described through physically realistic nonlinear force laws and frictional interactions. This leads to a richer kinetic formulation defined on an extended phase space that includes angular velocities and orientations. Suitable moment closures then yield hydrodynamic equations that track mass, linear momentum, and angular momentum, together with stress and couple-stress contributions arising from torque-generating interactions.
The objective of this Part II work is to establish a rigorous theoretical foundation for such a rotating particle–kinetic–hydrodynamic hierarchy. Our main contributions include:

•

a rigorous study on the behavior of total momentum and energy of a particle model for rotating floes with nonlinear contact forces;

•

the derivation of a kinetic model on an extended phase space incorporating rotational dynamics, followed by a study on the momentum and energy;

•

the development and study of a hydrodynamic system that captures macroscopic evolution of both linear and angular momentum and energies.

2
Particle description for ice floes

In this section, we introduce the particle model for the ice floes with rotation and nonlinear Hertzian contact forcing, followed by a study on its asymptotic collective behavior on momentum and energy.

2.1
The particle model

Consider colliding ice floes with the geometry of cylinders.
Given a system of
n
floes,
we denote by
r^{i}
the radius and
h^{i}
the thickness (or height) of the
i
-th floe with
i\in[n]:=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
.
The radius and thickness characterize the floe size.
In a realistic sea ice floe setting (particularly in marginal ice zones), the floe size often follow a power law distribution
[
64
]
, while the floe thickness distribution follows a Gamma distribution (see, for example,
[
65
,
11
,
66
]
for the Arctic region and
[
67
]
for the Antarctic region).
The mass of the
i
-th floe is
m^{i}=\rho_{ice}\pi(r^{i})^{2}h^{i}
, where the constant
\rho_{ice}
is the density of sea ice floes.
We assume that the mass of each floe does not change over time (thus, no melting, freezing, or fracturing).
We denote by
I^{i}=m^{i}(r^{i})^{2}
the moment of inertia.
The floe position is denoted by
\bm{x}^{i}=(x^{i},y^{i})^{T}
and the floe velocity is
\bm{v}^{i}=(u^{i},v^{i})^{T}
.
We denote the floe angular location as
\theta^{i}
and angular velocity as
\omega^{i}\hat{{\bm{z}}}
. Herein,
\hat{{\bm{z}}}
is the unit vector along the
z
-axis (perpendicular to the
(x,y)
plane).
We consider the angular velocity as a scalar in this dimension and omit the multiplication of
\hat{{\bm{z}}}
for simplicity.
Thus, if the result of the cross product is a vector along the
z
-axis, we consider it as a
z
-value scalar as in (
2.1b
) and (
2.1d
) below.
Lastly, let
\bm{u}_{o}=\bm{u}_{o}(x,y)
be the given ocean surface velocity.
The governing equations of rotating-colliding sea ice floe dynamics are given by Newton’s equations:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\bm{x}^{i}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=\bm{v}^{i},\quad i\in[n],

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\theta^{i}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=\omega^{i},

(2.1b)

\displaystyle m^{i}\frac{{\rm d}\bm{v}^{i}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}+\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij})+\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|:=\bm{F}^{i},

(2.1c)

\displaystyle I^{i}\frac{{\rm d}\omega^{i}}{{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(r^{i}\bm{n}^{ij}\times\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij})+\beta^{i}\left(\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}^{i}/2-\omega^{i}\right)\left|\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}^{i}/2-\omega^{i}\right|:=T^{i},

(2.1d)

where
|\cdot|
is the
\ell^{2}
-norm and

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}

\displaystyle=\big(\kappa^{ij}_{1}\delta^{ij}+\kappa^{ij}_{2}(\bm{v}^{i}-\bm{v}^{j})\cdot\bm{n}^{ij}\big)\bm{n}^{ij},

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}

\displaystyle=\zeta^{ij}\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\bm{t}}^{ij},\qquad\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}=\kappa^{ij}_{3}\sigma^{ij}\bm{t}^{ij},

\displaystyle\kappa^{ij}_{1}

\displaystyle=\pi E_{e}\chi^{ij}h^{ij}_{e}g\left(\frac{\delta^{ij}r_{e}^{ij}}{2(h^{ij}_{e})^{2}}\right),\quad g(\xi)=\frac{0.9117\xi^{2}-0.2722\xi+0.003324}{\xi^{2}-1.524\xi+0.03159}

\displaystyle\kappa^{ij}_{2}

\displaystyle=\eta\sqrt{5\kappa^{ij}_{1}m^{ij}_{e}},\quad\kappa^{ij}_{3}=6\frac{G_{e}}{E_{e}}\kappa^{ij}_{1},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\delta^{ij}

\displaystyle=d^{ij}-(r^{i}+r^{j}),\quad\mbox{with}\quad d^{ij}=|\bm{x}^{i}-\bm{x}^{j}|,

\displaystyle\sigma^{ij}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm d}\sigma^{ij}}{{\rm d}t}=(\bm{v}^{j}-\bm{v}^{i})\cdot\bm{t}^{ij}-r^{j}\omega^{j}-r^{i}\omega^{i},

\displaystyle\sigma^{ij}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{\text{start}}}^{t}\sigma^{ij}_{t}(s){\rm d}s=t_{c}^{ij}\sigma^{ij}_{t},\quad t_{c}^{ij}\approx 2.94\Big(\frac{m_{e}^{ij}}{\kappa_{1}^{ij}}\Big)^{2/5}|v_{i}-v_{j}|^{-1/5},

\displaystyle\alpha^{i}

\displaystyle=\pi\rho_{o}(2C_{v,o}r^{i}\cdot D^{i}+C_{h,o}(r^{i})^{2}),

\displaystyle\beta^{i}

\displaystyle=\pi(r^{i})^{4}\rho_{o}(C_{v,o}D^{i}+r^{i}C_{h,o}/5).

We discuss the physical quantities and parameters one by one below.
First,

E_{e}=\frac{E}{2(1-\nu^{2})},\quad G_{e}=\frac{E}{4(2+\nu)(1-\nu)},

(2.3)

where
E
is Young’s modulus, and
\nu
is Poisson’s ratio (assuming the same for all floes).
In the integration for
\sigma^{ij}
,
t_{\text{start}}
specifies the starting time of the collision, and
t_{c}^{ij}
is an approximate contact duration time that compensates the integration
[
35
]
.

\chi
is a characteristic function

\chi^{ij}=\begin{cases}1,\quad\text{when}\ i\neq j\text{ and }\delta^{ij}<0,\\
0,\quad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2.4)

which characterizes whether floes
i
and
j
are in contact or not.
We multiply this by
\kappa_{1}^{ij}
for notational simplicity.

\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}
and
\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}
are normal and tangential components of the contact force, respectively.
For notational convenience, we denote

\bm{f}_{c}^{ij}=\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}+\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}

with the auxiliary parameter
\zeta
defined as

\zeta^{ij}=\begin{cases}1,\quad&\text{when}\ |\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}|\leq\mu|\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}|,\\
\frac{\mu|\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}|}{|\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}|},\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.5)

This imposes the Coulomb friction law that plays an important role in limiting the tangential contact force relative to the magnitude of the normal contact force
[
43
]
:

|\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}|\leq\mu|\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}|,

where
\mu
is the coefficient of friction that characterizes the condition of the surfaces of two floes in contact.

We assume zero tangential damping as in
[
17
,
18
]
. The floe contact force
\bm{F}^{i}
and torque
T^{i}
are non-zero only when two floes are in contact, i.e.,
\delta^{ij}<0
.
We also remark that for
\delta^{ij}<0
, we have
\xi<0
and
g(\xi)>0
. This guarantees the correct sign of
\kappa^{ij}_{1}\delta^{ij}
in the term
\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}
.
We follow the Hertz contact theory
[
41
,
60
]
and adopt the model for the normal contact force (see the supplementary material of
[
39
]
).
By default,
i\neq j,\quad i,j\in[n]
. We assume
\delta^{ij}=0
when
i=j
(hence,
\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}=\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}=\bf 0
) for notational simplicity.

\sigma_{t}^{ij}
is the slip-rate along the tangential direction caused by both translation and rotation (relative contact velocity projected in the tangential direction).

\sigma
is the rotational deformation, i.e., the tangential shear deformation.

\rho_{o}
is ocean density,
D^{i}
is the ice-floe draft (the part of the ice floe below the water surface),
C_{v,o}
is ocean vertical drag coefficients,
C_{h,o}
is ocean horizontal drag coefficients. In default, we set
D^{i}=0.9h^{i}.

The vector
\bm{n}^{ij}:=\frac{\bm{x}^{j}-\bm{x}^{i}}{|\bm{x}^{j}-\bm{x}^{i}|}
is the unit normal vector pointing from the center of floe
i
to floe
j
(if
i=j
, let it be
\bm{z}
),

\bm{t}^{ij}
is the unit vector along the tangential direction (rotating
\bm{n}^{ij}
counterclockwise by
90^{\circ}
),
\bm{r}^{j}
is the radius multiplied by the associated normal vector pointing towards the center of the
j
-th floe,

E_{e}
is the effective contact modulus,
h^{ij}_{e}:=\min\{h^{i},h^{j}\}
is the effective contact thickness (part of the thickness in contact),
m^{ij}_{e}:=\frac{m^{i}m^{j}}{m^{i}+m^{j}}
is the effective mass,
r^{ij}_{e}:=\frac{r^{i}r^{j}}{r^{i}+r^{j}}
is the effective radius, and

\eta:=\frac{\ln e_{r}}{\sqrt{\ln^{2}e_{r}+\pi^{2}}}<0.

(2.6)

Here
e_{r}
is the restitution coefficient in
[0,1)
. The restitution coefficient for ice floes is a measure of kinetic energy loss during floe collisions. The coefficient is usually a positive real number between 0 and 1. A value of 0 indicates a perfectly inelastic collision, while a value of 1 indicates a perfectly elastic collision. The typical values of
e_{r}
for sea ice are between 0.1 and 0.3; see
[
51
]
. In ice floe studies, values between 0 and 1 are often employed; see, for example
[
38
]
.
Details are referred to equations (1), (8), (20), and (26) in the supplementary material of
[
39
]
.
We scale the summation of the contact force by the factor of
1/n
to apply the mean-field theory for deriving the kinetic and hydrodynamic models.
For a system with a fixed number of floes, this scaling factor may be understood as a factor of the mass and drag coefficient.

2.2
Asymptotic behavior

To study the asymptotic behavior of the particle model (
2.1
), we first define the strain and kinetic energies.
The normal strain energy for two colliding floes is defined as

M_{2,\bm{x}}^{ij}:=\int_{0}^{\delta^{ij}}\bm{f}_{\bm{n},s}^{ij}{\rm d}s=\int_{0}^{\delta^{ij}}\kappa^{ij}_{1}(s)s{\rm d}s.

This normal strain energy evolves with respect to time. Using the Leibniz integral rule, we have

\frac{{\rm d}M_{2,\bm{x}}^{ij}}{{\rm d}t}=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{0}^{\delta}\kappa^{ij}_{1}(s)s{\rm d}s=\kappa^{ij}_{1}(\delta^{ij})\delta^{ij}\frac{{\rm d}\delta^{ij}}{{\rm d}t}.

(2.7)

With this in mind, we define the floe moments as follows:

\displaystyle M_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i},\qquad\bm{M}_{1,\bm{v}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}\bm{v}^{i},\qquad M_{1,\omega}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(m^{i}\bm{x}^{i}\times\bm{v}^{i}+I^{i}\omega^{i}),

(2.8)

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=M_{2,\bm{v}}+M_{2,\bm{x}}+M_{2,\omega},

\displaystyle M_{2,\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}|\bm{v}^{i}|^{2},\qquad M_{2,\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}M_{2,\bm{x}}^{ij},\qquad M_{2,\omega}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I^{i}(\omega^{i})^{2},

where
M_{0}
is the zero-order moment, i.e., total mass.

\bm{M}_{1,\bm{v}}
is the total momentum.

M_{1,\omega}
is the total angular momentum, which includes the orbital angular momentum (due to translational motion) and the spin angular momentum (due to rotation about the center of mass).

M_{2,\bm{x}}
,
M_{2,\bm{v}},\ M_{2,\omega}
, and
M_{2}
represent the total normal strain energy, the total translational kinetic energy, the total rotational kinetic energy, and the total energy, respectively.
The scaling
\frac{1}{n}
in
M_{2,\bm{x}}
ensures that total strain energy remain
\mathcal{O}(1)
in the mean-field limit as
n\to\infty.

Note that each energies are by definition positive.

Lemma 2.1

(Total momentum balances)
.

Let
(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{v}^{i},\theta^{i},\omega^{i})
be a global solution to the system (
2.1
). Then, the following assertions hold.

1.

The total linear momentum satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\rm d}\bm{M}_{1,\bm{v}}}{{\rm d}t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|.\end{aligned}

(2.9)

2.

The total angular momentum satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\rm d}M_{1,\omega}}{{\rm d}t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[\alpha^{i}\bm{x}^{i}\times\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|+\beta^{i}\left(\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}^{i}/2-\omega^{i}\right)\left|\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}^{i}/2-\omega^{i}\right|\big].\end{aligned}

(2.10)

Proof.

For the first assertion, we first note that the relations

\delta^{ij}=\delta^{ji},\quad\bm{n}^{ij}=-\bm{n}^{ji},\quad\bm{t}^{ij}=-\bm{t}^{ji},\quad i,j\in[n],

to see the anti-symmetry of the floe-floe contact force:

\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}=-\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ji},\quad\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}=-\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ji},\quad i,j\in[n].

(2.11)

Now, we use (
2.1
), (
2.8
), and (
2.11
) to find

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\rm d}\bm{M}_{1,\bm{v}}}{{\rm d}t}&=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}\bm{v}^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}\frac{{\rm d}\bm{v}^{i}}{{\rm d}t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}+\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij})+\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|\Big)\\
&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}+\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|.\end{aligned}

For the second claim, using (
2.1
) and the property of the cross product
\bm{v}^{i}\times\bm{v}^{i}=0
, we calculate

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{{\rm d}M_{1,\omega}}{{\rm d}t}&=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(m^{i}\bm{x}^{i}\times\bm{v}^{i}+I^{i}\omega^{i})\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(m^{i}\frac{{\rm d}\bm{x}^{i}}{{\rm d}t}\times\bm{v}^{i}+m^{i}\bm{x}^{i}\times\frac{{\rm d}\bm{v}^{i}}{{\rm d}t}+I^{i}\frac{{\rm d}\omega^{i}}{{\rm d}t}\Big)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\bm{x}^{i}\times m^{i}\frac{{\rm d}\bm{v}^{i}}{{\rm d}t}+I^{i}\frac{{\rm d}\omega^{i}}{{\rm d}t}\Big)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\bm{x}^{i}\times\big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}+\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij})+\alpha^{i}\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|\big)\\
&\quad+\big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(r^{i}\bm{n}^{ij}\times\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij})+\beta^{i}\left(\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}^{i}/2-\omega^{i}\right)\left|\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}^{i}/2-\omega^{i}\right|\big)\Big)\\
&=:\mathcal{T}_{a}+\mathcal{T}_{b},\end{aligned}

where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{T}_{a}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\big(\bm{x}^{i}\times(\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}+\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij})+(r^{i}\bm{n}^{ij}\times\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij})\big),\\
\mathcal{T}_{b}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\alpha^{i}\bm{x}^{i}\times\left(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right)\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\right|+\beta^{i}\left(\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}^{i}/2-\omega^{i}\right)\left|\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}^{i}/2-\omega^{i}\right|\big).\end{aligned}

We denote the contact point of floes
i
and
j
as
\bm{c}^{ij}
. Then we have

\bm{c}^{ij}=\bm{x}^{i}+r^{i}\bm{n}^{ij},\qquad\bm{c}^{ji}=\bm{x}^{j}+r^{j}\bm{n}^{ji},\qquad\bm{c}^{ij}=\bm{c}^{ji}.

(2.12)

Using (
2.12
), anti-symmetry, and
(\bm{x}^{i}-\bm{x}^{j})\times\bm{n}^{ij}=0
, we calculate

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{T}_{a}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\bm{x}^{i}\times\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\big(\bm{x}^{i}\times\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}+(r^{i}\bm{n}^{ij}\times\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij})\big)\\
&=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\bm{x}^{j}\times\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\bm{c}^{ij}\times\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}\\
&=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\bm{x}^{i}-\bm{x}^{j})\times\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\big(\bm{c}^{ij}\times\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}+\bm{c}^{ji}\times\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ji}\big)\\
&=0.\end{aligned}

(2.13)

This leads to the desired result for the balance law of total angular momentum.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

In the derivation of (
2.13
), we used the contact point assumption/equality (
2.12
), which implies
\delta^{ij}=0
. This distinguishes it from the contact deformation assumption for computing the normal contact force.
That is, we assume that the floe particles deform upon contact, and the overlap (deformation)
\delta^{ij}
is used to compute the contact forces
\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}
and
\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}
. However, when computing torques, we assume that the contact occurs at a point located
on the undeformed geometry
, i.e., at a distance
r^{i}\bm{n}^{ij}
from the center.
This introduces a modeling approximation, which is justified in
[
59
,
53
]
:

1.

Deformations are small:
In most sea ice floe particle settings, overlaps are very small compared to particle size, so undeformed geometry introduces only second-order errors.

2.

Approximate consistency:
The total torque from each force pair cancels under Newton’s third law, ensuring conservation of angular momentum.

Lemma 2.3

(Total energy balance)
.

Let
(\bm{x}^{i},\bm{v}^{i},\theta^{i},\omega^{i})
be a global solution to system (

In what follows, we use handy notation:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\gamma_{o}:=\frac{\alpha}{m},\quad\gamma_{b}:=\frac{\beta}{I},\quad\gamma_{1}:=\frac{\kappa_{1}}{m},\quad\gamma_{2}:=\frac{\kappa_{2}}{m},\quad\gamma_{3}:=\frac{\zeta\kappa_{3}}{m},\quad\gamma_{4}:=\frac{\zeta\kappa_{3}}{I},\\

\partial_{t}F+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}F+\omega\partial_{\theta}F+\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot(\bm{f}[F]F)+\partial_{\omega}(f_{\omega}[F]F)=0.

(3.13)

3.2
Macroscopic behavior of the kinetic model

From now on, as long as there is no confusion, we suppress
t
-dependence in
F
. i.e.,

F(\bm{z})\equiv F(t,\bm{z}),\quad\bm{z}\in\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}.

For simplicity, we denote
\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{8}\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{4}

Next, we define the energy functional as follows.

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:=\mathcal{E}_{K}+\mathcal{E}_{P}+\mathcal{E}_{\omega},\\
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{K}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}m|\bm{v}|^{2}F(\bm{z}){\rm d}\bm{z},\quad\mathcal{E}_{\omega}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}I\omega^{2}F(\bm{z}){\rm d}\bm{z},\\
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P}:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{\delta(\bm{z},\bm{z}_{*})}\tilde{\kappa}_{1}(\eta)\eta{\rm d}\eta\right)F(\bm{z})F(\bm{z}_{*}){\rm d}\bm{z}{\rm d}\bm{z}_{*},\qquad\tilde{\kappa}_{1}(\delta(\bm{z},\bm{z}_{*}))=\kappa_{1}(\bm{z},\bm{z}_{*}).\end{cases}

is nonnegative. In the following two lemmas, we establish the macroscopic behavior of the kinetic description of the floe dynamics.

Lemma 3.2

(3.14)

Proof.

Theorem 3.3

\frac{{\rm d}\mathcal{E}}{{\rm d}t}\leq-\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}\alpha\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{\infty}-\bm{v}\right|^{3}F(\bm{z})d\bm{z}-\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}\beta|\omega|^{3}F(t,\bm{z})d\bm{z},

(3.22)

since the other two terms are non-positive (noting that
\kappa_{2}\leq 0
).
Thus, total energy is non-increasing.
Next, we integrate inequality (

), to get

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}\alpha\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{\infty}-\bm{v}\right|^{3}F(\bm{z})d\bm{z}+\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}\beta|\omega|^{3}F(t,\bm{z})d\bm{z}\right){\rm d}t\leq\mathcal{E}(0)-\inf_{t>0}\mathcal{E}(t)\leq\mathcal{E}(0),

\displaystyle\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}\alpha

\displaystyle\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{\infty}-\bm{v}\right|^{3}\partial_{t}F(\bm{z}){\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}\alpha\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{\infty}-\bm{v}\right|^{3}\big(\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}F+\omega\partial_{\theta}F+\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot(\bm{f}[F]F)+\partial_{\omega}(f_{\omega}[F]F)\big){\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=3\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}\alpha\left|\bm{u}_{o}^{\infty}-\bm{v}\right|(\bm{v}-\bm{u}_{o}^{\infty})\cdot(\bm{f}_{o}+\bm{f}_{c}[F])F{\rm d}\bm{z}:=\mathcal{T}_{\alpha},

) and the divergence theorem with fast decay in the density function

for vanishing boundaries.

\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
and

are bounded by some time-independent constant

.

Assume that there exists a compact set

and

such that

is compact, we can set

Moreover, all coefficients in the force fields are bounded on
K\times K
.

f[F]=f_{o}+f_{c}[F]\ \text{ and }\ f_{\omega}[F]=f_{\omega,o}+f_{\omega,c}[F],

one has

\|f_{o}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq\|\gamma_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\bar{U}^{2}\leq C,\qquad\|f_{\omega,o}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq\|\gamma_{\omega}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\bar{\Omega}^{2}\leq C.

\displaystyle\|f_{c,\bm{n}}[F](t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\gamma_{1}\delta\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{2})}\leq C,

\displaystyle\|f_{c,\bm{v}}[F](t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\gamma_{2}|(\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{*})\cdot\bm{n}|\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{2})}\leq C.

|\zeta\kappa_{3}\sigma|\leq\mu|\kappa_{1}\delta|

\|f_{c,\bm{t}}[F](t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq\left\|\frac{\mu|\kappa_{1}\delta|}{m}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K^{2})}\leq C.

\|f[F](t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}+\|f_{\omega}[F](t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq C\ \text{ uniformly in }\ t.

with a constant

and

are uniformly continuous on
[0,\infty)

2.6

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{K}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}m|\bm{v}|^{2}F(\bm{z}){\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}\alpha|v|^{3}F(t,\bm{z}){\rm d}\bm{z}\right)^{2/3}\left(\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}\frac{m^{3}F(t,\bm{z})}{\alpha^{2}}{\rm d}\bm{z}\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{1}}\alpha|v|^{3}F(t,\bm{z}){\rm d}\bm{z}\right)^{2/3},

where the positive constant

depends on the constants that bound the floe radius and thickness. Taking the limit

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{K}=0.

Similar arguments apply to estimate

F=F(t,\bm{x},\bm{y})\geq 0
be a sufficiently smooth solution of (
3.13

decays rapidly as

and as

Recall that the particle mass and moment of inertia depend on the size variables

From (
3.1
),

\frac{{\rm d}r}{{\rm d}t}=\frac{{\rm d}h}{{\rm d}t}=0\quad\implies\quad\frac{{\rm d}m}{{\rm d}t}=\frac{{\rm d}I}{{\rm d}t}=0.

We define the
mass-weighted
and
inertia-weighted
local moments (hydrodynamic fields):

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\rho(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}mF(t,\bm{x},\bm{y}){\rm d}\bm{y},\\[5.12128pt]
\displaystyle(\rho\bm{u})(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{v}F(t,\bm{x},\bm{y}){\rm d}\bm{y},\\[5.12128pt]
\displaystyle\rho_{I}(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}IF(t,\bm{x},\bm{y}){\rm d}\bm{y},\\[5.12128pt]
\displaystyle(\rho_{I}\bar{\omega})(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I\omega F(t,\bm{x},\bm{y}){\rm d}\bm{y},\\[5.12128pt]
\displaystyle\hat{\omega}(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}(m\bm{x}\times\bm{v}+I\omega)F(t,\bm{x},\bm{y}){\rm d}\bm{y}=\bm{x}\times(\rho\bm{u})+\rho_{I}\bar{\omega},\\[5.12128pt]
\displaystyle E(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\frac{1}{2}\Big(m|\bm{v}|^{2}+I\omega^{2}\Big)F(t,\bm{x},\bm{y}){\rm d}\bm{y}.\end{cases}

(4.1)

Herein,

\hat{\omega}
denotes total angular momentum density, while
\bar{\omega}
is the mean spin.
We introduce
{\bm{w}}:=\bm{v}-\bm{u}
and the following weighted fluxes:

\displaystyle P(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m{\bm{w}}\otimes{\bm{w}}F{\rm d}\bm{y},\qquad{\bm{q}}(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\Big(m|{\bm{w}}|^{2}+I(\omega-\bar{\omega})^{2}\Big){\bm{w}}F{\rm d}\bm{y},

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\bm{J}}(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I(\omega-\bar{\omega}){\bm{w}}F{\rm d}\bm{y},\qquad{\bm{J}}_{\rm orb}(t,\bm{x}):=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m(\bm{x}\times\bm{w})\otimes\bm{w}F(t,\bm{x},\bm{y}){\rm d}\bm{y},

where one may omit
\otimes
in the definition of the orbital flux tensor
{\bm{J}}_{\rm orb}
as it is 2D and
\bm{x}\times\bm{w}
gives a scalar (we keep it here for consistency).
Observe that

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=E_{K}+E_{R}+E_{\rm int},\qquad E_{K}:=\frac{1}{2}\rho|\bm{u}|^{2},\quad E_{R}:=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{I}|\bar{\omega}|^{2},

(4.3)

\displaystyle E_{\rm int}

\displaystyle=\rho e:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\Big(m|{\bm{w}}|^{2}+I(\omega-\bar{\omega})^{2}\Big)F{\rm d}\bm{y}.

With this setting in mind, we have the following balance laws.

Lemma 4.1

(Hydrodynamic balance laws)
.

Let
F
be a smooth solution of (
3.13
) with sufficient decay at infinity in
\bm{y}
.
Then the moments (
4.1
) satisfy the local balance laws:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\rho\bm{u})=0,

(4.4a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\bm{u})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\rho\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}+P)=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{f}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y},

(4.4b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\omega}+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\hat{\omega}\bm{u}+{\bm{J}}+{\bm{J}}_{\rm orb})=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\big(m\bm{x}\times\bm{f}[F]+If_{\omega}[F]\big)F{\rm d}\bm{y},

(4.4c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\Big(E\bm{u}+P\bm{u}+\bar{\omega}{\bm{J}}+\frac{1}{2}{\bm{q}}\Big)=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{v}\cdot\bm{f}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}+\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I\omega f_{\omega}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}.

(4.4d)

Proof.

We establish the balance laws in several steps as follows.

Step A: (Conservation of mass)
:
We multiply (
3.13
) by
m
and integrate the resulting relation over
\bm{y}
.
Using the integration by parts in
\theta
,
\bm{v}
and
\omega
, one has

\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{v}F{\rm d}\bm{y}=0.

This is exactly (
4.4a
) by the definition of
\rho\bm{u}
.

Step B: (Balance of Momentum)
:
Again, we multiply (
3.13
) by
m\bm{v}
and integrate the resulting relation over
\bm{y}
to find

\partial_{t}(\rho\bm{u})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v}F{\rm d}\bm{y}+\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{v}\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot(\bm{f}[F]F){\rm d}\bm{y}=0.

By integration by parts in
\bm{v}
(boundary term vanishes), one has

\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{v}\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot(\bm{f}[F]F){\rm d}\bm{y}=-\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{f}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}.

Next, we decompose
\bm{v}=\bm{u}+{\bm{w}}
to get

\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{v}\otimes\bm{v}F{\rm d}\bm{y}=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m(\bm{u}+{\bm{w}})\otimes(\bm{u}+{\bm{w}})F{\rm d}\bm{y}=\rho\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}+P,

since
\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m{\bm{w}}F{\rm d}\bm{y}=0
by the definition of
\bm{u}
.
This gives (
4.4b
).

Step C: (Spin angular momentum)
:
We multiply (
3.13
) by
I\omega
and integrate it over
\bm{y}
to get

\partial_{t}(\rho_{I}\bar{\omega})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I\omega\bm{v}F{\rm d}\bm{y}+\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I\omega\partial_{\omega}(f_{\omega}[F]F){\rm d}\bm{y}=0.

Integration by parts in
\omega
yields

\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I\omega\partial_{\omega}(f_{\omega}[F]F){\rm d}\bm{y}=-\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}If_{\omega}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}.

Moreover, we use
\bm{v}=\bm{u}+{\bm{w}}
and
\omega=\bar{\omega}+(\omega-\bar{\omega})
to obtain

\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I\omega\bm{v}F{\rm d}\bm{y}=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I(\bar{\omega}+(\omega-\bar{\omega}))(\bm{u}+{\bm{w}})F{\rm d}\bm{y}=\rho_{I}\bar{\omega}\bm{u}+{\bm{J}},

where we used

\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I(\omega-\bar{\omega})F{\rm d}\bm{y}=0,\qquad\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I{\bm{w}}F{\rm d}\bm{y}=0.

This yields

\partial_{t}(\rho_{I}\bar{\omega})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\rho_{I}\bar{\omega}\bm{u}+{\bm{J}})=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}If_{\omega}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}.

(4.5)

Step D: (Orbital angular momentum)
:
We multiply (
3.13
) by
m\bm{x}\times\bm{v}
and integrate the resulting relation over
\bm{y}
term by term.

(i) Time derivative.

\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m(\bm{x}\times\bm{v})\partial_{t}F{\rm d}\bm{y}=\partial_{t}\left(\bm{x}\times(\rho\bm{u})\right).

(ii)
\bm{x}
-transport.

We use

\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}F=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\bm{v}F),\qquad\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}(\bm{x}\times\bm{v})=0,

(since
\bm{v}\times\bm{v}=0
), to get

\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m(\bm{x}\times\bm{v})\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}F{\rm d}\bm{y}=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left(\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m(\bm{x}\times\bm{v})\otimes\bm{v}F{\rm d}\bm{y}\right)=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\big(\bm{x}\times(\rho\bm{u})\otimes\bm{u}+{\bm{J}}_{\rm orb}\big),

where the mixed terms vanish as before.

(iii)
\bm{v}
-divergence term.

Assuming sufficient decay in
\bm{v}
, so boundary terms vanish,

\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m(\bm{x}\times\bm{v})\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot(\bm{f}[F]F){\rm d}\bm{y}=-\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\big(\nabla_{\bm{v}}(\bm{x}\times\bm{v})\big)\cdot\bm{f}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}=-\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{x}\times\bm{f}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}.

The
\theta
-transport and
\omega
-flux terms vanish by using integration by parts. Collecting (i)–(iii), we obtain the local orbital angular momentum balance:

\partial_{t}\left(\bm{x}\times(\rho\bm{u})\right)+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\big(\bm{x}\times(\rho\bm{u})\otimes\bm{u}+{\bm{J}}_{\rm orb}\big)=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{x}\times\bm{f}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}.

(4.6)

Finally, summing up the spin and orbital angular momentum balances (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) to arrive at the desired balance law for total angular momentum (
4.4c
).

Step E: (Total translational and rotational energy)
:
We multiply (
3.13
) by
\frac{1}{2}\big(m|\bm{v}|^{2}+I\omega^{2}\big)
and integrate the resulting relation over
\bm{y}
.
The transport terms give

\partial_{t}E+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\tfrac{1}{2}\big(m|\bm{v}|^{2}+I\omega^{2}\big)\bm{v}F{\rm d}\bm{y},

and the
\theta
-term vanishes by integration by parts.
For the force terms, we use integration by parts in
\bm{v}
and
\omega
(the mixed terms vanish using integration by parts) to see

\displaystyle\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\tfrac{1}{2}m|\bm{v}|^{2}\nabla_{\bm{v}}\cdot(\bm{f}[F]F){\rm d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{v}\cdot\bm{f}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y},

\displaystyle\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^{2}\partial_{\omega}\big(f_{\omega}[F]F\big){\rm d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I\omega f_{\omega}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}.

Finally, we decompose the energy flux, use
\bm{v}=\bm{u}+{\bm{w}}
and
\omega=\bar{\omega}+(\omega-\bar{\omega})
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\tfrac{1}{2}\big(m|\bm{v}|^{2}+I\omega^{2}\big)\bm{v}F{\rm d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle=E\bm{u}+P\bm{u}+\bar{\omega}{\bm{J}}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\bm{q}},

where
P,{\bm{J}},{\bm{q}}
are defined in (
4.2
)–(
4.3
) and we used

\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m{\bm{w}}F{\rm d}\bm{y}=0,\qquad\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I(\omega-\bar{\omega})F{\rm d}\bm{y}=0.

Finally, we collect all the estimates to get the desired estimate.
∎

To close (
4.4
), we follow
[
23
,
28
]
and adopt the mono-kinetic ansatz

F(t,\bm{x},\bm{v},\theta,\omega,r,h)=\frac{\rho(t,\bm{x})}{m(r,h)}\Phi(t,\bm{x},\theta,r,h)\delta(\bm{v}-\bm{u}(t,\bm{x}))\delta(\omega-\bar{\omega}(t,\bm{x})),

(4.7)

where
\Phi\geq 0
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\Phi(t,\bm{x},\theta,r,h){\rm d}r{\rm d}h{\rm d}\theta=1,\qquad\text{for all }(t,\bm{x}).

(4.8)

Under (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), the weighted definitions are consistent:

\int mF{\rm d}\bm{y}=\rho,\qquad\int m\bm{v}F{\rm d}\bm{y}=\rho\bm{u},\qquad\rho_{I}(t,\bm{x})=\rho(t,\bm{x})\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\frac{I(r,h)}{m(r,h)}\Phi(t,\bm{x},r,h){\rm d}r{\rm d}h,

\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}I\omega F(t,\bm{x},\bm{y}){\rm d}\bm{y}=\rho(t,\bm{x})\ \bar{\omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\frac{I(r,h)}{m(r,h)}\Phi(t,\bm{x},r,h){\rm d}r{\rm d}h=\rho_{I}\bar{\omega}

With this in mind, using (
4.7
) and following
[
23
]
(Dirac delta function property), one arrives at

P\equiv 0,\qquad{\bm{q}}\equiv 0,\qquad{\bm{J}}\equiv 0,\qquad{\bm{J}}_{\rm orb}\equiv 0,\qquad\rho e\equiv 0,

and the energy reduces to the bulk form

E(t,\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\rho(t,\bm{x})|\bm{u}(t,\bm{x})|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{I}(t,\bm{x})\bar{\omega}(t,\bm{x})^{2}.

Then the balance laws (
4.4

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\rho\bm{u})=0,

(4.9a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\bm{u})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\rho\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u})=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{f}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y},

(4.9b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\omega}+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\hat{\omega}\bm{u})=\bm{x}\times\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{f}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}+\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}If_{\omega}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y},

(4.9c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(E\bm{u})=\bm{u}\cdot\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{f}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}+\bar{\omega}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}If_{\omega}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}.

(4.9d)

(4.11)

(a) For ocean-drag induced terms

(4.12)

Remark 4.2

(Contact operator)
.

The main difference between (

The local homogeneity assumption resembles the assumption on the contact operator so that the physical laws of mass, momentum and energy conservation
during collisions are satisfied (c.f.,
[
3

and
[

,
13
,

,
30
,

,
32
]

This assumption on contact operator allows the simplification of the right-hand side terms in (

This represents an idealized simplification of sea ice floe dynamics. In more realistic sea ice rheology, the contact-forcing terms in the density functionals generate the contact stress tensor within the ice cover. We refer to
[

27

40
]
for discussions of sea ice rheology and to Appendix A for further details.

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}}\kappa_{1}\bm{u}\cdot(|\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}|-(r+r^{*}))\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})F(t,\bm{z}^{*}){\rm d}\bm{z}^{*}F{\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\kappa_{1}\bm{v}\cdot(|\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}|-(r+r^{*}))\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})F(t,\bm{z}^{*}){\rm d}\bm{z}^{*}F{\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{P}}{{\rm d}t},

where the last equality follows (
3.19
).
Thirdly, for the damping term, we evaluate the integral in a similar fashion:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bm{u}\cdot\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}

\displaystyle m\bm{f}_{c,\bm{v}}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}{\rm d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bm{u}\cdot\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}}\kappa_{2}[(\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{*})\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})]\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})F(t,\bm{z}^{*}){\rm d}\bm{z}^{*}F{\rm d}\bm{y}{\rm d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\kappa_{2}\bm{u}\cdot[(\bm{u}-\bm{u}^{*})\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})]\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})F(t,\bm{z}^{*}){\rm d}\bm{z}^{*}F{\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{2}}\kappa_{2}[(\bm{u}-\bm{u}^{*})\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})]^{2}F(\bm{z})F(\bm{z}^{*}){\rm d}\bm{z}^{*}{\rm d}\bm{z},

where the last equality follows from (
3.20
).
Lastly, we combine the last two integrals to arrive at a similar estimate as (
3.21
):

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bm{u}\cdot\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}

\displaystyle m\bm{f}_{c,\bm{t}}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}{\rm d}\bm{x}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{\omega}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}If_{\omega,\bm{t}}[F]F{\rm d}\bm{y}{\rm d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{2}}\zeta\kappa_{3}[\bm{u}\cdot\sigma\bm{t}(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{*})+r\bar{\omega}\bm{n}(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{*})\times\sigma\bm{t}(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{*})]F(\bm{z})F(\bm{z}^{*}){\rm d}\bm{z}^{*}{\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{2}}\zeta\kappa_{3}\sigma[\bm{u}\cdot\bm{t}(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{*})+r\bar{\omega}]F(\bm{z})F(\bm{z}^{*}){\rm d}\bm{z}^{*}{\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{2}}\zeta\kappa_{3}\sigma[-\bm{u}^{*}\cdot\bm{t}(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{*})+r^{*}\bar{\omega}^{*}]F(\bm{z})F(\bm{z}^{*}){\rm d}\bm{z}^{*}{\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{2}}\zeta\kappa_{3}\sigma[(\bm{u}^{*}-\bm{u})\cdot\bm{t}-r\bar{\omega}-r^{*}\bar{\omega}^{*}]F(\bm{z})F(\bm{z}^{*}){\rm d}\bm{z}^{*}{\rm d}\bm{z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{2}}\zeta\kappa_{3}[(\bm{u}^{*}-\bm{u})\cdot\bm{t}-r\bar{\omega}-r^{*}\bar{\omega}^{*}]^{2}F(\bm{z})F(\bm{z}^{*}){\rm d}\bm{z}^{*}{\rm d}\bm{z}.

Summing up the estimates above and moving
-\frac{{\rm d}\mathcal{E}_{P}}{{\rm d}t}
to the right-hand side gives the desired result.

•

Convergence of translational velocities to the ocean velocity.

The quadratic ocean drag term in (

\alpha^{i}\big(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\big)\big|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\big|,

is dissipative with respect to the relative velocity
\bm{v}^{i}-\bm{u}_{o}^{i}
. Numerically, we observe that the mean mismatch

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\bm{v}^{i}(t)-\bm{u}_{o}^{i}|

up to small fluctuations due to intermittent collisions. In other words,

indicates that
\bm{v}^{i}(t)\to\bm{u}_{o}^{i}
in accordance with the theorem.
We also observe that the collisions are less frequent, as all floe velocities align with the ocean velocity.

•

Decay of angular velocity to zero.

Since
\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}^{i}\equiv 0

\beta^{i}\Big(\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}^{i}/2-\omega^{i}\Big)\Big|\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}^{i}/2-\omega^{i}\Big|=-\beta^{i}\omega^{i}|\omega^{i}|,

\omega^{i}\equiv 0
.

•

Momentum balance with ocean forcing.

The contact forces satisfy action–reaction at the pair level, hence the internal forces contribute zero net momentum:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}^{ij}+\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}^{ij}\big)=\bf 0,

up to numerical error. Therefore, the evolution of
M_{1,\bm{v}}(t)
is determined solely by the ocean drag (see Lemma
2.1
):

\frac{d}{dt}M_{1,\bm{v}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{i}\big(\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\big)\big|\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\big|.

Thus
M_{1,\bm{v}}(t)
is not conserved in general; instead it relaxes toward the ocean-drift momentum

M_{1,\bm{v}}^{\infty}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{i}\bm{u}_{o}^{i},

which is consistent with the observed convergence
\bm{v}^{i}\to\bm{u}_{o}^{i}
. In particular, after transients, the total momentum becomes approximately constant because
\bm{u}_{o}^{i}-\bm{v}^{i}\approx\bf 0
and the drag contribution becomes negligible.

•

Normal strain energy is collision-localized.

The total normal strain energy
U_{n}(t)
is nonzero only during contact events (when
\chi^{ij}=1
and
\delta^{ij}<0
). Numerically it appears as intermittent bursts (see the last plot in Figure
2
) corresponding to collisions, and decays back toward zero when the system becomes dilute and relative motion decreases. This behavior is consistent with the short-range Hertz-type contact interaction.

Results and discussion.

The continuum solution and the coarse-grained particle statistics match well at the reported times, providing direct numerical evidence for the consistency between the particle and hydrodynamic descriptions; see Figure

Mass density consistency.

•

Angular velocity consistency.

The angular velocity moments also exhibit strong agreement. The particle angular velocities
\omega^{i}(t)
are driven toward
\frac{1}{2}\nabla\times\bm{u}_{o}(\bm{x}^{i}(t))

\bm{u}_{o}

•

Overall consistency of the hierarchy.

Taken together, the agreement of

Moreover, one expects better agreement as the number of particles increases and as the time-step and mesh grid sizes are finer for higher numerical accuracy.

6
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have extended the particle–kinetic–hydrodynamic hierarchy developed in Part I to a substantially richer and more realistic setting by incorporating rotational degrees of freedom and nonlinear contact interactions among sea-ice floes.
Starting from a rigid-body particle description with force–torque coupling, we derived an associated kinetic equation on an extended phase space and obtained macroscopic hydrodynamic balance laws for mass, linear momentum, and angular momentum through moment closures.
We established the total energy dissipation, which captures the physics of energy loss due to the floe-floe collision.
The resulting framework reveals how nonlinear collisions, frictional effects, and rotational dynamics generate additional stress and dissipation mechanisms at the macroscopic level, thereby providing a systematic and physically consistent multiscale description of fragmented sea ice in the marginal ice zone.

Several important directions remain open for future research. One natural extension is to couple the present mechanical framework with a temperature field, allowing floe sizes, masses, and moments of inertia to evolve through melting and refreezing processes. Such a thermo-mechanical coupling would introduce additional transport and source terms at all scales and raise fundamental questions about energy consistency, scale separation, and closure strategies, leading to more realistic sea ice rheology. Another challenging direction concerns floe fracture and bonding, corresponding to a dynamically varying number of particles due to breakup, aggregation, or refreezing-induced bonding. From a modeling and analytical perspective, this leads to nontrivial difficulties in tracking collective behavior, conservation laws, and statistical descriptions, when the underlying particle number changes in time. Developing a coherent multiscale theory that accommodates variable particle numbers while retaining tractable kinetic and hydrodynamic limits remains an open and promising problem for future study.

Appendix A: Local Homogeneity Assumption

In the derivation of macroscopic balance laws (
4.15
) from the kinetic description of sea ice, we adopted the local homogeneity assumption to further simplify the integrals on contact operator in (
4.10
) and (
4.11
).
This assumption formalizes the notion that, at sufficiently small scales around a given macroscopic position, the ice floe distribution exhibits negligible spatial gradients. Under this somewhat strong assumption, contact-operator related terms vanish in analogy with
[
3
,
30
,
31
]
for gas particles, enabling the simplified hydrodynamic equations (
4.15
).

(Local homogeneity assumption)

We say that
F(\bm{x},\bm{v},\theta,\omega,r,h)
is locally homogeneous at
\bm{x}
if, for
\bm{s}
in a neighborhood of zero with
|\bm{s}|\ll L
for some macroscopic scale
L>0
,

F(t,\bm{x}+\bm{s},\bm{v},\theta,\omega,r,h)\approx F(t,\bm{x},\bm{v},\theta,\omega,r,h),

and similarly for its moments.
This condition asserts that the distribution varies slowly relative to the characteristic length scales of the collision/contact interactions.

The local homogeneity assumption reflects the physical idea that, at scales comparable with the interaction range of floes (e.g., contact kernel support), the macroscopic fields vary negligibly. In other words, the spacing between colliding or interacting floes is small relative to macroscopic gradients in density and velocity. This justifies treating
F
and its moments as essentially constant over the support of the collision/contact operator.

Recall (
4.14
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{52}

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{f}_{c,\bm{n}}[F](t,\bm{z})F(t,\bm{y})d\bm{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}}m\kappa_{1}\big(|\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}|-(r+r^{*})\big)\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})F(t,\bm{z}^{*})d\bm{z}^{*}F(t,\bm{y})d\bm{y},

where
\bm{y}=(\bm{v},\theta,\omega,r,h)
,
\bm{z}^{*}=(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{v}^{*},\theta^{*},\omega^{*},r^{*},h^{*})
.
Using the local homogeneity assumption and exchange of variables,
the internal integration is approximated as

\displaystyle m\bm{f}_{c,\bm{n}}[F](t,\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}}m\kappa_{1}\big(|\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}|-(r+r^{*})\big)\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})F(t,\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})d\bm{x}^{*}d\bm{y}^{*}

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}}m\kappa_{1}\big(|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*}|-(r^{*}+r)\big)\bm{n}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{x})F(t,\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*})d\bm{x}d\bm{y}^{*}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}}m\kappa_{1}\big(|\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}|-(r+r^{*})\big)\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})F(t,\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*})d\bm{x}d\bm{y}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}}m\kappa_{1}\big(|\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x}|-(r+r^{*})\big)\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})\big(F(t,\bm{x}^{*},\bm{y}^{*})d\bm{x}^{*}-F(t,\bm{x},\bm{y}^{*})d\bm{x}\big)d\bm{y}^{*}

\displaystyle\approx 0,

where the last approximation is based on the local homogeneity assumption of moments. Thus,
\mathcal{T}_{52}\approx 0.

This may be interpreted as the collisional force being approximately zero (forces surrounding a floe at
\bm{x}
cancel) in the distribution sense, as the number of floes goes to infinity, assumed in Section
3
for kinetic description.
Similarly, one can derive
\mathcal{T}_{53}\approx 0
and using this local assumption and that
(\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{*})\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})=(\bm{v}^{*}-\bm{v})\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{x})
and
\mathcal{T}_{54}\approx 0
using
\bm{t}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})=-\bm{t}(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{x})
.
The term
\bm{x}\times\mathcal{T}_{54}+\mathcal{T}_{56}
vanishes following a similar derivation in (
2.13
). This leads to the simplified hydrodynamic model (
4.15
) which was studied in the numerical experiments in Section
5
.

On the other hand, the local homogeneity assumption have limitations.
It is a heuristic approximation that ensures the contact operator conserves mass and momentum as in
[
3
,
30
,
31
]
for gas particles.
However, sea ice floe particles, with different Knudsen number
[
49
]
, are different from gas particles (typically for dilute regime with small rare collisions, i.e., Boltzmann–Grad limit).
They are most justified when macroscopic gradients vary on scales much larger than the contact interaction range.
In regimes with strong shear or boundary effects, deviations from local homogeneity should be considered and this would lead to more realistic sea ice rheology
[
63
,
27
,
40
]
.

We take the momentum balance law (
4.9b
) as an example to derive the contact stress tensor as in sea ice rheology discussed in
[
27
]
.
We recall the balance law for momentum in (
4.9b
):

\partial_{t}(\rho\bm{u})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\rho\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u})=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{f}[F]Fd\bm{y},\qquad\bm{f}[F]=\bm{f}_{o}+\bm{f}_{c}[F],

where
\bm{f}_{o}=\gamma_{o}(\bm{u}_{o}-\bm{v})|\bm{u}_{o}-\bm{v}|
and

\bm{f}_{c}[F](t,\bm{z})=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}}\bm{f}_{c}(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{*})F(t,\bm{z}^{*})d\bm{z}^{*}

with
\bm{f}_{c}(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{*})
containing the normal, damping, and tangential components defined in (
3.12
).
With mono-kinetic ansatz (
4.7
), we define and calculate the contact contribution

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{c}(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}m\bm{f}_{c}[F](t,\bm{x},\bm{y})F(t,\bm{x},\bm{y})d\bm{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}}m(\bm{y})\bm{f}_{c}(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{*})F(t,\bm{z})F(t,\bm{z}^{*})d\bm{z}^{*}d\bm{y}

\displaystyle=\rho(t,\bm{x})\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{3}}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}}\Phi\frac{\rho(t,\bm{x}^{*})}{m(r^{*},h^{*})}\Phi^{*}\bm{f}_{c}(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{*})d\bm{z}^{*}d\bm{y},

where
\Phi=\Phi(t,\bm{x},\theta,r,h)
and
\Phi^{*}=\Phi(t,\bm{x}^{*},\theta^{*},r^{*},h^{*})
.
To obtain the contact stress tensor, we adopt the Irving–Kirkwood form and introduce the bond-localization kernel

B(\bm{x};\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*}):=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{\delta}\!\big(\bm{x}-(1-s)\bm{x}-s\bm{x}^{*}\big)ds,

and use the standard Irving–Kirkwood identity

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\big((\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x})B(\bm{x};\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})\big)=\hat{\delta}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*})-\hat{\delta}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}).

Then the contact force density admits the stress representation

\bm{b}_{c}(t,\bm{x})=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\sigma_{c}^{\rm MK}(t,\bm{x}),

where the monokinetic contact stress tensor is

\sigma_{c}^{\rm MK}(t,\bm{x})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\hat{\mathbb{R}}_{4}^{2}}(\bm{x}^{*}-\bm{x})\otimes\big(\rho(t,\bm{x})\Phi\big)\frac{\rho(t,\bm{x}^{*})}{m(r^{*},h^{*})}\Phi^{*}\bm{f}_{c}(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{*})B(\bm{x};\bm{x},\bm{x}^{*})d\bm{z}^{*}d\bm{y}

Moreover, one may decompose the terms as

\sigma_{c}^{\rm MK}=\sigma_{\bm{n}}^{\rm MK}+\sigma_{\bm{v}}^{\rm MK}+\sigma_{\bm{t}}^{\rm MK},

obtained by replacing
\bm{f}_{c}
by
\bm{f}_{\bm{n}}
,
\bm{f}_{\bm{v}}
, and
\bm{f}_{\bm{t}}
, respectively.
Using the contact stress tensor, the momentum balance law (
4.9b
) reduces to

\partial_{t}(\rho\bm{u})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\rho\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u})=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\sigma_{c}^{\rm MK}+\bar{\alpha}(\bm{u}_{o}-\bm{u})|\bm{u}_{o}-\bm{u}|,

where
\bar{\alpha}
is defined in (
4.12
).
Herein, the right-hand side consists of a contact stress divergence and an effective ocean drag as in sea ice rheology discussed in
[
42
,
63
,
27
,
40
]
.
We remark that the balance law for angular momentum can be developed similarly. Incorporating rotational effects enriches the continuum description of sea ice rheology in the literature; a detailed development will be pursued in future work.
