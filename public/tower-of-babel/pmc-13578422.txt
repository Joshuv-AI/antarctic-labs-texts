Deep learning-based spatiotemporal estimation of lesion changes for patient-level assessment of breast cancer lung metastases on longitudinal CT
Authors: Jialin Shi, Yali Xu, Zhixian Chen, Ji Wu, Ning Ding, Xiaoping Yi, Feifei Wu, Xiaohong Yang, Yue Shi, Tingzi Yang, He Shi, Junqing Liang, Yanan Wang, Tianyu Ruan, Yiming Cao, Qiang Sun, Lingyan Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical management of breast cancer lung metastasis is challenging because of the complexity of dynamic lesion assessment. Traditional methods based on RECIST1.1 rely on size measurement, and existing studies require image registration and are limited to lesion-level assessment. In this study, we proposed a patient-level spatiotemporal assessment framework without registration to comprehensively analyze multiple lesions evolvement based on longitudinal CT images. Our method considers metastatic lesions that vary in size and often overlap with complex structures such as blood vessels and bones, and avoids potential registration errors. Our method outperforms state-of-the-art methods on both the Peking Union Medical College Hospital breast cancer lung metastasis dataset and the publicly available dataset. The model also showed excellent performance in a multicenter validation across four medical centers. We established a patient-level metastatic breast cancer assessment framework, providing a practical solution for longitudinal treatment monitoring.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Breast cancer has become one of the most commonly diagnosed cancers in the world1,2. The vast majority of breast cancer-related deaths are attributed to distant metastasis, which accounts for 30-40% of all breast cancer cases3. In the context of metastatic breast cancer, there are clinical challenges such as early diagnosis, ineffective treatment, high progression rates, and the low quality of life experienced by patients4,5. Pulmonary metastasis is a common site for visceral metastasis in breast cancer. In clinical practice, early diagnosis of metastatic lesions and monitoring of treatment response primarily rely on computed tomography (CT). Assessing the response of lung metastases to treatment necessitates multiple CT examinations for evaluation6,7. Continuous CT monitoring serves as a vital tool for the early diagnosis and efficacy evaluation of lung metastases in breast cancer8. By comparing the CT imaging characteristics at multiple time points, we can achieve an objective and accurate assessment of the changes in lung metastasis in breast cancer patients, which is crucial for seizing the opportunity to adjust treatment plans and can significantly improve patient prognosis. Nonetheless, estimating patient-specific changes in lesions due to breast cancer lung metastasis continues to present a major challenge in both clinical trials and real-world practice9. In this work, we investigate the use of longitudinal chest CT images as data inputs to study the patient-level evaluation of metastatic pulmonary nodules in breast cancer, thereby aiding in the clinical management of advanced breast cancer.

Radiologists often use electronic calipers to measure the diameter of nodules in two-dimensional CT scanning. According to the RECIST1.1 standard10, tumor changes are roughly evaluated and classified into three states: Partial Response (PR), Stable Disease (SD), and Progressive Disease (PD). However, this method involves manual positioning of the nodule, measuring the longest diameter of the nodule in two CT scans, and calculating the results according to the RECIST1.1 standard. Its clinical application is not only time-consuming but also heavily dependent on the operator’s experience11,12. While the RECIST1.1 relies on changes in tumor size for their response assessment, other morphological changes, such as changes of overall attenuation, tumor-tissue interface, and peripheral rim of enhancement, may occur even earlier in treatment than tumor size change13,14. In contrast, deep learning technology can mine multi-dimensional features of lesions (such as shape, density, volume, dynamic changes, etc.) and identify subtle differences15,16. They will recognize the changes in tumor architecture that sometimes happen before tumor shrinkage, therefore improving the diagnosing accuracy.

Recently, some qualitative and quantitative image analysis algorithms for assessing lesion changes have been proposed. For example, Galperin-Aizenberg et al.17 employed a technique that combines the optical flow method with temporal subtraction to assess interval changes in metastatic lung nodules on chest CT scans. Rochman et al.18 proposed an automated graph-based detection and classification method to analyze lesion changes in follow-up CT scans during tumor surveillance. Lin et al.19 combined convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to analyze sequential medical images of patients with metastatic colorectal cancer in order to predict early treatment response. Szeskin et al.20 proposed a fully automated end-to-end approach to analyze changes in liver lesions across sequential abdominal CECT scans of oncology patients by leveraging the immediately preceding scan and its corresponding lesion segmentation. However, these methods require pre-registered lesion alignment, where manual or computational registration incurs substantial costs and potential errors. Xavier Palou et al.21 proposed a method based on a 3D siamese neural network, which bypassed the traditional image pre-registration steps and directly realized the automatic recognition and evaluation of pulmonary nodules in patients’ continuous CT scanning. But this method transforms the registration problem into the nodule matching problem and still faces the error challenge of matching. Current methodologies predominantly focus on individual lesion analysis, whereas clinical practice demands patient-level assessment integrating longitudinal imaging data to evaluate multi-lesion dynamics.

In diagnosing and treating metastatic pulmonary nodules related to breast cancer, patient-level comprehensive assessment based on longitudinal image data encounters some challenges. First, metastatic breast cancer involves multiple metastatic lesions of different sizes. For example, the diameters of the lesion in the dataset we collected at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (XH-lung dataset) range from 1 mm to 35 mm. Therefore, the lesion estimation model must simultaneously process multi-scale nodules in a single examination and maintain stable recognition performance under complex background interference like vessels and bones, as shown in Fig. 1a and b. Second, predicting response involves assessing CT images from multiple time points while the patient is undergoing treatment rather than relying on a single time point. Traditional methods for evaluating nodules require the temporal model to compare changes in the same nodule across different time series, depending on the registration of nodules taken at different time points or on the premise that they have been accurately registered. However, both manual and machine registration may introduce errors, as illustrated in Fig. 1c, which can further diminish the accuracy of lesion assessments. Third, complex patterns of lesion changes are observed between longitudinal CTs, as shown in Fig. 1d. Smaller or early lesions may be challenging to identify due to insufficient resolution, especially within intricate anatomical structures. As the number of scans in longitudinal studies of patients increases, it becomes increasingly complex to accurately assess changes in images at different time points, to identify the emergence of new lesions, and to monitor the evolution of existing lesions, including complex changes such as fusion of lesions into masses.

To address these challenges, we innovatively propose a deep learning method, Multi-scale MedSAM-assisted Recurrent (MMR) network, for the patient-level lesion assessment of lung metastases of breast cancer without registration. Figure 1e illustrates the flowchart of patient-level lesion assessment for breast cancer lung metastases. Nodules from a patient’s lung CT scans are extracted and analyzed using the MMR network. The patient undergoes consecutive lung CT scans at current time point (T1) and previous time point (T1). Lung nodule images are extracted from these two scans and these images are input into the MMR network for analysis. The output of the MMR network consists of three possible outcomes: PR, SD, and PD. Figure 2 illustrates the lesion assessment pipeline. The overall framework consists of three modules. Step 1: Visual large model-assisted interference removal. The detector extracts the region of interest (ROI) of lung CT as the initial visual cues and generates the segmentation masks by the optimized prompt module MedSAM, which is termed as pMedSAM. In this case, we fine-tune the large medical image segmentation model MedSAM by iterative refinement mechanism to enhance small lesion segmentation while addressing the interference of pulmonary blood vessels and bones. Step 2: Multi-lesion multi-scale feature extraction. We expand the range of lesion sizes that the model can focus on and propose a multi-scale adaptive weighting net (MAW) to simultaneously process multiple lesions of different sizes in a patient (lesion sizes can differ by 35 times). Step 3: Patient-level spatiotemporal analysis. An image registration-free method is implemented for continuous lesion evaluation, effectively mitigating error accumulation inherent in conventional registration approaches. Unlike traditional single-lesion registration methods, we simulate the clinical operations of radiologists and select N of the largest nodules as the target lesions for observation. The temporal and spatial evolution patterns of lesions were analyzed based on the recurrent neural network (RNN) model. Integrating these three modules, the system analyzes longitudinal CT images of lung metastases from breast cancer and finally output patient-level lesion assessment results.

Overview of the MMR network architecture.

The inputs are lung CT images under continuous time series and the outputs are the results of patient lesion assessment (PR/SD/PD). a The segmentation assistance module pMedSAM aims to segment the lung nodule ROI, using a three-round iterative prompt mechanism to optimize the prompt according to the segmentation results and obtain a more accurate segmentation mask. b The MAW module aims to extract the complex multiple-scale lesion features. The original ROI image undergoes a weighting component to get the weight representation of large, medium, and small scales. The red dashed box represents other scales within the observable range. Features are extracted and weighted for three different scales, and then concatenated according to the weights. c The spatiotemporal learning module calculates feature differences for multiple timepoint images at T0 and T1. It learns feature changes at consecutive time points through RNN, and finally outputs the lesion classification results.

A total of 144 patients with 837 series of lung metastases from breast cancer in the XH-Lung dataset are used for our study. The experimental results show that our lesion evaluation method is effective under different lesion sizes and complex background interference, with an accuracy of 0.874 (sensitivity of 0.903, specificity of 0.859) and an AUC of 0.947. Besides, we collect 33 patients with 198 series from four external medical institutions as the multi-center dataset to verify the method. Specifically, it achieved an AUC of 0.902 and an accuracy of 0.846 at Hunan, an AUC of 0.938 and an accuracy of 0.875 at Shanxi, an AUC of 0.976 and an accuracy of 0.947 at Guangxi, an AUC of 0.877 and an accuracy of 0.838 at Inner Mongolia.

Results

Patient characteristics

XH-Lung dataset. We used the imaging data of patients with lung metastasis of breast cancer in the metastatic recurrence cohort of the Department of Breast Surgery, Peking Union Medical College Hospital (XH-Lung dataset). As of April 25, 2024, a total of 144 patients with lung metastases were included. With the approval of the Ethics Committee (approval number I-23PJ1889), 837 CT series of 144 patients were retrieved, and the imaging and clinical records were systematically reviewed and analyzed. CT scans were acquired by CT scanners sourced from some manufacturers, notably Siemens, GE Medical Systems, Canon Medical Systems, Philips Healthcare, United Imaging Healthcare, and SinoVision. The thickness of the CT slices is 5.00 mm. Scanning protocols vary based on the scanners employed and the specific clinical requirements, yet the majority are configured for chest CT plain scan.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Female patients; (2) Breast cancer confirmed by pathology; (3) Confirmed/suspected lung metastasis, lesions can be evaluated; (4) At least two CT sequences; (5) Continuous scanning interval 30-365 days; (6) Accessible clinical reports. Male patients with breast cancer were excluded. After screening, 20 patients (85 series) were excluded because of inflammatory nodules (57 series), single CT scan (2 series), male sex (7 series), pelvic and abdominal CT scan (6 series), missing clinical reports (3 series), and lung lesions that could not be evaluated (10 series). The final dataset consisted of 752 CT sequences from 124 patients. As shown in Fig. 3, the data is divided into a training set (105 cases, 600 series) and a test set (19 cases, 152 series). In the training set, 480 sequences (80%) were selected by stratified sampling, and the pMedSAM model was jointly trained by transfer learning and a weighted network.

XH-Lung dataset.

An 837 series of CT image sequences of patients with breast cancer lung metastases was collected from the radiology department. During the screening process, we excluded 85 series, and a total of 752 series were available for the study. These case sequences were divided into a training cohort (600 series) and a test cohort (152 series). In the training cohort, we additionally used 480 series (80% of the total training cohort) for pMedSAM for fine-tuning and weighted CNN training. Based on the disease state, we classified the CT image sequences two by two into a pair and categorized them as PR, SD and PD.

Two CT series were paired as a group for lesion assessment. The responses were labeled as PR, SD, or PD based on CT imaging reports and, if necessary, guidance from radiologists. Radiologists annotated each nodule using ITK-SNAP medical imaging software to create segmentation mask labels. Nodules were classified into 3 categories according to their maximum diameter: large (>15 mm), medium (5-15 mm), and small (<5 mm). This dataset division strategy enables the multiscale network to effectively learn feature representations of different nodule sizes while being consistent with the clinical decision process based on the lesion dimension. In our study, nodule diameters are mainly concentrated in a small range (0–5 mm), and the frequency decreases significantly with the increase of size, showing a typical right-skewed distribution. This pattern suggests a high prevalence of small nodules and relatively few large nodules in the study cohort.

Multi-center dataset. We conducted the multicenter validation study integrating datasets from four medical institutions (Hunan, Guangxi, Shanxi, and Inner Mongolia). As shown in Table 1b, a total of four independent datasets were included: the Hunan dataset contained 14 patients (115 CT series), the Guangxi dataset contained 5 patients (24 CT series), the Shanxi dataset contained 6 patients (14 CT series), and the Inner Mongolia dataset contained 8 patients (45 CT series). The datasets exhibit geographical diversity, encompassing central (Hunan), southern (Guangxi), northern (Shanxi), and northwestern (Inner Mongolia) regions of China. CT equipment parameters vary across centers, with three institutions utilizing GE Medical Systems scanners and one employing a Toshiba scanner. The thicknesses of the CT slices in different centers were 5.00 mm and 6.25 mm. Scanning protocols are heterogeneous, reflecting differences such as “CHEST+ABDOMEN 2Phase” and “Chest 5MM C-/C+” across sites. This inherent heterogeneity offers a valuable testbed for evaluating model adaptability to diverse technical settings and operational workflows. It effectively simulates real-world clinical environments, where algorithms must perform consistently across varying scanner types and imaging protocols.

Public DLS dataset. We also applied an open-source lesion tracking benchmark dataset, the Deep Longitudinal Study (DLS)22. The DLS is constructed from 3891 lesion pairs from the public DeepLesion database23. This dataset has information including patient id, series id, and lesion diameter. In contrast to the original private XH dataset, 80% of DLS lesions exceed 10 mm in diameter and represent typical lung nodules. During data processing, three radiologists with more than 10 years of clinical experience performed quality assessment, and finally, 277 image series of 148 patients were retained. Each validation sample is annotated with the disease change label (PR:SD:PD = 29:193:55) to support model training and subsequent analysis.

The performance comparison with state-of-the-art methods

We benchmark our method against state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods for lesion change assessment. The baseline methods include: (1) Spatial-Temporal Mixer (STM)24: A spatiotemporal framework originally designed for predicting pulmonary nodule growth trends by analyzing sequential CT scans. We adapted its objective to meet clinical needs for growth status evaluation by redefining its output from future growth prediction to multi-phase CT sequence-based growth trend assessment. This modification enables continuous temporal evaluation of lesion evolution. (2) SimU-Net20: An end-to-end deep learning pipeline for analyzing liver lesion changes in consecutive abdominal CECT scans. It synchronously processes paired CT sequences for automatic lesion identification, quantification, and dynamic change analysis. We implemented two evaluation strategies. SimU_RECIST1.1: Lesion change assessment based on RECIST 1.1 criteria using maximum diameter measurements from segmentation outputs. SimU_MR: Comprehensive evaluation combining segmentation results with MAW module and spatiotemporal feature learning. (3) SimS: A modified SimU-Net variant that processes only single-timepoint CT data from previous scans, excluding multi-temporal information integration. (4) SimP: A modified SimU-Net architecture that analyzes paired baseline and follow-up CT scans without incorporating segmentation data. (5) Siamese Neural Networks (SNN)21: A non-registration approach for unsupervised lesion re-identification through cross-temporal CT feature similarity analysis. Two evaluation modes were implemented. SNN_RECIST1.1: Growth estimation using spatial features with RECIST 1.1 criteria. SNN_FC: Qualitative assessment replacing growth detection with a tri-classifier (PR/SD/PD) for evolutionary pattern recognition.

The results of the performance comparison of the SOTA methods on the XH-Lung dataset are shown in Table 2a. All performance metrics reported in the tables are averaged over 3 independent runs. Experimental results show that the proposed MMR model shows performance advantages on the XH-Lung dataset. MMR was superior to the baseline methods in all evaluation indicators. For example, the accuracy is 0.874 ± 0.014 (relative improvement of 12.3% and absolute gain of 0.096 over the second best method). The sensitivity is 0.903 ± 0.020, the specificity is 0.859 ± 0.028, and the AUC value is 0.947. It is worth noting that the performance of the traditional RECIST evaluation paradigm (e.g., SimU_RECIST1.1) is significantly inferior to the baseline method, with an accuracy of 0.667 ± 0.001 and a sensitivity of 0.433 ± 0.002. The p-values were calculated via two-sided z-test. Statistical significance analysis (two-sided z-test) confirms that MMR’s accuracy is significantly higher than other baseline methods (p < 0.05).

The evaluation of visual model-assisted interference removal

To validate the effectiveness of the segmentation-assisted network on pulmonary blood vessel and bone interference, we conducted the grouping experiment of interference removal on the XH-Lung test set. The 50 pairs in the test set were divided into two subgroups: group A (30 pairs), where the lesion was independent with minimal interference (as shown in Fig. 5a); group B (20 pairs), where the lesion was affected by vascular/bone interference (as shown in Fig. 5b). We analyzed the performance of the model in each subgroup while controlling whether or not the segmentation-assisted network was added (Fig. 5c). In Group A which has the low level of background interference, the segmentation-assisted network improves the accuracy from 0.835 to 0.901 and the AUC from 0.877 to 0.935. This proves that even in scenes with less interference, feature expression can be enhanced through anatomical decoupling by a segmentation-assisted network, especially for semisolid and ground-glass nodules. For Group B, which had more vascular/bone interference, the network improved the accuracy from 0.741 to 0.889 while maintaining a steady increase in AUC from 0.767 to 0.892, demonstrating its ability to optimize performance by suppressing complex background interference.

Visual and quantitative evaluation of segmentation-assisted lesion recognition under different interference conditions.

a Lesion examples with little interference. b Lesion examples with blood vessel/bone interference. c ACC and AUC results with/without segmentation assistance for low-interference (Group A) and high-interference (Group B) cases. d Dice coefficients of different segmentation-assisted methods.

The introduction of the segmentation-assisted network yielded pronounced improvements for both Group A and Group B. For Group A, by providing a precise lesion mask, the network achieves anatomical decoupling, i.e., directing the model’s attention from background structures to the lesion itself. In group B, the segmentation module mainly suppresses high-intensity artifacts from blood vessels and bones. These results indicate that the proposed segmentation module can not only alleviate complex background interference but also generally amplify the features of low-contrast lesions, thereby enhancing the robustness of the model in different clinical scenarios.

To further validate the effectiveness of different segmentation methods for interference removal, we conducted comparative experiments among classical segmentation models (R2U-Net[35], SimU-Net[20]) and the prompt-optimized pMedSAM method. We use the Dice coefficient (measuring similarity between samples, ranging from 0–1 with higher values indicating better segmentation accuracy) as the evaluation metric. The results are shown in Fig. 5d. Under identical experimental configurations, the prompt-optimized pMedSAM model achieved superior segmentation performance (Dice=0.832), representing a 8.3% improvement over the baseline MedSAM (0.768 → 0.832). This enhancement demonstrates the critical role of prompt optimization in adapting foundation models for medical imaging challenges.

Multi-lesion multi-scale feature extraction

To verify whether MAW can enhance the model’s ability to extract features of nodules with different sizes and improve the recognition and representation of nodules, we designed the MAW ablation study. We manually divided the XH-Lung validation set into three subgroups according to the long axis diameter of the nodules: large group (diameter >15 mm, 23 pairs), medium group (5 mm ≤ diameter ≤15 mm, 18 pairs), and small group (diameter < 5 mm, 27 pairs). Figure 6a shows the performance comparison between the models with and without multi-scale feature extraction enabled in different subgroups. The version equipped with MAW has a significant improvement over the model without MAW. The accuracy of the small group increased from 0.778 to 0.889, and the accuracy of the large group increased from 0.870 to 0.935.

It is worth noting that the medium group maintains a good accuracy. This may be because the nodules with 5-15 mm show the best representative features in conventional CT imaging, and the single-scale network can extract effective features at this time. The multi-scale mechanism mainly enhances the feature extraction of small nodules with ambiguous features and large nodules with complex morphological patterns.

Verification of the effectiveness of the Top-N strategy

In the MMR network, we achieved the registration-free patient-level lesion evaluation method by selecting the largest N nodules as the network input based on the segmentation results. To determine the best value of N, we systematically tune this parameter using a control variable approach based on the segmentation output of the pMedSAM model. As shown in Fig. 6b, the experimental results show that when N = 3, the proposed model achieves the optimal performance in terms of lesion-matching accuracy, sensitivity, and AUC. This finding is in close agreement with clinical practice standards that prioritize monitoring the 2-3 largest target nodules for longitudinal assessment.

To further verify the effectiveness of our Top-N strategy, three experimental groups (Table 3a) were designed for comparison. (1) Expert group: using nodules labeled by radiologists with more than 20 years experience as input; (2) Registration group: using a rigid registration algorithm36 to achieve nodule spatial matching; (3) Non-registration group: including random selection, classifier-based selection, and the segmentation-assisted selection method (Ours). For classifier-based selection, a three-class classification model based on resnet50 (nodules are divided into three types: large, medium, and small) is used to select the top three confidence samples. The experimental results show that the segmentation-assisted selection method achieves excellent comprehensive performance (AUC = 0.954), and the accuracy and sensitivity indicators are close to the expert level.

Multi-center validation

To evaluate the generalization ability of the model, we collected independent external datasets from four medical centers and performed multicenter validation after standardized preprocessing. The results are shown in Table 3b. Our method showed consistently high performance in all centers. The performance results were AUC 0.976 and accuracy 0.947 in Guangxi, AUC 0.938 and accuracy 0.875 in Shanxi, AUC 0.902 and accuracy 0.846 in Hunan, and AUC 0.877 and accuracy 0.838 in Inner Mongolia. Although the data of each center varied in terms of region, equipment, and image acquisition, the accuracy of all centers exceeded 0.83, and the AUC value was also higher than 0.87. Moreover, the F1-scores across centers were consistently high (Hunan: 0.832, Shanxi_ 0.857, Guangxi_ 0.923, Inner Mongolia: 0.821). The comprehensive validation results confirmed the robust generalization performance of the proposed model on multicenter datasets and demonstrated the stable lesion assessment in real clinical settings.

Discussion

Through a visualized comparative study of misdiagnosis cases in the XH-Lung dataset (as shown in Fig. 7a, where each case group comprehensively presents T0/T1 lesion images, gold standard annotations, and model prediction results), we identified three critical error patterns. (1) Threshold determination drift. Individual variations in clinicians’ judgment thresholds for disease progression led to difficulties in establishing universal pathological change criteria. For instance, in case ID: FLC776F1T679_5, the model became susceptible to annotators’ subjective variations when lesion evolution magnitude fell within the critical zone of clinical judgment thresholds. (2) Complex background interference. Although the segmentation auxiliary network effectively suppressed interference from pulmonary vessels and bones, feature confusion still exists. As demonstrated in case ID: CSHC218FOF949_5, small nodules showed particularly evident misjudgments under strong background interference. (3) Cavitary nodule feature generalization deficiency. The model exhibited limited generalization capability for rare clinical cases with cavitary nodules, which represent challenging progression assessment targets. Case ID: LJL424EIN7335_5 illustrates how our model might misinterpret such cases. Analysis of cavitary nodule segmentation masks (Fig. 7b) revealed that even after data fine-tuning, the segmentation-assisted model struggled due to insufficient training samples containing cavitary nodules.

Evaluating the efficacy of salvage treatment for metastatic breast cancer remains a common clinical challenge, and subsequent treatment strategies must be adjusted based on the evaluation results25. The imaging modality selected for efficacy assessment typically depends on the location of metastasis. For lung metastases, PET/CT and non-contrast chest CT (i.e., chest CT without contrast, or chest CT plain scan) are commonly used. Although PET/CT has high sensitivity, its clinical use for routine monitoring is limited due to high equipment requirements, cost, and procedural complexity26–29. In contrast, chest CT plain scans offer key advantages in evaluating lung metastases from breast cancer, including better accessibility, lower cost, and ease of operation30. Traditional manual interpretation by radiologists or clinicians presents significant challenges, including limited efficiency and subjectivity in lesion assessment31. AI models based on chest CT plain scan imaging show great promise in improving diagnostic efficiency and the accuracy of treatment evaluation. In particular, advanced models that integrate multimodal treatment-related imaging features may play an increasingly important role in assessing therapeutic response in advanced malignancies32. However, clinical implementation of such AI tools must involve a comprehensive interpretation that integrates imaging results with the broader clinical context, rather than relying solely on algorithmic predictions. The lesion assessment model in this study currently uses 2D lesion slices as input, mainly considering both training efficiency and computational cost. However, it should be noted that the morphological features in 3D space, including the volume change rate, spatial invasion pattern, and heterogeneous distribution of lesions, may have more direct clinical significance for diagnostic evaluation and prognosis prediction. With the optimization of 3D-CNN architecture and the enhancement of GPU parallel computing power, the preprocessed 3D lesion voxel blocks can be explored as model input in the future.

Multi-scale feature extraction is one of the most commonly used AI techniques. It simulates the process by which pathologists continuously adjust the magnification of a microscope to obtain pathological features at different scales according to diagnostic need33,34. It was used to extract global and local features, assisting in the diagnosis of breast diseases35. In this study, we propose the MAW network as an effective implementation of multi-scale feature extraction technology for the task of identifying small pulmonary nodules. Ablation study results show that using multi-scale feature extraction to assist in the evaluation of pulmonary metastatic lesions can greatly enhance model performance (The AUC of the small nodule group improved from 0.838 to 0.882).

We employed a Top-N (N = 3) strategy to prioritize the largest lesions as observation targets, primarily emulating clinical RECIST. The multi-scale feature extraction mechanism of the MAW module effectively preserves and dynamically weights the features of small lesions during multi-scale fusion, ensuring their information is not completely overlooked. Experimental results (Fig. 6a) also show that even when selecting only the Top-3 lesions, the model’s recognition accuracy for small lesions improved from 0.778 to 0.889 (a relative improvement of 11.1%). However, clinically significant small lesions (e.g., newly developed or rapidly progressive nodules) may still be missed if their size is not included in Top-N selection. Future work will explore nodule selection mechanisms that incorporate temporal change metrics (e.g., volume growth rate) or malignancy risk scores to more comprehensively capture disease dynamics, particularly for monitoring small lesions with high clinical relevance.

Medical imaging is fundamental to the diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer, offering noninvasive and comprehensive visualization of both intra-tumor and peritumoral structures. However, a persistent challenge in clinical practice is the inconsistency of radiology reports, which stems largely from the subjective nature of image interpretation by radiologists. Even when reviewing the same imaging data, different radiologists may arrive at varying conclusions, especially in borderline cases near diagnostic thresholds36. These differences in clinical judgment translate directly into inconsistent reporting, which can compromise diagnostic reliability and affect treatment decisions. This variability is especially concerning in advanced breast cancer. AI models, particularly those based on deep learning radiomics, can automatically extract high-dimensional imaging features that are difficult to quantify through human observation alone37,38. These models are capable of capturing subtle patterns related to inter- and intra-tumor heterogeneity39, ensuring a more reproducible and standardized assessment across cases. By integrating AI-derived radiological features with clinicopathological data, it becomes possible to more accurately evaluate treatment responses and guide personalized therapeutic strategies. In doing so, AI not only alleviates the interpretation burden on radiologists40 but also significantly reduces report-to-report variability.

In the clinical transformation of medical imaging AI models, generalizability is one of the core indicators for evaluating the practical utility of a model. Most current radiomics and deep learning research heavily rely on single-center datasets with limited sample sizes for model development and validation. There are significant differences among the centers, such as the imaging equipment used, acquisition parameters, reconstruction algorithms, contrast agent types, and injection protocols. A systematic review has shown that different acquisition and reconstruction protocols significantly affect the consistency of radiomic features41. We developed the model based on a private dataset and conducted multi-center validations, which demonstrated strong performance. Nonetheless, such validation cannot fully capture population-level heterogeneity. The model excluded certain subtypes, such as isolated pleural nodules and mediastinal masses, due to limited sample availability. This exclusion restricted the algorithm’s diagnostic utility for particular metastatic. Male breast cancer cases were also excluded to avoid gender imbalance. Moreover, the model demonstrated suboptimal performance in cases involving cavity metastases or heavy background interference. These limitations reflect the potential opportunities for architectural improvements to enhance robustness in challenging clinical scenarios. Future research should emphasize expanding the training cohort, standardizing acquisition protocols, and developing subtype-specific optimization strategies to improve clinical utility.

From the Table 3b of multi-center external validation, we can observe that both the accuracy and AUC on the Inner Mongolia Cancer dataset were slightly lower than those of the other centers. We attribute this observation to the following three factors. First, compared to the Hunan test set, the Inner Mongolia test set has a smaller sample size (n = 8). Compared to the Shanxi and Guangxi datasets, the Inner Mongolia test set has an uneven distribution of follow-up time points (mostly 2–5 visits, but some cases have as many as 16 visits). Second, there are differences in scanning protocols and lesion distribution across multiple centers (domain shift), which leads to reduced model generalization. Third, our model has limitations in complex scenarios such as cavitary nodules and strong vascular/bone interference (see Fig. 7), and the Inner Mongolia dataset is subject to strong interference. All of these factors may contribute to the low AUC for this center, reflecting data heterogeneity and sample instability. Future work will improve the model’s cross-center generalization by integrating more diverse multi-center data and introducing techniques such as imaging normalization, domain adaptation, and small-sample fine-tuning.

While the MMR framework demonstrates promising performance across multiple internal and external datasets, several limitations warrant clarification. First, the model’s generalization to rare lesion types is limited. As shown in Fig. 7, the model is more likely to misclassify lesions with sparse training data, such as cavitary nodules, reflecting the limitations of sample-distribution bias on model robustness. Second, this method primarily utilizes 2D slice/ROI representations, failing to fully exploit complete 3D volumetric information (e.g., lesion volume change rate, spatial invasion pattern, and 3D morphological context). Although 2D designs are computationally and deployment-efficient, future implementations of 3D representations or hybrid 2D/3D strategies are expected to further enhance modeling of complex morphological evolution. Third, this study is based on retrospective multicenter data and has been validated across centers. However, its actual impact on treatment decisions and patient outcomes still needs to be assessed in larger cohorts and prospective clinical trials. Future work includes: (1) expanding and balancing the dataset to include multiple types such as cavitary nodules, pleural or mediastinal masses; (2) exploring 3D input or 2D/3D hybrid networks to utilize volumetric information; (3) facilitating prospective multicenter validation to promote clinical translation.

In conclusion, we proposed a patient-level automatic assessment model for breast cancer lung metastasis progression, aiming to provide clinical decision support based on deep learning for identifying disease status changes. The proposed model addresses three key challenges in lesion assessment: (1) the interference of bone and blood vessels hinders lesion feature extraction; (2) it is difficult to fully characterize the features of small nodules and simultaneously consider lesions of different sizes; (3) traditional single lesion assessment methods based on image registration have inherent registration errors. In this work, we first developed a segmentation-assisted method that can effectively extract nodule features under the interference of pulmonary vessels and bones. Second, a multi-scale feature extraction method MAW was proposed to successfully incorporate small nodules into lesion assessment. We proposed the first patient-level assessment method that did not require registration, which used a recurrent neural network to fuse spatiotemporal features. Experimental validation on the XH-Lung test set confirmed the effectiveness of the model and achieved accurate disease progression assessment. Furthermore, multi-center validation across four independent clinical centers demonstrated excellent performance, confirming the model’s generalizability across diverse populations and imaging protocols. Our future research will further enhance precision clinical decision-making for breast cancer lung metastasis by dynamically monitoring lesion changes to guide personalized treatment. Additionally, we aim to establish remote standardized assessment tools to improve diagnostic accessibility through systematic monitoring frameworks.

The proposed overall framework

The framework consists of three modules. Step 1: Visual large model-assisted interference removal. The detector extracts the region of interest (ROI) of lung CT as the initial visual cues and generates the segmentation masks by the optimized prompt module MedSAM, which is termed as pMedSAM. In this case, we fine-tuned the large medical image segmentation model MedSAM by the three-round iterative refinement mechanism to enhance small lesion segmentation while addressing the interference of pulmonary blood vessels and bones. The three rounds were empirically determined to provide a balance between accuracy and computational efficiency. Step 2: Multi-lesion multi-scale feature extraction. We propose a multi-scale adaptive weighting network (MAW) to adaptively fuse multi-scale nodule features to overcome the limitations of traditional networks for small lesion recognition. Step 3: Patient-level spatiotemporal analysis. An image registration-free method was implemented for continuous lesion evaluation, effectively mitigating error accumulation inherent in conventional registration approaches. Unlike traditional single-lesion registration methods, we simulated the clinical operation of radiologists and selected N of the largest nodules as the target lesions for observation. The temporal and spatial evolution patterns of lesions were analyzed based on the recurrent neural network (RNN) model. Integrating these three modules, the system analyzed longitudinal CT images of lung metastases from breast cancer and finally output patient-level lesion assessment results.

Visual model-assisted interference removal

We proposed pMedSAM to tackle the challenging task of lung-metastasis segmentation under vascular and bone interference. The pMedSAM is the prompt-optimized adaptation of the MedSAM model42, which is derived from the general-purpose SAM43 framework. Focusing on single-timepoint lung CT analysis, we employ the Faster-RCNN object detector44 for automated pulmonary metastatic nodule detection, generating initial prompts through center-expanded bounding box adjustments. All detected regions are standardized to 40 mm×40 mm physical dimensions to ensure dimensional consistency in nodule ROIs. We propose a three-round iterative prompt refinement mechanism that progressively enhances segmentation accuracy via sequential optimization. The process initiates with segmentation to identify target region centroids, which subsequently guide prompt generation for the next iteration. Through this cyclic optimization strategy, positional accuracy and segmentation precision achieve cumulative improvement across three processing stages, with each iteration dynamically adjusting prompts based on prior segmentation outcomes. This approach effectively addresses small lesion omission while demonstrating superior performance in complex medical imaging scenarios with background interference.

Using a patient-level data partitioning strategy, the dataset was divided into two groups: the first group is the pMedSAM model fine-tuning group and the second group is the MMR network training group. This partition prevents data leakage during model training. Firstly, the pMedSAM model was fine-tuned by the patient data of the first group to optimize its adaptability to the lung nodule segmentation task. During the subsequent MMR network training, the fine-tuned pMedSAM model parameters are kept frozen to ensure stability. Then, the optimized pMedSAM model segmented the nodule ROI region to generate the corresponding mask map. To preserve the segmentation information and original image details, the segmentation mask is cascaded with the original CT image channel to create a fusion feature map, which is used as the input of the subsequent feature extraction network.

Multi-lesion multi-scale feature extraction

To enhance the model’s capability in characterizing pulmonary nodules, we developed the Multi-scale Adaptive Weighting Net (MAW) based on the Adaptive Weighting CNN framework45. This architecture captures nodule details across multiple scales to improve recognition performance, particularly for small nodules. The method first processes the original ROI images through a Multi-scale Weighting CNN to generate three discriminative weight maps, which guide feature weighting in subsequent stages. Guided by clinical expertise, we crop both original ROI and segmentation mask images into small (10 mm × 10 mm), medium (25 mm×25 mm), and large (40 mm × 40 mm) scales. The 10 mm window targets sub-centimeter nodules (<10 mm) prone to missed diagnosis and challenging for benign-malignant differentiation. The 25 mm window focuses on typical suspicious nodules (10-30 mm), a critical screening range, while the 40 mm window captures large nodules and the surrounding anatomical context. This multi-scale design allows a small window to magnify subtle features of small nodules, while a large window can retain the complete morphological information of larger nodules. To strengthen scale-specific feature extraction, ResNet5046 processes each scale’s cropped images separately. The outputs are then integrated through weighted concatenation, enabling dynamic weight allocation based on nodule size. This adaptive mechanism significantly enhances small nodule characterization while maintaining global feature preservation for larger nodules, achieving comprehensive multi-scale representation of pulmonary lesions.

They are dynamically generated by a separate, pre-trained classification subnetwork. Based on the ResNet50 architecture, this subnetwork is first pre-trained on ImageNet and then fine-tuned on our XH-Lung dataset, which can classify lung nodules into three categories based on their maximum diameter: small (<5 mm), medium (5–15 mm), and large (>15 mm). For any input nodule ROI image, this subnetwork outputs a probability distribution [ω1,ω2,ω3] for the three categories, which is directly used as the fusion weights in Eq. (2).

The weighted network in MAW dynamically generates adaptive weights for small, medium, and large-scale features, and enhances multi-scale feature fusion by emphasizing nodule features of different sizes. Using ResNet50 as the backbone architecture, our implementation consists of three key steps: (1) extract 3901 manually labeled nodule samples (72% of the nodules in the training set) from the MMR network training data, classified by diameters - large (>15 mm), medium (5-15 mm), and small (<5 mm); (2) These labeled samples are used to train an ImageNet pre-trained ResNet50 model by classification learning; 3) The probabilities ω1, ω2, ω3 are output by ResNet50. They represent the probability distribution of small, medium, and large nodules, respectively, and are used as fusion weights. For smaller nodules, a higher ω1 value magnifies the scale features, while larger nodules show increased ω3 weights. This scale-adaptive weighting mechanism enables the model to strategically combine multi-scale features. It allows for effective capture of discriminative features across different nodule sizes through probability-driven feature fusion optimization.

Patient-level spatiotemporal feature learning

In clinical practice, radiologists typically assess disease progression in patients with lung metastases by manually tracking selected target lesions in sequential imaging studies according to RECIST 1.1 criteria. Traditional methods require time-consuming image registration for lesion comparison, and a lot of manpower is required due to the randomness of target lesion selection. To address these limitations, we propose a registration-free assessment method inspired by clinical expertise. The framework automatically selects the Top-N largest lung nodules from the pMedSAM segmentation results as key observation targets to monitor disease changes. The reasons are as follows. First, this Top-N selection strategy is clinically consistent with RECIST 1.1, which recommends tracking several representative target lesions to assess overall disease burden. Second, focusing on the largest nodules provides a more reliable and robust signal for longitudinal monitoring, as they are generally more likely to be accurately segmented and remain consistent over time. Conversely, including all detected nodules may introduce noise from segmentation errors, false positives, and lesions of low clinical relevance, potentially reducing model performance and increasing computational burden. This strategy effectively replaces the traditional registration process while reducing human intervention. Experimental validation shows that setting N = 3, which tracks the three largest nodules, achieves the best performance in capturing pathological changes and assessing lesion characteristics.

To comprehensively assess a patient’s condition, the proposed method integrates spatiotemporal features by analyzing multiple nodules across time series. As shown in Fig. 2c, we propose a spatiotemporal fusion method to concatenate multiple lung nodule features at each time point to form a unified spatial representation. By calculating the feature difference between consecutive time points, the temporal evolution law of the lesion is captured and the growth or shrinkage pattern of the lesion is revealed. The proposed method preserves spatial features and tracks temporal dynamics. The calculated temporal differences are then processed by a recurrent neural network (RNN), specifically a bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (Bi-GRU), which effectively models short-term temporal dependencies between adjacent time points. The Bi-GRU architecture was chosen for its ability to capture both forward and backward sequential context with few parameters and low computational overhead.

Implementation details

In this study, all experiments are performed on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU platform under CUDA 11.3 and implemented in Python 3.8 and PyTorch 1.12.1. The MMR network uses the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001, combined with the StepLR scheduler. The MMR network is optimized using the cross-entropy loss function. The batch size is 12, and the training epochs is 300.

For lung nodule segmentation, the LiteMedSAM framework is adopted. The open-source implementation is available at https://github.com/bowang-lab/MedSAM/tree/LiteMedSAM. We randomly select 1/3 of the samples from the XH-Lung dataset for model training. AdamW (initial learning rate of 5e−5) and ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler were used for optimization. The batch size is 4, and the training epochs are 500.

We developed a weighted network model based on the ResNet50 architecture with ImageNet pre-trained weights, trained on the XH-Lung dataset. This dataset contains 247 large nodules, 400 medium nodules, and 500 small nodules, which are divided into training and test sets with a 4:1 ratio. The model uses an SGD optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and a cosine annealing scheduler (minimum rate multiplier =0.01). The batch size is 16, and the training epochs are 500.

For data preprocessing, the XH-Lung DICOM datasets were processed through three steps. (1) Hounsfield Unit standardization with [−1000, 400] windowing followed by [0, 1] normalization, (2) spatial homogenization using DICOM pixel spacing metadata to resample images via bilinear interpolation, and (3) post-detection refinement where Faster R-CNN-derived bounding boxes underwent center-based expansion with 40 × 40 pixel initialization for nodule localization.

Data augmentation incorporated a 50% probability of horizontal flips during the training stage. The test samples strictly maintained the original orientation and did not undergo random transformations. Pixel intensity normalization followed a two-stage process: initial linear scaling from [0,255] to [0,1] succeeded by channel-wise standardization with (μ = σ = 0.5 per channel) to ensure distributional consistency across modalities.
