Green synthesized iron oxide (Fe2O3) nanoparticle affects the germination and growth-related characteristics of different basil (Ocimum basilicum L.) cultivars
Authors: Hassan Esmaeili, Fatemeh Asghartabar Kashi, Zinab Moradi Alvand, Liana Parseghian, Fatemeh Askari, Hasan Rafati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractConventional methods for nanoparticle (NP) synthesis often rely on unsafe chemical reduction processes. Plant-mediated green synthesis of NPs has emerged as a promising alternative to such toxic reductants. Seed priming is an effective technique for improving seed germination and plant growth characteristics. Nano-priming outperforms traditional seed priming methods owing to the nanoparticles’ nanoscale dimensions and unique physicochemical properties. In this study, the extract of Mentha pulegium L. plant was used for Fe2O3-NPs synthesis. Afterward, the seeds of four basil (Ocimum basilicum L.) cultivars (Dark opal, Iranian purple basil, Iranian green basil, and sweet basil) were treated with four concentrations of Fe2O3-NPs (0, 50, 100, and 200 ppm) for two exposure times (6 and 12 h) to evaluate seed germination and growth-related traits of the plants. The green synthesized NPs were characterized via different techniques, including UV-Vis absorption, Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The results showed that the average size of the prepared Fe2O3-NPs was around 82.24 nm. The response of basil cultivars was completely dependent on the dosage administered, duration of exposure, and cultivar type, as lower concentrations of iron nanoparticles provided a positive response to seed germination and growth parameters of cultivars. However, in dark opal basil cultivar, maximum values for shoot length (20.73 ± 1.78 mm), root length (29.68 ± 1.87 mm), and root fresh and dry weights were achieved at 200 ppm with 12 h exposure time. The highest germination percentage (95%) among all cultivars was obtained in dark opal cultivar using 100 ppm Iron NPs and 12 h treatment. The use of green-synthesized iron NPs for seed priming offers advantages such as eco-friendliness, reduced toxicity, enhanced nutrient delivery, and improved germination efficiency compared to conventional methods.Keywords: Green synthesis, Ocimum basilicum, Fe2O3-NPs, Germination, Nano-primingSubject terms: Biotechnology, Nanoscience and technology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Iron (Fe) serves as a vital micronutrient required for plant growth and development. Agricultural yields, food quality standards, and human dietary health are all closely tied to its availability in soil. However, the limited availability of iron to plants results from its tendency to form insoluble oxides or hydroxides in soil, especially in alkaline pH environments1. Iron deficiency can widely arise due to high pH, low organic matter, salt stress, extended droughts, high bicarbonate content in irrigation water, calcareous soils, and irregular fertilizer application. The application of synthetic Fe chelates, despite some risks, has proven to be a successful way to overcome Fe chlorosis2. Using nanotechnology and nanomaterials to create novel Fe fertilizers could offer substitute ways to treat Fe chlorosis symptoms. The properties of nanoparticles (NPs) can be enhanced or modified depending on their size, dispersion, and shape. Because of their greater surface area to volume ratio, nanoparticles are more active and have superior absorption than their bulk counterparts3.

Seed priming involves several biochemical alterations in the seed that increase the rate of germination, uniformity of development, and the ability of seedlings to withstand adverse environmental circumstances4. Nano-priming refers to the application of nanoparticles during the seed priming process to improve seed performance and plant growth. The seed nano-priming is known as a potential way to generate nanopores and enhance the absorption of water and nutrients5. Nano-priming enhances seed germination, plant growth, and physiological functions by modifying nutrient uptake, metabolic pathways, antioxidant activity, and photosynthetic efficiency6. Nanomaterials can increase seedling vigor, root and shoot development, and biomass production. Studies have suggested that nano-priming treatments may enhance seed germination and improve seedling vigor in some crop species7–9. For example, zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles have been shown to stimulate plant growth by enhancing the availability of micronutrients and activating antioxidant defenses10. Research has indicated that treating seeds with silver nanoparticle (Ag-NP) solutions can loosen the endosperm cell walls and seed coat, thereby promoting germination11. The Chickpea (Cicer arietinum) germination has significantly improved by iron oxide NPs priming, which has also increased the content of lipophilic non-enzymatic antioxidants12. The effective role of iron NPs in stimulating the growth of plants in both stressful and non-stressful environments has been demonstrated in previous study, which indicated their huge potential for application in agricultural productivity13. The Fe-NPs, Fe2O3-NPs, and Fe3O4-NPs are examples of oxidized iron nanoparticles that are effective in increasing plant growth14. The use of iron nanoparticles is believed to enhance productivity by optimizing key physiological processes such as seed germination, photosynthetic efficiency, plant growth, and the synthesis of metabolites, including proteins and nitrogen-containing compounds15,16. Research has also suggested that iron nanoparticles may stimulate plant growth by modulating the activity of antioxidant and Calvin cycle enzymes, as well as regulating phytohormone levels17. Research demonstrated iron nanoparticles (Fe-NPs) ability to enhance root and coleoptile elongation, improve seedling vigor, and accelerate germination rates18. Treatment of Zea mays L. cv. Merit with iron pyrite nanoparticles (Fe-pyrite NPs) led to a marked increase in leaf biomass (size and quantity) and elevated concentrations of calcium (Ca), manganese (Mn), and zinc (Zn) in leaf tissues19.

The annual plant Basil (Ocimum basilicum L.) has several cultivars, each known with unique characteristics in structure, leaf, flower, and stem traits. Their height ranged from dwarf varieties (15–30 cm) to tall (up to 1.5 m), while their leaves vary in size (1.8–12.4 cm long), shape (ovate, lanceolate), and color (green, purple, variegated). Their flowers also ranged from white to purple, with stem colors including green, purple, or red20.

Recently, green, simple, non-toxic, economical, and ecologically friendly NPs have been produced by using plant extract as a natural reducer and suitable replacement for current procedures21. However, herbal extract preparation, especially the extraction technique, plays a prominent role in plant-mediated NP synthesis22. Several reports have proposed the use of plant extracts from the Lamiaceae family for synthesizing various NPs, including silver, bismuth, gold, iron, zinc, and copper NPs through the green synthesis method. For instance, the Mentha longifolia leaf extract was employed for the green synthesis of ZnO and ZnO/CuO nanocomposites (NCs)23. Similarly, Mentha piperita leaf extract was used for the green synthesis of silver NPs in another study24. Previous reports have also described the production of Fe2O3-NPs using Citrus reticulum25, Olea European leaf extract26, Sida cordifolia extract, and Oscillatoria limnetica27. Pennyroyal (Mentha pulegium L.) is a flowering species in the Lamiaceae family, native to Europe, North Africa, and the Middle East. The plant’s pharmacological effects mostly originate from the presence of some significant groups of specialized (secondary) metabolites (SMs), such as flavonoids, essential oils, and phenolic compounds28, wherein these compounds can be an effective factor in the synthesis of NPs. This study sought to establish an eco-friendly, simple, and environmentally friendly approach for synthesizing iron oxide nanoparticles (Fe2O3-NPs) and to examine their impact on seed germination across various basil (Ocimum basilicum L.) cultivars, and assess growth-related traits during seedling development. The research was specifically designed to investigate the correlation between nanoparticle concentration (0, 50, 100, 200 ppm) and exposure duration (6 and 12 h), aiming to yield new insights into appropriate nano-priming protocols. Furthermore, the study addresses a significant gap in nano-priming research, as different basil cultivars may exhibit distinct responses to nanoparticle treatments due to genetic variations affecting seed coat permeability, metabolic pathways, and stress tolerance mechanisms. This cultivar-specific variability in nanoparticle response has not been systematically studied prior to this work.

Materials and methods

Materials and instruments

The Iron (II) sulfate (FeSO4) was purchased from Merck Company. For the ultrasonic-assisted extraction (UAE), an ultrasonic bath equipped with a heating system (KMH1-120W6501 type Pulse, Italy) operating at a 40 kHz frequency and power of 120 W was used. A gold film was used to load the dried particles onto a Philips XL30 series Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). The Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) device (Nanophox Sympatec GmbH, Claushtal, Germany), UV-Vis absorption spectra (Perkin-Elmer lambda 25 spectrophotometers, Alexandria, VA, 22309, USA), Infrared spectra (Japanese Jasco FT-IR460), and X-ray diffraction analysis (Panalytical X Pert PRO system) were used for NPs characterization. Seeds of Dark Opal and Sweet basil cultivars were purchased from Rocalba S.L. (Spain), while seeds of Iranian green and purple basil cultivars were obtained from Pakan Bazr Co. (Iran).

Preparation of the plant extract

The pennyroyal (Mentha pulegium) plant was botanically identified by Prof. Sonboli and deposited at the Medicinal Plants and Drugs Research Institute Herbarium of Shahid Beheshti University (voucher specimens No: MPH-2721). The plant was dried at room temperature in the shade and then powdered. Then, 20 g of the powder was mixed with 200 mL double-distilled water and extracted by an ultrasonic method for 20 min. The obtained extract was used for green synthesis.

Synthesis of Fe2O3 nanoparticles

To synthesize the Fe2O3-NPs, 50 mL of filtrated aqueous extract was taken in a 100 mL round-bottom flask, then 0.01 M Iron (II) sulfate was added dropwise to the extract at a temperature of 70 °C. The color change happened at the beginning of the reaction, and after 30 min, a sediment formed. The sediment was then placed in the oven until it was completely dry26.

Experimental design

In this study, the extract of the pennyroyal plant was used for Fe2O3-NPs synthesis. Afterward, the seeds of different basil cultivars, including Dark Opal, Sweet basil, Iranian green basil, and Iranian purple basil, were treated with various concentrations of synthesized Fe2O3-NPs (0, 50, 100, and 200 ppm) for two exposure times (6 and 12 h). This was done in a factorial, completely randomized design experiment with 50 replications per treatment (4 cultivars × 4 concentrations × 2 time points = 32 treatments, each with 50 seeds) to evaluate the effect of the nanoparticles on seed germination and seedling growth traits. After nanoparticle treatments, the seeds were incubated in a growth chamber at 25 °C for 12 days and were checked and watered with sterile distilled water every day to evaluate the germinated seeds. Growth-related traits of the seedlings were recorded at the end of the period.

Germination and growth-related traits measurement

A total of 10 seedlings were selected from each cultivar to measure the desired traits. A digital caliper was used to measure the length of the shoot (from the crown to the tip of the seedling) and the root (from the crown to the tip of the root) of each seedling. The fresh weight of both shoots and roots was measured immediately with a sensitive scale. The seedlings were placed in a 40 °C oven for 12 h, and their dry weight was measured. Statistical analysis

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) was accomplished on raw collected data by SAS software, and then Duncan’s test at the 5% significance level (p ≤ 0.05) was performed for mean comparisons. The graphs were also drawn by Origin software.

Results

Characterization of the synthesized Fe2O3 nanoparticles

The formation and stability of Fe2O3 nanostructures in an aqueous colloidal solution were investigated using UV-Vis spectral analysis (Fig. 1). The observed color change of the solution from brown to black signifies the formation of iron oxide nanoparticles, attributed to the excitation of surface plasmon resonance (SPR)-like phenomena characteristic of these nanoparticles30. UV-Vis spectroscopy, alongside X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, was employed to elucidate the optical properties and crystal structure of the synthesized Fe2O3 NPs. The UV-Vis spectra revealed a broad absorption band between 350 and 450 nm, consistent with findings in the literature31, further corroborated by the crystallographic data obtained from XRD.

UV–Vis absorption spectra of the (a) Extract (b) Fe2O3 NPs.

Characterization of the synthesized Fe2O3 NPs involved both X-ray Diffraction (XRD) and Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) to elucidate their structural and surface properties. The XRD patterns of the synthesized Fe2O3 NPs, collected over a range of 10° ≤ 2θ ≤ 80° (Fig. 2a), confirmed the successful biosynthesis of highly crystalline α-Fe2O3 NPs with a single-phase structure. The diffraction pattern displayed characteristic peaks at 2θ values of 24.29, 29.32, 35.52, 38.70, 43.50, 50.62, 54.50, 57.50, 62.24, 64.68, 72.50, and 75.10, corresponding to the (012), (104), (110), (006), (113), (024), (116), (112), (214), (300), (101), and (220) reflection planes, respectively26. Minor additional peaks were also observed, potentially arising from amorphous impurities or residual organic capping agents. The average crystallite size was estimated to be approximately 85 nm using the Debye–Scherrer equation, calculated from the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the prominent (110) peak (2θ = 35.52°).

(A) The XRD patterns and (B) FT-IR spectra of the Fe2O3 NPs.

The FT-IR spectra of the Fe2O3-NPs are presented in Fig. 2b. The spectra exhibited distinct absorption bands indicative of various functional groups present in the samples. Key bands were observed at: 3390 cm−1 (O–H stretching of hydroxyl groups), 2358 cm−1 (C–H stretching of alkane groups), 1627 cm−1 (C = O stretching), and 1394 cm−1 (C = C SP2 aromatic stretching). Notably, the absorption peak around 615 cm−1 is attributed to the characteristic Fe-O stretching vibration32. The presence of these functional groups strongly suggests the involvement of phytochemicals, such as amino acids, polyphenols, and flavonoids, which likely acted as bioreducing, biocapping, and biostabilizing agents during the synthesis. These agents effectively prevent NP aggregation and contribute to the overall stability of the synthesized Fe2O3NPs.

The SEM image of Fe2O3-NPs was provided, demonstrating the homogeneous, spherical, narrowly distributed, and regularly shaped nanoscale particles (Fig. 3b). The number frequency histograms showed that the particle size distribution of Fe2O3-NPs was on a linear scale, and the average particle size was 85.60 nm. Additionally, the mean particle diameter of Fe2O3-NPs determined by DLS was 82.24 ± 1.7 nm (Fig. 3a). Fe and O elements were detected in the spectra by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), indicating the production of Fe2O3-NPs. These findings were well aligned with the analysis presented above (Fig. 3c).

The mean particle size of Fe2O3 nanoparticles was determined using DLS (a) particles the size of 82.24 nm, SEM image, and number frequency histograms showing the particle size distribution of Fe2O3 nanoparticles in linear scale (b) particles the size of 85.60 nm, (c) EDX pattern of prepared Fe2O3 nanoparticles.

Effect of Fe2O3 nanoparticles on seed germination

The germination percentage (GP) of the dark opal basil (D.O.B) seeds was positively impacted by low concentration NP priming, wherein the concentration of 100 ppm with 12 h of priming time had the maximum percentage of seed germination (GP) in this cultivar (95.00 ± 2.24%), while the lowest value of this parameter (65.00 ± 1.22%) was recorded in the concentration of 200 ppm with 12 h of priming time and control in 6 h (Fig. 4. A). In the case of Iranian purple basil (I.P.B), all concentrations of nano-iron used in this study, except for concentration 200 ppm and 12 h exposure time, increased the GP, wherein the highest GP (85%) in this cultivar was obtained at 50 ppm nano-iron priming and 6 h exposure time. However, although the treatments of 50 and 100 ppm both in 12 h exposure time increased the germination percentage, this increase was not significantly different from the control at 12 h. The germination percentage in this cultivar was significantly reduced at the concentration of 200 ppm nanoparticles and 12 h exposure time (Fig. 4. B).

Effect of Fe2O3 nanoparticles on seed germination percentage (GP) of different basil cultivars (A Dark opal basil (DOB)), (B Iranian purple basil (IPB)), (C Iranian green basil (IGB)), (D Sweet basil (SB)).

Regarding Iranian green basil (I.G.B), the maximum percentage of seed germination in this cultivar (95.00 ± 3.02%) was observed in concentration of 200 ppm and 12 h exposure time. Concentrations of 50 and 100 ppm both in 12 h also caused a significant increase in GP (Fig. 4. C). The sweet basil variety (S.B) seeds just responded to the application of iron oxide nanoparticles at 50 ppm dosage and 12 h exposure time. No significant change was observed in seed germination percentage in other treatments (Fig. 4. D).

Figure 5 demonstrates that Fe2O3 nanoparticle (NP) seed priming generally enhances germination rate (GR) across four basil cultivars compared to controls, with effects modulated by concentration, exposure time, and cultivar. The optimal concentration appears cultivar-dependent: for example, Dark opal basil (Fig. 5. A) showed maximum GR at 100 ppm for 12 h, while Iranian purple basil peaked at 50 ppm for 6 h (Fig. 5. B). Although the Iranian green basil cultivar had the highest germination rate at 200 ppm and 12 h exposure time, a significant decrease in germination rate was observed for the sweet basil at this concentration both for 6 and 12 h. It can be roughly stated that the germination rate is inversely proportional to the mean germination time, although this relationship is not linear, as recorded in this study (Fig. 5A–D).

Effect of Fe2O3 nanoparticles on growth-related characteristics

Dark opal basil was the only cultivar that responded positively to the highest concentration (200 ppm), showing optimal growth parameters at this dose with a 12 h exposure. This treatment resulted in the maximum values for shoot length, root length, and root biomass compared to all other treatments (for detailed data, see Supplementary Table S1). Shoot biomass (fresh and dry weight) was also maximized at 100–200 ppm with 12 h of exposure. (Table 1). Iranian Purple Basil achieved its best growth at moderate concentrations (100 ppm), which yielded the highest root fresh weight, root dry weight, and shoot biomass. In contrast, the 200 ppm concentration was toxic to this cultivar, producing the lowest values for most growth parameters. Interestingly, the longest root length (23.90 ± 9.90) was observed in the control group (detailed results in Table S2). Iranian Green Basil was generally negatively affected by the nanoparticles. While a moderate dose (100 ppm for 6 h) slightly increased shoot length, all NP treatments significantly reduced root length, with the greatest inhibition at 200 ppm for 12 h. This treatment also resulted in the lowest shoot biomass (detailed results in Table S3). Sweet Basil performed best at a low concentration of 50 ppm for 12 h, which maximized shoot length and yield parameters (shoot and root fresh/dry weight). Similar to the Iranian Green cultivar, root length was greatest in the control group, and all NP treatments reduced it. The high dose (200 ppm) was again detrimental, producing the poorest growth (detailed results in Table S4). In conclusion, the key finding is that the impact of Fe2O3 nanoparticles is cultivar-specific. High concentrations (200 ppm) were stimulatory for Dark Opal but toxic for Iranian Purple, Iranian Green, and Sweet Basil. Furthermore, root development was particularly sensitive, often being inhibited by NP treatments even when other parameters improved. Discussion

The agricultural sector faces critical challenges related to sustainability, pesticide residues, fertilizer application, food safety, and environmental pollution. These issues exacerbate environmental degradation, pose health risks, and hinder the adoption of sustainable farming practices. Consequently, transitioning from conventional agriculture to sustainable methodologies is imperative33. Additionally, nanotechnology has emerged as a significant tool with substantial potential for advancing sustainable agriculture34,35. Although not directly measured in this study, our observed effects may be explained by the fact that nano-priming affected seed germination and seedling growth through multifaceted physiological and biochemical pathways including reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling and antioxidant activation, enhanced water uptake, and phytohormone regulation. Based on mechanisms proposed in previous literature Fe2O3 and Ag nanoparticles induced controlled reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which acts as a signaling molecule to break seed dormancy and activate antioxidant enzymes such as catalase and peroxidase36. This mitigates oxidative stress while promoting starch hydrolysis via α-amylase activation, accelerating germination37. Based on previous studies nanoparticles create nanopores in seed coats and cell walls, improving water imbibition and nutrient absorption, wherein Fe-pyrite NPs loosened endosperm cell walls in spinach seeds, enhancing water uptake and biomass production38. It has also been proven that Fe-NPs modulated phytohormones (e.g., gibberellins, ABA) and upregulated aquaporin genes led to cellular hydration and metabolic activity during germination39. Our results showed dose-dependent effects that align with reported hormesis responses, but the underlying molecular mechanisms require further investigation.

Iron nanoparticles (Fe NPs) produced using eco-friendly methods, such as pomegranate peel extract, markedly enhanced maize germination under salt stress conditions. For instance, applying 20 mM Fe NPs boosted root length by 283% and increased the seedling vigor index by 220%39. Additionally, these nanoparticles improved the uptake of micronutrients like calcium, manganese, and zinc in spinach leaves, an effect likely attributed to their strong surface reactivity and effective ion exchange capabilities38. Fe3O4 NPs reduced toxic metal concentrations (Cr³⁺, Pb²⁺) in sunflower seedlings by adsorbing pollutants, demonstrating dual roles in growth promotion and environmental remediation40. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) enhanced triploid watermelon germination rates by 31–35% and elevated soluble sugar levels, which are essential for early seedling energy reserves41. Seed priming with cobalt, manganese, copper, zinc, molybdenum, and selenium nanoparticles significantly improved germination energy and chlorophyll content in pea plants (cv. E-244), underscoring the synergistic benefits of micronutrient delivery42.

Regarding germination parameters in this study, nearly all cultivars exhibited no consistent toxicity (i.e., GR suppression below control) within the investigated range; nevertheless, 200 ppm caused inhibitory effects in most cultivars, except Dark Opal. However, a significant decrease was observed at a concentration of 200 for the sweet basil cultivar. This highlighted that lower Iron oxide (Fe2O3) nanoparticle doses applied as priming agents can exploit nano-specific properties to improve germination physiology, with outcomes finely tuned by genotype, NP dose, and priming duration. In this study, growth parameters in the most cultivars studied in this experiment, except for dark opal basil, cultivar showed sensitivity to high nanoparticle concentrations (200 ppm) and 12-h exposure time, which led to a significant reduction in most growth parameters. Although dark opal cultivar responded positively to high concentrations of Iron Oxide (Fe2O3) nanoparticle, growth parameters in the other three cultivars decreased at high concentrations. Our results confirmed a classic hormesis response, where the effect of Fe2O3 nanoparticles was biphasic: stimulatory at low concentrations but inhibitory at high doses (200 ppm) for most cultivars. This concentration-dependent phenomenon is well-documented in nanotoxicology. For instance, similar patterns have been observed where low doses of silica or silver nanoparticles promote growth, while higher concentrations induce oxidative stress and inhibit germination and root elongation37,41,43,44. This underscores that the benefits of nano-priming are not universal and are strictly dependent on genotype, dosage, and exposure time. Future research must standardize metal-based nanoparticles concentrations based on crop-specific thresholds and environmental conditions. There is considerable variation among different plant species in terms of response to nanopriming. For example, Fe3O4 NPs showed no significant effect on sunflower germination but reduced toxic element uptake40, whereas Fe-pyrite NPs drastically improved spinach biomass38. This study proved that the response to seed priming by iron nanoparticles varies not only in different species but also in cultivars of the same species.

Persistent concerns about nanoparticle accumulation in soil and food chains necessitate rigorous toxicological assessments. Although metallic nanoparticles like Fe and Zn are biodegradable, persistent accumulation of Ag or CuNPs in soils raises concerns about bioaccumulation45. Green-synthesized NPs (e.g., using plant extracts) offer safer alternatives39. In the nanopriming method, the unique physicochemical properties of metallic nanoparticles synthesized through environmentally friendly “green” methods can be used to increase seed germination and seedling vigor. This approach addresses challenges in agriculture, such as low germination rates in high-value crops and abiotic stress resilience, while aligning with sustainable practices46. Green synthesis utilizes plant extracts as reducing agents, avoiding toxic chemicals like sodium borohydride. For example, AgNPs derived from Azadirachta indica (neem) exhibit dual functionality: enhancing germination while providing antifungal protection46,47. Comparative studies have shown that plant-based metallic nanoparticles are less phytotoxic than chemically synthesized counterparts, with no adverse effects on fruit quality or soil microbiota44.

Despite many advantages, green synthesis faces limitations in scalability due to seasonal variability in plant phytochemicals and difficulties in achieving uniform nanoparticle size/distribution44,48. Advances in extraction protocols and nano-encapsulation techniques (e.g., dextran coatings) may address these issues.

Optimizing nanoparticle (NP) characteristics specifically size, surface coating, and concentration for target crops may maximize priming efficacy. Validation of laboratory results necessitates large-scale field trials. For example, multi-location field studies demonstrated that priming watermelon seeds with silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) significantly enhanced yield (up to 35.6%) without adverse effects on fruit quality, indicating potential for scalable application. Furthermore, integrating nano-priming with targeted delivery systems, such as pH-responsive NPs, offers a promising strategy to improve nutrient use efficiency and reduce dependency on conventional agrochemicals49. Plants established in natural ecosystems face significant challenges from abiotic stresses including extreme temperatures, salinity, drought, and flooding which elicit similar detrimental biochemical, molecular, and cellular responses. Research demonstrates that seed priming is an effective strategy to enhance germination rate, reduce emergence time, improve seedling establishment and subsequent growth, increase survival, accelerate flowering, and bolster tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses. However, NP toxicity can negatively impact germination and growth, dependent on factors like seed size, plant species, NP type, and concentration. Critical research gaps such as understanding NP impacts on gene regulation (up/down), enzyme activity, and protein accumulation in plants, optimizing NP treatments (type, concentration, exposure time) and their interactions with specific species/varieties and environmental conditions to maximize biomass and phytochemical production, and assessing the effects of NPs on human, animal, and ecosystem health remain.

Conclusion

The current study described a nontoxic, reusable, and environmentally friendly method for producing Fe2O3 NPs utilizing an ultrasonic approach and an aqueous extract of the Mentha pulegium L. plant. This eco-friendly approach eliminates toxic chemicals, providing a sustainable method for nanoparticle production. In modern agriculture, ensuring seed health and efficient germination is crucial for robust seedling development and enhanced plant productivity. Seed priming, utilizing organic or chemical agents, is employed to improve germination, seedling growth, and establishment. This study demonstrated that the effects of green-synthesized Fe2O3 nanoparticles on basil are highly dependent on cultivar, concentration, and exposure time. While lower concentrations (50–100 ppm) generally improved germination, the high concentration (200 ppm) was predominantly inhibitory, suppressing growth in three out of the four cultivars. Only the Dark Opal cultivar exhibited unique tolerance and positive growth responses at this dose. These findings critically highlighted that the success of nano-priming is not one-size-fits-all and underscored the imperative for cultivar-specific optimization of protocols. Furthermore, the observed toxicity thresholds emphasize the need for thorough environmental safety assessments alongside agronomic efficacy studies before any large-scale application.
