Hypospadias Associated With Fetal Growth Restriction: A Multicentric Descriptive and Prognostic Cohort Study
Authors: Manon Marquet, Olivia Anselem, Claire Bouvattier, Alexandre J Vivanti, Alexandra Benachi, Jean‐Marie Jouannic, Olivier Picone, Jonathan Rosenblatt, Laurent J Salomon, Vassilis Tsatsaris, Yoann Athiel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo determine the prevalence of genetic and endocrine abnormalities and to assess fetal, neonatal and surgical outcomes in cases of hypospadias associated with fetal growth restriction.MethodA multicentric retrospective study was conducted across five prenatal diagnosis centers in Paris. The cohort encompassed all fetuses diagnosed with the combination of fetal growth restriction < 10th percentile (FGR) and hypospadias from 2013 to 2021. Maternal data, fetal outcome and results of prenatal investigations were collected, along with postnatal data, encompassing endocrinological and genetic assessments, functional aspects and surgical outcomes.ResultsAmong the 82 patients included in the cohort, there were 14 (17%) terminations of pregnancy and four (5%) in utero deaths, leaving 64 (78%) live neonates, including five (6%) with early neonatal death. Among the 52 (63%) cases where hypospadias and FGR were considered as ultrasound‐isolated anomalies, six (12%, [3.2%–20.8%]) exhibited chromosomic, genetic, or endocrinological abnormalities diagnosed half prenatally and half postnatally. Fifty percent of the overall hypospadias were proximal. Most children underwent surgical intervention before reaching 2 years of age, with 50% encountering complications and often required reintervention.ConclusionThe association of FGR and hypospadias should not be underestimated as genetic or endocrinological abnormalities were identified even when hypospadias and FGR initially appear isolated. Additionally, the overall prognosis may be worsened using complex and iterative surgical procedures.

Introduction

Hypospadias, characterized by an abnormal urethral opening and curvature, leading to urinary and/or sexual function impairments, is the most frequent variation of sexual development (VSD) in males [1, 2], with one case per 125 to 300 births in France, and its incidence has doubled over the past decade [1, 2]. The pathophysiology and causes of hypospadias are poorly understood, and the recent rise in its incidence further underscores this unresolved issue. Environmental, genetic, and endocrine factors seem to exert a substantial influence [3, 4, 5, 6]. In recent literature, an association has been described between fetal growth restriction (FGR) and hypospadias, suggesting the involvement of placental dysfunction in the occurrence of this VSD [7, 8]. Indeed, during the first 14 weeks of gestation, placental human chorionic gonadotropin influences fetal testosterone secretion. It is hypothesized that placental insufficiency could disrupt the supply of nutrients and human chorionic gonadotropin to the fetus, leading to both FGR and hypospadias [9]. The increase in this incidence also raises the question of pre and post‐natal care. During the prenatal period, to date, no standardized guidelines exist for the etiological assessment of antenatally discovered hypospadias [6, 8, 10]. The central question concerns the extent of investigations to be undertaken. The question is whether hypospadias and FGR are the common consequence of a vascular anomaly or if they are two signs of another genetic or endocrine pathology, determining the potential extension of investigations. Prenatal investigations will influence the information provided to couples for the postnatal management of their child. This association therefore raises many questions as it remains poorly documented to date, particularly regarding its association with morphological anomalies, the presence of chromosomal, genetic, or endocrinological abnormalities, the prognosis associated with hypospadias, as well as the prognosis associated with FGR.

Our main objective was to determine the prevalence of genetic and endocrine abnormalities, while our secondary objective was to assess fetal, neonatal and surgical outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Population

A multicenter retrospective study was conducted across five prenatal diagnosis centers in Paris. The cohort encompassed all fetuses diagnosed with the combination of FGR and hypospadias, whether associated with morphological anomalies or not, from 2013 to 2021. In our study, FGR was defined as an estimated fetal weight below the 10th percentile, thus also including small for gestational age (SGA) fetuses. Fetal growth charts used to define FGR were those used in each prenatal diagnosis (WHO or CFEF or Intergrowth) [11, 12]. Confirmation of hypospadias was performed by a pediatrician and growth retardation was confirmed if birthweight was < 10th percentile on AUDIPOG chart [13]. In cases of unconfirmed hypospadias or growth retardation at birth, patients were excluded from the cohort.

Collection of Data

Consulting medical records in each center, the following data were collected. First, maternal data: age, medical, surgical, and obstetric history. Data related from the current pregnancy: method of conception, serum markers for screening for Down syndrome (First‐trimester screening includes free beta‐human chorionic gonadotropin (β‐hCG) and pregnancy‐associated plasma protein‐A (PAPP‐A), while second‐trimester screening involves total beta‐human chorionic gonadotropin (β‐hCG) and alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP)); first‐trimester ultrasound findings, including crown‐rump length and nuchal translucency measurement, gestational age at the time of diagnosis, estimation of fetal weight at the time of diagnosis and at the last documented ultrasound (reported in percentile), pathological Doppler measurement of uterine arteries, pathological umbilical Doppler measurement, pregnancy induced hypertension, pre‐eclampsia, associated morphological abnormalities, invasive sampling during the ante‐natal period and these results (trophoblast biopsy or amniocentesis, chromosomal and cytogenetic abnormalities, virology, hormonal abnormalities and search for smith‐lemli‐opitz syndrome (SLO)). Pregnancy outcomes were also collected: medical termination of pregnancy, intrauterine fetal demise or live birth, gestational age at birth, mode of delivery, birth weight, Audipog percentile and presence of vascular lesion on placental histopathological examination.

Then, neonatal and pediatric outcome and management: age, type of hypospadias (posterior, anterior, midshaft), associated morphological anomalies, postnatal endocrine or genetic assessment, surgical intervention, reoperation, complications (fistulas, strictures, recurrent urinary tract infections). To ascertain the outcome of children for whom neonatal and pediatric outcomes were not available in the medical records, parents were interviewed via telephone by a single operator to determine whether postnatal assessments (endocrinological, genetic) had been conducted, whether their child had undergone surgery, if so, how many times, and whether any complications had occurred.

Statistical Analysis

First, we examined all characteristics within our complete cohort, and subsequently, we analyzed these characteristics within a subgroup comprising cases of hypospadias and FGR without other suspected antenatal morphological abnormalities.

To describe our cohort, quantitative variables were presented as means with standard deviation, and qualitative variables as counts with percentages. The analyses were performed using R Studio V.1.2.1578.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Comité d’éthique et de la recherche en gynécologie obstétrique (CEROG 2023‐OBS‐0104). [14].

Results

Population

Among the 82 patients in the cohort, after excluding cases of hypospadias (8/103) or growth restriction unconfirmed at birth (5/103), as well as those with unavailable pregnancy outcome data, there were 14 terminations of pregnancy (TOP) (17%) and 4 in utero deaths (5%). This left 64 live neonates (78%), including 5 with early neonatal death. The indications for TOP included associated cardiac malformations, chromosomal abnormalities, symptomatic congenital cytomegalovirus infection, and severe FGR (Figure 1).

Maternal and Obstetrical Characteristics

The mean maternal age was 32.4 years, and the mean gravidity and parity were 2.1 and 0.6, respectively. Pregnancy was achieved through assisted reproductive technology in 8 cases (9.9%). Concerning vascular characteristics, pathological Doppler of uterine arteries was reported in 43 cases (52.4%), gestational hypertension in 22 cases (26.8%) and pre‐eclampsia in 15 cases (18.3%) (Table 1).

Prenatal Investigations and Anomalies

Invasive samples were performed in 64 cases antenatally (78.0%), revealing cytogenetic abnormalities in 5 fetuses (8.0%): fetal mosaic trisomy 16, 47, XYY syndrome and deletion 11q and 2 trisomy 18. In 52 cases (63%), the association between FGR and hypospadias was isolated, meaning no associated morphological abnormalities were found (Table 2). Postnatal Outcomes

Among live births, delivery was primarily vaginal, with a mean gestational age at birth of 35.1 weeks. By definition, 100% of neonates had a birth weight below the 10th percentile and 75% below the fifth percentile according to Audipog. Fifty percentage of overall hypospadias cases were proximal, mostly pertaining to posterior proximal hypospadias. Most children underwent surgical intervention before reaching 2 years of age, with 50% encountering complications (mainly fistula and stenosis). Notably, 45% of cases required reintervention, often due to these complications (Table 2).

Subgroup Analysis

Among the 52 (63%) cases where hypospadias and FGR were considered as ultrasound‐isolated anomalies, 6 (12% [3.2%–20.8%]) exhibited chromosomic, genetic, or endocrinological abnormalities. Among these, 3 were identified prenatally (fetal mosaic trisomy 16, 47, XYY syndrome and deletion 11q), while 3 were diagnosed postnatally (partial trisomy 18p and 9p, testosterone production deficiency and androgen receptor mutation) (Figure 2).

Discussion

Interpretation of Main Findings

This small but revealing cohort underscores the challenging prognosis of the association of FGR and hypospadias, marked by a high perinatal mortality rate (30%, including TOP) and intricate postnatal surgical management. Importantly, this association should not be underestimated as genetic or endocrinological abnormalities were identified in 12% of cases even when hypospadias and FGR initially appear isolated.

Results in the Context of What Is Known

Among the numerous questions raised when hypospadias is associated with FGR, the primary concern is the pathophysiology. Existing literature suggests a correlation between vascular‐origin FGR and hypospadias [15, 16]. Although our study does not establish a causal relationship, and retroactively determining the vascular nature of FGR is challenging, the rates of gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, pathological Doppler, and placental ischemic lesions appear to support this hypothesis [17, 18]. It is also hypothesized that maternal free HCG contributes to the early embryonic development of male genital organs, and an increased level might be associated with proximal hypospadias. In our cohort, we also observed elevated levels of this marker [9, 19].

Although a robust association is presumed to exist between vascular‐origin FGR and hypospadias, our cohort identifies syndromic cases or cases associated with cytogenetic anomalies, even when no other morphological abnormalities are found. This raises the question of prenatal assessment, particularly regarding the role of invasive sampling and the antenatal information provided to parents [1]. To enhance prenatal investigations, including genetic testing, it is imperative to seek additional data, especially in light of recent studies reporting a high rate of pathogenic variants in whole‐exome sequencing analyses [6, 8, 10].

In fact, in the absence of other morphological or cytogenetic anomalies revealed during antenatal investigations, the prognosis is related to both the severity of FGR and the form of hypospadias [8]. In France, the management of hypospadias in the postnatal period is currently undergoing a major paradigm shift, as until recently, the majority of hypospadias were treated at around 18 months, with a high re‐intervention rate in severe cases. The rate reported in our cohort appears to be higher than that reported in the literature. This may be explained by more severe forms in our cohort as hypospadias were diagnosed antenatally and also by their association with FGR. Indeed, it has been reported that hypospadias in infants born small for gestational age have a higher incidence of surgical complications and reoperation rate in comparison with hypospadias in infants born without growth restriction [8]. Since the decree of November 17, 2022, surgical treatment of a minor is only performed in cases of very serious medical reasons or at the request of the minor; otherwise, therapeutic abstention is practiced [20, 21]. The prognosis of hypospadias, intricately tied to the psychological, sexual, urinary, and fertility challenges it presents, is further compounded by the complexities and iterative nature of surgical interventions. Recent shifts in international legislation concerning disorders of sex development suggest that considering later surgical interventions may offer potential for enhancing the overall prognosis of affected children [10, 22, 23].

Strengths and Limits

The originality of this study lies in the investigation of the association between FGR and hypospadias, with a subgroup analysis focusing on the combination between isolated hypospadias and FGR. Additionally, this is a multicenter study, with patients exclusively evaluated in Prenatal Diagnosis Centers. The findings are consistent with some known results in the literature, particularly regarding complication rates. However, our study has significant limitations, including selection bias as only patients diagnosed antenatally were included, likely representing the most severe cases. Many cases of hypospadias are not diagnosed prenatally, likely the less severe ones. On the other hand, cases of hypospadias or FGR that were not confirmed at birth were excluded. Although this represents a small percentage of the cohort, it partially biases the interpretation of abnormality rates in the context of prenatal counseling. The possibility of non‐confirmation at birth should be discussed with the couple. Different growth charts were used to define FGR according to the practices of the centers. Moreover, the data collection is retrospective, and the sample size is small. The data obtained through telephone questionnaires with parents is likely less reliable than the data collected in the patient's medical records. Excluding TOP, the mortality rate is 4.9%. Unfortunately, our study does not allow us to conclude the prognostic contribution of IUGR to this rate.

Perspectives

A prospective study investigating the specific association between FGR and hypospadias could help to establish standardized prenatal management practices, particularly benefiting from the recent integration of exome sequencing. Moreover, such a study could furnish precise prognostic information for children, aligning with the most recent postnatal care recommendations.

Conclusion

The correlation between FGR and hypospadias demands careful consideration, as genetic or endocrinological anomalies have been identified even when hypospadias and FGR seem isolated initially. To advance both prenatal and postnatal assessments, incorporating genetic testing is essential, necessitating the acquisition of supplementary data. Furthermore, the prognosis may be adversely affected by complex and repetitive surgical interventions, highlighting the need for prospective evaluation within the current landscape of evolving management approaches.
