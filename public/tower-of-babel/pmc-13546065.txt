Dupilumab for Drug-Induced Bullous Pemphigoid: A Case Series and Literature Review
Authors: Nawara Sakpuwadol, Suthinee Rutnin, Wimolsiri Iamsumang, Kumutnart Chanprapaph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug-induced bullous pemphigoid (BP) is an increasingly recognized variant of BP with unique challenges in management, particularly when patients require continuation of the offending medication or are contraindicated to conventional immunosuppressive therapies. We report 4 patients with drug-induced BP, three associated with dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors and one with immune checkpoint inhibitor, successfully treated with dupilumab. All four patients had failed or were intolerant to conventional therapies. Following dupilumab initiation, all patients experienced significant improvement in pruritus within 2 weeks. Complete remission was achieved in 3 patients within 1–5 months of treatment and one patient had stable disease, enabling him to resume pembrolizumab therapy for esophageal cancer while on dupilumab. No significant adverse effects were reported during treatment periods. Our findings, supported by a literature review, suggest that dupilumab demonstrates promising efficacy and safety in treating drug-induced BP, with rapid improvement in pruritus and resolution of skin lesions. It may offer a valuable therapeutic alternative as a steroid sparing agent for elderly patients with multiple comorbidities and provides the potential to continue necessary medications (eg, cancer therapy) that may have triggered BP.

Introduction

Bullous pemphigoid (BP) is the most prevalent autoimmune blistering dermatosis, mainly affecting elderlies. It manifests as intensely pruritic tense blisters on unaffected skin or erythematous plaques, typically on the trunk and extremities.1 The pathogenesis involves tissue-bound and circulating antibodies targeting 2 hemidesmosome proteins at the cutaneous basement membrane zone, namely BP180 (BPAG2) and BP230 (BPAG1).1,2 Recent evidence shows T helper (Th) 2 immune response plays a crucial role in BP pathogenesis. Eosinophils and type 2 inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, and IL-13, contribute to lesion development through multiple mechanisms, including immunoglobulin class-switching, eosinophil chemotaxis, and direct tissue damage.3–5

While topical and systemic corticosteroids remain first-line treatments, supplemented by immunosuppressants, anti-inflammatories, biologics, and intravenous immunoglobulin in severe cases, these traditional therapies can cause significant adverse effects.6 In recent years, drug-induced BP has emerged as a distinct clinical entity presenting unique management challenges, especially when patients require continued use of the triggering medications or when conventional BP treatments are contraindicated. This therapeutic dilemma calls for investigating alternative treatment strategies that can effectively control BP while minimizing systemic immunosuppression.

Dupilumab, a fully human immunoglobulin (Ig)G4 monoclonal antibody blocks both IL-4 and IL-13 signaling. Originally approved for atopic dermatitis, asthma, and chronic rhinosinusitis, it also shows promising results for various type 2 inflammatory diseases, including BP.7,8 By targeting the IL-4/IL-13 axis central to BP pathogenesis, dupilumab offers a mechanism-based approach particularly valuable in drug-induced BP cases where conventional treatments are limited by safety concerns or contraindications. Despite emerging case reports suggesting efficacy, a significant knowledge gap remains regarding dupilumab’s role in drug-induced BP. Hereby, we report 4 patients with moderate-to-severe drug-induced BP with causality assessment of probable or definite based on the Naranjo adverse drug reaction probability scale and treated with dupilumab.9 We have also reviewed previous literature to contribute to the growing evidence base for this targeted biologic therapy in drug-induced BP cases.

Case Presentations

Case 1

A 92-year-old bedridden female presented with a 2-month history of severely itchy skin and developed multiple vesicles and bullae on the trunk and extremities for 1 month. Her comorbidities included hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and post-thyroidectomy hypothyroid. Recently, she had been taking linagliptin, a dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP4i), for 15 months.

On physical examination, the patient exhibited generalized vesicles and tense bullous lesions distributed on the trunk, extremities, and genitalia, accompanied by large erosions on both legs (Bullous Pemphigoid Disease Area Index [BPDAI]=77). Laboratory findings revealed high titers of absolute eosinophil count (AEC) (2700 cells/microliter) and anti-BP180 levels (>200 RU/mL), but IgE and anti-BP230 were within their normal ranges. The histopathological section demonstrated subepidermal vesicles with eosinophil infiltration and linear IgG and C3 deposition at the dermoepidermal junction (DEJ).

The diagnosis of DPP4i-associated BP was made based on the temporal relationship between linagliptin initiation and BP onset (15 months exposure), characteristic clinical presentation, and confirmatory immunopathological findings. With a well-documented association between DPP4is and BP and no other potential triggers identified, drug-induced etiology was determined. This led to linagliptin discontinuation. Five months of combined prednisone (10–20 mg/day), doxycycline (200 mg/day), and azathioprine (AZA) therapy (50–75 mg/day) led to marginal improvement (BPDAI=45) while developing several side effects including hyperglycemia, recurrent infections, and AZA-induced bone marrow suppression. Consequently, the decision was made to commence dupilumab, considering its efficacy without affecting the patient’s blood glucose levels and immune status. She received 600 mg of dupilumab subcutaneous injection (SC) followed by 300 mg every other week and attained a complete remission (CR) after 5 months (Figure 1). Since the patient showed good clinical response, intervals between each cycle of dupilumab were extended to 4–6 weeks. Case 2

An 89-year-old male with underlying type 2 diabetes mellitus presented with multiple, severely pruritic bullae on erythematous base on both hands and groins for 1 week. He has been taking combined agents of sitagliptin and metformin for approximately 4 years. Laboratory assessments showed high levels of anti-BP180 (>200 RU/mL), anti-BP230 (155.9 RU/mL), IgE (658 IU/mL), but normal AEC. Lesional biopsies displayed subepidermal separation and eosinophilic spongiosis with linear IgG and C3 deposition at the DEJ.

The diagnosis of DPP4i-associated BP was confirmed based on prolonged sitagliptin exposure, characteristic clinical and histopathological findings, and elevated anti-BP180 and anti-BP230 autoantibodies. Sitagliptin was discontinued. Treatments with prednisone (10–20 mg/day), doxycycline (200 mg/day), antihistamines, and high-potency topical corticosteroids resulted in minimal improvement with recurring bullae. This significantly affected his daily activities since the bullae predominated on the hands. Approximately 3 months after sitagliptin cessation, dupilumab 600 mg SC was administered, followed by 300 mg SC every other week. After 4.5 months of dupilumab therapy, the patient achieved CR concomitant with a reduction in his IgE levels to 141 IU/mL. He is currently receiving maintenance doses of dupilumab.

Case 3

An 84-year-old male presented with generalized vesicles and bullae over the entire body. Two months prior, he was diagnosed with BP and the patient received treatment with prednisone and dapsone; however, he developed dapsone-induced methemoglobinemia, prompting him to seek an alternative treatment. He has multiple underlying conditions, including diabetes mellitus, which has been controlled by linagliptin for 7 years.

His blood tests showed positive anti-BP230 (108.6 RU/mL), borderline anti-BP180 (18.7 RU/mL), and normal AEC levels. The diagnosis of DPP4i-associated BP was supported by long-term linagliptin exposure, positive BP autoantibodies, particularly anti-BP230, and HLA-DQB1*03:01 positivity, a genetic marker strongly associated with DDP4i-induced BP in Asians. This genetic predisposition, combined with extended linagliptin therapy and no other identifiable triggers, provided compelling evidence for DPP4i-associated BP.

Linagliptin was discontinued and the patient was given prednisone (10–20 mg/day), doxycycline (200 mg/day), and AZA (25–100 mg/day), which noted some improvement for a duration of 2 months. Unfortunately, he experienced a marked flare one month later. The management was altered to dupilumab 600 mg SC and 300 mg SC every 2 weeks. After the initial dose, the patient reported considerable improvement and achieved CR after the second cycle of dupilumab.

Case 4

A 72-year-old male presented with a 2-month history of erythema plaque and blisters around the exit site of his gastrostomy. He was undergoing treatment for esophageal cancer with pembrolizumab, an anti-program cell death ligand 1 (anti-PDL1), for approximately 1 year. Later on, pruritic plaque and bullae erupted on his face, trunk, and extremities, and a provisional diagnosis of BP was made.

Laboratory evaluation showed positive anti-BP180 (59.3 RU/mL), negative anti-BP230, and elevated IgE (901 IU/mL), and normal AEC. Anti-PDL1-induced BP diagnosis was established based on temporal association with pembrolizumab (after approximately 1 year of therapy), characteristic clinical presentation, positive anti-BP180 autoantibodies, and markedly elevated IgE. Though a biopsy was not performed due to patient refusal, the profile was consistent with immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-induced BP. Accordingly, pembrolizumab was suspended, and treatment with prednisone (15–30 mg/day) and doxycycline (100 mg/day) yielded suboptimal outcomes (BPDAI=20). Considering the necessity of pembrolizumab and inadequate responses to conventional therapies, a decision was made to initiate dupilumab therapy at a dosage of 600 mg SC, followed by 300 mg SC every other week. His BPDAI decreased to 10 within 2 weeks with considerable improvement in pruritus. The patient was able to resume pembrolizumab therapy while continuing dupilumab, which offered clinical stabilization of his BP while allowing crucial cancer treatment to continue (Figure 2). Discussion

Our case series highlights dupilumab’s efficacy in treating drug-induced BP from various medications, including DPP4is (linagliptin and sitagliptin) and ICIs (pembrolizumab). Despite the varying drug culprit, all patients demonstrated favorable responses to dupilumab therapy (Table 1). Drug-induced BP has emerged as an important clinical entity with the increasing use of novel therapeutic agents. Beyond DPP4is, which show the strongest association, other implicated medications include ICIs, anti-tumor necrosis factor agents, diuretics, and antibiotics.10 This trend presents significant clinical challenges as these medications are often essential for managing serious underlying conditions. The pathogenesis of drug-induced BP involves distinct mechanisms beyond those seen in idiopathic cases. For DPP4i-induced BP, inhibition of DPP4 may enhance type 2 inflammation by preventing degradation of proinflammatory chemokines, eg, eotaxin, thereby increasing eosinophil recruitment to the skin.17 Additionally, DPP4 inhibition may prevent cytokine cleavage, leading to their accumulation and possibly heightened inflammatory responses that favor autoreactive T-cell activation and autoantibody production against basement membrane proteins.18 ICI-induced BP occurs when PD-1/PD-L1 blockade disrupts immune tolerance, possibly leading to autoreactive T-cell activation, enhanced Th2 cytokine production, and autoantibody formation against BP180/BP230, ultimately causing dermoepidermal separation and blistering.19

Several distinguishing features exist between drug-induced and idiopathic BP. Previous studies have identified a unique non-inflammatory phenotype of DPP4i-associated BP characterized by less erythema and fewer inflammatory infiltrates despite extensive blistering.3,20 Immunologically, drug-induced BP often targets non-NC16A epitopes of BP180, particularly in patients carrying the HLA-DQB1*03:01 allele. This subtype may also present with more mucous membrane involvement and a distinct distribution pattern.21 Interestingly, European studies have not consistently found these phenotypic differences, suggesting possible regional or genetic variations in disease presentation.22

Treatment of drug-induced BP presents unique challenges, as standard therapies may be contraindicated due to patient comorbidities or may interfere with essential medications.6 The first intervention is discontinuation of the culprit drug, though this alone may be insufficient for symptom control. Conventional therapies including corticosteroids, tetracyclines, and immunosuppressants remain primary options.6 However, these may be problematic in older patients with multiple comorbidities due to risk of adverse effects.10

Given the possible involvement of type 2 inflammation in BP pathogenesis, dupilumab inhibits both IL-4 and IL-13, targeting the upstream immune dysregulation driving the disease.3–5 This mechanism explains the promising results observed in numerous case reports and series, including our patients.8,15 First, its targeted mechanism avoids broad immunosuppression, resulting in a favorable safety profile.7,8 Second, dupilumab demonstrates remarkable efficacy in alleviating pruritus, significantly improving quality of life.15,23 Third, the drug exhibits potential steroid-sparing effects.8 Finally, dupilumab may be administered alongside other essential medications without significant drug interactions, as demonstrated in our fourth case.24

Since the first published case of dupilumab for BP in 2018, there has been growing evidence supporting its efficacy in this condition.24 Table 1 summarizes our case series as well as published cases of drug-induced BP treated with dupilumab. The majority of these reports demonstrate favorable responses. Our findings align with previous reports regarding the rapid improvement in pruritus and subsequent resolution of skin lesions with dupilumab therapy. Drug-induced BP requires special therapeutic consideration, particularly when the offending medication cannot be discontinued due to underlying disease management requirements.

There were several limitations. Since this was a small case series, and although there was a substantial literature review, the mechanistic conclusion on the benefit of dupilumab on drug-induced BP cannot be made. Future prospective studies are warranted not only to validate the mechanism of drug-induce BP, but also to establish phenotypes/biomarkers to predict response to dupilumab. There was also incomplete data in the final case from lacking histopathologic results. Nevertheless, the clinical features, laboratory profiles as well as the definite casualty assessment were sufficient to confirm the diagnosis of drug-induced BP.

Conclusion

Dupilumab represents a promising therapeutic option particularly for drug-induced BP, as demonstrated by our case series. Its targeted mechanism addressing Th2-mediated immunopathology provides rapid improvement in pruritus, significant reduction in disease activity, and a favorable safety profile. Notably, dupilumab enables continuation of essential medications in cases where discontinuing the triggering drug is not feasible. Despite these encouraging results, significant knowledge gaps remain regarding optimal disease phenotypes and biomarkers for predicting its treatment response. Future research should identify specific characteristics that predict which BP patients will benefit and establish optimal treatment protocols for this condition.
