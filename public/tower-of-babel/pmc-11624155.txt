Understanding Return to Work After a Cancer‐Related Sickness Absence. Perceptions of Barriers and Facilitators Among all Relevant Stakeholders
Authors: Amaya Ayala‐García, Laura Serra, Dolors Rodriguez‐Arjona, Fernando G Benavides, Mireia Utzet
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThe aim of this study was to examine the themes acting as barriers or facilitators, from diagnosis and sickness absence (SA) to RTW and work retention, after a cancer‐related SA from the perspectives of all stakeholders in the Spanish setting.MethodsDescriptive qualitative approach with a socio‐constructivist perspective. Theoretical sampling was carried out until saturation. Six discussion groups (4–8 people/group) were conducted: three groups of people with a cancer‐related SA in Catalonia (Spain), one with oncology care professionals, and two with company representatives. An additional individual interview was conducted with a primary care physician. The sessions were held virtually and were recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic analysis and mixed coding.ResultsBarriers to RTW and work retention detected by stakeholders included insufficient information and guidance on the impact of cancer on work and SA management, lack of general knowledge and recognition of side effects, lack of consideration of job tasks by medical tribunals, and working in precarious employment. Facilitators included workplace support, psycho‐oncologists, patient associations, and working for a public company.ConclusionsBoth work interruption due to an SA and RTW, are key moments for determining cancer survivors' work retention. We found a general perception of lack of involvement of the social security system, companies, and health professionals in Spain in the impact of cancer on work.Implications for Cancer SurvivorsIntegrating the work sphere in cancer care from the beginning of cancer treatment, and by all stakeholders, could facilitate successful future RTW.Keywords: cancer, cancer survivors, employers, healthcare professionals, oncology, qualitative, return to work, sickness absence

Background

Cancer has a significant impact on individuals in Europe, with approximately 385 new cases per 100,000 persons [1]. In half of these individuals, the disease is diagnosed during their working life and usually requires an interruption of daily routines shaped by work. This interruption is usually temporary [2], and is subsidized by sickness absence (SA) benefits in countries with a robust social security system. As survival rates among people with a cancer diagnosis continue to increase [3], it is crucial to consider cancer survivors' labor participation and its role in quality of life, as work provides not only economic stability but also structure, a sense of normalcy, and social interaction [4]. Effective cancer care should encompass more than just medical treatment and should also address the working sphere [5]. From a societal perspective, survivors' participation in the labor market is vital for the sustainability of social security systems [6].

Interruptions of working life due to a cancer‐related SA normally end shortly after treatment completion, when most survivors endeavor to RTW [7]. In Spain, as in other European countries, the SA benefit period is limited to a maximum of 1 year. After that period, persons on SA undergo a review by a medical tribunal, which determines whether the benefit period can be extended for a maximum of an additional 6 months or whether they must RTW [8]. Persons who are permanently impaired may receive a permanent disability allowance [9].

The RTW process in cancer survivors is complex, influenced by clinical factors (e.g., cancer stage, chemotherapy), sociodemographic factors (e.g., age, gender, family support), and work‐related conditions (e.g., job strain, physical demands, workplace support) [10, 11, 12]. Survivors often face long‐term work limitations and reduced productivity due to ongoing treatment side effects and the disease itself [13]. Without comprehensive RTW guidance on reinsertion to labor force and adaptations [14], many survivors experience unemployment, early retirement, or frequent job changes [15]. Barriers such as bureaucracy, emotional needs, and uncertainty also complicate the process. Effective RTW interventions must address clinical, work‐related, and social factors to support survivors [16].

To date, the literature on cancer and working life has been predominantly quantitative, which leaves unanswered questions on the factors influencing outcomes in survivors. Recent studies have explored the separately perceptions of survivors [6, 17, 18], physicians and nurses [19, 20], and employers [21]. These studies reveal that survivors can find managing the impact of cancer on working life both difficult and lonely [17] and can even encounter discrimination and hostility at work [21]. Survivors report a lack of understanding and support from employers, managers, and occupational health services, as well as a lack of information on side effects, especially those involving cognitive difficulties. In addition, care planning has been shown to have a negative impact on RTW [18]. These perceptions are shared by other stakeholders, including insurance companies, physicians, and individuals close to survivors [22]. However, each RTW experience is unique and is shaped by personality and coping skills, contextual support, and the working environment. To improve understanding and support, it is essential to consider individual preferences regarding information‐seeking and effective communicating [18]. Despite the available evidence, there is a lack of research on the factors influencing work outcomes at various stages, including diagnosis, SA and RTW, and encompassing the perceptions of distinct stakeholders on survivors' needs when navigating the impact of cancer on work across these various stages.

The aim of this study was to identify the barriers and facilitators affecting the RTW process and sustained work participation among salaried workers, as perceived by various stakeholders, including cancer survivors. A particular aim was to identify work‐related themes, starting from diagnosis and SA, up to the reintegration process and long‐term occupational stability, with the goal of enhancing RTW after a cancer‐related SA.

Material and Methods

Design and Participants

A descriptive qualitative study was conducted from a socio‐constructivist perspective [23]. In accordance with the theoretical perspective used, data were collected using the discussion group technique for different profiles (survivors, oncology professionals, and company representatives). To supplement the group discussions, an individual interview was held with a primary care physician.

Participants consisted of individuals diagnosed with cancer who were employed at the time of diagnosis and who subsequently took a period of SA due to cancer, health care professionals involved in cancer treatment and follow‐up (oncologists, a primary care physician and psycho‐oncologists) and company representatives (human resources and health and safety at work prevention staff). All participants were fluent in Spanish or Catalan and were residents in Catalonia (Spain).

Additional inclusion criteria for cancer survivors were being employed at the time of diagnosis and to be taking an episode of SA due to cancer. To select the sample of survivors, we first conducted theoretical sampling until saturation [24]. This theoretical approach was performed considering the following characteristics to achieve the greatest representativeness possible: sex (female/male), age range (35–50/51–65 years), type of employment contract (permanent/temporary), cancer stage (I–IV), occupational category (manual/non‐manual), having dependent children, and marital status (married/registered or unregistered partner/single). Survivors were recruited through the Catalan Federation of Organizations Against Cancer (FECEC), an entity that collaborates with the main organizations dedicated to improving the quality of life of patients with cancer and their families in Catalonia. Most participants were women aged between 40 and 60 years with a diagnosis of breast cancer (Supplementary Table S1).

For the inclusion of oncology professionals and company representatives, we used snowball sampling. Oncology professionals were recruited through Hospital del Mar in Barcelona, a tertiary public hospital with which the research group collaborates. Most of these professionals were women, predominantly oncologists, with one psycho‐oncologist (Supplementary Table S3). Company representatives were recruited through the investigators' professional network. Most of them worked at the health and safety prevention departments of companies in the services sector (Supplementary Table S2).

Data Collection

Six discussion groups were formed with four to eight participants per group: three groups of survivors, one group of oncology professionals, and two groups of company representatives. To complement the information obtained from the healthcare point of view, we conducted an individual interview with a primary care physician to provide insights into managing sickness absence benefits, as primary care physicians also have a closer and long‐term relationship with patients, giving them unique insights into their worries and concerns in this regard throughout the cancer journey.

The discussion group and interview guide were structured around questions corresponding to the stage of the process and the themes involved in each stage (diagnosis, approval of SA, treatment time absent from work, end of SA and RTW) were listed based on investigators' knowledge and on previous literature on the themes affecting RTW, policies and guidelines (Supplementary Table S4).

The discussion groups, led by two co‐authors, one as the interviewer and the other as the observer, were performed online and lasted an average of 90 min. The groups were video‐recorded and transcribed verbatim by the same researchers who attended the interviews.

Analysis

To capture salient concepts and themes, the discourse from each discussion group was analyzed separately and summarized in a report [25] by A.A.G. and D.R.A.. After agreement between the two analysts, the results of the report of each interview were triangulated by all co‐authors of the study (A.A.G., D.R.A., M.U., L.S., and F.G.B.). Thematic analysis and mixed coding of the data were performed. The codes were generated inductively, guided by the analysis of the texts. These codes were shared and agreed upon by the research analysts first (A.A.G. and D.R.A.). Subsequently, the findings were organized into an overarching scheme encompassing the themes common to all the interviews. The codes and themes emerging from the data were grouped into categories using a mixed procedure by two analysts (A.A.G. and D.R.A.). Initially, broad categories derived from the literature served as a starting point. These categories were then refined by the patterns emerging during the analysis process with the rest of the coauthors (M.U., L.S., and F.G.B.). Several techniques were used to strengthen the quality of the qualitative analysis, such as triangulation by four of the co‐authors of the study at all steps of the study [26] and individual and team reflexivity.

The research team has extensive experience in qualitative research. D.R.A., a sociologist with specialized training in qualitative and participatory health research, works as a freelance researcher in public health. M.U., also a sociologist, has led numerous qualitative studies, while A.A.G. brings formal training in qualitative methods and has participated in multiple research projects. Additionally, experience of L.S. and F.G.B. in various qualitative studies further strengthened the team's approach to the research.

Results

Forty participants took part in the discussion groups, offering insights into the themes affecting cancer and work from various stakeholder perspectives. All interview statements on these topics were analyzed. While some themes were common across stages, their impact varied depending on the medical phase (diagnosis, treatment, and end of treatment), aligning with the start of SA, the SA period, and RTW. Receiving a cancer diagnosis was described by participants as a highly sensitive moment, requiring adjustments to daily routines, particularly concerning work and the need for sickness absence (SA). Barriers identified included a lack of guidance on the impact of cancer on work, health services, and social security, leading to uncertainty about the duration and management of SA. Both oncology professionals and primary care physicians acknowledged insufficient training or time to address work‐related issues, despite agreeing it would benefit patients (Supplementary Table S5). The primary care physician noted that survivors were not concerned about work at this stage.

Employment conditions were both barriers and facilitators to SA. High work responsibility and precarious contracts (temporary or unstable) made SA more difficult, with older survivors facing additional challenges. In contrast, permanent contracts facilitated managing work interruption. Survivors' attachment to their jobs influenced when they decided to take SA. Caregiving responsibilities also added difficulty in balancing work, treatment, and care. Oncologists highlighted that aggressive treatments played a key role in determining whether patients took SA.

2.Being on sickness absence. Receiving treatment, and management of the SA benefits

Being on sickness absence. Receiving treatment, and management of the SA benefits

For some participants, SA benefits allowed them to focus on recovery, while for others, it caused stress due to their inability to work. Two distinct experiences emerged: those for whom work was central to their lives felt psychological strain, while survivors with precarious employment faced job loss (Supplementary Table S5). Survivors with permanent contracts generally had a more positive SA experience, with job security and continued income providing stability. Experiences varied by company type, with public sector workers feeling job security, while private sector workers' experiences depended on company values and policies.

During treatment, concerns about side effects led to uncertainty regarding RTW timing. Survivors felt a lack of guidance from oncology professionals, with oncology care focusing mainly on treatment rather than work‐related issues. Psycho‐oncologists and patient associations were identified as key supports, providing critical information and resources regarding work and cancer.

Support at work or its absence played a crucial role in shaping the SA experience. Survivors with strong support from colleagues and employers reported a more positive experience, while others faced additional stress due to lack of support, such as companies not hiring temporary replacements or pressuring survivors to return early. Public sector companies generally placed less pressure on survivors, while private companies tended to have a more passive approach. Some company representatives explained that they have protocols in place to manage the SA process and minimize its impact on both the worker and the company. Overall, however, companies tended to have a passive approach.

Gender and age also influenced how survivors coped. Women were seen as more emotionally stable and pivotal in supporting their families, being able to manage both productive and reproductive work. Gender differences in partner support were noted (Supplementary Table S5). When women were survivors in the couple, the experience of cancer reversed their prior role of being a caregiver to one in which they required care, completely altering their lives and family dynamics. Age also played a role, with younger survivors and those near retirement more focused on their SA prognosis and RTW prospects, while older survivors, particularly women in their fifties, showed less interest in returning to work.

Additional barriers included misinformation and unclear guidelines for navigating SA bureaucracy, which intensified the stress of managing the absence from work.

3.End of sickness absence and return to work

End of sickness absence and return to work

Participants described the returning to work process as often unplanned and rushed. Medical tribunal evaluations, which decide on extending SA benefits or requiring RTW, were seen as barriers, especially as permanent disability recognition was rarely granted, leading to anxiety. Tribunal appearances often occurred before survivors felt ready, and requests to extend SA were frequently denied, even when survivors felt unprepared (Supplementary Table S5).

The main challenges were invisible side effects and a lack of tools to measure their impact on work ability. Medical evaluations were criticized for not considering psychological burdens or specific job demands, making RTW particularly hard for those with physically demanding roles. The tribunal's notification of the decision on SA was communicated by mail, sometimes reaching the workplace before the survivor, creating additional stress.

Company willingness to adjust work conditions greatly affected RTW success. Survivors unable to return on a full‐time basis found unchanged job demands difficult, as they were expected to work at the same level as that required of them before their cancer diagnosis, often leading them to seek new employment if no adjustments were made (Supplementary Table S5). Small companies struggled more with job modifications, while large companies were generally better equipped to provide flexible or gradual RTW options through collective agreements. Holiday time to extend their leave was also used for RTW flexibilization. The availability of these option often depended on the company ownership, which again emerged as a determining theme in safeguarding survivors' rights.

All stakeholders, including survivors, a major issue was medical tribunals deeming survivors fit to work even when they didn't feel ready, limiting SA extensions to a year or 18 months and seldom considering permanent disability (Supplementary Table S5). The possibility of granting a permanent disability was often not considered. Either of these outcomes would entitle survivors to financial support while unable to work because of poor health. When RTW was not possible and no financial support was granted, patient associations played a key role, assisting survivors in job searches, training, and advice, although survivors often faced discrimination in hiring due to health limitations.

As noted by oncologists, economic vulnerability was an additional barrier affecting both survivors´ emotional state during SA and the motivation ‐ not necessarily healthy ‐ to stay in a job at all costs after the RTW. This circumstance was directly related to the individual's personal situation and the availability of economic and emotional support.

Discusion

All stakeholders broadly agreed on the barriers and facilitators affecting survivors' work after cancer. Many barriers, such as lack of information on cancer's impact and SA management, are modifiable. However, structural barriers—like precarious employment, inflexible work arrangements, lack of recognition by medical tribunals of cancer's impact on work ability, and absence of RTW regulations—are harder to address. Further barriers mentioned by participants were economic vulnerability, and type of occupation, which are often intertwined with above themes and may be further exacerbated by older age.

Facilitators included company support, employment in the public sector or large firms with collective bargaining, and access to gradual RTW options. While many barriers and facilitators were present at all stages, their effects varied from diagnosis through RTW.

Survivors expressed dissatisfaction with the limited support and guidance from primary care physicians and oncologists on managing cancer's impact on work. They noted a lack of information on SA timing, treatment implications, long‐term side effects, SA process details, and employee rights. Primary care physicians and oncologists acknowledged wanting to offer this support but cited insufficient training and time.

These findings align with previous studies noting limited medical guidance on cancer symptoms, treatment side effects, and RTW timing related to work ability [27]. Consistent with our results, other studies highlight a need for early education on workplace accommodations, improved communication among employers, insurers, medical teams, and patients, and greater role clarity [22, 28, 29]. Moreover, uncertainty about survivors' ability to work and the possibility of job loss have been identified as key themes influencing RTW [29]. Patient associations and psycho‐oncologists partially address these gaps by helping patients manage uncertainties in their occupational future.

Many cancer survivors felt that the SA period was too short to address health impacts on work. Some believed they were unfairly denied permanent disability benefits, while others felt that a longer SA would allow full recovery before returning to work. SA spending in Europe is projected to decline overall, except for cancer‐related SA, which is expected to drive disability rates in OECD countries [30]. Allocating funds to support flexible SA options—such as part‐time SA or extending SA beyond 1.5 years when necessary—could help mitigate cancer‐related work disabilities. Northern European countries have introduced transitional benefits focused on rehabilitation and flexible work options, like retraining and labor market integration benefits [31].

We found that the lack of viable alternatives within the social security system led to situations such as company representatives being unable to comply with the verdicts of medical tribunals due to concerns for the wellbeing of the survivor's and others' safety (e.g., machine operators or drivers) or because job adaptation was not feasible due to sector or resource limitations. RTW was generally more successful when accommodations, like reduced hours, were available. Prior studies found that modifying workspaces greatly aids RTW success [32], and manual work or private‐sector employment negatively impacts RTW for cancer survivors [6]. These themes are linked to economic vulnerability, a major influence on RTW [33].

In this study, precarious employment and poor working conditions were significant barriers to managing work while under treatment and returning to work. These challenges also affected the decision‐making process regarding when and how to stop working. Precarious working conditions and job insecurity, particularly associated with temporary contracts, were also highlighted as a theme rendering survivors highly vulnerable to various forms of discrimination, as reported by previous research [34]. In contrast, public companies where job security is generally presumed, and workers' rights and wellbeing are prioritized beyond productivity concerns emerged as an important facilitator.

Similarly, support at work, which was directly linked to stable employment conditions [35], significantly influenced the approach to RTW. There is a consensus in the literature on the importance of the role played by employers and co‐workers in facilitating survivors' successful RTW [14]. Open and regular communication between the workplace and the person with cancer, both before and during their RTW, allowed shared understanding of ongoing challenges, including functional, cognitive and psychological implications, as well as recovery timeframes [29].

Implications

Based on these results, it is essential to create comprehensive information systems and guidelines starting at diagnosis, recognizing paid work as an aspect impacted by the treatment plan. This would allow survivors and employers to receive timely guidance on RTW. Survivors in precarious jobs require particular attention due to their higher risk of workplace discrimination and marginalization from the labor market.

Research should focus on understanding the specific adverse effects of various treatments to clarify how they impact survivors' work abilities. There is also a need for tools that assess work ability based on the demands of different job types.

Finally, revised employment policies are necessary to support both continued employment and hiring of cancer survivors, incorporating their health needs and enabling a gradual RTW process through structured protocols and practices.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several limitations. One limitation is the limited sample size (survivors N = 22; company representatives N = 11 and healthcare professionals N = 7). Consequently, caution should be exercised when generalizing the findings to larger populations. In addition, most of the sample of survivors were middle‐aged women, and there was a predominance of breast cancer. However, breast cancer is the most prevalent form of cancer and understanding of the consequences of its adverse effects is crucial. Likewise, most of the company representatives worked in large companies, which may have provided them with greater resources to address the challenges faced by survivors. All the healthcare professionals, except for one, worked in the same hospital setting. However, the hospital is a tertiary facility, covering a representative sample of the population in Barcelona receiving cancer care. Predominantly female composition of the oncologist group may introduce a potential gender‐related bias, and the perspectives shared might not fully reflect the experiences of male oncologists. Additionally, the absence of other healthcare professionals, such as nurses or social workers, who often have more direct and frequent patient contact, is another limitation. Our structured interview guide provided a useful framework but may have limited the exploration of emerging themes. We encouraged open responses and used triangulation to ensure comprehensive analysis.

This study contributes valuable insights to our understanding of the relationship between work and cancer by integrating the perspectives of all stakeholders involved. Through a qualitative approach, the study identifies both the barriers and facilitators experienced by the parties involved. The results highlight the importance of paid work to cancer survivors of working age and the need to improve the circuits that must be navigated by these survivors when they are diagnosed with cancer, as well as implementing reintegration policies. Making reintegration a primary goal of the social security system is crucial to ensure that inequalities are not created, and that this vulnerable population is not left unprotected and excluded from the dynamics of the labor market.

Conclusions

This study highlights the multifaceted barriers and facilitators impacting cancer survivors' RTW. Modifiable barriers, such as insufficient guidance on cancer's work‐related impacts, can be addressed with improved communication and education, while structural obstacles, like precarious employment and inadequate RTW regulations, require systemic changes. Supportive workplace environments, particularly in the public sector or large companies with collective agreements, and access to gradual RTW options emerged as critical facilitators. The findings underscore the need for comprehensive policies, workplace accommodations, and collaborative efforts among stakeholders to ensure sustainable RTW outcomes for survivors.
