A least squares finite element method to multi-species modeling of negative DC corona discharges in point-to-plane configurations
Authors: Samaneh Majazi, Samad Sobhanian, Ali Reza Ahmadi, Mahmood Moslehi Fard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a two-dimensional fluid model for simulating DC corona discharge in a point-to-plane configuration, implemented using the Least Squares Finite Element Method (LSFEM). The model accounts for electrons, positive ions, and negative ions, with transport described by the drift–diffusion approximation and coupled self-consistently to the electric field through Poisson’s equation. Field-dependent ionization, attachment, and recombination processes are incorporated to capture the essential physics of negative corona discharges. The LSFEM framework enables stable and accurate resolution of steep gradients and complex boundary conditions, while maintaining computational efficiency over long simulation times. Results reveal the temporal and spatial evolution of charged species, the redistribution of the electric field under space-charge effects, and the transition toward a quasi-steady state dominated by negative ion accumulation. Comparisons with previous numerical and experimental studies demonstrate that the proposed approach reproduces key features of corona behavior, offering a robust and efficient tool for both physical understanding and engineering design of corona-based systems.Keywords: Point-to-Plane, Corona discharge, Fluid model, Least square finite element method (LSFEM)Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Corona discharge has garnered significant scientific interest for many decades due to its diverse range of applications in engineering and industry, including ozone generation, air purification, and electrostatic precipitation1. In most of practical applications, corona discharge is generated by applying a direct current (DC) voltage, which facilitates relatively stable and controllable plasma formation2. To optimize the performance of industrial devices utilizing corona discharge, numerical and dynamic simulations of the discharge process are essential3. Among various electrode configurations, the point-to-plane setup is commonly studied because of its relative simplicity and relevance to practical systems4. However, accurately simulating corona discharge remains challenging due to the complex interplay of ionization, space charge effects, and nonlinear electric field distributions. Only a limited number of studies have implemented comprehensive two-dimensional fluid models to capture the spatial and temporal evolution of DC corona discharges1–4.

Hermstein extensively studied the regimes of positive glow corona discharge in air and its transition to spark formation5. Through experimental investigations, he demonstrated that negatively charged species, namely electrons and negative ions, play a crucial role in the development and maintenance of the positive glow region. Hermstein proposed a theoretical model in which a sheath of negative species forms in front of the anode, effectively sustaining the glow current by regulating the local electric field and charge balance6.

Morrow developed a comprehensive two-dimensional model of corona discharge that incorporates key physical processes such as ionization, electron attachment, and recombination, capturing both spatial and temporal dynamics. While his algorithm effectively describes the complex behavior of negative corona discharges in oxygen, the computational cost is substantial, particularly when employing fine spatial meshes to resolve detailed discharge structures and anomalous transient phenomena. Consequently, although this model is highly valuable for fundamental physical investigations, its computational intensity limits practicality to the rapid design and optimization of engineering devices7.

Adamiak et al. investigated positive corona discharge in a point-to-plane configuration, numerically simulating electric fields and the density of charged species. Their model accurately predicts ground electrode current density for moderately pointed electrodes, though extreme field gradients near very sharp tips required numerical approximations. Despite this, results remain valid for configurations where space charge effects dominate8.

Yanallah et al. presented a semi-analytical stationary model of a DC corona discharge in a point-to-plane electrode configuration filled with pure oxygen. Their approach involved solving the Gauss law and continuity equations to obtain spatial distributions of the electric field and charged species densities. The model predictions showed good agreement with both numerical simulations and experimental measurements, demonstrating the effectiveness of their method for capturing the essential physics of corona discharges under these conditions9.

Zhang et al. introduced computational models to study the DC corona discharge in both dynamic and steady-state conditions. For dynamic simulations in air, they combined a hybrid boundary-finite element technique with the method of characteristics (MoC) to predict electric field distributions and charge transport. This approach successfully calculated the average corona current under varying applied voltages, offering insights into transient behaviors10. In another study focused on steady-state conditions, they modeled negative corona discharge in pure oxygen using a point-to-plane electrode configuration at atmospheric pressure and room temperature. Their simulations revealed a sharp accumulation of negative oxygen ions near the discharge electrode, peaking rapidly before dissipating across the gap. This behavior highlights the interplay between ion generation (via electron attachment) and transport mechanisms in oxygen-rich environments11.

Pignole et al. developed an algorithm that incorporates transport phenomena to analyze ozone production in a positive corona discharge setup between a point and a plane electrode. They explored how factors such as mean flow velocity, temperature, and pressure affect the discharge characteristics. Their findings revealed that among these variables, the flow velocity plays the most significant role in ozone generation; higher flow speeds lead to increased ozone production12.

Atten et al. conducted simulations of corona plasma discharge using a hyperbolic needle-to-plate configuration. Instead of relying on the traditional Kaptzov theory, they directly derived the spatial boundary conditions for charged species density from the ionization criterion. This approach allows their method to be applied to various electrode geometries. Although their technique differs from other methods, all approaches tend to converge and produce similar results at low corona discharge currents13.

Peyrous et al. investigated ozone generation in a point-to-plane corona discharge setup. Their study demonstrated that applying a DC voltage with positive polarity to the point electrode results in higher ozone production compared to when a negative polarity is used14.

In 2011, Sattari and Castle developed a new two-dimensional model to simulate Trichel pulses within a 1 cm point-to-plane gap, considering three ionic species. They highlighted that using a variable time step method significantly accelerated the computations. Their simulation results showed good agreement with experimental data. However, to accurately discretize the continuity equation, they had to adhere to the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition, which required very small-time steps, around 1 picosecond, leading to increased overall computation time15.

While significant progress has been made in modeling corona discharges, many of the existing methods are either computationally demanding or rely on simplifying assumptions that limit their accuracy and applicability. In this study, a two-dimensional fluid model for DC corona discharge in a point-to-plane configuration is developed using the Least Squares Finite Element Method (LSFEM). This method provides strong numerical stability and handles complex boundary conditions more effectively than traditional approaches. By including multiple charged species and applying the drift-diffusion approximation, our model captures the key physical processes involved in the discharge. The goal is to offer a practical and efficient tool for simulating corona discharges, one that balances physical accuracy with computational speed, making it especially useful for design and optimization in practical applications.

Model description

Governing equations

To gain a clearer understanding of the simulation model, it is essential to first review the physical principles behind corona plasma discharge. A corona discharge typically appears as a bright glow near the tip of a sharp electrode, where a highly nonuniform electric field is present. In this region, free electrons naturally found in the gas are accelerated by the electric field. As these electrons gain energy, they collide with neutral gas molecules, generating electron-positive ion pairs through the process of ionization. A negative corona discharge occurs when a negative voltage is applied to a needle electrode, typically configured in a point-to-plane arrangement. In its early stage, this discharge is marked by regular, high-frequency current pulses, often referred to as Trichel pulses, resulting from the repetitive formation and quenching of small, localized ionization regions near the needle tip16. These pulses are generally more uniform and of higher amplitude than those observed in the initial phase of a positive corona. As the applied voltage increases, the discharge evolves through distinct regimes: from the Trichel pulse mode to a more continuous glow-like corona, and eventually to a more intense negative streamer corona. If the voltage is raised further, the process culminates in a complete electrical breakdown, producing a spark discharge17.

Low-temperature plasmas, characterized by weak ionization and strong non-equilibrium conditions, are prevalent in various industrial and natural systems1–4. In such plasmas, charged species interact via long-range electromagnetic forces while taking part in transport phenomena and chemical reactions. The mathematical description of these plasmas requires solving a set of coupled partial differential equations (PDEs) for the particle densities and electric potential. This work focuses on a plasma composed of electrons, positive ions, and negative ions in a 2D axisymmetric cylindrical domain, with the assumption of azimuthal symmetry (∂/∂φ = 0). The least squares finite element method (LSFEM)

This leads to a system of linear algebraic equations with a symmetric and positive definite coefficient matrix, enabling the use of fast and robust solvers. LSFEM is particularly useful for systems involving both hyperbolic and elliptic components, mixed formulations, or strong coupling across multiple fields.

In the context of plasma modeling, LSFEM offers a unified treatment of conservation laws, field equations, and constitutive relations. The use of equal-order finite element spaces for all variables simplifies implementation and avoids the stability constraints seen in Galerkin-based mixed methods. Moreover, the variational nature of LSFEM facilitates adaptive mesh refinement and provides a natural a posteriori error estimator. These advantages make LSFEM an attractive choice for high-fidelity simulation of multi-species plasma discharges with sharp gradients, nonlinearities, and geometric complexity21,22.

Solution strategy

The numerical solution of the coupled plasma system proceeds through a sequential time-stepping loop, integrated with nonlinear iterations. The process begins by initializing the densities , , , potential , and auxiliary variables and . At each time step , the source terms and are evaluated based on the electric field magnitude. These are used to assemble the full residual vector , capturing the imbalance in each governing and auxiliary equation.

To resolve the nonlinearity, Newton-Raphson iterations are applied. At each iteration , the Jacobian matrix is formed by differentiating the residuals with respect to the unknowns. The resulting linear system is solved for the update , and the solution is updated as . Convergence of the Newton–Raphson iterations is assessed using two complementary criteria. First, the norm of the nonlinear residual vector is monitored to quantify how close the current iterate is to the zero of the function. Second, the norm of the update is evaluated. This dual monitoring prevents premature convergence, particularly in cases where relaxation factors reduce the update magnitude or when solution variables reach very small values. The iteration is considered converged only when both norms fall below predefined tolerances.

After convergence at each time step, updated solutions are stored, and post-processing is conducted to compute diagnostic quantities such as the electric field distribution, species fluxes, charge imbalance, and reaction rates. This iterative procedure is repeated until the desired simulation end time is reached. The flow chart of the solution procedure is illustrated in Fig. 3. Results & discussion

Figure 4 presents the electron density distribution in the point-to-plane corona discharge at 1 ms and 1 s after the voltage is applied. At the very early stage (1 ms), electrons gather almost entirely in the immediate vicinity of the needle tip, the hot spot of the discharge where the electric field is most intense. In this region, the drift-diffusion model predicts rapid acceleration of electrons toward the anode, and every collision with a neutral molecule has the potential to trigger further ionization. This creates a dense but spatially confined electron cloud, similar to what Morrow7 and Adamiak et al.8 observed in their simulations. Away from the tip, electron density falls sharply due to two main losses: diffusion and attachment to oxygen molecules, producing negative ions. This early-stage pattern mirrors experimental and modeling studies of negative corona15,17, where the initial spark of ionization is tightly localized before spreading outward.

The electron density distribution at (a) 1 ms and (b) 1 s.

By 1 s, the electron population has expanded much farther into the gap, signalling the progression of the system toward a quasi-steady state. The peak near the tip remains, kept alive by continuous ionization, but the profile now stretches toward the plane electrode like a faint glow extending from the needle. The broadening is driven by diffusion, reduced local field intensity, and space-charge effects that gradually reshape the field lines. Similar long-time diffusion-driven broadening was reported by Yanallah et al.9 and Zhang & Adamiak11. The lower peak density compared to 1 ms is the footprint of steady electron loss through attachment, as described in Hermstein’s glow corona model6. The LSFEM successfully captures this transition from a sharp, bright spark to a softer, more distributed glow without numerical instabilities.

Figure 5 shows the distribution of positive ion density in the needle-to-plane configuration that begins almost in lockstep with the electrons. At 1 ms, the ions appear in the same region near the needle tip where electrons are generated, a natural consequence of each ionization event producing a positive ion alongside an electron. But unlike their faster electron partners, these heavy ions are slow movers. They tend to keep near their birthplace, creating a steep density gradient that fades quickly with distance into the gap. At this stage, their movement is dominated by electric field drift, with almost no contribution from diffusion, a behavior also reported by Adamiak & Atten8. Even so, this early build-up of positive space charge already begins to tug at the local electric field, a process noted in Sattari & Castle’s modeling work15.

The distribution of positive ion density at (a) 1 ms and (b) 1 s.

After 1 s, the positive ions have made a slight stretch toward the plane electrode, although their advance is more like a slow tide than a rushing current. The density profile is now smoother and more spread out, with a clear reduction in peak density due to recombination with electrons and negative ions. Experimental studies by Peyrous et al.14 similarly show that positive ion drift times are orders of magnitude longer than electron transit times. The LSFEM reproduces this gradual march of positive ions without spurious oscillations, demonstrating that it can resolve the slow evolution of heavy species over long timescales while keeping them coupled to the faster electron dynamics.

The distribution of the negative ion density in the needle-to-plane structure after 1 ms and 1 s is represented in Fig. 6. At 1 ms, these ions already exist, not right at the tip, but slightly downstream in a region where the field has weakened, and electrons no longer have enough energy to ionize, but still enough to attach to oxygen molecules. This creates a kind of shadow zone behind the main electron cloud, where attachment dominates. The resulting spatial offset between the peaks of electron and negative ion density has been observed in oxygenate discharges by Pontiga et al.17 and reproduced in the multi-species simulations of Zhang & Adamiak11. At this early stage, the distribution is still narrow, as the ions have had little time to drift or diffuse.

The distribution of negative ion density at (a) 1 ms and (b) 1 s.

Till 1 s, the negative ions have taken over much of the gap, particularly toward the plane electrode. They are created continuously through electron attachment and drift slowly toward the anode, like a creeping fog filling the space between electrodes. This wide, stable layer of negative charge plays an important role in screening the high-field region near the tip, a stabilizing mechanism described by Hermstein6 and confirmed in Yanallah et al.’s stationary model9. Sattari et al.15 also reported this persistent accumulation in time-resolved negative corona simulations. Our LSFEM results capture this evolution with high fidelity, underlining the fact that even the slowest, heaviest charged species can be decisive in shaping the long-term electric field structure of a corona discharge.

Figure 7 shows the distribution of the electric field in the needle-to-plane configuration after 1 ms and 1 s. At 1 ms, the electric field is sharply peaked at the needle tip, forming an intense and highly localized high-field region. This is the cause for the initial burst of electron acceleration and ionization, matching the early-stage profiles observed in previous point-to-plane corona simulations7,8. The field strength falls off steeply with distance from the tip, leaving most of the interelectrode gap under much weaker excitation. This spatial pattern reflects the geometry’s natural focusing effect, where the needle acts like an antenna for the applied voltage, concentrating the potential drop in a very small volume. In this regime, the electric field distribution is largely determined by the applied voltage and electrode shape, with only minor distortion from space-charge effects since charged species have not yet significantly redistributed.

The electric field distribution at (a) 1 ms and (b) 1 s.

By 1 s, the electric field profile is noticeably altered, with a reduction in the peak field at the needle tip and a more gradual drop across the gap. This transformation is a hallmark of space-charge-limited corona discharges6,9,14, where the accumulation of slow-moving positive and negative ions partially shields the electrodes. The strongest reduction occurs near the tip, where the dense cloud of ions, particularly negative ions in a negative corona, screens the electrode and limits further acceleration of electrons. The broader, flatter field profile indicates that the discharge has reached a quasi-steady balance between field-driven transport and charge buildup. This field redistribution not only influences ionization rates but also governs the long-term stability of the plasma, ensuring it remains in a self-sustained but non-disruptive regime.

Figure 8 illustrates the temporal variations of the number density of electrons, positive ions, and negative ions from the moment the discharge begins at (r = 0, z = 1 mm). Electrons rise rapidly within microseconds, reflecting their high mobility and immediate response to the applied field. This sharp initial growth is followed by a gradual leveling off as attachment to oxygen and recombination with positive ions start to offset ionization. Positive ions, in contrast, increase more slowly but continue to accumulate over a longer period due to their low mobility and steady production in ionization events, a pattern consistent with the trends reported by Peyrous et al.14 and Sattari et al.15. Negative ions exhibit the slowest initial growth, as their creation depends on the prior availability of electrons for attachment, but once they form, their population increases steadily.

Temporal variations of the different species and current density in the needle-to-plane structure at (r = 0, z = 1 mm), along with the net space charge in the gap.

Over longer timescales approaching 1 s, the system moves toward a near-steady state where the rates of production and loss for each species balance. Electron density stabilizes at a relatively low fraction of its peak, a result of persistent attachment losses. Positive ion density reaches its maximum and then slightly declines due to recombination, while negative ion density plateaus after a prolonged buildup. This temporal evolution mirrors the three-phase behavior described in earlier corona studies6,9,11,23: an initial ignition phase dominated by electrons, a growth phase driven by heavy ion accumulation, and a final saturation phase where space-charge effects control further development. The simulation results confirm that in oxygen-rich negative corona discharges, the eventual dominance of negative ions is central to shaping both the electric field and the overall discharge behavior.

To support the claim of quasi-steady behavior, the temporal evolution of several diagnostic quantities, including the electric field at the needle tip, the peak electron density, the peak negative ion density, and the net space charge in the gap, was monitored. All these quantities exhibit rapid variations during the initial ignition phase (t < 1 ms), followed by gradual smoothing over intermediate timescales (10− 3-10− 1 s). For t > 0.5 s, the temporal derivatives of these quantities fall below 1% per 0.1 s, indicating that the system approaches a slowly varying regime. This behavior is characteristic of the low-current DC negative corona in oxygen-rich gases, where sustained electron attachment leads to a stable accumulation of negative ions, which progressively screen the applied electric field24. In this regime, the discharge no longer exhibits strong oscillatory behavior and can therefore be described as approaching a quasi-steady state.

Conclusions

A detailed numerical investigation of a negative DC corona discharge in a point-to-plane geometry has been carried out using the Least Squares Finite Element Method. By simultaneously resolving the dynamics of electrons, positive ions, and negative ions, the model provides a coherent picture of discharge development from ignition to steady state. The simulations confirm that electron production is initially confined to a narrow high-field zone near the needle, followed by gradual broadening due to diffusion and field redistribution. Positive ions drift slowly toward the plane electrode, while negative ions, formed primarily through electron attachment, accumulate over much of the gap, eventually dominating the space-charge structure. This accumulation plays a decisive role in reducing the peak electric field at the needle tip, stabilizing the discharge, and preventing transition to breakdown.

The results are consistent with previously reported trends in experimental and numerical studies, validating both the physical model and the numerical method. Importantly, the LSFEM formulation proved capable of maintaining stability and accuracy in the presence of strong nonlinear coupling and large variations in spatial and temporal scales. These features make the approach particularly suitable for the simulation of complex plasma systems where multiple charged species, sharp gradients, and long integration times must be handled simultaneously. Beyond its application to corona discharge modeling, the methodology presented here can be extended to other low-temperature plasma configurations, supporting both fundamental research and the optimization of plasma-based industrial devices.
