Motivational factors and support systems for optimal diabetes nutritional management: a qualitative study on the experiences of individuals living with diabetes in Ghana
Authors: Kwabena Opoku-Addai, Gwendolyn Patience Mensah, Kwadwo Ameyaw Korsah, Esther Selasi Avinu, Frank Bediako Agyei, Ellen Marfowaah Ahenkorah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOptimal diabetes self-management by individuals living with the condition is associated with improved quality of life, proper glycaemic control, and reduced mortality rates. However, individuals living with diabetes mellitus can self-manage their condition effectively when they are well-motivated and supported. This study sought to explore the motivational factors and support systems individuals living with diabetes mellitus rely on to manage their diet.MethodsThis study employed an exploratory descriptive design with a qualitative approach. The purposive sampling approach was used to recruit fifteen participants, who were attending clinic at a teaching hospital. The participants were interviewed on one-on-one basis, and the data gathered was analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsTwo main themes, namely, motivational factors for optimal diabetes nutritional management and support systems for optimal diabetes nutritional management, emerged from the data gathered with seven sub-themes (two sub-themes with six sub-sub-themes for the first theme, and five sub-themes for the second theme). The participants of this study appeared to be well-motivated intrinsically and extrinsically with factors such as longevity and dependance on God, as well as had adequate support systems such as family support to enable them engage in optimal diabetes nutritional management. Nonetheless, the support systems were not incorporated into the management of their condition by the health professionals caring for them.ConclusionsParticipants in this study were adequately motivated to practice proper nutritional management and had varied support systems including family support and diabetes peer support groups to do so. Thus, there is the need for health professionals to explore ways of integrating the support systems of individuals living with diabetes into their management to ensure positive health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01386-1.

Background

Diabetes mellitus has a high morbidity and mortality rate worldwide [1, 2]. Half of individuals living with diabetes mellitus are not able to attain the recommended glycated haemoglobin level of less than 7%, required to achieve optimal health and reduce mortality associated with the condition [3]. The World Health Organization has indicated that diabetes mellitus will be the seventh leading cause of mortality globally by 2030 [4]. Optimal diabetes self-management by patients can greatly decrease their glycated haemoglobin levels to the recommended range, as well as improve their quality of life, ensure optimal glycaemic control, prevent or delay complications, and reduce the mortality associated with the condition [5, 6]. However, individuals living with diabetes mellitus can engage in optimal diabetes self-management when they are well-motivated and supported [7].

Research has indicated that just educating patients for them to engage in a healthy behaviour such as healthy eating has a success rate of about 10% [8]. The success rate however improves substantially when patients are both educated and motivated to engage in the healthy behaviour [8, 9]. It has also been established that there is significant improvement in the ability of patients with diabetes to self-manage their condition when they are not only educated, but also motivated to do so [10, 11]. Hence, key to diabetes self-management education and support is to empower individuals living with the condition with optimal diabetes self-management knowledge and skills, as well as the motivation required to engage in the management [12].

Health professionals can make use of the two forms of motivation, which include intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, in the care of patients. Research studies have highlighted that intrinsic and extrinsic motivation are critical in helping patients to engage in healthy behaviours [13, 14]. The importance of motivation cannot be underscored since it is also critical in attaining the third sustainable development goal of ensuring good health and well-being for individuals. This is because, individuals can be in good health and attain well-being when they are well-motivated intrinsically and extrinsically to engage in healthy behaviours [15]. Hence, individuals living with diabetes mellitus who are well motivated intrinsically and extrinsically to ensure optimal nutritional management often eat healthily, which is key to achieving optimal health and improved quality of life [16, 17].

Individuals with diabetes need to be seen as partners in diabetes care and management. Hence, health professionals do not need to command them in the management of their condition but should rather guide, motivate, and support them to ensure optimal diabetes self-management [18]. People living with diabetes mellitus decide how to manage their condition healthily, especially with regards to their diet or nutrition, hence, understanding the factors that motivate and support them to do so cannot be overemphasized [19, 20]. Though research studies have been carried out in Ghana to assess the dietary practices of patients with diabetes and the social support measures that help patients with type 2 diabetes to self-manage their condition [21–24], there is paucity of information on studies that centre on factors that motivate individuals with diabetes to eat healthily, as well as the support systems individuals with type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus rely on to undertake nutritional management of their condition. Hence, this study sought to explore the motivational factors and support systems individuals living with diabetes mellitus attending clinic at a teaching hospital in Ghana rely on to manage their diet.

Methods

Research design and setting

The research design used for this study was an exploratory descriptive design with a qualitative approach. This design enabled the researchers to explore and describe the motivational factors and support systems individuals living with diabetes mellitus rely on to manage their diet. The study was done at the National Diabetes Management and Research Centre (NDMRC) of a teaching hospital in Ghana. The teaching hospital where the study was conducted has a two thousand bed capacity, is the leading referral hospital in Ghana, and is among the three largest hospitals in Africa. The NDMRC runs a diabetic clinic and renders all the essential services needed for optimal diabetes care and management. The NDMRC is managed by professionals, made up of medical and paramedical staff. The centre renders diabetes care and management services such as health education, blood glucose monitoring, and administration of medication amidst others to patients with diabetes from Mondays to Fridays. The centre has more than five thousand registered patients and an average patient daily attendance of eighty.

Study population

The study population comprised of patients living with diabetes mellitus who were attending clinic at the teaching hospital where the study was conducted. The inclusion criteria comprised of individuals with type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus attending clinic at the NDMRC of the teaching hospital, who were aged at least eighteen years, and had been diagnosed with diabetes mellitus for at least six months. The exclusion criteria comprised of individuals with type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus attending clinic at the NDMRC of the teaching hospital who were seriously ill, less than eighteen years of age, and had been diagnosed with the condition for less than six months. Participants were recruited using the purposive sampling approach since the researchers were only interested in patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus who met the inclusion criteria in order to achieve the objectives of the study.

This study was conducted in line with the guidelines according to the Declaration of Helsinki [25]. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the institutional review board of the teaching hospital where the study was conducted (ethics number: KBTH-IRB /000130/2018). After obtaining ethical clearance, the head of the NDMRC granted the researchers access to the unit. The main researcher who has extensive training and education on conducting qualitative studies and qualitative interviews gave the necessary information about the research to the nurses and doctors at the centre. The nurses then introduced the main researcher to individuals with diabetes attending clinic at the centre. The main researcher verbally informed individuals with diabetes mellitus attending clinic at the NDMRC about the details of the study, and all questions they had on the study were answered to their satisfaction. Afterwards, those who met the inclusion criteria and willingly agreed to be part of the study were recruited and interviewed by the main researcher. Since the diabetic clinic of the NDMRC only takes care of patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus, the diagnosis of the participants recruited were self-reported and based on confirmation by nurses at the centre. Each participant willingly signed or thumb printed an informed consent form before he or she was interviewed in either English language or the local Twi language, depending on the participant’s preference. Fifteen participants were recruited and interviewed for this study based on data saturation. The sample size of qualitative research depends on the saturation of data, that is, the emergence of no new theme or sub-theme and the giving of similar responses by successive participants [26]. Data was collected within a period of two months in 2019. Participants of this study were given pseudonyms to ensure anonymity.

Data collection and analysis

Data was collected with a semi-structured interview guide (supplementary file 1) developed for this study. The interview guide was initially piloted on three individuals living with diabetes who met the inclusion criteria and were attending clinic at a university hospital in Ghana. The interview guide was revised after the pilot test to enhance understanding of the questions and to elicit the right responses from the participants. The data gathered from the research tool piloting was not included in the study findings. The participants of this study were mainly asked to talk about the factors that motivate them to undertake nutritional management of their condition. Examples of questions the participants were asked included how they motivate themselves intrinsically to ensure optimal diabetes nutritional management, the values and personal beliefs that enable them to eat healthily, and how they motivate themselves extrinsically to ensure optimal diabetes nutritional management amidst others. In addition, the participants were asked to mention the various support systems that enable them to eat healthily. Examples of questions they were asked included how their family members and significant others are involved in the dietary management of their condition, and how their health care providers support and help them in the dietary management of their condition amongst others. With the participants’ permission, each interview was audio recorded and had a duration of forty to sixty minutes.

Data gathered was analysed by the researchers using reflexive thematic analysis by Braun et al. [27] to produce a report. The researchers translated and transcribed all the interviews that were done in the local Twi language into English language with the help of a language translator. The researchers also transcribed all the interviews that were done in English language. The transcribed interviews were read and re-read to identify significant and interesting ideas, which were coded and matched with relevant extracts from the data. These ideas and codes were used to generate initial themes and matched with relevant extracts from the data. The initial themes with their extracts were then reviewed and refined by the researchers to develop two main themes with seven sub-themes and six sub-sub-themes, after which a research report was generated.

Trustworthiness for this study was ensured by using the strategies outlined by Lincoln and Guba [28]. These strategies include credibility, confirmability, transferability, and dependability. To ensure credibility, data analysis was done using the views of participants from the transcribed interviews. Two of the researchers (KOA and KAK) coded the transcripts separately and compared the results, after which the required changes were made in order to eliminate any biases on the part of the researchers and to use the accurate views of participants in data analysis. To ensure confirmability, the researchers derived the themes, sub-themes, and sub-sub-themes for the study from the data gathered and supported them with verbatim exemplars from the participants. To achieve transferability, the research methods that were employed for this study, such as the study setting, research design, sampling technique, and inclusion criteria amongst others were clearly outlined. In order to achieve dependability, an audit trail of all the processes involved in data collection and analysis were also clearly outlined.

Out of the fifteen participants, two-thirds (n = 10) were married and most (n = 12) had been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus. The highest educational level attained by most of the participants (n = 6) was the junior high school level. Motivational factors for optimal diabetes nutritional management

The participants indicated the motivational factors that enable them to engage in optimal diabetes nutritional management despite its challenging nature. They opined that changing their nutritional lifestyle after the diagnosis of their condition and sustaining it was daunting, but some internal and external motivational factors drive them to be able to eat healthily.

Intrinsic motivational factors

All the participants indicated that it was very difficult to eat healthily. They however cited four intrinsic motivational factors that drive them to eat healthily despite its challenging nature. These four intrinsic motivational factors cited were: Longevity; Desire to be healthy; Desire to avoid diabetes complications; To prove that a good lifestyle can cure diabetes.

Longevity

Almost half of the participants mentioned longevity as their source of motivation for eating healthily. A participant narrated.

“You know life is important, so I don’t even have the urge to go in for the things I have been told to avoid because life is important. I therefore avoid foods that are not good for me so that I can live long.” (Adwoa).

“You know life is important, so I don’t even have the urge to go in for the things I have been told to avoid because life is important. I therefore avoid foods that are not good for me so that I can live long.” (Adwoa).

Desire to be healthy

One-third of the participants indicated that the desire to be healthy was their source of motivation for eating healthily. A participant commented.

“I eat healthily because of good health. I’ve been told by the health professionals here that healthy eating will help me to be in good health, so I eat healthily because I want to be in good health. I still look healthy and fit at 86 years because I eat well.” (Kwabena).

“I eat healthily because of good health. I’ve been told by the health professionals here that healthy eating will help me to be in good health, so I eat healthily because I want to be in good health. I still look healthy and fit at 86 years because I eat well.” (Kwabena).

Desire to avoid diabetes complications

One-fifth of the participants said the desire to avoid diabetes complications was their motivational factor for engaging in proper nutritional management. One person opined.

“I don’t want my condition to worsen or become serious, so I strictly abide by what I’m told by the doctor.” (Yaa).

“I don’t want my condition to worsen or become serious, so I strictly abide by what I’m told by the doctor.” (Yaa).

To prove that a good lifestyle can cure diabetes

A highly educated participant who had been diagnosed with the condition for ten years mentioned that her motivation for eating healthily was to be the first person to prove that a good lifestyle can cure an individual of diabetes. She commented:

“They [dietitians, nurses, and doctors] say diabetes cannot be cured, it can only be managed. But I want to prove to them in the future that it can be cured. If you have a good lifestyle, it can be cured because it was that lifestyle which brought about the condition. I will be the one to bring that cure. That is what motivates me.” (Ama).

“They [dietitians, nurses, and doctors] say diabetes cannot be cured, it can only be managed. But I want to prove to them in the future that it can be cured. If you have a good lifestyle, it can be cured because it was that lifestyle which brought about the condition. I will be the one to bring that cure. That is what motivates me.” (Ama).

Extrinsic motivational factors

The participants also mentioned two extrinsic motivational factors that empower them to engage in healthy eating. The factors cited were: To give their children a better future; Depending on God.

To give their children a better future

One-third of the participants indicated that their main source of motivation for eating healthily was to be alive and healthy to enable them give their children a better future. One of them commented.

“My children are still young, so I don’t have to die and leave them behind. If you give birth and you are unable to take proper care of your children for them to become better placed in society, you have not been fair to them. So, I have to take proper care of myself, so that I will be able to take care of my children. So, my motivation for eating well is my children.” (Akwasi).

“My children are still young, so I don’t have to die and leave them behind. If you give birth and you are unable to take proper care of your children for them to become better placed in society, you have not been fair to them. So, I have to take proper care of myself, so that I will be able to take care of my children. So, my motivation for eating well is my children.” (Akwasi).

Depending on God

Less than one-fifth of the participants also cited depending on God as their main source of motivation for eating healthily. One person echoed.

“It is God who motivates me. I pray a lot too. I rely on God and pray to Him to be able to do all that the health care workers here have told me. I pray to Him to also take away this sickness from me, and to protect my children from the condition. So, depending on God is what motivates me.” (Dede).

“It is God who motivates me. I pray a lot too. I rely on God and pray to Him to be able to do all that the health care workers here have told me. I pray to Him to also take away this sickness from me, and to protect my children from the condition. So, depending on God is what motivates me.” (Dede).

Support systems for optimal diabetes nutritional management

The study participants additionally cited support systems that have been helpful to them in terms of eating healthily. Their narratives however indicated that the support systems were not included in the management of their condition by the health professionals rendering care to them.

Medical support

All the participants acknowledged the important role the nurses, dietitians, and doctors at the teaching hospital play in their lives by educating them on diabetes and its management, the essence of engaging in healthy lifestyles, and providing them with medical care. They underscored that the health professionals had been a major support system for them in managing their condition. Two participants narrated:

“As for the support systems, number one is the support from the doctors, dietitians and nurses here. Their medical treatment is number one, and they really take good care of us. The way they relate to us, teach us about the condition, and encourage us to abide by their instructions is exceptional. When we come for review and we are talking among ourselves, all of us praise them. They are really professionals and have us at heart.” (Kofi).

“As for the support systems, number one is the support from the doctors, dietitians and nurses here. Their medical treatment is number one, and they really take good care of us. The way they relate to us, teach us about the condition, and encourage us to abide by their instructions is exceptional. When we come for review and we are talking among ourselves, all of us praise them. They are really professionals and have us at heart.” (Kofi).

“The doctors, nurses and dietitians in this hospital have also been very helpful and supportive. They advise and encourage me a lot. They also give me quality health care. They really keep me going and are part of my support systems.” (Yaa).

“The doctors, nurses and dietitians in this hospital have also been very helpful and supportive. They advise and encourage me a lot. They also give me quality health care. They really keep me going and are part of my support systems.” (Yaa).

Family support

Almost all the participants cited support from their families as one of their key support systems. They added that their family members offer financial assistance to them, encourage them to eat healthy foods, support them with food items they need, as well as encourage them to avoid foods they have been asked by their health professionals to avoid. Nonetheless, from their narratives, this support system was not incorporated into the management of their condition by health professionals. Some of them commented:

“My sister has been sending me diabetes sugar from abroad, and she sends me money too. My husband also helps me a lot. My husband gives me money for everything I need because of my condition. He buys me fruits every time, and makes sure I eat well, and do what I have been asked to do. He always talks to me to avoid the things I have been asked to avoid so that I can live long.” (Akosua).

“My sister has been sending me diabetes sugar from abroad, and she sends me money too. My husband also helps me a lot. My husband gives me money for everything I need because of my condition. He buys me fruits every time, and makes sure I eat well, and do what I have been asked to do. He always talks to me to avoid the things I have been asked to avoid so that I can live long.” (Akosua).

“My children and family members provide money for me to buy drugs, food and whatever I need. They also assist me with all that I need to be able to manage my condition, that is why I have survived the condition for all these years. My children and family assist me in managing my condition on their own. They were not asked to do what they do for me by the health professionals. The health professionals do not even know them to inform them to do anything for me. The health professionals only care for me, educate and assist me to manage my condition well. They have never involved anyone else in the management of my condition.” (Dede).

Support from friends

Less than one-fifth of the participants also cited support from their friends as a support system that help them in the management of their condition. From their narratives, it was not the health professionals who integrated their friends into the management of their condition. One participant commented:“Currently, I’m staying with a friend of mine who I also work with. He really helps me in managing my condition and helps me with the things I need because of my condition. He encourages me a lot and ensures that I always eat the right foods and avoid the foods that will not help me. He really helps me to be able to cope with everything.” (Akwasi).

He encourages me a lot and ensures that I always eat the right foods and avoid the foods that will not help me. He really helps me to be able to cope with everything.” (Akwasi).

Spiritual support

One-fourth of the participants talked of prayers and faith in God as a support system that has greatly assisted them in effectively managing their condition. They also indicated that this support system was not incorporated into the management of their condition by health professionals. One person said:“It is God and prayers. God can do everything. Whatever a doctor says is out of the knowledge he has acquired from books, but with prayers and God, I’m very fine. Because apart from my wife, it is God and prayers that have kept me going all these years since I was diagnosed of diabetes.” (Kwame).

“It is God and prayers. God can do everything. Whatever a doctor says is out of the knowledge he has acquired from books, but with prayers and God, I’m very fine. Because apart from my wife, it is God and prayers that have kept me going all these years since I was diagnosed of diabetes.” (Kwame).

Diabetes peer support groups

One-fifth of the participants mentioned that they were informed of diabetes peer support groups by the health professionals at the teaching hospital but were not incorporated into any group by the health professionals. They added that they were only told where the group meets and informed to join the group. Two of them joined the group but one did not. The one who did not join the group echoed:“The doctors and nurses told me about a diabetes group here and asked me to go and join the group. But I never joined it because I’m not interested. How can I go and publicize my sickness? I can’t join any such group, it won’t be of any benefit to me, and the group cannot heal me of my disease.” (Kwame).

“The doctors and nurses told me about a diabetes group here and asked me to go and join the group. But I never joined it because I’m not interested. How can I go and publicize my sickness? I can’t join any such group, it won’t be of any benefit to me, and the group cannot heal me of my disease.” (Kwame).

The two participants who joined the diabetes peer support group mentioned that they had stopped attending meetings for years now and were not presently members of the group. They added that no diabetes peer support group currently exists at the teaching hospital. One person remarked:“The doctors and nurses told me about a diabetes support group, but I don’t go there anymore. We used to meet in a building here. I was going for some time, but for a long time now, I don’t go. I stopped going some time ago because I didn’t have time. So, there was a group, but it’s been years now since I saw the group. The group doesn’t exist anymore.” (Akosua).

Discussion

Intrinsic motivation, which deals with performing activities for an individual’s own benefit or gain, is key to engaging in a healthy behaviour [13]. Patients with diabetes who are intrinsically motivated have optimal health outcomes, better quality of life, and proper glycaemic control than those who are motivated by any other means [29, 30]. The findings of the current study revealed that most of the participants were motivated intrinsically to eat healthily. This is in line with the findings of another study conducted among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus [14]. The current study also found that majority of the participants emphasized on longevity, good health, and the avoidance of worsened health states as their intrinsic motivational factors. The findings concur with other studies conducted in England and Denmark among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus [31, 32]. These findings suggest that health professionals in Ghana can largely rely on intrinsic motivational factors to entice individuals living with diabetes to eat healthily. The intrinsic motivational factor of one participant was to prove that diabetes mellitus could be cured with a healthy lifestyle. This finding suggests that further studies must be carried out to explore the beliefs of patients with diabetes in Ghana. This will determine if this belief is widespread so that the necessary interventions can be rolled out.

Extrinsic motivation, which deals with the performance of activities by an individual for the benefit of someone else, is also key in achieving optimal diabetes nutritional management among individuals living with the condition [14, 33]. The present study’s findings showed that some of the participants were motivated extrinsically to eat healthily. Their main reason was to be able to take proper care of their children to have a better future. The findings are similar to that of other studies conducted among individuals diagnosed with gestational diabetes mellitus [34–36]. A few of the participants also cited reliance on God as an extrinsic motivator that enable them to eat healthily. This is partly in tandem with the findings of another study carried out among individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus [37]. These findings seem to indicate that patients with diabetes in Ghana can also be motivated to engage in healthy eating using extrinsic factors.

Support systems are mediums which provide information, as well as reliable and consistent help to patients to ensure optimal management of their conditions. Support systems are significantly associated with optimal nutritional management and glycaemic control among patients with diabetes mellitus [38]. The current study’s participants indicated that nurses, dietitians, and doctors at the teaching hospital were their main source of support in effectively managing their condition. These findings are similar to the findings of another study which revealed that nurses, doctors, dietitians, diabetes educators, pharmacists, and psychologists are important sources of support for individuals living with diabetes [39]. These findings suggest that, apart from doctors, nurses, and dietitians, other health professionals must be more involved in the care of patients with diabetes mellitus in Ghana. The current study also revealed that, apart from medical support, no other support system was incorporated into the management of the participants by the health professionals. This partly concurs with the findings of another study carried out among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus [40]. It is therefore recommended that workshops or seminars be organized for health professionals in Ghana on how to integrate social support systems into the management of individuals with diabetes mellitus to improve their health outcomes.

Most of the participants also cited support from their families as a key support system. They indicated that their family members support them to eat healthily through encouragement, financial assistance, and in food purchasing and preparation. This is in tandem with the findings of another study carried out on patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus [41]. This highlights the need for health professionals in Ghana to involve the families of patients with diabetes mellitus in their care.

Some participants cited spiritual support as part of their support system in optimally managing diabetes. They mentioned that, reading their Bibles, praying, as well as having faith, hope and belief in God assist them to cope better with their condition. This is not surprising due to the high level of spirituality or religiosity of a lot of Ghanaians. These findings are similar to that of other studies conducted on patients living with diabetes mellitus [42–46]. Though, research has found that spirituality and religiosity have a significant relationship with diabetes self-management practices such as optimal dietary management [43, 47], it remains unclear how these religious practices and beliefs help individuals living with diabetes mellitus to eat healthily. Further research is therefore needed to unravel and understand the complexities, role, and importance of spiritual support in the care and management of diabetes mellitus. These findings also suggest that it is prudent for health professionals in Ghana to take advantage of this support system and explore ways of integrating it in the management of individuals living with diabetes mellitus. Again, it is recommended that the Ministry of Health collaborate with the various faith-based organizations in Ghana and educate them on how they can support and help their members with diabetes mellitus, since spiritual support is a key support system.

Research conducted by Kadirvelu and colleagues [48], as well as Schiotz and colleagues [49] have posited that support from friends is significantly associated with healthy eating among individuals living with diabetes mellitus. A few of participants in the present study cited their friends as a support system. They emphasized that their friends encourage them and ensure that they eat healthily. These findings are in tandem with the findings of another study done among young adults diagnosed with type 1 diabetes mellitus [50]. The findings are also similar to that of other studies carried out among adolescents and children living with type 1 diabetes mellitus [51, 52]. These findings emphasize the need for health professionals in Ghana to involve the friends of individuals with diabetes mellitus in the management of their condition to ensure positive health outcomes.

The facilitation of individuals living with diabetes into diabetes peer support groups by health professionals can enhance the knowledge and skills of the individuals on diabetes self-management. It can also help them to overcome the diverse barriers they face with diet and equip them to engage in optimal self-management [53]. A diabetes peer support group is a group of people with diabetes sharing their knowledge and experiences on diabetes and its management. It also encompasses two individuals with diabetes sharing their knowledge and experiences on diabetes and its management [54]. The current study showed that most of the participants had not received education on diabetes peer support groups from health professionals or any other source. Also, almost all the participants had little to no knowledge on diabetes peer support groups and their relevance in the management of diabetes. Additionally, none of the participants presently belonged to any diabetes peer support group since none of them was incorporated into any diabetes peer support group. Furthermore, none of the participants was aware if any such group presently existed at the teaching hospital. These findings contradict the findings of another study conducted in New Zealand among patients with diabetes mellitus. The findings of that study revealed that the participants had adequate knowledge on diabetes peer support groups. In addition, the participants were well incorporated into diabetes peer support groups and were still active participants of the groups [55]. The contrasting findings may be due to the little to no knowledge of the health professionals at the teaching hospital in Ghana on diabetes peer support groups, its benefits, and the need to integrate such groups in the management of individuals with diabetes. These findings suggest that workshops or seminars should be organized for health care providers in Ghana to educate them on diabetes peer support groups, its benefits, and how to successfully incorporate them in the management of individuals living with diabetes. It is also recommended that health facilities in Ghana, including the teaching hospital where the current study was conducted, should establish functional diabetes peer support groups, and provide them with the needed resources and logistics.

Some participants of the current study identified the knowledge and experiences their colleagues with diabetes share with them when they attend the diabetic clinic for healthcare as a source of support to engage in healthy eating practices. They emphasized that they put into practice what they gain from the shared knowledge and experiences with their peers. This is in consonance with the findings of another study conducted among adults living with diabetes mellitus [56]. These findings suggest that the good will and generosity of some patients to share their experiences and knowledge on diabetes with their peers was the only peer support some of the participants of the present study had.

The limitations of this study were that it was based on the experiences of individuals living in a multicultural and multinational city where support systems and motivational factors may differ from those living in other cities and towns. There was also underrepresentation of patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus in the study due to type 2 diabetes mellitus being the commonest form of diabetes.

Conclusions

Adequate motivation and support systems can enable patients with diabetes to engage in optimal self-management of diabetes mellitus, especially its nutritional management. The participants of this study were intrinsically and extrinsically motivated to be able to engage in healthy eating, and also had varied support systems to enable them undertake optimal diabetes nutritional management. Despite the key roles the support systems play in the management of the participants’ condition, they were not incorporated into their management and care by the health professionals caring for them. It is recommended that health care providers in Ghana incorporate support systems such as diabetes peer support groups, friends, family, and spirituality of individuals living with diabetes into the management and care of their condition to enable them achieve positive health outcomes and optimal health. Also, to ensure generalization, future studies can be carried out quantitatively to assess whether adequate motivation and support systems actually translate to healthy diabetes eating practices.
