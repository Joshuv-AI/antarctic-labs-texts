Talin1 loss activates DRG neurons to accelerate bone remodeling and fracture healing in mice
Authors: Qinnan Yan, Donghao Gan, Kangtai Xu, Zecai Chen, Sixiong Lin, Bo Zhou, Zhen Xu, Qiyue Zhong, Chu Tao, Yiming Zhong, Shaochuan Huo, Weihong Yi, Zilong Wang, Xuejun Song, Lei Qin, Guozhi Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBone tissue is densely innervated by sensory nerve fibers, whose roles in bone remodeling and regeneration are poorly defined. This study aims to investigate the physiological function of Talin1, a key focal adhesion protein, in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons in pain processing and its specific impact on bone remodeling and fracture healing.Materials and methodsWe utilized a transgenic mouse model (Advillin-CreERT2; Talin1fl/fl) to delete Talin1 expression in sensory neurons under tamoxifen induction. Pain sensitivity, bone mass, and fracture healing were evaluated in adult mice. Transcriptomic analysis, immunofluorescence, western blot, scanning and transmission electron microscopy, alongside a highly localized pharmacological inhibition model using BIBN-loaded hydrogels, were employed to delineate the underlying mechanisms.ResultsTalin1 is predominantly expressed in C-fiber DRG neurons and its expression is significantly downregulated following bone fracture. Talin1 loss markedly activates DRG sensory neurons and increases mechanical, but not thermal, pain sensitivity in mice. Concurrently, Talin1 deficiency inhibits mitophagy and impairs mitochondrial function, as indicated by altered mitochondrial morphology, abnormal reactive oxygen species production and reduced mitochondrial membrane potential. Furthermore, beyond its role in nociception, Talin1 loss not only increases bone mass in both adult and aged mice but also accelerates fracture healing by modulating bone remodeling. This pro-healing phenotype coincides with increased expression of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) in DRG neurons. Critically, pharmacological inhibition of CGRP receptors at the fracture site by BIBN abolishes the fracture healing-promoting effect caused by Talin1 loss.ConclusionsOur studies demonstrate that Talin1 plays a pivotal role in modulating sensory neuron activation, pain perception, and bone remodeling. Specifically, Talin1 loss accelerates bone repair by upregulating CGRP, thereby establishing a Talin1-CGRP signaling axis that mediates sensory neuron control of fracture healing.The translational potential of this articleThis research highlights the dual role of Talin1 in sensory neurons in modulating both pain perception and bone remodeling, and emphasizes the potential for targeting Talin1 signaling as a therapeutic strategy to alleviate pain or to accelerate fracture healing.

Introduction

Sensory neurons, as the primary afferents, enable the perception of internal and external stimuli including temperature, odors, tastes, and pain [1]. Growing evidence starts to show that sensory neurons also play key roles in regulating the homeostasis of various tissues and organs. For example, cutaneous TRPV1+ neurons can induce protective type 17 innate immunity [2], whereas nociceptor innervation in the spleen enhances germinal center formation and humoral immune responses [3]. Nociceptive neurons also participate in modulating hematopoietic stem cell mobilization [4], and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)-expressing sensory neurons facilitate tissue repair processes [5].

Bone is extensively innervated by sensory nerves which extend from dorsal root ganglia (DRG) into the developing axial and appendicular skeleton. These sensory nerve fibers are primarily distributed in the periosteum, mineralized bone matrix, and bone marrow, with the majority of sensory nerve fibers being thinly myelinated A-δ fibers and peptide-rich C-fibers [6]. As mice age, the density of sensory nerve fibers in the periosteum gradually declines, whereas the overall density of sensory nerve fibers in the bone marrow remains stable across the lifespan [7]. Besides receiving skeletal pain signals, sensory nerve fibers have been shown to play functional roles in bone development and homeostasis [1,[8], [9], [10]], as well as in fracture healing and cranial defect repair [11,12]. For instance, the NGF-TrkA signaling pathway mediated by sensory neurons plays a key role not only in regulating bone mineralization and vascularization during endochondral ossification, but also in fracture healing [8,11,13]. Inhibition of this pathway in sensory nerves impairs nerve regeneration in injured soft tissues and suppresses the development of heterotopic ossification [14]. Moreover, osteoblasts can modulate bone formation by secreting PGE2, which activates corresponding receptors on sensory neurons [10]. Neuropeptides released from sensory neurons, such as Sema3A [9], CGRP [[15], [16], [17], [18]], and Substance P [19], are also critical regulators of bone homeostasis and fracture healing. Nevertheless, the precise effects of sensory neuron activation on bone remodeling in adult mice remain unclear. Furthermore, it is still unknown whether post-bone fracture pain, a prominent clinical symptom, serves a protective function that may actively influence the fracture healing process.

Talin1 is a ubiquitously expressed focal adhesion protein that connects the actin cytoskeleton network to integrins in the plasma membrane, thereby coupling the intracellular contractile machinery to the extracellular matrix (ECM). Global knockout of Talin1 results in embryonic lethality between stages E8.5-E9.5 in mice [20]. Tissue-specific knockout studies have shown that Talin1 is crucial for the function of various tissues and cells, including osteocytes, osteoclasts, platelets, intestinal epithelial cells and pancreatic β cells [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. In the nervous system, in vitro experiments using PC12 cells have shown that overexpression or activation of Talin1 significantly promotes axon growth [26]. Moreover, Talin1 mediates axonal regeneration after spinal cord injury [27]. However, the precise physiological function of Talin1 in sensory neurons, particularly its roles in bone homeostasis and fracture healing, remains to be elucidated.

In this study, we show that Talin1 is highly expressed in C-fiber DRG neurons, and its expression is significantly downregulated after bone fracture. Inducible deletion of Talin1 in sensory neurons induces mechanical allodynia, increases bone mass and accelerates bone fracture healing in adult mice. Talin1 deficiency inhibits mitophagy, triggers mitochondrial dysfunction and results in elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, neuronal excitation and increased CGRP expression, which further accelerate bone fracture healing. This study shall bring new insights into the functional roles of Talin1 in sensory neurons during bone remodeling and fracture healing.

Materials and methods

Animals

The generation of Talin1fl/fl mice was previously described [25]. The Advillin-CreERT2 mice were kindly provided by Professor Xuejun Song of Southern University of Science and Technology. Talin1fl/fl mice were crossed with Advillin-CreERT2 mice to generate Advillin-CreERT2; Talin1fl/fl mice. Primers used for mouse genotyping are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Tamoxifen (TAM) (Sigma, T5648) was dissolved in corn oil (10 mg/mL) and administered to Advillin-CreERT2; Talin1fl/fl mice (referred to as cKO) and their Cre-negative floxed Talin1 mice littermates (referred to as Con) by intraperitoneal injection with 100 mg/kg body weight per day for 5 days [28]. Mice were maintained in a humidity (40–60%) environment at 21 ± 2 °C and 12-hour light/dark cycles, with unrestricted access to food and water.

Behavior tests

The von Frey test was conducted to assess mechanical pain threshold in mice. Briefly, mice were placed in boxes situated on an elevated metal mesh floor. Their hind paws were stimulated with a series of von Frey filaments of progressively increasing stiffness (ranging from 0.16 to 2.00 g, Danmic Global), which were applied perpendicularly to the central plantar surface. The 50% paw withdrawal threshold was determined using the up-down method [29].

The hot plate test was conducted with a Hot/Cold Plate (Anhui Zhenghua Biologic Apparatus Facilities, ZH-6C) to examine thermal pain sensitivity as previously described [[29], [30], [31]]. Mice were placed on a hot plate maintained at 50 °C, 53 °C or 56 °C, and their reaction times were recorded when they started showing pain-avoidance behaviors, such as paw licking, shaking, or jumping. To prevent thermal injury, cutoff times of 45 s (for 50 °C), 40 s (for 53 °C) and 30 s (for 56 °C) were established.

The tail-flick test was conducted by gently restraining mice by hand with a terry cloth glove as previously described [32]. The distal 2 cm of their tails was submerged in hot water at 48 °C or 50 °C. The tail-flick latency was defined as the duration it took for a mouse to flick or withdraw its tail from the hot water. To prevent thermal injury, a maximum cutoff time of 15 s was established.

Femoral fracture model

A femoral fracture model was conducted with 12-week-old male mice as previously described [33]. Briefly, Talin1 deletion was induced through 5 consecutive days tamoxifen (TAM) injection. Two days after 5-day injection period, a femoral fracture was established in the mice. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% for induction and 1% for maintenance), followed by shaving the right hind limb and disinfecting with povidone-iodine. Subsequently, the right femur was exposed, and a transverse fracture was created at the midshaft. To stabilize the fracture fragments, a 23-gauge needle was inserted into the medullary cavity. Four weeks after fracture induction, the mice were euthanized, and the samples were collected and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA).

Micro-computerized tomography (μCT) analysis

Femurs that were fixed without undergoing demineralization treatment were utilized for μCT analysis (PINGSHENG SCIENTIFIC, NEMO Micro CT, NMC-200). In brief, femurs were scanned at settings of 90 kV, 40 mA with a voxel size of 13 μm. The raw data were processed and reconstructed, and three-dimensional images were subsequently observed using the Avatar data viewer software. For trabecular measurements, an area of interest measuring 1.5 mm in length, starting 0.5 mm proximal to the distal growth plate, was examined. For cortical bone analysis, a 1.0 mm length of mid-femoral cortical bone was assessed. The bone measurements conducted on the specimens included bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), cortical thickness (Ct.Th), total cross-sectional area (T.Ar), and marrow area (M.Ar) [34].

Histological evaluation

Mice were deeply anesthetized using isoflurane and underwent perfusion via the ascending aorta with 20 mL PBS (phosphate-buffered saline), followed by 40 mL pre-cooled 4% PFA. The L4-L6 dorsal root ganglia (DRG) were extracted and postfixed in 4% PFA overnight. Subsequently, the tissues were embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) compound after dehydration in 20% and 30% sucrose solutions sequentially (24 h each). DRG tissues were then sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm for histological analysis. Tibias or femurs were fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C, followed by decalcification in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for three weeks. Post-decalcification, the bones were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm for histological evaluation. Safranin O and Fast Green (SO&FG) staining was performed as previously described [35,36].

Calcein double labeling, MAR, MS/BS, and BFR

Calcein double labeling and measurements of MAR, MS/BS, and BFR were performed as previously described [37,38]. Briefly, calcein (Sigma, cat# C0875) was injected intraperitoneally into mice at a dose of 20 mg/kg on days 7 and 2 before sacrifice. Freshly isolated femurs were fixed in 4% PFA and embedded in T7200VLC Light-curing resin (EXAKT, 51000). The embedded blocks were sectioned into approximately 1 mm-thick slices using a water-cooled diamond-impregnated circular saw (EXAKT). These slices were then sanded down to a thickness of less than 50 μm using sandpaper of three different grits (800, 1200, and 2500), followed by polishing with 4000-grit sandpaper. After polishing, the slides were mounted in a water-soluble medium and covered with a plastic cover. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus-BX53) and analyzed for MAR, MS/BS, and BFR as previously described.

Serum ELISA assay

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for the serum was performed as previously described [37]. Briefly, the serum samples were collected from Con and cKO mice. Serum procollagen type 1 amino-terminal propeptide (P1NP) and collagen type 1 cross-linked C-telopeptide (CTX-1) were measured by using P1NP ELISA kit (Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., D721053) and CTX-1 ELISA kit (Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., D721204), respectively, according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the DRG of Con and cKO mice at 3 months after TAM injection with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) and treated with RNase-free DNase I (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan). One point five micrograms RNA per sample was used as input material for RNA sample preparation. Sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) following manufacturer's recommendations, and the library quality was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform by the Beijing Allwegene Technology Company Limited (Beijing, China) and paired-end 150 bp reads were generated. The high-quality clean reads were mapped to the reference genome sequence using STAR. After quantification of gene expression levels, differential expression analysis, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were performed using the DEGseq (2010) R package and KOBAS software.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

RT-qPCR analyses were performed according to a previous protocol [39]. Briefly, total RNA was isolated from all dorsal root ganglia using the Cell/Tissue Total RNA Kit (Yeasen, 19221ES50), according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA quality was assessed with NanoDrop 2000. One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA with the HiScript Ⅲ RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R323-01) according to the manufacturer's protocol. iTaq Universal SYBR Green SuperMix (Bio-Rad, 1725125) was used for real-time PCR amplification. The expression levels of genes were calculated by the ΔΔCt method. The primer pairs used for RT-qPCR analyses are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Preparation and injection of hydrogel

The lyophilized powder of SupGel-I (SG1001-1, DEMAL ORGANISM, China) was dissolved in 1 mL of sterile PBS. The centrifuge tube was then placed in a water bath at 37 °C for 5 h to facilitate hydrogel self-assembly. Subsequently, the LAP (Lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate) solution (PI001, DEMAL ORGANISM, Guangzhou, China) was added at a volume ratio of 9:1 and mixed thoroughly. Finally, the biological crosslinker (J-001, DEMAL ORGANISM, Guangzhou, China) was used to cure the hydrogel by irradiating it at 20 W and a wavelength of 405 nm for 5 min. For the preparation of drug-loaded hydrogels, BIBN-4096 (HY-10095, MCE, USA) was dissolved in the hydrogel prior to solidification to achieve a concentration of 0.2 μg/μL. After thorough mixing, the solution was transferred to a 1 mL syringe, and the hydrogel was cured using the biological crosslinker for 5 min. Subsequently, each mouse with a femoral fracture was injected with 50 μL of the drug-loaded hydrogel (containing 10 μg of BIBN-4096).

Drug loading and release

The release of the drug from the hydrogel was analyzed using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the model drug [40]. BSA was dissolved in the hydrogel prior to solidification to achieve a concentration of 0.2 μg/μL. After thorough mixing, the hydrogel was transferred to a 1 mL syringe mold. Subsequently, a cylindrical hydrogel with a diameter of 0.5 mm and a height of 0.5 mm was prepared through photopolymerization. The hydrogel sample was then placed in a 15 mL centrifuge tube containing 500 μL of PBS. Following this, the tube was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 80 rpm. Aliquots of the release medium (PBS) were collected at days 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 21, and 28 for analysis. Each time, 250 μL of the medium was removed and replenished with an equal volume of fresh PBS. Finally, the BSA concentration in the collected PBS was determined using the BCA assay kit (P0009, Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of the hydrogels

Initially, the hydrogel was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 6 h. Subsequently, it was rinsed three times in deionized water (30 min per rinse) and dehydrated in a graded series of alcohols (30%, 50%, 75%, and 100% ethanol), with each rinse lasting 30 min. The dried hydrogel was then cut and trimmed to expose its cross-sectional structures. Following this, it was coated with a thin layer of a platinum and gold mixture to enhance conductivity and contrast. The micromorphology of the hydrogels was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SU8100, HITACHI, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 5 keV.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Freshly isolated DRG tissues were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution (Servicebio, G1102) at 4 °C overnight, and then postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (Ted Pella Inc, 18456) at room temperature for 2 h. After washing with PBS, the tissues were dehydrated with different concentrations of ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 95%, 100% and 100% for 20 min each) and 100% acetone for 2 × 15 min. The process continued with infiltration in spurr resin (SPI, 02624-AB) at varying concentrations (50% spurr in acetone at 37 °C for 2-4 h; 66.7% spurr in acetone at 37 °C overnight; 100% spurr at 37 °C for two 8-h intervals) and concluded with polymerization for 48 h at 60 °C in an oven. Ultrathin sections of 60-80 nm thickness were sliced using a Leica Ultracut (Leica UC7) and placed on a copper mesh. The sections underwent post-staining with 2% uranyl acetate for 8 min, followed by three washes with 70% ethanol and three with ultrapure water. Subsequently, they were stained with 2.6% lead citrate solution (Sigma, 203580) for another 8 min. After three more rinses with ultrapure water, the sections were blotted with filter paper and left to dry overnight at room temperature. Micrographs were taken with a transmission electron microscope HT7800 (HITACHI, Japan) equipped with a Hitachi TEM system. For analysis, the area and crista number of each mitochondrion were determined in ImageJ following manual drawing of each organelle.

Cell culture

The isolation and culture of primary DRG neurons from adult mice were performed as previously described [32]. DRGs were harvested in HBSS and then incubated with collagenase A (0.2 mg/mL, Sigma Aldrich, 10103578001) and dispase-Ⅱ (3 mg/mL, Sigma Aldrich, D4693) at 37 °C for 90 min. After digestion, the DRGs were rinsed twice with HBSS and resuspended in Neurobasal medium (ThermoFisher, 3582901) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 2% B27 (ThermoFisher, 17504044). The digested DRGs were then placed on confocal dishes or 24-well plates pre-treated with 0.1 mg/mL poly-D-lysine (Beyotime, C0312). To increase the purity of neuronal cells, after allowing the cells to adhere for 2 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2, the non-adherent cells were removed before adding fresh growth medium to the culture.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed as previously described [36]. The primary DRG neurons were lysed using RIPA buffer (Sigma–Aldrich; R0278) supplemented with 1% Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (MCE; HY-K0010). Then, the cellular proteins were extracted and sequentially underwent electrophoresis, membrane transfer, incubation with primary and secondary antibodies, and detection of protein expression using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Bio-rad; 1705061).

Immunofluorescent (IF) and immunocytochemistry (ICC) assay

For DRG tissues, sections were rehydrated using PBS. For tibia or femur tissues, paraffin sections underwent dewaxing with xylene and rehydration through a series of ethyl alcohol solutions (100%, 95%, 70%) and then PBS, followed by antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6.8). Then the sections were incubated with blocking buffer (Beyotime, Cat# P0260) containing 1‰ Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. After blocking, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. For immunocytochemistry, primary neurons were seeded onto poly-D-lysine coated confocal dishes and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 72 h. Cells were then fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequent steps followed the standard immunofluorescence protocol for tissue sections. The primary antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Imaging was conducted using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope (Germany). For all comparative analyses, images from different experimental groups within the same experiment were captured using identical microscope settings (e.g., laser power, gain, and exposure time). Subsequent image processing and quantitative analysis were performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). For presentation, all images intended for direct comparison within a figure were processed with uniform parameters for contrast and brightness adjustment to ensure a fair and accurate visual evaluation. The colocalization analyses of PINK1, Parkin, LC3B, LAMP1, and Tom20 were performed using the Coloc2 plugin in ImageJ.

Measurements of mitochondrial ROS

Mitochondrial ROS levels in DRG neurons were assessed using the MitoSOX Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (MCE, HY-D1055) as previously described [28]. Following rehydration, the DRG sections were incubated with 5 μM MitoSOX for 15 min at room temperature under protection of light and were then counterstained with DAPI to visualize the nuclei. The fluorescence signals were visuslized using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope (excitation wavelength: 510 nm, emission wavelength: 580 nm).

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)

MMP was measured using an enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with JC-1 (Beyotime Biotechnology, C2003S). In brief, primary neuron cells were incubated with JC-1 staining solution at 37 °C for 20 min, washed with JC-1 staining buffer, and then immersed in the medium. Normal mitochondria with JC-1 aggregates produced red fluorescence, and depolarized mitochondria with JC-1 monomers produced green fluorescence. The fluorescence signals were analyzed using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope. Fluorescence intensity was quantified by imageJ, and the MMP was expressed as the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity.

Statistical analyses

The sample size per group was determined based on our previous experience. Animals used in this study were randomly grouped. μCT, IF and histology were performed and analyzed in a double-blinded way. GraphPad Prism 10 was used for data analysis. All data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (s.d.), unless noted otherwise. Statistical analyses were based on the number of animals per group. Typically, the two-tailed unpaired t-test was used for two-group comparisons. For two-factor comparisons, two-way ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons post hoc test.

Talin1 is predominantly expressed in C-fiber DRG neurons and downregulated after bone fracture

To understand the function of DRG neurons in bone fracture healing, we conducted a re-analysis of DRG transcriptomic data from post-fracture rats [41]. We found significant alterations of the focal adhesion signaling pathway at 3, 7, and 14 days after bone fracture (Fig. 1A), suggesting that focal adhesion molecules in DRG may contribute to either pain sensation or fracture healing. Talin1, a key component of focal adhesion complexes, has been shown to promote axonal growth when activated or overexpressed in sensory neurons [42]. However, it is still unknown whether Talin1 expression differs across sensory neuron subtypes. What is its precise physiological role in these neurons? How does it affect bone homeostasis and fracture healing? To answer these questions, we next re-analyzed the published single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset from mouse L3-L5 DRG [43]. Among all DRG neuron types, peptidergic (PEP) and non-peptidergic (NP) nociceptors contained a higher proportion of Talin1-positive cells (defined as those with a Talin1 unique molecular identifier count ≥1) compared to neurofilament (NFs) neurons (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1A and B). Given that both PEP and NP neurons are primarily small-diameter C-fiber nociceptors, whereas NFs neurons are typically large-diameter A-fibers [44], we conclude that Talin1 is predominantly expressed in C-fiber DRG neurons.

Next, we wondered whether the expression of Talin1 in DRG neurons is altered under pathological conditions. A femoral fracture model was constructed using 12-week-old wild-type male C57/BL6 mice. Three days post-fracture, ipsilateral L4-L6 DRGs (associated with the fractured femur) were collected for IF staining, with the L4-L6 DRGs from the intact contralateral side serving as controls (Fig. 1C). We found that the expression levels of Talin1 in ipsilateral DRG neurons were significantly lower than those in the contralateral DRG in neurons of all sizes (Fig. 1D and E). Moreover, compared with the intact contralateral DRG, the ipsilateral DRG after bone fracture showed a significantly increased proportion of p-Creb positive neurons (Fig. 1F and G), while the expression level of total Creb in DRG neurons remained unaltered (Supplementary Fig. 1C and D). These results indicate that DRG neurons were activated following bone fracture. In addition, the density of Iba1 positive cells (macrophages) in the ipsilateral DRG was also higher than that in the contralateral DRG (Fig. 1H and I), which is consistent with a previous study [45] and may contribute to both the initiation and persistence of bone fracture pain [46].

Talin1 loss activates DRG neurons and increases mechanical but not thermal pain sensitivity in mice

In all the following experiments, we used Talin1fl/fl mice as the control group (Con). To delete Talin1 expression in DRG neurons, we administered TAM intraperitoneally at a dose of 100 mg/kg body weight once daily for 5 consecutive days when the mice were 7 weeks old (Fig. 2A). Three months after TAM injection, we first performed IF staining on DRG neurons to examine the efficiency of Talin1 deletion. IF data showed that the expression level of Talin1 in the cKO DRG neurons was significantly lower than that in the control group (Fig. 2B and C). We next measured baseline mechanical pain sensitivity with von Frey filaments one week before TAM injection and at serial time points thereafter. The results showed that the withdrawal threshold of the cKO group was significantly lower than that of the Con group in both female and male mice two weeks after TAM injection. This hypersensitivity to mechanical pain in cKO animals was consistently observed at 4, 8, and 12 weeks after TAM injection. Importantly, the withdrawal threshold of cKO group reduced progressively from week 2 to week 8 after TAM injection and plateaued at weeks 8-12, whereas that of the control group remained unchanged (Fig. 2E and F). These results indicate that Talin1 loss in DRG neurons causes a pronounced mechanical allodynia phenotype in mice.

To evaluate whether this mechanical allodynia in cKO mice is linked to neuronal activation, we conducted IF staining on DRG tissues. Results revealed that the proportions of p-Erk1/2 and p-Creb positive neurons were markedly elevated in cKO neurons compared to those in controls (Fig. 2G–J). In contrast, the expression levels of total Erk1/2 and Creb proteins were similar between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 2E–H). These results suggest that Talin1 deletion specifically enhances the phosphorylation of Erk1/2 and Creb, indicative of DRG neuronal activation. To further verify the effect of Talin1 deletion on neuronal activation, we isolated primary DRG neurons from Con and cKO mice for Western blot analysis. The results showed that under normal culture conditions, Talin1 deletion did not significantly alter the expression levels of p-Erk1/2 and p-Creb. However, following 30 min of capsaicin stimulation, the levels of p-Erk1/2 and p-Creb were significantly elevated in the cKO group (Fig. 2K–N).

At 12 weeks post-TAM injection, we assessed the effect of Talin1 deletion on thermal pain sensitivity in mice using the hot plate and tail flick tests. The results revealed that, regardless of sex, there was no significant difference in the latency to thermal pain response between the cKO and Con groups under different noxious temperature stimuli (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D), indicating that Talin1 knockout does not affect the thermal pain sensitivity of mice.

Talin1 deficiency causes structural and functional disruption in mitochondria of DRG neurons

To explore potential mechanisms underlying Talin1 deletion-induced neuronal activation, transcriptome profiling of DRGs from cKO and control mice was performed. The DRG transcriptomes were then subjected to KEGG and GSEA analyses. The results unveiled a marked perturbation of the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) pathway (Fig. 3A) and a pronounced enrichment of respiratory-chain electron-transport genes (Fig. 3B), suggesting that Talin1 loss impairs mitochondrial function. Talin1 deficiency triggers PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagosome formation while impairing autophagic flux in DRG neurons

Mitophagy is essential for mitochondrial quality control. Given that Talin1 loss profoundly disrupts mitochondrial architecture and function, we next asked whether mitophagy was impacted by Talin1 deficiency. PINK1/Parkin signaling has been reported to have important regulatory functions in mitophagy [47,48]. Results from IF staining of DRG sections revealed robust increases in both PINK1 and Parkin signal intensities in Talin1-deficient neurons compared to controls (Fig. 4A–D). After confirming the elevated PINK1 and Parkin expression in cKO DRG neurons, we co-stained primary sensory neurons for Tom20-labelled mitochondria together with PINK1, Parkin and LC3B to assess mitophagosome formation. Pearson's correlation coefficients for PINK1, Parkin and LC3B versus Tom20 were all significantly higher in Talin1-deficient neurons than in controls (Fig. 4E–I, K). Collectively, these data demonstrate that Talin1 deletion initiates potent PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagosome formation. Pearson's correlation coefficient for LAMP1 versus Tom20 was significantly lower in Talin1-knockout neurons, indicating impaired delivery of mitophagosomes to lysosomes (Fig. 4J–L). In order to further determine the effect of Talin1 knockout on the autophagic flux, we isolated primary DRG neurons and treated them with the lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine (CQ), followed by Western blot analysis of LC3B and p62. We found that under basal culture conditions, Talin1 knockout significantly increased the protein level of LC3B-II. Upon CQ treatment, LC3B-II levels were markedly elevated overall, but no significant difference was observed between the Con and cKO groups. In contrast, p62 levels remained comparable between the two groups under both basal and CQ-treated conditions (Fig. 4M–P). These results indicate that Talin1 deletion impairs autophagic flux by inhibiting autophagosome degradation.

Taken together, our findings reveal that Talin1 deficiency triggers PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagosome formation while impairing autophagic flux in DRG neurons.

Nonetheless, the majority of these studies have employed developmental mouse models. To elucidate the regulatory function of sensory nerves on bone homeostasis in adult mice, we induced Talin1 deletion in mouse sensory neurons via intraperitoneal injection of TAM at 2 months of age, and then evaluated their bone mass at 8 and 15 months of age (Fig. 5A). Talin1 loss up-regulates CGRP expression in DRG neurons to facilitate fracture healing

To elucidate how Talin1 deletion increases bone mass and accelerates fracture healing, we performed differential expression analysis on DRG transcriptomes from Con and cKO mice. This revealed Talin1 down-regulation and a concurrent significant up-regulation of Calca (the gene encoding CGRP) in the cKO group (Fig. 6K). RT-qPCR analysis and IF staining of DRGs from both groups further demonstrated significantly elevated CGRP expression levels in the cKO group (Fig. 6L–N). Tibial sections from 8-month-old mice also showed markedly higher CGRP immunofluorescence in cKO mice than in controls (Fig. 6O and P). Several studies have reported the role of CGRP in promoting bone formation and fracture healing [[15], [16], [17],50]. Based on the above findings, we hypothesize that Talin1 loss accelerates fracture healing by enhancing CGRP expression. To verify our hypothesis, we developed a hydrogel for sustained release of BIBN, an inhibitor of the CGRP receptor. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that BIBN loading transformed the dense blank hydrogel into a highly porous scaffold: numerous irregular cavities and fissures appeared, pore interconnectivity increased, and the matrix became markedly looser (Fig. 6Q). Drug sustained-release experiments demonstrated that over 80% of BIBN was released within 28 days (Fig. 6R). 11-week-old control and cKO mice received TAM, then underwent a standard femur fracture; blank or BIBN-loaded hydrogel was placed at the fracture site before layered wound closure. The fractured femora were harvested 4 weeks later for analysis (Fig. 6S). μCT and SO&FG staining showed that the BIBN-loaded hydrogel significantly inhibited the accelerated fracture healing caused by Talin1 loss (Fig. 6T–W). Collectively, these data demonstrate that Talin1 deletion accelerates fracture healing via up-regulation of CGRP.

Discussion

Adhesion proteins are key regulators of tissue and organ development and homeostasis, yet they have been relatively understudied in the nervous system. In this study, we demonstrate that Talin1 is predominantly expressed in C-fiber DRG neurons. Following bone fracture, there is a marked decrease in Talin1 expression in DRG neurons, accompanied by significant neuronal activation and macrophage infiltration. Talin1 deletion activates sensory neurons, leading to mechanical hyperalgesia and promoting CGRP expression. We further observed that Talin1 deficiency in sensory neurons not only promotes bone remodeling and increases bone mass but also accelerates bone fracture healing via a CGRP-dependent mechanism. Therefore, we propose that following bone fracture, sensory neurons not only transmit pain signals to the central nervous system to elicit protective behaviors in the body, but also directly participate in and promote fracture healing.

In this study, we show that Talin1 loss induces a specific increase in mechanical pain hypersensitivity in mice, while thermal pain sensitivity remains unaltered. This outcome may be attributed to two primary factors. On one hand, the deletion of Talin1 may not affect the expression levels of thermal pain receptors; on the other hand, in sensory neurons, Talin2 protein (the other key member of the Talin family) may compensate for the loss of Talin1 protein. In fact, functional compensation between these two proteins has also been demonstrated in cardiomyocytes [26,51].

Mitochondrial dysfunction plays a crucial role in the development and maintenance of neuropathic pain by promoting oxidative stress, energy depletion, and neuronal excitability [52,53]. In this study, we found that Talin1 deficiency suppressed mitochondrial autophagy, resulting in mitochondrial structural and functional impairment in sensory neurons along with elevated ROS levels. This dysregulation may represent a potential mechanism contributing to the heightened pain sensitivity observed in Talin1- mice. However, we acknowledge that the causal relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal activation remains to be established. Specifically, functional rescue experiments, such as treatment with mitochondrial antioxidants (e.g., MitoQ) or mitophagy inducers, would be required to determine whether normalizing ROS levels or restoring mitophagy can rescue elevated p-Creb/p-Erk signaling and reduce CGRP expression in Talin1-deficient DRG neurons. Thus, future studies incorporating these pharmacological or genetic rescue strategies will be essential to validate this mechanistic model and to assess the therapeutic potential of targeting mitochondrial quality control in Talin1-related pain hypersensitivity.

Several studies have highlighted the involvement of adhesion molecules in mitochondrial regulation. For instance, Kindlin-2 is known to promote mitochondrial fusion, and Pinch1 suppresses fission [54,55]. Here, we report for the first time that Talin1 loss results in a notable increase in mitochondrial area, accompanied by the formation of a more elaborate and interconnected network-like architecture, which suggests that Talin1 may inhibit mitochondrial fusion or promote mitochondrial fission. These findings suggest that focal adhesion signaling regulates mitochondria with high specificity and complexity, possibly through coordinated actions of distinct adhesion molecules to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis and support cellular energy requirements.

Previous studies have demonstrated the regulatory roles of sensory nerves in bone, but the underlying mechanisms through which sensory neurons govern bone homeostasis remain poorly defined. Whether prolonged and sustained activation of sensory neurons is beneficial for maintaining bone mass, and whether such activation is advantageous for maintaining bone homeostasis in aged mice or under pathological conditions, are questions that require further investigation. In this study, we found that activation of sensory neurons induced by Talin1 loss in adulthood significantly enhances the activity of osteoblasts and osteoclasts in mice, accompanied by a marked increase in trabecular bone mass.

We found that Talin1 knockout induced mitochondrial dysfunction and sensory neuron activation, yet paradoxically promoted bone homeostasis and fracture healing in mice. This observation suggests a correlation between neuronal impairment and enhanced skeletal repair, which appears to contradict the prevailing view that intact sensory innervation is required for optimal bone homeostasis. Our data show that in young adult mice (8 months of age) and in fracture models, Talin1 deficiency increased bone mass and accelerated healing, likely mediated by Talin1 loss-driven neuronal upregulation of CGRP. However, with prolonged Talin1 deletion and advancing age, a trend toward reduced cortical bone thickness was observed at 15 months of age (although not statistically significant). This late-onset trend may result from excitotoxicity caused by mitochondrial dysfunction and sustained neuronal activation, potentially leading to gradual neuronal loss and decreased sensory innervation in cortical bone over time. Therefore, the seemingly paradoxical phenotypes—enhanced repair in the short term and a trend toward bone loss in the long term—may both stem from the same underlying pathophysiology: neuronal dysregulation and injury triggered by Talin1 deletion.

Our research findings also indicate that Talin1 loss in sensory neurons enhances fracture healing by increasing CGRP expression. Published reports demonstrate that CGRP enhances bone mass and promotes fracture healing through three mechanisms: First, CGRP promotes osteogenic differentiation by acting on receptors on the surface of periosteum-derived stem cells, thereby facilitating fracture healing [15]; Second, CGRP modulates bone mass by promoting the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells while inhibiting their adipogenic differentiation [16]; Third, CGRP promotes fracture healing by enhancing the bone formation capacity of osteoblasts, inhibiting the bone resorption ability of osteoclasts, promoting angiogenesis at the fracture site, and regulating the immune microenvironment [17]. Our data indicate that Talin1 loss in sensory neurons not only enhances osteoblast activity but also osteoclast activity. Therefore, while CGRP may mediate the elevated osteoblast activity observed in the cKO group, the underlying mechanisms related to the increased activity of osteoclasts in the Talin1 cKO group require further investigation. Furthermore, although the BIBN rescue experiment establishes the functional requirement of CGRP signaling, pharmacological blockade cannot distinguish CGRP derived from DRG neurons versus other sources; genetic validation in DRG neurons will be essential to definitively establish the cellular origin.

In conclusion, we establish the physiological role of the adhesion molecule Talin1 in sensory neurons and its crucial functions in maintaining adult bone homeostasis and fracture healing. Our results underscore the essential role of Talin1 in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis in sensory neurons. Disruption of this homeostasis is closely coupled with neuronal activation and heightened pain sensitivity. In addition, the positive correlation we observed between heightened pain sensitivity and accelerated fracture healing raises the possibility that nociceptive signaling may contribute to the bone repair process.

Collectively, our findings position sensory neuronal Talin1 as a pivotal integrator of nociception and skeletal repair. This reveals a unique therapeutic paradigm in which the Talin1 signaling pathway could be differentially modulated—potentially through the development of context-specific agonists or inhibitors—to achieve clinically desirable outcomes: either suppressing chronic pain or promoting bone regeneration.
