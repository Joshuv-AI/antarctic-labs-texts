DESY 24-160 QCD Lambda Parameter from Gradient Flow
Gerrit Schierholz

Abstract
Starting from the running coupling in the gradient flow scheme, the QCD lambda parameter Λ MS ¯ \Lambda_{\,\overline{\!\rm MS\!}\;} is determined analytically with respect to the reference scale w 0 w_{0} in the pure gauge theory. A key element is the gluon condensate, which, being invariant under RG transformations, establishes a formal relationship between Λ MS ¯ \Lambda_{\,\overline{\!\rm MS\!}\;} and w 0 w_{0} . Good agreement with numerical estimates is found.



1
Introduction

The parameter
\Lambda
is one of the fundamental quantities of QCD. It sets the scale for confinement and the running coupling constant
\alpha(\mu)
. In the pure gauge theory
\Lambda
is the only mass parameter. It is also the only free parameter that determines the entire spectrum from the ultraviolet to the infrared. A lattice calculation of
\Lambda
requires the selection of a reference scale that can be computed accurately. Preferred scales are the gradient flow scales
w_{0}
and
\sqrt{t_{0}}
. The lambda parameter is renormalization scheme dependent. It is common practice to express
\Lambda
in the
\,\overline{\!\rm MS\!}\;
scheme, which requires precise knowledge of the appropriate conversion factor.

The gradient flow is the perfect setting for this task. It is a multi-scale problem involving the passage from short-distance scales to the strongly coupled regime at long distances marked by
\Lambda
. The framework for dealing with physical problems involving different energy scales is the renormalization group flow. Exact renormalization group transformations are very difficult to implement numerically. The gradient flow
[
1
,
2
]
provides a powerful alternative for scale setting, with no need for costly ensemble matching. It can be regarded as a particular, infinitesimal realization of the coarse-graining step of momentum space renormalization group transformations
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
à la Wilson
[
7
]
, Polchinski
[
8
]
and Wetterich
[
9
]
, which leaves the long-distance physics unchanged. A particular example of an operator that is invariant under the gradient flow is the gluon condensate, which analytically connects
\Lambda
with the root of the flow time,
\sqrt{t}
.

In this Letter I will compute the lambda parameter in relation to
w_{0}
. The calculation is restricted to the pure SU(3) gauge theory so far. At the end of the Letter I will comment on the prospect of extending the computation to QCD with dynamical quarks.

2
Gradient flow and running coupling

The gradient flow
[
1
,
2
]
evolves the gauge field along the gradient of the action. The flow of gauge fields is defined by

\partial_{\,t}\,B_{\mu}(t,x)=D_{\nu}G_{\mu\nu}(t,x)\,,\quad G_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\,B_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\,B_{\mu}+[B_{\mu},B_{\nu}]\,,

(1)

where
D_{\mu}
is the covariant derivative and
B_{\mu}(t=0,x)=A_{\mu}(x)
is the initial gauge field generated by the action

S_{G}=\frac{1}{2g_{0}^{2}}\int d^{4}x\,\textrm{Tr}\,F_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\,,

(2)

where
g_{0}
is the bare coupling constant, which is kept fixed. The renormalization scale is set to
\mu=1/\sqrt{8t}
, where
\sqrt{8t}
is the smoothing range over which
B_{\mu}
is averaged.
The expectation value of the energy density

E=\frac{1}{4}\,G_{\mu\nu}^{a}\,G_{\mu\nu}^{a}

(3)

with
G_{\mu\nu}=G_{\mu\nu}^{a}\,\tau^{a}/2
defines a renormalized coupling in the gradient flow scheme
[
2
,
10
]
,

\alpha_{GF}(\mu)=\frac{4\pi}{3}\,t^{2}\langle E(t)\rangle\,,

(4)

which analytically continues the beta function

\frac{\partial\,\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}{\partial\ln\mu}=-2\,\frac{\partial\,\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}{\partial\ln t}=\beta_{GF}(\alpha_{GF})

(5)

into the nonperturbative regime. Integrating (
5
) gives the familiar result

\frac{\Lambda_{GF}}{\mu}=\left[4\pi b_{0}\alpha_{GF}(\mu)\right]^{-\frac{b_{1}}{2\,b_{0}^{2}}}\,\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{8\pi b_{0}\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}-\int_{0}^{\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}\!\!d\alpha\,\left[\frac{1}{\beta_{GF}(\alpha)}+\frac{1}{8\pi b_{0}\alpha^{2}}-\frac{b_{1}}{2b_{0}^{2}\alpha}\right]\right\}\,.

(6)

Formally,
\Lambda_{GF}
is a constant of integration. Actually, it sets the scale for the mass gap and the like. Equation (
6
) simplifies to

\frac{\Lambda_{GF}}{\mu}=\exp\left\{-\int^{\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}_{\alpha_{GF}(\Lambda_{GF})}\,\frac{d\alpha}{\beta_{GF}(\alpha)}\right\}\,,

(7)

using

\frac{b_{1}}{2b_{0}^{2}}\log{\left[4\pi b_{0}\alpha_{GF}(\Lambda_{GF})\right]}+\frac{1}{8\pi b_{0}\alpha_{GF}(\Lambda_{GF})}+\int_{0}^{\alpha_{GF}(\Lambda_{GF})}\!\!d\alpha\,\left[\frac{1}{\beta_{GF}(\alpha)}+\frac{1}{8\pi b_{0}\alpha^{2}}-\frac{b_{1}}{2b_{0}^{2}\alpha}\right]=0\,.

(8)

It should be noted that
d\alpha/\beta(\alpha)
is invariant under scheme transformations
GF\rightarrow S
. It follows that
\alpha_{GF}(\Lambda_{GF})=\alpha_{S}(\Lambda_{S})
. Under a change of renormalization scheme we then have

\log{\frac{\Lambda_{S}}{\Lambda_{GF}}}=-\int_{\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}^{\alpha_{S}(\mu)}\,\frac{d\alpha}{\beta(\alpha)}\,.

(9)

This must be true for all values of
\mu
. Taking
\mu
to infinity, we obtain the one-loop result

\Lambda_{S}=\exp\left\{\frac{t_{1}}{2b_{0}}\right\}\Lambda_{GF}\,,\quad\frac{1}{\alpha_{S}(\mu)}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}-4\pi\,t_{1}\,.

(10)

The conversion (
10
) is known for a variety of schemes
S
. For the
\,\overline{\!\rm MS\!}\;
scheme the result is
[
2
]

\frac{\Lambda_{\,\overline{\!\rm MS\!}\;}}{\Lambda_{GF}}=0.534\,.

(11)

3
Gluon condensate

Operators consisting of polynomials of
B_{\mu}(t,x)
, and its derivatives, are renormalized and finite for
t>0
, subject to
t
is large enough
[
11
]
. The running coupling and the energy density combine to form the gluon condensate, which in the gradient flow scheme at finite flow time is defined by

G=\frac{\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}{\pi}\,\langle G_{\mu\nu}^{a}G_{\mu\nu}^{a}\rangle\,.

(12)

The gluon condensate
G
is scheme dependent, which is reflected in the fact that it is not directly related to any observable. Using the small flow-time expansion,
G
lends itself to the definition of the renormalized gluon condensate at
t=0

[
12
]
. This is then converted to the familiar condensate in the
\,\overline{\!\rm MS\!}\;
scheme
[
13
]
.
Most importantly,
G
is invariant under changes of the scale parameter
\mu
. This has been demonstrated both perturbatively
[
14
,
15
]
(the two-loop result
[
15
]
is scale invariant, but not renormalization group invariant) and nonperturbatively
[
13
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. I provide a proof in
[
19
]
that is derived directly from the gradient flow. In the gradient flow scheme this reads

t\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\,t}G=0\,.

(13)

At flow time zero
G
can be connected to the vacuum expectation value of the trace of the energy momentum tensor
\Theta_{\mu\nu}
, which sometimes is taken for the gluon condensate. The energy momentum tensor is obtained by taking the functional derivative of the action with respect to the metric tensor
g_{\mu\nu}
. However, no closed expression is known for finite flow time beyond perturbation theory
[
20
]
.

In actual lattice calculations
G
is found to suffer from ultraviolet divergences due to mixing with the unit operator. In an unprecedented calculation the ultraviolet divergent contributions have been computed to
20th

[
21
]
and
32nd
order
[
22
]
in numerical stochastic perturbation theory. Under the gradient flow the perturbative contributions (although finite) will gradually be eliminated as the flow time
t
is increased, leaving behind the truly nonperturbative part. In the following we exclude the region near flow time zero. How large
t
has to be for the perturbative contributions to have vanished needs to be seen.
Writing

G=\frac{\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}{\pi}\,4\,\langle E(t)\rangle\,,

(14)

it follows that

t\frac{\partial}{\partial\,t}\,\alpha_{GF}(\mu)\,\langle E(t)\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\,\beta_{GF}\,\langle E(t)\rangle+\alpha_{GF}(\mu)\;t\frac{\partial}{\partial\,t}\,\langle E(t)\rangle=0\,.

(15)

Replacing
\langle E(t)\rangle
with
3\alpha_{GF}(\mu)/(4\pi t^{2})
in the right-hand expression, this leads to the beta function

\beta_{GF}=-2\,\alpha_{GF}

(16)

for corresponding values of
t
. The solution of (
16
) has the form

\alpha_{GF}(\mu)=8\,\Lambda^{2}t=\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\,.

(17)

To capitalize on this, we will need to know the scale factor
\Lambda^{2}=\alpha_{GF}(\mu)\,\mu^{2}
.

at

, corresponding to a lattice spacing
a=0.082\,\textrm{fm}
, and the clover definition of
G_{\mu\nu}
. To test for finite volume effects, the calculations were done on three lattice volumes,
16^{4}
,
24^{4}
and

, with
5,000
configurations each. Finite volume effects are expected to be small for
\sqrt{8t}\ll L
, where
L
is the linear extent of the lattice. On the larger lattices the data are compatible with a linear rise in
t
, as expected. On the smaller lattice finite volume corrections become clearly visible at
\sqrt{8t}\gtrsim L

[

to be sensitive to
O(a^{2})

\langle E\rangle
splits into discrete values depending on the topological charge
Q
. Asymptotically
\langle E\rangle\propto|Q|

, so that the higher charges become increasingly important. This calls for a precise knowledge of
\langle E\rangle
at large

, which requires ultra-high statistics and sufficiently smooth gauge fields to begin with.

4
Scale setting and
\Lambda
parameter

While for large values of
\alpha_{GF}
the beta function takes the form
\beta_{GF}=-2\alpha_{GF}\equiv\beta_{GF}^{\,>}
, for small values it can be approximated by the two-loop expression
\beta_{GF}=-4\pi b_{0}\alpha_{GF}^{2}-32\pi^{2}b_{1}\alpha_{GF}^{3}\equiv\beta_{GF}^{\,<}
. This is sketched in Fig.
2
. A smooth interpolation that connects the two beta functions is given, for example, by

\frac{1}{\beta_{GF}}=-\left[\,\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{GF}^{\,<}}\right)^{2n}+\left(\frac{1}{\beta_{GF}^{\,>}}\right)^{2n}\,\right]^{\,\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\scriptstyle 1/2n$}}\,,

(19)

which is represented by the connecting line. At
\alpha_{GF}=0
the integral (
6
) over the two-loop beta function vanishes,

\int_{0}d\alpha\,\left[\frac{1}{\beta_{GF}(\alpha)}+\frac{1}{8\pi b_{0}\alpha^{2}}-\frac{b_{1}}{2b_{0}^{2}\alpha}\right]=0\,,

(20)

so that

\log{\frac{\Lambda_{GF}}{\mu}}=-\frac{b_{1}}{2b_{0}^{2}}\log{\left[4\pi b_{0}\alpha_{GF}(\mu)\right]}-\frac{1}{8\pi b_{0}\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}-\int^{\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}\!\!\!d\alpha\,\left[\frac{1}{\beta_{GF}(\alpha)}+\frac{1}{8\pi b_{0}\alpha^{2}}-\frac{b_{1}}{2b_{0}^{2}\alpha}\right]\,.

(21)

For larger values of
\alpha_{GF}
this must match the ‘asymptotic’ result

-\int^{\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}\!\!d\alpha\,\frac{1}{\beta_{GF}(\alpha)}=\frac{1}{2}\log{\alpha_{GF}(\mu)}\,.

(22)

A suitable matching point is
\mu=\Lambda_{GF}
, for example. (See Fig.
3
.) From this follows that
\Lambda=\Lambda_{GF}
and

\alpha_{GF}(\mu)=\frac{\Lambda_{GF}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\,.

(23)

This offers the possibility of determining
\Lambda_{GF}
directly from the gradient flow, which is the key observation of this note.

It is practice to express dimensionful results from the lattice in units of a common scale parameter. Two such parameters are derived from the gradient flow,
\sqrt{t_{0}}
and
w_{0}
, which can be determined precisely with moderate effort. Setting

F(t)=t^{2}\,\langle E(t)\rangle\,,

(24)

the
\sqrt{t_{0}}
scale is defined by

F(t)\,|_{t=t_{0}(c)}=c\,.

(25)

Alternatively, the
w_{0}
scale is defined by

t\frac{\partial}{\partial t}F(t)\,|_{t=w_{0}^{2}(c)}=c\,.

(26)

In both definitions
c
is a constant, conventionally taken as
c=0.3
. We require a value of
c
such that
a\ll\sqrt{t_{0}}
to evade
O(a^{2})
effects and issues of renormalization
[
11
]
, and
\sqrt{8t_{0}}\ll L
to exclude finite volume corrections.

For flow times in the linear range we can write

F(t)=\Delta_{0}+\Delta\,t\,,

(27)

where
\Delta_{0}
is an unknown constant that can be either negative as in our case or positive
[
26
]
. We then have

\begin{split}\Delta_{0}+\Delta\,t_{0}(c)&=c\,,\\
\Delta w_{0}^{2}(c)&=c\,.\end{split}

(28)

As a result

t_{0}(c)=w_{0}^{2}(c)-\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\Delta}\,.

(29)

While
w_{0}^{2}(c)/c
is independent of
c
in the linear regime, and a true physical scale (proportional to
1/\Lambda^{2}
),
t_{0}(c)/c
is generally not. Only for large values of
c
can we expect that
t_{0}(c)\simeq w_{0}^{2}(c)
.

In Fig.
3
I show a detailed view of Fig.
1
for smaller values of
t/a^{2}
. At this lattice spacing,
a=0.082\,\textrm{fm}
, the nonperturbative linear regime begins approximately at
F(t)=0.3
, rendering the value
c=0.3
just acceptable. It shows that
c=0.4
or larger would be better in this case. The situation improves for smaller lattice spacings, with
c=0.3
moving into the linear regime
[
26
]
. We suggest to fit the (approximately?) linear part of
F(t)
by a straight line as shown in Fig.
3
. This gives

\frac{\partial F(t)}{\partial t}=\frac{c}{w_{0}^{2}(c)}\,,

(30)

which eliminates the need for
c
. In contrast,
c/t_{0}(c)=\partial F(t)/\partial t+\Delta_{0}/t_{0}(c)
becomes independent of
c
only for
t_{0}(c)\gg|\Delta_{0}|
.

5
Conclusions

The determination of the QCD lambda parameter has a long history. Unlike previous calculations, our work is based on the infrared behavior of the running coupling, which is most sensitive to lambda and provides a direct link to the nonperturbative quantities of the theory, such as the string tension
[
23
]
. The crucial point here is that the running coupling is a linearly rising function of flow time
t
for
t\gg a^{2}
, which is a direct consequence of the gluon condensate being scale invariant and is supported by numerical simulations. The most important result is that, to determine the lambda parameter, we only require knowledge of the scale setting parameter
w_{0}
. We have demonstrated how
w_{0}
should be calculated correctly in practice.

All this is under the premise that the gradient flow is a realization of momentum space renormalization group transformations. Significant work has been done to establish the connection between the gradient flow and renormalization group transformations
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

As a byproduct we obtain the (renormalized) gluon condensate
G
. The result is
G=19.45\,\Lambda_{\,\overline{\!\rm MS\!}\;}^{4}
. This is broadly consistent with recent results from the plaquette, where the perturbative contribution has been subtracted to an unprecedented order
[
21
,
22
]
. A formal derivation will be given elsewhere. The linear increase of the running coupling beyond bounds is sometimes referred to as infrared slavery, the flip side of asymptotic freedom. Our result that confinement simply follows from a nonvanishing gluon condensate is a valuable addition to the modelling of the QCD vacuum.

While the result for the pure gauge theory is interesting on its own, the final goal is to extend the calculations to multiple quark flavors. One way to achieve this is by using the ‘nonperturbative decoupling method’
[
30
]
, starting from the now exact, zero-flavor result (
34
). A direct calculation of
\Lambda_{\,\overline{\!\rm MS\!}\;}
(say) for three light flavors should also be possible. In this case the task is to align
\Lambda_{\,\overline{\!\rm MS\!}\;}
with the gradient flow scale
w_{0}
in the corresponding limit.
