Assessment of antibiotic prophylaxis post cardiac implantable electronic device implantation: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sarah Aljohani, Lama Alfehaid, Meshary Almeshary, Hind Almodaimegh, Majed Almutairi, Sultan Al Raddadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) manage arrhythmias and prevent sudden cardiac death, but related infections are rising. Pre-implantation antibiotics lower infection rates, while postoperative use is debated.PurposeTo evaluate current postoperative prophylactic antibiotic practices and CIED infection rates.MethodA retrospective cohort study at King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, included adults > 18 who underwent CIED implantation from 2017 to 2022. The primary outcome measured infection rates post-antibiotic prophylaxis. Data were analyzed using Stata 18.5.ResultsThe study included 200 patients, predominantly male (62%), with a median age of 64 years. Common comorbidities included hypertension (71.5%), diabetes (65.5%), and heart failure (64%). All patients received pre-operative antibiotics, primarily cefazolin (88.4%) and vancomycin (6.5%). Most patients (95%) received postoperative antibiotics, most commonly amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (83.6%), while 14% received combination therapy. Among patients who received postoperative antibiotics (n = 190), 1 definite infection (0.5%) and 3 possible infections (1.5%) were observed, with no infections identified among those who did not receive postoperative antibiotics (n = 10). Regarding duration, possible infections occurred in 1.0% of patients receiving antibiotics for more than 7 days and 2.3% of those receiving 7 days or less. In the regimen analysis, definite infection occurred in 0.6% of patients receiving single-agent therapy and none in those receiving combination therapy. Given the low number of events, these findings are descriptive and should be interpreted as exploratory.ConclusionProphylactic antibiotics for 7 days or less, or a single agent, was not associated with a detectable difference in infection rates. A larger prospective study including patients not receiving prophylactic antibiotics post-implantation is needed to distinguish infection rates between those who did and did not receive them.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13516-y.Keywords: Cardiac implantable electronic devices, Pocket infection, Antibiotics, Pacemaker, Arrhythmias, Postoperative

Introduction

Cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) are essential for the management of arrhythmias and the prevention of sudden cardiac death; however, CIED-related infections remain an important and growing clinical concern. The American Heart Association (AHA) has established a clinical definition of cardiovascular implantable electronic device infection (CIEDI) and defined specific criteria for pocket infection as well as lead or valvular infection to facilitate diagnosis and classify cases as definite, possible, or rejected infection [1].

The administration of pre-implantation prophylactic antibiotics has been shown to effectively reduce infection rates [2]. However, the role of postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis remains controversial. In 2019, two studies evaluating whether postoperative antibiotics reduce the rate of CIED-related infections were published [3, 4]. The first was a single-center retrospective cohort study comparing patients who received only preoperative prophylactic antibiotics with those who received both preoperative and postoperative antibiotics for three to five days, primarily cephalexin [3]. The study found no statistically significant difference in infection incidence between the two groups and suggested that postoperative antibiotics may be considered in selected patients based on individual risk factors, including comorbidities, age, and procedural characteristics [3].

The second study was a randomized, prospective, single-blinded controlled trial [4]. Patients were randomized into three groups: no antibiotics, one day of intravenous (IV) antibiotics, or one day of IV antibiotics followed by seven days of oral antibiotics. No significant difference in infection rates was observed among the groups at either six-month or two-year follow-up [4]. Given that the majority of CIED-related infections, particularly pocket infections, occur within the first few months after implantation, a six-month surveillance window is considered clinically appropriate for capturing infections most plausibly related to perioperative and postoperative antibiotic strategies.

According to the 2023 AHA Scientific Statement, preoperative cefazolin is recommended as standard practice [1]. Vancomycin may be used as an alternative in patients with penicillin or β-lactam allergy, a history of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infection, or in centers with a high prevalence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus [1]. The AHA also states that routine postoperative antibiotic use is not supported by current published data [1]. The PADIT (Prevention of Arrhythmia Device Infection Trial), the largest randomized controlled trial evaluating intensified perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis in CIED implantation, compared a conventional single-dose cefazolin regimen with an incremental strategy that included additional intraoperative and postoperative antibiotics. The trial demonstrated no significant reduction in device-related infections with the intensified approach, further questioning the incremental benefit of extended or broadened antibiotic strategies [5].

In routine clinical practice, variation in postoperative antibiotic regimens and duration may reflect clinician perception of patient risk, procedural complexity, or comorbid conditions rather than a proven treatment effect. Differences in antibiotic prescribing may therefore represent confounding by indication rather than true therapeutic superiority. Despite limited supporting evidence, postoperative antibiotics are routinely prescribed at our institution in varying regimens and durations based on clinical judgment rather than standardized protocol.

Accordingly, this study was designed to evaluate real-world practice variation and to assess whether differences in postoperative antibiotic duration or combination therapy were associated with differences in CIED-related infection rates. Rather than testing a predefined hypothesis of therapeutic benefit, our objective was to examine whether variability in prescribing practices translated into measurable differences in infection outcomes.

Method

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study conducted at King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC) in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The study included adult patients (aged > 18 years) who underwent CIED placement procedures, including Automated Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillator (AICD), Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy with defibrillator (CRTD), and Permanent Pacemaker (PPM), between 2017 and 2022. Ethical approval was obtained from King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC) (IRB approval number: IRB/2889/23). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data.

Patients were excluded if they did not receive preoperative prophylactic antibiotics, were lost to follow-up within six months, died, had the device removed, or received concomitant antibiotics for other clinical indications. A total of 200 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. All patients were followed for six months after the procedure. While the majority of infections appear within three months, we extend the duration to catch late infections that might be missed.

The primary outcome was the overall incidence of CIED-related infection among patients who received postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis. Secondary analysis were considered exploratory and included: (1) the difference in CIED-related infection rates between patients who received postoperative prophylactic antibiotics for more than 7 days versus 7 days or less, and (2) the difference in infection rates between patients who received multiple postoperative antibiotic agents versus a single agent.

Postoperative prophylactic antibiotic use was defined as the continuation of prophylactic antibiotics for more than 48 h after the procedure, with an active prescription starting on the day of implantation, documentation of recommendation by the primary consultant, and no other documented indication for antibiotic therapy. The antibiotic agents identified included Augmentin, Cefazolin, Ciprofloxacin, Clindamycin, Meropenem, Metronidazole, Piperacillin/Tazobactam, Vancomycin, and Mupirocin 2% Ointment 15 g. Topical mupirocin, when prescribed, was used as adjunctive local wound care and was not considered a systemic prophylactic agent for the purposes of exposure classification.

CIED-related infection classification was adjudicated according to the AHA clinical criteria using a combination of clinical findings, imaging results, and microbiological data [1]. Microbiological findings were interpreted in a clinical context. Isolated single positive blood cultures, particularly with organisms commonly associated with contamination such as coagulase-negative staphylococci, were not considered sufficient alone to define definite CIED infection unless accompanied by supportive clinical, imaging, or additional microbiological evidence. Cases with compatible clinical findings but limited microbiological confirmation were classified as possible infection.

Collected demographic variables included age, weight, and gender. Clinical data included history of device insertion or removal, prior CIED infection, previous positive cultures, antibiotic allergies, and antibiotic exposure within the year preceding CIED implantation, in addition to comorbidities. Procedural data included indication for CIED implantation, device type, vital signs, and inflammatory markers before and after the procedure.

Investigations used to identify CIED infection included two blood cultures, two wound cultures, positron emission tomography (PET) and computed tomography (CT) scans demonstrating abnormal activity at the pocket/generator site, pocket erythema, induration, or tenderness within 3 months of implantation, evidence of device erosion through the skin, purulent drainage from the pocket, fluctuance, and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) findings consistent with CIED infection or valvular endocarditis. Follow-up was conducted through retrospective review of institutional electronic health records rather than active patient contact.

Given the retrospective design and low event rate, no a priori sample size calculation was performed, and the study was not powered to detect small or moderate differences in infection rates between antibiotic duration or regimen groups. Data analysis was performed using Stata version 18.5.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 1244 patients screened, 200 patients were included (Fig. 1). The clinical characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. The median age of participants was 62.5 years (IQR: 52–72), with a predominance of males (62.5%) compared to females (36%). The mean weight was 77.1 kg (± 17.4). A significant proportion of the cohort had a history of heart failure (64%), hypertension (72%), and diabetes mellitus (64.5%). Only 15.5% of included patients had a prior CIED device, and 2% had a history of CIED infection. The most common current CIED devices were AICD (47%), followed by PPM (32.8%) and CRTD (20.2%). The main indications for CIED implantation were ischemic cardiomyopathy (58%), complete heart block (53%), ventricular arrhythmia (15%), and dilated cardiomyopathy (9%). 34% of patients had prior antibiotic exposure within the preceding year, and 5.5% reported antibiotic allergies, most commonly to penicillin (45.5% of reported allergies).

Pre- and post-operative prophylaxis

The antibiotic regimens administered pre-and post-operatively are outlined in Table 2. All patients received preoperative antibiotic therapy, with 88.5% receiving cefazolin as the primary agent. Post-operative antibiotics were administered to 95% of patients, with the most common regimens including Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (83.7%). Combination post-operative antibiotic therapy was received by 14% of included patients, with Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid plus ciprofloxacin being the most prescribed regimen. More than half the patients (52%) continued post-operative antibiotic therapy beyond 7 days.

Post-operative clinical data

Patients’ clinical data are detailed in Table 3. Most patients were afebrile, with a median temperature of 36.9 [IQR: 36.5–37.9] and an average WBC count of 9.6 [3.6–32]. Most patients had negative first (97%) and second blood cultures (99%), and most patients (99.5%) had negative first and second wound cultures. One patient (0.5%) met criteria for definite CIED infection based on PET/CT findings, with abnormal activity at the pocket/generator site. PET/CT can be useful when echocardiography is inconclusive in diagnosing CIED infections. It is particularly effective at identifying pocket infections and enhancing the diagnosis of extracardiac and systemic lead infections.

Post-operative antibiotic therapy outcomes are summarized in Tables 4, 5 and 6. In contrast, no infections were identified among the 10 patients (5%) who did not receive post-operative antibiotics. The absolute difference in definite infection between groups was 0.5%. However, the very small number of patients who did not receive post-operative antibiotics led to a marked imbalance between the comparison groups, limiting the robustness of direct comparisons.

Regarding duration of therapy, 104 patients (54.7%) received antibiotics for more than 7 days, while 86 patients (45.3%) received therapy for 7 days or less. Definite infection occurred in 0% of patients treated for more than 7 days compared to 1.2% of those treated for 7 days or less (absolute difference 1.2%). Possible infections were observed in 1.0% and 2.3% of patients in the longer- and shorter-duration groups, respectively.

In the analysis of the antibiotic regimen, 162 patients received single-agent therapy and 28 received combination therapy. Definite infection occurred in 0.6% of patients receiving single-agent therapy and 0% of those receiving combination therapy (absolute difference 0.6%). Possible infections were observed in 1.9% of patients receiving single-agent therapy and none in those receiving combination therapy.

Given the very low number of infection events across all analyses, these findings are descriptive and should be interpreted as exploratory rather than inferential.

Discussion

In this single-centre retrospective cohort study, we evaluated contemporary postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis practices following CIED implantation and their association with infection outcomes. The overall incidence of CIED-related infection was low across the cohort, regardless of the duration or combination of postoperative antibiotic regimens. Among the 190 patients who received postoperative antibiotics, only one definite infection and three possible infections were observed. Although possible infections were classified according to the AHA criteria, some cases may represent postoperative inflammatory changes or culture contamination rather than clinically confirmed device infection, particularly in the setting of isolated single positive blood cultures. Isolation of organisms such as coagulase-negative staphylococci from single culture sets may reflect contamination rather than true bacteremia and was interpreted cautiously in accordance with AHA diagnostic criteria. Therefore, definite infections likely provide a more conservative estimate of clinically meaningful outcomes.

The very low number of infection events substantially limited statistical power and increased the risk of type II error, restricting our ability to detect small or moderate differences between antibiotic strategies. Furthermore, only 10 patients did not receive postoperative antibiotics, resulting in a marked imbalance between the comparison groups. As postoperative antibiotic use was routine institutional practice during the study period, omission was not protocol-driven and was likely reflective of individual clinician judgment. Consequently, unmeasured differences in baseline risk or procedural complexity cannot be excluded, and direct comparisons between treated and untreated patients should be interpreted cautiously. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant associations between antibiotic duration or combination therapy and infection rates should not be construed as evidence of equivalence or non-inferiority. Rather, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Moreover, the small number of outcome events in subgroup comparisons precluded multivariable adjustment for clinically relevant confounders, and therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Our results align with previous studies. A single-center retrospective cohort study including 569 patients demonstrated no statistically significant difference in infection rates between patients who received postoperative cephalexin and those who did not [3]. Additionally, a large population-based study of 14,718 patients found no difference in mortality or infection rates between patients who received postoperative prophylactic antibiotics and those who did not following CIED implantation [6]. Importantly, the PADIT trial, the largest randomized controlled study in this field, showed no significant reduction in device-related infection rates with an intensified perioperative antibiotic regimen compared with conventional prophylaxis [5]. Collectively, these findings suggest that broader or prolonged antibiotic strategies may not provide additional benefit in preventing CIED infection. In contrast, an earlier retrospective review of 3,253 procedures conducted in Canada between 1991 and 2009 reported a reduction in pacemaker-related infections with four days of postoperative oral cephalexin or clindamycin following intravenous perioperative antibiotics [7]. However, that study did not evaluate variations in antibiotic selection, antimicrobial spectrum, or duration of therapy [7].

In our cohort, more than half of the patients continued postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis for more than 7 days. The most commonly prescribed agent was amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, which provides broader gram-positive, gram-negative, and anaerobic coverage compared with cephalexin, which primarily targets gram-positive cocci with limited gram-negative coverage [8]. Additionally, a subset of patients received combination systemic antibiotic therapy. While this may reflect attempts to broaden antimicrobial coverage, the heterogeneity in agent selection, antimicrobial spectrum, route of administration, combination therapy, and duration may have introduced exposure misclassification and limited the ability to detect regimen-specific effects. Further stratification by individual antibiotic regimens was not feasible given the small number of infection events.

Approximately 12% of patients received dual systemic therapy and 2% received triple systemic therapy, highlighting variability in prescribing practices. Although our study did not evaluate safety outcomes related to prolonged or combination antibiotic use, the observed practice variation underscores the importance of antimicrobial stewardship and the need for standardized institutional protocols.

Pocket irrigation with saline is considered reasonable during CIED implantation according to the AHA scientific statement [1]. At our institution, standard practice includes preoperative cefazolin administration and pocket irrigation with povidone, gentamicin, and normal saline for all CIED procedures. These measures may have contributed to the overall low infection rate observed.

Inflammatory markers were inconsistently documented and were not reliable indicators of infection in this cohort. Although one patient with a possible infection demonstrated elevated white blood cell count and a single positive blood culture, clinical diagnosis of CIED infection relies primarily on physical examination findings, imaging studies, and microbiological confirmation. Inflammatory markers such as WBC, CRP, and procalcitonin may assist in distinguishing systemic from localized pocket infection but lack specificity for definitive diagnosis [9–11].

In our cohort, the low incidence of infection also limited the ability to identify clinical predictors of CIED infection, such as heart failure, renal dysfunction, prior CIED procedures, or antibiotic exposure, which have been reported in previous studies. A notable strength of this study is the evaluation of real-world postoperative antibiotic practices across multiple regimens and durations.

However, several important limitations must be acknowledged. First, the very low number of infection events substantially reduced statistical power and increased the risk of type II error. The small number of outcome events precluded multivariable adjustment for clinically relevant confounders, and therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Second, the retrospective single-centre design introduces inherent susceptibility to confounding and limits generalizability to other institutions with differing clinical and antimicrobial stewardship practices. Third, only a small proportion of patients did not receive postoperative antibiotics, resulting in marked group imbalance and limiting the robustness of comparisons between treated and untreated patients. Additionally, the heterogeneity of postoperative systemic antibiotic exposure may have obscured meaningful differences between specific regimens. Furthermore, because follow-up was limited to institutional electronic health records, infections diagnosed or managed at external facilities may not have been captured, potentially leading to underestimation of infection incidence. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory rather than definitive.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort, extended or combination postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis was not associated with a statistically detectable reduction in CIED-related infection. However, given the low event rate and limited statistical power, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Larger, adequately powered prospective studies are needed to confirm these observations.
