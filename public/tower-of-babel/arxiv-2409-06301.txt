1. Introduction

Given a function defined by a Dirichlet series,

Before proving Theorem 1.1 in full generality, we mention how Landau’s theorem and knowledge of the mean value of the Riemann or Hurwitz zeta functions can yield Theorem 1.1(a) under (LC). This was in fact the way in which the authors of

Given a Dirichlet series with positive coefficients
f(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}n_{j}^{-s}
satisfying (1.1) and (LC), one considers an auxiliary Dirichlet series

3. Examples

In this section we will provide several examples illustrating our results.

Positivity of the coefficients.

The assumption that the coefficients
a_{j}
are positive is necessary. Consider for example
n_{j}=j
,
a_{j}=1+(-1)^{j}j^{\beta}
. Then
A(x)=x+O(x^{\beta})
, and
f(s)=\zeta(s)+(2^{1+\beta-s}-1)\zeta(s-\beta)
, so that no mean value exists for
\sigma\leq\beta+1/2
.

Abscissa of convergence of
\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}n_{j}^{-2\sigma}
.

Let
\alpha\in(-\infty,1)
, and set
n_{j}=j^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
and
a_{j}=n_{j}^{\alpha}=j^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}
.
Then

A(x)=\sum_{j\leq x^{1-\alpha}}j^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}=(1-\alpha)x+\zeta\Bigl(-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\Bigr)+O(x^{\alpha})

so (1.1) holds with
\beta=\alpha
if
\alpha>0
and
\beta=0
otherwise.
The Dirichlet series

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{2\sigma}}=\zeta\Bigl(\frac{2\sigma-2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\Bigr)

has abscissa of convergence
\sigma_{c}=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
. By adding a sparse sequence
(m_{j})_{j}
(e.g.
m_{j}=e^{j}
) with coefficients
b_{j}=m_{j}^{\beta}
, it is clear that there exists sequences satisfying

A(x)=\rho x+O(x^{\beta}),\quad A(x)=\rho x+\Omega(x^{\beta}),

and for which

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{2\sigma}}

has abscissa of convergence
\sigma_{0}
, for any fixed
\sigma_{0}\in(-\infty,\frac{1+\beta}{2}]
. Setting
n_{j}=\log j
and
a_{j}=e^{-n_{j}}=1/j
(
j\geq 2
) with
A(x)=x+\gamma-1+O(e^{-x})
, we can even have that
\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{2\sigma}}=\sum_{j\geq 2}(j(\log j)^{\sigma})^{-2}
has abscissa of convergence
\sigma_{c}=-\infty
.

Existence of the mean value beyond
\sigma=\frac{1+\beta}{2}
.

We consider the same class of examples as above. If
\alpha<0
, then
A(x)=(1-\alpha)x+O(1)
, and
f(s)=\zeta\bigl(\frac{s-\alpha}{1-\alpha}\bigr)
has a mean value if and only if
\sigma>\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
, and
\frac{1+\alpha}{2}<\frac{1}{2}
.

Another example, but with an ordinary Dirichlet series is given by

f(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{r(n)}{n^{s}}=4\zeta(s)\eta(s),

where
r(n)
counts the number of ways
n
can be written as a sum of two squares of integers, and where
\eta(s)=1-3^{-s}+5^{-s}+\cdots
. It is well known that
\sum_{n\leq x}r(n)=\pi x+O(x^{\theta})
for some
\theta\in(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2})
(indeed it is conjectured that
\theta
can be taken to be any number greater than
\frac{1}{4}
). Thus
\beta\geq\frac{1}{4}
. But it is well-known that the mean value exists for every
\sigma>\frac{1}{2}
. Here
\frac{1+\beta}{2}\geq\frac{5}{8}
. Alternatively, we could take
f(s)=\zeta(s)^{2}
with
A(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}d(n)=x\log x+(2\gamma-1)x+O(x^{\theta})
for some
\theta\in(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2})
(not necessarily the same
\theta
). Theorem 1.1+ says the mean value exists for
\sigma>\frac{1+\theta}{2}
but, again, it actually exists for every
\sigma>\frac{1}{2}
.

Sharpness of Theorem 1.1.

We can employ the same family of basic examples to show that Theorem 1.1 is sharp for every
\beta\in[0,1)
, both with respect to the condition
\sigma>\frac{1+\beta}{2}
, as with respect to the growth bound for
\beta<\sigma\leq\frac{1+\beta}{2}
.

Let
n_{j}=j^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}
and
a_{j}=n_{j}^{\beta}=j^{\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}}
. Then
f(s)=\zeta\bigl(\frac{s-\beta}{1-\beta}\bigr)
. Using the functional equation, it is straightforward to show that

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|\zeta(\sigma+it)|^{2}dt\sim k_{\sigma}T^{1-2\sigma},\quad\sigma<1/2,

for some
k_{\sigma}>0
(indeed
k_{\sigma}=\frac{\zeta(2-2\sigma)}{2-2\sigma}(2\pi)^{2\sigma-1}
) so that

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|f(\sigma+it)|^{2}dt\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\zeta(\frac{2\sigma-2\beta}{1-\beta})&\text{if }\sigma>\frac{1+\beta}{2};\\
\log T&\text{if }\sigma=\frac{1+\beta}{2};\\
c_{\sigma}T^{\frac{1+\beta-2\sigma}{1-\beta}}&\text{if }\sigma<\frac{1+\beta}{2},\end{array}\right.

for some
c_{\sigma}>0
.

An important subclass of Dirichlet series are those with
a_{j}=1
for every
j
, corresponding to the counting function of the sequence
(n_{j})_{j}
. Also in this subclass, Theorem 1.1 is sharp, as the following example shows. We will further see from this example that convergence of
\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}n_{j}^{-2\sigma}=\sum_{j}n_{j}^{-2\sigma}
is not sufficient for the existence of the mean value. It would also be interesting to investigate the sharpness of Theorem 1.1 in the class of Beurling zeta functions.

for
\sigma=1/2
), while for the second term we have

\int_{T}^{\infty}x^{-s}d\,\bigl(A(x)-A_{c}(x)\bigr)=O(T^{-\sigma})+s\int_{T}^{\infty}\frac{A(x)-A_{c}(x)}{x^{\sigma+it+1}}dx.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}|f(\sigma+it)-f_{c}(\sigma+it)|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\ll T^{2-2\sigma}(\log T)^{3}+T^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\biggl|\int_{T}^{\infty}\frac{A(x)-A_{c}(x)}{x^{\sigma+it+1}}dx\biggr|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\ll T^{2-2\sigma}(\log T)^{3}+\frac{T^{2-2\sigma}}{\sigma}+\frac{T^{1-2\sigma}}{\sigma^{2}}.

Indeed, the last term of the first line can be estimated in the same way as the estimation of
I(T)
from the proof of Proposition 2.2. The obtained estimates now imply that also

\frac{1}{\tau_{k}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{k}}|f(\sigma+it)|^{2}dt\gg_{\sigma}\tau_{k}^{\frac{1+\beta-2\sigma}{1-\beta}},\quad\text{ for }\beta<\sigma<\frac{1+\beta}{2}.

Finally we note that this example also shows that convergence of
\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}n_{j}^{-2\sigma}
is not sufficient for the existence of the mean value: here
\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}n_{j}^{-2\sigma}
converges if and only if
\sigma>\frac{1}{2}
, while the mean value exists only if
\sigma>\frac{1+\beta}{2}
.

4. Behavior on the line
\sigma=(1+\beta)/2

Theorem 1.1(b) yields the upper bound
\int_{0}^{T}|f(\frac{1+\beta}{2}+it)|^{2}dt\ll T\log T
on the “critical line”
\sigma=\frac{1+\beta}{2}
. In the case of the Riemann zeta function, we actually have the asymptotic
\int_{0}^{T}|\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)|^{2}dt\sim T\log T
. We first show, assuming some separation of the sequence
(n_{j})_{j}
, a similar asymptotic on the line
\sigma=\frac{1+\beta}{2}
. For this, we use the following mean value theorem for “general Dirichlet polynomials” due to Montgomery and Vaughan,
[
17
, Corollary 2]
.

Theorem
[Montgomery, Vaughan]

Suppose that
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dotsc,\lambda_{J}
are distinct real numbers, and that
a_{j}
,
j=1,\dotsc,J
are complex numbers. If
\delta_{j}=\min_{k\neq j}|\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}|
, then

\int_{0}^{T}\biggl|\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{j}\exp(i\lambda_{j}t)\biggr|^{2}dt=\sum_{j=1}^{J}|a_{j}|^{2}(T+3\pi\theta\delta_{j}^{-1}),

where
\theta
is a real number depending on the various parameters satisfying
|\theta|\leq 1
.

Theorem 4.1

Suppose that
n_{j+1}-n_{j}\gg\max\{a_{j},a_{j+1}\}
. Then

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl|f\Bigl(\frac{1+\beta}{2}+it\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}dt=\sum_{n_{j}\leq T^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}}\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{1+\beta}}+O\Biggl(\biggl(\sum_{n_{j}\leq T^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}}\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{1+\beta}}\biggr)^{1/2}\Biggr).

Note that in case of convergence of the series on the right, this reduces to
\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|\dotso|^{2}=O(1)
. In any case, as
a_{j}\ll n_{j}^{\beta}
, the series is
\ll\log T
.

Proof.

Setting
N=T^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}
, we have by Proposition 2.2

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl|(f-f_{N})\Bigl(\frac{1+\beta}{2}+it\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}dt\ll 1.

Applying the Montgomery–Vaughan Theorem, we get that

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl|f_{N}\Bigl(\frac{1+\beta}{2}+it\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}dt=\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}\bigl(1+O(T\delta_{j})^{-1}\bigr)\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{1+\beta}},

where
\delta_{j}:=\min_{k\neq j}|\log n_{j}-\log n_{k}|
, which is
\gg a_{j}/n_{j}
by assumption. Hence the big-
O
term above is

\ll\frac{1}{T}\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}\frac{a_{j}}{n_{j}^{\beta}}\ll\frac{N^{1-\beta}}{T}\ll 1.

The result now follows from Cauchy–Schwartz:

\int_{0}^{T}|f|^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}|f_{N}|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}|f-f_{N}|^{2}+O\Biggl(\biggl(\int_{0}^{T}|f_{N}|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}|f-f_{N}|^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}\Biggr).

a

\Box

Let us highlight the particular case
\beta=0
,
a_{j}=1
. If one is merely interested in the asymptotic
\sim\rho\log T
of the mean square on the line
\sigma=1/2
, one can ask for a slightly more general separation condition.

Corollary 4.2

Let
g:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
be a non-increasing function satisfying
1/g(x)=o(\log^{2}x)
. Let
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be an increasing sequence of real numbers,
n_{1}\geq 1
, satisfying
n_{j+1}-n_{j}\gg g(n_{j})
(
j\geq 1
) and
N(x)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}1=\rho x+O(1)
for some
\rho>0
. Set

f(s)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{s}},

initially defined for
\operatorname{Re}s>1
, and by analytic continuation to
\operatorname{Re}s>0
with the exception of a simple pole at
s=1
with residue
\rho
. Then

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|f(\sigma+it)|^{2}dt\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}f(2\sigma)&\text{if }\sigma>\frac{1}{2};\\
\rho\log T&\text{if }\sigma=\frac{1}{2}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Following the above proof using the Montgomery–Vaughan Theorem, we get, assuming without loss of generality that
g(n_{j+1})\gg g(n_{j})
,

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl|f_{N}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+it\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}dt=\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}\Bigl(1+O\Bigl(\frac{n_{j}}{Tg(n_{j})}\Bigr)\Bigr)\frac{1}{n_{j}}.

The contribution from the
O
-term is bounded by

\ll\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}\frac{1}{Tg(n_{j})}\ll\frac{N}{Tg(N)}.

Hence we require that
N
is such that
N/g(N)\prec T\log T
. When invoking Proposition 2.2 with
\beta=0
and
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}
, we also require that
T/\log T\prec N\prec T\log T
. Taking
N=T\sqrt{g(T)}
, this is satisfied, while
N\leq T
implies
N/g(N)=T\sqrt{g(T)}/g(N)\leq T/\sqrt{g(T)}=o(T\log T)
.

a

\Box

Without sufficient separation of the
n_{j}
, the contribution of the off-diagonal terms can become significant and the result may fail.

Proposition 4.3

Let
\delta>0
be arbitrary. Then there exists an increasing sequence of real numbers
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
satisfying
N(x)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}1=\rho x+O(1)
for some
\rho>0
,
n_{j+1}-n_{j}\gg n_{j}^{-\delta}
, and for which

\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl|f\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+it\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}dt\not\sim\rho\log T.

Proof.

Let
L
be a large fixed integer and set
m_{k,l}=k+lk^{-\delta}
for
k\geq k_{0}
,
l=0,1,\dotsc,L-1
. Here
k_{0}
is such that
k+Lk^{-\delta}<k+1
if
k\geq k_{0}
. We then set
n_{j}=m_{k,l}
if
j=(k-k_{0})L+l+1
; clearly
N(x)=Lx+O(1)
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl|f_{T}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+it\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}\Bigl(1-\frac{|t|}{T}\Bigr)_{+}\Bigl|f_{T}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+it\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}dt=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n_{j},n_{r}\leq T}\frac{s^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{T}{2}\log\tfrac{n_{j}}{n_{r}}\bigr)}{(n_{r}n_{s})^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n_{j}\leq T}\frac{1}{n_{j}}+\sum_{k\leq T/L-1}\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}}\frac{s^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{T}{2}\log\tfrac{m_{k,l_{1}}}{m_{k,l_{2}}}\bigr)}{k+1}.

Now
\log\frac{m_{k,l_{1}}}{m_{k,l_{2}}}\sim(l_{1}-l_{2})k^{-1-\delta}
, so there is a constant
c
such
s^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{T}{2}\log\tfrac{m_{k,l_{1}}}{m_{k,l_{2}}}\bigr)\geq 1/2
whenever
k\geq cT^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}
.
We get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl|f_{T}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+it\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n_{j}\leq T}\frac{1}{n_{j}}+\sum_{cT^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}\leq k\leq T/L-1}\sum_{l_{1}<l_{2}}\frac{1}{2(k+1)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L}{2}\log T+\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}\frac{L(L-1)}{4}\log T+o(\log T).

The claim follows upon choosing
L
sufficiently large in terms of
\delta
, and applying Proposition 2.2.

a

\Box

It is an interesting question how small a “gap function”
g
can be so that
n_{j+1}-n_{j}\gg g(n_{j})
implies that the mean square of
f
on
\sigma=1/2
is asymptotic to
\rho\log T
. We have shown that
g(x)\succ 1/\log^{2}x
suffices but that
g(x)\gg x^{-\delta}
is not sufficient, for each
\delta>0
.

Theorem 4.1 can also be used to show that in general

\frac{1}{T\log T}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl|f\Bigl(\frac{1+\beta}{2}+it\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}dt

does not converge as
T\to\infty
, even for ordinary Dirichlet series. We shall consider the case
\beta=0
(a simple adjustment can be done for other values of
\beta
). Let
n_{j}=j
and
a_{j}=1+\eta_{j}
, where

\eta_{j}
is chosen so that
\sum_{j\leq x}\eta_{j}=0
or 1. Indeed, let
M_{n}=2^{2^{n}}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and define

\eta_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}(-1)^{n}&\mbox{ for $M_{2N}\leq n<M_{2N+1}$}\\
0&\mbox{ for $M_{2N+1}\leq n<M_{2N+2}$}\end{array}\right..

Thus
A(x)=x+O(1)
and

\sum_{n_{j}\leq T^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}}\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{1+\beta}}=\sum_{j\leq T}\frac{1+2\eta_{j}+\eta_{j}^{2}}{j}=\log T+\sum_{j\leq T}\frac{\eta_{j}^{2}}{j}+O(1).

But the sum on the right is not asymptotic to
c\log T
since

\sum_{j=M_{2N}}^{M_{2N+1}}\frac{\eta_{j}^{2}}{j}=\sum_{j=M_{2N}}^{M_{2N+1}}\frac{1}{j}\sim\frac{1}{2}\log M_{2N+1}\quad\mbox{ while }\quad\sum_{j=M_{2N+1}}^{M_{2N+2}-1}\frac{\eta_{j}^{2}}{j}=0.

For the general case, take
n_{j}=j^{\frac{1}{1-\beta}}
and
a_{j}=n_{j}^{\beta}(1+\eta_{j})
, with
\eta_{j}
as above. Furthermore:

Proposition 4.4

For the above example,

\frac{1}{T^{2-2\sigma}}\int_{0}^{T}|f(\sigma+it)|^{2}dt

does not converge as
T\to\infty
, for any
0<\sigma<\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.
Write
f
for
f(\sigma+it)
and
f_{N}
for
f_{N}(\sigma+it)
.

Fix
\sigma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and let
0<a<1<A
. By Proposition 2.3, we have

\int_{T}^{2T}|f_{aT}|^{2}\ll a^{1-2\sigma}T^{2-2\sigma},

while by the Remark following Proposition 2.2,

\int_{T}^{2T}|f-f_{AT}|^{2}\ll\frac{T^{2-2\sigma}}{A^{2\sigma}},

both uniform in
a,A
, for
T\geq T_{0}(A)
, some
T_{0}(A)
. Hence, given
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
a_{0},A_{0}>0
such that

\int_{T}^{2T}|f_{aT}|^{2}+|f-f_{AT}|^{2}<\varepsilon T^{2-2\sigma}
for
a\leq a_{0}
,
A\geq A_{0}
.

Let
\Sigma=f_{AT}-f_{aT}=\sum_{aT<n\leq AT}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{\sigma+it}}
. Then
\int_{T}^{2T}|f-\Sigma|^{2}<2\varepsilon T^{2-2\sigma}

|z+w|^{2}\leq(1+\sqrt{\varepsilon})|z|^{2}+(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}})|w|^{2}

\int_{T}^{2T}|\Sigma|^{2}\leq(1+\sqrt{\varepsilon})\int_{T}^{2T}|f|^{2}+4\sqrt{\varepsilon}T^{2-2\sigma}.

Similarly vice versa, with
\Sigma
and
f
swapped.

Now suppose,
\int_{T}^{2T}|f|^{2}\sim cT^{2-2\sigma}
for some
c>0

\delta>0

such that

\frac{1}{T^{2-2\sigma}}\int_{T}^{2T}\biggl|\sum_{aT<n\leq AT}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{\sigma+it}}\biggr|^{2}\in(c-\delta,c+\delta)

for

large enough and any

and
A\geq A^{\prime}
.

By the same method and using the fact that
\int_{T}^{2T}|\zeta(\sigma+it)|^{2}\,dt\sim dT^{2-2\sigma}

\frac{1}{T^{2-2\sigma}}\int_{T}^{2T}\biggl|\sum_{aT<n\leq AT}\frac{1}{n^{\sigma+it}}\biggr|^{2}\in(d-\delta,d+\delta)

for
T
sufficiently large whenever
a\leq a^{\prime}
and

.

But (4.1) and (4.2) are in contradiction. Choose

and

and take

On the other hand, if

for all

5. The number of zeros of general Dirichlet series

In 1913, Bohr and Landau
[
2
]
observed that the boundedness of the mean square of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
for
\sigma>1/2
implies that its number of zeros up to height
T
in half-planes to the right of the critical line
\sigma=1/2
is bounded by a constant times
T
. More precisely, setting

N(\sigma,T)=\#\{s^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}+it^{\prime}:\zeta(s^{\prime})=0,\,\sigma^{\prime}\geq\sigma,\,|t^{\prime}|\leq T\},

they showed that
N(\sigma,T)\ll_{\sigma}T
for
\sigma>1/2
. Armed with our new mean value theorem, we can now show a similar statement about the zeros of general Dirichlet series with positive coefficients. Incidently, this answers a question posed in
[
3
]
. In that paper, a zero-density estimate was obtained for the number of zeros of Beurling zeta functions, that is, general Dirichlet series of the form

\zeta_{\mathcal{P}}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}}{n_{j}^{s}},

where
(n_{j})_{j}
is the sequence of generalized integers from a Beurling generalized number system
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})
with corresponding multiplicities
a_{j}
. The obtained zero-density estimate holds in the range
\sigma>\frac{2+\beta}{3}
, in case that
\beta
is such that
\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}a_{j}=\rho x+O(x^{\beta})
holds for some
\rho>0
. It was asked whether anything better than the “trivial” estimate
N(\sigma,T)\ll T\log T
could be obtained in the range
\frac{1+\beta}{2}<\sigma\leq\frac{2+\beta}{3}
. The following theorem provides a positive answer.

Theorem 5.1

Let
f(s)=\sum_{j}a_{j}n_{j}^{-s}
be a general Dirichlet series with positive coefficients satisfying
(1.1)
. Given
\sigma>\beta
,
T>0
, let
N(\sigma,T)
be the number of zeros
s^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}+it^{\prime}
of
f(s)
satisfying
\sigma^{\prime}\geq\sigma
and
|t^{\prime}|\leq T
. Then for
\sigma>\frac{1+\beta}{2}
,

N(\sigma,T)\ll_{\sigma}T.

Proof.
The proof is very similar to the proof in the case of ordinary Dirichlet series having finite order and a mean value, see e.g.
[
21
, §9.623]
. We give an overview here, but refer to
[
21
]
for more details.

Notice first that
f(s)=a_{1}n_{1}^{-s}+O(n_{2}^{-\sigma})
for
\sigma
sufficiently large. Fix
\sigma_{1}
so that
C_{1}<|f(\sigma_{1}+it)|<C_{2}
for certain positive constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, and so that
f(s)
has no zeros in the half-plane
\sigma\geq\sigma_{1}
. Assume that
f
has no zeros on the rectangle with vertices
\sigma\pm iT
,
\sigma_{1}\pm iT
, by slightly decreasing the value of
\sigma
and increasing the value of
T
if necessary. By a formula of Littlewood,

2\pi\int_{\sigma}^{\sigma_{1}}\bigl(N(\alpha,T)-\chi_{[1,\infty)}(\alpha)\bigr)d\alpha=\int_{-T}^{T}\log|f(\sigma+it)|dt-\int_{-T}^{T}\log|f(\sigma_{1}+it)|dt+2\int_{\sigma}^{\sigma_{1}}\arg f(\alpha+iT)d\alpha.

Here,
\chi_{[1,\infty)}
is the characteristic function of
[1,\infty)
, representing the contribution of the pole at
s=1
, and the branch of
\arg f(s)
is chosen which is zero when
s=\sigma>1
is real (when also
f(s)
is real), and which is continuous along the broken line
[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{1}+iT]\cup[\sigma+iT,\sigma_{1}+iT]
.

The second integral is plainly
O(T)
, as
\log|f(\sigma_{1}+it)|\ll 1
. For the third integral, we observe that
\arg f(\sigma_{1}+iT)\ll T
.
The variation of
\arg f(s)
along the line
[\sigma+iT,\sigma_{1}+iT]
is bounded by a constant times the number of zeros of
\operatorname{Re}f
along this line. Using Jensen’s theorem and the fact that
f
has finite order, one sees that this variation is
O(\log T)
, so that also the third integral is
O(T)
. Finally for the first integral, we use Jensen’s inequality and the existence of the mean to see that

\int_{-T}^{T}\log|f(\sigma+it)|dt=T\int_{-T}^{T}\log|f(\sigma+it)|^{2}\frac{dt}{2T}\leq T\log\biggl(\int_{-T}^{T}|f(\sigma+it)|^{2}\frac{dt}{2T}\biggr)\ll T.

a

\Box

Of course, as evident from the Riemann zeta function, the condition
\sigma>\frac{1+\beta}{2}
is sharp.

Appendix: (A) General Mellin–Stieltjes transforms

Theorem 1.1 can be extended from Dirichlet series to more general Mellin–Stieltjes transforms. Let
A:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a non-decreasing and right-continuous function with
A(x)=0
for
0\leq x<1
. Suppose
A(x)=\rho x+O(x^{\beta})
for some
\rho>0
and
0\leq\beta<1
. Set

f(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-s}dA(x).

Then Theorem 1.1 holds, upon replacing
\sum_{j}\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{2\sigma}}
by

\iint_{x=y}(xy)^{-\sigma}dA(x)dA(y).

The above integral actually reduces to a series. To see this, we observe that the product of two positive measures vanishes on the diagonal as soon as one of them is continuous: let
F
,
G
be non-decreasing right-continuous functions, and suppose
F
is continuous. Then for all
x\geq 0
,
dF\times dG(\{(u,u):0\leq u\leq x\})=0
. Indeed, let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. As
F
is uniformly continuous on
[0,x]
, we may take a
\delta>0
sufficiently small such that
|u-v|\leq\delta\implies|F(u)-F(v)|<\varepsilon
. Covering the diagonal with small squares, and assuming that
x/\delta
is an integer (which we may by making
\delta
smaller), we have

\displaystyle dF\times dG

\displaystyle(\{(u,u):0\leq u\leq x\})

\displaystyle\leq(F(\delta)-F(0))(G(\delta)-G(0))+\dotsb+(F(x)-F(x-\delta))(G(x)-G(x-\delta))

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\bigl(G(\delta)-G(0)+\dotsb+G(x)-G(x-\delta)\bigr)=\varepsilon(G(x)-G(0)).

But
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, so the claim follows.

Let
(n_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be the increasing sequence of points of discontinuity of
A
. Then

A(x)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq x}a_{j}+A_{c}(x)

for certain positive real numbers
a_{j}
and a continuous non-decreasing function
A_{c}
. By the observation, we obtain

\iint_{x=y}(xy)^{-\sigma}dA(x)dA(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{2\sigma}}.

(B) Bessel’s inequality

Here we show (1.3) (see also e.g.
[
6
, p. 11]
).
By absolute convergence, we have for
\sigma>1

\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}f(\sigma+it)n_{j}^{it}\,dt\to a_{j}n_{j}^{-\sigma}

as
T\to\infty
. Equivalently,

\frac{1}{2iT}\int_{\sigma-iT}^{\sigma+iT}f(s)n_{j}^{s}\,ds\to a_{j}.

But the analytic continuation of
f
implied by (1.1) also gives
f(\sigma+it)=o(t)
uniformly for
\sigma\geq\beta+\delta
for any
\delta>0
. It follows that (B2) and hence (B1) also holds for every
\sigma>\beta
. For any fixed
N
, we therefore have with
f_{N}(s)=\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}a_{j}n_{j}^{-s}
that

\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}f\overline{f_{N}}=\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}\frac{a_{j}}{n_{j}^{\sigma}}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}f(\sigma+it)n_{j}^{it}\,dt\to\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{2\sigma}},

as
T\to\infty
. Thus

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|f-f_{N}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|f|^{2}+\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|f_{N}|^{2}-2\mbox{Re}\biggl(\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}f\overline{f_{N}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}|f|^{2}-\sum_{n_{j}\leq N}\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{2\sigma}}+o(1).

As
N
is arbitrary, it follows that

\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}|f(\sigma+it)|^{2}\,dt\geq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{n_{j}^{2\sigma}}.
