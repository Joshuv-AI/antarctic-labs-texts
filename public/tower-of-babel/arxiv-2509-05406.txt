“Industrial-Scale” Black Hole Selection without a Satellite
Zexuan Wu, Subo Dong, Andrew P. Gould, Przemek Mróz, A. Mérand

Abstract
The forthcoming GRAVITY+ instrument promises to usher in an era of “industrial-scale” mass measurements of isolated black holes (BHs), with the potential to assemble a sample of many tens of BHs via interferometric microlensing over several years. A key challenge will be selecting interferometric follow-up targets from an order-of-magnitude larger pool of ongoing microlensing events when using traditional selection based on event timescale alone. Gould 2023 proposed a criterion optimized for BH selection using space-based microlens parallax measurements enabled by a satellite. We adapt it to work with microlens parallax constraints obtainable from ground-based data only. Using Galactic simulations, we show that our selection criterion is highly efficient, expecting to detect about a dozen BHs per year with GRAVITY+ from following up ∼ 35 \sim 35 selected events.



I
Introduction

Stellar-mass black holes (BHs) can be produced at the end of massive stellar evolution.
The Galaxy may host
\sim 10^{8}\text{--}10^{9}
stellar-mass BHs
(
Shapiro & Teukolsky 1983
;
van den Heuvel 1992
;
Brown & Bethe 1994
;
Timmes et al. 1996
)
, but few are observationally confirmed due to the difficulty of detection.

Known stellar-mass BHs are almost exclusively identified in binary systems. The Galactic systems include two dozen BHs detected through the long-practiced method of X-ray binaries
(
Remillard & McClintock 2006
;
Corral-Santana et al. 2016
)
and a few BHs in wide binaries via recent radial velocity and the
Gaia
astrometric surveys
(
El-Badry 2024
, see review by)
. In addition, over a hundred extragalactic BH systems have been discovered in merging compact binaries via gravitational-wave radiation by LIGO
(
Abbott et al. 2023a
;
The LIGO Scientific Collaboration et al. 2025
)
.

Isolated (i.e., single and non-accreting) BHs are expected to be abundant in the Galaxy, comprising a significant fraction of the overall stellar-mass BH population
(
Wiktorowicz et al. 2019
;
Olejak et al. 2020
, see, e.g.,)
.
However, only one isolated BH has been confirmed to date: OGLE-2011-BLG-0462, identified via astrometric microlensing using the
Hubble Space Telescope
(
HST
) data collected over a decade
(
Sahu et al. 2022
;
Mróz et al. 2022
;
Lam & Lu 2023
;
Sahu et al. 2025
)
.

Microlensing is the only known method for detecting isolated, dark objects such as stellar-mass BHs. In a microlensing event
(
Paczynski 1986
)
, a foreground object (the “lens”) temporarily magnifies the light of a background star (the “source”), producing two images near the angular Einstein radius
(
Einstein 1936
)
:

\theta_{\rm E}=\sqrt{\kappa M\pi_{\rm rel}};\quad\kappa=\frac{4G}{c^{2}\rm au}\simeq 8.14{\frac{{\rm mas}}{M_{\odot}}};\quad\pi_{\rm rel}=\pi_{\rm l}-\pi_{\rm s},

(1)

where
M
is the lens mass,
\kappa
is a constant, and
\pi_{\rm rel}
is the relative lens-source
trigonometric parallax. The relative proper motion between the lens and source,
{\bm{\mu}_{\rm rel}}
, induces a transient brightening event with a characteristic timescale (the Einstein timescale)
t_{\rm E}=\theta_{\rm E}/\mu_{\rm rel}
, which is routinely measured from a microlensing light curve.
All else being equal,
t_{\mathrm{E}}\propto\sqrt{M}
, so a BH lens, being more massive than a typical stellar lens, tends to produce a longer
t_{\mathrm{E}}
.
However, the timescale
t_{\rm E}
is a degenerate combination involving the lens mass, distance, and transverse velocity.

The central challenge in unambiguously identifying a BH lens lies in measuring its mass
(
Gould 2000
;
Agol et al. 2002
)
. To break the parameter degeneracy and determine the mass of a dark lens, one must measure two additional quantities: the Einstein radius
\theta_{\rm E}
and the microlens parallax vector
\bm{\pi}_{\rm E}

(
Gould 2004
)
:

\bm{\pi}_{\rm E}=\frac{\pi_{\rm rel}}{\theta_{\rm E}}\frac{\bm{\mu}_{\rm rel}}{\mu_{\rm rel}},

(2)

With both
\theta_{\rm E}
and
\bm{\pi}_{\rm E}
measured, the degeneracy in determining the physical properties of the lens can be fully lifted:

M=\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{E}}}{\kappa\pi_{\mathrm{E}}};\quad\pi_{\mathrm{rel}}=\theta_{\mathrm{E}}\pi_{\mathrm{E}};\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{rel}}=\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{E}}}{t_{\mathrm{E}}}\frac{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\mathrm{E}}}{\pi_{\mathrm{E}}}

(3)

The microlens parallax
\bm{\pi}_{\rm E}
can be constrained either from simultaneous observations at two widely-separated sites
(
Refsdal 1966
)
or from light-curve distortions due to Earth’s orbital motion
(
Gould 1992
)
. Measuring
\theta_{\rm E}
, however, is the principle obstacle for detecting BH lenses
(
Dong et al. 2019
;
Gould et al. 2023
, see relevant discussions in)
. Although
HST
astrometric microlensing has delivered a breakthrough by measuring
\theta_{\rm E}
of OGLE-2011-BLG-0462, the approach is too slow (one in a decade) to accumulate a statistically significant sample.

A direct and precise measurement of
\theta_{\rm E}
can be achieved with long baseline (
\gtrsim 100
m) optical/infrared interferometry, whose milliarcsecond (mas) angular resolution can resolve the two microlensed images separated by
\simeq 2\theta_{\rm E}

(
Delplancke et al. 2001
;
Dalal & Lane 2003
;
Rattenbury & Mao 2006
;
Cassan & Ranc 2016
)
. Unlike astrometric microlensing, which requires multi-epoch data usually spanning years to measure
\theta_{\rm E}
, the interferometric microlensing needs only one (or possibly two, closely-spaced) epochs.
The GRAVITY instrument
(
GRAVITY Collaboration et al. 2017
)
on the Very Large Telescope Interferometer (VLTI) enabled the first resolution of microlensed images
(
Dong et al. 2019
)
. However, the
Dong et al. 2019
detection was near VLTI/GRAVITY’s sensitivity limit, restricting the observable events to only a handful of exceptionally bright ones per year.

The ongoing GRAVITY+ upgrade marks a transformative sensitivity enhancement
(
GRAVITY+ Collaboration et al. 2022a
;
Bourdarot et al. 2024
)
. Recently,
Mróz et al. 2025
reported the first microlensing observation with GRAVITY Wide
(
GRAVITY+ Collaboration et al. 2022b
)
, an intermediate phase of the upgrade, demonstrating its greatly boosted capability to access dozens of long-timescale microlensing events (
t_{\rm E}>50
d) per year. Once fully operational, GRAVITY+ is expected to be further enhanced, capable of detecting
\sim 10\text{--}20
BHs per year, thereby enabling the rapid collection of a large sample.

However, such “industrial-scale” BH production raises a challenge of target selection. Only selecting long events with
t_{\rm E}>50
d would require VLTI to follow up an order of magnitude more non-BH events, potentially placing heavy demand on resources. To address this,
Gould 2023
proposed an optimized strategy that additionally vets events using a
\pi_{\rm E}/t_{\rm E}
criterion, with
\pi_{\rm E}
measured from a satellite.

Motivated by
Gould 2023
, we develop a BH event selection criterion relying on available ground-based data, i.e., without a satellite, and evaluate its performance with Galactic simulations.

II
Selection Criteria

Gould 2023
introduced the following
\pi_{\rm E}/t_{\rm E}
criterion for selecting VLTI follow-up events:

\pi_{\rm E}<\frac{\mu_{\rm thresh}t_{\rm E}}{\kappa M_{\odot}}=\frac{t_{\rm E}}{8.14\,\rm yr}\frac{\mu_{\rm thresh}}{1\,{\rm mas}\,{\rm yr}^{-1}},

(4)

where
\mu_{\rm thresh}
is a constant expressed in units of proper motion. This criterion is equivalent to a mass-dependent threshold for relative proper motion of
\mu_{\rm rel}<\mu_{\rm thresh}(M/M_{\odot})
. As shown by
Gould 2023
, a reasonable value is
\mu_{\rm thresh}=1.5
mas/yr, corresponding to
\pi_{\rm E}<0.025\,(t_{\rm E}/50\,{\rm d})
. Applying it effectively eliminates a significant fraction of low-mass “contaminants” (
M\lesssim 1\,M_{\odot}
) while retaining most BHs. See § 2.2.2 of
Gould 2023
for a detailed discussion.

From ground-based-only light curves, the microlens parallax vector
\bm{\pi}_{\rm E}
is often poorly constrained at the time of interferometric follow-up. Instead, the so-called one-dimensional (1D) microlens parallax is typically much better constrained, namely the component of
\bm{\pi}_{\rm E}
parallel to Earth’s projected acceleration,
\pi_{{\rm E},\parallel}

(
Gould et al. 1994
;
Smith et al. 2003
;
Gould 2004
)
.

We modify the
\pi_{\rm E}/t_{\rm E}
criterion by replacing
\pi_{\rm E}
in Equation
4
with
\pi_{{\rm E},\parallel}
. Throughout this paper, we evaluate the following
\pi_{{\rm E},\parallel}/t_{\rm E}
criterion,

\pi_{{\rm E},\parallel}<0.025\,(t_{\rm E}/50\,{\rm d}).

(5)

III
Galactic simulations

We adopt simple toy models and perform simulations of microlensing events to assess the effectiveness of this VLTI selection criterion. Our Galactic simulations mostly follow
Jung et al. 2021
, an updated implementation of the
Han & Gould 2003
model widely used in microlensing studies, with the main modification being the treatment of black hole lenses.

III.1
BH recipe

Our basic approach to assigning remnant masses follows
Gould 2000
, in which ascending mass thresholds in the stellar initial mass function (
Chabrier 2003
in our case) determine whether progenitors become white dwarfs (WDs), neutron stars (NSs), or BHs. While
8\,M_{\odot}
is generally regarded as the boundary between WDs and NSs, the initial-final mass relation mapping progenitor masses to remnant masses of BHs and NSs is strongly debated
(
Pejcha 2020
, see, e.g., review by)
. Given this uncertainty, we adopt a simple cut at
20\,M_{\odot}
: progenitors with
8<M/M_{\odot}\leq 20\,M_{\odot}
form NSs, and those with
M>20\,M_{\odot}
become BHs.

For the BH mass function (BHMF) in the simulations, we adopt an empirical approach. BHs in X-ray binaries are found to cluster around
\sim 7\text{--}8\,M_{\odot}

(
Bailyn et al. 1998
;
Özel et al. 2010
)
, consistent with the isolated BH OGLE-2011-BLG-0462 at
\sim 7.9\,M_{\odot}

(
Mróz et al. 2022
)
. In comparison, the LIGO binary BH mass distribution exhibits two overdensities: one near
\sim 10\,M_{\odot}
, similar to the peak of X-ray binaries, and another at
\sim 35\,M_{\odot}

(
Abbott et al. 2023b
;
Mehta et al. 2025
)
. Interestingly, the three Gaia astrometric BHs fall near these LIGO peaks: two at
\sim 9\text{--}10\,M_{\odot}

(
El-Badry et al. 2023a
;
El-Badry et al. 2023b
)
close to the lower peak, and Gaia-BH3 at
\sim 33\,M_{\odot}
near the higher peak
(
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2024
)
. Motivated by these observations, we consider the following two BHMFs in our simulations:

1.

GWTC-3
: A standard
\tt{POWER\ LAW}+\tt{PEAK}
mass function, which is a truncated power-law distribution with a smooth low-mass turn-on and a high-mass peak, inferred from the Gravitational-Wave Transient Catalog 3 (GWTC-3)
(
Abbott et al. 2023b
)
.

2.

Özel+10
: A Gaussian distribution
(7.8\pm 1.2\,M_{\odot})
derived from BHs in X-ray binary systems
(
Özel et al. 2010
)
.

shows these BHMFs, along with the BH mass estimates of the individual systems mentioned above.

III.2
Natal kicks

The high space velocities of neutron stars are generally thought to arise from “natal kicks” imparted by asymmetric supernova explosions at their birth
(see, e.g.,
Lai et al. 2001
, and references therein)
. In our simulations, we adopt a Maxwellian velocity distribution for NSs
(
Hobbs et al. 2005
)
, with a dispersion of
\sigma=217\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}

(
Disberg & Mandel 2025
)
. Whether and to what extent BHs receive natal kicks is, however, far less certain. Some studies on the spatial and velocity distributions of X-ray binaries suggest some BHs may receive substantial kicks of
\sim 100\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}

(
Repetto et al. 2017
;
Zhao et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, whereas analyses of the isolated BH and certain cases of X-ray and wide binaries indicate much lower, or even negligible, kicks
(
Sahu et al. 2022
;
Andrews & Kalogera 2022
;
Koshimoto et al. 2024
;
Burdge et al. 2024
;
El-Badry et al. 2023a
;
El-Badry et al. 2023b
)
.

Owing to these uncertainties, we assume that the velocity distribution of BHs follows that of their birth stellar populations (i.e., zero kick) in our default analysis.

We also explore simple Maxwellian distributions for BH kicks with mean velocities
\bar{v}_{\rm kick}
of
100
,
200
, and
400\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}
. Natal kicks can increase averaged
\mu_{\rm rel}
, leading to shorter mean
t_{\rm E}
. In addition, they alter the spatial distributions of the lenses, most notably increasing the disk scale height, which in turn reduces the event rate
(
Sweeney et al. 2024
, see, e.g.,)
. We employ simplified treatments in our toy models. For the disk, we follow
Koshimoto et al. 2024
by using their analytical models fitted to numerical integration results of
Tsuna et al. 2018
. A weight factor is applied to the event rate to account for the modified Galactic scale height and surface density, which depend on the kick velocity, Galactocentric distance and vertical velocity dispersion. For the bulge, we conduct simple numerical experiments on BH orbital evolution with varying kick velocities in the Galactic potential, using the
galpy
package
(
Bovy 2015
)
. The BHs are initially distributed according to an exponential density profile (effective radius
r_{e}=1
kpc) with a 1D velocity dispersion of
\sigma=100\,{\rm km}\,{\rm s}^{-1}
. After reaching a steady-state distribution, we estimate the best-fit effective radius as a function of
\bar{v}_{\rm kick}
, with
r_{e}=1.10,1.46,\text{and}\ 2.36
kpc for
\bar{v}_{\rm kick}=100,200,\text{and}\ 400\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}
, respectively, and apply the corresponding weight factor to the event rate.

IV
Results

Our Galactic simulations yield catalogs of microlensing events, from which we generate mock light curves with
1
-day cadence and photometric uncertainties based on OGLE-IV data
(
Skowron et al. 2016
)
. The peak time
t_{0}
and impact parameter
u_{0}
are randomly drawn from uniform distributions during the bulge observing season and between 0 and 1, respectively. In practice,
\pi_{{\rm E},\parallel}
will be measured as late as possible prior to the VLTI observing window in order to make the most accurate measurement possible. In this paper, we represent this decision time by
t_{\rm VLTI}=t_{0}+{\rm max}\{t_{\rm eff},5\,\text{d}\}
, where
t_{\rm eff}\equiv t_{\rm E}u_{0}
is the effective timescale, which is often well determined from incomplete light curves
(
Yoo et al. 2004
;
Dong et al. 2006
)
. We derive
\pi_{{\rm E},\parallel}
by fitting the mock light curve up to
t_{\rm VLTI}
.

V
Summary

With Galactic simulations, we demonstrate that the
\pi_{{\rm E},\parallel}/t_{\rm E}
criterion is effective in selecting BHs with natal kicks
\lesssim 100
km/s for industrial-scale VLTI/GRAVITY+ mass measurements. We expect to detect about a dozen BHs per year by following up
\sim 35
selected events with VLTI/GRAVITY+, making it promising to build a large sample within a few years to study the mass, distance, and transverse velocity distributions of isolated BHs in the Galactic bulge and disk.
