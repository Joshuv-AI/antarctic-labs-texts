Examining the effects of two dietary intervention approaches on disordered eating-related thoughts and behaviors in adults with moderate to moderately severe depressive symptoms
Authors: Ingrid A Worth, Celina R Furman, Kexin R Li, Kendrin R Sonneville, Ashley N Gearhardt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltra-processed foods comprise a majority of the American diet and have been linked to increased risk for depression. In response, minimally processed dietary interventions are increasingly being evaluated as tools to improve mental health, including within the growing “Food Is Medicine” movement. However, because these approaches emphasize reducing ultra-processed foods, they raise concerns that dietary restriction may inadvertently increase disordered eating–related outcomes. This analysis focused specifically on safety, examining whether these interventions were associated with unintended increases in disordered eating–related outcomes.MethodsParticipants (N = 31) were randomly assigned to either implement dietary change via self-implemented nutritional guidance or a commercial meal delivery service for 2 weeks. Disordered eating–related outcomes were assessed using the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-QS) and Dutch Eating Behavior Questionnaire (DEBQ) at intake, pre-intervention, post-intervention, and 1- and 6-month follow-ups.ResultsAcross dietary conditions, no increases were observed in DEBQ restrained or external eating following the intervention. Background

Ultra-processed foods–foods that have been substantially altered from their natural state through industrial processing and often contain added sodium, sugar, and fats–comprise nearly 60% of the American diet [1–3]. These foods are often highly palatable and offer affordability, convenience, and long shelf life [4–6]. However, a growing body of research indicates negative implications for population health and well-being, including mental health. For example, numerous longitudinal cohort studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses have identified high consumption of ultra-processed foods as a risk factor for depression [7–16]. Emerging research further suggests that diets higher in minimally processed foods, such as those that remain close to their natural state (e.g., fruits, vegetables, non-refined grains), may alleviate symptoms of depression or lower depression risk [17–22]. While these findings have generated enthusiasm for dietary approaches to support mental health, they also raise important questions about potential psychological costs of dietary change.

Diets prioritizing minimally processed foods typically require reducing or restricting ultra-processed foods. Although such changes are intended to promote physical and mental health, they may have unintended consequences for disordered eating–related symptoms (e.g., disinhibition, binge-type eating, preoccupation with food, weight cycling). Research on restrictive dietary interventions–including those focused on dietary quality and/or caloric intake–has yielded mixed findings, with some studies reporting increased risk of disordered eating–related symptoms [23–26], and others reporting null or protective effects [27–30]. This heterogeneity highlights a critical gap in understanding when and for whom dietary restraint may confer risk versus benefit. Increasingly, evidence suggests that restraint may not be inherently harmful, but rather that its effects may depend on how it is implemented, the broader intervention context, and individual vulnerability factors [29, 31].

For example, adults with depression are already at elevated risk for disordered eating due to overlapping psychological vulnerabilities, such as persistent negative affect, reduced self-worth, and difficulties with emotion regulation and cognitive flexibility [32–37]. Dietary approaches that emphasize restriction or rigid food rules may amplify cognitive rigidity, self-criticism, and rumination, leading dietary lapses to be interpreted as failure or shame [38–40]. Conversely, dietary approaches that alleviate depressive symptoms may help reduce these vulnerabilities and lower disordered eating risk for some individuals. Importantly, these questions have yet to be examined in the context of dietary interventions for depression [41, 42], leaving uncertainty about the safety of quality-focused dietary approaches in this clinical population.

Understanding whether shifts to minimally processed diets increase disordered eating risk is especially critical and timely given the growing popularity of the Food is Medicine movement, which promotes food-based interventions as tools to improve physical and mental health [43–45]. These approaches, which often emphasize reductions in ultra-processed foods, are increasingly being implemented in healthcare systems, public health programs, and commercial settings [44–47], and have strong public support across informal and formal polls [48, 49]. As such interventions become more widespread, it is essential to evaluate not only their benefits, but also their full range of psychological outcomes. In this context, evidence of symptom stability rather than improvement may itself be clinically informative, given longstanding concerns that dietary restriction can exacerbate disordered eating risk.

The current research

To address this need, this paper reports exploratory analyses of data from a randomized pilot trial that tested two minimally processed dietary interventions designed to reduce depressive symptoms among adults with moderate to moderately severe depression [50]. Individuals were excluded from participation if they reported receiving a diagnosis of a clinical restrictive eating disorder in the last five years, due to limited prior research and ethical safety considerations; however, disordered eating–related thoughts and behaviors were assessed prospectively to monitor potential adverse effects. Data were collected at intake, pre-intervention, post-intervention, and at 1- and 6-month follow-ups using the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-QS) [51], and the Dutch Eating Behavior Questionnaire (DEBQ) [52]. For the dietary intervention, participants were randomly assigned to a nutritional guidance or meal delivery condition for 2 weeks. Both approaches required a shift away from ultra-processed foods toward minimally processed foods but differed in structure and external support. The present analyses focused on safety, examining whether these dietary interventions were associated with (1) changes in disordered eating–related thoughts and behaviors over time and (2) whether trajectories differed by intervention condition.

Methods

Transparency and openness

The University of Michigan Institutional Review Board approved all procedures for the parent study (HUM00231527) and the primary aims and outcome measures were preregistered on ClinicalTrials.gov on January 16, 2024 within 21 days of first participant enrollment (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06242665). This paper’s analyses were pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) prior to being conducted (https://osf.io/5xmyn). For brevity, only procedures and measures relevant to this paper’s analyses are described below. The full protocol and measures, data for the current study aims, and analytic code can be found on the OSF project page (https://osf.io/pcufr/; also see [50]).

Participants

Participants were recruited from UMHealthResearch and social media platforms from December 2023 to July 2024 to participate in a research study about how different foods impact feelings. Eligibility required participants to be adults aged 18 years or older, have a PHQ-8 score ≥ 8 on the day of their first laboratory visit; have consumed at least two ultra-processed food items the day before screening; have a BMI ≥ 18.5; and had no diagnosis of restrictive eating disorder (e.g., anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, purging disorder, avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder) within the last five years. Binge eating disorder was not an exclusion criterion (n = 1). A full list of inclusion/exclusion criteria is included in the primary aims paper and on the study’s pre-registration on ClinicalTrials.gov.

Thirty-one participants were enrolled in the study and completed all in-person and remote study tasks (see Fig. 1 for CONSORT diagram). Consistent with the inclusion criteria, participants endorsed moderate to moderately severe depressive symptoms at intake and consumed diets high in added sugar and low in dietary fiber; a pattern associated with higher ultra-processed food consumption [53]. These characteristics confirm that participants were appropriate candidates for a dietary intervention aimed to reduce depressive symptoms by shifting consumption from ultra-processed toward minimally processed foods. Demographics for each condition and the total sample, including sex, race, ethnicity, education, income, age, intake BMI, and intake PHQ-8 score are included in Table 1.

Research design

Participation included a combination of remote activities and three in-person visits over the course of ~ 21 days. In-person assessments occurred during Visit 1 (intake), Visit 2 (pre-intervention), and Visit 3 (post-intervention). A 1-week baseline observation period occurred between Visits 1 and 2, followed by a 2-week dietary intervention between Visits 2 and 3. Remote follow-up assessments occurred at 1- and 6-month intervals following the completion of Visit 3.

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:2 ratio to nutritional guidance or meal delivery, prioritizing a larger sample in the meal delivery group to allow adequate power for analyses of this more structured intervention. All participants received nutritional guidance encouraging them to follow a diet consisting mainly of minimally processed foods (e.g., fruits and vegetables; plain fresh or frozen meat, fish, and poultry; whole, intact grains) while avoiding ultra-processed food items (e.g., foods with added sauces or syrups that contain preservatives and/or added sugar; breaded or fried foods; combination frozen foods, such as pizza or frozen dinners; juices, sodas, energy drinks). These categories were based on the NOVA Classification System [54]; however, the classification and use of the term ‘ultra-processed foods’ remain an area of active discussion and are not universally standardized; interpretations should be considered in this context.

In the nutritional guidance condition, participants (n = 11) were responsible for planning, shopping for, and preparing their own meals in accordance with the nutritional guidance and were provided with a small box of three snacks (e.g., nuts, dried fruit, plain popcorn) as an illustrative example of minimally processed foods. In contrast, participants in the meal delivery condition (n = 20) received three meals a day through a commercial meal delivery service to support them in following the nutritional guidance and were advised to supplement meals when hungry with minimally processed foods outlined in the nutritional guidance. A full description of randomization procedures, intervention protocols, and study tasks and timeline are described in the primary aims paper [50].

The following measures were assessed in each laboratory and remote follow-up assessment (descriptive information for each measure by condition and for the total sample are included in Table 1).

EDE-QS

The EDE-QS is a validated 12-item, self-report questionnaire that measures disordered eating thoughts and behaviors over the past 7 days [51]. Participants are asked to report their experiences on disordered eating across three domains including dietary restraint (e.g., Have you gone for long periods of time [e.g., 8 or more waking hours] without eating anything at all in order to influence your weight or shape?”), eating concern (e.g., “Has thinking about food, eating or calories made it very difficult to concentrate on things you are interested in [such as working, following a conversation or reading]?”), shape/weight concern (e.g., “Have you been deliberately trying to limit the amount of food you eat to influence your weight or shape [whether or not you have succeeded]?”), and behaviors such as loss-of-control eating and purging (e.g., Have you had a sense of having lost control over your eating [at the time that you were eating]?). The first 10 items are rated on the number of days on which they experienced each symptom using a 4-point scale ranging from “0 days” to “6–7 days”, where higher scores indicated more severe symptoms. The last two items are rated on the intensity of the symptoms using a 4-point scale ranging from “Not at all” to “Markedly.”

DEBQ

The DEBQ is a 33-item self-report questionnaire that measures disordered eating behaviors across three subscales: restrained eating, emotional eating, and external eating [52]. The 10-item restrained eating subscale evaluates the tendency to consciously control food intake to manipulate body shape or weight (e.g., “Do you deliberately eat less in order to not become heavier?”). The 13-item emotional eating subscale measures the tendency to eat in response to negative feelings (“Do you have a desire to eat when you are feeling lonely?”). The 10-item external eating subscale assesses the tendency to eat in response to external food-related cues (e.g., “Do you have a desire to eat when you have nothing to do?”). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“Never”) to 5 (“Very Often”), with higher scores reflecting more frequent eating styles in the relevant subscale.

Data analytic plan

Data were analyzed using both between- and within-subject predictors. The between-subjects predictor was condition (nutritional guidance vs. meal delivery), and the within-subjects predictor was assessment. Outcome measures included the EDE-QS and the restrained eating, emotional eating, and external eating subscales of the DEBQ [51, 52].

Preliminary analyses

As preregistered, distributions of all scales were examined for normality using skewness, kurtosis, and visual inspection of histograms. Potential outliers were identified as values exceeding ± 3 standard deviations from the sample mean. To confirm the stability of outcome variables prior to intervention, intake (Visit 1) and pre-intervention (Visit 2) scores were compared using linear mixed-effects models that included condition, visit, and their interaction as fixed effects, with random intercepts for participants. All models were conducted using the lme4 package in R [55].

Primary analyses

To evaluate whether disordered eating–related thoughts and behaviors changed following the intervention, baseline was defined as the average of Visit 1 and 2 scores. Subsequent assessments (post-intervention and follow-ups) were modeled as time indicators relative to this baseline, allowing changes after the intervention to be directly estimated.

Significant changes in outcome variables were assessed for using linear mixed-models with condition and visit main effects, and a condition-visit interaction to examine whether changes in outcome variables differ for the nutritional guidance (vs. meal delivery) condition. Four separate models were tested, including the EDE-QS total score and the DEBQ restrained, emotional, and external eating subscales as outcome variables. All analyses were conducted both with and without BMI as a covariate to examine its potential influence on outcomes. Random intercepts for participants were included in all models.

Results

Preliminary analyses

Outlier analyses identified a single value exceeding ± 3 standard deviations for the EDE-QS and a single value exceeding ± 3 standard deviations for the DEBQ restrained eating subscale. Because the present study represents a safety analysis intended to capture the full range of participant responses, we deviated from the preregistered plan to winsorize outliers (i.e., replace values with the nearest value within ± 3 standard deviations) and instead retained all observed values in the primary analyses. Results were consistent when analyses were conducted using the preregistered winsorized data; these models are reported in the Supplemental Material.

Examination of distributions using skewness, kurtosis, and visual inspection of histograms indicated that all outcome variables were approximately normally distributed. Linear mixed-effects models comparing Visit 1 (intake) and Visit 2 (pre-intervention) confirmed that all outcome variables were stable prior to intervention (p’s > 0.22; Tables A–E in Supplementary Material).

Discussion

This study examined whether two dietary approaches to eating a more minimally processed diet produced unintended increases in disordered eating-related thoughts and behaviors among adults with moderate to moderately severe depression who did not endorse receiving a recent diagnosis of restrictive eating disorder. Across both intervention conditions, disordered eating–related symptoms did not increase from baseline; in fact, EDE-QS and emotional eating modestly decreased post-intervention, and emotional and external eating remained lower than baseline at follow-up. These changes were not moderated by intervention condition, suggesting there may be multiple ways to implement minimally processed dietary approaches safely in adults with depression, even when some form of dietary restraint is involved. This is notable given longstanding concerns that dietary interventions involving restriction may unintentionally increase eating disorder risk, particularly among individuals with mood disturbances [25, 29].

Dietary restraint and eating disorder risk

These findings build on prior research, which increasingly emphasizes that the type, intent, and context of dietary restraint may be critical in determining whether restraint confers risk or benefit [29, 56]. For instance, rigid, rule-based restraint and prolonged caloric restriction are more consistently associated with adverse outcomes such as loss-of-control eating and binge episodes [27, 28]. In contrast, flexible restraint, which allows individuals to make dietary choices within boundaries, has often been found to have neutral [29], or even protective, effects [27, 28, 57].

Although the present study did not directly compare rigid versus flexible restraint, the two interventions differed in their degree of structure, which may have implications for how restraint was experienced and enacted. The nutritional guidance condition allowed participants to independently select and prepare meals within broad recommendations, allowing more flexibility in food choices and integration with existing eating patterns. In contrast, the meal delivery condition provided pre-prepared meals intended to serve as the primary source of intake, representing a more structured and externally guided form of restraint. Thus, while both conditions involved limiting ultra-processed foods, they differed in the degree of autonomy and flexibility afforded to participants.

Importantly, the intent of restraint in both interventions also diverged from rigid forms typically associated with eating disorder risk. Although both interventions were relatively restrictive by definition (i.e., avoiding specific, often highly palatable ultra-processed foods), they emphasized quality-focused changes rather than caloric restriction. Participants were explicitly encouraged to eat when hungry, to avoid limiting the quantity of food consumed, and to focus on minimally processed, nutrient-dense foods rather than total energy intake. Thus, this quality-focused framing may not have activated the maladaptive cognitive and behavioral processes implicated in disordered eating, such as rigid food rules, heightened self-criticism, excessive self-monitoring, fasting, purging, or other compensatory behaviors [29, 31].

Why restricting ultra-processed foods may not increase disordered eating-related thoughts and behaviors

Beyond distinctions in the structure of restraint, the specific focus on reducing ultra-processed foods may help explain why the present interventions were associated with stable or modest improvements in disordered eating–related outcomes, particularly emotional and external eating. Ultra-processed foods are typically nutrient-poor, with higher added sugars and lower fiber, which has been linked to glycemic dysregulation, alterations in gut microbiota, increased inflammation, and changes in neuroendocrine and neural signaling—all processes implicated in mood disturbances [10, 58–60]. Reducing exposure to these foods and increasing intake of minimally processed, nutrient-dense foods may help regulate these physiological processes, contributing to reductions in depressive symptoms. Because negative affect is a strong predictor of disordered eating-related thoughts and behaviors, including emotional and external eating [37, 61], improvements in mood may in turn reduce vulnerability to these outcomes. Additionally, ultra-processed foods are often highly palatable and engineered to increase reward value and cravings, making them difficult to consume in moderation [62]. Reducing exposure may therefore decrease cravings and the salience of foods that can trigger dysregulated eating behaviors or heightened preoccupation with food [61].

Taken together, restricting ultra-processed foods while encouraging intake of minimally processed foods may operate through two complementary pathways: (1) improving mood-related processes that underlie emotional and external eating, and (2) reducing exposure to foods that induce cravings and promote disinhibited eating. Importantly, these pathways are speculative, as the design of the two interventions may have also mitigated common risks associated with dietary restraint. For example, in the present study, the provision of food, particularly in the meal delivery condition, and the use of clear, structured dietary guidance may have reduced food-related stress, decision fatigue, and cognitive burden around eating. As a result, it is difficult to disentangle the independent effects of food quality from the supportive structure of the intervention as mechanisms of change, highlighting the need for future work that more directly isolates these components (e.g., quality-focused dietary changes, flexible vs. rigid implementation, supportive intervention structure) and particularly among individuals at elevated risk for disordered eating.

Limitations and considerations for generalizability

While these findings are promising, several limitations warrant cautious interpretation and highlight directions for future research. First, the absence of a true control group prevents definitive attribution of effects to the interventions rather than other study-related factors, such as those discussed earlier. The modest sample size, particularly in the nutritional guidance condition, also limited statistical power to detect small effects or differences between interventions, and precluded examination of additional covariates (e.g., baseline depression, demographics, intervention adherence), which may meaningfully shape outcomes. Finally, the 2-week intervention period limits study implications to short-term safety, demonstrating no immediate increases in disordered eating–related thoughts and behaviors following either quality-focused dietary approach. However, 2 weeks is shorter than the time over which restrictive dieting efforts are typically sustained in real-world contexts, such as weight-loss or fad dieting (4–6 weeks) [63], and substantially shorter than the ongoing process required to integrate a minimally processed dietary pattern into daily life as a lifestyle modification for depression. Future research should address these limitations by employing randomized designs with appropriate control conditions, larger and more diverse samples, and sufficient power to examine relevant covariates and moderators of intervention effects. In addition, longer intervention periods with extended follow-up assessments will be critical for determining whether observed patterns of stability persist over time and for evaluating the durability and safety of these dietary approaches in real-world contexts.

In addition to these study limitations, it is important to consider features of the current sample related to baseline eating behavior and psychopathology that may impact generalizability of findings. Although there was a small baseline difference in restrained eating between conditions, both groups were characterized by relatively low levels of restraint across the study. This difference may have introduced some initial variability in how participants engaged with the interventions; however, changes in restrained eating over time did not differ between conditions, suggesting that intervention effects were not influenced by baseline levels of restraint in this sample. Nevertheless, this restricted range may limit generalizability to individuals with higher or more variable levels of dietary restraint.

It is also essential that these research questions be examined among individuals with current or recent clinical eating disorders, especially given their lifetime prevalence of comorbid depression (49.5% anorexia nervosa; 76.3% bulimia nervosa; 65.5% binge-eating disorder) [64]. Prior research suggests that disinhibited or dysregulated eating may precede, rather than result from, dietary restraint [31, 65], and participants in the present study were screened to exclude current or recent restrictive eating disorders. Accordingly, in this sample with low baseline eating-related psychopathology, the brief intervention was not associated with the emergence of new disordered eating–related thoughts and behaviors. However, these findings cannot speak to how minimally processed dietary interventions might influence symptom trajectories among individuals with pre-existing vulnerabilities. Additionally, although statistically significant, the observed improvements in EDE-QS and emotional and external eating may not be clinically meaningful, particularly given the already low baseline levels in this study’s sample. Examining these effects in higher-risk samples will be especially important for evaluating both potential harms and whether either intervention approach yields clinically meaningful benefits.

This study also focused on individuals who regularly consumed ultra-processed foods, as the interventions were intended to support a shift toward greater consumption of minimally processed foods. However, for individuals already limiting ultra-processed foods, it is possible that the provision of structured nutritional guidance and meal delivery could be experienced as either more or less restrictive than their typical diet. For some, such approaches may introduce a degree of constraint by limiting food choice, including access to preferred minimally processed foods. This could be perceived as restrictive rather than alleviating restriction, depending on individual preferences and prior eating habits. At the same time, for others, the convenience of provided meals may reduce cognitive and logistical burden associated with food preparation, which could reduce perceptions of restriction by minimizing the need for active decision-making and self-regulation around food choices. Future research is needed to determine how such approaches impact disordered eating-related thoughts and behaviors, particularly if they are to be implemented at scale as a means of increasing access to and convenience of minimally processed foods.

Finally, the demographic composition of the sample–predominantly female, non-Hispanic White, highly educated, and economically middle-class–may further limit generalizability. Gender differences in the expression and reporting of disordered eating are well documented [66–68], and commonly used self-report measures may more readily capture disordered eating cognitions as they present among women, while obscuring different risk pathways in men or gender-diverse individuals. For instance, differing underlying motivations (e.g., weight loss, performance, or body composition goals) or stigma may shape symptom expression and reporting [69]. Additionally, higher socioeconomic status may buffer against food-related stress and facilitate more flexible implementation of dietary guidance [70, 71]. In populations facing financial strain, food insecurity, or limited access to minimally processed foods, quality-focused dietary restraint may operate differently, with cycles of restriction and overeating driven more by scarcity than choice.

Conclusions

Overall, this research provides preliminary support for the safety of two minimally processed dietary interventions in adults with moderate to moderately severe depressive symptoms who do not have current or recent restrictive disordered eating. Importantly, the interventions were not associated with increases in disordered eating–related thoughts and behaviors and were instead linked to modest improvements or null effects. In the context of the rapidly expanding Food is Medicine movement, this insight is especially timely. As such interventions proliferate, it is critical to understand not only their potential benefits for mental and physical health, but also their psychological safety, particularly in populations at elevated risk for disordered eating. More broadly, these findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of dietary restraint, highlighting that restraint may not be inherently harmful for all individuals and that its effects likely depend on implementation, context, and individual susceptibility. Future randomized controlled trials in larger and more diverse samples are needed to examine longer-term outcomes and potential moderators, and to test these questions in individuals with higher levels of disordered eating. This work will be critical for designing dietary interventions that improve nutrition and mental health while minimizing potential risk.
