Efficacy and safety of micro-focused ultrasound for middle and lower face rejuvenation: A prospective study
Authors: Yuchen Lou, IJung Hsieh, Suiqing Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To evaluate the efficacy and safety of micro-focused ultrasound (MFUS) for rejuvenation of the middle and lower face in Chinese patients. This prospective, single-arm, interventional study enrolled 20 Chinese patients with facial laxity. All received a single session of treatment with the Peninsula MFUS device. Efficacy was assessed by two independent, blinded dermatologists using paired, standardized pre- and post-treatment photographs and the validated 5-point Investigator Global Aesthetic Improvement Scale (IGAIS). A novel quantitative metric - the reduction in a defined mandibular area (Area x) calculated from standardized lateral photographs - was employed to objectively measure tissue lift. Patient satisfaction and treatment-related adverse events were recorded. All participants completed the 6-month follow-up. Based on pooled IGAIS scores from both assessors, 100% of patients showed improvement at 3 and 6 months. The peak response was observed at 3 months, with 32.5% rated as “very much improved” and 57.5% as “much improved”. The quantitative mandibular area (Area x) showed a statistically significant reduction from baseline at 1 month (P < 0.0001) and 3 months (P = 0.0116), indicating objective tissue lift. Overall patient satisfaction was high (100% satisfied at 3 months). No major complications occurred. The most common side effect was transient, mild-to-moderate muscle pain (reported by 90% of participants, median duration 6 days). In this prospective pilot study, MFUS treatment was effective and well-tolerated for tightening the middle and lower face in Chinese patients, providing both subjective improvement and objective evidence of mandibular lift. The treatment demonstrated a favorable safety profile.

Introduction

In the past, facial laxity was primarily addressed through surgical lifting procedures. However, in recent years, nonsurgical facial rejuvenation techniques - including light-based devices, injectables, and radiofrequency - have gained popularity as alternatives to surgery for mild to moderate facial laxity [1]. Among these, micro‑focused ultrasound (MFUS), also known as high‑intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU), has emerged as a promising non‑invasive modality for facial tightening [2].

MFUS delivers focused acoustic energy to generate controlled thermal injury in target tissues. This leads to focal coagulation necrosis, triggering an inflammatory cascade that stimulates neocollagenesis and tissue contraction. By penetrating deeper tissue layers while sparing the epidermis, MFUS can promote substantial collagen remodeling with a favorable side‑effect profile compared with more superficial energy‑based treatments. These characteristics of MFUS have piqued interest in its application for rejuvenating facial skin and deeper tissues [3].

Although multiple clinical studies have reported the efficacy and safety of MFUS for facial skin tightening in Caucasian populations [4–7], data on its use in Asian individuals, especially in mainland Chinese patients, remain sparse [8, 9]. This prospective study aimed to evaluate the clinical performance and safety of a specific MFUS device (Peninsula MFUS; MFUS One, Peninsula Co.) for rejuvenation of the middle and lower face in a cohort of Chinese patients.

Materials and methods

This prospective, single-arm, interventional study involved 20 consecutive patients seeking treatment for skin laxity in the middle and lower face and mandibular region. All treatments were conducted at a single center using Peninsula MFUS (MFUS One, Peninsula Co.) from February 2023 to December 2024. The study received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee.

Inclusion criteria: Healthy males or females, aged 30–60 years, with skin laxity on their face and neck, who were willing and able to provide informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: Those who were pregnant or lactating, had severe sun damage, excessive skin laxity on the lower face and neck, keloid scars or open wounds in the treatment areas, suffering from severe or cystic facial acne, had a history of cosmetic treatments in the target area (including skin tightening procedure within the past year; injectable filler or Botox within the past 6 months; ablative or non-ablative resurfacing/rejuvenating treatment or light treatment within the past 6 months; dermabrasion or deep facial peels within the past 6 months), had undergone isotretinoin treatment within the past 6 months, and were unable to understand the treatment protocol or to give informed consent.

Sample Size Consideration: This study was designed as a preliminary prospective investigation to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and initial efficacy signals of the Peninsula MFUS device in a Chinese population. A formal sample size calculation was not performed a priori, as the primary aim was to collect pilot data and assess treatment response variability for future larger-scale studies. The sample size of 20 participants is consistent with similar early-phase or pilot studies in cosmetic device research [2–4, 10, 11] and allowed for detailed individual assessment using both qualitative and quantitative measures over a 6-month follow-up period.

Prior to treatment, patients were fully informed about the indications, techniques and potential adverse effects of the planned procedure. Informed consent was documented for all participants. A total of 20 women (aged 32–59 years), underwent MFUS treatment.

All participants were treated without topical anesthesia. The treatment protocols included: M4.5, 6.63 W (level V), with 150 lines on the lower face and mandibular region; D4.5, 6.63 W (level V), 10 Hz, with 4800 dots performed in 8 min on the lower face, perioral and preauricular areas; M3.0, 6.63 W (level V), with 150 lines on the lower face and mandibular region; D3.0, 6.63 W, 10 Hz, with 6000 dots performed in 10 min on the lower face, mandibular region, and middle face. The micro-focused probes were uniformly set to a length of 25 mm with an interval of 1.5 mm. Each half of the lower two-thirds of the face received a total of 300 lines and 10,800 dots, following the recommended treatment lines and zones (Fig. 1). All treatments were performed by the same practitioner.

Follow-up visits occurred at 3 days, 1 month, 3 months and 6 months after treatment. Digital photographs were taken under consistent conditions (same room, standardized lighting and camera angles) along with VISIA Skin Analysis System (Canfield Scientific, Inc, Parsippany, NJ, USA) detection before treatment, immediately after treatment, 3 days, 1 month, 3 months and 6 months after treatment. Two experienced dermatologists compared the pre-treatment photographs with randomized post-treatment photographs and scored them using a validated 5-point Investigator Global Aesthetic Improvement Scale (IGAIS) for skin laxity [6, 9, 12, 13]: -1-worse, 0-no change, 1-improved, 2-much improved, 3-very much improved. The individual IGAIS scores from both assessors were pooled and analyzed for each time point. This resulted in a total of 40 evaluable scores per time point (20 subjects × 2 assessors). The frequencies of each IGAIS category (from − 1 to 3) were then calculated as a percentage of the total scores for that time point. This method captures the full spectrum of evaluator impressions and is commonly used in aesthetic studies with limited sample sizes.

Blinded Photographic Assessment Procedure: To minimize evaluator bias during the comparative assessment, a rigorous blinding and randomization protocol was implemented. All post-treatment photographs (1, 3, and 6 months) for each subject were compiled by a research coordinator who was not involved in the subsequent evaluation. For each subject, the three post-treatment timepoint folders (each containing frontal, left 45°, and right 45° standardized views) were assigned arbitrary numerical codes (1, 2, 3). These codes were then re-randomized using a computer-generated random number sequence, effectively concealing the true chronological order of the post-treatment visits from the assessors. The randomized post-treatment photograph sets, along with the unaltered baseline photograph set, were then presented to two independent dermatologist assessors. These assessors were fully blinded to the subject’s identity and the timepoint labels of all post-treatment images. They performed the IGAIS scoring by comparing each randomized post-treatment set against the same baseline set. After completing all assessments, the scores were returned to the research coordinator, who matched the randomized codes back to the actual follow-up timepoints for data analysis.

Quantitative analysis of mandibular lift (Fig. 2): For both halves of each image, a horizontal line (Line a) was drawn from the point where the columella met the nostril. A vertical line (Line b) was then drawn from the angulusoris. The area (x) bounded by Line a, Line b, and the mandibular margin line was calculated using AutoCAD software (AutoCAD 2025). The defined area (x) was calculated separately for the left and right hemiface and then the two hemifacial areas were then summed to yield the total Area (x) for each subject at each time point, thereby providing a composite measure of overall mandibular contour change. A reduction in area x was interpreted as an objective indication of tissue lift and mandibular contour enhancement. The absolute change in area x (in square pixels) between time points was used for statistical comparison. This approach is novel but adapted from established methods of quantifying tissue lift using area reduction on standardized lateral photographs in previous aesthetic ultrasound studies [4, 14]. To evaluate the reliability of the Area x measurement, a subset of images (n = 10, randomly selected across different time points) was independently re-analyzed by a second, blinded researcher using the same AutoCAD protocol.

Patient satisfaction scale: 0-dissatisfied, 1-mildly satisfied, 2-satisfied, 3-very satisfied. Pain Visual Analogue Score (VAS): 0 (no pain) − 10 (severe pain). Each subject’s pain score was recorded immediately after treatment. Adverse effects from the focused ultrasound treatment were documented immediately after treatment and during the follow-up visits.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables (e.g., age, BMI, and the defined mandibular area) were assessed for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Based on the normality assessment, appropriate parametric tests (Student’s t-test and ANOVA) were applied. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To account for multiple comparisons across time points, P values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. A corrected P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For ordinal data such as IGAIS scores, VAS scores, and Patient satisfaction scores, non-parametric tests were employed to compare scores across time points. Inter-rater agreement was calculated using Cohen’s Kappa statistic to verify the consistency of the evaluations. Inter-observer reproducibility was quantified using a two-way mixed-effects model for calculating the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) for absolute agreement. An ICC value > 0.90 was considered indicative of excellent reliability.

Results

All 20 subjects completed a single session of micro-focused ultrasound (MFUS) treatment and attended a 180-day follow-up visit. The average age of participants was 43.75 years (range 32–59) ± 7.26 years. The average Body Mass Index (BMI) was 22.9 (range 18.3–26.2) ± 2.55 kg/m2. All participants were women with Fitzpatrick skin types III-IV. All subjects successfully completed the 6-month follow-up (Table 1).

The IGAIS scores were assessed at 1 month, 3 months and 6 months post-treatment, with evaluations conducted blindly by two independent assessors and frequencies of each IGAIS score were counted (Table 2). Inter-rater agreement is 0.65, which was calculated using cohen’s kappa statistic, indicating substantial agreement. This supports the consistency of the evaluations. The results revealed that 92.5% of participants experienced aesthetic improvement at 1 month, with 100% reporting improvement at both 3 and 6 months. No significant differences in demographic characteristics (age, BMI, and skin type) were observed among efficiency levels. The most pronounced improvement in skin laxity was recorded at 3 months after MFUS treatment, where 32.5% of participants reported being “very much improved” and 57.5% reported being “much improved”. Substantial improvements were visually documented in both frontal and lateral views of a treated subject (Fig. 3), particularly along the mandibular line.

Quantitative analysis of mandibular lift involved calculating the total area reduction of the defined region (x) of both lower hemiface (Table 4; Fig. 4). The average area (x) at baseline was 137,075 ± 11,641 (measured in square pixels). Improvements in mandibular lift were observed in all subjects, though to varying degrees. This area decreased to 133,818 ± 12,295 at 1 month (P = 0.0001) and to 133,266 ± 10,489 at 3 months (P = 0.0116). The inter-observer reproducibility analysis for the defined mandibular area (Area x) demonstrated excellent agreement between the two independent researchers. The calculated intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.92 (95% CI: 0.91–0.99), confirming the high reliability and consistency of this quantitative measurement method.

The VISIA system was used to attend standardized photographs and quantitatively evaluate changes in skin topography. But no statistically significant improvements were detected by the VISIA system in the measured parameters of wrinkles (P = 0.65, P = 0.52, P = 0.76), pore appearance (P = 0.66, P = 0.61, P = 0.69), or skin texture (P = 0.58, P = 0.51, P = 0.62) at the 1-, 3-, or 6-month follow-ups compared to baseline.

The average VAS score for pain associated with MFUS treatment was 3.15 ± 2.05 points (range 1–6), with discomfort primarily linked to the M4.5 transducer. But the pain was tolerable, and no subjects requested treatment discontinuation due to pain during treatment. Erythema and edema were common acute and transient responses. 90% of the participants (18/20) reported muscle pain in the treated area following treatment. The median duration of muscle pain was 6 days (range 3–14). Pain was most described as mild to moderate and typically associated with facial movement or palpation. No one required oral analgesic intervention. No major side effects, such as skin burns or paresis, were observed during the 6-month follow-up. No other adverse effects were investigated.

Discussion

Surgery remains the gold standard for predictable and dramatic facial rejuvenation, yet patient demand for less invasive alternatives continues to grow [1].

MFUS exerts its tightening effect through controlled thermal injury. The ultrasound energy is converted to heat, creating discrete thermal injury zones (TIZ) within the superficial musculoaponeurotic system (SMAS) and adjacent layers. These TIZ are reported to measure approximately 1 mm³, with sharp borders that spare surrounding tissue [15]. The immediate collagen denaturation and contraction are followed by a prolonged phase of neocollagenesis and neoelastogenesis, which can sustain clinical improvement for a year or longer [6]. The 4.5-mm focal depth transducer induces fractional and subtle thermal damage within the SMAS layer and deep fat tissues, primarily contributing to the lifting of the facial contour. While the 3.0-mm focal depth transducer causes similar damage to the superficial fat layer and facia, facilitating tightening of the facial contour. Together, these mechanisms are believed to enhance skin tightening for rejuvenation purposes.

While MFUS efficacy has been documented in Western populations and in some East‑Asian regions such as Korea and Taiwan [9, 16], prospective data from mainland China are still limited. Zhu et al. recently conducted a randomized controlled trial with the same Peninsula MFUS device and confirmed its safety and efficacy, although that study did not employ validated clinical scales [8]. Our work builds upon that foundation by incorporating both blinded investigator assessments (IGAIS) and a novel quantitative measure of mandibular lift.

Our study introduced a novel quantitative measurement - the reduction in a defined mandibular area (Area x) - as an objective measure of tissue lift. This method is adapted from previously reported photographic area‑reduction techniques used to quantify submental and lower‑face lifting after energy‑based treatments [4, 14]. The mean reduction we observed is comparable in magnitude to submental area reductions reported in previous studies that employed similar photographic measurement techniques. For instance, Oni et al. reported an average submental area reduction of 45 mm² at 90 days in a Caucasian cohort [14], while Lu et al. reported a 26 mm² reduction in Asian patients at the same time point [9]. Our results fall within this range, suggesting a comparable biological effect across ethnicities when using analogous assessment methodologies. Minor variations in the absolute degree of lift may be attributed to differences in baseline laxity, treatment protocols (e.g., energy settings, transducer focal depths, number of lines), or patient demographics (e.g., age, BMI). Notably, the high inter-observer reproducibility (ICC = 0.92) of our Area x measurement strengthens its reliability as a research tool and supports its potential for standardized application in future comparative studies.

Our findings indicate that the peak aesthetic improvement and mandibular lifting effect were observed at the 3-month follow-up, with some decline noted by 6 months. This temporal pattern is consistent with the known biological mechanism of MFUS, where initial tissue contraction and early neocollagenesis are followed by a remodeling phase [10]. The proportion of patients rated as “improved” or better on the IGAIS reached 100% at 3 and 6 months in our study. This high responder rate is consistent with pooled data from a recent systematic review, which reported that 92% of patients (n = 337) demonstrated improvement at 90 days post-treatment [4]. The observed effect trajectory suggests that the treatment provides significant but not permanent tightening. The decline by 6 months underscores the potential need for periodic maintenance sessions to sustain clinical outcomes. Future studies should systematically evaluate the optimal interval and protocol for repeat treatments, as well as their cumulative efficacy and safety profile. In clinical practice, this information can help set realistic patient expectations and guide long-term treatment planning.

The high incidence of post-treatment muscle pain (90%) aligns with the deep thermal effects of MFUS on the SMAS and muscular layers. While the median pain duration was 6 days and resolved spontaneously in all subjects, this side effect should be clearly communicated during pre-treatment counseling. In our study, the pain was well-tolerated and did not necessitate pharmacological management or treatment modification. However, in clinical practice, clinicians may consider some anesthesia strategies before treatment, and advising patients on simple analgesics (e.g., acetaminophen) or topical cooling strategies during the first week post-treatment if discomfort is significant. Future studies with larger samples could further stratify pain profiles and identify predictors of prolonged discomfort. Apart from tolerable pain, no other significant side effects associated with the MFUS device were observed in this study.

This study has several limitations. First, the lack of a control or comparison group prevents definitive attribution of observed improvements solely to MFUS treatment, as placebo effects, natural facial changes over time, or variations in photographic conditions could contribute to perceived outcomes. Although we used pre-treatment photographs as internal controls and standardized imaging protocols with VISIA system to minimize bias, future randomized controlled trials are needed to establish causal efficacy. Second, the small sample size (n = 20) and the exclusive inclusion of female Chinese patients of specific age range (30–60 years) and Fitzpatrick skin types (III-IV) limit the statistical power and the generalizability of our findings. The results may not be fully representative of males, older or younger individuals, other ethnic groups, or individuals with different skin types. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence specific to this demographic subset. Third, our assessment relied in part on qualitative scales (IGAIS) and patient-reported satisfaction, which are subjective in nature. Furthermore, we were unable to perform ultrasonographic measurements of tissue depth and structural changes due to equipment constraints; such objective data could have enhanced the mechanistic understanding of MFUS-induced tissue tightening. Finally, the statistical analysis has certain limitations. Although we adjusted our statistical approach to include normality testing and used non-parametric tests for ordinal scales, the small sample size inherently limits statistical power. Additionally, despite applying corrections for multiple comparisons in post-hoc analyses, the risk of type I error cannot be fully eliminated in a study of this scale. The lack of a formal a priori sample size calculation is acknowledged; however, this study was designed as a pilot investigation to inform future adequately powered trials. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable preliminary evidence supporting the use of MFUS for mid-to-lower facial rejuvenation in Chinese women, with quantitative mandibular area reduction suggesting a tangible treatment effect.

In conclusion, this prospective, interventional, pilot study demonstrates that MFUS treatment with the Peninsula device effectively improves mild‑to‑moderate laxity in the middle and lower face of Chinese women, with a favorable safety profile. The combination of validated clinician scales, patient‑reported outcomes, and a reproducible quantitative metric provides a multidimensional assessment of treatment response. Future studies should focus on larger, more diverse cohorts, incorporate controlled designs, and explore optimal protocols for repeated treatments.
