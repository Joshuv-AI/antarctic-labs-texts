Secant varieties of Segre-Veronese varieties ℙ m × ℙ n \mathbb{P}^{m}\times\mathbb{P}^{n} embedded by 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 , 2 ) \mathcal{O}(1,2) are non-defective for n ≫ m 3 n\gg m^{3} , m ≥ 3 m\geq 3 Thanks: This work has been supported by European Union’s HORIZON–MSCA-2023-DN-JD programme under the Horizon Europe (HORIZON) Marie Skłodowska-Curie Actions, grant agreement 101120296 (TENORS)
Matěj Doležálek, Nikhil Ken

Abstract
. We prove that for any m ≥ 3 m\geq 3 , n ≫ m 3 n\gg m^{3} , all secant varieties of the Segre-Veronese variety ℙ m × ℙ n \mathbb{P}^{m}\times\mathbb{P}^{n} have the expected dimension. This was already proved by Abo and Brambilla in the subabundant case, hence we focus on the superabundant case. We generalize an approach due to Brambilla and Ottaviani into a construction we call the inductant . With a combinatorial investigation of these constructions, the proof of non-defectivity reduces to checking a finite collection of base cases, which we verify using a computer-assisted proof.

1.
Introduction

The study of secant varieties is a classical problem of algebraic geometry. When restricted to varieties such as the Segre or Veronese varieties, it also presents a geometric formulation of questions concerning border rank or symmetric border rank in tensor spaces. The famous theorem of Alexander and Hirschowitz
[
alexander-hirschowitz95
,
alexander-hirschowitz97
]
solved completely the problem of determining the dimensions of secant varieties of Veronese varieties. It says that this dimension is always the so-called
expected dimension
, except for an explicitly enumerated and relatively small set of defective cases. Brambilla and Ottaviani
[
brambilla-ottaviani
]
later gave an alternative presentation, some of whose techniques motivate this article.

For Segre varieties or for Segre-Veronese varieties, complete solutions in the spirit of the Alexander-Hirschowitz theorem are currently not known, although many partial results are available. Strassen
[
strassen69
,
strassen83
]
originally studied the problem of tensor rank (and border rank) in connection with complexity of matrix multiplication. Lickteig
[
lickteig
]
provided further results on three-factor Segre varieties, notably in the case of
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
.
Abo, Ottaviani and Peterson
[
abo-ottaviani-peterson
]
studied
(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{k}
in greater generality using a general inductive technique; they also classified defective
s
-th secant varieties of Segre varieties for small
s
. Blomenhofer and Casarotti
[
blomenhofer-casarotti
]
gave a unified treatment for Segre-Veronese varieties, Grassmannians and other varieties and proved non-defectivity of their
s
-th secant varieties for
s
outside of a certain small interval.

In the case of Segre-Veronese varieties, the problem of defective secant varieties has also received much attention in recent years, especially in the two-factor case. Bernardi, Carlini and Catalisano
[
bernardi-carlini-catalisano
]
studied
s
-th secant varieties of
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
embedded by
\mathcal{O}(1,d)
,
d\geq 3
and proved non-defectivity for
s
outside of an interval bounded by multiples of
n+1
nearest to
\frac{(n+1)\binom{m+d}{d}}{m+n+1}
. In
[
ballico-bernardi-catalisano
]
, Ballico, Bernardi and Catalisano completely characterized dimensions of secant varieties to
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
embedded by any
\mathcal{O}(a,b)
.

In general, the trend with Segre-Veronese varieties seems to be that factors of high degree make the problem easier.
Most notably in this vein, Abo, Brambilla, Galuppi and Oneto
[
abo-brambilla-galuppi-oneto
]
proved recently that Segre-Veronese varieties
\mathbb{P}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{P}^{n_{k}}
embedded by
\mathcal{O}(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})
with
k\geq 3
and all
d_{i}\geq 3
never have defective secant varieties. This result built on earlier works by Galuppi and Oneto
[
galuppi-oneto
]
and by Ballico
[
ballico
]
. Ballico
[
ballico2024
]
proved further improvements on the results of Abo, Brambilla, Galuppi and Oneto which allow for some of the factors to be of degree
d_{i}=2
. All of these results rely on first proving non-defectivity in some concrete multidegrees – such as
\mathcal{O}(3,3)
,
\mathcal{O}(3,4)
and
\mathcal{O}(4,4)
in
[
galuppi-oneto
]
– and subsequently performing inductions on degrees and on the number of factors.

These inductive techniques leave the cases with small degrees as seemingly the most interesting. The most notable of these are
\mathcal{O}(1,1,1)
and
\mathcal{O}(1,2)
, the latter of which is the focus of this article. In this domain, Cartwright, Erman and Oeding
[
cartwright-erman-oeding
]
provided equations defining
s
-th secant varieties of
\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
embedded by
\mathcal{O}(1,2)
for small
s
. The case of
\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
is also notable because it contains an infinite family of examples of defective secant varieties
[
abo-brambilla
, Theorem 5.3]
.
Abo and Brambilla worked with a general
\mathbb{P}^{m}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
, proving the following:

Theorem

(
[
abo-brambilla
, Theorem 3.13]
)
.

Let
m
,
n
be positive integers with
n\geq m-2
. Then for any
s\leq\underline{s}(m,n):=\left\lfloor\frac{(m+1)(n-m+2)}{2}\right\rfloor
, the Segre-Veronese variety
\mathbb{P}^{m}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
embedded by
\mathcal{O}(1,2)
has a non-defective
s
-th secant variety.

For
s<\frac{(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}}{n+m+1}
, the secant variety cannot fill the ambient space
\mathbb{P}^{(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}-1}
and hence the cases
s\leq\left\lfloor\frac{(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}}{n+m+1}\right\rfloor
are called
subabundant
. Since the bound
\left\lfloor\frac{(m+1)(n-m+2)}{2}\right\rfloor
in the result of Abo and Brambilla equals
\left\lfloor\frac{(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}}{n+m+1}\right\rfloor
for
n
large enough with respect to
m
, this settles all subabundant cases for sufficiently large
n
.
Abo
[
abo
]
also investigated
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
and
\mathbb{P}^{n+1}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
with
\mathcal{O}(1,2)
, proving that these are non-defective except for
\mathbb{P}^{4}\times\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

Our main result in this paper is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

If
m\geq 3
and

n\geq\begin{cases}m^{3}-m-3,&\text{if $m$ is even and $n$ is odd},\\
\left\lceil\frac{m^{3}-2m-5}{2}\right\rceil,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

then all secant varieties of the Segre-Veronese variety
\mathbb{P}^{m}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
embedded by
\mathcal{O}(1,2)
are non-defective.

We will prove this through Theorems
and
.
Our focus will be on the so-called
superabundant
case, when
s\geq\left\lceil\frac{(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}}{n+m+1}\right\rceil
,
in order to complement the subabundant result due to Abo and Brambilla. In particular, we will prove that under the bounds of Theorem
1.1
, the
s
-th secant variety for
s=\overline{s}(m,n):=\underline{s}(m,n)+1
is superabundant and non-defective. This will then immediately imply the same also for
s\geq\overline{s}(m,n)
, hence obtaining Theorem
1.1
when combined with the result of Abo and Brambilla.

Previously, there remained even for large
n
an interval of values of
s
for which the non-defectivity of the secant variety was not known. We are able to close this gap (for large
n
) by systematically handling the combinatorics of configurations that arise from specializing points to subspaces.

Note that cases
m=1
and
m=2
have already been completely solved (see
[
abo-brambilla
, Corollary 1.4]
), and leaving them out allows us to sidestep the defective cases with
m=2
and
n
odd.
Let us also note that our result is consistent with the list of defective cases which was conjectured to be exhaustive in
[
abo-brambilla
, Section 5]
.

To close out this introduction, we outline the structure of the paper. In Section
2
we overview the basic setting of secant varieties and prepare some arithmetic related to secant varieties of Segre-Veronese varieties embedded by
\mathcal{O}(1,2)
. In Section
3
, we introduce the notions of
coordinate configurations
and the
inductant
construction. In Section
, we apply these to prove Theorem
1.1
. Finally, in Section
, we describe the computations which were needed to supply the base cases of inductive proofs in Section
; the source code and resulting certificates for these computations are available on GitHub at
[
gitrepo
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Segre-Veronese varieties and their secant varieties

Throughout this paper, let
U
,
V
be
(m+1)
- and
(n+1)
-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector spaces respectively and let
\mathbb{P}^{m,n}=\mathbb{P}(U)\times\mathbb{P}(V)
. Let us fix coordinate systems
x_{0},\dots,x_{m}
on
U
and
y_{0},\dots,y_{n}
on
V
. Further, let
X_{m,n}
denote the Segre-Veronese variety
\mathbb{P}^{m,n}
embedded by
\mathcal{O}(1,2)
in
\mathbb{P}^{(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}-1}
.

The variety
\mathbb{P}^{m,n}=\mathbb{P}(U)\times\mathbb{P}(V)
is naturally equipped with the bigraded algebra
\Sym U\otimes\Sym V
, which we identify with the polynomial algebra
R=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{m},y_{0},\dots,y_{n}]
. For any set
L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{m,n}
, we may consider its bihomogeneous ideal
\mathcal{I}_{L}
, which is generated by all bihomogeneous polynomials that vanish at all points
P\in L
. For any bihomogeneous ideal
\mathcal{I}\leq R
, we denote its bidegree
(a,b)
homogeneous part by
\mathcal{I}(a,b)
. A homogeneous
f\in R
is said to be
singular
at some
P\in\mathbb{P}^{m,n}
if all its partial derivatives vanish when evaluated at
P
.

In general, with a variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{N}
, its
s
-th secant variety
\sigma_{s}(X)
is defined as the Zariski closure of the union of all linear spans of
s
-tuples of points on
X
, that is

\sigma_{s}(X):=\overline{\bigcup_{P_{1},\dots,P_{s}\in X}\langle P_{1},\dots,P_{s}\rangle}.

The expected dimension of
\sigma_{s}(X)
is then
\min\{N,s(\dim X+1)-1\}
, we say
\sigma_{s}(X)
is
defective
, if it does not have the expected dimension, and
non-defective
otherwise. In particular, we say
X
itself is
defective
, if
\sigma_{s}(X)
is defective for some
s
, and
non-defective
, if all
\sigma_{s}(X)
are non-defective. Further,
\sigma_{s}(X)
is said to be
subabundant
(resp.
superabundant
), if
s(\dim X+1)-1\leq N
(resp.
s(\dim X+1)-1\geq N
). It is further said to be
equiabundant
, if it is both sub- and superabundant.

One may investigate
\dim\sigma_{s}(X)
by taking the tangent space at a generic point. By Terracini’s lemma, the tangent space to
\sigma_{s}(X)
at a generic point
Q
in the span of
s
generic points
P_{1},\dots,P_{s}\in X
equals the span of tangent spaces to
X
at
P_{1},\dots,P_{s}
. Returning to the Segre-Veronese variety
X=X_{m,n}
, we may use this view to reformulate the problem of finding
\dim\sigma_{s}(X_{m,n})
as follows:
If
P_{1},\dots,P_{s}
are chosen generically on
\mathbb{P}^{m,n}
and
\mathcal{I}\leq\Sym U\otimes\Sym V=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{m},y_{0},\dots,y_{n}]
is the bihomogeneous ideal consisting of polynomials that are singular at each
P_{i}
, then the affine dimension of
\sigma_{s}(X_{m,n})
equals

\codim\mathcal{I}(1,2)=(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}-\dim\mathcal{I}(1,2),

where we consider the dimension of
\mathcal{I}(1,2)
as a vector space over
\mathbb{C}
.
Thus we may formulate our inquiries in terms of how many independent conditions does being singular at
s
generic points of
\mathbb{P}^{m,n}
put on bidegree
(1,2)
polynomials – non-defectivity means
\dim\mathcal{I}(1,2)=\max\left\{0,\ (m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}-s(m+n+1)\right\}
in this language.

Abo and Brambilla
[
abo-brambilla
, Corollary 3.14]
proved that
\sigma_{s}(X_{m,n})
is non-defective for any
s\leq\underline{s}(m,n):=\left\lfloor\frac{(m+1)(n-m+2)}{2}\right\rfloor
. Since this
\underline{s}(m,n)
equals
\left\lfloor\frac{(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}}{n+m+1}\right\rfloor
for

n>\begin{cases}m^{3}-2m,&\text{if $m$ is even and $n$ is odd,}\\
\frac{(m-2)(m+1)^{2}}{2},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

this means that the subabundant secant varieties of
X_{m,n}
are known to be non-defective under these conditions. For superabundant cases, Abo and Brambilla proved non-defectivity for
s
satisfying asymptotically tight bounds, which are however strictly higher than
\left\lceil\frac{(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}}{n+m+1}\right\rceil
even for large
n
. Thus in this paper, our goal is to improve these non-defectivity results in the superabundant case. This will obtain non-defectivity of
X_{m,n}
for
n\gg m^{3}
when combined with the results on subabundant cases due to Abo and Brambilla.

2.2.
Arithmetic considerations

Let

\displaystyle\overline{s}(m,n)

\displaystyle:=\left\lfloor\frac{(m+1)(n-m+2)}{2}\right\rfloor+1,

\displaystyle\overline{r}(m,n)

\displaystyle:=\left\lceil\frac{(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}}{n+m+1}\right\rceil.

We will observe in Lemma
2.2
that these two functions agree once
n
is sufficiently large with respect to
m
. By definition,
s=\overline{r}(m,n)
is always the smallest value of
s
for which the
s
-th secant variety of
X_{m,n}
is superabundant. The point of considering
\overline{s}(m,n)
is that we express
\overline{r}(m,n)
as a case-wise polynomial function, although this expression holds only for large values of
n
.

Note that in
\overline{s}(m,n)
, we may split cases based on parity of
m
and
n
to arrive at the following case-wise definition:

\overline{s}(m,n)=\begin{cases}\frac{(m+1)(n-m+2)+1}{2},&\text{if $m$ is even and $n$ is odd,}\\
\frac{(m+1)(n-m+2)+2}{2},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Due to this arithmetic behavior, a splitting of cases into “
m
even,
n
odd” and “the rest” will be common throughout this paper, so we will use the following shorthand:

Definition 2.1
.

Let us say a pair of non-negative integers
(m,n)
is

•

ugly
, if
m
is even and
n
is odd,

•

nice
, otherwise.

Lemma 2.2
.

If

(2.1)

n\geq\begin{cases}3\binom{m+1}{3}-m-1,&\text{if $(m,n)$ is nice,}\\
6\binom{m+1}{3}-m-1,&\text{if $(m,n)$ is ugly,}\end{cases}

then
\overline{r}(m,n)=\overline{s}(m,n)
and

(2.2)

(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}-(n+m+1)\overline{r}(m,n)=\begin{cases}3\binom{m+1}{3}-n-m-1,&\text{if $(m,n)$ is nice,}\\
3\binom{m+1}{3}-\frac{n+m+1}{2},&\text{if $(m,n)$ is ugly.}\end{cases}

Proof.

In
\mathbb{Q}[m,n]
, we may observe

(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}=(n+m+1)\cdot\frac{(m+1)(n-m+2)}{2}+3\binom{m+1}{3}

and hence also

(m+1)\binom{n+2}{2}=(n+m+1)\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{(m+1)(n-m+2)}{2}\right\rfloor+\begin{cases}3\binom{m+1}{3},&\text{if $(m,n)$ is nice,}\\
3\binom{m+1}{3}+\frac{n+m+1}{2},&\text{if $(m,n)$ is ugly.}\end{cases}

Note that if we prove
\overline{r}(m,n)=\overline{s}(m,n)
, then this previous equality already rearranges to (
2.2
).
Subsequently, once
n
is large enough so that

n+m+1\geq\begin{cases}3\binom{m+1}{3},&\text{if $(m,n)$ is nice,}\\
3\binom{m+1}{3}-\frac{n+m+1}{2},&\text{if $(m,n)$ is ugly,}\end{cases}

we observe
\overline{r}(m,n)=\left\lfloor\frac{(m+1)(n-m+2)}{2}\right\rfloor+1=\overline{s}(m,n)
. This bound rearranges to (
2.1
).
∎

3.
Families of coordinate configurations

In this section, we introduce the inductant construction which we will use to present our inductive strategy for the proof of non-defectivity of the superabundant cases of secant varieties of Segre-Veronese varieties. We formulate this in bidegree
(1,2)
only for the sake of simplicity, but we hope the ways to generalize to other bidegrees or even to Segre-Veronese varieties with more factors can be easily inferred.

In inductive proofs of non-defectivity of secant varieties, it is common to specialize double points on hyperplanes. This approach does not work well for bidegree
(1,2)
however because of the many defectivities in bidegrees
(1,1)
and
(0,2)
. This is similar to the cubic case of the Alexander-Hirschowitz theorem, where Brambilla and Ottaviani
[
brambilla-ottaviani
]
were able to overcome this issue by specializing points to higher-codimensional subspaces. Such a specialization may be repeated several times, which leads one to consider collections of subvarieties stemming from such subspaces alongside double points chosen with various constraints.

Definition 3.1
.

Let us say a subspace
U^{\prime}\leq U
is a
coordinate subspace
, if it is defined by equations
x_{i}=0
,
i\in I
for some
I\subseteq\left\{0,1,\dots,m\right\}
. Analogously, let us say a
V^{\prime}\leq V
is a coordinate subspace if it is given by
y_{j}=0
,
j\in J
where
J\subseteq\left\{0,1,\dots,n\right\}
. Let us say an algebraic subvariety
L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{m,n}
is a
coordinate subvariety
, if it is of the form
\mathbb{P}(U^{\prime})\times\mathbb{P}(V^{\prime})
for coordinate subspaces
U^{\prime}
,
V^{\prime}
of
U
,
V
respectively. We then also write
L\simeq\mathbb{P}^{\dim U^{\prime}-1,\dim V^{\prime}-1}
.

Definition 3.2
.

For integers
k,m,n\geq 0
, let three
2^{k}
-tuples of nonnegative integers
p_{I}
,
\tilde{u}_{I}
,
\tilde{v}_{I}
indexed by sets
I\subseteq\left\{1,\dots,k\right\}
and satisfying the conditions

(3.1)

\displaystyle\forall t\in\left\{1,\dots,k\right\}:\quad\sum_{J\not\ni t}\tilde{u}_{J}>0,\quad\sum_{J\not\ni t}\tilde{v}_{J}>0,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\forall I:\quad\text{if $p_{I}>0$,}\quad\text{then }\displaystyle\sum_{J\cap I=\emptyset}\tilde{u}_{J}>0\text{ and }\sum_{J\cap I=\emptyset}\tilde{v}_{J}>0

and
\sum\tilde{u}_{I}=m+1
,
\sum\tilde{v}_{I}=n+1
define a
coordinate configuration

Z=Z(\left\{p_{I}\right\},\left\{\tilde{u}_{I}\right\},\left\{\tilde{v}_{I}\right\})
as follows:

We partition the coordinates
x_{0},\dots,x_{m}
into
2^{k}
sets of sizes
\tilde{u}_{I}
and for
t=1,\dots,k
, let the coordinate subspace
U_{t}\leq U
be given by equations
x_{i}=0
for all
x_{i}
from the sets corresponding to
I\ni t
. Analogously, we construct the coordinate subspaces
V_{t}\leq V
,
t=1,\dots,k
and then set coordinate subvarieties
L_{t}:=\mathbb{P}(U_{t})\times\mathbb{P}(V_{t})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{m,n}
.

Then, we choose
p_{I}
points on
\bigcap_{t\in I}L_{t}
(we interpret the empty intersection as
\mathbb{P}^{m,n}
) for each
I
, and we make this choice in a general fashion.

The coordinate configuration
Z
then consists of
L_{1},\dots,L_{k}
and double points supported in the points chosen above.

Let us comment and expand on parts of the Definition
3.2
; afterward, we will illustrate both the definition and our comments in Example
3.3
.

First, let us note that choosing different partitions of
x_{0},\dots,x_{m}
resp.
y_{0},\dots,y_{n}
into sets of sizes
\tilde{u}_{I}
resp.
\tilde{v}_{I}
only changes the resulting configuration
Z
by a permutation of coordinates, i.e. by automorphisms of the underlying vector spaces – therefore, we will disregard these differences. Further, since
U_{t}
satisfies exactly those coordinate equations that correspond to index sets
I\ni t
, it will have
\codim U_{t}=\sum_{I\ni t}\tilde{u}_{I}
, and analogously
\codim V_{t}=\sum_{I\ni t}\tilde{v}_{I}
. Correspondingly then, the sums
\sum_{J\not\ni t}\tilde{u}_{J}
, resp.
\sum_{J\not\ni t}\tilde{v}_{J}
give
\dim U_{t}
, resp.
\dim V_{t}
, so the condition (
3.1
) says that
L_{t}=\mathbb{P}(U_{t})\times\mathbb{P}(V_{t})
should be non-empty. We also see that
\codim L_{t}=\sum_{I\ni t}(\tilde{u}_{I}+\tilde{v}_{I})
.

Further, in this view,
\tilde{u}_{\emptyset}
and
\tilde{v}_{\emptyset}
give the numbers of coordinate equations that are not satisfied by any of the respective coordinate subspaces.

Next, we comment on the conditions (
3.2
).
Notice that
\sum_{J\cap I=\emptyset}\tilde{u}_{J}
equals
\dim\bigcap_{t\in I}U_{t}
: indeed, this intersection satisfies exactly those equations that are satisfied by at least one
U_{t}
,
t\in I
, and in turn
U_{t}
satisfies exactly those coordinate equations that correspond to an index set
J\ni t
. Thus

\codim\bigcap_{t\in I}U_{t}=\sum_{J\cap I\neq\emptyset}\tilde{u}_{J},

and subtracting from
m+1=\sum\tilde{u}_{J}
yields
\dim\bigcap_{t\in I}U_{t}=\sum_{J\cap I=\emptyset}\tilde{u}_{J}
. Analogously,
\sum_{J\cap I=\emptyset}\tilde{v}_{J}=\dim\bigcap_{t\in I}V_{t}
. Hence, the conditions (
3.2
) merely state that if some points are to be constrained to
\bigcap_{t\in I}L_{t}
, then this intersection ought to be non-empty. Note that in verifying the conditions (
3.2
), it is clearly enough to check sets
I
that are inclusion-maximal among those with
p_{I}>0
.

Further, the discussion of the previous paragraph shows that whenever the intersection
\bigcap_{t\in I}L_{t}
is non-empty, its codimension is

\codim\bigcap_{t\in I}L_{t}=\sum_{J\cap I\neq\emptyset}(\tilde{u}_{J}+\tilde{v}_{J}).

Whenever we have a coordinate configuration
Z=Z(\left\{p_{I}\right\},\left\{\tilde{u}_{I}\right\},\left\{\tilde{v}_{I}\right\})
defined as above, it will be useful for us to denote codimensions
u_{I}:=\codim\sum_{t\in I}U_{t}
,
v_{I}:=\codim\sum_{t\in I}V_{t}
, because they will come up shortly in Definition
3.4
. A sum of coordinate subspaces is the coordinate subspace given by coordinate equations common to all the summands, and in turn, the codimension of a coordinate subspace is the number of coordinate equations it satisfies. Therefore,
u_{I}
is the number of coordinate equations satisfied simultaneously by all
U_{t}
,
t\in I
. With this and the analogous statement for
v_{I}
, we obtain

u_{I}=\sum_{I\subseteq J\subseteq\left\{1,\dots,k\right\}}\tilde{u}_{J},\qquad v_{I}=\sum_{I\subseteq J\subseteq\left\{1,\dots,k\right\}}\tilde{v}_{J}

by the construction described in Definition
3.2
, because the coordinate equations corresponding to
J
are common to all
U_{t}
,
t\in I
if and only if
I\subseteq J
.
Note that the tuples

Lemma 3.5

homogeneous parts and obtain

(3.7)

\dim\mathcal{I}_{L_{B}}(1,2)=\dim\mathcal{I}_{L_{A}}(1,2)-\dim\mathcal{I}_{L_{A^{\prime}}}(1,2)

from the exact sequence.

Now let us consider any double point
P
of
A(m,n)
constrained to some
\bigcap_{t\in I}L_{t}
. In the construction of
B(m,n)
, it might either be left untouched or it might be further constrained to
L_{k+1}
. In the former case, its contribution to
\vdim B(m,n)
is the same as its contribution to
\vdim A(m,n)
, while it is not present in
\vdim A(M,N)
at all. In the latter case, its (negative) contributions to
\vdim B(m,n)
,
\vdim A(m,n)
,
\vdim A(M,N)
are (in the notation of (
3.5
))

\displaystyle\min\left\{u^{\prime}_{I\cup\left\{k+1\right\}}(m,n)+v^{\prime}_{I\cup\left\{k+1\right\}}(m,n),m+n+1\right\},

\displaystyle\min\left\{u_{I}(m,n)+v_{I}(m,n),m+n+1\right\},

\displaystyle\min\left\{u_{I}(M,N)+v_{I}(M,N),M+N+1\right\}

respectively. Implicitly, based on the fact that we are in fact handling some point, (
3.4
) takes effect, so we know that
m+n+1
(resp.
M+N+1
) is achieved in the above minima only if the respective index set is empty.
Note that
I\cup\left\{k+1\right\}
cannot be empty, so the first expression is always just

u^{\prime}_{I\cup\left\{k+1\right\}}(m,n)+v^{\prime}_{I\cup\left\{k+1\right\}}(m,n)=u_{I}(m,n)+v_{I}(m,n)-u_{I}(M,N)-v_{I}(M,N).

Now it becomes clear that for
I\neq\emptyset
, this will equal the difference

\left(u_{I}(m,n)+v_{I}(m,n)\right)-\left(u_{I}(M,N)+v_{I}(M,N)\right),

which is the difference of the other two expressions; whereas for
I=\emptyset
, we will get

\displaystyle(m+n+1)-(M+N+1)

\displaystyle=(m+1)+(n+1)-(M+1)-(N+1)=

\displaystyle=u_{\emptyset}(m,n)+v_{\emptyset}(m,n)-u_{\emptyset}(M,N)-v_{\emptyset}(M,N).

So in either case, we obtain that the contribution of
P
to
\vdim B(m,n)
equals the difference of its contributions to
\vdim A(m,n)
and
\vdim A(M,N)
.

Putting the results on ideals and on contributions of points together, we get
\vdim B(m,n)=\vdim A(m,n)-\vdim A(M,N)
.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
B
be an
(M,N)
-inductant of
A
and suppose
B
is defined on
(m,n)
. If
B(m,n)
and
A(M(m,n),N(m,n))
are both non-defective and subabundant (resp. superabundant, resp. equiabundant), then so is
A(m,n)
.

Proof.

Let us again write
M:=M(m,n)
,
N:=N(m,n)
for short.
The fact that
A(m,n)
will have the same abundancy as
B(m,n)
and
A(M,N)
follows immediately from Lemma
3.10
, so let us focus on showing
A(m,n)
will also be non-defective.

Let us prove non-defectivity of
A(m,n)
in the case when the configurations are superabundant; the subabundant version follows analogously, and the equiabundant version follows from their conjunction.
Observe that akin to the short exact sequence (
3.6
), we have the exact sequence

(3.8)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{I}_{B(m,n)}(1,2)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{I}_{A(m,n)}(1,2)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{I}_{A(M,N)}(1,2),}

where the map
\mathcal{I}_{B(m,n)}(1,2)\to\mathcal{I}_{A(m,n)}(1,2)
is the inclusion map and
\mathcal{I}_{A(m,n)}(1,2)\to\mathcal{I}_{A(M,N)}(1,2)
is the restriction to the extra subvariety used in the inductant construction, which is then identified with

so the inequality gives

, i.e. the ideal vanishes in the bidegree

must be superabundant, this means it is non-defective.
∎
