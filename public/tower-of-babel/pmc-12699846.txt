Effects of occupational stress on the distribution of circulating T lymphocyte subsets and psychological health of young anaesthesiologists
Authors: Rui Xu, Wenyi Chen, Jianshe Wang, Xiahao Ding, Shouqiang Zhu, Xue Han, Yiming Zhou, Zhengliang Ma, Tianjiao Xia, Xiaoping Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-intensity work environments and high psychological stress place anaesthesiologists in a state of chronic stress. T lymphocyte subsets are an essential component of the peripheral immune cells and are important in the pathophysiology of chronic stress. The aim of this study was to investigate the levels of psychological stress, the expression levels of circulating T lymphocyte subsets, and the correlation between them.MethodsThis study included 61 participants from two tertiary hospitals in Nanjing, China. The Chinese Perceived Stress Scale (CPSS), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scales were used to assess psychological stress levels. The percentage of circulating T lymphocyte subsets was determined using flow cytometry.ResultsThe proportion of anaesthesiologists with high stress levels was 45.90%. The numbers of γδT cells, T helper 1 (Th1) cells, T helper 17 (Th17) cells, and the ratio of Th17/regulatory T (Treg) cells exhibit significant differences in T lymphocyte subsets across different psychological stress levels. γδT, Th1, and Th17 cell counts were higher in high-stress participants with anxiety and depression than in others.ConclusionsHigh occupational stress is associated with negative emotions and may also affect some subsets of circulating T lymphocytes in young anaesthesiologists.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR1900021637. Background

With societal development, increasing attention is directed to mental health. Stress is defined as a perceived or actual threat to the physiological or psychological steady state of the human body. Chronic stress arises when the body is exposed to various adverse internal or external environments or stimuli over a prolonged period, typically manifesting as anxiety, depression, and related symptoms [1]. Chronic stress can gradually affect physiological health, contributing to cardiovascular, immune system, and neurodegenerative diseases, among others [2, 3].

Medical professionals, particularly young anaesthesiologists, exhibit higher levels of psychological stress compared to other occupational groups [4–6]. The working environment of young anaesthesiologists is characterised by high demands and rapid paced, requiring management of multiple surgeries daily. Variability in surgical type and patient conditions necessitates continual adjustments to anaesthesia plans, placing these clinicians under sustained pressure [7, 8]. Such chronic occupational stress poses risks to their mental health and professional development. Furthermore, exposure to anaesthetic agents may exert additional impact on physical and mental health [9, 10]. The health and well-being of anaesthesiologists directly influence surgical quality, underscoring the importance of addressing occupational stress and mental health Perceived stress emphasises self-cognition and perceptions related to stress, largely independent of emotional states and life events [11]. The three commonly used instruments for assessing perceived stress include the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), Impact of Event Scale (IES), and Stress Assessment Measure (SAM) [12, 13]. In China, the Chinese PSS (CPSS) is widely employed. These tools demonstrate excellent reliability and validity [14]. Individual anxiety and depressive states are usually assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) scales [15, 16].

Chronic stress can lead to changes in immune function and inflammatory responses, reducing immune surveillance and placing the body in a state of prolonged mild systemic inflammation [17]. Chronic stress causes a series of peripheral marker changes, among which T cells and their subsets play an important role in the pathophysiology of chronic stress [18]. Chronic psychological stress produces peripheral T helper 17 (Th17)/regulatory T (Treg) cells (Th17/Treg) immune imbalance by regulating the FKBP5/NF-κB signalling pathway in mice [19]. Moreover, the CD27+CD28+ Th and Treg cell subsets play a significant mediating role in the influence of long-term psychological stress on anxiety [20]. Accordingly, it is plausible that the high-pressure working environment may affect the immune system of young anaesthesiologists.

Therefore, this study recruited young anaesthesiologists from tertiary general and children’s hospitals in economically developed areas of China. This study aimed to investigate the level of psychological stress, the expression level of peripheral blood T lymphocyte subsets, and the association between them in young anaesthesiologists. The findings are expected to inform strategies for maintaining the physical and mental health of anaesthesiologists.

Methods

Participants

Young anaesthesiologists working at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital and Nanjing Children’s Hospital were selected for this prospective study. All participants signed an informed consent form, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (Nanjing, China, No. 2018-283-01) and Nanjing Children’s Hospital (Nanjing, China, No. 202004017-1). The study was registered prior to patient enrolment at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry according to the ICMJE guidelines (registration number: ChiCTR1900021637; registration date: 2 March 2019). The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) anaesthesiologists working in clinical settings, and 2) age 25–40 years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) participants with alcohol or drug addiction, 2) participants with history of acute infection in the last 2 weeks, 3) participants with history of antibiotic and antiviral medication in the last 2 weeks, 4) participants who were pregnant, 5) participants with history of immune system disorders, and 6) those unwilling to undergo recruitment or refusal to sign an informed consent form. From November 2019 to January 2021, a total of 61 study participants were included in this study, including 40 anaesthesiologists from the Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital and 21 anaesthesiologists from the Nanjing Children’s Hospital.

Assessment of psychological stress level

CPSS score: The perception of stress was assessed by the study participants’ evaluation of events and situations that occurred in the past month, responding to the level of perceived stress, including 14 entries suggesting a sense of loss of control and tension, with a total score of 56. The higher the score, the greater the perceived stress of the individual, with 25/26 being the optimal cut-off value for the scale. Scores ≤ 25 reflect normal stress and not hazardous to the individual’s health, whereas ≥ 26 suggests excessive stress and health risk stress (HRS). Anaesthesiologists who completed the scale were grouped, according to this cut-off: those scoring ≤ 25 were categorised into the low stress group and those scoring ≥ 26 into the high stress group.

Scores 0–4: no depressive symptoms; >4: depressive symptoms; 5–9: mild depressive symptoms; 10–14: moderate depressive symptoms; and 15–27: severe depressive symptoms.

Scores 0–4: no anxiety symptoms; >4: anxiety symptoms; 5–9: mild anxiety symptoms; 10–14: moderate anxiety symptoms; and 15–21: severe anxiety symptoms.

Detection of circulating T lymphocyte subsets

Fasting venous blood (2 mL) was collected from the study participants early in the morning and placed into EDTA-K2 anticoagulation tubes. A total of 200 µL of whole blood was transferred into a flow cytometry tube and stained with the following antibodies: APC/Vio770-anti-CD3 (Miltenyi Biotec, Cologne, Germany), PerCP-anti-CD4 (BioLegend, CA, USA), PE/Cy5-anti-CD25 (BioLegend, CA, USA), of PE/Cy7-anti-CXCR5 (BioLegend, CA, USA), PerCP/Cy5.5-anti-TCRγ/δ (BioLegend, CA, USA). Cells were incubated for 20 min at 4 ℃, followed by the addition of 2 mL cell staining buffer (CSB), washed, and centrifuged at 300 g/min for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded and 1 mL of Foxp3 fixation/permeabilisation solution (BD Biosciences, CA, USA) was added, mixed immediately, and incubated for 60 min at 4 ℃. Subsequently, 2 mL washing buffer was added, centrifuged at 300 g/min for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was resuspended in the remaining volume of membrane breaking solution, and intracellular staining was performed using PE/Dazzle594-anti-Foxp3 (BioLegend, CA, USA) antibody, PE-anti-GATA3 (BioLegend, CA, USA) antibody, AF488-anti-T-bet (BioLegend, CA, USA) antibody, and APC-anti-RORγt (BioLegend, CA, USA) antibody., Cells were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C, protected from light, washed with 2 mL of membrane-breaking solution, centrifuged at 300 g/min for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded and the cells were resuspend in 300 µL CBS. T cell subsets were analysed using Cytek® Aurora flow cytometer (Cytek, Shanghai, China). Cell populations were defined as follows: Th1 cells: CD3+CD4+T-bet+ cells [23], Th2 cells: CD3+CD4+GATA3+ cells [24], Th17 cells: CD3+CD4+RORγt+ cells [23], Treg cells: CD3+CD4+CD25+Foxp3+ cells [25], Tfh cells: CD3+CD4+CXCR5+ cells [26], and γδT cells: CD3+TCRγ/δ+ cells [27].

Statistical analysis

SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Science) software (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. Categorical data were expressed as percentages (%) and analysed using the chi-square test. Continuous data with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and differences between two groups were assessed using the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. Differences among three or more groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a post-hoc test (Bonferroni test). Non-normally distributed continuous data were expressed as median (lower quartile–upper quartile) ([M (Q1, Q3)]), and non-parametric tests were used for two independent samples. p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

High stress can cause chronic stress among young anaesthesiologists

The CPSS, PHQ-9, and GAD-7 scales were distributed to assess the psychological stress levels of 61 young anaesthesiologists. The screening of the scale results showed that the effective scale recovery rate was 100%. There were 28 young anaesthesiologists in a high-stress state, accounting for 45.90% of the total study participants, with an average score of 30.68 ± 4.41. A total of 33 participants were in a low-stress state, and the average score of the scale was 19.79 ± 3.77. No statistically significant differences were observed in the perceived stress levels of young anaesthesiologists among different sexes, educational backgrounds, professional titles, working years, and work units (Table 1). The scores on the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scales of young anaesthesiologists in the high-stress group were significantly higher than those in the low-stress group (p < 0.001) (Table 2), suggesting that young anaesthesiologists in high-stress conditions are more likely to experience negative emotions such as anxiety and depression.

Changes in T cell subsets in the peripheral blood of high-stress young anaesthesiologists

To further explore whether high stress could affect the immune system, we detected circulating T lymphocyte subsets in all participants. No significant difference was observed in the total number of T lymphocytes between high- and low-stress groups (Fig. S1, A-C), while γδT cells of the high-stress group were higher than those of the low-stress group (Fig. 1, A; Fig. S1, B). Further subdivision of the CD4+ T cell subsets showed that the numbers of Th1, Th17, and the ratio of Th17/Treg were significantly increased in the high-stress group (Fig. 1, B-D; Fig. S2, B, and C), although no substantial change was observed in the total CD4+ T cells, Th2 cells, Tfh cells, and Treg cells (Fig. S1, D-K). These results suggest that individuals with high stress levels may have a mild systemic inflammatory state due to increased T cell subsets that release proinflammatory factors.

Elevated γδT, Th1, and Th17 subsets in high‑stress anaesthesiologists across anxiety‑ and depression‑severity groups

People with high stress are known to be more prone to anxiety and depression. To explore whether negative emotions further affect the immune system, we analysed the T cell subsets of participants with scores > 4 on the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scales. Based on scoring standards and participant scale results, participants were divided into three groups. Participants with scores ≤ 4 being divided into no anxiety or depression, 5–9 being divided into mild anxiety or depression, and ≥ 10 being divided into obvious anxiety or depression. No significant changes were found in T lymphocyte subsets in participants with anxiety or depression alone (Fig. S3, A to I; Fig. S4, A to I). We then combined the CPSS, GAD-7, and PHQ-9 scores for the analysis. Interestingly, 92.9% of the high-stress participants had both anxiety and depression. The high-stress participants with obvious anxiety and depression had higher levels of γδT cells and Th17 cells than participants without or with mild anxiety and depression (Fig. 2, A & B). High-stress participants with mild anxiety and depression had higher levels of Th1 cells than the other groups (Fig. 2C). Other T lymphocyte subsets did not show any significant differences among the three groups (Fig. S5, A to F).

Discussion

The 2016 Medscape Physician Lifestyle Report in the United States showed that the highest incidence of burnout was among intensive care unit and emergency department physicians (55%), followed by anaesthesiologists (50%). The high-pressure working environment and high mental stress exposes young anaesthesiologists to a chronic stress state [28]. When events or environmental demands exceed an individual’s coping ability, psychological stress may occur [29]. The current study participants were mainly junior anaesthesiologists, such as residents (60.7%) and attending physicians (54.1%), who are young for anaesthesiologists. Our study found that 45.9% of anaesthesiologists were under high stress. Chronic stress is a major cause of anxiety and depression and often affects the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which regulates physiological response to stress. Cortisol is the terminal hormone of the HPA axis [30], and prolonged exposure to elevated cortisol levels can induce structural changes in the brain areas responsible for regulating stress responses (such as the hippocampus), thereby promoting anxiety and depressive states [31]. The GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scales exhibit high sensitivity in detecting individual psychopathological changes. In this study, we found that the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scale scores of anaesthesiologists in the high-stress group were higher than those in the low-stress group. This result is similar to those of previous studies, suggesting that anxiety and depressive symptoms are prevalent in high-stress populations.

Activation of Th1 and Th17 cells can lead to increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IFN-γ, IL-2, TNF-β, and IL-17, thereby sustaining and amplifying the inflammatory response [32, 33]. γδT cells, primarily located in the epidermis and mucosa, are the first line of defence of human cellular immunity [34]. They also secrete IL-17 and IL-2. Studies have confirmed that γδT cells participate in the process of chronic stress-induced depressive behaviour [35]. In the current study, it was found that high-stress anaesthesiologists have higher levels of Th1, Th17, and γδT cells compared with their lower-stress counterparts. This is consistent with our hypothesis that high stress probably causes changes in the immune system and places the body in a state of mild inflammation. Although the numbers of some T cell subsets changed, the total number of T cells did not increase, indicating that cellular immunity was not significantly activated. This may reflect the short-term nature of perceived stress in some participants or the absence of stress-induced proliferation of CD3+ T cells over the study period, which can be considered a positive finding. Zhang et al. [36]found that chronic social stress led to an increase in Tregs and a decrease in CD4+ T cells; however, this was in a murine model, and occupational stress in humans is known to be more complex. Acute stress results in a decline in the CD4+/CD8+ T cells ratio in women with chronic fatigue and stress, demonstrating the compounded blunting effects of fatigue and chronic stress on adaptive immune function [37]. Although our results did not find significant differences in CD4+ T cells, these conclusions were consistent. Chronic stress can impair the immune response and affect long-term health. Stratification by anxiety and depression levels revealed that Th17 and γδT cells levels were higher in high-stress participants with significant anxiety and depression; however, Th1 cells were highest in high-stress participants with mild chronic stress. Th2 cells resist the Th1-mediated inflammatory response by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4 [38, 39]. Marshall et al. studied the impact of examination pressure on the immune systems of healthy medical students and found that under high-stress conditions, the balance of Th1/Th2 cytokines shifts towards Th2 and the Th1/Th2 ratio is downregulated, leading to individual immune disorders [40]. In the current study, Th2 cells showed the same trend as Th1 cells. Th1/Th2 cells may be more sensitive to chronic occupational stress, and the release of mutually antagonistic inflammatory factors increases as symptoms intensify, resulting in higher Th1 levels in participants with significant chronic stress and in participants without mild stress.

This study has some limitations. The study participants were mainly junior anaesthesiologists, such as residents and attending physicians, and the number of participants in the trial was limited. The severity of anxiety and depression symptoms among the participants in the study was not significantly different, and the number of participants suffering from moderate-to-severe chronic stress was small, which was not sufficient to group them separately. Due to the limited number of samples, the actual function of each cell subset was not investigated, and only the proportions and changes in their phenotypes were examined. This study was designed as an exploratory observational analysis. Accordingly, no a priori sample size calculation was performed prior to recruitment, so the current sample size may be insufficient to robustly detect moderate group differences. The specific mechanisms underlying T cell changes caused by chronic stress warrant further investigation. However, the findings indicate that nearly half of young anaesthesiologist in the two fast-paced tertiary hospitals experienced high stress accompanied by anxiety and depression. In addition, high stress and negative emotions were associated with changes in peripheral blood T-cell levels. Although further basic experimental research is needed to elucidate the specific mechanism, and larger, hypothesis-driven prospective studies informed by the effect size observed here, this study provides valuable insights that may inform strategies to improve the mental health of anaesthesiologists.

Conclusions

In this study, young anaesthesiologists frequently experience high occupational stress, which is associated with increased Th1, Th17, and γδT cells. In addition, Th17 and γδT cell levels of high-stress anaesthesiologists with pronounced anxiety and depression were higher than those of others, whereas Th1 cells were highest in high-stress anaesthesiologists with mild chronic stress. These findings underscore the impact of high occupational stress on emotional well-being and immune function in young anaesthesiologists.
