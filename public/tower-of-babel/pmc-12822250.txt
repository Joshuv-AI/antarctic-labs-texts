Human leptospirosis in northern Iran: a population-based epidemiological study using infectious disease surveillance system data

Introduction

Leptospirosis is an acute febrile zoonotic disease caused by pathogenic spirochetes of the Leptospira genus. These bacteria can persist in contaminated water or soil, affecting both humans and a wide range of animal species [1]. Like other zoonotic diseases, leptospirosis imposes a considerable economic burden. In 2019, global economic losses attributed to leptospirosis were estimated at 29.3 billion USD [2, 3]. Beyond its economic impact, leptospirosis presents significant public health challenges, with approximately one million human cases reported annually worldwide, resulting in about 60,000 deaths [4]. Furthermore, the disease is responsible for an estimated 2.9 million disability adjusted life years (DALYs) lost each year due to morbidity and mortality [5].

The risk of leptospirosis emerging or re-emerging in various regions is heightened by factors such as rapid urbanization, inadequate sanitation, poor waste management, and insufficient surveillance and control programs. Despite its significance, leptospirosis remains a neglected disease. In fact, the World Health Organization (WHO) classifies it among the 17 neglected tropical diseases [6]. Clinically, leptospirosis presents with a broad spectrum of symptoms, making its diagnosis and differentiation within surveillance systems particularly challenging [7, 8].

Leptospirosis occurs worldwide but is most prevalent in tropical and subtropical regions, including South and Southeast Asia, Oceania, the Caribbean, parts of sub-Saharan Africa, and regions of Latin America [9]. According to the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), in 2022, Estonia, Portugal, the Netherlands, Malta, and France reported the highest incidence rates of leptospirosis in Europe [10]. In Denmark, the average incidence rate was 0.23 per 100,000 population, with the highest frequency observed among males and individuals aged 40–49 years. The most commonly reported sources of exposure included international travel, agricultural work, and recreational contact with freshwater [11]. In Ecuador, the incidence of leptospirosis declined from 3.3 per 100,000 population in 2013 to 0.8 per 100,000 in 2018. The majority of cases were among men and individuals aged 20–49 years, with most infections occurring in coastal regions [12]. In New Caledonia, the mean annual incidence was 30.6 per 100,000 population, with a case fatality rate of 2.3%. The disease was more common in men and individuals aged 30–39 years, with the highest incidence recorded between March and May [13].

In many developing countries, such as Malaysia, India, Sri Lanka, and Brazil, leptospirosis is endemic, with thousands of cases reported annually. A central challenge in these regions is the low level of public awareness regarding the risks, symptoms, modes of transmission, and contributing factors of leptospirosis. This lack of knowledge hampers community engagement in preventive measures and contributes to the persistence of high disease incidence [14]. In Iran, the average incidence of leptospirosis in 2015 was reported at 0.53 per 100,000 population. Evidence indicates that the provinces of Gilan, Mazandaran, and Golestan experience the highest incidence of the disease nationally [15, 16].

Residents of northern provinces are at increased risk of exposure to Leptospira due to the region’s unique geographical characteristics, occupational patterns, cultural practices, lifestyle, and recreational activities, all of which contribute to the higher burden of the disease in this area. To date, few studies have investigated leptospirosis in northern Iran, and limited information is available regarding its recent epidemiological status, particularly in Mazandaran Province. Notably, no previous research has been conducted in Babol County (a County in Mazandaran Province) to evaluate the epidemiology of leptospirosis. Therefore, the present study aims to characterize the epidemiological patterns of leptospirosis in Babol County over six years (2019–2024). By describing and analyzing current trends, this study seeks to provide evidence-based recommendations for improving prevention and control strategies.

Methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study examined the epidemiological status of leptospirosis in Babol County, the second most populous county in northern Iran, over six years from January 2019 to December 2024. The study population included all patients diagnosed with suspected, probable, or confirmed leptospirosis during this period at healthcare centers (including urban and rural Comprehensive Health Service Centers And the centers affiliated with them, doctor’s offices, medical clinics, Laboratories and hospitals) affiliated with Babol University of Medical Sciences.

According to the national communicable disease surveillance system, a suspected case of leptospirosis was defined as a patient presenting with fever and myalgia, combined with positive epidemiological evidence such as contact with surface water (e.g., washing or swimming) or exposure to rodents and domestic animals. A probable case was defined as a suspected case with a positive serological test, either by immunofluorescence antibody assay (IFA) or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). A confirmed case was defined as a probable case with either (1) a positive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test, (2) isolation of Leptospira species from a clinical sample in a specific culture medium, or (3) a fourfold or greater rise in antibody titers (IFA or ELISA) within two weeks [17].

To investigate the epidemiological patterns of leptospirosis, we analyzed the following variables: demographic characteristics (age, sex, and occupation); place of residence (urban or rural); history of farm work, drinking, or contact with stagnant or running water within one month prior to symptom onset; history of contact with animals (domestic or wild) within one month prior to symptom onset; history of fishing within one month prior to symptom onset; source of drinking water (piped water, spring, or well); travel history within one month prior to symptom onset; case classification (probable or confirmed); outcome (recovery or death); and temporal distribution of cases (by month and year).

Study data were obtained from the Communicable Disease Surveillance System of Babol County, provided by the Health Deputy of Babol University of Medical Sciences. Suspected, probable, and confirmed cases of leptospirosis were reported to the Health Deputy through urban and rural Comprehensive Health Service Centers And the centers affiliated with them, hospitals, laboratories, doctor’s offices and medical clinics. They were registered in real-time in the Ministry of Health and Medical Education’s integrated portal system.

For this study, suspected cases were excluded due to the nonspecific clinical features of leptospirosis and the potential for misclassification with other diseases. To ensure accuracy, only probable and confirmed cases were included in the analysis. Additionally, cases in which patients resided outside Babol County but sought treatment within the county were excluded. Due to the limited availability of specific diagnostic tests in the region and the fact that final laboratory results could not fully confirm many cases, probable cases were retained in the study to provide a more reliable estimate of the disease burden.

For descriptive statistics, means and standard deviations were reported for quantitative variables, and frequencies and percentages were reported for qualitative variables. Crude and age-standardized incidence rates of leptospirosis, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were calculated. To estimate crude incidence rates, population data for Babol County, stratified by sex, place of residence, and age group, were extracted from census reports published by the National Statistics Center of Iran. Age-standardized incidence rates were calculated using the direct standardization method, with Segi’s world standard population as the reference [18].

Temporal trends in leptospirosis incidence during the study period were analyzed using the Cochran–Armitage trend test. Prevalence odds ratios (PORs) with 95% CIs were estimated to compare the likelihood of disease occurrence between males and females, urban and rural residents, and across age groups. Data analyses were performed using SPSS version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and STATA version 14 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Additionally, to examine temporal changes in age-standardized incidence rates, a Joinpoint regression analysis was conducted using a log-linear model. The final model was selected using the weighted Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Trend analyses were performed with the Joinpoint Regression Program version 5.3.0 (National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Results

Between 2019 and 2024, a total of 344 cases of leptospirosis were reported. The highest number of cases occurred in 2024, with 103 cases (29.9%), while the lowest was observed in 2021, with 31 cases (9.0%). Of all reported cases, 296 (86.0%) were male, and 242 (70.3%) resided in rural areas. The mean age of patients across the study period was 49.6 ± 14.5 years, with a range from 8 to 86 years. The 50–59-year age group had the highest frequency, with 105 cases (30.5%), whereas the 0–9-year age group had the lowest frequency, with only 1 case (0.3%). Occupational analysis revealed that agricultural workers represented the majority of cases: 146 (42.4%) were rice farmers, and 76 (22.1%) were farmers of other types. Additionally, 305 patients (88.7%) reported a history of farm work within one month prior to the onset of symptoms. Most patients (314 cases; 91.3%) reported using piped water as their primary source of drinking water. The overall case fatality rate was low, with two deaths (0.6%) recorded during the study period (Table 1).

Crude incidence rates were consistently higher than age-standardized incidence rates across all study years (Fig. 1). The highest crude and age-standardized incidence rates were observed in 2024, at 17.3 and 14.4 per 100,000 population, respectively, while the lowest were recorded in 2021, at 5.4 and 4.5 per 100,000, respectively. In 2019, crude and age-standardized incidence rates were 10.1 and 8.1 per 100,000, respectively. Both rates showed an increasing trend over the study period, reaching 17.3 and 14.4 per 100,000 in 2024, with the upward trend being statistically significant (P < 0.001). When stratified by sex and place of residence, the highest incidence rates in 2024 were recorded among males, females, and rural residents. In contrast, the peak incidence among urban residents occurred in 2022. The lowest incidence rates were observed in 2021 for males and rural residents, in 2020–2021 for females, and in 2020 for urban residents. Overall, all four groups showed a statistically significant increasing trend in incidence rates during the study period (Table 2).

Discussion

The findings of this study on leptospirosis incidence in Babol County between 2019 and 2024 demonstrated that, despite some fluctuations over the study period, the overall trend was one of increasing incidence. The incidence was higher during the spring and summer months compared to the other seasons. In terms of age distribution, the highest number of cases occurred among individuals aged 50–59 years, while the lowest frequency was observed in children under 10 years of age. The results also indicated that incidence rates were higher among males than females and among rural residents compared to urban residents. In terms of occupational distribution, the majority of leptospirosis cases were found among farmers, particularly those working in rice fields, with most patients reporting a history of working in these fields.

Our study demonstrated fluctuations in the incidence of leptospirosis during the study years, with a marked increase in 2024, reaching 17.3 per 100,000 population. To our knowledge, no recent studies have investigated the current status of leptospirosis in Iran, particularly in the northern provinces. Previous epidemiological studies in the country were conducted prior to our study period; therefore, meaningful comparisons with more recent trends in other regions of Iran could not be made. Nonetheless, compared with earlier reports, our findings confirm that northern Iran remains one of the areas with the highest burden of leptospirosis in the country [15, 16].

The present study also showed that the incidence of leptospirosis was highest during the spring and summer months, particularly in April and May. Previous analyses of leptospirosis trends in Iran similarly reported peak incidence in May and June [16]. The seasonal pattern observed in our study can be attributed to the onset of agricultural activities, particularly rice cultivation, which is often conducted under flooded conditions, thereby increasing the risk of exposure. Recreational water activities, such as swimming in ponds and rivers, also become more frequent in late spring and summer, further contributing to increased transmission of the disease. Additionally, due to the continuation of agricultural work and the practice of double-cropping rice in the summer, the incidence remains elevated during this season.

The age-specific distribution revealed the highest incidence among individuals aged 50–59 years, while the lowest incidence was observed in children under 10 years old. This pattern is consistent with a previous study from Iran, which reported the highest incidence among individuals aged 35–64 years and the lowest in the 0–14 year age group [16]. Our findings also align with international studies conducted in China, European Union countries, the United States, Ukraine, Australia, and Thailand, all of which demonstrated higher incidence rates in older adults, particularly within working-age groups [19–24]. Our findings indicated that the incidence of leptospirosis was higher in men compared to women. This sex difference has been consistently reported in studies conducted in various regions worldwide [11, 16, 19, 21, 23–25]. The higher incidence among men is primarily related to occupational, environmental, and behavioral factors. Men are more likely to be engaged in high-risk occupations and outdoor activities, which increase exposure to contaminated environments. Additionally, lower use of personal protective equipment among men has been suggested as another contributing factor to their elevated risk [26, 27].

The present study also demonstrated a marked disparity between rural and urban areas, with significantly higher incidence in rural settings. This finding is consistent with previous research from Iran and Thailand, which also documented a greater burden of leptospirosis in rural areas compared to urban settings [16, 24]. The higher incidence in rural regions can be primarily attributed to increased exposure to contaminated sources and specific environmental conditions. In these areas, many residents are engaged in farming, animal husbandry, and agricultural labor, which involve frequent contact with surface water, soil, and mud contaminated with animal urine. Other contributing factors include high livestock density, the presence of rodents near human dwellings, reliance on surface water for drinking and washing, humid climatic conditions, and heavy rainfall, which leads to flooding and the spread of bacteria [28, 29]. However, contrasting results have been reported elsewhere. A study from Brazil found no significant difference in leptospirosis incidence between urban and rural populations; in some regions, incidence was even higher in urban settings [25].

The present study also demonstrated that the majority of leptospirosis cases were associated with agricultural occupations, particularly rice farming. Consistent with our findings, national-level data from Iran similarly indicated that rice farmers had a substantially higher frequency of leptospirosis compared to other occupational groups [16]. A study from Thailand similarly reported that more than half of all leptospirosis cases occurred among farmers [24]. In Denmark, although only 25% of cases were considered occupationally related, farmers still accounted for the largest share within this subgroup [11]. Likewise, research from China has shown that agriculture was the most common occupation among patients in both the northern and southern regions of the country [19]. The elevated incidence among farmers especially rice cultivators can be explained by the nature of their work environment. Rice farmers typically spend long hours in flooded, waterlogged fields where Leptospira bacteria can persist in stagnant and muddy water. The urine of infected rodents and domestic animals, such as rats, cattle, and dogs, frequently contaminates such waters. Direct and repeated contact with these contaminated sources, particularly through minor skin abrasions or exposure to the mucous membranes of the eyes, nose, and mouth, facilitates bacterial entry and infection. Moreover, the humid climatic conditions of paddy fields, combined with limited use of personal protective equipment, such as boots and gloves, further exacerbate the risk of infection in this occupational group [30–33].

Strengths and limitations

This study presents an updated epidemiological profile of human leptospirosis in northern Iran from 2019 to 2024. Its major strengths include the use of the most recent surveillance data, the calculation of both crude and age-standardized incidence rates, and the application of robust statistical methods to assess temporal trends in disease incidence. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, reliance on registry-based data may have resulted in underreporting and an underestimation of the actual burden of leptospirosis in the region. Second, the analysis was limited to a single county in northern Iran; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to other provinces or to the country as a whole. Finally, the lack of detailed genetic or molecular data to identify the predominant Leptospira serovars limited our ability to conduct a more comprehensive assessment of transmission patterns.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that the incidence of leptospirosis in Babol County increased from 2019 to 2024, with an epidemiological pattern characterized by peaks in the spring and summer months, as well as higher rates among men, rural residents, and farmers particularly rice growers. This pattern underscores the significant role of environmental, occupational, and climatic factors in disease transmission. These results highlight the need for targeted policies, including occupational health education for high-risk groups, public awareness campaigns about the disease and its risk factors, and strengthened surveillance and reporting systems within healthcare facilities.
