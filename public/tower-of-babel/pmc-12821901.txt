Dapagliflozin alleviates sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity through AMPKα-PPARα axis and enhances the sensitivity of renal cell carcinoma to sunitinib
Authors: Shi-yu Huang, Min Hu, Yu-jie Chen, Jia-chen Liu, Zhi-yuan Yao, Zhi-yuan Chen, Xiu-heng Liu, Lei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSunitinib is an orally administered novel multi-targeted therapy for treating tumors especially for gastrointestinal stromal tumors and metastatic renal cell carcinoma (RCC) but associated with cardiovascular toxicity. Dapagliflozin is a sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor that not only improves heart failure caused by various factors but also plays a role in mediating apoptosis in tumor cells. However, the role of dapagliflozin in sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity remains unclear.MethodsImmunodeficient mice were subcutaneously injected with RCC cells to establish a xenograft tumor model, and were treated with sunitinib and dapagliflozin to explore the impact of dapagliflozin on the antitumor effects of sunitinib and its cardiac toxicity. Additionally, C57BL/6 mice were administrated with sunitinib to study its cardiac toxicity on normal mice. Finally, RNA-seq analysis was utilized to elucidate the specific mechanisms by which dapagliflozin mitigates sunitinib-induced cardiac toxicity.ResultsDapagliflozin attenuates sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity while potentiating the antitumor efficacy of sunitinib. In addition to causing cardiomyocyte apoptosis and oxidative stress, sunitinib worsens cardiac function and affects electron transport chain (ETC) activity, leading to reduced cardiac energy supply and disrupting fatty acid metabolism. Fortunately, dapagliflozin can rescue ETC activity, promote fatty acid metabolism, reduce cardiac oxidative stress levels, and ultimately prevent cardiac dysfunction. Mechanistically, dapagliflozin exerts a protective effect against sunitinib-induced cardiac toxicity by activating PPARα through an AMPKα-dependent pathway.ConclusionsOur results demonstrate that dapagliflozin not only enhances the antitumor efficacy of sunitinib against renal cell carcinoma but also alleviates sunitinib-induced cardiac toxicity in mice via modulation of the AMPKα-PPARα axis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04576-z.

Background

Cardiotoxicity associated with cancer therapeutics has emerged as a notable concern, a challenge that is poised to persist given the proliferation of medications directed towards kinases that exhibit mutations or heightened expression levels in cancer [1, 2]. RCC stands as the predominant form of kidney cancer, with approximately 30% of patients diagnosed with this condition manifesting metastatic spread [3–5]. Sunitinib has since become widely accepted as the standard of care for first-line therapy in patients with clear-cell RCC, with good and intermediate risk disease [6, 7]. Sunitinib is an orally bioavailable tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) that targets VEGFR1-3, PDGFRα/β, KIT, FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3), colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R), and rearranged during transfection (RET) receptor tyrosine kinases [8]. Although sunitinib makes sense from a cancer treatment perspective, it is possible that it will carry a greater risk of cardiotoxicity [9]. Multiple studies indicate that a sizeable subset of sunitinib-treated patients develop left ventricular (LV) dysfunction and overt heart failure [10, 11]. Moreover, the utilization of sunitinib is linked to an elevated incidence of congestive heart failure (CHF) in cancer patients [12]. Comprehending the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying cardiac toxicity induced by targeted cancer treatments like sunitinib is imperative for the advancement of safer and more efficacious therapeutics. Certain investigations have revealed that sunitinib induces mitochondrial dysfunction and augments apoptosis in cardiomyocytes [13, 14]. However, the specific mechanisms underlying sunitinib-induced cardiac toxicity remain unclear and necessitate further investigation.

Dapagliflozin, a potent and specific inhibitor of the SGLT2, was originally formulated for the treatment of type 2 diabetes [15]. Numerous investigations employing animal models have validated the cardioprotective properties of dapagliflozin in conditions such as cardiac injury and heart failure induced by pressure overload, myocardial infarction, diabetic cardiomyopathy, ischemia/reperfusion injury, and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, among others [16–19]. Notably, research has shown that dapagliflozin displays anti-proliferative and anti-tumor effects in human kidney and breast tumor cells by inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, inhibiting tumor growth [20, 21]. Consequently, we postulate that dapagliflozin may mitigate sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity in mice. In this study, we observed that treatment with sunitinib induced cardiotoxicity in immunodeficient mice and immunocompetent mice, characterized primarily by left ventricular dysfunction, myocardial cell apoptosis, oxidative stress, and energy metabolism disturbances associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. Dapagliflozin was found to effectively ameliorate these sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicities. Subsequently, it was observed that the safeguarding effect of dapagliflozin against sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity was not contingent on SGLT2. Mechanistically, our investigation unveiled that the cardioprotective benefits of dapagliflozin were mediated through the AMPKα-PPARα axis, which regulated mitochondrial function, ameliorated apoptosis, and mitigated oxidative stress. These results delineate a specific mechanism of action for dapagliflozin in sunitinib-related cardiotoxicity and may offer a potential therapeutic target.

Methods

Animals and treatment

All procedures were approved by the ethics committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University and conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by the National Institutes of Health. BALB/c nude mice and C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Institute of Laboratory Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Medical Science (Beijing, China), and kept in a specific pathogen-free barrier system with free access to the standard laboratory chow diet. For the xenograft tumor model, 5 × 106 786-O cells in 100 μL PBS were subcutaneously implanted into the left flank of 6-week-old BALB/c nude mice. Tumor volumes were measured every 3 days and calculated using the formula (L × W2)/2. Once the tumors reached a size of approximately 50 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to different treatment groups and administered either vehicle, sunitinib (40 mg/kg/day), dapagliflozin (1.5 mg/kg/day), or a combination of sunitinib and dapagliflozin via oral gavage. Upon completion of the study, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were harvested for further analysis. Similarly, C57BL/6 mice was treated with sunitinib (40 mg/kg/day) and dapagliflozin (1.5 mg/kg/day). The doses of sunitinib (40 mg/kg/day) and dapagliflozin (1.5 mg/kg/day) were selected based on human equivalent dose conversion and prior preclinical studies, ensuring clinically relevant exposure, reproducible efficacy, and the induction of cardiotoxicity for mechanistic investigation [16, 22].

Cell cultures and treatments

The human KIRC cell lines 786-O and 769-P were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Cytiva, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cell lines were incubated under identical conditions in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To establish sunitinib-resistant KIRC cell populations, 786-O and 769-P cells were exposed to escalating concentrations of sunitinib (2–10 μmol/L). Following 10 months of culture, sunitinib-resistant derivatives of 786-O cells (786-O-R) and 769-P cells (769-P-R) were generated. Sunitinib (#S7781) was procured from Selleck Chemicals. Neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) were isolated from the neonatal rats as previously describe [23], and 6 μM sunitinib was used to induce cardiomyocyte toxicity. Besides, 6 μM dapagliflozin was used to explore its protection role in vitro.

Echocardiography

Mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane, following which echocardiography was performed utilizing the Vevo® 3100 High-Resolution Preclinical Imaging System from FUJIFILM Visual Sonics. This comprehensive evaluation aimed to assess global and regional cardiac dynamics subsequent to sunitinib administration. Parameters related to cardiac function, including left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS), were determined through short-axis M-mode analysis for the acquisition of cardiac cycles.

Histological analysis

Histopathology analysis was performed as previously reported [24]. Briefly, cardiac samples were excised, fixed in 4% neutral formaldehyde for 48 h, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 5-μm sections. For evaluation of collagen deposition, sections were stained with picrosirius red (PSR) staining and the quantification and analysis were performed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Additionally, cardiac sections were stained with Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA) to assess and quantify the cross-sectional area of cardiomyocytes.

DHE, DCFH-DA, and TdT-mediated dUTP nick end-labeling staining

The cardiac superoxide anion (O2−) levels were assessed through DHE staining, following a previously outlined protocol [23]. In essence, fresh frozen cardiac sections were exposed to a 5 μmol/L DHE solution at 37 °C for 30 min in darkness, with fluorescent intensity subsequently quantified using a DP74 fluorescence microscope. Intracellular ROS levels were determined via DCFH-DA staining in vitro. TUNEL staining, utilizing a commercial kit (C2086, Beyotime), was employed to assess cell apoptosis both in vivo and in vitro. The apoptotic index was calculated as the ratio of TUNEL-positive nuclei to the total number of nuclei.

Biochemical analysis

Fasting blood glucose (FBG) was examined using a One Touch Ultra Easy glucometer (Life Scan, Wayne, PA, USA). Serum levels of cardiac isoform of creatine kinase isoenzymes (CK-MB) and LDH were measured by an automatic biochemical analyzer (ADVIA® 2400 Siemens Ltd, Tarrytown, NY, USA) as previously described [25, 26].

Nile red staining

Nile red staining was employed to assess lipid accumulation both in vivo and in vitro, as detailed previously [27]. In summary, heart sections or cell coverslips were subjected to incubation with 10 µg/mL Nile red overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, cell nuclei were visualized using the SlowFade™ Gold antifade reagent with DAPI. The resulting images were captured using a DP74 fluorescence microscope manufactured by OLYMPUS.

Transcriptome sequencing of heart tissue

After undergoing cardiac ultrasound, sunitinib, sunitinib + dapagliflozin mice were euthanized, and total RNA was extracted using a Trizol reagent. After that, the quality of the extracted total RNA samples was checked with RNA 6000 Nano kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). RNA-seq and subsequent analyses were performed by Bioyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Initially, oligo (dT) beads were employed to purify the PCR products after total RNA extraction. Subsequently, mRNA was fragmented using a fragmentation buffer. The first cDNA strand was synthesized utilizing the mRNA template along with six-base random hexamers. Following this, the second cDNA strand was generated by introducing buffer, dNTPs, RNase H, and DNA polymerase I. The resulting double-stranded cDNA underwent end-repair, addition of poly (A), connection for sequencing, purification and fragment selection using magnetic beads, and PCR amplification to establish the library.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated utilizing TRIzol reagent, followed by cDNA synthesis conducted with the Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Gene expression analysis was carried out using the Roche LightCycler® 480 detection system with SYBR Green 1 Master Mix from Roche, with GAPDH serving as the internal control.

Measurements of TG and ATP levels

The myocardial TG level was quantified using a commercially available kit (#A110, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) following the manufacturer’s protocols. For the assessment of cardiac ATP levels (#S0026, Beyotime), fresh heart samples were homogenized and centrifuged to isolate cell-free supernatants. These supernatants were subsequently treated with ATP detection working solution. Luminescence values were recorded and then standardized against total protein content.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted from mouse heart tissues using a lysis buffer containing a mixture of protease inhibitor cocktail (#11,852,700, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and phosphatase inhibitor (#04906837001, Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay kit (#23,227, Invitrogen). The proteins were electrophoresed on SDS-PAGE (10% or 12% gel) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (#IPFL00010, Millipore, MA, USA) following established methods [28]. After a 2-h sealing period with a 5% skimmed milk solution, the membranes underwent overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-BAX (#2772, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA); anti-BCL2 (#3498, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA); anti-SOD2 (#113,141, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA); anti-AMPKα (#2542, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA); anti-ACC (#2178, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA); anti-OXPHOS (ab110413, Abcam, Cambridge, UK); and anti-GAPDH (#2118, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Following this, the membranes were exposed to secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase for 2 h at room temperature. Visualization of the blots was achieved using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (#1,705,061, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, USA), and the resulting images were analyzed utilizing ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistics

All data is presented as the mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were employed for comparisons between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey post hoc tests was utilized for comparisons involving three or more groups. Changes over time between groups were assessed via repeated measures analysis of variance. A P-value below 0.05 indicates statistical significance.

Results

Dapagliflozin sensitizes renal cell carcinoma to sunitinib therapy, alleviating sunitinib resistance

RCC is one of the most common cancers worldwide. Sunitinib, a multitarget receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) inhibitor, is a first-line targeted drug for recurrent and unresectable RCC patients and is approved by the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology [29]. To evaluate the effect of dapagliflozin on the anti-tumor activity of sunitinib, 786-O cells were subcutaneously implanted into the left flank of nude mice. Once the tumor volume reached 50 mm3, nude mice were treated orally with dapagliflozin and sunitinib for 21 days (Fig. 1A). Moreover, the body weight of nude mice was detected every 3 days to evaluate toxicity of dapagliflozin and sunitinib, and there was no substantial weight loss in the treated mice (Additional file 1: Fig. S3D). In comparison to control nude mice, those administered sunitinib exhibited a marked decrease in tumor size. Notably, mice treated with sunitinib in combination with dapagliflozin demonstrated significantly reduced tumor volumes when compared to those treated solely with sunitinib (Fig. 1B, C). To further investigate the synergistic effect of dapagliflozin on the anticancer activity of sunitinib, we assessed the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values in RCC cells following treatment with sunitinib and dapagliflozin. As demonstrated in Fig. 1D, dapagliflozin treatment significantly reduced the IC50 values of sunitinib in both 786-O and 769-P cell lines, suggesting its potential role in modulating sunitinib sensitivity in RCC. Approximately 10–20% of patients with advanced RCC exhibit primary resistance to sunitinib, and most of the remaining patients may develop acquired drug resistance and tumor progression after 6–15 months of therapy [30]. To further investigate the therapeutic potential of dapagliflozin in counteracting sunitinib resistance, sunitinib-resistant RCC cell lines (786-O-R and 769-P-R) were generated through chronic, dose-escalating exposure to sunitinib (2–10 μM) over a 10-month period (Fig. 1E). As expected, sunitinib-resistant cells exhibited enhanced proliferation in response to sunitinib treatment compared to the parental cells (Fig. 1F, Additional file 1: Fig. S2A). In 786-O-R and 769-P-R cells, treatment with dapagliflozin consistently reduced the IC50 values of sunitinib as well, indicating that dapagliflozin alleviated sunitinib resistance in RCC (Fig. 1G, Additional file 1: Fig. S2B). These findings suggest that dapagliflozin could enhance the sensitivity of RCC cells to sunitinib treatment, thereby augmenting the antitumor efficacy of sunitinib.

Dapagliflozin administration mitigated sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity in nude mice

Dapagliflozin is a highly selective SGLT2 inhibitor with potential effects on glucose and insulin metabolism; we assessed systemic blood glucose and insulin levels 21 days after dapagliflozin treatment. As depicted in Additional file 1: Fig. S3A, no significant differences were observed in fasting blood glucose or insulin levels across the four experimental groups following dapagliflozin administration. Similarly, no significant changes in liver function, glycosuria, and body weight were observed in nude mice following dapagliflozin treatment (Additional file 1: Fig. S3B–D). Sunitinib treatment provoked severe myocardial injury in nude mice, demonstrated by pronounced increases in the cardiac damage markers CK-MB and LDH [31]. However, co-administration of dapagliflozin effectively attenuated these sunitinib-induced cardiotoxic responses (Fig. 2A). Sunitinib has been shown to trigger cardiomyocyte apoptosis; however, this effect can be attenuated by dapagliflozin treatment (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, dapagliflozin administration attenuated the elevation in caspase3 activity induced by sunitinib (Fig. 2C). Consistent with our expectations, dapagliflozin reduced C-caspase3 and BAX expression and increased BCL2 and SOD2 expression upon sunitinib stimulation (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A, B). Next, we examined the potential of dapagliflozin to attenuate sunitinib-mediated oxidative stress injury in the heart. As shown in Fig. 2D–E, dapagliflozin treatment significantly attenuated sunitinib-induced ROS generation, accompanied by reduced levels of H2O2 and O2⁻. Glutathione (GSH), the predominant cellular redox buffer in the heart, was significantly depleted upon sunitinib exposure, while dapagliflozin treatment effectively restored its concentration (Fig. 2F). We also observed that dapagliflozin counteracted sunitinib’s stimulatory effects on abnormal MDA accumulation as well as 3-NT and 4-HNE generation (Additional file 1: Fig. S4C). Previous clinical studies have demonstrated that sunitinib treatment leads to reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and elevated mean systolic blood pressure in patients [14]. We first measured the mean arterial pressure (MAP) in nude mice, which revealed that sunitinib treatment significantly elevated MAP, while dapagliflozin administration effectively suppressed this increase (Fig. 2G). As anticipated, sunitinib treatment induced significant cardiac dysfunction in nude mice, evidenced by reduced ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS). Dapagliflozin administration significantly prevented these deleterious hemodynamic alterations (Fig. 2H, I). Subsequently, we investigated whether sunitinib treatment would induce cardiac remodeling. Consistent with prior reports [32], sunitinib administration promoted significant myocardial fibrosis in murine models, as demonstrated by PSR staining. Notably, dapagliflozin treatment significantly ameliorated this fibrotic response (Fig. 2J). Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining revealed no statistically significant differences in cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area across all four experimental groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S4D). Besides, no significant differences in heart weight-to-body weight ratios were observed among the four experimental groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S4E). Dapagliflozin attenuates sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity via SGLT2-independent pathways in C57BL/6 mice

As dapagliflozin is a highly selective sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor, we initially characterized the tissue distribution patterns of SGLT2 expression to elucidate the potential dependence of its cardioprotective effects on SGLT2 signaling in sunitinib-associated cardiotoxicity. As shown in Fig. 3A, immunoblotting analysis revealed that SGLT2 expression was exclusively detected in kidney tissue, with no observable expression in intestinal or cardiac tissues, including isolated cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts. Notably, we identified that SGLT1 (a paralog of SGLT2 responsible for the residual 10% of urinary glucose reabsorption) exhibited distinct tissue distribution patterns, with detectable expression in renal, intestinal, and cardiac tissues but absence in isolated primary cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts (Fig. 3A). Next, we knocked down SGLT2 with shSglt2 in C57BL/6 mice. The expression of SGLT2 was blocked in the kidney, while the protein level of SGLT1 was slightly increased in kidney tissue, but not in heart tissue and small intestine tissue in SGLT2-silenced mice, indicating an organ-specific adaptive response to SGLT2 functional deficiency (Fig. 3B). To investigate sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity and its underlying mechanisms, SGLT2-knockdown mice were administered daily oral doses of sunitinib (40 mg/kg) and dapagliflozin (1.5 mg/kg) for 21 consecutive days. Consistent with previous reports, sunitinib treatment significantly reduced both EF and FS in mice. Dapagliflozin administration effectively attenuated these impairments; notably, SGLT2 knockdown could not attenuate sunitinib-induced cardiac dysfunction and did not alter dapagliflozin’s cardioprotective efficacy (Fig. 3C, D). Moreover, sunitinib treatment significantly increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis, which was markedly attenuated by dapagliflozin co-treatment. However, SGLT2 knockdown did not affect dapagliflozin’s anti-apoptotic efficacy (Fig. 3E). Consistent with apoptosis findings, SGLT2 knockdown neither altered sunitinib-induced ROS overproduction nor impaired dapagliflozin’s ROS-scavenging capacity (Fig. 3F–H). Moreover, PSR staining revealed that dapagliflozin treatment significantly attenuated sunitinib-induced myocardial fibrosis independent of SGLT2 (Fig. 3G, I). Besides, immunofluorescence staining confirmed that sunitinib induced inflammatory cell infiltration in myocardial tissue, whereas dapagliflozin effectively attenuated this effect (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). Dapagliflozin prevents sunitinib-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and lipotoxicity

To further investigate the impact of dapagliflozin on mitigating sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity, we conducted RNA sequencing on cardiac tissues. This analysis revealed differential expression of 3885 genes (fold change > 2; adjusted P value < 0.05) between sunitinib-treated and sunitinib + dapagliflozin-treated groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S6A). The upregulated KEGG pathways indicate that oxidative phosphorylation may be a pivotal factor in the improvement of sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity by dapagliflozin (Fig. 4A). Analysis of the transcriptome data indicated that treatment with dapagliflozin significantly upregulated genes related to the mitochondrial ETC (Fig. 4B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) also indicated a significant increase in oxidative phosphorylation in the heart following treatment with dapagliflozin (Additional file 1: Fig. S6B). A subset of target genes identified in the transcriptome analysis was further validated through quantitative real-time PCR (Additional file 1: Fig. S6C). Furthermore, immunoblotting analysis of the primary subunits from complex I to V revealed a significant reduction in the expression of mitochondrial complexes by sunitinib, whereas dapagliflozin reinstated their expression (Fig. 4C). Sunitinib similarly attenuated the activity of mitochondrial complexes, whereas co-treatment with dapagliflozin prevented the decline in mitochondrial complex activity (Fig. 4D). Moreover, the KEGG and GO analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) unveiled that various signaling pathways were intricately linked to metabolism, particularly focusing on fatty acid metabolism and the citrate cycle (TCA) (Fig. 4A, Additional file 1: Fig S7A, B). The heart primarily relies on fatty acids to generate energy. Fatty acid metabolism produces acetyl-CoA, FADH2, and NADH. Acetyl-CoA enters the TCA to generate ATP, along with FADH2 and NADH. Subsequently, FADH2 and NADH are converted through the ETC to produce ATP, which sustains cardiac energy supply (Fig. 4E). As anticipated, findings from Nile red staining suggested a significant escalation in lipid accumulation in the hearts following sunitinib treatment (Fig. S7C). Intriguingly, dapagliflozin proficiently suppressed lipid accumulation, concomitant with a reduction in triglyceride (TG) levels in the hearts (Additional file 1: Fig S7C, D). It is known that inhibition of the ETC complexes disrupts mitochondrial ATP production and NAD +/NADH balance due to hindered electron flow and impaired oxidative phosphorylation [33, 34]. As depicted in Fig. 4F, G, the decline in ATP levels and Mito NAD/NADH ratio in the heart induced by sunitinib were reversed upon administration of dapagliflozin. Defective mitochondrial function was closely linked with lipid peroxidation and ATP production [35]. The administration of dapagliflozin reinstated mitochondrial quantity, cristae density, and mitigated mitochondrial swelling (Fig. 4H, I). Furthermore, the augmented mitochondrial count in sunitinib-exposed hearts following dapagliflozin treatment was corroborated by heightened mtDNA/nDNA ratios (Fig. 4J). In conclusion, the findings of the study confirm that sunitinib treatment not only induces myocardial oxidative stress and cardiomyocyte apoptosis but also impacts mitochondrial function, leading to disruptions in fatty acid metabolism and inadequate energy supply to the heart. To further confirm the beneficial effects of dapagliflozin on sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity in vitro, neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) were isolated from the neonatal rats. As shown in Fig. 5A, the viability of NRCMs gradually decreases with increasing concentrations of sunitinib treatment, prompting us to conduct subsequent experiments using a concentration of 6 μM. Dapagliflozin significantly improved cell viability in sunitinib-treated NRCMs (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, dapagliflozin significantly reduced the presence of TUNEL-positive nuclei in sunitinib-exposed NRCMs (Fig. 5C). Additionally, dapagliflozin-treated NRCMs displayed diminished generation of free radicals in response to sunitinib, as demonstrated by DCFH-DA staining (Fig. 5D). The elevation of MDA, 4-HNE, and 3-NT levels induced by sunitinib was mitigated by dapagliflozin administration (Fig. 5E). In alignment with these changes, BCL2 and SOD2 protein levels were elevated, while BAX protein levels were reduced in dapagliflozin-treated NRCMs under sunitinib stimulation (Additional file 1: Fig. S8A). Moreover, dapagliflozin attenuated sunitinib-induced lipid accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 5F, G). Similar to in vivo results, dapagliflozin stimulation recovered ATP production in NRCMs (Fig. 5H). Subsequently, we assessed the impact of dapagliflozin on mitochondrial respiratory capacity by evaluating the oxygen consumption rate (OCR), a robust marker of mitochondrial function. Treatment with sunitinib led to a decrease in the maximal OCR, which was reversed by dapagliflozin in NRCMs (Fig. 5I, J). Dapagliflozin ameliorates sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity through PPAR signaling pathway

Next, we investigated the mechanism by which dapagliflozin improves sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, treatment with dapagliflozin markedly enhanced the PPAR signaling pathway, a nuclear receptor superfamily that governs mitochondrial function and lipid metabolism regulation [36, 37]. Similarly, our transcriptome data demonstrated that numerous downstream targets of the PPAR signaling pathway, particularly Ppara in sunitinib-stimulated hearts, were upregulated by dapagliflozin administration (Additional file 1: Fig. S9A). Hence, we initially assessed whether dapagliflozin mitigated sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity through the restoration of the PPAR signaling pathway. Both transcriptome analysis and PCR data revealed a significant increase in the transcription levels of Ppara following dapagliflozin stimulation, while Pparg and Ppard showed no apparent changes (Fig. 6A, Additional file 1: Fig S9B). In addition, certain co-activators essential for the functioning of PPAR proteins in the regulation of mitochondrial function and lipid metabolism, including estrogen-related receptors (ERRs, encoded by Esrrb and Esrrg) and PGC1 (encoded by Ppargc1a and Ppargc1b), were also upregulated upon dapagliflozin administration, without impacting mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) and Esrra (Fig. 6A, Additional file 1: Fig. S9B) [38]. Immunoblotting analysis results indicated that dapagliflozin can enhance the expression and nuclear accumulation of PPARα in the heart following sunitinib stimulation (Fig. 6C). Surprisingly, dapagliflozin only promoted the expression of PGC1α in the heart after sunitinib stimulation, without affecting the expression of PGC1β, ESRRβ, and ESRRγ (Fig. 6B). In order to confirm the essential role of PPARα, GW6471 was employed to inhibit PPARα. As depicted in Fig. 6D, the protective effect of dapagliflozin against sunitinib-induced cardiac dysfunction was abolished by GW6471. Consequently, dapagliflozin failed to improve cardiomyocyte apoptosis and ROS over-production after GW6471 administration (Fig. 6E–G, Additional file 1: Fig. S9C). Likewise, the suppressive impact of dapagliflozin on sunitinib-induced cardiac remodeling was counteracted by PPARα inhibition (Fig. 6F, H). Moreover, the ameliorative effects of dapagliflozin on sunitinib-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid accumulation were impeded following PPARα inhibition (Fig. 6I, Additional file 1: Fig. S9D–E). To further ascertain the specific role of PPARα rather than PPARδ and PPARγ, we inhibited PPARδ with GSK3787 and inhibited PPARγ with GW9662. As expect, the administration of PPARδ or PPARγ inhibitors did not influence cardiomyocyte apoptosis, cardiac dysfunction, or mitochondrial dysfunction in sunitinib-stimulated hearts (Additional file 1: Fig. S10A–D). To elucidate the role of PGC1α, we suppressed PGC1α with SR-18292. As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S11A–E, the suppressive impact of dapagliflozin on sunitinib-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis, cardiac dysfunction, and mitochondrial dysfunction was significantly nullified by PGC1α inhibition. Dapagliflozin enhances the PPARα signaling pathway through AMPKα activation

AMPKα emerges as a promising target for restoring metabolic disorders and cardiac dysfunction by modulating PPARα [39]. Furthermore, a prior investigation demonstrated that dapagliflozin induces AMPK phosphorylation, thereby mitigating hypoxia/reoxygenation-induced cardiac dysfunction and oxidative damage [40]. Our transcriptomic analysis unveiled that dapagliflozin administration elevated various downstream targets of the AMPK signaling pathway in sunitinib-stimulated hearts (Fig. 7A), particularly highlighting the upregulation of Ppargc1a, identified by our study, as a pivotal factor in dapagliflozin-mediated protective effects against sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity. Subsequently, we assessed whether AMPK plays a pivotal role in the protective effects mediated by dapagliflozin. As shown in Fig. 7B, dapagliflozin treatment rescued the decreased levels of p-AMPKα induced by sunitinib stimulation. Concurrently, dapagliflozin treatment also restored the activity of AMPKα in the heart, primarily reflected by the elevation of p-ACC levels. As expected, inhibition of AMPKα by BAY-3827 resulted in a decrease in the levels of PPARα and PGC1α in the heart once again (Fig. 7C). Meanwhile, the suppressive impact of dapagliflozin on sunitinib-induced apoptosis and oxidative damage was nullified by the inhibition of AMPKα (Fig. 7D–F, Additional file 1: Fig. S12A). Additionally, dapagliflozin administration failed to ameliorate sunitinib-related cardiac dysfunction after AMPKα inhibition, as determined by the decreased EF and FS, and increased fibrotic area (Fig. 7G, Additional file 1: Fig. S12B). Consistent with the above findings, the enhanced mitochondrial function conferred by dapagliflozin administration was also negated by AMPKα inhibition (Additional file 1: Fig. S12C–E). Next, we used AICAR to activate AMPKα in order to investigate whether activation of the AMPKα-PPARα pathway could further alleviate sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity. As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S13, AICAR further attenuated sunitinib-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis and oxidative stress, and reduced triglyceride accumulation in cardiac tissue. Moreover, AICAR treatment led to a further increase in myocardial ATP levels. However, AICAR did not appear to further enhance the beneficial effects of dapagliflozin on sunitinib-induced cardiac dysfunction. We next performed validation experiments to determine whether the AMPKα-PPARα pathway mediates the protective effects of dapagliflozin in tumor-bearing mice. As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S14, consistent with the observations in non-tumor-bearing mice, dapagliflozin treatment activated the AMPKα-PPARα signaling pathway. Moreover, inhibition of AMPK with BAY-3827 in tumor-bearing mice abolished the protective effects of dapagliflozin against sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity. Discussion

Sunitinib has exhibited considerable clinical efficacy in managing advanced or metastatic RCC. Nevertheless, cardiovascular toxicity associated with sunitinib treatment poses a significant barrier to its widespread safe utilization in clinical settings. Multiple investigations have demonstrated that patients undergoing sunitinib therapy often present a heightened prevalence of hypertension, reduce LVEF, and congestive heart failure [41–43]. Among patients treated with sunitinib at our institution, 15% developed symptomatic grade 3/4 heart failure [43]. Future studies of sunitinib-related cardiotoxicity are urgently needed, particularly as the oncologic indications for this drug continue to expand. In this study, we found that dapagliflozin not only attenuates sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity but also potentiates its antitumor efficacy. We found that the cardiac toxicity of sunitinib treatment not only involves stimulating myocardial cell apoptosis and promoting excessive ROS generation but also affects the ETC, leading to cardiac energy metabolism disturbances and influencing cardiac fatty acid metabolism. The metabolic toxicity of sunitinib on the heart may be related to its impact on mitochondrial function, as previous studies have confirmed that sunitinib treatment results in impaired cardiac mitochondrial function [14, 44]. Interestingly, dapagliflozin exerts its effects by enhancing the PPAR signaling pathway to ameliorate sunitinib-induced cardiac toxicity, rather than depending on SGLT2. Mechanistically, AMPKα is required for dapagliflozin mediated activation of PPARα.

Although sunitinib and other VEGF-directed therapies have significantly improved clinical outcomes for RCC patients, they have also been associated with several cardiovascular toxicities, including hypertension, LV dysfunction, and heart failure [45, 46]. Several retrospective studies performed in a variety of treatment settings have reported that the incidence of sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity specifically in RCC patients ranges from approximately 3 to 30% [11, 47]. Clinical trials have substantiated that 10 out of 36 (28%) patients receiving the recommended sunitinib dosage experienced absolute reductions in LVEF of at least 10%, with 7 out of 36 (19%) exhibiting LVEF reductions of 15% or more. Sunitinib led to elevated average systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels, and 35 out of 75 (47%) subjects developed hypertension exceeding 150/100 mm Hg [14]. Considering the therapeutic significance of VEGF receptor TKIs, it is imperative to enhance our comprehension of the cardiovascular risks associated with treatment in the broader RCC patient cohort, with the aim of informing efficient cardioprotective surveillance protocols. In this study, we observed that dapagliflozin concurrently ameliorates the cardiac toxicity induced by sunitinib and enhances the anti-tumor efficacy of sunitinib. Surprisingly, we found that dapagliflozin monotherapy alone can also delay tumor growth rates (Fig. 1B, C). Consistent with the results of our study, Wang and co-workers identified that dapagliflozin has high efficiency and low toxicity and could be a new therapeutic target for RCC, and dapagliflozin exerts a higher cytotoxic effect on human RCC, regulates the cell cycle and apoptosis, and reduces the glucose uptake and SGLT2 expression of Caki-1 cells [21]. Besides, Kim et al. suggested that dapagliflozin may be a feasible drug candidate for the treatment of human renal cancer [48]. Nevertheless, a pharmacovigilance assessment utilizing the US FDA Adverse Event Reporting System revealed an imbalanced correlation between SGLT2 inhibitors and renal cancer [49]. Further clinical trials are required to determine whether dapagliflozin can function independently as an anti-tumor agent for RCC. In addition, we selected 6 µM dapagliflozin for the treatment of NRCMs based on previous studies, without performing a dose–response experiment. Future investigations assessing the dose-dependent cardioprotective effects of dapagliflozin would therefore be valuable.

The heart exhibits a notable metabolic adaptability enabling it to modify its energetic substrates, and mitochondria are the primary site for fatty acid oxidation and provide 90% of the ATP in heart. However, in pathological states such as heart failure, the heart loses its metabolic flexibility and capacity to efficiently generate energy [50]. This metabolic deficiency, in turn, impacts cardiomyocyte biology and mitochondrial function [51]. Our study revealed that sunitinib treatment affects ETC activity, thereby impacting cardiac energy metabolism and leading to myocardial lipid accumulation, potentially attributed to sunitinib-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. Fortuitously, dapagliflozin can effectively ameliorate the mitochondrial toxicity induced by sunitinib. Previous study found that dapagliflozin could decrease cardiac apoptosis and improve cardiac mitochondrial function, biogenesis, and dynamics in cardiac ischemia/reperfusion injury [52]. PPARα is highly expressed in cardiac muscle and plays essential roles in regulating mitochondrial homeostasis by interacting with other co-activators [53]. Although adaptability can elevate the transcription levels of co-activators such as Ppargc1a, Ppargc1b, Esrrg, and Esrrb, it only enhances the expression level of Ppargc1a. Several earlier studies have identified that the PPARα/PGC1α axis regulates cardiac mitochondrial function [37, 54]. Our findings indicate that the improvement of sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity by dapagliflozin is lost after PPARα inhibition. A limitation is that we did not utilize transgenic mice in our study; employing transgenic mice would enhance the precision of our results. Mechanistically, dapagliflozin elevated the expression and nuclear accumulation of PPARα in an AMPKα-dependent manner. Dapagliflozin, as a selective SGLT2 inhibitor, reduces intracellular glucose availability and promotes a mild energetic stress, leading to an increased AMP/ATP ratio, which directly activates AMPKα through phosphorylation at Thr172 by upstream kinases such as LKB1 [55, 56]. In addition, several studies have shown that dapagliflozin enhances mitochondrial efficiency and decreases oxidative stress, both of which can indirectly stabilize AMPKα activation [40, 57]. Consistent with these findings, our transcriptomic analysis showed enrichment of AMPK signaling and upregulation of its downstream metabolic targets following dapagliflozin treatment. In our experiments, dapagliflozin restored sunitinib-suppressed p-AMPKα and p-ACC levels, indicating recovery of cellular energy sensing. Importantly, inhibition of AMPKα with BAY-3827 abolished these effects, supporting a causal role of AMPKα activation in mediating the action of dapagliflozin. Taken together, dapagliflozin may promote AMPKα phosphorylation primarily by inducing a cellular energy shift (increased AMP/ATP ratio) and enhancing mitochondrial function, thereby activating the upstream LKB1-AMPK axis. Although our study demonstrates that dapagliflozin enhances the antitumor response to sunitinib, the underlying mechanism remains incompletely defined. Based on prior evidence, several plausible explanations include metabolic reprogramming through AMPK activation, potential mitochondrial impairment, reduced glucose availability in tumor cells, alterations in lipid metabolism, and modulation of the tumor microenvironment [58–60]. These hypotheses provide a biologically reasonable framework but require further validation. Future studies will focus on assessing metabolic and mitochondrial signaling in tumor cells, as well as determining whether dapagliflozin influences intracellular sunitinib accumulation or resistance-related pathways.

Conclusions

Our study proposes that the AMPKα-PPARα axis plays a pivotal role in the protective effects of dapagliflozin against sunitinib-induced cardiotoxicity in mice. Above all, our study has identified a potentially valuable mechanistically based therapeutic application for combating sunitinib-induced cardiac toxicity.
