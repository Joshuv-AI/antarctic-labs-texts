Congenital hepatic arteriovenous fistula: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Xinghui Yu, Abdisamat Ababakri, Yan Xie, Wentao Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatic arteriovenous fistula refers to the direct abnormal communication between the hepatic artery and the portal vein or hepatic vein. Clinical symptoms of hepatic arteriovenous fistula primarily depend on the blood flow volume, location, and duration of the fistula. Symptoms may include liver fibrosis, portal hypertension, enlarged heart, and ultimately heart failure. Congenital hepatic artery-portal fistula is a rare condition, predominantly observed in children or infants. Although interventional hepatic artery embolization is typically the initial treatment choice, it may not always be effective.Case presentation.We present a case of a 22-year-old Chinese male patient with hepatic arteriovenous fistula who underwent multiple interventional therapies. The patient was diagnosed with “hepatic artery-portal fistula, viral hepatitis B, and ascites” at Shengjing Hospital in August 2018, following complaints of abdominal distension. Hepatic artery embolization and diuretic treatment were administered, resulting in symptom relief. However, after discharge, the patient experienced recurrent abdominal distension, hematemesis, and melena. Consequently, hepatic artery embolization was performed periodically in May 2020, November 2020, January 2021, May 2021, and August 2021 to manage these conditions. During the hepatic artery embolization procedure, the contrast agent was monitored entering the portal vein directly from the hepatic artery. In the present case, interventional therapy was unable to completely block the fistula, providing only temporary relief. Consequently, liver transplantation is considered the ultimate treatment option for complex and diffuse intrahepatic vascular fistulas following hepatic artery embolization. The patient has currently been approved and registered for liver transplantation.ConclusionsPatients with diffuse hepatic arteriovenous fistula are prone to symptoms of portal hypertension, such as gastrointestinal bleeding caused by esophageal-gastric varices and ascites resulting from poor venous return, which significantly impact patient safety and quality of life. Arterial embolization provides only temporary symptom control. In cases where diffuse hepatic arteriovenous fistula is suspected or when interventional treatment fails, prompt liver transplantation should be considered. Therefore, this case report aims to enhance the understanding of the timing and selection of hepatic arteriovenous fistula treatment, along with providing a review of previous studies.Keywords: Congenital hepatic arteriovenous fistula, Liver transplantation, Hepatic artery embolization, Portal hypertension

Case presentation

A 22-year-old Chinese male patient presented with abdominal distension. He was initially diagnosed with “hepatic artery-portal fistula, viral hepatitis B, and ascites” at Shengjing Hospital in August 2018, following complaints of abdominal distension. He underwent hepatic artery embolization and received diuretic treatment, which provided relief from his symptoms. After discharge, the patient experienced recurrent episodes of abdominal distension, hematemesis, and melena. To manage his condition, hepatic artery embolization was performed periodically in the following months: May 2020, November 2020, January 2021, May 2021, and August 2021. Additionally, 3 weeks prior to the time of writing, the patient had presented with approximately 500 mL of melena without an obvious cause, leading to immediate treatment at Shengjing Hospital. Conservative measures, including stomach acid suppression, hemostasis, and anemia correction, were implemented, resulting in temporary stabilization of his condition. The patient’s medical record revealed a 22-year history of viral hepatitis B and the use of tenofovir alafenamide since May 2020. He has no personal history of smoking or alcoholism. Regarding his family history, his father is in good health, while his mother is a carrier of hepatitis B. The patient was referred to our hospital for preoperative evaluation for liver transplantation. Liver function tests did not show any significant abnormalities, and the patient’s anemia improved after receiving blood transfusion (Table 1). However, this test for hepatitis B virus yielded a positive result (Table 2). Angiography performed during interventional embolization through the femoral artery revealed the direct flow of contrast agent from the hepatic artery into the portal vein (Fig. 1). Further examination of the liver using plain computed tomography (CT) scan revealed irregular liver shape and small nodular high-density shadows in the right lobe (S5, 6, and 8), measuring less than 5 mm in diameter. The left lobe of the liver, common hepatic artery, and splenic artery displayed multiple metallic dense shadows and radial metal artifacts (Fig. 2). Enhanced CT scan of the liver did not reveal any abnormal enhancement in liver parenchyma (Fig. 3). Reconstructed CT scan of the liver exhibited multiple dense metallic shadows in the course of the common hepatic artery, splenic artery, left portal vein, and left hepatic vein. Additionally, tortuous vascular shadows were observed in the splenic hilum area and around the esophagus and gastric fundus (Fig. 4). Gastroscopy identified four varicose veins in the esophagus, located 20 cm away from the incisors, extending to the cardia. Multiple varicose veins were also observed in the fundus of the stomach near the cardia. The anterior pyloric area of the gastric antrum displayed scattered flake hyperemia and localized flake erosion (Fig. 5). To manage and prevent gastrointestinal bleeding, varicose veins were treated through injection of sclerosing agent and band ligation during gastroscopy.

Literature review

A literature search was conducted using PubMed to gather English language reports on congenital HAVF and its treatment over the past 20 years, from 2001 to 2021. A total of ten publications were selected, which included 26 patients spanning from neonates to a 73-year-old woman. Clinical symptoms observed in these patients comprised abdominal pain, diarrhea, hematemesis, melena, anemia, ascites, severe undernutrition, cardiac dilatation, and heart failure. Treatment options included endovascular embolization (n = 22, 84.6%), beta-blocker therapy (n = 2, 7.7%), and surgical interventions (n = 2, 7.7%), such as liver transplantation and left hepatectomy (Table 3) [1–10].

Following embolization treatment, two children died due to progressive liver dysfunction and abdominal compartment syndrome. Additionally, one child required liver transplantation after interventional embolization. However, the remaining patients maintained a favorable condition throughout the follow-up period. Liver imaging conducted 8–9 months after beta blocker therapy indicated the disappearance of congenital HAVF in two neonates. In the case of a 2-year-old girl with ineffective radiographic embolization and surgical ligation of the left hepatic artery, left hepatectomy was performed as a means to treat the HAVF. Another patient with congenital HAVF underwent three interventional embolizations, but these failed to improve the symptoms of cardiac dilatation and heart failure. Subsequently, this patient received a liver transplantation, which resulted in recovery.

Discussion

HAVF refers to an abnormal direct communication between the hepatic artery and the hepatic vein or portal vein. These three types of shunts indicate abnormal connections among the hepatic artery, hepatic vein, and portal vein.

The causes of this condition can be classified as congenital or iatrogenic, the latter resulting from procedures such as liver biopsy, transhepatic biliary puncture, or surgery [11]. While most iatrogenic HAVFs can be successfully treated with conservative approaches or interventional embolization, the patient described in this case presents with a congenital HAVF. Congenital arteriovenous fistulas are rare, occurring more commonly in children, and they often have a more severe presentation, faster progression, and higher mortality rate [2]. The occurrence of congenital HAVF in a 22-year-old Chinese male patient is unusual. Currently, there is no standardized management plan for this situation.

The clinical symptoms of HAVF in patients primarily depend on the location, blood flow, and duration of the fistula [12]. Patients with HAVF may experience symptoms such as portal hypertension, liver fibrosis, spontaneous liver hemorrhage, cardiomegaly, and even heart failure [13, 14]. Digital subtraction angiography is the gold standard for diagnosing HAVF. Once the cause, location, and extent of the HAVF are determined, hepatic artery embolization is typically considered as the first-line treatment. However, there are no biomarkers or additional criteria to identify patients at higher risk of embolization failure for the rarity of HAVF. For unsuccessful embolization, complex or diffuse intrahepatic vascular fistulas, surgical options such as liver transplantation should be considered, potentially serving as the final treatment option. However, there have been limited reports on the use of liver transplantation for HAVF [3]. In the future, with the increasing discovery of HAVF, there is an opportunity to expand its embolization alternatives and liver transplantation standards.

In this case, the diagnosis of congenital diffuse HAVF is clear, and the patient has experienced recurrent symptoms including abdominal distension, melena, and hematemesis. As a result, some experts suggest liver transplantation as a treatment option for recurrent or refractory portal hypertension [15]. Liver transplantation has been proposed as a long-term solution for cases of recurrent portal hypertension [16]. However, there is no consensus regarding the optimal timing of liver transplantation for patients with HAVF, particularly in cases of recurrent or refractory portal hypertension. Considering the patient’s age, the progressive status of the disease, and the limitations of interventional embolization, early liver transplantation is recommended upon diagnosis of diffuse congenital HAVF to prevent complications associated with portal hypertension, such as hypersplenism, primary peritonitis, gastrointestinal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, hypoproteinemia, and end-stage liver failure [17].

Conclusion

We present a case of congenital HAVF, which contributes to the understanding of treatment options for this rare condition. The patient experienced recurrent symptoms of abdominal distension, melena, and hematemesis, which persisted despite six episodes of interventional embolization. Therefore, for patients with complex and diffuse HAVF, prompt liver transplantation is recommended to control disease progression and prevent complications associated with portal hypertension.
