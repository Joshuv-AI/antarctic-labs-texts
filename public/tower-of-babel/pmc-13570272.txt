A practical workflow for analyzing organelle spatial proximity with super-resolution microscopy
Authors: Xiaoyu Ren, Ying Fan, Wenwen Jing, Junjing Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organelles establish dynamic contacts to facilitate inter-organelle communication. Although their structures and dynamics are commonly observed by light microscopy, the spatial precision of organelle proximity is restricted by optical diffraction limit. While super-resolution microscopy has significantly advanced the visualization of subcellular ultrastructure, optimizing imaging parameters and robust downstream analysis remains a key challenge. Here we present a practical workflow for live-cell super-resolution imaging and quantitative analysis of organelle spatial proximity. By combining super-resolution microscopy and machine learning-driven batch image processing, this method enables accurate estimation of organelle morphology and juxtapositions. Notably, this workflow is broadly adaptable to different subcellular structures, labeling strategies and imaging conditions. It is designed to be accessible to most cell biology laboratories without requiring large-scale training datasets or extensive computational resources.

Introduction

Eukaryotic cells are compartmentalized into functionally specialized organelles that act in concert. Organelles form dynamic contact sites to exchange lipids, calcium, proteins etc. [1] The two membranes engaged in contact are juxtaposed within approximately 10-80 nm, and stabilized by molecular tethers without undergoing fusion [2]. However, the contact sites are often transient and spatially restricted, making their visualization and quantification an essential methodological challenge in cell biology.

Imaging organelle contact sites requires approaches with high spatial and temporal resolution. Though conventional light microscopy has been widely used to monitor organelle morphology and dynamics, its spatial resolution is limited by the optical diffraction barrier of ∼200 nm. Super-resolution microscopy provides an effective solution to this challenge. Among available super-resolution techniques, structured illumination microscopy (SIM) and pixel-reassignment techniques such as Airyscan, offer practical advantages for live-cell imaging [3]. These approaches combine improved spatial resolution (84 nm for SIM and 120 nm for Airyscan), with relatively low phototoxicity and rapid acquisition speed [4,5], making them well suited for long-term imaging of dynamic organelle interactions in living cells.

After acquiring raw data, image restoration and processing are required to extract organelle morphology for downstream quantitative analysis. Segmentation of fluorescence image is typically performed using intensity-based thresholding [6]. However, this approach often fails to achieve accurate segmentation in super-resolution images, as the complex subcellular structures and heterogeneous fluorescence patterns complicate threshold selection for accurately resolving fine structures. In contrast, machine-learning-based segmentation provides a more flexible and reliable alternative. Instead of relying on pixel intensity alone, supervised pixel classification incorporates multiple image features, including intensity, texture and edge information, allowing more precise segmentation of biological structures from tissue to subcellular organelles [[7], [8], [9]]. Moreover, user-friendly machine-learning tools such as ilastik allow researchers to train segmentation classifiers using only a few manual annotations, without requiring extensive programming experience [10]. When combined with widely-used platforms such as ImageJ/Fiji, this strategy provides an accessible workflow for processing and analyzing super-resolution images.

Here, we present a practical imaging and quantification workflow for analyzing organelle spatial proximity with super-resolution microscopy. This workflow encompasses SIM and Airyscan microscopy for monitoring organelle interactions, image processing, image segmentation by pixel classification, and quantitative analysis of inter-organelle distances. By harnessing widely-used open-source software, including ImageJ/Fiji and ilastik, this workflow provides a generalizable strategy for analyzing organelle juxtapositions. This approach may help researchers move beyond conventional fluorescence colocalization measurement toward more direct and morphology-based quantification of organelle spatial proximity.

This workflow comprises super-resolution imaging, image reconstruction, pixel classification-based segmentation, and quantitative analysis of organelle spatial proximity (Fig. 1). To acquire images with high spatial precision, rapid acquisition rate, and low phototoxicity, we employed Hessian-SIM and Zeiss Airyscan super-resolution microscopes, and captured the ultrastructure of mitochondria outer membrane, mitochondria inner membrane and microtubule cytoskeleton (Fig. 2A). Compared to confocal microscopy, SIM and Airyscan provided more detailed delineation of subcellular structures. Especially for mitochondria, both super-resolution techniques could resolve the fine structure of outer and inner membrane, while confocal microscope only captured the overall mitochondria morphology. Reconstruction and pre-processing of raw super-resolution data

Both SIM and Airyscan techniques required image reconstruction of raw data. The Airyscan raw images were acquired using a 32-element detector array [4], and then mathematically combined using Zeiss commercial software (Fig. 2B). SIM images were acquired with patterned illumination of 3 phase shifts and 3 rotations (2D) or 5 rotations (3D) [11]. Raw data were processed by frequency unmixing and shifting in Fourier space, followed by Wiener deconvolution and sparse deconvolution, which could be performed using commercial software and open-source ImageJ/Fiji plugins [12,13]. Deconvolution contributed to reduced image noise and improved spatial resolution (Fig. 2C).

Image segmentation of subcellular structures

After image pre-processing, segmentation was applied to produce binary mask delineating the structure boundaries. Traditional segmentation of fluorescence images is based on thresholding of pixel intensity value. To better segment the subcellular structures, we then applied machine-learning based pixel classification algorithm. In confocal images, this algorithm yielded smoother segmentation boundaries and less jagged artifacts (Fig. 3A). In Airyscan and SIM images, pixel classification also achieved more continuous and accurate segmentation of subcellular structures, including the mitochondria outer and inner membranes, as well as the microtubules (Fig. 3B and C). Furthermore, thresholding method also failed to preserve intact microtubule cytoskeleton across different threshold values, since setting a lower threshold might introduce more background noise, whereas a higher threshold risked omitting genuine weak signals. In contrast, pixel classification algorithm was able to distinguish genuine weak signals from background noise, yielding more precise and continuous segmentation in both bright and dark image regions (Fig. 3D). For 3D images, pixel classification algorithm also provided more detailed and intact segmentation than traditional thresholding method (Fig. 3E).

To further evaluate their segmentation performance, we compared the output masks of the two approaches against ground-truth on the Airyscan image set of microtubules (Supplementary Fig. 1A). By overlaying the segmentation results with the ground truth, we found that though thresholding could identify the majority of microtubule structures, it omitted substantial weak signals, leading to more false negatives, especially at the periphery of microtubule cytoskeleton (Supplementary Fig. 1B). In contrast, pixel classification method rarely produced false negatives in these regions (Supplementary Fig. 1C), indicating its improved sensitivity to weak signals. Consistent with these observations, segmentation metrics including F1 score, Jaccard index and sensitivity were higher in pixel classification masks, while no significant difference was observed in precision between the two approaches (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Together, these results indicated that pixel classification exhibited higher segmentation accuracy than traditional thresholding method.

Pixel classification-based segmentation using ilastik software

Pixel classification algorithm consisted of pixel features and sigma scale selection, manual annotation, and classifier training (Fig. 4A). In ilastik software, the pixel features used for training included intensity, texture and edge information, which were pre-defined to compromise speed of training and prediction accuracy [10]. The sigma scale of pixel features was also a key parameter for segmentation, which was used to smooth the image before applying a filter [10]. Excessively high sigma blurred the image, resulting in the loss of fine structural details, while excessively low sigma induced oversensitivity to noise. Optimized sigma value was indispensable for accurately segmenting high spatial-resolved structures (Fig. 4B). Following feature and sigma selection, annotation of the target organelles and background regions was performed in a representative image based on prior knowledge of the organelle morphology (Fig. 4C). These annotations, together with the pixel features, were used to train a Random Forest classifier, followed by pixel classification to achieve batch images segmentation [10].Fig. 4Image segmentation by pixel classification algorithm.A. To further assess the reproducibility of the pixel classification segmentation, we independently trained the classifier with different manual annotations, while keeping other parameters unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 2A). The two runs exhibited similar segmentation outputs, with 0.89 ± 0.05 F1 score (mean ± SD), 0.81 ± 0.08 Jaccard index (mean ± SD), 0.97 ± 0.01 precision (mean ± SD), and 0.83 ± 0.08 sensitivity (mean ± SD). Besides, when compared against the ground truth, the two runs achieved comparable F1 score and Jaccard index (Supplementary Fig. 2B). These results indicated high reproducibility of the segmentation workflow.

For studies regarding organelle interactions, analyzing fluorescence colocalization is a common research paradigm [14]. Here we extracted the overlap regions between the mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) masks segmented from live-cell SIM images (Fig. 5A), which might mark the potential tethering molecular complexes regulating organelle interactions. However, at the super-resolution level, contact sites might not only appear as overlapping fluorescence signals. The organelles might remain closely apposed with a characteristic nanoscale gap between their membranes [2]. Therefore, analysis for organelle interaction focused not only on signal overlap, but also on spatial proximity. To estimate the inter-organelle distance, we first inverted the ER binary mask to obtain an image with white background set to 255 pixel value, and black ER network set to 0 value. Next, we performed distance transform of the inverted image. The intensity value of each pixel in the transformed distance map represented its Euclidean distance to the nearest ER boundary. Finally, we segmented the mitochondria binary mask to generate individual Regions of Interest (ROIs) for each mitochondrion. The minimum pixel intensity within each ROI on the distance map was measured to determine the distance from each mitochondrion to the ER network (Fig. 5C and D). Together, these results illustrated the inter-organelle spatial proximity, marking candidate tethers for the dynamic organelle contacts.

Discussion

In this study, we provide a practical workflow for investigating organelles spatial proximity in super-resolution images. This workflow consists of Hessian-SIM and Airyscan imaging, pixel classification-based segmentation, and spatial proximity analysis. It establishes a standardized image-based pipeline for estimating organelle interactions.

A critical aspect of this workflow lies in the careful selection of the super-resolution technique. Super-resolution microscopies can be broadly classified into two categories: one improves the overall resolution of the architecture; and the other reconstructs the whole structure by determining single-molecule positions [15]. The former includes pixel-reassignment techniques (notably Zeiss Airyscan and Nikon NSPARC), SIM, and stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy. STED microscopy is able to achieve a lateral resolution of 40 nm, through narrowing the effective point spread function with the high power torus-shaped depletion laser. However, the depletion beam increases photobleaching, limiting its applications in long-term imaging of live cells [16]. For super-resolution imaging with low phototoxicity, SIM and pixel-reassignment techniques improve spatial resolution through computational reconstruction. SIM projects patterned illumination onto samples using moderate-intensity excitation, and reconstructs high-frequency information from Moiré fringes [17]. Pixel-reassignment techniques computationally reassign and deconvolve images captured from the multi-element array detector, enabling high-resolution imaging at low illumination doses [4]. However, both approaches rely on computational image reconstruction. The resulting images may be affected by reconstruction artifacts and noise amplification [18]. Therefore, appropriate settings of acquisition and deconvolution parameters are required to ensure high fidelity. As for the other major class of super-resolution techniques, single-molecule localization microscopy (SMLM), records the positions of individual fluorescent molecules to achieve a lateral resolution of 5-30 nm, enabling detailed imaging of subcellular compartments at nanometer-scale [3]. Yet the image acquisition may take several minutes to an hour, limiting its temporal resolution [3]. However, the state-of-art Minimal Photon Flux Imaging (MINFLUX) provides nanometer spatial and millisecond temporal resolution [19], which has emerged as a powerful tool to study subcellular architecture and membrane contacts [20,21].

Following image acquisition and restoration, accurate segmentation is a critical step for subsequent analysis. Our study suggested that pixel classification method exhibited higher segmentation accuracy than traditional thresholding method, as evidenced by higher F1 score and Jaccard index when compared against the ground truth. However, the segmentation metric precision was comparable between the two methods, suggesting that they exhibited similar ability to avoid misidentifying background noise as genuine signals. Nevertheless, sensitivity was also higher in pixel classification method, indicating that it produced less false negatives, consistent with the findings that pixel classification rarely omitted weak signals in the periphery of microtubule cytoskeleton. Together, these results suggested that the improved segmentation accuracy of pixel classification might result from its enhanced sensitivity to weak signals.

In recent years, advances in deep learning have propelled the field of image segmentation, holding great potential for automated analysis. Deep learning-based segmentation algorithms are pre-trained on extensive image datasets, and applicable to diverse imaging modalities. Models such as Cellpose-SAM, CellViT, MedSAM have proven effective for segmenting cells, nucleus and organs [[22], [23], [24]]. However, most models to date only work well on the overall morphology of specific objects with clear boundaries. Organelle structures of super-resolution images are more topologically complex. Organelle-specific training datasets and algorithms remain limited compared with those for cell or nuclear segmentation.

Another important methodological issue concerns how organelle contact sites are defined. In this workflow, potential contact regions are defined on the basis of segmented organelle masks and their spatial proximity. This enables the estimation of contact-oriented parameters such as contact numbers, area, shape parameters, and the spatial distribution of contact regions within individual cells. For live-cell datasets, the same framework can also be extended to quantify persistence, association, and dissociation over time. However, these estimates are not necessarily equivalent to functional interactions. Organelle membrane contacts are dependent on the mechanical action of molecular tethers. Detecting closely apposed organelles without perturbing molecular tethers might mistake random, non-functional juxtapositions for true interactions. Depletion of established tethering proteins could be a critical experimental strategy to confirm functional significance. A reduction in spatial proximity, such as decreased overlap area or increased gap distance following such perturbations, could indicate that the observed spatial proximity might reflect functional interactions.

Taken together, this workflow provides a practical and generalized framework for investigating the spatial proximity of organelles at super-resolution level, marking potential hotspots for molecular tethering.

Image processing

Deconvolution of Airyscan and SIM images were processed using ZEN (Zeiss) and FINER (CSR Biotech) software respectively. Pixel classification-based segmentation was processed using ilastik software. The following image features were incorporated during pixel classification in ilastik software: Color/Intensity (Gaussian Smoothing), Edge (Laplacian of Gaussian, Gaussian Gradient Magnitude, Difference of Gaussians), Texture (Structure Tensor Eigenvalues, Hessian of Gaussian Eigenvalues). The sigma values are listed as follows: 0.7 for mitochondria outer membrane and microtubules, 1.6 for mitochondria inner membrane and ER. Volumetric rendering for 3D images were processed using Imaris 9.5.1 (Oxford Instruments). Thresholding-based segmentation, binary mask overlap, and quantification of the overlapped regions were processed and analyzed using ImageJ/Fiji. The detailed procedures are listed as follows:

Thresholding-based segmentation: “Image” -> “Adjust” -> “Threshold” - > select appropriate threshold value -> “Apply”.

Binary mask overlap: “Process” -> “Image Calculator” - > select the two binary masks - > select the operation “AND”.

Quantification of the overlapped regions: “Analyze” -> “Set Measurements” - > select area, shape descriptors, perimeter -> “Analyze Particles” - > confirm appropriate size of the regions to be analyzed - > select “Add to Manager” - > open “ROI Manager” - > deselect all ROIs -> “Measure”.

Quantification of the inter-organelle distance: select ER binary mask - > confirm the pixel size calibration -> “Invert” -> “Distance Map”- > measure the minimum intensity of individual mitochondrion ROIs.

Binary mask comparison

Binary mask comparison was performed using ImageJ/Fiji plugin Mask Instant Comparator. Briefly, ground truth masks were established based on manual expert annotations of Airyscan image set of microtubules. Pixel classification-based and threshold-based segmentation was performed on the same image set, followed by comparison against the ground truth masks at pixel level. The pixel numbers of true positive (TP), false positive (FP) and false negative (FN) were estimated. The segmentation metrics were calculated as follows: F1 score = 2TP/(2 TP + FP + FN); Jaccard index = TP/(TP + FP + FN); precision = TP/(TP + FP); sensitivity = TP/(TP + FN).

Computational resources

For image segmentation and analysis, images were processed on a standard desktop computer with AMD Ryzen 7 5800X 8-Core Processor, 32 GB RAM and NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1650 GPU. For volumetric rendering, 3D images were processed on a workstation with Intel Xeon Silver 4110 CPU, 256 GB RAM, and NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 GPU.

Cell lines and sample preparations

HeLa cells were generously provided by Prof. Lingling Chen from Center for Excellence in Molecular Cell Science, and Caco-2 cells were kept by our labs. For imaging, the cells were seeded on the coverslips or glass bottom dishes.

Reagents and antibodies

The following reagents and antibodies were used in this study: MitoTracker Orange CMTMRos (M − 7510) from Thermo Fisher Scientific, live orange mito (LVORANGE) from Abberior, STAR RED (STRED) from Abberior, EZ Cap™ ER-EGFP Probe mRNA (R1112) from ApexBio Technology, anti-Tom 20 from nano-imaging, mouse anti α-tubulin (T-5168) from Sigma-Aldrich, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (A21202) from ThermoFisher Scientific.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells plated on clean coverslips were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 37 °C or −20 °C pre-chilled 100 % methanol at −20 °C for 20 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.3 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min, and blocking with 10 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature. Then, cells were incubated with first antibodies at −4 °C overnight, and secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Lastly, the coverslips were mounted with Mowiol mounting medium.

Statistics

Paired Student's t-test and One-way ANOVA analysis with multiple comparisons test were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.
