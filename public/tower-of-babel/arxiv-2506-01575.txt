Pluri-Gaussian rapid updating of geological domains
Sultan Abulkhair, Peter Dowd, Chaoshui Xu

Abstract
Over the past decade, the rapid updating of resource knowledge and the integration of real-time sensor information have garnered attention in both industry and academia. However, most studies on rapid resource model updating have focused on continuous variables, such as grade variables and coal quality parameters. Geological domain modelling is an essential component of resource estimation, which is why it is crucial to extend data assimilation techniques to enable the rapid updating of categorical variables. In this paper, a methodology inspired by pluri-Gaussian simulation is proposed for near-real-time updating of geological domains, followed by the updating of grade variables within these domain boundaries. The proposed algorithm consists of a Gibbs sampler for converting geological domains into Gaussian random fields, an ensemble Kalman filter with multiple data assimilations (EnKF-MDA) for rapid updating, and rotation based iterative Gaussianisation (RBIG) for multi-Gaussian transformation. We demonstrate the algorithm using a synthetic case study with observations sampled from the ground truth, as well as a real case study that uses production drilling samples for joint updating of geological domains and grade variables. Both case studies are based on real data from an iron oxide-copper-gold deposit in South Australia. This approach enhances resource knowledge by incorporating both categorical and continuous variables, leading to improved reproduction of domain geometries, closer matches between predictions and observations, and more geologically realistic resource models.

1
Introduction

Resource modelling is a fundamental aspect of mining as it describes the location, quality, and quantity of mineral resources, which are essential for mine planning and optimisation
(
Rossi and Deutsch 2014
)
. An important part of resource modelling is segmenting the ore body into spatial domains that have distinct geological and statistical characteristics. One way to account for these different domains and their grade distributions is through cascade modelling. In this method, the domains are modelled first, followed by estimating or simulating the grades within each domain
(
Dowd 1986
;
Jones et al 2013
;
Madani and Abulkhair 2020
;
Abulkhair and Madani 2022
;
Zhexenbayeva et al 2024
)
.

Conventional deterministic methods, such as wireframing, can produce accurate models when the drill hole spacing is dense and the geological domains are not overly complex
(
Dowd 1986
;
Srivastava 2005
)
. However, in practice, mining deposits often involve multiple interconnected domains, and the available data may not be dense enough to create precise geological models. In contrast, stochastic geostatistical methods can generate multiple equally probable realisations of these domains while also quantifying uncertainty, particularly around the domain boundaries. Techniques such as sequential indicator simulation (SIS)
(
Journel 1983
;
Deutsch 2006
)
and pluri-Gaussian simulation (PGS)
(
Dowd et al 2003
;
Emery 2007
;
Armstrong et al 2011
)
are commonly used for this purpose.

PGS is considered a superior method compared to SIS due to its capability to incorporate contact relationships among multiple categories. This is achieved by representing geological domains as two or more Gaussian random fields (GRFs), using techniques such as Gibbs sampling to enforce these contact relationships
(
Emery 2007
)
.
Madani and Emery 2015
introduced an improved hierarchical approach that can use more than two GRFs and local proportions governed by an interpreted geological model. Additionally, truncating non-stationary GRFs enhances the effectiveness of PGS in complex and heterogeneous environments, such as porphyry copper deposits
(
Madani and Emery 2017
)
.

Another alternative is multiple-point statistics (MPS) methods, which offer a better reproduction of connectivity, especially for heterogeneous and long-range structures
(
Mariethoz and Caers 2014
)
. For instance, the direct sampling (DeeSse) algorithm applies the distance function to directly scan the training image and obtain spatial variability information
(
Mariethoz et al 2010
)
. Aside from its applications in hydrogeology and petroleum facies modelling, DeeSse has been applied to model complex domains such as barren dykes
(
Talebi et al 2019
)
, coal seam layers
(
Abulkhair and Madani 2022
)
and gold veins
(
Zhexenbayeva et al 2024
)
. The latter study showed considerably more accurate modelling of thin, long-range veins compared to PGS. However, the crucial downside of MPS remains its high simulation runtime and difficulty in deriving an appropriate training image.

Nevertheless, regardless of the choice of modelling methodology, geological uncertainty remains a significant challenge, particularly around domain boundaries. Any of the aforementioned methods can only ensure precisely accurate results at the drill hole locations. As mining operations go deeper and grades decrease, it becomes increasingly important to ensure accurate resource modelling. One effective way to achieve this is by integrating real-time sensor observations available during mining operations at various stages of the mining value chain. For example, the ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF) has been extensively applied in the rapid updating of resource and grade control models over the past decade
(
Benndorf 2015
;
Benndorf 2020
;
Wambeke and Benndorf 2017
;
Prior et al 2021b
;
Prior et al 2021a
;
Talesh Hosseini et al 2023
)
. Alternatively, methods, such as residual kriging to update GRF realisations
(
Chevalier et al 2015
)
, actor-critic reinforcement learning
(
Kumar and Dimitrakopoulos 2022
)
, and EnKF with multiple data assimilations (EnKF-MDA)
(
Abulkhair et al 2025
)
have also been proposed for the same task. For instance, EnKF-MDA is based on updating the same data multiple times with an inflated measurement error
(
Emerick and Reynolds 2012
;
Emerick and Reynolds 2013
)
. It offers a significantly closer match between model-based predictions and observations, especially in highly heterogeneous cases.

Most studies on rapid resource model updating, however, have focused on continuous variables (e.g., grades, coal quality parameters, and compositional data). The main challenge with categorical variables (e.g., lithology, alterations, and facies) is how complicated it is to transform them into a format suitable for data assimilation with EnKF. Due to the Gaussianity assumption of EnKF, geological domains need to be parameterised into a continuous multivariate Gaussian space. For instance,
Talesh Hosseini et al 2023
proposed a discrete wavelet transform to turn indicator variables into frequency coefficients and a subsequent EnKF rapid updating. However, it should be noted that the results validating this approach only involved two domains, meaning that its applicability to cases with complex contact relationships between multiple domains is unclear. Similarly, several methods have been proposed for the history matching of facies models, including PGS
(
Liu and Oliver 2005
)
, principal component analysis (PCA)
(
Sarma et al 2008
)
, level set functions
(
Chang et al 2010
)
, and deep generative models
(
Canchumuni et al 2019
)
.

This study continues the work of
Abulkhair et al 2025
, who proposed an algorithm that combines EnKF-MDA and multi-Gaussian transformation to rapidly update multivariate resource models. EnKF-MDA minimised the deviations between predictions and observations compared to conventional EnKF, while rotation based iterative Gaussianisation
(
Laparra et al 2011
;
Abulkhair et al 2023
;
Cook et al 2023
)
helped to take into account complex multivariate relationships. In this paper, we extend this algorithm to update geological domains using a methodology inspired by PGS, in which new categorical observations are parameterised using a Gibbs sampler to rapidly update prior GRFs, followed by truncation. Cross-correlated grades are then updated within each domain, allowing for the joint updating and reduction of the uncertainty associated with domain boundaries and corresponding grade distributions.

The next section presents a detailed methodology for PGS, rapid updating of multivariate resource models using EnKF-MDA and RBIG, and the proposed pluri-Gaussian rapid updating algorithm. Results are comprised of a synthetic and a real case study based on real data from an iron oxide-copper-gold (IOCG) deposit in South Australia. In a synthetic study, 2D prior PGS realisations are updated using observations sampled from the ground truth model. On the other hand, real reverse circulation (RC) drilling samples containing geological domains and three grade variables are used as observations in a real case study. Finally, the paper concludes with a summary of results, potential limitations and future research directions.

2
Methodology

2.1
Pluri-Gaussian simulation

To simplify the rapid updating process, it is assumed that prior realisations of geological domains are modelled using PGS. In this way, the proposed data assimilation algorithm will have the simulations of GRFs to work with as prior model-based predictions. The brief workflow of PGS used for this study is as follows:

[1.]

1.

Defining the truncation rule, which maps the contact relationships between domains. Based on the proportions of each domain, the truncation thresholds (i.e., upper and lower bounds of GRFs for the specific domain) can be derived. In this paper, the number of GRFs is limited to two, so the truncation rule is defined by the 2D Gaussian space.

2.

Modelling variograms of GRFs by either inferring them from the mathematical relationship between indicator variograms and variograms of standard GRFs
(
Emery 2007
)
, or directly estimating them empirically using pairways likelihood
(
Desassis et al 2015
)
.

3.

Converting indicator variables of the conditioning data into GRFs via the Gibbs sampler (Freulon and de Fouquet, 1993) using the proportion thresholds from the truncation rule and variogram models of GRFs. This process can be defined as follows for a particular domain and is repeated for a set number of iterations:

\Upsilon(x)=\Upsilon_{K}(x)+\sigma_{K}R(x),

(1)

where
\Upsilon_{K}(x)
and
\sigma_{K}
are respectively, the kriging estimate and its standard deviation at the selected data point conditioned to other data points,
R(x)
is a residual obtained from a standard normal distribution
N(0,1)
based on
\left[\frac{a_{min}-\Upsilon_{K}(x)}{\sigma_{K}},\frac{a_{max}-\Upsilon_{K}(x)}{\sigma_{K}}\right]
, and
[a_{min},a_{max}]
is the interval from the truncation thresholds of a domain.

4.

Simulating the GRFs at the block model locations, conditional to the Gaussian values obtained from step 3. This can be achieved using geostatistical simulation methods, such as sequential Gaussian simulation and turning bands simulation
(
Isaaks 1991
;
Emery and Lantuéjoul 2006
)
.

5.

Truncating the simulated realisations of GRFs to obtain the realisations of domains according to the truncation rule defined in step 1.

2.2
EnKF-MDA and RBIG for rapid updating

When it comes to the rapid updating of continuous variables, there are many viable options. For instance, EnKF-MDA paired with a multi-Gaussian transformation offers improved data assimilation accuracy for updating multivariate resource models
(
Abulkhair et al 2025
)
. Several algorithms can be used for the multi-Gaussian transformation, including the projection pursuit multivariate transform (PPMT)
(
Barnett et al 2014
)
, flow transformation (FA)
(
van den Boogaart et al 2017
)
, and rotation based iterative Gaussianisation (RBIG)
(
Laparra et al 2011
)
. Nevertheless, RBIG is considerably faster and achieves comparable performance to PPMT and FA, making it an optimal method for time-sensitive tasks such as rapid updating
(
Abulkhair et al 2023
)
. The steps of the combined EnKF-MDA and RBIG algorithm are the following:

[1.]

1.

Extracting a block model neighbourhood around available observations defined by the pre-selected number of blocks from each observation.

2.

Transforming a combined vector of n neighbourhood realisations and observations with m number of variables at time t into independent multi-Gaussian factors
\left({Z_{m}^{n,t}}^{\text{RBIG}},{O_{m}^{t}}^{\text{RBIG}}\right)
using RBIG and storing all the iterations of transformation functions and rotation matrices as
F_{\text{RBIG}}^{t}
. The following is a single iteration of RBIG:

\left(Z_{m}^{n,i+1},O_{m}^{i+1}\right)=R^{i}\Phi^{i}\left(Z_{m}^{n,i},O_{m}^{i}\right),

(2)

where
\Phi^{i}
is a marginal Gaussianisation based on histogram equalisation at iteration
i
,
R^{i}
is a PCA rotation matrix,
Z_{m}^{n,i}
are
n
neighbourhood realisations, and
O_{m}^{i}
are observations with a number of variables
m
.

3.

Computing the model-based predictions
H_{m}^{n,t}
at observation locations.

4.

Adding the measurement error
\varepsilon
to observations.

5.

Calculating the Gaspari-Cohn correlation filter for covariance localisation

\alpha(r)=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{4}r^{5}+\frac{1}{2}r^{4}+\frac{5}{8}r^{3}-\frac{5}{2}r^{2}+1&0\leq r<1\\
\frac{1}{12}r^{5}-\frac{1}{2}r^{4}+\frac{5}{8}r^{3}+\frac{5}{3}r^{2}-5r+4-\frac{2}{3}r^{-1}&1<r\leq 2\\
0&\hphantom{.1<}r>2\end{cases},

(3)

where
r
is a normalised distance between two locations defined by
\frac{d}{L}
,
d
is a distance and
L
is a predefined localisation radius.

6.

Compute the Kalman gain

K_{m}^{t}=\alpha(r)C_{YD}^{m,t}\left(\alpha(r)C_{DD}^{m,t}+C_{D}^{m,t}\right)^{-1},

(4)

where
C_{YD}^{m,t}
is the experimental covariance between realisations and model-based predictions,
C_{DD}^{m,t}
is the experimental covariance of model-based predictions and
C_{D}^{m,t}
is the experimental covariance of observations.

7.

Update the prior realisations

{Z_{m}^{n,t+1}}^{\text{RBIG}}={Z_{m}^{n,t}}^{\text{RBIG}}+K_{m}^{t}\left({O_{m}^{t}}^{\text{RBIG}}-H_{m}^{n,t}\right).

(5)

8.

Repeating steps 3-7 for a predefined number of data assimilations and ensure that the random noise added to observations differs from previous data assimilations.

9.

Back-transforming updated realisations into the original state

Z_{m}^{n,t+1}={F_{\text{RBIG}}^{t}}^{-1}\left({Z_{m}^{n,t+1}}^{\text{RBIG}}\right),

(6)

where
{F_{\text{RBIG}}^{t}}^{-1}
is an inverse RBIG transformation and
Z_{m}^{n,t+1}
are back-transformed updated neighbourhood realisations.

10.

Replacing the previous block model realisations with updated results.

2.3
Proposed joint updating of grades and geological domains

We propose a rapid updating algorithm inspired by the applications of PGS in cascade modelling of geological domains and their corresponding grades. To achieve this, EnKF-MDA is used first to update simulated realisations of GRFs with new observations and then to separately update grades within updated domains. The steps of the proposed algorithm are as follows:

[1.]

1.

Extracting the neighbourhood around new observations from the prior block model realisations.

2.

Applying the Gibbs sampler to new observations using the same truncation rule and variograms that were used during the prior PGS modelling of geological domains.

3.

Applying EnKF-MDA to update prior realisations of GRFs based on observations of domains converted to GRFs in step 2.

4.

Truncating the updated realisations based on the original truncation rule.

5.

Replacing the previous GRF and domain realisations with updated models.

6.

In each domain, separately updating cross-correlated grade variables using the EnKF-MDA + RBIG approach described in Section
2.2
.

3.2
Synthetic case study

In this synthetic case study, we used a 2D section of the prior block model at an elevation of 350 m. Figure
4
illustrates the most probable domains obtained from the 100 PGS realisations and two GRFs in the first realisation. The outcome of this synthetic experiment relies on the ground truth model, which represents a true but unknown environment. Therefore, it is crucial to model the ground truth using a different methodology from PGS to avoid introducing unnecessary biases. DomainMCF geological modelling software
(
Kapageridis et al 2021
)
was applied to create a ground truth model based on the same drill hole data as the PGS model. A total of 2045 observations (about 25% of the ground truth) were randomly sampled and subdivided into 20 time periods to use for sequential rapid updating (Figure
5
).

Figure 4:
From left to right: the most probable domains from prior realisations, a single realisation of the first and second GRFs.

Figure 5:
From left to right: the ground truth, observations of geological domains, and time periods for sequential updating.

At each time period, geological domains were converted into two GRFs according to the prior variograms and the truncation rule using the Gibbs sampler. Then, the EnKF-MDA updated the prior GRF realisations based on new observations in ten data assimilations and truncated them back to the categorical space. For simplicity, a 10% error was added to the observations to introduce the measurement or geological logging uncertainty. Figure
6
shows the final updated GRFs in the first realisation and the most probable model, which closely matches the ground truth. It is also visually noticeable how the updating improved the reproduction of the long-range structure of the DOLT domain, even on the second GRF. Another difference is the SKRN domain on the top part of the model, which is now much larger.

Figure 6:
From left to right: a single realisation of the first and second GRFs and the most probable domains from 100 updated realisations.

The confusion matrices in Figure
7
clearly illustrate the difference between the prior and updated models. The prior model, which matched only 77% of the ground truth, frequently confused the SKRN with VOLC, DOLT with HEM, and at times VOLC with HEM. These mismatches were largely addressed through rapid updating, leading to an accuracy of 93%. However, the DOLT domain continues to pose challenges for accurate modelling even after rapid updating. This can be attributed to the complexity of the domain geometry and its contact relationships with other domains, which are difficult to account for in the truncation rule.

Figure 7:
Confusion matrices between true and predicted domain labels for both the prior and updated models.

A more detailed comparison of the prior and updated models is shown in Figure
8
. The probabilities of the most probable domains indicate that the biggest challenge for PGS was the uncertainty due to insufficient conditioning data, which resulted in lower probabilities for VOLC, DOLT, and SKRN. The rapid updating significantly minimised uncertainty for the entire model, apart from the boundaries between domains. However, this is realistic considering that observations are also slightly uncertain, which can affect truncation and sometimes mismatch the neighbouring domains.

Figure 8:
Probability of the most probable domain (top) and accuracy compared to the ground truth (bottom) for prior and updated models.

The accuracy results, defined by the rate of matching between realisations and the ground truth, present a more interesting picture. In the prior model, significant inaccuracies occurred in the regions with the lowest probabilities and in the long-range structures of DOLT and VOLC. As expected, the prior predictions of HEM and DOLM were mostly accurate, except for the contacts with other domains. After the updates, mismatches are primarily located at the boundaries between domains, which are introduced mainly during the truncation process. The thresholds are sensitive, and additional uncertainty (e.g., measurement or logging error) can lead to a mismatch between two neighbouring domains. This can be particularly detrimental when dealing with hard boundaries, as such mismatches negatively impact the economic value of the block.

Due to the unavailability of the actual dates of the RC drilling, observations were subdivided into 50 time periods based on the clustering of coordinates. At each time period, the updating process begins by extracting the neighbourhood around new observations. Geological domain observations are then converted into two GRFs based on the truncation rule and variograms used during the prior PGS modelling. Next, both prior realisations of GRFs and new observations serve as inputs for EnKF-MDA to perform rapid updating. It is important to note that observations do not include the SKRN and DOLT domains, and the number of VOLC samples is relatively small. As a result, the GRFs are often not sufficiently Gaussian to satisfy the Gaussianity assumption of EnKF-MDA. Therefore, an additional normal score transformation is performed before data assimilation. Figure

As noted in the synthetic case study, the truncation thresholds are sensitive to uncertainties, particularly those associated with hard domain boundaries. The primary purpose of rapid updating is to integrate real-time observations into resource models, thereby facilitating rapid decision-making under uncertainty. Therefore, the uncertainty of domain boundaries will remain the major challenge, especially for the proposed methodology, as it is highly dependent on the contact relationships. Even though the final updated GRF realisations very closely match the observations (see Figure
11
), there can still be mismatches between neighbouring domains. Overall, the results show that the updated domains accurately match the observations, with an accuracy of 97.84% and mean squared error (MSE) reductions of 98.24% and 97.24% for the two GRFs. Figure

illustrates the prior and updated most probable geological domains at an elevation of 9890 m. The significant difference is that rapid updating removed the DOLT domain and a small VOLC structure. This may have an important impact on the economic value in this section of the deposit, as both of these domains have lower average grades.

The next step is to update the cross-correlated Au, Cu and U grades within each domain. For this, RBIG is a recommended addition to EnKF-MDA, as it can quickly transform multivariate data into independent multi-Gaussian factors and ensure the reproduction of multivariate relationships
(
Abulkhair et al 2023
;
Abulkhair et al 2025
)
. However, RBIG and PPMT struggle to reproduce extreme values in highly skewed cases. This can also be true for the conventional geostatistical co-simulation methods. For instance, Figure
13
shows the updated model-based predictions against observations using the methodology explained in Section
2.2
. Both gold and copper have good MSE reductions of 87.69% and 86.59%, while uranium deviates from the diagonal line at the tail of the distribution, and its error is reduced by only 74.84%. Visually, there are clear extreme values, particularly in prior gold and uranium predictions, which deviate from observations even after the updates.

Figure 13:
Predictions versus observations plots before and after the rapid updating of Au, Cu, and U using the algorithm by
Abulkhair et al 2025
.

One way to minimise the deviations between extreme values of predictions and observations is to use FA
(
van den Boogaart et al 2017
;
Prior et al 2021b
)
, since it produces fewer artifacts in the presence of extreme values compared to RBIG and PPMT
(
Cook et al 2023
)
. Nevertheless, FA is currently considerably slower than PPMT, which, in turn, is slower than RBIG. Another recent solution is a non-monotonic transformation of GRFs, which has demonstrated better reproduction of the tails of skewed distributions compared to PPMT and co-simulation
(
Khorram et al 2025
)
. This method is, unfortunately, too complex and time consuming, as it involves multiple indicator variables, three stages of model parameter inference, simulation, and particle filtering. In this study, a simple and straightforward approach is proposed to update extreme values separately, addressing the issue of reproducing skewed distributions. As all three variables are heavily right-skewed to a similar degree, the
95^{th}
percentile is selected as a threshold of extreme values for all three variables. Figure
14
shows that the proposed approach was able to improve the results, especially for uranium, in which MSE was reduced by 86.57%. Overall, the improvements compared to Figure 13 are 1.7% for gold, 5.67% for copper and 11.73% for uranium.

percentiles. The important difference is the removal of the DOLT domain, as shown in Figure

. This DOLT structure appears visually out of place when examining the models of grades, which is one of the drawbacks of hard domains. The orebody with high copper content is significantly narrower and more connected in the updated model, and the same can be said for the high uranium zone. This factor will be important to consider when calculating the economic value of these blocks since uranium is a deleterious element in this deposit. Overall, the rapid updating helped to minimise the overestimation of grades and corrected the results. In a practical scenario, near-real-time joint updating of geological domains and grade variables would enable rapid decision-making, including mine planning and optimisation of comminution and processing.

4
Conclusions

This paper presents a data assimilation algorithm designed for the joint rapid updating of geological domains and cross-correlated grade variables. The concept is inspired by the PGS algorithm, a widely used geostatistical simulation method for modelling facies and domains. The Gibbs sampler provides a straightforward way for converting categorical data into GRFs, which are then suitable for updating with EnKF-MDA. Following the principles of cascade modelling, the next step involves updating cross-correlated grade variables within each geological domain. Overall, the proposed approach has demonstrated its effectiveness in both synthetic and real case studies based on data from an IOCG deposit in South Australia.

Additionally, the proposed approach addresses the issue of accurately updating highly skewed distributions, which was one of the limitations observed in the combined use of EnKF-MDA and RBIG. By separately updating extreme values, the error reduction for the uranium grade improved from 75% to 87%. However, some limitations still need to be addressed in future research. Firstly, the proposed approach assumes that prior realisations were modelled using PGS and that GRF realisations are always available. This assumption was made to simplify the method and reduce computation runtime, as converting hundreds of block model realisations into GRFs using the Gibbs sampler for each time period is time-consuming. Moreover, the algorithm is currently limited to two GRFs, which may not be sufficient for more complex scenarios.

To make the algorithm truly comprehensive and capable of updating the entire resource knowledge, it must also incorporate geometallurgical information. However, since geometallurgical variables related to comminution and processing are often non-additive, their geostatistical modelling and rapid updating with EnKF may not be the most appropriate strategy. In future research, we will focus on extending the current algorithm to integrate non-additive data. For example, rapid resource model updating can be incorporated into the primary-response framework
(
Coward et al 2009
)
, as updating primary variables enhances the prediction of response parameters.
