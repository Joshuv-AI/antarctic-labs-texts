In Vitro Single-Cell Transcriptomic Profiling of Cultured Stem Cells From Apical Papilla and Dental Pulp Stem Cells: Unveiling Cellular Heterogeneity
Authors: Maanas S. Shah, Mohamed Al Sayegh, Mennatullah M. Khalil, Mano Sundarabupathi, Omer AlKhnbashi, Lakshman Samaranayake, Costerwell Khyriem, Mehar Sultana, Hiroshi Egusa, Mohamed Jamal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDental-derived mesenchymal stem cells show considerable variability in their differentiation potential due to the use of nonspecific surface markers and technical limitations in isolation protocols. This study aimed to employ single-cell RNA sequencing to compare the cellular composition of cultured stem cells from apical papilla (SCAPs) and dental pulp (DPSCs), with the goal of detecting subpopulations underlying their divergent regenerative behaviour and identifying markers that can facilitate future isolation and functional targeting.MethodSCAP and DPSC tissues were obtained from 3 human donors and cultured to passage 2. Single-cell suspensions were sequenced to generate gene expression profiles. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed using the Seurat package and visualised using uniform manifold approximation and projection. Cluster-specific differential gene expression was computed as log2 fold change, followed by gene set enrichment analysis. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was used to map lineage progression based on transcriptional gene expression.ResultsTranscriptomic analysis identified 12 distinct clusters shared across DPSC and SCAP cultures. While both cell types contributed comparably to overall biological processes, key differences emerged within specific clusters. Clusters 3, 4, 5, 6, and 9 expressed high levels of proliferative markers (MKI67, TOP2A, and TYMS), suggesting active proliferating populations. Cluster 9 was notable for the coexpression of pericyte-associated markers (NOTCH3, PDGFRB) alongside canonical mesenchymal stromal cell markers (MCAM, THY1, DCN), identifying a previously uncharacterised progenitor-like subset. NOTCH3 and PDGFRB were also present in a more mature fibroblast-enriched population in cluster 7, dominated by collagen-related genes. IGFBP3 and IGFBP5 were selectively enriched in SCAP-derived clusters 7 and 9, whereas IGFBP4, 6, and 7 were expressed across both DPSC and SCAP populations. Clusters 10 and 11, primarily derived from DPSCs, were enriched in stress-response, heat shock, and apoptotic genes, which may reflect culture-induced adaptations. Pseudotime trajectory inference positioned cluster 9 at a putative progenitor-like node; however, this represents a hypothesis-generating model based on transcriptional similarity in cultured cells rather than validated lineage relationships.ConclusionThis study provides a high-resolution single-cell transcriptomic comparison of cultured SCAPs and DPSCs, revealing distinct transcriptional profiles and cellular heterogeneity. SCAPs may harbour a broader spectrum of proliferative progenitors under these culture conditions, particularly in clusters 7 and 9, enriched in IGF-, PDGFRB-, and NOTCH-associated genes. These findings generate testable hypotheses regarding subpopulation-specific regenerative roles. Future studies using prospective isolation based on NOTCH3+/PDGFRB+ and functional in vitro and in vivo validation are needed to establish their regenerative potential and translational relevance.

Introduction

Multipotent mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are immature, undifferentiated cells with the ability to differentiate into multiple lineages.1 MSC-like populations have been isolated from a variety of mature tissues, including bone marrow, skin, adipose tissue, and dental pulp.2 Among these, dental-derived MSCs (dMSCs) have attracted increasing attention as a promising cell source for therapeutic and regenerative applications. dMSCs have been successfully isolated from diverse dental tissues, including dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), periodontal ligament stem cells, stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth, and stem cells from the apical papilla (SCAPs).3 In vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated their capacity for osteogenic, chondrogenic, adipogenic, and odontogenic differentiation.3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Similar to bone marrow MSCs, dMSCs adhere to plastic, exhibit trilineage differentiation potential, and express MSC markers such as CD73, CD90 (THY1), and CD105.8, 9, 10 They also express pericyte- and progenitor-related markers, including STRO-1, α-SMA, CD146 (MCAM), NG2, and NOTCH3.11, 12, 13 However, isolating pure subpopulations based on these markers remains challenging. For example, STRO-1+ cells have shown overlapping expression with hematopoietic markers such as CD34 and CD117,14,15 whereas THY1, consistently expressed in in vitro conditions, remains poorly characterised in native tissues, raising questions about its validity as a definitive in vivo MSC marker.16 These inconsistencies underscore the inherent heterogeneity of dMSCs and suggest that culture conditions may influence marker expression and cellular phenotype. Furthermore, this heterogeneity complicates efforts to isolate consistent subpopulations with reliable odontogenic capacity. Additionally, the reliance on predefined surface markers may miss key functionally relevant subsets. Therefore, there remains a critical need to identify and characterise robust dMSC subpopulations with defined regenerative properties and odontogenic potential that can be used for in vitro modelling, preclinical research, and clinical translation.

Recent advances in transcriptomic profiling have provided powerful tools to dissect cellular complexity and resolve cell-type-specific behaviour at single-cell resolution.17,18 Conventional methods such as flow cytometry and bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) have been widely used to assess cellular identity and gene expression. However, bulk RNA-seq captures averaged signals from pooled populations, masking the contribution of rare or transitional subtypes.19, 20, 21 Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) addresses this limitation by enabling high-throughput, high-resolution analysis of transcriptomes at the individual cell level. This approach reveals the heterogeneity, lineage relationships, and regulatory programs operating within complex tissues.17,19,21 In the context of dental research, scRNA-seq has been successfully applied to map cellular hierarchies and differentiation trajectories in murine and human tooth development.22,23 While most studies to date have focused on developing or diseased dental tissues, few have compared cultured dMSC populations in detail.24, 25, 26, 27 The limited existing studies underscore the importance of understanding how in vitro expansion influences subpopulation composition and functional potential.

The objective of this study was to use scRNA-seq to compare the cellular composition of cultured SCAPs and DPSCs, two anatomically distinct yet developmentally related dental MSC sources. Understanding transcriptional heterogeneity and identifying subpopulation-specific markers is particularly relevant for regenerative endodontics, where successful maturogenesis in immature teeth relies on harnessing stem/progenitor populations capable of driving pulp-dentin complex formation. By resolving subcluster-specific transcriptional profiles, this study aims to pinpoint putative progenitor-like populations and surface markers that may facilitate future functional targeting and prospective isolation strategies, ultimately guiding therapeutic approaches for dental tissue regeneration.

Materials and methodology

Patient selection and sample collection

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Mohammed Bin Rashid University of Medicine and Health Sciences (MBRU) IRB-2023-391) and conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975, revised in 2024). The study was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory Studies in Endodontology 2021 guidelines28 (Figure 1). After obtaining written informed consent, volunteers (American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA I), aged 13 to 22 years) attending Dubai Health Dental Hospital, United Arab Emirates, for the extraction of premolars as part of an orthodontic treatment plan were recruited for the study. Sample handling and preparation of single-cell suspensions

Collected tissue samples were transported in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (1X; calcium- and magnesium-free; Thermo Fisher Scientific© Inc.) supplemented with 1% Primocin and transferred to the laboratory within 10 minutes. Tissues were minced into 0.5- to 1-mm fragments and enzymatically digested using 3 mg/mL type I collagenase and 4 mg/mL Dispase II (Sigma-Aldrich©, Merck KGaA, Germany) at 37 °C for 15 minutes, with manual agitation every 5 minutes. Samples were subsequently vortexed for 10 seconds, gently triturated using wide-bore pipette tips, and filtered through a 40-µm cell strainer (Thermo Fisher Scientific© Inc.). Cell suspensions were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 minutes, and pellets were resuspended in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM, Gibco©) supplemented with 20% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific© Inc.) and 1% Primocin. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ in 25-cm² flasks and expanded for 10 days. All samples were collected at early passages (P2). Prior to scRNA-seq, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline and prepared as single-cell suspensions. Cell concentration and viability were assessed using a brightfield cell counter (DeNovix Inc., Delaware, US). All samples exceeded 90% viability, with concentrations adjusted to 900 to 1200 cells/µL. Cell debris and large cell clumping rates were maintained below 5%.

scRNA-seq library generation and sequencing

The six single-cell suspensions (3 DPSCs and 3 SCAPs) were loaded into the 10X Genomics Chromium© controller, and libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v3 according to the manufacturer’s protocol, including Gel Bead-in-Emulsion (GEM) generation, barcoding, complementary DNA amplification, and library construction (Figure 2). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq© platform to a depth of approximately 50,000 reads per cell. Raw sequencing reads were processed to remove adaptor contamination and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38-1.2.0), and gene expression quantification was performed using Cell Ranger count. Since the analysed cells were expanded under controlled in vitro culture conditions, uniformly high RNA integrity and low mitochondrial transcript fractions were expected. Quality control metrics were assessed using distributions of nFeature_RNA (number of detected genes per cell), nCount_RNA (Unique Molecular Identifier (UMIs) per cell), and percentage mitochondrial RNA (Supplementary Figure S1). As per protocol, cells with fewer than 2200 detected genes or more than 9800 genes were excluded to remove poorly captured cells and potential doublets, respectively. Additionally, cells with mitochondrial RNA percentages exceeding 10% were excluded to remove the clearly defined low-quality tail. These quality control thresholds were selected based on established scRNA-seq best-practice guidelines and Structured Transparent Accessible Reproducible (STAR)-based protocols to maximise retention of high-quality cells while minimising technical artifacts. scRNA-seq data processing and integration

All downstream analyses were conducted using the Seurat R package (v4.4.0) with R version 3.6.4, and Seurat versions were synchronised across all analytical steps. Data normalisation, log transformation, identification of highly variable genes, and scaling were performed using standard Seurat workflows. Batch correction and dataset integration across the 6 biological replicates were performed using the Harmony algorithm (version 1.2), implemented within the Seurat workflow, to mitigate donor-specific and technical batch effects. To demonstrate the effectiveness, pre- and postintegration uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualisations, along with detailed workflow parameters (SCTransform normalisation, Harmony integration, and downstream clustering steps), are provided in Supplementary Figure S2, illustrating the reduction of donor/library-driven clustering while preserving shared biological structure across samples. Principal component analysis was used for dimensionality reduction, and the number of significant components was determined using the elbow plot method, which plateaued at approximately 10 to 12 principal components. UMAPs were used to explore the cellular diversity within and between SCAP and DPSCs. Cell cycle regression was not performed following integration, as per-donor cell cycle overlays demonstrated a continuous G1-S-G2/M gradient across libraries, indicating that cell cycle variation constituted an orthogonal axis rather than a primary driver of clustering; corresponding visualisations are provided (Supplementary Figure S3) to confirm that the integrated structure reflected shared transcriptional states rather than phase-specific segregation.

Clustering, cell annotation, and downstream analysis

Graph-based clustering was performed using a k-nearest neighbour graph and the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 0.5. Clusters were visualised using UMAP and t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding analysis to identify the cell type, graph-based clustering, and marker analysis based on the top-expressed genes for every cluster. Differential gene expression between groups was estimated as fold change between cluster-specific expression levels using the FindMarkers function (adjusted P < .05) and curated reference gene sets derived from published dental scRNA-seq studies22,24,26,27 and public databases, including GeneCards, ImmGen, and the Human Protein Atlas.

Finally, gene expression patterns in different clusters were combined with prior biological knowledge using Gene Ontology (GO) analysis to conduct gene set enrichment analysis. The biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components associated with these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were assessed using the clusterProfiler package with the aid of GO, String Core Data Resource, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway web-based platforms. The rank-based Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA framework, enrichment parameters, and a user-curated pathway compendium are presented in the supplementary data. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted using Monocle3 (v 1.2) to infer lineage relationships and transcriptional dynamics based on gene expression similarity. Trajectory robustness was assessed using a leave-one-donor-out (LODO) framework, in which pseudotime was recomputed following the sequential exclusion of each donor. Concordance between LODO and full-dataset trajectories was quantified using Spearman correlation of pseudotime values across shared cells, and cluster identity of the earliest state was determined from median pseudotime distributions. Root sensitivity was further evaluated by iteratively assigning each Seurat cluster as the trajectory root using Monocle3. Cell cycle dependence was examined by correlating pseudotime with S and G2/M scores and by comparing phase distributions across quantile-binned pseudotime before and after regression. RNA-velocity directionality was evaluated using per-cell velocity source scores (outgoing minus incoming transition probabilities), summarised at the cluster level to assess directional flow (Supplementary Figures S4-S6).

Results

A total of 18,955 DPSCs and 20,678 SCAPs were acquired from 3 different individuals (1 female and 2 males) for scRNA-seq analysis. Through unbiased clustering based on transcriptional variabilities between gene expression patterns, the dataset of all 6 samples identified 12 (0-11) clusters. UMAP, used to project high-dimensional gene expression data into lower dimensions, is depicted in Figure 2A. Figure 2B further illustrates variations in contributions of DPSC and SCAP samples to each cluster, emphasising distinct cellular compositions between the 2 sources. While most clusters (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8) demonstrated a relatively balanced contribution from both cell samples (Figure 3C), more than two-thirds of cluster 7 and the entire cluster 9 were exclusively composed of cells from SCAP samples, underscoring a source-specific enrichment in these clusters. The Venn diagram (Figure 3D) illustrates gene expression overlaps and specificity between the 2 cell populations. While both cell populations demonstrated a shared set of core genes related to extracellular matrix organisation and tissue remodelling (THBS1, PCNA, POSTN, COLA1A), as well as proliferative cell cycle–associated genes (STMN1, MKI67, BIRC5, HMGB1), the exclusive markers in SCAP were mainly derived from cluster 9 (IGFBP3, IGFBP5, NOTCH3, FOXC2, GALNT1, KCNK2, RUNX2, ANKH, EBF1, EBF3), whereas specific markers related to hypoxia, stress adaptation, and apoptosis (GADD45A, GADD45B, TNFRSF10D, HSPA1A, SELENOK), unique to clusters 10 and 11, were exclusively detected in DPSC cultures. Interestingly, markers associated with smooth muscle function, TAGLN and ACTA2, were also present in DPSC samples alone. Immature subpopulations committed to dentin formation, which express PTN (NEGF1/pleiotrophin), were predominantly detected in SCAP samples, whereas preodontoblasts that express NES (Nestin), before dentin formation, were exclusively detected in DPSC samples. Remarkably, markers associated with adipogenic differentiation, EBF1, EBF3, and PPARG, were exclusively expressed in SCAP samples. Clusters 0, 1, and 2 were characterised by the expression of signature genes such as POSTN, FBN2, PDGFRA, THBS1, and ANGPT1, indicative of stromal and vascular-associated cell populations (Figure 4B). Gene enrichment analysis of these clusters highlighted biological processes, including extracellular matrix organisation, tissue remodelling, angiogenesis, and vascular differentiation, collectively suggesting a critical role in tissue maintenance and structural support. Furthermore, GO analysis revealed significant enrichment of WNT and PDGF signalling pathways within these clusters, pointing toward their potential involvement in stem cell regulation, tissue morphogenesis, and developmental processes (Figure 4C).

As shown in the middle panel of Figure 3C, the GO terms (GO:0000278, GO:0006259, GO:0051301) and reactome pathways (R-HSA-73886, R-HSA-69620, R-HSA-1640170) associated with clusters 3, 4, 5, and 6 were predominantly enriched for processes involved in maintaining chromosomal stability (eg, TOP2A, CENPU, DIAPH3), cell cycle progression through mitosis (eg, MKI67, CENPF, PBK), and DNA repair, replication, and recombination (eg, PCNA, MCM3, BRCA1). In addition, these clusters exhibited exclusive expression of classical proliferative markers corresponding to distinct phases of the cell cycle (G1, G1/S, G2, and M phases), such as MKI67, TOP2A, STMN1, NUSAP1, MCM3, HELLS, CCNB1, CENPF, UBE2S, CDC20, and BIRC5 (Figure 4B).

Interestingly, a distinct cluster (cluster 9) was exclusively identified, characterised by expression of smooth muscle pericyte markers such as NOTCH3 and PDGFRB, along with cell cycle–related genes of clusters 3, 4, 5, and 6. Notably, this unique amalgamation of pericyte markers, proliferating markers, and pan-MSC markers (MCAM, THY1) suggested a previously uncharacterised progenitor cluster. Further investigation into DEGs detected in cluster 9 showed a significant overlap with subsets of markers present in cluster 7. While clusters 7 and 9 both exhibited similar expression of markers such as NOTCH3, PDGFRB, CYP1B1, and SRGN, cluster 7 lacked the immature, proliferative cell cycle–associated genes (Figure 4B). Moreover, it was enriched for markers associated with a more mature smooth muscle pericyte phenotype, including ACTA2, TAGLN, FN1, FGF7, and COL6A1 (Figure 4B). This suggests that, despite a shared pericyte-like signature, cluster 7 may represent a more differentiated, quiescent stromal population, whereas cluster 9 retains a more progenitor-like, proliferative identity. These findings warrant further investigation to elucidate the functional roles and lineage potential of these distinct subpopulations.

Clusters 7 and 9 surprisingly expressed genes related to lipid synthesis (EBF1, EBF2, and EBF3), APOE, and CYP1B1 as well (Figure 4B). Further, regardless of the cell source, clusters 7 and 9 also demonstrated markers associated with the regulation of the insulin-like growth factor receptor signalling pathway. The superprotein family of insulin-like growth factor binding proteins (IGFBPs 3-7), along with IGF receptors (IGF1R, IGF2R), were detected with varying positive log2 fold change values within these clusters (Figure 4B). Contrarily, downregulation of these IGF-related genes (IGFBP3-7, IGF1-2 receptors) was observed in clusters 10 and 11. Moreover, genes designated as heat shock proteins (HSPA5, DNAJC3, DNAJB11, HSP90B1) in cluster 10, along with those associated with positive regulation of cell death and apoptotic cell death, were highly expressed (TGIF1, TNFRSF10B, SGK1, GADD45A, GADD45B, ID2, EGR1, FOSB, SOD2, SNAI1, CXCL3) in clusters 10 and 11 (Figure 4B). GO analysis related to these markers, typically associated with P53, FOXO, and MAPK pathways, was related to response to stress, tumour necrosis factor, and NF-kappa B signalling pathways, indicative of apoptotic-related biological processes (Figure 4C).

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

Pseudotime trajectory analysis suggested a continuum of transcriptional states across clusters, with a branching pattern indicative of potential divergent cellular programs, originating from a common root state (cluster = 9). Cluster 9 was positioned at the root of the reconstructed trajectory, suggesting it may represent a transcriptionally early or less differentiated state within the cultured population. One branch showed upregulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) and mineralisation genes (eg, SPARC, COL1A1, POSTN), suggesting mesenchymal differentiation. The other branch retained early proliferative markers (MKI67, TOP2A, HMGB2), indicating a self-renewing or transit-amplifying state. Early pseudotime states were enriched in stemness-associated genes (ID1, SOX4, LGR5), while later stages expressed mineralising cell markers (RUNX2, MSX1, DLX5, ALPL), marking the transition toward lineage commitment. Conversely, cell cycle genes (CDK1, CCNB1, TOP2A) persisted along the proliferative trajectory. These trajectories represent computationally inferred transcriptional states rather than definitive lineage relationships. Cluster-level analysis of pseudotime values showed that small error bars within the boxplots (eg, cluster 6 positioned at the lower end of the plot with a low mean pseudotime) indicated synchronised, early progenitor states, while broader distributions (eg, cluster 0 with higher mean pseudotime and a broader error distribution) suggested heterogeneous, possibly terminally differentiated populations, traversing multiple differentiation stages (Figure 5). LODO analyses demonstrated stable pseudotime ordering across donor exclusions, with cluster 9 consistently identified as the earliest state based on median pseudotime. Pseudotime showed minimal association with cell cycle scores, and all phases were continuously distributed along the trajectory, indicating that the inferred structure was not driven by proliferative status. Discussion

There has been a persistent effort in regenerative dentistry to identify dental stem cell populations capable of reconstructing the pulp-dentin complex and other dental structures. Among the various stem cells derived from dental tissues, SCAPs and DPSCs are of particular interest due to their developmental origin and regenerative potential.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 This study aimed to unveil the cellular landscape of cultured SCAP versus DPSCs at a single-cell resolution and to define distinct subclusters contributing to the functional diversity between these cell sources.

Our scRNA-seq analysis identified 12 distinct clusters across both SCAP and DPSC populations. Most of the clusters demonstrated a considerable overlap in transcriptional profiles between the 2 populations. However, subtle but distinct transcriptional identities were also identified. For instance, RUNX2, a key transcription factor essential for osteoblastic differentiation, was exclusively detected in SCAP samples, which is in contrast with studies reporting that mineralisation-related genes were more prominently expressed in mature dental pulp.30,31 Furthermore, preodontoblast differentiation markers such as Nestin (NES)32 were exclusively detected in DPSC samples, while immature subpopulations committed to dentin formation, marked by high PTN,33 were predominantly detected in clusters 0, 1, and 2 of SCAP samples. Clusters 3, 4, 5, and 6 exhibited a balanced representation of both cell types and were enriched for genes associated with proliferation and cell cycle progression, indicating the presence of a core proliferative compartment responsible for population maintenance. However, cluster 9 was found exclusively in SCAPs and was notable for its dual expression of proliferative and early mesenchymal/pericyte-associated genes, suggesting a transitional progenitor population unique to SCAPs. Similarly, cluster 7, another SCAP-dominant population, also showed pericyte-related expression, albeit with reduced proliferation markers and increased maturation signals, indicating a more differentiated state relative to cluster 9. In contrast, clusters 10 and 11 were composed almost entirely of DPSC-derived cells and were enriched in genes related to stress response (GADD45A, GADD45B, TNFRSF10D, HSPA1A, SELENOK), heat shock proteins (HSPA5, DNAJC3, DNAJB11, HSP90B1), and apoptotic signalling (TGIF1, TNFRSF10B, SGK1, ID2, EGR1, FOSB, SOD2, SNAI1, CXCL3). It is important to note that the transcriptional profiles captured in this study reflect culture-adapted cellular phenotypes, which may differ from native in vivo stem cell states and niche-dependent behaviours. This differential clustering highlights transcriptional heterogeneity between SCAPs and DPSCs, with SCAPs enriched in progenitor-associated gene signatures and DPSCs showing stress-related expression patterns; however, functional implications cannot be inferred without experimental validation.

Cluster 9 represents a transcriptionally distinct population enriched in proliferative and mesenchymal-associated genes, suggesting a potential progenitor-like state that warrants further functional investigation. This cluster coexpresses typical cell cycle–related and proliferation genes (MKI67, TOP2A, TYMS), along with canonical MSC markers (THY1, MCAM, CD44) and pericyte-associated genes such as RGS5, TIMP3, NG2, NOTCH3, and PDGFRB, suggesting a dual identity of proliferation and stemness potential. In parallel, cluster 7 expresses markers associated with pericyte and smooth muscle identity, such as RGS5, MYH11, TIMP3, NOTCH3, PDGFRB, ACTA2, TAGLN, FN1, FGF7, and COL6A1. However, unlike cluster 9, cluster 7 does not exhibit cell cycle/proliferation genes, suggesting a more quiescent and differentiated stromal phenotype. The shared expression of pericyte and early mesenchymal markers with cluster 9 may indicate a lineage connection and possibly a hierarchical relationship. Although pseudotime trajectory inference positioned cluster 9 at the root of the reconstructed trajectory, this designation should be interpreted in a hypothesis-generating context. Pseudotime analyses infer transcriptional similarity rather than true lineage relationships and therefore do not establish definitive progenitor-descendant hierarchies. Moreover, the heterogeneity observed in this study reflects culture-adapted cellular phenotypes, which may differ from native in vivo stem cell states and niche-dependent behaviours. Within these limitations, pseudotime inference nevertheless highlighted cluster 9 as a putative progenitor-like population, potentially giving rise to multiple downstream subpopulations, including cluster 7. The transcriptional differences between clusters 7 and 9 may reflect distinct cellular states within SCAP cultures; however, hierarchical relationships cannot be definitively established from transcriptomic similarity alone. Protein interaction analysis revealed coordinated signalling networks within clusters 7 and 9, indicating transcriptional specialisation that requires functional validation. Taken together, cluster 7 may represent a more differentiated stromal-like transcriptional state; however, its specific biological role remains to be experimentally determined.

The insulin-like growth factor (IGF) axis—including IGF-1, IGF-2, their receptors, and binding proteins (IGFBP1-6)—plays an important role in regulating stem cell behaviour, particularly in odontogenesis, mineralisation, and tissue repair.34, 35, 36 Our scRNA-seq data revealed that IGFBP3 and IGFBP5 expression, known to support early differentiation and odontoblast maintenance,35 was selectively enriched in SCAP-derived clusters 7 and 9, while IGFBP4, 6, and 7 were present in both DPSC and SCAP populations. Their restricted expression suggests a potential association with progenitor-enriched transcriptional states, although functional significance remains to be validated. In contrast, DPSC-dominant clusters 10 and 11 showed downregulation of IGF-related genes (IGFBP3-7, IGF1/2 receptors), alongside increased cellular stress, senescence, and expression of apoptotic markers. These findings identify IGF-axis components as transcriptionally enriched features of SCAP-dominant clusters, generating hypotheses regarding their potential regulatory roles.

NOTCH signalling has been widely implicated in stem cell maintenance, self-renewal, and lineage specification, including in dental and craniofacial tissues.13,37,38 The expression of NOTCH3 and its ligand JAG1, along with MSC markers MCAM and STRO-1, has been identified in apical papilla tissue and cultured SCAPs.13 Furthermore, immunostaining revealed coexpression of NOTCH3 and MCAM around blood vessels in the apical papilla, with peripheral localisation of NOTCH3, supporting its spatial specificity.13 Additionally, NOTCH3 expression declined upon osteogenic differentiation, suggesting a potential role in maintaining SCAP stemness.39,40 These findings thus provided insights into the regulatory role of NOTCH3 in SCAP function. The identification of NOTCH3 as a potential MSC marker was further reinforced by single-cell profiling of dental pulp versus periodontal ligament cells, shedding light on different dental cell sources with MSC subpopulations.26 The study revealed that MSCs represented a large fraction of the periodontium and the dental pulp and were characterised by expression of FRZB, NOTCH3, MYH11, and CD90.26 Furthermore, a subpopulation that maintained its phenotypic characteristics after culturing DPSCs and remained stable regardless of physiological senescence or dental caries was also identified at a single-cell resolution.25 The study highlighted that the “MCAM+/JAG+ but negative to PDGFRA” subpopulation showed higher proliferation capacity and enhanced in vitro and in vivo outcomes that may be used for further tissue regeneration applications.25 Similarly, previous scRNA-seq analysis also identified PDGFRB⁺ DPSCs during dental pulp development, characterised by superior vasculogenic capabilities, including enhanced cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation.41 The study highlighted the PDGFR/PI3K/AKT signalling pathway as a key regulator of their proliferative and proangiogenic properties, underscoring their significant clinical translational potential.41 Thus, the absence of fibroblast markers such as PDGFRA, alongside the presence of pan-MSC markers (MCAM, THY1) with pericyte-specific markers such as NOTCH3 and PDGFRB, further distinguishes this cluster from more differentiated stromal subsets. Taken together, these findings identify cluster 9 as a SCAP-enriched subpopulation with transcriptional features suggestive of a progenitor-like state; however, its regenerative capacity remains to be determined through prospective isolation and functional assays. Beyond their spatial specificity, NOTCH3 and PDGFRB emerge as potential markers for prospectively isolating SCAP subpopulations with progenitor and odontogenic potential. The selective enrichment of NOTCH3 and PDGFRB in progenitor-enriched SCAP clusters and their limited presence in DPSC-dominant clusters support their value as functional and surface-accessible markers. Future studies employing NOTCH3/PDGFRB-based isolation strategies may enable targeted functional assessment of candidate progenitor subpopulations for potential tissue engineering applications.

A key strength of this study lies in the use of advanced scRNA-seq and biological triplicates, which provided a high-resolution, reproducible view of the transcriptional landscape of SCAPs and DPSCs. This design helped account for donor-specific variability and strengthened the robustness of our findings. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. As with all single-cell transcriptomic studies, gene expression can be influenced by the cell cycle stage, cellular and metabolic activity, and differentiation status.42 These factors make it challenging to annotate cell clusters to specific cell types. Also, variations in tissue collection methods and presequencing procedures, including whether cells are cultured or directly sequenced, may affect the cellular composition captured by scRNA-seq.43 Extended culturing may induce phenotypic drift, senescence, or stress responses that obscure native cellular identities. Consequently, while the identified clusters provide insights into potential functional heterogeneity, extrapolation to native tissue populations should be done cautiously. These findings offer a valuable platform for hypothesis generation, but the observed heterogeneity may be influenced by in vitro culture conditions rather than solely intrinsic tissue-specific programs. The relatively small and predominantly adolescent donor cohort may also limit the generalisability of these findings to broader age groups. Differential expression analyses were conducted using pooled single-cell comparisons; therefore, identified transcriptional differences should be interpreted as dataset-level associations rather than donor-independent effects. Moreover, the pseudotime framework in this study is best interpreted as a model of transcriptional organisation within culture-adapted stem cell populations rather than a direct reconstruction of in vivo developmental dynamics. Furthermore, regeneration of the pulp-dentin complex is influenced not only by stem cell identity but also by microenvironmental variables, including bioactive scaffolds and adjunctive biologics such as platelet-rich plasma.44 These factors should be considered when interpreting the functional implications of the identified clusters. Moving forward, functional validation of clusters 7 and 9 is warranted to confirm their proposed superiority in dental tissue regeneration. These findings, while derived from cultured cells, provide a transcriptomic roadmap to identify progenitor-enriched SCAP populations (eg, NOTCH3+/PDGFRB+ clusters) that could also be leveraged to enhance outcomes in regenerative endodontic procedures. Given that SCAP survival and functional contribution depend on the apical papilla and Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath, and that MSC availability declines with age, these populations may be particularly relevant for optimising cell-based revitalisation strategies in immature teeth, as highlighted by systematic reviews45 and prior studies.46,47 Prospective isolation using markers such as NOTCH3 and PDGFRB may allow for enrichment of progenitor-rich SCAP subpopulations and enable in vitro assays for differentiation potential, immunomodulation, and tissue repair. In vivo lineage tracing studies and transplantation models will be instrumental in determining their regenerative efficacy and potential translational value. Further investigation into the effect of environmental cues, such as hypoxia and inflammation, on cluster dynamics could reveal context-dependent shifts in lineage behaviour.

Conclusion

This study presents one of the first comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic comparisons of cultured SCAPs and DPSCs, offering an in-depth resolution of their cellular heterogeneity. Our data reveal distinct transcriptional identities and putative functional hierarchies within cultured dental stem cells, including a SCAP-enriched subpopulation of proliferative and multipotent progenitors. SCAP-derived clusters 7 and 9 were identified as key subpopulations with progenitor and stromal features, enriched in IGF and NOTCH signalling components. While these observations provide valuable insights, functional implications remain speculative and require prospective isolation and experimental validation. Overall, this study provides a high-resolution transcriptional atlas of cultured SCAPs and DPSCs, generating testable hypotheses regarding subpopulation-specific regenerative roles that can be explored through in vitro differentiation assays and in vivo transplantation models.
