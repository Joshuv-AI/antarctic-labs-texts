Extremal conditional independence for Hüsler–Reiss distributions via modular functions
Karel Devriendt, Ignacio Echave-Sustaeta Rodríguez, Frank Röttger

Abstract
. We study extremal conditional independence for Hüsler–Reiss distributions, which is a parametric subclass of multivariate Pareto distributions. As the main contribution, we introduce two set functions, i.e. functions which assign a value to the distribution and each of its marginals, and show that extremal conditional independence statements can be characterized by modularity relations for these functions. For the first function, we make use of the close connection between Hüsler–Reiss and Gaussian models to introduce a multiinformation-inspired measure m HR m^{\rm{HR}} for Hüsler–Reiss distributions. For the second function, we consider an invariant σ 2 \sigma^{2} that is naturally associated to the Hüsler–Reiss parameterization and establish the second modularity criterion under additional positivity constraints. Together, these results provide new tools for describing extremal dependence structures in high-dimensional extreme value statistics. In addition, we study the geometry of a bounded subset of Hüsler–Reiss parameters and its relation with the Gaussian elliptope.



1.
Introduction

When several components of a system become large at the same time, decisions depend on how these extremes occur jointly rather than on each margin in isolation. Examples include simultaneous river floods, joint price spikes, or infrastructure failures. In such problems one wants models that describe which subsets can become large together and which parts of the system effectively decouple once others are known.
A popular model for such questions are generalized multivariate Pareto distributions, which can be obtained as the limit of threshold exceedances
(
Rootzén and
Tajvidi 2006
)
.
Here, we consider a multivariate observation as extreme if at least one entry exceeds a high threshold
u
, where the limit is taken as
u\to\infty
.
While this approach allows to model multivariate extremes of large systems, its support is by construction not a product space.
Thus, standard notions of conditional independence and graphical models are not applicable.
A suitable alternative called
extremal conditional independence
was introduced by
Engelke and
Hitz 2020
, which sparked a new research direction for extremal dependence modeling. We refer to the review article of
Engelke
et al. 2024
for further details and references.

In this paper, we study extremal conditional independence for a parametric subclass of multivariate Pareto distributions.
This subclass is the family of Hüsler–Reiss distributions, which can be parametrized by a
variogram
matrix
\Gamma
.
This family of distributions can be considered as an extremal analogue of the multivariate Gaussians, due to many remarkable properties.
For example, if the threshold exceedances in a system converge to a Hüsler–Reiss distribution, the limit of threshold exceedances of any subsystem will also be Hüsler–Reiss, parameterized by the corresponding submatrix of
\Gamma
.
Furthermore, the Hüsler–Reiss distribution allows an alternative parameterization by a
precision matrix

\Theta
, which can be interpreted as the Laplacian matrix of a signed graph
(
Devriendt 2022
)
.
As Hüsler–Reiss distributions allow a parametric encoding of extremal conditional independence, they give rise to extremal graphical models, where zeros in
\Theta
encode the pairwise extremal Markov property
(
Hentschel
et al. 2025
)
.

As the main contribution of this paper we introduce two criteria that encode arbitrary extremal conditional independence statements for Hüsler–Reiss distributions.
First, we introduce a general criterion based on Gaussian multiinformation.
Here, we exploit that Hüsler–Reiss distributions can be characterized by multivariate Gaussians where both the covariance and mean depend on the variogram matrix
\Gamma
.
For a
d
-variate distribution, this gives rise to a set function
m^{\rm{HR}}
that assigns to each subset
I\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
of indices the value

m^{\rm{HR}}(I)\,:=\,-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\begin{pmatrix}-\Gamma_{I,I}/2&\mathbf{1}\\
-\mathbf{1}^{\top}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

The main result is that this set function satisfies a linear modularity relation of the form
m^{\rm{HR}}(ABC)+m^{\rm{HR}}(C)=m^{\rm{HR}}(AB)+m^{\rm{HR}}(BC)
if and only if there is an extremal conditional independence relation between the nonempty disjoint subsets
A,B,C
of variables; we refer to Theorem
3.5
for details and notation.
We highlight that this provides the first parametric encoding of extremal conditional independence for multivariate conditioned sets, since all previous results were restricted to conditioned sets that are singletons
(
Engelke
et al. 2024
;
Engelke
et al. 2025
)
.

The second set function is based on an invariant that naturally shows up in the Hüsler–Reiss parameterization and its exponent measure density. For a
d
-variate distribution with variogram
\Gamma
, this gives rise to a set function
\sigma^{2}
that assigns to each subset
I\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
of indices the value

\sigma^{2}(I):=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbf{1}^{T}\Gamma_{I,I}^{-1}\mathbf{1}\big)^{-1}.

Under the condition that the associated precision matrix
\Theta
has nonpositive off-diagonal entries and that
\Gamma^{-1}
has positive row sums, we show that
\sigma^{2}
satisfies modularity relations if and only if an associated conditional independence relation holds; this is Theorem
4.4
.
We illustrate both results in detail for Hüsler–Reiss graphical models for the
4
-cycle graph. We note that the sign restriction on
\Theta
is equivalent to extremal multivariate total positivity of order 2 (
\text{EMTP}_{2}
) for Hüsler–Reiss distributions, which is an important notion of positive dependence
(
Röttger et al. 2023
)
. If
\Gamma
is interpreted in terms of resistance geometry, the set function
\sigma^{2}
is called the resistance radius, while the condition that
\Gamma^{-1}
has positive rowsums imposes positive resistance curvature.
We refer to
Devriendt 2022
for further reading on resistance geometry.

Finally, we investigate the geometry of variogram matrices and introduce the Hüsler–Reiss elliptope as a natural choice for a bounded geometric object defined by variogram matrices. We discuss the relationship of the Hüsler–Reiss elliptope with the classical Gaussian elliptope, and visualize our findings in three dimensions.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Multivariate extremes and extremal conditional independence

Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
be a
d
-variate random vector, where each margin
X_{i}
follows a continuous probability distribution. In extremal dependence modeling we are interested in modeling simultaneous occurrences of rare events.
If the limit exists, the extremal correlation coefficient

\chi_{ij}:=\lim_{q\to 0}\mathbb{P}(F_{i}(X_{i})>1-q\mid F_{j}(X_{j})>1-q),

where
F_{i}
is the cumulative distribution function of
X_{i}
,
is a bivariate summary statistic for extremal dependence.
If
\chi_{ij}>0
, we call
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
asymptotically dependent, while for
\chi_{ij}=0
, one speaks of asymptotic independence.
In order to focus on the extremal dependence structure we define a standardized vector
\mathbf{X}^{*}
via
X_{i}^{*}=-\log(1-F_{i}(X_{i}))
, such that all
X_{i}^{*}
follow a standard exponential distribution.

In order to model the extremal behaviour of
\mathbf{X}^{*}
, we use the approach of multivariate threshold exceedances. Here, one considers any observation of
\mathbf{X}^{*}
as extreme if at least one of its components exceeds a high threshold
u\in\mathbb{R}
.
The limiting distribution of this approach, if it exists
(
Rootzén and
Tajvidi 2006
)
, is a (generalized) multivariate Pareto distribution

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{Y}\leq\mathbf{y})=\lim_{u\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(\mathbf{X}^{*}-u\mathbf{1}\leq\mathbf{y}\mid\max_{i\in[d]}X_{i}^{*}\geq u\big),

where
\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
is the all-ones vector. Under the assumption of asymptotic dependence, the distribution of
\mathbf{Y}
is supported on the
L
-shaped space

\mathcal{L}:=\big\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\max_{i\in[d]}y_{i}>0\big\}

and has a probability density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The density of
\mathbf{Y}
is proportional to the density
\lambda(\mathbf{y})
of the exponent measure corresponding to
\mathbf{Y}
, restricted to
\mathcal{L}
, see e.g.
Engelke
et al. 2024
for details.
When
\mathbf{Y}
is restricted to a positive canonical halfspace
\mathcal{L}^{k}:=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{L}:y_{k}>0\}
for some
k\in[d]
, the resulting vector
\mathbf{Y}^{k}:=\mathbf{Y}|\{Y_{k}>0\}
has probability density
\lambda(\mathbf{y})
on its support
\mathcal{L}^{k}
. Thus, for this paper, we can treat
\lambda(\mathbf{y})
as a function whose restriction to any positive canonical halfspace gives the probability density of
\mathbf{Y}^{k}
.

For any subset
I\subseteq[d]
, the limiting distribution (
1
) of a marginal vector
\mathbf{X}_{I}^{*}
is again a multivariate Pareto vector
\mathbf{Y}_{(I)}
whose exponent measure density is given by
\lambda_{I}(\mathbf{y}_{I})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-|I|}}\lambda(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}_{[d]\setminus I}
; we note that
\mathbf{Y}_{(I)}
typically follows a different distribution than the marginal vector
(Y_{i})_{i\in I}
of
\mathbf{Y}
. This notion of marginalization gives rise to conditional densities
\lambda(\mathbf{y}_{A}|\mathbf{y}_{C}):=\frac{\lambda_{AC}(\mathbf{y}_{AC})}{\lambda_{C}(\mathbf{y}_{C})}
, where we recall that
AC
abbreviates the union
A\cup C\subseteq[d]
. Because
\mathbf{Y}^{k}
has density
\lambda(\mathbf{y})
on its support
\mathcal{L}^{k}
and because the latter is a product space, the conditional vector
\mathbf{Y}^{k}_{A}|\{\mathbf{Y}^{k}_{C}=\mathbf{y}_{C}\}
has density
\lambda(\mathbf{y}_{A}|\mathbf{y}_{C})
, for any nonempty disjoint sets
A,C\subset[d]
with
k\in C
.

Since the support
\mathcal{L}
of
\mathbf{Y}
is not a product space, the standard notion of conditional independence is not meaningful for multivariate Pareto distributions.

Engelke and
Hitz 2020
proposed an alternative based on factorization of the exponent measure density, which is equivalent to standard conditional independence for the collection of vectors
\{\mathbf{Y}^{k}\}_{k\in[d]}
.

Definition 1

(
(
Engelke and
Hitz 2020
, Definition 5, Proposition 1)
)
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}
be a multivariate Pareto vector with exponential margins, and denote its exponent measure density by
\lambda
. Let
A,B,C
be a partition of the index set. We say that
\mathbf{Y}_{A}
is
extremal conditionally independent

of
\mathbf{Y}_{B}
given
\mathbf{Y}_{C}
, denoted by
\mathbf{Y}_{A}\perp_{e}\mathbf{Y}_{B}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C}
, if and only if

\lambda(\mathbf{y})\lambda_{C}(\mathbf{y}_{C})\,=\,\lambda_{AC}(\mathbf{y}_{AC})\lambda_{BC}(\mathbf{y}_{BC})\quad\text{~for all $\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{L}$.}

The extremal conditional independence condition can also be stated in terms of conditional densities as
\lambda(\mathbf{y}_{AB}|\mathbf{y}_{C})=\lambda(\mathbf{y}_{A}|\mathbf{y}_{C})\lambda(\mathbf{y}_{B}|\mathbf{y}_{C})
. The notion of extremal conditional independence in Definition
1
allows for flexible modeling of dependence structures in multivariate extremes. In particular, it gives rise to directed and undirected notions of extremal graphical models. If
G=(V,E)
is an undirected graph, we say that a multivariate Pareto vector
\mathbf{Y}
satisfies the
undirected extremal global Markov property
with respect to
G
, when for any disjoint
A,B\subset V
with separating set
C
, we have
\mathbf{Y}_{A}\perp_{e}\mathbf{Y}_{B}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C}
; here, the margins
[d]
are identified with the vertex set
V
. A vector
\mathbf{Y}
satisfies the
undirected extremal pairwise Markov property
with respect to
G
, when for any non-edge
ij\notin E
, we have
Y_{i}\perp_{e}Y_{j}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{V\backslash\{i,j\}}
.
For further details on extremal graphical models we refer to
Engelke and
Hitz 2020
;
Röttger et al. 2023
;
Röttger
et al. 2023
;
Hentschel
et al. 2025
;
Engelke
et al. 2025
.

2.2.
Hüsler–Reiss distributions

In this article we focus on a parametric subclass of multivariate Pareto distributions, which can be considered as an extremal analogue to multivariate Gaussian distributions. Similar to how a Gaussian distribution is parametrized by its covariance matrix, a
Hüsler–Reiss
multivariate Pareto distribution is parametrized by its
variogram

\Gamma
. A variogram is a conditionally negative definite matrix which, for
d
-variate distributions, is a matrix in the following set

\mathcal{D}^{d}\,:=\,\big\{\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\,:\,\Gamma=\Gamma^{T},~\text{diag}(\Gamma)=0\text{~and~}\mathbf{x}^{T}\Gamma\mathbf{x}<0~\forall\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{x}\perp\mathbf{1}\big\}.

The exponent measure density of a Hüsler–Reiss distribution with variogram
\Gamma
is given by:

(2)

\lambda(\mathbf{y})\,:=\,\sqrt{\frac{-(2\pi)^{1-d}}{\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma)}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}&1\end{pmatrix}\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\right)\quad\text{~where~}\quad\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma):=\begin{pmatrix}-\Gamma/2&\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{1}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Here, the matrix
\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma)
is called the
Cayley--Menger matrix
1
1

1

The name “Cayley–Menger matrix” is adopted from the context of distance geometry, where the matrix
\operatorname{CM}(-2D)
associated to a Euclidean distance matrix
D
plays an important role.
of
\Gamma
. An alternative way to parametrize Hüsler–Reiss distributions is via its
precision matrix

\Theta
. This is a positive semidefinite matrix with
\ker(\Theta)=\rm{span}(\mathbf{1})
and can be obtained from the variogram via the
Fiedler–Bapat identity

(
Devriendt 2022
)

(3)

\begin{pmatrix}\Theta&\mathbf{p}\\
\mathbf{p}^{T}&\sigma^{2}\end{pmatrix}\,=\,\begin{pmatrix}-\Gamma/2&\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{1}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1},

where the vector
\mathbf{p}
and scalar
\sigma^{2}
are fully determined by the precision matrix or variogram; see also Section
4
. In terms of the precision matrix, the Hüsler–Reiss exponent measure density is written as

(4)

\lambda(\mathbf{y})\,=\,\sqrt{(2\pi)^{1-d}\det(\Theta_{\backslash k,\backslash k})}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Theta&\mathbf{p}\\
\mathbf{p}^{T}&\sigma^{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\right),

where
k
can be any element of
[d]

\backslash k:=[d]\backslash k

Hüsler–Reiss distributions have the special property that their marginals
\mathbf{Y}_{(I)}
, i.e., the limiting distribution of
\mathbf{X}^{*}_{I}
in (
1
) for some
I\subseteq[d]

I

\Gamma_{I,I}
of
\Gamma
. For the corresponding precision matrix, we use the notation

\begin{pmatrix}\Theta(I)&\mathbf{p}(I)\\
\mathbf{p}(I)^{T}&\sigma(I)^{2}\end{pmatrix}\,:=\,\begin{pmatrix}-\Gamma_{I,I}/2&\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{1}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}.

We note that with this notation, we have
\Theta([d])=\Theta,\mathbf{p}([d])=\mathbf{p}
and
\sigma^{2}([d])=\sigma^{2}
. In Section
4
we will say more about how these
I

I\subseteq[d]
.

The specific definition of the Hüsler–Reiss exponent measure density allows one to derive a closed-form expression for conditional exponent measure densities. For any two nonempty disjoint subsets
A,C\subset[d]
,
the conditional exponent measure density
\lambda(\mathbf{y}_{A}|\mathbf{y}_{C})
is Gaussian with covariance matrix and mean:

\Sigma^{*}=-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{A,A}-\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{A,C}&\mathbf{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{C,C})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{C,A}\\
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\\
\end{pmatrix}\;\text{~and~}\;\mu^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{A,C}&\mathbf{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{C,C})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{C}\\
1\\
\end{pmatrix},

see
Engelke
et al. 2025
. Note that the covariance matrix
\Sigma^{*}
is a Schur complement of the Cayley–Menger matrix, resembling the covariance structure of conditional Gaussian distributions.

Due to this characterization of
\lambda(\mathbf{y}_{A}|\mathbf{y}_{C})
as a Gaussian density, the Hüsler–Reiss distribution allows for a parametric description of arbitrary extremal conditional independence statements; we recall the notation
AB=A\cup B
and
\backslash A=[d]\setminus A

When used as indices, we display a singleton set
\{i\}

Lemma 2.1

(
Engelke

Röttger

.

Let
\mathbf{Y}
be a Hüsler–Reiss vector with variogram
\Gamma

\Theta

\{i\},\{j\},C\subset[d]
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

Y_{i}\perp_{e}Y_{j}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C}
,

(ii)

\Theta(Cij)_{ij}=0,

(iii)

\det\left(\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{Ci,Cj})\right)\;=\;0,

(iv)

\det\bigl(\Theta_{\backslash Ci,\backslash Cj}\bigr)=0.

In particular, for the case

Y_{i}\perp_{e}Y_{j}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\backslash ij}\iff\Theta_{ij}=0.

Thus for a given graph

This provides a parametric representation of the graphical model, see also

3.
Extremal conditional independence via Gaussian multiinformation

We first recall the concept of multiinformation and some relevant results for this article; we refer to
Studený 2005
as our main reference.

Definition 2

(Relative entropy)
.

Let
P
and
Q
be two probability measures with probability densities
p
and
q
, defined over the same space
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
. Then, the
relative entropy of
P
with respect to
Q
is defined as

H(P|Q):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}p(x)\log\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right)dx.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a
d
-variate random vector that follows a continuous probability measure
P
with density
p
. Denote by
P_{I}
and
p_{I}
the probability measure and density of the marginal vector
\mathbf{X}_{I}
for any
I\subseteq[d]
. Given a partition
A,B,C
of
[d]
, the vector
\mathbf{X}_{A}
is conditionally independent of
\mathbf{X}_{B}
given
\mathbf{X}_{C}
if and only if
p(\mathbf{x}_{AB}|\mathbf{x}_{C})=p(\mathbf{x}_{A}|\mathbf{x}_{C})p(\mathbf{x}_{B}|\mathbf{x}_{C})
. We denote this by
\mathbf{X}_{A}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathbf{X}_{B}|\mathbf{X}_{C}
.

Definition 3

(Multiinformation)
.

Let
P
be a probability measure defined over a space
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
multiinformation

m_{P}
of a subset
I\subseteq[d]
is defined as:

m_{P}(I):=H(P_{I}|\prod\limits_{i\in I}P_{i}).

We note that this concept was originally defined in
Watanabe 1960
under the name total correlation. The multiinformation function allows for an alternative description of conditional independence statements in terms of modularity equations, as illustrated by the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1

(
Studený 2005
)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a random vector with probability measure
P
and finite multiinformation. Then for any disjoint
A,B,C\subset[d]
the multiinformation is supermodular

m_{P}(ABC)+m_{P}(C)\geq m_{P}(AC)+m_{P}(BC),

with equality (
=
modular) if and only if the subsets satisfy the conditional independence relation:

(5)

m_{P}(ABC)+m_{P}(C)-m_{P}(AC)-m_{P}(BC)=0\iff\mathbf{X}_{A}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathbf{X}_{B}\mid\mathbf{X}_{C}.

Note that for an empty conditioning set
C
in (
5
), the marginal independence statement
\mathbf{X}_{A}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathbf{X}_{B}
is equivalent to

m_{P}(AB)-m_{P}(A)-m_{P}(B)=0\quad\text{~for any disjoint $A,B\subset[d]$.}

Example 2

(Gaussian distributions)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a
d
-variate Gaussian distribution with correlation matrix
R
, the multiinformation function has the explicit expression

(6)

\displaystyle m_{P}(I)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(R_{I,I})),

for any
I\subseteq[d]
.
Thus, for any disjoint
A,B,C\subseteq[d]
we obtain that
\mathbf{X}_{A}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathbf{X}_{B}\mid\mathbf{X}_{C}
is equivalent to

-\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(R_{ABC,ABC}))-\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(R_{C,C}))+\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(R_{AC,AC}))+\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(R_{BC,BC}))=0.

3.1.
Extremal conditional independence for Hüsler–Reiss distributions

In this section, we introduce a set function that encodes extremal conditional independence for Hüsler–Reiss distributions via modularity, similar to Theorem
3.5
. Due to the shape of the support
\mathcal{L}
of a Hüsler–Reiss vector
\mathbf{Y}
, densities can never be fully factorized and the standard definition of multiinformation cannot be used. As an alternative, we propose a set function that encodes extremal conditional independence relations for Hüsler–Reiss distributions based on the Gaussianity of conditional exponent measure densities (see
Section

2.2
).

Definition 4
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}
be a Hüsler–Reiss vector with variogram
\Gamma
. For
\emptyset\neq I\subseteq[d]
, we define
m^{\rm{HR}}(I)
as

m^{\rm{HR}}(I)\,:=\,-\frac{1}{2}\log\det\begin{pmatrix}-\Gamma_{I,I}/2&\mathbf{1}\\
-\mathbf{1}^{\top}&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

and we let
m^{\rm{HR}}(\emptyset):=0
.

Note that
m^{\rm{HR}}
is not the actual multiinformation for the Hüsler–Reiss distribution, as its support is not a product space. We start by showing a few basic properties of this function.

Proposition 3.2
.

The function
m^{\rm{HR}}
satisfies

(i)

m^{\rm{HR}}(i)=0
for all
i\in I
,

(ii)

m^{\rm{HR}}(ij)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(\Gamma_{ij})
for all
ij\subseteq[d]
,

(iii)

m^{\rm{HR}}(I)-m^{\rm{HR}}(I\backslash i)=\frac{1}{2}\log(\Theta(I)_{ii})
for all
i\in I\subseteq[d]
of size
|I|>1
.

Proof.

Expressions (i) and (ii) follow by direct computation. Expression (iii) follows by writing out the difference on the lefthandside as

m^{\rm{HR}}(I)-m^{\rm{HR}}(I\backslash i)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{I\backslash i,I\backslash i})}{\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{I,I})}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\log(\Theta(I)_{ii}),

where the last step follows by the Fiedler–Bapat identity, and the cofactor formula for matrix inversion.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Property (ii) in Proposition
3.2
implies that the set function
m^{\rm{HR}}
contains all information about the parameterization: all entries of the variogram can be retrieved by the transformation
\Gamma_{ij}=e^{-2m^{\rm{HR}}(ij)}
. As a result, one may think of the function
m^{\rm{HR}}
on subsets of
[d]
as yet another parameterization.

We note that
m^{\rm{HR}}
is not standardized to nonnegative values.
We can derive a standardization for any
k\in I
. Let

\Sigma^{(k)}:=-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{I\setminus k,I\setminus k}-\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{I,k}&\mathbf{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{k,k})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{k,I}\\
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\\
\end{pmatrix}

be the covariance matrix of the conditional Hüsler–Reiss exponent measure density
\lambda(\mathbf{y}_{I\setminus k}|y_{k})
.
Then, by standard Schur complement arguments,
m^{\rm{HR}}(I)=-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\Sigma^{(k)})
.
If we standardize
\Sigma^{(k)}
to a correlation matrix, the resulting Gaussian multiinformation is equal to

m^{\rm{HR}}(I)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in I\setminus k}\log\left(\Sigma^{(k)}_{ii}\right)
,
which is nonnegative. As this function depends on
k
and our interest is in extremal conditional independence, we omitted this standardization in the definition of
m^{\rm{HR}}
.

In addition to the alternative representation of
m^{\rm{HR}}
based on the covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(k)}
, we collect further alternative representations in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

The following expressions are equivalent for the function
m^{\rm{HR}}
and any nonempty
I\subseteq[d]
:

(i)

m^{\rm{HR}}(I)\,=\,-\frac{1}{2}\log\big(-\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{I,I})\big),

(ii)

m^{\rm{HR}}(I)=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\Theta(I)_{\backslash k,\backslash k})
for any
k\in I
,

(iii)

m^{\rm{HR}}(I)=\frac{1}{2}\log\operatorname{Det}(\Theta(I))-\frac{1}{2}\log(|I|)
, where
\operatorname{Det}
is the pseudo-determinant, i.e. the product of all nonzero eigenvalues.

(iv)

m^{\rm{HR}}(I)=-\log\int_{y_{k}>0}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{I}&1\end{pmatrix}\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{I,I})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{I}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\right)d\mathbf{y}_{I}+\frac{|I|-1}{2}\log(2\pi)
for any
k\in I
.

(v)

m^{\rm{HR}}(I)=\tfrac{1}{2}\log\big(\sum_{T}\prod_{ij\in E(T)}(-\Theta(I)_{ij})\big)
, where
T
runs over all spanning trees of the graph corresponding to
\Theta(I)
.

Proof.

Expression (i) follows from the definition of
\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma)
and basic identities for determinants. Expression (ii) follows by observing that
\Theta(I)_{\backslash k,\backslash k}
is the inverse of the matrix
\Sigma^{(k)}
defined above. For expression (iii), we use the identity
\operatorname{Det}(\Theta(I))=|I|\det(\Theta(I)_{\backslash k,\backslash k})
, as derived in
(
Röttger et al. 2023
, Eq. (23))
.
Expression (iv) follows from the fact that the Hüsler–Reiss exponent measure density (
4
) is a probability density on each canonical halfspace
\mathcal{L}^{k}
, and thus

\int_{y_{k}>0}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{I}&\mathbf{1}\par\end{pmatrix}^{T}\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{I,I})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{I}\\
\mathbf{1}\end{pmatrix}\right)d\mathbf{y}_{I}=\left((2\pi)^{1-|I|}\det(\Theta(I)_{\backslash k,\backslash k})\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Introducing (ii) then leads to expression (iv). Finally, expression (v) follows from (ii) and the weighted matrix-tree theorem, see e.g.
Röttger et al. 2023
.
∎

In the following theorem, we show that
m^{\rm{HR}}
is supermodular and can encode arbitrary extremal conditional independence for Hüsler–Reiss distributions via modularity.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}
be a Hüsler–Reiss vector. Then for any nonempty disjoint
A,B,C\subset[d]
, the function
m^{\rm{HR}}
is supermodular

m^{\rm{HR}}(ABC)+m^{\rm{HR}}(C)\geq m^{\rm{HR}}(AC)+m^{\rm{HR}}(BC),

with equality (
=
modular) if and only if the subsets satisfy the extremal conditional independence relation

m^{\rm{HR}}(ABC)+m^{\rm{HR}}(C)=m^{\rm{HR}}(AC)+m^{\rm{HR}}(BC)\iff\mathbf{Y}_{A}\perp_{e}\mathbf{Y}_{B}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C}.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
A,B,C
is a partition of the index set
[d]
. The conditional exponent measure density
\lambda(\mathbf{y}_{AB}\mid\mathbf{y}_{C})=\frac{\lambda(\mathbf{y})}{\lambda(\mathbf{y}_{C})}
of a Hüsler–Reiss vector
\mathbf{Y}
with variogram
\Gamma
and exponent measure density
\lambda(\mathbf{y})
is an
|AB|
-variate Gaussian with covariance matrix

\displaystyle\Sigma^{*}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{AB,AB}-\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{AB,C}&\mathbf{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{C,C}&\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{1}^{\top}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{C,AB}\\
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\\
\end{pmatrix},

see Section
2.1
. Let
R^{*}
be the correlation matrix of
\Sigma^{*}
and note that
\Sigma^{*}=d_{\Sigma^{*}}R^{*}d_{\Sigma^{*}}
where
d_{\Sigma^{*}}:=\sqrt{\text{diag}(\Sigma^{*})}
.
As a result, we have

\displaystyle\log\det(R^{*})

\displaystyle=\log\det(\Sigma^{*})-\sum_{i\in AB}\log(\Sigma^{*}_{ii})

\displaystyle\log\det(R^{*}_{A,A})

\displaystyle=\log\det(\Sigma^{*}_{A,A})-\sum_{i\in A}\log(\Sigma^{*}_{ii}).

Using standard properties of Schur complements, we furthermore find that

\det(\Sigma^{*})=\frac{\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma)}{\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{C,C})}\quad\text{~and~}\quad\det(\Sigma^{*}_{A,A})=\frac{\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{AC,AC})}{\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{C,C})}.

Together, we thus find that
-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\Sigma^{*})=m^{\rm{HR}}(ABC)-m^{\rm{HR}}(C)
and similarly
-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\Sigma^{*}_{A,A})=m^{\rm{HR}}(AC)-m^{\rm{HR}}(C)
.
By
3.1
and these identities, it then holds that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(R^{*}))+\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(R^{*}_{A,A}))+\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(R^{*}_{B,B}))\geq 0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(\Sigma^{*}))+\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(\Sigma^{*}_{A,A}))+\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(\Sigma^{*}_{B,B}))\geq 0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle~~m^{\rm{HR}}(ABC)-m^{\rm{HR}}(C)-(m^{\rm{HR}}(AC)-m^{\rm{HR}}(C))-(m^{\rm{HR}}(BC)-m^{\rm{HR}}(C))\geq 0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle~~m^{\rm{HR}}(ABC)+m^{\rm{HR}}(C)-m^{\rm{HR}}(AC)-m^{\rm{HR}}(BC)\geq 0,

which confirms that
m^{\rm{HR}}
is supermodular.

Finally, since the conditional exponent measure density is the density of an
|AB|
-variate Gaussian with correlation matrix
R^{*}
, we know by Theorem
3.1
that extremal conditional independence
\mathbf{Y}_{A}\perp_{e}\mathbf{Y}_{B}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C}
is equivalent to the equality

-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(R^{*})+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(R^{*}_{A,A})+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(R^{*}_{B,B})=0.

Rewriting the lefthandside as above using
m^{\rm{HR}}
, this establishes the desired equivalence:

\displaystyle m^{\rm{HR}}(ABC)+m^{\rm{HR}}(C)-m^{\rm{HR}}(AC)-m^{\rm{HR}}(BC)=0\iff\mathbf{Y}_{A}\perp_{e}\mathbf{Y}_{B}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C}.

∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Under the same conditions as
3.5
, we have
\mathbf{Y}_{A}\perp_{e}\mathbf{Y}_{B}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C}
if and only if

\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{ABC,ABC})\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{C,C})\,-\,\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{AC,AC})\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{BC,BC})\,=\,0.

To illustrate Theorem
3.5
and Corollary
3.6
we return to our running example.

Example 3
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}
be a Hüsler–Reiss graphical model with respect to the four-cycle as in Example
1
. This means that
\mathbf{Y}
satisfies two extremal conditional independence statements
Y_{1}\perp_{e}Y_{3}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{24}
and
Y_{2}\perp_{e}Y_{4}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{13}
. In terms of the result of
3.5
, we get the following equalities:

\displaystyle m^{\rm{HR}}(1234)+m^{\rm{HR}}(24)-m^{\rm{HR}}(124)-m^{\rm{HR}}(234)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle m^{\rm{HR}}(1234)+m^{\rm{HR}}(13)-m^{\rm{HR}}(123)-m^{\rm{HR}}(134)

\displaystyle=0.

Using Corollary
3.6
, under the assumption of a conditionally negative definite variogram we obtain

\displaystyle\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{1234,1234})\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{24,24})

\displaystyle-\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{124,124})\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{234,234})=0,

\displaystyle\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{1234,1234})\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{13,13})

\displaystyle-\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{123,123})\det\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{134,134})=0.

These are polynomial equations in the entries of

, which explicitly expressed are

\displaystyle\left(\Gamma_{12}\Gamma_{23}-\Gamma_{14}\Gamma_{23}-\Gamma_{12}\Gamma_{24}+2\Gamma_{13}\Gamma_{24}-\Gamma_{14}\Gamma_{24}-\Gamma_{23}\Gamma_{24}+\Gamma_{24}^{2}-\Gamma_{12}\Gamma_{34}+\Gamma_{14}\Gamma_{34}-\Gamma_{24}\Gamma_{34}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\left(\Gamma_{12}\Gamma_{13}-\Gamma_{13}^{2}-\Gamma_{12}\Gamma_{14}+\Gamma_{13}\Gamma_{14}+\Gamma_{13}\Gamma_{23}+\Gamma_{14}\Gamma_{23}-2\Gamma_{13}\Gamma_{24}+\Gamma_{12}\Gamma_{34}+\Gamma_{13}\Gamma_{34}-\Gamma_{23}\Gamma_{34}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=0.

Since both

and

are conditional independence statements for singletons, by Lemma
2.1
they are given by the vanishing minors

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\det\left(\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{124,234})\right)

\displaystyle\propto\Gamma_{12}\Gamma_{23}-\Gamma_{14}\Gamma_{23}-\Gamma_{12}\Gamma_{24}+2\Gamma_{13}\Gamma_{24}-\Gamma_{14}\Gamma_{24}-\Gamma_{23}\Gamma_{24}+\Gamma_{24}^{2}-\Gamma_{12}\Gamma_{34}+\Gamma_{14}\Gamma_{34}-\Gamma_{24}\Gamma_{34},

\displaystyle 0

4.
Extremal conditional independence via
\sigma^{2}

In this section, we consider a second set function that encodes extremal conditional independence for Hüsler–Reiss distributions. Recall the invariant
\sigma^{2}
and vector
\mathbf{p}
, which naturally appears in the Hüsler–Reiss exponent measure density
\lambda(\mathbf{y})
; see (
3
) and (
4
). By associating to each marginal
\mathbf{Y}_{(I)}
the corresponding invariant
\sigma^{2}(I)
, we obtain a function on subsets of the index set. Our main result states that for a Hüsler–Reiss distribution which satisfies EMTP
2
and
\mathbf{p}>0
, modularity of the set function
\sigma^{2}
is equivalent to extremal conditional independence for the corresponding modular sets.

4.1.
Marginalization and properties of
\mathbf{p}
and
\sigma^{2}

From the Fiedler–Bapat identity (
3
), one can immediately derive the following explicit expressions for
\mathbf{p}
and
\sigma^{2}
in terms of the variogram:

\mathbf{p}=\frac{\Gamma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^{T}\Gamma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}\quad\text{~and~}\quad\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbf{1}^{T}\Gamma^{-1}\mathbf{1}\big)^{-1}.

Since marginalization of Hüsler–Reiss distributions corresponds to taking submatrices at the level of the variogram, we likewise find that the same equations as above hold for
\mathbf{p}(I)
and
\sigma^{2}(I)
, when replacing
\Gamma
by
\Gamma_{I,I}
for any nonempty
I\subseteq[d]
with
|I|>1
; for single marginals
Y_{i}
, we define
\mathbf{p}(i)=1
and
\sigma^{2}(i)=0
for consistency with equation (
3
). We furthermore note that
\sigma^{2}(ij)=\Gamma_{ij}/4
for all
ij\subseteq[d]
and thus that, as was the case for
m^{\rm{HR}}
, the set function
\sigma^{2}
contains all information about
\Gamma
.

While the expressions above for
\mathbf{p}
and
\sigma^{2}
allow for immediate computation, we mention a result that is more useful when comparing between different margins
I^{\prime},I\subseteq[d]
. The following results are an immediate consequence of the inverse relation (
3
) between the variogram and precision matrix.

Proposition 4.1

(
Fiedler 2011
)
.

Let
\Theta
be a Hüsler–Reiss precision matrix. Then for nonempty
I\subseteq[d]
we have

\displaystyle\Theta(I)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{I,I}-\Theta_{I,\backslash I}(\Theta_{\backslash I,\backslash I})^{-1}\Theta_{\backslash I,I}

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(I)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}_{I}-\Theta_{I,\backslash I}(\Theta_{\backslash I,\backslash I})^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{\backslash I}

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(I)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}-\mathbf{p}_{\backslash I}^{T}(\Theta_{\backslash I,\backslash I})^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{\backslash I}.

Proof.

Combining the Fiedler–Bapat identity for both
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{(I)}
, we find

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\Theta(I)&\mathbf{p}(I)\\
\mathbf{p}(I)^{T}&\sigma^{2}(I)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\Gamma_{I,I}/2&\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{1}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{pmatrix}-\Gamma/2&\mathbf{1}\\
\mathbf{1}^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}_{I+,I+}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\Theta_{I,I}&\mathbf{p}_{I}\\
\mathbf{p}_{I}^{T}&\sigma^{2}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\Theta_{I,\backslash I}&\mathbf{p}_{\backslash I}\end{pmatrix}(\Theta_{\backslash I,\backslash I})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\Theta_{\backslash I,}\\
\mathbf{p}_{\backslash I}^{T}\end{pmatrix},

where
I+
denotes the set
I
including the last row/column index, and the last equality is a standard result for Schur complements. This completes the proof.
∎

The following two closure properties follow immediately.

Proposition 4.2

(
Röttger et al. 2023
;
Devriendt 2022
)
.

If a Hüsler–Reiss distribution is EMTP
2
, then so are all of its marginals.
Furthermore, if a Hüsler–Reiss distribution is EMTP
2
with
\mathbf{p}\geq 0
(respectively,
\mathbf{p}>0
), then so are all of its marginals.

We list a number of equivalent representations of
\sigma^{2}(I)
:

Lemma 4.3

(
Hentschel
et al. 2026
;
Devriendt 2022
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a variogram matrix and
I\subseteq[d]
with
|I|\geq 1
. Then

(i)

\sigma^{2}(I)=\frac{\det(-\Gamma_{I,I}/2)}{\det(\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{I,I}))}=\Big(\frac{-1}{2}\Big)^{|I|}\cdot\frac{\det(\Gamma_{I,I})}{\det(\operatorname{CM}(\Gamma_{I,I}))}.

(ii)

\sigma^{2}(I)=-2\log\int_{\mathbf{y}_{I}^{\top}\mathbf{p}(I)>0}\lambda_{I}(\mathbf{y}_{I})d\mathbf{y}_{I}
,

(iii)

\sigma^{2}(I)=\max\{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{T}\Gamma_{I,I}\mathbf{x}\,:\,\mathbf{1}^{T}\mathbf{x}=1\}
,

(iv)

\sigma^{2}(I)=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j\in I}\Theta(I)_{ij}(\Gamma_{ik}-\Gamma_{jk})^{2}
for any
k\in I
,

(v)

\sigma^{2}(I)=\frac{1}{4|I|}\operatorname{Tr}(\Gamma_{I,I}\Theta(I)\Gamma_{I,I}).

Proof.

The first expression follows by the Fiedler–Bapat identity and the cofactor formula for matrix inversion. The second expression is shown in
Hentschel
et al. 2026
and expressions (iii)-(v) are shown in
Devriendt 2022
.
∎

4.2.
Extremal conditional independence

Under the conditions of
\text{EMTP}_{2}
and
\mathbf{p}>0
, the following theorem shows that modularity of
\sigma^{2}
encodes extremal conditional independence for Hüsler–Reiss distributions, similar to
m^{\rm{HR}}
. We note that submodularity and the fact that modularity implies graph separation statements were proven before in
(
Devriendt 2022
, Chapter 6)
in the context of graph theory and Laplacian and effective resistance matrices.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}
be a Hüsler–Reiss vector that satisfies EMTP
2
and
\mathbf{p}\geq 0
. Then for any nonempty disjoint
A,B,C\subset[d]
, the function
\sigma^{2}
is submodular

\sigma^{2}(ABC)+\sigma^{2}(C)\leq\sigma^{2}(AC)+\sigma^{2}(BC),

with equality (
=
modular) if and only if the subsets satisfy the extremal conditional independence relation:

\sigma^{2}(ABC)+\sigma^{2}(C)=\sigma^{2}(AC)+\sigma^{2}(BC)\iff\mathbf{Y}_{A}\perp_{e}\mathbf{Y}_{B}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
A,B,C
is a partition of
[d]
. Under the assumption that
C
is nonempty and that
\mathbf{Y}
is
\text{EMTP}_{2}
, we have that
L:=\Theta_{AB,AB}
is an M-matrix, since it is a positive definite matrix with non-positive off-diagonal entries, where positive definiteness holds because it is a submatrix of the Laplacian matrix of a connected graph
(
Devriendt 2022
, Property 3.26)
.
Since
K:=L^{-1}
is the inverse of an M-matrix it has nonnegative entries
(
Fiedler 2011
, Theorem A.3.2)
. Using Schur complements, we obtain

\displaystyle(L_{A,A})^{-1}

\displaystyle=K_{A,A}-K_{A,B}K_{B,B}^{-1}K_{A,B}^{\top},

\displaystyle(L_{B,B})^{-1}

\displaystyle=K_{B,B}-K_{A,B}^{\top}K_{A,A}^{-1}K_{A,B}.

Using Proposition
4.1
, we can thus write:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(ABC)=:\sigma^{2},

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(AC)=\sigma^{2}-\mathbf{p}_{B}^{\top}(L_{B,B})^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{B}=\sigma^{2}-\mathbf{p}_{B}^{\top}(K_{B,B}-K_{A,B}^{\top}K_{A,A}^{-1}K_{A,B})\mathbf{p}_{B},

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(BC)=\sigma^{2}-\mathbf{p}_{A}^{\top}(L_{A,A})^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{A}=\sigma^{2}-\mathbf{p}_{A}^{\top}(K_{A,A}-K_{A,B}K_{B,B}^{-1}K_{A,B}^{\top})\mathbf{p}_{A},

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(C)=\sigma^{2}-\left(\mathbf{p}_{A}^{\top}K_{A,A}\mathbf{p}_{A}+\mathbf{p}_{B}^{\top}K_{B,B}\mathbf{p}_{B}+2\mathbf{p}_{A}^{\top}K_{A,B}\mathbf{p}_{B}\right).

Combining everything, we have

(7)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(AC)+\sigma^{2}(BC)

\displaystyle-\sigma^{2}(ABC)-\sigma^{2}(C)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}_{B}^{\top}K_{A,B}^{\top}K_{A,A}^{-1}K_{A,B}\mathbf{p}_{B}+\mathbf{p}_{A}^{\top}K_{A,B}K_{B,B}^{-1}K_{A,B}^{\top}\mathbf{p}_{A}+2\mathbf{p}_{A}^{\top}K_{A,B}\mathbf{p}_{B}.

Due to positive definiteness of
K_{A,A}
and
K_{B,B}
, the first two summands above are nonnegative. Furthermore, since
\mathbf{p}
is nonnegative and
K_{A,B}
is nonnegative as a submatrix of an inverse
M
-matrix, we furthermore know that
\mathbf{p}_{A}^{\top}K_{A,B}\mathbf{p}_{B}\geq 0
. This proves the submodularity statement in the theorem.

If
\mathbf{p}>0
, the sum in equation (
7
) is equal to zero if and only if
K_{AB}=\mathbf{0}
, since otherwise there would be at least one positive summand. By the cofactor expression for matrix inversion, the entries of
K_{A,B}=(\Theta_{AB,AB})^{-1}_{A,B}
are given by the minors
\det(\Theta_{\backslash Ci,\backslash Cj})
, where
i\in A,j\in B
.
The vanishing of these minors is equivalent to

Y_{i}\perp_{e}Y_{j}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C},

see Lemma
2.1
.
By
Röttger et al. 2023
, we have under
\text{EMTP}_{2}
that

\mathbf{Y}_{A}\perp_{e}\mathbf{Y}_{B}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C}

is equivalent to the collection of all statements
Y_{i}\perp_{e}Y_{j}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C}
for all pairs
i\in A
,
j\in B
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Example 4
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}
be a Hüsler–Reiss graphical model with respect to the four-cycle as in Example
1
, and which satisfies EMTP
2
and
\mathbf{p}>0
. By the pairwise Markov property,
\mathbf{Y}
satisfies two extremal conditional independence statements
Y_{1}\perp_{e}Y_{3}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{24}
and
Y_{2}\perp_{e}Y_{4}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{13}
. By Theorem
4.4
, these extremal conditional independences imply the following modularity equations:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(1234)+\sigma^{2}(24)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}(124)+\sigma^{2}(234),

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(1234)+\sigma^{2}(13)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}(123)+\sigma^{2}(134).

These modularity equations can be reduced to the following polynomial equations:

\displaystyle q_{13\mid 24}(\Theta,\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\Theta_{13}}{\Theta_{11}\Theta_{33}\big(\Theta_{11}\Theta_{33}-\Theta_{13}^{2}\big)}\Big(p_{1}^{2}\,\Theta_{13}\Theta_{33}-2\,p_{1}p_{3}\,\Theta_{11}\Theta_{33}+p_{3}^{2}\,\Theta_{11}\Theta_{13}\Big)\;=\;0,

\displaystyle q_{24\mid 13}(\Theta,\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\Theta_{24}}{\Theta_{22}\Theta_{44}\big(\Theta_{22}\Theta_{44}-\Theta_{24}^{2}\big)}\Big(p_{2}^{2}\,\Theta_{24}\Theta_{44}-2\,p_{2}p_{4}\,\Theta_{22}\Theta_{44}+p_{4}^{2}\,\Theta_{22}\Theta_{24}\Big)\;=\;0.

We observe that the denominators are nonzero, as they are determinants of positive definite submatrices. Furthermore, the equations hold under extremal conditional independence by Lemma
2.1
, as
Y_{1}\perp_{e}Y_{3}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{24}\Leftrightarrow\Theta_{13}=0
and
Y_{2}\perp_{e}Y_{4}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{13}\Leftrightarrow\Theta_{24}=0
.
However, we can only certify equivalence under the conditions of Theorem
4.4
, because only then the polynomials inside the brackets are guaranteed to be nonzero.

To conclude, we note that even though the full distribution may not satisfy EMTP
2
and
\mathbf{p}>0
, Theorem
4.4
still applies when restricted to a marginal
\mathbf{Y}_{(I)}
which does satisfy these properties.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}
be a Hüsler–Reiss vector. Then for any nonempty disjoint subsets
A,B,C\subset[d]
such that the marginal vector
\mathbf{Y}_{(ABC)}
is EMTP
2
with
\mathbf{p}>0
, we have

\mathbf{Y}_{A}\perp_{e}\mathbf{Y}_{B}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{C}\iff\sigma^{2}(ABC)+\sigma^{2}(C)=\sigma^{2}(AB)+\sigma^{2}(BC).

Proof.

This follows because conditional independence for
A,B,C
relative to
\mathbf{Y}
is equivalent to conditional independence for
A,B,C
relative to
\mathbf{Y}_{(ABC)}
. The corollary then follows by Theorem
4.4
.
∎

5.
Geometry of variogram matrices

The set of all valid correlation matrices of a multivariate Gaussian distribution is a bounded subset of the positive semidefinite cone. This is typically referred to as the Gaussian elliptope.

Definition 5

(Gaussian elliptope)
.

The
d
-dimensional Gaussian elliptope
is defined as:

\mathcal{E}_{d}:=\left\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\,\middle|\,R=R^{\top},\;R\text{ is positive semidefinite},\;\mathrm{diag}(R)=\mathbf{1}\right\}.

We also write
\mathcal{E}_{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\binom{d}{2}}
for the projection of this set onto the coordinates corresponding to the upper-triangular off-diagonal entries.

Since the matrices in the elliptope are symmetric and have diagonal elements fixed to one, the dimension of this set without further constraints is
\binom{d}{2}
. It is a bounded set, since by construction the correlations of two Gaussians is bounded in the interval
[-1,1]
. The boundary
\partial\mathcal{E}_{d}
of the Gaussian elliptope is the intersection of
\mathcal{E}_{d}
with the hypersurface of singular matrices, cut out by the polynomial
\det(R)=0
. Its interior
\mathcal{E}_{d}^{\circ}
contains positive definite correlation matrices, which is the generic case.

We aim to find and study a similar object for Hüsler–Reiss distributions. In the case of Gaussians, the standard parameterization via (positive semidefinite) covariance matrices is normalized by rescaling rows and columns to obtain a diagonal of ones. For Hüsler–Reiss distributions, the standard parameterization is via (conditionally negative definite) variograms
\mathcal{D}^{d}
, but this is again an unbounded set. In order to obtain a bounded set, we consider variograms with bounded
\sigma^{2}
. Taking the upper-triangular off-diagonal entries, we can again think of this as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\binom{d}{2}}
.

Definition 6

(Hüsler–Reiss elliptope)
.

We define the
d
-dimensional Hüsler–Reiss elliptope
as:

\mathcal{F}_{d}:=\left\{\Gamma\in\mathcal{D}^{d}\,\middle|\,\sigma^{2}(\Gamma)\leq 1\right\}.

Remark 5.1
.

Any variogram
\Gamma\in\mathcal{D}^{d}
corresponds to a Euclidean distance matrix that contains the squared Euclidean distances between
d
affinely independent points in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. In other words, the entries
\Gamma_{ij}
are the squared edge lengths of a simplex. Moreover, the squared circumradius of this simplex is
\sigma^{2}
and the barycentric coordinate of the circumcenter is
\mathbf{p}
; see
Fiedler 2011
. In particular, this means that the Hüsler–Reiss elliptope corresponds to the set of all Euclidean distance matrices of simplices with circumradius at most one.

We can relate the Hüsler–Reiss elliptope to the Gaussian elliptope by the following map:

Proposition 5.2
.

If
\Gamma
is a variogram, then
R(\Gamma):=-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\Gamma+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}
is a correlation matrix of rank
d-1
with
\ker(R)=\rm{span}(\mathbf{p})
and thus
\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{1}\neq 0
. If
R
is a correlation matrix of rank
d-1
with
\ker(R)=\rm{span}(\mathbf{w})
and
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{1}\neq 0
, then
\Gamma(R):=2(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-R)
is a variogram with
\sigma^{2}=1
and
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{w}/(\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{1})
.

Proof.

For any nonzero vector
\mathbf{x}\in\rm{span}(\mathbf{1})^{\perp}
, the quadratic form
\mathbf{x}^{T}R(\Gamma)\mathbf{x}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{x}^{T}\Gamma\mathbf{x}
is positive by conditional negative definiteness, so
R(\Gamma)
has at least
\dim({\rm{span}(\mathbf{1})}^{\perp})=d-1
positive eigenvalues. Since
\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{1}=1
by definition of the vector
\mathbf{p}
, we find
R(\Gamma)\mathbf{p}=0
and thus, by the former part that
\ker(R(\Gamma))=\rm{span}(\mathbf{p})
and that it is a positive semidefinite matrix. This proves that
R(\Gamma)
is a correlation matrix.

For the converse, we proceed again by considering the quadratic form
\mathbf{x}^{T}\Gamma(R)\mathbf{x}=-2\mathbf{x}^{T}R\mathbf{x}
with nonzero
\mathbf{x}\in\rm{span}(\mathbf{1})^{\perp}
. By positive semidefiniteness of
R
and
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{1}\neq 0
, it follows that this quadratic form is negative and thus that
\Gamma
is a variogram. Since
\Gamma(R)\mathbf{w}=2(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-R)\mathbf{w}=2(\mathbf{1}^{T}\mathbf{w}).\mathbf{w}
, the claim about
\mathbf{p}
and
\sigma^{2}
follow from their defining equation
\Gamma(R)\mathbf{p}=2\sigma^{2}\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{1}=1
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Consider the following subset of the boundary of the Gaussian elliptope:

\partial\mathcal{E}_{d}^{*}:=\big\{R\in\mathcal{E}_{d}\mid\ker(R)=\rm{span}(\mathbf{w})\textup{~with~}\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{1}\neq 0\big\}\subset\partial\mathcal{E}.

Then the Hüsler–Reiss elliptope, as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\binom{d}{2}}
, is equal to

\mathcal{F}_{d}\,=\,2(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-\mathcal{E}_{d}^{\circ}\cup\partial\mathcal{E}_{d}^{*})\,=\,\bigcup\limits_{t\in(0,1]}2t(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-\partial\mathcal{E}_{d}^{*}).

Proof.

Recall that the boundary of the elliptope contains singular correlation matrices so indeed
\partial\mathcal{E}^{*}_{d}\subset\partial\mathcal{E}
. We first show the second equality (the union expression) and then the first.

We will show that the map
\varphi:\widetilde{R}\mapsto 2(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-\widetilde{R})
is a bijection between
\mathcal{S}:=\bigcup_{t\in(0,1]}2t\cdot(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-\partial\mathcal{E}^{*}_{d})
and
\mathcal{F}_{d}
, both taken as subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. The claimed equality for
\mathcal{F}_{d}
then follows by projecting onto the
\binom{d}{2}
upper-diagonal entries.
(Surjectivity)
For any
\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}_{d}
, we know by Proposition
5.2
that the matrix
R(\Gamma)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\Gamma+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}
is a correlation matrix of rank
d-1
with
\ker(R)=\rm{span}(\mathbf{w})
and
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{1}\neq 0
, and thus that
R(\Gamma)\in\mathcal{S}
. Since
0<\sigma^{2}\leq 1
, we furthermore know that
\sigma^{2}R(\Gamma)\in\mathcal{S}
and that
\varphi(\sigma^{2}R(\Gamma))=\Gamma
; as
\Gamma
was arbitrary, we have shown that
\varphi
is surjective.

(Injectivity)
Let
\widetilde{R},\widehat{R}\in\mathcal{S}
be such that
\varphi(\widetilde{R})=\varphi(\widehat{R})=\Gamma
, then by definition of the map
\varphi
, it immediately follows that
\widetilde{R}=\widehat{R}
. This proves injectivity and thus establishes the second expression for
\mathcal{F}_{d}
.

The proof of the first statement follows similarly. For the boundary part
\partial\mathcal{E}_{d}^{*}
, the equality follows from the second statement with
t=1
. For the interior, we consider the map
\psi:\widetilde{R}\mapsto 2(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-\widetilde{R})
from
\mathcal{E}_{d}^{\circ}
to
\mathcal{F}_{d}
, both taken as subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. The image of
\widetilde{R}\in\mathcal{E}^{\circ}_{d}
under this map is a variogram since
\mathbf{x}^{T}(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-\widetilde{R})\mathbf{x}<0
for all nonzero
\mathbf{x}\perp\mathbf{1}
by positive definiteness of
\widetilde{R}
.
(Injectivity)
Since
\psi(\widetilde{R})=\psi(\widehat{R})\Leftrightarrow 2(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-\widetilde{R})=2(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}-\widehat{R})\Rightarrow\widetilde{R}=\widehat{R}
the map is injective.
(Surjectivity)
Let
\Gamma
be any variogram with
\sigma^{2}<1
. Then we know that
\psi(-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T})=\Gamma
by construction. We show that
-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}
lies in
\mathcal{E}^{\circ}
: it is symmetric, has unit diagonal and it is positive definite because
\mathbf{x}^{T}(-\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T})\mathbf{x}>0
for all nonzero
\mathbf{x}\perp\mathbf{1}
and

\mathbf{1}^{T}(-\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T})\mathbf{1}=d^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Gamma\mathbf{1}\geq d^{2}-d^{2}\sigma^{2}>0,

where the first inequality follows because
\sigma^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\max\{\mathbf{x}^{T}\Gamma\mathbf{x}:\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{1}=1\}
(see expression (iii) in Lemma
4.3
) and the second one because
\sigma^{2}<1
. This completes the proof after projecting onto
\mathbb{R}^{\binom{d}{2}}
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

The second expression for
\mathcal{F}_{d}
in Proposition
5.3
suggests that the subset
\partial\mathcal{E}^{*}_{d}
of the Gaussian elliptope boundary already describes the Hüsler–Reiss elliptope up to rescaling by
t\in(0,1]
. This suggest that one could also focus on variograms with
\sigma^{2}=1
.
