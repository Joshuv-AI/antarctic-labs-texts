Minimizing Completion Times of Stochastic Jobs on Parallel Machines is Hard
Benjamin Moseley, Kirk Pruhs, Marc Uetz, Rudy Zhou, [2ex] Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, PA, USA

Abstract
This paper considers the scheduling of stochastic jobs on parallel identical machines to minimize the expected total weighted completion time. While this is a classical problem with a significant body of research on approximation algorithms over the past two decades, constant-factor performance guarantees are currently known only under very restrictive assumptions on the input distributions, even when all job weights are identical. This algorithmic difficulty is striking given the lack of corresponding complexity results: to date, it is conceivable that the problem could be solved optimally in polynomial time. We address this gap with hardness results that demonstrate the problem’s inherent intractability. For the special case of discrete two-point processing time distributions and unit weights, we prove that deciding whether there exists a scheduling policy with expected cost at most a given threshold is #P-hard. Furthermore, we show that evaluating the expected objective value of the standard (W)SEPT greedy policy is itself #P-hard. These represent the first hardness results for scheduling independent stochastic jobs and min-sum objective that do not merely rely on the intractability of the underlying deterministic counterparts.

Introduction

This paper addresses a long-standing open question in stochastic scheduling theory: whether the computational complexity of stochastic parallel machine scheduling fundamentally differs from that of its deterministic counterpart, even in the most basic settings. In deterministic scheduling on parallel identical machines, minimizing the total completion time
\sum_{j}C_{j}
when all jobs have equal weight is solvable in polynomial time by scheduling jobs in shortest processing time (SPT) order
[
3
]
. Here
C_{j}
is the completion time of job
j
under a scheduling policy. By contrast, for the stochastic version of the same problem, where processing times are random and revealed only upon completion, the computational complexity of computing an optimal scheduling policy has remained unresolved since the early 1980s.

Despite decades of research on stochastic scheduling,
computational hardness results were previously known only for more structured problems; discussed in more detail below.
This paper provides the first evidence that the classical stochastic parallel machine scheduling for minimizing
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{j}C_{j}
] is computationally intractable, thereby separating the stochastic problem from its deterministic counterpart.

We consider a standard stochastic scheduling model with
m

parallel identical machines
and
n

non-preemptive
jobs. Each job
j
must be assigned to a single machine and, once started, runs to completion without interruption. The processing time
p_{j}
of job
j
is unknown in advance and is modeled as a nonnegative random variable
X_{j}
, independently distributed across jobs. Following recent work on approximation algorithms for stochastic scheduling, we focus on a discrete variant of the problem in which each
X_{j}
has finite support, and in particular we assume that each distribution has
support size at most two
. Each job
j
additionally may have a weight
w_{j}
.

Under a scheduling policy, let
S_{j}
and
C_{j}
denote the (random) start and completion times of job
j
, respectively. By definition,

\mathbb{E}[C_{j}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{j}]+\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]
.

The objective is to find, or evaluate, a scheduling policy that minimizes the expected weighted sum of completion times,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum\nolimits_{j}w_{j}C_{j}\right].

Optimal scheduling policies for this problem are known only in highly restricted special cases. When all weights are equal and processing times follow exponential distributions, the greedy SEPT (Shortest Expected Processing Time first) policy is optimal
[
32
,
5
]
. More generally, SEPT is still an optimal policy if the processing time distributions are pairwise stochastically comparable
[
31
]
.
With exponentially distributed jobs, the optimality of SEPT also extends to weighted jobs whenever the SEPT order is agreeable with the job weights
[
18
]
.
Outside of such special settings, however, it is well understood that optimal policies must be
adaptive
, reacting dynamically to realized processing times
[
29
,
26
,
6
]
. Moreover, the adaptivity gap between non-adaptive and adaptive policies can grow proportionally to the coefficient of variation of the processing time distributions
[
26
,
6
]
.

Beginning with
[
22
]
, a substantial literature has developed approximation algorithms for stochastic scheduling, typically based on linear programming relaxations of the optimal adaptive policy
[
22
,
27
,
21
,
26
,
2
,
11
,
12
,
16
]
. Almost all of these approximation guarantees scale with the largest squared coefficient of variation of the random variables
X_{j}
, defined as
{\mathbb{V}\rm ar}[\,X_{j}\,]/\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}[\,X_{j}\,]$}^{2}
. For the special case of unit weights, an exception is
[
14
]
, whose performance guarantee instead depends on the number of machines
m
. More recently, for two-point Bernoulli processing times, sublinear-in-
m
approximation guarantees have been obtained that do not depend on second moments
[
10
,
1
]
: However, the approximation guarantee in
[

]
, while sublinear in

, contains large poly-logarithmic factors in the number of jobs

, and the guarantee in
[
1
]
is
\textup{O}(\,\log n\,)
, but only quasi-polynomial time.

Despite the sophistication of these approximation results, strong lower bounds on the computational complexity of stochastic scheduling have remained largely absent. When job weights are arbitrary, the problem is NP-hard, since it generalizes deterministic scheduling on parallel machines, which is NP-hard
[
8
]
. However, for the unweighted case
w_{j}=1
for all jobs
j
, deterministic scheduling is optimally solvable by SPT
[
3
]
. Quite strikingly, the computational complexity of the corresponding stochastic problem —minimizing
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{j}C_{j}]
on parallel identical machines— has remained open for over four decades. In particular, it is still open if the stochastic problem can be solved optimally in polynomial time.

In summary, the lurking fundamental question is:

Is it computationally hard to compute an optimal scheduling policy for minimizing expected total completion time on parallel identical machines?

Despite sustained interest in stochastic scheduling since the 1980s, this question has not been resolved. This paper gives a partial answer, testifying the problem being computationally harder than the corresponding deterministic counterpart.

1.1
Results

This paper provides the first indication that this stochastic scheduling problem is unlikely to admit a polynomial-time solution, even when all job weights are equal. We show that the decision problem of whether there exists a scheduling policy whose expected cost is at most a given threshold is #P-hard.
This hardness result holds even under

restricted stochasticity
, namely with independent two-point processing time distributions.

Beyond computing optimal policies, we also study the complexity of evaluating simple and widely used scheduling policies. Perhaps the most popular such policy is WSEPT (Weighted Shortest Expected Processing Time first), which schedules jobs greedily in non-increasing order of the ratios
w_{j}/\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]
. WSEPT is optimal on a single machine for both deterministic
[
28
]
and stochastic processing times
[
24
]
. On parallel machines, it remains optimal for exponential distributions when weights are agreeable with expected processing times
[
18
]
. In the case with unit weights, WSEPT reduces to SEPT,
which is optimal for exponential distributions
[
32
,
5
]
and more generally for stochastically orderable processing times
[
31
]
. Even beyond these cases, WSEPT achieves constant-factor approximations when the coefficient of variation is bounded by a constant
[
22
,
15
]
.

We show that computing the exact expected objective value of either WSEPT or SEPT is itself a #P-hard problem. This result highlights a fundamental barrier not only to computing optimal policies, but also to precisely evaluating the performance of simple, fixed scheduling rules.

Existing Lower Bounds.

Hardness results for stochastic scheduling problems are known only in more structured settings, and for other, arguably “simpler” objective functions that are more amenable to lower bound constructions: First, stochastic scheduling with alternate precedence constraints, and makespan objective for a given subset of mandatory jobs is known to be PSPACE-hard
[
23
, Thm. 19.7]
. Here, alternate precedence constraints means that we are given a directed acyclic graph, a job can only be started if
any one
of its predecessors has been finished. Moreover, non-mandatory tasks only need to be processed if needed as predecessors of mandatory jobs. Second, in precedence-constrained stochastic project scheduling, a.k.a. PERT problems (Program Evaluation and Review Technique), computing the expected makespan is a known #P-hard problem
[
13
]
.
Both hardness results rely crucially on the presence of additional structure, specifically, in the form of (alternate) precedence constraints. This allows reductions from other, structured problems: the PSPACE-hardness follows from reductions from quantified satisfiability, which also has a given order of associated variables and quantifiers, and the #P-hardness of expected makespan for PERT problems exploits the hardness of reliability problems in directed graphs
[
30
]
.
Third, a computationally hard problem with less structure is the problem to compute the probability for the sum of independent Bernoulli trials being bounded by some threshold. This can be equivalently interpreted as a single machine stochastic scheduling problem with makespan objective, and is #P-hard, too
[
20
]
. This latter result has been used to show that also the computation of the expected makespan of stochastic jobs on parallel machines is #P-hard, under the additional assumption that the jobs are pre-assigned to the machines
[
9
]
.
Prior to the present work, however, no comparable hardness results were known for less structured stochastic scheduling problems, specifically not for the min-sum objective and without precedence constraints.

2
Hardness of evaluating WSEPT and SEPT

We first prove the results on WSEPT and SEPT. For the rest of the paper, for simplicity, we assume the objective is to minimize the total (weighted) expected starting times
{\mathbb{E}}[\,\sum_{j}w_{j}S_{j}\,]
instead of
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}[\,\sum_{j}w_{j}C_{j}\,]$}=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}[\,\sum_{j}w_{j}S_{j}\,]$}+\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}[\,\sum_{j}w_{j}X_{j}\,]$}
. This is w.l.o.g. as long as we talk about exact computation and not approximation, because the jobs are non-preemptive. Therefore,
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}[\,C_{j}\,]$}=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}[\,S_{j}\,]$}+\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}[\,X_{j}\,]$}
, and the latter term is a constant independent of the scheduling policy.

The reductions that are to follow are based on the #P-hardness of the problem to count the number of feasible solutions for a knapsack constraint. This problem is one of the most basic #P-hard counting problems
[
25
]
, see also
[
17
,
7
]
.

Definition 1

(
Knapsack)
.

An instance of
Knapsack
has
n\geq 2
items with integer sizes
s_{i}
and an integer knapsack bound
B
, where
1\leq s_{i}\leq B
and
B\geq 2
. A feasible solution is a subset of items
W\subseteq[n]
that fulfills the knapsack constraint
\sum_{i\in W}s_{i}\leq B
.

We will use the following lemma where we consider restricted instances on the
Knapsack
counting problem.

Lemma 1
.

The problem to count the number of feasible solutions for any instance
(B,s_{1},\dots,s_{n})
of problem
Knapsack
is a
#P
-hard problem, and this is true also if we assume that
B+1<\sum_{j\in[n]}s_{j}\leq 3B/2
.

The simple proof is deferred to the appendix.

3
Stochastic scheduling is presumably hard(er)

Here we aim to separate the deterministic scheduling problem to minimize the total completion time on parallel machines from the corresponding stochastic problem. Recall that the deterministic problem can be solved optimally in polynomial time by computing an SPT schedule
[
4
]
(shortest processing time first). The first step is to show that optimally solving the stochastic problem at least entails a computationally hard (sub) problem in the following sense.

Theorem 3
.

Consider an instance of stochastic parallel machine scheduling with stochastic jobs to minimize
{\mathbb{E}}[\,\sum_{j}C_{j}\,]
. Then the computation of the minimum expected cost
{\mathbb{E}}[\,\sum_{j}C_{j}\,]
is a
#P
-hard problem.

The proof will rely on the following lemma that specifies how an optimal scheduling policy acts on the instances that were used to prove Theorem
2
. Effectively, we will show that the optimum policy must follow SEPT, if we choose the problem parameters more carefully. Here observe that, since not all processing time distributions are stochastically comparable, this observation does not follow from
[
31
]
.

As a first step, we show that the optimum policy must start all blocker jobs at time 0 when we choose the probability
q
, the probability for the blocker jobs being long, small enough. Intuitively, with small enough
q
it is better to gamble on
all
blocker jobs being of size
0
, because then all the knapsack jobs can be started at time 0, too. This yields a total cost
0
for them. Once this is established, and no matter how many of the blocker jobs turn out long, all the remaining jobs are in fact stochastically comparable, and hence it follows from
[
31
]
that SEPT is an optimal scheduling policy for all remaining jobs.
Before giving the proof of Theorem
3
, we turn the intuition about the blocker jobs always going first into a formal argument.

Lemma 7
.

Consider Instances 1 or 2 as described in the proof of Theorem
2
. Assume that
q
, the probability for the blocker jobs being long, is chosen small enough. Then
any optimum policy must schedule all blocker jobs at time
0
.

Proof.

Consider a scheduling policy
\Pi
that does
not
always start all blocker jobs at time
0
.
That means that, while there is still at least one blocker job that is yet unscheduled, it either schedules one or several knapsack jobs at time 0, or the dummy job, or it leaves at least one machine idle at time 0. In either of these cases, we argue that it would have been strictly better to start one (more) blocker job at time 0 instead.

Given such behavior of policy
\Pi
, we can condition on the decision when the scheduling policy
\Pi
decided to
not
schedule a blocker job on the last available machine at time 0, while at least one blocker job, say job
b
, would have been available. Instead, policy
\Pi
either leaves that machine idle or it schedules a knapsack job or the dummy job. In either case, we claim that the earliest point in time when blocker job
b
is started by policy
\Pi
is at time
1/n
. This is true because, if
\Pi
schedules a knapsack or the dummy job, its size is at least
1/n
, and if it leaves a machine idle, a simple argument shows that it must do that for at least
1/n
time, which is the minimum size of any job.
We next show, by defining an alternative policy
\Pi^{\prime}
, that a
1/n
delay for a blocker job is too expensive.

First, observe that the expected remaining cost of policy
\Pi
, conditioned on the described state, is at least
1/n+c_{\Pi}(\text{remain})
, where
c_{\Pi}(\text{remain})
denotes the smallest possible expected cost for scheduling all remaining jobs on the set of available machines. Here, we define the term
c_{\Pi}(\text{remain})
so as to ignore the existence of blocker job
b
, which we do because its smallest possible expected cost is accounted for in the term
1/n
.

Consider policy
\Pi^{\prime}
, identical to
\Pi
up to the moment when
\Pi
did
not schedule blocker job
b
on the last available machine at time 0. Instead, we let
\Pi^{\prime}
start job
b
at time 0.

Conditioned on the given state, and the blocker job
b
turning out short,
\Pi^{\prime}
continues to simulate
\Pi
, with the same expected remaining cost
c_{\Pi}(\text{remain})
.
Conditioned on the blocker job turning out long, denote by
c_{\Pi^{\prime}}(\text{remain})
the expected cost of
\Pi^{\prime}
for scheduling all the remaining jobs with SEPT.
Note that in this case,
\Pi^{\prime}
has one machine
less
available for the remaining jobs when compared to
\Pi
. Then the expected remaining cost for
\Pi^{\prime}
is at most

(1-q)\cdot c_{\Pi}(\text{remain})\ +\ q\cdot c_{\Pi^{\prime}}(\text{remain}).

Here, the first term accounts for the event where blocker job
b
turns out short, and
the second term accounts for the event when blocker job
b
turns out long.

Now it follows that the expected cost of
\Pi
minus the expected costs of
\Pi^{\prime}
is at least

1/n+q\left(c_{\Pi}(\text{remain})-c_{\Pi^{\prime}}(\text{remain})\right).

(2)

Observe that
c_{\Pi}(\text{remain})-c_{\Pi^{\prime}}(\text{remain})
will generally be negative. However, if we choose
q
, the probability for the blocker jobs having processing time
0
, small enough, then (
2
) is strictly positive. Here,
q=1/(2n^{2}B)
is sufficient, because
c_{\Pi}(\text{remain})\geq 0
and
c_{\Pi^{\prime}}(\text{remain})\leq 2nB
. Hence, for small enough
q
,
\Pi^{\prime}
has strictly smaller expected cost in comparison to
\Pi
, so
\Pi
cannot be optimal.∎

That at hand, we can prove Theorem
3
using the same arguments as in the proof of Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We argue by considering the same two instances that were used in order to prove Theorem
2
. By choosing a small enough probability
q
for the blocker jobs being long, Lemma
7
shows that SEPT is an optimal scheduling policy.
This is true because the knapsack jobs together with the dummy job are stochastically comparable, and then for arbitrary machine availability times, SEPT is an optimal scheduling policy
[
31
]
.
As a consequence, we can replicate the same arguments as in Theorem
2
:
The only case when there is a nonzero contribution to the difference
\Delta
, is when
all
blocker jobs are long. In that case, again the optimum policy schedules the knapsack jobs identically in SEPT order on the free machine in both instances, as long as that machine still offers a starting time
\leq B+1
.
Hence exactly like in the proof of Theorem
2
, for each NO realization, there is precisely one job that will start
1/n
time earlier in Instance 1, than it does in Instance 2.
This allows us to do the counting of NO instances for the given knapsack constraint. So assuming we could compute the expected cost that is achieved by an optimal scheduling policy, we could compute the number of feasible solutions for
Knapsack
. To see that all necessary computations are indeed polynomial time, it suffices to observe that with the chosen probability
q=1/(2n^{2}B)
, we have that
|q^{m-1}|\in\mbox{$\textup{O}(\,m(\log n+\log B)\,)$}
.
∎

With Theorem
3
at hand, we finally conclude with our main result, showing that scheduling of stochastic jobs is hard in the following sense.

Theorem 4
.

Consider stochastic parallel machine scheduling to minimize
{\mathbb{E}}[\,\sum_{j}C_{j}\,]
. Then the problem to decide if there exists a policy with expected cost
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}[\,\sum_{j}C_{j}\,]$}\leq x
for any given instance and
x>0
, is a
#P
-hard problem.

Proof.

The idea is to make use of Instance 1 and Instance 2 in the proof of Theorem
3
, and argue that an an oracle to decide if a policy exists with
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}[\,\sum_{j}C_{j}\,]$}\leq x
for any
x>0
, allows to compute the exact value of the minimum expected cost for both instances
by binary search.
The reason why this works is the following: Both instances have
m
machines,
m+n
jobs, and the following properties:

•

Every realized job size is a multiple of
\frac{1}{n}
in the range
[0,B+1+\frac{1}{n}]
,

•

the probability of any joint realization of all job sizes is a multiple of
q^{m-1}\cdot(\frac{1}{2})^{n}
, where we take
q=\frac{1}{2n^{2}B}
.

Now by Lemma
7
and the proof of Theorem
3
we know that we can restrict to non-idling policies for these two instances. That implies that we have a finite and discrete set of possible values for the expected cost
{\mathbb{E}}[\,\sum_{j}C_{j}\,]
, and the expected cost of any non-idling and non-optimal policy exceeds that of an optimal policy by at least

q^{m-1}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\,.

Here, the product of the first two terms is a lower bound on the probability of any event, and the third term is the smallest realized processing time.
That implies that the number of required iterations of binary search to find the exact optimum value for these two instances is logarithmic in the above term, which is still polynomial in
n,m
, and
\log B
. Now Theorem
3
yields the claimed result.
∎

Conclusions

Observe that the hardness results of Theorems
3
and
4
still allow that there is an algorithm that computes an optimal scheduling policy
\Pi
in polynomial time. Here, one may imagine
\Pi
as some polynomial space automaton that can decide, for any given state, which job should be scheduled next. In fact, (W)SEPT, or any other list scheduling policy, can be thought of as a simple version of such automaton, and indeed, we can easily compute the (W)SEPT order, yet cannot in general compute its expected cost. We leave it as an interesting open question to show the hardness of computing
the optimal policy
\Pi
itself.

Here we give a proof of Lemma
1
, showing that the counting problem for the restricted version of
Knapsack
is indeed #P-hard.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

First, it is well known that the counting problem for
Knapsack
, so the problem to count the number of feasible solutions for a knapsack constraint, is #P-hard: This follows from the NP-hardness of the problem to decide if there exists a solution
W
with
\sum_{i\in W}s_{i}=B

[
19
]
, because the corresponding NP-hardness reduction is parsimonious
[
25
]
. Consequently, also the problem to count the number of solutions
W
with
\sum_{i\in W}s_{i}\leq B
is #P-hard, through solving the latter problem for
B
and
B-1
.
As to the second part of Lemma
1
, first note that when
\sum_{i\in[n]}s_{i}\leq B+1
, by integrality there are precisely
2^{n}-1
feasible solutions, hence w.l.o.g. we may assume
\sum_{i\in[n]}s_{i}>B+1
.
We aim to show that also the constraint
\sum_{j\in[n]}s_{j}\leq 3B/2
is w.l.o.g. To that end, observe that any instance
I=(B,s_{1},\dots,s_{n})
with
S:=\sum_{i}s_{i}
and
f
feasible knapsack solutions, can be transformed into an equivalent instance
I^{\prime}
where
B^{\prime}
is only an epsilon fraction smaller than
S^{\prime}
: Add to the
n
given items a set of
k
dummy items with sizes
s^{\prime}_{i}=S
, so that
S^{\prime}=(k+1)S
and let
B^{\prime}:=kS+B
. Then
B^{\prime}\geq\frac{k}{k+1}S^{\prime}
, and the number of feasible solutions for
I^{\prime}
is
f^{\prime}=(2^{k}-1)2^{n}+f
, because as soon as at least one dummy item is
not
included, all other items fit.
Hence the counting problem for instance
I^{\prime}
also solves the counting problem for the original instance
I
. Letting
k=2
gives precisely
\sum_{j\in[n]}s_{j}\leq 3B/2
.
∎
