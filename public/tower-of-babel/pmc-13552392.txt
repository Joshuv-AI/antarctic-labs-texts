In Countries Where Dog Bites are Endemic, Health Authorities Must Provide Appropriate Diagnostic Tools for Rabies [Letter]
Authors: Josef Finsterer
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with interest the article by Ochora et al regarding an 8-year-old boy who sustained a dog bite complicated by a suspected rabies virus infection; clinically, this manifested as headache, dysarthria, agitation, quadriparesis, respiratory insufficiency, urinary retention, altered consciousness, and ultimately death.1 While the study is noteworthy, several points warrant discussion.

First, the rabies infection was merely suspected but not confirmed by appropriate testing.1 Diagnosing rabies virus infection in a living patient requires detecting the virus via RT-PCR in saliva, identifying rabies antigen in cutaneous nerves at the base of hair follicles via skin biopsy, or detecting rabies antibodies in serum or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).2 Unless these tests are performed, the diagnosis remains unconfirmed.

Second, the patient did not undergo a lumbar puncture or CSF analysis to either confirm the rabies infection or rule out differential diagnoses.1 Only Plasmodium falciparum infection was adequately excluded. However, the clinical presentation could also have been caused by infections with other neurotropic viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, or parasites. These alternative pathogens could have been transmitted by the dog bite, or the patient could have acquired the infection during the 16-day interval between the bite and the onset of neurological symptoms. Since therapeutic management differs significantly depending on the pathogen, definitive identification of the causative agent is essential. Pathogen identification is also necessary for epidemiological reasons.

A third point is that no contrast-enhanced cerebral imaging was performed on the patient.1 To determine whether the patient’s neurological deficits were attributable to meningitis, encephalitis, perivasculitis, or vasculitis, performing cerebral imaging, along with CT angiography or MRI angiography, would have been crucial. Rabies infections are known to manifest as perivasculitis, endothelialitis, and vasculitis.3 Furthermore, rabies is known to cause encephalitis.4

A fourth point is that no autopsy was performed on the index patient.1 To clarify whether the suspected rabies infection was indeed the cause of the neurological deficits and the subsequent death, a post-mortem examination of the brain tissue would have been essential. Viral antigens in fresh brain tissue smears can be detected via direct immunofluorescence, and histopathological staining can reveal typical inclusion bodies, known as Negri bodies, within infected neurons.2,5

A fifth point is that no autopsy was performed on the dog.1 To determine whether the animal was actually infected with rabies, an examination of the dog, specifically the brain, for the presence or absence of rabies virus would have been indispensable. Without confirmation of an actual infection in the dog, transmission to the patient remains unconfirmed.

Particularly in countries where dog bites and rabies are endemic, health authorities should equip medical facilities with technologies that enable the definitive identification of rabies infections, thereby ensuring that affected patients receive appropriate treatment in accordance with established guidelines. This saves costs and reduces preventable deaths.
