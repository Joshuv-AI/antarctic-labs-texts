A universal photo-assisted kinetic enhancement strategy for high-rate batteries via non-planar dye sensitization
Authors: Xinyu Liu, Lu Liang, Jin Ren, Huilin Liu, Jiale Wan, Xiaolin Guo, Yueyao Dong, Hang Qi, Yizhe Meng, Shuwei Hao, Chunhui Yang, Yunfei Shang, Liwei Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Integrated photo-assisted rechargeable batteries have emerged as a highly promising strategy for solar-to-chemical energy conversion. However, the rapid recombination of photocarriers under intense ion flux fails to provide an additional driving force to promote both interfacial charge transfer and lithium-ion (Li+) diffusion, thereby severely constraining their high-rate capability due to sluggish overall kinetics. Herein, we propose a universal photo-assisted strategy utilizing non-planar titanium oxo phthalocyanine (TiOPc) as a stable photosensitizer. This unique molecular configuration effectively mitigates intermolecular aggregation and drives efficient exciton dissociation, thereby suppressing carrier recombination and steering photocarriers to the interface to accelerate both charge-transfer and Li+ diffusion kinetics. Furthermore, this photo-assisted strategy is well compatible with layered oxide cathodes and sodium battery systems. This work establishes a rational molecular-engineering paradigm to overcome the inherent kinetic limitations of next-generation fast-charging light-responsive energy storage by suppressing photocarrier recombination.

Introduction

Renewable energy sources, epitomized by solar power, are central to mitigating global energy crises and achieving carbon neutrality.1–3 However, the intrinsic intermittency of solar radiation necessitates the coupling of photovoltaic systems with large-scale energy storage devices.4,5 Transmission losses and complex integration inherently limit traditional discrete photovoltaic-battery systems.6,7 To bypass these parasitic losses and minimize system footprint, integrated photo-assisted rechargeable batteries, which enable direct solar-to-chemical energy conversion and storage within a single electrode, have emerged as a transformative paradigm for high-efficiency energy exploitation.8–10 Ideally, an integrated photo-assisted battery should: (1) achieve direct solar-to-chemical conversion to minimize energy dissipation;11 (2) maintain robust long-term cycling stability under continuous illumination;12 and (3) facilitate rapid interfacial charge transfer to enable high-power operation and efficiently harvest intense solar fluxes.13

By integrating photon energy into electrochemical processes, photo-assisted batteries can generate additional charge carriers that modulate interfacial properties under illumination. This effect drastically lowers interfacial charge-transfer resistance and reduces electrochemical polarization,14 thereby facilitating faster ion desolvation15 and diffusion.16 To maximize these benefits, researchers have explored diverse photoactive materials, including semiconductor heterojunctions,17,18 perovskites,19 and transition metal oxides.20,21 However, achieving both broad light absorption and efficient lithium storage simultaneously remains fundamentally challenging for these materials, owing to rapid recombination of photogenerated carriers and sluggish lithium-ion (Li+) diffusion kinetics. To circumvent this bottleneck, dye sensitization strategies have been extensively investigated. Organic dyes have been extensively employed as interfacial photosensitizers due to their strong visible-light absorption and tunable energy levels.22,23 Pioneering studies using representative photosensitizers have established that photo-induced electron–hole pairs can effectively facilitate delithiation and promote interfacial ion transfer.24–26 However, the photo-assisted enhancement of these conventional planar dyes is severely diminished under fast-charging conditions. This failure stems from two fundamental bottlenecks. First, the substantial electrochemical polarization induced by high-rate cycling shifts the local interfacial potential, triggering the irreversible oxidative degradation of the delicate organic frameworks. Second, the inherent planarity of most dyes leads to severe π–π stacking and exciton quenching,27 triggering rapid carrier recombination before effective redox coupling can occur, thereby fundamentally failing to overcome sluggish charge-transfer kinetics. Consequently, there is an urgent need to design structurally stable dyes capable of translating photon energy into a sustained kinetic driving force to fundamentally overcome sluggish charge-transfer kinetics and Li+ diffusion kinetics under high-rate conditions.28

Among them, metal phthalocyanines (MPcs) possess a highly delocalized 18π-electron conjugated macrocyclic network, endowing the framework with exceptional structural and electrochemical stability. Building upon this foundation, titanium oxo phthalocyanine (TiOPc) leverages its unique non-planar configuration to effectively hinder π–π stacking and intermolecular aggregation, thereby modulating the photophysical behavior and facilitating charge separation. Herein, we report a photo-assisted electrode strategy using non-planar TiOPc as an efficient interfacial sensitizer to overcome sluggish high-rate kinetics in rechargeable batteries. By leveraging the intrinsic out-of-plane TiO dipole, TiOPc suppresses intermolecular π–π aggregation and establishes a localized internal electric field that drives rapid exciton dissociation. Comprehensive physical characterization and electrochemical analyses demonstrate that axial TiO coordination with cathode active sites substantially lowers the interfacial charge transfer resistance and mitigates irreversible lattice distortion. Consequently, the sensitized LiFePO4 (LFP@TiOPc) cathode achieves a discharge capacity of 109.9 mA h g−1 at 8C under illumination, delivering a 72.1% capacity enhancement relative to dark conditions alongside excellent cycling retention (91.4% after 150 cycles). Furthermore, parallel evaluations in sodium-ion systems and layered oxide cathodes confirm the broad effectiveness of this non-planar sensitization approach for high-rate energy storage. By leveraging non-planar molecular engineering to resolve the kinetic mismatch between light harvesting and rapid electrochemical reactions, this study effectively mitigates sluggish charge-transfer kinetics and ion diffusion kinetics in high-rate batteries, thereby providing a universal paradigm for the development of next-generation fast-charging, photo-responsive energy storage systems.

Results and discussion

Rational screening of photosensitizers and their kinetic modulation mechanisms

To maximize the potential of dye-sensitized strategies, rational dye selection is paramount, particularly for high-rate conditions. MPcs offer a quantifiable advantage over conventional organic dyes and certain metal complexes (Fig. 1a). Specifically, MPcs feature a stable 18π-electron conjugated macrocycle that endows them with exceptional electrochemical stability, alongside inherently superior molar extinction coefficients (>105 M−1 cm−1) for highly efficient solar energy capture.29 Unlike widely commercialized and extensively studied centrosymmetric MPcs such as copper phthalocyanine (CuPc), cobalt phthalocyanine (CoPc), and zinc phthalocyanine (ZnPc), which adopt nearly planar geometries, titanium oxo phthalocyanine (TiOPc) possesses a distinct non-planar configuration induced by its axial TiO bond. Furthermore, although other metal phthalocyanines also exhibit asymmetric geometries, TiOPc is specifically selected for its superior light-harvesting capabilities and outstanding photophysical properties.30 Planar MPcs are inherently susceptible to strong π–π stacking and severe aggregation. This severe overlap of π-electron clouds exacerbates non-radiative recombination, causing excitons to quench before they can diffuse to the interface for dissociation, thereby compromising photoelectric conversion efficiency. In TiOPc, the axial TiO bond acts as a rigid physical spacer, widening the intermolecular distance and lowering π–π stacking energy to mitigate non-radiative exciton quenching.31 Furthermore, its strong permanent dipole promotes localized polarization for efficient carrier separation32 and induces tilted surface anchoring via TiO coordination. This suppresses face-to-face molecular overlap and eliminates catalytic hotspots that trigger electrolyte oxidation during cycling. The essential role of the non-planar geometry was further confirmed by comparing TiOPc with planar phthalocyanine analogs (CuPc and ZnPc) under identical conditions (Fig. S1). As a result, LFP@ZnPc and LFP@CuPc show modest capacity enhancements of only 3.78% and 5.22% at 1C under illumination, respectively, which are far inferior to the 10.55% enhancement achieved by LFP@TiOPc. This sharp contrast underscores the distinct optical and kinetic advantages endowed by the non-planar structure of TiOPc.

Dye selection and photoelectrode working mechanism. (a) Radar chart quantifying multidimensional performance. (b) Schematic illustration of the photo-assisted kinetic enhancement mechanism at the cathode interface.

Fig. 1b illustrates the photo-assisted enhancement mechanism of the TiOPc-sensitized cathode. Upon illumination, TiOPc is excited to generate electron–hole pairs. During discharging, the photogenerated electrons directly participate in the reduction reaction at the cathode side to promote Li+ intercalation. The holes are rapidly quenched by recombining with the electrons released from the anode oxidation and flowing through the external circuit. During charging, the photo-generated holes migrate to the sensitizer/active material interface, providing an additional energetic driving force to facilitate the oxidative delithiation of the active material. Concurrently, the photogenerated electrons are directed toward the anode via the external circuit to participate in Li+ reduction, effectively lowering the charging overpotential. By translating photon energy into a continuous kinetic driving force, the TiOPc sensitization accelerates interfacial charge transfer and facilitates Li+ transport.

Photophysical properties and charge-generation mechanism of TiOPc

To confirm the crystalline phase of the photosensitizer, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the TiOPc powder was conducted. As shown in Fig. S2, the TiOPc powder exhibits intense diffraction peaks at 9.68°, 24.08°, and 27.3° corresponding to the characteristic reflections of Y-TiOPc,33 accompanied by minor, weak peaks at 12.6° and 28.7° indexed to α-TiOPc.34 The significant intensity difference reveals a mixed-phase crystalline structure with the Y phase as the dominant component, which underlies the excellent optoelectronic properties of the material. Following structural verification, the ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectrum of TiOPc dye was acquired in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) solution (Fig. 2a). The spectrum exhibits broad and intense absorption across the visible region, originating from π–π* transitions within the conjugated phthalocyanine macrocycle. The absorption band observed between 300–500 nm is assigned to the B band, while the more intense band spanning 600–700 nm corresponds to the Q band.35 The optical bandgap energy (Eg) is estimated to be 1.73 eV utilizing the Kubelka–Munk function.36 Mott–Schottky analysis (Fig. 2b) reveals a positive slope characteristic of p-type semiconductors, yielding a flat-band potential (EFB) of 4.61 V vs. Li/Li+.37 Therefore, the energy potentials of the conduction band minimum (ECB) and valence band maximum (EVB) are determined to be 2.98 V and 4.71 V vs. Li/Li+, corresponding to the LUMO and HOMO levels, respectively.22 The energy-level alignment (Fig. 2c) indicates that the energy bands of TiOPc encompass the working voltage range of LFP (2.5–4.0 V vs. Li/Li+). Upon illumination, TiOPc generates a sufficient energetic driving force that facilitates the separation of photogenerated carriers, enabling them to participate in the charge–discharge processes of the cathode by promoting interfacial charge transfer.

Subsequently, the recombination processes of photogenerated charge carriers are investigated by PL spectra collected in NMP solution (Fig. 2d).38 An asymmetric broad emission band spanning from 650 to 850 nm is observed, with its peak center at 700 nm. Compared with pure TiOPc, the emission spectral profile of the LFP@TiOPc composite remains largely consistent, but exhibits a significant quenching of the emission intensity. This quenching is attributed to charge transfer from TiOPc to the LFP interface, which thereby suppresses the original radiative recombination process. To quantify the excited-state dynamics, time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements are conducted (Fig. 2e). The decay curves of both samples are well described by a biexponential decay model, with fitting parameters summarized in Table S1. This model consists of a fast-decay component (τ1) and a slow-decay component (τ2). The fast component is attributed to non-radiative recombination or charge trapping processes, whereas the slow component is attributed to intrinsic radiative recombination.39 Notably, both τ1 and τ2 of LFP@TiOPc are significantly shortened compared with those of pristine TiOPc. Accordingly, the lifetime of the 700 nm emission band declined from 3.69 ns (TiOPc) to 3.18 ns (LFP@TiOPc). More importantly, the introduction of the LFP interface provides an additional rapid non-radiative pathway in the form of efficient electron injection from TiOPc to LFP. This new decay channel accelerates excited-state quenching, as evidenced by an increased relative contribution of the fast τ1 component and the concurrent decrease in τ2. These results demonstrate that a substantial fraction of photogenerated carriers undergo rapid interfacial charge transfer rather than radiative recombination. The above spectroscopic data indicate that, owing to the intimate interfacial coupling between TiOPc and LFP, charge separation is effectively promoted and charge carrier recombination is suppressed upon illumination. To further verify the generation of photoinduced charge carriers, particularly the oxidative holes, we conduct in situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy using 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxy (TEMPO) as a hole-trapping probe (Fig. S3). Upon illumination, the characteristic triplet signal of TEMPO exhibits a discernible decrease in intensity. This attenuation is attributed to the oxidation of paramagnetic TEMPO radicals into diamagnetic TEMPO+ species by photogenerated holes.40 This provides strong evidence for the generation of active holes and confirms effective electron–hole separation driven by the intrinsic electric field of TiOPc.

Building on the microscopic evidence of carrier generation from EPR, the macroscopic charge-transfer behavior is further evaluated by in situ photocurrent measurements under open-circuit voltage (OCV) conditions (Fig. 2f). Under illumination, the LFP electrode exhibits a rapid current decay, signifying a lack of persistent photoactivity. Conversely, the LFP@TiOPc photoelectrode delivers a stable and significantly enhanced photocurrent response. The spectroscopic and photoelectrochemical results confirm that TiOPc facilitates rapid interfacial charge separation within the LFP@TiOPc electrode, effectively suppressing recombination and establishing the kinetic basis for the enhanced battery performance.

Photo-induced electrochemical kinetics of LFP@TiOPc electrodes

The TiOPc loading was systematically optimized across different TiOPc-to-cathode mass ratios (1 : 80, 1 : 60, and 1 : 40), with the 1 : 60 ratio yielding optimal performance (Fig. S4). This trade-off arises because insufficient loading (1 : 80) fails to provide continuous interfacial coverage, while excessive TiOPc (1 : 40) increases charge transport resistance due to redundant organic layer stacking. To rigorously decouple photoinduced kinetic enhancements from purely photothermal contributions, initial evaluations confirm that the physical integration of TiOPc remains electrochemically inert in the dark. As shown in Fig. S5, the LFP@TiOPc electrode under dark conditions (LFP@TiOPc-Dark) and the LFP electrode in the dark (LFP-Dark) exhibit nearly identical discharge capacities and cycling stability, confirming that the kinetic benefits are exclusively triggered by light. Therefore, the subsequent analysis focuses on comparing LFP@TiOPc under illumination (LFP@TiOPc-Light), LFP under illumination (LFP-Light), and LFP-Dark.

To quantify the electrochemical kinetics, we first perform cyclic voltammetry (CV) at various scan rates (Fig. 3a and S6). All systems exhibit characteristic redox peaks associated with the Fe2+/Fe3+ couple of LFP,41 indicating that the fundamental reaction mechanism remains unchanged upon TiOPc sensitization and light irradiation. While increasing the scan rate inevitably exacerbates the polarization and peak shifts of LFP, the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode consistently maintains the smallest peak potential separation (ΔEp). Comparison of CV curves at a scanning rate of 0.2 mV s−1 (Fig. 3b) reveals that the ΔEp of the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode is remarkably minimized to 255 mV, which is substantially narrower than those of LFP-Light (316 mV) and LFP-Dark (345 mV). Furthermore, the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode exhibits a higher oxidation peak current compared to LFP-Light and LFP-Dark under identical conditions. Similar trends are observed at other scan rates (Fig. S7). The cell exhibits increased peak currents and reduced polarization at all scan rates, indicating that TiOPc sensitization significantly enhances the redox kinetics of LFP during the charge and discharge processes under illumination. To further elucidate the electrochemical reaction kinetics, the Li+ diffusion coefficient (DLi+) is determined using the Randles–Sevcik equation:421Ip = 2.69 × 105n3/2SDLi+1/2Cv1/2where Ip denotes the peak current, n is the number of electrons transferred, S is the electrode area, C represents the Li+ concentration, and v is the scan rate. With n, S, and C held constant, the slope of the curve (Ip/v1/2) represents the Li+ diffusion rate. As shown in Fig. 3c, the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode exhibits markedly higher DLi+ during both oxidation and reduction processes, demonstrating that photoexcitation effectively promotes Li+ transport. Furthermore, to elucidate the interfacial charge-transfer behavior and eliminate the interference of photothermal effects, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements are conducted. The results further corroborate that the kinetic enhancement of LFP@TiOPc under illumination cannot be attributed solely to thermal effects (Fig. 3d). Based on the equivalent circuit fitting (Fig. S8), the charge-transfer resistance (Rct) of the LFP@TiOPc electrode is 52.5 Ω, which is lower than 65.8 Ω of the LFP-Light electrode and 76.1 Ω of the LFP-Dark electrode (Table S2). Mechanistically, the vertical permanent dipole of TiOPc generates a built-in intramolecular electric field that overcomes exciton binding energy to promote rapid exciton dissociation. Concurrently, the polar TiO group coordinates with unsaturated transition metal sites on the cathode, establishing directional pathways for charge transport and significantly lowering interfacial charge transfer resistance. This reduction confirms that the separated photo-generated charge carriers in TiOPc successfully migrate to the interface to directly participate in the interfacial redox processes, thereby intrinsically accelerating the charge-transfer kinetics. Additionally, post-cycling EIS measurements (Fig. S9) verify that TiOPc mitigates continuous impedance accumulation, ensuring sustained transport kinetics during extended cycling.

The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) provides the most compelling evidence for intrinsic kinetic improvement. As revealed by the full GITT profiles (Fig. 3e), the kinetic advantage of the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode persists throughout both the charge and discharge processes, indicating a sustained rather than transient enhancement mechanism. A locally magnified view of a single titration step (Fig. 3f) intuitively displays the variation in internal polarization. Compared to the LFP-Dark and LFP-Light electrodes, the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode exhibits significantly reduced polarization during current pulses and faster voltage relaxation toward equilibrium. Specifically, it demonstrates a minimized relaxation voltage drop (ΔE) of merely 21.4 mV, compared to 25.2 mV for LFP-Light and 31.6 mV for bare LFP-Dark. Both the LFP-Light and LFP-Dark electrodes require a higher potential to overcome the resistance to Li+ diffusion during charging–discharging compared with the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode.43 While basic photothermal heating induces a minor enhancement in the Li+ diffusion of the LFP-Light electrode, the profoundly larger improvement observed for the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode far exceeds any purely thermal contribution. While CV evaluates the macroscopic dynamic diffusion kinetics under a continuous voltage sweep, GITT describes the quasi-equilibrium kinetic properties and internal resistance at varying states of charge. Both test results show a highly consistent trend, indicating that the kinetic enhancement of the LFP@TiOPc electrode under illumination originates from photogenerated charge carriers rather than purely thermal effects.

Photo-enhanced electrochemical performance of batteries

Although the light-driven mechanism of TiOPc has been elucidated, evaluating its long-term cycling stability under practical configurations is essential. As illustrated in Fig. S10, the assembled photo-assisted battery device employs a cathode case with an 8-mm-diameter optical aperture sealed with a transparent PET film exhibiting a visible-light transmittance of approximately 78%, allowing unimpeded light irradiation onto the electrode surface (Fig. S11). As shown in Fig. 4a, the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode exhibits consistently higher discharge capacities over a wide range of current densities than the LFP-Dark and LFP-Light electrodes. Notably, the specific capacity fully recovers when the current density is restored to 0.5C after high-rate cycling, demonstrating that the photo-enhanced electrochemical kinetics induced by TiOPc are highly reversible. Furthermore, the corresponding galvanostatic charge–discharge profiles of the three systems at different rates are recorded (Fig. S12), and systematic performance comparison with various reported LFP cathodes further confirms that LFP@TiOPc under illumination achieves outstanding specific capacities across 0.5–8C by alleviating high-rate polarization via photoelectric promotion (Table S3), outperforming reported photomodified LFP electrodes including quantum dot, plant dye, and MXene composite counterparts, and verifying the superiority of the nonplanar TiOPc sensitization strategy for fast Li+ deintercalation/intercalation. Based on these curves, the electrode polarization (ΔV) is quantitatively analyzed. As depicted in Fig. 4b, the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode exhibits a consistently lower polarization voltage at varying rates compared with the control groups. This trend is highly consistent with the kinetic advantages established in the aforementioned CV and GITT analyses. It further confirms that photo-induced charge carriers generated from TiOPc effectively facilitate Li+ transport and mitigate electrode polarization under high-rate operations.

Cycling stability is a critical metric for evaluating the practical applicability. Fig. 4c compares the long-term cycling performance at a high current density of 8C (1C = 170 mA g−1). The relatively low discharge capacity results from slow lithium diffusion-induced high polarization, poor contact between the light-transmissive Al mesh and electrode, and extra interfacial resistance from the PET-sealed light-accessible cell structure. These structural compromises provide directions for developing superior transparent conductive substrates and cell assemblies in follow-up research. The LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode delivers a discharge capacity of 109.9 mA h g−1, representing a 72.1% increase relative to the LFP-Dark electrode (63.86 mA h g−1). Although the LFP-Light electrode exhibits a transient capacity boost driven by the photothermal effect, it undergoes rapid capacity decay within the first few cycles. This phenomenon clearly indicates that pure photothermal heating accelerates electrode degradation during high-rate operation. In contrast, the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode retains 91.4% of its initial capacity after 150 cycles, corresponding to a negligible fading rate of 0.057% per cycle. This excellent cycling stability demonstrates that the significant performance enhancement predominantly originates from the continuous kinetic driving force provided by photo-generated charge carriers, which substantially outweighs the contributions of thermal effects. The operational duration under illumination is mainly limited by cell-level thermal accumulation around the optical window rather than intrinsic material degradation. This operational constraint can be mitigated via advanced thermal management and solid-state electrolytes. In addition, long-term cycling behavior at 1C under dark and light conditions was evaluated to complement the high-rate investigation (Fig. S13). LFP@TiOPc maintains its photo-enhanced capacity during early low-rate cycling, which aligns with the trend observed in rate capability tests. Furthermore, compared with other modified LFP cathodes reported to date (Fig. 4d), the photo-assisted LFP@TiOPc battery demonstrates superior rate capability, underscoring the potential of TiOPc as an effective photoactive modifier for durable, high-power energy storage systems.26,44–46

To evaluate the applicability of this photo-assisted strategy, we introduce TiOPc into a variety of representative cathode materials, including layered LiNi0.6Co0.2Mn0.2O2 (NCM622), LiCoO2 (LCO), and Na3V2(PO4)3 (NVP) as a representative of Na superionic conductor (NASICON)-type cathode. Systematic rate capability evaluations and galvanostatic charge–discharge cycling at multiple rates ranging from 0.5C to 8C under dark and light conditions were conducted for all modified electrodes (Fig. S14 and 4e–g). Across these systems, the TiOPc-sensitized electrodes exhibit higher discharge capacities, significantly outperforming the photothermal controls. This indicates the universality of the TiOPc sensitization strategy, proving its potential for diverse high-power energy storage systems. Similarly, as observed across all galvanostatic charge–discharge profiles, the TiOPc-sensitized electrodes consistently exhibit lower charge voltages and higher discharge voltages under illumination. This indicates reduced polarization voltage and enhanced redox kinetics of the cathode active materials. These consistent electrochemical results firmly confirm the broad applicability of the TiOPc sensitization strategy, proving its potential for diverse high-power lithium and sodium energy storage systems.

Interfacial charge transfer dynamics and spatial distribution of TiOPc

To elucidate the structural interactions during high-rate operation, ex situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is performed on the LFP@TiOPc electrodes. The cells are first charged to 4.0 V (100% SOC) at 8C under both dark and illuminated conditions, and the electrodes are subsequently extracted for measurement. The XPS survey spectra of the LFP@TiOPc electrodes under both dark and light conditions (Fig. S15) clearly resolve the characteristic peaks of C, Ti, and Fe. Compared to the dark sample, the Ti 2p peaks of TiOPc in the electrode charged under illumination (Fig. 5a) exhibit a shift toward higher binding energies. This shift is attributed to photo-generated charge carriers from TiOPc facilitating interfacial electron transfer under illumination, enabling deeper delithiation of the LFP at 8C. This photo-assisted charge transfer reduces the electron density around the Ti center in TiOPc, resulting in a more electron-deficient chemical environment. This mechanism is fully corroborated by the Fe 2p spectrum (Fig. 5b), which displays an increased proportion of Fe3+ in the illuminated electrode relative to its dark counterpart, indicating more complete delithiation.

(c) SEM image and the corresponding EDS mapping of the LFP@TiOPc-Light electrode after 100 cycles at 8C. (d) ICP analysis of Ti content on the LFP@TiOPc electrode before and after 100 cycles at 8C.

These combined results demonstrate that TiOPc maintains a relatively uniform dispersion within the electrode during prolonged cycling. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses and optical microscopy (Fig. S16) confirm that the initial introduction of TiOPc causes no distinct change in the electrode surface and particle morphology. Morphological evolution and elemental distribution are examined using SEM and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. The uncycled LFP@TiOPc cathode exhibits a typical granular morphology with evenly distributed particles and uniform Fe and Ti signals (Fig. S17). As shown in Fig. 5c, after 100 cycles at a high rate of 8C under illumination, the photo-assisted cathode maintains its fundamental morphological features with no apparent pulverization or aggregates and retains its uniform Fe and Ti signal distribution. These combined results demonstrate that TiOPc maintains a uniform dispersion within the electrode even during prolonged cycling. Complementary inductively coupled plasma (ICP) measurements of the LFP@TiOPc electrode after 100 cycles at a high rate of 8C reveal a negligible change in the macroscopic elemental Ti content (Fig. 5d), confirming the absence of noticeable TiOPc dissolution into the electrolyte. The XPS, SEM, EDS, and ICP findings collectively demonstrate that TiOPc maintains a uniform spatial distribution within the LFP@TiOPc electrode, ensuring sustained interfacial electron transfer during the high-rate charge–discharge process.

Morphological characterization studies before and after cycling (Fig. S18) demonstrate that the LFP@TiOPc composite possesses favorable structural integrity under high-rate conditions. No additional redox peaks appear in the charge–discharge profiles measured at 8C (Fig. S19a), which verifies that TiOPc can passivate surface active sites of the material through coordination of axial titanium-oxygen double bonds (TiO) and inhibit the side decomposition of electrolyte. Meanwhile, XRD patterns collected after 100 cycles at 8C (Fig. S19b) show sharp diffraction peaks with negligible peak shifts for LFP@TiOPc; by contrast, pristine LFP suffers severe peak broadening and obvious peak shifts, presenting a sharp distinction between the two samples. In addition, Raman spectra recorded before and after cycling further support the stable lattice chemical bonds of the material (Fig. S20). The characteristic peak at 949 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of Fe–O bonds in the LFP lattice, and this peak shifts to a much smaller extent for LFP@TiOPc after cycling compared with pristine LFP. The above results reveal that the coordination between axial TiO groups and surface transition metal sites passivates the unsaturated active sites on LFP without destroying its crystal framework. In summary, TiOPc modification effectively mitigates irreversible lattice distortion accumulated during long-term high-rate cycling and stabilizes the overall cathode framework.

Conclusions

In summary, we have established a molecular-engineering paradigm to resolve the kinetic mismatch in photo-assisted fast-charging batteries by introducing non-planar TiOPc as a high-efficiency interfacial sensitizer. The non-planar architecture of TiOPc effectively suppresses intermolecular aggregation, thereby significantly facilitating the separation of photo-generated charge carriers. Comprehensive spectroscopic and electrochemical evidence, including TRPL, GITT, and EIS, unambiguously confirms that this photogenerated driving force effectively lowers the charge-transfer barrier and facilitates rapid ion migration, far exceeding the influence of photothermal effects on the reaction kinetics. Consequently, the TiOPc-sensitized LFP electrode delivers a remarkable discharge capacity of 109.9 mA h g−1 at 8C under illumination, representing a 72.1% enhancement over dark conditions and significantly outperforming baseline photothermal effects. Furthermore, the successful extension of this sensitization approach to layered oxide cathodes and sodium systems underscores its universal applicability across diverse energy storage chemistries. This study proposes a generalizable strategy to address the kinetic bottlenecks in high-rate batteries and to optimize photo-assisted energy storage, thereby establishing a conceptual framework for constructing advanced light-responsive devices.
