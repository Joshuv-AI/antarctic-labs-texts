Workplace violence and psychosocial risks exposures: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Marina Moreno-Martínez, Josep Vidal-Alaball, Artur Dalfó-Pibernat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWorkplace violence has emerged as a major occupational health concern in healthcare settings, with growing evidence of its negative impact on workers’ physical and mental health, as well as on organizational performance. The present study aimed to describe the prevalence of workplace violence and to examine its association with sociodemographic characteristics, working conditions, and psychosocial risk factors among primary healthcare professionals.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted in 2025 among healthcare professionals working in the Primary and Community Care Management of Central Catalonia, Spain. Data were collected through an anonymous online questionnaire that included sociodemographic and occupational variables, exposure to workplace violence, health-related outcomes, and psychosocial risks assessed using validated instruments, including the Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire (COPSOQ III). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests and Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate, Kendall’s Tau-b to assess associations between ordinal variables and a multivariable binary logistic regression model to identify independent factors associated with the reporting of violence. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.ResultsA total of 420 healthcare professionals participated in the study, of whom 68.1% reported having experienced workplace violence. Verbal and psychological violence was the most prevalent form (98.6%). A clear positive gradient was observed between adverse psychosocial risk exposure and workplace violence. The multivariable model identified high emotional demands as the strongest independent predictor; professionals in the most unfavorable category were 7.34 times more likely to report violence (95% CI: 3.46–15.57). Other significant factors included being male (aOR 3.63; 95% CI: 1.52–8.65), having a permanent contract (aOR 2.37; 95% CI:1.25–4.51), being in direct patient care (aOR 3.74; 95% CI: 1.83–7.68), lack of organizational justice (aOR 2.23; 95% CI: 1.04–4.80), and poor self-rated health (aOR 2.98; 95% CI: 1.26–7.06).ConclusionsWorkplace violence is highly prevalent in primary care and strongly associated with adverse psychosocial conditions. These findings suggest that managing emotional demands and organizational justice could be relevant areas for strategies aimed at addressing workplace violence and supporting healthcare workers’ well-being.

Background

Workplace violence constitutes an increasingly significant global problem, with important consequences for the physical and mental health of workers, as well as for the overall functioning of organizations [1]. It manifests in multiple forms—ranging from physical assaults to psychological harassment, workplace bullying, threats, and even social exclusion—and may originate from both internal sources (colleagues, supervisors) and external ones (users or patients) [2]. Various international organizations, such as the International Labour Organization (ILO), have emphasized the need to address this phenomenon as a priority in occupational health and safety [3].

Healthcare professionals are among the occupational groups most frequently exposed to violence perpetrated by third parties. Evidence indicates that approximately 20% of these workers experience violent incidents each year at the global level [4]. This high vulnerability is primarily related to the complex interactions that occur with patients and their families. In many cases, the frustration of the latter—arising from factors such as long wait times, refusal of certain requests, or unfavorable medical news—may trigger aggressive behaviors toward healthcare personnel [5].

In parallel, the concept of psychosocial risks has gained increasing relevance in recent decades, serving as a construct that encompasses all organizational, social, and environmental factors that impact the psychological and emotional well-being of workers. These include high job demands, limited autonomy, role ambiguity, organizational justice, job instability, and lack of support from supervisors or colleagues [6]. Scientific literature has demonstrated that prolonged exposure to these risks not only increases the incidence of mental health disorders such as burnout but can also contribute to the development of conflictive and dysfunctional workplace environments [5, 7, 8].

Stressful working conditions, poor work organization, and the absence of effective conflict resolution mechanisms tend to generate environments in which the likelihood of aggressive or abusive behaviors increases [9, 10]. In this regard, workplace violence may be understood both as a direct consequence of poorly managed psychosocial risks and as an additional risk factor that reinforces a cycle of deterioration in the work climate and workers’ health [5]. Therefore, the aim of the present study is to describe the prevalence of workplace violence and to identify its association with sociodemographic characteristics, working conditions, and psychosocial factors in the work environment among healthcare professionals belonging to the Primary Care and Community Health Management of Central Catalonia of the Catalan Health Institute in 2025.

Methods

Design and sample

A multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted using an online survey administered via Microsoft Forms, a secure web-based platform for data collection compliant with international data protection standards and hosted within the institutional Microsoft 365 environment of the Catalan Health Institute. The study was carried out in the Primary and Community Care Management of Central Catalonia under the Catalan Health Institute (a public health institution in Catalonia, Spain), which includes 33 primary care teams distributed across 37 primary care centers and 96 local clinics. According to the latest institutional Annual Report (2023) [11], the workforce includes 1,857 healthcare professionals, including 464 physicians, 533 nurses, 607 administrative and support staff, and 253 professionals from other health-related occupations (such as nursing assistants, nutritionists, physiotherapists, social workers, pharmacists, dental hygienists, and emotional well-being specialists).

Based on these figures and considering that the estimated prevalence of workplace violence among healthcare professionals is approximately 20% [4], the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 218 participants. This estimation assumed a 95% confidence level and a precision of ± 5% points for a population proportion expected to be around 20%. A convenience sampling strategy was employed. An invitation to participate in the study was distributed via corporate email, with messages disseminated through the official mailing lists managed by the Communications Department of the Primary and Community Care Management of Central Catalonia. The survey remained open from April 2 to May 10, 2025. The reporting of the online survey followed the CHERRIES (Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys) guidelines [12].

Instrument and variables

A self-administered questionnaire consisting of 48 close-ended items was used, presented on a single webpage and divided into five sections: sociodemographic data (7 items), working conditions (5 items), psychosocial risks (22 items), health (3 items), and workplace violence (11 items). The survey combined ad hoc items and items drawn from previously validated Spanish-language instruments:

COPSOQ III: The Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire assesses psychosocial risks in the workplace. Scores range from 0 to 100 for each domain, with tertile-based cut-off points: scores below 33.3 indicate the most favorable exposure level, scores between 33.3 and 66.6 indicate intermediate exposure, and scores above 66.6 reflect the most adverse exposure level for health. The Spanish adaptation and validation were conducted by Moncada Lluís et al., reporting a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80 [13].“Violence Against Physiotherapy Professionals in Spain” Questionnaire: This questionnaire contains 137 items, including multiple-choice, Likert-scale, and open-ended questions. It is structured into six sections; for the present study, we selected only the items related to verbal/psychological, sexual, and physical violence, as well as the responses to violent incidents experienced throughout the participant’s professional career within the current organization. The instrument was originally developed in Spanish by Boo-Mallo et al. [14]. For the purposes of this study, workplace violence was operationalized as a dichotomous variable (Yes/No). Participants were classified in the “violence” group if they reported experiencing at least one incident of verbal/psychological, physical, or sexual aggression within their current organization; otherwise, they were classified as “no violence”.

Prior to dissemination, the questionnaire was pilot-tested with 10 healthcare professionals; their responses were excluded from the final analysis. All questions were mandatory for survey completion and identical for all respondents, with no randomization, although conditional branching was applied. Responses were automatically stored in the platform’s secure database.

Analysis

A descriptive analysis was conducted, estimating absolute and relative frequencies for qualitative variables, as well as the mean and standard deviation for quantitative variables. The chi-square test and the Mann–Whitney U test for independent samples were used to assess statistical significance, depending on the nature of the variables. In addition, Kendall’s Tau-b was calculated to evaluate the association between variables. Data were analyzed using a multivariable binary logistic regression model to identify factors independently associated with workplace violence. The initial model included variables that showed clinical relevance or a significant association in the bivariate analysis, specifically: gender, age, professional category, job position, type of contract, quantitative and emotional demands, work-life conflict, social support from superiors, role clarity, job insecurity, organizational justice, workplace commitment, burnout, self-rated health and self-efficacy.

To ensure model parsimony, a stepwise backward elimination procedure based on the Likelihood Ratio (LR) test was applied. At each step, the variable with the least contribution to the model’s fit was removed until only significant predictors remained. Age was maintained in the final model as a predefined adjustment variable regardless of its significance level to control for potential confounding. Results are expressed as Adjusted Odds Ratios with their 95% Confidence Intervals. Statistical significance was defined as p<.05. The analysis was performed using SPSS version 29 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

A total of 420 healthcare professionals completed the survey: 144 (34.3%) were nurses, 109 (26.0%) administrative staff, 91 (21.7%) physicians, 37 (8.8%) nursing assistants, and 39 (9.3%) other healthcare professionals (including nutritionists, physiotherapists, social workers, and emotional well-being specialists). Overall, 286 (68.1%) participants reported having experienced workplace violence (Table 1). The most common type of violence was verbal/psychological (98.6%), followed by physical violence (11.9%) and sexual violence (11.2%).

Violence according to sociodemographic characteristics

The most frequent perpetrators were external to the organization, with 79.7% of violent incidents perpetrated by patients and 46.5% by patients’ relatives. Internally sourced violence was distributed as follows: 24.8% perpetrated by coworkers from a different professional category, 19.2% by coworkers from the same professional category, and 14.3% by management. The severity of the aggression was classified as moderate in 42.0% of cases, mild in 38.8%, and severe in 19.2%. The most commonly reported consequences included anxiety, fear, and depression (35.0%); loss of self-esteem, hopelessness, and helplessness (31.1%); disturbances in sleep, eating habits, concentration, and memory (27.3%); and muscle tension or pain (27.3%). Following the violent incident, 12.6% required pharmacological treatment and 13.3% required non-pharmacological treatment. Additionally, 7.6% required temporary work disability leave.

Regarding sex, 84.7% of men and 65.4% of women reported having experienced violence (p=.003). By occupational group, physicians were the most frequently affected (80.2%), followed by administrative staff (73.4%) and nurses (68.1%) (p<.001). Professionals with permanent contracts reported higher exposure to violence (73.5%) than those with temporary contracts (55.6%) (p<.001) (Table 1).

Percentages of violence in relation to exposure to psychosocial health risks

Emotional demands demonstrated the strongest association with the outcome (p<.001; Tau-b=0.306); in the most unfavorable category, 81.9% of workers reported violence compared to 18.1% who did not, representing a 63.8%-point difference. Significant associations were also found for work–life conflict (p<.001; Tau-b=0.206) and organizational justice (p<.001; Tau-b=0.219), where violence prevalence reached 83.1% and 83.5%, respectively, in the most adverse exposure categories. Similarly, burnout (p<.001; Tau-b=0.226) and workplace commitment (p<.001; Tau-b=0.213) showed notable differences, with a prevalence of violence exceeding 80% among those in the least favorable categories. Other factors also showed statistically significant relationships, including quantitative demands (p=.008; Tau-b=0.135), role clarity (p=.024; Tau-b=0.108), and supervisor social support (p=.047; Tau-b=0.110). Regarding health-related variables, both self-efficacy (p=.005; Tau-b=0.155) and self-rated health (p<.001; Tau-b=0.176) showed marked differences, with prevalence rates rising by more than 25% points across categories. In contrast, job influence (p=.673; Tau-b=0.013) and peer social support (p=.795; Tau-b=0.031) showed no meaningful differences between groups. Notably, job insecurity presented a significant inverse association (p<.001; Tau-b=-0.170), contrary to the general trend. These findings provided the basis for selecting potential predictors and confounders for the subsequent multivariable analysis.

Multivariable analysis: factors associated with workplace violence

The multivariable binary logistic regression model identified several independent factors associated with workplace violence (Multivariable logistic regression analysis of factors associated with workplace violence (final model) in Table 3). After adjusting for potential confounders, including age and gender, the variables that remained significantly associated with violence were type of contract, job position, emotional demands, organizational justice, and self-rated health. The final multivariable logistic regression model (Step 9) demonstrated a strong overall fit to the data (Likelihood Ratio Χ2 = 115.309; df = 15; p=.000). The model’s explanatory power, as measured by Nagelkerke’s R2, was 0.336, indicating that the independent variables included explain 33.6% of the variability in the reporting of workplace violence. Furthermore, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test confirmed the adequacy of the model (Χ2 = 5.775; df = 8; p=.672), showing no significant differences between observed and predicted values. Regarding sociodemographic factors, men were significantly more likely to report having experienced violence compared to women (aOR 3.63; 95% CI 1.52–8.65). While age was included in the model as an adjustment variable, it did not show a statistically significant association with the outcome (p=.805).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis of factors associated with workplace violence (final model)

In terms of occupational characteristics, professionals with permanent contracts showed a higher likelihood of reporting violence than those with temporary contracts (aOR 2.37; 95% CI 1.25–4.51). Regarding position held in the company, personnel in direct patient care were 3.74 times more likely to report exposure to violence compared to those in direction and management roles (95% CI 1.83–7.68). Within the occupational groups, the “Others” category (including social workers, nutritionist, physiotherapist and emotional well-being representative) showed a significantly lower association with violence compared to administrative professionals (aOR 0.27; 95% CI 0.10–0.70).

Among the psychosocial exposures, high emotional demands showed the strongest association with the outcome. Individuals in the most unfavorable exposure category were 7.34 times more likely to report workplace violence than those in the most favorable category (95% CI 3.46–15.57). Furthermore, workers reporting the most unfavorable levels of organizational justice had a higher likelihood of violence (aOR 2.23; 95% CI 1.04–4.80) compared to those in the most favorable category. Finally, poor self-rated health was also independently associated with a higher reported prevalence of violence (aOR 2.98; 95% CI 1.26–7.06).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to describe the prevalence of workplace violence and to identify its association with sociodemographic characteristics, working conditions, and psychosocial factors in the work environment among healthcare professionals from the Primary and Community Care Management of Central Catalonia in 2025. The results of this study show a high prevalence of workplace violence among healthcare professionals, with more than two-thirds of participants reporting at least one violent incident in the workplace. This finding is consistent with a recent meta-analysis reporting a prevalence of 61.9% (95% CI: 56.1–67.6) among healthcare professionals worldwide [15]. Consistent with previous research, verbal and psychological violence was the predominant manifestation, establishing itself as the most frequent type of aggression in care settings [15, 16]. The primary perpetrators were external agents, specifically patients (79.7%) and relatives (46.5%), reaffirming that violence in the health sector is predominantly Type II—that is, exerted by users toward professionals [16].

Regarding the professional profile, male gender proved to be a heavy independent predictor (aOR 3.63), which invites deep reflection on reporting differences. This observation is also reflected in the literature, where men experienced more workplace violence than women (61.4% (58.2–64.2%) vs. 48.8% (46.9–50.8%)), respectively [17]. This finding might be related to gender differences in the perception and recognition of violent behaviors, as suggested by previous literature, although our study did not directly assess subjective perception. Acquadro et al. (2019) observed that women tend to describe episodes of aggression with a more relational or contextual approach, while men use more direct expressions linked to violence [18]. This difference in interpretation could contribute to a certain under-recognition of the more subtle forms of psychological and verbal violence against women, a phenomenon also described in other studies on violence in the healthcare setting. In light of this evidence, this synthesis suggests that future prevention strategies should move beyond incident frequency and address gender-based conceptualizations of violence to avoid under-reporting, as the interpretation of aggression is clearly context-dependent [18, 19].

Occupational stability also emerged as a significant factor. Professionals with permanent contracts showed a significantly higher likelihood of reporting violence (73.5%) compared to those with temporary contracts (55.6%), maintaining an independent association in the multivariable model (aOR 2.37). This observation aligns with the findings of Giménez et al. (2021), whose systematic review indicated that workplace violence tends to occur more frequently among professionals with full-time or permanent employment, possibly due to their heavier workload, direct exposure to patient care, and the accumulation of emotional demands arising from clinical practice. This finding could support the “cumulative exposure” hypothesis: it is plausible to suggest that permanent staff may have a longer tenure in their positions, potentially increasing the temporal window of opportunity for violent encounters. Permanent contracts could act as a proxy for professional stability and longer-term exposure. In contrast, temporary workers often rotate more frequently and remain on teams for shorter periods within the healthcare system, which might result in fewer opportunities for cumulative exposure [20]. Furthermore, staff in direct patient care were 3.74 times more likely to report violence than those in management, reinforcing the premise that physical and clinical proximity is the primary risk scenario [21, 22].

The analysis of psychosocial risk exposures revealed a clear dose-response relationship: as the work environment becomes more adverse, the proportion of professionals reporting violence increases drastically. High emotional demands emerged as the most critical risk factor, increasing the likelihood of reporting violence by 7.34 times compared to those with favorable demands. This finding is fundamental, as the healthcare sector is characterized by situations of high emotional intensity which, added to healthcare pressure, facilitate the breakdown of professional role expectations and the outbreak of aggression [20, 23]. The fact that the association with emotional demands (aOR 7.34) showed a much higher magnitude than that of demographic variables—such as sex (aOR 3.63)—highlights that the psychological burden of the task is a more powerful independent predictor of workplace violence than the professional’s individual profile. This shift in perspective is fundamental: it suggests that vulnerability lies in the work design rather than the individual. Consequently, while resilience training is common in the literature, our findings indicate that systemic management of psychological burden is the primary lever for change. This places the organizational nature of clinical work at the epicenter of the observed conflict [20, 23].

This individual burden is inextricably linked to the organizational climate. The lack of organizational justice emerged as a key structural predictor (aOR 2.23), validating previous models suggesting that a work environment perceived as unfair is associated with experiences of violence. In this sense, we interpret organizational justice as a structural pillar; its absence may erode the professional’s emotional resources, making the environment more prone to conflict outbreaks. This reinforces the thesis that violence is a symptom of organizational health, not just an interpersonal event [16]. Moreover, the strong bivariate link between violence and burnout (> 80%) reinforces the thesis that both phenomena coexist in a cycle of professional deterioration [20, 24]. Given the cross-sectional design of this research, it is not possible to establish whether exhaustion precedes aggression or is its direct consequence. However, the literature suggests a bidirectional relationship where violence depletes staff resources, and already fatigued personnel are more vulnerable to new conflicts [20, 25]. Interestingly, while burnout and work-life conflict were significant in bivariate analyses, their absence in the final model suggests their influence may be mediated by more structural factors like organizational justice or emotional demands.

Finally, poor self-rated health was independently associated with a tripled risk of violence (aOR 2.98). The consequences reported by participants—such as anxiety, fear, and depression (35.0%), sleep disturbances, and hopelessness—are consistent with the precursor symptomatology of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) and burnout. Meta-analytical literature indicates that professionals exposed to workplace violence are 2.13 times more likely to develop PTSD and 2.25 times more likely to suffer from burnout [24]. In this context, the self-efficacy identified in our results may act as a mediating resilience factor. High self-efficacy allows victims to employ more adaptive coping strategies, reducing the likelihood of aggression leading to chronic psychiatric pathologies [25, 26].

Strengths and limitations

This study presents several strengths, such as it was conducted in a primary care setting with broad geographical coverage, allowing for the inclusion of a multidisciplinary sample of healthcare professionals with diverse professional backgrounds. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes the establishment of causal relationships between psychosocial exposures and workplace violence. The observed findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations representing a coexistence of factors at a specific point in time without establishing temporality. Second, due to the geographical extent of the study, an anonymous online questionnaire was used. While this is an efficient strategy employed by international organizations (Eurofound), it does not guarantee that each participant responded only once. Furthermore, the response rate could not be estimated, as the number of workers who opened the email invitation to participate is unknown. The use of a voluntary online survey and convenience sampling could introduce a risk of selection bias. Although all types of contracts were included to improve representativeness, those with temporary contracts may have checked their institutional email less frequently, which could have reduced their participation. On the other hand, it is possible that workers who were sensitized to the issue would be more motivated to participate. This “self-selection” could lead to an overestimation of the reported data, although the observed prevalence coincides with the literature precedents. Finally, although the multivariable model adjusted for key variables like age and sex, the possibility of residual confounding remains. Other unmeasured factors, such as specific personality traits or previous history of trauma, could influence both the perception of psychosocial risks and the reporting of violence. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings and their potential generalizability to other healthcare contexts.

Conclusions

This study reveals a high prevalence of workplace violence (68.1%) among primary care professionals, primarily characterized by verbal and psychological aggression from patients and relatives. The findings reveal exploratory associations between the reporting of violent incidents and specific individual and organizational factors. Notably, while male gender and permanent contracts were associated with higher reporting rates, the strongest observed relationships involved psychosocial factors, such as high emotional demands and a lack of organizational justice. The magnitude of these associations suggests that the psychological burden of clinical tasks and the organizational climate coexist significantly with experiences of violence in this setting. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, these findings should be interpreted as identified associations that highlight the importance of the work environment in the professional experience, rather than evidence of a direct causal link.

Future research should include longitudinal studies to establish causality between psychosocial risks and workplace violence, as well as the evolution of the impact on the professional’s health. Furthermore, it is essential to delve deeper into the differences between occupational groups. On the other hand, qualitative studies would be needed to collect subjective experiences of violence, contextual factors, and interpersonal dynamics within the healthcare environment.
