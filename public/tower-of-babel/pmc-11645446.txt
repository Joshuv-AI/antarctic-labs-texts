Pharmacokinetic study of polymyxin B in healthy subjects and subjects with renal insufficiency
Authors: Yu‐Wei Fang, Chien‐Hsien Huang, Tsrang‐Neng Jang, Shih‐Sen Lin, Jing‐Tong Wang, Yen‐Ta Huang, Ming Hsien Tsai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolymyxin B is a viable option for treating antibiotic‐resistant infections; however, current data on its pharmacokinetics, particularly in patients with renal insufficiency, remain inconclusive and necessitates further investigation. To address this gap, we conducted an open‐label, single‐center, single‐dose, parallel‐group pharmacokinetic study. Participants received an intravenous dose of 0.75 mg/kg of polymyxin B and were categorized based on their renal function: those with normal function (creatinine clearance [CLcr] ≥ 90 mL/min), mild renal insufficiency (CLcr 60–89 mL/min), and end‐stage kidney disease patients on intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) (CLcr < 10 mL/min). The pharmacokinetic parameters assessed included the area under the curve (AUC), maximum concentration (C max), clearance rate (CL), volume of distribution (Vz), and half‐life (t 1/2). Results indicated that subjects with mild renal insufficiency exhibited pharmacokinetic profiles similar to healthy individuals. Nevertheless, in patients undergoing long‐term IHD, we observed significant differences: the AUC was 58% higher, C max was 29% lower, CL was 42% lower, Vz was 60% larger, and t 1/2 was extended by 10 h compared to healthy controls. Secondary outcomes revealed good tolerability of polymyxin B across all groups, with no serious adverse effects related to renal function. In summary, while kidney function may have a slight impact on the pharmacokinetic of polymyxin B, it does not compromise the drug's therapeutic effectiveness.

INTRODUCTION

During the World Antibiotic Awareness Week in November 2017, the World Health Organization (WHO) highlighted antibiotic resistance as a major threat to global health. Owing to increased resistance, polymyxins (such as polymyxin B and colistin) are preferred for treating infections caused by multidrug‐resistant (MDR) bacteria such as MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Klebsiella pneumoniae. 1 , 2

Discovered in the 1940s and first used in the 1950s, intravenous (IV) use of polymyxin B declined in the 1980s because of toxicity, leading to its topical use. 3 However, with the growing prevalence of drug‐resistant infections, IV polymyxin B is being used again because it is possibly more effective than colistin. 1 Polymyxin B contains a fatty acid chain linked to a heptapeptide ring containing both D‐ and L‐amino acids. 4 Its structure makes it positively charged, allowing it to interact with the lipopolysaccharide molecules on the outer membrane of gram‐negative bacteria (GNB). This interaction breaks the membrane and leaks the cell content, quickly killing the bacteria. 1 Recent studies show polymyxin B to have less nephrotoxicity rates and more effectiveness against MDR GNB than its counterparts. 5 , 6

Polymyxin B is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, limiting its oral use; it is typically administered intravenously or intramuscularly for systemic infections. The drug is widely distributed in tissues such as the lungs, liver, and kidneys but has limited penetration into the central nervous system, which affects its efficacy for central nervous system infections. It is minimally metabolized and remains largely unchanged in circulation, which is crucial for its activity. Moreover, polymyxin B is excreted by the kidneys but undergoes extensive tubular reabsorption, leading to its primary elimination through nonrenal biliary excretion. 7 , 8

Since 1998 in the United States, the advised dose of polymyxin B is 1.5–2.5 mg/kg/day for patients over 2 years old with normal kidneys, and not exceeding 2.5 mg/kg or 200 mg, whichever is lower. Initially, adjusting the dose of polymyxin B for patients with varying renal function is considered essential, although it is contentious due to inconsistencies among studies. Some studies recommend not adjusting the dosage based on renal function, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 whereas others suggest that adjustments should be made. 17 , 18 , 19 After discussions by some experts, the International Consensus Guidelines for the Optimal Use of the Polymyxins, published in 2019, recommended that no change is needed for patients with kidney problems or those on dialysis. 20 This suggestion has a strong theoretical basis in metabolism studies which reported that only a tiny amount of polymyxin B leaves the body through the kidneys with the majority being reabsorbed and removed through other methods. 12 , 13 , 21 Besides, low‐dose administration of polymyxin B was identified as an independent predictor of treatment failure and in‐hospital mortality. 22 Given this information, it is more important to focus on a dose that effectively kills bacteria with minimal side effects rather than adjusting the dose based on kidney function. 10 , 11

Therefore, this study was conducted to evaluate the pharmacokinetics (PKs) of a single dose of polymyxin B administered via IV infusion in healthy subjects, patients with mild renal insufficiency, and patients with end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) and long‐term intermittent hemodialysis (IHD). The PK profile of subjects with renal insufficiency of different severities was compared with those of healthy subjects.

METHODS

Study design and patients

This open‐label, single‐center, single‐dose (0.75 mg/kg), parallel‐group study evaluated the PK and safety profiles of polymyxin B administered via IV infusion to healthy subjects and subjects with renal insufficiency. The study enrolled adult subjects in the following three categories based on renal function data from July 2022 to March 2023, in the medical center of Shin‐Kong Wu Ho‐Su Memorial Hospital in Taiwan: (1) Arm 1 (normal renal function group) were patients with creatinine clearance (CLcr) ≥ 90 mL/min; (2) Arm 2 (mild renal insufficiency group) were patients with CLcr between 60 and 89 mL/min; (3) Arm 3 (long‐term IHD group) were patients with ESKD receiving IHD therapy three times a week for at least 3 months prior to dosing of the study drug (CLcr < 10 mL/min). CLcr was calculated based on serum creatinine (SCr) levels using the Cockcroft–Gault formula. 23 By using these study groups, we aimed to minimize confounding factors commonly seen in patients with serious infections, enabling us to specifically evaluate the impact of renal function on polymyxin B pharmacokinetics. Details of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as the protocol of data collection and sampling schedule, are provided in Method S1 and Figure S1 of Supplementary file, respectively.

Polymyxin B concentration measurements

TTY Biopharm Company (Taipei, Taiwan) supplied lyophilized powder of polymyxin B for injection (Batch No. 040221120300). Blood samples of approximately 5 mL for polymyxin B testing were collected in dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (K2EDTA) tubes, immediately mixed by flipping the tube 8–10 times and centrifuging within 30 min, thereafter, at 2000× g for 10–15 min at 4°C. The separated plasma was stored in two polypropylene tubes, one with 1 mL and the other with the rest, and frozen at −70°C or lower within 2 h until shipped for analysis. Urine samples were also collected, measured, and stored in two tubes of 2 mL each at −70°C until shipping. Before shipping, the blood and urine samples were packed with dry ice, frozen for 72 h, and then monitored at a consistent temperature. The concentrations of polymyxin B, including its variants in both blood and urine, were analyzed by QPS using liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS). 24

Measurement of plasma protein binding

All samples were stored at −70°C until analysis. Study samples were dialyzed using a Thermo Scientific™ Single‐Use RED Plate (Waltham, MA, USA) at 37°C on a vortex mixer at approximately 800 rpm for 4 h. After equilibrium dialysis, postdialysis samples were prepared alongside blank samples, quality control (QC) samples, and calibration standards, and analyzed using LC‐MS/MS. Plasma protein binding ratios were calculated using the following equation: Unbound fractionfu=Concentration of buffer chamber/Concentration of plasma chamber×100%,and bound fraction=100%−fu.

Appropriateness of measurements

The PK parameters of polymyxin B were assessed through analysis of blood and urine. The fu of polymyxin B was assessed by protein‐binding analysis of blood. The primary efficacy end point was to determine the following PK parameters for polymyxin B: (1) maximum observed plasma concentration (C max); (2) area under the plasma concentration versus time curve from time 0 to the last measurable concentration (AUC0‐last); (3) area under the plasma concentration versus time curve from time 0 extrapolated to infinity (AUC0‐inf); and (4) clearance (CL), calculated as Dose/AUC0‐inf; (5) terminal elimination rate constant (Kel); (6) apparent elimination half‐life (t 1/2), calculated as ln2/Kel; (7) apparent volume of distribution (Vz), calculated as Dose/(Kel*AUC0‐inf); (8) cumulative amount of analyte excreted in urine from 0 to time t (Ae(0‐t)), calculated as the summation of the amounts, product of urine volume and concentration, excreted in subsequent collection intervals (only for Arms 1 and 2); (9) cumulative percentage of analyte excreted in urine from 0 to time t (%) (Fe(0‐t)), calculated as Ae(0‐t)/Dose*100 (only for Arms 1 and 2) and renal clearance (CLR), calculated as Ae(0‐t)/AUC(0‐t) (only for Arms 1 and 2); and (10) nonrenal clearance (CLNR), calculated by subtracting CLR from CL (only for Arms 1 and 2). The secondary efficacy end point was the determination of the f u of polymyxin B by protein binding assessment.

Safety analyses

The secondary safety goal was to evaluate safety and comfort, including adverse events, reactions at the infusion site, laboratory tests, physical examinations, vital signs, and electrocardiograms (ECGs). Adverse events were grouped using MedDRA 25.1 and included all types that appeared during the study, sorted by body system and other details.

Statistical analysis

No statistical hypotheses were established in this study. The sample size was based on both empirical and clinical considerations. The plasma PK parameters were calculated through noncompartmental methods using Phoenix WinNonlin version 8.3 based on individual plasma concentrations of polymyxin B from the actual blood sampling time points. The urine PK parameters were calculated via noncompartmental methods using Phoenix WinNonlin version 8.3 based on individual urine concentrations of polymyxin B. Nominal urine sampling periods were used to evaluate the urine PK parameters. All statistical PK analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4.

The AUC and C max of subjects with renal insufficiency of different severities were compared with those of healthy subjects (Arm 2 vs. Arm 1 and Arm 3 vs. Arm 1), and a two‐sided 90% confidence interval (CI) for the geometric least squares mean ratio (GMR) was presented for each of these comparisons. The least‐squares mean (LSM) of the AUC and C max for each arm was estimated using a linear mixed‐effects model with the arm as a single fixed factor on the log‐transformed AUC and C max. The relationships between PK parameters (e.g., CLR for Arms 1 and 2; and AUC, C max, and CL for all Arms) and renal function (CLcr) were evaluated using linear regression.

Ethics statement

This study was performed in accordance with the clinical research guidelines established by the Basic Principles defined in U.S. 21 Code of Federal Regulations Part 312.20, the principles enunciated in the Declaration of Helsinki (Fortaleza, Brazil, October 2013) and the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use harmonized tripartite guideline regarding Good Clinical Practice. The Ethics Committee of the Shin‐Kong Wu Ho‐Su Memorial Hospital (No. 20220206 M) reviewed the study protocol and ensured that all aspects, including how we obtained consent, met these abovementioned standards and human protection rules (FDA Title 21 CFR Parts 50 and 56). This study was listed on ClinicalTrials.gov under NCT05359627. Informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

RESULTS

Disposition of subjects

Twenty‐nine subjects were screened, but only 22 subjects were included in this study: eight subjects in Arm 1 (normal renal function group), seven subjects in Arm 2 (mild renal insufficiency group), and seven subjects in Arm 3 (long‐term IHD group). All 22 participants completed the study (Figure 1). Table 1 presents the demographics and characteristics of the 22 participants divided across the study arms, providing a detailed snapshot of the population involved. Notably, the gender distribution revealed nine women (40.91%) and 13 men (59.09%), with a significant male majority in Arm 1 (87.50%) and a female majority in Arm 2 (71.43%). Participant ages ranged widely, with an overall average of 46.6 years (range 35–67), suggesting a middle‐aged cohort. All the participants were of Asian ethnicity. Each category exhibited slight variations in body mass index (BMI); however, all the values remained within a similar range.

The relationship between PK parameters and CLcr

Positive linear relationships were found between polymyxin B's C max and CLcr and between polymyxin B's CL and CLcr with estimated slopes (90% CIs) of 10.2 (6.10 to 14.2) and 0.0104 (0.00792 to 0.0128), respectively (Figure 3a,b). On the contrary, negative linear relationships were found between polymyxin B's AUC0‐last and CLcr and between polymyxin B's AUC0‐inf and CLcr with estimated slopes (90% CIs) of −106 (−153 to −58.5) and −105 (−149 to −59.8), respectively (Figure 3c,d).

Urine PK parameters of polymyxin B

Table S1 of Supplementary file shows that about 1.67% and 1.53% of the dose were found to be polymyxin B in the urine of healthy individuals and those with mild kidney issues, respectively, within 24 h after the dose, mainly within the first 6 h after beginning polymyxin B infusion. The renal clearance rates of polymyxin B were significantly lower than the nonrenal clearance rates in both healthy participants and those with mild kidney problems, indicating that polymyxin B is mainly eliminated through nonkidney routes.

Protein binding

Table S2 of Supplementary file shows no obvious differences in the protein binding results among the three groups. The mean unbound fraction of polymyxin B1 was 4.7% (range 3.9%–6.6%), 5.0% (range 3.0%–7.6%), and 4.6% (range 3.4%–5.9%) for Arms 1, 2, and 3, respectively. For polymyxin B2, the mean unbound fraction was 11.1% (range: 9.0%–12.7%), 10.9% (range: 6.6%–17.2%), and 10.1% (range: 6.2%–15.8%) for Arms 1, 2, and 3, respectively. The mean unbound fraction of total polymyxin B (B1 + B2) was 6.1% (range 4.6%–7.5%), 5.8% (range 3.6%–8.4%), and 5.4% (range 3.3%–7.2%) for Arms 1, 2, and 3, respectively.

Safety of polymyxin B administration

Most participants (86.36%) had gastrointestinal problems, and everyone in Arm 3 was affected. Oral numbness was common, affecting 72.73% of patients, especially in Arms 2 and 3. Dry mouth was less frequent, seen in 18.18% of the participants in Arms 1 and 2. Nervous system issues were also significant, affecting 81.82%, with all patients in Arm 3 being affected. Half of the participants reported reduced sensitivity to stimuli, while dizziness and headaches were less common, affecting 40.91% and 9.09% of participants, respectively (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

In our study, the PK profiles of polymyxin B, including AUC, C max, CL, Vz, and t 1/2, were similar in subjects with mild renal insufficiency and healthy subjects. A very low proportion of polymyxin B was detected in the urine of subjects with mild renal insufficiency and healthy subjects. However, the proportion of patients with long‐term IHD differed considerably. Long‐term IHD subjects had a 58% higher AUC, 29% lower C max (2800 vs. 3960 ng/mL), 42% reduced clearance rate (1.50 vs. 2.59 L/h), 60% larger Vz (32.4 vs. 20.2 L), and a t 1/2 10 h (15.2 vs. 5.4 h) longer than those of healthy individuals. This highlights how chronic conditions alter drug function in the body, 25 including their distribution, binding, metabolism, and excretion. Moreover, polymyxin B was generally safe and well‐tolerated at a single IV dose when administered to healthy participants and patients with renal insufficiency and ESKD receiving IHD.

When comparing our results with a previous study by Liu et al., 26 who administered the same single dose of 0.75 mg/kg polymyxin B to healthy Chinese subjects, we observed comparable pharmacokinetic parameters in our healthy group (Arm 1). The key parameters from our study versus Liu's study were as follows: C max 3960 ± 650 ng/mL versus 4660 ± 461 mg/L; AUC0‐inf 20,500 ± 4440 ng·h/mL versus 22,600 ± 2040 mg·h/L; total clearance 2.59 ± 0.338 L/h versus 1.96 ± 0.14 L/h (at 70 kg); and half‐life 5.44 ± 0.491 h versus 5.44 ± 0.741 h, respectively. The similarity between these results demonstrates the reliability of our findings.

A trace amount of polymyxin B was detected in the urine of subjects with mild renal insufficiency and healthy subjects, consistent with past studies in humans and animals which found that polymyxin B primarily exits the body through nonrenal routes. Zavascki et al. 12 reported that only 0.04%–0.86% of polymyxin B was recovered in the urine of four patients, while Sandri et al. 13 observed a median of 4.04% urinary recovery in 17 patients, with values ranging from 0.98% to 17.4%. Supporting this, animal research by Abdelraouf et al. 27 demonstrated that less than 1% of polymyxin B remained unchanged in rat urine after 48 hours. Furthermore, Manchandani et al. 24 , 28 confirmed that less than 5% of polymyxin B was excreted through urine in rats, underscoring consistently low renal elimination.

However, in the present study, the PK profile changed significantly in patients with ESKD. The overall body clearance decreased in Arm 3, indicating that polymyxin B was eliminated more slowly in patients with ESKD, potentially leading to higher levels of the drug in the body compared to other groups. Our study suggests that kidney function influences how polymyxin B is excreted, challenging the notion that it is primarily eliminated nonrenally. This weak relationship was confirmed by similar studies. Manchandani et al. 14 observed a negligible correlation with the determination coefficient (r 2) below 0.3, and Wang et al. 29 also reported a minor correlation (r 2 = 0.1736). Yu et al. 17 identified an r 2 of 0.31 from their study, reinforcing this modest link between kidney function and polymyxin B clearance. We did not assess the impact of dialysis on polymyxin B clearance, nor did we quantify drug loss during the dialysis procedure. Such calculations would require analysis of drug concentrations in the dialysate samples. Although drug elimination through dialysate has not been previously analyzed in IHD patients, studies in two patients receiving continuous venovenous hemodialysis reported that 12.2% and 5.62% of drug clearance were attributed to dialysis. 9 The very low unbound fraction of polymyxin B observed across all three study arms, which is consistent with previously published reports, 30 suggests that drug loss into the dialysate during dialysis would be minimal.

Although polymyxin B is eliminated through a nonkidney pathway, kidney problems can affect the behavior of the drug in the body. Although polymyxin B is primarily eliminated via nonrenal pathways, kidney issues can still affect the drug's behavior in the body. This influence occurs through mechanisms other than merely slowing renal elimination. For drugs that are not eliminated via the kidneys, elevated drug exposure (AUCs) observed in patients with severe renal impairment may be attributed to the effects of uremic toxins on drug transporters and/or metabolic enzymes in both intestinal and hepatic tissues. 31 , 32 The detection of polymyxin B in bile samples indicates that the biliary system may play a role in the drug's elimination pathway. 24 However, the mechanisms underlying the nonrenal clearance of polymyxin B remain incompletely understood, warranting further studies to elucidate its alternative elimination pathways. This evidence of biliary elimination further supports the rationale that altered hepatic transporters and/or enzymes in patients with severe renal impairment may contribute to the observed increase in AUC.

The exact reason for lower C max in long‐term patients with IHD is complex; however, research suggests it may be primarily attributed to an increased volume of distribution. 33 , 34 An increased volume of distribution is observed in ESKD patients undergoing IHD, attributed to fluid retention, hypoalbuminemia, and reduced protein binding. 35 Alterations in total body water content in ESKD patients can have a major impact on the volume of distribution. 36 Theoretically, patients with ESKD often exhibit decreased protein binding due to hypoalbuminemia, alterations in protein binding sites, and binding competition with other medications or accumulated uremic albumin‐bound retention solutes, which could contribute to differences in drug plasma concentrations between ESKD patients and healthy subjects. 37 However, our results showed no obvious differences in the unbound fraction of polymyxin B between these populations, suggesting that the protein binding of polymyxin B is not substantially affected by ESKD‐specific factors. Based on our comprehensive literature review, this appears to be the first study to report the unbound fraction of polymyxin B in ESRD patients undergoing IHD. The lower C max of polymyxin B observed in ESRD patients undergoing IHD appears to be minimally affected by protein binding characteristics. Moreover, uremic toxins may intensify inflammatory processes, causing endothelial damage and elevated vascular permeability, 38 thereby facilitating rapid drug extravasation into interstitial spaces after administration. Regarding the potential impact of dialysis on polymyxin B clearance, it is important to note that according to our study design, subjects in Arm 3 received polymyxin B 1 day after their third dialysis session of the week, with their next dialysis scheduled 3 days later. This timing ensures that dialysis would not affect the observed C max values. According to our PK study, patients with kidney issues on long‐term dialysis showed a decreased elimination rate of polymyxin B leading to 58% higher drug exposure (AUC) compared to healthy individuals. However, this increase does not necessitate a dose adjustment for polymyxin B unless the AUC doubles. 14 , 39 This conclusion aligns with findings from other studies, indicating no need for dose adjustment in patients with ESKD. Wang et al. 15 found that patients with moderate to severe kidney impairment had a 33% higher AUC than those with normal kidney function. They observed no significant difference in rates of acute kidney injury between the two groups, suggesting that adjusting the polymyxin dosage based on kidney function may be unnecessary. Similarly, studies by Peyko et al. 16 and Maniara et al. 40 showed no significant benefits in terms of safety or effectiveness when adjusting doses based on kidney function. Their findings suggest that unadjusted doses may lead to better outcomes for infection control, fewer kidney injuries, and lower mortality rates. This indicates that avoiding dosage modifications could potentially reduce the risk of kidney injury and death.

Our study elucidates the differential pharmacokinetics of polymyxin B in patients with mild kidney impairment and those on long‐term dialysis, populations often excluded from clinical trials, thus enhancing the broader applicability of our findings. We also provided specific PK parameters, including peak drug concentrations, CL, and t 1/2, which are critical for clinicians and researchers. The study mainly explored a small number of kidney problems, which might overlook how drugs function in people with severe kidney issues who are not on dialysis. While it offers insights into drug disposition and distribution, it does not correlate these findings with therapeutic efficacy, performance, or safety profiles. Therefore, further research is warranted to include a broader spectrum of renal function impairment, investigate the temporal dynamics of drug effects, explore additional influencing factors, and establish clear associations between pharmacokinetics and clinical outcomes. Kidney function can influence the pharmacokinetics of polymyxin B; however, the variation is modest and does not significantly impact the clinical efficacy of the treatment. Consequently, adjusting the polymyxin B dose based on kidney function is not recommended. Nevertheless, continuous monitoring of renal function remains important.
