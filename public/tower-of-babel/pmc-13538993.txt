Bone turnover biomarkers and semi-quantitative bone histomorphometry in chronic kidney disease: Comparison between patients with and without fractures
Authors: Sushil Sharma, Premjit Sujir, Pranav Rajasekharan, Prem Kotian, Manikanta Bhupal, G. N. Sudhanva, Karthik Parimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is associated with increased fracture risk not fully explained by reduced bone mineral density. CKD–mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD), involving abnormalities in bone turnover, mineralization, and microarchitecture, contributes to skeletal fragility. However, the relationship between routine biochemical markers and semi-quantitative bone histomorphometry remains inadequately defined, particularly in resource-limited settings and the Indian population. The objective of this study was to compare biochemical markers and semi-quantitative bone histomorphometric findings in CKD patients with and without fractures.MethodsIn this hospital-based comparative cross-sectional study, 48 patients with CKD stage 3–4 were divided into fracture (n = 24) and non-fracture groups (n = 24). Serum calcium, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, parathyroid hormone (PTH), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) were measured. Bone samples were obtained intraoperatively or via transiliac crest biopsy. Semi-quantitative bone histomorphometry was assessed by a pathologist. Statistical analysis included appropriate group comparisons.ResultsFracture patients had significantly lower vitamin D (p = 0.007) and calcium (p = 0.005), and higher PTH (p = 0.002) and ALP (p = 0.001). Semi-quantitative histomorphometric evaluation showed increased osteoid volume, reduced trabecular thickness, and elevated osteoblastic and osteoclastic activity.ConclusionIn our study cohort, CKD patients with fractures exhibited a high-turnover bone phenotype with increased osteoid volume and altered bone histology. Biochemical markers may serve as cost-effective adjuncts for fracture risk stratification, particularly where advanced diagnostics are limited. Findings should be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional design and small sample size.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a significant global health burden, contributing to considerable morbidity and mortality. Among its systemic complications, disturbances in bone and mineral metabolism are particularly important and are collectively described as CKD–mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD).1,2 These abnormalities arise from complex disturbances in calcium and phosphate balance, reduced activation of vitamin D, and secondary hyperparathyroidism.

Individuals with CKD face a significantly higher risk of fractures than the general population. Importantly, this increased risk cannot be fully attributed to reduced bone mineral density alone, indicating that qualitative alterations in bone—such as changes in turnover, mineralization, and microarchitecture—play a critical role.3,4 Secondary hyperparathyroidism leads to increased bone turnover and resorption, while vitamin D deficiency contributes to secondary hyperparathyroidism and altered bone metabolism in patients with CKD.5,6

Although dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry is commonly employed to measure bone mineral density, it does not capture these qualitative abnormalities. In contrast, biochemical markers including parathyroid hormone (PTH), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), calcium, and vitamin D provide indirect insights into bone metabolism and are readily available in routine clinical practice. However, their correlation with bone histomorphometry, the reference standard for evaluating bone turnover and mineralization, remains inconsistent and not fully established.7,8

Bone histomorphometry provides detailed information regarding bone microarchitecture and cellular activity but is invasive and not routinely performed.8,9 Establishing a reliable relationship between biochemical markers and histological parameters could facilitate non-invasive assessment of bone health in CKD.8,9

Data from the Indian population examining this relationship, particularly in patients with fractures, are limited. To our knowledge, no prior Indian study has directly compared biochemical markers with semi-quantitative bone histomorphometry in fracture-stratified CKD cohorts.

The present study aimed to compare biochemical markers and semi-quantitative bone histomorphometric findings between CKD patients with and without fractures. We hypothesized that patients with fractures would demonstrate a high-turnover bone phenotype associated with distinct biochemical abnormalities.

Methods

This comparative cross-sectional study was carried out at Kasturba Medical College, Mangalore, and allied hospitals between June 2024 and February 2026. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC KMC MLR 03/2024/177), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion.

A total of 48 adult individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD) from stage 3-4 were enrolled and categorised into two groups:• CKD patients with fractures (n = 24)• CKD patients without fractures (n = 24)

CKD was defined and staged in accordance with the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines based on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). 1

Patients aged 18–70 years with diagnosed CKD were included. Exclusion criteria were prior renal transplantation, history of parathyroidectomy, prolonged corticosteroid use, malignancy, metabolic bone disorders (including Paget’s disease and osteogenesis imperfecta), and patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis.

The sample size was calculated based on the findings of a previous study by Ureña et al. Using a confidence level of 90%, a statistical power of 80%, and the reported difference in mineral mass between fracture and non-fracture CKD patients, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 24 participants per group. Accordingly, 24 patients with fractures and 24 patients without fractures were included, giving a total sample size of 48 participants.

Venous blood samples were collected under aseptic conditions. Serum calcium, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, parathyroid hormone (PTH), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels were measured using standardized laboratory assays.

Bone samples in the fracture group were obtained intraoperatively from fracture-edge bone or routinely discarded bone fragments during surgical fixation of fractures involving the proximal femur (neck of femur and intertrochanteric fractures), distal radius, and humerus. No additional surgical procedure was performed for study purposes. In the non-fracture group, bone samples were obtained from the iliac crest under local anaesthesia. Specimens were processed using standard histopathological techniques and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histological assessment included osteoid volume, trabecular thickness, osteoblastic activity, and osteoclastic activity based on a semi-quantitative morphological evaluation of routine H&E-stained sections. Osteoblastic and osteoclastic activity were graded according to the reporting pathologist’s overall assessment of the relative prominence of osteoblasts and osteoclasts and categorized as increased or reduced. These parameters were selected because they were consistently assessable using routine hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections and reflected the principal features of bone turnover evaluated in the present study. Formal quantitative histomorphometric parameters, such as bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV), cortical thickness, osteoid thickness, and dynamic indices, require standardized undecalcified bone histomorphometry with specialized techniques, which were not performed in this study. Histological evaluation was performed by an experienced pathologist.

For semi-quantitative analysis, osteoid volume, trabecular thickness, osteoblastic activity and osteoclastic activity were categorized according to the reporting pathologist’s assessment. Formal quantitative histomorphometric measurements were not performed.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out using SPSS (version 29).

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to evaluate normality. Depending on data distribution, group comparisons were performed using either the independent t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test.

A p-value < 0.05 was taken as statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that our study cohort of CKD patients with fractures exhibited a distinct skeletal phenotype characterized by high bone turnover, increased osteoid volume and altered bone histology. Both biochemical and semi-quantitative histomorphometric findings consistently support this observation. The findings of this study support the concept that fracture risk in CKD is driven not only by reduced bone mass but also by qualitative alterations in bone structure and turnover, which are not captured by conventional bone mineral density assessment.3,4

Elevated PTH levels in fracture patients reflect secondary hyperparathyroidism, a hallmark of CKD-MBD. Excess PTH stimulates osteoclastic activity, leading to increased bone resorption, cortical thinning, and disruption of trabecular architecture. These findings are consistent with previous reports by Cunningham et al., who described the central role of secondary hyperparathyroidism in driving high-turnover bone disease in CKD. The reduced trabecular thickness observed in the fracture group may reflect increased trabecular remodeling associated with secondary hyperparathyroidism. 5

Vitamin D deficiency, which was significantly more pronounced in fracture patients, may contribute to altered bone metabolism and secondary hyperparathyroidism in CKD. Lower vitamin D levels observed in the fracture group may have contributed to impaired bone mineralization and increased osteoid volume.6,10

Higher alkaline phosphatase levels in the fracture group were consistent with increased osteoblastic activity observed on semi-quantitative histological assessment, supporting a high-turnover bone phenotype. Similarly, lower serum calcium levels observed in the fracture group may have contributed to impaired bone mineralization and altered trabecular structure, further emphasizing the importance of calcium homeostasis in maintaining bone integrity.7,11

Although elevated PTH levels were observed in both study groups, the fracture group demonstrated substantially greater semi-quantitative evidence of increased bone turnover. This suggests that elevated circulating PTH alone may not fully determine skeletal remodelling in CKD. The biological response to PTH is influenced by several factors, including vitamin D deficiency, calcium homeostasis, duration and severity of secondary hyperparathyroidism, skeletal responsiveness to PTH, and the heterogeneous spectrum of CKD–mineral and bone disorder. Consequently, patients with similar biochemical abnormalities may exhibit different histological patterns and fracture susceptibility. The lower prevalence of increased osteoblastic and osteoclastic activity in the non-fracture group despite elevated PTH may therefore reflect differences in the underlying skeletal response rather than serum PTH concentration alone.

Previous studies have reported variable relationships between biochemical markers and bone histology in CKD. Although our study demonstrated differences in biochemical markers and semi-quantitative bone histomorphometric findings between fracture and non-fracture groups, direct comparison with studies employing formal quantitative histomorphometry should be made with caution. This finding contrasts with previous work by Nickolas et al., who demonstrated that biochemical markers correlate only modestly with bone microarchitecture, highlighting the limitations of relying solely on serum parameters. Similarly, Malluche et al. emphasized that while markers such as PTH and alkaline phosphatase reflect bone turnover, they do not consistently predict underlying histological patterns. In contrast, the more pronounced biochemical and semi-quantitative histomorphometric differences observed in the present study may reflect differences in study design, direct histological assessment, and the relatively homogeneous study population.3,8

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that routinely available biochemical markers may provide useful adjunctive information in assessing bone health in CKD patients, particularly in settings where bone biopsy is not feasible. However, these markers cannot replace histomorphometry and should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and imaging findings. Previous studies have highlighted the limitations of bone mineral density alone in predicting fracture risk in CKD, further supporting the need for adjunctive markers of bone quality.12–20

Conclusion

In our study cohort, CKD patients with fractures exhibited a high-turnover bone phenotype with increased osteoid volume and altered semi-quantitative bone histomorphometric findings. CKD patients with fractures demonstrated altered biochemical markers together with corresponding semi-quantitative bone histomorphometric changes, suggesting that routinely available biochemical markers may provide useful adjunctive information in fracture risk assessment. However, these findings require validation in larger, prospective studies before routine clinical application. 1
