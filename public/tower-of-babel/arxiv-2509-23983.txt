Stationary densities and delocalized domain walls in asymmetric exclusion processes competing for finite pools of resources
Sourav Pal, Parna Roy, Abhik Basu

Abstract
We explore the stationary densities and domain walls in the steady states of a pair of asymmetric exclusion processes (TASEPs) antiparallelly coupled to two particle reservoirs without any spatial extent by using the model in Haldar et al, Phys. Rev. E 111 , 014154 (2025). We show that the model admits delocalized domain walls which exist for some choices of the model parameters that define the effective entry and exit rates into the TASEP lanes. Surprisingly, in the parameter space spanned by these model parameters, the delocalized domain walls covers an extended region. The corresponding phase diagrams in the plane of the control parameters have different topology from those for an open TASEP or other models with multiple TASEPs connected to two reservoirs.



I
INTRODUCTION

Driven diffusive systems
driv1
;
driv2
;
driv3
;
driv4
can show highly nontrivial
properties even with purely local dynamics in even one dimension (1D). A paradigmatic
example is the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process
(TASEP)
krug-prl
;
derrida1
;
derrida2
;
derrida3
. It consists of a 1D lattice
with open boundaries. A TASEP’s dynamics are stochastic and subject to exclusion at all sites, meaning that it can only hold a single particle per site. A particle is entered at one of the boundaries at a given rate, hops unidirectionally to the subsequent sites at a rate of unity until it reaches the other end, and then leaves the system at another specified rate. The TASEP shows three different phases as a function of the entry and exit rates at the boundary: the low-density phase is given by steady-state densities in bulk that are less than half, the high-density phase by densities greater than half, or the maximal current phase with densities of just half.

The TASEP was first put forth as a minimal model to explain how proteins are synthesized in biological cells
macdonald
. Subsequently it was found to display boundary-induced phase transitions
krug-prl
;
derrida1
;
derrida2
. TASEP has been generalized to model various phenomena in biological and social systems, which has resulted in the identification of new phases and novel collective phenomena. For example, it has been found that traffic jams can be caused by the particle exchange between the environment and the TASEP lane
ef-lktasep-prl
;
ef-lktasep-pre
, which has been confirmed experimentally
traffic-exp
. Further investigation of these findings revealed that molecular motors belonging to the kinesin-8 family can govern the kinetics of microtubule depolymerization, which in turn leads to microtubule length regulation
melbinger1
;
melbinger2
. Furthermore, systems with two TASEP lanes coupled by infrequent particle-switching events have been found to exhibit complex phenomena, e.g., domain wall delocalization and traffic jams
reichenbach1
;
reichenbach2
;
reichenbach3
.

TASEP has also been investigated on simple networks, examining the requirements for the existence of the different phases and domain walls
net1
;
net2
;
net3
on different segments of the networks. These studies have been inspired by the dynamics of molecular motors traversing intricate networks of microtubules in real cells
net-exp
. In yet another generalization of TASEP, the interaction of particle number conservation with TASEP on an inhomogeneous ring has been explored and found to produce macroscopically nonuniform stable states of various kinds
def1
;
def2
;
sarkar-basu
;
banerjee-sarkar-basu
;
def5
;
def6
;
def7
;
def8
;
def9
;
def10
. Combining TASEP with the symmetric exclusion process (SEP) provides yet another intriguing generalization of TASEP. Both half-closed
half1
;
half2
and fully open systems
half3
have been used in this context. These studies have produced some noteworthy findings, including complex space-dependent stationary states in both TASEP and SEP lanes
half3
, self-organized temporal patterns
half2
, and tip localization of motors
half1
. In approaches complementary to Refs.
ef-lktasep-prl
;
ef-lktasep-pre
, a TASEP lane is considered to be connected to an embedded three-dimensional (3D) particle reservoir via particle exchanges in order to mimic the coupled system of molecular motors and microtubules. For instance, Refs.

;
Schadschneider

In this work, we study a distinct class of TASEP models that are very different from those that have been discussed above. These models place a limit on the total number of particles in the combined system by connecting the TASEP to a particle reservoir at both ends rather than having open boundaries. These models are inspired by the fact that
in the real world, limited amount of resources are available for use. For instance, finite availability of ribosomes in mRNA translocation for protein synthesis in eukaryotic cells
adams-schmittmann-zia
, or a finite number of vehicles in a closed network
ha-den
. This necessitates the extension of an unconstrained TASEP into systems that are composed of TASEP(s) connected to particle reservoir(s) with overall particle conserving dynamics. These models can also describe traffic dynamics, where a network of roads having traffic flow contains a limited fixed number of vehicles
traff1
; see Ref.
traff2
for similar studies. In these models, the primary effect of the finite availability of resources is that the effective entry rate of the particles to the TASEP, e.g., the actual protein synthesis taking place, sensitively depends upon the available resources. As the resources go up (reduce), protein synthesis rates increase (decrease). The main qualitative question in each of these studies is how the steady-state occupations and currents differ from open TASEPs due to the limited pool of accessible particles. From the standpoint of nonequilibrium statistical mechanics, these models serve as minimal models for “boundary-induced nonequilibrium phase transitions with a conservation law”. In the recent past, there have been several studies focusing on aspects of the stationary states in TASEPs with finite resources systems and are composed of single TASEP or more
ha-den
;
adams-schmittmann-zia
;
ciandrini
;
cook-dong-lafleur
connected to a particle reservoir. How the phase diagrams and stationary densities and domain walls are controlled by the finite resources is the major theme of these works. In all these previous studies, the effective entry rate depends on the instantaneous reservoir population, however the particle exit rates from the TASEP lane to the reservoir are considered a constant, independent of reservoir population. Moreover, only one reservoir is employed in all these studies. Considering reservoir population-dependent exit rates allow one to study the “reservoir crowding effect”, proposed and studied in Ref.
astik-parna
. The role and effects of distributed resources, as opposed to a single point reservoir, has also been explored in the recent past. These studies include research on how two reservoirs interact with two TASEPs
sourav-1
;
sourav-2
;
astik-erwin
and how reservoirs interact in a bidirectional system
arvind-bi
. See Ref.
seppa
for a related study on a TASEP with
K
-exclusion, which generalizes the conventional TASEP and allows for up to
K
particles at each site.

In this article, we explore the stationary densities and the domain walls in a model with two asymmetric exclusion processes coupled antiparallelly to two particle reservoirs. This was originally proposed and studied in Ref.
astik-erwin
. By considering a reduced parameter space distinct from that considered in Ref.
astik-erwin
, we show that the stationary densities in the two TASEP lanes are statistically identical, which can be spatially uniform or nonuniform. In the latter case, we find a pair of
delocalized domain walls
(DDW), one in each of the TASEP lanes, spanning completely or partially the lanes. The phase diagrams of the system in this reduced parameter space as functions of the control parameters are obtained, which are quite different from those found in Ref.
astik-erwin
, or for other models of TASEPs with finite resources. The most striking feature is that the phase space region in the plane of the control parameters where the DDWs are found is
extended
, as opposed to just a line found usually in other models of TASEP with or without open boundaries, or with finite resources; see, e.g.,
blythe
;
sarkar-basu
;
astik-parna
;
astik-erwin
among others. Apart from its theoretical novelty, this has profound phenomenological implications: it means that for the control parameters in an extended region of the phase space, the stationary densities actually have large fluctuations, since DDWs are nothing but
moving
density shocks. The remainder of this article is organized as follows: In Section
II
, we discuss the model that we study. Then in Section
III
, we outline the construction of the MFT for this model. Next in Section
IV
, we present the phase diagrams in the plane of the control parameters. Then in Section
V
, we use MFT to obtain the stationary densities in the different allowed phases and corroborate them by extensive MCS studies, and also discuss the absence of nonidentical stationary densities in the two TASEP lanes. Then in Sec.
VI
, we calculate the different phase boundaries, and the nature of the phase transitions in the model. We conclude by providing a short summary of our results and future prospects in Section
VII
. Additional results and some technical details are available in Appendix
A
and Appendix
B
for interested readers.

II
THE MODEL

We briefly revisit the construction and dynamics of the model proposed and studied in Ref.
astik-erwin
that we use for our work. The model is composed of two 1D lattices,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, of equal length exhibiting dynamical rules of TASEP which are connected to two reservoirs,
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, with finite particle-carrying capacity at their ends, see Fig.
1
for the schematic diagram. The TASEP lanes are of equal length
L
with thier sites labeled as
j=1,2,...,L
. Assumed to be point reservoirs without any spatial extent or internal dynamics,
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
respectively have
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
number of particles in them that are functions of time. The particles in the system interact with each other only through hard-core exclusion, meaning that each site on both TASEP lanes can be occupied by no more than one particle at a given time. Thus, each TASEP site has only two states: either empty or occupied. We follow the dynamics prescribed in Ref.
astik-erwin
. We briefly describe that below for the convenience of the reader.

(1)

At each time step, one site is randomly selected from each of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.

(2)

If the selected site is
j=1
and empty, a particle enters from

(
R_{2}
) into
T_{1}
(
T_{2}
) with rate
\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}
(
\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}
).

(3)

If the site is
j=L
and occupied, a particle exits from
T_{1}
(
T_{2}
) to
R_{2}
(
R_{1}
) with rate
\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}
(
\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}
).

(4)

The effective rates depend on the instantaneous reservoir populations
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
(Eqs. (
2
)-(
4
)).

(5)

If
1<j<L
, a particle hops to the next site
j+1
– rightward in
T_{1}
, leftward in
T_{2}
– at rate 1, if site

.

The total particle number in the system, including the reservoirs and the TASEP lanes, is denoted by

, which is strictly

where

is the occupation number of the

-th site of
i
-th lane with

As in Ref.

, the effective entry and exit rates of particles into and out of the TASEP lanes are parametrized by the parameters

(

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}f(N_{1}),\quad

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}g(N_{2}),

(2)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}f(N_{2}),\quad

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}

The functions

(4)

We define the filling factor

(5)

which serves as a measure of overall resource availability. In this model,

lies within the range

, corresponding to an empty (

), fully occupied (

To reduce the number of control parameters, we consider the symmetric case

III
MEAN-FIELD THEORY

In this section, we analyze the model at the mean-field
blythe
level, following the logic developed in Ref.
astik-erwin
.

Neglecting correlations in the mean-field theory (MFT), the time-averaged density
\rho_{j}^{(i)}\equiv\langle n_{j}^{(i)}\rangle
of bulk sites
1<j<L
of
i
-th lane evolve according to the following equations of motion:

\partial_{t}\rho_{j}^{(i)}=\rho_{j-1}^{(i)}(1-\rho_{j}^{(i)})-\rho_{j}^{(i)}(1-\rho_{j+1}^{(i)}),

(6)

with boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\rho_{1}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(i)},

(7)

\displaystyle\rho_{L}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}.

(8)

Eqs. (
6
), (
7
), and (
8
) are invariant under the following set of transformations (see Appendix
A
for details):

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\beta,

(9)

\displaystyle\rho_{j}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow

\displaystyle 1-\rho_{L-j+1}^{(i)},

(10)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow

\displaystyle 2-\mu.

(11)

To move toward a continuum description, we introduce a coarse-grained spatial variable
x=j\epsilon
, with lattice spacing
\epsilon=1/L
. In the limit
L\gg 1
,
x
becomes quasi-continuous, and the density
\rho^{(i)}(x,t)
is defined accordingly.

In the steady state, the mean-field particle current

J^{(i)}[\rho^{(i)}(x)]=\rho^{(i)}(x)(1-\rho^{(i)}(x))

becomes constant. Denoting the constant currents in lanes
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
as
J^{(1)}
and
J^{(2)}
respectively, the flux-balance-equation relating these currents to the reservoir occupation numbers
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
reads:

\frac{dN_{1}}{dt}=J^{(2)}-J^{(1)}=-\frac{dN_{2}}{dt}.

(12)

In the steady state, reservoir populations are time-independent and Eq. (
12
) implies

\displaystyle J^{(1)}=J^{(2)}=J,\quad\text{a constant current},

(13)

which leads to

\rho^{(1)}(1-\rho^{(1)})=\rho^{(2)}(1-\rho^{(2)}),

(14)

where
\rho^{(1)}
and
\rho^{(2)}
denote the steady-state densities on
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, respectively. Thus either
\rho^{(1)}=\rho^{(2)}
, or
\rho^{(1)}+\rho^{(2)}=1
.

Let us now identify the possible phases in the system. Solving the steady-state current-density relation

J=\rho(1-\rho),

(15)

for each TASEP lane yields two solutions for the density:

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-4J}\right)\coloneqq\rho_{\pm}.

(16)

Thus, the steady-state density in each lane can be spatially uniform in one of three possible phases: the low-density (LD) phase where
\rho=\rho_{-}<1/2
, the high-density (HD) phase where
\rho=\rho_{+}>1/2
, or the maximal current (MC) phase where
\rho=1/2
. Another possibility is a spatially nonuniform density profile that connects LD and HD domains, known as the domain wall (DW) phase or shock phase (SP).

Since the model comprises of two TASEP lanes, each capable of being in one of the four phases, one might expect
4^{2}=16
possible phase combinations. However, not all combinations are realized, being not permitted by the dynamics. It turns out that the
only
solutions for the stationary densities as admitted by the dynamics are those which give equal stationary densities in both the lanes:
\rho^{(1)}=\rho^{(2)}
in the bulk, giving LD-LD, HD-HD, MC-MC, and DW-DW phases. The unequal steady state solutions where
\rho^{(1)}\neq\rho^{(2)}
are absent as a consequence of the boundary conditions controlled by
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
and
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}
; see also Sec.
V.5
for more discussions.

;
chou-mallick-zia
;

and

V
STEADY STATE DENSITIES AND PHASE BOUNDARIES

In this section, we employ MFT to determine the steady-state bulk densities in the four possible phases of the model — LD-LD, HD-HD, MC-MC, and DW-DW — as well as the phase boundaries that separate them. We also provide a detailed explanation of why asymmetric phases such as LD-HD, LD-DW, HD-DW, and their particle-hole symmetric counterparts do not appear in our model.

V.1
LD-LD phase

In the LD-LD phase, the stationary densities in both TASEP lanes are equal:

\rho^{(1)}=\rho^{(2)}\equiv\rho_{\text{LD-LD}}<\frac{1}{2},

where
\rho^{(1)}
and
\rho^{(2)}
denote the bulk densities in
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, respectively. In the LD-LD phase,
\rho^{(1)}=\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}<1/2
and
\rho^{(2)}=\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}<1/2

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\alpha\frac{N_{1}}{L}=\alpha\frac{N_{2}}{L}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle N_{1}=N_{2}.

(17)

PNC relation in this phase reads:

\displaystyle N_{0}=N_{1}+N_{2}+L(\rho^{(1)}+\rho^{(2)})

(18)

Recalling

and using Eq. (
17

and
N_{2}

\frac{N_{1}}{L}=\frac{\mu}{1+\alpha}=\frac{N_{2}}{L}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}=\frac{\mu\alpha}{1+\alpha},

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}=\beta\bigg(1-\frac{\mu}{1+\alpha}\bigg).

)], indicating that both TASEP lanes are empty, as expected.
The dependence of
\rho_{\text{LD-LD}}
on
\alpha
is shown in Fig.
3
(a), where it can be observed that for large values of
\alpha
,
\rho_{\text{LD-LD}}
saturates to
\mu
.

At this point, we find the range of
\mu
that permits LD-LD phase in the phase diagram. Let
\mu=3/2-\epsilon
, with
0<\epsilon<3/2
. Substituting this into inequality (
20
) gives

\alpha>\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,

(26)

and inequality (
24
) becomes

\frac{1}{\alpha}>2(1-\epsilon).

(27)

For
0<\epsilon<1
, inequality (
27
) implies

\alpha<\frac{1}{2(1-\epsilon)},

(28)

which, when combined with (
26
), yields a consistent range for
\alpha
. For
1<\epsilon<3/2
, inequality (
27
) instead gives

\alpha>2(1-\epsilon),

(29)

which is again compatible with (
26
). Thus, for all
0<\epsilon<3/2
, i.e., for
0<\mu<3/2
, the inequalities (
20
), (
23
), and (
24
) defining the LD-LD phase region are simultaneously satisfied.
Now consider
\mu=3/2+\epsilon
, with
0<\epsilon<1/2
. Substituting this into (
20
) gives

\alpha>\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

(30)

while (
24
) translates

\alpha<\frac{1}{2(1+\epsilon)}.

(31)

However, since

\frac{1}{2(1+\epsilon)}<\frac{1}{2}<\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon

for
0<\epsilon<1/2
, the two inequalities (
30
) and (
31
) are incompatible. This contradiction shows that the LD-LD phase cannot exist for
3/2<\mu<2
.

This is consistent with the Monte Carlo simulation (MCS) results in Figs.
2
and
8
where the LD-LD phase appears for

0<\mu<\frac{3}{2},

(32)

and is
absent
for
3/2<\mu<2
.

V.2
HD-HD phase

This phase also corresponds to the solution
\rho^{(1)}=\rho^{(2)}\equiv\rho_{\text{HD-HD}}>1/2
. In the HD-HD phase, characterized by high densities in both TASEP lanes, the bulk densities are given by
\rho^{(1)}=1-\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}>1/2
and
\rho^{(2)}=1-\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}>1/2
.
Imposing
\rho^{(1)}=\rho^{(2)}
leads to

\displaystyle 1-\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=1-\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\implies\quad

\displaystyle 1-\beta\left(1-\frac{N_{2}}{L}\right)=1-\beta\left(1-\frac{N_{1}}{L}\right)

\displaystyle\implies\quad

\displaystyle N_{1}=N_{2}.

(33)

The PNC condition then determines
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
in terms of the control parameters
\beta
and
\mu
:

\frac{N_{1}}{L}=\frac{\mu-1+\beta}{1+\beta}=\frac{N_{2}}{L}.

(34)

Again,
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
scale linearly with the system size
L
, and depend linearly on the filling factor
\mu
. Since
0<N_{1},N_{2}\leq L
, Eq. (
34
) yields

\frac{2-\mu}{1+\beta}<1\quad\text{and}\quad\mu\leq 2.

(35)

Substituting Eq. (
34
) in Eqs. (
2
)-(
4
) one gets the effective entry and exit rates in the HD-HD phase:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}=\frac{\alpha(\mu-1+\beta)}{1+\beta},

(36)

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}=\frac{(2-\mu)\beta}{1+\beta}.

(37)

Analogous to an open TASEP, the effective rates in the HD-HD phase obtained in Eqs. (
36
) and (
37
) must satisfy
\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}<\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}

and
\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}<1/2
for
i=1,2
resulting in the following two inequalities:

\displaystyle\frac{2-\mu}{1+\beta}<\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},

(38)

\displaystyle\frac{(2-\mu)\beta}{1+\beta}<\frac{1}{2}.

(39)

Inequalities (
35
), (
38
), (
39
) together define the HD-HD region in the
\alpha
-
\beta
plane for a fixed
\mu
, see the HD region displayed in the phase diagrams of Figs.
2
and
8
.

The steady-state densities in both lanes in the HD-HD phase are obtained as

\rho_{\text{HD-HD}}=\frac{1-\beta+\mu\beta}{1+\beta}.

(40)

As expected,
\rho_{\text{HD-HD}}
is independent of the entry rate parameter
\alpha
and is governed solely by the exit dynamics. When
\mu=\mu_{\text{max}}=2
, we have
N_{1}=N_{2}=L
[from Eq. (
34
)], and the bulk density is
\rho_{\text{HD-HD}}=1
[from Eq. (
40
)], indicating that both TASEP lanes are fully occupied with
L
particles in each. Consequently, the system is completely filled with a total particle number
N_{0}=4L
.
Moreover, for large values of
\beta
, the density approaches
(\mu-1)
, consistent with the trend observed in Fig.
3
(b).

The range of
\mu
for HD-HD phase to exist is obtained from MFT as following. Assume
\mu=1/2+\epsilon
, where
0<\epsilon<3/2
. Substituting into inequality (
35
) yields:

\beta>\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,

(41)

while inequality (
39
) becomes:

\frac{1}{\beta}>2(1-\epsilon).

(42)

For
0<\epsilon<1
, inequality (
42
) implies:

\beta<\frac{1}{2(1-\epsilon)},

(43)

which, when combined with (
41
), provides a consistent range for
\beta
, confirming the feasibility of the HD-HD phase in this regime.
For
1<\epsilon<3/2
, inequality (
42
) instead implies:

\beta>2(1-\epsilon),

(44)

which also remains compatible with (
41
). Therefore, the inequalities (
35
), (
38
), and (
39
) are simultaneously satisfied for the full range
0<\epsilon<3/2
, i.e., for
1/2<\mu<2
, justifying the existence of the HD-HD phase in this regime.
We now show that the HD-HD phase does not occur for
0<\mu<1/2
. Let
\mu=1/2-\epsilon
, with
0<\epsilon<1/2
. Substituting this into inequality (
35
) gives:

\beta>\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

(45)

while (
39
) becomes

\beta<\frac{1}{2(1+\epsilon)}.

(46)

However, for
0<\epsilon<1/2
, we have:

\frac{1}{2(1+\epsilon)}<\frac{1}{2}<\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

so the inequalities (
45
) and (
46
) are mutually incompatible. This contradiction excludes the possibility of the HD-HD phase when
0<\mu<1/2
.

These analytical results are consistent with the MCS results presented in the phase diagrams of Fig.
2
and Fig.
8
where the HD-HD phase is found for

\frac{1}{2}<\mu<2,

(47)

and is
absent
for
0<\mu<\frac{1}{2}
.

V.3
MC-MC phase

In the MC-MC phase, both lanes maintain a density
\rho^{(1)}=\rho^{(2)}\equiv\rho_{\text{MC-MC}}=1/2
in the bulk, corresponding to the maximal current
J_{\text{max}}=1/4
. In analogy with an open TASEP,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\alpha\frac{N_{1}}{L}>\frac{1}{2},

(48)

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\beta\left(1-\frac{N_{2}}{L}\right)>\frac{1}{2},

(49)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}=\alpha\frac{N_{2}}{L}>\frac{1}{2},

(50)

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}=\beta\left(1-\frac{N_{1}}{L}\right)>\frac{1}{2},

(51)

in the MC-MC phase.
These imply

\frac{1}{2\alpha}<\frac{N_{i}}{L}<1-\frac{1}{2\beta},\quad i=1,2,

(52)

which leads to the constraint

\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\beta}<2\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\alpha+\beta<2\alpha\beta.

(53)

The PNC condition in the MC-MC phase reads

\displaystyle N_{0}=2\mu L=N_{1}+N_{2}+L(\rho^{(1)}+\rho^{(2)})

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle(2\mu-1)=\frac{N_{1}}{L}+\frac{N_{2}}{L}.

(54)

Combining the conditions (
48
) and (
50
) with Eq. (
54
) gives

\mu>\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right).

(55)

Similarly, combining conditions (
49
) and (
51
) with Eq. (
54
) gives

\mu<\frac{1}{2}\left(3-\frac{1}{\beta}\right).

(56)

Inequalities (
53
), (
55
), and (
56
) together define the MC-MC region in the
\alpha
–
\beta
phase diagram for a fixed
\mu
.

To find the range of
\mu
for MC-MC phase existence, consider first the case
\mu=1/2+\epsilon
, where
0<\epsilon<1
. Substituting into inequality (
55
) yields

\frac{1}{\alpha}<2\epsilon,

(57)

and inequality (
56
) becomes

\frac{1}{\beta}<2(1-\epsilon).

(58)

These conditions can be satisfied for appropriate values of
\alpha
and
\beta
, consistent with (
53
), confirming that the MC-MC phase is allowed when
1/2<\mu<3/2
.
Now, consider
\mu=1/2-\epsilon
with
0<\epsilon<1/2
. Then inequality (
55
) becomes:

\frac{1}{\alpha}<-2\epsilon,

(59)

which is impossible since
\alpha>0
and the right-hand side is negative. Hence, MC-MC cannot occur in this regime.
Similarly, for
\mu=3/2+\epsilon
with
0<\epsilon<1/2
, inequality (
56
) becomes:

\frac{1}{\beta}<-2\epsilon,

(60)

which again cannot be satisfied for
\beta>0
. This rules out the MC-MC phase for
\mu>3/2
.
Therefore, the MC-MC phase is restricted to the interval
1/2<\mu<3/2
, in full agreement with the MCS observations in Figs.
2
and
8
.

This result can also be understood from particle number conservation. In the MC-MC phase, each lane contains
L/2
particles, so the total number of particles in the lanes is
L
. Using the relation
N_{0}=2\mu L
, particle number conservation implies:

N_{1}+N_{2}=N_{0}-L=(2\mu-1)L.

To ensure
N_{1}+N_{2}>0
, it follows that
\mu>1/2
. Furthermore, since
N_{1},N_{2}\leq L
, the total number of particles in the system must satisfy
N_{0}\leq 3L
in the MC-MC phase, which in turn implies
\mu<3/2
.

V.4
DW-DW phase

We now investigate the nonuniform steady states in which domain walls are formed connecting the LD and HD phases. Assuming both the TASEP lanes to have one DW, for a particular choice of
\alpha
and
\beta
, let the DWs formed in
T_{i}
be located at
x_{w}^{(i)}
,
i=1,2
. In MFT, these DWs are given by:

\rho^{(i)}(x)=\rho_{\text{LD}}^{(i)}+\Theta(x-x_{w}^{(i)})(\rho_{\text{HD}}^{(i)}-\rho_{\text{LD}}^{(i)}),

(61)

where

\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function defined as
\Theta(x)=1(0)
for
x>(<)0
. Similar to an open TASEP, one has
\rho_{\text{LD}}^{(i)}=\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}
and
\rho_{\text{HD}}^{(i)}=1-\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}
in the DW-DW phase of the present model. Now, current conservation in the DW-DW phase implies
\rho_{\text{LD}}^{(i)}+\rho_{\text{HD}}^{(i)}=1
which, along with the solution
\rho^{(1)}=\rho^{(2)}
, infers
\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}
. This in turn yields:

\frac{N_{1}}{L}=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}=\frac{N_{2}}{L}

(62)

in the DW-DW phase. Thus, effective entry and exit rates [see Eqs. (
2
)-(
4
)] in both lanes are obtained as

\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}=\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}.

(63)

Consequently, the steady state bulk densities read

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{LD}}^{(i)}=\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}=1-\rho_{\text{HD}}^{(i)},

(64)

Analogous to the open TASEP, we must have
\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}<1/2
in the DW-DW phase of our model. Hence the condition

\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\beta}>2\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\alpha+\beta>2\alpha\beta.

(65)

must be met within the DW-DW region in
\alpha
-
\beta
phase diagram.

Next we determine the locations,
x_{w}^{(1)}
and
x_{w}^{(2)}
, of the DWs formed in
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. The PNC relation in the DW-DW phase translates:

2\mu L=N_{1}+N_{2}+L\int_{0}^{1}dx\big[\rho^{(1)}(x)+\rho^{(2)}(x)\big],

(66)

Plugging the expressions of
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
from Eq. (
62
) and densities from Eqs. (
61
) and (
64
) into (
66
), we obtain

2\left(\mu-1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}+\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\right)=(x_{w}^{(1)}+x_{w}^{(2)})\left(\frac{2\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}-1\right).

(67)

This equation cannot be solved to determine both
x_{w}^{(1)}
and
x_{w}^{(2)}
uniquely keeping the parameters
\alpha,\,\beta
and
\mu
fixed, thereupon, indicating the domain walls to be
delocalized
in space. Physically,
shifting a DW in any direction in one lane is compensated by an opposite shift of the DW in another lane, keeping PNC unaffected. Thus all such pairs of
(x_{w}^{(1)},x_{w}^{(2)})
give valid DW positions. The inhererent stochasticity of the dynamics ensures that eventually the system visits all such solutions, displaying only the envelop of all the DWs under long time averaging. Since each channel is uniform having unit hopping rate in the bulk, the probability of finding a DW in a given channel is a constant. This in turn gives that the envelop of such a DW as an inclined lines, i.e., a DDW profile, spanning either the full length of the each lane, or only partially. In case of a partially spanning DDW, the fraction spanned by a DDW in each of the lanes is equal. This is a consequence of PNC.
Thus in the DW-DW phase, the domain walls in both lanes become delocalized due to the freedom in their relative positions, constrained only by the total particle number conservation, see Eq. (
67
), see also Ref.
sm-inhomogeneous-tasep
.

One can find the DW height,
\mathcal{H}^{(i)}
, of lanes
T_{i}
,
i=1,2
in terms of the control parameters as:

\mathcal{H}^{(i)}=\rho_{\text{HD}}^{(i)}-\rho_{\text{LD}}^{(i)}=1-\frac{2\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}.

(68)

Note that
\mathcal{H}^{(i)}
is independent of
\mu
, although the positions of the DWs depend on
\mu
via Eq. (
67
). As
\mu
increases, additional particles accumulate in the HD regions, pushing the domain walls toward the entry ends.

To find the permissible range of
\mu
for the DW-DW phase, we evaluate Eq. (
67
) at the two extreme limits of the domain wall positions. First, consider the case where the domain walls are located at the exit end of both lanes, i.e.,
x_{w}^{(1)}=x_{w}^{(2)}=1
. In this configuration, the LD domains extend across the entire length of each lane. Conversely, when the domain walls are located at the entry end, i.e.,
x_{w}^{(1)}=x_{w}^{(2)}=0
, both lanes are entirely in the HD phase. Therefore,
0\leq(x_{w}^{(1)}+x_{w}^{(2)})\leq 2
depending on the location of the DWs. Substituting this into Eq. (
67
), we obtain the following condition on
\mu
in the DW-DW regime where
\alpha+\beta>2\alpha\beta
(see (
65
)):

\frac{\beta(1+\alpha)}{\alpha+\beta}<\mu<\bigg[1+\frac{\beta(1-\alpha)}{\alpha+\beta}\bigg].

(69)

Inequalities (
65
) and (
69
) together define the DW-DW region in the
\alpha
-
\beta
phase diagram for any
0\leq\mu\leq 2
. This concludes the DW-DW phase is always present in our model, see Figs.
2
(a)-
2
(d) and Figs.
8
(a),
8
(b).

We now examine whether the DDWs are completely or partially delocalized. The condition
\rho^{(1)}=\rho^{(2)}
along with PNC, implies that the time-averaged positions of both domain walls satisfy
\langle x_{w}^{(1)}\rangle=\langle x_{w}^{(2)}\rangle=x_{0}
in the steady state, where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes an average over steady-state realizations. In a closed system with biased hopping, particles tend to accumulate behind local inhomogeneities (in this case, the reservoirs). Assuming linear profiles for the DDWs, the average domain wall position
x_{0}
can be determined from PNC as:

\displaystyle 2\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{1}}{L}+\frac{N_{2}}{L}+\int_{0}^{x_{0}}\left(\rho_{\text{LD}}^{(1)}+\rho_{\text{LD}}^{(2)}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{x_{0}}^{1}\left(\rho_{\text{HD}}^{(1)}+\rho_{\text{HD}}^{(2)}\right)\,dx.

(70)

Substituting the expressions from Eqs. (
62
) and (
64
) into Eq. (
70
), we obtain the average wall position in terms of model parameters:

x_{0}=\frac{\alpha+2\beta-\alpha\beta-\mu(\alpha+\beta)}{\alpha+\beta-2\alpha\beta}.

(71)

Equation (
71
) shows, for fixed values of
\alpha
and
\beta
,
x_{0}
decreases with increasing
\mu
, indicating that the domain walls shift toward the entry-end of the respective lane.

. The geometric constructions of DDWs, illustrated in Fig.

V.5
Absence of the asymmetric phases

We now show that this model with
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\alpha
and
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\beta
does not admit any asymmetric phases such as LD-HD, LD-DW, or HD-DW and their particle-hole symmetric counterparts. We provide a detailed argument for the absence of these phases.

Absence of LD-HD and HD-LD phases:

Suppose an LD-HD phase exists where
T_{1}
is in the LD phase and
T_{2}
in the HD phase, with bulk densities
\rho^{(1)}=\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\alpha N_{1}/L
and
\rho^{(2)}=1-\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}=1-\beta(1-N_{1}/L)
. Current conservation implies:

\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}+\rho^{(2)}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\frac{N_{1}}{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}.

(72)

The corresponding value of
N_{2}
can be obtained using PNC and Eq. (
72
), giving

\frac{N_{2}}{L}=2\mu-1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}.

(73)

The effective entry and exit rates then read

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}=\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta},

(74)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(2\mu-1-\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\right),

(75)

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\beta\left(2-2\mu+\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\right).

(76)

Since
T_{1}
is in the LD phase, we must have
\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}<\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}
, leading to

\mu<\frac{\alpha+3\beta}{2(\alpha+\beta)}.

(77)

Similarly, for
T_{2}
to be in the HD phase, the condition
\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}<\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}
must hold, which implies

\mu>\frac{\alpha+3\beta}{2(\alpha+\beta)}.

(78)

The two conditions in Eqs. (
77
) and (
78
) cannot be simultaneously satisfied, thus ruling out the possibility of an LD-HD phase. A similar argument applies to discard the occurrence of the HD-LD phase, which is the particle-hole symmetric counterpart of LD-HD.

Absence of LD-DW, DW-LD, HD-DW, and DW-HD phases:

Let us start with the LD-DW phase, where
T_{1}
is in the LD phase and
T_{2}
in the DW phase. The bulk densities are given by:
\rho^{(1)}=\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\alpha N_{1}/L
and
\rho^{(2)}(x)=\rho^{(2)}_{\text{LD}}+\Theta(x-x_{w})(\rho^{(2)}_{\text{HD}}-\rho^{(2)}_{\text{LD}})
, where
\rho^{(2)}_{\text{LD}}
and
\rho^{(2)}_{\text{HD}}
are the LD and HD densities of
T_{2}
, and
x_{w}
denotes the domain wall location.

From current conservation, we must have:

\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\rho^{(2)}_{\text{LD}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow N_{1}

\displaystyle=N_{2}.

(79)

Moreover, the domain wall condition in
T_{2}
requires:

\displaystyle\rho^{(2)}_{\text{LD}}+\rho^{(2)}_{\text{HD}}=1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\alpha\frac{N_{2}}{L}

\displaystyle=\beta\left(1-\frac{N_{1}}{L}\right).

(80)

Using Eq. (
79
) in Eq. (
80
), we find:

\frac{N_{1}}{L}=\frac{N_{2}}{L}=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}.

(81)

Substituting this back into the expressions for the effective rates [using Eqs. (
2
)–(
4
)], we obtain:

\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}=\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta},\quad\text{for }i=1,2.

(82)

This leads to a contradiction. For
T_{1}
to be in the LD phase, the necessary condition is
\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}<\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}
, whereas for
T_{2}
to be in the DW phase, we require
\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}
. However, Eq. (
82
) shows that
\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}
, which implies that
T_{1}
must also be in the DW phase. Therefore, the LD-DW phase is not allowed within our model.

By the particle-hole symmetry, the DW-LD phase is also ruled out. A similar argument applies to the HD-DW and DW-HD phases, excluding them as well.

The phase diagrams of our model (Figs.

We identify the boundaries delineating different phases according to MFT. This can be achieved by equating the currents in corresponding phases.

As the system transitions from the LD-LD phase to the MC-MC phase, the bulk density gradually increases, reaching

\beta=\frac{1}{3-2\mu}.

We now discuss the nature of the phase transitions across these phase boundaries.
The

VII
CONCLUSION

In this work, we have investigated the stationary densities and domain walls in a model composed of two TASEP lanes connected antiparallelly to two particles reservoirs. This was originally proposed in Ref.
astik-erwin
as a model for TASEPs with distributed resources. Considering the same model but in a
different
reduced parameter space, where the entry rates in both the TASEP lanes are equal and parametrized by
\alpha
and the exit rates are in both lanes are also equal and parametrized by
\beta
. In addition, two functions
f
and
g
are defined, which control the effective entry and exit rates, respectively.
The exact quantitative results depend upon the forms of the functions
f
and
g
selected here, but even if
f
and
g
have different functional forms, the results will still be qualitatively similar as long as they are increasing and decreasing monotonically with their arguments and the resources are finite. To keep things simple, the functions
f
and
g
are connected in a straightforward way and are presumed to have simple forms. This guarantees that
f
increases monotonically with the reservoir population while
g
decreases monotonically. We study the models by employing mean-field theories, supplemented by extensive Monte Carlo simulations. We calculate the stationary densities and the phase diagrams in terms of
\alpha,\beta
and filling factor
\mu
.

In spite of the simplifying reduction of the model, it shows hitherto unknown and complex phase behavior. For instance, the two TASEP lanes have have the same phases — LD-LD, HD-HD, MC-MC and DW-DW; distinct phases in the two TASEP lanes are
not
possible, unlike in the work of Ref.
astik-erwin
. This forms a major conclusion from this work. Furthermore, the phase diagrams are very different from those reported in Ref.
astik-erwin
. Remarkably, in the DW-DW phase this model admits domain walls in
each
of the TASEP lane only in the form of a pair of delocalized domain walls (DDWs), and not a single localized domain wall (LDW). Rather interestingly, the phase space region occupied by the DDW solutions covers an extended region. This is in stark contrast to other known models of TASEP, whether with open boundaries, or closed geometries or finite resources such as the one studied in Ref.
astik-erwin
. Indeed, all such other models of TASEP, the phase space region for a single or more than one DDWs is inevitably a line in the plane of the control parameters. This is not just a theoretical novelty, it is strong phenomenological significance as well. For instance, in possible practical realizations of this model, the presence of a pair of DDWs in an extended region of the phase space means that for an extended range of the control parameters (here,
\alpha,\beta
), the stationary densities are actually strongly fluctuating even in the thermodynamic limit (see Figs.
4
and
5
), since the DDWs necessarily involve mass fluctuations that scale with the system size (and hence survive in the thermodynamic limit). Considering this as a simplified description for ribosome translation along messenger RNA strands in biological cells with a fixed number of ribosomes available, we expect for some ranges of the entry and exit rate parameters, there should be large fluctuations of the ribosome numbers along the messenger RNA strands.
We have further studied the phase transitions, all of which are continuous in nature, with the bulk density appearing as the order parameter. We have also calculated all of
the phase boundaries analytically within mean-field theory, which are in good agreement with the corresponding MCS results.
We have also identified the different threshold values of
\mu
at
which different phase transitions occur in this model (see Table
2
). We have also calculated
the occupations
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
of the two reservoirs, which are always equal, independent of the phases on the TASEP lanes. Thus the model in this particular reduced parameter space chosen here does not allow any population imbalances in different parts of a bulk system.

We now briefly compare and contrast our results with those in Ref.
astik-erwin
. While the two models are structurally same and both admit the particle-hole symmetry, the reduced parameter space chosen here is distinct from the one considered in Ref.
astik-erwin
. As a result, the phase diagrams in the present study are quite different from those reported in Ref.
astik-erwin
. The two TASEP lanes have statistically identical stationary densities here, unlike in Ref.
astik-erwin
, where distinct stationary densities on the two TASEP lanes are also possible. Related to this, the two reservoirs always have the same steady state occupations in the present study, ruling out any macroscopic population imbalances, which differs from Ref.
astik-erwin
. Furthermore, all the phase boundaries in this study correspond to continuous transitions, whereas discontinuous transitions were also found in Ref.
astik-erwin
. Lastly and as mentioned above, the present model admits a pair of DDWs over extended regions of the parameter space, whereas for model in Ref.
astik-erwin
, a pair of DDWs are possible only for the control parameters falling on lines in the phase space. On the other hand, there are no localized domain walls in the present model, which are found in Ref.
astik-erwin
for the control parameters in extended regions of the phase space.

This work can be extended in various ways. For instance, one can increase the number of TASEP lanes and number of reservoirs. Our above framework including our mean-field analysis can be easily extended to any
number of reservoirs and TASEP lanes with equal entry and exit rates (as here). Nonetheless, we expect the broad qualitative
features observed in our study to persist. For instance, it is reasonable to expect that all the channels are to be in their LD (HD) phases for very low (high) resources
\mu
. As
\mu
rises DW and MC phases should appear. It is expected that these DWs should all be delocalized for reasons similar to the above. One can also consider using reservoirs having functions defining the entry and exit rates that are different from the ones used here. These qualitative predictions can be confirmed by stochastic simulation studies. Furthermore, making the parameters
a,b
, which parametrize the effective entry and exit rates, fluctuate stochastically with the specified distributions would be still another intriguing extension. Our study here should serve as a catalyst for further investigation in these directions.

Appendix A
Particle-hole symmetry and phase diagrams for
1<\mu\leq 2

A particle-hole symmetry is embedded in the dynamics of our model due to the specific choice of the rate functions
f
and
g
[see Eq. (
4
)]. This symmetry operates as follows: the absence of a particle at a site corresponds to the presence of a hole, and the hopping of a particle in one direction is equivalent to the hopping of a hole in the opposite direction. Consequently, the entry of a particle into a TASEP lane corresponds to the exit of a hole from that lane at the same rate, and vice versa.

For clarity, we rewrite the equations of motion for the entry site (
j=1
), bulk sites (
1<j<L
), and exit site (
j=L
) of the TASEP lanes
T_{i}
, with
i=1,2
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{1}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}(1-\rho_{1}^{(i)})-\rho_{1}^{(i)}(1-\rho_{2}^{(i)}),

(91)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{j}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\rho_{j-1}^{(i)}(1-\rho_{j}^{(i)})-\rho_{j}^{(i)}(1-\rho_{j+1}^{(i)}),

(92)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{L}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\rho_{L-1}^{(i)}(1-\rho_{L}^{(i)})-\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(i)}\rho_{L}^{(i)}.

(93)

We now consider the following transformations:

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\beta_{i},

(94)

\displaystyle\rho_{j}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow 1-\rho_{L-j+1}^{(i)},

(95)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow 2-\mu.

(96)

Under transformation (
95
), the terms in the entry and exit equations [Eqs. (
91
) and (
93
), respectively] involving nearest-neighbor hopping are interchanged:

\rho_{1}^{(i)}(1-\rho_{2}^{(i)})\;\longleftrightarrow\;\rho_{L-1}^{(i)}(1-\rho_{L}^{(i)}),

and the site densities at the boundaries transform as

\rho_{1}^{(i)}\;\longleftrightarrow\;1-\rho_{L}^{(i)}.

Next, we analyze how the effective boundary rates transform. From particle number conservation and using the filling factor
\mu=N_{0}/2L
, we have:

2\mu=\frac{N_{1}}{L}+\frac{N_{2}}{L}+\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\rho_{j}^{(i)}.

(97)

Applying transformation (
95
), the density sum transforms as

\sum_{j=1}^{L}\rho_{j}^{(i)}\;\longleftrightarrow\;L-\sum_{j=1}^{L}\rho_{j}^{(i)},

and together with (
96
), the reservoir terms satisfy:

\frac{N_{1}}{L}+\frac{N_{2}}{L}\;\longleftrightarrow\;\left(1-\frac{N_{2}}{L}\right)+\left(1-\frac{N_{1}}{L}\right),

which implies the component-wise transformations:

\frac{N_{1}}{L}\;\longleftrightarrow\;1-\frac{N_{2}}{L},\qquad\frac{N_{2}}{L}\;\longleftrightarrow\;1-\frac{N_{1}}{L}.

Substituting into the expressions for the effective rates:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(1)}=\alpha_{1}\frac{N_{1}}{L}

\displaystyle\;\longleftrightarrow\;\beta_{1}\left(1-\frac{N_{2}}{L}\right)=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(1)},

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}=\alpha_{2}\frac{N_{2}}{L}

\displaystyle\;\longleftrightarrow\;\beta_{2}\left(1-\frac{N_{1}}{L}\right)=\beta_{\text{eff}}^{(2)}.

Thus, under the transformations (
94
)–(
96
), the entry-site equation (
91
) maps to the exit-site equation (
93
), demonstrating a symmetry between particle entry and hole exit. For bulk sites, Eq. (
92
) transforms into:

\partial_{t}\rho_{L-j+1}^{(i)}=\rho_{L-j}^{(i)}\left(1-\rho_{L-j+1}^{(i)}\right)-\rho_{L-j+1}^{(i)}\left(1-\rho_{L-j+2}^{(i)}\right),

confirming that the bulk dynamics are invariant under the particle-hole transformation.

\mu\leq 1
; see Fig.
2

1<\mu\leq 2
, which—by symmetry—can be inferred from the previously obtained phase diagrams corresponding to
\mu\leq 1
via the transformations (
94
)–(
96
). Figures

(a) (
\mu=9/8
),

\mu=11/8
), and

(c) (
\mu=13/8

(c) (

),

\mu=5/8
), and

(a) (
