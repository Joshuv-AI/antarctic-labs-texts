Normal forms of elliptic automorphic Lie algebras and Landau–Lifshitz type of equations
Sara Lombardo, Casper Oelen

Abstract
We present normal forms of elliptic automorphic Lie algebras with dihedral symmetry of order 4, which arise naturally in the context of Landau–Lifshitz type of equations. These normal forms provide a transparent description and allow a classification of such Lie algebras over ℂ {\mathbb{C}} . Using this perspective, we show that a Lie algebra introduced by Uglov, as well as the hidden symmetry algebra of the Landau–Lifshitz equation introduced by Holod, can each be realised as an elliptic 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 2 , ℂ ) \mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}) -current algebra. Furthermore, we realise the Wahlquist–Estabrook algebra of the Landau–Lifshitz equation in terms of elliptic automorphic Lie algebras. This construction reveals that, as a complex Lie algebra, it is isomorphic to the direct sum of an 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 2 , ℂ ) \mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}) -current algebra and the two-dimensional abelian Lie algebra ℂ 2 {\mathbb{C}}^{2} . Finally, we apply the automorphic Lie algebra framework to an n n -component generalisation of the Landau–Lifshitz equation due to Golubchik and Sokolov in the case n = 3 n=3 .



1
Introduction

Automorphic Lie algebras are Lie algebras of invariants that first emerged in the context of integrable systems. They originated in the study of algebraic reductions of Lax pairs by Lombardo and Mikhailov
[
31
,
32
]
, related to the notion of reduction groups, proposed by Mikhailov in
[
35
]
and
[
36
]
. While a precise definition will be given in Section
2
, for now the reader may think of automorphic Lie algebras as Lie algebras of meromorphic maps (usually with prescribed poles) from a compact Riemann surface
X
into a finite-dimensional Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
, which are equivariant with respect to a finite group
\Gamma
acting on
X
and on
{\mathfrak{g}}
, both by automorphisms. The group
\Gamma
plays the role of the reduction group in the original context of integrable systems.

While we emphasise here the equivariant (automorphic) property of these algebras, it is helpful to set them in the context of other infinite dimensional Lie algebras which are important in mathematical physics; automorphic Lie algebras are obtained by imposing a discrete group symmetry on a current algebra of Krichever–Novikov (KN) type. KN algebras
[
30
,
42
,
3
]
provide a general geometric framework for infinite-dimensional Lie algebras built from meromorphic data on curves, while automorphic Lie algebras are obtained by taking invariants of KN-type current algebras under finite reduction groups. They share the same geometric origin, almost-grading, and representation-theoretic features, but automorphic Lie algebras encode additional discrete symmetries. Although automorphic Lie algebras are sometimes described as ‘generalised Kac-Moody (KM) algebras’ (replacing the cyclic group of the KM algebra with the reduction group
\Gamma
), this is not accurate in Kac’s sense, in that KM algebras are defined by generalised Cartan matrices while automorphic Lie algebras are defined by invariant-theoretic data and root systems, Weyl groups, and reflection geometry are no longer intrinsic. In other words, automorphic Lie algebras generalise loop-type realisations, not Cartan-matrix theory.

Automorphic Lie algebras
where

X
has genus 0 have been extensively studied in the past two decades by Knibbeler, Lombardo and Sanders
[
33
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
. More recently, hyperbolic automorphic Lie algebras have been introduced by Knibbeler, Lombardo and Veselov
[
29
]
. The study of automorphic Lie algebras on genus 1 Riemann surfaces, also known as elliptic automorphic Lie algebras, has been initiated in a PhD thesis by Oelen
[
38
]
, which resulted in a classification by Knibbeler, Lombardo and Oelen in
[
24
]
. One of the main objectives of the theory is to obtain certain normal forms of these algebras, which make it possible to identify their Lie-isomorphism types. In this context, the recent work by Knibbeler
[
23
]
contains a uniform construction of normal forms in the case of genus 0.

Prior to the systematic study of elliptic automorphic Lie algebras, examples have appeared in the literature, particularly in the context of integrable systems
; this is not entirely surprising, given their relation to KN algebras illustrated above.
For example, Reiman and Semenov-Tyan-Shanskii
[
39

introduced Lie algebras of automorphic, meromorphic
\mathfrak{sl}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
-valued functions on a complex torus in relation to Lax equations with

[
51

studied the

[
43
]

(see Section
3

More recently, elliptic automorphic Lie algebras with

were investigated by Skrypnyk in
[
48

, in which various algebraic constructions related to these Lie algebras were carried out.

In the current paper, we present a new method for obtaining
certain normal forms
and for classifying automorphic Lie algebras that have appeared frequently in the context of integrable systems. Namely, those algebras with
{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
X
a complex torus, and symmetry group
\Gamma=D_{2}
, where
D_{2}
is the dihedral group of order 4 – also known as Klein’s four group. We shall assume throughout the current paper that
D_{2}
is embedded as translations in

. In Section
2
we describe this new method, which employs theta functions, and which has the benefit of being more transparent from an algebraic point of view, whilst having the potential of being generalisable to higher rank Lie algebras and different symmetry groups.
The normal forms show that the automorphic Lie algebras under consideration are KN-type current algebras on an elliptic curve.

We extend
D_{2}
-automorphic Lie algebras with one orbit of poles to those with finite unions of orbits of poles.
Moreover, we consider a broader class of automorphic Lie algebras with the same geometric and group-theoretic setup but with
{\mathfrak{g}}
an arbitrary complex reductive Lie algebra. Finally, we briefly touch upon the case of
{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{R}})

In Section

We also find a new basis of the hidden symmetry algebra of the Landau–Lifshitz equation by Holod

In this paper, we show that for

On curves of positive genus, constructing ZCRs presents a difficulty as a consequence of the Riemann–Roch theorem, cf.

One way around this difficulty is based on an ansatz of the form of the Lax pair related to Mikhailov’s reduction group. More precisely, one imposes a reduction on the Lax pair or ZCR by means of the action of a (finite) group, see also

2
Basic notions and constructions

In this section, we first briefly introduce the concept of automorphic Lie algebras after which we discuss the necessary functional aspects required for the construction of an intertwining operator. We apply this operator to obtain certain normal forms analogous to the Chevalley normal form of finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras, and establish isomorphisms between a class of automorphic Lie algebras and elliptic current algebras. Finally, we touch upon a generalisation of our constructions to real Lie algebras of invariants.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a complex finite-dimensional Lie algebra and let
X
be a compact Riemann surface. Let
\Gamma
be a finite group.
Suppose that
\sigma:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(X)
is an injective homomorphism, where
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
denotes the automorphism group of
X
(i.e., the group of biholomorphic maps
X\rightarrow X
). Assume that
\rho:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})
is a representation, where
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})
is the group of Lie algebra automorphisms of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\in X
, and let
\mathbb{X}=X\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\sigma(\Gamma)\cdot\{p_{i}\}
. Thus
\mathbb{X}
is the Riemann surface obtained from
X
by removing a finite union of
\Gamma
-orbits. We denote by
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}
the ring of regular functions on
\mathbb{X}
; that is, the ring of meromorphic functions on
X
that are holomorphic on
\mathbb{X}
. Let
\tilde{\sigma}
be the induced homomorphism on
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}
, defined by
\tilde{\sigma}(\gamma)f=f\circ\sigma(\gamma^{-1})
, for
f\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}
.

We consider the current algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}
, equipped with the Lie bracket

[A\otimes f,B\otimes g]=[A,B]\otimes fg,

for
A,B\in{\mathfrak{g}}
and
f,g\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}
, extended
{\mathbb{C}}
-linearly. The group
\Gamma
acts diagonally on
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}
via

\rho(\gamma)\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(\gamma)(A\otimes f)=\rho(\gamma)A\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(\gamma)f,

(4)

for all
A\in{\mathfrak{g}},f\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}
, and
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
The automorphic Lie algebra
({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}})^{\rho\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(\Gamma)}
is the fixed-point Lie subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}
with respect to the action of
\Gamma
as defined in (
4
):

\displaystyle({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}})^{\rho\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(\Gamma)}=\{a\in{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}:\rho(\gamma)\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(\gamma)a=a,\,\,\forall\gamma\in\Gamma\}.

Equivalently, we can view
({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}})^{\rho\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(\Gamma)}
as the Lie algebra of holomorphic maps
\varphi:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{g}}
, meromorphic at
X\setminus\mathbb{X}
, that are
\Gamma
-equivariant in the sense that

\varphi(\sigma(\gamma)z)=\rho(\gamma)\varphi(z)

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
z\in\mathbb{X}
, with the pointwise bracket inherited from
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

A classification of automorphic Lie algebras with
{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
X
a complex torus, and
\Gamma
embedding simultaneously in
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})
and
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
, such that
X
is punctured at precisely one orbit of
\Gamma
, can be found in
[
24
]
.
In this section, we will be concerned with the case where
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a finite-dimensional complex reductive Lie algebra and where
X
is a complex torus
{\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}
. The group
\Gamma
will be the dihedral group
D_{2}
of order 4. The full setup is described below.

Next, we construct certain normal forms of a class of automorphic Lie algebras and establish their Lie algebra isomorphism classes over
{\mathbb{C}}
. Let us first introduce the objects we will be working with. Let
X
be the complex torus
X={\mathbb{C}}/({\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\tau)
, with
\tau\in\mathbb{H}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\mathrm{Im}(z)>0\}

\mathbb{X}=X\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\sigma(D_{2})\cdot\{p_{i}\},

where
p_{i}\in X
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The group
D_{2}
acts on
X
, via the homomorphism
\sigma
, by translations over two distinct half periods of
X
. We consider representations
\rho:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
(
\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
is the group of inner automorphisms of

) which factor through
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})=GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})/\{k\mathrm{Id}:k\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\}
where
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
. The automorphic Lie algebras we consider are defined by

({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}})^{\rho\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}.

For simple

, we construct a normal form of these automorphic Lie algebras, showing that they are, in essence, the same as

, except that the base field

is replaced by a ring of automorphic functions. The normal form sheds light on a number of fundamental properties, and will allow us to determine the

-isomorphism classes of these Lie algebras. It will be obtained through the construction of a suitable

1&0\end{pmatrix}.

To define an automorphic Lie algebra, we need to choose a representation
\rho:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
and a homomorphism
\sigma:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(T)
. Let us first discuss representations of
D_{2}
on
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}).

Consider the Heisenberg group
\mathrm{He}_{n}
for
n=2
, which is a central extension of
D_{2}\cong C_{2}\times C_{2}
by
C_{2}
(
C_{2}
denotes the cyclic group of order 2). The relevance of the Heisenberg group will become clear below. It has order 8 and can be presented by

\displaystyle\mathrm{He}_{2}=\langle t_{1},t_{2},\epsilon:t_{1}^{2}=t_{2}^{2}=\epsilon^{2}=1,\,\,[t_{1},t_{2}]=\epsilon,\,\,[t_{1},\epsilon]=1,\,\,[t_{2},\epsilon]=1\rangle,

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the group commutator.
The group
\mathrm{He}_{2}
is isomorphic to the dihedral group
D_{4}
.

Consider the faithful, irreducible linear representation
\rho^{\prime}:\mathrm{He}_{2}\rightarrow GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
defined by

\rho^{\prime}(t_{1})=T_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho^{\prime}(t_{2})=T_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(19)

Note that
\rho^{\prime}(\epsilon)=\rho^{\prime}([t_{1},t_{2}])=-\mathrm{Id}
.
Each linear representation of
\mathrm{He}_{2}
gives rise to a projective representation
\mathrm{He}_{2}/\langle\epsilon\rangle\cong D_{2}\rightarrow PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.
We shall slightly abuse notation and use the symbols
t_{1},t_{2}
to generate the group
D_{2}
from now on. The representation
\rho^{\prime}
induces a faithful irreducible representation
\rho:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
via

\displaystyle\rho(t_{1})=\mathrm{Ad}(T_{1}),\quad\rho(t_{2})=\mathrm{Ad}(T_{2}),

(20)

where
\mathrm{Ad}:GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
is the adjoint map
\mathrm{Ad}(g)X=gXg^{-1}
. Note that
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\cong\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
.
The representation
\rho
is the unique faithful representation of
D_{2}
on
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, up to conjugation in
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
, since isomorphic finite subgroups of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
are conjugate; see, for example,
[
24
, Lemma 4.2]
.

Having specified the action of
D_{2}
on
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, we now consider the action of
D_{2}
on a complex torus
T={\mathbb{C}}/({\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\tau)
.
Define the homomorphism
\sigma:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(T)
by

\sigma(t_{1})z=z+\tfrac{1}{2},\quad\sigma(t_{2})z=z+\tfrac{\tau}{2}.

(21)

Thus,
D_{2}
acts on
T
by translations, and in particular this action is fixed-point free.

We now introduce a meromorphic matrix-valued map
\Omega
on
{\mathbb{C}}
, which will be used later in the construction of normal forms. Let

\displaystyle\Omega(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{-}(z)\theta_{2}(2z|2\tau)&\theta_{3}(2z|2\tau)\\
\psi_{+}(z)\theta_{3}(2z|2\tau)&\theta_{2}(2z|2\tau)\end{pmatrix},

(22)

where

\psi_{\pm}(z)=\pm\frac{\theta_{4}^{2}(0|\tau)}{\theta_{3}(0|\tau)}\frac{\theta_{3}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)}-\frac{\theta_{3}^{2}(0|\tau)}{\theta_{4}(0|\tau)}\frac{\theta_{4}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)}.

(23)

The functions
\psi_{\pm}
are meromorphic on
T
with simple poles precisely at
\{0,\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{\tau}{2},\tfrac{1+\tau}{2}\}
, and they transform under the
D_{2}
-action as

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm}(z+\tfrac{1}{2})=-\psi_{\pm}(z),\quad\psi_{-}(z+\tfrac{\tau}{2})=\psi_{+}(z).

The matrix
\Omega(z)
satisfies the following transformation rules:

\displaystyle\Omega(z+\tfrac{1}{2})

\displaystyle=\rho^{\prime}(t_{1})\Omega(z),

(24a)

\displaystyle\Omega(z+\tfrac{\tau}{2})

\displaystyle=e^{-\pi i\left(2z+\tfrac{\tau}{2}\right)}\rho^{\prime}(t_{2})\Omega(z),

(24b)

\displaystyle\Omega(-z)

\displaystyle=-\Omega(z)\rho^{\prime}(t_{1}).

(24c)

Lemma 2.1
.

The determinant of
\Omega(z)
is

\det\Omega(z)=\theta_{2}^{2}(0|\tau)\theta_{1}(2z|\tau).

Proof.

Using the theta function identities stated in this section, we compute

\displaystyle\det\Omega(z)

\displaystyle=\theta_{2}^{2}(2z|2\tau)\psi_{-}(z)-\theta_{3}^{2}(2z|2\tau)\psi_{+}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)}\Bigg[(\theta_{3}(2z|\tau)\theta_{3}(0|\tau)-\theta_{4}(2z|\tau)\theta_{4}(0|\tau))\left(-\frac{\theta_{4}^{2}(0|\tau)}{\theta_{3}(0|\tau)}\theta_{3}(2z|\tau)-\frac{\theta_{3}^{2}(0|\tau)}{\theta_{4}(0|\tau)}\theta_{4}(2z|\tau)\right)-

\displaystyle\quad(\theta_{3}(2z|\tau)\theta_{3}(0|\tau)+\theta_{4}(2z|\tau)\theta_{4}(0|\tau))\left(\frac{\theta_{4}^{2}(0|\tau)}{\theta_{3}(0|\tau)}\theta_{3}(2z|\tau)-\frac{\theta_{3}^{2}(0|\tau)}{\theta_{4}(0|\tau)}\theta_{4}(2z|\tau)\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)}\Bigg[2\theta_{3}^{2}(0|\tau)\theta_{4}^{2}(2z|\tau)-2\theta_{4}^{2}(0|\tau)\theta_{3}^{2}(2z|\tau)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{2}^{2}(0|\tau)\theta_{1}^{2}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)}

\displaystyle=\theta_{2}^{2}(0|\tau)\theta_{1}(2z|\tau),

where we have made use of (
16
) in the second-last step.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The matrix
\Omega(z)
first appeared, to the best of our knowledge, in
[
38
]
in a different form, and subsequently in
[
24
]
, where related equivariance properties were discussed. Interestingly,
\Omega(z)
is closely related to a matrix
g(u)
that appeared independently in
[

]
in the context of quantum integrable systems, more precisely in connection to the algebraic Bethe ansatz.
The matrices
\Omega(z)
and

coincide up to renaming

to

, multiplication by a scalar function (since

is not elliptic, whereas

is), and multiplication by a constant diagonal matrix.

Throughout the paper we assume that
D_{2}
is embedded in
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
via
\sigma
as defined in (
21
), and we usually write
D_{2}
instead of
\sigma(D_{2})
.

The map
\Omega
is holomorphic on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}
. The function
\tau\mapsto\theta_{2}(0|\tau)
is a nowhere vanishing (holomorphic) function on
\mathbb{H}
, which follows from
[
5
, Chapter V, §8, Corollary 3]
. Together with the fact that
\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)=0
precisely when

, Lemma
2.1
shows that, for every
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
, it is a holomorphic map
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}\rightarrow GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
which is meromorphic at
\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}
.

We now consider the ring of regular functions
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
on the punctured torus
T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
. We begin by studying the affine algebraic curves (
3
) for
\mathbb{K}={\mathbb{C}}
and in the case it is elliptic, that is, for
n=3
. The material treated here is well-known, but we include it for completeness; see for example
[
4
,
5
]
for related discussions.

2.1
Preliminary results on elliptic curves

Fix
(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
pairwise distinct, and consider the curve
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
which is defined by the vanishing of the polynomials
\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{3}^{2}-r_{3}+r_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}^{2}-\lambda_{3}^{2}-r_{3}+r_{2}
.
Its projective closure

\overline{E}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}:\begin{cases}\lambda_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{3}^{2}-(r_{3}-r_{1})\lambda_{0}^{2}=0,\\
\lambda_{3}^{2}-\lambda_{2}^{2}-(r_{2}-r_{3})\lambda_{0}^{2}=0,\end{cases}

in homogeneous coordinates
[\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{1}:\lambda_{2}:\lambda_{3}]\in\mathbb{P}^{3}({\mathbb{C}})
defines a smooth projective curve of genus 1, and hence a compact Riemann surface. We may identify it with the complex torus
T={\mathbb{C}}/({\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\tau)
for a suitable
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
determined by the parameters
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}
, as we will describe below.

Let
C_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
be the Weierstrass curve

C_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}:y^{2}=(x-r_{1})(x-r_{2})(x-r_{3})

(25)

in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
and denote by
\overline{C}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
the projective closure of
C_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
given by
y^{2}z=(x-r_{1}z)(x-r_{2}z)(x-r_{3}z)
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}({\mathbb{C}})
.
The curve
\overline{E}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
is a degree-4 unramified cover of
\overline{C}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
. This can be seen as follows. From the defining equations of
\overline{E}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
, we have

\lambda_{1}^{2}+r_{1}\lambda_{0}^{2}=\lambda_{2}^{2}+r_{2}\lambda_{0}^{2}=\lambda_{3}^{2}+r_{3}\lambda_{0}^{2},

so that the expression
\lambda_{0}(\lambda_{j}^{2}+r_{j}\lambda_{0}^{2})
is independent of
j
. Let

\tilde{x}=\lambda_{0}(\lambda_{j}^{2}+r_{j}\lambda_{0}^{2}),\quad\tilde{y}=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3},\quad\tilde{z}=\lambda_{0}^{3},

and consider the morphism

\pi:\overline{E}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}\rightarrow\overline{C}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}},\quad\pi([\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{1}:\lambda_{2}:\lambda_{3}])=[\tilde{x}:\tilde{y}:\tilde{z}],

(26)

cf.
[
44
]
. One can verify that we indeed have
[\tilde{x}:\tilde{y}:\tilde{z}]\in\overline{C}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
.

We let the group
D_{2}=\langle t_{1},t_{2}\rangle
act on
\overline{E}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
via

t_{1}\cdot[\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{1}:\lambda_{2}:\lambda_{3}]=[\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{1}:-\lambda_{2}:-\lambda_{3}],\quad t_{2}\cdot[\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{1}:\lambda_{2}:\lambda_{3}]=[\lambda_{0}:-\lambda_{1}:-\lambda_{2}:\lambda_{3}],

and note that this action is fixed-point free. The map
\pi
is constant on orbits of
D_{2}
, and each point of
\overline{C}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
has exactly four distinct pre-images. Hence
\pi
is a degree-4 unramified cover. It follows that the quotient curve satisfies
\overline{E}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}/D_{2}\cong\overline{C}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
, and the analogous statement for the corresponding affine curves holds as well.

We shall mostly work with the affine curve
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
(thus in the chart
\lambda_{0}\neq 0
). For later reference, we record that
D_{2}
acts via
\sigma
, interpreted as automorphism of
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
, on a point
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})\in E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
as

\sigma(t_{1})(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=(\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2},-\lambda_{3}),\quad\sigma(t_{2})(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=(-\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}).

(27)

Consider again the curve
C_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
defined in (
25
).
It is well known that for any pairwise distinct complex numbers
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}
, the curve
C_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
admits a uniformisation in terms of the Weierstrass-
\wp
function. We remind the reader that this is the meromorphic function
\wp_{{\Lambda}}
on
{\mathbb{C}}
which is periodic with respect to a lattice
{\Lambda}={\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\tau
and defined by

\wp_{{\Lambda}}(z)=\frac{1}{z^{2}}+\sum_{\omega\in{\Lambda}\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{1}{(z-\omega)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\right).

It has poles of order 2 precisely at the lattice
{\Lambda}
. Due to
{\Lambda}
-periodicity,
\wp_{{\Lambda}}
descends to a meromorphic function on
{\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}
. It satisfies

(\wp_{{\Lambda}}^{\prime})^{2}=4\wp_{{\Lambda}}^{3}-g_{2}({\Lambda})\wp_{{\Lambda}}-g_{3}({\Lambda})

(28)

where
\wp_{{\Lambda}}^{\prime}
denotes the derivative and
g_{2}({\Lambda}),g_{3}({\Lambda})\in{\mathbb{C}}
are the elliptic invariants. For later reference we mention that the elliptic invariants transform as

g_{2}(\alpha{\Lambda})=\alpha^{-4}g_{2}({\Lambda}),\quad g_{3}(\alpha{\Lambda})=\alpha^{-6}g_{3}({\Lambda})

(29)

under scaling of the lattice by
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
. It follows from the definition of
\wp_{{\Lambda}}
that

\wp_{\alpha{\Lambda}}(z)=\alpha^{-2}\wp_{{\Lambda}}(\alpha^{-1}z)

for any
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}.

The relation between the numbers
r_{i}
in the definition of
C_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
and the lattice
{\Lambda}={\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\tau
is given by

\lambda(\tau)=\frac{r_{3}-r_{2}}{r_{3}-r_{1}},

(30)

where
\lambda(\tau)=\frac{\theta_{2}^{4}(0|\tau)}{\theta_{3}^{4}(0|\tau)}
is the modular lambda function
[
5
, Chapter VII]
.

The right-hand side of (
28
) factors as
4(\wp_{{\Lambda}}-e_{1})(\wp_{{\Lambda}}-e_{2})(\wp_{{\Lambda}}-e_{3})
, where
e_{1}=\wp_{{\Lambda}}(1/2)
,
e_{2}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\wp_{{\Lambda}}((1+\tau)/2)}
and
e_{3}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\wp_{{\Lambda}}(\tau/2)}
. The numbers
e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}
satisfy

e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}=0,\quad e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}=\frac{g_{3}}{4},\quad e_{2}e_{3}+e_{3}e_{1}+e_{1}e_{2}=-\frac{g_{2}}{4}.

It follows that for distinct numbers
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
such that
a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}=0
, the curve
C_{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}}
can be uniformised by
x=\wp_{{\Lambda}}(z)
and
y=\frac{1}{2}\wp_{{\Lambda}}^{\prime}(z)
for a
\tau
related to the
a_{i}
via (
30
). Moreover, the sets of numbers coincide:
\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}=\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
.

The quantity
\lambda(\tau)=\frac{e_{3}-e_{2}}{e_{3}-e_{1}}
determines the elliptic curve
C_{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}}
up to isomorphism through the corresponding value of the
j
-invariant
[
5
, Chapter VII, §8]
:

j(\tau)=\frac{4}{27}\frac{(\lambda(\tau)^{2}-\lambda(\tau)+1)^{3}}{\lambda(\tau)^{2}(\lambda(\tau)-1)^{2}}.

In particular, two elliptic curves
C_{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}}
and
C_{e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime},e_{3}^{\prime}}
are isomorphic if and only if
j(\tau)=j(\tau^{\prime})
, where
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are chosen so that
\lambda(\tau)
and
\lambda(\tau^{\prime})
correspond to the triples
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
and
(e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime},e_{3}^{\prime})
, respectively. Two complex tori
T_{i}
with modular parameters
\tau_{i}
, for
i=1,2
, are isomorphic if and only if
[\tau_{1}]=[\tau_{2}],
where
[\tau]
denotes the
SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
-orbit of
\tau
,

[\tau]=SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})\cdot\tau=\left\{\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}:\,a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{Z}},\,ad-bc=1\right\}.

Here
A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
acts on
\tau
via
A\cdot\tau=\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}
, see for example
[
17
, Theorem 11.1.4]
.

Let us now describe how to uniformise the curve
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
.
Consider the following meromorphic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}
:

\mu_{i}(z)=\frac{\theta_{1}^{\prime}(0|\tau)}{\theta_{i+1}(0|\tau)}\frac{\theta_{i+1}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)},\quad i=1,2,3.

(31)

By the quasi-periodicity of the theta functions, each
\mu_{i}
descends to a meromorphic function on the torus
T={\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}
, holomorphic on
T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
, since
\theta_{1}(2z)
vanishes exactly on
\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}
. The poles are simple and for the residue at
z=0
(the coefficient of the
z^{-1}
term in the Laurent expansion of
\mu_{i}(z)
at
z=0
, denoted by
\mathrm{res}_{z=0}\mu_{i}(z)
), it holds that
\mathrm{res}_{z=0}\mu_{i}(z)=\frac{1}{2}
for
i=1,2,3
. It is well known (see
[
34
, Section 3.3]
) that

\wp_{{\Lambda}}(2z)=\mu_{i}(z)^{2}+e_{i},\quad i=1,2,3.

(32)

These identities, and the fact that the map
\pi
(
26
) induces a covering
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}\rightarrow C_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
, together with the uniformisation of
C_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
in terms of
\wp_{{\Lambda}}
, show that the functions
\mu_{i}
uniformise the curve
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
. Indeed, setting

b_{i}=r_{i}-\frac{1}{3}(r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}),\quad i=1,2,3,

we have
b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}=0
. Define the affine coordinate

x=\wp_{{\Lambda}}(2z)+\frac{1}{3}(r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}).

We may identify
e_{i}=b_{i}
for a suitable choice of
\tau
, so that (
32
) gives

\mu_{i}(z)^{2}=\wp_{{\Lambda}}(2z)-e_{i}=x-r_{i}.

Recall that the map
\pi
was defined using the coordinate
\tilde{x}=\lambda_{i}^{2}+r_{i}
(in the chart
\lambda_{0}=1
).
Hence we may take
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}(z)
for
i=1,2,3
, and choose a
\tau
such that (
30
) holds.

It follows that the
\mu_{i}
define a holomorphic embedding of the punctured complex torus
T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
into
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, via

z\mapsto(\mu_{1}(z),\mu_{2}(z),\mu_{3}(z)).

This embedding extends to a projective map
\phi:{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}({\mathbb{C}})
given by

\phi:z\mapsto\left[\frac{\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{1}^{\prime}(0|\tau)}:\frac{\theta_{2}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)}:\frac{\theta_{3}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{3}(0|\tau)}:\frac{\theta_{4}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{4}(0|\tau)}\right],

which is
{\Lambda}
-periodic by the quasi-periodicity of the theta functions and therefore descends to a holomorphic map
\tilde{\phi}:T\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}({\mathbb{C}})
. The image of
\tilde{\phi}
coincides with the smooth projective curve
\overline{E}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
so that
\tilde{\phi}
gives a complex analytic isomorphism
T\cong\overline{E}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
.

The curve
C_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
has exactly one point at infinity, namely
[x:y:z]=[0:1:0]
on
\overline{C}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
. The curve
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
has exactly four points at infinity, namely
[\lambda_{0}:\lambda_{1}:\lambda_{2}:\lambda_{3}]=[0:1:\pm 1:\pm 1]
on
\overline{E}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
.
It follows that
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}\cong\overline{E}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{\infty\}
, where
\infty=[0:1:1:1]
.
The points at infinity of
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
correspond to
z=0,\frac{1}{2},\frac{\tau}{2},\frac{1+\tau}{2}\in T
in the uniformisation by the
\mu_{i}
(
31
). Thus, we obtain a complex analytic isomorphism

E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}\cong T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}.

We now discuss some functional aspects. Denote by
{\mathbb{C}}[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}]
the ring of polynomials in the
\lambda_{i}
. Let
I_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}]
be the ideal generated by the polynomials
\lambda_{i}^{2}-\lambda_{j}^{2}-r_{j}+r_{i}
, where
i,j=1,2,3.
The coordinate ring of
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
, i.e., the ring of regular functions on
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
, denoted by
{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}]
, is

{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}]={\mathbb{C}}[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}]/I_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}.

Since the
\mu_{i}
uniformise
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
, analytically the ring
{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}]
can be realised as

\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}={\mathbb{C}}[\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}].

We will now study the action of
D_{2}
on
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{p\}}
, the
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra of meromorphic functions on
T={\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}
that are holomorphic outside
D_{2}\cdot\{p\}
, for some
p\in T
. Recall that
\gamma\in D_{2}
acts on
f\in\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{p\}}
as
\gamma\cdot f=f\circ\sigma(\gamma^{-1})
, where
\sigma
is defined in (
21
).
Let
\{\alpha_{00},\alpha_{01},\alpha_{10},\alpha_{11}\}
denote the set of characters of
D_{2}=C_{2}\times C_{2}
, defined by
\alpha_{ij}(t_{1},t_{2})=\chi_{i}(t_{1})\chi_{j}(t_{2})
, where
\chi_{0},\chi_{1}
are the characters of
C_{2}
. (Recall that these are defined as
\chi_{i}(r^{j})=(-1)^{ij}
for
i,j=0,1
and where
r
generates
C_{2}
.) The next lemma describes the isotypical components

\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{\alpha_{ij}}=\{f\in\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}:\gamma\cdot f=\alpha_{ij}(\gamma)f,\,\,\forall\gamma\in D_{2}\}

(without loss of generality, we take
p=0
) in terms of the functions
\mu_{i}
and the Weierstrass
\wp
-function associated to the lattice
{\Lambda}
.
In other words,
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{\alpha_{ij}}
is the (simultaneous)
\alpha_{ij}
-eigenspace for the action of
C_{2}\times C_{2}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The isotypical components of the action of
D_{2}
on
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{D_{2}}:=\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{\alpha_{00}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}],

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{\alpha_{10}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}]\mu_{3}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}]\mu_{1}\mu_{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{\alpha_{01}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}]\mu_{1}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}]\mu_{2}\mu_{3},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{\alpha_{11}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}]\mu_{2}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}]\mu_{1}\mu_{3}.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}={\mathbb{C}}[\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}]
, where the
\mu_{i}
are as defined in (
31
). Using (
5
), one verifies that
D_{2}
acts on the
\mu_{i}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(z+\tfrac{1}{2})

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}(z),

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(z+\tfrac{\tau}{2})

\displaystyle=-\mu_{1}(z),

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(z+\tfrac{1}{2})

\displaystyle=-\mu_{2}(z),

\displaystyle\mu_{2}(z+\tfrac{\tau}{2})

\displaystyle=-\mu_{2}(z),

\displaystyle\mu_{3}(z+\tfrac{1}{2})

\displaystyle=-\mu_{3}(z),

\displaystyle\mu_{3}(z+\tfrac{\tau}{2})

\displaystyle=\mu_{3}(z).

It follows that the right-hand sides of the equalities in the statement of the lemma are contained in the corresponding isotypical components. We shall prove the identities directly.

Introduce the divisor
D=(0)+(\tfrac{1}{2})+(\tfrac{\tau}{2})+(\tfrac{1+\tau}{2})
on
T={\mathbb{C}}/({\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\tau)
, and consider the vector subspace of the field
\mathcal{M}(T)
of meromorphic functions on
T
which have poles that are bounded by
D
:

L(D)=\{f\in\mathcal{M}(T):\mathrm{div}(f)+D\geq 0\},

where
\mathrm{div}(f)
is the divisor of the function
f
.
Let us write
\wp=\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}
. The functions
\mu_{i}^{2}
satisfy
\mu_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\wp-e_{i}
, for
i=1,2,3
by (
32
), and it holds that
\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}=-\frac{1}{16}\wp^{\prime}
. The latter equality can be deduced from the fact that
\wp^{\prime}\in L(3D)^{\alpha_{00}}
and using that
\wp^{\prime}
and the
\mu_{i}
are odd functions, together with the Laurent expansion of
\wp^{\prime}
and
\mu_{i}
about
z=0
.

The claim follows from the Riemann–Roch Theorem: for any divisor
D^{\prime}
on a complex torus with
\deg(D^{\prime})>0
, one has
\dim L(D^{\prime})=\deg(D^{\prime})

[
37
, Proposition V.3.14]
, where
\deg(D^{\prime})
denotes the degree of
D^{\prime}
. Using that the
\mu_{i}
are linearly independent over
{\mathbb{C}}
, we now show that for the divisor
D
defined above,
\dim L(nD)^{\alpha_{ij}}=n
for any
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}
and
i,j=0,1
.

Note that
L(D)={\mathbb{C}}\langle 1,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}\rangle
, i.e., the complex vector space spanned by
1
and the
\mu_{i}
. For any
n\geq 2
, the quotient vector space
L(nD)/L((n-1)D)
has dimension
4n-(4(n-1))=4
since
\deg(D)=4
. In each case, we can form products
p_{ij}
of the
\mu_{k}
such that

p_{ij}\in L(nD)^{\alpha_{ij}}
and
p_{ij}\notin L((n-1)D)^{\alpha_{ij}}
, for all choices of
i,j\in\{0,1\}
. More explicitly, for
k\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle L(2kD)

\displaystyle=L((2k-1)D)\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\langle\wp^{k},\wp^{k-1}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}:i,j=1,2,3\rangle,

\displaystyle L((2k+1)D)

\displaystyle=L(2kD)\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\langle\wp^{k-1}\wp^{\prime},\wp^{k}\mu_{i}:i=1,2,3\rangle.

From these decompositions, explicit bases for the isotypical components
L(nD)^{\alpha_{ij}}
can be obtained directly.
Finally, since
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{\alpha_{ij}}=\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}L(nD)^{\alpha_{ij}}
, the claim follows.
∎

2.2
Normal forms of automorphic Lie algebras

We are interested in finding normal forms of the automorphic Lie algebras

\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho):=({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S})^{\rho\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}

(33)

which are analogous to the Chevalley normal forms
of
complex simple Lie algebras. Recall that throughout the current paper, we assume that
D_{2}
is the group of translations over half-periods of
T={\mathbb{C}}/({\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\tau)
, as in (
21
), so we omit
\sigma
in the notation. We also assume that
S
is a nonempty finite subset of
T
and that the representation
\rho:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})
factors through
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.

Any complex simple Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
of rank
\ell
with root system
\Phi
has a basis, known as a Chevalley basis, given by
\{h_{i},a_{\alpha}:i=1,\ldots,\ell,\text{and }\alpha\in\Phi\}
such that the brackets are given by

\displaystyle[h_{i},h_{j}]

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle[h_{i},a_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\alpha(h_{i})a_{\alpha},

\displaystyle[a_{\alpha},a_{-\alpha}]

\displaystyle=h_{\alpha},

\displaystyle[a_{\alpha},a_{\beta}]

\displaystyle=\pm(r+1)a_{\alpha+\beta},\quad

\displaystyle\alpha+\beta\in\Phi,

\displaystyle[a_{\alpha},a_{\beta}]

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\alpha+\beta\notin\Phi\cup\{0\},

where
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
, and
r
is the greatest positive integer such that
\beta-r\alpha
is a root, and where
h_{\alpha}
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linear combination of the
h_{i}
(dual to
\frac{\alpha}{(\alpha,\alpha)}
), see
[
16
, Section 25]
.

Note that
\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
carries a natural module structure over
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}
, defined by

g\cdot\left(\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes f_{i}\right)=\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes(gf_{i}),

(34)

where
g\in\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}
and
\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes f_{i}\in\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
.
We will use the notation

\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}\langle B\rangle:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}b_{i}

to denote the free
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}
-module with basis
B=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\}
.

Definition 2.4

(Normal form of
\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
)
.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a finite-dimensional complex simple Lie algebra with Chevalley basis
\{h_{i},a_{\alpha}:i=1,\ldots,\ell,\text{and }\alpha\in\Phi\}
. A collection of elements
\{H_{i},A_{\alpha}:i=1,\ldots,\ell,\text{and }\alpha\in\Phi\}\subset\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
defines a normal form of
\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
if

\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)=\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}\left\langle\{H_{i},A_{\alpha}\}\right\rangle,

(35)

and the map

{\mathfrak{g}}\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho),\quad h_{i}\mapsto H_{i},\,\,a_{\alpha}\mapsto A_{\alpha}

is a Lie algebra embedding.
We call the right-hand side of (
35
) a normal form of
\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho).

The elements
H_{i},A_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
satisfy the same Lie bracket relations as the
h_{i},a_{\alpha}
. Thus a normal form of the
D_{2}
-automorphic Lie algebras (
33
) is directly analogous to a Chevalley basis of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

In general, it holds that
({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}})^{\Gamma}\not\cong{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{X}}^{\Gamma}
whenever there exists some
x\in\mathbb{X}
such that
\Gamma_{x}:=\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\gamma\cdot x=x\}
is nontrivial, assuming that
\Gamma_{x}
acts nontrivially on
{\mathfrak{g}}
, cf.
[
29
,
8
]
. In the present setup, the group
\Gamma=D_{2}
acts fixed-point freely on
T
, so
\Gamma_{x}=\{1\}
for all
x\in T
.

For
\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
to admit a normal form, it is necessary that
{\mathfrak{g}}
can be realised
D_{2}
-equivariantly inside
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}
. Our strategy for constructing normal forms is to exhibit such an embedding
\varphi:{\mathfrak{g}}\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
, where the matrix
\Omega(z)
defined in (
22
) plays a central role.

If a normal form exists, then
\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
is a free module of rank
\dim({\mathfrak{g}})
over the ring of invariants
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}
with basis
\{H_{i},A_{\alpha}\}
. Moreover, the induced map

\widehat{\varphi}:{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho),\quad\widehat{\varphi}(A\otimes f)=f\cdot\varphi(A)

is an isomorphism of Lie algebras and
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}
-modules.

Thus, the existence of a normal form is equivalent to realising
\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
as a current algebra over
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}
with underlying Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
. Normal forms provide a powerful tool for classifying
\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
up to
{\mathbb{C}}
-Lie algebra isomorphism, as we will see in Corollary
2.13
.

We aim to find normal forms of
\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{g},\tau,S,\rho)
, in the sense of Definition
2.4
, where
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})
and
\rho
is defined in (
20
); that is, a basis
(H,E,F)
over
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}
such that

\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S,\rho)=\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}\left\langle H,E,F\right\rangle\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}},

where the
H,E,F
satisfy the relations
[H,E]=2E
,
[H,F]=-2F
and
[E,F]=H
, and such that the bracket is linearly extended over
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}^{D_{2}}
. The techniques used to obtain this normal form can, under certain circumstances, be extended to more general base Lie algebras
{\mathfrak{g}}
. A normal form of
\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,\{0\},\rho)
was obtained in
[
24
]
using a different language.

The next lemma is the first step towards obtaining normal forms. It is a slight extension of Lemmas 6.17 and 6.18 in
[
24
]
, which construct a
D_{2}
-equivariant automorphism of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
. Our formulation uses a different approach: instead of the intertwiner built from square roots of elliptic functions in
[
24
]
, we work with the matrix
\Omega(z)
expressed in theta functions.

We consider the case of a (nonempty) finite union of orbits of punctures,

S=\bigcup_{i=0}^{n-1}D_{2}\cdot\{p_{i}\}=D_{2}\cdot\{p_{0},\ldots,p_{n-1}\}.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
p_{0}=0
by translating the set by
-p_{0}
.

Recall from Lemma
2.1
that
\Omega
is a holomorphic
GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-valued map on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}
, meromorphic at
\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}
. Precomposing the adjoint map
\mathrm{Ad}:GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
with
\Omega
yields a map

\omega:{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\tfrac{1}{2}{\Lambda}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})),\quad\omega(z)=\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega(z)).

Below we show that this map descends to a holomorphic map on
T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
, meromorphic at
D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
. We identify
\omega
with its descended map on
T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
and write simply
\omega
. We may also view
\omega=\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega)
as a map
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
.

The current algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}
carries a natural
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}
-module structure defined by
g\cdot(A\otimes f)=A\otimes(gf)
, where
f,g\in\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S}
and
A\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
. Define the induced endomorphism
\tilde{\omega}
on
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
by

\tilde{\omega}(A\otimes f)=f\cdot\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega)A.

Pointwise, this is given by
\tilde{\omega}(A\otimes f)(z)=f(z)\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega(z))A
for
z\in T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
. This map will serve as the intertwining operator discussed earlier.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and set
p_{0}=0\in T
. Let
S=\{p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1}\}\subset T
and
{\mathbb{T}}=T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S
. Then the endomorphism

\tilde{\omega}(A\otimes f)=f\cdot\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega)A

defines an
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}
-linear automorphism of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}
.
Moreover, the associated map
\omega(z)=\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega(z))
is a
D_{2}
-equivariant map on
T
in the sense that

\omega(\sigma(t)z)=\rho(t)\omega(z),\quad t\in D_{2},

where
z\in T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
, and
\rho
is defined in (
20
) and
\sigma
in (
21
). Consequently,
\omega
defines an embedding of Lie algebras
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S,\rho)
.

Proof.

We will first prove the case of a single orbit of punctures, and then extend to the general case of
n
orbits.

Using that
\rho(t_{i})=\mathrm{Ad}(T_{i})
together with the transformation rules (
24a
) and (
24b
), we obtain the
D_{2}
-equivariance

\omega(\sigma(t)z)=\rho(t)\omega(z),\quad t\in D_{2}.

(36)

In particular,
\omega(z+1)=\omega(z)
and
\omega(z+\tau)=\omega(z)
so that
\omega
descends to a well-defined map on
T
.

The map
\tilde{\omega}
is clearly
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
-linear. We define an
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
-linear endomorphism

\eta:\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}\rightarrow\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}},\quad\eta(A\otimes f):=f\cdot\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega^{-1})A.

One checks that for all
A\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
and
f\in\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
, we have

(\tilde{\omega}\circ\eta)(A\otimes f)=\tilde{\omega}(f\cdot\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega^{-1})A)=f\cdot\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega)(\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega^{-1})A)=A\otimes f,

and similarly
\eta\circ\tilde{\omega}=\mathrm{Id}
. So
\eta=\tilde{\omega}^{-1}
and hence
\tilde{\omega}
is an
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
-linear automorphism. This completes the proof in the one-orbit case.

Now let
S\subset T
be any finite set containing
0
, and set
{\mathbb{T}}=T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S
. Let
a=\sum_{i}A_{i}\otimes f_{i}\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}
. For
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{\omega}(a)(z)}=\sum_{i}f_{i}(z)\Omega(z)A_{i}\Omega(z)^{-1}.

Since
0\in S
, it follows that
\tilde{\omega}(a)
has no poles outside
D_{2}\cdot S
(because conjugating with
\Omega(z)
only introduces poles in
D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
). Hence
\tilde{\omega}
defines an automorphism of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}
, and the equivariance property (
36
) for the associated map
\omega
still holds.

The final claim follows from the equivariance property of
\omega
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

The operator
\tilde{\omega}
maps the subalgebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}^{\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}
onto the automorphic Lie algebra
\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S,\rho)
. In particular, it establishes an isomorphism of Lie algebras and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}^{\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}
-modules

\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}^{\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}\xrightarrow{\,\tilde{\omega}\,}\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S,\rho).

Letting
E:=\tilde{\omega}(e\otimes 1)
,
F:=\tilde{\omega}(f\otimes 1)
and
H:=\tilde{\omega}(h\otimes 1)
, we see that

E(\sigma(t)z)=\rho(t)E(z),\quad F(\sigma(t)z)=\rho(t)F(z),\quad H(\sigma(t)z)=\rho(t)H(z),\quad t\in D_{2},

and these satisfy the usual
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-relations

[H(z),E(z)]=2E(z),\quad[H(z),F(z)]=-2F(z),\quad[E(z),F(z)]=H(z),

for all
z\in T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
.

Thus, conjugation by
\Omega(z)
embeds
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

D_{2}
-equivariantly into the current algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}
.

Remark 2.7
.

The matrix
\Omega(z)
is not unique among matrices whose adjoint action satisfies (
36
). For example, for any choice of
a_{i},b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, we may replace
\Omega(z)
by

\tilde{\Omega}(z):=T_{1}^{a_{1}}T_{2}^{a_{2}}\Omega(z)T_{1}^{b_{1}}T_{2}^{b_{2}},

where
T_{1},T_{2}
are defined in (
19
). Since
T_{1}T_{2}=-T_{2}T_{1}
, the equivariance property of
\mathrm{Ad}(\tilde{\Omega})
(
36
) remains unchanged. This fact will be used later in this section.

We have seen that
\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega):{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
is a holomorphic map, meromorphic at
\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}
, which descends to a holomorphic map on
T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
. This map serves in the construction of an operator that intertwined the actions defined by
\sigma
and
\rho
as in (
21
) and (
20
), respectively.

2.3
Normal forms of
\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)

We now describe a procedure, inspired by
[
23
, Theorem 3.3]
, to generalise this construction and obtain a map
T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})
, where
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a complex simple Lie algebra. This procedure establishes an explicit isomorphism between
\mathfrak{A}({\mathfrak{g}},\tau,S,\rho)
, for suitably chosen data, and a current algebra, in a manner analogous to that described in Remark
2.6
.

Given such a
{\mathfrak{g}}
, one canonically associates to it the adjoint group
G_{\mathrm{ad}}
; see, for example,
[
7
, Section 1.1]
.
The group
G_{\mathrm{ad}}
may be defined as the unique connected Lie group with Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
such that the adjoint representation
\mathrm{Ad}:G_{\mathrm{ad}}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})
is faithful.
Since the kernel of
\mathrm{Ad}
is the centre
Z(G_{\mathrm{ad}})
, it follows that
Z(G_{\mathrm{ad}})
is trivial.

The group of inner automorphisms of
{\mathfrak{g}}
, denoted by
\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
, is defined to be
\mathrm{Ad}(G_{\mathrm{ad}})
.
It is known that
\mathrm{Ad}(G_{\mathrm{ad}})=\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})^{0}\cong G_{\mathrm{ad}}
, where
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})^{0}
denotes the connected component of
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})
containing the identity.

We now consider homomorphisms
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow G_{\mathrm{ad}}
. Note that
G_{\mathrm{ad}}
is a complex connected, centreless simple Lie group. The existence of an
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{g}}
is equivalent to the existence of a homomorphism
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\to{\mathfrak{g}}
, which integrates to a homomorphism
SL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow G_{\mathrm{ad}}
.
Homomorphisms
SL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\to G_{\mathrm{ad}}
, up to conjugation by
G_{\mathrm{ad}}
, are classified by nilpotent orbits in
{\mathfrak{g}}
, and hence by weighted Dynkin diagrams; see
[
7
, Section 3.5]
.

Such homomorphisms factor through
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
precisely when
-\mathrm{Id}\in SL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
is mapped to the identity in
G_{\mathrm{ad}}
, which holds precisely when the associated weighted Dynkin diagram has labels in
\{0,2\}
. Precomposing with
\mathrm{Ad}
yields a homomorphism
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
. In particular, this construction applies to any simple
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

A homomorphism
\overline{\rho}:PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
factors uniquely as
\overline{\rho}=\mathrm{Ad}\circ\Psi
, where
\Psi:PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow G_{\mathrm{ad}}
is a homomorphism, since
\mathrm{Ad}:G_{\mathrm{ad}}\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
is an isomorphism. We may summarise the idea of generalising
\Omega(z)
in the following way:

\Omega(z)\xmapsto{\Pi}[\Omega(z)]\xmapsto{\mathrm{Ad}\circ\Psi}\mathrm{Ad}(\Psi([\Omega(z)])),

where
\Pi:GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
is the canonical projection. Note that
z\mapsto[\Omega(z)]
defines a map on
T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
by (
24a
) and (
24b
).

The first step toward the explicit construction of a map
T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})
is to factorise the matrix
\Omega(z)
as

\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{-}(z)\theta_{2}(2z|2\tau)&\theta_{3}(2z|2\tau)\\
\psi_{+}(z)\theta_{3}(2z|2\tau)&\theta_{2}(2z|2\tau)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\theta_{3}(2z|2\tau)}{\theta_{2}(2z|2\tau)}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\frac{\psi_{+}(z)}{2\theta_{2}(0|\tau)}\frac{\theta_{2}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\det(\Omega(z))}{\theta_{2}(2z|2\tau)}&0\\
0&\theta_{2}(2z|2\tau)\end{pmatrix}.

This can be written as

\Omega(z)=\Delta(z)\exp\left(\frac{\theta_{3}(2z|2\tau)}{\theta_{2}(2z|2\tau)}e\right)\exp\left(\frac{\psi_{+}(z)}{2\theta_{2}(0|\tau)}\frac{\theta_{2}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)}f\right)\exp\left(\log\left(\frac{\Delta(z)}{\theta_{2}(2z|2\tau)}\right)h\right),

(37)

where
\Delta(z)=\sqrt{\det(\Omega(z))}
,
\psi_{+}
is a meromorphic function on
T
defined in (
23
), and
h,e,f
are the standard basis elements of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.

Suppose that
\Psi
is a homomorphism
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow G_{\mathrm{ad}}
, and let
\mathrm{d}\Psi:\mathfrak{pgl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow{\mathfrak{g}}
be the derivative at the identity of
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
. The exponential intertwines
\Psi
and
\mathrm{d}\Psi
so that
\Psi(\exp(A))=\exp(\mathrm{d}\Psi(A))
for all
A\in\mathfrak{pgl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
. We let

H=\mathrm{d}\Psi\left(\left[\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\right]\right),\quad E=\mathrm{d}\Psi\left(\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right]\right),\quad F=\mathrm{d}\Psi\left(\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\right]\right),

where
[A^{\prime}]
stands for the equivalence class of
A^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{gl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
in
\mathfrak{pgl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.
We use the factorisation of
\Omega(z)
(
37
) to obtain

\Psi([\Omega(z)])=\exp\left(\frac{\theta_{3}(2z|2\tau)}{\theta_{2}(2z|2\tau)}E\right)\exp\left(\frac{\psi_{+}(z)}{2\theta_{2}(0|\tau)}\frac{\theta_{2}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)}F\right)\exp\left(\log\left(\frac{\Delta(z)}{\theta_{2}(2z|2\tau)}\right)H\right),

(38)

and applying
\mathrm{Ad}
yields a map
T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})
.

The next result generalises Lemma
2.5
to complex reductive Lie algebras
{\mathfrak{g}}
. Recall that reductiveness means
{\mathfrak{g}}=[{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathfrak{g}}]\oplus Z({\mathfrak{g}})
, where
[{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathfrak{g}}]
is the semisimple derived subalgebra and
Z({\mathfrak{g}})
is the centre of
{\mathfrak{g}}
. For reductive
{\mathfrak{g}}
it need not hold that
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}})^{0}=\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
. For example, if
\mathfrak{a}
is abelian, then
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{a})^{0}=GL(\mathfrak{a})
and
G_{\mathrm{ad}}=\{1\}
, and thus
\mathrm{Inn}(\mathfrak{a})=\{\mathrm{Id}\}
, see
[
7
, Section 1.2]
. In our context of representations
D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
, this implies that
D_{2}
acts trivially on
Z({\mathfrak{g}})
(since
Z({\mathfrak{g}})
is abelian), and we may identify
\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})=\mathrm{Inn}([{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathfrak{g}}])\times\{\mathrm{Id}_{Z({\mathfrak{g}})}\}
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a finite-dimensional complex reductive Lie algebra and suppose that
\rho:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
is a representation that factors through a representation
\overline{\rho}:PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
. Let
\sigma:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(T)
be defined by
\sigma(t_{1})z=z+\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\sigma(t_{2})z=z+\tfrac{\tau}{2}
.
Then there exists an
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}})
such that

\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(\sigma(t)z)=\rho(t)\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z)

for all
t\in D_{2}
and
z\in{\mathbb{T}}=T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S
, where
S
is a nonempty, finite set containing
0
.

Proof.

Step 1:
{\mathfrak{g}}
simple.

Assume first that
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a finite-dimensional complex simple Lie algebra.
By assumption,
\rho
factors as
\rho=\overline{\rho}\circ\delta
, where
\overline{\rho}:PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
and
\delta:D_{2}\rightarrow PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
are homomorphisms. We may assume without loss of generality that
\delta
is injective.

Let

\tilde{\delta}:D_{2}\rightarrow PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
be defined by

\tilde{\delta}(t_{1})=\left[\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\right],\quad\tilde{\delta}(t_{2})=\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\right].

Recall that
\tilde{\delta}
is the unique injective homomorphism up to conjugation in
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
. Let
M\in PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
be such that
\delta(t)=M\tilde{\delta}(t)M^{-1}
for all
t\in D_{2}
. Then
\rho
is conjugate to
\overline{\rho}\circ\tilde{\delta}
in
\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
, i.e.,

\overline{\rho}(\tilde{\delta}(t))=\overline{\rho}(M)^{-1}\rho(t)\overline{\rho}(M).

Moreover,
\overline{\rho}=\mathrm{Ad}\circ\Psi
, for a unique homomorphism
\Psi:PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow G_{\mathrm{ad}}
, where
G_{\mathrm{ad}}
denotes the adjoint group associated with
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

The holomorphic map
\Omega^{\prime}:{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}\rightarrow PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
given by
\Omega^{\prime}(z)=[\Omega(z)]
descends to a holomorphic map on
T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
. We introduce
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}
, viewed as an
\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
-valued map on
{\mathbb{C}}
, by

\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z):=\overline{\rho}(M)\overline{\rho}(\Omega^{\prime}(z)).

By Lemma
2.5
,

\displaystyle\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(\sigma(t)z)

\displaystyle=\overline{\rho}(M)\overline{\rho}(\tilde{\delta}(t))\overline{\rho}(\Omega^{\prime}(z))

\displaystyle=\overline{\rho}(M)\overline{\rho}(M)^{-1}\rho(t)\overline{\rho}(M)\overline{\rho}(\Omega^{\prime}(z))

\displaystyle=\rho(t)\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z)

for all
t\in D_{2}
and
z\in T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
. The explicit form of
\overline{\rho}(\Omega^{\prime}(z))
is given by the adjoint map
\mathrm{Ad}
applied to (
38
) for the relevant
\Psi
.
By construction,
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z)
is an inner automorphism of
{\mathfrak{g}}
for every
z
in its domain of definition.

Note that
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}
plays an analogous role to
\omega=\mathrm{Ad}(\Omega)
in Lemma
2.5
. To avoid overloading the notation, we do not introduce the separate symbol
\tilde{\Omega}_{\overline{\rho}}
for the induced map on
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}
, but the construction of Lemma
2.5
should be kept in mind when interpreting
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}
.

We will show that
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}
preserves the location of the poles, i.e., that
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}
induces an automorphism of
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
. From this, it will follow that it is an automorphism of
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, let
\mathrm{Sym}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2}):=({\mathbb{C}}^{2})^{\otimes n}/S_{n}
where
S_{n}
is the symmetric group on
n
symbols which acts on
({\mathbb{C}}^{2})^{\otimes n}
by permutations. Let
\mathrm{Det}:GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
be the determinant representation, which is given by
\mathrm{Det}(g)=\det(g)
.
The finite-dimensional irreducible representations of
GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
are given by

V_{\lambda}=\mathrm{Sym}^{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}({\mathbb{C}}^{2})\otimes\mathrm{Det}^{\lambda_{2}},\quad\text{where}\quad\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},\,\,\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2},

cf.
[
11
, Proposition 15.47]
. Among these, those that factor through
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
correspond to
\lambda_{1}=-\lambda_{2}=n
, i.e.,
V_{(n,-n)}
.
Concretely,
V_{\lambda}
may be identified with the space of homogeneous polynomials in
x,y
of degree
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
. Here,
g\in GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
acts on a polynomial
P(x,y)
as
g\cdot P(x,y)=P(g^{-1}x,g^{-1}y)
, where we identify
x
with
(1,0)^{T}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
and
y
with
(0,1)^{T}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Thus the representations that factor through
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
correspond to spaces of even degree homogeneous polynomials.

To show that, for fixed
x\in{\mathfrak{g}}
, the map
z\mapsto\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z)x
defines a
{\mathfrak{g}}
-valued meromorphic map on
{\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}
, we use two facts. First, by Lemma
2.1
,
\det\Omega
vanishes exactly on
\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}
. Second, the matrix entries of
\Omega
are meromorphic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}
whose possible poles lie in
\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}
. Since
V_{(n,-n)}=\mathrm{Sym}^{2n}(V)\otimes\mathrm{Det}^{-n}
and every finite-dimensional representation of
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
is completely reducible (hence in particular
\overline{\rho}
), it follows that
\overline{\rho}(\Omega^{\prime}(z))x=\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z)x
is meromorphic on
{\mathbb{C}}
with at most poles only in the same set, namely those in
\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}
. By the equivariance properties we derived earlier in the proof, we have

\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z+1)=\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z),\quad\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z+\tau)=\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z),

so that we in fact have meromorphic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}
, holomorphic outside
D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
. Indeed, if
x\in{\mathfrak{g}}
and
X(z)=\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z)x
, then
X(z+1)=X(z)
and
X(z+\tau)=X(z),
for all
z\in T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
. Thus,
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}
preserves
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
, and it is an automorphism of
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}
by the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
2.5
.

Step 2:
{\mathfrak{g}}
semisimple.

Suppose that
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{g}}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}
, with each
{\mathfrak{g}}_{k}
simple. Since
\rho(D_{2})\subset\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
, the action preserves the simple summands. Thus,
\rho
decomposes correspondingly as
\rho=\rho_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\rho_{m}
, where
\rho_{k}:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}}_{k})
is a representation which factors through
\overline{\rho}_{k}:PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}}_{k})
. Define

\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}=\Omega_{\overline{\rho}_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus\Omega_{\overline{\rho}_{m}}.

It is clear that we still have
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}\in\mathrm{Aut}({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}})
, as well as the stated equivariance properties.

Step 3:
{\mathfrak{g}}
reductive.

Write
{\mathfrak{g}}=[{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathfrak{g}}]\oplus Z({\mathfrak{g}})
, where
Z({\mathfrak{g}})
is the centre of
{\mathfrak{g}}
. The semisimple part decomposes as
[{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathfrak{g}}]={\mathfrak{g}}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}
where all
{\mathfrak{g}}_{k}
are simple. Since
\rho
preserves this decomposition of
{\mathfrak{g}}
, it splits as
\rho=\rho_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\rho_{m}\oplus\rho_{Z({\mathfrak{g}})}
, where
\rho_{Z({\mathfrak{g}})}:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}(Z({\mathfrak{g}}))
is the trivial representation since
\mathrm{Inn}(Z({\mathfrak{g}}))=\{\mathrm{Id}_{Z({\mathfrak{g}})}\}
, where
\mathrm{Id}_{Z({\mathfrak{g}})}
is the identity map on
Z({\mathfrak{g}})
.
Define

\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}=\Omega_{\overline{\rho}_{1}}\oplus\dots\oplus\Omega_{\overline{\rho}_{m}}\oplus\mathrm{Id}_{Z({\mathfrak{g}})},

where
\mathrm{Id}_{Z({\mathfrak{g}})}:{\mathbb{T}}\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}(Z({\mathfrak{g}}))
is the identity map on
{\mathbb{T}}
. Then
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(\sigma(t)z)=\rho(t)\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(z)
.
Finally, it is clear that
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}
is an automorphism of
{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}
. This completes the proof.
∎

The ring of invariants of
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}
with respect to the action of
D_{2}
is given by
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{D_{2}}={\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}],

cf. Lemma
2.3
. Note that

{\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}]\cong{\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{{\Lambda}},\wp_{{\Lambda}}^{\prime}]\cong{\mathbb{C}}[x,y]/(y^{2}-4x^{3}+g_{2}({\Lambda})x+g_{3}({\Lambda}))=:R_{{\Lambda}}

as
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebras (recall the differential equation satisfied by
\wp_{{\Lambda}}
(
28
)). A direct way of seeing this, is by noting that
p(x,y)\mapsto p(\alpha^{2}x,\alpha^{3}y)
provides an isomorphism
R_{{\Lambda}}\rightarrow R_{\alpha{\Lambda}}
, making use of the modularity properties of
g_{2}
and
g_{3}
(
29
).

The next proposition describes the ring of invariants when we puncture
T
at the set
D_{2}\cdot S
, where
S\subset T
is a nonempty finite set. As before, we may assume without loss of generality that
p_{0}=0\in S
. The result states that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}^{D_{2}}
is generated over
{\mathbb{C}}
by
\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}
,
\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}
, and certain functions

on

, which account for the punctures other than those in
D_{2}\cdot\{0\}=\{0,\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{\tau}{2},\tfrac{1+\tau}{2}\}
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
{\mathbb{T}}=T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{p_{0}=0,p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1}\}
. The ring of invariants is given by

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}^{D_{2}}={\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime},\xi_{p_{1}},\dots,\xi_{p_{n-1}}],

where
\xi_{p}(z)=\mu_{1}(z)\mu_{1}(z-p)
and
\mu_{1}(z)=\frac{\theta_{1}^{\prime}(0|\tau)}{\theta_{2}(0|\tau)}\frac{\theta_{2}(2z|\tau)}{\theta_{1}(2z|\tau)}.
In other words,
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}^{D_{2}}
is obtained from
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{D_{2}}={\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}]
by adjoining
\xi_{p}
for every
p\in S\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Consider the divisor
D=\sum_{p\in D_{2}\cdot S}(p)
on
T
, where we may assume without loss of generality that
S=\{p_{0}=0,p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1}\}\subset T
is chosen so that
p_{i}\notin D_{2}\cdot\{p_{j}\}
for all
i\neq j
.
That is, the
p_{i}
lie in distinct
D_{2}
-orbits.
Recall that, by the Riemann–Roch theorem,
\dim L(D)=\deg(D)
. Here, the degree of
D
is
\deg(D)=\#(D_{2}\cdot S)=4n
.
Introduce for
p\in S\setminus\{0\}
the function
\xi_{p}
on
T
defined by

\xi_{p}(z)=\mu_{1}(z)\mu_{1}(z-p).

(39)

Note that
\xi_{p}
is
D_{2}
-invariant by Lemma
2.3
and hence defines a meromorphic function on
{\mathbb{C}}/\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}
with order 1 poles precisely in
\{0,p\}
.
Define
\xi_{0}:=1
.
Let
t_{q}:T\rightarrow T
be the translation
t_{q}(z)=z-q
, where
q\in T
.
Consider the set

J:=\bigcup_{p\in S}\left\{\xi_{p},\mu_{1}\circ t_{p},\mu_{2}\circ t_{p},\mu_{3}\circ t_{p}\right\}\subset L(D).

The functions in
J
are linearly independent over
{\mathbb{C}}
because the
\mu_{i}\circ t_{p}
lie in distinct isotypical components, and the pole sets
D_{2}\cdot\{p\}
are disjoint for
p\neq q
. Moreover, the functions
\xi_{p}
have poles only at
\{0,p\}
, ensuring linear independence. Therefore,
\dim{\mathbb{C}}\langle J\rangle\geq 4n
, where
{\mathbb{C}}\langle J\rangle
is the linear space spanned over
{\mathbb{C}}
by the set
J
.
Since
{\mathbb{C}}\langle J\rangle\subset L(D)
, we have
{\mathbb{C}}\langle J\rangle=L(D)
.

For
k\geq 1
, we have
\dim L((k+1)D)/L(kD)=4n(k+1)-4nk=4n
. For
k=1
, a basis of the quotient vector space is

\bigcup_{p\in S}\left\{\mu_{1}^{2}\circ t_{p},(\mu_{1}\mu_{2})\circ t_{p},(\mu_{1}\mu_{3})\circ t_{p},(\mu_{2}\mu_{3})\circ t_{p}\right\}+L(D).

It follows that

\displaystyle L(2D)

\displaystyle=L(D)\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\big\langle\bigcup_{p\in S}\left\{\mu_{1}^{2}\circ t_{p},(\mu_{1}\mu_{2})\circ t_{p},(\mu_{1}\mu_{3})\circ t_{p},(\mu_{2}\mu_{3})\circ t_{p}\right\}\big\rangle,

and inductively,

L(kD)=L((k-1)D)\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\big\langle\bigcup_{p\in S}\left\{\prod(\mu_{i_{1}}\mu_{i_{2}}\cdots\mu_{i_{k}})\circ t_{p}\right\}\big\rangle,

where the product is over
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{k}
(this can be made more explicit using the same idea as in the proof of Lemma
2.3
).

Next, we compute the invariant subspaces of
L(kD)
for all
k
.
Clearly,
L(0)^{D_{2}}=L(0)={\mathbb{C}}
, and it follows that
L(D)^{D_{2}}={\mathbb{C}}\langle\bigcup_{p\in S}\{\xi_{p}\}\rangle
since
S
has size
n
and the
\mu_{i}\circ t_{p}
account for
3n

{\mathbb{C}}
-linearly independent functions which are not invariant.
To obtain
L(2D)^{D_{2}}
and
L(3D)^{D_{2}}
, we use that
\mu_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}+c_{i}
for some
c_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}=-\frac{1}{16}\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}
(see the proof of Lemma
2.3
). We deduce

L(2D)^{D_{2}}=L(D)^{D_{2}}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\big\langle\bigcup_{p\in S}\{\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}\circ t_{p}\}\big\rangle,\quad L(3D)^{D_{2}}=L(2D)^{D_{2}}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\big\langle\bigcup_{p\in S}\{\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\circ t_{p}\}\big\rangle.

(40)

Inductively,

\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}L(kD)^{D_{2}}=\bigoplus_{p\in S\setminus\{0\}}{\mathbb{C}}\xi_{p}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\big[\bigcup_{p^{\prime}\in S}\{\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}\circ t_{p^{\prime}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\circ t_{p^{\prime}}\}\big].

Making use of (
40
), we infer

\xi_{p}^{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}\langle 1,\xi_{p},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}\circ t_{p},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}\rangle,\quad\xi_{p}^{3}\in{\mathbb{C}}\langle 1,\xi_{p},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}\circ t_{p},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\circ t_{p},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\rangle.

This implies that we can write
\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}\circ t_{p}
and
\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\circ t_{p}
in terms of algebraic expressions of
\xi_{p}
,
\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}
and
\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}
, and thus
{\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}\circ t_{p},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\circ t_{p}]\subset{\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime},\xi_{p}]
, for all
p\in S
.
We may conclude that

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}^{D_{2}}=\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}L(kD)^{D_{2}}={\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}][\xi_{p_{1}}]\ldots[\xi_{p_{n-1}}]={\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime},\xi_{p_{1}},\dots,\xi_{p_{n-1}}].

Finally, we observe that if
S=\{0\}
, we recover the case of one orbit of punctures:
\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{D_{2}}={\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}]
.
∎

The next result appears in Theorem 6
[

]
for
{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, but here it is extended to arbitrary finite unions of orbits of
D_{2}

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a finite-dimensional complex reductive Lie algebra. Let
\rho:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
be a representation that factors through a representation
\overline{\rho}:PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})\rightarrow\mathrm{Inn}({\mathfrak{g}})
and let
\sigma:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(T)
be a homomorphism that embeds
D_{2}
as translations of
T
. Let
S=\{p_{0}=0,p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1}\}
and
{\mathbb{T}}=T\setminus D_{2}\cdot S
. Then there is an isomorphism of Lie algebras and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}^{\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}
-modules:

({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}})^{\rho\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}\cong{\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime},\xi_{p_{1}},\ldots,\xi_{p_{n-1}}].

When
{\mathfrak{g}}
is simple, the automorphic Lie algebra
({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}})^{\rho\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}
has a normal form

({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}})^{\rho\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}^{\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}\big\langle\{\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(h_{i}\otimes 1),\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}(a_{\alpha}\otimes 1)\}\big\rangle,

where
\{h_{i},a_{\alpha}:i=1,\ldots,\ell,\text{ and }\alpha\in\Phi\}
is a Chevalley basis for
{\mathfrak{g}}
, and
\Omega_{\overline{\rho}}
is the intertwiner constructed in Proposition
2.8
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\sigma

Remark 2.11
.

The ring of regular functions on the punctured complex torus

, denoted by

, may be identified with the ring

Corollary 2.13

Suppose

We suppress the notation of

and

are associative, commutative algebras with unit and

is a complex nonabelian reductive Lie algebra. This result follows from the fact that

We set

3
Normal forms of Uglov’s Lie algebra and Holod’s Lie algebra

In this section, we discuss two infinite-dimensional Lie algebras that have appeared in the context of integrable systems. The first is the Lie algebra
\mathcal{E}_{k,\nu^{\pm}}
, introduced by Uglov in
[
51
]
, which has a known realisation in terms of elliptic automorphic Lie algebras.
The Lie structure can be conveniently written in terms of the elliptic
r
-matrix of Sklyanin. Although we will not focus on
r
-matrices in the present paper, we mention that
[
51
]
specialises a general construction of Cherednik
[
6
]
. Different approaches exist
[
14
]
, and were later unified by Skrypnyk in
[
45
]
. The second one is the well-known hidden symmetry algebra of the Landau–Lifshitz equation introduced by Holod in
[
15
]
. This Lie algebra appears frequently in the Adler–Kostant–Symes (AKS) scheme in the construction of integrable PDEs
[
44
,
49
]
.

Using the fact that
\mathcal{E}_{k,\nu^{\pm}}
can be realised as an elliptic automorphic Lie algebra, and using results from Section
2
, we obtain a new normal form of this algebra. This normal form reveals that
\mathcal{E}_{k,\nu^{\pm}}
is in fact an elliptic current algebra. More precisely, we show that there is a Lie algebra isomorphism
\mathcal{E}_{k,\nu^{\pm}}\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}R_{k,\nu^{\pm}}
, where
R_{k,\nu^{\pm}}
is a ring of functions on an elliptic curve with modulus
k
.
Furthermore, we show that Holod’s algebra can be interpreted as an elliptic automorphic Lie algebra with symmetry group
D_{2}
and with, generically, three orbits of punctures. We present a basis for Holod’s algebra which reveals it to be isomorphic to an elliptic current algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}R^{\prime}
, where
R^{\prime}
is a ring of functions defined on an elliptic curve.
To our knowledge, the identification of both Uglov’s and Holod’s algebra as elliptic current algebras has not previously been observed.

We begin revisiting the
D_{2}
-automorphic Lie algebra based on
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
with precisely one orbit of punctures as defined in Section
2
, namely

\mathfrak{A}(\tau,\{0\}):=\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,\{0\},\rho)=(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}})^{\rho\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})},

where

,

, and where
\rho
is defined in (
20
). By
[
24

(or Theorem
2.10
) we know that

\mathfrak{A}(\tau,\{0\})\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{{\Lambda}},\wp_{{\Lambda}}^{\prime}].

We now compute a set of three elements that generate

as a Lie algebra over

. That is,

Consider the following basis elements of

-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad v_{3}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathrm{i}\\

Introduce the elements

We now show that the automorphic Lie algebra

As a Lie algebra,

of
(1,2,3)

\mathfrak{A}={\mathbb{C}}\langle X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\rangle

We will need the following properties of the automorphic Lie algebras

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

be finite sets
such that no two distinct elements lie in the same

-orbit

i)

\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S)\subset\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S^{\prime})
.

ii)

If

, then
\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S)\cap\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S^{\prime})=\{0\}
.

iii)

\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S)=\bigoplus_{p\in S}\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,\{p\})

as a direct sum of Lie subalgebras.

Proof.

i)
This follows from the definition of
\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S)
.

ii)
Recall that
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})^{D_{2}}=\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})^{\alpha_{00}}=\{0\}
, see (
49
). In particular, any nonzero element
a\in\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S)
cannot be constant and must therefore have poles contained in

. Hence, if
S\cap S^{\prime}=\emptyset
,

cannot belong to
\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S^{\prime})
.

iii)
We note that by part

, we have

\bigoplus_{p\in S}\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,\{p\})\subset\mathfrak{A}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}),\tau,S).

The reverse inclusion follows from the relations

\mu_{i}(z)\mu_{j}(z-p)=c_{1}\mu_{k}(z)+c_{2}\mu_{k}(z-p),\quad p\notin D_{2}\cdot\{0\},

for certain
c_{1},c_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
depending on
i,j
, and where
(i,j,k)
is a permutation of
(1,2,3)
.
By translation invariance, it suffices to establish these relations in this form. They can be
proved by taking the divisor

D:=\sum_{\gamma\in D_{2}}(\gamma\cdot 0)+\sum_{\gamma\in D_{2}}(\gamma\cdot p)

on
T
. Since
\deg(D)=8
, it follows from Riemann–Roch that
\dim L(D)=8
. The space

is spanned by the eight functions
1,\mu_{1}(\mu_{1}\circ t_{p}),\mu_{i},\mu_{i}\circ t_{p}
, for
i=1,2,3
, where we recall that
t_{p}(z)=z-p
. Now, for

, the function
\mu_{i}(\mu_{j}\circ t_{p})
belongs to

, and by considering how it transforms under

and by comparing its poles, we see that it must be a
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear combination of
\mu_{k}
and
\mu_{k}\circ t_{p}
.

Hence, any element

can be decomposed as

, where

.
∎

-matrices

51

-orbits

Theorem

establishes that

\mathcal{H}_{\tau}=\mathfrak{A}(\tau,\{0,\pm z_{0}\})={\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}},\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime},\xi_{-z_{0}},\xi_{z_{0}}]\left\langle H,E,F\right\rangle.

The above description of
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}
may be simplified in the following way.
The divisor of
\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}/\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}

\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}^{\prime}}{\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}}\right)=-(-z_{0})-(z_{0})-(0)+(1/4)+(\tau/4)+((1+\tau)/4).

(57)

We may take a
{\mathbb{C}}

Consider the divisor

Case 2.

Remark 3.8
.

Both Lie algebras
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}
and
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathbb{C}}[\wp,\wp^{-1},\wp^{\prime}]
(with
\wp:=\wp_{\frac{1}{2}{\Lambda}}
) are almost-graded, but with different almost-gradings
[
45
]
. In particular, the isomorphism
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathbb{C}}[\wp,\wp^{-1},\wp^{\prime}]
obtained in Theorem
3.7
does not preserve the almost-graded structures.

The normal form of
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}
in Theorem
3.7
is given in terms of the modulus
\tau
, i.e., in terms of a uniformisation of the underlying elliptic curve
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
. We can also give a more intrinsic description of
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}
as follows. Recall that
\lambda=\lambda_{i}^{2}+A_{i}
with
A_{i}=r_{i}-\frac{1}{3}(r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3})
. Reformulating Theorem
3.7
, we obtain

\mathcal{H}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}={\mathbb{C}}[\lambda,\lambda^{-1},\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}]\left\langle\tilde{H},\tilde{E},\tilde{F}\right\rangle\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,R),

where
\tilde{H},\tilde{E},\tilde{F}
(expressed in terms of
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
) are defined in (
45
), and where

R={\mathbb{C}}[x,x^{-1},y]/\left(y^{2}-(x-A_{1})(x-A_{2})(x-A_{3})\right).

These generators satisfy the standard
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-relations.
The normal form of
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}
allows us to determine its
{\mathbb{C}}
-isomorphism classes, as stated in the next corollary.

Corollary 3.9
.

For the Holod algebra
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}
, we have
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\cong\mathcal{H}_{\tau^{\prime}}
as Lie algebras if and only if
[\tau]=[\tau^{\prime}]
.

Proof.

We will use Corollary
2.13
to prove the claim. Let
T={\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}
and
T^{\prime}={\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}^{\prime}
, where
{\Lambda}={\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\tau
and
{\Lambda}^{\prime}={\mathbb{Z}}+{\mathbb{Z}}\tau^{\prime}
for some
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}.
Recall that
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}=(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0,\pm z_{0}\}})^{\rho\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}.

The complex tori
T
and
T^{\prime}
are isomorphic

if and only if there exists
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
such that
{\Lambda}^{\prime}=\alpha{\Lambda}
. Equivalently,
[\tau]=[\tau^{\prime}]
.
Now, the points
\pm z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}
are the zeros of
\wp_{{\Lambda}}(2z)
if and only if
\pm\alpha z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}/\alpha{\Lambda}
are the zeros of
\wp_{\alpha{\Lambda}}(2z)
.
An isomorphism
{\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}\cong{\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}^{\prime}
is given by
\phi(z)=\alpha z
,
which maps the set of punctures
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S:=\{0,\pm z_{0}\}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}/{\Lambda}

to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}S^{\prime}:=\{0,\pm\alpha z_{0}\}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}/\alpha{\Lambda}
.
Consequently,
\phi(2S)=2S^{\prime}
.
Since
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}=\mathfrak{A}(\tau,S)
and
\mathcal{H}_{\tau^{\prime}}=\mathfrak{A}(\tau^{\prime},S^{\prime})
, we may apply Corollary
2.13
, which establishes that
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\cong\mathcal{H}_{\tau^{\prime}}
if and only if
[\tau]=[\tau^{\prime}]
.
∎

4
Multicomponent Landau–Lifshitz systems

In this section, we consider the multicomponent Landau–Lifshitz system introduced in
[
12
]
,

S_{t}=\left(S_{xx}+\frac{3}{2}\langle S_{x},S_{x}\rangle S\right)_{x}+\frac{3}{2}\langle S,RS\rangle S_{x},\quad\langle S,S\rangle=1,

where
R=\mathrm{diag}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})
and
S=(s^{1}(x,t),\ldots,s^{n}(x,t))^{T}\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
, with
n\geq 2
. We assume that the parameters
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\in\mathbb{K}
are pairwise distinct, i.e.,
r_{i}\neq r_{j}
for all
i\neq j
.

Although the system is defined more generally, we will focus on the case
\mathbb{K}={\mathbb{C}}
and
n=3
, for which the system coincides with the higher symmetry of third order for the fully anisotropic Landau–Lifshitz equation. We retain the general
n
notation in some places to indicate that the methodology can, in principle, be applied to general
n
.

We show that the zero-curvature representation (ZCR) for this equation found in
[
21
]
, admits a natural interpretation in terms of the automorphic Lie algebra

(\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}])^{D_{2}},

where
\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}
is the Lie algebra of the matrix Lie group
O(3,1)
, and
{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}]
is the coordinate ring of the elliptic curve
E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
. The action of
D_{2}
on
\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}]
will be specified below.

We show that the above automorphic Lie algebra is isomorphic, as a complex Lie algebra, to the direct sum of two copies of the current algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}}^{D_{2}}
. Furthermore, we shall make the connection between the WE (Wahlquist–Estabrook) algebra of the multicomponent Landau–Lifshitz system
(
1
)
for
n=3
and the above automorphic Lie algebra.

Finally, we show that the realisation of
\mathfrak{R}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}
as an automorphic Lie algebra (see Remark
3.4
) leads to a transparent description of the prolongation algebras of both the fully anisotropic Landau–Lifshitz equation and the non-singular Krichever–Novikov equation. In particular, it follows from this that both are isomorphic to the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{S}_{{\Lambda}}
defined as

\mathfrak{S}_{{\Lambda}}:=\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{{\Lambda}},\wp_{{\Lambda}}^{\prime}]\oplus{\mathbb{C}}^{2},

where the sum is a direct sum of Lie algebras and
{\Lambda}
is a suitable lattice.

The notation
{\mathbb{T}}
will be used throughout this section to denote a complex torus
T
punctured at a single orbit, namely
{\mathbb{T}}=T\setminus D_{2}\cdot\{0\}
. We largely follow the approach and notations of
[
21
]
.

Consider the real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}_{n,1}\subset\mathfrak{gl}_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})
, which is the Lie algebra of the matrix Lie group
O(n,1)
of linear transformations preserving the standard bilinear form of signature
(n,1)
. Explicitly,

\mathfrak{so}_{n,1}=\{X\in\mathrm{Mat}_{n+1}(\mathbb{R}):X^{T}I_{n,1}=-I_{n,1}X\},

where
I_{n,1}=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,\ldots,1,-1)\in GL_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})
and
(\cdot)^{T}
denotes matrix transpose. Denote by
E_{ij}
the matrix with a 1 in position
(i,j)
and zeros everywhere else. The following elements form a basis for
\mathfrak{so}_{n,1}
:

A_{ij}:=E_{ij}-E_{ji},\quad i<j\leq n,\quad B_{l,n+1}:=E_{l,n+1}+E_{n+1,l},\quad l=1,\ldots,n.

(58)

Recall the algebraic curve (
3
) in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
defined by

E_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}}:\lambda_{i}^{2}-\lambda_{j}^{2}=r_{j}-r_{i},\quad i,j=1,\ldots,n.

The genus of the (compactified) curve is given by
1+(n-3)2^{n-2}
; see
[
12
]
.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
viewed as a real Lie algebra, and
its complexification
(\mathfrak{so}_{3,1})_{{\mathbb{C}}}:=\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathbb{C}}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{so}(4,{\mathbb{C}})\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
as a complex Lie algebra. More generally, one has
(\mathfrak{so}_{n,1})_{{\mathbb{C}}}\cong\mathfrak{so}(n+1,{\mathbb{C}})
.

Consequently, viewing
\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}]
as a complex Lie algebra, we obtain a natural isomorphism

\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}]\cong\left(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}]\right)\oplus\left(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}]\right),

where the sum is a direct sum of complex Lie algebras.

Following
[
21
]
, we now introduce a
\mathfrak{so}_{n,1}
-valued ZCR for the multicomponent Landau–Lifshitz system (
1
). Let
D_{x}
and
D_{t}
denote the total derivative operators with respect to
x
and

corresponding to (
1
), respectively. The ZCR is given by

\displaystyle M(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(E_{i,n+1}+E_{n+1,i})\otimes s^{i}\lambda_{i},

(59a)

\displaystyle N(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=D_{x}^{2}(M)+[D_{x}(M),M]+\left(r_{1}+\lambda_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\langle S,RS\rangle+\frac{3}{2}\langle S_{x},S_{x}\rangle\right)M,

(59b)

\displaystyle D_{x}(N)-D_{t}(M)+[M,N]=0,

(59c)

where
S(x,t)=(s^{1}(x,t),\ldots,s^{n}(x,t))^{T}
,
\langle S,S\rangle=1
,
and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
are parameters satisfying (
3
).

The Lax matrices
M
and
N
in (
59
) can be interpreted as regular maps
E_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}}\rightarrow\mathfrak{so}_{n,1}
.
For
n=3
(the elliptic case), we construct an action of
D_{2}
on

\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{R}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}}\cong\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}]

and show that
M
and
N
may be interpreted as
D_{2}
-equivariant maps on the elliptic curve

.
That is, they are
D_{2}
-invariant matrices with respect to the action
\rho\otimes\sigma.
In this way, they naturally define elements of an elliptic automorphic Lie algebra.

We now describe the ingredients needed to make the automorphic Lie algebra framework available. Define a representation
\tilde{\rho}:D_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{so}_{3,1})

\tilde{\rho}(t_{1})=\mathrm{Ad}\begin{pmatrix}1&&&\\
&-1&&\\
&&-1&\\
&&&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\tilde{\rho}(t_{2})=\mathrm{Ad}\begin{pmatrix}-1&&&\\

&&1&\\

Recall the homomorphism

\sigma(t_{1})(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=(\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2},-\lambda_{3}),\quad\sigma(t_{2})(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=(-\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}),

We now show that the elements

Lemma 4.1

Let

For

We now relate this automorphic Lie algebra to the WE algebra of the multicomponent Landau–Lifshitz equation for
n=3
and focussing on

. In

\displaystyle=0,\quad i\neq j\neq k\neq i,

\displaystyle[p_{i},[p_{i},p_{k}]]-[p_{j},[p_{j},p_{k}]]

There is an isomorphism

To emphasise the dependence on the parameters

It follows from Proposition

3.4
that

{\mathfrak{g}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\cong\mathfrak{R}_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}\cong(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}})^{D_{2}}.

Moreover,
L(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\cong{\mathfrak{g}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})
according to
[

, Theorem 4]
.
By Theorem
2.10

we obtain

{\mathfrak{g}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathbb{C}}[x,y]/(y^{2}-4x^{3}+g_{2}({\Lambda})x+g_{3}({\Lambda})).

We now show that this implies an isomorphism of complex Lie algebras

(\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{R}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}})^{\tilde{\rho}\otimes\tilde{\sigma}(D_{2})}\cong{\mathfrak{g}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\oplus{\mathfrak{g}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}),

(62)

where the sum is a direct sum of Lie algebras.

Recall the basis elements of
\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}
defined in (
58
). Consider the following linear combinations of the
A_{ij}
and
B_{ij}
, which form a basis of
(\mathfrak{so}_{3,1})_{{\mathbb{C}}}
:

K_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(A_{jk}-\mathrm{i}B_{jk}),\quad L_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(A_{jk}+\mathrm{i}B_{jk}),

where
(i,j,k)
is a cyclic permutation of
(1,2,3)
. We complexify the representation
\tilde{\rho}
defined in (
60
), and we use the same notation for the result.

The Lie subalgebras
{\mathbb{C}}\langle K_{1},K_{2},K_{3}\rangle
and
{\mathbb{C}}\langle L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}\rangle
are
D_{2}
-submodules of
(\mathfrak{so}_{3,1})_{{\mathbb{C}}}
and satisfy

[K_{i},K_{j}]=\varepsilon_{ijk}K_{k},\quad[L_{i},L_{j}]=\varepsilon_{ijk}L_{k},\quad[K_{i},L_{j}]=0.

Thus each subalgebra is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, and there is a
D_{2}
-equivariant Lie algebra isomorphism
(\mathfrak{so}_{3,1})_{{\mathbb{C}}}\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, where the sum is of Lie algebras. This establishes (

We summarise these results as follows.

Proposition 4.2
.

There is an isomorphism of complex Lie algebras

(\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}])^{\tilde{\rho}\otimes\sigma(D_{2})}\cong{\mathfrak{g}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})\oplus{\mathfrak{g}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}),

where the sum is of Lie algebras.
Moreover, for

L(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})={\mathbb{C}}\langle Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}\rangle\subset(\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}\otimes_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathbb{C}}[E_{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}}])^{\tilde{\rho}\otimes\sigma(D_{2})},

The WE algebra of the Landau–Lifshitz equation is closely related to the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})

where

is the two-dimensional complex Lie algebra. This algebra is not usually realised as an automorphic Lie algebra with symmetry group

Similarly, the generalised prolongation algebra of the non-singular Krichever–Novikov equation, as described in

We summarise these observations in the following corollary.

Corollary 4.3
.

The prolongation algebra in the sense of Wahlquist–Estabrook of the Landau–Lifshitz equation are isomorphic, as Lie algebras, to

\mathfrak{S}_{{\Lambda}}=\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathbb{C}}[\wp_{{\Lambda}},\wp_{{\Lambda}}^{\prime}]\oplus{\mathbb{C}}^{2},

in the Landau–Lifshitz equation (

Remark 4.4
.

The Lie algebra generated by
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
is often denoted in the literature
[
40
,
21
]
as
\mathfrak{R}(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
or
\mathfrak{R}_{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}}
, where the

correspond to the

in the definition of

(

5
Conclusion and outlook

We have shown that the automorphic Lie algebras
({\mathfrak{g}}\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}})^{D_{2}}
based on a complex reductive Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
, are current algebras for any punctured complex torus
{\mathbb{T}}
(cf. Theorem
2.10
). In this construction, the group
D_{2}
acts by inner automorphisms on
{\mathfrak{g}}
(factoring through
PGL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
) and acts faithfully and fixed-point freely on
T
.

The special case of
{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
has notable applications in the theory of integrable systems. When there is a single
D_{2}
-orbit of punctures, the corresponding algebra arises in the context of the Wahlquist–Estabrook (WE) algebra of the Landau–Lifshitz equation and the prolongation algebra of the Krichever–Novikov equation. Two or three orbits of punctures correspond to Lie algebras introduced by Uglov and Holod, respectively, again in the context of integrable systems. In particular, the normal forms of these algebras show that they – originally defined either via generators and relations (Uglov) or a basis over
{\mathbb{C}}
(Holod) – are isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}(2,R)
for a suitable ring of elliptic functions
R
(cf. Theorems
3.5
and
3.7
).

We have explicitly implemented the automorphic Lie algebra framework in the context of an
n
-component generalisation of the Landau–Lifshitz equation by Golubchik and Sokolov (
1
) in the case
n=3
(Section
4
). In particular, we showed that the Lax matrices
M
and
N
(
59
) of this equation for
n=3
, also admit a natural interpretation in terms of an automorphic Lie algebra based on
\mathfrak{so}_{3,1}
. A natural question is whether this perspective extends to
n>3
: can the WE algebra of (
1
) be realised as an automorphic Lie algebra with base Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}_{n,1}
(or a different Lie algebra) and on a higher-genus algebraic curve?

The above WE algebra is closely related to a Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}(n)
(
61
). The realisation of
{\mathfrak{g}}(3)
as an automorphic Lie algebra reveals a transparent structure: it is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}[\wp,\wp^{\prime}])
. It would be interesting to explore whether analogous results hold for
{\mathfrak{g}}(n)
with
n>3
, and whether similar normal forms can be found. Specifically, it would be interesting to investigate whether the Lie algebras
{\mathfrak{g}}(n)
for
n>3
are also isomorphic to current algebras on higher-genus curves.
Realising general WE algebras as automorphic Lie algebras could provide a deeper understanding of their structural properties, including representation-theoretic aspects, and may offer new perspectives in the classification of integrable PDEs.

Finally, given that the automorphic Lie algebras
(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{T}})^{D_{2}}
, for different numbers of orbits of punctures, appear in (quantum) integrable systems, it is natural to ask whether other infinite-dimensional Lie algebras in this context also admit such a realisation. Specifically, one could ask whether they can be realised as a
D_{2}
-automorphic Lie algebra with additional punctures. This would provide new and transparent characterisations of these algebras.
