Supersymmetric BMS 4 Algebras Revisited: Electric/Magnetic Superalgebras and Free Field Realization
Yu-fan Zheng

Abstract
In this work, we present a systematic classification of supersymmetric extensions of the BMS 4 algebra and their realizations in free field theories. By requiring that supercharges admit finite-dimensional subsectors, we identify ten distinct electric super BMS 4 algebras and six magnetic ones. The electric case is characterized by supercharge anticommutators closing on supertranslations, while the magnetic case necessarily involves superrotations. To realize these algebras in free field theories, we follow a constructive procedure: first identify the modes bilinears of which generate the symmetry algebra, then determine the fields with appropriate transformation properties under the BMS 4 algebra, and finally construct consistent theories whose equations of motion admit the desired supersymmetry. Notably, R R -symmetry with nonvanishing spin is essential for the Type II-II, Type I-II, and Type II-I magnetic super BMS 4 algebras, shedding new light on spacetime structure of string theory for flat holography. Moreover, in the Type I-I theory, R R -symmetry relates electric and magnetic scalars, indicating their equal significance. In addition, R R -symmetry maps the electric scalar to a spin- 1 1 field and vice versa, offering a novel perspective on supersymmetric extensions of soft theorems.

In the context of supersymmetry, fermionic charges act as modes of supersymmetric currents. (2.22)

holomorphic (
2.9b

2.9b

\displaystyle\{L_{n},\bar{L}_{\bar{n}},M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}}\},\quad\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=\left(r-\frac{1}{2}s\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\bar{r}-\bar{s}\right)M_{r+s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

(2.23)

\displaystyle\{L_{n},\bar{L}_{\bar{n}},M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(1,\frac{3}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}}\},\quad\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(1,\frac{3}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=\left(r-\frac{1}{2}s\right)\frac{3}{2}\bar{r}M_{r+s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

(2.28)

All of the above algebras contain finite-dimensional subalgebras. However, except for the case (

\frac{3}{2}
or spin-
\frac{5}{2}
components. Such higher-spin fermionic generators are not compatible with the Haag–Łopuszański–Sohnius theorem, which allows only spin-
\frac{1}{2}
supercharges in a consistent extension of the Poincaré algebra. Therefore, the only physically admissible super Poincaré algebra is the finite-dimensional subalgebra of (

2.3
Magnetic chiral super BMS
4
algebras

In the magnetic case, it’s required the existence of supercharges
Q^{a}
such that
\{Q^{a},Q^{b}\}\propto L
. Since
L
is purely holomorphic, consistency demands that the supercharges anticommuting into
L
must also be purely holomorphic. Otherwise, the relation
\{Q^{a}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{b}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=G_{r+s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}}
would generate unphysical bosonic generators
L_{n,\bar{n}}
. An analogous argument applies to the anti-holomorphic sector. Therefore, it suffices to consider
\{Q^{a}_{r},Q^{b}_{r}\}\propto L
with
Q^{a}_{r}\in(l_{a},0)
and
Q^{b}_{r}\in(l_{b},0)
, together with
\{Q^{c}_{\bar{r}},Q^{d}_{\bar{r}}\}\propto\bar{L}
where
Q^{c}_{\bar{r}}\in(0,l_{c})
and
Q^{d}_{\bar{r}}\in(0,l_{d})
.

Assuming a single set of supercharges
Q
generates
L
, the only consistent choice is
Q\in\left(\frac{1}{2},0\right)
such that
\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}\}=L_{r+s}
. The Jacobi identity for
\{M,Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}\}
then implies a nonvanishing commutator
[M,Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}]
. Applying the rules of (
2.9
), one finds two distinct chiral magnetic BMS
4
algebras:

Type I

\{L_{n},\bar{L}_{\bar{n}},M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}}\}
:

[M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}]=\left(\frac{1}{2}r-\frac{1}{2}s\right)Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r+s,\bar{r}},\quad\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}\}=L_{r+s},\quad\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=M_{r+s,\bar{s}},

(2.29)

Type II

\{L_{n},\bar{L}_{\bar{n}},M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}}\}
:

[M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}]=Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{r+s,\bar{r}},\quad\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}\}=L_{r+s},\quad\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=-\left(r-\frac{1}{2}s\right)M_{r+s,\bar{s}},

(2.30)

In Type I,
[M,Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}]\propto Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}
satisfies (
2.9b
), whereas in Type II,
[M,Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}]\propto Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}
satisfies (
2.9a
). Unlike the electric case, the supercharges
Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r}
in (
2.29
) and (
2.30
) are not extended to
Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r,\bar{r}}
, thereby avoiding the introduction of nonphysical generators
L_{m,\bar{m}}
. In contrast, the holomorphic index
r
of
Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}}
in (
2.29
) is required for the nonvanishing commutator
[M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}]=Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{r+s,\bar{r}}
.

However, a mixture of the above two types does not yield a physically consistent superalgebra. Specifically, if one assumes that
\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}}\}
are the supercharges, the Jacobi identities for
\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}}\}
and for
\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)},Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}}\}
become mutually contradictory, unless one introduces an additional bosonic generator
\tilde{M}^{(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})}
. The resulting extended algebra takes the form:

\displaystyle\{L_{n},\bar{L}_{\bar{n}},M_{r,\bar{r}},\tilde{M}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}}\},

(2.31)

\displaystyle[M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}]=\frac{a}{2}(r-s)Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r+s,\bar{r}}+(1-a)Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{r+s,\bar{r}},

\displaystyle[\tilde{M}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}]=\frac{1-ab}{2c}(r-s)Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r+s,\bar{r}}+\frac{(a-1)b}{c}Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{r+s,\bar{r}}

\displaystyle\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}\}=L_{r+s},\quad\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=bM_{r+s,\bar{s}}+c\tilde{M}_{r+s,\bar{s}},

\displaystyle\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=\left(r-\frac{1}{2}s\right)\left(\frac{1-ab}{a-1}M_{r+s,\bar{s}}+\frac{ac}{a-1}\tilde{M}_{r+s,\bar{s}}\right).

where
a,b,c
are arbitrary constants. Importantly, the additional generator
\tilde{M}
lacks a clear physical interpretation. Therefore, such a mixed construction does not correspond to a physically meaningful symmetry.

Observing (
2.29
), we note that
Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}
and
Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}
are not related by conjugation. This makes it clear that both (
2.29
) and (
2.30
) describe chiral superalgebras, as only the holomorphic Virasoro algebra
L_{n}
appears in anticommutators of super charges. For this reason, (
2.29
) and (
2.30
) are considered as holomorphic in this paper. Anti-holomorphic counterparts
\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r,\bar{r}}\}
and
\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{r,\bar{r}}\}
can be defined through conjugation, yielding super BMS
4
algebra structures analogous to (
2.29
) and (
2.30
).

For a super BMS
4
algebra containing both holomorphic and anti-holomorphic sectors, simply including the corresponding supercharges is insufficient. For instance, consider holomorphic supercharges
\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}}\}
and anti-holomorphic supercharges
\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r,\bar{r}}\}
, both of Type I as in (
2.29
). The Jacobi identity for
\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}\}
is satisfied only if a bosonic generator
R^{(0,0)}
is introduced, such that

\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}\}\propto R^{(0,0)}.

(2.32)

Within the finite-dimensional subalgebra,
R=R^{(0,0)}_{0,0}
naturally functions as an
R
-symmetry. Consequently, the super BMS
4
algebra requires the presence of
R
-symmetry even when it is not an extended supersymmetry algebra. This motivates the construction of magnetic super BMS
4
algebras combining different types of supercharges, and we label the resulting algebras Type A-B for the holomorphic part being Type A and anti-holomorphic part being Type B. The resulting superalgebras are:

Type I-I

\{L_{n},\bar{L}_{\bar{n}},M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r,\bar{r}},R^{(0,0)}_{r,\bar{r}}\}
:

\displaystyle[M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}]=\left(\frac{1}{2}r-\frac{1}{2}s\right)Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r+s,\bar{r}},\quad[M_{r,\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{s}}]=\left(\frac{1}{2}\bar{r}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{s}\right)\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

(2.33)

\displaystyle\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}\}=L_{r+s},\quad\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=M_{r+s,\bar{s}},

\displaystyle\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{s}}\}=\bar{L}_{\bar{r}+\bar{s}},\quad\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=M_{s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

\displaystyle\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=\left(\frac{1}{2}r-\frac{1}{2}s\right)R^{(0,0)}_{r+s,\bar{s}},\quad\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=-\left(\frac{1}{2}\bar{r}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{s}\right)R^{(0,0)}_{s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

\displaystyle[R^{(0,0)}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}]=-\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r+s,\bar{s}},\quad[R^{(0,0)}_{r,\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{s}}]=Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

Type II-II

\{L_{n},\bar{L}_{\bar{n}},M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{r,\bar{r}},R^{(1,1)}_{r,\bar{r}}\}
:

\displaystyle[M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}]=Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{r+s,\bar{r}},\quad[M_{r,\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{s}}]=\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{r,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

(2.34)

\displaystyle\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}\}=L_{r+s},\quad\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=-\left(r-\frac{1}{2}s\right)M_{r+s,\bar{s}},

\displaystyle\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{s}}\}=\bar{L}_{\bar{r}+\bar{s}},\quad\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=-\left(\bar{r}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{s}\right)M_{s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

\displaystyle\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=R^{(1,1)}_{r+s,\bar{s}},\quad\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},Q^{(1,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=-R^{(1,1)}_{s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

\displaystyle[R^{(1,1)}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}]=\left(\frac{1}{2}r-s\right)\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{r+s,\bar{s}},\quad[R^{(1,1)}_{r,\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{s}}]=-\left(\frac{1}{2}\bar{r}-\bar{s}\right)Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}}.

Type I-II

\{L_{n},\bar{L}_{\bar{n}},M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r,\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{r,\bar{r}},R^{(0,1)}_{r,\bar{r}}\}

\displaystyle[M_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}]=\left(\frac{1}{2}r-\frac{1}{2}s\right)Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{r+s,\bar{r}},\quad[M_{r,\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{s}}]=\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{r,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

(2.35)

\displaystyle\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}\}=L_{r+s},\quad\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=M_{r+s,\bar{s}},

\displaystyle\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{s}}\}=\bar{L}_{\bar{r}+\bar{s}},\quad\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=-\left(\bar{r}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{s}\right)M_{s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

\displaystyle\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=\left(\frac{1}{2}r-\frac{1}{2}s\right)R^{(0,1)}_{r+s,\bar{s}},\quad\{\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{r}},Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{s}}\}=R^{(0,1)}_{s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

\displaystyle[R^{(0,1)}_{r,\bar{r}},Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}]=-\bar{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},1)}_{r+s,\bar{s}},\quad[R^{(0,1)}_{r,\bar{r}},\bar{Q}^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\bar{s}}]=\left(\frac{1}{2}\bar{r}-\bar{s}\right)Q^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

as well as Type II-I, which is the conjugate of Type I-II in (
2.35
). Owing to this conjugation relation, we will omit the discussion of Type II-I, as it follows analogously from the Type I-II case.

For each case, we introduce distinct
R
-symmetry charges. In particular,
R^{(0,1)}
,
R^{(1,0)}
, and
R^{(1,1)}
carry nonvanishing spin indices. This implies that, unlike the conventional
R
-symmetries which commute with spacetime symmetries, these
R
-symmetries transform covariantly under Lorentz rotations, distinguishing them from the usual extended super Poincaré algebras.

Finally, let us consider the case where two sets of
Q
s anticommute to
L
. To avoid introducing nonphysical
L_{n,\bar{n}}
generators, the anti-holomorphic spins of these
Q
s must also vanish. Additionally, the spin-statistics requirement restricts us to two possibilities:

\displaystyle\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},\tilde{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}\}=L_{r+s},

\displaystyle\text{satisfying \eqref{eq:TensorProductOfVirReprsSolution2}},

(2.36)

\displaystyle\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},\tilde{Q}^{(\frac{3}{2},0)}_{s}\}=\left(\frac{3}{2}r-\frac{1}{2}s\right)L_{r+s},

\displaystyle\text{satisfying \eqref{eq:TensorProductOfVirReprsSolution2}}.

The first case reduces to the extended super BMS
4
algebras of Type I (
2.29
), Type II (
2.30
), or their combinations, via appropriate rotations of
Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r}
and
\tilde{Q}^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{s}
. In the second case, an additional bosonic generator arising from
\{\tilde{Q}^{(\frac{3}{2},0)},\tilde{Q}^{(\frac{3}{2},0)}\}
would be required to satisfy the Jacobi identity of
\{Q^{(\frac{1}{2},0)}_{r},\tilde{Q}^{(\frac{3}{2},0)},\tilde{Q}^{(\frac{3}{2},0)}\}
. However, this generator lacks a well-defined physical motivation. Hence, no unextended super BMS
4
algebra exists with two sets of
Q
s anticommuting to
L
. The same argument applies to the anti-holomorphic sector.

To conclude, there are six unextended magnetic super BMS
4
algebras: chiral Type I and Type II, as well as nonchiral Type I-I, Type I-II, Type II-I, and Type II-II. In these algebras, the finite-dimensional subalgebra of Type I contains only spin-
\frac{1}{2}
supercharges. However, it violates the ansatz that the anticommutators of supercharges yield translations. Therefore, none of the magnetic superalgebras are connected to the super Poincaré algebra.

In this section, we present free field realizations of the various super BMS
4

3.1
Symmetry generators in terms of field modes

It is standard in QFT, particularly in 2D free CFTs and in BMS
3
and BMS
4
free theories, to express symmetry generators in terms of field modes
[
60
,
61
,
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
]
. The conventional approach to obtain the symmetry generators of a given free theory involves first computing the energy-momentum tensor in terms of the fields, then performing a Laurent or Fourier expansion of the fields according to their equations of motion (EOMs), and finally substituting this expansion to read off the generators. Here, since the symmetry algebra is already known, we adopt an inverse approach: we construct the symmetry generators using a specific ansatz for the field modes, and then build the corresponding field theory consistent with these mode expansions.

The expansion of fields is analogous to the expansion (
2.2
) of conserved currents. For example, a time independent field is expanded as

\phi(t,z,\bar{z})=\sum_{r,\bar{r}}\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}_{r,\bar{r}}z^{-r-h}\bar{z}^{-\bar{r}-\bar{h}}.

(3.1)

Consequently, the modes of free fields
\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}_{r,\bar{r}}
also fall into the charge representations discussed in Section
2.1
. For simplicity, we show only the holomorphic part:

[L_{m},\phi^{(h)}_{r}]=((h-1)m-r)\phi^{(h)}_{m+r},

(3.2)

Similarly, we label the representations by
l=h-1
. Assuming that bilinears of modes
\phi^{i}
form a set of charges
Q
in the
(l_{Q})
representation,

Q^{(l_{Q})}_{m}=\sum_{r}c^{l_{Q}}_{l_{i},l_{j}}(r,n)\phi^{i}_{r}\phi^{j}_{m-r},

(3.3)

the consistency condition from the commutator
[L_{m},Q]
imposes a constraint on the function
c
:

((l_{i}+1)m-r)c(r-m,t)+(l_{j}m-t+r)c(r,t)-(l_{Q}m-t)c(r,t+m)=0.

(3.4)

Note that this equation for
c
differs from the equation for
f
in (
2.8
). For constructing free theories, it suffices to consider the following solutions:

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{Q}}(r,n)=1,

\displaystyle l_{Q}=l_{i}+l_{j}+1,

(3.5a)

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{Q}}(r,n)=l_{Q}r-(l_{i}+1)n,

\displaystyle l_{Q}=l_{i}+l_{j}+2,

(3.5b)

Other solutions can be found in Appendix
A
.

In the free theory, another crucial point is the canonical quantization. Suppose
\varphi
and
\phi
are two sets of canonically conjugate modes. Their commutation relation is expected to take the form

[\varphi_{r},\phi_{s}\}=g^{l_{I}}_{l_{\varphi},l_{\phi}}(r,s)\delta_{r+s}I

(3.6)

where
\delta
denotes the Kronecker delta and
I
is the identity operator. In particular, the identity generator
I
is regarded as a charge in the representation with (anti-)holomorphic labels
l_{I}=\bar{l}_{I}=0
and (anti-)holomorphic indices
m_{I}=\bar{m}_{I}=0
. Indeed, if we formally extend
I
to
I_{m}
, we have

[L_{m},I_{n=0}]=(l_{I}*m-1*n)I_{m}=(0*m-1*0)I_{m}=0,

(3.7)

showing that additional generators
I_{m\neq 0}
do not enter the theory as symmetry generators. Therefore,
\varphi
,
\phi
, and
I
can all be viewed as charges in the theory, and equation (
3.6
) implies that
g(r,s)\delta_{r+s}
satisfies the constraint (
2.8
). The discrete solution relevant for this situation is

f^{l_{I}}_{l_{\varphi},l_{\phi}}(r,s)=\delta_{r+s},\quad l_{\varphi}+l_{\phi}=-1,\quad l_{I}=0.

(3.8)

As an example, consider the BMS
4
algebra. Suppose the theory contains bosonic modes
\varphi^{(-\frac{3}{4},-\frac{3}{4})}
and
\phi^{(-\frac{1}{4},-\frac{1}{4})}
, which are canonically conjugate to each other. Their commutation relation reads

[\varphi^{(-\frac{3}{4},-\frac{3}{4})}_{r,\bar{r}},\phi^{(-\frac{1}{4},-\frac{1}{4})}_{s,\bar{s}}]=\frac{i}{4\pi^{2}}\delta_{r+s}\delta_{\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

(3.9)

where the identity operator is implicit. Using these modes, we can construct the BMS
4
algebra as

\displaystyle L_{m}=-4\pi^{2}i\sum_{s,\bar{s}}\left(s-\frac{1}{4}m\right)\varphi_{s,\bar{s}}\phi_{m-s,-\bar{s}},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{\bar{m}}=-4\pi^{2}i\sum_{s,\bar{s}}\left(\bar{s}-\frac{1}{4}\bar{m}\right)\varphi_{s,\bar{s}}\phi_{-s,\bar{m}-\bar{s}},

\displaystyle M_{r,\bar{r}}=2\pi^{2}i\sum_{s,\bar{s}}\phi_{s,\bar{s}}\phi_{r-s,\bar{r}-\bar{s}}.

\displaystyle[L_{m},\phi^{(-\frac{1}{4},-\frac{1}{4})}_{s,\bar{s}}]=\left(-\frac{1}{4}m-s\right)\phi^{(-\frac{1}{4},-\frac{1}{4})}_{m+s,\bar{s}},

(3.11)

\displaystyle[\bar{L}_{\bar{m}},\varphi^{(-\frac{3}{4},-\frac{3}{4})}_{s,\bar{s}}]=\left(-\frac{3}{4}\bar{m}-\bar{s}\right)\varphi^{(-\frac{3}{4},-\frac{3}{4})}_{s,\bar{m}+\bar{s}},

\displaystyle[M_{r,\bar{r}},\varphi^{(-\frac{3}{4},-\frac{3}{4})}_{s,\bar{s}}]=\phi^{(-\frac{1}{4},-\frac{1}{4})}_{r+s,\bar{r}+\bar{s}},

\displaystyle[M_{r,\bar{r}},\phi^{(-\frac{1}{4},-\frac{1}{4})}_{s,\bar{s}}]=0.

2.9a
) and (
2.9b
) up to overall factors respectively. In this example, negative spin indices are allowed because the existence of finite-dimensional subsectors is not essential for field modes.

4
Discussion

In this paper, we conducted a systematic study of the supersymmetric extensions of the BMS
4
algebra. In Section
2
, we explored all possible electric and magnetic super BMS
4
algebras that admit finite-dimensional subalgebras. For the electric case, we imposed the condition that the anticommutator of supercharges contains only supertranslations
M
, whereas for the magnetic case, the inclusion of superrotations
L
and
\bar{L}
is required. Our analysis revealed ten distinct electric super BMS
4
algebras, two types of chiral magnetic super BMS
4
algebras, and four nonchiral magnetic superalgebras formed from combinations of two chiral superalgebras.

In Section
3
, we implemented an inverse construction to realize these superalgebras in free field theories. Notably, we identified a simple theory (
3.21
) that realizes (
2.19
), (
2.20
), and (
2.21
), consisting of one electric Carrollian scalar and two electric Carrollian spinors. For magnetic supersymmetry, there is a unique realization for each type. Although the of theory (
3.39
) resembles the electric theory (
3.21
), the conformal dimensions of the fermions differ, highlighting the distinct nature of magnetic constructions. The significance of magnetic Carrollian scalars is particularly evident in theory (
3.47
), while theory (
3.69
) plays a key role in the study of theories with bulk electromagnetic fields. Especially, part of (
3.69
) was studied in
[
21
]
as model of perturbative supergravity. The constructions presented in Section
3
focus on the simplest scalar supermultiplets, but the framework allows straightforward study of more general multiplets for further applications.

The global part of the BMS
4
algebra is isomorphic to both the
4
D Poincaré algebra and the
3
D Carrollian conformal algebra. Similar relations hold for their supersymmetric extensions and the corresponding field theory realizations. As a supersymmetric extension of the
4
D Poincaré algebra, it is imposed strict constraints by Haag–Łopuszański–Sohnius theorem. Indeed, the super Poincaré algebra coincides with the global part of the electric super BMS
4
algebra (
2.19
). The remaining electric superalgebras discussed in Section
2.2
involve supercharges with spin greater than
\tfrac{1}{2}
, and therefore their global parts do not correspond to physically realized superalgebras. Similarly, the magnetic superalgebras, whose supercharge anticommutators also produce superrotations, lack a direct physical superalgebra interpretation for their global sectors. Nevertheless, this does not diminish their relevance in the context of flat holography. On the contrary, these algebras highlight how supersymmetric extensions of BMS
4
can go beyond the strict confines of super Poincaré, potentially capturing structures that only emerge in asymptotic or holographic regimes. In particular, such algebras may arise from large-radius limits of extended super asymptotic symmetries, and the appearance of
R
-symmetry offers a novel perspective on how internal symmetries interplay with asymptotic supersymmetry.

The BMS
4
algebra is closely related to the
3
D Carrollian conformal algebra in both algebraic and field-theoretic aspects. By requiring that supercharges admit finite-dimensional subsectors, the global part of all superalgebras discussed in this work can be naturally reinterpreted as
3
D super Carrollian conformal algebras. This dual perspective not only unifies the classification but also connects the free field realizations to a broader Carrollian framework. Indeed, all the free theories constructed here can be viewed as super Carrollian conformal theories containing fields of diverse spins and conformal dimensions. The interplay between electric and magnetic Carrollian scalar theories, related by the
R
-symmetry of the Type I-I magnetic superalgebra, further illustrates how Carrollian holography accommodates supersymmetric structures beyond conventional relativistic situations. In this way, our results underline the role of Carrollian methods as a powerful organizing language for flat space holography and pave the way for future explorations of supersymmetric Carrollian conformal field theories
[
70
]
.

There are several promising directions for extending this study:

Super BMS
4
algebra from asymptotic symmetry.
The origin of the BMS
4
algebra lies in the study of the symmetries of asymptotically flat gravity. In a similar manner, the super BMS
4
algebra can also be derived from this perspective
[
71
,
72
,
73
,
74
,
40
]
. It would be interesting to extend such an asymptotic analysis to the new super BMS
4
algebras discussed in this work, and further investigate the physical interpretation of these new superalgebras.

Extended supersymmetry.
Extended super BMS
3
and BMS
4
algebras with
\mathcal{N}
copy of electric super charges was well studied in the literature
[
75
,
76
,
73
,
74
,
77
,
78
,
51
,
55
]
. The presence of
R
-symmetry in the magnetic Type I-I, Type II-II, and Type I-II algebras indicates that these should also be regarded as extended superalgebras. Interestingly, in general superalgebras, the
R
-symmetry can carry nonzero spin. It is straightforward to consider the case of conventional extended supersymmetry, i.e., multiple sets of the same supercharges, including both electric and magnetic types. In such scenarios,
R
-symmetries with generic spin are naturally expected in general extended super BMS
4
algebras. Furthermore, the physical interpretation of these
R
-symmetries, particularly those with spin, is an intriguing question. From a holographic perspective, such novel
R

More general supersymmetry.
In this work, we focused on supercharges in the charge representation (
2.3

w
-algebras
[
48

79

Extending the present framework to encompass higher-spin symmetries would not only generalize the classification but also provide algebraic tools for organizing scattering data in flat space, in close analogy with the role of
W

2

Holography with supersymmetry.
This paper has classified all possible super BMS
4
algebras in the charge representation with finite subalgebras, providing a firm foundation for understanding supersymmetry in flat holography. These results serve as fundamental building blocks for constructing Fock spaces, boundary field theories, and analyzing constraints imposed by supersymmetry in holographic setups. In particular, the structure of
R
-symmetry offers guidance for constructing full
10

Additionally, the results presented here suggest new directions for celestial holography: free superfield theories allow for explicit computation of correlation functions, thereby establishing bridges between supersymmetric scattering amplitudes and celestial correlators. Notably, the theory (
3.69

1

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{k}}(r,n)=1,

\displaystyle l_{k}=l_{i}+l_{j}+1,

(A.7a)

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{k}}(r,n)=l_{k}r-(l_{i}+1)n,

\displaystyle l_{k}=l_{i}+l_{j}+2,

(A.7b)

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{k}}(r,n)=r((2+l_{j})r-n),

\displaystyle l_{i}=-1,\quad l_{k}=l_{j}+2,

(A.7c)

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{k}}(r,n)=n(r+(l_{i}+1)n),

\displaystyle l_{i}+l_{j}=-3,\quad l_{k}=0,

(A.7d)

discrete

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{k}}(r,n)=\delta_{r},

\displaystyle l_{i}=0,\quad l_{k}=l_{j},

(A.8a)

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{k}}(r,n)=n\delta_{r},

\displaystyle l_{i}=l_{k}=0,\quad l_{j}=-1,

(A.8b)

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{k}}(r,n)=\frac{1}{n}\delta_{n-r}-\frac{1}{r}\delta_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\delta_{r},

\displaystyle l_{i}=l_{j}=0,\quad l_{k}=-1,

(A.8c)

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{k}}(r,n)=\delta_{r}\delta_{n},

\displaystyle l_{i}=l_{j}=0,\quad l_{k}=-1,

(A.8d)

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{k}}(r,n)=\delta_{n},

\displaystyle l_{i}+l_{j}=l_{k}=-1,

(A.8e)

\displaystyle c_{l_{i},l_{j}}^{l_{k}}(r,n)=r\delta_{n},

\displaystyle l_{i}=l_{j}=l_{k}=-1.

(A.8f)

These solutions remain valid under the exchange
l_{i}\leftrightarrow l_{j}
accompanied by the shift
n\to n-r
. Among them, only (
A.7a
) and (
A.7b
), which correspond to (
3.5
), are relevant for constructing the field modes.
