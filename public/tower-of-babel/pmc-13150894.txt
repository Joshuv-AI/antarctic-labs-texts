Coping strategies and associated factors in a sample of Iranian nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ali Sarvarian, Zahra Tagharrobi, Khadijeh Sharifi, Zahra Sooki, Mohammad Zare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate coping strategies and associated factors among clinical nurses in Iran in 2023.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingHospitals in Kashan, Iran.ParticipantsA total of 400 nurses were selected through stratified random sampling from different hospital departments.Primary and secondary outcome measuresCoping strategies were measured via the Potential Factors Related to Coping Strategies Questionnaire, the Work-Family Conflict Scale and the Ways of Coping Questionnaire. Coping strategies were quantified with scores ranging from 0 to 198. Associations with potential influencing factors were assessed.ResultsThe coping strategies score was 94.22±25.49 (95% CLM 91.72 to 96.72). Notably, 83.5% of the variation in coping strategy scores could be attributed to emotion-focused coping. The simultaneous inclusion of work-family conflict and spouse participation in household affairs was found to be significant (F=9.85, p<0.0001). Work–family conflict was positively associated (B=0.82, 95% CI 0.38 to 1.26), whereas spouse participation was negatively associated (B=−8.43, 95% CI −16.22 to −0.64). Together, these variables accounted for 6.4% of the variance in coping strategies.ConclusionsNurses in Kashan hospitals used moderate levels of coping strategies, which were mainly emotion-focused. It is recommended that hospitals provide guidance on these strategies, especially during pandemic crises.

Introduction

Coping strategies or styles are considered crucial factors influencing the process of adaptation to various conflicts.1 These strategies include the behaviours that people exhibit when confronted with intense emotions.2 The significant impact of coping strategies on physical and mental health has prompted researchers to take a closer look at this important area.3 In general, coping strategies are categorised into different subtypes. Lazarus and Folkman (1987)4 divided these strategies into two main categories: problem-focused strategies and emotion-focused strategies. Problem-focused coping strategies address stressful situations or stimuli directly and aim to reduce or eliminate them. In contrast, some people use emotion-focused coping strategies to mitigate burnout due to negative emotions and to cope with unsolvable problems.57

There are different approaches to coping with negative emotions.7 In this context, Endler and Parker (1994)8 present an important classification that outlines three categories of coping strategies: task-oriented coping, emotion-oriented coping and avoidance-oriented coping. Individuals who use task-oriented coping strategies actively seek more information about the problem, cognitively restructure the problem, make calculations and set priorities to solve the problem effectively. In contrast, avoidance-oriented coping strategies involve behaviours aimed at avoiding or escaping stressful situations. In addition, emotion-oriented coping strategies involve actions in which individuals attempt to relieve their unpleasant feelings without addressing the root cause of the problem. These include expressions of anger, sadness, crying, blaming and rumination.9

Coping mechanisms play a crucial role in understanding, alleviating or even eliminating stress in the workplace, especially in clinical settings.5 The demands of the nursing profession often result in many nurses experiencing significant stress in a variety of situations. In this context, nurses use conscious efforts and specific coping strategies to manage stress and challenges related to work–family conflict.10 The implementation of effective stress management practices can mitigate the harmful effects of stress, thereby improving job satisfaction and the quality of care services.6 Conversely, a lack of effective and principled stress management can jeopardise nurses’ physical and mental health and negatively impact their personal, family and professional performance.10 Therefore, exploring coping strategies among nurses provides valuable insights for managers and policymakers seeking to promote the mental health of this important professional group.5 11

Several studies have examined the coping strategies that people prefer in stressful life situations. Research shows that nurses often use task-focused coping strategies, which are considered to be the most effective for coping with stress.6 12 13 Other studies also suggest that the frequency of coping strategy use among nurses is moderate.5 11 Importantly, contextual factors significantly influence both the extent of strategy use and the outcomes of these strategies. In this context, the nature of the stressful situation, the personality of the individual and the social environment may influence the choice and effectiveness of certain coping strategies.2 For example, several studies have indicated that the adoption and frequency of certain coping strategies are influenced by factors such as gender,14 15 age,16 17 marital status,16 18 work experience,1619 number of children,20 work shifts,21 workplace14 and organisational commitment.11 Recent post-pandemic research has further corroborated these associations, highlighting that sociodemographic variables continue to play a critical role in shaping nurses’ coping mechanisms in the evolving healthcare landscape. Conversely, some studies have shown that certain factors, including gender,15 22 level of education,18 23 number of children, place of residence, organisational position, work shifts and employment status,18 have no significant influence on the adoption or extent of coping strategies. The discrepancies in these results may be related to different occupational, situational, cultural and social contexts. Therefore, a more thorough investigation of this matter in different communities and occupational groups is needed, especially in high-stress occupations.

A comprehensive literature review of coping strategies revealed that several studies focused specifically on nurses.5 6 11 13 18 24 25 Reports of the use of coping strategies or preferred coping methods varied among the different groups. In addition, inconsistencies in the identification of predictors of coping strategies have been reported in previous studies. In the Iranian healthcare context, nursing staff have faced compounded stressors due to the prolonged COVID-19 pandemic and concurrent economic challenges, which have intensified work–family conflicts.26 Given the need for current, relevant data to effectively plan and optimise the situation, the present study investigated coping strategies and associated factors among clinical nurses in Iran following the COVID-19 pandemic.

Materials and methods

Sampling methods

The present study is a cross-sectional study designed on the basis of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. The sample size was determined on the basis of the coping strategies variable from the study by Mahmoudirad et al. In their study, the Ways of Coping Questionnaire (WCQ) score for coping strategies was 107.47±15.15.11 The required sample size was calculated as 384 via equation 1, with a precision of 0.1 SD. However, to account for potential non-response and ensure a final analytical sample of at least 400, the recruitment target was adjusted a priori via equation 2, where n is the target sample size and d is the estimated non-response rate (set at 10%). Consequently, 440 eligible nurses were invited to participate through stratified random sampling.

The inclusion criteria for the study included a university degree in nursing, at least 1 year of work experience in nursing, and no known mental disorders (based on self-reports). Refusal to respond to the data collection instruments and incomplete responses (defined as questionnaires with any missing items) on the Work-Family Conflict Scale (WAFCS) were considered exclusion criteria. Sampling was conducted across the seven hospitals affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences via stratified random sampling within departmental categories among eligible participants. Sampling was conducted over 2 months, from July to August 2023.

Data collection process

The first author of the article visited the administrative office of each hospital; after explaining the research methodology, he prepared a list of the clinical departments and the nurses working there. Finally, considering the required sample size and the number of nurses working in that department, a number of nurses who met the inclusion criteria were selected via simple random sampling. In collaboration with the head nurses of each department, the first author then visited the departments at the beginning of each shift, following the work schedule of the selected nurses. After approval from the nurses, general instructions were given for completing the data collection instruments, which were to be collected at the end of the nursing shift. If the instruments were not completed within the designated timeframe, arrangements were made with the respective nurse for the submission of the completed instruments.

Data collection instruments

The data collection instruments included the Potential Factors Related to Coping Strategies Questionnaire, the WAFCS and the Coping Strategies Questionnaire (WCQ). The data collection instruments used were self-administered questionnaires completed by the nurses.

The Potential Factors Related to Coping Strategies Questionnaire included 43 variables and was developed on the basis of a literature review.11 14 15 18 20 23 These variables included age, gender, education level, being a native, place of residence, housing status, marital status, spouse’s education, spouse’s occupation, number of children, age of youngest child, monthly family income, spouse’s involvement in household affairs, caring for older adults or people with disabilities, support from relatives, experiencing major stress in the past 6 months, workplace stress, participation in stress management courses, regular exercise, leisure activities, satisfaction with welfare facilities, satisfaction with physical and mental health, nursing work experience, employment status, job position, type and predominant shift, department of employment, length of employment in the current department, workload in the department, overtime hours, liability insurance, job satisfaction, satisfaction with salary, interest in the nursing profession, willingness to leave the job and having a second job. The questionnaire also considered factors such as the level of satisfaction with the performance and attitudes of managers, doctors and colleagues, as well as feedback from patients and their families (online supplemental 1). In addition, the variable ‘work–family conflict’ was included as a relevant factor. Faculty members of Kashan University of Medical Sciences confirmed the content validity of the questionnaire. Additionally, reliability was assessed through the test-retest method with 10 nurses over a 1-week interval, resulting in a 95% agreement rate in their responses.

The WAFCS was developed by Carlson et al.27 This scale comprises 18 items, which are divided into two dimensions: work interferes with family (WIF) and family interferes with work; each dimension includes nine items. Each item is answered on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The reliability coefficients for both dimensions were reported to exceed 0.75. In this study, only the WIF dimension was used, which includes nine items reflecting work‒family conflict on a scale of 9–45; higher scores indicate greater work‒family conflict.27 The content validity of the Persian version of the WAFCS was confirmed, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was reported to be 0.77.28 In this study, the reliability of the WIF dimension was estimated to be 0.88 via Cronbach’s alpha coefficient method.

The Coping Strategies Questionnaire (WCQ), developed by Lazarus and Folkman in 1985, contains 66 items (online supplemental 2). According to the standard scoring manual, 50 items were used to calculate the eight subscale scores, while the remaining 16 filler items were excluded from the analysis. This questionnaire is divided into two primary strategies: problem-focused coping and emotion-focused coping. The problem-focused strategy includes subscales for problem solving, seeking social support, accepting responsibility and positive reappraisal. In contrast, the emotion-focused strategy subscale includes subscales for confrontive coping, distancing, self-controlling and escape-avoidance (table 1). Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not used at all) to 3 (used very frequently). Overall, coping strategy scores can range from 0 to 198, with higher scores indicating more effective coping mechanisms. To evaluate the subscales, scores for the items within each subscale are added together. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.66 for the problem-focused strategies subscale and 0.79 for the emotion-focused strategies subscale. Additionally, an overall reliability coefficient of 0.89 was determined.7 29 In the current study, the reliability values for the total scale and the problem-focused and emotion-focused strategies were 0.94, 0.87 and 0.85, respectively.

Data analysis

The data were analysed via SPSS V.22. Coping strategies were determined on a scale of 9–45; their range was also estimated with 95% confidence in the target population. Kurtosis and skewness were used to examine the normality of the data, with a range of ±2 considered normal. To prioritise the components of the questionnaire and assess the contribution of each problem-focused and emotion-focused strategy in explaining the outcomes of the coping strategies, a stepwise multiple linear regression was applied.

To identify the relevant factors, the analysis was conducted in two phases. In the first phase, each factor was examined individually via univariate tests. In particular, independent t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests (for non-normally distributed data) were used to examine the relationships between binary categorical factors (such as gender) and coping strategies. In addition, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Kruskal-Wallis test (for non-normally distributed data) were used for multilevel categorical factors (such as marital status). Pearson’s correlation coefficient and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (for non-normal data distribution) were used for related quantitative factors.

In the second phase, a stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to explore the potential relationships between the identified factors and to assess the contribution of each factor in explaining the variation in coping strategies scores. In this phase, variables with p values less than 0.2 in the previous phase (univariate analysis) were included as independent variables, and the coping strategies score was the dependent variable.30 Importantly, similar analyses were conducted for both problem-focused and emotion-focused strategies. In all the cases, a significance level of less than 0.05 was considered.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Of the 440 nurses invited to participate, 40 declined or were unreachable, resulting in a non-response rate of 9.1%. The final analytical sample consisted of 400 nurses (response rate=90.9%) who completed the data collection instruments. The age of the participants was 35.07±8.11 years. The sample consisted of 77% female participants, and 89% of the nurses had a bachelor’s degree (table 2). In addition, the workplace stress score was 7.93±2.25 (on a scale of 0–10) (table 3).

The overall rate of coping strategies used by the nurses was 94.22±25.49 (on a scale of 0–198). With 95% confidence, the estimated range for the target population is between 91.72 and 96.72 (table 1). Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that the eight subscales accounted for 97.9% of the variance in coping strategy scores, including planful problem solving, seeking social support, accepting responsibility, positive reappraisal, confrontive coping, distancing, self-controlling and escape-avoidance. The contributions of each subscale were as follows: 66.4%, 13.5%, 6.6%, 4.6%, 3.2%, 2.0%, 1.1% and 0.5%, respectively (F=2288.807, p<0.0001).

Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that both emotion-focused and problem-focused strategies accounted for 97.9% of the variation in coping strategies, with emotion-focused strategies accounting for 83.5% and problem-focused strategies accounting for 14.3% (F=9007.871, p<0.0001).

Univariate analysis revealed a significant association between the use of coping strategies and several variables, including educational level, spouse’s involvement in household affairs, workload in the department, satisfaction with salary and work‒family conflict scores among nurses (p<0.05) (tables 2 and 3).

Stepwise multiple linear regression was conducted to identify factors associated with coping strategies, controlling for potential confounders. For the total coping strategies score (table 4), the final model was statistically significant (F(2, 397)=9.85, p<0.0001), explaining 6.4% of the variance (R2=0.06). Work–family conflict was a significant positive predictor (β=0.21, t=3.67, p<0.0001), whereas spouse involvement in household affairs was a significant negative predictor (β=−0.12, t=−2.13, p=0.034).

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate coping strategies and associated factors among a sample of Iranian nurses. The results revealed that the level of coping strategies used by the participants was moderate. These findings are consistent with those of Mahmoudirad et al, who reported a mean score of approximately 107 for coping strategies among nurses in Birjand.11 In addition, nursing students from Greece, the Philippines and Nigeria had scores of 2.21±0.47 on a scale of 0–4 for coping strategies.5 Egyptian nurses also scored 2.13±0.35 (on the same scale),31 which emphasises the consistency of our study’s results with those of other studies.

The present study revealed that two coping strategies, emotion-focused strategies and problem-focused strategies, accounted for 97.9% of the variation in coping strategies used, with the largest contribution attributed to the emotion-focused strategy. The diversification of multiple social roles can affect various aspects of human life, including family dynamics, and can lead to stress. To navigate complex situations, people often employ dynamic strategies that evolve. In situations where the challenges are within a person’s capabilities, problem-focused coping strategies are typically used. However, when individuals realise that their efforts may not yield positive results or may even complicate the situation, they tend to choose emotion-focused coping strategies to alleviate the emotional distress associated with the problem.5

In this context, various studies have investigated how people deal with stressful life situations and what strategies they use to cope with stress. In contrast to the findings of the present study, some studies suggest that nurses often use problem-focused coping strategies as the most effective means of coping with stress.6 12 13 22 24 25 31 Problem-focused coping is about actively managing or changing the problems that cause stress, whereas emotion-focused coping focuses on regulating emotional responses to these stressors.5 The COVID-19 pandemic has had a significant impact on nurses, many of whom are beyond their control. As a result, nurses faced challenges such as livelihood problems, inadequate salaries and delayed payments, with these problems becoming increasingly apparent within Iranian hospital systems post-pandemic. In addition, the predominance of female participants in the studied samples led to a greater reliance on emotion-focused coping strategies.22

The results of the current study indicate that work–family conflict is positively and significantly associated with the coping strategies used. Conversely, spouses’ involvement in household affairs showed a negative and significant relationship with the coping strategies used. These two variables together accounted for 6.4% of the variance in coping strategy scores, with work–family conflict making the largest contribution. For the emotion-focused and problem-focused strategies identified in the WCQ, both work–family conflict and spouse involvement in household affairs were significantly related to emotion-focused strategies in the final model and predicted a 7% change in the dependent variable. For the problem-focused strategies, only work–family conflict showed a significant relationship with the dependent variable in the regression model, explaining 2.6% of the variance. Consequently, the multiple analyses conducted to assess the predictors of coping strategies and their dimensions after controlling for other factors highlighted the significant influence of work–family conflict. These results indicate that work–family conflict is a significant predictor of coping strategy use, suggesting that it is a key factor necessitating adaptive responses among nurses, particularly during crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic and economic challenges. As a result, nurses often turn to various coping strategies to manage this stress.32 The effectiveness of these strategies is largely influenced by the individual’s perception of the stressor, self-efficacy and resilience. In addition, the success of coping mechanisms depends on factors such as personality traits, environmental conditions, available resources and organisational support.3234 Iranian nurses reported high levels of work–family conflict during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.26 The nature of their challenges, combined with the restrictions imposed by hospital conditions and organisational structures beyond their control, has prompted nurses to favour emotion-focused coping strategies. While these strategies do not address core issues directly, the findings of the present study suggest that despite the moderate use of coping strategies among nurses, the level of work–family conflict remains relatively high. It is important to consider the cross-sectional design of this study and the simultaneous evaluation of variables when these results are interpreted.

In this study, spouse involvement in household affairs was identified as a predictor of coping strategies, particularly in terms of general and emotion-focused coping. These results are consistent with the research conducted by Benetti et al in Brazil.35 Several studies have shown that support from family members, including spouses and children, can alleviate workplace pressures and help nurses develop effective coping strategies. Family support includes various forms, such as sharing responsibilities, providing emotional support and fostering a nurturing home environment.36 37 Considering the cultural context of Iranian society, it seems that the spouses of Iranian nurses play a crucial role in creating a supportive atmosphere and providing emotional support. This observation may help explain the significant effect of spouse involvement on the use of emotion-focused coping strategies.

Other studies have reported results different from those of the present study regarding the factors associated with coping strategies. For example, although the findings of the present study are consistent with those of Darvishpour et al in Gilan, who reported no significant relationships between gender, number of children, place of residence, education level, organisational position, employment status, work shift and housing situation with the use of coping strategies, a significant correlation was found between coping strategy scores and age, marital status and work experience.18 In contrast, a Greek study conducted in 2016 revealed that nurses with more work experience and specialised training were more likely to use problem-focused coping strategies. In this context, some factors may affect the coping strategies chosen by nurses, such as gender, educational level, job position, number of patients and workplace stress.38

Experts believe that contextual factors have a significant influence on the coping strategies that individuals use. Elements such as the nature of a stressful situation, the individual’s personality and the surrounding social context can affect both the choice and effectiveness of particular coping strategies.2 Research suggests that the adoption of certain coping methods is associated with several factors, including gender,14 15 age,18 23 marital status,18 21 work experience,18 number of children,20 shift work,21 work environment14 and organisational commitment.11 Conversely, some studies have shown that other factors, such as gender,15 22 educational level,18 23 number of children, place of residence, organisational position, shift work and employment status,18 have no significant correlations with the use of coping strategies. The different results of these studies regarding the factors influencing coping strategies and their dimensions are probably due to the different occupational, situational, cultural and social contexts that characterise the samples studied.

In summary, various studies indicate that nurses use different coping strategies that can be categorised into emotion-focused strategies.24 39 These studies showed that nurses use emotion-focused coping techniques to manage their emotional reactions to stress and challenges in the workplace. These strategies enable nurses to regulate their emotions and improve their performance at work. However, it is important to recognise that nurses also use problem-focused strategies in various forms. In contrast to the present study, some studies have identified problem-focused strategies as the preferred approach among nurses.24 31 39 The use of problem-focused strategies enables nurses to actively and effectively address professional challenges, identify and analyse problems, plan solutions and use resources and past experiences. In addition, the use of problem-focused strategies can help individuals manage stressful situations and overcome the challenges associated with the nursing profession.25 Nurses, therefore, tend to use one or more subtypes of either problem-focused or emotion-focused coping strategies, which are influenced by factors such as the nature of the challenges they encounter, their level of competence, previous experiences and learning, and the prevailing organisational culture in their workplace. Therefore, healthcare managers and policymakers need to pay attention to this issue.

The present study benefits from a stratified sample, an adequate sample size and multiple analyses. However, it appears that the critical conditions of the pandemic have impacted factors such as nurses’ personality traits, self-efficacy and resilience,4042 thus influencing the level and type of coping strategies employed by clinical nurses. This represents a limitation of the study. Furthermore, the high linear regression coefficients observed in this study support this assertion, suggesting that other variables also play a role in estimating the scores for the use of coping strategies and their dimensions, which were not considered in this study. Additionally, although participants with known mental disorders were excluded, this was based on self-reports, which may not have identified undiagnosed conditions or undisclosed psychiatric treatment.

Conclusion

The current study revealed that the coping strategies used by nurses are moderate. A significant contribution to the variance in these coping strategies is attributed to emotion-focused approaches. In addition, the results revealed a relationship between work–family conflict and the level of a spouse’s involvement in household affairs, as well as the coping strategies used. On the basis of these findings, it is recommended that nurses receive support to optimise the use of coping strategies to address work-related stressors. In addition, the limitations of this study should be considered as much as possible, and a cross-sectional study should be conducted to comprehensively identify the factors influencing nurses’ coping strategies and their various dimensions. Qualitative research is also recommended to gain deeper insights into the factors that influence the use of coping strategies.
