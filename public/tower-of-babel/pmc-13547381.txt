Benchmarking RNA-seq with the Quartet and MAQC reference materials to establish best practices for accurate alternative splicing analysis
Authors: Duo Wang, Jiaxin Zhao, Qingwang Chen, Yanxi Han, Yaqing Liu, Yuanfeng Zhang, Cong Liu, Wanwan Hou, Ying Yu, Leming Shi, Yuanting Zheng, Jinming Li, Rui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious limited characterization of short-read RNA-seq (srRNA-seq) accuracy in alternative splicing (AS) analysis due to methodological diversity and lack of reference standards, has left unclear how to achieve optimal performance—an issue increasingly critical with the rise of long-read sequencing. To address this, we conduct a large-scale reference-based benchmarking study across 42 laboratories and 207 analysis pipelines leveraging the Quartet and MAQC reference materials. Here, we show that high data quality and depth improved the accuracy of splice junction detection, as well as isoform- and event-level quantification and differential analysis. Best practices for experimental and bioinformatic design are identified, with optimal pipelines achieving Pearson and Matthews correlation coefficients of 0.79 and 0.68 for isoform-level quantification and differential analysis, and 0.41 and 0.41 for event-level analyses, respectively. This corresponds to improvements of 0.21–0.45 and 0.51–0.67 at the isoform level, and 0.09–0.27 and 0.16–0.34 at the event level relative to the poorest-performing pipelines across laboratories. Beyond technical workflows, low expression or coverage and high compositional complexity represent general constraints on accuracy. Collectively, this study provides practical guidance for maximizing AS profiling accuracy with existing methodologies, contributing to effective srRNA-seq application in RNA splicing research.Subject terms: RNA sequencing, RNA splicing, RNA sequencing

Introduction

Alternative splicing (AS) is a post-transcriptional process by which different combinations of splice sites are selectively utilized to produce multiple mRNA isoforms from a single gene, contributing to transcriptomic and proteomic diversity1. Aberrant AS is linked to various diseases, particularly cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and autoimmune diseases2–4. Thus, detection of AS is essential for unraveling developmental mechanisms, elucidating disease pathogenesis, and identifying biomarkers for disease diagnosis and therapy. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) enables transcriptome-wide profiling of RNA splicing, providing quantification of AS-derived isoform abundances, measurement of AS events via percent spliced-in (PSI), and the comparative analysis of their differential expression across physiological and pathological conditions5. This technology has greatly advanced the study of RNA splicing6–9. Despite its widespread use, short-read RNA-seq (srRNA-seq) faces challenges in fully capturing alternative splicing (AS) regions, and the limited knowledge regarding its accuracy hinders clinical translation. This issue has grown increasingly pressing as long-read RNA-seq (lrRNA-seq) has demonstrated superior performance in resolving full-length isoforms10,11, raising critical questions about whether srRNA-seq remains reliable for AS profiling in clinical applications.

Previous benchmarking studies have substantially advanced our understanding of srRNA-seq-based AS analysis. Owing to the lack of ground truths, most focused on evaluating consistency across sequencing platforms or analysis tools. Early efforts by the MicroArray/Sequencing Quality Control (MAQC/SEQC) consortium and the Association of Biomolecular Resource Facilities (ABRF), employed MAQC RNA reference materials in multi-center evaluations and revealed that isoform-level analysis is more challenging than gene-level analysis12,13. Subsequent studies assessed the consistency of analysis tools for isoform quantification, AS event analysis, and differential expression or splicing analyses, revealing particularly poor overlap at the AS event level14–19. To approximate accuracy, synthetic RNA spike-ins were introduced; for example, the Long-read RNA-Seq Genome Annotation Assessment Project revealed that srRNA-seq achieved high quantification accuracy for Spike-In RNA Variants (SIRV)20. Moreover, previous evaluations of analysis tools have primarily relied on simulated data, incorporated different combinations of tools, and reported differences in accuracy particularly at the event level, while the resulting performance rankings were not always consistent21–26. Despite these advances, fundamental questions remain unresolved: how accurate srRNA-seq is for isoform and AS event analysis, and how to achieve the highest possible accuracy using existing methods.

Specifically, previous consistency-based analyses have been unable to clearly reflect the overall accuracy of srRNA-seq in AS analysis or to determine which experimental protocols and bioinformatics tools achieve the highest practical accuracy. Furthermore, benchmarking using spike-ins or simulated data, which are far less complex than real human transcriptomes, tends to overlook influencing factors and may lead to overestimation of performance21,27,28. Additionally, benchmarking at the event level has been particularly hindered, largely due to differences among AS analysis tools in algorithms, event-type definitions, and output formats. Consequently, most prior studies have focused on performance at the gene level where spliced events occur18,23,24, substantially limiting the understanding of event-level analysis accuracy. When overall accuracy remains unclear, the factors affecting srRNA-seq accuracy in AS analysis are also poorly characterized. Therefore, reference-based benchmarking of srRNA-seq accuracy in isoform and AS event profiling, along with standardized benchmarking at the AS event level, is essential for achieving accurate AS analysis.

In this work, to address these limitations, we conducted a comprehensive benchmarking study of srRNA-seq for AS analysis using the Quartet and MAQC RNA reference materials29, spanning 42 real-world laboratories and 207 bioinformatics pipelines. The well-validated Quartet reference datasets, established in our companion study under the Quartet project using long-read sequencing, served as the ground truth, covering 10,218 isoforms and 6032 AS events30. Additional ground truths were generated through RT-qPCR validation of 121 isoforms and 59 events, as well as the creation of known mixing ratios in the sample design. Leveraging these large-scale, multi-type ground truths, we systematically evaluated the accuracy and consistency of splice junction identification, isoform and AS event quantification, and differential expression or splicing analyses. We further provided best-practice recommendations by investigating technical and biological factors affecting isoform- and event-level performance across multiple centers, platforms, and pipelines. Together, this study facilitates achieving optimal performance of srRNA-seq in AS profiling and provides important guidance for its effective application in both research and clinical settings.

Results

Study design

This study generated multi-center srRNA-seq datasets involving 42 laboratories and 207 analysis pipelines for comprehensive srRNA-seq benchmarking using the Quartet and MAQC RNA reference materials (Fig. 1a). The sample preparation and data generation were detailed in our previous study28. The Quartet samples consisted of M8, F7, D5, and D629, and two additional samples, T1 and T2, were generated by mixing M8 and D6 at ratios of 3:1 and 1:3, respectively28. The MAQC samples consisted of A and B, derived from cancer cell lines and human brain tissues, respectively, and were included for comparison with the Quartet samples to assess the generalizability of the conclusions to more complex transcriptomic contexts. The detailed experimental and analysis workflow is illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 1–2.

This sample design incorporated three types of ground truth for isoform and event-level evaluations, including the Quartet reference datasets, RT-qPCR-validated reference datasets, and the built-in truth based on the known mixing ratios (Methods). The Quartet reference datasets were generated by integrating seven lrRNA-seq batches and four analysis pipelines, minimizing biases from individual libraries, platforms, and tools, and ensuring robustness. Orthogonal validation of 121 isoforms and 59 events using RT-qPCR demonstrated high concordance with the reference datasets, with Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) of 0.95 and 0.76 for isoform- and event-level quantification, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3). These RT-qPCR results were also used as an independent ground truth. Additionally, the known mixing ratios between samples T1 and T2 were used as built-in truth to evaluate the performance of isoform quantification of srRNA-seq.

The 42 laboratories involved diverse srRNA-seq workflows, including two mRNA enrichment methods, eight library preparation kits, and two sequencing platforms, reflecting real-world practice. Subsequent data analysis was performed using four mapping tools, two reference-based transcript isoform assembly tools, eight isoform quantification tools, seven DEI analysis tools, and six AS event analysis tools, resulting in a total of 207 distinct analysis pipelines (Fig. 1b).

Leveraging these extensive datasets, we first evaluated the accuracy and consistency across laboratories and analysis pipelines, focusing on splice junction identification, isoform and AS event quantification, as well as the identification of differentially expressed isoforms (DEI) and differential splicing events (DSE). Then, we investigated key factors influencing accuracy, considering technical variables in both experimental and bioinformatics processes as well as biological features of isoforms and AS events, ultimately providing best-practice recommendations for experimental and bioinformatics design to achieve optimal accuracy with current methods (Fig. 1a). Collectively, we further developed a one-stop RNA-seq quality control pipeline that enables basic QC metric assessment, ground-truth-based performance scoring, and optimal pipeline selection for RNA-seq data.

Quality control for identifying low-quality srRNA-seq data

We examined the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of quantification data at the gene, isoform, and event levels, which reflected the ability to distinguish biological signals from technical noise (Supplementary Fig. 4a). SNR values varied widely across laboratories (gene level: 0.1–36.1; isoform level: 0–32.1; AS event level: 0–20.6). Notably, the mean SNR for AS events was significantly lower than that for isoform-level, and both were lower than gene-level (20.8, 18.4, and 10.6 for gene, isoform, and event level, respectively). This indicates that achieving quantitative accuracy becomes increasingly challenging when transitioning to resolution of isoforms or individual splicing events within each isoform.

By incorporating the SNR metric with previously used basic srRNA-seq quality metrics28, data from 19 laboratories met all quality criteria, while the remaining 23 laboratories were flagged as low quality (Supplementary Fig. 4b and Supplementary Data 1). The low-quality classifications were primarily attributed to poor library preparation stability, low sequencing quality, and cross-contamination.

srRNA-seq missed certain splice junctions captured by lrRNA-seq

Accurate junction-level resolution is essential for reliable AS analysis using srRNA-seq. To evaluate the accuracy in junction detection, we constructed junction reference datasets using seven batches of high-quality lrRNA-seq generated in our companion study30 (Supplementary Fig. 5). These reference datasets resolved annotated junctions for 6,130 genes, including 48,366 positive and 35,766 negative junctions. In addition, 69,013 high-confidence novel positive junctions were identified.

All laboratories demonstrated generally high accuracy for annotated junctions, with a mean F1 score of 0.97 (0.93–0.98) (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). The false-negative (FN) rate was significantly higher than the false-positive (FP) rate, with mean values of 4.8% (1.6–13.0%) and 1.5% (0.5–7.1%), respectively. For novel junctions, the mean FN rate was as high as 45.1% (28.4–72.4%), highlighting the limited ability of srRNA-seq to discover novel isoforms or events. High-quality laboratories consistently exhibited relatively lower FN rates.

Both technical and biological factors influenced junction detection in srRNA-seq. For both the Quartet and MAQC samples, laboratories tended to cluster according to sequencing technology, mRNA enrichment method, and library strandedness, highlighting protocol effects (Supplementary Fig. 7). Sequencing depth was a key determinant, with higher depth strongly reducing FN rates for both annotated and novel junctions (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Junction-specific features associated with FN events included lower read counts, uneven coverage, longer junction-correlated isoforms, more exons per isoform, and lower mappability (Supplementary Figs. 6e and 8–14), indicating that short reads struggle to capture junctions in complex isoforms or poorly mappable regions, especially at low depth. FP were linked to slightly higher GC content, lower mappability, and shorter isoforms with fewer exons, suggesting that misalignments may occur in specific genomic contexts.

High quality and depth improve isoform-level quantification and differential analysis

For isoform quantification, substantial inter-laboratory variation was observed, with PCA analysis indicating that inter-laboratory differences often obscured biological signals (Fig. 2a). High-quality RNA-seq data clustered primarily by sample group rather than by laboratory, with the Quartet and MAQC samples exhibiting highly consistent clustering patterns (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 15). Benchmarking against the Quartet and RT-qPCR references demonstrated high accuracy, with mean PCC of 0.71 (0.61–0.77) and 0.91 (0.86–0.93), respectively, for high-quality laboratories (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 16). Higher root mean square error (RMSE) and lower PCC against the Quartet reference datasets highlighted the greater challenge of accurately quantifying a broader, more complex isoform set. The built-in truth was used to evaluate the laboratories’ ability to recover the expected mixing ratios (Methods), showing trends consistent with evaluations against both reference datasets (Fig. 2c). Moreover, isoform quantification accuracy correlated positively with sequencing depth and negatively with junction detection errors, as higher FN junction rates were associated with poorer accuracy (Fig. 2e, f).

For DEI identification, laboratories varied widely in the number of DEI in both the Quartet and MAQC samples, which was strongly associated with sequencing depth (Supplementary Fig. 17). Benchmarking against the Quartet and RT-qPCR reference datasets showed generally high precision of 0.81 (0.38–0.88) and 0.87 (0.76–1), respectively, but recall remained modest at 0.38 (0.005–0.61) and 0.50 (0.10–0.71), yielding moderate Matthews correlation coefficients (MCC) of 0.51 (0.03–0.65) and 0.59 (0.28–0.69) (Fig. 2g, h). Recall improved with higher depth, and MCC positively correlated with data quality (Fig. 2i, j). Tracing all FN DEI revealed that FNs were characterized by low coverage of reads and high inter-replicate coefficient of variation (CV) (Fig. 2k), where low coverage caused inaccurate isoform quantification and poor reproducibility undermined the statistical robustness of differential expression analysis.

Identifying technical and biological factors for optimal isoform-level quantification and differential analysis

To assess the technical and biological determinants of isoform-level quantification and differential analysis performance, we examined 19 different experimental protocols from high-quality laboratories and 201 isoform-level analysis pipelines to identify best practices (Fig. 1 and Table 1). We then assessed various isoform features to evaluate their specific impact on srRNA-seq methodologies.

Cluster analysis of laboratories based on accuracy metrics against three ground truths showed that the highest accuracy was associated with Poly(A) selection and higher sequencing depth, while three laboratories (lab04, lab33, and lab36) using rRNA depletion also performed well due to exceptionally high sequencing depth (Fig. 3b). The 201 isoform-level analysis pipelines included six transcript isoform assembly pipelines, 21 isoform quantification pipelines, and 11 differential isoform expression analysis methods, and the performance of each analysis step was evaluated sequentially (Supplementary Fig. 2). The choice of assembly tool had a substantial impact on the number and composition of assembled isoforms. In both the Quartet and MAQC samples, StringTie tended to assemble a higher proportion of isoforms that exactly matched the reference annotation, whereas Scallop2 generated a larger proportion of discordant isoforms (Supplementary Fig. 18a, b). Assembly performance across all pipelines also appeared to be influenced by RNA-seq experimental protocols; rRNA depletion and higher sequencing depth generally resulted in a greater number of non-exactly matched isoforms. Using the reference annotation as the truth, StringTie exhibited overall higher sensitivity and precision in both Quartet and MAQC samples (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 18c). Consistent results were obtained when assembly was performed using the RefSeq reference annotation (Supplementary Fig. 19).

The 21 isoform quantification pipelines comprised eight quantification tools, most of which showed high agreement with the RT-qPCR reference datasets (PCC > 0.8) (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 20). In contrast, the Quartet reference datasets revealed clearer performance differences among pipelines, with RSEM, eXpress, and Cuffdiff demonstrating higher accuracy, followed by StringTie and pseudo-alignment-based tools (Sailfish, Salmon, and Kallisto) (Fig. 3d–f). StringTie showed slightly higher accuracy when using reference annotation-guided quantification compared with post-assembly quantification. Built-in truth further reinforced the performance ranking, except for Cuffdiff, which showed poorer recovery of the mixing ratios (RMRs) due to lower reproducibility in isoform quantification (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 21). featureCounts was included as an example of a gene-level quantification tool that remains used in practice for isoform analyses. It showed limited ability to distinguish isoforms of the same gene (Fig. 3e), likely due to difficulties in assigning reads from shared exons to individual isoforms. Despite exhibiting high PCC and low RMSE relative to the reference datasets, this apparent agreement may be attributable to the predominance of MANE transcripts in the reference datasets and the use of relative expression measures, both of which could mask their limited ability to distinguish isoforms. Therefore, gene-level analysis tools, such as featureCounts, are not well suited for analyses requiring isoform-level resolution. Similarly, pipelines using Bowtie2 aligner also exhibited reduced isoform discrimination ability, likely owing to the loss of isoform-informative reads caused by untrimmed alignments. Overall, the optimal quantification pipelines achieved mean PCC of 0.79 (0.66–0.87) and 0.93 (0.91–0.94) against Quartet and RT-qPCR reference datasets, respectively, with improvements of 0.35 (0.21–0.45) and 0.47 (0.33–0.57) across laboratories over the poorest pipelines.

The performance ranking of isoform quantification pipelines was further validated using ground-truth-free analyses in both the Quartet and MAQC samples, which showed highly consistent results. Consistency-based clustering separated pipelines into three groups: pipelines unsuitable for isoform analysis, StringTie-based pipelines, and the remaining pipelines based on transcriptome alignment or pseudo-alignment; this clustering pattern closely mirrored their accuracy distributions (Supplementary Fig. 22). In addition, ground-truth-free metrics, including SNR and CV, exhibited trends similar to those observed in accuracy-based evaluations (Supplementary Fig. 21). We also evaluated the impact of reference annotation and found that using the RefSeq reference retained the same performance ranking (Supplementary Fig. 23). Additionally, to assess parameter sensitivity, 24 alternative parameters were included (Supplementary Table 1). Several parameters related to isoform abundance correction and algorithmic optimization yielded slightly improved accuracy; nevertheless, tool performance rankings across parameter settings remained highly consistent with those obtained using default settings (Supplementary Figs. 24 and Supplementary Notes).

For DEI identification, 11 methods derived from different modes of seven differential analysis tools were combined with the above quantification pipelines for cross-evaluation. Using the Quartet and RT-qPCR reference datasets as benchmarks, statistical power of differential analysis was evaluated using the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR), with edgeR (qCML and GLM) and NOISeq (real) showing overall superior performance (Fig. 3g). When a uniform threshold for log2FC and statistical significance was applied, most methods achieved high MCC values, particularly edgeR, limma, DESeq2, and Cuffdiff (Fig. 3h). Consistent conclusions were obtained when differential analysis was performed based on the RefSeq reference annotation (Supplementary Fig. 25). Differential analysis tools exhibited varying sensitivity to isoform quantification pipelines, highlighting the importance of selecting compatible method combinations (Supplementary Fig. 26-27). Among these combinations, the optimal pipeline reached MCC of 0.68 (0.53–0.76) and 0.71 (0.59–0.77) for the Quartet and RT-qPCR references, respectively, representing inter-laboratory improvements of 0.57 (0.51–0.67) and 0.62 (0.51–0.78) over the poorest pipeline.

Isoform-inherent features determine their detectability by srRNA-seq, reflecting methodological limitations. The effects of seven factors on isoform quantification pipelines were investigated, excluding featureCounts-based pipelines. Benchmarking against the Quartet reference datasets, most quantification pipelines performed poorly with high K-values (indicating isoform-exon structure complexity), short isoforms, large isoform numbers per gene, short constituent exons, high GC content, and low expression levels (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 28–34). High k-values, large number of isoforms per gene, and high GC content reflect increased isoform complexity, which makes accurate quantification more challenging. In addition, shorter isoforms and exons are less likely to be experimentally sampled after mRNA fragmentation, and result in fewer reads aligning to the isoform at the same expression level. This reduces the statistical power of quantification and increases susceptibility to bias, which are consistent with previous observations17,31. The effect of exon number varied by pipeline, with Cuffdiff and StringTie performing poorly for isoforms with more exons, while other tools showed the lowest performance at intermediate exon numbers. Based on the built-in truth, these findings were largely consistent, except for pipelines employing the Bowtie2 aligner (Supplementary Figs. 28–34). Finally, ANOVA-based analysis revealed isoform expression levels as the primary factor affecting most pipelines, followed by isoform complexity such as K-value and isoform number (Fig. 3j).

High quality and depth improve event-level quantification and differential analysis

The performance of srRNA-seq in AS event analysis across real-world laboratories was evaluated for both event quantification and DSE analysis, using SUPPA2 to infer AS events from isoform expression data. Substantial variation was observed even among 19 high-quality laboratories, with PCA showing clustering by laboratory rather than sample groups (Fig. 4a). Such inter-laboratory variations were likely driven by differences in experimental protocols, which could be consistently observed in both the Quartet and MAQC samples (Supplementary Fig. 35). Event-level quantification accuracy was generally low and highly variable (Fig. 4b), and high-quality laboratories performed relatively better relative to the Quartet and RT-qPCR reference datasets, respectively (Fig. 4c). Quantification accuracy improved with greater sequencing depth and was closely linked to junction detection, with higher FN junction rates corresponding to lower PCC (Fig. 4d, e). Inter-laboratory variations in quantification performance resulted in limited overlap of detected events, with only a small subset consistently identified across laboratories in both the Quartet and MAQC samples (Supplementary Fig. 36). Such reduced concordance was primarily associated with threshold effects at extreme PSI values (very high or very low) and low expression levels of the corresponding isoforms.

For DSE identification, benchmarking against the Quartet and RT-qPCR reference datasets revealed low recall and precision for DSE analysis, with mean MCC of 0.16 (0.05–0.24) and 0.12 (0–0.28), respectively (Fig. 4f, g). Identifying technical and biological factors for optimal event-level quantification and differential analysis with SUPPA2

To investigate factors influencing AS event quantification and differential analysis and to identify best practices, we selected SUPPA2 as a representative event-level analysis tool. Its compatibility with 21 isoform quantification pipelines enabled cross-assessment of the impact of 19 experimental protocols, upstream analysis pipelines, and AS event-associated features (Fig. 1 and Table 1).

Among experimental factors, the mRNA enrichment method and strandedness were the primary sources of inter-laboratory variation in AS event quantification, accounting for 32% of the variance (Fig. 5a), with library preparation kits, sequencing depth, and read length also contributing. Clustering high-quality laboratories based on RMSE showed that experimental protocols yielding the highest AS event quantification accuracy were consistent with those optimal for isoform expression. Specifically, the most accurate cluster included laboratories using Poly(A) selection with higher sequencing depth, except for two rRNA-depletion laboratories (lab33 and lab36) with exceptionally high sequencing depth (Fig. 5b).

In bioinformatics processes, the choice of isoform quantification method significantly affected SUPPA2’s performance in detecting AS events. Benchmarking against the Quartet and RT-qPCR reference datasets showed that SUPPA2 achieved relatively higher dPSI quantification accuracy when combined with Salmon, RSEM, Cuffdiff, Sailfish, or Kallisto, with the maximum mean PCC values of 0.36 (0.19–0.49) and 0.55 (0.42–0.66), respectively (Fig. 5c). Given the marked inconsistency in dPSI distributions among tools, normalized RMSE (nRMSE) was used to minimize its impact, showing consistent trends with PCC (Fig. 5c). For DSE identification, pipelines with higher dPSI quantification accuracy also performed better, yielding higher AUPR values (Fig. 5d). After applying uniform thresholds for dPSI and p value, similar trends were observed for MCC, with the optimal pipelines achieving MCC values of 0.41 (0.28–0.51) and 0.27 (0.04–0.42) based on the two reference datasets, respectively (Fig. 5e). For comparison, the pipelines combining featureCounts with SUPPA2 exhibited the poorest accuracy in both event quantification and DSE analysis (Fig. 5c–e). In both the Quartet and MAQC samples, these pipelines showed aberrant PSI distributions centered around 0.5 and dPSI distributions concentrated near 0, further supporting the conclusion that gene-level quantification tools such as featureCounts are not well suited for downstream analyses requiring AS resolution (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 38). Overall, the optimal pipelines improved PCC by 0.21 (0.05–0.36) and 0.36 (0.15–0.61), and MCC by 0.36 (0.28–0.46) and 0.21 (0.01–0.37), compared with the poorest pipelines, based on the Quartet and RT-qPCR reference datasets.

Ground-truth-free metrics, including SNR and standard deviation (SD), showed similar trends, except that the StringTie-SUPPA2 combination exhibited better quantification reproducibility and data quality (Supplementary Fig. 39). Notably, distributions of these metrics were consistent across both the Quartet and MAQC samples. Additionally, cross-reference evaluation using the RefSeq reference annotation for AS event quantification and differential analysis yielded consistent pipeline performance rankings (Supplementary Fig. 40).

Inherent features of AS events also constrained analysis accuracy. The effects of six factors on event quantification were investigated across combinations of SUPPA2 and above isoform quantification pipelines, excluding featureCounts-based pipelines. Benchmarking with the Quartet reference datasets revealed that SUPPA2’s quantification accuracy decreased for events with more adjacent AS events, fewer associated isoforms, higher GC content, lower mappability, intermediate PSI values, and lower expression levels of related isoforms (Fig. 5g–h and Supplementary Figs. 41–46). The effects of these factors on quantification reproducibility were similar to those on accuracy. Finally, we quantified the magnitude of the impact on quantification accuracy and found that, except for pipelines combined with Bowtie2-eXpress or Bowtie2-Salmon, the primary determinants were the expression levels of associated isoforms and the PSI levels (Fig. 5g).

Substantial variations across different AS event analysis tools

Different alternative splicing (AS) event analysis tools employ distinct algorithmic strategies, leading to divergent event definitions and output formats. We selected six widely used tools representing different principles: SUPPA2 based on isoform expression; rMATS and PSI-Sigma based on junction read counts; and MAJIQ, Spladder, and Whippet based on splice-graph construction. Among these, MAJIQ analyzes local splicing variations (LSVs) from the perspective of individual exons, Whippet partitions gene structures into non-overlapping nodes, and Spladder directly extracts events from splice graphs. We further provided a standardized benchmarking pipeline to harmonize formats of each event type, including skipping exon (SE), alternative 5′ splice site (A5SS), alternative 3′ splice site (A3SS), alternative first exon (AF), alternative last exon (AL), mutually exclusive exon (MX), and retained intron (RI) (Supplementary Fig. 47). The pipeline directly extracts the defined coordinates for each event type, together with quantification and differential analysis results corresponding to exon-skipping and intron-retention states (Supplementary Fig. 48). By constructing a simulated dataset with known events, we demonstrated that this harmonization process does not introduce artifacts (Supplementary Fig. 49).

These tools showed limited overlap in detected AS events in both the Quartet and MAQC samples, especially for AF, AL, MX, and RI (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 50–51). Using the recommended thresholds, the overlap in detected DSE was even smaller, with most identified by only a single tool (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 52). rMATS and Whippet detected substantially more DSE across event type; Spladder identified the highest number of RI DSE; SUPPA2 detected more AF/AL DSE, whereas MAJIQ consistently detected very few DSE (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 53). For dPSI quantification, SE, A3SS, A5SS, and RI showed higher inter-tool consistency, whereas MX and AF/AL events, which involve multiple exons, exhibited lower consistency across both the Quartet and MAQC samples. Among tools, PSI-Sigma and MAJIQ demonstrated relatively higher PCC with other tools, whereas SUPPA2 and Whippet exhibited the lowest (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 54).

Using the Quartet and RT-qPCR reference datasets as benchmarks, PSI-Sigma, MAJIQ, and Spladder showed relatively higher agreement in dPSI quantification, with mean PCC values of 0.41 (0.31–0.51), 0.41 (0.32–0.50), and 0.38 (0.27–0.50) for the Quartet reference datasets, and 0.62 (0.47–0.76), 0.60 (0.34–0.77), and 0.76 (0.64–0.87) for the RT-qPCR reference datasets, respectively (Fig. 6e). Quantification performance was strongly dependent on coverage, with reduced accuracy and reproducibility at low coverage, and was also influenced by PSI, particularly at intermediate levels (Supplementary Fig. 55).

For DSE identification, precision-recall curves indicated generally low statistical power across all tools (Supplementary Fig. 56). rMATS achieved the highest AUPR, followed by SUPPA2, MAJIQ, PSI-Sigma, and Whippet, while Spladder showed the poorest performance (Fig. 6f). When applying tool-specific DSE thresholds, the MCC values varied substantially across tools relative to the reference datasets (Fig. 6g). SUPPA2 outperformed the other tools, while Whippet also achieved relatively high MCC values with the RT-qPCR reference datasets. Overall, the performance gap between the best- and poorest-performing tools reached up to 0.16 (0.09–0.27) and 0.31(0.03–0.46) in PCC for event quantification, and 0.26 (0.16–0.34) and 0.18 (0–0.30) in MCC for differential analysis, according to the Quartet and RT-qPCR reference datasets. Using the RefSeq annotation had a more pronounced effect on SUPPA2, primarily reflected by an increased number of detected DSE (Supplementary Fig. 57a). Despite this, the overall ranking of tools for dPSI quantification (PCC) and differential analysis (AUPR) remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 57b–d).

To assess parameter sensitivity, 16 variable parameters were evaluated (Table S1). Overall, dPSI quantification showed high consistency with default settings. However, parameters related to differential analysis thresholds or statistical models substantially influenced the number of detected DSE, which was also reflected in differences in AUPR (Supplementary Fig. 58). Nevertheless, the performance rankings of these tools were largely preserved under alternative parameter settings.

Discussion

This study represents the most extensive cross-laboratory benchmarking of srRNA-seq for accurate AS profiling, leveraging the Quartet and MAQC reference materials and large-scale, multi-type ground truths. Our objectives were to address key challenges in srRNA-seq applications: (i) the current accuracy of AS analysis in real-world settings, (ii) best practices for experimental protocols and bioinformatic pipelines to achieve the most accurate isoform- and AS event-level analysis using existing methods. The final recommendations are summarized in Table 1.

A key strength of this study lies in the use of robust ground truth. The Quartet reference datasets were derived from multiple batches of lrRNA-seq, which are considered advantageous for capturing multiple splice junctions within transcripts, enabling unambiguous isoform identification, and supporting accurate isoform quantification and event discovery32, as also confirmed in our companion study30. Orthogonal validation using RT-qPCR further strengthens confidence in the evaluation. These strategies, based on multi-method consensus or gold-standard approaches, are both recommended for constructing ground truth27, and are superior to previously used computational simulations or synthetic RNA controls20–25. Furthermore, the use of built-in truth based on known mixing ratios enabled evaluation across all transcriptomic features, extending our conclusions to the full detectable transcriptome33,34. Notably, Quartet reference datasets showed the strongest discriminative power. In addition, MAQC samples were included for parallel analysis with the Quartet samples to represent more complex transcriptomes with aberrant splicing. Although ground truth is unavailable for MAQC, highly similar patterns were observed between the two sample types in data quality and in the consistency of isoform- and event-level quantification and differential analyses across 42 laboratories and 207 pipelines. These results further support the generalizability of our conclusions to more complex transcriptomic contexts.

Data quality is a dominant determinant of AS analysis accuracy. Higher quality consistently improved the accuracy of junction detection, as well as isoform- and event-level quantification and differential analyses, highlighting the importance of rigorous quality control prior to downstream analysis28. High-quality laboratories achieved mean PCC values of 0.79 and 0.90 for isoform quantification, and mean MCC values of 0.68 and 0.70 for DEI analysis based on the Quartet and RT-qPCR reference datasets, respectively. For event-level analysis, mean PCC values were 0.41 and 0.62 for event quantification, with corresponding MCC values of 0.40 and 0.27 for DSE analysis. These accuracy levels are slightly lower than those reported in previous studies based on simulated or small-scale RT-qPCR datasets16,18,24,35, likely because the broader and more comprehensive reference datasets used here imposes greater challenges. Beyond annotated transcriptomes, high FN rates (~45.1%) in novel junction detection indicated inherent limitations of short reads for identifying novel alternative splicing features.

Our benchmarking provides best-practice recommendations for experimental and bioinformatics design (Table 1). Unlike most previous evaluations that focused on isolated experimental methods or individual analysis steps14–18,21–25, we encompassed entire experimental and computational workflows across multiple centers. This comprehensive perspective enables full consideration of interactions between different steps and provides direct, practical guidance for isoform- and AS event- level profiling.

In the experimental process, both mRNA enrichment method and strandedness were the primary contributors to inter-laboratory variation at the isoform and event levels. Quantification accuracy was strongly associated with the use of Poly(A) selection and higher sequencing depth. This is consistent with previous reports showing that Poly(A) selection provides more accurate gene quantification in high-quality RNA samples28,36,37. Although the benefits of increased sequencing depth are well recognized, the appropriate depth depends on transcriptome complexity, sequencing mode, library type, and RNA quality. Liu et al. reported that 100 M reads are sufficient to detect most genes and AS events, whereas differential expression analyses may require over 300 M reads38. Our results corroborate this, as performance continued to improve even in laboratories with sequencing depths exceeding 400 million reads.

In bioinformatics design, STAR and HISAT2 were superior aligners, offering high mapping efficiency28 and comparable accuracy when combined with downstream analysis tools. For isoform-level analysis, RSEM, eXpress, and Cuffdiff performed better for quantification. Our findings are consistent with previous evaluations based on RT-qPCR or microarray data, which demonstrated higher accuracy for eXpress and RSEM14,16,25. In contrast, Zhang et al., using simulated data, reported slightly better performance for pseudo-alignment tools22. This discrepancy likely reflects the simplified characteristics of simulated data, which lack variants, sequencing errors, intronic signals, and non-uniform coverage, as also noted by Sarantopoulou et al.26. Among reference-based assembly tools, StringTie outperformed Scallop2 in assembly, but assembly-based quantification by StringTie was overall less accurate than annotation-based approaches. For DEI analysis, previous evaluations of differential expression tools have mainly focused on the gene level23,28,39–41, we extended to isoform-level analysis and found that tool performance rankings were broadly consistent between the isoform and gene levels. Specifically, edgeR and NOISeq-real showed strong overall performance, while DESeq2, limma, and Cuffdiff also performed well under empirical thresholds. Overall, the large performance gap between optimal and poorest pipelines underscores the importance of tool selection based on benchmarking.

For event-level analysis, substantial variations were observed across tools. SUPPA2 performance depended on upstream isoform quantification and was generally improved when combined with Salmon, Sailfish, Kallisto, Cuffdiff, or RSEM. To further enable direct evaluation across different event analysis tools and establish best practices, we implemented an event-format harmonization pipeline for standardized benchmarking of six widely used tools with distinct analytical principles. Although DICAST was previously developed for benchmarking AS event analysis15, it included tools later shown to perform poorly24, did not cover the most widely used tools currently in practice, and lacked support for quantitative evaluation. We incorporated SUPPA2, rMATS, PSI-Sigma, MAJIQ, Spladder, and Whippet, representing several distinct AS event algorithmic strategies of isoform expression-based, junction read-based, and splice graph-based approaches42. As reported in prior event-level benchmarking studies18,23,24, overlap among six tools was limited, both for detected events and DSE, largely due to differences in the event databases inferred from reference transcriptomes and in the algorithms used to define and quantify each event type. Overall, rMATS and SUPPA2 showed more balanced performance in quantification and differential analysis, consistent with previous evaluations based on simulated data24. By contrast, PSI-Sigma, MAJIQ, and Spladder performed well in quantification but had limited sensitivity for detecting differential splicing, as reflected by the smaller number of DSE identified.

Our evaluations based on the Quartet reference datasets also provide a perspective on the comparison between srRNA-seq and lrRNA-seq. Together with recent benchmarking efforts for lrRNA-seq20,30,32,43, our study provides a comprehensive insight into current AS analysis technologies, guiding users in choosing between short- and long-read sequencing for splicing studies. For isoform-level analysis, we demonstrated that the optimal workflow shows acceptable concordance with lrRNA-seq for annotated isoforms. The advantages of greater sequencing depth and lower cost further highlight its practical utility. For event-level analysis, the main challenge lies in discrepancies across tools, indicating a need for further algorithm development and optimization towards standardization. Potential directions include the establishment of comprehensive event annotation databases, harmonization of event definitions and output formats, integration of multiple tools to improve DSE analysis, and targeted optimization for complex event types. In addition, we highlight methodological and analytical limitations of srRNA-seq. Isoform-level analysis is challenged by low expression and high transcript complexity, while event-level analysis is hindered by low coverage, intermediate PSI values, and complex event structures, particularly MX and AF/AL events. These limitations should also be explicitly considered in practical applications and guide future methodological development.

The limitations of this study should also be acknowledged. First, benchmarking analyses were performed predominantly using the same reference annotation, thereby reflecting analysis performance of srRNA-seq under conditions of exact transcript annotation matching. Although RefSeq was included for cross-evaluation, direct comparison with the reference datasets required the inclusion of only those isoforms or events that were completely matched across annotations. Consequently, our findings cannot fully address the impact of incomplete or discordant annotations on AS analyses, an issue that has been increasingly recognized as a major challenge in transcriptome studies44,45. Second, the tools evaluated were selected based on their widespread use or previously reported superior performance and therefore do not encompass all available tools or tool-specific parameter settings. As a result, the best practices proposed here should be interpreted within the scope of the evaluated tools and scenarios involving identical or fully matched transcript annotations.

Overall, this study elucidates srRNA-seq accuracy in AS analysis, identifies key technical determinants, and provides best-practice recommendations for experimental and bioinformatic designs (Table 1). Moreover, a one-stop RNA-seq quality control pipeline incorporating the best-performing tools was constructed to facilitate convenient processing and analysis of srRNA-seq data. Collectively, these insights will support the effective application of srRNA-seq and advance research on alternative splicing, ultimately contributing to a deeper understanding of transcriptomic complexity and its functional implications.

Methods

RNA reference samples preparation

The Quartet RNA reference materials were obtained from four Epstein-Barr virus-immortalized B-lymphoblastoid cell lines derived from the Quartet family, which have been certified by China’s State Administration for Market Regulation29. The MAQC reference materials, also known as Universal Human Reference RNA (740000, Agilent Technologies) and Human Brain Reference RNA (QS0611, Thermo Fisher Scientific), were developed from ten cancer cell lines (MAQC A) and brain tissues from 23 donors (MAQC B), respectively, and were referred to as MAQC samples A and B by the MAQC Consortium46. The details of RNA reference sample preparation were provided in our previous study28. In brief, four Quartet samples (M8, F7, D5, and D6) were used, and two additional samples (T1 and T2) were generated as defined mixtures of M8 and D6 at ratios of 3:1 and 1:3, respectively. Each sample was provided in triplicate to 42 laboratories for sequencing.

srRNA-seq data

Details of srRNA-seq data generation are available in our previous study28. Initially, 45 laboratories were enrolled, of which 42 provided complete sequencing data and were included in this study. Each laboratory applied its own routine library preparation and sequencing protocol, thereby reflecting real-world srRNA-seq practices. Laboratory identifiers were retained as lab01–lab45 for consistency with previous studies.

RT-qPCR validation

A total of 121 isoforms and 59 AS events were selected for RT-qPCR experiments in the Quartet samples, aiming to orthogonally validate the Quartet reference datasets and to provide an independent “gold standard” for AS detection accuracy assessment. SYBR Green dye-based RT-qPCR was used to validate the quantification of isoforms and AS events. Specific primers were designed to target isoform-unique sequences or sequences corresponding to isoforms that either include or exclude the AS event. For cases where dye-based RT-qPCR results were inconsistent with the Quartet reference datasets, additional validation was performed using the TaqMan probe method. The TaqMan probes were designed on isoform-unique sequences or sequences specific to AS event-related isoforms. C1ORF43 was selected as the reference for the PCR method. Primers and probes were synthesized by Sangon Biotech, and the sequences are shown in Supplementary Data 3.

The RT-qPCR experiment mainly consisted of reverse transcription and PCR steps. Five μg of each RNA sample was reverse transcribed using the PrimeScriptTM RT reagent Kit (RR037A, Takara) in a 50 μl reaction. This reaction mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, then for 5 s at 85 °C and finally for termination at 4 °C. cDNA was used as a template for qPCR. The dye-based qPCR reactions were performed in a final volume of 20 μL using Premix Ex TaqTM II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (RR820A, Takara) containing 1 μL of cDNA, and 0.8 μL of each forward and reverse primer. The probe-based qPCR reactions were carried out using Premix Ex TaqTM (RR390A, Takara) containing 2 μL of cDNA, 0.4 μL of each forward and reverse primer, and 0.8 μL of TaqMan probes in a 20 μL final volume reaction. The qPCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System using the following cycling conditions: 30 s at 95 °C followed by 45 cycles of 5 s at 95 °C and 34 s at 56 °C. Three replicates per sample per gene were conducted to eliminate random variations.

Normalized isoform expression was quantified using the delta Ct method, with the C1ORF43 gene serving as the endogenous control. Fold changes (FC) for isoform expression were calculated using the delta delta Ct method for the three Quartet sample comparisons (M8/D6, F7/D6, and D5/D6). PSI for AS events was calculated as the ratio of the expression level of isoforms containing a specific AS event to the total expression of all isoforms, and dPSI was calculated as the difference in PSI values between the three sample pairs. An isoform is classified as a DEI when the student’s t test p < 0.05 and FC ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5. An event is classified as a DSE when the student’s t test p < 0.05 and dPSI ≥ 0.05 or ≤ −0.05.

The ground truth inherent in RNA reference samples

The sample design incorporated three types of ground truth: the Quartet reference datasets, the RT-qPCR reference datasets, and the built-in truth based on the mixing ratios of the T1 and T2 samples.

Quartet reference datasets

The details for constructing the Quartet reference datasets are available in our companion study30. Briefly, 12 batches of lrRNA-seq data were evaluated, demonstrating unambiguous isoform detection and quantification performance. Among these, seven batches with high SNR were selected to construct the reference datasets, including five batches generated on the Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) platform, of which two used direct RNA sequencing protocols and three used PCR-cDNA sequencing protocols, as well as two batches generated on the Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) platform using the MAS-ISO-Seq (Kinnex) library protocol. The clean reads were aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome using Minimap2 (v2.24-r1122)47. Four isoform analysis tools, five tools were benchmarked using the SIRV Set 448, and four with high accuracy were selected for isoform quantification based on the GENCODE annotation (release 43) (https://ftp.ebi.ac.uk/pub/databases/gencode/Gencode_human/release_43/gencode.v43.chr_patch_hapl_scaff.annotation.gtf.gz), including IsoQuant (v3.6.1)49, Bambu (v3.6.0)50, StringTie2 (v2.2.1)51 and Oarfish (v0.6.2)52. Differential analysis was performed using edgeR (v4.0.0)53, and AS events analysis was conducted using SUPPA2 (v2.4)35. The results were integrated using a four-step intersection strategy to retain features consistently supported across different libraries, platforms, and analytical pipelines: (i) selecting features reproducibly detected across technical replicates for each sample; (ii) retaining features supported across most lrRNA-seq batches and calculating mean relative expression for those with low expression uncertainty; (iii) selecting differentially expressed features supported across most lrRNA-seq batches; and (iv) retaining features supported across multiple analysis pipelines. This strategy was modified from the previous approaches for constructing gene-level Quartet reference datasets29. The reference datasets provide isoform-level and AS event-level ratio-based quantification results, as well as differential analysis results, for the M8/D6, F7/D6, and D5/D6 sample pairs. The isoform reference datasets comprise 10,218 results, reporting relative expression as log₂FC, among which 1077 isoforms were classified as DEI. The AS event reference datasets contain 5574 results, reporting relative quantification as dPSI, among which 78 events were defined as DSE.

The Quartet reference datasets were orthogonally validated using RT-qPCR experiments, with a total of 237 isoforms and 152 AS events overlapping between the two datasets across the three sample pairs (Supplementary Fig. 3).

RT-qPCR reference datasets

The selected isoforms and AS events were validated across the M8/D6, F7/D6, and D5/D6 sample pairs, yielding a total of 362 and 177 relative quantification results for isoforms (log₂FC) and AS events (dPSI), respectively. Among them, 78 were identified as DEI and 41 as DSE. The RT-qPCR reference datasets are shown in Supplementary Data 4.

Construction of the Quartet junction reference datasets

Seven batches of high-quality long-read sequencing data from our companion study were used to construct reference datasets (details in Supplementary Methods)30. First, positive and negative junctions supported by long-read sequencing data across multiple batches were selected. Second, a candidate gene set with sufficient read coverage was identified. Finally, positive and negative junctions located within the candidate gene set were further screened, resulting in a reference dataset in which all annotated positive and negative junctions within these genes were known, as well as an additional dataset containing novel junctions.

Relative expression calculation

Relative expression quantification was performed on a feature-by-feature basis within each laboratory. Specifically, for each isoform, the mean of expression profiles of replicates of reference sample(s) (for example, D6) was first calculated and then subtracted from the log2TPM values of that isoform in other samples.

Evaluation of srRNA-seq performance in isoform and AS event detection

The evaluation of srRNA-seq performance was conducted at three levels. First, data quality was assessed using the SNR. Second, metrics reflecting the accuracy of quantification and differential analysis for isoforms and AS events were calculated based on the three types of ground truth. Finally, reproducibility metrics were included to assess the consistency of quantification across replicate samples.

SNR

The PCA-based SNR was used to assess the data quality of the Quartet and mixed samples at the isoform expression and AS event PSI level, and the calculation method of PCA-based SNR is as shown in the previous study29. Isoform-level analysis was based on TPM (transcripts per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads) values, including only those isoforms with TPM > 0 in at least one sample. For AS event-level analysis, PSI values were used, and only events with PSI values between 0 and 1 in all samples were included in the analysis. An SNR below 10 was considered indicative of low-quality data. In addition to calculating the SNR across all 18 samples, we also computed SNR17, representing the SNR calculated from any 17 out of the 18 samples (12 Quartet and 6 mixed samples). If the removal of a particular sample results in a significant increase in SNR17 compared to SNR18 (with a difference greater than 6), the quality of the srRNA-seq data of the excluded sample is considered low, and the sample is treated as an outlier. Such random occurrences of problematic library preparation or sequencing in individual samples are referred to as random failures.

PCC

PCC was used to assess the consistency between srRNA-seq-derived relative isoform expression (log₂FC) and AS event relative quantification (dPSI) with the reference datasets.

RMSE and nRMSE

RMSE was used to quantify the absolute deviation between srRNA-seq-derived relative quantification of isoforms and AS events and the reference datasets. In addition, RMSE was also used to assess the consistency between the observed relative isoform expression in the sample pairs of T1/D6 and T2/D6 and the expected relative expression calculated from the sample pair of M8/D6 using Eq. where θi and θ^i denote the expected and observed quantification results, respectively, and θ^max and θ^min denote the maximum and minimum observed quantification values.

Recall, precision, and F1 score

To evaluate the performance of srRNA-seq in detecting junctions, DEI, and DSE, recall, precision, and F1 score were calculated based on the comparison with the reference datasets. Recall was defined as the proportion of true positives (TP) correctly identified among all reference positives (TP + FN), while precision was defined as the proportion of TP among all detected positives (TP + FP). The F1 score, representing the harmonic mean of recall and precision, was calculated using Eq. (3).

AUPR

AUPR was used to evaluate the statistical power of differential analysis tools in ranking truly differential features toward the top. For isoform-level analysis, features with |log2FC | > 1 in the reference datasets were considered true positives, whereas for event-level analysis, |dPSI | > 0.05 was used as the threshold.

MCC

The MCC was used to evaluate the accuracy of DEI or DSE identification for a given pair of samples based on the Quartet and RT-qPCR reference datasets. Then MCC was calculated using Eq. (4).

CV and SD

The reproducibility of isoform expression (TPM) and AS event quantification (PSI) across three replicates was assessed using coefficient of variation (CV) and standard deviation (SD), respectively. Values were summed across all isoforms and AS events to provide an overall measure of reproducibility.

RMR

RMR refers to evaluation based on the built-in ground truth, assessing how well RNA-seq isoform quantification recovers the expected mixing ratios in the T1/D6 and T2/D6 sample pairs. Performance was measured by comparing observed and theoretical values using RMSE or PCC.

Uniformed srRNA-seq data analysis pipeline for 42 laboratories

The GRCh38 reference genome (https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-109/fasta/homo_sapiens/dna/Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.dna.primary_assembly.fa.gz) and transcriptome (https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-109/fasta/homo_sapiens/cdna/Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.cdna.all.fa.gz) were obtained from Ensembl. Gene annotation from Ensembl release-109 (https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-109/gtf/homo_sapiens/Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.109.gtf.gz) was used.

To evaluate the performance of srRNA-seq experimental processes, srRNA-seq data from all 42 laboratories were processed using a uniform data analysis pipeline, thereby eliminating variations introduced by the bioinformatics processes. Preliminary processing of raw reads was performed using fastp (v0.23.2) to remove adapter sequences54. Cleaned reads were aligned to the GRCh38 genome using STAR (v2.7.10b)55. The STAR --twopassMode Basic option was applied to improve junction detection, and splice junctions were extracted from BAM files using the sjFromSAMcollapseUandM_inclOverlaps.awk script provided by STAR. Isoform quantification was conducted with StringTie (v3.0.0) using the -e option to enable expression estimation guided by the annotation56. Log2 transformation of isoform expression values was carried out on TPM, after adding a pseudocount of 0.01 to avoid infinite values. Differential isoform expression analysis was performed using edgeR (v4.4.2) in the classical mode53. Isoforms with log2FC greater than ≥ 1 or ≤ −1 and FDR < 0.05 were classified as DEI. For AS event analysis, StringTie-derived TPM data were used as input to SUPPA2 (v2.4)35. SUPPA2 was run with recommended --gene-correction option when performing differential splicing analysis. AS events with dPSI ≥0.05 or ≤ −0.05 and p < 0.05 were considered DSE.

Benchmarking srRNA-seq analysis pipelines

To specifically assess the impact of bioinformatics designs, we incorporated four alignment tools, two reference-based transcript isoform assembly tools, eight isoform quantification tools, seven DEI detection tools, and six AS event detection tools to combine 201 isoform-level and 26 AS event-level analysis pipelines. These pipelines were applied to high-quality srRNA-seq data based on the Ensembl reference annotation. Unless otherwise specified, all tools were run with default parameters. The details of srRNA-seq analysis tools, versions, and the command line are listed in Supplementary Data 5.

Alignment tools

Four alignment tools, STAR (v2.7.10b)55, HISAT2 (v2.2.1)57, Subjunc (v2.0.8)58, and Bowtie2 (v2.5.4)59 were included. STAR, HISAT2, and Subjunc were applied to align reads to the reference genome, while Bowtie2 was used to align reads to the reference transcriptome. In addition, STAR was also employed for transcriptome alignment using the parameters --quantMode TranscriptomeSAM and --quantTranscriptomeBan Singleend. For Cuffdiff analysis, STAR was run with the additional parameters --outSAMstrandField intronMotif and --outFilterIntronMotifs RemoveNoncanonical, while HISAT2 was run with the --dta-cufflinks option. Subjunc alignments were not used for Cuffdiff analysis, as it does not provide parameters compatible with Cuffdiff.

Transcript isoform assembly tools

Two reference-guided transcript isoform assembly tools, StringTie (v3.0.0)51 and Scallop2 (v1.1.2)60, were included. Because StringTie supports isoform quantification using the -e option, isoform quantification was additionally performed using StringTie-assembled transcripts as the reference. In contrast, quantification of transcripts assembled by Scallop2 depends on external tools; therefore, quantification based on Scallop2-assembled transcripts was not evaluated. Assembly results were evaluated by comparison with the reference annotation using GffCompare (v0.12.6)61, which classifies assembled transcripts into different class codes based on intron or splice junction concordance with reference transcripts.

Isoform quantification tools

Eight commonly used tools were included for isoform expression quantification, consisting of genome-alignment-based tools like featureCounts (v2.0.8)62, StringTie (v3.0.0)51, and Cuffdiff (v2.2.1)63, transcriptome-alignment-based tools like RSEM (v1.3.3)64, eXpress (v1.5.1)65, and Salmon (v1.10.1)66 in alignment-based mode, as well as pseudo-alignment-based tools like Kallisto (v0.51.1)67, Sailfish (v0.10.0)68, and Salmon (v1.10.1)66 in mapping-based mode. Among these, featureCounts is designed for gene-level quantification but is still used in practice for isoform-level analyses69. It was included primarily as an example to demonstrate how inappropriate use or application of methods can affect downstream analysis. For parameter settings, StringTie performed isoform quantification using both the reference annotation and the assembled transcripts. featureCounts was run with the additional parameters -g transcript_id, -O, and -M. RSEM performs both alignment and quantification directly by specifying the --hisat2-hca, --star, or --bowtie2 options, rather than requiring a separate alignment step. Salmon was run using optional parameters: --gcBias, --seqBias, and --posBias to correct GC biases, sequence-specific biases, and 5’ or 3’ positional bias. TPM was used for isoform-level analyses for all tools.

Differential isoform expression analysis tools

Seven differential expression analysis tools, including Cuffdiff (v2.2.1)63, Ballgown (v2.38.0)70, edgeR (v4.4.2)71, limma (v3.62.2)72, DESeq2 (v1.46.0)73, NOIseq (v2.50.0)74, and Sleuth (v0.30.1)75, along with different modes, were combined to generate a total of 11 analysis approaches. Among these tools, edgeR and DESeq2 are based on negative binomial models, Cuffdiff employs a beta-negative binomial model, and NOISeq uses a non-parametric approach. Ballgown and limma are based on linear models, while Sleuth applies an error linear model. These tools collectively represent the major statistical algorithms currently used for differential expression analysis. For differential expression analysis tools, we also applied the available analysis modes. Specifically, for edgeR, three versions were considered: v1, based on the quantile-adjusted conditional maximum likelihood (qCML) method; v2, using generalized linear models (GLM) for parameter estimation; and v3/v4, which adopt the quasi-likelihood (QL) generalization of the negative binomial (NB) model. These complementary strategies were included to comprehensively evaluate the impact of different statistical tools on the detection of DEIs. For limma, we used the voom and trend modes, in which the mean-variance relationship for count data was modeled either with precision weights or with an empirical Bayes prior trend. For NOISeq, we applied both the real and BIO modes, with the latter optimized for biological replicates. Regarding input data, Ballgown directly uses isoform quantification results from StringTie, while Sleuth operates directly on the quantification results from Salmon, Kallisto, and Sailfish. In contrast, the remaining tools were provided with raw counts from isoform quantification tools, except for RSEM, for which effective counts were used as recommended.

AS event analysis tools

Six AS event analysis tools were employed, including SUPPA2 (v2.4), rMATS (v4.3.0)76, PSI-Sigma (v2.3)77, MAJIQ (v2.5.6)78, Spladder (v3.1.1)79, and Whippet (v1.6.2)80. SUPPA2 was run using transcript-level quantification data (TPM) generated from 21 isoform quantification pipelines. Among these, SUPPA2 was used as an example for investigating factors affecting isoform quantification accuracy, primarily because it is widely used, can be directly integrated with the 21 upstream isoform quantification pipelines constructed in this study for cross-validation, and enables direct comparison with the SUPPA2-derived Quartet reference datasets. During standardized benchmarking of the six tools, TPM data generated from the STAR-Salmon pipeline were used as input, as this pipeline demonstrated relatively superior performance. rMATS was run with cleaned reads as input and performed alignment using its built-in STAR module prior to AS event detection. MAJIQ, PSI-Sigma, Spladder, and Whippet were executed based on STAR-aligned data. Following the rMATS manual, DSE was defined using |IncLevelDifference | ≥ 0.05 and FDR ≤ 0.01. For PSI-Sigma, DSE were identified with |ΔPSI (%)| ≥ 10 and FDR (BH) < 0.01. MAJIQ employed a probability threshold greater than 0.95, whereas SUPPA2 used |dPSI | ≥ 0.05 and p ≤ 0.05. For Whippet, the recommended thresholds of |ΔPSI | > 0.1 and probability ≥ 0.90 were applied. For Spladder, empirical thresholds of |dPSI | ≥ 0.05 and pval_adj < 0.05 were applied. These tools were also run using the RefSeq reference annotation, except for PSI-Sigma, which is specifically designed for the Ensembl reference annotation only.

Parameter sensitivity evaluation

To evaluate the parameter sensitivity of bioinformatics tools, we selected key or recommended parameters documented in the user manuals for eight isoform quantification tools and six event-level analysis tools (Table S1). These parameters can be broadly categorized into those related to algorithmic or statistical models and those associated with analysis thresholds, output options, or additional correction. Parameter evaluations were conducted using two representative RNA-seq data batches, lab01 and lab04, which respectively encompass the Poly(A) selection or rRNA depletion methods, and include both stranded and non-stranded libraries.

Cross-reference evaluation

To evaluate the impact of reference annotations, all pipelines were additionally run using the RefSeq annotation on two representative RNA-seq batches (lab01 and lab04). The RefSeq reference annotation release CF_000001405.40-RS_2023_03 was used (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq/H_sapiens/annotation/annotation_releases/GCF_000001405.40-RS_2023_03/GCF_000001405.40_GRCh38.p14_genomic.gtf.gz). When comparing isoform- and event-level results based on the RefSeq reference annotation to the reference datasets, the GENCODE and RefSeq GTF files were compared to establish gene and transcript correspondences between the two annotations using GffCompare (v0.12.6)61. Specifically, isoforms with class codes “=“ or “c” were used for ID matching. Under these criteria, 25.9% (65,288/251,839) of GENCODE isoforms and 32.6% (65,288/200,213) of RefSeq isoforms were retained for downstream comparisons. For events, using SUPPA2 as an example, 13.5% (40,220/297,228) of GENCODE-based events and 27.7% (40,220/145,314) of RefSeq-based events were retained. This mapping was then applied to the reference datasets, resulting in 9298 isoforms and 3466 events being matched to the RefSeq annotation.

Principal variance component analysis

PVCA was performed to estimate the sources of variation in isoform (TPM) or AS event (PSI) expression profiles across laboratories using the pvca package (v1.40.0)81. We examined a total of 11 experimental factors, including RNA input, mRNA enrichment method, strandness, library kit, read length, library concentration, sequencing platform, flowcell, lane, the number of reads in the exonic region, and insert size. Sample grouping also served as a factor in PVCA, allowing for a comparison between biological differences among samples and variations introduced by various factors.

Annotation of features for junction, isoform and AS event

Features were annotated for each junction, isoform, and AS event, including mappability, GC content, the number of isoforms per gene, isoform expression, isoform length, the number of exons per isoform, exon length, the K value, the number of adjacent AS events, and PSI level. Detailed definitions of these features are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Standardized benchmarking tool

The outputs of six alternative splicing detection tools, SUPPA2, rMATS, PSI-Sigma, MAJIQ, Spladder, and Whippet, were converted into a unified format. Definitions of the unified format for the seven AS event types are provided in the Supplementary Methods. This standardized benchmarking pipeline was validated through tool-by-tool concordance checks of detected event number before and after harmonization, manual spot checks, and evaluation using a simulated dataset with known ground truth to ensure that no artifacts were introduced. The results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 48–49. Under standardized event definitions, overlap of AS events and DSE and consistency of dPSI quantification were assessed across six tools.

Simulated data generation and analysis

Simulated data were generated using ASimulatoR (v1.0.0)82. The reference genome for chr1 (https://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-109/fasta/homo_sapiens/dna/Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.dna.chromosome.1.fa.gz) and the Ensembl reference annotation were provided as input. The gene number was set as max_genes = 1000. Event frequencies were set as event_freq = setNames(rep(1/9, 9), c(‘es’, ‘mes’, ‘ir’, ‘a3’, ‘a5’, ‘afe’, ‘ale’, ‘mee’, ‘es,ir,mes,a3,a5,afe,ale,mee’)), assigning equal proportions of event types across all genes. In total, 1,554 events were generated, comprising 222 events for each type.

Construction of one-stop srRNA-seq quality control pipeline

A Nextflow-based srRNA-seq quality control pipeline, ASBench, was developed. First, we integrated FastQC, FastQ Screen, and Qualimap to perform quality control at the FASTQ and BAM levels, generating core quality metrics including duplication rate, proportions of exonic/intronic/intergenic reads, mapping rates, strandedness, and 3’/5’ bias. Second, RNA-seq data are processed through a rapid STAR-StringTie-SUPPA2 pipeline for isoform- and event-level analyses, and the SNR and ground-truth-based quantitative scoring are calculated. Using the performance score ranges observed for high-quality laboratories in this study as a reference, ASBench informs users whether their data are suitable for downstream analyses. Finally, we incorporated RSEM, eXpress, and Cuffdiff for isoform quantification, edgeR and DESeq2 for differential expression analysis, as well as rMATS and SUPPA2 for event-level analyses, thereby providing users with a set of recommended best-practice pipelines for alternative splicing analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical packages (v4.5.1) and Python (v3.10.10). PCA was conducted with the univariance scaling, using the prcomp (v3.6.2) function. PVCA was performed by the pvca package (v1.40.0)83 to quantify the proportion of variance explained by each influencing factor.
