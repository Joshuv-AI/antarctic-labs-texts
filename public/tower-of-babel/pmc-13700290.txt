The role of program and policy level interventions in addressing intention to leave and turnover among health workers in Ethiopia – a repeated cross-sectional study
Authors: Jemal Mohammed Ali, Zeine Abosse, Tegbar Yigzaw Sendekie, Jelle Stekelenburg, Shelemo Shawula, Asfaw Demissie, Mesfin Goji, Miruts Goshu, Gizachew Yismaw, Firew Ayalew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh levels of health workers’ intention to leave and turnover continue to undermine health workforce availability and health system performance, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Ethiopia has implemented a series of national Human Resources for Health (HRH) policy and programmatic interventions aimed at improving health worker motivation, retention and working conditions. This study compared intention to leave and turnover among selected health professionals between 2014 and 2022 and explored changes observed during the implementation period of these interventions.MethodsA repeated cross-sectional mixed-methods study was conducted using nationally representative surveys in 2014 and 2022. Both surveys employed the same structured questionnaire and a two-stage cluster sampling design, with sample sizes calculated assuming a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error and 10% non-response rate. The 2014 survey included 1,356 health workers from 224 health facilities, while the 2022 survey included 1,754 health workers from 176 facilities. In addition, qualitative key informant interviews were conducted in 2022 with health sector leaders, managers and health workers involved in implementing HRH motivation and retention interventions to provide contextual insights into the quantitative findings. Quantitative data were analysed using SPSS, while qualitative data were analysed using thematic analysis.ResultsBetween 2014 and 2022, intention to leave declined from 49.0% to 17.5% (absolute reduction: 31.5% points; p < 0.001), while the annual turnover rate decreased from 4.5% to 3.5% (absolute reduction: 1.0% point; p < 0.001) among medical doctors, health officers, nurses, midwives and anaesthetists. Similar reductions were observed in both hospitals (47.3% to 17.9%) and health centres (51.4% to 16.1%). Annual turnover also declined among medical doctors, nurses and anaesthetists. Key informants perceived improvements in selected financial and non-financial incentive packages, training opportunities, supportive supervision and workplace allowances. However, inadequate housing, unfair supervisory practices, low salaries, increasing costs of living and workplace safety concerns remained important challenges affecting workforce retention.ConclusionsSubstantial reductions in health workers’ intention to leave and turnover were observed during the implementation period of Ethiopia’s national HRH policy and programmatic interventions. Although qualitative findings suggest perceived improvements in several workforce retention strategies, the repeated cross-sectional design does not permit causal attribution of the observed changes to the interventions. Persistent concerns regarding remuneration, housing, supervision and rising living costs indicate that continued investment in both financial and non-financial retention strategies remains essential for strengthening Ethiopia’s health workforce.Keywords: Intention to leave, Turnover, Health workers, Program and policy interventions, Hospital, Ethiopia

Background

The ongoing global shortage of healthcare professionals is a critical challenge that severely limits the capacity of healthcare systems to deliver quality services [1]. According to the International Labor Organization (ILO), there will be a global shortfall of 18.3 million health workers to achieve Universal Health Coverage (UHC) by 2030 [2]. There is a serious shortage of health workers in Africa, which is affecting access to and provision of health services, despite efforts by countries in the region to strengthen the workforce. In 47 African countries, there are just 1.55 health workers (physicians, nurses, and midwives) per 1,000 people, well below the WHO’s recommended 4.45 per 1,000 needed to ensure essential health services and universal health coverage [3]. Persistent shortages are further exacerbated by high levels of workforce turnover and attrition, which continue to undermine health system performance and the continuity of healthcare services [4]. Employee turnover refers to the movement of employees out of an organization during a specified period and may be classified as either voluntary or involuntary [5]. Voluntary turnover occurs when employees choose to leave their employment, such as through resignation, migration or career change, whereas involuntary turnover occurs when employment ends because of employer-initiated termination, organizational restructuring, disability or death. In the health sector, voluntary turnover is of particular concern because it is often influenced by modifiable organizational and workplace factors and therefore represents an important target for policy intervention. Intention to leave is defined as an employee’s conscious and deliberate willingness to leave their current organization and is widely recognized as one of the strongest predictors of subsequent voluntary turnover [6, 7, 8]. Attrition refers to the gradual reduction of the workforce resulting from retirement, resignation, migration, career change or death without replacement [5]. However, the lack of internationally standardized definitions and measurement approaches for turnover and attrition continues to limit comparisons across countries and constrains evidence-informed workforce planning [9].

The rising intention of public health workers to leave their jobs is a growing concern in healthcare because it threatens to exacerbate existing and anticipated workforce shortages, undermining the performance of health systems [8]. The rate of intention to leave varies geographically and across different healthcare professions. Meta-analysis of health worker survey data from six African countries revealed that the average intention to leave among health workers was 42% [10]. Although national figures for the magnitude of intention to leave among multiple health cadres are not currently available in Ethiopia, a systematic review of research conducted between 2012 and 2022 found an estimated pooled prevalence of intention to leave at 58.9% among health workers in Ethiopia [11]. The intention to leave jobs, resulting in actual turnover, poses substantial and expensive challenges in both remote rural areas and well-developed urban regions [12To effectively address healthcare worker shortages and plan for the future, increased focus on workforce attrition is crucial. High attrition rates translate to a significant loss of public resources invested in educating and preparing healthcare professionals [13]. The annual health worker turnover rates in Africa vary widely, from 3% to a staggering 44%, with voluntary attrition ranging from 0.3% to 28% [9]. In Ethiopia, documented health worker turnover rates vary considerably, ranging from 6.1% in Harari to 38.5% in Addis Ababa [14], which could also be an accurate reflection of the fact that staff turnover among healthcare workers remains a concern even at relatively developed urban areas [12].

Between 2015 and 2022, the Ethiopian Ministry of Health implemented a series of national Human Resources for Health (HRH) policy and programmatic initiatives aimed at strengthening workforce management, motivation and retention [15]. [. The Ministry of Health launched a national Human Resources for Health (HRH) strategic plan (2016 to 2025) to address key human resources management challenges [16]. As part of the plan, the Ministry and Regional Health Bureaus (RHBs) developed and implemented financial and non-financial incentives to enhance staff retention at all levels of the health system. Financial incentive packages were revised but are pending approval by the Council of Ministers [15]. Furthermore, efforts were made to create a conducive work environment for health workers by promoting teamwork, communication, and trust among staff and leaders. While professional development and promotion initiatives were established for all health workforce across the country during the strategic plan implementation period, they have not been implemented consistently across all professions and regional states [17]. The Ministry of Health and RHBs regularly reviewed career structures for all health professionals. Additionally, the health sector, in collaboration with development partners, universities, and health science colleges, conducted motivation and retention studies to assess the extent of staff retention issues. Though staff turnover remains a concern, the ministry worked closely with development partners, NGOs, donor agencies, higher learning institutions, the private sector, and professional associations to improve staff retention efforts to meet national health sector goals and priorities [15 & 16]. Although these initiatives were expected to strengthen workforce retention, several contextual factors—including the COVID-19 pandemic, inflation, expansion of health professional education and broader socioeconomic changes—occurred during the same period and may also have influenced workforce dynamics. Consequently, changes observed over time cannot be attributed solely to these interventions.

A recent global meta-analysis of 345 studies examining health workers’ intention to leave emphasized the need for robust evaluations of interventions designed to improve workforce retention, particularly among physicians and nurses [18]. In Ethiopia, most published studies have focused on specific professional groups or geographical areas and have primarily estimated the prevalence of turnover intention and associated factors rather than examining changes over time [11]. Consequently, national evidence describing changes in workforce retention indicators following implementation of major HRH policy and programmatic initiatives remains limited. This study examined changes in intention to leave and annual turnover among health professionals in Ethiopia from 2014 to 2022, focusing on doctors, nurses, midwives, anesthetists, and health officers. It also gathered perceptions from health sector stakeholders regarding workforce motivation, retention, and HRH policy interventions during this period. Rather than causal evaluations, the study highlights temporal changes observed during the implementation of Ethiopia’s National Human Resources for Health Strategic Plan within a broader health system and socioeconomic context.

Methods

Study design and settings

Repeated cross-sectional studies employed in 2014 and 2022 to assess the Human Resources for Health (HRH) interventions in reducing intention to leave and turnover rates among health workers serving in public health facilities. The investigators used the same structured questionnaires in both data collection periods to obtain credible and comparable information between the two study periods. In 2022, key informant interviews were conducted to gain deeper insights into potential improvements regarding intention to leave and turnover since 2014. The study included health managers from the Ministry of Health (MOH), Regional Health Bureaus (RHBs), as well as heads of public hospitals and health centers who participated in the interviews. Participants should have work experience in the health sectors to share their experience in perceived improvements and related challenges of HRH interventions on health workers’ job satisfaction, motivation and retention.

Study population

The studies conducted in 2014 and 2022 focused on public health sector workers in Ethiopia. In 2014, the study included five categories of healthcare professionals: medical doctors, midwives, anesthetists, nurses, and health officers. In 2022, the scope was expanded to encompass pharmacists and medical laboratory professionals based on the MOH’s recommendation. In 2014, the estimated population of targeted health professionals included 1,544 medical doctors, 38,916 nurses, 5,203 midwives, 417 anesthetists, and 4,782 health officers across 3,465 public health facilities [19]. By 2022, the number of health professionals had significantly increased to 17,665 doctors, 103,107 nurses, 23,333 midwives, 1,879 anesthetists, 33,172 health officers, 20,673 medical laboratory professionals and technicians, and 32,374 pharmacists and pharmacy technicians across 4,207 public health facilities. (health center and hospitals) [20].

Sampling

A two-stage cluster sampling design was employed to ensure a nationally representative sample for both studies. Both studies aimed to achieve a 95% confidence level with a 5% margin of error, a design effect of 1.2 and a 10% non-response rate. The sample sizes were different for the two studies based on the availability of prior data on job satisfaction levels. In 2014, with no existing national data, a sample size of 1,356 health workers were included by assuming a 50% job satisfaction rate. In 2022, using the job satisfaction data from the 2014 study, which were 41.6% at hospitals and 60.2% at health centers [21], the calculated sample size increased to 1,712 health workers. In 2014, sixteen health workers were randomly selected per facility for interviews. In 2022, the sample size varied based on facility type: fourteen health workers were randomly selected per hospital (two from each group of health professionals), while six were targeted per health center. Due to their limited numbers, all anesthetists working in hospitals were included in both studies. The selection of health facilities also followed a random process using the lottery method. The 2014 study included 224 facilities (107 hospitals and 117 health centers), while the 2022 study encompassed 176 facilities (82 hospitals and 94 health centers). In 2022, the number of hospitals and HCs were distributed into various regions based on the regions’ relative size of health professionals. Hospitals within each region were stratified into three categories (primary, general and referral). The regional sample was then allocated to these categories, with due consideration paid to the number of each category of hospitals in each region (Fig. 1).

Key informant interviews

In 2022, qualitative key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted to complement the quantitative findings and explore stakeholders’ perspectives on changes in health worker motivation, retention, and factors influencing intention to leave during implementation of Ethiopia’s 2016–2025 Human Resources for Health (HRH) Strategic Plan. Using purposive sampling, 42 participants, including leaders and human resource managers, health workers from 32 health facilities, and nine Regional Health Bureaus—were selected based on their roles and participation in planning, implementing, supervising, or being affected by HRH interventions.

Data collection tools and procedures

We used a job satisfaction and motivation assessment tool that was developed by Management Science for Health (MSH). The tool was tested in African countries and used in Ethiopia to conduct a study of health professionals’ job satisfaction and motivation in 2014 across all regions of the country, as well as in other similar studies in Ethiopia [21, 22, 23, 24]. Participants’ responses to the direct question about intention to leave their jobs in the coming year were documented as a yes/no answer for each respondent. The tool also consisted of 20 items related to the decision to leave jobs. The rate at which health professionals leave their work per year was measured by the ratio “a/b,” where a equals the number of health professionals (total or particular health professional) who have left the health facility in the studied Ethiopian budget year (July 2021 to June 2022) and b equals the number of health professionals (total or particular health professional category included in the current study) that the health facility had at the beginning of July 2021 (the first month of the fiscal year). A similar measurement was also applied in the 2014 study.

To gain deeper insights into Human Resources (HR) practices related to intention to leave and turnover, a pre-tested semi-structured interview guide focused on perceived improvements and changes in the factors triggering health workers to leave their jobs was used.

All questionnaires were translated into local languages, Amharic and Afan Oromo, to ensure participant comprehension. Data collectors were trained in both quantitative and qualitative data collection methods, quality assurance procedures, and ethical considerations. They ensured minimal disruption to patient care during the data collection process. Data collectors explained the study’s objectives and obtained written informed consent from participants before proceeding. Face-to-face interviews were conducted in private settings to maintain participant confidentiality. Data were captured electronically using the Kobo Toolbox. Key informant interviews utilized audio recordings and field notes for data capture. The study team provided daily oversight of data collection procedures to ensure quality. Interviews were conducted at mutually agreed times using a pre-tested semi-structured interview guide by trained qualitative data collectors under close supervision after obtaining informed consent.

Data analysis

The data collected through the Kobo Toolbox was exported to SPSS version 25 for descriptive and inferential statistical analysis. Mean and standard deviation were calculated alongside percentages to check data consistency and completeness.

We calculated and compared the proportion of study participants who said they intended to leave their job in the upcoming year in the two study points. We summarized the levels of agreement of health professionals on 20 factors influencing their decision to leave, using percentages. Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory identifies and analyzes factors that contribute to employee satisfaction (motivating factors) and factors that lead to dissatisfaction (demotivating factors) in the workplace. Factors that contribute to satisfaction include company policies, work environment, supervision, etc. On the other hand, factors that lead to dissatisfaction have a direct impact on employee turnover [25]. This study categorized intention-to-leave factors into seven sub-scales: Living Conditions (7 items), Limited Professional Development Opportunities (3 items), Poor Work Climate Factors (3 items), Limited Social Capital at the Workplace (2 items), Inadequate Supervisory Support (2 items), High Work Pressure (2 items), and Low Basic Salary (1 item). Composite mean scores were calculated for each subscale, ranging from 1 (not important) to 5 (extremely important). The outcome variable intention to leave was stratified by facility type (health center/hospital) and by health workers background characteristics. An independent sample t-test was used to compare the intention to leave and the mean score of each factor to identify changes between the two study periods. In addition, annual staff turnover data per facility type and per health professional category were calculated and compared. Furthermore, we performed multivariable logistic regressions for the two study periods and calculated effect size by subtracting the odds ratio from 2022 to 2014 for each factor (independent variable) to identify contributing factors for the reduction of intention to leave their job.

A thematic approach was employed for qualitative data analysis. This process involved a meticulous examination of interview data, followed by coding, organization, reduction, and interpretation. The researchers utilized a hybrid coding approach, incorporating both pre-determined codes and codes that emerged from the data analysis. The data was entered into NVivo 12 software to facilitate analysis. The qualitative analysis was led by the principal investigator (JM) and reviewed collaboratively with ZA and FA to enhance the consistency and credibility of the findings. Because the qualitative component was designed to obtain perspectives from key administrative, managerial levels involved in HRH planning and implementation, and health workers participant selection was guided by representation rather than statistical sampling. Interviews continued until sufficient information had been obtained to address the study objectives and no substantially new themes emerged during the final interviews.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to strict ethical guidelines. Approval was obtained from both Institutional Review Boards of the Ethiopian Public Health Institute and Johns Hopkins University. The Ministry of Health (MoH) provided support letters to facilitate data collection.

Prior to participation, all eligible individuals received detailed information regarding the study objectives, potential benefits, and any associated risks. Throughout the data collection process, participant confidentiality and privacy were maintained. Data collectors interacted with participants in a non-judgmental and empathetic manner, ensuring a clear understanding of their voluntary participation rights, including the right to refuse or withdraw from the study at any time.

Respondent Characteristics and Intention to Leave in 2014 Vs. 2022

The data shows a significant decrease in the intention to leave their job among all health workers by 32% points (95%CI = 28.8%, 35.2%, p < 0.001). In 2014, 49.9% of male health workers and 48.3% of female health workers reported intending to leave their jobs. These figures dropped to 18.4% and 15.0% in 2022, respectively. Similarly, the intention to leave decreased across facility types, falling from 47.3% in 2014 to 17.9% in 2022 in hospitals, and from 51.4% in 2014 to 16.1% in 2022 among health center staff.

The study also revealed a significant decrease in intention to leave current jobs among all five assessed professional categories (medical doctors, health officers, nurses, midwives, and anesthetists) from 49% in 2014 to 17% in 2022: medical doctors (47.5% to 28.1%), midwives (43.5% to 14.8%), anesthetists (48.2% to 13.1%), nurses (50.2% to 10.8%), and health officers (61.4% to 19.5%) between 2014 and 2022. Medical doctors and health officers consistently reported higher intention to leave in both 2014 and 2022 studies. On another note, the intention to leave decreased among those with undergraduate qualifications, but increased from 26.5% in 2014 to 31.7% in 2022 among participants with postgraduate training.

Perceived changes in factors contributing to health workers’ intention to leave their jobs between 2014 and 2022 studies

The significance of the diverse factors that health professionals consider when deciding to leave their positions is presented (Table 2). Among the many, low pay, high cost of living and poor access to higher education emerged as the top reasons underlying intention to leave their job. In 2014, the top 7 reasons were low pay, limited promotion, poor access to higher education, concerns about workplace safety, lack of recognition, limited in-service training, and high cost of living. In the 2022 study, the top 7 reasons were low pay, high cost of living, concerns about workplace safety, poor access to education, limited opportunities for promotion, lack of housing and unfair treatment by supervisors.

Respondents said that there has been some improvement in the availability of educational opportunities. However, they noted that they noted that opportunities increased only for medical doctors and health extension workers in recent years.

In the qualitative component, respondents highlighted the need for improvement in supervisory support at all levels of the health system, despite acknowledging the existing leadership commitment and support. They also pointed out that staff recognition does not encompass all departments and that efforts in this area have declined over the past four years. Additionally, the reporting of medical errors in various media outlets has led to a decline in trust in health professionals and the healthcare sector as a whole. It is recommended that the sector focus on rebuilding trust within communities.

Comparison of mean scores of importance of factors for making decisions to leave jobs by socio-demographic and facility characteristics

An analysis of the mean scores reflecting the perceived significance of different factors that influence healthcare professionals’ decision to resign from their positions, organized by the specific types of health professionals is presented (Table 3) The top three factors with the largest change in their importance to make decisions for leaving jobs in 2014 and 2022 studies included: limited professional development opportunities (mean score decreased from 4.1 in 2014 to 2.9 in 2022), supervisory support (mean score decreased from 3.5 in 2014 to 2.6 in 2022) and work climate (mean score decreased from 3.7 in 2014 to 2.9 in 2022). Mean scores for the importance of the factor to leave jobs among medical doctors decreased from 4.0 in 2014 to 2.9 in 2022, and among midwives, the mean score decreased from 4.1 in 2014 to 3.1 in 2022.

Respondents for qualitative interviews indicated some level of improvement in dealing with implementation challenges related to factors influencing decisions to leave, such as in-service training, duty payment, and risk and hardship allowances. Though transportation is still a challenge in many facilities but participants from Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa indicated that the facility arranged transportation service for the staff or created a system that enables health workers to use free public transport, which has helped them to save money, respond to inflation, and arrive at workplaces on time. Key informants from the 2022 study emphasized a relatively better enhancement in professional development opportunities for health extension workers and general practitioners (GPs). Notably, specialty programs for GPs in Addis Ababa, SNNP, Adama, and Afar regions have conspicuously contributed to increased retention among these professionals. They also indicated that these efforts should be made available for other health professions as well in more transparent and accountable manner. Participants also noted that the health sector should work more to avail further professional development opportunities for all health workers. Moreover, health professionals and HR managers reported improvements in supervisory support, although they noted that these supports were implemented inconsistently across the health sector.

Factors contributing to the variation of intention to leave jobs in 2014 and 2022 studies

The findings from the analysis examining how various factors impact health workers’ intentions to leave their positions, as explored in both the 2014 and 2022 studies, reveal important insights into the evolving dynamics of job satisfaction and retention in the healthcare sector (Table 4). The multivariable logistic regression analysis reveals that inadequate professional development opportunities remained statistically significant across both studies, with influences on the reduction of intention to leave with an effect size (difference in AOR) of 0.01. In 2014, health workers with limited professional development opportunities were 1.24 times more likely to intend to leave (AOR of 1.24, with 95% CI [1.07, 1.45]). This association remained significant in 2022, (AOR of 1.23 and 95% CI [1.02, 1.50]). Poor Living conditions emerged as a significant factor influencing intention to leave among health workers in the 2014 study only. Health workers facing poor living conditions were 1.24 times more likely to intend to leave (AOR of 1.24 with 95%, CI [1.06, 1.46]). However, this association was not statistically significant in the 2022 data. It is important to note that under poor living conditions, the cost of living was identified as an important factor by 94% for health workers to leave their jobs in 2022. During the qualitative interviews with participants in Addis Ababa, it was found that the city administration health bureau exempted income tax on the health professionals’ salaries during the COVID-19 pandemic, which served as an additional source of motivation. However, participants also reported that their salaries have not kept pace with the level of service they provide. This has made it difficult to cover basic expenses due to inflation, leaving many unable to afford necessities like food. Respondents also indicated that there were issues with the poor management and implementation of certain allowances, such as risk and hardship allowances. It was further noted that there is a large variation in the rates of risk allowances among different professions, and some professionals do not receive the allowance at all.

Work pressure was also a significant factor for health workers to leave their jobs in the 2014 study with (an AOR of 0.90 (95% CI [0.81, 0.99]). However, this relationship was not statistically significant in the 2022 study. Similarly, Social capital at workplaces and supervisory support also exhibited a potentially diminished influence on intention to leave among health workers in the 2022 data compared to 2014.

The impact of work climate factors on the decisions of health workers to leave jobs has decreased, with the average scoring dropping from 3.7 in 2014 to 2.9 in 2022. Among the work climate factors, the percentage of study respondents affected by inadequate access to supplies and equipment slightly decreased from 89.4% in 2014 to 81.2% in 2022. Interviews with key informants confirmed these improvements, with participants noting consistent availability of medical supplies due to efforts by the Ethiopian Pharmaceuticals and Supply Services (EPSS) and increased budget allocations. However, while managers indicated that health facilities have sufficient equipment, healthcare professionals reported a shortage of personal protective equipment (PPE) and some other medical supplies.

Annual turnover of health professionals working in health facilities in 2014 and 2022

The overall annual turnover rate among health professionals in this study was 3.5% in 2022. Comparing the findings with the 2014 study, the annual turnover rate reduced by 1% (4.5% in 2014 to 3.5% in 2022; p < 0.001) among medical doctors, health officers, nurses, midwives, and anesthetists. This study identified a statistically significant decrease in turnover rates between 2014 and 2022 for medical doctors (1.9%), nurses (1.1%), and anesthetists (5.2%). The turnover rates for midwives and health officers, however, did not change significantly (Table 5). Turnover was much higher among doctors (7.5%) and anesthetists (6.1%), and lowest among nurses (2.5%).

Discussion

This repeated cross-sectional mixed-methods study compared national estimates of health workers’ intention to leave and annual turnover between 2014 and 2022 and explored stakeholders’ perceptions of workforce retention during the implementation of Ethiopia’s 2016–2025 Human Resources for Health (HRH) Strategic Plan. Overall, substantial reductions were observed in both intention to leave and annual turnover among the selected health professional groups. Qualitative findings also suggested perceived improvements in several financial and non-financial retention strategies, including training opportunities, supportive supervision, and workplace incentive packages. However, these findings should be interpreted within the broader health system context. During the study period, Ethiopia experienced several major contextual changes, including the COVID-19 pandemic, increasing inflation and living costs, expansion of health professional education, regional insecurity, and changing labour market opportunities, all of which may also have influenced health workforce dynamics. Therefore, given the repeated cross-sectional design, the observed changes should be interpreted as temporal changes during the implementation period rather than direct effects of specific HRH policy and programmatic interventions. The observed reduction in intention to leave between 2014 and 2022 is encouraging and suggests improvements in retention of workforce over time. However, the observed changes should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect the combined influence of HRH policy and programmatic initiatives together with broader contextual changes occurring during the study period. Our 2022 estimate is considerably lower than the pooled national prevalence of 58.9% reported in a recent systematic review conducted in Ethiopia [14]. This difference is likely explained by variations in study populations, professional cadres, geographical coverage and study periods. In addition, a recent mixed-methods study conducted among nurses in Ethiopia similarly identified remuneration, professional development opportunities, organizational support and working conditions as important determinants of health worker retention, supporting the relevance of these factors in the Ethiopian context [26]. This study found that health professionals’ (medical doctors, health officers, nurses, midwives, and anesthetists) intention to leave their current jobs decreased significantly. The estimates in the two study periods are notably lower than the pooled national average of 58.9% reported in a previous study [14]., Our findings in 2022 align more closely with studies from other low- and middle-income countries. The intention to leave the rate of 18% observed in Uganda [27] and 18.8% in Tanzania [28] are comparable to our results. In contrast, a study from Israel [29] reported a much lower intention to leave rate (9%). These variations likely reflect differences in work climate, living conditions, and other factors that could be more favorable in developed nations like Israel compared to our study setting.

In both 2014 and 2022 studies health workers indicated that high cost of living and inadequate educational facilities for children remain significant factors within the category of living conditions that influence health workers’ decisions to leave their jobs. These findings align with other studies in Ethiopia [22, 30 & 31].

Participants perceived improvements in selected financial and non-financial incentive packages, including duty payments, hardship allowances and risk allowances. These findings are consistent with studies from Ethiopia [22, 23, 33], Zimbabwe [34] and Ukraine [35], which demonstrate that both financial and non-financial incentives contribute to improved workforce motivation and retention. Nevertheless, respondents consistently reported that salaries had not kept pace with rising living costs, indicating that additional efforts are needed to improve the overall competitiveness of health sector remuneration. Limited opportunities for professional development emerged as a significant factor influencing the intention to leave jobs in both of our studies. Under this category, a high proportion (92%) of health workers emphasized the value of educational opportunities as a reason to stay in their current positions. These findings align with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia [23, 24, 36, 37] as well as another study encompassing seven Sub-Saharan African countries [38].

Educational and professional development opportunities remained among the strongest determinants of health workers’ intention to leave in both surveys. Although respondents acknowledged efforts by the Ministry of Health and Regional Health Bureaus to expand access to postgraduate education and career development during implementation of the HRH Strategic Plan, participants also reported persistent inequities across professional groups and regions. Continued investment in transparent and equitable career development pathways is therefore likely to remain important for workforce retention. This finding aligns with similar studies in Ethiopia [14, 23& 32], Zimbabwe [34], Kenya [39] Ghana [40] and Ukraine [35]. In the 2022 study, except for medical doctors, other healthcare professionals have experienced a decrease in opportunities, particularly for pursuing master’s degrees. Respondents from the Regional Health Bureaus (RHBs) have emphasized efforts to improve access to higher education for the entire health workforce. Addressing issues like this in a timely manner will enhance the retention of health workers in the country.

Our study aligns with previous studies done in Ethiopia [33, 41 & 42], in Kenya [39] and Jordan [43] in demonstrating the significant influence of workplace factors on health professionals’ intention to leave. Participants perceived improvements in workplace conditions, including greater availability of selected medical supplies, equipment and supportive management practices. However, deficiencies in essential equipment, infrastructure and workplace safety remained evident in several facilities, suggesting that further investments are required to ensure a consistently supportive working environment across the health system.

Social capital in the workplace encompasses interconnectedness, networking, trust among staff, and recognition for good work to retain high-performing employees [44]. Both the 2014 and 2022 studies identified a link between social capital and intention to leave jobs among health workers. This finding aligns with other studies conducted in Ethiopia [31, 45], Ghana [40], and China [9]. Some medical equipment and other safety tools are not adequately available at the study sites. This suggests further efforts are needed to ensure all necessary equipment is readily available and facilitate high-quality healthcare delivery.

Our study revealed that “lack of recognition for good work” is a significant factor influencing employees’ intention to leave their jobs. Despite its strong impact, the implementation of recognition practices remains inconsistent across facilities and regions in the country. Our findings are consistent with other studies in Ethiopia [41, 42], Kenya [40] and Nigeria [46]. These findings highlight the importance of effective recognition programs in reducing staff turnover. Recognition of good performance remained inconsistent across facilities despite being strongly associated with health workers’ intention to leave. Developing standardized national guidance for staff recognition and performance appreciation may help strengthen employee motivation and organizational commitment. These findings are consistent with the mid-term evaluation findings of the national HRH strategic plan for 2016–2025 [17].

Our findings identified supportive supervision as an important factor associated with health workers’ intention to leave, consistent with studies from Saudi Arabia [47] and Japan [48]. Although many previous studies in Ethiopia have primarily focused on estimating the prevalence of intention to leave rather than examining specific workplace determinants, our findings highlight the importance of regular supervisory support, constructive feedback, and responsive management in promoting workforce retention. Compared with the 2014 survey, both the quantitative and qualitative findings suggested improvements in supervisory practices during the implementation period of the National Human Resources for Health (HRH) Strategic Plan. However, given the repeated cross-sectional design, these improvements cannot be attributed solely to HRH interventions. Qualitative interviews revealed considerable variation in supervisory practices across facilities. While some managers were reported to provide regular supportive supervision and timely feedback, others conducted supervision infrequently and did not consistently follow up on identified challenges. Participants also noted that frequent turnover of health facility managers disrupted continuity of supervision, as newly appointed leaders often required time to understand local contexts and establish effective working relationships with staff. These findings underscore the need to institutionalize standardized, supportive supervision practices and strengthen leadership continuity to improve health worker motivation and retention across the health system. A high intention to leave among healthcare workers poses a major challenge to the healthcare system, as it can lead to actual staff turnover. However, our study found a statistically significant decrease in annual turnover rates among medical doctors, nurses, and anesthetists, despite the persistently high intention to leave. This positive trend aligns with the findings from Senegal [49] and Ghana [50]. However, despite recent reductions, Ethiopia’s healthcare system still faces significant challenges due to high overall turnover rates among health professionals. Health centers are particularly impacted, further straining an already stretched workforce density and hindering progress towards achieving universal health coverage (UHC) for the nation. The prevalence of intention to leave among health professionals remains even more concerning than the actual turnover statistics. These intentions have significant implications for a country striving to meet the minimum health workforce needs at each facility and for health workers aiming to deliver high-quality care to their communities. Ultimately, achieving UHC in Ethiopia remains a distant goal if these staffing issues are not addressed.

Limitations of the study

This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the repeated cross-sectional design allows comparison of workforce indicators over time but does not permit causal inference regarding the effects of HRH policy and programmatic interventions. The observed changes may also have been influenced by broader contextual factors, including the COVID-19 pandemic and socioeconomic changes during the study period. Second, although nationally representative, the number of physicians recruited from health centers was lower than planned, and laboratory and pharmacy professionals were only included in the 2022 survey, limiting some comparisons. Third, intention to leave represents a behavioral intention rather than actual turnover and may have been influenced by social desirability bias despite measures to ensure confidentiality and standardized data collection. Finally, the qualitative component was designed to capture the perspectives of health facility leaders, managers, and health workers involved in or affected by the planning and implementation of HRH interventions, rather than to represent the views of all health workers. However, triangulation with the quantitative findings strengthened the interpretation and credibility of the results.

Conclusion

This repeated cross-sectional mixed-methods study found substantial reductions in health workers’ intention to leave and annual turnover between 2014 and 2022, alongside perceived improvements in selected financial and non-financial incentives, supportive supervision, and workplace conditions during the implementation of Ethiopia’s Human Resources for Health (HRH) Strategic Plan. However, given the study design, these findings should be interpreted as temporal changes rather than direct effects of specific interventions. Despite these improvements, workforce retention remains a major challenge, with low salaries, rising living costs, limited career development opportunities, inadequate housing, and inconsistent supportive supervision continuing to influence health workers’ decisions to leave. Sustained investment in financial and non-financial retention strategies, strengthened HRH policy implementation, and regular health labour market monitoring are essential to improve workforce retention and advance progress towards Universal Health Coverage.
