Ionolysis of flexible polyurethane waste using ionic liquids for efficient and sustainable polyol recovery
Authors: Sajid Hussain, Gabriele Rossignolo, Filippo Bombarda, Diego Penzo, Muhammad Haris, Carlo Boaretti, Martina Roso, Michele Modesti, Alessandra Lorenzetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chemical recycling of polyurethane waste is essential for advancing circular economy objectives, as current technologies struggle to efficiently recover monomeric building blocks. Here, we present an efficient ionolysis-based recycling approach using 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bromide (EMIM-Br) as bifunctional solvent-catalyst. Under mild conditions (150°C, 1 MPa), the ionic liquid disrupts hydrogen bonding and selectively cleaves urethane linkages, yielding virgin-like polyols. Polyols are efficiently separated from the reaction mixture, and the ionic liquid is readily recycled. EMIM-Br achieved a 94% depolymerization yield and 73% polyol recovery. Physicochemical analysis confirmed that the recovered polyols closely match commercial standards. Notably, incorporation of 25% recycled polyol into new formulations produced flexible PU foams with viscoelastic properties comparable to those of the reference material. This work offers a sustainable and scalable strategy for polyurethane foam recycling, addressing key limitations in current technologies and supporting resource-efficient materials development.

Introduction

Polyurethane (PU) is one of the most widely used polymers in the world and consequently, with a large annual production, i.e., 28 million metric tons of PU was produced in 20201. Polyurethanes are mainly used as insulators, foams, elastomers, coatings, and adhesives2,3. Polyurethane foams (PUF) are generally classified into two main categories, i.e., rigid and flexible foams4. The high global demand and wide range of applications of PUF has resulted in the generation of large volumes of PUF waste5. Disposal and handling large quantities of waste polymers, especially PUF is a great challenge. The conventional techniques for solid disposal, such as landfilling, are not suitable for PU waste management because it has low density, making its transportation and disposal in landfills more challenging. Further, the degradation of PU is an extremely slow process6. Globally, approximately 2 million tons of PU are landfilled every year that can lead to the release of toxic organic pollutants such as aromatic amines, which can contaminate soil and groundwater, creating another environmental challenge7–9. Keeping in view of these logistical and environmental implications, it is critically important that the PUF are recycled in a sustainable way10. The PU waste recycling can be divided into four categories: mechanical, chemical, thermochemical, and energy recovery11.

Mechanical recycling reduces the particle size of polyurethane (PU) waste, facilitating reprocessing; however, the thermosetting nature of PUF restricts its recyclability and mechanical performance12. Although various attempts have been made to mechanically recycle PUF, the resulting material generally exhibits poor mechanical properties and limited applicability13. Thermo-chemical methods such as pyrolysis and gasification can convert PU waste into fuels; however, significant energy loss and the generation of hazardous emissions undermine their feasibility14,15. Likewise, incineration releases toxic compounds such as isocyanates, hydrocyanic acid, and dioxins, presenting significant environmental and health hazards16. Biodegradation of PU by enzymes, bacteria, and fungi can cleave ester bonds; however, the process is inherently slow and strongly influenced by the polymer’s crystallinity and degree of cross-linking17. Therefore, further developments are still needed to achieve sustainable and efficient biodegradation of PUFs. Recently, a new class of enzymes has been identified that can effectively hydrolyze urethane bonds18, thus addressing one of the major limitations which is traditionally associated with the enzymatic depolymerization of PUFs19–21.

Chemical recycling is the most viable strategy, as it depolymerizes PU into valuable polyols for recycled PUF production, supporting a circular economy11. Chemical recycling techniques include hydrolysis, acidolysis, alcoholysis, aminolysis, and glycolysis12,22–26.

Hydrolysis is a cost-effective technique for the depolymerization of PUF because pressurized steam is used to hydrolyze the urethane bonds and thus polyols and amines are released27. However, separation and purification of polyols from the reaction mixture reduces the economic feasibility of the process as recovered polyols must be processed for the removal of aqueous phase, which can significantly increase the energy intensiveness of the process28. Moreover, hydrolysis is also known to trigger the production of aromatic amines in the reaction system, which are an environmental hazard due to their carcinogenic nature29. Further, the hydrolyzed products can only be used for the production of rigid foams due to higher hydroxyl values (OHV), which further limits the application of recovered polyols30. Alternatively, PUF can also be depolymerized through acidolysis which primarily employs carboxylic acids as a cleavage agent, and it degrades PUF to yield polyols31. Though the process can be carried out at lower temperature and atmospheric pressure, but the reaction mixture is comprised of unwanted substances such as amine salts, amides, and oligo urea13 and retrieving the desired polyols from this mixture is a challenging task as numerous separation processes are needed, undermining the processing and economic feasibility of the process24. Aminolysis has also been employed for the depolymerization of PU foams, which is carried out using hydroxyl and amino-derived compounds. Similar to acidolysis, the process can be run under milder reaction conditions, but the recovered polyols have limited applications, i.e., hardener for epoxy resin32,33. Moreover, difficulty in the separation of amines from the reaction system also makes it a non-viable solution for the recycling of PUF33. Polyurethanes can also be recycled through glycolysis, which is considered the most promising process because the recovered polyols do not require any post-processing, as the employed glycols can also serve as building blocks in the production of recycled polyurethanes34,35. However, the process requires large volumes of glycols, and the nature of these glycols has a great influence on the glycolyzed product and finally obtained recycled PUF36. Further, glycolysis is generally carried out at elevated temperatures, and prolonged reaction times are needed to depolymerize PUF. This can lead to the release of toxic and hazardous aromatic amines. Moreover, the elevated temperature can also degrade the parent, high molecular weight polyols into smaller molecular weight fragments, which may not be suitable for the production of flexible PUF37.

Besides, the depolymerized product obtained from chemical recycling of PUF is either directly reused in recycled PUF production or subjected to phase separation to recover polyols. However, the complex composition of the reaction mixture, comprising of oligomers, polyols, partially cleaved urethane fragments, urea/polyurea, aromatic amines, and aliphatic amines, etc., limits the proportion of recovered material that can be effectively reincorporated into recycled PUF. Furthermore, the recovered polyols typically exhibit low purity, primarily due to elevated concentrations of aromatic amines, making it challenging to precisely control the properties of the recycled foam. These limitations highlight the need for a recycling process that enables efficient PU depolymerization and the recovery of high-purity polyols. Additionally, the process should minimize the formation of undesirable byproducts, such as aromatic amines, by favoring a reaction pathway that produces hydroxyl-terminated oligomers. These oligomers not only reduce byproduct formation but also facilitate better integration with virgin materials during PUF synthesis, improving the consistency and performance of the final product. In view of these challenges, it is essential to develop a PUF depolymerization process that operates under milder conditions than conventional methods, such as hydrolysis and glycolysis, while enabling the recovery of polyols that closely resemble virgin materials, thereby facilitating the production of recycled flexible PUF with preserved properties.

Ionic liquids (ILs) are unique chemicals which are entirely composed of bulky ions, thus enabling them to exhibit remarkable solvent and catalytic properties under mild, near-ambient conditions. Their negligible vapor pressure, high thermal stability, wide temperature window, and tunable physicochemical characteristics make them ideal candidates for the chemical recycling of polymers38–40. ILs can efficiently depolymerize polymers under milder conditions by selectively breaking their chemical bonds. By enabling closed-loop recycling, ILs present a sustainable alternative to conventional recycling methods, aligning with the principles of green chemistry and the circular economy41. Their potential to dissolve and process a wide range of polymers, including challenging materials such as cross-linked/ thermosetting polymers, makes them a promising media for sustainable polymer waste management. Conventional chemical recycling of PUFs typically involves the use of a solvent as a bond-cleaving agent, combined with an appropriate catalyst to promote depolymerization. However, the separation of these components from the reaction mixture remains a significant challenge, often compromising the overall feasibility and economic viability of the recycling process. The ILs offer a promising alternative, as they can function simultaneously as both the solvent and the catalyst for the depolymerization of polymers42–45. Moreover, ILs can be conveniently separated from the reaction mixture and recycled, contributing to the development of more sustainable recycling processes while also promoting the production of more uniform recycled products. Despite their potential, the application of ILs in polymer depolymerization has so far been limited, primarily being explored either as co-catalysts or as solvents/co-solvents, rather than being fully utilized as integrated solvent-catalyst systems41,46–51.

For the first time, we report a novel and simple recycling strategy for flexible PUF waste through Ionolysis, wherein a single ionic liquid serves as the depolymerizing medium, thus leveraging the dual functionality of ILs as both solvents and catalysts52–54. Moreover, we have introduced a new class of chemical reactions, “Ionolysis,” which deconstructs the target chemicals (PUF) by the sole action of ions. The ILs functioned simultaneously as a cleavage agent and a catalytic medium, facilitating selective bond scission and promoting depolymerization. Furthermore, unlike previous IL+catalyst systems that rely on multiphase hydrolysis and are susceptible to water contamination and elevated hydroxyl values (OHVs), ionolysis enables the facile recovery of high-quality polyols with OHVs closely matching those of virgin materials. Consequently, it allows their direct reuse in the production of recycled PUF with preserved viscoelastic properties. Since microwave-assisted heating is an energy-efficient technology that has been extensively studied and has already found applications at the industrial scale55–60. This is why microwave-assisted ionolysis of PUF has very high potential for industrial scalability.

Although the reaction of polyols with isocyanates generates urethane linkages, other side reactions also produce unwanted species. Mechanism of PUF depolymerization by ionolysis

The depolymerization of PUF involves a series of mechanisms, namely, disruption of hydrogen bonding, solvation of polar groups in PUF and finally catalytic cleavage of urethane bonds along with chain scission. Polyurethane has very strong hydrogen bonding, and the ILs first disrupt it through the action of its cation and anion. The nitrogen in (EMIM+) has a positive charge, making it a strong electrophile. The N–H group in the urethane linkage contains a hydrogen bond donor which interacts with the nitrogen atom of the imidazolium cation. This electrostatic interaction can weaken the hydrogen bonding between the N–H group in urethane and other polar groups of the polymeric chains, such as C=O, disrupting the hydrogen bonding in the polymeric network. On the contrary, the Br⁻ anion, being a weak base, can interact with N–H group of urethane by accepting hydrogen because it is a weak acid, and this interaction leads to the formation of a weak hydrogen bond52,61–63. This competition for the hydrogen from the urethane linkage can further weaken the hydrogen bonding between the polymer chains, disrupting the PUF structure. Simultaneously, the EMIM-Br solvates the polar functional groups which are present in its structure. These include urethane (-NH–COO), which is highly polar due to the carbonyl (C=O) and amid (N–H), residual hydroxyl (OH) from unreacted polyols, and ether (C–O–C) groups. Once, the polymeric structure of PUF is significantly weakened by the disruption of hydrogen bonding and higher mobility of solvated polymeric chains, the nucleophilic attack of Br- breaks down the urethane bond, leading to the depolymerization of PUF (Fig. 2). Since the reaction medium consists of an imidazolium-based IL, the Br⁻ anion exhibits pronounced nucleophilic character in this environment64,65. Unlike protic solvents, EMIM-Br provides an aprotic and weakly coordinating medium, where Br⁻ is not strongly immobilized through hydrogen bonding with the cation66. This reduced ion pairing allows the Br⁻ anion to remain relatively free and accessible, significantly enhancing its nucleophilic reactivity toward the electrophilic carbonyl carbon in urethane linkages. Crucially, the Br⁻ functions as a nucleophilic catalyst; after facilitating the cleavage of the urethane bond, it is fully regenerated, maintaining the stoichiometric balance and chemical identity of the ionic liquid.Fig. 2Ionolysis mechanism for the depolymerization of PUF.The proposed mechanism highlights how the ionic liquid facilitates depolymerization through the A disruption of hydrogen bonds, B solvation of the polymer chains, and C a targeted nucleophilic attack on the urethane carbonyl groups.

To elucidate the depolymerization mechanism by ionolysis and verify the chemical stability of the IL, detailed ¹H NMR analysis of fresh and recycled EMIM·Br, the reaction mixture, and the recovered polyols are performed (Supplementary Information Supplementary Figs. 1–S4). The spectra reveal distinct spectral changes that collectively confirm the cleavage of urethane linkages and regeneration of polyol chains.

The ¹H NMR spectrum of the fresh EMIM·Br (Supplementary Fig. 1) displays characteristic resonances of the imidazolium cation. The distinct singlet at δ 10.08 ppm corresponds to the C2–H proton of the imidazolium ring, a diagnostic feature of 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium salts. The aromatic signals at δ 7.56 ppm are attributed to the C4–H and C5–H protons of the ring, while signals at δ 4.27 ppm and δ 3.98 ppm correspond to the ethyl (–CH₂CH₃) and N-methyl (–CH₃) substituents, respectively. After the ionolysis process, the recycled IL (Supplementary Fig. 2) retains these characteristic spectral features, confirming its high thermal and chemical robustness. The C2–H signal shifts slightly upfield to δ 9.65 ppm, which can be attributed to subtle changes in the electronic environment arising from transient coordination or hydrogen-bonding interactions with residual polyol intermediates or solvent molecules. These findings provide clear evidence for the dual functionality of the IL during the depolymerization process, which we term ionolysis. This process is characterized by a synergistic interplay between the cation and anion that differs fundamentally from traditional solvolysis (e.g., glycolysis or hydrolysis). The EMIM⁺ cation functions as a non-innocent solvating agent; it disrupts the extensive inter- and intramolecular hydrogen-bonding networks between urethane linkages, effectively “swelling” the polymer matrix and increasing the steric accessibility of the carbamate groups. Concurrently, the Br⁻ anion acts as a potent nucleophile in this aprotic environment. The Br⁻ attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of the urethane group, leading to the formation of transient carbamate-like intermediates and the subsequent release of polyol segments. We use the term ionolysis to specifically describe this bond-cleavage pathway because the “lysis” (splitting) of the polymer backbone is initiated directly by ionic species. This mechanism is supported by our ¹H NMR data (Supplementary Figs. 3-S4), which identifies the specific aromatic amine and carbamate byproducts that result from this direct ionic attack. This process proceeds without the need for external nucleophilic solvents, distinguishing it from conventional catalytic transesterification pathways.

Effect of ionic liquid on ionolysis

The influence of ILs on PUF depolymerization and subsequent polyol recovery from the reaction mixtures was examined, and the results are presented in the Fig. 4. The reason for initially employing six ILs is that the constituent ions greatly influence the properties of an ionic liquid and thus also affect the ionolysis process. Selecting an optimal cation and anion pair is critical for sustainable process development. Since PUF depolymerization by ILs is influenced by both of its constituent ions, i.e., the cation and anion, the results have been discussed accordingly (Fig. 4). It has been observed that as the cation size increases from EMIM to HMIM, both the extent of PUF depolymerization and the resultant polyol recovery decrease (Fig. 4a, b). This is because, as the alkyl chain length increases, the cation becomes larger and bulkier, reducing the nucleophilic reactivity of the anion due to steric hindrance66,67. Additionally, the increasing cation size increases the charge delocalization, thus weakening its ability to interact strongly with the hydrogen bond in the N–H group of urethane68. The reduced ability of larger cations to disrupt hydrogen bonding in urethane leads to decreased PUF depolymerization. On the other hand, the anion also plays a critical role in PUF depolymerization, as it is responsible for cleaving the urethane bond through nucleophilic attack. It has been observed that ILs with a bromide (Br⁻) anion result in higher PUF depolymerization compared to their tetrafluoroborate (BF₄⁻) -based counterparts. The reason is that Br⁻ exhibits stronger nucleophile properties than BF₄⁻ in ionic liquids due to its higher electron density and lower charge delocalization69. Br⁻ readily donates its lone pair for nucleophilic attacks, whereas BF₄⁻ is a weak nucleophile because its negative charge is stabilized by four electronegative fluorine atoms70. The percentage recovery of polyols from the ionolyzed product is significantly influenced by both the cation and anion of the ionic liquid (IL). ILs with bulkier cations, such as BMIM and HMIM, act as better solvents due to their lower melting points. However, this enhanced solvation ability hinders polyol recovery via ethyl acetate washing, as polyols exhibit higher miscibility with these ILs. This trend has been observed when comparing polyol recovery efficiencies across different ILs under identical ethyl acetate washing conditions. Similarly, ILs with Br⁻ exhibit higher melting points compared to BF₄⁻-based ILs, and their relatively narrow liquid-phase temperature window facilitates more efficient polyol recovery71,72. This is because their melting points align closely with the boiling point of ethyl acetate, enabling effective separation even at elevated temperatures. In contrast, BF₄⁻-based ILs, with their lower melting points and stronger affinity for polyols, hinder recovery, as increased temperatures further enhance polyol miscibility, ultimately suppressing polyol recovery efficiency72.

These findings are further supported by the Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra of the ionolyzed products obtained from different ILs (Fig. 4c, d). The extent of polyol recovery was qualitatively assessed by monitoring the intensity of -OH stretching vibrations in the range of 3200–3600 cm⁻¹. The results indicate that EMIM-Br facilitated the highest release of polyols into the reaction mixture. Likewise, the degree of PUF depolymerization was evaluated by analyzing the C=O stretching vibrations associated with urethane bonds in the range of 1700–1750 cm⁻¹. The lower intensity of this band was observed for EMIM-Br, confirming its superior efficiency in PUF ionolysis. Furthermore, the ionolyzed product obtained from EMIM-Br exhibited relatively lower viscosity, indicating a higher degree of PUF depolymerization. Moreover, EMIM-Br also produced an ionolyzed product with a relatively higher hydroxyl value (OHV) compared to the other ionic liquids, indicating an enhanced release of hydroxyl-containing polyols in the mixture (Fig. 4e, f). It is important to note that the extent of depolymerization is higher than polyol recovery due to several factors. One major factor is the production of polyols with high miscibility in ionic liquids (ILs), which hinders their efficient extraction using ethyl acetate and thus leads to a partial loss of polyols in the solvent itself. Additionally, side reactions, particularly carbamate formation, contribute to the reduced polyol recovery by altering the chemical composition of the depolymerized products73.

Ionolysis of PUF with EMIM-Br and recycling of IL

EMIM-Br⁻ achieved the highest PUF depolymerization efficiency (94%) and polyol recovery yield (73%) and this is why all subsequent experiments were conducted exclusively with this ionic liquid for polyol recovery and the preparation of recycled flexible PUF. Moreover, the employed IL has been recycled thrice and is characterized by FTIR (Fig. 3a). It has been observed that the fresh IL exhibits characteristic transmittance bands which correspond to its molecular structure74. On the other hand, the recycled IL has shown additional reflectance bands, indicating the presence of impurities acquired during PUF depolymerization. For instance, the increased intensity of the reflectance band at 3200–3300 cm-1, corresponding to N–H stretching vibrations, indicates the presence of aromatic amines which are produced during the depolymerization process75. Similarly, the appearance of new bands near 1700 cm⁻¹ (C=O stretching of urethane and urea), 1510 cm⁻¹ related to N–H bending, and 1200 cm⁻¹ associated with C–N stretching, suggests the presence of polyurethane degradation products such as urea, amines, and isocyanate-derived fragments76,77. In addition, GPC analysis of the recycled IL Fig. 3c revealed the absence of any high-molecular-weight species, confirming that the IL remains chemically stable throughout the reaction and does not undergo degradation or interact with polyurethane fragments.

The purity of the recovered polyols was also characterized and compared with the ionolyzed product, as shown in Fig. 3b. It has been observed that the ionolyzed product has higher intensity of reflectance bands, compared to that of recovered polyols. For instance, the band appearing at 3200–3600 cm−1 which corresponds to -OH stretching vibrations of polyols, suggests that the ionolyzed product has a higher concentration of released polyols, but only high molecular weight (MW) polyols are extractable from the mixture, as evidenced by the reduced intensity of peaks observed in the recovered polyols. Similarly, the ionolyzed product has higher band signal intensity at 3100–3200 cm−1, associated with N–H stretching vibrations of aromatic amines. However, a lower band signal has been observed in the case of recovered polyols which stipulates that ethyl acetate not only retrieves high MW polyols but also limits the transfer of unwanted aromatic amines into the polyol phase. Likewise, the recovered polyols are also free from any unwanted polyurethane degradation products which are present in the ionolyzed product, as reflected by the reduced intensity of band signal at 1720 cm−1. However, the dark color of recycled polyol might be due to its partial oxidation, which is a common phenomenon, especially during distillation and storage phases.

The distribution of molecular weights in the recovered polyols has been analyzed by GPC, and results are presented in Fig. 3c. It has been observed that the major fraction of recovered polyols is comprised of high MW polyols, i.e., MW-5778 g mol−1 along with minute fractions of low MW polyols. The average MW of the recovered polyols is determined by considering all molecular weight fractions and is 5410 g mol−1 which is slightly lower than virgin polyols, stipulating the effectiveness of ionolysis process in producing virgin like polyols. Comparative evaluation of foams

Finally, virgin and recycled polyols have been utilized for the preparation of reference and recycled flexible PUFs. These foams are first characterized by scanning electron microscope (SEM) to compare their morphologies (Fig. 5). The results clearly indicate that the reference foam has homogeneous open cells with uniform pore sizes and well-defined cell walls. On the other hand, the recycled foam is comprised of large and relatively irregular pores, with noticeable cell wall disruptions. This is primarily due to the fact that both polyols had different viscosities, leading to less uniform mixing, thus yielding less uniform cell structure in the flexible PUF. Further, these deformations can also arise due to uneven molecular weight distribution in recovered polyols as evidenced by GPC analysis78,79 (Fig. 3c). Moreover, the presence of aromatic amines in the recovered polyols may have triggered additional catalytic reactions, leading to faster foaming reactions as observed during the foaming process, ultimately limiting the reactants to form uniform cell structures80,81. Despite these morphological variations, the recycled foam still maintains a comparable porous network, exhibiting analogous flexibility which has also been confirmed by Dynamic Mechanical Analysis (DMA) (Fig. 3d). These results suggest that though recycling imparted structural heterogeneity, the fundamental foam properties are sustained due to the synergistic effect of virgin and recycled polyols. To further assess the influence of recycled polyol addition on the viscoelastic properties of foam, DMA is performed. The storage modulus (E’), loss modulus (E”), and damping factor (tan δ) have been analyzed as a function of temperature, and the results are presented in Fig. 3d. First, glass transition temperatures (Tg) of reference and recycled foams are determined from their tan δ peaks, measured at −50 and −55 °C, respectively. The slightly lower Tg for the recycled foam indicates minor disruptions in phase separations between its soft and hard segments, induced by the addition of recycled polyols79. These microphase modifications result in morphology changes, influencing the thermal and mechanical behavior of the foam. Moreover, it has also been observed that both foams have shown a characteristic drop in E’ near Tg, indicating the relaxation of soft segments in the foam. However, it has been noticed that the E’ of both foams in the high temperature region is almost similar to each other, indicating comparable viscoelastic profiles82. Likewise, the E” of reference and recycled foams have also shown resembling peak shift, further stipulating the comparable flexibilities. Above all, both foams have also displayed identical damping behaviors (tan δ), confirming that they have matching viscoelastic performances83. These results indicate that incorporating 25% recycled polyols slightly modifies the thermomechanical properties of the foam while preserving its essential characteristics for practical applications.

Finally, the compression set test is performed to evaluate the elastic recovery of the reference and recycled foams employing 50% compression at 70 °C for 22 h. The reference foam exhibited a compression set of 9%, while the recycled foam showed a relatively higher compression set of 17%. These results indicate that the recycled foam has comparatively lower elastic recovery, potentially due to changes in its crosslink density or molecular integrity caused by the recycling process84. However, it is worth noting that despite incorporating a higher fraction of recycled polyols, i.e., 25% in the recycled foam, it has exhibited a comparable flexibility profile. These findings demonstrate that partial substitution of virgin polyol with recycled polyol obtained from ionolysis can be a viable approach for sustainable PUF production, promoting circular economy principles.

Conclusion

The ionolysis process employing EMIM-Br as an ionic liquid presents a highly efficient and sustainable strategy for the chemical recycling of flexible PUFs, enabling the recovery of high-purity, virgin-like polyols. Indeed, the chemical recycling can be carried out at a low temperature (150 °C) which is fairly lower than the common conditions used in other recycling processes. The recovered polyols are almost free from any unwanted PU degradation products, and, in particular, the aromatic amines are quite low (<0.1%), and are characterized by hydroxyl values, functionalities, and molecular weights that make them suitable for flexible PUFs production. Indeed, the successful incorporation of these recycled polyols into new polyurethane formulations demonstrated that a high substitution, i.e., 25%, does not undermine the key structural and viscoelastic properties of the resulting foam. This highlights the potential of ionolysis as a viable process to mitigate polyurethane waste. Moreover, this recycling strategy demonstrates economic feasibility by operating under mild conditions and without requiring any consumable reactants, while converting solid PUF waste directly into reusable polyols. Furthermore, the single-IL process, with complete solvent and IL recovery, offers simplicity and alignment with green chemistry principles. Incorporating 25% recycled polyols into new foams is well above current industrial practice, further validating the industrial and economic viability of the approach. Overall, this work advances the field of polyurethane recycling by demonstrating a promising route toward high-performance, sustainable material recovery.

Methods

Materials

Scrap flexible polyurethane (PU) waste was obtained from automobile seats, characterized by low density. The polyether-based flexible foam primarily consists of toluene diisocyanate (TDI) and a mixture of trifunctional high-molecular-weight polyols. Bromoalkenes, including bromoethane, 1-bromobutane, and 1-bromohexane, along with 1-methylimidazole, sodium tetrafluoroborate, ethyl acetate, and acetone, were used in the synthesis of ionic liquids (ILs). The synthesized ILs include 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bromide (EMIM-Br), 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (EMIM-BF₄), 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bromide (BMIM-Br), 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (BMIM-BF₄), 1-hexyl-3-methylimidazolium bromide (HMIM-Br), and 1-hexyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (HMIM-BF₄). Ethyl acetate was further used in the recovery of polyols from the ionolyzed PU foam mixture. Methods

Synthesis of ionic liquids

Equimolar amounts of bromoalkenes and 1-methylimidazole were reacted to form quaternary ammonium bromide salts. 1-Methylimidazole was added to a 1 L round-bottom flask, followed by the addition of 300 mL of acetone as a solvent. The solution was stirred continuously, and bromoalkanes were introduced dropwise using a burette. To dissipate the heat generated by this exothermic reaction, the flask was maintained in an ice bath. The reaction was allowed to proceed under stirring until completion. The resulting quaternary bromide salts were separated by filtration and washed thrice with 50 mL of ethyl acetate to remove residual bromoalkenes and unreacted imidazole. The purified quaternary bromide salts were dissolved in 300 mL of acetone, and an equimolar amount of sodium tetrafluoroborate was added to the solution under continuous stirring. The reaction was carried out at 40 °C until the formation of sodium bromide, which precipitated as a byproduct (Fig. 6). The precipitated sodium bromide was removed by filtration, and the filtrate containing the desired ionic liquid was washed with ethyl acetate to eliminate any residual impurities. Finally, the ILs were purified by vacuum distillation at 100 °C for 2 h to remove residual solvents and stored under an inert atmosphere for further characterization.Fig. 6Two-step synthesis of Ionic Liquids.The reaction scheme illustrates the initial A quaternization of 1-methylimidazole with a bromoalkane followed by a B anion exchange step to exchange the bromide anion with tetrafluoroborate.

Ionolysis of PUF

The Ionolysis experiments were performed using a multi-modal, lab-scale MicroSYNTH microwave reactor (Milestone S.R.L., Sorisole, Italy) was employed, equipped with hermetically sealed 100 mL PTFE vessels, a magnetic stirring mechanism, and a fiber-optic temperature sensor, which enables precise monitoring and control of the reaction temperature. The particle size of flexible PUF was reduced to 1 mm using ZM 300 centrifugal mill (Retsch, Germany) and PUF powder was dried in an oven to remove any traces of moisture. Initially, a control experiment was performed using only the ionic liquid (IL) in the microwave reactor to evaluate its microwave absorption capacity. A steady increase in temperature was observed under microwave irradiation, confirming that the IL efficiently absorbs microwaves. The standard Ionolysis experiments were carried out by using solid polyurethane and IL in (1:1.5) weight by weight and transferring them into the PTFE vessel, and both were well mixed prior to microwave irradiation. The reaction system was heated for 30 min at a temperature of 150 °C and a maximum pressure ceiling of 10 Bar was set. Moreover, additional control experiments were conducted using PUF alone and PUF in combination with polyethylene glycol (PEG 400) in the absence of any ILs and when subjected to microwave irradiation under identical conditions; in both cases, the PUF remained unaffected and no depolymerization was revealed. Furthermore, the process scalability was also investigated using glass reactor with tenfold capacity under identical conditions. Once the reaction time elapsed, the mixture was allowed to cool down. The ionolyzed product was transferred into a flask and was thoroughly washed with 500 mL of ethyl acetate to recover all the polyols released in the reaction mixture. The addition of ethyl acetate induces the formation of a biphasic system. The upper organic phase consists of ethyl acetate containing the recovered polyols, while the lower phase comprises of the ionic liquid. This phase separation facilitates efficient recovery of the polyols and reuse of the ionic liquid due to its immiscibility with ethyl acetate. The ethyl acetate mixture containing polyols was vacuum distilled at 100 °C for 1 h, and pure polyols were stored in a glass bottle. The remaining mixture was mixed with 100 mL of distilled deionized water to recover the IL from the mixture. Finally, the IL and water mixture was also subjected to vacuum distillation to recover IL. This step was carried out to understand if IL has undergone any degradation. In the remaining experiments, the Ionolyzed product was merely washed with ethyl acetate (EA), and the residual mixture was recycled back for Ionolysis along with the addition of supplementary polyurethane (Fig. 7). Ethyl acetate has been chosen to selectively recover polyols because it is immiscible with the ILs, thus eliminating additional separation steps and minimizing the loss of ILs. Unlike water, ethyl acetate requires lower distillation energy, enhancing the process efficiency and sustainability. Foam preparation

The recovered polyols from the Ionolysis of flexible PUF wastes were tested to evaluate flexible PUF production suitability and subsequently characterized for performance evaluation. A recycled flexible foam was produced using 25% of recycled polyols, and its properties were eventually compared with the reference foam obtained using 100% virgin polyols. The 25 wt% recycled polyols were incorporated into the foam formulations as a proof of concept because lower incorporation levels (e.g., 10%) would be too small to demonstrate meaningful effects, while higher levels (>30%) could compromise the production of recycled PUF with desirable and comparable properties. In all formulations, the foams were prepared using a one-shot method involving the addition of the isocyanate to a premix comprising the different additives and reactants (polyols, catalysts, surfactants, and blowing agent). Upon vigorous mechanical agitation for about 10 s, the resulting mixture was poured in a pre-heated open mold to allow foam expansion. The obtained PUF foams were subsequently heated at 70 °C for 24 h in order to complete the cross-linking reactions before the characterization phase.

Characterization analysis

Characterization of ionic liquid

The synthesized ILs were characterized using FTIR and Proton Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (¹H NMR) spectroscopy. FT-IR spectra were collected using a Nicolet iS50 FT-IR spectrometer [Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham (MA), USA] using a Smart iTR attenuated total reflectance sampling accessory equipped with a diamond crystal. All the spectra were collected in the 4000−720 cm−1 wavenumber range. The absence of peaks related to the starting materials confirmed the successful synthesis of the ionic liquid. ¹H NMR spectra of ILs were recorded in DMSO-d₆, with chemical shifts (δ) referenced to tetramethylsilane (TMS). The combined FTIR and ¹H NMR data confirmed the successful synthesis and high purity of ILs. [Supplementary Data -1]

Characterization of recovered polyol

The recovered polyols obtained through Ionolysis were qualitatively investigated by FTIR by looking at significant variations of urethane, alcoholic, and amine peaks. FT-IR spectra were collected using a Nicolet iS50 FT-IR spectrometer [Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham (MA), USA] using a Smart iTR attenuated total reflectance sampling accessory equipped with a diamond crystal. All the spectra were collected in the 4000−720 cm−1 wavenumber range.

The hydroxyl value (OHV) of the ionolyzed products and recovered polyols were evaluated according to the procedure reported in ASTM D4274-1185 using a Mettler Toledo G20S compact titrator [Mettler Toledo, Columbus (OH), USA], while the dynamic viscosity was measured at 25.0 ± 0.5 °C using a cone and plate viscometer [ATAC NμLine, Analytical Technology & Control Limited, Devizes (Wiltshire), UK] according to ASTM D48781586.

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was employed to analyze the molecular weight distribution of recovered polyols, separated from the ionolyzed mixture. For this analysis, a Jasco PU-980 pump model [JASCO, Easton (MD), USA] equipped with three Phenogel [Phenomenex, Torrance (CA), USA] size exclusion columns (particle diameter: 5 μm, pore diameter: 1000, 100, and 50 Å) and a JASCO RI-830 refractive index detector were used. Before injection, the samples were dissolved in THF at the 0.04% w/w concentration and filtered using the 0.1 μm PTFE filter. Samples were eluted at 40 °C with a flowrate of 0.35 mL min−1. Polyol standards [Fluka Analytical, Charlotte (NC), USA] were used to build a calibration line.

The aromatic amine content in the recovered polyols was evaluated by reversed-phase HPLC. Specifically, a Waters e2695 separation model coupled with a Waters 2489 UV detector [Waters Corporation, Milford (MA), USA] by employing double wavelength. To perform the analysis, the following operating conditions were adopted: hydrophobic C18 column with a hydrophilic endcap (pore diameter: 300 Å, particle size: 5 μm, diameter × length: 4.6 × 250 mm); the eluents are: Milli-Q ultrapure water (acidified with 0.05% of trifluoroacetic acid) and HPLC-grade methanol, starting condition 97% of water, with a gradient of 1% min−1 for 10 min. HPLC samples were prepared by taking a known weight of recovered polyols and subsequently diluting it with a 0.1 N potassium hydroxide solution in methanol in order to take into account the amphoteric character of amines. This solution was sonicated, filtered using 0.2 μm PTFE filters, and finally 10 μL sample was injected in the HPLC column.

Characterization of PUF

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed for the analysis of the foam morphology and to measure the average cell diameter according to ASTM D3576-2087. SEM micrographs were collected using a Jeol 6490 SEM (Jeol Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) on gold-sputtered samples with an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Representative images (parallel to foam growth) were taken from at least three different regions of each sample to ensure an accurate characterization of the cell size distribution, cell uniformity, and surface morphology. Apparent foam density was measured according to ASTM D1622-2088. to comply with a fixed target reference density of 44 ± 3 kg m−3 for homogeneous comparison of the samples.

Compression set test: Foam samples were cut to standard dimensions of 50 mm × 50 mm × 25 mm and placed between two parallel plates, according to ISO 1856. The specimens are compressed 50% of their initial thickness, and the samples were held under compression at 70 °C in an oven for 22 h. After the compression period, the load was released, and the samples were allowed to recover for 24 h at the conditioning temperature of (23 °C, 50% RH). The thickness of the foam was measured before and after compression using a digital caliper (±0.01 mm).
