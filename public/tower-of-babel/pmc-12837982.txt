Comorbidity of internet gaming disorder and anxiety in first-year Chinese university students: the mediating role of behavioral inhibition and activation systems
Authors: Jie Chen, Zhenzhen Zhang, Siwen Fu, Biyu Wen, Zeming Chen, Xiaochun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInternet gaming disorder (IGD) and anxiety frequently co-occur among young adults and represent an increasing mental health concern in China. Nevertheless, the psychological network underlying this comorbidity and the identification of potential intervention targets remain poorly understood.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of 605 Chinese university freshmen to investigate the association between internet gaming disorder (IGD), anxiety. And the behavioral inhibition system (BIS)/ behavioral activation system (BAS) was examined as potential mediators of this relationship.ResultsAnxiety was positively associated with Internet gaming disorder (IGD), with the behavioral inhibition system (BIS) functioning as a complementary partial mediation. Complementary configurational and network analyses further identified heightened punishment/ reward sensitivity and loss of control over mind and behavior as key psychological symptoms linking IGD to anxiety.ConclusionsThese findings highlight anxiety’s central role in IGD, with an imbalance in reward and punishment sensitivity closely associated with the comorbidity. Intense gaming rewards reinforce the drive to continue playing, while cessation triggers negative emotions like anxiety. These results emphasize the need for interventions that promote healthier emotional regulation in individuals with IGD and comorbid anxiety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07663-w.Keywords: Internet gaming disorder, Anxiety, BIS/BAS, Comorbidity, Young adults, I-PACE

Background

Internet gaming disorder (IGD), a behavioral addiction characterized by persistent, repetitive, and uncontrollable gaming, adversely affects academic performance, social functioning, and both physical and psychological health. It has emerged as a global public health concern [1–5]. In China, the number of game users exceeded 674 million in 2024, with university students representing a major active subgroup (CADPA, http://m.cadpa.org.cn/3277/202501/41718.html). The reported prevalence of IGD among Chinese university students ranges from 1.2% to 57.5% [6], underscoring their high risk and the need for further investigation.

Accumulating evidence indicates a strong comorbidity between internet gaming disorder (IGD) and psychiatric conditions, including anxiety, depression, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder, alexithymia, and obsessive–compulsive disorder [7–16]. A meta-analysis reported that 92% of individuals with IGD also experienced anxiety, with higher comorbidity rates observed in males [12]. The incidence of anxiety among individuals with IGD is more than twice that of the general population [17]. Nevertheless, the psychological network underlying this comorbidity remain poorly understood.

The I-PACE model [18, 19] explains the formation and maintenance of internet use disorders, such as internet gaming disorder (IGD), social media addiction, and online shopping addiction. It suggests that psychopathological features, personality traits, and social cognitions serve as susceptibility factors. Specifically, depression, anxiety, and attention deficits are common comorbidities associated with internet addiction [20, 21], while high impulsivity and low self-esteem are linked to IGD [22]. Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory (RST), proposed by Gray, identifies two neural systems: the Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS), related to punishment sensitivity and negative affect, and the Behavioral Activation System (BAS), associated with reward sensitivity and positive affect [23–25]. A heightened BIS predisposes individuals to anxiety [26], while a heightened BAS facilitates emotional regulation and reduces anxiety [27]. Anxiety, along with high BIS and low BAS, are vulnerability factors for IGD. This study hypothesizes that anxiety is comorbid with IGD, and that high BIS and low BAS contribute to its development.

Significant differences in BIS and BAS-F scores have been reported between individuals with and without internet gaming disorder (IGD) [28]. Imbalances between the BIS and BAS are associated with altered functional connectivity between the amygdala and striatum in individuals with IGD [24]. Among university students, IGD has been significantly correlated with BIS [28], whereas another study found that only BAS-F significantly correlated with IGD risk [29]. This finding aligns with evidence linking IGD to depression, in which altered connectivity between the posterior cingulate cortex and medial prefrontal cortex may underlie comorbidity network [30]. Given the inconsistent relationships reported between IGD, BIS, BAS, and anxiety—considered a prodromal feature of depression—further empirical research is needed to clarify whether BIS/BAS imbalances explain IGD–anxiety comorbidity.

To address this gap, the present study employed an integrated methodological framework combining partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM), fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA), and network analysis. PLS-SEM and CB-SEM are both structural equation modeling (SEM) techniques used primarily for theory validation. While CB-SEM focuses on model fit, PLS-SEM emphasizes the ability of independent variables to explain variance in dependent variables. PLS-SEM offers several advantages: it is not constrained by sample size or distribution shape, it is well-suited for complex models exploring relationships between multiple latent variables, and it prioritizes predictive power, making it particularly suitable for exploratory research. These advantages make PLS-SEM more flexible and effective, especially in studies with small sample sizes or those focused on prediction. PLS-SEM enables the testing of linear mediating relationships among latent variables [31], fsQCA identifies nonlinear configurational pathways [32], and network analysis highlights core and bridge symptoms within comorbid network [33].

Drawing on reinforcement sensitivity theory (RST) and I-PACE model, we hypothesized that imbalances between BIS and BAS was associated with anxiety in individuals with internet gaming disorder (IGD). Specifically, we proposed the following hypotheses: H1: Anxiety was positively associated with IGD; H2: Anxiety was positively associated with BIS; H3: Anxiety was negatively associated with BAS; H4: BIS was positively associated with IGD; H5: BAS was negatively associated with IGD; H6: BIS mediates the association between IGD and anxiety; and H7: BAS mediates the association between IGD and anxiety (Supplementary Fig S1).

Methods

Participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted at Shanghai University of Sport using cluster convenience sampling, yielding 605 valid responses. Participants ranged in age from 17 to 23 years (M = 18.33, SD = 0.72); 327 (54.1%) were male, and 302 (49.9%) reported being the only child.

Procedure

Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires completed in classroom settings under the supervision of a trained graduate assistant who provided standardized instructions. Completion took approximately 15 min. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Sport (Approval No. 102772024RT170), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measures

Subjective socioeconomic status

Perceived socioeconomic status was assessed using the Subjective Socioeconomic Status Scale (SSES) [34, 35]. Participants rated their social standing on a 10-rung ladder (1 = lowest, 10 = highest), with higher scores reflecting more favorable socioeconomic conditions.

20-item internet gaming disorder test (IGD-20)

Problematic video game use was assessed using the Chinese version of the Internet Gaming Disorder Scale-20 items (IGD-20) [36–38]. This scale consists of 20 items rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) (Supplementary Table S1). It includes six dimensions: salience, tolerance, mood modification, withdrawal, conflict, and relapse. Salience: Item 1, 7 and 13 (e.g., “I often lose sleep because of long gaming sessions”), all scored positively. Tolerance: Item 3, 9 and 15 (e.g., “I have significantly increased the amount of time I play games over past year”), all scored positively. Mood modification: Items 2, 8, and 14, with Item 2 reverse-scored (e.g., “I never play games in order to feel better”). Withdrawal: Items 4, 10, and 16 (e.g., “When I am not gaming, I feel more irritable”), all scored positively. Conflict: Items 5, 11, 17, 19, and 20, with Item 19 reverse-scored (e.g., “I have lost interest in other hobbies because of my gaming”). Relapse: Items 6, 12, and 18 (e.g., “I would like to cut down my gaming time but it’s difficult to do”), all scored positively. Each subscale score was calculated by summing the respective items, and the total IGD score was the sum of all 20 items. Higher scores indicate greater severity of Internet gaming disorder. Participants with an IGD-20 total score ≥ 71 were classified as meeting the diagnostic threshold for Internet gaming disorder [37].

Generalized anxiety disorder scale (GAD-7)

Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) [39, 40]. The scale comprises seven items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all to 3 = nearly every day) (Supplementary Table S1). It is a unidimensional measure, and all items are positively scored (e.g., Item 1: “Feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge”). The total score is calculated by summing the seven items, with higher scores indicating greater levels of anxiety. A total score of 10 or above indicates clinically significant anxiety symptoms [40].

Behavioral inhibition/activation systems (BIS/BAS)

Reinforcement sensitivity traits were assessed using the Behavioral Inhibition System/Behavioral Activation System (BIS/BAS) Scales [23, 41]. In studies with Chinese university students, two reverse-scored items from the original 20-item version were deleted (“Even if something bad is about to happen to me, I rarely experience fear or nervousness” and “I have very few fears compared to my friends”), resulting in an 18-item version [41, 42] (Supplementary Table S1). The scale uses a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree) and consists of two systems: the Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS) and the Behavioral Activation System (BAS). BIS: Items 5, 9, 12, 14, and 18 (e.g., “Criticism or scolding hurts me quite a bit”) are positively scored. BAS: Comprises three subdimensions: Reward Responsiveness: Items 2, 4, 13, and 17 (e.g., “When I’m doing well at something, I love to keep at it”); Drive: Items 1, 6, 8, and 16 (e.g., “I often lose sleep because of long gaming sessions”); Fun Seeking: Items 3, 7, 10, 11, and 15 (e.g., “I’m always willing to try something new if I think it will be fun”). All BIS/BAS items are positively scored. Subscale scores were calculated by summing the respective items, with higher BIS and BAS scores indicating greater sensitivity to punishment and reward, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0, SmartPLS 3.2.9, fsQCA 4.1, and R 4.4.2. Harman’s single-factor test was applied to assess common method bias. Descriptive statistics and gender comparisons were performed for all measures.

Reliability and validity of the scales were assessed using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM), conducted with SmartPLS 3.2.9. Given the sample size of 605 participants, a case omission distance of 7 was chosen, with eight iterations to ensure convergence. The evaluation process included examination of factor loadings, Cronbach’s α, composite reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and potential collinearity issues. Bootstrapping with 5000 samples (α = 0.05, two-tailed) was used to validate the hypothesized model, with the calculation of f² and predictive relevance (Q²) to assess the model’s strength and predictive accuracy. Mediation analyses were performed to quantify direct, indirect, and total effects, as well as variance accounted for (VAF) [31]. While PLS-SEM proves effective in modeling complex relationships between latent variables, it does not account for potential nonlinear relationships between IGD, BIS, BAS-R, BAS-D, BAS-F and anxiety.

To explore nonlinear configurational relationships, fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) supplemented PLS-SEM. Variables were calibrated into fuzzy-set membership scores (ranging from 0 to 1) using direct calibration anchored at the 95th, 50th, and 5th percentile distribution points. Necessary condition analyses were performed before sufficiency analyses, setting raw consistency thresholds ≥ 0.80, proportional reduction of inconsistency (PRI) consistency ≥ 0.50, and a minimum case frequency of 3 to construct truth Table [32]. Simplified and intermediate solutions were used to identify core and peripheral conditions, resulting in multiple configurations.

To address the limitations of latent models and identify precise intervention targets, network analysis was conducted using R 4.4.2. Items from the IGD-20 (20 items), BIS (5 items), BAS-R (4 items), BAS-D (4 items), BAS-F (5 items), and GAD-7 (7 items) formed the network variables. The qgraph package (Lavaan function) was used to estimate the adaptive covariance matrix, which formed the basis for constructing the network structures. Centrality indices, including strength, expected influence, closeness, and betweenness, were used to determine the significance of nodes, while bridge centrality indices quantified the comorbid interrelationships. Node expected influence served as the primary indicator due to its stability. The bootnet package was used to calculate edge and node stability through stability coefficients [33].

In summary, the integration of PLS-SEM (for model validation), fsQCA (for configurational causality), and network analysis (for variable interaction visualization) provides a comprehensive multidimensional approach to understanding the IGD-anxiety comorbidity. This approach offers robust evidence for identifying targeted interventions to address this complex issue.

Results

Common method bias

Harman’s single-factor test was performed to assess common method variance. Eight factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, with the first factor accounting for 21.9% of the variance—well below the 40% threshold [43]. These results indicate that common method bias was not a serious concern in this study.

Descriptive statistics and gender differences

The mean subjective socioeconomic status score among the 605 participants was 5.16 (SD = 1.50). The mean scores for internet gaming disorder (IGD), anxiety, the behavioral inhibition system (BIS), the behavioral activation system(BAS), the behavioral activation system- Reward (BAS-R), the behavioral activation system- Drive (BAS-D) and the behavioral activation system- Fun Seeking (BAS-F) were 41.89 (SD = 12.88), 3.90 (SD = 4.46), 14.79 (SD = 3.13), 40.87 (SD = 5.98), 13.41 (SD = 2.01), 12.21 (SD = 2.27) and 15.56 (SD = 2.68), respectively.

No significant gender differences were observed for age, only-child status, socioeconomic status, anxiety, BIS, BAS, BAS-R, BAS-D or BAS-F. However, a significant gender difference was found for IGD (t(602) = 6.71, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.53), with males scoring higher than females. Detailed results are reported in Table 1.

Measurement model

The measurement model demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity. Cronbach’s α and composite reliability (CR) values for all constructs exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70 [44]. Specifically, IGD (α = 0.94, CR = 0.95), anxiety (α = 0.90, CR = 0.92), BIS (α = 0.81, CR = 0.87), and BAS (α = 0.88, CR = 0.89) showed strong internal consistency. Convergent validity was supported by average variance extracted (AVE), all of which exceeded 0.50 [44]. Discriminant validity was confirmed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion, as the square root of each construct’s AVE exceeded its correlations with other constructs (Table 2). In addition, heterotrait–monotrait ratios (HTMT) ranged from 0.13 to 0.41, below the 0.85 cut-off [45], further supporting adequate discriminant validity (Supplementary Table S2).

Explained variance and effect sizes

Anxiety explained 13% of the variance in BIS (R² = 0.13, p < .001) and 2% of the variance in BAS (R² = 0.02, p = .005). Anxiety explained 1% of the variance in IGD (R² = 0.01, p = .05). Effect size (f²) analyses indicated that anxiety had small effects on IGD (f² = 0.01), BAS (f² = 0.02) and moderate effect on BIS (f² = 0.15). BIS demonstrated a small effect on anxiety (f² = 0.01), and BAS also showed a small effect (f² = 0.02). Predictive relevance (Q²) values for IGD, BIS, and BAS were all greater than zero [47], confirming acceptable predictive validity (Supplementary Table S3).

Sufficient condition configurations

The fsQCA identified three types of solutions: complex, parsimonious, and intermediate. Among these, the intermediate solution was considered the most appropriate for the present study. Table 6 summarizes the specific results, detailing six distinct configurations that may lead to elevated Internet gaming behavior. The overall solution coverage (0.52) indicates that a substantial proportion of Internet gaming behavior can be explained by these configurations, while the overall consistency (0.77) suggests that the identified configurations provide a reliable representation of the outcome [48].

Discussion

This study applied reinforcement sensitivity theory (RST), I-PACE model and a multi-method framework (PLS-SEM, fsQCA, and network analysis) to examine the psychological network linking IGD, BIS, BAS, and anxiety among Chinese university freshmen. PLS-SEM confirmed that anxiety was positively associated with IGD, with BIS functioning as a complementary partial mediation. fsQCA identified six distinct configurations, supporting the BIS/BAS imbalance hypothesis, and highlights the central role of anxiety. Network analysis identified “dependence on gaming” (IGD) and “uncontrollable worry” (anxiety) as core symptoms, with “experiencing intense emotional reactions” from gaming making it difficult to “stop playing” as key bridging nodes. Furthermore, males reported higher IGD scores. Collectively, these findings provide a multidimensional perspective on IGD–anxiety comorbidity and inform potential interventions core symptoms.

Consistent with prior studies [50, 51], anxiety was associated with IGD, suggesting that problematic gaming is likely to involve emotional distress. Although some research indicates that gaming may temporarily relieve anxiety by providing perceived control [52, 53], such avoidance-based coping strategies often worsen psychological outcomes over time [54]. From a neurobiological perspective, the addicted brain undergoes “neural hijacking,” progressing through three distinct phases: (1) binge/intoxication, characterized by dopamine release in the ventral tegmental area (VTA), reinforcement in the nucleus accumbens, and habit formation via the basal ganglia; (2) withdrawal/negative affect, marked by reduced dopamine activity, diminished nucleus accumbens activation, and amygdala hyperactivation, producing negative affect such as anxiety; and (3) craving/preoccupation, involving impaired dorsolateral prefrontal cortex functioning, orbitofrontal cortex hyperactivity, hippocampal memory encoding, and insular-driven impulsivity [55, 56]. Similar neural activation patterns are observed between substance addiction and IGD [57]. Dysregulated prefrontal–amygdala circuits during the withdrawal phase appear to be associated with anxiety, underscoring the role of BIS/BAS imbalances in IGD-related comorbidity [58].

Importantly, this study demonstrated that BIS positively mediated the relationship between IGD and anxiety. Elevated BIS reflects heightened attention to punishment-related environmental cues, which is associated with abnormal activity in the amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, and hippocampus [24, 59, 60]. Such hypervigilance fosters avoidance behaviors and, while amplifying anxiety, also exacerbates the severity of internet gaming disorder. However, the calculated VAF was only 23.08%, suggesting that BIS has a moderate influence on the outcome. This indicates the need for larger sample sizes to further validate these findings.

The fsQCA results revealed that there is no single pathway leading to internet gaming disorder (IGD). Instead, five distinct configurations, incorporating the presence and absence of specific conditions, were identified as contributing to this outcome. Across all configurations, anxiety consistently emerged as a core condition, highlighting its central role in the manifestation of IGD across varying behavioral activation–inhibition profiles. Additionally, in each configuration path, BAS and BIS were inversely related. For instance, Configuration 2, characterized by high anxiety, high BIS, and low BAS-D, demonstrated an 85% probability of effectively explaining the occurrence of high IGD. In individuals with high anxiety, the imbalance between sensitivity to punishment and reward results in gaming becoming a utopian escape from problems, further supporting the BIS/BAS imbalance hypothesis, which is a recognized risk factor for several psychiatric disorders [61, 62]. This imbalance promotes maladaptive cognitive biases and avoidance behaviors, such as procrastination or social withdrawal, thereby increasing vulnerability to gaming addiction.

Network analysis identified “I feel irritable when not gaming” (Withdrawal) as a core symptom of internet gaming disorder (IGD), consistent with previous studies [63, 18]. Addicts typically experience three distinct states: intoxication and euphoria, severe negative emotions following withdrawal, and craving. When individuals with IGD cease gaming, they may experience negative emotions such as anxiety and depression, which are associated with increased activation of the stress response system, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis [64]. A central symptom of anxiety, “uncontrollable worry,” likely reflects rumination, which is linked to abnormal connectivity between the amygdala and prefrontal cortex [65]. “Inability to stop gaming” (Relapse), “tendency to seek pleasurable activities” (BAS-F), and “excessive negative sensitivity” (BIS) constitute comorbid symptoms of IGD and anxiety. This suggests that individuals with IGD may engage in gaming as a response to heightened emotional reactions to negative events. By gaming, they experience a temporary pleasurable state, reinforcing the behavior and contributing to the development of a vicious cycle.

Gender differences also emerged, with males reporting significantly higher IGD levels, consistent with prior evidence [66–70]. Meta-analyses suggest that men are more inclined toward gaming, whereas women prefer social media use, reflecting differences in neural reward systems [42]. From an evolutionary standpoint, male tendencies toward aggression and dominance align with gaming’s competitive elements, while female tendencies toward social interaction may explain greater engagement with social media [71, 72].

This study is the first to integrate PLS-SEM, fsQCA, and network analysis to examine the comorbidity of Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) and anxiety among Chinese university freshmen. The results confirm the mediating role of BIS in the relationship between anxiety and IGD, revealing several configurational pathways that exacerbate IGD behavior. Anxiety, alongside an imbalance in punishment and reward sensitivity, plays a central role in these pathways. This study also identifies the core symptoms of the comorbid network, with “positive” emotional experiences from gaming making it difficult for individuals to stop playing. These results extend current research on IGD and emphasize the comorbidity problem of IGD and anxiety, offering potential directions for clinical interventions targeting this comorbidity.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference; predictions regarding anxiety, BIS, BAS, and IGD are based on the reinforcement sensitivity theory and the I-PACE model, which may not be accurate. Longitudinal studies are needed. Second, the use of convenience sampling (first-year students) limits the generalizability of the findings; future research should employ random sampling across diverse populations. Third, reliance on self-report measures introduces subjectivity and potential bias, and experimental and multimodal methods (e.g., behavioral tasks, neuroimaging) could enhance validity. Fourth, psychometric limitations (e.g., low reliability for certain BAS items) emphasize the need for refined, culturally validated instruments in future studies. Lastly, the model developed in this study demonstrates low explanatory power and effect size, indicating the need for cautious interpretation of the results and further validation with larger samples.

Conclusion

This study employed an integrated approach combining PLS-SEM, fsQCA, and network analysis to examine the psychological network between IGD and anxiety among Chinese university freshmen. Results highlighted the central role of anxiety in IGD, with an imbalance in reward and punishment sensitivity closely linked to IGD. Intense rewards from gaming strongly reinforce the drive to continue playing, and cessation triggers negative emotions such as anxiety. These findings underscore the importance of guiding individuals toward healthier emotional values, providing potential intervention targets for those with IGD and comorbid anxiety.
