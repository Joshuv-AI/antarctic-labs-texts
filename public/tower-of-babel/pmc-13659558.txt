Editorial for PREOPANC-2, a multicenter, open-label, phase 3 randomized trial comparing neoadjuvant FOLFIRINOX vs. neoadjuvant gemcitabine based chemoradiotherapy in resectable and borderline resectable pancreatic cancer
Authors: Chunjie Li, Kian-Huat Lim
Published: 2026

Pancreatic cancer (PC, refers to ductal adenocarcinoma) remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with recent gains of survival rate driven predominantly by improved systemic chemotherapy in patients with resectable pancreatic cancer or borderline resectable pancreatic cancer (RPC, BRPC) disease. Due to the high propensity for early or occult metastasis, the complexity of PC surgery, and challenges in delivering adjuvant chemotherapy at the intended dose and schedule following surgery, there has been a shift over the past decade toward administering neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) for these patients. This approach aims to downstage tumors to increase the likelihood of an R0 resection, eradicate occult metastases, and identify patients with aggressive disease biology who are unlikely to benefit from surgery. As such, multiple retrospective and prospective studies have been performed to identify the optimal neoadjuvant regimens that can improve the surgical and survival outcomes. The recently published PREOPANC-2 trial provides critical comparative data between two established neoadjuvant paradigms and compels reassessment of how we optimize perioperative therapy for RPC/BRPC patients.

PREOPANC-2 was a multicenter, open-label, phase III randomized trial enrolling patients with RPC and BRPC (1). Patients were assigned to neoadjuvant classical FOLFIRINOX q2weeks for 8 cycles followed by no adjuvant treatment, or 3 cycles of neoadjuvant gemcitabine (weekly for 3 out of 4 weeks for each cycle), combined with radiation given during cycles 2, plus 4 cycles of adjuvant gemcitabine [chemoradiotherapy (CRT) group], based on CRT derived from the original PREOPANC-1 regimen (2,3). The total planned treatment length for FOLFIRINOX and CRT was 22 vs. 40 weeks. The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS). For modified intention-to-treat population (mITT), no difference in OS was seen between the two groups (21.9 months in the FOLFIRINOX group vs. 21.3 months in the CRT group, P=0.32) at a median follow up of 42.3 months. No difference was found between the treatment groups regarding the number of patients who underwent an R0 resection [78 (60%) of 131 patients in the FOLFIRINOX group vs. 88 (67%) of 131 patients in the CRT group, P=0.25]. Toxicity profiles differed with greater systemic adverse events in the FOLFIRINOX group and more localized treatment-related effects in the CRT group. However, it is unexpected that the proportions of patients with at least one adverse event or serious adverse event ≥ grade 3 were similar between both groups. These findings are notable because FOLFIRINOX demonstrated superiority over gemcitabine in metastatic [PRODIGE 4/ACCORD 11 (4)] and adjuvant [PRODIGE 24 (5)] settings. In addition, these results also challenge the assumption that intensified systemic therapy alone is a better option to improve outcomes in RPC and BRPC patients.

There have been tremendous efforts dedicated to establishing an effective NAT. PREOPANC-1 is the first phase III trial showing improved R0 resection rates and enhanced long-term survival with neoadjuvant gemcitabine-based CRT compared with upfront surgery in patients with RPC and BRPC (2,3). Importantly, 5-year OS improved despite modest median OS differences, highlighting the biologic selection effect of NAT. ESPAC-5F, a randomized phase II trial, further strengthened this paradigm in BRPC, demonstrating significantly improved 1-year survival with NAT (including FOLFIRINOX, Gemcitabine/capecitabine and capecitabine based chemoradiation) compared with immediate surgery (6), and reinforcing the benefits of upfront systemic management. Alliance A021501, another randomized phase II trial, evaluated mFOLFIRINOX with or without hypofractionated radiation in BRPC (7). Unexpectedly, the addition of radiation did not improve outcomes, suggesting limited add-on benefit after multi-agent chemotherapy. In RPC population, NORPACT-1 randomized phase II trial compared neoadjuvant FOLFIRINOX with upfront surgery but did not demonstrate an OS advantage in the intention-to-treat population (8). However, challenges in treatment delivery and crossover complicate interpretation. Similarly, SWOG S1505 evaluated perioperative mFOLFIRINOX vs. gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel and showed comparable median OS (~23 months), establishing feasibility but not superiority (9). Prospective non-randomized data also emphasize biological selection. Alliance A021101 demonstrated high R0 resection rates after mFOLFIRINOX followed by capecitabine-based chemoradiation in BRPC (10). Murphy et al. reported a median OS of 37.7 months with total NAT (11). A meta-analysis confirmed improved R0 resection and survival, particularly in BRPC (12), while retrospective series from high-volume centers reported median OS exceeding 58 months with FOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel based TNT strategies (13) (Table 1).

Within this landscape, PREOPANC-2 illustrated some strengths in this field. It is unique and pioneering in directly comparing two robust NAT in a multicenter, randomized phase III trial, although it did not demonstrate superiority of FOLFIRINOX. The comparable OS data suggests that both strategies can be considered when selected appropriately. Additionally, data in the PREOPANC-2 study must be carefully examined and interpreted in order to draw the most appropriate guidance for real-world practice. First, the PREOPANC-2 study, the classical FOLFIRINOX regimen was administered and intended for 8 cycles, with no adjuvant therapy planned. Due to systemic toxicities, 82% of patients needed dose reduction, resulting in only 81 % completing 4 cycles and only 62% of patients completing all 8 intended cycles. On the contrary, in the CRT group 88% of patients completed all neoadjuvant regimen; and 54% of whom underwent resection completed adjuvant gemcitabine. Therefore, it remains possible that the full potential of FOLFIRINOX might not have been realized due to the low treatment completion rate. Notably, 14/156 (~9%) of FOLFIRINOX group, vs. 28/166 (~17%) of CRT group were not resected. In a post-hoc analysis, reasons for abandoning resection were occult metastatic disease and locally unresectable disease (8% vs. 15%, P=0.04; 1% vs. 2%, P=0.70 for FOLFIRINOX vs. CRT). Statistically significantly, almost twice as many patients in the CRT group did not proceed to resection due to occult metastatic disease. Pathological complete response and ypN0 were 10% vs. 5%, P=0.28 and 47% vs. 58%, P=0.007 for FOLFIRINOX vs. CRT groups. Although the proportion of ypN0 resections was higher in FOLFIRINOX group, it did not prolong the OS, which indicates local pathological improvement does not necessarily directly translate into a superior OS. These data suggest that FOLFIRINOX provides better systemic disease control, likely suppressing occult metastatic disease, but its lower treatment completion rate is limited by toxicity. In contrast, CRT appears to achieve better local disease control, as reflected by higher ypN0 rates and treatment completion rates, but offers less effective systemic control, resulting in a greater likelihood of occult metastatic disease being detected at surgery and subsequent abandonment of resection.

Several limitations of the PREOPANC-2 trial should also be acknowledged. The FOLFIRINOX arm, as designed, did not demonstrate superiority against CRT. However, from the OS analysis, the potential benefit of FOLFIRINOX over CRT appeared to be more obvious in BRPC group after 30 months of follow up. Therefore, it is critical that more detailed analysis is needed from the PREOPANC-2 study to elucidate the OS, PFS and pattern of recurrence for patients who completed/did not complete 8 cycles of neoadjuvant FOLFIRINOX. Another critical consideration is that no adjuvant therapy was planned for the FOLFIRINOX arm, whereas 54% of patients who underwent resections and 40% of all patients randomized to CRT arm completed all four additional cycles of adjuvant gemcitabine. This imbalance complicates interpretation of the study findings, particularly because the survival benefit observed in the PRODIGE 24 trial was achieved with 6 months (12 cycles) of adjuvant FOLFIRINOX. Consequently, the FOLFIRINOX arm in the current study represents a nonstandard, abbreviated neoadjuvant treatment approach, limiting direct comparisons with outcomes reported in PRODIGE 24. It would therefore be informative to perform a subgroup analysis comparing outcomes among patients in the CRT arm who were unable to complete planned adjuvant therapy, as this may provide a more appropriate comparator for the shorter-course FOLFIRINOX regimen. In current practice, the optimal adjuvant strategy following NAT and surgery remains poorly defined. Decisions must incorporate pathological response, nodal status, margin status, postoperative CA19-9 levels, and patient recovery. Emerging tools such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) may further refine risk stratification and guide therapy. Given the poor prognosis of recurrent PC, the curative importance of complete surgical resection, and the challenges of delivering postoperative therapy, these data reinforce the value of maximizing treatment in the neoadjuvant setting. However, key questions remain: whether patients treated with neoadjuvant FOLFIRINOX benefit from additional adjuvant CRT, and conversely, whether those receiving neoadjuvant CRT should be considered for adjuvant. Incorporating more active regimens, such as gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, particularly in the neoadjuvant setting, may further improve R0 resection rates and survival. Finally, quality-of-life data from this trial is still pending and has not yet been reported with the other clinical outcomes. Quality of life is an important factor in real-world treatment selection and may affect treatment completion, yet it is often underappreciated in clinical trial design. Hopefully, these data will provide additional insight into the differences between the two treatment groups.

Despite these nuances, we believe the PREOPANC-2 trial provides several important insights for future practice and research. First, the comparable survival outcomes observed in both treatment groups, despite the use of two very different therapeutic modalities, raise the possibility that individual tumors may be preferentially susceptible to one regimen over the other. In the future, more accurate predictive biomarkers incorporating next-generation sequencing of tumor and blood samples, including ctDNA profiling before, during and after treatment, should be developed to guide selection and potentially the sequencing of these two modalities to maximize survival outcomes. For example, tumors with deficiencies in DNA repair pathways may derive greater benefit from platinum-based therapy (14). Second, local control remains relevant. Although distant relapse predominates, local progression contributes to morbidity, and adjuvant CRT may retain value in margin- or vascular-involved disease after receiving neoadjuvant systemic chemotherapy. Third, ensuring delivery of systemic therapy is critical. Neoadjuvant approaches guarantee treatment exposure that is often difficult postoperatively, highlighting the need to optimize sequencing strategies. Fourth, it remains important to clarify the benefit of radiation in RPC/BRPC. Although the benefit of radiation following mFOLFIRINOX is still questionable based on Alliance A021501, it seems like total neoadjuvant starting from mFOLFIRINOX followed by concurrent chemoradiation before surgery showed promising outcomes according to other phase 2 trials such as Alliance A021101. A well-designed randomized phase III study incorporating perioperative systemic regimen that is comparable to PRODIGE 24, without or without radiation, is warranted. Until then, in current practice, use of CRT or radiotherapy (RT) should be discussed in multidisciplinary conferences and considered for patients who are unable to tolerate systemic chemotherapy, do not achieve sufficient downstaging or likely to have R1 or R2 resection based on restaging imaging. Finally, the modest efficacy of current chemotherapy and radiation underscores that meaningful advances will require the integration of novel, more effective therapeutics. KRAS mutations occur in over 90% of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). KRAS G12C inhibitors such as sotorasib and adagrasib have demonstrated activity in advanced PDAC, although this subtype accounts for only 1–2% of cases. The promising efficacy of pan-RAS(ON) inhibitors such as daraxonrasib in advanced, chemo-refractory settings suggests that RAS inhibitors will undoubtedly become critical components of future clinical trials for patients with RPC/BRPC (15).

In summary, the PREOPANC-2 study provides high-level evidence that eight cycles of neoadjuvant FOLFIRINOX do not significantly outperform gemcitabine-based perioperative CRT in patients with RPC/BRPC. However, a well-designed phase III trial incorporating contemporary, optimized perioperative systemic therapy is still needed to more clearly define the role of CRT in this setting. Until such data are available, treatment decisions should be made within a multidisciplinary framework, taking into account patient performance status, anticipated tolerability, likelihood of treatment completion, and the flexibility to adapt therapy based on toxicity, radiographic response, and biomarker changes. Ultimately, meaningful improvements in outcomes will likely require the development of predictive biomarkers to guide treatment selection and the integration of novel therapeutic agents.
