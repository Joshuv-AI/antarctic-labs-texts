Performance of five trauma scoring systems in predicting in-hospital outcomes in geriatric trauma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zahra Ramezani, Mohsen Rastkar, Pejman Pourfakhr, Mahdi Sharif-Alhoseini, Vafa Rahimi-Movaghar, Payman Salamati, Zahra Karimi, Vali Baigi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPredicting unfavorable outcomes in geriatric trauma patients (GTPs) remains a significant challenge, as trauma remains a leading cause of mortality in this population. This study aimed to compare the performance of five trauma scoring systems, including three age-specific models, in predicting in-hospital mortality and the need for mechanical ventilation (MV) among Iranian GTPs.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included trauma patients ≥ 65 years from July 29, 2016 to September 19, 2024. The GERtality score, Geriatric Trauma Outcome Score (GTOS), GTOS II, Trauma Injury Severity Score (TRISS), and adjusted TRISS (aTRISS) were calculated for each patient. We evaluated the statistical performance of these scores using the concordance statistic (C-index) and assessed calibration by comparing observed versus predicted mortality risks through calibration plots. Clinical usefulness was evaluated with decision curve analysis (DCA) and net benefit (NB).ResultsOf 1,081 GTPs, a total of 86 (7.9%) required MV, and 93 individuals (8.6%) were deceased during hospitalization. The GERtality score (C-index = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.85 to 0.93) and GTOS (C-index = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.84 to 0.93) demonstrated the highest predictive value for in-hospital mortality. For MV, GERtality and GTOS exhibited the highest predictive performance (C-index = 0.82). The calibration slopes for all scoring systems were close to 1.0 (range: 0.97 to 0.99), with confidence intervals including 1.0, indicating good agreement between predicted and observed mortality risks. Based on decision curve analysis, the GERtality score demonstrated the highest NB across a broad range of threshold probabilities (approximately 0.05 to 0.75), supporting its superior clinical utility in predicting both mortality and MV.ConclusionsAlthough all scoring systems demonstrated acceptable performance, the GERtality score exhibited the highest predictive value among the five systems for both in-hospital mortality and the need for MV in GTPs.Trial registration numberNot applicable.Keywords: Geriatric trauma, Trauma scoring system, Mortality, Mechanical ventilation, GERtality, Prediction model

Introduction

Trauma and its associated complications account for approximately 8% of all-cause mortality worldwide, with an estimated 4.4 million trauma-related deaths reported in 2021 [1]. While the incidence of traumatic injuries is higher among the young population; however, there is a concerning rise trend in trauma incidence among the geriatric population [2]. This growing trend is primarily caused by demographic changes, including an aging population and susceptibility to specific injuries, such as falling among this group [3]. Notably, traumatic mortality rates for trauma in individuals aged 65 and older are reported as 31–61% higher than those observed in younger patients [4]. There are several risk factors in geriatric trauma patients (GTPs), including preexisting cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, renal failure, and poor nutritional status, which contribute to their increased morbidity and mortality [5, 6]. Consequently, the effective management of GTPs is crucial for reducing the risk of adverse outcomes.

A fundamental component of trauma management is the rapid and accurate assessment of patients’ clinical status on arrival at a trauma center, which guides critical medical decisions. Trauma scoring systems assist emergency department physicians in this process. Given the significant influence of age on trauma outcomes, a number of scoring systems have been developed explicitly for use in specific age groups, such as the GTPs. Among the geriatric-specific scoring systems, the Geriatric Trauma Outcome Score (GTOS), GTOS II, and the GERtality score incorporate age-related physiological factors, enhancing assessment accuracy and aiding in better clinical decision-making to prevent poor outcomes [7–9].

Recent studies worldwide have validated the prognostic utility of geriatric-specific trauma scores in predicting in-hospital outcomes [8, 10, 11]. However, research in Iran remains limited and has predominantly focused on general trauma scoring systems rather than age-specific tools in GTPs [12, 13]. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the predictive performance of five trauma scoring systems, three geriatric-specific (GTOS, GTOS II, and GERtality) and two general (Trauma Injury Severity Score [TRISS] and adjusted TRISS [aTRISS]), for in-hospital mortality and mechanical ventilation (MV) requirements in Iranian GTPs. This study was conducted based on the hypothesis that geriatric-specific trauma scoring systems would demonstrate strong discriminative performance and clinical utility in predicting outcomes among Iranian GTPs.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted on trauma patients aged ≥ 65 referred to Sina Hospital, Tehran, Iran, from 29 July 2016 to 19 September 2024. The study was conducted within the framework of the National Trauma Registry of Iran (NTRI), and patients were identified through this registry. As a result, all patients meet the registry’s inclusion criteria, including hospitalization for more than 24 h, deceased patients within the first 24 h of hospitalization, or patients transferred from the intensive care unit (ICU) of another hospital to the current hospital’s ICU, with a length of stay of less than 24 h. Patients discharged home from the emergency department and individuals transferred out were excluded from this study [14–16].

Data collection and variables

The data for this study were collected using two approaches: the NTRI database and the targeted extraction of specific data from patient records. The registry process involves identifying patients with traumatic injuries based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes and registering those who meet specific criteria (e.g., hospital stay over 24 h, death, or ICU transfer) [17]. Trained registrars, usually nurses, collect comprehensive data (99 variables) via bedside interviews, physical exams, and hospital records, which are uploaded to a web-based portal. To ensure data accuracy, trained reviewers and a surgeon controller carefully assess the collected information, adhering to established guidelines for injury severity assessment [17]. The initial data extracted from the NTRI database encompassed the following variables: age, sex, the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), injury mechanism (falling, road traffic crash, and others), type of injury (isolated vs. multiple trauma), MV, and in-hospital mortality.

The classification of patients into isolated and multiple trauma groups was based on whether the injury involved a single system or multiple systems, as determined by the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) [18, 19]. In-hospital mortality was defined as death occurring during the index hospitalization only; deaths that occurred after discharge were excluded. MV was defined as invasive ventilation, either emergency or elective, initiated due to respiratory failure.

Subsequently, two reviewers—a physician and a medical student—obtained additional variables from the patients’ medical records via the Hospital Information System (HIS). These variables included the number of Packed Red Blood Cells (pRBCs) received, surgical interventions, the AIS, and the Injury Severity Score (ISS). Additionally, an anesthesiologist assessed and assigned the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score to all patients. Finally, using the extracted variables, trauma scoring systems—including GERtality, GTOS, GTOS II, Trauma Injury Severity Score (TRISS), and adjusted TRISS (aTRISS)—were calculated for each patient. The main outcomes analyzed were in-hospital mortality and the need for mechanical ventilation (MV).

The AIS evaluates the severity of individual injuries across body regions, with scores ranging from 1 (mild injury) to 6 (fatal injury) [20]. The ISS is determined by summing the highest AIS scores from the three most severely injured body regions, with the total score ranging from 1 to 75, reflecting overall injury severity [20]. The ASA score evaluates a patient’s overall health and comorbidities by classifying the severity of underlying conditions on a scale from 1 to 6, with 1 indicating a healthy patient and 6 representing a brain-dead patient undergoing organ donation [21].

To ensure consistency and reproducibility in variable abstraction and trauma score calculation, we assessed both inter- and intra-observer agreement. For inter-rater reliability, two reviewers independently extracted data from 35 randomly selected patient records. Intra-rater agreement was evaluated by having each reviewer re-abstract data from 15 anonymized records after a 15-day interval. Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC), Cohen’s kappa, and weighted kappa were used depending on the type of variable. ICC values were all above 0.86, and kappa values ranged from 0.88 to 0.92, indicating strong reliability. For the ASA score, which was assigned by an anesthesiologist, intra-rater agreement was assessed by re-evaluating 20 anonymized cases with a 20-day interval, yielding a weighted kappa of 0.89. All reviewers were trained in standardized data collection protocols, and trauma scores were calculated using objective clinical data, based on predefined criteria, to reduce the risk of observer bias.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate based on their distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables across outcome groups (in-hospital mortality and MV), depending on the normality of data. Associations between categorical variables and outcomes were evaluated using the chi-square test.

Discriminative performance of each scoring system was assessed using the concordance statistic (C-index), equivalent to the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). To assess the risk of overfitting and to obtain optimism-corrected estimates, we used bootstrap resampling with 500 iterations. In each iteration, the C-index was calculated for both the bootstrap sample and the original dataset, and the average optimism was subtracted from the apparent C-index to yield an optimism-adjusted C-index.

The calibration of each model was evaluated by comparing predicted probabilities with observed outcomes. This was quantified using the calibration intercept (assessing systematic under- or overestimation) and calibration slope (measuring agreement between predicted risks and actual outcomes). A calibration slope close to 1 and an intercept near 0 indicate good calibration. To evaluate clinical usefulness, we conducted decision curve analysis (DCA) for each scoring system. To determine optimal cutoff points, we employed a 5-fold cross-validation procedure, consistent with TRIPOD recommendations for internal validation. The dataset was randomly divided into five equal subsets (folds). In each iteration, four folds (80%) were used to derive the optimal threshold based on Youden’s Index, while the remaining fold (20%) was used for validation. This process was repeated so that each fold served once as the validation set. We reported the cross-validated C-index (cvC-index) and its standard deviation across folds to assess both performance and stability. The final cutoff point reported in the manuscript represents the average of the five thresholds derived through cross-validation.

Finally, to examine the association between the derived cutoff values and outcomes, we constructed multiple logistic regression models, adjusting for relevant covariates including age, ISS, GCS, cause and type of trauma, and ASA classification. Variable selection was guided by the Hosmer–Lemeshow approach to ensure model parsimony and relevance. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 14.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and R software, following the TRIPOD statement for transparent and reproducible reporting of prediction models.

Results

A total of 1,081 patients were included in the study, as illustrated in the STROBE-compliant flowchart (Fig. 1). Of 1,081 GTPs, 586 (54.21%) were male. The mean (SD) age of the patients was 76.04 (7.8) years, with a significant difference between those who survived and those who did not (p < 0.001). The primary cause of trauma was falling (712 (65.9%)), followed by road traffic crashes (301 (27.8%)). Among all, 86 (7.9%) required MV, and 93 (8.6%) patients died during their hospitalization. The GERtality score had a median (IQR) of 2.0 (2.0) for deceased patients, compared to 0.0 (1.0) for survivors (p < 0.001). The GTOS showed higher values for deceased patients (median (IQR) = 110.5 (15.0)) vs. survivors (91.5 (19.0)) (p < 0.001). All scoring systems exhibited significant differences between in-hospital deceased and non-deceased patients and between those requiring MV and those not (Ps < 0.001) (Table 1).

Discussion

Prediction of unfavorable outcomes in GTPs is a critical challenge, as the global population constantly ages and the number of traumatic injuries grows in this vulnerable demographic [27, 28]. Iran is no exception to this trend, as well. Addressing these issues requires the development and application of reliable scoring systems to predict adverse outcomes such as mortality, improve clinical management, inform decision-making processes, and assist physicians in providing accurate prognostications for both patients and their families [27, 29]. Several scoring systems have been proposed to address this challenge. Recent evidence by Rahmatinejad et al. highlights that predictive algorithms improve risk stratification and clinical decision-making by providing objective, data-driven assessments. These findings underscore the practical value of integrating validated, objective scoring systems into clinical practice, where they can complement, rather than replace, clinical judgment to enhance patient outcomes and optimize resource allocation [30].

In this study, we analyzed the predictive value of several trauma scores, including GERtality, GTOS, GTOS II, TRISS, and aTRISS, for mortality and MV in a cohort of 1,081 GTPs. Based on the literature, an AUC of 0.80 or higher is considered an acceptable diagnostic test, with an AUC of 0.90 or above regarded as excellent [31]. In the current study, all trauma scores demonstrated acceptable predictive performance. Notably, GERtality, a specifically designed score for GTPs, stood out for its excellent predictive power for both in-hospital mortality and MV. Additionally, GTOS, GTOS II, TRISS, and aTRISS, which adjusts the TRISS for GTPs, also illustrated acceptable performance for predicting in-hospital mortality, with a descending predictive power. GERtality’s performance for predicting in-hospital mortality was slightly better than for MV, but GERtality, aTRISS, and TRISS all demonstrated good predictive accuracy for MV in GTPs. In contrast, the predictive accuracy of GTOS and GTOS II was somewhat limited for MV prediction.

The superior performance of GERtality aligns with its prior validations in GTPs, such as the original development cohort by Scheerer et al. [7]. Our findings reinforce this result, underscoring GERtality’s robust predictive value across different healthcare settings. Similarly, Chiu et al. validated GERtality in Hong Kong, reporting strong performance for in-hospital mortality prediction comparable to our results [32]. The consistency between our study and these earlier validations suggests GERtality’s potential for broad applicability in diverse geriatric trauma populations. Clinically, while the score is still in its early stages of validation, it offers significant promise for integration into existing workflows, complementing other well-established scores, which are routinely utilized in emergency and critical care settings [27].

In contrast, the GTOS, which has been shown to accurately assess the likelihood of death within 24 h of trauma, helping to guide clinical decision-making and resource allocation [33, 34], although broadly validated, has exhibited variable predictive performance across different studies [10, 11, 27, 35, 36]. While we found GTOS to be acceptably predictive for in-hospital mortality, its accuracy was lower compared to GERtality, which is closely paralleled with prior studies such as that by Barea-Mendoza et al., which indicated that GTOS calibration needs further refinement in GTPs [37]. Moreover, earlier validations by Zhuang et al. and Ravindranath et al. found GTOS useful for early mortality prediction and rapid triage of GTPs [10, 38], supporting its utility as a highly relevant tool in geriatric trauma care despite variability in reported performance across populations. This variability in performance across different studies highlights the necessity of population-specific validations and suggests that potential factors, such as regional differences in trauma management protocols or patient demographics, might influence predictive accuracy. The simplicity of GTOS allows it to be easily implemented in trauma settings, especially when vital signs and mental status information are unavailable [11]. When used alongside other clinical data, the GTOS can aid in making more informed decisions about patient care and resource allocation [22].

Consistent with our findings, Ravindranath et al. found that the GTOS II score was not superior to other scores in predicting unfavorable discharge and did not demonstrate as strong a discriminatory ability between survivors and non-survivors as we observed in our study [38]. In contrast, Meagher et al. suggested that GTOS II might be more effective in identifying high-risk GTPs than traditional scores [39].

The TRISS, which combines physiological and anatomical injury assessments with factors such as age and injury mechanism, is one of the most widely used scoring systems to predict trauma outcomes [36, 40, 41]. Despite its broad application, several studies have suggested that TRISS may be less suitable for GTPs due to its failure to account for specific factors, such as the age range and demographics of the study populations [42–44]. In this study, we utilized both the traditional TRISS coefficients and an alternative version, the aTRISS, based on the study by Domingues et al. [45]. In line with our findings, the performance of TRISS, with an AUC of 0.67 in the study by Wilson et al., highlights its limited performance in predicting mortality in GTPs [46]. However, Liu et al., in a recent meta-analysis assessing the performance of various trauma scoring systems for predicting mortality in GTPs, suggested that TRISS may provide superior predictive accuracy compared to other models [47].

In this context, we also incorporated decision curve analysis and net benefit as complementary evaluation tools to better understand the clinical implications of each score. Traditional metrics such as AUC and sensitivity provide statistical insights, but they do not directly inform whether applying a model in clinical practice would lead to better outcomes [48]. Net benefit analysis allowed us to assess the trade-offs between true positives and false positives across a range of clinically meaningful thresholds [48]. This approach enables a more nuanced understanding of how the use of each scoring system might influence clinical decision-making, resource allocation, and patient care in real-world settings [48]. Our findings showed that although all evaluated scoring systems demonstrated acceptable discriminative performance, the DCA revealed that only the GERtality model provided acceptable clinical utility for both outcomes. This model exhibited the highest NB across a wide range of threshold probabilities and consistently outperformed the other models in terms of clinical usefulness. In contrast, despite their acceptable statistical performance, the GTOS and GTOS II models showed limited clinical utility in the DCA, with NBs close to or below zero at many thresholds. These findings suggest that using GTOS and GTOS II in clinical practice may lead to unnecessary interventions or failure to identify high-risk patients in a timely manner.

Accurate scoring systems are crucial for prioritizing treatment, forecasting recovery, assessing trauma care, and distributing therapeutic resources, especially given the increasing incidence of major trauma among vulnerable populations like the elderly [49, 50]. GTPs often experience worse outcomes compared to younger adults due to their different physiological responses to injury [51], making it essential to have reliable prognostic models to guide clinical decision-making and improve patient care [50]. The management of these patients requires a multidisciplinary approach and a deep understanding of aging physiology [52]. In our study, GERtality showed the highest predictive performance for both mortality and MV needs in GTPs, offering a strong tool for risk stratification. Although models such as GTOS and TRISS also play important roles, further research is needed to validate and improve the calibration of these scoring systems.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The first limitation is that patient follow-up was restricted to the hospitalization period, ending at either discharge or death. As a result, the predictive performance of these scoring systems beyond discharge remains unknown due to the absence of post-discharge data. Furthermore, although the NTRI is a multicenter registry with a minimal dataset, addressing this research question required an extended dataset, prompting us to conduct the study as a single-center analysis. While single-center studies inherently limit the generalizability of findings to other centers, it is worth noting that Sina Hospital serves as a national referral center, receiving patients from a wide range of provinces across Iran. Although the absence of certain variables such as frailty indices, detailed comorbidities, medication use, shock parameters, and the exact cause of death may have influenced the results of our study, it is important to recognize that the trauma scoring systems themselves, by design, are composite indices. As evident from their calculation methods, these scores typically incorporate weighted combinations of multiple variables to summarize patient condition and risk. Therefore, the primary focus in most studies, including ours, is on evaluating the predictive performance of these composite scores rather than each individual input variable. Nonetheless, to mitigate the potential impact of this limitation, we went beyond discrimination analysis and also assessed the association between each scoring system and the study outcomes using multiple logistic regression models. In these models, we adjusted for key clinical variables including age, ISS, GCS, and importantly, the ASA, which served as a proxy for underlying comorbidities [53]. Additionally, age, which has a well-established strong correlation with frailty [54], was included in the models and may partially account for the influence of frailty in the absence of a formal frailty index.

Another important consideration is that, although the National Trauma Registry of Iran (NTRI) is a multicenter, hospital-based registry that includes institutions across various levels of care (e.g., primary, secondary, and tertiary centers), our study was conducted at Sina Hospital—a national tertiary/quaternary referral center. During the study period, no trauma patients were transferred out of this facility. Therefore, while “transferred out” is a general exclusion criterion within the NTRI framework, it was not applicable in the context of our study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, GERtality proved to be the most accurate scoring system for predicting mortality and MV needs in GTPs, outperforming other models like GTOS, GTOS II, TRISS, and aTRISS. GERtality, with its targeted approach for GTPs, proves to be a valuable tool for clinicians in assessing the severity of trauma and predicting adverse outcomes. This study emphasizes the importance of age-specific trauma scoring systems in the effective management and prognostication of GTPs, improving decision-making and resource allocation in trauma care.
