Dissociation kinetics of G proteins from G protein–coupled receptors and effects of allosteric modulation
Authors: Jinan Wang, Anh T. N. Nguyen, Victor A. Adediwura, Cam Sinh Lu, Samantha M. McNeill, Manuela Jörg, Peter J. Scammells, Arthur Christopoulos, Lauren T. May, Yinglong Miao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceProtein–protein interactions (PPIs) are critical in cellular signaling but pose significant challenges for simulations due to slow dynamics. Dissociation of intracellular proteins from G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs), primary targets of ~1/3 of marketed drugs, remains unexplored in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. We have employed a novel PPI-Gaussian accelerated MD (PPI-GaMD) method to investigate dissociation kinetics of G proteins from GPCRs. Our simulations successfully captured G protein dissociation, and the predictions agreed with available experimental data. Further PPI-GaMD simulations and kinetic assays showed that positive allosteric modulators reduced the dissociation rate of G protein from a model GPCR. We have provided insight into GPCR–G protein dissociation kinetics and effects of allosteric modulation. This will facilitate allosteric drug discovery of GPCRs.

G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) are key cellular signaling proteins and represent primary targets of ~1/3 currently marketed drugs (1). GPCRs preferentially couple with specific subtypes of intracellular heterotrimeric G proteins, which subsequently dissociate from their receptors to regulate various downstream signaling pathways within the cell (2). It is important to investigate GPCR–G protein interactions, especially the G protein dissociation kinetics that play a pivotal role in signal transduction.

Important insights into structural changes of GPCRs bound to different G proteins have been obtained through structural biology techniques such as X-ray crystallography and cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) (3). Notably, cryo-EM has enabled structural determination of the same GPCR bound to different G proteins, shedding light on the mechanism of GPCR–G protein coupling selectivity (4, 5). For instance, cryo-EM structures of the β1-adrenergic receptor (β1AR) bound to Gi and Gs proteins (4), as well as the kappa opioid receptor (KOR) bound to Gi1, GoA, Gz, and Gg proteins (5), have offered valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying G protein selectivity. However, superposing structures of β1AR bound by the Gi and Gs proteins reveals nearly identical conformations for both the receptor and G proteins, with root mean square deviation (RMSD) of only 0.4 Å for the receptor (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Similarly, the Gi1- and GoA-coupled KOR exhibited receptor RMSD value of 0.7 Å (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Only subtle structural differences have been identified in the binding of different G proteins to the same GPCR, and these differences have yet to fully clarify the mechanism of G protein selectivity. Furthermore, structural biology techniques primarily offer static snapshots of GPCR–G protein complexes, leaving the dynamic aspects of protein interactions largely unexplored. This highlights the need to investigate the binding kinetics of GPCR–G protein interactions. A comprehensive understanding of G protein selectivity requires examining the dynamic processes by which GPCRs interact with specific G proteins.

Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) experiments showed that the lifetime of GPCR–G protein complexes determines the efficiency and selectivity of G protein coupling to muscarinic GPCRs (6, 7). The binding kinetics of Gi/o, Gq/11, Gs, and G12/13 proteins to muscarinic M1, M2, and M3 receptors were measured in the absence of nucleotides (6). A strong correlation was identified between the GPCR–G protein lifetime/dissociation rates (koff) and their coupling efficiencies in live cells (6). Furthermore, ligands with different efficacies have been found to regulate the kinetics of GPCR–G protein interactions (7, 8). However, these experiments are very expensive, time-consuming, and are limited to studies of few GPCR–G protein complexes (6, 7, 9–12).

On the other hand, computational modeling has proven useful in studying protein–protein interactions (PPIs), including the GPCR–G protein interactions (13–16). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have identified several critical regions for GPCR–G protein interactions, such as the transmembrane helix 6 (TM6) intracellular domain and the Gα α5 helix (17–19). These simulations have shown that conformational dynamics of the receptor–G protein complex depend on the bound ligands (20) and, consistent with the NMR experiments, that both agonist and G protein are required for full activation of GPCRs (21). Moreover, MD simulations have suggested that binding of the active GPCR to G protein is necessary for nucleotide release from the G protein (22–25). Recently, dynamic spatiotemporal intermolecular contacts were determined for GPCR–G protein coupling selectivity and promiscuity based on MD simulations of 10 GPCR–G protein complexes (14). MD simulations suggested that GPCRs maintain contacts with a common central interface, while the selectivity results from G protein specific contacts at the periphery of the interface. Nevertheless, to date MD simulations have not captured the dynamic processes of GPCR–G protein binding and dissociation, due to limited simulation timescales.

To overcome the limitations of conventional MD (cMD), enhanced sampling methods have been developed during the last several decades, as reviewed earlier (26–29). However, only few of these methods have been applied to study GPCR–G protein interactions. They include Umbrella Sampling (19, 30, 31), Metadynamics (32–35), and Gaussian accelerated MD (GaMD) (36–38). Umbrella Sampling has been used to calculate free energy profiles of the TM6 outward movement during receptor coupling to the G proteins (39). Metadynamics simulations have been performed to investigate dynamic effects of different ligands and intracellular binding partners on the β2AR (34) and examine differences of GPCRs coupled by the G protein versus G protein mimetic nanobody (35). However, Umbrella Sampling and Metadynamics require predefined collective variables (CVs) before running the simulations, which could be challenging in case of complicated G protein dissociation from the GPCRs. The CVs could place potential constraints in the protein dissociation pathways. In case important CVs are missing, these simulations could also suffer from “hidden energy barriers”. For other related simulation methods, Tempered Binding has been successfully applied to simulate protein–protein association by scaling atomic protein interactions by a factor that is selected from a ladder of discrete values, which are system dependent (40). Markov State Models could require expensive simulation data (e.g., thousands of microseconds) in order to sufficiently sample the PPIs and estimate the kinetic rates (41).

GaMD simulations have successfully captured spontaneous binding of a G protein mimetic nanobody to the M2 muscarinic GPCR, shedding light on dynamic GPCR–G protein interactions (37). In this regard, regular GaMD applies boost potentials globally on the entire system to facilitate protein binding and conformational changes. Compared with protein binding, dissociation of protein complexes takes place on even longer timescales (seconds to hours) (6, 7, 9–12, 42) (Fig. 1), leaving MD simulations of GPCR–G protein dissociation largely unexplored. To address this challenge, we will apply our recently developed protein–protein interaction GaMD (PPI-GaMD) method (43). PPI-GaMD differs from regular GaMD by selectively boosting the nonbonded protein interaction energy, significantly lowering the energetic barriers for protein dissociation. Another boost potential is applied to the remaining system potential energy to enhance protein conformational changes. Microsecond PPI-GaMD simulations have been able to capture repetitive protein dissociation and binding, enabling predictions of protein binding kinetics (43). It shows further improved efficiency compared with regular GaMD and other enhanced sampling methods in simulations of PPIs. Therefore, we will apply PPI-GaMD to simulate the slow G dissociation from GPCRs.

For GPCRs, allosteric modulators offer distinct advantages over orthosteric agonists, particularly in terms of higher selectivity, as they bind to topographically distant (“allosteric”) sites that often exhibit divergent sequences and conformations (44, 45). Interestingly, structures of many GPCRs in the presence and absence of allosteric modulators often appear similar (43, 44). This suggests that dynamics may also play a crucial role in GPCR allosteric modulation (46), influencing downstream signaling, such as G protein dissociation. However, additional research is required to elucidate the mechanisms underpinning allosteric modulation of G protein binding kinetics (44).

In this study, we have conducted a benchmark analysis of PPI-GaMD (47) to predict the G protein dissociation rates of five GPCR–G protein systems for which experimental kinetic data are available (Fig. 1). PPI-GaMD simulations successfully captured the dissociation of G proteins from the GPCRs, allowing us to characterize the associated free energy landscapes and predict the G protein dissociation rate constants. Encouraged by the strong agreement of our simulation predictions with the experimental data, we performed further PPI-GaMD simulations to investigate the effects of allosteric modulators on Gi protein dissociation from the adenosine A1 receptor (A1R). PPI-GaMD simulations predicted changes in dissociation rates of the Gi protein from the A1R in the presence of positive allosteric modulators (PAMs). A bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) biosensor approach assessed Gβγ interactions in the absence and presence of PAMs. The simulations and experiments were consistent with each other, providing valuable insights into how allosteric modulators can influence the GPCR–G protein dissociation kinetics.

Results

Comparison of G Protein Dissociation Rates Predicted from PPI-GaMD Simulations with FRET Experimental Data.

Three independent 500 ns PPI-GaMD simulations were performed on five GPCR–G protein complexes starting from their cryo-EM structures, including the α2AAR-Gi, β1AR-Gs, β2AR-Gs, A2AR-Gs, and M2R-Go (Fig. 1 D–H and SI Appendix, Table S1). The center-of-mass distance between the receptor NPxxY motif and the last five residues of Gα5 helix (GPCR:NPxxY-G:α5 distance) were calculated to monitor the G protein dissociation during PPI-GaMD simulations (Fig. 1 I–M). With close examination of the simulation trajectories, a cutoff distance was set to >30 Å for the unbound states between the GPCRs and G proteins. The G proteins spontaneously dissociated from the receptors in each PPI-GaMD simulation of all GPCR–G protein systems. We recorded the time periods for the G protein in the bound (τB) state throughout the PPI-GaMD simulations as ~261.8 ± 28.8 ns, 123.2 ± 23.8 ns, 255.8 ± 23.8 ns, 206.9 ± 29.7 ns, and 419.3 ± 33.9 ns for the α2AAR-Gi, β1AR-Gs, β2AR-Gs, A2AR-Gs, and M2R-Go systems, respectively (Fig. 1 I–M). Without reweighting of the PPI-GaMD simulations, the G protein dissociation rate constants (koff*) of the α2AAR-Gi, β1AR-Gs, β2AR-Gs, A2AR-Gs, and M2R-Go were calculated directly as 4.82 ± 0.44 × 106 s−1, 1.54 ± 0.21 × 107 s−1, 6.03 ± 2.65 × 106 s−1, 5.27 ± 0.68 × 106 s−1, and 4.33 ± 2.58 × 106 s−1, respectively (SI Appendix, Table S2). Compared to the G protein residence time observed in FRET experiments, which ranges from seconds to hours, PPI-GaMD simulations accelerated the G protein dissociation by ~6 to 8 orders of magnitude.

Next, we reweighted the PPI-GaMD simulations to calculate acceleration factors of the G protein dissociation and recover the original dissociation rate constants using the Kramers’ rate theory (SI Appendix, Tables S2 and S3). In the reweighted PMF profiles, the G protein dissociation free energy barriers (∆Foff) for the α2AAR-Gi, β1AR-Gs, β2AR-Gs, A2AR-Gs, and M2R-Go systems were 12.30 ± 0.29, 11.67 ± 2.02, 14.07 ± 0.24, 12.40 ± 0.60, and 15.76 ± 1.64 kcal/mol, respectively. Before reweighting, energy barriers from the PPI-GaMD simulation modified PMF profiles of these systems were significantly lower to 2.77 ± 0.19, 1.13 ± 0.18, 1.88 ± 0.44, 2.78 ± 0.32, and 2.83 ± 0.24 kcal/mol, respectively (SI Appendix, Table S3). The curvatures of the reweighed (w) and modified (w∗, no reweighting) free energy profiles were calculated near the protein bound (“B”) low-energy well and energy barrier (“Br”), as well as the ratio of apparent diffusion coefficients calculated from the PPI-GaMD simulations with reweighting (D) and without reweighting (modified, D∗) (SI Appendix, Table S3). According to the Kramers’ rate theory, G protein dissociation was significantly accelerated by factors of 3.21 × 107 for α2AAR-Gi, 9.05 × 106 for β1AR-Gs, 4.07 × 106 for β2AR-Gs, 1.22 × 105 for A2AR-Gs, and 1.03 × 107 for M2R-Go, respectively. Therefore, the average reweighted koff were calculated as 0.15 ± 0.08 s−1, 0.17 ± 0.07 s−1, 1.48 ± 1.05 s−1, 1.02 ± 1.00 s−1, and 0.42 ± 0.42 s−1, being consistent with the corresponding experimental measurements of 0.077 ± 0.012 s−1 for α2AAR-Gi (12), 0.12 ± 0.01 s−1 for β1AR-Gs (10), 0.387 ± 0.068 s−1 for β2AR-Gs (7), 0.068 ± 0.007 s−1 for A2AR-Gs (10) and 0.029 ± 0.004 s−1 for M2R-Go (6), respectively (Fig. 1 I–M and SI Appendix, Table S2). It is important to note that the FRET measurements were performed between the receptor C terminus and N terminus of Gγ for the α2AAR-Gi and β2AR-Gs systems (Fig. 1A), whereas between the Gα and Gβγ for the β1AR-Gs, A2AR-Gs, and M2R-Go systems (Fig. 1B). These measurements were different from the GPCR:NPxxY-G:α5 distance monitored in the PPI-GaMD simulations (Fig. 1 I–M). Therefore, the comparison of G protein dissociation rates between simulations and experiments were rather more qualitative, confirming mostly the trend of our kinetics predictions.

Intracellular Loop 2 (ICL2) and TM5/6 Play Critical Roles in G Protein Dissociation.

We further examined the pathways of G protein dissociation from the GPCRs in the PPI-GaMD simulations. Using β2AR-Gs as an example, two distinct G protein dissociation pathways (P1 and P2) were identified (Fig. 2 and Movies S1 and S2). The P1 pathway involved interactions of the G protein with the receptor ICL2 region (Fig. 2B and Movie S1), whereas the P2 pathway showed more interactions of the G protein with the receptor TM5 and TM6 (Fig. 2E and Movie S2). Therefore, the GPCR:NPxxY-G:α5 distance and the number of contacts between the receptor ICL2 and G protein (Ncontact-(ICL2-G)) were selected as reaction coordinates to calculate the 2D free energy profile. Three system conformational states were identified according to the low-energy wells shown in the free energy profile calculated by combining three PPI-GaMD production simulations, i.e., the dissociated “D,” intermediate “I1” and “I2” states (Fig. 2A), although certain variations were observed among the free energy profiles calculated from the three individual simulations (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The reaction coordinate values were then recorded at the energy minimum in each low-energy well, although the low-energy regions could cover a range of values. The cryo-EM bound state B (PDB: 9DHR) was observed in a low-energy region, with the (Ncontact-(ICL2-G), GPCR:NPxxY-G:α5 distance) centered around (45, 14.3Å). In the D state, these values shifted to approximately (0, 55.0 Å) (Fig. 2A). The intermediate I1 and I2 states centered around (61, 16.0 Å) and (4, 17.7 Å), respectively. This suggested an increase in the number of contacts between the receptor ICL2 and G protein in the I1 state, which aligned with the P1 dissociation pathway of the G protein (Fig. 2B). Receptor residues R1313.50, Y141ICL2, and Q142ICL2 formed critical interactions with the Gs protein in the PPI-GaMD simulations. Such interactions were also highlighted in an experimentally determined intermediate state of β2AR-Gs (48). Specifically, the receptor residues R1313.50 and Q142ICL2 formed salt-bridge and hydrogen bond interactions with E392 and Y391 of the Gs protein, respectively. Additionally, receptor residues Y141ICL2 and Q142ICL2 formed hydrophobic interactions with the C-terminus of the Gα5 helix as the latter moved closer to the receptor ICL2 (Fig. 2D). In pathway P2, the G protein interacted with the receptor TM5/6 during dissociation. The receptor residues K2676.29 and I2335.72 formed hydrogen bond interactions with Gs residues L394 and R385, respectively. Most of the hydrophobic interactions formed between receptor TM5/6 and the C terminus of the Gα5 helix, involving receptor residues Q2295.68 and L2305.69, as well as Gs residues L388 and L394 (Fig. 2G). To assess the generalizability of the G protein dissociation pathways and intermediates across different GPCR–G protein systems, we calculated free energy landscapes using the same reaction coordinates for the α2AAR-Gi, β1AR-Gs, A2AR-Gs, and M2R-Go systems (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). We identified similar low-energy states (D, I1, and I2) across these complexes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). In the D state, (Ncontact-ICL2-G, GPCR:NPxxY-G:α5 distance) centered around (0, 55.0 Å), (0, 60.0 Å), (0, 55.0 Å), and (0, 50.0 Å) for the α2AAR-Gi, β1AR-Gs, A2AR-Gs, and M2R-Go, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The above distance values were identified from the local energy minimum positions in the PMF profiles, although the low-energy regions could cover a wide range of distances. In the I1 state, these values centered around (10, 15.0 Å), (30, 15.2 Å), (25, 18.0 Å), (61, 16.0 Å), (19, 15.5 Å), and (37, 19.3 Å), respectively. In the I2 state, (Ncontact-ICL2-G, GPCR:NPxxY-G:α5 distance) centered around (5, 19.0 Å), (27, 25.6 Å), (30, 15.2 Å) and (7, 17.0 Å) for the α2AAR-Gi, β1AR-Gs, A2AR-Gs, and M2R-Go, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Notably, pathway P1 involved mainly the I1 intermediate state, while pathway P2 involved mainly the I2 intermediate state (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

In addition, we evaluated the receptor conformational changes and agonist dynamics during G protein dissociation. The receptor intracellular TM3-TM6 distance and agonist RMSD, relative to the initial structure with the receptor TM helices aligned, were used for further free energy calculations. We identified low-energy states for each GPCR–G protein system (SI Appendix, Figs. S5 and S6). We identified similar low-energy states (B and I1) across these complexes, except for the β1AR-Gs system, which exhibited another low-energy intermediate I2 state (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The I2 intermediate state was also observed in the β2AR-Gs and M2R-Go (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Notably, a significant increase was observed in the TM3-TM6 distance upon G protein dissociation from the β1AR, β2AR, A2AR, and M2R. In the intermediate I2 state, the receptor TM6 intracellular end moved further outward from TM3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), being consistent with a recent finding that showed significantly increased flexibility of TM6 during Gs protein dissociation from the β2AR (49).

Additional free energy calculations using the agonist RMSD and GPCR:NPxxY-G:α5 distance as reaction coordinate revealed low agonist fluctuations with RMSD ≤ 2.2 Å in the low-energy states of the α2AAR-Gi and β1AR-Gs complexes (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). In comparison, a significant increase was observed in the agonist RMSD in the G protein dissociated states in the β2AR-Gs, A2AR-Gs, and M2R-Go (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). It is important to note that the relatively short PPI-GaMD simulations performed in this study could not capture the complete GPCR deactivation and agonist dissociation. Additionally, the simulations were not fully converged, which may affect the accuracy of the free energy calculations. Nonetheless, the observed flexibility increases of both the agonist and receptor during G protein dissociation highlight the allosteric stabilizing effects mediated by G protein, consistent with prior findings (50, 51).

PAMs Reduced the GPCR–G Protein Dissociation Rate.

Next, we extended our study to explore whether the binding of PAMs influenced GPCR–G protein dissociation kinetics, using the A1R-Gi complex as a model system. PPI-GaMD simulations and BRET experiments were conducted on the A1R coupled to Gi and the adenosine receptor agonist 5′-(N-ethylcarboxamido)adenosine (NECA), in the absence and presence of the A1R PAMs (T62 and MIPS521) (Fig. 3).

Activation of the A1R leads to the dissociation of the Gi/o protein heterotrimer, allowing the Gβγ subunits to bind and activate downstream signaling effectors. We evaluated the kinetics of Gβγ interactions using BRET biosensors, namely Venus-tagged Gβ1γ2 subunits and NanoLuc-tagged masGRK3ct—a nanoluciferase-tagged and membrane-associated C-terminal fragment of G protein receptor kinase (GRKct) that binds free Gβγ dimers (Fig. 3A). Robust concentration-dependent Gβγ-GRKct interactions were observed following exposure to 0.1 nM to 1 μM NECA (SI Appendix, Fig. S7) with a potency (pEC50) of 8.29 ± 0.07 (n = 3). These data demonstrated the BRET biosensor approach provided sufficient kinetic resolution alongside a robust signal-to-noise ratio to assess Gβγ kinetics following A1R activation. We then selected 1 μM NECA concentration, which stimulated maximal Gβγ-GRKct interaction, to evaluate the ability of the A1R PAMs (10 μM MIPS521 and T62) to modulate agonist-mediated protein interaction kinetics (Fig. 3B). BRET measurements encompassed multiple G protein signaling steps, including the separation of the Gα and Gβγ subunits, and Gβγ association with GRKct. However, as these processes were the same across these systems, the differences observed in the BRET measurements likely reflected variations in the dissociation kinetics of Gi protein from A1R bound by different ligands. The rate of Gβγ-GRKct interactions following A1R exposure to the agonist NECA was 4.09 ± 0.41 min−1 (i.e., 0.068 ± 0.006 s−1). In the presence of the A1R PAMs, T62, and MIPS521, the rate of NECA-mediated Gβγ-GRKct interactions significantly decreased to 1.59 ± 0.13 min−1 (i.e., 0.027 ± 0.002 s−1) and 1.86 ± 0.13 min−1 (i.e., 0.031 ± 0.006 s−1), respectively (Fig. 3C; **P < 0.01, one-way ANOVA with Dunnett post hoc test).

Five 500 ns PPI-GaMD production simulations were performed on the NECA-bound A1R-Gi, A1R-Gi-T62, and A1R-Gi-MIPS521 complexes (SI Appendix, Table S4). The Gi protein dissociated from the A1R in the A1R-Gi, A1R-Gi-T62, and A1R-Gi-MIPS521 systems at approximately 137.2 ± 44.3 ns, 279.0 ± 26.9 ns and 373.9 ± 40.3 ns, respectively. Using the same protocol as for the GPCR–G protein benchmark systems, we calculated the reweighted Gi protein dissociation rates koff as 5.45 ± 4.87 s−1, 1.39 ± 0.71 s−1, and 0.45 ± 0.31 s−1, respectively (Fig. 3 E–G and SI Appendix, Table S6). Therefore, the T62 and MIPS521 PAMs of A1R reduced the dissociation rate of Gi protein in the PPI-GaMD simulations despite large estimate error bars, which correlated well with the overall trend of Gβγ-GRKct interaction rates measured in the BRET experiments (Fig. 3).

Three low-energy states, including the I1 and I2 intermediates and dissociated D, were identified similarly in free energy profiles of the A1R-Gi, A1R-Gi-T62, and A1R-Gi-MIPS521(Fig. 4). In the intermediate I1 state, receptor residues Q2105.68, L1073.52, P112ICL2, and L113ICL2 formed interactions with residues L354, C352, and N348 in the G protein (Fig. 4B). With binding of T62, more interactions formed between receptor residues I2075.65, Q2105.68, V1093.54, R1083.53, P112ICL2, and L113ICL2, with G protein residues L354, C352, N348, L349, I345, and I344, respectively (Fig. 4E). With MIPS521 binding, the strongest interactions were detected among the three studied systems, involving receptor residues L2366.37, I2326.33, L1073.52, R1083.53, P112ICL2, and L113ICL2, with G protein residues L354, C352, N348, L349, I345, and I344 (Fig. 4H). PAM binding enhanced the interaction between the A1R ICL2 and the G protein Gα5 helix, consistent with the observed decrease in G protein dissociation rates upon PAM binding. In the intermediate I2 state, receptor residues L2366.37, L2015.59, L2065.64, and Q2105.68 formed interactions with G protein residues L354, L349, F355, I345, and K346 (Fig. 4C). With T62 binding, receptor residues L2366.37, Y2005.58, V2035.61, I2075.65, and Q2105.68 formed interactions with G protein residues L354, L349, F355, and I345 (Fig. 4F). With MIPS521 binding, receptor residues L2366.37, Y2005.58, V2035.61, I2075.65, and Q2105.68 formed interactions with G protein residues L354, L349, F355, I345, and K346 (Fig. 4I). Consistent with other GPCR–G protein systems, two G protein dissociation pathways (P1 and P2) were also identified in the A1R-Gi systems (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Notably, the G protein dissociation pathway aligned with previous GaMD simulations of the G protein binding to the A1R (52).

To assess the impact of PAM binding and G protein dissociation on the receptor and agonist dynamics, free energy profiles were calculated using GPCR:NPxxY-G:α5 distance, agonist RMSD relative to the starting conformations, and TM3-TM6 distance as reaction coordinates. For A1R-Gi, five low-energy states were identified: bound (B), intermediate (I2), and dissociated (“D1,” “D2,” “D3”). The A1R-Gi-T62 and A1R-Gi-MIPS521 systems sampled only three low-energy states, i.e., the bound (B) and dissociated (D1, D2), with significantly reduced conformational space (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). This highlights the higher flexibility of the receptor and agonist in the absence of PAM and further supports the allosteric mechanism by which PAM regulates conformational changes in the receptor and agonist to modulate downstream signaling, consistent with our previous findings (51).

Discussion

Previous studies suggested that PPIs often involve multiple protein binding and dissociation pathways, with dissociation typically following the reverse pathway of binding (47). In this study, PPI-GaMD simulations have also revealed two distinct G protein dissociation pathways, i.e., P1 and P2. These pathways closely aligned with findings from a recent time-resolved cryo-EM study of β2AR-Gs (53) and its intermediate structure (48). Additionally, the P1 pathway in the A1R system aligned well with previous GaMD simulations of the G protein binding to the A1R (52). The simulations highlighted the critical roles of receptor TM5/6 and ICL2 in the G protein dissociation (SI Appendix, Table S8). Additionally, the receptor DRY motif engages with the G protein through intermediate states, while the Gα5 helix of the G protein mediates most interactions (SI Appendix, Table S8). This finding aligns with previous studies highlighting the critical role of these regions in GPCR–G protein coupling (48, 54).

This study has several limitations. First, our models omitted the flexible α-helical domain of the G protein, which was missing in the cryo-EM structures but known to influence G protein activity and dissociation dynamics (23, 55, 56). Second, our simulations employed nucleotide-free states of G proteins, whereas the nucleotide exchange is an essential biological step for G protein activation and subsequent dissociation (57). Third, we did not include the GPCR ICL3, a region implicated in GPCR signaling regulation (58). Future studies will aim to incorporate these missing parts and improve our simulation models to better represent the experimental biological conditions.

The GPCR–G protein dissociation kinetics were estimated based on only 3 to 5 PPI-GaMD simulations with one dissociation event per trajectory for each system. Our G protein dissociation rates were reported with large SD. Meanwhile, the free energy profiles calculated from PPI-GaMD simulations were not fully converged since certain variations were observed among the individual simulations, especially with large statistical uncertainties in the intermediate regions (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). It is difficult to calculate accurate free energy profiles for such large systems due to the rare-event nature of the G dissociation processes. Consequently, our predictions should be considered as semiquantitative, especially in comparison with experimental data with different conditions. Robust estimation of the protein kinetic rates and free energy profiles would ideally require capturing repetitive G protein dissociation and rebinding in each simulation to ensure statistical significance and reproducibility. Nevertheless, we observed complete G protein dissociation in each of the PPI-GaMD simulations. Two representative pathways of G protein dissociation are shown in Movies S1 and S2. Despite statistical uncertainties, our analyses suggested that pathway P1 involved the I1 intermediate state in which the G protein interacted with the receptor ICL2 region, while pathway P2 involved the I2 intermediate state in which the G protein interacted more with the receptor TM5 and TM6 (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Note that one low-energy state label corresponds to one unique conformation of the system. To improve the statistics, future studies could employ a larger number of PPI-GaMD simulations with extended lengths, as well as further method developments to enhance particularly protein rebinding.

Our study reinforced the concept that G protein binding allosterically stabilizes GPCR in its active state and enhances agonist binding, consistent with previous studies (50). Combining PPI-GaMD simulations and BRET biosensor experiments, we further demonstrated that PAM binding reduced the dissociation rate of G protein from the A1R and subsequent Gβγ interactions, thereby enhancing the receptor signaling strength. It is important to note that the BRET experiments encompassed more complex processes, including the G protein dissociation, separation of the Gα and Gβγ subunits, and subsequent Gβγ association with GRKct. In comparison, the PPI-GaMD simulations captured only the initial G protein dissociation. Since the downstream processes, such as the separation of Gα and Gβγ, and the Gβγ association with GRKct were the same across the three systems, the differences observed in the BRET experiments likely reflected variations in the initial kinetics of G protein dissociation. Although the simulation and experimental data could not be compared directly due to these limitations, our computational predicted trend agreed well with the experimental data, supporting the conclusion that PAMs reduced G protein dissociation kinetics and enhanced receptor signaling activity.

In summary, PPI-GaMD simulations successfully captured spontaneous G protein dissociation and the calculated dissociation rates were mostly consistent with experimental findings across five GPCR–G protein systems with available experimental data. Furthermore, complementary PPI-GaMD simulations and fluorescence experiments revealed that PAMs reduced G protein dissociation rates. These findings provide critical insights into the mechanisms of GPCR signaling and allosteric modulation, enabling a detailed characterization of the kinetics and pathways of G protein dissociation—a key event in the GPCR signaling cascade—and advancing allosteric drug discovery for GPCRs.

Materials and Methods

3xHA-A1R-ΔGall-HEK293a cells were transiently transfected with Gαi2, Venus-tagged Gβ1γ2 and NanoLuc-tagged masGRK3ct (59). BRET experiments were performed using increasing concentrations of NECA in the absence and presence of 10 μM T62 or MIPS521. The BRET assays measured the rate of Gβγ–masGRK3ct interactions to assess the effects of allosteric modulators on A1R mediated G protein dissociation. PPI-GaMD simulations were performed on the α2AAR-Gi, β1AR-Gs, β2AR-Gs, A2AR-Gs, M2R-Go, A1R-Gi, A1R-Gi-T62, and A1R-Gi-MIPS521, following a previously established protocol (47, 51). The CHARMM36m (60, 61) force field parameter set was used for the protein and lipids. The CGenFF force field (62, 63) was used for agonists and PAMs. NAMD2.12 (64) was used for initial energy minimization, thermalization, and 20-ns cMD equilibration. The output files of cMD simulations using NAMD were converted to AMBER format using the ParmEd tool AMBER package. PPI-GaMD simulations implemented in a graphics processing unit version of AMBER 22 (47, 65) were performed for the GPCR–G protein systems. The PPI-GaMD simulations involved a short cMD of 14 ns, 46-ns PPI-GaMD equilibration, and finally three or five independent 500 ns GaMD production runs with randomized initial atomic velocities for each system. Details of BRET experiments, PPI-GaMD simulations, system setup, and simulation analysis are provided in SI Appendix.
