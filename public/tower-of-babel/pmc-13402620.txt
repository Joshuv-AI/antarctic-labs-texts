Microscopic image enhancement for accurate sickle cell disease identification using intuitionistic fuzzy driven retinex
Authors: Jayasurya Ramakrishnan, Sriramakrishnan Pathmanaban, Vinodkumar Arumugam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSickle cell disease (SCD) is a debilitating genetic blood disorder that must be identified and diagnosed early for effective treatment planning. Several factors hinder early identification in microscopic images, including uneven blood smears, lack of focus during capture, inconsistent illumination, and improper microscope settings. This paper addresses the aforementioned factors using the Intuitionistic Fuzzy-Driven Retinex (IFDR) method. The work contains two phases: microscopic image enhancement using IFDR and classification using DenseNet121. IFDR is a hybrid model that combines the Intuitionistic Fuzzy Index (IFI) with Single-Scale Retinex (SSR) to enhance uncertainty modeling and correct illumination variations in microscopic images. The IFI module enhances structural details and contrast in local area, while the SSR component performs global illumination balancing, resulting in diagnostically improved images. The enhanced images provide richer visual information, including clearer sickle cell edges, improved detection of overlapping cells, higher contrast, and less noise. For sickle cell identification, the enhanced images are used instead of the original images to train and test the DenseNet121 model, achieving competitive accuracy. The dataset used in this study was obtained from Kaggle. The performance of the proposed model is compared to the original and enhanced images. The classification accuracy for the original image is 0.877, whereas the proposed enhancement technique yields an accuracy of 0.964 for sickle cell identification. Comprehensive ablation studies were performed, confirming the superiority of the proposed hybrid method over its individual components. From experimental results, it is observed that the enhanced images yield better performance than the fine-tuned models and state of the art methods. Further, Grad-Cam-based explainable AI is employed to interpret the model classification results on enhanced images.Keywords: Intuitionistic Fuzzy, Image Enhancement, Deep Learning, Sickle Cell Disease, Single Scale RetinexSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

The primary function of red blood cells (RBCs), which are essential components of human blood, is to transport oxygen from the lungs to organs1. RBCs typically have a 120-days lifespan, after which they are removed from circulation and replaced by freshly formed bone marrow cells. The RBC counts usually range from 4.7 to 6.1 million cells per micro liter for males, whereas 4.2 to 5.4 million cells per micro liter for females, depending on age, gender, and overall health2. The RBC analysis yields important insights into the physical state, which helps in diagnosis and treatment planning.

Anemia is a hereditary disease caused by the production of abnormal hemoglobin, which leads to the formation of sickle-shaped RBC cells in blood3. In contrast, defective hemoglobin will have a crescent shape, commonly known as the “sickle” shape4. The main function of hemoglobin is to carry oxygen molecules across the body via blood; the sickle-shaped defective hemoglobin cannot carry oxygen, which leads to obstruction of blood circulation. The lifespan of a sickle cell is also notably smaller than that of a healthy cell; a defective cell’s lifespan stays up to 10 to 20 days, whereas a healthy cell can remain alive up to 120 days. Patients with sickle cell disease frequently suffer from intense pain, organ harm, anemia, organ damage and high susceptibility to infection5.

Problem statements

Early identification of sickle cell disease is extremely important for its treatment and prevention of problems6. Several factors can affect the quality of microscopic pictures, including uneven blood smear, lack of focus while capturing, inconsistent illumination, microscopic settings, and low resolution7. It can make it difficult to distinguish them from other cells or identify abnormalities, especially sickle cells, for proper diagnosis. Microscopic image enhancement is a crucial preprocessing step aimed at improving the visual quality and diagnostic utility of images captured through microscopes. These images, particularly in biomedical applications, often suffer from challenges such as low contrast, non-uniform illumination, noise, and blur due to limitations in sample preparation, staining variations, and optical systems.

Several techniques were developed for image enhancement, including log transformation, gamma correction, histogram equalization, contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) and filtering methods8. These techniques are application-dependent and are particularly effective when applied to digital images. Limited research has focused on enhancing microscopic images through approaches such as fuzzy logic9 and Retinex-based methods10.

In current decades, deep learning and artificial intelligence combo gives groundbreaking solutions across a wide range of real-world challenges, which include healthcare & medical imaging11,12, agriculture & food security13, industry & manufacturing14, transportation & smart cities15, security & surveillance16,17, and natural language processing (NLP)18. Advancements of artificial intelligence is now automated the diagnostic equipment with the help of deep learning on microscopic images to detect the sickle cells19,20. Using deep learning for the classification of results enhances it to a great extent and makes it more precise for the detection of sickle cells, enabling timely treatment and customized care plans21.

Especially, DenseNet121 yields good classification accuracy on microscopic images for many applications like Fungi and Malaria Parasites detection22,23. There are several advantages to using DenseNet121 based classification in microscopic images. In DenseNet121, each layer receives input from all previous layers, which preserves fine-grained features. Enhance feature reuse, which is critical for recognizing small, subtle patterns like those in microscopic images. It reduces the vanishing gradient problem, which is especially helpful for detecting minute organisms or cells. DenseNet121 is more parameter-efficient compared with other CNNs with similar depth and has less overfitting on small medical datasets. These advantages are enhancing the performance of DenseNet121 on microscopic images.

Related works

The following literature was done with enhancement techniques on digital images using fuzzy logic, Retinex, etc. Ragavendirane and Dhanasekar propose a hybrid IFI-HER technique to improve low-light images by combining a new Intuitionistic Fuzzy Generator (IFG) with Histogram Equalization Retinex (HER)24. Driven by the detail and color loss in low light, the approach improves contrast with natural colors retained. The approach includes fuzzification of the image, creation of an IFI, application of HER, and choosing the optimum result using entropy optimization. The suggested method exceeds conventional methods in measures such as PSNR, AMBE, MI, and BRISQUE, with better visibility and edge precision. It performs very well in adverse illumination conditions. It is not as suitable for other types of degradations (such as motion blur) and real-time implementation because it has a high computational time.

Babu et al. have introduced a new image enhancement method by combining Intuitionistic Fuzzy (IF) transformation with ABR-fractal fractional differentiation for enhanced global and local contrast in low-light images25. The method starts by transforming the image into an intuitionistic fuzzy space for enhanced brightness and reduction of ambiguity among pixel intensities. Then, a fractal-fractional differential operator under the ABR model is used to enhance local details and textures using a specially designed mask that relies on fractional order and fractal dimension. Lastly, a fusion mechanism integrates the globally and locally processed images into one output. This approach surpasses the classical enhancement methods in quantitative measurements such as entropy, contrast, and texture energy (through GLCM), and has greater edge and fine detail preservation. This renders it particularly efficient for low-illumination and low-contrast image situations. Yet, its reliance on multiple stages of transformation, with parameter adjustment, restricts real-time application and efficiency for distortions other than those of an illumination nature.

Liu et al. have proposed a method to enhance low-light images, intended to recover details hidden by low illumination26. The algorithm is in a multi-stage process: image transformation from RGB to HSV color space, V component dynamic range stretching, estimation of illumination by adaptive gamma correction, and brightness enhancement using the Retinex model. The approach is computationally effective, surpassing existing techniques in qualitative as well as quantitative evaluation. RBFA captures higher visibility, enhanced brightness uniformity, and natural color retention and is the best for low-light conditions.

Research gaps and limitations of existing methods

From the literature, despite significant advancements in microscopic image enhancement and sickle cell detection, several limitations persist in existing approaches. Most conventional methods primarily focus on either contrast enhancement or illumination correction independently. It leads to suboptimal performance when dealing with complex microscopic images that exhibit both uneven illumination and inherent uncertainty. Additionally, many techniques fail to preserve fine structural details, such as cell boundaries and overlapping regions, which are critical for accurate diagnosis.

Traditional methods like histogram equalization, CLAHE, or Retinex variants end up over-enhancing images or losing important, subtle diagnostic information in medical images. Though fuzzy logic–based methods have been promising, most methods so far have used only traditional fuzzy sets, which only address one level of uncertainty (membership). This reduces the method’s capability to address the vagueness and ambiguity inherent in medical images, especially in noisy or low-light situations. Despite considerable progress in general image enhancement techniques, microscopic image enhancement for sickle cell detection remains underexplored and underdeveloped. While a few studies have attempted to apply fuzzy logic for microscopic enhancement, they often overlook the uncertainty and ambiguity in pixel intensity, especially in borderline or partially deformed cells.

Motivations

These conventional approaches also struggle with low contrast and uneven illumination inherent in stained blood smear images. Other factors are high noise levels and cell overlap that obscure critical diagnostic details, and inadequate edge preservation, which is essential for detecting the elongated or crescent shapes of Sickled cells. Existing methods lack robustness and adaptability to variations in staining protocols, microscope settings, and magnification scales, which affects their generalizability.

Furthermore, existing deep learning-based classification models often rely on raw or insufficiently enhanced images, thereby limiting their ability to achieve robust and reliable performance. Despite advancements in image processing, there remains a notable gap and increasing need for specialized image enhancement techniques for microscopic images, especially those used in the identification of sickle cell disease. These gaps and challenges highlight the need for a computationally efficient hybrid framework that jointly performs local and global enhancement, uncertainty modeling, and illumination correction to improve microscopic image quality for accurate sickle cell identification.

Novelty of the proposed work

To address the above mentioned challenges, this study proposes a hybrid IFDR model that integrates IFI for effective uncertainty representation and structural enhancement with SSR for global illumination compensation. This combined approach aims to produce diagnostically meaningful enhanced images, thereby improving the accuracy and reliability of sickle cell identification. The novelty of the IFDR framework lies in:

The integration of IFI and SSR to jointly perform uncertainty modeling, local structural enhancement, and global illumination correction.

Difference from existing fuzzy-based and retinex-based methods

In contrast to conventional fuzzy-based methods that mainly emphasize local contrast enhancement, the proposed approach employs the IFI to more effectively capture uncertainty while maintaining structural integrity. Similarly, while conventional Retinex-based methods focus on illumination correction, they often neglect structural enhancement and may introduce artifacts. The proposed framework differs by combining both approaches in a unified manner, where IFI enhances local structures, and SSR performs global illumination balancing.

Phase I: image enhancement - intuitionistic fuzzy driven retinex (IFDR)

Under the enhancement approach, intuitionistic fuzzy is used to increase the light and brightness of low-contrast microscopic images43. As a factor of application, the image sets are microscopic images extracted from blood samples, where images are low-light and lack features for analysis. Low-light image enhancement is a crucial function in micro imaging, where some key details are usually lost because of insufficient light or illumination in the machine settings or sensor constraints. The basic idea of intuitionistic fuzzy is to enhance the visibility of image features and make it visually good44. Enhancement procedures are not just necessary to enhance overall image quality but also to highlight low-contrast features that can be clinically important. In such applications, the application of Intuitionistic Fuzzy Set Theory appears to be a promising avenue. As compared to conventional fuzzy sets that model only one kind of uncertainty through membership functions, intuitionistic fuzzy sets include membership and non-membership degrees and a hesitancy component for supporting richer modeling of uncertainty.

This phase explains the hybridization of Intuitionistic fuzzy and single-scale Retinex for microscopic image enhancement. Intuitionistic Fuzzy Logic is an extension of traditional fuzzy logic introduced by Atanassov42. Unlike classical fuzzy sets, which define each element with a single membership value (µ), intuitionistic fuzzy sets define three parameters for each element: Membership degree (µ), non-membership degree (ν), and Hesitation degree (π) and Intuitionistic Fuzzy Index (IFI). With Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE)45 included, the technique guarantees localized contrast enhancement, efficiently processing low-contrast as well as unevenly lit images. The Single-Scale Retinex (SSR) is an image enhancement method modelled after the human visual sighting system46. The main idea is to increase the visual quality of photographs by correcting lighting changes (reflectance and illumination) and boosting the contrast of the image. The SSR has several disadvantages despite its ability to reduce noise and enhance local details and contrast. The technique is not suited for images with extremely non-uniform lighting since it cannot compress the dynamic range and reproduce tone simultaneously. Phase II: Image Classification

In recent times, deep learning has emerged as a strong method for automated diagnosis of diseases. For sickle cell classification, DenseNet121 is employed with the enhanced image , given by the IFDR. DenseNet121 is a CNN architecture and is able to extract features from given images. It can learn more deeply about the sickle cell structure, shape, position, and edges if trained with an IFDR-based enhanced image. This phase is aimed at enhancing gradient flow and parameter efficiency using dense connectivity to enhance images. Each layer of DenseNet121 takes inputs from all its preceding layers and transfers its feature maps to all the following layers. Such a pattern of connectivity assists in reducing the vanishing gradient problem, encourages feature reuse, and decreases the number of parameters as compared to conventional deep networks. Our preferred model for sickle cell classification effectively differentiates sickle cells and normal cells accurately using enhanced images. The model is trained to identify required features from microscopic images and classify them efficiently through a multi-stage feature extraction and classification process.

DenseNet121 is made up of four densely connected blocks, which are interspersed by transition layers comprising batch normalization, convolution, and pooling processes48. The model architecture for DenseNet121 is shown in Fig. 3. The core structure of our model is a DenseNet121 architecture that is based on dense connections between layers to provide feature extraction and gradient flow. The model takes as input 128 × 128 × 3 RGB images, which are initially passed through an initial convolutional and pooling layer for low-level feature extraction. The key components of the DenseNet121 model are explained in the sections below.

The DenseNet121 architecture for sickle cell classification.

The input layer is initially zero-padded to provide spatial consistency. A convolutional layer (64 filters, kernel size 7 × 7, and stride 2) is utilized, and then batch normalization is followed by a ReLU activation function. A max-pooling layer (kernel size 3 × 3, and stride 2) is employed to reduce the data in feature maps to reduce computational complexity without losing vital spatial information. Each Dense block contains 1 × 1 convolutions for reducing dimensionality and 3 × 3 convolutions for extracting spatial features. Batch normalization and ReLU activation to stabilize training and add non-linearity. Concatenation layers are used to preserve information flow between layers. This method ensures maximum feature reuse, enhancing efficiency and accuracy.

After passing through the 3 dense blocks, three separate Global Average Pooling (GAP) layers are applied at different stages after each major dense block. GAP layers significantly reduce the number of parameters while retaining critical spatial information, making the model lightweight and efficient. Three dense layers, each with 64 neurons, are applied with batch normalization and dropout regularization to prevent overfitting. These dense layers are combined into one feature representation (192 neurons total) before classification. A 16-neuron dense layer fine-tunes the extracted features before classification.

The model has 7.2 million trainable parameters, well-organized to optimize feature extraction while being computationally feasible. Batch normalization and dropout regularization are utilized at various stages to enhance generalization and prevent overfitting. The model is trained with a binary cross-entropy loss function, optimized with an Adam optimizer for rapid convergence49,50. The last output layer (2 neurons, Softmax activation) gives the probability of the input image being in either the normal or sickle cell category.

The enhanced images are used for classification (). The images are resized to 128 × 128 pixels and normalized to ensure model input uniformity. An 80 − 20 split is used for training and testing the model with a uniform distribution of classes maintained to handle the data bias at the image level. Class weights are calculated dynamically using the inverse frequency class weighting method based on training label distribution to handle class imbalance and achieve fair learning between categories. To ensure class balance between sickle and normal cells, a stratified sampling strategy was employed during the splitting process.

Model Interpretation and Post-Processing

For qualitative analysis, we employed Grad-CAM from Explainable AI (XAI)55, which shows the areas the model pays attention to at the time of classification. The ground-truth region annotations are not available for objective validation of the highlighted areas. Therefore, the Grad-CAM analysis is qualitative in nature and is intended only to provide visual insights into the model’s attention patterns, rather than to claim definitive interpretability or quantitative localization performance. The visualizations effectively show that the model trained using augmented images demonstrates improved feature localization and attention as opposed to original images, substantiating the influence of our preprocessing strategy. It allows DenseNet121 to focus on more meaningful sickle cell regions, improving interpretability and classification accuracy.

Table 13 shows the DenseNet121 interpretation results given by the Grad-CAM on both classes with and without IFDR-based image enhancement. These images are generated between the convolution layers, one of which retains the sickle cells spatial information. Feature maps from these layers preserve where sickle cells are present in the image. From Table 13, red, yellow, and blue colors indicate high, medium and low feature spatial importance, respectively, while classification.

On cautious inspection of the Grad-CAM visualizations, it appears that the model might be focusing on red areas with dense cellular boundaries, elongated contours, and irregularly shaped erythrocyte structures. Highlighted regions appear to correspond with some of the crescent-shaped or curved cell morphologies that are often associated with sickle cell disease. This could suggest that the network is learning shape features that are useful for positive-class discrimination. However, these observations should be taken with a grain of salt, as Grad-CAM yields coarse attention maps but not exact cellular localization. The highlighted areas may also correspond to overlapping cells, staining variation, illumination difference or background texture patterns in the smear images. Without expert-annotated ground truth regions, it is not possible to claim that the model is detecting only sickle-shaped cells. The Grad-CAM results are therefore presented as qualitative evidence of possibly attending to clinically relevant morphological patterns, not as definitive proof of interpretability.

Limitations and challenges

Due to the limited source of datasets, the current dataset employed, though well-annotated and balanced, is from one institution, and this could constrain generalizability to various populations and imaging settings. In addition, although the task at hand is binary classification, real-world applications tend to require multi-class classification of other red blood cell abnormalities. Working on class imbalances is required addition efforts and timing. Another important limitation is the difficulty in acquiring large-scale clinical datasets with reliable annotations.

Conclusion and future works

The work has proposed an Intuitionistic Fuzzy driven Retinex (IFDR), which is a mathematical model for microscopic image enhancement on SCD. It can adjust the contrast, sharpness, light, and illumination for each image locally. The model outputs clear cell boundaries and distinct shapes, enabling effective differentiation between normal and sickle cells. Followed by this, a DenseNet121-based model is utilized for sickle cell disease classification, effectively learning the differences between normal and sickle cells using the enhanced images. These enhanced images lead the way for deep learning models to learn well with the sickle cell features map and produce increased classification accuracy for sickle cell identification compared to the image without enhancement, as well as conventional methods like Intuitionistic Fuzzy and SSR. The proposed results were compared with various pre-trained models and state-of-the-art methods, demonstrating that the current approach outperforms existing techniques across most of the evaluation metrics. The future work focuses on the abnormal RBC cell automatic counting and segmentation, which will give a quantitative measure. Additionally, the ratio of abnormal to normal cells provides an objective marker of disease severity, symptoms, survival rate, and progression. We plan to collaborate with hematopathologists in future studies to obtain expert annotations and conduct qualitative as well as quantitative validation of the Grad-CAM attention regions.
