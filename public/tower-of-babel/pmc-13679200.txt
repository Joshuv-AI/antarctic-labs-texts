Comparative chloroplast genomics of Ajania sericea and sympatric Asteraceae species identifies shared candidate molecular features associated with alpine adaptation
Authors: Hongting Yang, Siyu Wu, Qian He, Haoxi Xin, Wenmiao Li, Xi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAjania sericea is endemic to the Dali region of Yunnan and is currently the southernmost known member of the genus Ajania worldwide. It co-occurs with Aster brachytrichus, Anaphalis pannosa, and Leontopodium dedekensii in alpine habitats of northwestern Yunnan, where plants are exposed to strong ultraviolet radiation, low temperatures, strong winds, and drought. To explore whether sympatric Asteraceae species from different lineages share chloroplast genomic features associated with alpine response, we sequenced, assembled, and compared the chloroplast genomes of these four species.ResultsAll four chloroplast genomes exhibited the typical quadripartite structure, with the highest GC content in the IR regions (42.99%-43.07%). A total of 142–145 gene copies were annotated. Ana. pannosa and L. dedekensii lacked ycf15, and Ana. pannosa further lacked trnT-GGU. Codon usage bias was generally weak, with preferred codons mainly ending in A/T, while neutrality and ENC-plot analyses suggested that factors other than mutational pressure, potentially including natural selection, may also contribute. Five hypervariable regions (petN, trnY-GUA, pafI-trnS-GGA, ndhC-trnV-UAC, and rpl32) were identified as potential candidate DNA barcode regions for Asteraceae. Phylogenetic analyses based on maximum likelihood and Bayesian inference yielded consistent topologies, and Aj. sericea was most closely related to Aj. nematoloba. Ka/Ks analysis indicated that most chloroplast protein-coding genes were under purifying selection, whereas only a few genes, including rpl14, rpl33, and rps2, showed relatively elevated Ka/Ks values or candidate selection signals. Integrated analyses of sequence variation, selection signals, and functional annotation suggested that rpl14, rpl33, petN, ndhC, and ndhF were mainly associated with chloroplast translation, photosynthetic electron transport, and redox regulation. In addition, the chloroplast genomes of three Aj. sericea samples collected at different elevations were highly similar, with no clear elevation-associated plastome differentiation detected.ConclusionsThis study showed that the chloroplast genomes of four sympatric Asteraceae species were highly conserved in overall structure, but still exhibited certain differences in gene loss, IR boundary variation, repeat sequence composition, and local hypervariable regions. These features provide useful references for interspecific differentiation studies and the development of potential molecular markers in Asteraceae. No clear elevation-associated plastome differentiation was detected among the three Aj. sericea samples within the elevational range examined in this study. Overall, the candidate genes identified in this study, including rpl14, rpl33, petN, ndhC, and ndhF, suggest potentially shared molecular response features among sympatric Asteraceae species in alpine environments and provide new chloroplast genomic evidence for understanding their environmental responses and evolutionary divergence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13312-7.Keywords: Ajania sericea, Chloroplast genome, Codon usage bias, Phylogenetic relationships, Molecular evolution

Background

Northwestern Yunnan, located in the Hengduan Mountains region, is characterized by high elevations and complex topography. The alpine and subalpine habitats in this region have long been subjected to multiple environmental stresses, including intense ultraviolet radiation, low temperatures, strong winds, and seasonal drought, all of which impose strong selective pressures on plant survival and reproduction [1]. Dali Yangyushan represents a typical example of such an environment. Ajania sericea, a species endemic to Dali, Yunnan, is currently the southernmost known member of the genus Ajania. It occurs mainly on rocky slopes and forest margins at elevations of 2,600–3,100 m on and around Yangyushan. Owing to its narrow distribution range and limited population size, it has been assessed as Vulnerable (VU) [2]. The species is characterized by a low-growing habit, prostrate and branched stems, tomentose leaves, and numerous small capitula, all of which are typical alpine morphological traits [3]. Other sympatric Asteraceae species, such as Aster brachytrichus, Anaphalis pannosa, and Leontopodium dedekensii, although belonging to different genera and being phylogenetically distant from A. sericea, also exhibit strikingly similar morphological features, including dwarf stature, cushion-forming growth, and densely tomentose leaves. Whether these distantly related Asteraceae species, which have long coexisted in the same alpine habitat, also share molecular characteristics associated with environmental stress remains an important question for understanding the adaptive mechanisms of alpine plants.

The chloroplast genome provides an appropriate entry point for addressing this question. Originating from an endosymbiotic event, it typically encodes 110–130 genes, most of which are involved in photosynthesis and basic metabolic processes. Owing to its high copy number, maternal inheritance, and lack of recombination, the chloroplast genome has been widely used in phylogenetic reconstruction and species identification [4, 5]. Although its overall structure is relatively conserved, variation in IR expansion and contraction, gene or intron loss, codon usage bias, and repetitive sequence composition provides abundant evolutionary information [6–8]. Certain regions, owing to their relatively rapid evolutionary rates, may serve as candidate DNA barcodes for the identification of closely related taxa [9]. At the protein-coding gene level, genes associated with photosystem electron transport and transcriptional regulation in alpine Asteraceae, such as ndh, ycf1, ndhF, and rpoC2, often exhibit signals of positive selection or elevated evolutionary rates, and are thought to be closely associated with adaptation to alpine stresses such as high light intensity and low CO₂ availability [10–12]. Taken together, the chloroplast genome combines structural conservation with functional variation and can provide evidence for the molecular responses of alpine plants to environmental stress from multiple perspectives, including structural characteristics, sequence variation, and selective pressure.

Although numerous studies have examined chloroplast genome variation within individual genera or among closely related species, it remains unclear whether distantly related Asteraceae species coexisting in the same alpine habitat share adaptive molecular features at the chloroplast genome level. In this study, we investigated Aj. sericea and three sympatric Asteraceae species, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, and L. dedekensii, through chloroplast genome sequencing, assembly, and comparative analyses. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) characterize the structural features and variation patterns of their chloroplast genomes; (2) compare codon usage bias, repeat sequence composition, and variation in IR/SC boundaries; (3) identify highly variable regions and evaluate their potential as candidate molecular markers; (4) reconstruct the phylogenetic relationships among related Asteraceae taxa; and (5) determine whether these sympatric species share candidate chloroplast molecular features associated with adaptation or response to alpine environments. Our findings are expected to provide new chloroplast genomic evidence for understanding common molecular responses and evolutionary divergence in alpine Asteraceae, and to contribute to the assessment and conservation of genetic resources in narrowly distributed vulnerable species such as Aj. sericea.

Results

Basic characteristics of chloroplast genomes

In this study, the chloroplast genomes of Aj. sericea from three elevational groups, namely low (L), medium (M), and high (H), were assembled and compared. The results showed that the three samples were highly similar in total genome length, lengths of the LSC, SSC, and IR regions, base composition, and overall genome structure. Their total genome sizes were 151,054 bp, 151,052 bp, and 151,066 bp, respectively, with a maximum difference of only 14 bp. In addition, all three samples had the same overall GC content (37.47%), and no obvious structural variation was detected (Tables S1 and S2). Therefore, unless otherwise noted, the low-elevation sample (L) was used as the representative sample of A. sericea in subsequent analyses. The chloroplast genomes of A. sericea, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, and L. dedekensii were 151,054 bp, 152,697 bp, 152,730 bp, and 151,105 bp in length, respectively. All four species exhibited the typical quadripartite structure of angiosperm chloroplast genomes, consisting of a pair of inverted repeat regions (IRa and IRb), one large single-copy region (LSC), and one small single-copy region (SSC) (Fig. 1). Base composition differed only slightly among the four species, with A. sericea having the highest GC content (37.47%) and a corresponding AT content of 62.53% (Table 1). Overall, the GC content of the IR regions (42.99%−43.07%) was markedly higher than that of the LSC (35.04%–35.57%) and SSC regions (30.83%–31.29%).

Gene annotation identified 142–145 genes, including IR-duplicated copies across the four Asteraceae species. These comprised 95–97 protein-coding genes, 8 rRNA genes, and 39–40 tRNA genes. Comparative analysis showed that both Ana. pannosa and L. dedekensii lacked the ycf15 gene, whereas Ana. pannosa additionally lacked the trnT-GGU gene. Among the protein-coding genes, 18 contained introns; clpP1 and rps12 each contained two introns, whereas the remaining genes each contained one intron (Table 2). In addition, the ncRNA composition was largely conserved among the four species. Analysis of codon usage bias and influencing factors

The ENC values for Aj. sericea L, M, and H were all 49.10, indicating that these three accessions exhibited a similar overall strength of codon usage bias in their chloroplast genomes. Similarly, the ENC values for Aj. sericea, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, and L. dedekensii were 49.10, 49.58, 49.02, and 48.95, respectively, indicating weak overall codon usage bias across the four Asteraceae species. Further comparison based on relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) revealed highly consistent codon usage patterns among the four species. A total of 29 high-frequency codons with RSCU > 1 were identified, including 16 ending with T, 12 ending with A, and 1 ending with G. Of the 30 low-frequency codons with RSCU < 1, 16 ended with C, 12 with G, and 1 with T. Methionine and tryptophan, each encoded by a single codon, showed RSCU values of 1, indicating no usage bias (Fig. 2; Table S4). These results demonstrate that high-frequency codons preferentially end in A/T, whereas low-frequency codons tend to end in C/G. Moreover, the usage frequencies of synonymous codons for the same amino acids were highly consistent across species, reflecting a highly conserved codon usage pattern (Table S4).

Analysis of Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) in Chloroplast Genomes of Aj. sericea, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, and L. dedekensii

To explore the mechanisms underlying codon usage bias, neutrality plot analysis was performed (Fig. 3a, c, e, g). GC12 and GC3 values ranged from 0.324 to 0.583 and from 0.144 to 0.348, respectively. All 54 genes lay off the diagonal line, with regression coefficients ranging from −0.005 to 0.154 and extremely low R2 values (0.001–0.012), indicating no significant linear correlation between GC12 and GC3. These results suggest that mutational pressure alone cannot fully explain the observed codon-usage patterns and that other evolutionary factors may also contribute. ENC-plot analysis (Fig. 3b, d, f, h) further showed that most genes fell below the expected curve, with concentrated GC3 values, suggesting that factors other than background mutational bias may also contribute to the observed codon-usage patterns. In addition, frequency distributions of ENC ratios showed that 42.6%, 35.2%, 37.0%, and 40.7% of genes fell within the expected ENC ratio range (−0.028 to 0.037), implying that codon usage in these genes was affected by mutational pressure. The remaining larger proportions of genes (57.4%, 64.8%, 63.0%, and 59.3%) deviated from the expected values, suggesting that additional evolutionary factors may also contribute to their codon-usage patterns (Table 3). Collectively, the neutrality and ENC-plot analyses suggest that the observed codon-usage patterns cannot be fully explained by mutational pressure alone and may be influenced by multiple evolutionary factors.

SSR and long repeat sequence analysis

Among the low (L), medium (M), and high (H) samples of Aj. sericea, the composition and distribution patterns of simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were largely consistent, and no obvious differences were observed. Long repeat sequences were also highly conserved overall, except that the H sample contained one additional palindromic repeat compared with the L and M samples (Table 4). Across Aj. sericea, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, and L. dedekensii, a total of 60 to 95 SSR loci were identified. Among these species, Ast. brachytrichus harbored the highest number of SSR loci (95), whereas L. dedekensii had the fewest (60). Most SSRs were located in the LSC region (Fig. 4e; Table S5). Notably, mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant SSR type in all four species, accounting for 52.11%, 38.95%, 46.91%, and 58.33% of the total SSRs in Aj. sericea, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, and L. dedekensii, respectively. Among them, A/T mononucleotide repeats accounted for 36.84%–58.33% of total SSRs, indicating a general preference for A/T bases in SSRs (Fig. 4a, b; Table S5). In addition, 39 to 59 long repeats were detected among the four wild Asteraceae species, with Ana. pannosa harboring the highest number (59) and Aj. sericea the lowest (39). Palindromic repeats were the predominant type, followed by forward repeats, whereas complementary repeats were not detected in Aj. sericea or Ana. pannosa (Fig. 4c). Most long repeats ranged from 30 to 40 bp in length (Fig. 4d).

Nucleotide diversity (Pi) analysis

Because the chloroplast genome sequences of the low (L), medium (M), and high (H) samples of Aj. sericea were highly similar, with no variable sites detected among them, only Aj. sericea_L was retained as the representative sequence for nucleotide diversity analysis and subsequently compared with Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, and L. dedekensii. A total of 754 polymorphic sites were identified across the chloroplast genomes of the four species by sliding-window analysis. The Pi values ranged from 0 to 0.1633644, with an average of 0.0163583. Five highly variable regions (Pi > 0.06) were identified, including three intergenic regions, trnY-GUA, pafI-trnS-GGA, and ndhC-trnV-UAC, and two coding regions, petN and rpl32. Among these, trnY-GUA exhibited the highest Pi value (0.1485421), whereas pafI-trnS-GGA showed the lowest Pi value among the highly variable regions (0.0601762) (Fig. 5; Table S6).

Sequence diversity analysis

To assess genomic divergence among Aj. sericea, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa and L. dedekensii, six complete chloroplast genome sequences of Ajania were downloaded from NCBI as reference genomes. Using the mVISTA software for chloroplast genome alignment, comparative sequence analyses were conducted on 12 Asteraceae chloroplast genomes, including Aj. sericea from three altitudinal regions. The results showed that the Aj. sericea samples from the three altitudinal regions were highly consistent with the other six Ajania species in genome structure and gene order, indicating high conservation of chloroplast genomes within the genus. In contrast, Aj. sericea showed considerable sequence divergence from Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa and L. dedekensii, with the most significant variation detected in non-coding regions (CNS). However, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa and L. dedekensii still maintained a certain level of sequence conservation among themselves, further indicating that closely related species possess relatively stable and conserved genome structures (Fig. 6).

IR region boundary analysis

A comparative analysis of the inverted repeat/single-copy (IR/SC) boundaries and their adjacent genes was conducted for the chloroplast genomes of 12 Asteraceae species, including the low (L), medium (M), and high (H) samples of Aj. sericea (Fig. 7). All species exhibited the typical quadripartite structure, consisting of one large single-copy (LSC) region, one small single-copy (SSC) region, and two inverted repeat regions (IRa and IRb), with IR lengths ranging from 24,841 to 25,051 bp. The SSC/IRb junction (JSB) was located between ndhF and trnN in the three Aj. sericea samples, as well as in Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, L. dedekensii, and Aj. pacifica. In contrast, in Aj. khartensis, Aj. nematoloba, Aj. przewalskii, Aj. ramosa, and Aj. tenuifolia, the JSB was located between ndhF and ycf1, indicating a certain degree of IR boundary contraction and expansion among species. In Aj. sericea, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, L. dedekensii, and Aj. pacifica, the distance from trnN to the SSC/IRa junction (JSA) ranged from 883 to 915 bp, whereas the distance from ndhF to the SSC/IRb junction (JSB) ranged from 19 to 78 bp. The ycf1 gene spanned the JSB in Aj. khartensis, Aj. nematoloba, Aj. przewalskii, Aj. ramosa, and Aj. tenuifolia, with most of the gene located in the IRa region. By contrast, in Aj. sericea, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, L. dedekensii, and Aj. pacifica, ycf1 spanned the JSA, with most of the gene located in the SSC region. The lengths of the ycf1 fragments in the SSC and IRa regions ranged from 3,720 to 4,560 bp and from 556 to 588 bp, respectively.

The three Aj. sericea samples (L, M, and H) showed highly consistent IR/SC boundary positions and adjacent gene arrangements. Specifically, the LSC/IRb junction (JLB) was located within rps19, the LSC/IRa junction (JLA) was located between trnH and rpl2, and the SSC/IRb junction (JSB) was located between ndhF and trnN, with only slight differences in boundary distances. For example, the distance from rpl2 to JLB/JLA was 114 bp in the L sample and 115 bp in both the M and H samples. These results indicate that the IR/SC boundary structure is highly conserved among Aj. sericea samples from different elevations. Although the boundary positions varied slightly among the Asteraceae species examined, the composition and order of adjacent genes were generally conserved.

Phylogenetic analysis

Based on the coding sequences (CDS) of chloroplast genomes from 40 Asteraceae species, phylogenetic trees were constructed using Bayesian inference (BI) and maximum likelihood (ML) methods, with Gamocarpha alpina and Gamocarpha macrocephala from Calyceraceae as outgroups. Both methods produced highly consistent topologies, and most nodes were strongly supported (Fig. 8). The phylogenetic trees showed that all Asteraceae species formed a major clade clearly separated from the outgroups. The target species, Aj. sericea, clustered with Ajania nematoloba, Ajania tenuifolia, Chrysanthemum przewalskii, and Ajania khartensis, with Aj. sericea showing the closest relationship to Aj. nematoloba (100% support). Notably, Ajania pacifica clustered with Chrysanthemum species in both analyses, suggesting a closer relationship with Chrysanthemum at the chloroplast genome level.

ML and BI trees of 40 Asteraceae species were constructed based on CDS sequences of chloroplast genomes

The phylogenetic trees further revealed three major clades within Asteraceae. The first clade consisted of 15 species from the Filagininae and Helichrysinae groups. The second clade comprised three Aster species from the Asterinae group, forming an independent monophyletic lineage. The third clade contained 21 species from the Artemisinae, Anthemidinae, Matricarinae, and Glebionidinae groups. Within this clade, Leontopodium dedekensii and Leontopodium souliei clustered together with 100% support, while Anaphalis pannosa and Anaphalis cinerascens also formed a clade with full support. In the third major clade, all Chrysanthemum and Ajania species were grouped, indicating that these two genera are most closely related within the Anthemideae.

Selection pressure analysis

Based on the phylogenetic relationships and the designated foreground branches, Ka/Ks ratios were calculated for 69 chloroplast protein-coding genes among Aj. sericea_L, Aj. sericea_M, Aj. sericea_H, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, and L. dedekensii. Owing to the limited sequence divergence and the insufficient number of mutation sites among Aj. sericea_L, Aj. sericea_M, and Aj. sericea_H, only the Ka/Ks heatmap for Aj. sericea_L versus the other three species is shown here. The selective pressure analysis based on phylogenetic partitioning showed that both target lineages had relatively high ω values, with ω values of 2.24430 and 2.24432 for lineage 1 and lineage 2, respectively (Fig. 9).

Phylogenetic tree and foreground branch settings for Aj. sericea, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, L. dedekensii, and related taxa

Pairwise comparisons of the 69 chloroplast protein-coding genes showed that the ω values of most genes were less than 1, with 66 genes exhibiting signals of purifying selection (Fig. 10). Among the few genes with relatively high ω values, the ribosomal protein genes rpl14, rpl33, and rps2 showed Ka/Ks values greater than 1 in comparisons between Ast. brachytrichus and Ana. pannosa, between Ast. brachytrichus and L. dedekensii, and between L. dedekensii and Ana. pannosa. Overall, the Ka/Ks values of chloroplast protein-coding genes in these four wild Asteraceae species were predominantly below 1, with only a few genes showing elevated Ka/Ks values.

Functional annotation of candidate loci and their potential associations with alpine adaptation

Functional annotation of the 69 shared chloroplast protein-coding genes revealed that these genes were mainly assigned to the GO Level 2 category of cellular process under biological process and to the categories of cellular anatomical entity and intracellular under cellular component (Fig. 11a). KEGG classification showed that these genes were primarily enriched in metabolic pathways, photosynthesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and ribosome-related pathways (Fig. 11b). Based on hypervariable region screening, Ka/Ks analysis, and structural variation comparison, several candidate loci were preliminarily identified in this study (Table 5). Their functional attributes were then classified by integrating annotations from the GO, KEGG, KOG, Swiss-Prot, and NR databases to evaluate whether shared functional patterns existed among the studied species. The candidate loci included rpl14, rpl33, and rps2, which showed relatively high Ka/Ks ratios; petN, rpl32, and ndhC, which were located in hypervariable regions; ycf15 and trnT-GGU, which were absent from Ana. pannosa or L. dedekensii; and ycf1 and ndhF, which were associated with IR boundary shifts. According to the integrated multi-database annotations, these candidate loci were grouped into four functional modules (Fig. 12). Specifically, rpl14, rpl33, rps2, and rpl32 were assigned to the chloroplast ribosome and translation module; petN was assigned to the photosynthetic electron transport module; ndhC and ndhF were assigned to the NDH complex and redox regulation module, whereas ycf1, ycf15, and trnT-GGU were classified as structural or functionally uncharacterized loci.

Candidate loci and their functional modules

Functional grouping of candidate loci and their inferred links to alpine selective pressures

Overall, although the candidate loci varied somewhat across species, their annotated functions were mainly concentrated in chloroplast translation, photosynthesis, and redox regulation, indicating a degree of functional clustering across species. Given the typical alpine environmental stresses, such as low temperature, high irradiance, and drought, the first three functional modules may be involved in the molecular processes underlying plant adaptation to similar environmental pressures. These results suggest that the candidate loci may participate in the responses of sympatric Asteraceae species to alpine stress. It should be emphasized that the “shared characteristics” referred to here indicate the functional clustering of candidate loci rather than identical sequence variations among different species. In addition, the functional annotation evidence matrix of the candidate loci (Fig. 13) supported the above classification. Among them, rpl14, rps2, ndhC, and ndhF received relatively consistent annotations across the GO, KEGG, KOG, Swiss-Prot, and NR databases. The annotations of rpl33 and rpl32 were also relatively reliable, whereas petN was supported mainly by KEGG, Swiss-Prot, and NR. In contrast, ycf15 was annotated primarily in Swiss-Prot and NR; ycf1 received moderate support from GO, Swiss-Prot, and NR; whereas trnT-GGU lacked corresponding annotation information.

Integrated annotation evidence for candidate loci

Discussion

Conservatism in the overall structure of chloroplast genomes among Asteraceae plants within the same region

In this study, we assembled and compared the chloroplast genomes of four sympatric Asteraceae species, Aj. sericea, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, and L. dedekensii. All four plastomes exhibited the typical quadripartite structure of angiosperms, with only limited variation in GC content (37.14%–37.47%) and gene number (142–145), consistent with previous reports for Asteraceae chloroplast genomes [13–16]. The IR regions had markedly higher GC contents than those of the LSC and SSC regions, a pattern commonly associated with the enrichment of rRNA genes and biased gene conversion in IR regions [17, 18]. The organization of genes adjacent to the IR/SC boundaries was also generally conserved across the 12 taxa included in the comparison: rps19 occurred at the LSC/IRb boundary, whereas ndhF and ycf1 were located near the SSC/IR boundaries, in agreement with patterns reported in Asteraceae genera such as Helianthus and Artemisia [19, 20]. Within the taxonomic sampling of this study, these findings indicate that the four sympatric species retain a broadly conserved plastome framework despite their phylogenetic separation, and no major genome rearrangements were detected.

Localized diversification and characteristics of hypervariable regions in the chloroplast genomes of Asteraceae plants from the same region

Against this conserved genomic background, localized interspecific differences were detected in IR boundaries, gene presence or absence, repeat composition, and nucleotide variability. In several closely related Ajania taxa, including Aj. khartensis, Aj. ramosa, and Aj. tenuifolia, the JSB was located between ndhF and ycf1, whereas in Aj. sericea, Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, and L. dedekensii it was located between ndhF and trnN. These differences are consistent with modest lineage-specific expansion or contraction of the IR regions, a common source of plastome-length and boundary variation in angiosperms [21]. In addition, ycf15 was absent from Ana. pannosa and L. dedekensii, and trnT-GGU was absent from Ana. pannosa. Because the functional status and evolutionary significance of ycf15 remain uncertain, its absence should not be interpreted directly as evidence of environmental adaptation [22]. The loss of trnT-GGU may be functionally compensated through wobble pairing or import of nucleus-encoded tRNAs into the chloroplast [23, 24]; consequently, these gene losses may reflect neutral or nearly neutral evolutionary changes rather than adaptive differentiation [25]. Five hypervariable regions (Pi > 0.06)—petN, trnY-GUA, pafI-trnS-GGA, ndhC-trnV-UAC, and rpl32—were also identified and may provide candidate regions for future phylogenetic analysis and DNA barcoding [26–32]. Repeat-sequence analysis revealed additional interspecific differences: Ast. brachytrichus contained the greatest number of SSR loci (95), L. dedekensii the fewest (60), Ana. pannosa the greatest number of long repeats (59), and Aj. sericea the fewest (39). These differences may reflect lineage-specific evolutionary history and local sequence composition [33, 34]. Although accelerated evolution or selection signals in chloroplast genes have been reported in some alpine or stressful environments and may occur alongside changes in nearby repetitive sequences [35], the localized differences observed here did not alter the overall plastome architecture. Their principal value in the present study is therefore as evidence of interspecific differentiation and as a basis for future marker development.

Chloroplast genome variation among Ajania sericea samples collected at three elevations

The chloroplast genomes of the Aj. sericea samples collected at low (2608.5 m), middle (2883.2 m), and high (3054.3 m) elevations were highly similar in total length, base composition, IR/SC-boundary positions, and long-repeat composition, with a maximum length difference of only 14 bp. Thus, no clear elevation-associated plastome differentiation was detected among the three samples within the approximately 2600–3050 m range examined. This high similarity is consistent with the maternal inheritance, limited recombination, and relatively slow evolutionary rate of chloroplast genomes [36]. However, the elevational comparison was restricted to a single species and included only one sampled individual at each elevation. The absence of clear differentiation in these three plastomes therefore cannot be generalized to population-level altitudinal differentiation in Aj. sericea or to other alpine Asteraceae. Moreover, chloroplast structural variation alone has limited resolution for detecting localized population-level shifts. Broader population sampling and high-resolution nuclear markers will be required to evaluate fine-scale elevational differentiation more reliably.

Shared characteristics of codon usage preferences in sympatric Asteraceae species

The ENC values of chloroplast protein-coding genes in the four Asteraceae species ranged from 48.95 to 49.58, indicating weak overall codon-usage bias. Nevertheless, all four species showed a clear preference for A/T-ending codons, broadly consistent with previous studies of Asteraceae chloroplast genomes [37–41]. Neutrality and ENC-plot analyses suggested that factors other than mutational pressure, including natural selection, may contribute to the observed codon-usage patterns. Similar patterns have been reported in several alpine or high-altitude plants, including Meconopsis, Soroseris hookeriana, and Rhodiola [42, 43], whereas mutational pressure appears to make a stronger contribution in some Phyllostachys species and Citrus reticulata [44, 45]. Because the four Asteraceae species examined here occur in alpine environments characterized by strong ultraviolet radiation and low temperatures, chloroplast genes associated with photosynthesis and protein synthesis may experience partly similar constraints on translational efficiency [46]. However, codon usage is influenced by multiple interacting factors, including nucleotide composition, gene-expression level, and tRNA abundance, and the descriptive analyses used here cannot distinguish their individual contributions. Without transcriptomic profiles and information on tRNA copy number or abundance, the contribution of natural selection remains speculative and should be regarded as an indirect interpretation rather than direct evidence of a shared adaptive response [47].

Candidate molecular signals under phylogenetic constraints

Phylogenetic analysis showed that Ajania and Chrysanthemum clustered together, consistent with the findings of Liu et al. based on single-copy nuclear genes and chloroplast DNA sequences [48], whereas Aster, Leontopodium, and Anaphalis each formed independent lineages. This pattern indicates that the plastome relationships recovered in the present study primarily reflect phylogenetic history rather than sympatric distribution. Selection-pressure analyses further showed that the great majority of chloroplast protein-coding genes had pairwise Ka/Ks ratios below 1, whereas only a small number exhibited relatively elevated Ka/Ks values or lineage-level differences in ω. This pattern is broadly consistent with previous studies in Asteraceae and indicates that purifying selection predominates across the plastome [49, 50]. The elevated values and lineage-level differences observed here should nevertheless be interpreted as candidate selection signals rather than definitive evidence of positive selection. Functional annotation placed several candidate loci in chloroplast translation, photosynthetic electron transport, and redox regulation. The ribosomal genes rpl14 and rpl33 participate in chloroplast ribosome assembly and translation; previous work has reported strong-light responses of rpl14, rps2, and rpl32 in high-altitude Arabidopsis [51] and impaired low-temperature recovery following loss of rpl33 [52]. petN encodes an essential small subunit of the cytochrome b6f complex [53], whereas ndhC and ndhF encode components of the NDH complex involved in electron transport and redox regulation under changing environmental conditions [54–56]. These external studies provide relevant functional context, but they do not demonstrate that the same loci mediate alpine adaptation in the species examined here. No direct physiological assays, expression analyses, or functional experiments were conducted in the study species. Accordingly, rpl14, rpl33, petN, ndhC, and ndhF should be regarded strictly as exploratory candidate loci, and their possible roles in alpine stress tolerance require validation using nuclear genomic, transcriptomic, physiological, and functional evidence.

Study limitations

Several methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, elevational variation was evaluated only in Aj. sericea across a relatively narrow range of approximately 2600–3050 m, with one sampled individual at each elevation; this design does not support population-level inference or broad generalization about altitudinal differentiation. Second, chloroplast genomes are relatively conserved, usually maternally inherited, and provide only a single genomic compartment. Chloroplast structural variation alone therefore has limited power to detect localized population-level changes, and broader population sampling with high-resolution nuclear markers is needed. Third, attributing codon-usage patterns to natural selection remains speculative without transcriptomic profiles and information on tRNA copy number or abundance. Fourth, the focal comparative dataset included only four sympatric species, which limits its taxonomic coverage and inferential scope, as well as the generalizability of conclusions regarding regional Asteraceae evolution and overall plastome conservation. Finally, no direct physiological assays, expression analyses, or functional experiments were performed in the study species. The loci identified here should therefore be treated as exploratory candidates rather than experimentally validated genes for alpine adaptation. These limitations require that the genomic patterns reported in this study be interpreted cautiously and primarily as hypotheses for future testing.

Conclusions

This study compared the chloroplast genomes of Aj. sericea and three sympatric Asteraceae species and found a broadly conserved plastome framework in terms of genome organization, GC content, gene composition, and IR/SC-boundary structure. Localized differences included the absence of ycf15 and trnT-GGU, modest shifts in IR boundaries, variation in repeat numbers, and hypervariable regions such as petN, trnY-GUA, and pafI-trnS-GGA. These features provide useful information for interspecific comparison and future molecular-marker development. The three Aj. sericea samples showed high plastome similarity within the sampled elevational range, but this observation should not be generalized to population-level altitudinal differentiation. Codon-usage bias was weak and favored A/T-ending codons; the observed patterns may reflect the combined effects of mutational pressure and other evolutionary factors, including selection. Most chloroplast protein-coding genes were under purifying selection, whereas only a few showed relatively elevated Ka/Ks values or lineage-level variation in ω. The functional clustering of rpl14, rpl33, petN, ndhC, and ndhF highlights chloroplast processes that warrant further investigation in relation to alpine stress, but does not demonstrate shared adaptive responses. These loci remain exploratory candidates, and their ecological and adaptive significance requires further validation through nuclear genomic, transcriptomic, physiological, and functional studies.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and processing

A total of six samples representing four wild Asteraceae species were sequenced in this study, including three Ajania sericea samples collected at low (2608.5 m), middle (2883.2 m), and high (3054.3 m) elevations, designated L, M, and H, respectively, and one sample each of Aster brachytrichus, Anaphalis pannosa, and Leontopodium dedekensii. All samples were collected from Yangyu Mountain, Dali, Yunnan Province, China (100°15′ E, 26°09′ N; 2608–3054 m) on October 23, 2024, during the vigorous vegetative growth stage. All plant materials were identified by Associate Professor Xi Chen. Voucher specimens were deposited in the Life Science Museum of Dali University, with the following unique voucher numbers: Y-SE-001 for A. sericea, Y-BT-002 for A. brachytrichus, Y-PA-003 for A. pannosa, and Y-DD-004 for L. dedekensii. Fresh young leaves were collected from healthy plants without visible pest or disease symptoms, rapidly frozen on dry ice, and stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction.

For comparative genomic and phylogenetic analyses, 36 complete chloroplast genome sequences of related Asteraceae taxa were downloaded from NCBI GenBank. Their taxon names and accession numbers are provided in Supplementary Table S8. Six phylogenetically closely related taxa were selected for subsequent comparative analyses: Ajania pacifica (NC_050690.1), Ajania khartensis (NC_079654.1), Ajania nematoloba (NC_079655.1), Chrysanthemum przewalskii (NC_079656.1), Ajania ramosa (OP723184.1), and Ajania tenuifolia (NC_079657.1).

DNA extraction and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted separately from the six samples using a modified CTAB method [57]. 0.15–0.30 g of young leaf tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen and incubated in 700 μL of preheated CTAB extraction buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol at 65 °C for 1 h. The lysate was extracted with 500 μL chloroform–isoamyl alcohol and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. Approximately 300 μL of the aqueous phase was mixed with 600 μL isopropanol and precipitated at − 20 °C for 2 h. The DNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and dissolved in 50 μL TE buffer. DNA concentration, purity, and integrity were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Approximately 1 μg of qualified DNA per sample was used to construct libraries with the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA Sample Prep Kit. DNA was fragmented to 300–500 bp using a Covaris M220 system, followed by end repair, 3′ A-tailing, indexed-adapter ligation, eight PCR cycles, size selection by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, and quantification using a TBS380 PicoGreen system. The final insert size was approximately 450 bp.

Library construction and sequencing were performed by Shanghai Biozeron Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (2nd Floor, Building 7, No. 1180 Xingxian Road, Jiading Industrial Zone, Shanghai, China; http://www.biozeron.com/). Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 2 × 150-bp paired-end reads. This strategy provides paired-read information suitable for de novo plastome assembly while maintaining high sequencing accuracy and throughput.

Representative chloroplast genome sequences of the four species generated in this study have been deposited in GenBank at NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) under the following accession numbers: Ajania sericea, PX316520.1; Aster brachytrichus, PX316521.1; Anaphalis pannosa, PX316522.1; and Leontopodium dedekensii, PX316523.1.

High-quality sequence acquisition

Raw reads were filtered using Trimmomatic v0.39 [58] (https://github.com/usadellab/Trimmomatic). Adapter sequences and non-ACGT bases at the 5′ ends were removed, terminal bases below Q20 were trimmed, reads containing ≥ 10% ambiguous bases were discarded, and reads shorter than 75 bp after trimming were removed. After quality control, 5.28–6.73 Gb of clean data were obtained per sample, with Q20 values of 98.22–98.41%, Q30 values of 95.49–95.96%, and GC contents of 37.04–37.91%. Detailed sequencing-quality statistics are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Chloroplast genome assembly, annotation, and analysis

Clean reads were assembled de novo using GetOrganelle v1.7.5 [59] (https://github.com/Kinggerm/GetOrganelle) with the parameters “-F embplant_pt -R 15 -k 21,45,65,85,105”; all other parameters were left at their default settings. Plastid-derived reads were iteratively recruited and assembled within the GetOrganelle workflow. Candidate chloroplast contigs were compared with the NT database and closely related reference plastomes to confirm plastome identity, contig order, sequence orientation, starting position, and the boundaries of the LSC, SSC, and IR regions. Assembly completeness and circularization were assessed using assembly statistics, plastome structure, and mapped-read coverage. Each sample yielded a complete chloroplast genome sequence without ambiguous bases and displayed the expected quadripartite structure comprising the LSC, SSC, IRa, and IRb regions. Mapped reads continuously spanned the junction between the end and beginning of each assembled sequence, with coverage comparable to the genome-wide average depth.

Genome annotation was performed using GeSeq [60] (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/geseq.html). Protein-coding genes were identified using a sequence-identity threshold of 60%, whereas rRNA, tRNA, and DNA searches used a threshold of 35%. tRNA genes were further checked using tRNAscan-SE v2.0.7 (https://trna.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/). Redundant predictions were removed, and gene positions, boundaries, start and stop codons, and intron boundaries were manually verified using BLAST results and SnapGene Viewer v7.2.1 (https://www.snapgene.com/snapgene-viewer). Physical maps of the chloroplast genomes were generated using OGDRAW [61] (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html).

Codon preference analysis

Shared protein-coding genes with coding sequences longer than 300 bp and an ATG start codon were retained for codon-usage analysis. The effective number of codons (ENC) and relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values were calculated using CodonW v1.4.2. RSCU was defined as the ratio of the observed frequency of a codon to its expected frequency under equal use of synonymous codons. Codons with RSCU > 1 were considered preferentially used, whereas those with RSCU < 1 were considered less frequently used. ENC theoretically ranges from 20 to 61, with lower values indicating stronger codon-usage bias. Codon-usage tables were generated using CUSP in EMBOSS v6.6.0.0, and the GC contents at the first, second, and third codon positions (GC1, GC2, and GC3) were calculated from the codon counts. GC12 was calculated as the mean of GC1 and GC2. Neutrality and ENC plots were used to evaluate the relative contributions of mutational pressure and other evolutionary factors to codon-usage patterns.

Repeat sequence analysis

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were identified in the assembled and corrected complete chloroplast genome sequences using MISA (MIcroSAtellite identification tool). Minimum repeat thresholds were 10 for mononucleotide repeats, 5 for dinucleotide repeats, 4 for trinucleotide repeats, and 3 for tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide repeats. Adjacent SSRs separated by less than 100 bp were defined as compound SSRs. Primers flanking the identified SSR loci were subsequently designed using Primer3 (http://www.simgene.com/Primer3) for downstream molecular marker development. Long repeat sequences in the chloroplast genomes were analyzed using REPuter (http://bibiserv.techfak.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/). The parameters were set to a minimum repeat length of 30 bp, a maximum Hamming distance of 3, and a maximum of 5000 computed repeats. Four types of repeats were recorded: forward, reverse, complementary, and palindromic repeats.

Sequence divergence analysis

Nucleotide diversity (Pi) analysis was performed on the complete chloroplast genome sequences using DnaSP v5.10 [62] to identify divergence hotspots and screen potential candidate DNA barcode regions. The chloroplast genomes were first subjected to multiple sequence alignment and then imported into DnaSP. Coding and non-coding regions were extracted separately, and sequence orientation and coordinates were standardized before Pi calculation. A sliding-window analysis was conducted to evaluate genome-wide Pi variation (window = 300 bp, step = 200 bp). Regions with Pi > 0.06 were regarded as candidate hypervariable regions. These regions were further assessed according to region type, sequence length, and alignment quality, and fragments suitable for primer design were retained for subsequent molecular marker development.

IR region boundary analysis

Large-scale syntenic relationships between the target and reference genomes were analyzed using AliTV v1.0.6 (https://alitvteam.github.io/AliTV/d3/AliTV.html). Pairwise alignments of corresponding regions were then performed using LASTZ v1.04.52 to verify local collinearity and arrangement patterns and to identify translocations (Trans), inversions (Inv), or combined translocation and inversion events (Trans + Inv). Contraction and expansion of IR boundaries were subsequently analyzed using IRscope [63].

Selection pressure analysis

Shared chloroplast protein-coding genes and their corresponding CDS and amino acid sequences were extracted using PhyloSuite v1.2.3 [64]. Codon-based alignments were generated using ParaAT v2.0, and pairwise Ka, Ks, and ω (Ka/Ks) values were calculated using KaKs_Calculator 3.0 [65]. Undefined estimates and extreme Ka/Ks values greater than 45 were excluded from subsequent comparisons.

Lineage-level variation in selective pressure was evaluated separately using branch models implemented in CodeML within PAML v4.10.7 [66]. Based on the chloroplast phylogeny, two target branch sets were defined and analyzed independently. In the first analysis, the branches leading to Ast. brachytrichus, Ana. pannosa, and L. dedekensii were jointly designated as foreground branches, whereas the remaining branches were treated as background branches. In the second analysis, the branch leading to the Aj. sericea clade comprising the L, M, and H samples was designated as the foreground branch, with the remaining branches treated as background branches. For each target branch set, a two-ratio branch model allowing the foreground and background branches to have different ω values was compared with the one-ratio M0 model, which assumed a single ω value across all branches. Likelihood-ratio tests were used to evaluate differences in model fit. The CodeML branch-model analyses were therefore conducted independently from the pairwise Ka/Ks estimates generated using KaKs_Calculator 3.0.

Phylogenetic analysis

The phylogenetic dataset comprised 36 downloaded Asteraceae chloroplast genomes, four representative plastomes generated in this study, and two outgroup species, Gamocarpha alpina and G. macrocephala. The full list of taxa and accession numbers is provided in Supplementary Table S8.

Shared orthologous CDSs were extracted using PhyloSuite and aligned using MAFFT v7.526 [67]. The aligned CDSs were concatenated into a combined matrix, and the best-fit substitution model, GTR + I + G, was selected using MrModeltest 2 [68]. A maximum-likelihood tree was constructed in MEGA11 [69] with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Bayesian inference was performed using MrBayes v3.2.7a in the PhyloSuite workflow with 2,000,000 MCMC generations. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized and edited using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

Criteria for identifying candidate loci and shared features

Predicted chloroplast proteins were searched against the NCBI NR database and UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot using BLASTp with an E-value cutoff of 1 × 10−5, retaining the best valid hit. KOG/COG categories were assigned using DIAMOND v2.1.8 searches against eggNOG v5.0.2 with the same cutoff. KEGG Orthology assignments were obtained using KofamScan v1.3.0 with KOfam profile hidden Markov models and predefined KO-specific thresholds, and GO terms were extracted from the eggNOG results. Candidate loci were screened by integrating sequence variation, pairwise Ka/Ks, structural divergence, and functional annotations. Candidate loci were defined as those meeting at least one of the following criteria: (1) protein-coding regions located within candidate hypervariable regions identified by nucleotide diversity analysis (Pi > 0.06), or functionally annotated genes directly adjacent to such regions; (2) protein-coding genes exhibiting relatively high Ka/Ks values in interspecific pairwise comparisons (e.g., Ka/Ks > 1 or values markedly higher than those of most other genes); (3) loci associated with structural variations, such as gene loss or IR/SC boundary shifts, that may affect chloroplast function; and (4) loci annotated in the GO, KEGG, KOG, Swiss-Prot, or NR databases as being involved in processes related to environmental stress responses, including chloroplast translation, photosynthetic electron transport, and redox regulation. Shared candidate features were defined as candidate loci identified in different species that, although not identical, converged on the same or closely related functional modules.
