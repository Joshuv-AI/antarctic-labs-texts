A lattice Monte Carlo model for amyloid fibril formation
Authors: Hisashi Okumura, Linh Truong Hoai, Pornthep Sompornpisut, Satoru G. Itoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proteins can aggregate to form amyloid fibrils, which are associated with a group of diseases collectively known as amyloidoses. Molecular dynamics simulations are widely used to study protein aggregation; however, it remains computationally challenging to investigate the entire aggregation process, from initial nucleation to the formation of mature amyloid fibrils, within a single simulation. In this study, we propose a Monte Carlo simulation based on a mathematical lattice model in which proteins are represented as spheres on a lattice. Motivated by the role of intramolecular β-sheet structures in accelerating aggregation, we classify protein states into three categories: monomer, intermediate possessing an intramolecular β-sheet structure, and fibril. By tuning the monomer-to-intermediate transition probability PMI and the intermediate-to-fibril transition probability PIF, both kinetics and morphology of amyloid fibril formation can be systematically controlled. When PMI is low, a lag time appears in the early stage of aggregation, whereas increasing PMI shortens the lag time. High values of both PMI and PIF lead to rapid aggregation and the formation of many short fibrils, while low values result in slower aggregation and fewer but longer fibrils. These results are consistent with experimental observations and indicate that amyloid fibril formation can be understood as a crystal growth process. This approach is expected to provide further insight into the universal mechanisms of amyloid fibril formation in homogeneous aqueous environments, as well as in heterogeneous and nonequilibrium systems.

Significance

The novelty of this study is treating amyloid fibril formation as a mathematical modeling framework, providing a complementary perspective to atomistic simulations. Theoretical studies of amyloid fibrils have mainly relied on all-atom molecular dynamics simulations. In contrast, lattice models have been successfully developed for liquid–gas transitions, polymer physics, and protein folding with the Go model, where abstraction of atomistic details enables description of essential physical mechanisms. The present study lies along this line and captures fundamental features of amyloid fibril formation with high computational efficiency. The proposed lattice model offers a versatile approach for investigating amyloid fibril formation.

Introduction

Proteins are biological molecules composed of amino acids and normally fold into specific native structures to perform their biological functions. However, when their concentration increases due to aging or other factors, proteins may aggregate to form oligomers, which are roughly globular assemblies, or amyloid fibrils, which are elongated, needle-like structures. Such protein aggregates are associated with around fifty diseases [1–3]. For example, Alzheimer’s disease is caused by aggregates of amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides and tau proteins, Huntington’s disease by aggregates of polyglutamine, and Parkinson’s disease by aggregates of α-synuclein [4].

Theoretical studies of protein aggregation have been carried out mainly using all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. These simulations have elucidated, at atomic resolution, structural transformation processes of protein aggregates under a variety of conditions, including aggregation in aqueous solution [5–14] and at interfaces [15,16], elongation of amyloid fibrils [17], equilibrium structures of elongated fibrils [18], disruption of amyloid fibrils induced by ultrasonic waves [19,20] or laser irradiation [21–23], and inhibition of amyloid fibril formation by small molecules [24–29]. These achievements have been summarized in several review articles [30–34] and perspective papers [35–37]. However, because all-atom MD simulations are computationally demanding, it is not feasible to reproduce the entire aggregation pathway—from nucleation to aggregate growth and finally to the formation of mature amyloid fibrils—within a single MD simulation. Instead, each process has been simulated separately depending on the specific situation. Therefore, there is a clear need for a simple mathematical model that can represent the entire process from nucleation to the formation of mature amyloid fibrils in a unified framework.

As such a model, we focus here on lattice models. Lattice models have long been used as theoretical frameworks that retain the essential features of molecular systems while simplifying their behavior to a minimal representation. For example, liquid–gas phase transitions are now generally studied using all-atom MD simulations [38,39]; however, they were historically described using lattice gas models and formulated through their correspondence with the Ising model [40]. Furthermore, in polymer physics, studies based on lattice polymer chain models have clarified fundamental properties such as excluded-volume effects and scaling laws [41,42]. For proteins as well, folding and unfolding transitions have been theoretically analyzed using a lattice model known as the Go model [43,44]. Amyloid fibril formation can be regarded as a phase-transition-like phenomenon from soluble monomers to insoluble ordered aggregates. In light of the historical development of lattice models—from liquid–gas phase transitions to polymers and proteins—describing amyloid formation using lattice models is expected to be an effective approach for understanding universal mechanisms that are independent of molecular-level details.

Recently, two of the authors of this paper, H.O. and S.G.I., developed a lattice Monte Carlo (MC) method to describe the elongation of Aβ amyloid fibrils and constructed a mathematical model that reproduces experimental results obtained by high-speed atomic force microscopy [45]. In that model, key features of Aβ amyloid fibrils were successfully captured: (1) Aβ peptides align in two parallel rows; (2) the fibrils elongate only in one direction; (3) when an Aβ peptide binds to the end of one row, the other row elongates before further growth of the first row, so that one row does not advance far ahead of the other; and (4) elongation can temporarily stall during growth. In that study, detailed modeling was employed to accurately reproduce the characteristic features of Aβ amyloid fibrils. On the other hand, amyloid fibril formation is observed not only for Aβ but also for more than 35 different proteins and peptides [46]. Therefore, in the present paper, we propose a simple lattice model that incorporates only more general features common to many proteins and peptides. We perform MC simulations of amyloid fibril formation based on this model. This lattice model enables efficient simulation of the amyloid fibril formation process within a short computational time.

In the model presented in this paper, we focus on the role of intramolecular β-sheet structures, such as β-hairpins, in promoting amyloid fibril formation. This feature has been observed in the aggregation of several proteins, including Aβ, tau, and polyglutamine. Accordingly, we classify aggregating proteins into three structural states: monomers without intramolecular β-sheet structure, intermediate state molecules possessing intramolecular β-sheet structures, and amyloid fibrils characterized by intermolecular β-sheet formation. We assume that monomers lacking β-sheet structure first transform into intramolecular β-sheet conformations, which subsequently serve as nuclei for amyloid fibril formation. Based on this concept, we introduce a lattice model for MC simulations of amyloid fibril formation in the next section. We then describe the details of the MC simulations using this model. Finally, we discuss how the amyloid fibril formation process changes as the parameters appearing in the model are varied.

Methods

Most of the proteins and peptides that form amyloid fibrils are intrinsically disordered proteins and do not adopt a specific structure in the monomeric state in aqueous solution. That is, in solution, monomers can take a variety of conformations, including random-coil, helical, and β-hairpin structures. Among these, intramolecular β-sheet conformations such as β-hairpins are thought to act as nuclei for amyloid fibril formation by promoting aggregation [6,7,13,14,47,48]. Intramolecular β-sheet structures enhance the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds with other proteins, leading to the formation of intermolecular β-sheet structures. As a result, protein aggregates with ordered intermolecular β-sheet structures are formed.

In the present model, we focus on the presence or absence of intramolecular and intermolecular β-sheet structures and classify the state of each protein molecule into the following three categories, as illustrated in Fig. 1:

(1) a monomer without intramolecular β-sheet structure

(2) a monomer possessing an intramolecular β-sheet structure

(3) a protein forming intermolecular β-sheet structures, i.e., an aggregated protein in an amyloid fibril.

States (1), (2), and (3) are referred to as the monomer (M), intermediate (I), and fibril (F) states, respectively. Protein molecules in the M, I, and F states are represented as white, pink, and red spheres, respectively. The diameter of each sphere is denoted by σ, and all spheres are assumed to occupy sites on a simple cubic lattice with lattice spacing σ. This assumption is a coarse-grained simplification. For example, the transition from the M state to the I state represents a conformational change within a single molecule (e.g., β-hairpin formation), and the overall molecular size is not expected to change significantly. Therefore, assuming an identical diameter is reasonable given the resolution of a coarse-grained lattice model.

Each sphere possesses not only one of the three states, M, I, or F, but also a degree of freedom corresponding to its elongation direction. Specifically, each sphere can bind to other spheres only along one of the x, y, or z axis (or equivalently, along two opposite directions for a given axis).

The MC simulation of the system is performed as follows. Each time step consists of two parts: a molecular translation and rotation part, and a state transition part. In the first part, the molecules are translated and rotated using the Metropolis algorithm [49]. Trial moves with random displacement and rotation are proposed for monomer and intermediate molecules: the positions of these molecules are randomly displaced by σ in one of the x, y, or z directions, and their elongation directions are also randomly changed. Whether these trial moves are accepted or rejected is determined by the Metropolis criterion using the potential energy difference Δu=uNEW–uOLD and the Boltzmann factor exp(–Δu/kBT), where kB is the Boltzmann constant. In contrast, the positions and the elongation directions of fibril molecules remain fixed throughout the simulation.

In the second part, monomer and intermediate molecules can change their states with certain probabilities. When a fibril molecule and a monomer molecule align their elongation directions and occupy adjacent sites along that direction, the monomer molecule transforms into a fibril molecule with probability 1, as illustrated in Fig. 3(a). Similarly, when a fibril molecule and an intermediate molecule align their elongation directions and are adjacent along that direction, the intermediate molecule transforms into a fibril molecule with probability 1. When a monomer molecule and an intermediate molecule align their elongation directions and are adjacent along that direction, as shown in Fig. 3(b), both molecules transform into fibril molecules with probability PIF. Thus, for a molecule to transition to the fibril state, at least one of the two molecules must be in the intermediate or fibril state. In other words, two molecules in the monomer state do not form the fibril state even when they are adjacent. This modeling choice corresponds to the fact that intermolecular β-sheet formation is enhanced by the presence of intramolecular β-sheet structures [6,7,13,14,47,48]. Once a molecule transforms into the fibril state, it never reverts to the monomer or intermediate state and remains in the fibril state permanently. A monomer molecule transforms into an intermediate molecule with probability PMI, while an intermediate molecule transforms into a monomer molecule with probability PIM, as illustrated in Fig. 3(c).

These processes are illustrated as a flowchart in Fig. 4. Note that the first part, highlighted in pink, is based on the Metropolis criterion and thus satisfies the detailed-balance condition. In contrast, the second part, highlighted in light blue, is not based on the Metropolis criterion. Therefore, the overall MC simulation does not satisfy the detailed-balance condition.

Simulation details

As initial conditions, N=1000 monomer molecules were randomly placed on a simple cubic lattice, with the constraint that no two molecules occupied neighboring lattice sites. Ten independent initial configurations were generated in this manner, one of which is shown in Fig. 5. MC simulations were performed starting from each of these initial configurations. To investigate how aggregation behavior depends on the effective concentration, initial configurations were prepared using simulation boxes with the side length of L=50σ and 100σ. We set the monomer-to-intermediate and intermediate-to-monomer transition probabilities to be equal (PMI=PIM) in these simulations. This choice corresponds to the assumption that the free energy barrier from the M state to the I state is equal to that from the I state to the M state. While transition rates may differ in real systems, we adopted this simplification because our aim here is to capture general features rather than to quantitatively reproduce specific systems. Simulations were performed for three values of PMI=0.0001, 0.001, and 0.01. For the intermediate-to-fibril transition probability, three values were used: PIF=0.01, 0.1, and 1.0. The temperature T was set such that β=1/kBT=ε. MC simulations were continued until more than 99% of the molecules reached the fibril state under each condition. The results were averaged over ten independent simulations for each set of parameters. Under these conditions, we investigated how the amyloid fibril formation process depends on the model parameters.

Results and discussion

The aggregation processes observed in the simulations are shown in Movies S1–S8. Amyloid fibrils are gradually formed over time in all systems. The probability of fibrous particles as a function of MC steps is shown in Fig. 6. Figure S1 shows the same data over the entire simulation time, covering different ranges of MC steps. Here, the probability is defined as the fraction of fibril molecules relative to the total number of molecules. Both the monomer-to-intermediate transition probability (PMI) and the intermediate-to-fibril transition probability (PIF) are found to strongly influence the fibril formation kinetics. At low PMI (PMI=0.0001), a pronounced lag phase is observed in the early stage of aggregation (Figs. 6a–6c and 6j–6l), indicating that fibril formation does not initiate immediately. This behavior suggests that the formation of the intermediate state, corresponding to intramolecular β-sheet (β-hairpin formation), constitutes the rate-limiting step at early times. When PMI is increased to 0.001 and 0.01, the lag phase becomes shorter and aggregation is accelerated (Figs. 6d–6i and 6m–6r), consistent with more frequent generation of fibril-competent intermediates. Increasing PIF also accelerates fibril formation. When PIF is low (PIF=0.01), the curves increase only gradually and require a longer time to reach saturation, indicating that the structural transition from the intermediate state to the fibril state limits growth. In contrast, when PIF is increased to 0.1 and 1.0, the probability of fibrous particles rapidly increases, indicating that once intermediate molecules are generated, nucleation and fibril elongation occur efficiently. Thus, the combined effects of PMI and PIF are cooperative: when both probabilities are low, aggregation is slow, whereas rapid fibril formation is observed when both PMI and PIF are high. This result indicates that both monomer-to-intermediate and intermediate-to-fibril transitions play key roles in controlling amyloid fibril formation kinetics. The simulation box size also affects the kinetics of fibril formation. In the larger box (Figs. 6j–6r), aggregation is generally slower than in the smaller box (Figs. 6a–6i) under identical (PMI, PIF) conditions. This behavior arises from the reduced effective concentration in the larger volume, which lowers the collision frequency between monomer and intermediate molecules and delays nucleation. Overall, these results indicate that amyloid fibril formation kinetics depend on the efficiency of intermediate generation, the efficiency of transition from intermediates to fibrils, and the effective protein concentration.

Next, we examined how the simulation parameters influence the morphology of the formed amyloid fibrils. Figure 7 shows the final structures obtained from the MC simulations. In the small box (L=50σ), when both PMI and PIF are low (PMI=0.0001, PIF=0.01), the number of fibrils formed is limited, whereas each amyloid fibril grows relatively long (Fig. 7a). As PIF is increased to 0.1 and 1.0, the number of amyloid fibrils increases, while the length of each fibril decreases (Figs. 7b and 7c). Similarly, increasing PMI to 0.001 and 0.01 results in the formation of a larger number of shorter amyloid fibrils (Figs. 7d–7i). When both PMI and PIF are high, structures that appear branched can be observed. However, these structures arose from independent growth of multiple short fibrils that subsequently encounter each other orthogonally during elongation. In the large box (L=100σ), similar trends are observed (Figs. 7j–7r): increasing PMI and PIF results in an increased number of fibrils and a corresponding decrease in their individual lengths.

For the lowest values of PMI and PIF (PMI=0.0001, PIF=0.01) and a system size of L=50σ, fibrils consisting of more than 20 molecules were observed, with the longest fibrils containing up to 25 molecules. These aggregates exhibit characteristic fibrillar structures within the framework of the present model. Although such fibrillar aggregates are formed, their lengths remain relatively limited compared with experimentally observed mature amyloid fibrils. The limited fibril length can be attributed to several factors. First, the system size and the total number of molecules are finite, which restricts the maximum achievable fibril length. Second, under conditions where nucleation occurs frequently, molecules are distributed among multiple growing fibrils, preventing the formation of very long fibrils. In addition, once a molecule enters the F state, it does not revert to the M or I states in this model.

Figure 8 shows the distributions of amyloid fibril lengths obtained from the simulations. These distributions were calculated as follows: when a fibril consisted of n molecules, a weight of n was added to the histogram bin at n; after summing over all fibrils, the histogram was normalized by the total number of molecules. Accordingly, the resulting distributions represent the probability that a randomly chosen molecule belongs to a fibril of a given length. These distributions reflect how the monomer-to-intermediate transition probability PMI and the subsequent intermediate-to-fibril transition probability PIF influence the final fibril morphology. In the small simulation box (L=50σ), when both PMI and PIF are low, a broad distribution ranging from short to long fibrils is obtained. As PMI and PIF increase, short fibrils become dominant and long fibrils are no longer formed. Comparison between the two box sizes shows that, under identical (PMI, PIF) conditions, the distributions for the larger box (L=100σ) are shifted toward shorter fibrils (Figs. 8j–8r). Thus, while the qualitative dependence on PMI and PIF is common to both systems, the maximum fibril length decreases in larger volumes.

These results can be understood in terms of how PMI and PIF influence the fibril formation rate. When both probabilities are high, nucleation occurs ubiquitously throughout the system, resulting in the formation of many short amyloid fibrils. In contrast, when PMI and PIF are low, fibril formation proceeds more slowly, allowing individual fibrils to elongate and thus grow to greater lengths. This trend is consistent with experimental observations of amyloid fibril formation. For example, Yagi-Utsumi et al. investigated amyloid fibril formation of the Tottori-type mutant Aβ peptide under microgravity conditions in space and under normal gravity on Earth [50]. The Tottori-type mutant Aβ aggregates more rapidly than wild-type Aβ. Under terrestrial conditions, it rapidly forms predominantly short fibrils. In contrast, under microgravity conditions, where convection is suppressed, aggregation proceeds more slowly, and long amyloid fibrils are formed. The differences in fibril morphology observed in these experiments can be successfully captured by our lattice model by representing fast and slow aggregation regimes through the parameters PMI and PIF. The shorter fibril lengths observed in the larger box can be attributed to the reduced effective concentration, which lowers the frequency of collisions between monomer or intermediate molecules and fibril ends. As a result, monomer and intermediate molecules tend to undergo fibril formation independently before encountering existing fibril ends, leading to an increased number of shorter fibrils.

Such aggregation behavior is closely analogous to crystal growth. In crystal growth, slow growth from a limited number of nuclei typically yields large single crystals, whereas stirring or rapid quenching induces widespread nucleation and leads to the formation of many small crystals. Thus, the balance between nucleation and subsequent growth determines the final size and number of crystals, indicating that the resulting structures are governed primarily by kinetic factors rather than thermodynamic equilibrium. Amyloid fibril formation has similarly been described as a nucleation–elongation process that shares common mechanistic principles with crystallization phenomena [51,52]. Similarly, in the present model, although the fibril state is thermodynamically stable, the morphology of the resulting fibrils is determined by kinetic factors. Our MC simulations demonstrate that amyloid fibril formation can be interpreted within this crystal-growth framework. In this analogy, proteins correspond to solute molecules in crystal growth, and amyloid fibrils correspond to the growing crystalline phase. From this perspective, amyloid fibrils—often regarded primarily as pathological agents in the human body—may also be viewed as legitimate subjects of solid-state physics.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a lattice model that consistently describes protein aggregation from its initial stages to the formation of mature amyloid fibrils using a Monte Carlo (MC) approach. In this model, each protein is represented as a sphere on a lattice. Each protein can exist in one of three states—the monomer, intermediate, or fibril state—and additionally possesses a degree of freedom corresponding to one of the three elongation directions along the x, y, or z axes. A monomer molecule transforms into an intermediate molecule with a certain probability PMI, while an intermediate molecule transforms back into a monomer molecule with the same probability. When a monomer and an intermediate molecule align their elongation directions and occupy adjacent lattice sites along that direction, the two molecules transition to the fibril state with probability PIF. By tuning the probabilities PMI and PIF, a wide range of amyloid fibril morphologies can be generated. The simulations presented here show that high values of PMI and PIF lead to rapid aggregation and the formation of many short amyloid fibrils, whereas low values of PMI and PIF result in slower aggregation and the formation of fewer but longer amyloid fibrils. This behavior is closely analogous to that experimentally observed in crystal growth.

A major advantage of this lattice model is its extremely low computational cost compared with all-atom molecular dynamics simulations, which enables observation of long-time behavior from nucleation to mature amyloid fibril formation. Simulating this entire process using all-atom models is computationally expensive and typically requires advanced techniques such as replica-exchange [53,54] and replica-permutation methods [55–57]; even then, simulations often require over hundreds of nanoseconds to microseconds [13]. In the present model, the intermediate state is intended to represent a monomer possessing an intramolecular β-sheet structure, and in all-atom simulations, the investigation of such intramolecular β-sheet structures alone has motivated the development of specialized enhanced-sampling techniques [58]. In contrast, the MC approach employed here does not explicitly treat detailed intramolecular structural changes. This simplification enables a unified description of amyloid fibril formation from the earliest stages to the final structures. At the same time, this approach cannot capture the detailed conformational changes of individual molecules, and the model parameters cannot be directly derived from atomistic features or used for quantitative predictions at the atomic level.

Although the present study focuses on amyloid fibril formation in bulk solution, the lattice model can be readily extended to investigate aggregation under different environmental conditions. For example, protein aggregation is known to occur preferentially at hydrophilic–hydrophobic interfaces [15,59–61], and such interfaces could be incorporated into the model. Moreover, simulations of amyloid fibril formation inhibitors have attracted increasing attention in recent years [28,29], and the present framework could be extended to study such effects. The model could also be further developed to describe nonequilibrium systems under external field [62]. Such extensions are expected to provide further insight into the universal mechanisms underlying protein aggregation and amyloid fibril formation.
