Effects of agro-ecological origin and harvest time of three medicinal plants on their anthelmintic efficacy
Authors: Alowanou G Géorcelin, Yanik Akin, Paolo D A Lessé, Kétomon P Challaton, Basile Konmy, Salako Valère, Habirou Kifouly, Carlosse Djeho, Pascal Olounladé, Sylvie Hounzangbé-Adoté
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal nematode infections, particularly those caused by Haemonchus contortus, remain a major constraint to livestock health and productivity. With the increasing prevalence of resistance to synthetic anthelmintics, the search for effective plant-based alternatives has intensified. This study presents the first systematic comparison of how agro-ecological conditions and seasonal variation influence both the phytochemical composition and the in vitro anthelmintic efficacy of three medicinal plants widely used in ethnoveterinary practice: Bridelia ferruginea, Mitragyna inermis, and Combretum glutinosum. Leaves from these plants were harvested across contrasting ecological zones of Benin during both the dry and wet seasons. Acetone–water extracts were prepared and evaluated for their ability to inhibit H. contortus larval migration. In parallel, total phenols, condensed tannins, and flavonoids were quantified using spectrophotometric assays. Phytochemical profiles varied significantly (p < 0.01) across both seasons and ecological zones, with generally higher concentrations of phenolic compounds recorded during the wet season (p < 0.05). In contrast, the strongest anthelmintic activity (≥ 25% and < 80% inhibition) was consistently obtained with dry-season extracts. Notably, extracts of C. glutinosum and B. ferruginea from zone IV achieved 65% and 75% inhibition, respectively, while M. inermis from zone II reached 60% inhibition at 600 µg/mL, with clear dose-dependent responses. Across nearly all zones, dry-season extracts outperformed their wet-season counterparts. Agro-ecological and seasonal factors affect both phytochemistry and anthelmintic activity; dry-season harvests in Sudano-Guinean and Sudanian zones maximize efficacy, supporting their use in sustainable parasite control for small ruminants in tropical regions.Keywords: Bridelia ferruginea, Combretum glutinosum, Mitragyna inermis, Haemonchus contortus, Seasonal variationSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques

Introduction

Helminth infections, particularly those caused by gastrointestinal nematodes such as Haemonchus contortus, pose a major threat to livestock health and productivity in tropical regions1. These parasites are responsible for substantial economic losses through reduced weight gain, lowered milk yield, and increased morbidity and mortality. Although synthetic anthelmintics remain the mainstay of parasite control, their use is increasingly challenged by high treatment costs and the rapid emergence of anthelmintic resistance (AR). Chartier et al.2 highlighted that AR is now a global concern. In Africa, AR in H. contortus has become a growing issue. Ndiaye et al.3 first reported benzimidazole resistance in gastrointestinal nematodes of small ruminants in the Gambia and Senegal. More recently, Karshima et al.4 conducted a systematic review confirming the widespread occurrence of AR across Africa, including West Africa, with rising prevalence in sheep and goats. Molecular investigations by Mohammedsalih et al.5 further identified novel β-tubulin mutations at codon 198 associated with high levels of benzimidazole resistance, demonstrating the genetic basis of treatment failure. Together, these findings underscore the urgent need for alternative, sustainable parasite-control strategies.

Against this background, medicinal plants have attracted growing interest due to their potential anthelmintic properties. Ethnobotanical surveys conducted by Koné and Atindehou6 and by Kaboré et al.7 have documented the traditional use of Mitragyna inermis (Willd.), Combretum glutinosum (Perr.), and Bridelia ferruginea (Benth.) across tropical Africa to treat helminth infections in both humans and animals. Additional studies confirmed that different parts of B. ferruginea6,8, C. glutinosum7,8, and M. inermis6,9 are employed in managing gastrointestinal disorders and parasitic infections in West and Central Africa. These plants are rich in secondary metabolites, particularly tannins, phenols, and flavonoids, which are thought to underlie their antiparasitic effects10. Experimental studies have shown significant antiparasitic activity: Djoueché et al.8 demonstrated that B. ferruginea extracts inhibited H. contortus larval development, while Lasisi and Kareem11 reported its activity against Fasciola gigantica, Taenia solium, and Pheretima posthuma. Diehl et al.12 confirmed the larvicidal activity of M. inermis extracts against H. contortus, and later in vitro studies by Alowanou et al.10,13 demonstrated the efficacy of M. inermis, C. glutinosum, and B. ferruginea extracts against egg hatching, larval migration, and adult motility. In vivo investigations by Alowanou et al.1 further revealed that M. inermis leaf powder disrupts multiple developmental stages of H. contortus, including egg hatching, larval migration, and adult survival.

Despite this promising evidence, the phytochemical composition of medicinal plants and thus their biological efficacy varies considerably with environmental factors such as agro-ecological zone, soil type, and season. Makkar et al.14 emphasized that variations in rainfall, temperature, and soil characteristics strongly influence the synthesis of bioactive compounds. Gaudin et al.15 further demonstrated that seasonality and ecology can significantly affect the anthelmintic activity of plants. Yet, there remains limited research on how these environmental variations impact the efficacy of M. inermis, C. glutinosum, and B. ferruginea.

The present study addresses this gap by evaluating the in vitro anthelmintic activity of these three plant species collected from different agro-ecological zones of Benin during both the dry and rainy seasons. Specifically, it investigates how geographic and seasonal factors influence their phytochemical composition and corresponding efficacy against H. contortus. The findings are expected to provide valuable insights for veterinary practitioners, livestock farmers, and researchers, informing best practices for the use of traditional medicinal plants in parasite control. Ultimately, this work aims to contribute to more sustainable and locally adapted strategies for improving livestock health in tropical regions.

Materials and methods

Plant material collection, identification, and voucher specimens

The plant materials Bridelia ferruginea Benth. (Euphorbiaceae), Combretum glutinosum Perr. ex DC. (Combretaceae), and Mitragyna inermis (Willd.) Kuntze (Rubiaceae) were collected with authorization from the Directorate of Forestry and Natural Resources, Ministry of Environment and Sustainable Development of Benin (Research authorization No. N°2023/210/DEFN/MEPN). Botanical identification and authentication of the plant species were conducted at the National Herbarium of Benin, University of Abomey-Calavi, under the expertise of Professor Akpovi Akoègninou, a distinguished plant taxonomist and Director of the Herbarium. Voucher specimens were deposited in the herbarium under the accession numbers AA6527/HNB (B. ferruginea), AA6528/HNB (C. glutinosum), and AA6529/HNB (M. inermis).

Pretreatment of plant leaves

After collection, fresh leaves of Bridelia ferruginea, Combretum glutinosum, and Mitragyna inermis were carefully cleaned to remove dust, soil particles, and other extraneous materials. The leaves were then air-dried under shade at ambient temperature (27 °C) in a well-ventilated room to prevent direct exposure to sunlight and to minimize the degradation of thermolabile and photosensitive compounds. Drying was continued until a constant weight was achieved.

The dried leaves were subsequently ground into a fine powder using an electric grinder and sieved to obtain a homogeneous particle size. The powdered plant material was stored in airtight containers, protected from light and moisture, at room temperature until extraction.

Preparation of plant extracts

Fifty grams (50 g) of powdered leaves from Bridelia ferruginea, Combretum glutinosum, and Mitragyna inermis were each macerated in 500 mL of an acetone: distilled water mixture (70:30, v/v). Extraction was conducted under continuous magnetic stirring for 2 h at 50 °C to enhance the release of phytochemicals. The resulting mixtures were filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and the filtrates were concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator. The remaining aqueous phase was oven-dried at 40 °C for 5 to 7 days until complete solvent removal. Dried extracts were stored in airtight containers at room temperature (25–27 °C) until use in bioassays.

Larval migration Inhibition (LMI) assay

Preparation of infective larvae (L3)

Third-stage infective larvae (L3) of Haemonchus contortus were obtained by coproculture of fecal samples collected from donor sheep that had been experimentally infected with a pure H. contortus strain at the Experimental Farm of the Laboratory of Ethnopharmacology and Animal Health, Faculty of Agricultural Sciences, University of Abomey-Calavi (Benin). Coprocultures were maintained at room temperature (25–27 °C) for 10 days, after which larvae were harvested using the Baermann apparatus, exploiting larval hygrotropism to separate them from fecal debris.

The H. contortus strain used originated from the Laboratory of Animal Parasitology at the Toulouse Veterinary School, Paul Sabatier University of Toulouse III (France), where it had been maintained under controlled laboratory conditions. This reference strain was routinely used for in vitro assays and was known to be susceptible to major anthelmintic classes, such as benzimidazoles (e.g., albendazole and thiabendazole). For the present study, the strain was multiplied at the Experimental Farm of the Laboratory of Ethnopharmacology and Animal Health using mono-infected donor sheep to preserve strain purity.

Prior to experimentation, the susceptibility of the H. contortus isolate was confirmed through a series of in vitro bioassays designed to detect potential anthelmintic resistance. The isolate’s response to three major drug classes was assessed using standardized protocols. An Egg Hatch Assay (EHA) was first performed with thiabendazole as the reference benzimidazole compound following the procedures of Cole et al.17. The half-maximal effective concentration (EC₅₀) obtained (0.08 µg/mL) was within the threshold expected for susceptible isolates (< 0.1 µg/mL). Subsequently, a Larval Development Assay (LDA) using ivermectin aglycone was carried out to evaluate susceptibility to macrocyclic lactones. The concentration required to inhibit 50% of larval development (EC₅₀ = 2.1 ng/mL) corresponded to values typical of non-resistant isolates. Finally, a Larval Migration Inhibition Assay (LMIA) was conducted with levamisole as a positive control. The assay demonstrated ≥ 95% inhibition of larval migration at 250 µg/mL, confirming a high level of sensitivity to levamisole. The inhibition profiles obtained across all assays aligned with those reported for susceptible H. contortus reference strains18, thereby confirming that the isolate used in the present study did not display any phenotypic evidence of anthelmintic resistance.

Larval migration inhibition test

The larval migration inhibition (LMI) test was conducted following the method described by Rabel et al.19, with minor modifications. A standardized suspension containing approximately 1000 third-stage larvae (L3) per mL was prepared. Aliquots of the suspension were exposed for 3 h under ambient laboratory conditions (20 ± 1 °C; relative humidity 60–75%) to plant extract solutions at concentrations of 150, 300, 600, and 1200 µg/mL. Each concentration was tested in triplicate. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.15 M, pH 7) with 1% solvent served as the negative control, while levamisole (250 µg/mL) was used as the positive control. Levamisole was selected because it has been widely employed as a reference drug in previous larval migration inhibition assays and, importantly, the H. contortus strain used in this study has not shown resistance to this anthelmintic in earlier experiments1. After exposure, larvae were washed three times by centrifugation (Hettich EBA 12R Microcentrifuge; 4500 rpm, 5 min, 20 °C) and transferred onto 20 μm nylon mesh filters. Migration was then allowed to occur for 3 h at 23 ± 1 °C under the same ambient conditions. Migrated larvae were collected in a final volume of 1.5 mL, and larval counts were made in 200 µL aliquots under a stereomicroscope. Assessment of extract effectiveness

The anthelmintic activity of the plant extracts was evaluated using the Larval Migration Inhibition (LMI) assay, as previously described. The percentage of LMI reflects the ability of each extract to interfere with larval motility and migration relative to the untreated control, serving as a measure of anthelmintic potency. In the absence of universally accepted cut-off values for classifying plant extract efficacy, we adopted a pragmatic categorization to facilitate consistent interpretation: extracts showing less than 25% inhibition were considered inactive or negligible, those with 25–49% inhibition were classified as low activity, 50–74% as moderately active, 75–89% as highly active, and ≥ 90% as very highly active or potent, comparable to the effects of standard anthelmintics such as ivermectin.

These thresholds are consistent with interpretive approaches commonly reported in the literature, where some studies rely on dose–response metrics (EC₅₀/EC₉₀) or define high inhibition arbitrarily to select potent extracts18,20. Our classification provides a harmonized framework for comparing plant-derived anthelmintic activity across assays, allowing a nuanced and reproducible assessment of extract performance and facilitating the identification of promising candidates for further pharmacological development.

Quantification of plant phytochemicals

Determination of total phenolic content

Total phenolics were quantified using the Folin–Ciocalteu method described by O Singleton et al. 21. A calibration curve was prepared using a stock solution of gallic acid (10 mg/mL) diluted to obtain a range of working standards. In test tubes, 125 µL of each standard or plant extract was mixed with 625 µL of 10% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent. After 5 min, 500 µL of sodium carbonate solution (75 mg/mL) and 4.75 mL of distilled water were added. Tubes were mixed and incubated in the dark for 30 min at room temperature (25–27 °C). Absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a BioMate 3 UV-Visible spectrophotometer. Results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of dry extract.

Determination of total flavonoid content

Flavonoids were quantified following the method Zhishen et al.22 and Kim et al.23, using rutin as the reference standard. A stock solution of rutin (10 mg/mL) was used to prepare a standard curve. In test tubes, 500 µL of extract or standard was mixed with 500 µL of 2% aluminum chloride and 3 mL of methanol. After gentle shaking, samples were incubated for 10 min in the dark at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 415 nm. Results were expressed as mg rutin equivalents (RE) per gram of dry extract.

Determination of condensed tannins

Condensed tannins were measured using the vanillin-HCl method24. A 500 µL aliquot of standard or extract was mixed with 3 mL of 4% vanillin in methanol, 1.5 mL of fuming HCl, and 2 mL of methanol. After incubation for 15 min at room temperature, absorbance was read at 500 nm. Catechin was used as the standard, and results were expressed as mg catechin equivalents (CE) per gram of extract.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using R software25. Two separate analyses were conducted:

Effect of agro-ecological zones and seasons on phytochemical content

To assess the effects of agro-ecological zone (AEZ II, III, IV, VI and VIII) and season (dry versus wet) on the total phenol, tannin, and flavonoid contents of B. ferruginea, M. inermis, and C. glutinosum, a two-way ANOVA was performed for each plant species. Fixed factors included zone, season, and their interaction.

Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were checked using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Where assumptions were not met, data were either log-transformed or analyzed using Kruskal–Wallis tests, followed by Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons. Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was used for post hoc analysis of significant ANOVA results (p < 0.05). Boxplots were generated using the ggplot2 package to visually compare phytochemical concentrations across zones and seasons, and to illustrate distribution, central tendency, and potential outliers.

Effect of plant extracts from different agro-ecological zones over two seasons on larval migration Inhibition

The larval migration inhibition (%) of H. contortus was analyzed using two-way ANOVA, considering agro-ecological zone (II, III, IV, VI and VIII) and season (dry vs. wet) as fixed factors. Analyses were performed separately for each plant species (B. ferruginea, M. inermis, C. glutinosum) and each concentration level (150, 300, 600, 1200 µg/mL).

Normality and variance homogeneity were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests. If necessary, data were transformed (e.g., arcsine square root transformation). When ANOVA revealed significant effects or interactions, Tukey’s HSD test was applied for pairwise comparisons (p < 0.05). Boxplots were constructed to visualize differences in larval inhibition across treatments, highlighting the effect of zone and season on extract efficacy. These visualizations helped detect patterns and potential outliers consistent with the statistical findings.

Discussion

This study highlights the significant influence of agro-ecological zone and season on the in vitro anthelmintic activity of Mitragyna inermis, Combretum glutinosum, and Bridelia ferruginea. These environmental factors, including seasonal variation and geographic origin, appear to modulate both the phytochemical composition and the biological efficacy of the plants tested against H. contortus larvae.

Phytochemical analyses revealed substantial variations in the concentrations of total phenols, tannins, and flavonoids across seasons and agro-ecological zones. Notably, higher levels of these bioactive compounds were generally observed during the rainy season, which is consistent with previous findings by Makkar et al.14, who reported that climatic conditions significantly influence secondary metabolite profiles in medicinal plants. Similarly, Alamgir26 emphasized that the bioactive content of plants varies with environmental parameters such as season, plant developmental stage, time of harvest, and geographic location.

These phytochemical variations had direct consequences on the anthelmintic activity of plant extracts. For instance, B. ferruginea leaves collected from agro-ecological zone IV demonstrated superior inhibition of larval migration compared to samples from other zones. Likewise, M. inermis from zone II and C. glutinosum from zone IV exhibited pronounced in vitro activity against H. contortus larvae. Such differences are likely due to zone-specific climatic or edaphic factors particularly soil properties and moisture availability which influence both the quantity and quality of the secondary metabolites responsible for anthelmintic effects.

These results align with the findings of Marais et al.27 and Heckendorn et al.28, who noted that the antiparasitic efficacy of plant extracts often correlates with their phenolic content. Tiemann et al.29 further demonstrated that cultivation location directly affects polyphenol concentrations, while Cadot et al.30 observed that the quality of tannins, especially prodelphinidin (PD) content, is negatively influenced by sandy soils with low water retention capacity. This reinforces the idea that both soil type and water availability play critical roles in determining the phytochemical profiles of plants.

Interestingly, despite lower phytochemical concentrations during the dry season, some extracts displayed greater anthelmintic activity. For example, C. glutinosum and M. inermis harvested during the dry season showed enhanced larval migration inhibition. This discrepancy suggests that factors beyond the mere concentration of major bioactive compounds may contribute to efficacy. It is plausible that synergistic interactions among secondary metabolites, or the contribution of additional bioactive constituents such as non-phenolic compounds (e.g., alkaloids), which may be more active during the dry season, are involved. Similar observations were made by Athanasiadou et al.31, who reported that the antiparasitic activity of plant extracts does not always correspond directly with the levels of known active constituents.

Beyond the quantity of tannins, their structural characteristics also play a decisive role in biological activity. Condensed tannins differ mainly in their composition of PD versus procyanidin (PC) subunits. PD-rich tannins generally exert stronger activity against gastrointestinal nematodes than PC-rich tannins, due to their higher degree of hydroxylation and stronger protein-binding affinity31,32,34. In the context of our study, the seasonal patterns observed may be partly explained by shifts in CT composition. Previous studies have shown that rainy season conditions often favor the biosynthesis of PC units, which are less potent against parasites, whereas water stress during the dry season promotes the accumulation of PD units. This could explain why, despite lower total tannin concentrations, C. glutinosum and M. inermis harvested in the dry season displayed higher larval migration inhibition. Thus, seasonal modulation of PD: PC ratios may underlie the apparent paradox between phytochemical concentration and biological efficacy.

The practical implications of these findings are considerable. To optimize the use of these plants as ethnoveterinary anthelmintics, harvesting strategies must take into account the season and agro-ecological origin. Based on our results, C. glutinosum and M. inermis are most effective when collected during the dry season in agro-ecological zones IV and II, respectively, while B. ferruginea maintains stable activity across seasons. Achieving comparable efficacy during the rainy season may require higher doses; however, even natural products should be used cautiously to avoid potential toxicity. Consequently, plant extracts and other bioactive compounds are best employed as part of an integrated parasite management strategy, complementing conventional anthelmintics rather than serving as high-dose standalone treatments32,33.

These findings are consistent with those of Hoste et al.34, who demonstrated that the anthelmintic efficacy of tannin-rich plants depends not only on their growing conditions but also on the structural characteristics of condensed tannins. Similarly, Gaudin et al.15 observed considerable variation in the anthelmintic potency of plant extracts collected from different regions of Burkina Faso, underscoring the importance of both environmental influences and tannin composition.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the anthelmintic properties of B. ferruginea, M. inermis, and C. glutinosum vary significantly according to agro-ecological zone and season, and that these variations are likely driven not only by the total content of tannins and flavonoids but also by the PD: PC composition of condensed tannins. These insights emphasize the need for targeted harvesting strategies and highlight the potential of selecting PD-rich accessions or populations to maximize the antiparasitic efficacy of these plants in small ruminant production systems.

While this study provides valuable insights into the influence of agro-ecological zone and season on the anthelmintic activity of Mitragyna inermis, Combretum glutinosum, and Bridelia ferruginea, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on total phenolic, flavonoid, and tannin measurements without performing compound-specific identification using advanced techniques such as HPLC-MS or LC-MS/MS. As a result, the precise bioactive compounds responsible for the observed anthelmintic effects remain unidentified, limiting mechanistic interpretation. Second, the results were obtained using in vitro assays. Although in vitro larval migration inhibition provides an indication of potential efficacy, it does not fully replicate the complex physiological and metabolic conditions of the host. Therefore, in vivo studies are necessary to confirm the efficacy, optimal dosage, and safety of these plant extracts under realistic animal production conditions.

Future research should focus on identifying the environmental determinants particularly soil characteristics, water availability, and climatic variables that most strongly influence the biosynthesis and structural composition of bioactive compounds, including condensed tannins and their prodelphinidin: procyanidin (PD: PC) ratios. Detailed phytochemical profiling using advanced analytical techniques such as HPLC-MS or LC-MS/MS is essential to identify the specific compounds responsible for the observed anthelmintic activity and to explain the seasonal and agro-ecological variations in efficacy. Furthermore, research should explore synergistic or additive effects among different classes of phytochemicals, including phenolics, flavonoids, alkaloids, and saponins, and further elucidate their mechanisms of action. To confirm the biological relevance of these findings, in vivo validation under realistic field conditions for example, in extensive or regenerative livestock farming systems is crucial. Such studies will help determine the actual efficacy, optimal dosage, and safety of these plant-based formulations in practical production settings, ensuring their suitability for sustainable parasite control programs.

In addition, further studies should investigate the synergistic or additive effects among different classes of phytochemicals such as phenolics, flavonoids, alkaloids, and saponins to better elucidate their mechanisms of action. Previous work has shown that condensed tannins can impair the integrity and motility of nematode larvae interfere with cuticular permeability, and inhibit enzymatic systems involved in parasite nutrition and metabolism35–37.

Finally, complementary in vivo studies are required to validate the efficacy, optimal dosage, and safety of these plant extracts under realistic livestock production conditions, thereby supporting their potential integration into sustainable parasite control strategies.
