One-step gas-phase syntheses of few-layered single-phase Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 MXenes with high stabilities
Authors: Fen Yue, Maoqiao Xiang, Jie Zheng, Jiuyi Zhu, Jiake Wei, Puheng Yang, Hebang Shi, Qinghua Dong, Wenjun Ding, Chenchen Chen, Yafeng Yang, Chuanfang John Zhang, Huigang Zhang, Qingshan Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the iteration of etching techniques, MXenes have exhibited astounding accomplishments. Nevertheless, intricate procedures, expensive precursors, and degradation present obstacles for practical application. Although chemical vapor deposition has been developed as a solution, direct syntheses of few-layered single-phase MXenes remain an open challenge, especially for Ti2NCl2. Here, we propose a one-step gas-phase synthetic method to fabricate few-layered single-phase Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2. Design of the activation section and segregation from the synthetic zone are the key factors. The reaction paths, synthetic mechanism, and degradation behavior are revealed. Due to the low proportion of Ti vacancies, the time constant of the Ti2CCl2 solution is 73 times longer than that of Ti2CClx. Furthermore, the specific capacity for Li+ storage with Ti2NCl2 is 1.37 times greater than that of Ti2CCl2. The one-step gas-phase method will accelerate practical application of MXenes.Subject terms: Nanoparticle synthesis, Two-dimensional materials, Nanoparticles

Introduction

MXenes have garnered significant attention owing to their remarkable electronic, optical, and mechanical properties1–3. In the past decade, numerous scientists have explored syntheses and applications4–12 with great effort and imagination, and astounding accomplishments have been realized13–19. However, direct syntheses of few-layered single-phase MXenes still face significant challenges.

MXenes are typically synthesized via etching, wherein MAX precursors (where M represents an early transition metal, A stands for Al, Si, Zn, and Ga, X is carbon and/or nitrogen) are first synthesized at high temperature and then etched with hazardous HF or Lewis acidic molten salts20. The etching method has exhibited excellent universality. Since Ti3C2Tx was first synthesized in 201121, more than 50 types of MXenes have been reported22. However, to obtain few-layered or single-layered MXenes, multiple rounds of washing, centrifugation, delamination, filtration or freeze drying are required23,24. Moreover, it is difficult to obtain pure functional groups due to substitution of the surface functional groups during the washing stage, which leads to a mixture of functional groups25,26, such as -F and/or -Cl, -O, and -OH. Furthermore, during the etching process, cationic defects (single vacancies, vacancy clusters, and voids) are inevitably formed27, which decreases the environmental stability28,29. For instance, a few-layered Ti2CTx MXene solution will survive for several hours in air30. Additionally, due to the cost of MAX precursors and the lengthy process, the prices of few- or single-layered MXenes are exceptionally high and limit practical industrial application. For example, the price of the most common commercial few-layered Ti3C2Tx (99% purity) is approximately 7.5 times greater than the price of gold. A scalable, inexpensive, and concise direct synthesis for producing few-layered single-phase MXenes is required for explorations of the inherent physical and chemical properties and practical application.

To achieve this goal, the primary focus is to identify suitable and economical titanium sources. In a recent breakthrough, Wang et al. 1 substituted the costly Ti2AlC precursor with Ti foil and TiCl4, enabling direct syntheses of Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2 MXene carpets. However, the synthesized Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2 contained TiC and TiN impurities. Moreover, Xiang et al. 31 discovered that TiCl3 served as the sole titanium source for synthesizing Ti2CCl2 and subsequently developed a scalable fluidized bed gas synthetic technique. These two reports initiated a new phase for direct syntheses of MXenes and inspired continuous efforts to eliminate impurities and reduce the costs of the syntheses.

Syntheses of single-phase titanium-nitride MXenes is more challenging1,31 than synthesizing titanium-carbide MXenes. This is due to the higher formation energy and lower cohesion energy of titanium-nitride MXenes32–37, implying that the titanium-nitride MXenes are less stable. Direct syntheses of single-phase Ti2NCl2 MXenes have not been achieved with either the conventional etching method or the chemical vapor synthetic method. In addition, the reported fluidized bed process requires prefabrication of TiCl3 powder, which is highly susceptible to oxidation and poses challenges for storage and transfer, thereby adding complexity to the manufacturing process and consequently increasing the cost. Moreover, the oxidation resistance and mechanism for Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2 have not been elucidated due to the lack of efficient methods for synthesizing single-phase MXenes.

Here, we report a one-step gas-phase synthetic process for direct synthesis of few-layered single-phase Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2, which significantly decreases the synthetic flow. The mechanism for the synthesis of Ti2NCl2 is elucidated by calculating the chemical potentials for Ti, N, and Cl and designing the reaction path. The as-synthesized Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 have low Ti vacancy densities and excellent conductivities and stabilities. The time constant of the Ti2CCl2 solution is 73 times longer than that of the etching-derived Ti2CClx MXene solution. The synthesized Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 MXenes provide a pathway for exploring their inherent physicochemical properties. Furthermore, the developed one-step gas-phase synthesis method shows the potential for continuous and scalable production of high quality Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 powders at a low cost, offering an opportunity to bring Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 MXenes and their composite materials from the laboratory to practical applications.

Results

Syntheses of Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2

To avoid handling the thorny TiCl3 precursor and improve the synthetic efficiency, we designed a one-step reactor and developed a corresponding synthetic process for fabricating single-phase Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2, as shown in Fig. 1a. TiCl4, a challenging titanium source for carbonization or nitride (Supplementary Fig. 1), was activated with a titanium sponge at 1000 °C in the activation region. Then, the generated gas was immediately transported by Ar to a low-temperature synthetic region for subsequent carbonization and/or nitridation.

The trigonal phases Ti2CCl2 (Fig. 1b) and Ti2NCl2 (Fig. 1c) were obtained at 770 °C and 650 °C with CH4 and N2 used as the carbon and nitrogen sources, respectively. According to the Rietveld structural refinement, the lattice parameters for Ti2CCl2 were a = b = 3.25402 Å and c = 8.63556 Å (Supplementary Table 1), which were similar to previous values1,3. TiC with the face-centered cubic (FCC) structure and free carbon impurities were not detected, as confirmed by the Raman spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 2). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the synthesized Ti2CCl2 exhibited a three-dimensional “flower-like” structure composed of two-dimensional flakes (Fig. 1d). Individual two-dimensional flakes were obtained through grinding and ultrasonic dispersion (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 3). The dispersion of Ti2CCl2 showed the typical Tyndall effect (Supplementary Fig. 4). Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) results (Supplementary Table 2) indicate that the Ti2CCl2 MXenes were not obviously damaged after ultrasonication. Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images clearly showed, in Fig. 1f, that the outer Cl atoms were positioned on top of the second layer of Ti atoms, consistent with the theoretical model. The atomic ratio of Ti, C, and Cl in the synthesized powder is approximately 2: 1: 1.99, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 5 and Table 3, further confirming the composition of Ti2CCl2. The thicknesses of the as-synthesized Ti2CCl2 flakes were approximately 4.3 nm (Supplementary Fig. 6), corresponding to approximately 5 layers of stacked Ti, C, and Cl atoms.

Hence, by designing a precursor activation section and developing a one-step reactor, we synthesized few-layered Ti2CCl2 flakes directly, which has been a challenging task with the cost-effective TiCl4 titanium source1,31. Compared with traditional etching methods, one-step methods avoid delamination and tedious centrifugal separation, significantly shortening the process flow. Additionally, in addition to CH4, C2H4 can also serve as a carbon source for the syntheses of Ti2CCl2 flakes (Supplementary Fig. 7). However, the temperature window for synthesizing single-phase Ti2CCl2 is extremely narrow (Supplementary Fig. 8). The main reason is that during the synthesis, C2H4 decomposes into CH4 or C while releasing a significant amount of heat (Supplementary Fig. 7); this causes difficulty in accurately controlling the synthetic temperature, because the actual local reaction temperature was higher than the set temperature of the furnace. In contrast, within the synthetic temperature range, the decomposition of CH4 hardly occurred. Therefore, it was more reasonable to utilize CH4 as a carbon source.

The lattice parameters for Ti2NCl2 (a = b = 3.22176 Å and c = 8.53473 Å; Supplementary Table 4) were slightly smaller than those of Ti2CCl2. Face-centered cubic TiN impurities were not detected (Fig. 1c), which was consistent with the Raman spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 9) and the Ti, N, and Cl contents (the atomic ratio is approximately 2: 0.97: 2.02, Supplementary Fig. 5 and Table 3). To the best of our knowledge, direct synthesis of single-phase Ti2NCl2 without any posttreatment has never been realized. The morphology (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Fig. 1g, h) and atomic stacking arrangement (Fig. 1i) of Ti2NCl2 were similar to those of Ti2CCl2. The Ti2NCl2 MXenes also did not show obvious changes after ultrasonication, as revealed by the EDX and X-ray diffraction (XRD) results (Supplementary Table 5 and Figure 11). The thicknesses of the Ti2NCl2 flakes (approximately 3.8 nm, Supplementary Fig. 12) were slightly less than those of the Ti2CCl2 flakes.

To understand the local structures around the Ti atoms, the extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectrum of the as-synthesized Ti2NCl2 was fitted. The Ti–N, Ti–Cl, and Ti–Ti scattering paths were identified (Fig. 1j, k), and their coordination numbers (Supplementary Table 6) were 3, 3, and 6, respectively, consistent with the crystallographic models. These results indicated that the as-synthesized Ti2NCl2 had a very low concentration of titanium vacancies. To quantify the titanium vacancies, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra of Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 were analyzed and compared with those of conventional Ti2NClx and Ti2CClx obtained by etching. As shown in Fig. 1l, neither Ti2NCl2 nor Ti2CCl2 exhibited signature peaks for Ti atomic vacancies, whereas these peaks were detected for Ti2NClx and Ti2CClx, which confirmed the low proportions of titanium vacancies in Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2. When C2H4 and N2 were introduced simultaneously, a Ti2(C,N)Cl2 powder was obtained at 700 °C (Supplementary Fig. 13). The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of the synthesized powder (Supplementary Fig. 14 and Table 7) further confirmed the formation of Ti2(C,N)Cl2. Four-probe measurements indicated that the conductivities of the as-synthesized Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2 (Supplementary Fig. 15) were much greater than those of the Ti2CClx and Ti2NClx prepared via the etching method (Supplementary Fig. 16). Due to their excellent conductivities, Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 may have great application prospects in electricity-related fields.

We found that segregation of the activation zone from the carbon/nitrogen synthetic zone was crucial for fabricating high-quality powders. Without implementing segregated reactions, that is, when Ti, TiCl4, CH4 or N2 reacted at the same position, a single-phase powder was not obtained (Supplementary Fig. 17). In addition, if solid TiCl3 powder was directly carbonized and nitrided without vaporization, i.e., in a gas–solid reaction system, Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2were obtained (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). However, the as-synthesized powders contained TiN or TiC impurities and exhibit multilayered structures with holes or chimney-like channels (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). These three-dimensional multilayered powders required additional stripping and delamination processes to obtain two-dimensional flakes (Supplementary Fig. 22). In addition, to increase the synthetic efficiency, a modified fluidized bed process was developed for the syntheses of Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 on seed powders (Supplementary Fig. 23). By designing a pre-reaction activation and ingeniously devising a single-step partitioned reactor, the long-standing challenge of synthesizing high-purity, low-defect Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 MXenes has been addressed.

Synthetic mechanism for Ti2NCl2

To parse the reaction process and understand the synthetic mechanism, the possible reactions occurring in the activation region and in the synthetic region were investigated. Figure 2a shows the equilibrium compositions as a function of temperature for the Ti-TiCl4 (Ti = 1 mol, TiCl4 = 3 mol) system. Gas-phase TiCl3 began to form above 550 °C. With increasing temperature, the content of gas-phase TiCl3 sharply increased. The equilibrium yield of gas-phase TiCl3 reached a peak of approximately 90% at 1000 °C (Fig. 2a), which was approximately 3.2 times that of the H2-TiCl4 system (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 24). The formation of TiCl3 in the Ti-TiCl4 system at 1000 °C was confirmed by the XRD pattern (Supplementary Fig. 25). The ratio of TiCl4 to Ti played a crucial role in determining the composition of the product and the yield of TiCl3. When the molar ratio of TiCl4/Ti was less than 2, TiCl2 impurities formed (Supplementary Fig. 26). When the TiCl4/Ti molar ratio was greater than 3, even though the content of TiCl3 in the equilibrium component increased (Supplementary Fig. 27), the utilization rate of titanium gradually decreased (Supplementary Fig. 28). Therefore, to generate as much TiCl3 in situ as possible and improve the yield of MXene, the optimal temperature and molar ratio of TiCl4/Ti in the activation region were determined to be 1000 °C and 3, respectively.

To understand the synthetic mechanism for Ti2NCl2, we calculated the cohesion energies and formation enthalpies of several key compounds in the Ti-N-Cl system (Supplementary Note 1) and plotted the chemical potential diagrams (Supplementary Note 2). The calculated cohesive energies (Supplementary Fig. 29) indicated that formation of single-phase Ti2NCl2 in the Ti-N-Cl system was challenging because TiN is a more stable phase than Ti2NCl2. However, by carefully manipulating the chemical potentials of the constituent elements (μTi, μN, and μCl), we identified a predominant area (Fig. 2d) where only Ti2NCl2 existed, without impurities such as TiN, Ti2N, Ti3N4, TiNCl, or Ti3N2Cl2. Hence, the synthesis of single-phase Ti2NCl2 in a Ti–N–Cl system is thermodynamically feasible.

To clarify the boundary conditions for synthesizing single-phase Ti2NCl2, the titanium sources, nitrogen sources, and synthetic temperatures were systematically investigated. When TiCl4 was activated by Ti and nitrided by N2, the temperature range for synthesizing single-phase Ti2NCl2 was 600 °C to 700 °C (Fig. 2c). Above 700 °C, the TiN impurity phase formed. Surprisingly, Ti2NCl2 was not obtained if N2 was replaced by NH3. The products were either amorphous TiN with NH4Cl impurities or FCC TiN (Supplementary Fig. 30). If TiCl4 was not activated by Ti and reacted directly with N2 or H2, the lowest initial reaction temperature was approximately 900 °C, and the only product was the TiN coating (Supplementary Fig. 31). In addition, if TiCl4 was not activated and reacted directly with NH3, Ti2NCl2 was not synthesized (Supplementary Fig. 32). The initial reaction temperature for the TiCl4-NH3 system was quite low. TiNCl was detected at approximately 450 °C, that is, TiCl4 + 4NH3 = TiNCl + 3NH4Cl. Single-phase TiNCl was obtained at 475 °C (Supplementary Fig. 33). With continuous increases in the synthetic temperature ( > 550 °C), TiNCl and NH4Cl were no longer detectable, and TiN was the sole product. Based on these experimental results, a reaction pathway map was constructed (Fig. 2e). It is infeasible to synthesize Ti2NCl2 with TiCl4 used as the sole titanium source and NH3 as the nitrogen source. However, single-phase Ti2NCl2 was synthesized by carefully designing the reaction path and developing a suitable reactor.

Stabilities of Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2

The stabilities of MXenes are crucial for practical application30,38. However, the degradation behaviors and oxidation mechanisms of MXenes are not well understood39,40. The attenuation rate of the (0001) peak intensity in the XRD spectra was employed to characterize the stabilities of the Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 powders in the given atmosphere. Even after being stored in argon (Ar) for 1 year, the (0001) peak intensities for Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2 barely changed (Supplementary Fig. 34), indicating that Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2 did not spontaneously degrade. After 75 days in dry air (H2O, 0.77 kPa), the (0001) peak intensities for Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2 decreased by 1.22% and 10.43%, respectively (Fig. 3a). With increasing water vapor pressures up to 3.2 kPa (wet air), the degradation rates for both Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2 were significantly accelerated (Supplementary Fig. 35 and Fig. 3b). Therefore, H2O was the main factor in the degradations. The variation of the O/Ti atomic ratio in Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2 from the EDX results (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9) is consistent with the decay trend of the (0001) peak from the XRD results. The degradation behavior was expressed with an empirical equation (A = Aunre + Aree-T/τ, where Aunre and Are are the unreactive and reactive components in the MXene nanosheets, respectively)30,38. The obtained time constants (τ) of the Ti2CCl2 was 18.5 times that of the Ti2NCl2 powder (Supplementary Table 10 and Fig. 3b), indicating that Ti2CCl2 was comparatively more stable.

To understand the degradation process of the colloidal suspension, both the as-synthesized Ti2CCl2 and the etched Ti2CClx were dispersed in deionized water at room temperature in air. The absorption intensities at 440 nm in the ultraviolet visible (UV‒vis) spectra (Supplementary Fig. 36 and Table 11) were determined and plotted as a function of time (Fig. 3c). After 9 days, 97.82% of the Ti2CClx in the aqueous solution had undergone degradation, while in stark contrast, only 16.91% of the Ti2CCl2 had degraded. The time constant for the Ti2CCl2 solution (63.3 days) was 73 times that of the Ti2CClx solution (0.86 days), suggesting significantly greater stability for Ti2CCl2. The high stability of Ti2CCl2 was further confirmed by the SEM, TEM and XRD analyses of the Ti2CCl2 dispersion after various times (Supplementary Figs. 36–38). To the best of our knowledge, this is the longest time constant for an MXene sample with a Ti2C framework in water without extra protective measures.

To understand the origin of the high stability, we simulated the degradation process for single-layer, defect-free Ti2CCl2 (Fig. 3d), defective Ti1.8CCl2 (10% Ti vacancies), and Ti1.9CCl2 (5% Ti vacancies) in 200 H2O molecules with a reactive force field (ReaxFF)38,40 and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD). The collapse threshold for the two-dimensional Ti2C framework in Ti2CCl2 (~ 500 K) was much greater than that for defective Ti1.8CCl2 (~ 300 K), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 39. More defects resulted in more complete structural damage, as seen from a comparison between Ti1.8CCl2 and Ti1.9CCl2 (Supplementary Fig. 40) at the same temperature, indicating that defects indeed accelerated degradation. To decipher the degradation kinetics, we analyzed the average numbers of Ti–Cl, Ti–C, C–C, and Ti–O bonds during the degradation of Ti2CCl2 and Ti1.8CCl2 at 400 K. Figure 3e shows that the average numbers of Ti–C and C–C bonds in Ti2CCl2 remained unchanged over time. Although the average numbers of Ti–Cl bonds decrease in Ti2CCl2, most Cl atoms remain attached to the surface of the Ti2C framework (Supplementary Fig. 41). As degradation time increases, H2O molecules continuously attack Ti2CCl2, causing Cl atom to depart and gradually move away from the surface of the Ti2C framework, while O atom gradually approaches the Ti2C framework structure. A small number of Ti–O bonds in Ti2CCl2 was observed around 2014 ps (Supplementary Fig. 42). However, the Ti–O bonds in Ti1.8CCl2 were detected at 200 ps, and the Ti–Cl bonds and Ti–C bonds decreased drastically to 0.85 and 0.77 at 600 ps, respectively.

More importantly, degradation of the Ti2C framework always started at Ti defect sites (Supplementary Fig. 43), indicating that Ti defects were the centers or origin of degradation. Indeed, the Ti vacancy increased the 2p electron density of the nearest Cl atoms (Fig. 3f), which may have facilitated the breakage of Ti–Cl bonds owing to attack of a H+ from H2O41,42. The exposed Ti atoms readily bonded with OH– and O2–, accelerating the hydrolysis of Ti1.8CCl2. This simulation highlighted the importance of eliminating Ti defects in designing high-performance MXenes with appreciable stabilities.

Li+ storage property of Ti2NCl2

Compared to high-entropy oxide nanomaterials, Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 feature a two-dimensional layered structure, offering abundant pathways for the intercalation/deintercalation, storage, and movement of lithium ions, alongside exceptional electrical conductivity that enables rapid electron transport. Compared to graphite, Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 could offer a large number of active sites due to their special surface functional groups and provide hybrid lithium storage mechanism. Hence, the Li+ storage capacities of Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 were investigated in a 1 M LiPF6/ethylene carbonate-dimethyl carbonate electrolyte. During the initial cyclic voltammetry (CV) cycle (Supplementary Fig. 44), the reduction peaks appearing at 1–3 V indicates the formation of solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)43, which quickly stabilized after a few subsequent cycles. The rectangular and symmetric cyclic voltammetry curve strongly suggested a pseudocapacitive Li+ storage mechanism. Figure 4a shows the rectangular CV curves produced for the Ti2NCl2 electrode at a scan rate of 0.5 mV s–1 with different negative cutoff potentials, which confirmed the pseudocapacitive behavior. Figure 4b shows the calculated specific lithiation capacities of the Ti2NCl2 electrode as a function of the charge/discharge time and scan rate (Supplementary Fig. 45 and Table 12). The specific lithiation capacity of the Ti2NCl2 electrode at a scan rate of 0.5 mV s–1 (360 mAh g–1) was 1.37 times greater than that of the Ti2CCl2 electrode (Supplementary Fig. 46, 262 mAh g–1). The inset of Fig. 4b shows a linear relationship in the low scan rate range (<20 mV s−1) with a fitted value of b equal to 0.82, which falls between 0.5 and 1, indicating a hybrid storage mechanism involving Li+ intercalation/deintercalation and pseudocapacitive processes44,45. The oxidation-reduction peaks in the CV curve obtained at a scan rate of 0.5 mV s–1 (Supplementary Fig. 46) confirmed the intercalation/deintercalation process. Moreover, we summarized the specific lithiation capacities of Ti2C/Ti2N-based MXenes reported in the recent literature (Supplementary Table 13), which highlighted the excellent Li+ storage capacity of the as-synthesized Ti2NCl2.

Figure 4c shows the voltage profile of the Ti2NCl2 electrode, as determined with galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) measurements. At a low current density of 0.1 A g−1, the maximum capacity of the Ti2NCl2 electrode was 447 mAh g−1 (574 F g−1). With increasing current density, the Ti2NCl2 electrode exhibited capacities of 156 mAh g−1 and 40 mAh g−1 at current densities of 2.0 A g−1 and 10 A g−1, respectively. Due to the lower proportion of pseudocapacitive capacity (16.3%) compared with Ti2CCl2 (55.5%, Supplementary Fig. 47), the overall capacity was reduced at high current density during the GCD test. In the high-frequency regions of the Nyquist plots, as shown in Fig. 4d, significant increases in the impedance at lower frequencies were detected, which confirmed pseudocapacitive behavior of the Ti2NCl2 electrode across the entire potential range. As the potential decreased, the diameters of the semicircles in the Nyquist plots gradually diminished, indicating faster kinetics for the reactants.

In conclusion, by designing an activation process, carefully regulating the partial pressure of TiCl4, selecting suitable precursors, and devising a partitioned reactor, we directly fabricated few-layered single-phases of Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2. The persistent challenge of producing single-phase Ti2NCl2 has been addressed. A modified fluidized bed process was developed to mass-produce Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2, greatly simplifying the operating conditions and reducing the cost. More importantly, the as-synthesized Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 materials exhibited low titanium vacancy densities, excellent conductivities, and outstanding stabilities. The specific capacity for Li+ storage by Ti2NCl2 was much greater than that of the reported Ti2CCl2. This one-step gas-phase synthetic strategy will pave the way for fabricating other MXenes.

Methods

Fabrication of Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 MXenes

TiCl4 (liquid) was carried by Ar (200 sccm) to react with titanium sponge in activation region at 1000 °C. The synthesized gas was transported to the synthetic region to react with the carbon/nitrogen source: for Ti2NCl2 with N2 (50 sccm) at 650 °C, for Ti2CCl2 with CH4 or C2H4 (50 sccm) at 760 °C, and for Ti2(C,N)Cl2 with both N2 (50 sccm) and C2H4 (50 sccm) at 700 °C. After 30 min, Ti2NCl2, Ti2CCl2, or Ti2(C,N)Cl2 was gradually cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, the product was carefully sealed with argon. The details of the raw materials can be found in the Supplementary Information.

Obtaining few-layered Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 MXenes solution

Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 powders, synthesized via the one-step gas-phase direct synthesis method, exhibits a three-dimensional “flower-like” structure composed of two-dimensional flakes. To obtain individual two-dimensional flake, Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 powders were gently ground in one direction (such as clockwise) for approximately 5 min, followed by ultrasonication in ethanol for 10 min at 0 °C. The resulting supernatant liquid is the Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 MXenes solution. For comparison, Ti2NClx and Ti2CClx MXene were fabricated by molten salt-based etching of commercial Ti2AlN and Ti2AlC according to the literature3,46. The details of the fabrications of Ti2NClx and Ti2CClx can be found in the Supplementary Information.

Materials characterization

Morphological and elemental analyses were conducted with FESEM (JEOL, JSM-7001F, Japan) with an EDX detector (X-Max, Oxford Instruments, England). HRTEM and HAADF-STEM images were obtained with JEM-2100 PLUS (JEOL, Japan) and Talos F200X electron microscopes, respectively. The thicknesses of individual Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 flakes were observed with an AFM instrument (MultiMode 8, Bruker). The EPR spectra were recorded with an electron paramagnetic resonance spectrometer (EMXplus, Bruker, USA). The electronic conductivities were tested by an HPS2662 Four-probe resistance meter (Helpass Electronic Technologies, Inc, China). Ti2NCl2, Ti2CCl2, Ti2NClx, and Ti2CClx powders were pressed into pellets (10 mm diameter, 0.5 mm thickness) with a close relative density of 45%, respectively.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD patterns were obtained with an X’Pert PRO diffractometer (PANalytical, the Netherlands) with Cu Kα (1.542 Å) radiation as an X-ray source at 40 kV and 40 mA. The obtained solutions of Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 MXenes were drop-casted onto quartz glass plates or the Si wafer zero-background holder, followed by using a rubber suction bulb to blow air and accelerate the evaporation of the liquid droplets. After approximately 3 min, the droplets dried, and the two-dimensional Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 MXenes adhered to the glass plates. This process was repeated three times for testing.

Quantitative analysis of element content

To test the content of Ti and Cl elements, 50 mg of Ti2NCl2 or Ti2CCl2 powder was dissolved using a microwave digestion instrument (TANK40, Sineo Microwave Chemistry Technology (Shanghai) Co.Ltd, China) and diluted to 25 mL. Then, 1 mL of the resulting solution was further diluted to 1000 mL, and 10 mL of the diluted solution was used for subsequent analysis. The Ti and Cl contents in the diluted solution were determined using an Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometer (ICP-OES, iCAP 6300, Thermo Scientific, USA) and an Automatic Potentiometric Titrator (ZDJ-4A, Shanghai Precision Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China). To determine the C and N contents in the synthesized products, 50 mg of Ti2CCl2 and Ti2NCl2 were analyzed using a Carbon/Sulfur Analyzer (CSA, CS-2800G, NCS Testing Technology Co., Ltd., China) and an NHO Analyzer (G8, Bruker, Germany), respectively.

Raman spectroscopy

The as-synthesized resultant powders were sonicated in ethanol solution for 5 min, and then dropped on a glass slide. After dried in vacuum for 30 min, the sample was characterized by a high-resolution confocal Raman microscopy (HR 800, Horiba, Japan) using a 533 nm light source. The power of the lasers was kept in the 0.5–1 mW range.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS analysis of all samples were performed on a Shimadzu AXIS SUPRA+ spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan) using monochromatized Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV) as an X-ray source under ultra-high vacuum conditions. All XPS spectra were calibrated by C 1s (284.8 eV) for any remaining charge-induced shifts. Deconvolution of the high-resolution XPS spectra was performed in Casa XPS software using asymmetric Lorentzian-Gaussian curves and a Tougaard background. The Ti 2p region consists of the two 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 spin-orbit split components, and the peak area ratio was fixed to 2:1. The Ti 2p region was fit using 3 pairs of 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 components.

X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS)

The Ti K-edge X-ray absorption fine structure spectrum (XAS) was collected at a Synchrotron Radiation Facility (EasyXAFS 300 + , Quantum Design, USA). The XAS used a silicon drift detector with a Si (001) crystal as the monochromator. The beam size was limited by the horizontal and vertical slits with the area of 3 × 13 mm2 during the XAS measurements. A titanium foil standard was used as a reference for energy calibration and was measured simultaneously with experimental samples. Data collected were processed using Athena software (version 0.9.26)47 by extracting the EXAFS oscillations χ(k) as a function of photoelectron wave number k. The Artemis software (version 0.9.26) was adopted to fit the theoretical paths using FEFF61248. Data sets were simultaneously fitted in the R-space with k-weights of 1, 2 and 3. The Hama Fortran program with Morlet mother wavelet was used for wavelet transform.

UV-Vis Spectrophotometer

UV-Vis measurements of the MXene colloidal solutions were performed on UV6100 UV-Vis Spectrophotometer. Prior to the measurements, the MXene solution was diluted to a concentration of 0.005 mg ml–1. To minimize oxidation during the transfer, the delaminated MXenes were sealed in small transfer boxes filled with Ar. All the UV-Vis spectra were normalized to the local minimum at 210 nm, and the intensity at 440 nm was extracted as an indicator of concentration change as a function of time.

MD calculations

All of the MD calculations were performed with the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) package code. The ReaxFF potential for Ti-C-O-Cl-H systems was adopted for the molecular dynamic simulations38,41 and has been applied to analyze the oxidation of Ti3C2O2 MXenes in O2, H2O, and H2O2 environments38. The initial model for the Ti2CCl2-water system with a dimensionality of approximately 32 × 34 × 24 Å3 was composed of 240 Ti, 120 C, 240 Cl, and 400 water molecules. The Cl- functional groups were fully terminated on both surfaces of Ti2C. Five and 10% of the Ti atoms in Ti2CCl2 were randomly removed. The temperature and pressure were controlled with a Nosé–Hoover thermostat and barostat methods with damping times of 100 and 1000 timesteps, respectively. The timestep was 0.2 fs. As MD simulations require femtosecond time steps to resolve individual bond vibrations49,50, a temperature (400 K) higher than the experimental temperature (300 K) was used to accelerate the dynamics of the system during the short time scale of the MD simulations.

Electrochemical characterization

Ti2NCl2 and Ti2CCl2 electrodes were prepared by mixing the synthesized MXenes, Super P carbon black, and polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) powder at a weight ratio of 8:1:1. The mixture was subsequently dispersed into N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) to form a slurry with a mortar and pestle. The slurry was coated onto an Al foil and dried overnight at 60 °C under vacuum before the coated foil was cut into 10 mm-diameter discs for use as working electrodes. Metallic lithium foil was used as the combined counter and reference electrode, LP30 (1M LiPF6 solution in a mixed solvent of EC and DMC with a volume ratio of 1:1) was used as the electrolyte, and a 25 μm-thick PP/PC/PP film was used as the separator. CV, GCD and Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted with a CHI760E electrochemical workstation (Chen Hua Instrument, China).
