Efficacy of indocyanine green-mediated photodynamic therapy in the treatment of Candida albicans-penetrated denture base material: an in vitro study
Authors: Asli Sinci, Işil Turp, Özcan Erdoğan, Volkan Turp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDenture stomatitis is a frequently encountered inflammatory condition among complete denture wearers. Candida species are the principal etiological agents and are capable of colonizing both the denture surface and surrounding oral tissues, as well as infiltrating the denture base material. Effective management necessitates the elimination of microorganisms from both surface and subsurface regions of the denture. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the efficacy of indocyanine green (ICG)-mediated photodynamic therapy (PDT) against C. albicans inside acrylic resin.MethodsA total of 108 polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) discs with a radius of 10 mm and thickness of 2 mm were produced. The samples were contaminated with C. albicans. The following nine groups were evaluated: control group (C); nystatin-treated (1 mL, 100.000 IU) group (N); microwave-disinfected (650 W, 3 min) group (M); laser-treated (810 nm, diode laser, continuous mode 30 s, 24 J/cm², 300 mW) group (L); ICG-treated (10 mg/mL) group (ICG10); 10 mg/mL ICG-mediated PDT group (L-ICG10), 5 mg/mL ICG-mediated PDT group (L-ICG5); 2 mg/mL ICG-mediated PDT group (L-ICG2); and 1 mg/mL ICG-mediated PDT group (L-ICG1). After being vortexed, the C. albicans on and in the denture base material were extracted, and the resulting solutions of the samples were inoculated into Petri dishes containing Sabouraud agar media. Colonies of C. albicans biofilms on media were counted with a colony counting device after incubation for 24 h. Eleven samples from each group were used for statistical analysis, and 1 spare sample from each group was used for scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test and the Kruskal‒Wallis test with Bonferroni correction were used for statistical analysis (p˂0.05).ResultsL-ICG10, L-ICG5 and ICG10 reduced the number of C. albicans colonies on the denture base material (p˂0.05), whereas the other groups did not significantly differ from the control group (p > 0.05). C. albicans forms were detected in the SEM images of the cross-sections of all the groups except the L-ICG10 group.ConclusionWithin the limitations of the current study, 10 mg/mL ICG-mediated PDT can be used to decontaminate the denture base from C. albicans.

Background

Removable dentures are widely used for the rehabilitation of partial and complete edentulism. However, denture stomatitis, a form of oral candidiasis, is quite common due to the coverage of the palate by the denture base. Its incidence has been reported to be between 11% and 65% [1]. It is often related to poor oral and denture hygiene [2–6]. In a recent study, it was concluded that denture-related stomatitis can develop quite quickly, and the colony counts of Candida species increased significantly even after one month of new denture use [7]. Trauma caused by ill-fitting dentures, smoking, continuous wearing of dentures, medications, systemic diseases such as diabetes mellitus, and decreased salivary flow are listed as predisposing factors [8].

The most significantly isolated microorganism in denture stomatitis is Candida albicans (C. albicans) [1, 5, 6, 9, 10]. C. albicans can grow as a biofilm on soft and hard surfaces of the oral cavity. The roughness of the intaglio surface of the polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) denture base promotes bacterial and fungal colonization [2]. Moreover, C. albicans can also penetrate acrylic [6, 10, 11]. Porosities within acrylic resin generate fluid-retentive voids that facilitate microbial adhesion and subsequent colonization. Therefore, acrylic resin dentures are a potential source of reinfection after primary denture stomatitis is treated. When the same denture continues to be used, the recurrence of infection has often been reported after the conventional treatment of denture stomatitis [1, 12]. Rebasing the denture can be considered to avoid reinfection, but it has several disadvantages. Rebasing causes extra costs, and the patient must remain toothless during laboratory procedures. This may be challenging for patients when being toothless is not an option for social or professional reasons. Thus, disinfection of the denture base becomes crucial for patient comfort as well as preventing recurrence.

Various recommendations have been made to treat denture stomatitis, such as reducing nocturnal denture wearing, enhancing denture hygiene, and relining or even replacing dentures with topical and systemic antifungal agents [1, 5, 6, 13–16]. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is another treatment method that has been utilized in the management of oral candidiasis in addition to periodontal diseases, oral cancers, premalignant lesions, endodontic infections, oral mucositis, peri-implant diseases, dental caries, and viral infections of the orofacial region [17]. PDT has recently emerged as an adjunctive therapy to conventional antimicrobial treatment for various bacterial and fungal infections [4, 9, 18–22]. PDT is an approach based on interactions among three factors: a nontoxic light-sensitive chemical substance called a photosensitizer (PS), a light source with a wavelength specific to activate the PS, and molecular oxygen [6, 9, 18–21, 23]. In this method, the energy from the light source is absorbed by the PS, the PS is activated, and the stimulation of the PS results in the production of singlet oxygen and reactive oxygen species [6, 9, 13, 16, 18, 20, 21, 24, 25]. These reactive oxygen species destroy the cell membranes, enzymes, and DNA of target microorganisms. Cell death occurs without damaging the host cell [1, 16, 18, 21]. The procedure is fast, local, easy and involves the development of nonmicrobial resistance [1, 6, 18, 19, 21].

Various PSs, such as toluidine blue, methylene blue, curcumin, and indocyanine green (ICG), are used in PDT [2, 6, 20, 24]. ICG-mediated PDT has been reported to be effective against C. albicans in several in vitro studies. These studies evaluated ICG-mediated PDT directly on C. albicans [26–29]. One study compared the efficacy of ICG-mediated PDT with that of nystatin in vivo, and promising results were obtained [9]. However, the elimination of C. albicans from denture base resin is critical for preventing the recurrence of denture stomatitis. To date, the efficacy of ICG-mediated PDT for denture base disinfection has not been evaluated.

ICG is a near-infrared dye that absorbs light in the 750–900 nm range (with maximum absorption at 810 nm). This spectral property enables ICG-mediated PDT to achieve greater tissue penetration than other photosensitizers [17, 23]. This situation suggests that the effect may also occur deeper in the denture base material, resulting in better disinfection than with other PSs. Thus, PDTs with different concentrations of ICG were examined against C. albicans strains that have penetrated acrylic denture base resins, and their effectiveness with conventional medical treatment and microwave disinfection was compared. The null hypothesis was that ICG-mediated PDT is ineffective against C. albicans capable of penetrating acrylic discs.

Methods

Sample size calculation

The study comprised eight experimental groups and one control group. A priori statistical power analysis was performed to estimate the sample size required for each group. Power analysis was carried out via IBM SPSS Statistics 20.0 software (SPSS Version 20.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) with reference to previous studies [27]. For the 95% confidence level and 80% power, each group required 11 samples in the 9-group design. One spare acrylic disc for each group was also prepared for SEM imaging. A total of 99 samples (n = 11) were prepared for evaluation and statistical analysis, and 9 samples were prepared for SEM imaging.

Sample preparation

Teflon molds with a radius of 10 mm and a thickness of 2.2 mm were conventionally packed with Type III dental stone (Elite model type III; Zhermack, Badia Polesine, Italy) into a metal flask to fabricate acrylic samples. A 10 mm radius enabled the samples to be placed in Falcon tubes, and a 2.2 mm thickness was chosen to reach a thickness of 2 mm after the fabrication procedure was completed. The 2 mm thickness imitates the thickness of an acrylic denture base. The flasks were kept under hydraulic pressure of 2 atm for 45 min while the dental stone was set. The Teflon molds were removed, and heat-polymerizing polymethyl methacrylate (Meliodent Heat Cure; Heraeus Kulzer, Hanau, Germany) was mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (23.4 g powder, 10 mL liquid). The acrylic resin was packed into molds and polymerized via hydroflask curing under 2 atm pressure. Polymerization of acrylic samples was carried out in a water bath at 70 °C for 7 h and then at 100 °C for 1 h.

Following polymerization, the flasks were allowed to cool to room temperature. After deflasking, the samples were ground with 600-grit silicon carbide paper at 200 rpm under water cooling (Minitech 233; PRESI; Hagen; Germany). The irregularities on the surface were removed, and a thickness of 2 mm was achieved. Fine finishing and polishing were not applied to the acrylic discs to imitate the unpolished intaglio surface of a complete denture. A total of 108 acrylic discs were prepared with a radius of 10 mm and thickness of 2 mm The acrylic disc samples were immersed in distilled water for 24 h to remove the residual monomers after polymerization. The samples were sterilized in petri dishes in an autoclave at 121 °C for 15 min.

The reference strain C. albicans ATCC 90,028 was obtained from the culture collection of the Department of Medical Microbiology, Istanbul University, and was maintained as a reference strain for research and quality control purposes. The reference strain of C. albicans was cultured in Sabouraud dextrose broth (Saburaud dextrose broth; Condalab, Madrid, Spain) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, a suspension adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard was prepared via fresh cultures in Sabouraud dextrose broth.

Biofilm formation on acrylic discs was performed in sterile Falcon tubes, each containing nine samples. Eleven tubes were used; one sample was taken from each tube for each group (n = 11). This approach was employed to ensure uniform initial biofilm formation across all groups. The Falcon tubes were incubated at 35 °C for 5 days with a McFarland 0.5 standard C. albicans suspension. After the biofilm formation process, the fungal biofilm became visible on the surfaces.

Prior to treatment, each acrylic disc sample was individually rinsed twice in separate sterile Falcon tubes containing 10 mL of sterile distilled water to remove nonadherent C. albicans cells from the surface.

Study groups

Control group (Group C): No antifungal treatment was applied.

Nystatin-treated group (Group N): First, 1 mL of nystatin solution (Mikostatin; Deva; İstanbul; Turkey) was applied to each acrylic disc in a sterile Falcon tube for 1 min.

Microwave-disinfected group (Group M): Each acrylic disc was transferred to a sterile Petri dish and exposed to microwave irradiation (MW81Y-WP; Samsung, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea) at 650 W for 3 min.

In the laser group (Group L), the acrylic disc was transferred to a sterile petri dish. The entire upper and lower surfaces of the samples were irradiated in an S-shaped scanning pattern via a diode laser (Cheese dental diode laser; Wuhan Gigaa Optronics Technology, Wuhan, China) at a wavelength of 810 nm in continuous mode, with an energy density of 22.8 J/cm² and 300 mW of power. The parameters were consistent with those reported in a previous study evaluating IGG-mediated PDT [30]. Each side of the acrylic disc was irradiated for 60 s; a total of 120 s of irradiation was applied via an optical fibre with a diameter of 400 μm. The total energy delivered was 36 J.

ICG groups (ICG10, L-ICG10, L-ICG5, L-ICG2, L-ICG1): ICG powder (Verdye 25 mg; Diagnostic Green, Aschheim-Dornach, Germany) was reconstituted in sterile distilled water to prepare solutions at defined concentrations of 10 mg/mL for the ICG10 and L-ICG10 groups; 5 mg/mL for the L-ICG5 group; 2 mg/mL for the L-ICG2 group; and 1 mg/mL for the L-ICG1 group. Owing to the instability of the solution, all the solutions were freshly prepared immediately prior to use and protected from light during the experiments. The acrylic discs were then transferred to sterile Petri dishes for each group. A total of 20 µL of ICG solution at specified concentrations was applied to each acrylic disc via a micropipette, ensuring complete and uniform coverage of all the surfaces. Following application, the solution was maintained in contact with the acrylic discs for 10 min under light-protected conditions. The entire upper and lower surfaces of the samples were irradiated in an S-shaped scanning pattern via a diode laser. (Cheese dental diode laser; Wuhan Gigaa Optronics Technology, Wuhan, China) at a wavelength of 810 nm in continuous mode, with an energy density of 22.8 J/cm² and 300 mW of power. Each side of the acrylic disc was irradiated for 60 s; a total of 120 s of irradiation was applied to the L-ICG10, L-ICG5, L-ICG2, and L-ICG1 groups. Lasers were not applied to the ICG10 group.

Colony counting procedures

Following the treatment procedures, each acrylic disc was transferred to a sterile Falcon tube and rinsed once with 10 mL of sterile distilled water. After the washing procedure, each acrylic disc was transferred to a sterile Falcon tube containing 10 mL of 0.9% saline. The tubes were vortexed for 1 min and then left undisturbed for 9 min to facilitate the detachment of C. albicans cells from the acrylic surface into the suspension. A 1 mL aliquot of the suspension was inoculated onto Petri dishes containing Sabouraud dextrose agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow colony development. Colony-forming units (CFUs) were quantified via a colony counter (Scan 100; Interscience, Saint Nom la Bretèche, France), and the data were recorded.

SEM imaging

Following treatment, SEM was performed on one additional sample per group to qualitatively assess the presence and distribution of C. albicans. Nonvortexed samples from each group were cut into two halves via a diamond saw under continuous water cooling. Cross-sectional surfaces were imaged to evaluate the location of C. albicans within the acrylic denture base rather than on the surface. The imaging sites were selected randomly. The samples were sputter-coated with gold for 120 s at 10 mA (Turbo QS; Quorum Technologies; Laughton; UK) and examined via SEM (Zeiss Evo 10 SEM; Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) at magnifications of 300×, 500×, and 1000×. SEM analysis was conducted solely for qualitative demonstration; no quantitative assessment or statistical analysis was performed.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed via the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences program (SPSS Version 22.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed via the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. As the data did not follow a normal distribution, intergroup comparisons were conducted via the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons. The effect size (η²) for the Kruskal‒Wallis test was calculated. A p value ˂ 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Discussion

The denture should be free of pathogens as well as oral mucosa to cure denture stomatitis. Studies should focus on denture cleansing and disinfecting [7]. Thus, the present in vitro study evaluated the efficacy of ICG-mediated PDT at different concentrations against C. albicans colonizing acrylic resin denture base materials. Compared with the L-ICG10, L-ICG5 and ICG10 groups, Group C presented significantly greater mean colony counts (p˂0.05) (Table 3). Accordingly, the null hypothesis that ICG-mediated PDT is ineffective against C. albicans capable of penetrating acrylic discs was rejected. The significantly reduced colony counts in the L-ICG10 and L-ICG5 groups indicate that C. albicans is susceptible to ICG-mediated PDT when colonizing both the surface and subsurface regions of acrylic resin. This means that, when the methods of the groups mentioned above are used for the treatment of denture stomatitis, the patient’s base plate can be cleaned, thereby avoiding situations such as rebasing that could leave the patient without dentures. In addition, the Candida load within the base plate can be reduced in this way even before symptoms of stomatitis appear, making it suitable for prophylactic use.

PDT employing a range of PSs, including methylene blue, toluidine blue, aminolevulinic acid and ICG, has been widely investigated for antifungal applications. Although no consensus has been established regarding the optimal PS type or concentration, PDT has demonstrated efficacy against Candida species, including antifungal-resistant strains [9, 16, 18, 19, 25–29]. However, none of these PSs have been evaluated for their capacity to disinfect the denture base materials into which C. albicans can penetrate up to a depth of 40 μm, as observed in the present study (Fig. 3). ICG was selected as the PS in this study because it is activated by near-infrared light, which enables greater penetration depth than other PSs do [23]. The present study is the first to investigate the disinfection of denture base materials via ICG-mediated PDT.

The concentration of PS is a critical factor influencing the efficacy of PDT. In the present study, the L-ICG10 and L-ICG5 groups (10 mg/mL and 5 mg/mL ICG, respectively) presented significantly lower C. albicans colony counts than did the control group. These findings are in agreement with those of Tavangar et al. [29], who demonstrated the antifungal efficacy of 5 mg/mL ICG in an in vitro model. In contrast, Fekrazad et al. [27, 28] reported that 1 mg/mL ICG-mediated PDT was effective in reducing C. albicans viability. However, this finding is not consistent with the present findings, in which 1 mg/mL ICG-mediated PDT did not yield a significant effect. This discrepancy may be explained by methodological differences, as previous studies did not investigate acrylic resin substrates into which C. albicans can penetrate to depths of up to 40 μm (Fig. 3). Consequently, although low PS concentrations may be effective against surface-associated cells, they may be insufficient to eliminate C. albicans embedded in an acrylic matrix. Furthermore, the significant difference observed between the L-ICG10 and L-ICG1 groups indicates that higher PS concentrations may enhance penetration into the material and thereby increase antifungal efficacy. Although concentrations of 5 mg/mL and 1 mg/mL have been commonly employed in the literature, the inclusion of the 10 mg/mL group in the present study was intended to evaluate its potential for improved disinfection of acrylic substrates. The lack of efficacy observed at 1 mg/mL in the present study supports the validity of this approach.

PDT requires three fundamental components: a PS, light of appropriate wavelength and oxygen; the use of a PS or light alone does not constitute PDT [9]. In the absence of light activation, any observed biological effect is attributable to dark toxicity, defined as the intrinsic cytotoxicity of PS at any given concentration [23]. Dark toxicity is influenced by both the PS concentration and the characteristics of the target cells or the microorganisms. The cytotoxic effects of ICG without light activation have been investigated in different human cell types. Pourhajibagher et al. [24] reported a concentration-dependent reduction in fibroblast viability (98.6% to 87.8%) across different concentrations of ICG (500, 750, 1000, 1250, 1500, 1750, and 2000 µg/mL). Peters et al. [32] demonstrated that ICG was noncytotoxic to retinal pigment epithelial cells at concentrations of *125 µg/mL, whereas higher concentrations induced cytotoxicity. Turp et al. [30] observed cytotoxic effects at 5 mg/mL in fibroblasts. In contrast, Fekrazad et al. [27]. reported no significant dark toxicity of 1 mg/mL ICG to C. albicans. These findings indicate that the cellular response to ICG is dependent on both concentration and cell type [23, 29] and that the relatively low susceptibility of C. albicans may be related to structural factors, including its nuclear membrane and relatively large cell size [28]. In the present study, the colony counts of the ICG10 group were lower than those of the control group (C), even in the absence of light activation. Although the acrylic resin denture base may have acted as a diffusion barrier, this effect is likely attributable to the dark toxicity of ICG at 10 mg/mL, a concentration exceeding those evaluated in previous studies [27].

Laser irradiation alone has been reported to be effective against pathogenic endodontic bacteria [22, 33], with its antimicrobial effect primarily attributed to the photothermal mechanisms resulting from laser-induced temperature increases [34]. Kim et al. [20] demonstrated that the biofilm temperature increases with increasing PDT application. Tavangar et al. [29] reported that both laser irradiation alone and ICG-mediated PDT were effective against C. albicans, suggesting that photochemical and photothermal effects may act synergistically. In contrast, the present study revealed that laser irradiation alone (L group) was not effective against C. albicans. This discrepancy may be explained by methodological differences, including variations in laser parameters and the presence of an acrylic resin denture base. The acrylic substrate may function as a thermal buffer, dispersing the heat generated by laser irradiation and thereby reducing the localized temperature increase required for the photothermal antimicrobial effect. In support of this explanation, Turp et al. [30] reported no cytotoxic effect under similar laser parameters, with even an increase in cell proliferation observed in laser-only groups. Therefore, the absence of a reduction in C. albicans colony counts in the present study may be attributed to insufficient photothermal energy or limited heat transfer through the acrylic material. Future studies incorporating direct measurements of temperature changes during laser application are needed to further elucidate this phenomenon.

Compared with the nystatin-treated group (N), the L-ICG10 group presented lower colony counts, whereas no statistically significant difference was detected between the L-ICG5 and N groups. In agreement with these findings, Tavangar et al. [29] reported no statistically significant difference between nystatin treatment and ICG-mediated PDT at a concentration of 5 mg/mL and against Candida strains. The variation in the findings between studies may be explained by the presence of acrylic resin substrates in the present study, which may influence antimicrobial efficacy.

Microwave disinfection has been reported to be particularly effective in reducing the invasive pseudohyphal and hyphal forms of C. albicans. Silva et al. [10] demonstrated that weekly microwave disinfection using a household microwave oven (650 W for 3 min) achieved efficacy comparable to that of nystatin treatment for denture stomatitis. However, in the present study, no statistically significant differences were detected between the microwave-treated group (M) and the other groups. This discrepancy may be attributed to methodological differences, as the abovementioned study involved an in vivo study evaluating the cumulative effects of repeated microwave disinfection over a two-week period, whereas the present study assessed the impact of a single microwave exposure in vitro using acrylic samples. Moreover, microwave disinfection has been associated with dimensional changes in acrylic resin denture base materials [10]. In light of these findings, the clinical applicability of microwave disinfection as a treatment modality for denture stomatitis may be questionable.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of the cross-sectional samples revealed no detectable C. albicans in the L-ICG10 group. In contrast, both yeast and hyphal forms were identified in the C, N, M, ICG10, L, L-ICG5, L-ICG2, and L-ICG1 groups. Yeast cells were observed as oval to ellipsoidal structures, either as single cells or budding forms, particularly in the L, ICG10 and L-ICG5 groups. Hyphal forms, characterized by filamentous and nodular structures, were observed in the C, N, M, ICG10, L-ICG2 and L-ICG1 groups (Fig. 2). In the L-ICG2 group, hyphae were detected at a depth of approximately 30 μm from the acrylic surface, whereas in the L-ICG1 group, hyphal penetration extended to approximately 40 μm (Fig. 3). These findings demonstrate the ability of C. albicans to penetrate the acrylic resin matrix, which is consistent with previous studies reporting that C. albicans is present not only on the surface but also within acrylic material [11, 13].

The present study is subject to several limitations. Although the acrylic samples were fabricated in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, complete standardization of the discs may have been limited due to inherent variability in the fabrication process. Moreover, a single-species biofilm model was employed; however, denture biofilms in the oral environment are typically multispecies, mature and embedded within an extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix, which may increase resistance to antimicrobial treatments. Consequently, the complexity of the oral biofilm environment could not be fully replicated in this study. In addition, potential secondary effects of laser transmission through acrylic discs, resulting in repeated exposure of C. albicans and ICG, were not accounted for in the calculation of laser energy density. In the present study, nystatin was applied as a single treatment; however, standard clinical protocols recommend its administration four times a day for two weeks in the management of mucosal infections, in addition to overnight immersion of dentures in nystatin solution [35]. In contrast, no established protocol exists for the application of ICG-mediated PDT to denture materials. To ensure methodological standardization across treatment modalities, a single application of each procedure was employed to evaluate their relative effectiveness in disinfecting the denture base rather than their potential for complete elimination of infection.

The present study may be considered a preliminary investigation into the efficacy of ICG-mediated PDT for denture stomatitis. Further studies are needed to optimize the PDT parameters, evaluate its effectiveness against complex and multispecies biofilms, and investigate its applicability across different denture base materials.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of the present study, the following conclusions can be drawn: ICG-mediated PDT at concentrations of 10 mg/mL and 5 mg/mL and the dark toxicity of 10 mg/mL ICG can be used for removing C. albicans from the infected base plate in a short time without leaving the patient without a denture and causing social difficulties.
