An efficient federated learning based defense mechanism for software defined network cyber threats through machine learning models
Authors: Rashid Amin, Antonio Costanzo, Lial Raja Alzabin, Aqsa Aqdus, Syafiq Fauzi Kamarulzaman, Asma Hassan Alshehri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoftware-Defined Networking (SDN) is flexible, provides centralized control, and is vendor-independent; however, due to its centralized architecture, it is extremely susceptible to complex cyber-attacks. The classical means of intrusion detection proves to be unable to cope with dynamic and large-scales threats, which prove to be high-false positive as well as slow responses. This work proposes an AI-based federated defense system, which incorporates XGBoost to identify threats correctly, LightGBM to provide adaptive responses in real-time, and Federated Learning to share intelligence among each other without jeopardizing data confidentiality. Based on high-dimensional network traffic, log files, system activities, the framework uses feature engineering to improve anomaly differentiation and boost SDN resilience. Extensive testing on benchmark datasets (NSL-KDD and CICIDS2017) shows that the suggested solution has a 96.3% detection rate, which minimizes false positives and is 7.8% more effective than traditional ML-based intrusion detection systems. The framework can be easily scaled, it saves privacy, and is flexible to new zero-day threats, fitting the current SDN-based environments.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The high-speed digital transformation has brought a revolution in the manner of how organizations develop, operate, and protect their networks1. The flexibility, programmability, and centralized control of Software-Defined Networking (SDN) have proven to be a promising paradigm. SDN enables easy management of the traffic, including the allocation of resources, by decoupling the control plane and the data plane. Nevertheless, this centralized nature itself causes SDN to be an ideal scapegoat of a cyberattack2,3. Attackers frequently use the gaps in the northbound and southbound APIs, SDN controllers, and virtualized services and cause network disruption, data breach, and unauthorized access4,5.

Conventional types of cybersecurity controls, including rule-based firewalls and signature-based intrusion detection systems (IDS), no longer work well in this dynamic threat environment6,7. They are slow to identify new attacks or zero-day attacks and have a high false-positive rate and are very costly to compute. Furthermore, such systems are not flexible, because they cannot automatically develop as the attack strategies keep on changing. As a result, businesses are increasingly at risk of losses, unavailability of services, and losses in image8.

The development of artificial intelligence (AI) and, most specifically, machine learning (ML) has provided fresh possibilities to improve cybersecurity defences9,10. State-of-the-art ML models can identify anomalies, reveal latent patterns in high-dimensional network traffic, and autonomously respond to threats11,12. Among them, XGBoost and LightGBM have demonstrated impressive classification and scalability to a large amount of data. Nevertheless, the majority of the existing research uses the models individually and ignores the possibility of integrating detection accuracy and adaptive mitigation in real-time13,14.

The second burning question is that there is no collaboration between organizations in dealing with cyber threats. Enterprise reluctance to share sensitive raw data because of the privacy problem means that the existing centralized solutions would not be able to completely achieve the benefits of collective intelligence15. This restricts the area of threat detection, and it is a challenge to resist advanced attacks. Federated Learning (FL) is a potentially effective approach that enables the distributed training of models across multiple organizations without exposing them to confidential data. However, its potential to offer SDN-based cybersecurity in conjunction with sophisticated ML models has yet to be fully investigated16,17. To overcome these difficulties, we propose an innovative AI-based protection model that combines XGBoost, LightGBM, and Federated Learning into a composite framework for SDN security. Our solution is capable of providing precise identification of complex hazards, live responsive mitigation, and privacy-preserving collaboration among organizations. When these strengths are integrated, the framework will significantly enhance detection accuracy, minimize false positives, and be scalable in a dynamic network environment. Related work

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in cybersecurity has gained increasing interest because it can identify more sophisticated patterns and respond to changes in threats18. This part describes topical literature in three key categories ML-based intrusion detection systems (IDS), SDN-oriented solutions to cybersecurity, and federated learning-based collaborative security. We also emphasize the shortcomings of the previous literature and the way our framework would improve the state-of-the-art19,20.

The utilization of supervised and ensemble ML in intrusion detection has been studied in many works. The Maddireddy et al.21 development was an AI-driven early threat detection model based on anomaly detection and classification models using various datasets. On the same note, Petrovic et al.22 analysed the use of ML in the development of resilient cyberinfrastructures. Although these articles show promise of ML, they tend not to be scalable and not very adaptable to real time SDN environment. In addition, the majority use centralized data, which is restrictive to generalizability to distributed settings.

Volk23 and Yaseen24 emphasized the disruptive nature of the AI in detecting intrusions in essential infrastructures and SDN. Agarwal25 proposed real-time SDN-based DDoS detection based on ML, and Radoglou-Grammatikis et al.26 proposed SDN-reinforcement learning to industrial healthcare systems. Even though these works enhance the accuracy of detection, they are often characterized by high false positives, high latency or low adaptive response. Moreover, they fail to discuss the sharing of intelligence that any collaborative effort should take to counter the distributed attacks.

Recent literature highlights the application of Federated Learning (FL) to enable organizations to share intelligence without disclosing sensitive data. Sikandar et al.27 provided an extensive overview of FL attacks and defenses, whereas Liu et al.28 discussed privacy and security in federated execution. But the majority of FL-based cybersecurity frameworks priorities either privacy or distributed training efficiency, not incorporating more sophisticated ML models in real-time SDN-based intrusion detection.

More recent studies on intrusion detection in IoT/IIoT and cyber-physical systems have been on hybrid deep learning models and explainability to enhance accuracy and trust. Nandanwar and Katarya, in TL-BILSTM IoT29: Transfer Learning Model for Prediction of Intrusion Detection System in IoT Environment, present a hybrid CNN-BiLSTM model with transfer learning to detect botnet attacks (e.g. Mirai, BASHLITE) in a diversity of multiple IoT devices, and achieves a high accuracy, 99.5, in the NBaIoT data, and 3–16 better than earlier approaches. In the article Deep Learning Enabled Intrusion Detection System in Industrial IoT Environment30, AttackNet, an adaptive CNN-GRU model, is presented as a model applicable to detect NBaIoT botnets with the NBaIoT data, with an accuracy of approximately 99.75, precision and recall of around 99.75, and multi-class classification of ten different classes. In the meantime, An Explainable and Resilient Intrusion Detection System for Industry 5.0 is an architecture that uses BiLSTM31, BiGRU, fully connected layers and softmax classifier and augmented with SHAP explanations that are tested on the CICDDoS2019 dataset; the system demonstrates high detection performance but also gives interpretable feature-importance information to security analysts.

Proposed architecture using XGBoost and light GBM

The suggested method constructs a boosted cybersecurity structure that improves threat detection and response competencies by employing the assets of XGBoost and LightGBM. Whereas LightGBM focuses on adaptive reaction mechanisms, allowing real-time alterations to security measures contingent on identified threats, XGBoost is used for precise and effective threat detection by appraising complex patterns within large datasets. Federated learning is another feature of the system that leases several businesses’ work composed of improving their models without sacrificing data confidentiality.

This approach ensures a highly flexible, scalable, and effective defense against emerging online threats. The model training methodology for intrusion detection presents itself in Fig. 2. The NSL-KDD dataset follows normalization then moves to data cleaning before performing label encoding before being split into 80% training and 20% testing sets. The processed data receives input from two models, namely XGBoost and LightGBM to detect malicious activities through the analysis of network traffic patterns. The training process of models relies on performance evaluation metrics which measure the capability of models to detect normal traffic from anomalous traffic.

Machine learning workflow for intrusion detection.

We came up with a hybrid decision pipeline that would utilize XGBoost and LightGBM complementary to each other. XGBoost is utilized as the main detection engine, taking advantage of its gradient-boosted ensemble learning to provide a high quality of accurate detection of anomalous and malicious traffic patterns. When anomalies are identified, the outputs are adaptively redirected into LightGBM-driven mitigation phase that takes advantage of the leaf-wise splitting approach as well as histogram-based feature bundling to offer low-latency mitigation responses. This combination guarantees that the framework can be both highly responsive and reactive to mitigation in real-time and has a high rate of detection, which is impossible to ensure with single-model approaches.

AI-driven threat detection with XGBoost

The proposed system utilizes XGBoost to achieve precise and scalable threat identification in the network. The Network Traffic Data collection system gathers data from multiple locations inside the Software Defined Networking (SDN) architecture. The raw traffic data undergoes feature extraction, resulting in the generation of high-dimensional input vectors that are subsequently integrated into the XGBoost model. By employing gradient-boosted decision trees, XGBoost analyzes this information and identifies patterns of dangerous activity, making it very efficient for classification tasks such as detecting anomalies or potential cyber threats. By its special optimization for large-scale data with high dimensionality, XGBoost enables real-time processing and continuous learning from streaming network traffic. This incorporation guarantees the detection of emerging attack routes with low delay and exceptional accuracy. The XGBoost algorithm operates by iteratively enhancing the predictive accuracy of an ensemble of decision trees by applying Gradient Boosting.

Adaptive threat response approach with LightGBM

The LightGBM model works in a way that categories decision trees working sequentially, trying to minimize the errors of prior decision trees. In particular, the model incorporates gradient boosting, which allows for maximizing performance and contributes to real-time decision-making. For adaptive threat response, the system takes a real-time stream of network traffic data, and using the LightGBM tool’s feature importance feature, it is possible to observe various important features that are representative of emergent threats on the network. The model is fine-tuned through such factors as the learning rate, the decision tree depth, and the number of records allowed at each leaf node. A robust decision tree is created and updated for each category of detected anomalies, incorporating feedback; it remains sensitive to new, contradicting patterns to provide dynamic protection against emerging attack vectors. This enables the system to propose effective and accurate mitigation measures tailored to the identified threat profiles. Enhancing collaboration through federated learning

With Federated Learning, participants with distributed nodes can cooperate in sharing intelligence without having to transmit local data that is considered private. Every node performs computations on the data set available locally to optimize model parameters. It sends this information to a central node through secure channels, which could be Federated Averaging or Differential Privacy. This structure of the architecture guarantees privacy and adhesion to the rules of the data as well as allowing the nodes to participate in the global threat intelligence sharing system. The system successively recognizes several attack types using numerous datasets from various nodes. Regularly solidifying the model enhances the system’s efficiency, and adaptive optimization algorithms mitigate potential issues such as communication delays among nodes or model divergence, thereby making the method more reliable. Figure 5 represents the working process of the Federated Learning part in the suggested cybersecurity architecture.

Enhancing collaboration through federated learning.

The first step consists of local training of nodes in which each node employing SDN creates a local model using a local dataset. Notably, model updates are computed locally at the nodes and therefore, gaining raw data access does not occur, so privacy of data is maintained. After local models are trained, nodes send encrypted updates (e.g., gradients or weights) to a central server through privacy-preserving mechanisms like Federated Averaging or Differential Privacy. The updates played at all nodes are aggregated by a central node to create a strengthened version of the global model and the information is transferred to all nodes. This revised model of the world enables the system to become aware of emerging attack types in unison, thanks to its ability to learn from various distributed environments. Lastly, it optimizes the global model with adaptive optimization algorithms to hone and fine-tune the model at a faster rate, with minimal communication burden. It improves with time on the accuracy of detections. Such a configuration will allow sharing of intelligence in a collaborative and privacy-constrained manner.

Real-time threat detection outputs and the corresponding mitigation responses generated are checked for effectiveness and correctness. False positives and negatives are recorded in the system feedback database and used to update the models periodically so that they may incorporate new attack patterns. Anomaly detection mechanisms detect any deviations from a norm and then require further tuning of the model. Sophisticated monitoring utilities monitor system indices including detection latency, resource usage, and others, for proper functioning. This cycle, coupled with feedback from actual processes running in the environment, improves the model’s forecasts and optimizes the system’s core functioning for the future. AI-driven cybersecurity framework architecture

The introduced conceptual AI-based cybersecurity system is designed to address the increasing complexity and effectiveness of cyber threats by leveraging advanced machine learning, distributed computing, and adaptive response strategies. The architecture combines three major parts: XGBoost for threat identification, LightGBM for adaptive threat handling, and federated learning for intelligence sharing, and improves the system’s precision, speed, and capacity. The logistics of the work starts with the acquisition of raw traffic data from network devices for feature extraction. These are then transformed into machine-learning-ready structured datasets where needed. These features are fed into the XGBoost model, and they learn to detect patterns suggestive of threats. Its decision trees are boosted with a gradient that allows for accurate identification due to the flexibility it has in dealing with non-linear relationships between features together with handling imbalanced datasets. If a threat source is detected, the LightGBM module is invoked for the following action of adaptive threat response.

Based on the leaf-wise growth strategy, LightGBM quickly assesses the severity and type of the identified threat and provides the possibility of real-time repositioning. This enables the system to assign correct resources and also initiate pre-programmed response measures or promote the escalation based on threats’ density. The flexibility of this component enables high system performance, little time loss, and few false alarms, thereby making the system more efficient in practical applications. Collaborative intelligence is built upon federated learning, which can enable distributed nodes or organizations to contribute to and gain from a shared threat detection and response model. This method of training can be safely performed between participants without sharing raw data, in contrast to traditional approaches, such as centralized ones, which would lead to violations of privacy and data protection legislation. Periodically, the shared model is revised based on aggregated gradients from all the nodes to ensure the system adapts to new attacks while retaining data ownership. This system uses architecture with a feedback loop where the system can be refined and monitored. Accompanying loops monitor the performance of such programs in real-time and from historical data, including detection rate and speed as well as false positives.

These are used to perform incremental re-estimation of the models and improve the system response to new threats while offering the best performance ever. The use of a common operational picture enables administrators to access real-time data on the system’s performance through a monitoring dashboard, allowing them to make informed decisions about the system’s responses to emerging situations. The interacting components of the system are linked by a strong messaging and orchestration layer that guarantees consistency in the distribution of resulting data and decisions across the modules. The architecture is designed to be extendible and adaptive, allowing for the integration of AI and other threat detection models as the system develops and the threats become more diverse. In total, this project proposes a multi-level, AI-based approach for defense through adaptability, a collective strategy of networked actors, and persistent evolution, thus providing an efficient and integrated, tactical, and dynamic solution to contemporary digital threats.

AI-driven cybersecurity framework architecture.

In a bid to uphold secure yet privacy-respecting collaborative learning, the framework has been enhanced to embrace federated learning, whereby models are trained through local nodes using privately owned data and only gradients or model variables are shared in an encrypted fashion. It is combined centrally over secure proofs such as FedAvg or differential privacy so that a global model can be updated and pushed back to the nodes without sharing raw data. Additionally, the architecture features an ongoing monitoring and feedback module. This module will have a performance dashboard, a false positives tracker and model re-trainer whereby over a period of time the detection and response engines will change as per what is happening in real time and in the history logs. This is a feedback mechanism that ensures there are low false positives and high accuracy of detecting false positives.

Flow of data and interactions

The proposed architecture’s data flow should facilitate the effective integration of key elements, including XGBoost, LightGBM, and Federated Learning, while addressing efficiency, security, and scalability issues. At this level, the raw network traffic data is harvested from different sources distributed over the network including firewalls, IDS and endpoint computers. This data is normalized, filtered, cleansed, and transformed from raw data format into proper format which is acceptable by the machine learning model. The preprocessed data is first input to XGBoost model, such that highly accurate threat is detected based on traffic behavior analysis and abnormal/malicious activities. The decision trees inside XGBoost are gradient boosted to manage big data characterized by high dimensionality without compromising time as it seeks to identify threats. Metadata comprising threat type, threat severity, and confidence score is then passed to the next phase for response formulation. The LightGBM module working with the metadata, initiates the response strategy decision-making.

With the help of its leaf-wise splitting tree algorithm, LightGBM assesses other contextual facts about a detected threat, including its potential impact and the state of the system at the time of intrusion, and assigns and performs corresponding counteractions. This may involve removing affected nodes off the network, or shutting out any traffic that is deemed forbidden, or merely informing the administrators. The idea of LightGBM is therefore to adapt as much as possible the answer of the system to the threats while remaining as light as possible in terms of the consumption of the resources and reply time. For shared threat intelligence in collaboration, federated learning is used to transfer the update of the models securely from one node to another. Nodes participate and learn local models on their data and contribute only gradients or weights to a central aggregator. The updated models are in turn again nudged back to the nodes while the global model of several nodes and the nodal sub-models get the accumulated learning without leakage of the identity of individuals or any violation of privacy laws.

It means that this distributed learning mechanism allows architecture to be flexible to the new threats in the various territories. It constantly updates itself with an output of threats detected and the processes that the system has followed in response to threats. Finally, this real-time feedback information is used together with past information to fine-tune these models step by step. Even the interactions must be optimized, which is why all key parameters like detection rate, false alarms, and response time are being accompanied. The device management layer controls data exchange and coordinates work between the components, shields data confidentiality, and increases the system’s stability. This clear stream of data and conversation makes it possible to continue to be the architecture active and flexible to meet new security threats and implement advanced solutions to them.

Figure 8 shows the general structure of the proposed AI-based cybersecurity system that is composed of data- driven intelligence, distributed learning, and auto mitigation. The architecture is initiated by gathering raw data of the network traffic by SDN-based network traffic collectors. To make this data machine learning ready, they go through a preprocessing pipeline which involves normalization, encoding, and data splitting. These pre-processed characteristics are then passed to the threat intelligence engine based on XGBoost model that, using gradient-boosted decision trees, can examine the behaviors of the traffic and detect the anomalies with a high degree of precision. After a threat has been detected, metadata including type of threat, its severity, and confidence level is created and sent on to the adaptive response component.

Flow of data and interactions.

LightGBM engine follows leaf-wise growth strategy, analyzes these metadata, and takes some proper countermeasures through the mitigation dispatcher which could be anything like alerting, blocking, or isolation of the affected components. In a bid to uphold secure yet privacy-respecting collaborative learning, the framework has been enhanced to embrace federated learning, whereby models are trained through local nodes using privately owned data and only gradients or model variables are shared in an encrypted fashion. It is combined centrally over secure proofs such as FedAvg or differential privacy so that a global model can be updated and pushed back to the nodes without sharing raw data. Also, the architecture has ongoing monitoring and feedback modules. This module will have a performance dashboard, false positives tracker and model re-trainer whereby over a period of time the detection and response engines will change as per what is happening in real time and in the history logs. This is a feedback mechanism that ensures there are low false positives and high accuracy of detecting false positives.

Statistical validation

The strength and consistency of the findings in the experiment is paramount in the field of cybersecurity research, especially in the assessment of the intrusion detection frameworks of Software-Defined Networking (SDN). We have taken a strict statistical validation protocol Fig. 10 to reduce the impact of randomness like partitioning of the dataset, initializing the model and using stochastic optimization. Experiments were conducted through stratified 5-fold nested cross-validation, where the outer loop approximated the generalization capability of the models and the inner loop was the one that optimized the hyperparameters. This design is able to prevent information leakage and the result of selecting the models to the final evaluation is not biassed.

Cross-validation, repeated runs, confidence intervals and hypothesis testing of results in robust and reproducible form.

The whole nested cross-validation process was repeated again five times using other random seeds, giving 25 independent evaluations per model. In the performance measures we took the Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score and AUC after every run. The results are provided in the form of mean, standard deviation, which also gives some measure of central tendency and variability of the results between the folds and repetitions. To measure the uncertainty of these estimates, we have also calculated 95% confidence intervals.

In addition to descriptive statistics, we used statistical hypothesis testing to determine whether the differences observed between our framework and baseline models were statistically significant. To make comparisons of two variables, we employed paired t-tests when the assumptions of normality had been met and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests when they had not. Comparison of two or more classifiers was done using Friedman test, then Nemenyi post-hoc test with HolmBonferonni correction as to control multi comparisons. The effects sizes, including the Cohen d, were used to supplement the p-values and to present the magnitude of the improvement as well as offer meaningful significance beyond the statistical significance levels.

Federal learning In federated learning, further validation strategies were proposed. We measured performance by the client node and summed up performance by node to determine both local and global performance. Additionally, we did leave-one-client-out validation, in which all but one client were used to train and the left-out client was used to test to determine cross-client generalization. This method demonstrates the framework’s strength in more heterogeneous, distributed settings where the data distributions are not uniform among participants. By means of this overall validation plan we made sure that the improvements in detection accuracy, latency and scalability which we were reporting were not by mere chance, but by a systematic, repeatable, statistically significant improvement.

Latency and real-time response

Latency is considered to be one of the most significant performance metrics in SDN environments since a delay on the detection and mitigation may lead to extensive network disruption. A proper cybersecurity structure should consequently act within the almost real time to maintain continuity of services and avoid cascading failures. Our suggested framework has a latency averaging between 821 ms, which is significantly better than conventional solutions. Conversely, Petrovic et al.22 recorded latency as high as 45 ms whereas Volk23 and Agarwal25 also recorded higher delays of 38 ms and 32 ms respectively. Our system has been improved due to its modular pipeline design: detection is performed with XGBoost to recognize anomalies quickly, and mitigation is performed in parallel with LightGBM. Such asynchronous decoupling enables classification and response in parallel without a bottleneck system. Our framework has low latency and as such, attack signatures can be detected nearly in real time and adaptive mitigation measures (e.g., quarantining malicious nodes or blocking suspicious flows) can be implemented immediately. This responsiveness is necessary to counter fast attacks, such as DDoS floods or zero-day exploits, where milliseconds can be the difference between a network holding its own against an attack or suffering a complete collapse.

Moreover, the federated learning aspect of our system will help reduce the latency indirectly since it lowers raw data transfer among nodes. Rather than relaying complete datasets to a central server, the model updates are only shared and lowers the communication overhead and system responsiveness in distributed SDN deployments.

Network-level performance metrics

In addition to the detection accuracy and scalability, network level performance is also important when looking at the practicality of an SDN security framework. An intrusion detection and mitigation system must not only spot attacks but also maintain QoS (quality of service) on the entire network. We analyzed four important metrics that are bandwidth usage, packet loss, processing delay, and propagation / queuing delays.

Our framework consistently achieved bandwidth efficiency of over 95% even in heavy attack scenarios. This efficiency stems largely from using federated learning updates instead of sharing raw data. By using only model gradients to communicate between nodes and controllers, the amount of communication overhead was reduced significantly. In contrast, Liu et al.28 reported bandwidth utilization becomes 82% under DDoS conditions due to excessive raw data transmission. This shows the capability of our model to achieve stable performance without overloading the control channel.

Packet loss is a direct measure of the network’s stability under stress. Our model contained losses below 4.7%, even when the number of SDN nodes was as high as 100. This is a vast improvement from Liu et al.28, in which losses were greater than 8.2% under DDoS attacks. The reduction is the result of LightGBM’s real-time adaptive response, which quickly prevents malicious flows from causing congestion.

The delay of the system’s processing process was between 10 and 17 ms, which could ensure timely response without bottlenecks. This efficiency can be attributed to LightGBM’s leaf-wise tree building and histogram-based feature optimization, which speed up classification problems in high-dimensional datasets. By contrast, Liu et al.28 reported processing delays averaging 27 ms, limiting their framework’s real-time applicability.

Dynamic rerouting strategies with the help of the SDN controller reduced propagation and queuing delays. By using the parallel classification feature of XGBoost, the malicious traffic for the system came to be detected at an early stage, enabling the system to prioritise the legitimate traffic. The result was an average combined propagation and queuing delay of 12 ms, much lower than Liu et al.28, where it frequently exceeded 25 ms because of inefficient traffic prioritization.

Comparative discussion

The comparative analysis of existing approaches to SDN cybersecurity reveals that, although significant progress has been made, each category of solution has inherent limitations that diminish its practical applicability in large-scale, real-time network environments.

Traditional machine learning (ML) methods, such as the one used by Agarwal25, exhibit moderate accuracy in detecting the presence of this disease ( 93%) and the implementation complexity is relatively low. They are nice because they are easy to deploy and easy to interpret. But their dependency on static patterns often causes them to have a higher false positive and an inability to adapt quickly to zero day or evolving attacks. Moreover, their response times are slower, as most models need to be retrained entirely to include new attack data, making them less suitable for dynamic SDN environments, where it is more important to have the closed loop work in real time.

Deep learning-based approaches (e.g. Zhang et al.32) push detection accuracy above 95% by the convolutional or recurrent architecture of neural networks. Their capacity to capture the nonlinear patterns and high-dimensional traffic data makes them very useful in identifying sophisticated types of attacks. However, there are two significant shortcomings to these methods. First of all, they are very computationally intensive and often require GPUs or even specialized hardware which is not always possible in latency-sensitive SDN control planes. Second, they are designed in a generalized security context, that is, they do not directly optimize for unique characteristics of SDN architectures, such as centralized controllers and dynamic routing policies. Thus, while very good in theory, deep learning methods do have practical deployed challenges in real-world SDN-based networks.

Federated learning (FL) approaches (e.g., Liu et al.28, Rahman et al.34) are an important step towards privacy- preserving, collaborative security. By allowing distributed training without sharing raw datasets, FL can help us to overcome data governance issues in multiple organizations as shown in Table 4. These studies result in highlighting the importance of cross-domain learning where models get a boost from diverse data sources without violating privacy. However, most of the FL-based solutions are built as proof-of-concepts and not coupled with adaptive real-time response mechanisms. While they are better at privacy and mitigating centralization risks, they will not usually have the ability to respond instantaneously to threats, as SDN controllers would for high-speed attacks, like DDoS floods.

Ablation study

To quantify the contribution of each of the major components in the proposed architecture in more detail, we did a systematic ablation study between four configurations: (1) XGBoost only, (2) LightGBM only, (3) combined XGBoost + LightGBM pipeline trained centrally (Federated Learning disabled), and (4) full proposed framework (XGBoost + LightGBM + Federated Learning). The results of all experiments were obtained using the same preprocessing pipeline and evaluation protocol (stratified 5-fold nested cross-validation, repeated 5 times with different random seeds). For each, we measured the detection metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score and AUC), the false positive rate (FPR) and the average end-to-end detection to mitigation latency. Reported values mean +- standard deviation of all the cross-validation runs.

The Table 5 verifies a number of important conclusions regarding the contribution of each module to the overall system. First, XGBoost alone provides a strong detection performance (Accuracy approx. 94.5%) and a relatively high AUC (approx. 0.958), that proves that it is an effective model to handle complex, high-dimensional attack signatures. However, the XGBoost-only setup exhibits a higher false positive rate and more end-to-end latency compared with the setup with the complete pipeline, suggesting that in order to ensure operational efficiency and a low alarm volume in SDN scenarios, a highly accurate detector is not enough.

Second, LightGBM alone gives slightly lower detection measures than XGBoost in our experiments (Accuracy approx. 93.8%), however, it has the lowest latency (approx. 11 ms). This reflects the engineering benefits of LightGBM in terms of the leaf-wise growth and histogram-based splitting which makes it suitable for low-latency inference on streaming flows. The tradeoff is a slightly increased false positive rate, which is not desirable in isolation as too many false alarms are a burden to network operators and can cause them to take unnecessary mitigation actions.

Third, an ensemble of XGBoost and LightGBM in a centralized (non-federated) pipeline is also a meaningful boost over the single model baselines: Accuracy is increased by approximately 95.2%, FPR is decreased by approximately 4.8%, and AUC is increased by approximately 0.972. These results indicate that a staged architecture, which combines a fine-grained detector (XGBoost) that identifies suspicious flows with a fast and adaptive model (LightGBM) to execute the mitigation process, captures the complementary strengths as it provides less false alarms while still being responsive. Finally, when Federated Learning is enabled on top of the hybrid pipeline, the best outcomes are achieved on all the dimensions reported. The complete framework provides about 1.1% point accuracy improvement with respect to the centrally trained hybrid system (and about 1.8–2.5 points of accuracy improvement with respect to single model baselines), a reduced false positive rate of approximately 3.7%, and the best AUC (approximately 0.982). The federated aggregation helps in this generalization, where diverse local patterns from diverse nodes are integrated without sharing of raw traffic, which makes the model less overfitted to the distribution of any individual node, and is responsible for the measured increase in detection performance and a reduction in false alarms. Moreover, the communication efficient FL setup keeps the average latency low (around 10 ms) because only model updates (rather than raw logs that are bulk) are communicated and because the mitigation is done locally with LightGBM once a suspicious flow is detected.

To help support the statistical robustness of these observations, we used paired hypothesis testing on the per fold results. Paired t-tests (where normality is verified via Shapiro-Wilk) and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests where appropriate are used to determine that the increase of the full framework over both single model baselines as well as the non-federated hybrid are statistically significant (p < 0.05). Effect-size measurements (Cohen’s d) for the differences between the full framework and the ablated frameworks indicate that there are medium to large practical differences between the full framework and ablated variants. These results taken together show that all of these components contribute meaningfully, in that XGBoost provides discriminative power; LightGBM provides real-time mitigation capability; and Federated Learning provides better generalization and privacy - only the integrated system achieves the combination of high accuracy, low false-positive, low latency, and scalability that SDN deployments need.

Discussion

The results of this paper prove that the suggested XGBoost, LightGBM, Federated Learning structure offers valuable development in terms of protecting SDN infrastructures against contemporary cyber threats. The combination of the complementary abilities of these three elements in the framework provides the best detection accuracy, fewer false positives, and real-time flexibility. The closeness of accurate classification (96.3) with a high degree of precision (95.8) and recall (96.6) underscores that it can classify the benign and malicious traffic reliably. This balance allows blocking of legitimate traffic to be avoided without blocking malicious flows being blocked as quickly as possible which is important in latency sensitive SDN settings.

Among the most remarkable findings of the present study is the decrease in latency as compared to current practices. The average response time of 821 ms of the system is far superior to baseline solutions including Petrovic et al.22 with a maximum delay of 45 ms. The latter enhancement can be explained by the fact that the pipeline is designed as a modular scheme, with XGBoost quickly identifying a problem, and LightGBM performing mitigation simultaneously. This separation of both detection and response will get rid of bottlenecks that are prevalent in monolithic systems. The minimized delay can be of special use in reducing in real-time, high-speed attacks like the distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) flood, where even a single millisecond of latency can increase the intensity of an attack on the network.

The proposed framework has another strength, which is scalability. Up to 100 SDN nodes were experimented with showed a only slight increase in the time of computation, 40 s at 10 nodes, and 120 s at 100 nodes. As a contrast, other competing systems like those by Volk23 and Petrovic et al.22 showed sharp rises in processing time with larger deployments. This is due to federated learning aggregation that restricts the transmission of raw data but instead exchanges minimal model updates that greatly minimize the communication overhead. This aspect does not only guarantee scalability in large-scale deployments (enterprise or ISP networks) but also fosters collaboration across the organizations and at the same time maintains high levels of sensitivity in data.

In addition to improving performance, the framework has a significant privacy-saving benefit of federated learning. In a real world situation, organizations do not willingly publish raw network data because of confidentiality or legal regulations. Our model enables unrestricted model training by involving no central data pooling, thereby striking a balance between collaboration and adherence to privacy policies, such as GDPR and HIPAA. In comparison to.

Rahman et al.34 and Liu et al.28, which focused on federated learning in the cloud setting, and did not offer real-time responsiveness, our system has been able to combine privacy preservation with low-latency and adaptive defence which is a critical gap in the literature.

Comparing it to the deep learning methods like Zhang et al. Our model has a better balance in trade-off compared to32, that made high accuracy but with the price of computational overhead and SDN-specific optimization. The gradient boosting techniques also consume less computation power compared to the deep neural networks, yet they have similar accuracies. This efficiency allows the proposed system to be more widely deployed in resource-constrained settings, including edge nodes or IoT-integrated SDNs, in which deep learning approaches can be unfeasible because of the large amounts of hardware (GPU) needed.

The other important observation is that the framework is able to sustain network-level quality of service (QoS) even during an attack. The loss rate of packets is never higher than 4.7% and the bandwidth is used at a minimum of 95%, which means that the essential services are not disrupted in even the situation of the active attack. This robustness is the direct product of dynamic rerouting features of the real-time mitigation pipeline and SDN controller. Conversely, the authors28 found that the loss of packets was more than 8% in the similar conditions, which highlights the significance of combining adaptive ML-based mitigation with federated updates.

The necessity to incorporate all three elements of the framework is also proved in the ablation study. Although XGBoost does not offer real-time flexibility, it offers high detection accuracy. LightGBM is the only one that provides low latency but not precision. Likewise, with federated learning disabled, there are poorer generalization and collaborative resilience. The overall integrated model was always more effective than these ablated versions on all the metrics used in the evaluation, which validates the fact that detection, mitigation, and federated collaboration synergy are critical to strong SDN security.

Regardless of these strengths, the study also presents a number of limitations that can be filled in through improved research. To start with, despite the high accuracy of the model on benchmark machines (NSL-KDD, CICIDS2017, and more recent benchmarks such as Edge-IIoTset and CIC IoT-DIAD 2024), its effectiveness in real-world production SDN systems is yet to be tested on a grand scale. The traffic that exists can be noisy, incomplete and dynamic attack patterns, which can affect performance. Second, the reliance of the system on hyperparameter tuning implies that achieving the best results implies that the system should be carefully configured, which is not always possible with non-expert users. Lastly, although federated learning minimizes communication overhead, it also creates synchronization issues in the case of scaling to hundreds of nodes and future work should optimize it using asynchronous or hierarchical aggregation schemes.

Overall, the discussion demonstrates that the framework in question effectively addresses significant gaps in current SDN cybersecurity platforms. It is accurate, scalable and flexible and also preserves privacy in a manner that has never been done simultaneously in previous work. Although deployment and optimization issues are still present, the system provides a solid foundation for future research in federated, AI-driven, real-time cybersecurity solutions, specifically tailored to SDN and beyond.

Conclusion

This study introduces a new approach to incorporating Artificial Intelligence (AI)-based Cybersecurity in Software- Defined Networking (SDN), designed to utilize XGBoost, LightGBM, and Federated Learning to achieve accurate threat detection, adaptive real-time response, and privacy-preserving collaboration. By utilizing gradient boosted models and federated intelligence sharing, the framework addresses critical challenges in SDN security, including high rates of false positives, latency, and scalability issues. Experimental validation was carried out on benchmark datasets and proved to be better with accuracy averaging 96.3%, false alarms being reduced and resilience in large scale, distributed environment increasing. Compared to traditional ML, deep learning, and existing FL-based solutions, the proposed framework is a balanced solution in terms of detection accuracy, efficiency, scalability and data privacy. It can be a practical solution for modern SDN deployments. The combination of algorithmic details and complexity analysis together with ablation studies further contributes towards increasing the transparency and reproducibility of the research.

In summary, this research defines a scalable, adaptive and privacy-preserving defense framework for SDN that is better than the current methods. By rectifying its shortcomings in future versions, the framework has the potential to become a holistic solution that can secure programmable networks of the future against increasingly sophisticated cyber threats.
