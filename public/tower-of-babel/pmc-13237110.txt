Experimental evaluation of sustainable jute–basalt hybrid FRP systems for flexural strengthening of RC beams with variable wrapping and light-weight aggregate replacement
Authors: Panumas Saingam, Muhammad Adnan Hanif, Fahad Ahmed, Burachat Chatveera, Gritsada Sua-iam, Ali Ejaz, Qudeer Hussain, Preeda Chaimahawan, Chisanuphong Suthumma, Afaq Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research experimentally assesses the flexural strengthening of reinforced concrete (RC) beams through the use of sustainable jute–basalt (JB) hybrid fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) systems with variable wrapping schemes and lightweight aggregate (LWA) replacement. A total of 18 beams with identical geometry and reinforcement were tested under four-point bending, including controls, basalt FRP (BFRP), jute FRP (JFRP), and hybrid JB FRP systems in bottom-only, U-wrap, and full-wrap configurations. The results indicate that FRP confinement significantly modified failure modes, transitioning from the brittle crushing in controls to rupture- or debonding-controlled mechanisms. BFRP significantly enhanced strength, achieving up to 36.8% greater capacity in full-wrap beams, while JFRP improved ductility but was more prone to premature debonding. Hybrid JB FRP systems demonstrated the most balanced performance, with U-wrap hybrids achieving 39.6% higher load capacity and maintaining significant deformation capacity even in LWA concrete. Load–strain responses confirmed yielding of steel reinforcement in all cases, though maximum strains were reduced after confinement due to premature fiber debonding or rupture at smaller deflections. The use of LWA reduced ductility of control beams, but hybrid U-wrap systems successfully compensated for this limitation, providing the highest load and deflection values among all specimens. These results emphasize the potential of hybrid natural–mineral FRP systems as sustainable alternatives to synthetic composites, providing competitive ductility and reinforcement for structural retrofitting applications in both conventional and lightweight concretes.

Introduction

Reinforced concrete (RC) infrastructure is increasingly required to meet higher performance demands under aging, deterioration, and service-condition changes, while society simultaneously expects lower environmental burdens from retrofit solutions1,2. Externally bonded fiber‑reinforced polymer (FRP) systems have matured into a practical alternative to steel plate bonding due to their higher specific strength and stiffness, superior durability, and ease of installation3–6. Decades of laboratory and field investigations have established the effectiveness of carbon‑ and glass‑FRP systems for flexural and shear upgrading of beams using plate and sheet configurations, near‑surface mounted (NSM) reinforcement, and hybrid schemes7–10. Nevertheless, widespread deployment is moderated by cost, energy‑intensive manufacturing, and end‑of‑life considerations, which motivate the search for lower‑impact materials without sacrificing structural performance11,12. Natural‑fiber and mineral‑based FRPs have emerged as credible candidates to reduce embodied impacts while retaining many of the mechanical and durability benefits of synthetic FRPs13. Among natural fibers, jute is attractive because of its low cost, availability, and promising tensile properties when appropriately treated and embedded in polymer matrices14. Basalt fibers, derived from melt‑spun from igneous rock, offer a mineral alternative with good tensile strength, thermal stability, and chemical resistance. These fibers have been widely studied for strengthening RC members and often exhibit performance comparable to glass‑FRP in aggressive environments15,16. Reviews and component‑level studies indicate that both jute‑FRP (JFRP) and basalt‑FRP (BFRP) can contribute to sustainable retrofit strategies, though each material presents distinct advantages: jute tends to enhance ductility and energy dissipation, whereas BFRP typically provides higher strength retention and environmental durability17,18.

In addition to material selection, the configuration and anchorage of externally bonded reinforcement govern the degree to which the composite action is mobilized and premature debonding is suppressed. Classical works on debonding mechanics, corner radii, and end‑anchorage effects demonstrated that bottom‑only laminates are cost‑effective but prone to intermediate crack‑induced debonding, while U‑wraps and proper anchorage increase reliability and usable strain19–21. NSM reinforcement and mechanical/bi‑directional anchor solutions have been proposed to delay or suppress critical failure modes and improve serviceability22,23. Recent studies report that U‑wraps, spike anchors, and hybrid anchorage schemes markedly improve flexural capacity, ductility, and deformation compatibility in FRP‑strengthened beams19,22. Contemporary literature includes several strands directly relevant to sustainable strengthening. Comprehensive state‑of‑the‑art reviews summarize construction applications of FRPs, durability pathways, and design considerations24–26. Experimental programs have examined flexural strengthening with various FRP systems and anchor details27 repair of damaged beams using steel‑reinforced polymer (SRP) composites23; performance of non‑prismatic members18; and hybrid or alternative materials including aluminum plates and low‑cost glass‑fiber wraps for deep beams20,28. Parallel sustainability and circular‑economy literature emphasizes minimizing embodied impacts and valorizing waste streams in civil infrastructure29,30. Sustainable concrete production increasingly incorporates recycled and waste-derived aggregates to reduce environmental footprint and resource consumption31. Natural aggregates from construction debris, industrial waste, and agricultural residues can partially replace conventional aggregates while maintaining structural performance32. However, the interaction between sustainable aggregate replacement and FRP strengthening effectiveness remains under-explored, presenting opportunities to optimize both mixture-level and member-level sustainability33. Together, these lines of research set the stage for natural‑fiber/mineral‑fiber hybrids that can leverage complementary behaviors while advancing sustainability objectives. Hybridization is a proven route to tailor stiffness, strength, and post‑yield behavior by combining constituent fibers with different failure strains and environmental responses34–36. For RC beams, strategic hybridization of a high‑strength mineral fiber (basalt) with a lower‑modulus natural fiber (jute) can potentially (I) raise ultimate capacity through the BFRP component, (ii) increase deformation capacity and energy absorption via the jute component, and (iii) improve environmental credentials relative to purely synthetic systems. Despite this promise, to the author’s knowledge, the literature still lacks systematic experimental evidence for jute–basalt hybrid (JB) FRP systems in flexural strengthening under controlled variations of wrapping configuration and substrate composition. Existing experimental works on anchors and U‑wraps focus predominantly on CFRP systems19,27; investigations featuring natural‑fiber or mineral‑fiber hybrids remain comparatively sparse and often address only a single configuration or member type18,20. Moreover, while anchorage is known to be decisive for exploiting FRP strain capacity37,38, there is limited guidance on how anchor‑enhanced U‑wraps interact with hybrid natural/mineral FRPs. To address these gaps, the present study provides an experimental evaluation of sustainable hybrid JB FRP systems for flexural strengthening of RC beams tested under four‑point bending. Beams share a common geometry and reinforcement layout, while the strengthening variables include fiber system (jute, basalt, and jute–basalt hybrid), wrapping scheme (bottom, U‑wrap, U‑wrap with anchors), and aggregate replacement level to reflect sustainability‑oriented concrete mixtures. The program is designed to quantify changes in load capacity, stiffness, ductility, energy dissipation, and failure modes, and to interrogate how anchorage mediates the mobilization of hybrid FRP strength. By positioning hybrid JB FRP systems against their single‑fiber counterparts, the results speak directly to material selection for sustainable retrofit. By comparing bottom, U‑wrap, and U‑wrap with full wrap strip schemes, the study clarifies configuration‑specific mechanisms and provides evidence to inform detailing recommendations. By embedding the tests in concretes with partial natural‑aggregate replacement, the work links member‑level strengthening performance to mixture‑level sustainability choices, an intersection that remains under‑explored in the FRP retrofit literature39,40.

Despite significant research on FRP-strengthened RC beams, most previous studies have focused on single-fiber systems and conventional concrete. Limited attention has been given to hybrid FRP systems that combine natural and mineral fibers, particularly in conjunction with sustainable concrete materials. In this context, the present study investigates the structural performance of RC beams strengthened using a hybrid jute–basalt FRP system with different wrapping configurations. The use of jute fibers offers a sustainable and environmentally friendly alternative to synthetic fibers, while basalt fibers provide enhanced mechanical strength and durability. By integrating these materials, the study aims to develop a hybrid strengthening approach that balances sustainability and structural efficiency. Furthermore, the effectiveness of these strengthening schemes is evaluated for beams cast with lightweight aggregate concrete, thereby addressing an important research gap related to the strengthening of sustainable concrete systems. The outcomes of this study provide new experimental insights into the combined effects of hybrid FRP reinforcement, wrap configurations, and sustainable concrete materials on the shear performance and ductility of RC beams.

Experimental program

Materials

The experimental program was designed to evaluate the flexural strengthening efficiency of jute, basalt, and hybrid JB FRP systems applied to RC beams incorporating different levels of recycled aggregate. The selection of materials followed standard practices to ensure reproducibility and comparability with related studies (Fig. 1). Ordinary Portland cement conforming to ASTM C15041 Type I was used, with a Blaine fineness of 330 m2/kg and specific gravity of 3.14. Natural river sand served as fine aggregate, meeting ASTM C3342 grading requirements, with a fineness modulus of 2.6, specific gravity of 2.68, and water absorption of 1.2%. Natural coarse aggregates (NCA) consisted of crushed natural stone with a maximum nominal size of 20 mm, specific gravity of 2.65, and water absorption of 0.8%. Standard tests were performed to confirm compliance with ASTM C12743 and C12844 for density and absorption, and ASTM C13145 for abrasion resistance as shown in Table 1. Lightweight aggregates (LWA) were sourced from local suppliers and comprised naturally occurring pumice stone aggregates with characteristic porous structure and light gray coloration. The pumice aggregates were mechanically processed and graded to achieve the same nominal size distribution as the natural coarse aggregates, with particle sizes ranging from 4.75 mm to 19 mm to maintain consistency in concrete mix design. The vesicular nature of the pumice particles, evident from their highly porous surface texture, contributes to their lightweight properties and enhances thermal insulation characteristics. The LWA were comprehensively characterized for key physical and mechanical properties including specific gravity, water absorption capacity, crushing strength, and impact value as shown in Table 1. The irregular angular shape and rough surface texture of the pumice particles, as observed in the sample, provide enhanced mechanical interlocking with the cement paste matrix. Crushing strength testing demonstrated adequate mechanical performance for structural lightweight concrete applications. Gradation analysis confirmed compliance with ASTM C33046 limits for lightweight aggregates. Two replacement levels were incorporated in the experimental program: 0% (control mixture with NCA only) and 15% (mixture with partial LWA replacement by volume). In this research, a 15% replacement level of lightweight aggregate (LWA) was selected based on existing literature that explores various percentages, including 10%, 20%, 25%, 50%, and 100%, primarily due to cost considerations and the availability of lightweight aggregates47,48. Additionally, utilizing 15% enabled a manageable testing framework, given the large size of the beams (1.9 m) and the total number of beams (seven) allocated for LWA. Three types of FRP systems were employed: JFRP, BFRP, and hybrid JB FRP. Jute fibres were supplied in plain woven form, treated with a 5% NaOH solution to enhance bonding. The jute fabric had an areal density of 250 g/m², nominal thickness of 0.35 mm per layer, tensile strength of 420 MPa, and elastic modulus of 12 GPa. Basalt fibers were provided as bidirectional woven fabric with an areal density of 300 g/m², thickness of 0.40 mm per layer, tensile strength of 1200 MPa, and modulus of 50 GPa. The hybrid system was constructed by alternating jute and basalt layers, ensuring basalt layers were in direct contact with the concrete surface. A two-component epoxy adhesive was used as the bonding agent, mixed at a resin-to-hardener ratio of 2:1 by weight, with a tensile strength of 35 MPa and an elastic modulus of 3.1 GPa. Deformed steel bars of 16 mm diameter (Grade 420) were used as longitudinal tension reinforcement, while 12 mm diameter deformed bars were used in compression. Shear reinforcement consisted of plain 6 mm stirrups at 150 mm spacing. Tensile coupon tests confirmed yield strength of 445 MPa, ultimate strength of 585 MPa, and modulus of elasticity of 200 GPa.

Specimen geometry and experimental matrix

All beams were designed with cross-sectional dimensions of 150 mm × 200 mm and a clear span of 2000 mm. The effective span between supports was 1800 mm, and the beams were reinforced with two 16 mm tension bars, two 12 mm compression bars, and 6 mm stirrups spaced at 150 mm. A cover of 25 mm was constantly provided on all sides of the beam. Figure 2 shows the cross-sectional dimensions along with reinforcement details of the specimens. A systematic specimen designation protocol was implemented to uniquely identify each test specimen through a four-component nomenclature system. The first component (B0 or B15) denotes the percentage of NCA replacement with LWA, representing 0% and 15% replacement levels, respectively. The second component specifies the fibre reinforcement system employed: B for BFRP, J for JFRP, and BJ for the hybrid JB FRP system. The third component characterizes the strengthening configuration: Bt for bottom-only application, U for U-wrap configuration, and UA for U-wrap with full wrap strips. The fourth component indicates the number of FRP layers applied to the specimen. The comprehensive experimental program comprised 18 reinforced concrete beam specimens, strategically distributed across the test variables to enable systematic comparison of strengthening effectiveness. The specimen distribution included two unstrengthen control beams, six specimens strengthened with BFRP systems, six specimens with JFRP systems, and four specimens with hybrid JB FRP systems. The complete experimental matrix detailing all specimen configurations, material properties, and test parameters is presented in Table 2. In the hybrid JB-FRP strengthening configurations, the notation “3L + 2L” represents the total number of FRP layers used in the system, consisting of three layers of one fiber type and two layers of the other. In all hybrid configurations, the basalt FRP layers were placed adjacent to the concrete surface, followed by the jute FRP layers applied externally. This stacking sequence was intentionally adopted to utilize the higher tensile strength and stiffness of basalt fibers at the interface with the concrete substrate, which enhances stress transfer and improves the effectiveness of the strengthening system. The jute FRP layers were applied as the outer layers to contribute to crack bridging and deformation capacity while also improving the sustainability of the strengthening system due to the natural origin of the fibers.

FRP strengthening systems

The experimental program incorporated three distinct strengthening configurations: bottom-only, U-wrap, and U-wrap with full wrap strips, each designed to investigate different failure mechanisms and bond characteristics. The complete FRP strengthening procedure for all three configurations is illustrated in Fig. 3. In bottom-only configuration, FRP sheets were bonded exclusively to the soffit of the beams to provide additional flexural tensile resistance. This configuration represents the most economical strengthening approach but is susceptible to intermediate crack-induced debonding and end peeling failures. For the specimens strengthened with U-wrap and full wrap strip configurations, full wrap strips were provided to enhance the bond performance and prevent premature debonding of the FRP sheets. The full wrap strips were installed around full section at predetermined locations to improve the load transfer between the FRP reinforcement and the concrete substrate (Fig. 3e. This system was intended to delay debonding failure and allow the FRP reinforcement to mobilize a greater portion of its tensile capacity. In the previous studies, similar strengthening systems i.e., bottom only, U-wrap only and U wrap together with full wrap strips were also investigated49,50. All FRP installations were executed using the wet lay-up technique following established industry practices. The prepared concrete surfaces were initially primed with a thin, uniform layer of epoxy primer to enhance adhesion and prevent air entrapment at the interface. Subsequently, FRP sheets were saturated with epoxy resin and carefully positioned on the substrate. Adjacent sheets were overlapped by 150 mm in the fibre direction to ensure load transfer continuity. A grooved roller was systematically applied to consolidate the composite system, eliminate entrapped air bubbles, and achieve intimate contact between the FRP and concrete substrate. Following installation, all strengthened specimens were cured under controlled room temperature conditions (20 ± 2 °C) for a minimum of seven days prior to mechanical testing to ensure complete resin polymerization and bond development.

Concrete mix design and specimen preparation

Concrete mixes were proportioned to achieve a target compressive strength of 30 MPa at 28 days following ACI mix design standards. The water-cement ratio was maintained constant at 0.45 for all mixtures, and the detailed mix proportions for both 0% and 15% replacement levels are presented in Table 3. In the LWA mixtures, 15% of the NCA was replaced with pumice stone LWA by volume. The concrete constituents, comprising cement, fine aggregates, natural and lightweight aggregates, and water, were mixed using a mechanical concrete mixer to ensure uniform consistency and homogeneous distribution. Fresh concrete was placed into cylindrical and beam moulds in successive layers, with each layer being thoroughly compacted using an immersion vibrator to eliminate entrapped air and achieve adequate consolidation. Following casting, the top surfaces were levelled and finished using a steel trowel to provide a smooth, uniform surface. Workability testing was conducted on fresh concrete to assess consistency and placement characteristics. Compressive strength evaluation was performed on cylindrical specimens tested on the same day as the corresponding beam flexural tests to ensure representative strength values. All specimens were cured under moist burlap covering for 28 days under standard laboratory conditions. Prior to FRP application, beam surfaces underwent comprehensive preparation to optimize the bond interface. The concrete surfaces were mechanically ground, sandblasted, and thoroughly cleaned to achieve the required surface roughness for enhanced FRP-concrete bond performance. Additionally, beam edges were rounded to a radius of 15 mm to prevent stress concentrations and potential premature failure at sharp corners during FRP installation and testing. Test setup and instrumentation

Following the 28-day curing period, all beam specimens were subjected to four-point bending tests to evaluate their flexural performance under monotonic loading, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The test configuration consisted of simply supported beams with a clear span of 1800 mm and two symmetrically positioned loading points located 600 mm from the mid-span, creating a constant moment region of 600 mm between the loading points. A comprehensive instrumentation system was implemented to capture the complete load-deformation response throughout the testing program. Displacement measurements were obtained using four linear variable displacement transducers (LVDTs) strategically positioned to monitor critical deformation parameters: three LVDTs were mounted along the beam soffit at the mid-span and beneath each loading point, while the fourth LVDT was positioned at the loading actuator to monitor crosshead displacement. Applied loads were continuously monitored using a 500 kN (50-ton) capacity load cell integrated with the hydraulic loading system. The loading protocol involved monotonic displacement-controlled loading applied through a hydraulic actuator at a constant rate of 2 mm/min until specimen failure. All instrumentation signals, including load cell and LVDT outputs, were simultaneously recorded using a computerized data acquisition system operating at a sampling frequency of 10 Hz to ensure comprehensive capture of the structural response, including any sudden load drops or brittle failure events. Strain gauges were attached to bottom steel bars to record strain of steel bars.

Results and discussion

Failure modes

This section presents the load–deflection behavior of the tested beams and highlights the influence of the strengthening configurations and concrete type on stiffness, peak load, and post-peak response. The observed failure patterns of the beams are shown in Figs. 6 and 7 are summarized in Table 4. The control beams in both groups failed in typical flexural modes, characterized by flexural cracking initiating at midspan followed by progressive propagation and eventual crushing of the compression zone concrete. The use of LWA in Group 2 specimens resulted in noticeably wider cracks and earlier crack propagation compared to the NA control specimen (Control-0%), confirming the lower stiffness and weaker interfacial transition zone associated with LWA concretes. BFRP strengthened beams exhibited improved cracking resistance and delayed failure compared to controls. In Group 1, bottom-only strengthened specimens (B0-B-Bt-2 L) failed by midspan debonding after significant flexural cracking, while U-wrap beams (B0-B-U-2 L) shifted the debonding closer to the ends. The U-wrap (B0-B-UA-2 L) achieved the most effective performance, sustaining higher loads until FRP rupture and concrete crushing occurred. A similar sequence was observed in Group 2 beams with LWA concrete, though debonding initiated earlier and cracks were wider (see Table 4), particularly in the bottom-only configuration (B15-B-Bt-2 L). Nevertheless, the U-wrap (B15-B-UA-2 L) demonstrated that full wrap strips could compensate for the weaker bond in LWA beams by ensuring rupture-controlled failure instead of premature debonding. JFRP systems produced more ductile failure modes, with extensive crack distribution across the span. In Group 1, bottom-only strengthened beams (B0-J-Bt-2 L and B0-J-Bt-3 L) showed flexural cracking with localized debonding of sheets, while the three-layer specimen displayed improved resistance by delaying debonding. U-wrap jute beams (B0-J-U-2 L and B0-J-U-3 L) achieved greater ductility, failing through a combination of fiber rupture, debonding, and localized crushing. Group 2 beams with LWA concrete followed the same trends but with earlier crack initiation and larger crack widths (Table 4). The influence of LWA was most evident in B15-J-U-2 L, which developed multiple wide cracks before debonding and rupture, emphasizing the greater challenge of bond retention in lightweight concrete. The introduction of full wrap strips significantly influenced the observed failure mechanisms of the strengthened beams. In specimens without full wrap strips, failure was primarily governed by premature debonding or separation of the FRP sheets from the concrete surface, which limited the effective utilization of the FRP tensile capacity. In contrast, the full wrap strips with U-wrap exhibited improved composite action between the FRP and the concrete substrate. As a result, failure modes shifted toward more desirable mechanisms, including FRP rupture or shear failure of the concrete after substantial stress mobilization in the FRP layers. In some cases, localized stress concentrations developed near the strip locations. These observations indicate that the full wrap strips effectively delayed debonding and enhanced the structural contribution of the FRP strengthening system.

Hybrid JB FRP beams combined the benefits of both fibers. In Group 1, the bottom-only hybrid beam (B0-BJ-Bt-2 L) showed more uniform crack distribution than its single-fiber counterparts, while the U-wrap hybrid (B0-BJ-U-2 L) resisted higher loads and failed through hybrid rupture after extensive cracking. In LWA beams, hybrid systems again proved advantageous. The bottom-only hybrid specimen (B15-BJ-Bt-2 L) outperformed beams with jute or basalt alone, while the U-wrap hybrid (B15-BJ-U-2 L) demonstrated the most favorable failure pattern, marked by delayed debonding, uniform cracking, and ultimate failure through fiber rupture at elevated loads (Table 4). This performance can be attributed to the enhanced load-transfer mechanism facilitated by the U-wrap design. By extending the FRP application to the sides of the beams, the wrap actively engages in resisting forces, which helps distribute loads more evenly across the beam sections. Taken together, the results confirm that the use of lightweight aggregates reduces crack resistance and bond efficiency, leading to earlier debonding and wider cracks compared to normal aggregate concretes. However, optimized strengthening strategies—particularly U-wraps and hybrid JB FRP systems—proved effective in mitigating these drawbacks, shifting the governing failure mode from premature debonding to desirable rupture-controlled mechanisms. Previous studies have also reported the improve performance of U-wrap strengthening as compared to the FRP applied only at the bottom51,52.

Load-carrying capacity and ductility of strengthened beams

The maximum load-carrying capacity and ductility of all tested beams are summarized in Table 5. The ductility is simply considered the ultimate deflection for the sake of reference in this work. The results indicate that FRP confinement significantly influenced both the strength and deformation behaviour of RC beams, with variations depending on the confinement material type, strengthening configuration, and the use of LWA. For beams in Group 1 (0% LWA), the control specimen reached a peak load of 83.1 kN with a maximum midspan deflection of 68.05 mm. Strengthening with BFRP in bottom-only configuration (B0-B-Bt-2 L) did not improve capacity and even slightly reduced it, failing primarily due to midspan debonding. However, the U-wrap configuration (B0-B-U-2 L) increased capacity by 14.26%, while the U-wrap (B0-B-UA-2 L) achieved the most substantial gain, enhancing capacity by 36.77% and ductility by approximately 38% compared to the control. JFRP strengthened beams also showed considerable improvement. The bottom-only two-layer specimen (B0-J-Bt-2 L) performed similar to the control, whereas the three-layer specimen (B0-J-Bt-3 L) increased capacity by almost 20%. U-wrap jute specimens (B0-J-U-2 L and B0-J-U-3 L) provided ductile responses, achieving higher load capacity but at reduced deflections, indicating partial debonding or rupture at smaller deformation levels. Hybrid jute–basalt systems delivered the most balanced performance: B0-BJ-Bt-2 L and B0-BJ-U-2 L improved both strength (by 8–38%) and maintained ductility at levels close to the control, with the U-wrap hybrid (B0-BJ-U-2 L) proving particularly effective.

The influence of LWA concrete in Group 2 specimens was evident in both control and strengthened beams. The control beam with 15% LWA replacement (Control-15%) carried a slightly higher load (91.7 kN) than the Control-0% beam, but with lower ductility, reflecting the reduced stiffness and cracking resistance of LWA mixes. Similar patterns were also observed in previous studies53,54. Wu et al. (2025) developed high-strength, core-shell lightweight aggregates (LWA) using abandoned fly ash, leading to improved performance in lightweight aggregate concrete. The failure mode of the LWA structure was found to be similar to that of the control structure when compared to conventional concrete. Enhancing the strength of the lightweight aggregate, incorporating glass fiber, and increasing the reinforcement rate effectively reduced the initial crack width and improved the flexural bearing capacity of the LWA structure55. In another study, Alshannag et al., 2025 reported that the flexural response of LWA beams was similar to that of normal weight concrete beams56.

Among strengthened specimens, BFRP again showed clear trends: the bottom-only configuration (B15-B-Bt-2 L) slightly underperformed compared to the control, while the U-wrap (B15-B-U-2 L) and U-wrap (B15-B-UA-2 L) increased capacity by 26.50% and 38.14%, respectively. Notably, the U-wrap with strips in LWA beams reached a maximum load of 126.7 kN, which is the highest among all Group 2 specimens. JFRP confined beams with LWA (B15-J-Bt-2 L, B15-J-U-2 L) showed significant capacity improvements (up to 17%), but strain values and reduced deflections suggested earlier debonding and rupture of jute fibers. Hybrid JB FRP strengthened beams were again superior in terms of balanced performance. The bottom-only hybrid (B15-BJ-Bt-2 L) reached 115.6 kN, while the U-wrap hybrid (B15-BJ-U-2 L) recorded the highest load capacity of 128.1 kN and a deflection of 99.0 mm, demonstrating the combined benefit of strength from basalt and ductility from jute. The U-wrap FRP strengthened beams exhibited higher load-carrying capacity for several interconnected reasons. First, the U-wrap configuration enhances load distribution by providing support not only at the soffit but also along the sides of the beams. This design enables a more uniform distribution of tensile stresses, mitigating localized failures and effectively spreading the load across a larger beam area. Additionally, the U-wrap FRP increases the tensile capacity of the beam by resisting tensile forces, thereby reducing the likelihood of tensile rupture. Another critical factor is the prevention of premature debonding; the U-wrap arrangement improves adhesion and friction with the concrete surface, allowing the system to sustain higher loads without detaching. Furthermore, this design promotes increased ductility, enabling the beams to deform under load without instant failure and leading to enhanced overall performance. Finally, the inclusion of full wrap strips further reinforces the beam at crucial sections, contributing to the increased load capacity and delaying various failure modes. Collectively, these features underscore the superior performance of U-wrap FRP strengthened beams in enhancing structural integrity. In the past, similar trends regarding the enhanced load-carrying capacity of U-wrap FRP strengthened beams have been observed in other studies57–59.

The steel strain results shown in Table 5 confirmed that all beams exceeded the yield strain of reinforcement (2240 μm/m), meaning yielding of steel occurred in every case. Interestingly, the maximum strain in steel did not consistently increase with FRP confinement; in fact, several strengthened beams exhibited lower strain values compared to their respective controls. This observation is consistent with the failure mode analysis. In some cases, debonding or premature rupture of the FRP sheets occurred at relatively smaller deflections, which limited the extent of strain development in the longitudinal reinforcement. For instance, B15-B-U-2 L and B0-J-U-3 L failed through fiber debonding at earlier stages, leading to lower recorded strains despite higher ultimate loads. Conversely, full wrap configurations and hybrid systems allowed higher strain mobilization in the reinforcement, as premature debonding was suppressed. It should be noted that reinforcement strain data for specimen B0-J-U-2 L could not be recorded due to a strain gauge malfunction, although its load capacity is included in the analysis. In past, Nawaz et al., 2022 evaluated the flexural performance of all lightweight concrete beams strengthened with carbon fibre reinforced polymer (CFRP) sheets. Testing fifteen beams revealed that CFRP significantly increased load capacity by 28% to 102% compared to strengthened specimens60. In another study, Aljaafreh et al., 2016 examined 40 lightweight reinforced concrete (LWRC) beams strengthened with carbon fibre-reinforced polymer (CFRP) composites under four-point bending tests. It explores variables like reinforcement ratio and CFRP dimensions. Results indicated limited increases in load capacity, with jacketing providing the best strength enhancement (41%) but decreasing ductility. Some other studies also reported considerable increase in load carrying capacity of light weight aggregate concrete beams by using externally wrapped FRP61–64.

Load–deflection response of strengthened beams

The load–deflection behaviour of the tested beams is presented in Figs. 8 and 9. Figures 8(a–c) show the responses of Group 1 specimens containing natural aggregates (0% LWA replacement), while Figs. 9(a–c) illustrate the responses of Group 2 beams with 15% LWA replacement. The results clearly demonstrate that both the confinement material and the strengthening technique significantly influenced stiffness, capacity, and post-yield ductility, and that the use of LWA modified the overall response. In Group 1 beams (Fig. 8), the control specimen exhibited typical flexural behaviour with gradual stiffness degradation after yielding. Strengthening with BFRP (Fig. 8a) significantly altered the response. The bottom-only configuration (B0-B-Bt-2 L) had limited effect, producing a curve nearly identical to the control. By contrast, the U-wrap configuration (B0-B-U-2 L) increased both load capacity and stiffness, while the U-wrap (B0-B-UA-2 L) achieved the best overall response, carrying the highest load with sustained deflection capacity beyond 90 mm. This confirms that U-wrap effectively prevented premature debonding and allowed the FRP to fully mobilize. For JFRP strengthened beams (Fig. 8b), the load–deflection curves were characterized by more ductile post-yield behaviour, but lower peak loads compared to basalt systems. The three-layer bottom-only specimen (B0-J-Bt-3 L) achieved a noticeable strength increase compared to the two-layer specimen, but its deflection capacity was reduced due to early fiber rupture. U-wrap jute beams (B0-J-U-2 L and B0-J-U-3 L) sustained higher loads than the control but failed at smaller deflections, highlighting that debonding of natural fibers occurred earlier. Hybrid JB FRP systems (Fig. 8c) demonstrated the most balanced behaviour, combining strength improvements with reasonable deflection capacity. The U-wrap hybrid specimen (B0-BJ-U-2 L) reached the highest load among Group 1 beams, while maintaining a deflection comparable to the control, evidencing the synergistic action of jute and basalt fibers.

The effect of LWA replacement was evident in Group 2 beams as shown in Fig. 9. Load–strain response of steel reinforcement

The load–strain responses of the longitudinal reinforcement are presented in Figs. 10 and 11 for Group 1 (0% LWA) and Group 2 (15% LWA) beams, respectively. The control specimens in both groups displayed the expected strain development, with reinforcement yielding evident at approximately 2000 μm/m and continued strain hardening until failure. These curves confirm that yielding of steel reinforcement occurred in all beams, consistent with the strain values reported in Table 5. For Group 1 beams (Fig. 10a–c), the effect of strengthening was strongly dependent on confinement material and technique. BFRP strengthened beams (Fig. 10a) demonstrated reduced steel strains compared to the control, particularly in the bottom-only configuration (B0-B-Bt-2 L), which failed prematurely due to debonding. The U-wrap (B0-B-U-2 L) sustained higher loads but still showed lower maximum steel strains than the control. The U-wrap (B0-B-UA-2 L) carried the highest load among Group 1 basalt beams, but reinforcement strains were limited, indicating that failure was governed by FRP rupture before steel reinforcement could fully mobilize its ductility. JFRP confined beams (Fig. 10b) exhibited more ductile responses, with distributed cracking and stable load–strain development. The three-layer specimen (B0-J-Bt-3 L) increased capacity, although its maximum strain was lower than the control due to premature fiber rupture. The strain gauge on specimen B0-J-U-2 L malfunctioned during testing, and therefore reliable strain data could not be recorded for this beam. Its load-carrying capacity has already been reported in previous section, but the strain response is not reported here. The other U-wrap specimen (B0-J-U-3 L) sustained higher load but recorded lower reinforcement strain, indicating early rupture of jute fibers constrained strain development. Hybrid JB FRP reinforced beams (Fig. 10c) achieved the most balanced performance, where the U-wrap hybrid (B0-BJ-U-2 L) carried higher loads while maintaining steel strains comparable to the control, reflecting better synergy between FRP and reinforcement.

In Group 2 beams with 15% LWA (Fig. 11a–c), the same trends were observed but with more pronounced reductions in steel strain. The control beam (Control-15%) developed lower strain than Control-0%, consistent with the lower ductility associated with LWA concretes. BFRP strengthened beams (Fig. 11a) demonstrated improved load capacity, yet reinforcement strains were significantly lower, especially in the U-wrap specimen (B15-B-U-2 L), which showed premature debonding. The U-wrap (B15-B-UA-2 L) again provided the best performance, carrying the highest load while limiting reinforcement strains due to rupture of FRP sheets. JFRP confined beams (Fig. 11b) provided enhanced strength, but maximum reinforcement strains were below that of the control, confirming that premature debonding and fiber rupture in LWA concrete restricted full strain development. Hybrid JB FRP systems (Fig. 11c) showed superior behaviour, with the U-wrap hybrid specimen (B15-BJ-U-2 L) achieving the highest load among all LWA beams, though the reinforcement strain remained lower than that of the natural aggregate control. The consistent observation across both groups is that FRP strengthening reduced reinforcement strains despite higher load capacities. This finding is closely related to the failure modes described in Sect. 3.1. Once FRP sheets debonded or ruptured, load transfer to steel reinforcement was interrupted, leading to lower strain accumulation. While confinement improved strength, it also constrained deformation capacity of reinforcement, producing reduced steel strains. This explains the paradox noted in Sect. 3.2, where ductility indices did not always increase despite confinement. The load capacity and strain development were better balanced, indicating that improved bonding and hybridization mitigate premature de-bonding effects. In summary, the load-strain responses indicate that all beams reached reinforcement yielding; however, FRP strengthening constrained ultimate reinforcement strains owing to debonding or rupture at reduced deflections. U-wraps and hybrid JB FRP systems provided the optimal equilibrium by enhancing load capacity while keeping strain development in proximity to control beams.

Results and discussion

The experimental results indicate that beams cast with lightweight aggregate concrete (LWAC) exhibited lower ductility compared to those made with normal aggregate concrete. This behavior can be attributed to the intrinsic characteristics of lightweight aggregates, which generally possess lower stiffness and reduced crushing resistance compared with natural aggregates. As a result, LWAC tends to exhibit reduced fracture energy and a more brittle post-peak response, which contributes to the observed reduction in ductility.

In addition, the mechanism of aggregate interlock along shear cracks plays a critical role in the shear resistance of reinforced concrete beams. In LWAC, the relatively weaker aggregate particles may fracture under shear loading, leading to a reduction in aggregate interlock and consequently limiting the deformation capacity of the beam. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies on lightweight aggregate concrete, where reduced aggregate interlock and crack-bridging capability resulted in lower ductility compared with conventional concrete.

Despite this reduction in ductility, some LWAC beams achieved comparable or slightly higher load capacities when strengthened with FRP systems. This behavior can be explained by the significant contribution of externally bonded FRP reinforcement in enhancing the shear resistance and controlling crack propagation. The FRP sheets effectively bridge shear cracks and provide additional tensile resistance, which compensates for the reduced aggregate interlock in LWAC. Consequently, the strengthening system allows the beams to attain higher load levels even though the overall deformation capacity remains lower than that of normal aggregate concrete beams. These findings highlight the importance of considering both the material characteristics of LWAC and the strengthening mechanism of FRP systems when evaluating the structural performance of strengthened beams.

Conclusions

Based on the experimental investigation and analysis of failure modes, capacity, ductility, and load–deflection and load–strain responses, several key conclusions can be drawn. These findings provide insight into the effectiveness of hybrid jute–basalt (JB) FRP systems and the influence of lightweight aggregates on the flexural behaviour of strengthened RC beams. The principal conclusions derived from the study are as follows: All beams exhibited flexural failure; however, the reinforcement using FRP systems changed the primary failure mechanism from concrete crushing to debonding or rupture-controlled reactions. U-wraps and hybrid JB FRP systems were the most successful in postponing premature debonding. BFRP provided the highest strength gains, with U-wrap specimens increasing load capacity by up to 36.8% compared to controls. JFRP contributed to more ductile responses but suffered from early debonding and fiber rupture, particularly in U-wrap configurations. Hybrid JB FRP systems achieved the best balance of strength and ductility. The U-wrap hybrid beams recorded the highest capacity and largest deflection, outperforming single-fiber systems in both natural and LWA concretes. In group 1, the ductility of FRP strengthened beam B0-B-UA-2 L was increased by 3793% as compared to the control beams i.e., Control-0%. Whereas in group 2, the ductility of FRP strengthened beam B15-BJ-BU-2 L was incrased by 60.82 as compared to the control beam i.e., Control-15%.Load–strain curves confirmed yielding of steel reinforcement in all specimens. However, maximum strains were often reduced after confinement, indicating that premature debonding or rupture of FRP sheets limited strain development despite higher load capacity. The hybrid systems mitigated this drawback, sustaining higher load with more stable strain transfer. The use of LWA reduced ductility of control beams due to lower stiffness and weaker bond characteristics. Nevertheless, hybrid U-wrap systems effectively compensated for this reduction, enabling rupture-controlled failure with high strength and deformation capacity. The combined findings demonstrate that sustainable hybrid JB FRP systems can serve as effective alternatives to synthetic FRPs for retrofitting RC beams, providing strength and ductility enhancements while incorporating natural fibers and lightweight aggregates to reduce environmental impact.

The present study focuses on experimentally evaluating the structural behavior of RC beams strengthened with hybrid jute–basalt FRP systems under different wrapping configurations. Although the experimental results provide valuable insight into the strengthening efficiency and failure mechanisms, comparisons with existing analytical models and design provisions were beyond the scope of the current investigation. Future research should evaluate the applicability of established FRP design guidelines, such as those proposed by American Concrete Institute in ACI 440.2R and the provisions of the fib Model Code 2010, to hybrid FRP systems incorporating natural fibers. Such studies would allow direct comparison between predicted and experimentally measured capacities and may facilitate the development of improved analytical models or design recommendations for sustainable hybrid FRP strengthening techniques.
