ABTB1 facilitates the replication of influenza A virus by counteracting TRIM4-mediated degradation of viral NP protein
Authors: Wenjun Shi, Zhibo Shan, Li Jiang, Guangwen Wang, Xuyuan Wang, Yu Chang, Yuzhen Hu, Bo Wang, Qibing Li, Yihan Wang, Guohua Deng, Jianzhong Shi, Yongping Jiang, Xianying Zeng, Guobin Tian, Hualan Chen, Chengjun Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfluenza A viruses (IAVs) continue to cause tremendous economic losses to the global animal industry and respiratory diseases and deaths among humans. The nuclear import of the vRNP complex, composed of polymerase basic protein 1 (PB1), polymerase basic protein 2 (PB2), polymerase acidic protein (PA), nucleoprotein (NP), and viral RNA, is essential for the efficient replication of IAV. Host factors involved in this process can be targeted for the development of countermeasures against IAV infection. Here, we found that Ankyrin Repeat and BTB Domain Containing 1 (ABTB1) promotes the replication of IAV, and positively regulates the nuclear import of the vRNP complex. ABTB1 did not interact directly with NP, indicating that ABTB1 plays an indirect role in facilitating the nuclear import of the vRNP complex. Immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry revealed that Tripartite Motif Containing 4 (TRIM4) interacts with ABTB1. We found that TRIM4 relies on its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity to inhibit the replication of IAV by targeting and degrading NP within the incoming vRNP complex as well as the newly synthesized NP. ABTB1 interacted with TRIM4, leading to TRIM4 degradation through the proteasome system. Notably, ABTB1-mediated degradation of TRIM4 blocked the effect of TRIM4 on NP stability, and largely counteracted the inhibitory effect of TRIM4 on IAV replication. Our findings define a novel role for ABTB1 in aiding the nuclear import of the vRNP complex of IAV by counteracting the destabilizing effect of TRIM4 on the viral NP protein.KEYWORDS: Influenza A virus, NP, ABTB1, TRIM4, nuclear import of vRNP

Introduction

Influenza A virus (IAV) is a single-stranded, negative-sense RNA virus with an eight-segmented genome. Based on the antigenicity of the two surface glycoproteins, hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA), IAV is currently categorized into 18 HA subtypes and 11 NA subtypes [1]. Besides the threats posed by seasonal H1N1 and H3N2 viruses, more subtypes of avian IAVs have acquired the ability to cross the species barrier to infect humans [2–7], raising huge concerns of causing new influenza pandemics [8–13]. Ten essential proteins and up to eight accessary proteins are encoded by the genome of IAV [14]. The viral genome RNA (vRNA) is bound by three polymerase subunits [i.e. polymerase basic protein 2 (PB2), polymerase basic protein 1 (PB1), polymerase acidic protein (PA)] and nucleoprotein (NP), forming a viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) complex. This complex plays a central role in the life cycle of IAV by catalyzing the transcription and replication of the viral genome [15].

The replication cycle of IAV involves intricate interactions with a large number of host cellular proteins and RNAs [16–28], which dictates the viral pathogenicity and transmissibility [16,19,23–26]. The transcription and replication of the IAV genome occur in the nucleus of infected cells [29]. Upon viral infection, the vRNP complex is transported into the nucleus [30], where it catalyzes the first round of viral mRNA synthesis (primary transcription). After translation, the newly synthesized polymerase subunits and NP enter the nucleus to catalyze further mRNA transcription and vRNA replication [30,31]. Thus, the efficient nuclear import of the incoming vRNP complex and the newly synthesized polymerase subunits and NP is a key step for IAV replication.

The vRNP complex and its component proteins must pass through nuclear pore complexes (NPCs) to translocate from the cytoplasm into nucleoplasm [32]. Unlike small molecules that can directly pass through NPCs by passive diffusion, the vRNP complex and its components can only translocate across the NPCs via the classical nuclear import pathway, which is mediated by the adaptor, isoforms of importin α, and the receptor importin β [33]. The nuclear import of vRNP involves interactions between the nuclear localization signal (NLS) on the NP of the vRNP and importin α1 and α5 [34,35], which occurs only after the dissociation of M1 from the vRNP [36], because that is when the short motifs of the NLS of the NP in the vRNP become exposed to interact with the transport adaptors. The newly synthesized polymerase subunits and NP in the cytoplasm use different transport adaptors to enter the nucleus, among which NP binds importin α1, α3, α5, and α7, to access the classical nuclear import pathway [35,37,38].

Several host factors have been identified to be involved in regulating the transport of vRNPs or newly synthesized NP into the nucleus of infected cells. Phospholipid scramblase 1 (PLSCR1) forms an integrative complex with NP and isoforms of importin α in the cytoplasm, which prevents importin α and importin β from forming a functional receptor complex, thereby suppressing the nuclear import of NP [33]. Moloney leukemia virus 10 (MOV10) disrupts the interaction between NP and importin α, resulting in the retention of NP in the cytoplasm [38]. Heat shock protein 40 (HSP40) promotes the association between NP and importin α, thus facilitating the nuclear import of vRNPs [39]. Eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1 delta (eEF1D) impedes the nuclear import of NP by impairing the interaction between NP and importin α5 [40]. Bcl10-interacting protein with CARD 1 (BinCARD1) was recently found to bind to NP and specifically enhance the interaction between NP and importin α7, thus facilitating the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and newly synthesize NP [41]. Given the importance of nuclear translocation of vRNPs in the IAV life cycle, we are convinced that other unknown host factors also participate in the regulation of the nuclear import of IAV vRNPs.

Ankyrin repeat and BTB domain containing 1 (ABTB1) (also known as BPOZ), which is encoded by a 1434-bp open reading frame, contains an N-terminal ankyrin repeat, a bipartite nuclear localization signal, and two BTB/POZ domains [42]. Four isoforms of ABTB1, produced from alternative splicing, were deposited in the UniProt Database, with isoform 2 (also known as BPOZ-2) as the canonical sequence that encodes 478 amino acids [43]. ABTB1 was reported to inhibit the progression of the cell cycle at the G(1)/S transition [43]. Although ABTB1 is mainly expressed in the cytoplasm, treatment with MG132 recruits ABTB1 to the nucleus where it interacts with the ubiquitin E3 ligase CUL3 and is degraded by the 26S proteasome [44].

Here, we found that ABTB1 plays an important role in the efficient nuclear import of the vRNP complex of IAV. ABTB1 does not directly engage in the translocation of the vRNP complex into nucleus because it does not interact directly with NP. By using immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry, we found that TRIM4 interacts with ABTB1. TRIM4 expression mediates the robust degradation of viral NP within the incoming vRNP complex and of the newly synthesized NP. ABTB1 degraded TRIM4 through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, thereby counteracting the degradative effect of TRIM4 on NP and the suppression on viral replication.

Results

ABTB1 positively regulates IAV replication

By using a replication-competent Venus-expressing H5N1 virus (H5N1 NA-Venus) [45] in a whole genome siRNA library screen targeting 21,585 mRNAs (our unpublished data), we identified ABTB1 as a potential proviral host factor for the replication of IAV. To confirm that ABTB1 is indeed involved in IAV replication, we performed siRNA knockdown experiments to downregulate the expression of ABTB1 in A549 cells and subsequently infected the cells with A/WSN/33 (WSN, H1N1) (MOI = 0.01), A/Anhui/2/2005 (AH05, H5N1) (MOI = 0.1), or A/chicken/Shanghai/SC197/2013 (SH13, H9N2) (MOI = 0.1) virus. We found that siRNA targeting ABTB1 effectively silenced the expression of ABTB1, by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) (Figure 1(A)) and western blotting (Figure 1(B)), while having no effect on cell viability (Figure 1(C)). SiRNA knockdown of ABTB1 led to 5.6-/7.5-, 14.6-/6.6-, and 4.8-/6.5-fold reductions in growth titres at 24 and 48 h post infection (p.i.) for the WSN (H1N1) (Figure 1(D)), AH/05 (H5N1) (Figure 1(E)), and SH/13 (H9N2) (Figure 1(F)) viruses, respectively, indicating that ABTB1 is important for the efficient replication of different subtypes of IAV. To rule out the possibility of off-target effects of the siRNA treatment, we generated an ABTB1 knockout (ABTB1_KO) A549 cell line by using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Sequencing analysis revealed a 35-bp deletion of ABTB1 in the ABTB1_KO cells (Figure 1(G)), which caused a frameshift and loss of ABTB1 expression (Figure 1(H)) without adversely affecting cell viability (Figure 1(I)). The titres of WSN (H1N1) virus grown in ABTB1_KO A549 cells showed a 16- and 26-fold decrease compared with those of control A549 cells at 24 and 48 h p.i., respectively (Figure 1(J)), indicating that knockout of ABTB1 expression also significantly inhibits IAV replication.

We next examined the effect of upregulating ABTB1 expression on the replication of IAV. We transduced A549 cells with a retrovirus encoding ABTB1 to establish a stable ABTB1-overexpressing A549 cell line. As expected, ABTB1 was overexpressed at both the mRNA and protein level in these cells compared with the empty retrovirus-transduced control cells (Figure 1(K-L)). Overexpression of ABTB1 led to a 7.2- and 6.8-fold increase in virus titres at 24 and 48 h p.i., respectively (Figure 1(M)).

To assess the response of ABTB1 to IAV infection, we determined the expression of ABTB1 in A549 cells infected with WSN (H1N1) virus (MOI = 5). We found that the level of ABTB1 expression was significantly induced at 6, 12, and 24 h p.i. (Figure 1(N)). These data indicate that ABTB1 is biologically significant during the course of IAV infection.

Taken together, our data show that ABTB1 is a positive regulator of IAV replication and suggest that ABTB1 may play an important role in IAV life cycle.

ABTB1 is involved in the early stages of the IAV life cycle

To investigate the stage of the IAV life cycle in which ABTB1 is engaged, ABTB1_KO or control A549 cells were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 5. The cellular distribution of viral NP was visualized by means of confocal microscopy. We observed viral NP in the nuclei of the control A549 cells, but seldom detected it in the nuclei of the ABTB1_KO cells at 3 h p.i. At 4 h p.i., viral NP had densely accumulated in the nuclei of most of the control A549 cells, but was weakly observed in the nuclei of only a few ABTB1_KO cells (Figure 2(A)). These results indicate that knockout of ABTB1 impedes the early stage of the replication cycle of IAV. At 7 h p.i., viral NP was already localized in the cytoplasm of most of the control cells, indicating that the nuclear export process was largely complete. By contrast, viral NP exhibited mixed distribution in ABTB1_KO cells: it was still retained in the nuclei of certain cells, whereas still did not go through the nuclear import process in most of the cells. These results indicate that due to the defect in the early stages of viral replication caused by ABTB1 knockout, the overall replication cycle of WSN (H1N1) virus is delayed in ABTB1_KO cells compared with control cells.

We next examined the expression of viral NP protein in ABTB1_KO and control A549 cells at 2, 3, 4, and 7 h p.i. with WSN (H1N1) virus (MOI = 5) by western blotting with a mouse anti-NP mAb. The expression levels of NP were reduced at different timepoints p.i. in ABTB1_KO cells compared with those in control cells (Figure 2(B)). These data corroborate our earlier finding that knockout of ABTB1 expression impedes the early stage of the IAV life cycle.

To explore the effect of ABTB1 overexpression on the early stage of the IAV life cycle, the distribution or level of viral NP was visualized by confocal microscopy or detected by western blotting in ABTB1-overexpressing or control A549 cells infected with WSN (H1N1) virus (MOI = 5) at 2, 3, 4, and 7 h p.i. Overexpression of ABTB1 led to accelerated nuclear localization of the vRNP complex (Figure 2(C)) and enhanced synthesis of NP (Figure 2(D)), thus further confirming that ABTB1 is involved in the early stages of the IAV life cycle.

ABTB1 promotes the nuclear import of the vRNP complex of IAV

Delivery of the vRNP complex into the nucleus of infected cells requires several steps and the use of the host endocytic and cytosolic trafficking machinery. We therefore use confocal microscopy to determine the specific step of the IAV life cycle at which ABTB1 functions. First, ABTB1-specific siRNA- or scrambled siRNA-treated A549 cells were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 20, and then stained with an anti-HA mAb at the indicated timepoints to examine receptor binding and endocytosis. We found that WSN (H1N1) virus bound equally well to the surface of A549 cells treated with siRNA targeting ABTB1 or scrambled siRNA, and that WSN (H1N1) virus was efficiently internalized into cells treated with both types of siRNA (Figure 3(A)). These results indicate that silencing ABTB1 expression has no effect on the receptor binding and endocytosis of IAV.

To examine the fusion process, we used R18 (octadecyl rhodamine B chloride, red) and DiOC [3,3 = -dioctadecyl-5,5 = -di(4-sulfophenyl)oxacarbocyanine, green] to generate a dual-labeled virus, which enables the measurement of the fusion process in a quantitative manner. Prior to the process of membrane fusion, R18 quenches the fluorescence of DiOC; when membrane fusion occurs, DiOC and R18 diffuse into the endosomal membrane, thereby stopping the quenching process, at which point DiOC fluorescence is detectable. A549 cells transfected with siRNA targeting vATPase subunit vATP6V1B2, an established positive control that plays an essential role in IAV entry into host cells [46], displayed very few fusion sites at 3 h p.i. (Figure 3(B)). By contrast, a mass of fusion sites were observed in A549 cells transfected with siRNA targeting ABTB1 or with scrambled siRNA, indicating that ABTB1 does not affect the fusion process of IAV entry.

The uncoating process of IAV, characterized by the dissociation of the vRNP complex from M1, enables complex release into the cytosol. To determine whether ABTB1 participates in the uncoating process of IAV, A549 cells transfected with siRNA targeting ABTB1 or vATP6V1B2, or with scrambled siRNA were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 100 in the presence of cycloheximide (CHX) to block the synthesis of new proteins. At 3 h p.i., the infected cells were fixed and stained with a rabbit anti-M1 pAb, and the cellular distribution of M1 was visualized by means of confocal microscopy. The vATP6V1B2-knocked down cells exhibited M1 aggregation in the cytoplasm, indicating that the process by which the vRNP complex dissociates from M1 was inhibited (Figure 3(C)). Under treatment with CHX, M1 was dispersed in the cytoplasm of A549 cells transfected with siRNA targeting ABTB1 or with scrambled siRNA, demonstrating that the uncoating process was completed (Figure 3(C)). In general, no difference was observed in the cellular distribution of M1 between ABTB1-specific siRNA- and scrambled siRNA-treated A549 cells. These data demonstrate that ABTB1 has no role in the uncoating process of IAV entry.

To investigate whether ABTB1 is engaged in the nuclear import of the incoming vRNP complex, A549 cells transfected with siRNA targeting ABTB1 or with scrambled siRNA were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 50, in the presence of CHX. At 3, 4, and 5 h p.i., the infected cells were fixed and stained with a mouse anti-NP mAb and the cellular distribution of NP was visualized by means of confocal microscopy. ABTB1-knocked down cells showed an obvious reduction in the nuclear localization of NP compared with scrambled siRNA-treated cells (Figure 3(D)). Consistent with this result, in the presence of CHX, the nuclear import of the vRNP complex was dramatically enhanced in ABTB1-overexpressing A549 cells compared with the pairwise control cells infected with WSN (H1N1) virus (MOI = 50) (Figure 3(E)). Together, these data indicate that ABTB1 plays an important role in the nuclear import of the incoming vRNP complex.

TRIM4 negatively regulates the replication of IAV

The incoming vRNP complex of IAV is imported into the nucleus of infected cells through the classical nuclear import pathway, which is mediated by the interaction between viral NP and isoforms of importin α. We therefore performed a co-IP assay to explore whether ABTB1 exerts its role in the nuclear import of vRNP complex by interacting directly with NP. As shown in Supplementary Figure S1, no interaction between ABTB1 and NP was observed.

The lack of direct interaction between ABTB1 and NP prompted us to hypothesize that the facilitating role of ABTB1 in the nuclear import of the incoming vRNP complex may be indirect. We therefore carried out an immunoprecipitation plus mass spectrometry assay to identify the potential target of ABTB1. HEK293T cells transfected for the expression of Flag-ABTB1 were immunoprecipitated with a mouse anti-Flag mAb or an IgG control, and then western blotted or stained with Coomassie blue (Supplementary Figure S2). Bands that were present in the samples immunoprecipitated by the anti-Flag mAb but not by the IgG control were cut out and identified by mass spectrometry analysis. In this way, TRIM4 was identified as an apparent interactor of ABTB1 (Supplementary Figure S2).

To determine the association between TRIM4 and IAV infection, A549 cells infected with WSN (H1N1) were western blotted for the detection of TRIM4. The expression of TRIM4 was gradually increased upon the infection of WSN (H1N1) virus (Supplementary Figure S3A). Moreover, treatment with increasing amounts of interferon β (IFN-β) did not induce the expression of TRIM4 (Supplementary Figure S3B).

Three isoforms, TRIM4α, TRIM4β and TRIM4γ, are encoded by the human TRIM4 gene. TRIM4β contains the same structural domains (i.e. RING, B box, CC, and SPRY) as TRIM4α except for a 26-aa deletion in the linker region between the B box and CC domain, whereas TRIM4γ possesses the 26-aa deletion as well as an incomplete SPRY domain (Supplementary Figure S3C). To reveal which isoform of TRIM4 is stimulated during IAV infection, we transfected HEK293T cells with plasmids expressing TRIM4α, TRIM4β, or TRIM4γ; concurrently, A549 cells infected with WSN (H1N1) virus for 12 and 24 h were western blotted for the detection of TRIM4. IAV infection stimulated the expression of TRIM4β (Supplementary Figure S3D), and the interaction between TRIM4β and ABTB1 was then verified by use of a co-IP assay. We found that Myc-TRIM4β was co-immunoprecipitated with Flag-ABTB1 by an anti-Flag mAb, and vice versa (Supplementary Figure S4).

To investigate the role of TRIM4 in the replication of IAV, we examined whether overexpression of TRIM4β could affect the growth of IAV. We found that Flag-tagged TRIM4β was well expressed in transiently transfected HEK293T cells (Figure 4(A)), and the overexpression of Flag-TRIM4β in HEK293T cells led to 23-/65-, 25-/50-, and 26-/40-fold reductions of growth titres of WSN (H1N1), AH05 (H5N1) and SH13 (H9N2) virus at 24 and 48 h p.i., respectively (Figure 4(B-D)), indicating that TRIM4 negatively regulates the replication of IAV.

We next examined the effect of downregulating TRIM4 expression on the replication of IAV by using siRNA treatment. A549 cells were transiently transfected for the expression of Flag-tagged TRIM4β for 24 h, followed by treatment with two siRNAs targeting TRIM4 or with scrambled siRNA. The two TRIM4-specific siRNAs efficiently silenced the expression of TRIM4β (Figure 4(E)). A549 cells treated with TRIM4-specific siRNAs or scrambled siRNA were then infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 0.01. We found that siRNA treatment had no effect on the cell viability (Figure 4(F)), and knockdown of TRIM4 expression by the two specific siRNAs increased the growth of WSN (H1N1) virus at 24 and 48 h p.i. (Figure 4(G)). In addition, we generated a TRIM4 knockout (TRIM4_KO) A549 cell line by using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Confocal microscopy analysis revealed the complete knockout of TRIM4 expression in TRIM4_KO cells (Figure 4(H)), without causing any adverse effects on cell viability (Figure 4(I)). The titres of WSN (H1N1) virus grown in TRIM4_KO A549 cells increased 12- and 3-fold compared with those of control cells at 24 and 48 h p.i., respectively (Figure 4(J)), indicating that knockout of TRIM4 also promotes the replication of IAV. Together, these data demonstrate that TRIM4 negatively regulates the replication of IAV.

TRIM4β suppresses the nuclear import of the vRNP complex of IAV

To explore the stage of the IAV replication cycle that involves TRIM4β, HEK293T cells transfected with empty vectors or plasmids expressing TRIM4β were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus (MOI = 5), followed by confocal microscopy analysis to visualize the expression and localization of NP at the indicated timepoints. We found that at 3 h p.i., NP clearly accumulated in the nuclei of the control cells, whereas it was weakly detected, and sequestered, in the cytoplasm of HEK293T cells overexpressing TRIM4β, indicating that the early stage of the viral life cycle was blocked (Figure 5(A)). At 7 h p.i., NP was detected in both the nucleus and cytoplasm of the control cells, a sign of active synthesis of new NP as well as nuclear export of the vRNP complex. By contrast, NP was still weakly detected in the cytoplasm of HEK293T cells overexpressing TRIM4β. These data indicate that the exogenous expression of TRIM4β blocks the progression of the IAV life cycle.

To examine the effect of downregulating TRIM4 expression on the replication cycle of IAV, TRIM4_KO and control A549 cells were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 5. At 2, 3, 4, and 7 h p.i., the cellular distribution of viral NP was visualized by means of confocal microscopy. We observed clearer nuclear accumulation of NP at 2 h p.i. and overall accelerated progression of virus life cycle in TRIM4_KO A549 cells compared with control cells (Figure 5(B)).

Next, TRIM4_KO and control A549 cells were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 50, and CHX was then added to inhibit the synthesis of new proteins. At 3, 4, and 5 h p.i., the nuclear accumulation of the vRNP complex was noticeably facilitated in TRIM4_KO A549 cells compared with control cells (Supplementary Figure S5), indicating that TRIM4 suppresses the nuclear import of incoming vRNP complex. These results, together with the above data, demonstrate that TRIM4 negatively regulates the nuclear import of the vRNP complex of IAV.

TRIM4 targets and degrades viral NP through K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation

To investigate the underlying mechanism of the inhibitory role of TRIM4 on the replication cycle of IAV, A549 cells transfected with empty vectors or plasmids expressing TRIM4β were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus (MOI = 5) for 4 h and then subjected to confocal microscopy. We found that A549 cells transfected with empty vectors exhibited dense accumulation of NP in the nucleus, whereas viral NP was weekly detected, sequestered in the cytoplasm, and clearly co-localized with TRIM4β in cells overexpressing exogenous TRIM4β (Figure 6(A-B)). Given that co-expression of TRIM4β suppressed the nuclear accumulation of NP, we wondered whether TRIM4β functions to degrade NP. To test this hypothesis, HEK293T cells were transfected for 24 h with constructs expressing Flag-TRIM4β and then infected with WSN (H1N1) virus (MOI = 5). At 6 and 10 h p.i., the cells were fixed and subjected to confocal microscopy. NP was very weakly detected in cells robustly expressing TRIM4β (Figure 6(C)), indicating that the NP within the incoming vRNP complex and newly synthesized NP are destroyed due to the potent expression of TRIM4β in such cells.

To further explore the targeting and degrading effect of TRIM4β on viral NP, HEK293T cells were transfected with constructs to express V5-WSN NP and gradually increasing amount of Flag-TRIM4β. The reduction in NP levels largely correlated with the gradual increase in TRIM4β expression (Figure 6(D)). To determine whether the degradative effect of TRIM4β on the NP of WSN (H1N1) virus also applied to other IAV subtypes, we co-transfected plasmids expressing the NP of AH05 (H5N1), A/Anhui/1/2013 (AH13, H7N9), or SH13 (H9N2) virus with constructs expressing Flag-TRIM4β into HEK293T cells. Western blot analysis showed that the expression of the NP of different subtypes of IAV was markedly reduced in the presence of increasingly expressed TRIM4β (Figure 6(E-G)).

TRIM4 is an established ubiquitin E3 ligase [47,48]. We therefore speculated that the ubiquitin E3 ligase activity of TRIM4 may mediate the ubiquitination of NP and direct the ubiquitinated NP into the proteasomal degradation pathway. To investigate this possibility, HEK293T cells were transfected with constructs to express V5-WSN NP and Flag-TRIM4β, and MG132 was added to inhibit protein degradation through the proteasomal pathway. We found that the addition of MG132 essentially prevented the degradation of NP (Figure 6(H)), indicating that TRIM4β indeed mediates the degradation of NP through the proteasomal pathway. Given that ubiquitinated proteins degraded via the proteasomal pathway are mostly modified through the Lys48 (K48)-linkage, we examined the effect of complementing K48 ubiquitin (all lysine residues except K48 were mutated to arginine) expression on the degradation of NP. The expression of HA-tagged K48 ubiquitin alone promoted the degradation of NP, and the co-expression of HA-Ub K48 and Myc-TRIM4β caused more remarkable degradation of NP (Figure 6(I)), implying that the degradative effect of TRIM4β on NP is most likely achieved through catalyzing the K48-linked ubiquitination of NP. Finally, in the presence of MG132, TRIM4β-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination of NP was directly revealed in HEK293T cells transfected with constructs for the expression of NP, TRIM4β, and Ub K48 or Ub K48R (only the K48 residue was mutated to arginine) in combination or alone (Figure 6(J)).

Together, these results demonstrate that TRIM4β targets and degrades the NP of different IAV subtypes through K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation.

ABTB1 abolishes the inhibitory effect of TRIM4 on NP stability and viral replication by degrading TRIM4 through the proteasomal system

Given that ABTB1 interacts with TRIM4 and TRIM4 inhibits the nuclear import of the vRNP complex by targeting and degrading NP, we then sought to define the interrelationship between ABTB1 and TRIM4. To this end, we examined the expression of ABTB1 and TRIM4 when they were co-expressed in HEK293T cells, by using confocal microscopy. The co-expression of ABTB1 and TRIM4β remarkably reduced the fluorescence intensity of TRIM4β (Figure 7(A)), indicating that the presence of ABTB1 is able to degrade TRIM4β. Consistent with this data, western blotting showed that gradually increasing the amount of ABTB1 reduced the level of TRIM4β in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 7(B)), confirming that ABTB1 acts to degrade TRIM4β. To further reveal the degradation specificity of ABTB1 to TRIM4, TRIM35, which does not interact with ABTB1 (Figure 7(C)), was co-expressed with ABTB1. As shown in Figure 7(D), exogenously expressed ABTB1 had no effect on the stability of TRIM35, thus demonstrating that ABTB1 specifically targets and degrades TRIM4.

ABTB1 is an adaptor protein for E3 ubiquitin ligase [44], directing target proteins to the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway. To explore the possible pathways in which ABTB1 is involved to degrade TRIM4, different inhibitors were used to examine whether the degradative effect of ABTB1 on TRIM4 could be suppressed. Compared with DMSO control, only treatment with MG132 dramatically inhibited the degradative effect of ABTB1 on TRIM4β (Figure 7(E)), indicating that ABTB1-mediated degradation of TRIM4 is achieved through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway.

Finally, we attempted to define the interrelationship between ABTB1, TRIM4, and NP. HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids expressing the NP of WSN (H1N1) virus alone or in combinations with TRIM4β and ABTB1, and the amount of each protein was determined at 36 h post-transfection. We found that ABTB1-mediated degradation of TRIM4 abolished the degradation of NP mediated by TRIM4 (Figure 7(F)). This finding was also confirmed in HEK293T cells that were transfected individually or in combination with plasmids expressing TRIM4β and ABTB1, and were subsequently infected with WSN (H1N1) virus (MOI = 5) (Supplementary Figure S6). In addition, we examined how the interplay among ABTB1, TRIM4, and NP affected the replication of IAV. HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids to express TRIM4β alone or in combination with ABTB1 (Figure 7(G)), and were then infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 0.01. We found that at 24 and 48 h p.i., the expression of TRIM4β dramatically reduced the growth titres of WSN (H1N1) virus, whereas co-expression with ABTB1 significantly counteracted the growth defect of WSN (H1N1) virus caused by TRIM4β (Figure 7(H)). These results indicate that TRIM4 suppresses the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and of newly synthesized NP by sequestering and degrading the viral NP in the cytoplasm, which is counteracted by the degradative effect of ABTB1 on TRIM4. As a result, the interplay between ABTB1 and TRIM4 dictates the stability of viral NP, the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and newly synthesized NP, and the growth of IAV.

Proposed model for the role of ABTB1 in facilitating the nuclear import of the vRNP complex of IAV by targeting and degrading TRIM4

IAV infection triggers the expression of both ABTB1 and TRIM4. In the cytoplasm, TRIM4 targets and catalyzes the K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of IAV NP, thereby suppressing the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and viral replication. However, the inhibitory role of TRIM4 in the life cycle of IAV is counteracted by ABTB1. ABTB1 interacts with TRIM4, leading to the degradation of TRIM4 through the proteasomal system. Of note, ABTB1-mediated degradation of TRIM4 counteracts the destabilizing effect of TRIM4 on IAV NP, and antagonizes the inhibitory effect of TRIM4 on IAV replication (Figure 8).

Proposed model for ABTB1-mediated regulation of the nuclear import of IAV vRNP by targeting and degrading TRIM4. In the cytoplasm, TRIM4 inhibits the nuclear import of vRNP by interacting with and targeting NP into the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway. TRIM4 is also an interacting partner and target of ABTB1. The presence of ABTB1 leads to the proteasomal degradation of TRIM4. Consequently, the degradation of NP, impairment of the nuclear import of the vRNP complex, and reduction of virus replication mediated by TRIM4 are largely counteracted by ABTB1.

Discussion

The nuclear import of the vRNP complex of IAV is mediated by the interaction of the viral NP protein with adaptors of the host nuclear import pathway. Upon the translocation of the vRNP complex into the nucleus of infected cells, the transcription and replication of the viral genome is initiated. Host factors acting to aid or counteract the nuclear import of the vRNP complex significantly alter the replication dynamics and biological properties of IAV. In this study, we found that ABTB1 positively regulates the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and IAV replication. The lack of a direct interaction between ABTB1 and viral NP indicated that ABTB1 plays an indirect role in the nuclear import of the vRNP complex. When mass spectrometry was employed, TRIM4 was found to interact with ABTB1. We found that IAV infection induces the expression of TRIM4, and TRIM4 functions to dramatically inhibit the replication of IAV. The nuclear import of the incoming vRNP complex and newly synthesized NP was potently suppressed by TRIM4 in the cytoplasm. Moreover, TRIM4, acting as a ubiquitin E3 ligase, mediated the K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of NP in a dose dependent manner. Further studies showed that the interaction between ABTB1 and TRIM4 led to the degradation of TRIM4 via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Finally, ABTB1-mediated degradation of TRIM4 blocked the downregulating effect of TRIM4 on NP expression and viral growth. Overall, our study demonstrates that TRIM4 sequesters the incoming vRNP complex and newly synthesized NP in the cytoplasm and degrades viral NP, thereby dampening the replication of IAV, whereas ABTB1 acts to indirectly support the replication of IAV by interacting with and promoting the degradation of TRIM4. Of note, the expression of both ABTB1 and TRIM4 is induced upon IAV infection, emphasizing the biological significance of their roles in the replication cycle of IAV. In future study, a diverse range of human seasonal IAVs and avian IAVs could be used to further examine whether ABTB1 and TRIM4 play a broad role in the replication of IAV. In addition, the expression patterns of ABTB1 and TRIM4 in clinical cases will also be beneficial to evaluate their importance in the pathogenesis of IAV and to determine whether they can be potential targets for the development of therapeutic countermeasures.

TRIM family proteins are extensively involved in a wide range of biological processes through their E3 ubiquitin ligase activity [49]. During IAV replication, the viral proteins are subjected to extensive post-translational modifications, among which TRIM family proteins play an important role in this process. TRIM23-mediated K27-linked auto-polyubiquitination is essential for the activation of the TRIM23-TBK1-p62 autophagic axis and the consequent restriction of IAV replication [50]. TRIM25 activates RIG-I-mediated type I IFN signalling by inducing K63-linked polyubiquitination of the RIG-I CARD domain [51], whereas the NS1 protein of IAV interacts with TRIM25 and blocks K63-linked ubiquitination of RIG-I [52], leading to suppression of the RIG-I signalling pathway. TRIM35 activates RIG-I mediated type I IFN signalling by catalyzing K63-linked polyubiquitination of TRAF3 and mediates K48-linked polyubiquitination and degradation of IAV PB2, thereby suppressing the replication and pathogenesis of IAV [23]. TRIM14, TRIM22, and TRIM41 interact with IAV NP, and induce the polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of NP [53–55]. Lastly, TRIM56 inhibits the replication of IAV by impeding viral RNA synthesis [56]. A role for TRIM4 as an E3 ubiquitin ligase was demonstrated in the activation of the type I IFN response by its catalysis of the K63-linked ubiquitination of RIG-I as well as in the regulation of TRPM8 channel function through its mediation of the K63-linked ubiquitination of TRPM8 [47,48]. However, TRIM4 has never been directly implicated in the regulation of the viral replication cycle. Here, we found that TRIM4 induces robust K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of IAV NP, thereby blocking the nuclear import of newly synthesized NP and incoming vRNP complex and suppressing viral propagation. TRIM4 is well expressed in most of the target organs of IAV (e.g. lung, spleen and brain) [57], emphasizing the importance of TRIM4 in the antiviral immune response against infection and the pathogenesis of IAV.

The nuclear import of the vRNP complex is mediated by the interaction between the NLS of NP and isoforms of importin α, which, in turn, is recognized by the transport receptor importin β. The tertiary importin α-importin β-vRNP complex is subsequently delivered into the nucleus through the nuclear pore complex. Due to its importance in the replication cycle of IAV, the nuclear import of the vRNP complex becomes the focus of action of proviral or restricting host factors, such as PLSCR1, MOV10, eEF1D, HSP40, and BinCARD1 [33,38–41]. These proviral or restricting host factors play their roles in the nuclear import of the vRNP complex and NP protein by directly interacting with NP. In contrast, in the present study, we identified ABTB1 as a supportive host factor in the nuclear import of the vRNP complex. Notably, the role of ABTB1 in promoting the nuclear import of the vRNP complex is not achieved by directly interacting with NP. To reveal the means utilized by ABTB1 to promote the replication of IAV, we used mass spectrometry analysis to identify the downstream interacting partner of ABTB1. We found that ABTB1 interacts with and targets TRIM4 into the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway, thereby counteracting the degradative effect of TRIM4 on NP. Therefore, ABTB1 promotes the nuclear import of vRNP complex in an indirect way. These data highlight the complexity of the interplay among host factors in the regulation of the nuclear import of the vRNP complex. TRIM4 targets viral NP for ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, and in turn, TRIM4 itself is also a target of the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway in which ABTB1 participates.

In summary, our study revealed that the host factor ABTB1 positively regulates the replication of IAV by promoting the nuclear import of the vRNP complex. The lack of interaction between ABTB1 and viral NP protein indicates that ABTB1 functions in an indirect manner. Mass spectrometry identified TRIM4 as an interacting partner and downstream effector of ABTB1. TRIM4 mediated the K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of viral NP, thereby suppressing the nuclear import of the incoming vRNP complex and newly synthesized NP and impairing viral growth. Furthermore, the interaction between ABTB1 and TRIM4 led to the proteasomal degradation of TRIM4. Strikingly, ABTB1-mediated degradation of TRIM4 largely abolished the degradation of NP and impairment of viral growth mediated by TRIM4. Collectively, our data reveal a mechanism of interplay among ABTB1, TRIM4, and NP in the regulation of the replication cycle of IAV, which can be manipulated for the development of countermeasures against IAV infection.

Materials and methods

Facility

All experiments with A/Anhui/2/2005 (AH05, H5N1) virus were conducted within the enhanced animal biosafety level 3 (ABSL3+) facility in the Harbin Veterinary Research Institute (HVRI) of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS), which is approved for such use by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of China and the China National Accreditation Service for Conformity Assessment. The details of the facility and the biosafety and biosecurity measures used have been previously reported [13].

Cells and viruses

A549 (ATCC, CCL-185), HEK293T (ATCC, CRL-3216) and MDCK (ATCC, PTA-6500) cells were cultured in F12K (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), in DMEM (Life Technologies) containing 10% FBS and in DMEM containing 5% newborn calf serum (NCS, Sigma-Aldrich), respectively. All media were supplemented with 100 units/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Life Technologies). All cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2. A/WSN/33 (WSN, H1N1), A/Anhui/2/2005 (AH05, H5N1), and A/chicken/Shanghai/SC197/2013 (SH13, H9N2) virus were propagated in MDCK cells or 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs as previously described [25,33,45].

Plasmids

The open reading frame (ORF) of human ABTB1 was cloned into the mammalian expression vector pCAGGS with a Flag or V5 tag at the N-terminus. The ORFs of human TRIM4α, TRIM4β, and TRIM4γ were cloned into pCAGGS, and the ORF of TRIM4β was also cloned into pCAGGS with a Flag or Myc tag at the N-terminus. The ORFs of PB2, PB1, PA, and NP derived from WSN (H1N1) virus were cloned into pCAGGS as described previously [33]. pQCXIN-ABTB1 was constructed by inserting the ABTB1 ORF into the pQCXIN vector (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA). Guide RNA targeting ABTB1 or TRIM4 was inserted into the PX458 vector (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) to construct a stable ABTB1_KO or TRIM4_KO cell line. The generation of the pCAGGS constructs for the expression of V5-TRIM35, Flag-WSN NP, HA-WSN NP, V5-WSN NP, V5-AN05 NP, V5-AH13 NP, V5-SH13 NP, HA-Ub (K48), and HA-Ub (K48R) were described previously [19,23]. All plasmid constructs were confirmed by sequencing.

Antibodies and reagents

Mouse anti-NP monoclonal antibody (mAb) was prepared in our laboratory as described previously [58]. The following primary antibodies were purchased from commercial sources: rabbit anti-NP polyclonal antibody (pAb) (PA5-32242, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), rabbit anti-M1 pAb (GTX125928, GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA), rabbit anti-HA pAb (GTX127357, GeneTex), rabbit anti-HA tag pAb (51064-2-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-V5 pAb (AB3792, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), rabbit anti-GAPDH pAb (10494-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), mouse anti-actin mAb (sc-47778, Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA), mouse anti-Flag mAb (F3165, Sigma-Aldrich), mouse anti-Myc mAb (M4439, Sigma-Aldrich), mouse anti-V5 mAb (V8012, Sigma-Aldrich), rabbit anti-Flag pAb (F7425, Sigma-Aldrich), rabbit anti-Myc pAb (C3956, Sigma-Aldrich), mouse anti-ABTB1 mAb (CF811778, OriGene, Rockville, MD, USA), and rabbit anti-TRIM4 pAb (PAB32752, Bioswamp, Wuhan, China). The secondary antibodies used for western blotting were Dylight 680 goat anti-rabbit IgG (RS23720), Dylight 800 goat anti-rabbit IgG (RS23920), Dylight 680 goat anti-mouse IgG (RS23710), and Dylight 800 goat anti-mouse IgG (RS23910), purchased from Immunoway (Plano, TX, USA); the secondary antibodies used for confocal microscopy were Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (A11034) and Alexa Fluor 633 goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (A21052), obtained from Invitrogen. IFN-β (I9032), MG132 (HY-13259), Chloroquine (HY-17589A), and 3-MA (HY-19312) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

SiRNA knockdown and virus infection

SiRNA knockdown and virus infection were performed as described previously [19,59]. Briefly, siRNA targeting ABTB1 (5’-GCAAUAAAGCUUGAAGGCA-3’), TRIM4 (5’- GAAGTTGAGAGTAGAGATA3’; 5’-GAGATTGAACAAAGAAGAA-3’), or scrambled siRNA (Genepharma, Shanghai, China) was transfected into A549 cells seeded in 12-well plates by using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Invitrogen) at a concentration of 30 nM. At 48 h post-transfection, the knockdown efficiency was measured by use of RT-qPCR, western blotting, or confocal microscopy. Concurrently, siRNA-treated A549 cells were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 0.01. Supernatants were collected at 24 and 48 h p.i. and the virus titres were determined by means of plaque assays on MDCK cells.

RT-qPCR assays

Total RNA of A549 or HEK293T cells was extracted by using the RNeasy Plus minikit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA), which combines high-quality RNA purification with effective gDNA removal with unique gDNA Eliminator columns. The first-strand cDNAs were synthesized with an oligo(dT) primer and random 6-mers by using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). RT-qPCR assays were performed by using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNase H Plus, TaKaRa). Primers used for RT-qPCR of ABTB1 were as follows: ABTB1-F, 5’-GGAGGTGAATGTGCGGGACAAGTGGG-3’; ABTB1-R, 5’-CCGCTGCAAGAAGTCGTCATAGTAATCCC-3’. Relative RNA quantities were determined by using the comparative cycle threshold method, with the cellular GAPDH gene serving as the internal control. Dissociation curve analysis was performed after each assay to ensure specific detection.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined by using a CellTiter-Glo kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) as described previously [33]. Briefly, control A549, ABTB1_KO A549, or TRIM4_KO A549 cells were cultured in opaque-walled 96-well plates for 24 h; A549 cells seeded in opaque-walled 96-well plates were treated for 48 h with siRNA targeting ABTB1 or TRIM4, or with scrambled siRNA at a concentration of 30 nM. Then, 100 μL of CellTiter-Glo reagent was added directly into each well to induce cell lysis at room temperature (RT) for 10 min on a shaker. Luminescence was measured with a GloMax 96 Microplate Luminometer (Promega).

Generation of ABTB1_KO A549 and TRIM4_KO A549 cell lines and virus infection

ABTB1_KO A549 and TRIM4_KO A549 cell lines were established by using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. The guide RNA (gRNA) sequences, 5’-GGAGACGGACTCTATCCCGC-3’, 5’-CTTCCGCCTGGCGCGGCTTG-3’, and 5’-GAGCACGTGAGTGAAGACGT-3’, targeting exon 1, 2 and 3 of ABTB1, or gRNA sequences, 5’-ACTCGATGGACACCGGGTCC-3’, 5’- CGCCGATGGGTGCCGACATT-3’, and 5’-CTCTCGAAGGCCTCGTCGAT-3’, targeting exon 1 of TRIM4, were inserted into the gRNA expression cassette of the pX458 vector [60]. The set of three gRNA constructs of ABTB1 or TRIM4 were co-transfected into A549 cells by using a Neon Transfection System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The electrotransfected cells were cultured until they reached 70%–90% confluency, and were then trypsinized and separated into single cells by using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) with a SH800S Cell Sorter (Sony Biotechnology, San Jose, CA, USA). Each colony was individually propagated in 24-well plates, and the knockout of ABTB1 or TRIM4 expression was confirmed by direct sequencing, and western blotting or confocal microscopy. Subsequently, the ABTB1_KO A549, TRIM4_KO A549, or control A549 cells were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 0.01. Supernatants were collected at 24 and 48 h p.i., and virus titres were determined by use of plaque assays on MDCK cells.

Establishment of a stable A549-ABTB1 cell line overexpressing ABTB1 and virus infection

To establish a stable A549 cell line overexpressing ABTB1, AmphoPack-293 packaging cells (Clontech) were cultured in 10-cm dishes to 80%–90% confluency and were then transfected with the retroviral construct pQCXIN-ABTB1 or with empty pQCXIN vector by using Lipofectamine LTX and Plus Reagents. At 48 h post-transfection, viral supernatants were concentrated, and used to transduce A549 cells cultured in 6-cm dishes. The transduction was repeated once to enrich for transductants at an interval of 48 h. The confluent transduced cells were split and cultured in medium supplemented with 1000 μg/mL G418 for selection for about 10 days. The surviving cells were individually cloned into 96-well plates by using the SH800S Cell Sorter. Each colony was individually propagated in 24 well-plates, and the overexpression of ABTB1 was confirmed by RT-qPCR and western blotting. The A549-ABTB1 cells or A549 control cells were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 0.01. Supernatants were collected at 24 and 48 h p.i., and virus titres were determined by use of plaque assays on MDCK cells.

Viral infection and confocal microscopy

To visualize the cellular localization of the vRNP complex and newly synthesized NP, the following cells were used: A549 stably overexpressing ABTB1, ABTB1 or TRIM4 knockout A549, HEK293T transiently overexpressing TRIM4, A549 cells treated with the indicated siRNA (30 nM) for 48 h, and A549 or HEK293T control cells. These cells were grown in glass-bottom dishes and infected with WSN (H1N1) virus with the indicated MOI. At the indicated timepoints p.i., the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 1 h and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min. The permeabilized cells were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h, and then incubated with primary antibodies (rabbit anti-Flag pAb, and/or mouse anti-NP mAb) at 4°C overnight. The cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies [Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), and/or Alexa Fluor 633 goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L)] for 1 h. After three washes, the cells were incubated with DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min to stain the nuclei. Images were acquired by using a LSM 980 with fast AiryScan confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

For binding assays, A549 cells seeded in glass-bottom dishes were treated with the indicated siRNA, and at 48 h post-transfection were pre-incubated on ice for 30 min, followed by infection with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 20 on ice for 1 h. The infected cells were then washed 3 times with cold PBS and fixed with 4% PFA. Bound virus particles were stained with a rabbit anti-HA pAb and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), and visualized by confocal microscopy.

For endocytosis assays, A549 cells seeded in glass-bottom dishes were treated with the indicated siRNA, and at 48 h post-transfection were pre-incubated on ice for 30 min, followed by infection with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 20 on ice for 1 h. After the inoculum was removed, the infected cells were incubated for 30 min at 37°C. The cells were washed three times with cold PBS (pH = 1.3) to remove non-internalized virus particles and fixed with 4% PFA. The permeabilized cells were stained with a rabbit anti-HA pAb and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), and visualized by confocal microscopy.

For fusion assays, WSN (H1N1) virus was labeled with R18 (octadecyl rhodamine B chloride) and SP-DiOC18 [3,3 = -dioctadecyl-5,5 = -di(4-sulfophenyl)oxacarbocyanine] (Life Technologies) at final concentrations of 23 and 46 μM, respectively. After intense vortexing at RT for 1 h, the labeled viruses were filtered through a 0.22-μm filter. A549 cells seeded in glass-bottom dishes were treated with the indicated siRNA for 48 h (siRNA targeting the vATPase subunit vATP6V1B2 was used as a positive control), and then infected with the labeled WSN (H1N1) virus for 30 min on ice. The cells were washed three times with cold PBS, incubated at 37°C for 3 h, and fixed with 4% PFA. The cells were then visualized by using the LSM 980 with fast AiryScan confocal microscope.

For uncoating assays, A549 cells seeded in glass-bottom dishes were treated with the indicated siRNA for 48 h, and then infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 100 on ice for 1 h. The cells were washed three times, incubated at 37°C for 3 h in the presence of 1 mM CHX to block viral protein synthesis, and fixed with 4% PFA. The cells were then stained with a rabbit anti-M1 pAb and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), and visualized by confocal microscopy.

To specifically visualize the nuclear import of the incoming vRNP complex, A549 cells grown in glass-bottom dishes were transfected with the indicated siRNA. At 48 h post-transfection, the siRNA-treated cells were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus at an MOI of 50 on ice for 1 h. The cells were then incubated for 3, 4, or 5 h at 37°C in the presence of 1 mM CHX. After fixation with 4% PFA, the cells were stained with a mouse anti-NP mAb and Alexa Fluor 633 goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L), and visualized by confocal microscopy. In separate experiments, the nuclear import of incoming vRNP complex was also examined in ABTB1-overexpressing or TRIM4_KO A549 cells and the corresponding control cells that were infected with WSN (H1N1) virus (MOI = 50) in the presence of 1 mM CHX, with the same procedures as in the siRNA-treated cells.

To examine the co-localization of TRIM4β and IAV NP, A549 cells were transfected for 48 h to express Flag-TRIM4β, and were then infected with WSN (H1N1) virus (MOI = 5). At 4 h p.i., the infected cells were incubated with a mouse anti-NP mAb and a rabbit anti-Flag pAb, stained with Alexa Fluor 633 goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), and visualized by confocal microscopy. Co-localization of TRIM4β and IAV NP was analyzed by ZEN 2.3 imaging software.

To visualize the effect of ABTB1 on the expression of TRIM4, HEK293T cells were transfected individually or in combination with plasmids expressing Flag-ABTB1 and Myc-TRIM4β. At 48 h post-transfection, the cells were incubated with a rabbit anti-Flag pAb and a mouse anti-Myc mAb, stained with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) and Alexa Fluor 633 goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L), and visualized by confocal microscopy.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

The interaction between proteins expressed in HEK293T cells was examined by using a co-immunoprecipitation assay. HEK293T cells grown in 6-well plates were transfected with the indicated plasmids by using Lipofectamine LTX and Plus Reagents (Invitrogen). At 48 h post-transfection, the cells were washed twice with cold PBS and lysed with IP buffer (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA) containing complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) for 30 min on ice. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4°C for 10 min, and the supernatants were mixed with the indicated antibodies and Protein G-Agarose beads (Roche). The mixture was gently rocked overnight at 4°C, and washed four times with wash buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM PMSF). The bound proteins were then boiled in 2x SDS sample buffer, separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and detected by western blotting.

Western blotting

Protein samples separated by SDS-PAGE were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (GE Healthcare). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in PBST at RT for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4°C with appropriately diluted primary antibodies in PBS. After three washes with PBST, the membranes were incubated with DyLight 680 or 800 goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) and DyLight 680 or 800 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) at RT for 1 h. After three washes with PBST, the blots were visualized by using an Odyssey CLX infrared imaging system (Li-Cor BioSciences, Lincoln, NE, USA).

Protein degradation inhibitor treatments

To determine whether TRIM4 mediates the degradation of IAV NP via the proteasomal degradation pathway, HEK293T cells were transfected for 24 h with plasmids expressing Flag-TRIM4β and V5-WSN NP, and were then treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (10 μg/mL) for 8 h. To determine the pathway in which ABTB1 degrades TRIM4, HEK293T cells were transfected for 24 h with plasmids expressing ABTB1 and TRIM4β, and were then treated with DMSO, MG132 (10 μg/mL), and autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA, 5 mg/mL) or Chloroquine (CQ, 20 μM) for 8 h. Cell lysates were analyzed by western blotting with a rabbit anti-Flag pAb, rabbit anti-V5 pAb, mouse anti-ABTB1 mAb, or rabbit anti-TRIM4 pAb.

Ubiquitination assay

To analyze the effect of TRIM4 on the ubiquitination of IAV NP, HEK293T cells were transfected for 36 h with the indicated combinations of plasmids expressing Flag-WSN NP, Myc-TRIM4β, HA-Ub K48, and HA-Ub K48R. MG132 (10 μg/mL) was added to the cells at 8 h post-transfection. Whole cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with a mouse anti-Flag mAb, and analyzed by western blotting with a rabbit anti-HA pAb, rabbit anti-Flag pAb, and rabbit anti-Myc pAb.

Statistical analyses

Data were statistically analyzed by using the Student’s t test of GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). A P value of < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.
