Navigating the manifold: A transformative approach to AI image generation
Authors: Alec D. MacCall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Using an external starting image and sparse action prompts, Transformative AI treats image generation as iterative navigation through an irregular, high-dimensional manifold. This user-guided process preserves expressive development and explores regions apparently inaccessible to text-to-image workflows. I illustrate this with an example and compare it to a text-to-image equivalent.

Main text

Several recent studies have explored the limits of diversity exhibited by generative AI image systems, generally concluding that such systems produce a restricted repertoire of themes. My own experimental artistic use of generative AI has led me to similar observations. Here, I describe a modified approach to image-making intended to preserve variety and enhance the user’s creative influence.

Recent papers offer descriptive models of the latent space of an image library. Simonen et al.1 visualize it as a landscape, while Cropley2 and Hintze et al.3 favor a three-dimensional shape, though all agree the true dimensionality is far higher. I refer to this library as a “manifold” of indeterminate dimensionality, simplified to three dimensions for discussion. Cropley envisions the manifold as an implicitly solid box, concluding that the AI is constrained to think “inside the box.” Hintze et al. identify a small number of repeating themes—presumably attractor-like regions—that may reduce output diversity.

Users of generative AI have a limited set of inputs with which to navigate this manifold. The two recognized inputs are text and an initial image. Text-to-image prompting is the most common mode, often using elaborate descriptions. Less frequently, a starting image is supplied. Based on my experience with the system used by Copilot (specifications are unrevealed, but public comments suggest a generator similar to DALL·E 3, with Copilot acting as an intermediate “broker”), there appears to be a third, hidden input: a cache-like memory that temporarily stores visual and semantic information while a sequence of image modifications progresses. This resembles a hidden state vector in the parlance of Markov Brains,4 which is presumably functionally important but is undocumented and inaccessible to the user.

Copilot initially denied the existence of such a cache until I demonstrated it using an easily replicated probe. After loading the presumed cache through several iterations of image generation, I supplied a featureless mid-gray panel and asked for a demanding but vague transform called a “morph,” roughly equivalent to smearing the input. In a stateless system, this should yield a uniformly gray output or trivial variation. Instead, I consistently observed structured motifs—edges, textures, colors, and ghostlike forms—absent from the gray input but strongly resembling recent outputs. I refer to this as the “gray-panel morph test.”

Hintze et al.3 used a minimal Markov Brain configuration: a single image generator recursively connected to a single interpreter. The generator produced a temporary output image (the explicit state vector), which the interpreter converted into new text-to-image instructions, continuing until convergence to an invariant repeating image. In my Copilot experience, these convergences resembled the influence of a hidden cache. The lead author confirmed that their minimal network employed no intermediate broker or thread-like structure, so each component call was indeed separate and clean (A. Hintze, personal communication).

As a former modeler, I ground my artistic work in a hypothetical model that may be useful to other artists and perhaps to researchers. The presence of multiple attractor states within the manifold is well established. Rather than imagining attractors embedded in a solid mass, I envision the manifold as an irregular, perhaps amorphous, Swiss-cheese-like volume. It likely contains internal holes of unrepresented space; local density varies within represented regions and also near the “thin” areas between adjacent holes. This model allows for numerous novel locations—novel relative to existing representation—unlike Cropley’s solid box. From a local viewpoint, internal holes and the true external boundary may be nearly indistinguishable.

The Swiss-cheese image becomes more important in a dynamic model where image generation actively explores this space. Internal holes are navigationally easy to reach, enabling results not represented in the library. In this dynamic model, navigational paths move through fields of influence created by the manifold, the cache, and the textual input. The initial image contributes mainly an initial condition or location that is relatively independent of the manifold. These fields act as vector forces extending through holes and into unexplored regions.

This system appears to have a consistent grammatical geometry. Concrete nouns—identifiable “things”—indicate manifold locations that act as attractors. Verbs act independently of location and function as vector-like paths, moving the developing image along a corresponding “axis,” used here as a loose analogy to a principal component or eigenvector. Adverbs refine the orientation and strength of this axis. Abstract nouns and adjectives seem to behave as localized rather than global vectors. These grammatical roles are not apparent in text-to-image prompting, which emphasizes “things,” but they become clear when the user begins with a starting image and simplifies the text.

To gain artistic independence, I developed “Transformative AI,” a set of user-guided techniques that reduce the constraints imposed by the library manifold. Image generation always begins with an externally created initial image, ideally a hand-crafted work. To avoid system recognition (a persistent problem), the best initial images are minimally representational: sketches, doodles, and simple watercolor paintings occupy a simple “space” with easily defined operational axes. Detailed images—photographs or conventional AI images—contain too many competing semantic attractors and tend to be intractable.

Image processing uses sparse textual prompts that avoid recognizable things. The text typically instructs the system to perform an action. These actions move the image in directions the user anticipates will lead toward a desirable result. Restarts are easy, so failures are not problematic. The process is repeated iteratively and ends when the user is satisfied. This contrasts with text-to-image workflows, where the user begins with a relatively specific idea of the final image.

Subjectively, Figure 6 lacks the expressive tension and gestural quality of Figure 5. Figure 5 still feels as though it could represent a “real” place; Figure 6, semantically absorbed into the library manifold, is clearly imaginary. This comparison illustrates the core thesis: Transformative AI preserves expressive life by navigating the manifold through user-guided vector fields rather than allowing the system to collapse itself into an attractor-driven re-creation of library content.
