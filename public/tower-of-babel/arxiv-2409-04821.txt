Adjacency Labeling Schemes for Small Classes
Édouard Bonnet, Julien Duron, John Sylvester, Viktor Zamaraev

Abstract
A graph class admits an implicit representation 1 1 1 To simplify and lighten the abstract, we give the definition of implicit representations for factorial classes. The reader will find the general definition in the introduction. if, for every positive integer n n , its n n -vertex graphs have a b ⁡ ( n ) b(n) -bit (adjacency) labeling scheme with b ⁡ ( n ) = O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) b(n)=O(\log n) , i.e., their vertices can be labeled by binary strings of length b ⁡ ( n ) b(n) such that the presence of an edge between any pair of vertices u , v u,v can be deduced solely from the labels of u u and v v . The famous Implicit Graph Conjecture posited that every hereditary (i.e., closed under taking induced subgraphs) factorial (i.e., containing 2 O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) 2^{O(n\log n)} n n -vertex graphs) class admits an implicit representation. The conjecture was finally refuted [Hatami and Hatami, FOCS ’22], and does not even hold among monotone (i.e., closed under taking subgraphs) factorial classes [Bonnet et al., ICALP ’24]. However, monotone small (i.e., containing at most n ! ​ c n n!c^{n} many n n -vertex graphs for some constant c c ) classes do admit implicit representations. This motivates the Small Implicit Graph Conjecture : Every hereditary small class admits an O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) -bit labeling scheme. We provide evidence supporting the Small Implicit Graph Conjecture. First, we show that every small weakly sparse (i.e., excluding some fixed bipartite complete graph as a subgraph) class has an implicit representation. This is a consequence of the following fact of independent interest proven in the paper: Every weakly sparse small class has bounded expansion (hence, in particular, bounded degeneracy). The latter generalizes and strengthens the previous results that every monotone small class has bounded degeneracy [Bonnet et al., ICALP ’24], and that every weakly sparse class of bounded twin-width has bounded expansion [Bonnet et al., Combinatorial Theory ’22]. Second, we show that every hereditary small class admits an O ⁡ ( log 3 ⁡ n ) O(\log^{3}n) -bit labeling scheme, which provides a substantial improvement over the best-known polynomial upper bound of n 1 − ε n^{1-\varepsilon} on the size of adjacency labeling schemes for such classes. To do so, we establish that every small class has neighborhood complexity O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) , also of independent interest. We then apply a classic result, due to Welzl [SoCG ’88], on efficiently ordering the universe of a set system of low Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension such that every set can be described as the union of a limited number of intervals along this order.

Introduction

A
class
of graphs is a set of graphs which is closed under isomorphism.
For a class of graphs
\mathcal{X}
we denote by
\mathcal{X}_{n}
the set of graphs in
\mathcal{X}
with vertex set
[n]
.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be a class of graphs and
b:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
be a function.
A
b(n)
-bit adjacency labeling scheme
(or simply
b(n)
-bit labeling scheme
) for
\mathcal{X}
is a pair (encoder, decoder) of algorithms where for any
n
-vertex graph
G\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
the encoder assigns binary strings, called
labels
, of length
b(n)
to the vertices of
G
such that the adjacency between any pair of vertices can be inferred by the decoder only from their labels.
We note that the decoder depends on the class
\mathcal{X}
, but not on the graph
G
.
The function
b(\cdot)
is the
size
of the labeling scheme.
Adjacency labeling schemes were introduced by Kannan, Naor, and Rudich
[
KNR88
,
KNR92
]
,
and independently by Muller
[
Mul88
]
in the late 1980s and have been actively studied since then.

The binary word, obtained by concatenating labels of the vertices of a graph
G\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
assigned by an adjacency labeling scheme, uniquely determines graph
G
.
Thus, a
b(n)
-bit labeling scheme cannot represent more than
2^{nb(n)}
graphs on
n
vertices, and therefore, if
\mathcal{X}
admits a
b(n)
-bit labeling scheme, then
|\mathcal{X}_{n}|\leqslant 2^{nb(n)}
.
This implies a lower bound of
\frac{\log|\mathcal{X}_{n}|}{n}
on the size
b(n)
of any adjacency labeling scheme for
\mathcal{X}
.
We say that a graph class
\mathcal{X}
admits an
implicit representation
, if it admits an information-theoretic
order optimal
adjacency labeling scheme, i.e., if
\mathcal{X}
has a
b(n)
-bit labeling scheme, where
b(n)=O(\log|\mathcal{X}_{n}|/n)
.
A natural and important question is: which classes admit an adjacency labeling scheme of a size that matches this information-theoretic lower bound?

Of particular interest is the case of adjacency labeling schemes of size
O(\log n)
.
This is because, under the natural assumption that vertices get assigned pairwise distinct labels,
\lceil\log n\rceil
is a lower bound on the size of any labeling
scheme. Thus, understanding the expressive power of the labeling schemes of size of order
\log n
is a natural question.

By the above discussion adjacency labeling schemes of size
O(\log n)
can only exist for (at most)
factorial
graph classes, i.e., classes
\mathcal{X}
in which the number
|\mathcal{X}_{n}|
of
n
-vertex graphs grows not faster than
2^{\Theta(n\log n)}
. It is known that the latter condition alone is not enough to guarantee adjacency labels of size
O(\log n)

[
Mul88
]
. Kannan, Naor, and Rudich
[
KNR88
]
asked if the extra restriction on
\mathcal{X}
of being

, i.e., closed under taking induced subgraphs, would be enough for the existence of

-bit adjacency labeling scheme.
This question was later stated by Spinrad
[

]
in the form of a conjecture, reiterated by Scheinerman
[

.

(

Any hereditary factorial graph class admits an

-bit labeling scheme.

The conjecture remained open for three decades until it was recently refuted in a strong form by Hatami and Hatami, who showed that for any
\delta
there exists a hereditary factorial class that does not admit an
(n^{1/2-\delta})
-bit labeling scheme
[
HH22
]
. This refutation leaves wide open the question
of characterizing hereditary graph classes that admit
O(\log n)
-bit labeling schemes and no plausible conjectural dichotomy is currently available.

It was recently shown that IGC does not hold even in the family of
monotone
graph classes, the hereditary classes which are closed under taking (not necessarily induced) subgraphs
[
BDS+24b
]
. On the positive side, it was shown in the same work that IGC holds for every monotone

class, i.e., a monotone class
\mathcal{X}
with
|\mathcal{X}_{n}|\leqslant n!\cdot c^{n}
for some constant

The hereditary small classes form a subfamily of hereditary factorial classes that contains many classes of practical or theoretical importance. For instance, forests
[

]
, planar graphs
[
Tut62
]
, classes of bounded treewidth
[

]

All of these classes are known to admit order-optimal, and, sometimes, even asymptotically optimal adjacency labeling schemes.
For example, forests admit a

-bit labeling scheme
[

]
, planar graphs admit a
(1+o(1))\log n
-bit labeling scheme
[

]
, classes of bounded treewidth admit a
(1+o(1))\log n
-bit labeling scheme
[

]
, proper minor-closed classes admit a

It is known that not every hereditary (and even monotone) small class admits an asymptotically optimal labeling scheme of size

However, this does not rule out the existence of order optimal labeling schemes of size

Alon

To our knowledge, no better upper bound was established for hereditary small classes.

The importance of hereditary small classes and the existence of

1.1
Our contribution

In this paper we provide evidence toward the Small Implicit Graph Conjecture in two independent directions.
First, we show that the Small IGC holds in the important special case of weakly sparse graph classes.
Secondly, we obtain an adjacency labeling scheme of polylogarithmic size for any hereditary small class substantially improving upon the best-known polynomial-size upper bound on adjacency labeling schemes for such classes.

Our first result is a consequence of a structural property of small classes of graphs.
To state this property, we need to introduce some terms.
A hereditary class
\mathcal{X}
is said to be
weakly sparse
if there exists an integer
t
such that the complete bipartite graph
K_{t,t}
is not a subgraph of any graph of
\mathcal{X}
.
The
degeneracy
of a graph
G
is the minimum number
k
such that every induced subgraph of
G
contains a vertex of degree at most
k
.
We defer a formal definition of
bounded expansion
until
Section

2
as it is somewhat technical.
Instead, we note that
bounded expansion
generalizes
bounded degeneracy
, thus
Theorem

1.1
a fortiori holds when the former is replaced by the latter.

Theorem 1.1
.

Every weakly sparse small class has bounded expansion.

This structural insight into small graph classes is of independent interest due to the significance of weakly sparse graph classes.
The notion of weakly sparseness is the broadest form of sparsity.
It generalizes the properties of bounded degree, bounded degeneracy, and nowhere denseness
[
NO12
]
.
By the celebrated Kővári–Sós–Turán theorem
[
KST54
]
, among hereditary classes, weakly sparse classes are precisely the classes of graphs with a truly subquadratic number of edges.
It is often observed that the extra restriction of being weakly sparse significantly simplifies the structure of graphs possessing a particular property.
For example, weakly sparse graph classes of bounded clique-width have bounded treewidth
[
GW00
]
; weakly sparse graph classes of bounded twin-width have bounded expansion, and thus bounded degeneracy
[
BGK+22
]
(a fact that

1.1

[

]

[

]
; weakly sparse graph classes that are well-quasi-ordered by the induced-subgraph relation have bounded pathwidth
[
ALR19
]
, etc.

A conjecture from 2016
[

]
, driving an ongoing program in algorithmic graph theory, suggests
that, under some widely-believed complexity-theoretic assumption, a hereditary graph class admits a fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) first-order (FO) model-checking algorithm if and only if it is monadically dependent.

Theorem 1.3

This result is based on our second structural result of independent interest that hereditary small graph classes have low neighborhood complexity.

The neighborhood complexity is a measure of structural complexity of graphs. Informally (a formal definition is given in

We omit formal definitions and result statements, and refer the reader to the respective work.

Set systems, shatter functions, and neighborhood complexity.

A pair

is a family of subsets of

The

The

\pi_{\mathcal{S}}(m)=\max_{A\subseteq X,\,|A|=m}|\{Y\cap A~:~Y\in\mathcal{S}\}|.

The set system
(X^{*},\mathcal{S}^{*})

The shatter function of

is called the

of

Note that if

(X,S)

is the incidence matrix of
(X^{*},\mathcal{S}^{*})
.
A subset

.

Given a vertex

.

For neighborhood set systems, the primal and dual shatter functions coincide. That is, for any

\pi_{\mathcal{N}_{G}}(m)=\pi_{\mathcal{N}_{G}}^{*}(m)\qquad\text{for all }m\in[n].

\nu_{G}(m)=\pi_{\mathcal{N}_{G}}(m)=\pi_{\mathcal{N}_{G}}^{*}(m)

.

Given a graph class

, the

of

Together with the known labeling scheme for bounded degeneracy graphs (see e.g.

The proof of

contains

This is a consequence of the forward direction of a characterization of classes of (un)bounded expansion by Dvořák

Theorem 3.1

(
[

A hereditary graph class

has unbounded expansion if and only if there is an integer

\mathcal{X}
contains the

Theorem 3.2

(
[
KO04
, Theorem 2]
)

For all

We now extract the announced fact, of which we will only need the

Corollary 3.3

A weakly sparse graph class

is of unbounded expansion if and only if there is an integer

such that for any integer

contains the

Proof.

The

For the

.
If

If instead

contains graphs of arbitrarily large average degree.
Therefore,

contains 1-subdivisions of graphs of arbitrarily large average degree, i.e., the sought statement holds for

∎

Equipped with this tool, the proof of

shows the contrapositive as follows.
Assuming a weakly sparse class
\mathcal{X}
has unbounded expansion, we fix a positive integer

\mathcal{X}
contains

Let

First, we extract from

with large minimum degree that contains a bounded-degree spanning tree. This is possible due to

and the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4

(

, Lemma 3.3]
)

Let

be a graph of minimum degree

Then

has an induced subgraph

of minimum degree at least

Next, we use the properties of

to show that it contains many (not necessarily induced) subgraphs each with a small number of automorphisms, which are translated to many

-subdivision of the original graph

by counting subgraphs of

The quantitative connection is made possible by the following observations.

Observation 3.5

For any integer

and

and

are isomorphic.

Observation 3.6
.

Let

be a graph.
For any integer

of

, the graph
r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(H)
is isomorphic to an induced subgraph of
r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(F)
.

Observation 3.7
.

Let
G
be a connected graph containing a vertex of degree at least
3
, then

\mathrm{aut}(r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(G))=\mathrm{aut}(G).

Proof.

We start by showing that any automorphism
\varphi
of
r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(G)
maps branching vertices to branching vertices and subdivision vertices to subdivision vertices. First, note that as
G
has a vertex of degree at least
3
, the graph
r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(G)
also has a vertex of degree at least
3
(hence a branching vertex) say
u
, which must be mapped by
\varphi
to a vertex of degree at least
3
(hence, to a branching vertex), say
v
.
Now, since
G
is connected, the branching vertices are exactly the ones that are at distances
0~(\text{mod}~r+1)
from
u
, and they are mapped to vertices that are at distances
0~(\text{mod}~r+1)
from
v
, and thus, to branching vertices.
Hence, the restriction
\tilde{\varphi}
of
\varphi
to the branching vertices of
r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(G)
is a permutation of the vertices of
G
.
We claim that
\tilde{\varphi}
is an automorphism of
G
.
Indeed, two vertices
a
and
b
are adjacent in
G

if and only if

a
and
b
are at distance
r+1
in
r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(G)

if and only if

\varphi(a)
and
\varphi(b)
are at distance
r+1
in
r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(G)

if and only if

\tilde{\varphi}(a)=\varphi(a)
and
\tilde{\varphi}(b)=\varphi(b)
are adjacent in
G
.

From this, we deduce that
\varphi\mapsto\tilde{\varphi}
is a bijection between the automorphisms of
r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(G)
and the automorphisms of
G
: it is injective since an automorphism of
r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(G)
is uniquely determined by the images of the branching vertices, and surjective since there are at least as many automorphisms of
r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(G)
as there are of

. Hence,
\mathrm{aut}(r\text{-}\operatorname{subd}(G))=\mathrm{aut}(G)
.
∎

Before proceeding to our main result, we provide some basic facts about automorphisms, which we will rely on.
Given two graphs
F
and
G

\#\mathrm{Sub}(F\rightarrow G)
the number of subgraphs of
G
isomorphic to

, and by
\#\mathrm{Emb}(F\rightarrow G)
the number of embeddings of

into

the number of injective homomorphisms from
F
to
G

\#\mathrm{Emb}(F\rightarrow G)=\#\mathrm{Sub}(F\rightarrow G)\cdot\mathrm{aut}(F).

For instance, if
G
is the cycle on an even number
n
of vertices, and
F
is the disjoint union of
n/2
edges, then
\#\mathrm{Sub}(F\rightarrow G)=2
and
\mathrm{aut}(F)=(n/2)!\cdot 2^{\frac{n}{2}}
, so
\#\mathrm{Emb}(F\rightarrow G)=(n/2)!\cdot 2^{\frac{n}{2}+1}
.

We will use the following two well known facts (see
[
KLT02
]
).

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
F
be a spanning subgraph of a graph
G
. Then

\mathrm{aut}(G)\leqslant\#\mathrm{Emb}(F\rightarrow G)=\#\mathrm{Sub}(F\rightarrow G)\cdot\mathrm{aut}(F).

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
G
be a connected graph of maximum degree
\Delta
.
Then

\mathrm{aut}(G)\leqslant n\cdot\Delta!\cdot(\Delta-1)^{n-\Delta-1}\leqslant n\Delta^{n}.

We apply the above two facts to upper bound the number of automorphisms of a graph containing a bounded-degree spanning tree in terms its average degree.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
and
G
be a connected
n
-vertex graph with at most
dn
edges, which has a spanning tree
T
of maximum degree
\tau
. Then
\mathrm{aut}(G)\leqslant(6\tau d)^{n}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.9
,
\mathrm{aut}(T)\leqslant n\tau^{n}\leqslant(2\tau)^{n}
.
Moreover,
\#\mathrm{Sub}(T\rightarrow G)
is at most the number of ways of choosing
n-1
edges among
E(G)
. Hence

\#\mathrm{Sub}(T\rightarrow G)\leqslant\binom{dn}{n-1}\leqslant\left(\frac{edn}{n-1}\right)^{n-1}=\left(1+\frac{1}{n-1}\right)^{n-1}\cdot(ed)^{n-1}\leqslant e(ed)^{n-1}\leqslant(ed)^{n},

where in the penultimate inequality we used the fact that
1+x\leqslant e^{x}
for any real
x
.
Hence, by
Lemma

3.8
, we have
\mathrm{aut}(G)\leqslant\#\mathrm{Sub}(T\rightarrow G)\cdot\mathrm{aut}(T)\leqslant(ed)^{n}\cdot(2\tau)^{n}\leqslant(6\tau d)^{n}
.
∎

4
Short adjacency labels for small classes

In this section we prove:

See
1.3

To do so, we first show a result of independent interest (in
Section

4.1
), that the neighborhood complexity of any small class is at most
O(n\log n)
. Then, we prove in
Section

4.2
that such neighborhood complexity implies
O(\log^{2}n)
contiguity. The latter together with
Proposition

2.2
implies
Theorem

1.3
.
The core part of this proof strategy is to show how the bound on the neighborhood complexity can be translated to the bound on contiguity.
This is done via a classical result from the theory of set systems of bounded Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension on so-called
paths with low crossing number
(
Theorem

4.1
) applied to neighborhood set systems.
While the result about paths with low crossing number is a known fact, we need it in somewhat unusual form that we could not find elsewhere in the literature.
Therefore, in
Appendix

A
we present a self-contained proof of this result in a suitable form.

4.1
Neighborhood complexity of small classes

In this section we show that small classes have low neighborhood complexity.

See
1.4

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a hereditary small class of graphs and
c\geqslant 1
be a constant such that
|\mathcal{C}_{n}|\leqslant n!\cdot c^{n}
holds for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
To prove the claim, we will show that for every graph
H
in
\mathcal{C}
it holds that

\nu_{H}(m)<9c^{2}m\log(m+1)\text{ \qquad for all }m\in[|V(H)|].

Suppose this is not the case. Then, by definition, there exists a graph
H\in\mathcal{C}
and an
n
-element set
A\subseteq V(H)
such that

|\{N_{H}(v)\cap A~:~v\in V(H)\}|\geqslant 9c^{2}n\log(n+1)
.
Note that the latter inequality implies
n\geqslant 4
.
Let
B^{\prime}\subseteq V(H)
be any maximum-size set of vertices whose neighborhoods have pairwise distinct intersections with
A
in the graph
H
, and let
B:=B^{\prime}\setminus A
.
Denote by
G
the subgraph of
H
induced by
A\cup B
.
By the assumption, we have

1.

|A|=n
,

2.

|B|\geqslant|B^{\prime}|-|A|\geqslant 9c^{2}n\log(n+1)-n>8c^{2}n\log n
,

3.

A\cap B=\emptyset
, and

4.

all vertices in
B
have pairwise distinct neighborhoods in
A
in graph
G
.

Let
n_{1}:=n\lfloor\log n\rfloor
and
N:=n_{1}+n
.
We will show that the hereditary closure of
G
contains more than
N!c^{N}
labeled graphs on
N
vertices, i.e., that
|\text{Lab}(\text{IndSub}_{N}(G))|>N!c^{N}
, where
\text{IndSub}_{N}(G)
is the set of
N
-vertex induced subgraphs of
G
.
This would contradict our assumption on the number of graphs in
\mathcal{C}
.
To do this, let us first fix a labeling of
A
using the integers from
1
to
n
.
We claim that any two distinct labeled subsets
X_{1},X_{2}\subseteq B
of size
n_{1}
, labeled with the integers from
n+1
to
N
, induce two distinct labeled graphs
G_{1}=G[A\cup X_{1}]
and
G_{2}=G[A\cup X_{2}]
contained in
\mathcal{C}
.
Indeed, since all vertices in
B
have pairwise distinct neighborhoods in
A
,
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
contain a vertex with the same label in
[n+1,N]
but different neighborhoods in
A
(i.e. different neighborhoods among vertices with labels from
[n]
).

Hence, the number of
N
-vertex labeled graphs in
\mathcal{C}
that are induced subgraphs of
G
is lower bounded by the number of ways to choose a labeled subset of size
n_{1}
from
B
, which is

n_{1}!\cdot\binom{|B|}{n_{1}}\geqslant n_{1}!\cdot\left(\frac{|B|}{n_{1}}\right)^{n_{1}}>n_{1}!\cdot(8c^{2})^{n_{1}}.

(4)

We want to show that this is more than the total number of
N
-vertex labeled graphs in
\mathcal{C}
, which, by assumption, does not exceed

N!\cdot c^{N}=(n_{1}+n)!\cdot c^{n_{1}+n}\leqslant n_{1}!\cdot(n_{1}+n)^{n}\cdot c^{2n_{1}}.

(5)

From
Eq.

4
and
Eq.

5

n_{1}!\cdot(8c^{2})^{n_{1}}\geqslant n_{1}!\cdot(n_{1}+n)^{n}\cdot c^{2n_{1}},

which is equivalent to establishing that

8^{n_{1}}=2^{3n_{1}}\geqslant(n_{1}+n)^{n}=2^{n\log(n_{1}+n)}.

The latter indeed holds since

\displaystyle n\log(n_{1}+n)

\displaystyle\leqslant n\log(2n\lfloor\log n\rfloor)

\displaystyle\leqslant n\log 2+n\log n+n\log\lfloor\log n\rfloor

\displaystyle\leqslant n(1+\log 2)+n\lfloor\log n\rfloor+n\log\lfloor\log n\rfloor

\displaystyle\leqslant n\lfloor\log n\rfloor+n\lfloor\log n\rfloor+n\log\lfloor\log n\rfloor

\displaystyle\leqslant n_{1}+n_{1}+n_{1}=3n_{1},

where the penultimate inequality holds because
n\geqslant 4
.

4.2
From neighborhood complexity to contiguity

In this section, we first show how to translate a bound on neighborhood complexity to a bound on contiguity (
Theorem

4.3
), and then apply it to obtain bounds on contiguity for hereditary small classes (
Theorem

4.4
).
A crucial technical tool that we use to establish this translation is a suitable form of a known result about paths with low crossing number (
Theorem

4.1
). To state this result we must first introduce the required notions.

Let
(X,\mathcal{S})
be a set system.
A set
S\in\mathcal{S}

crosses
a 2-element set
\{x,y\}\subset X
if exactly one of
x
and
y
belongs to
S
.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a multiset of 2-element subsets in
X
.
The
crossing number of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to a set
S\in\mathcal{S}
is the number of elements in
\mathcal{F}
that are crossed by
S
.
The
crossing number of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
\mathcal{S}
is the maximum crossing number of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to a set
S\in\mathcal{S}
.
If
(X,\mathcal{F})
, considered as a graph, is a path spanning all elements of
X
, then we say that
(X,\mathcal{F})
is a path on
X
.
In this case, the crossing number of
\mathcal{F}
is referred to as the crossing number of the corresponding path.
We are now ready to state the main technical tool whose self-contained proof can be found in
Appendix

A
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
be an
n
-element set,
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
be a strictly increasing function, and
d
be a natural number.
Let
(X,\mathcal{S})
be a set system of VC dimension
d
that satisfies
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}^{*}(m)\leqslant f(m)
for all
m\in[n]
.
Then there exists a path on
X
with crossing number at most

2\log|\mathcal{S}|+10d\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{f^{-1}(j/2)}.

We will apply
Theorem

4.1
to neighborhood set systems.
Its importance in our context is due to (a) the fact that the dual shatter function of the neighborhood set system of a graph coincides with its primal dual function (
Observation

2.3
), i.e., the neighborhood complexity of the graph; and
(b) the following observation about close relationship between the crossing number of a path for the neighborhood set system and the contiguity of the graph.

Observation 4.2
.

Let
G
be an
n
-vertex graph.
If there exists a path on
V(G)
that has crossing number with respect to
\mathcal{N}_{G}
at most
k
, then the contiguity of
G
is at most
k/2+1
.

Proof.

Let
\{\{v_{1},v_{2}\},\{v_{2},v_{3}\},\ldots,\{v_{n-1},v_{n}\}\}
be a path on
V(G)
with crossing number with respect to
\mathcal{N}_{G}
at most
k
.
We claim that the linear ordering
\sigma=v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
of
V(G)
witnesses the target bound on the contiguity of
G
.

Let
v
be an arbitrary vertex in
G
and let
N
be its neighborhood.
For convenience, mark a vertex of
G
by 1 if it belongs to
N
and 0 otherwise.
This marking together with the ordering
\sigma
correspond to the binary word
w=w_{1}w_{2}\ldots w_{n}
, where
w_{i}
is the mark of
v_{i}
for every
i\in[n]
.
A relation between pairs crossed and neighborhoods partitioned now follows from two observations:

1.

in the word
w
, the maximal intervals of consecutive 1s correspond to intervals of consecutive vertices in
\sigma
that partition the neighborhood
N
of
v
;

2.

each pair of consecutive letters
w_{i}w_{i+1}
in
w
with
w_{i}\neq w_{i+1}
corresponds to a crossing of
\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}
by
N
.

To conclude, each interval in the partition of
N
corresponds to two crossings apart from the at most two intervals at each end of the ordering which each corresponds to one crossing. Thus the number of intervals is at most
2+\lfloor\frac{k-2}{2}\rfloor\leqslant k/2+1
, as claimed.
∎

The next theorem translates a bound on neighborhood complexity to a bound on contiguity. It follows directly from
Theorem

4.1
,
Observation

4.2
,
Observation

2.3
, and the definition of neighborhood complexity.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
G
be an
n
-vertex graph,
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
be a strictly increasing function, and
d
be a natural number.
If
(V,\mathcal{N}_{G})
has VC dimension
d
and
\nu_{G}(m)\leqslant f(m)
for all
m\in[n]
, then

\mathrm{ctg}(G)\leqslant 1+\log n+5d\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{f^{-1}(j/2)}.

We now apply
Theorem

4.3
to hereditary small classes, which have
O(n\log n)
neighborhood complexity by
Theorem

1.4
, to obtain a bound on their contiguity in closed form.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a hereditary small class, then the contiguity of
\mathcal{X}
is
O(\log^{2}n)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{X}
is a small class, by
Theorem

1.4
there exists some constant
C
such that
\nu_{\mathcal{X}}\leqslant f(n)
where
f(n):=Cn\log n
.
Consequently, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
n
-vertex graph
G\in\mathcal{X}
, we have
\pi_{\mathcal{N}_{G}}(m)\leqslant f(m)
for all
m\in[n]
.
Thus, by definition, the VC dimension
d
of
(V(G),\mathcal{N}_{G})

satisfies
2^{d}\leqslant Cd\log d
, which implies

d\leqslant\log C+\log d+\log\log d\leqslant\log C+4d/5,

and therefore
d\leqslant 4\log C
.

Now, by
Theorem

4.3
, we have

\mathrm{ctg}(G)\leqslant 1+\log n+20\cdot\log C\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{f^{-1}(j)}.

(6)

Since
f
is increasing on
[1,\infty)
with image
[0,\infty)
, its inverse
f^{-1}
exists on
[0,\infty)
, and is also increasing.
The exact inverse
f^{-1}
is somewhat complicated, in particular
f^{-1}(1)=e^{W(\ln(2)/C)}
, where
W(\cdot)
is the Lambert function.
We consider
f^{-1}(1)>0
just as a constant depending on
C
, and show that

f^{-1}(x)\geqslant\frac{x}{C\log x}\qquad\text{ for all }x\geqslant 2.

(7)

To show (
7
), since
\log x\geqslant 1
for all
x\geqslant 2
and we can assume
C\geqslant 1
, we have

f\left(\frac{x}{C\log x}\right)=C\cdot\frac{x}{C\log x}\cdot\log\left(\frac{x}{C\log x}\right)\leqslant x.

(8)

Thus, applying
f^{-1}
to both sides of (
8
) gives
f^{-1}(x)\geqslant\frac{x}{C\log x}
, establishing (
7
).

Then, by (
6
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{ctg}(G)

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\log n+20\cdot\log C\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{f^{-1}(j)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\log n+20\cdot\log C\cdot e^{-W(\ln(2)/C)}+20\cdot\log C\cdot\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{C\log j}{j}

\displaystyle=\Theta(\log^{2}n),

as claimed.
∎

The main
Theorem

1.3
of this section is a consequence of
Theorems

1.4
,
4.4
and
2.2
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we obtained strong evidence in support of the Small Implicit Graph Conjecture that posits that every hereditary small class admits an
O(\log n)
-bit adjacency labeling scheme. Specifically, we showed that (1) every weakly sparse small class admits such a labeling scheme; (2) every hereditary small class admits an
O(\log^{3}n)
-bit adjacency labeling scheme.
To obtain these results we established two structural properties of hereditary small classes that are of independent interest.
The first property is that every weakly sparse small class has bounded degeneracy, and even, bounded expansion.
The second property is that every hereditary small class has neighborhood complexity
O(n\log n)
.

The latter property leaves a tantalizing open question of whether the bound on the neighborhood complexity can be further improved.
All hereditary small classes known to us have linear neighborhood complexity (e.g., classes of bounded twin-width
[
BKR+22
,
BFLP24
]
), which motivates the following:

Conjecture 5.1
.

Every hereditary small class of graphs has neighborhood complexity
O(n)
.

If this conjecture is true, our approach would imply
O(\log^{2}n)
-bit labeling schemes for all hereditary small classes.

Appendix A
Paths with low crossing number

The main goal of this section is to provide a self-contained proof of
Theorem

4.1
.
To state the theorem, we recall definitions of some crucial notions.
Given a set system
(X,\mathcal{S})
, we say that a set
S\in\mathcal{S}

crosses
a 2-element set
\{x,y\}\subset X
if exactly one of
x
and
y
belongs to
S
.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a multiset of 2-element subsets in
X
. The crossing number of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to a set
S\in\mathcal{S}
is the number of elements in
\mathcal{F}
that are crossed by
S
. The crossing number of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
\mathcal{S}
is the maximum crossing number of
\mathcal{F}
with respect to a set
S\in\mathcal{S}
.
If
(X,\mathcal{F})
, considered as a graph, is a tree or a path spanning all elements of
X
, then we say that

(X,\mathcal{F})
is a tree or a path on
X
, respectively. In this case, the crossing number of
\mathcal{F}
is referred to as the crossing number of the corresponding tree or path, respectively.

See
4.1

This theorem is a known result stated in an unusual form, but one that is useful for us.
The main feature of the above formulation is that the bound on the dual shatter function is given in a general form (expressed as a function
f
) rather than polynomial; in turn, the bound on the crossing number is expressed via the inverse of
f
.
More specifically, the above formulation has two main differences compared to the standard one (for instance
[
Mat10
,
Wel88
]
).
First, in a standard formulation, the bound on the dual shatter function is
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}^{*}(m)\leqslant m^{r}
for some constant
r>1
.
Second, the resulting bound on the crossing number of a path on
X
is in the form of
O(n^{1-1/r})
.
Note that if we take
f(m)
to be
m^{r}
in
Theorem

4.1
, then
f^{-1}(m)=m^{1/r}
and

2\log|\mathcal{S}|+10d\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(j/2)^{1/r}}

can be upper bounded by
O(n^{1-1/r})
, which recovers the result in a standard form.

The benefit of the present formulation is that one can apply
Theorem

4.1
when a preferable upper bound on
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}^{*}(m)
is non-polynomial. For example, if
f(m)=m\cdot g(m)
for some non-decreasing function
g(m)
, then
Theorem

4.1
gives a bound of
O(g(n)\cdot\log n)
on the crossing number. In particular, this becomes useful when
g(m)=m^{o(1)}
; e.g., if
g(m)=\log(m)
, the bound on the crossing number becomes
O(\log^{2}n)
.

To provide a self-contained proof of
Theorem

4.1
,
we need to state a number of auxiliary known results in a suitable (non-standard) form and
repeat their proofs with some adjustments. Before each statement we explain how its formulation and proof differs from standard ones. We hope this exposition might be of use in some other work.

Packing lemma.

We begin with Haussler’s Packing Lemma
[
Hau95
]
(
Lemma

A.1
), which is an improvement over a quantitatively weaker Packing Lemma obtained by Dudley
[
Dud78
]
and re-discovered by Welzl
[
Wel88
]
. The proof below is a simplification by Chazelle of Haussler’s proof
[
Hau95
]
, which is taken from Matoušek’s book
[
Mat10
]
.

A set system
(X,\mathcal{S})
is
\delta
-separated

if for any two distinct sets
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
their symmetric difference contains at least
\delta
elements, i.e.,
|(S_{1}\setminus S_{2})\cup(S_{2}\setminus S_{1})|\geqslant\delta
.
In a standard formulation, the lemma assumes that the shatter function
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}(m)
is bounded by
O(m^{r})
for some
r
, and the bound on the cardinality of a
\delta
-separated set
\mathcal{P}
is
O((n/\delta)^{r})
.
For our purposes, in
Lemma

A.1
, we state the bound on
|\mathcal{P}|
directly in terms of the shatter function.
Note that the standard form of the lemma follows from ours if one uses the bound
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}(m)=O(m^{r})
.
The difference of the proof below from the one in
[
Mat10
]
is that in
Eq.

13

we bound
t
in terms of the shatter function directly, rather than in terms of the bound on the shatter function.

Lemma A.1

(Packing Lemma, see
[
Mat10
, Lemma 5.14]
)
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{S})
be a set system on an
n
-element
set of VC dimension
d
.
Let
\delta\in[n]
be an integer, and let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
be
\delta
-separated.
Then,

|\mathcal{P}|\leqslant 2\cdot\pi_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\left\lceil\frac{4dn}{\delta}\right\rceil\right).

Before we prove this lemma we must introduce an auxiliary graph construction. For a set system
(X,\mathcal{S})
, we define the
unit distance graph

\operatorname{UD}(\mathcal{S})
to be the graph with

V(\operatorname{UD}(\mathcal{S}))=\mathcal{S}\qquad\text{and}\qquad E(\operatorname{UD}(\mathcal{S}))=\{\{S,S^{\prime}\}:|S\Delta S^{\prime}|=1\}.

We will also need the following bound on the number of its edges.

Lemma A.2

(
[
Mat10
, Lemma 5.15]
)
.

If
(X,\mathcal{S})
is a set system of VC dimension
d
on a finite set
X
then
the unit distance graph
\operatorname{UD}(\mathcal{S})
has at most
d|\mathcal{S}|
edges.

With this, we are now ready to prove the Packing Lemma.

Proof of
Lemma

A.1
.

Choose a random
s
-element subset
A\subseteq X
, where
s
is given by

s:=\left\lceil\frac{4dn}{\delta}\right\rceil.

(9)

Set
\mathcal{Q}:=\{S\cap A~:~S\in\mathcal{P}\}
, and for each set
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
define its weight
w(Q)
as the number of sets
S\in\mathcal{P}
with
S\cap A~=Q
. Note that

\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}w(Q)=|\mathcal{P}|.

Let
E:=E(\operatorname{UD}(\mathcal{Q}))
be the edge set of the unit distance graph
\operatorname{UD}(\mathcal{Q})
, and define an edge weighting of
\operatorname{UD}(\mathcal{Q})
by giving each edge
e=\{Q,Q^{\prime}\}\in E
weight
w(e):=\min(w(Q),w(Q^{\prime}))
. Let

W=\sum_{e\in E}w(e),

where we note that this random variable depends on the random choice of
A
. The desired bound on
|\mathcal{P}|
in the Packing Lemma is obtained by estimating the expectation of
W
in two different ways.

First, we claim that for any set
A\subseteq X
, we have the following (deterministic) bound on
W

W\leqslant 2d\cdot\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}w(Q)=2d\cdot|\mathcal{P}|.

(10)

To see this, note first that the VC dimension of
\mathcal{P}
is at most
d
, and thus, by
Lemma

A.2
, the unit distance graph
\operatorname{UD}(\mathcal{Q})
has some vertex
Q_{0}
of degree at most
2d
. By removing
Q_{0}
, the total edge weight drops by at most
2d\cdot w(Q_{0})
, and we are left with a unit distance graph on
(X,\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})
, where
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\mathcal{Q}\setminus Q_{0}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
has VC dimension at most
d
. We can thus repeat this process until no vertices are left, and we see that the sum of edge weights removed is at most
2d
times the sum of vertex weights, as claimed.

Next, we bound the expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\,W\,\right]
from below by considering the following
random experiment. First, we choose a random
(s-1)
-element set
A\subseteq X
, and then we choose a random element
a\in X\setminus A^{\prime}
. The set
A=A^{\prime}\cup\{a\}
is a uniform random
s
-element subset of
X
, we consider
\operatorname{UD}(\mathcal{Q})
with the same edge weighting as above. Each edge of
\operatorname{UD}(\mathcal{Q})
is a pair of sets of
\mathcal{Q}
differing in exactly one element of
A
. We let
E_{1}\subseteq E
be the edges for which the difference element is
a
, and let
W_{1}
be the sum of their weights. By symmetry, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\,W\,\right]=s\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\,W_{1}\,\right].

(11)

We are going to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\,W_{1}\,\right]
from below.
Let
A^{\prime}\subset X
be an arbitrary but fixed
(s-1)
-element set.
We estimate the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\,W_{1}\mid A^{\prime}\,\right]
;
that is, the expected value of
W_{1}
when conditioned on a fixed set
A^{\prime}
, and
a\in X\setminus A^{\prime}
is selected uniformly at random.
Divide the sets of
\mathcal{P}
into equivalence classes
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{P}_{t}
according to their intersection with the set
A^{\prime}
. By the definition of
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}(x)
and since it is non-decreasing, we have

t\leqslant\pi_{\mathcal{S}}(s-1)\leqslant\pi_{\mathcal{S}}(s).

(12)

Let
\mathcal{P}_{i}
be one of the equivalence classes, and
b:=|\mathcal{P}_{i}|
. Suppose that an element
a\in X\setminus A^{\prime}
has been chosen. If
b_{1}
sets of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
contain
a
and
b_{2}=b-b_{1}
sets do not contain
a
, then the class
\mathcal{P}_{i}
gives rise
to an edge of
E_{1}
of weight
\min(b_{1},b_{2})
. Note that
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
depend on the choice of
a
while
b
does not.

For any non-negative real numbers
b_{1},b_{2}
with
b_{1}+b_{2}=b
, we have
\min(b_{1},b_{2})\geqslant b_{1}b_{2}/b
.
The value
b_{1}b_{2}
is the number of ordered pairs of sets
(S_{1},S_{2})
with
S_{1},S_{2}
being two sets from the class
\mathcal{P}_{i}
where one contains
a
and the other one does not.
Now, since
\mathcal{P}
is
\delta
-separated by hypothesis, if
S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
are two distinct sets, then they differ in at least
\delta
elements.
Therefore, the probability that
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
differ in a random element
a\in X\setminus A^{\prime}
is at least
\frac{\delta}{n-s+1}\geqslant\delta/n
.
Hence the expected contribution of each pair
(S_{1},S_{2})
of distinct sets of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
to the quantity
b_{1}b_{2}
is at least
\delta/n
, thus

\mathbb{E}\left[\,b_{1}b_{2}\mid A^{\prime}\,\right]\geqslant b(b-1)\cdot\frac{\delta}{n}.

Consequently, the expected contribution
\mathbb{E}\left[\,\min(b_{1},b_{2})\mid A^{\prime}\,\right]
of the equivalence class
\mathcal{P}_{i}
to the sum of edge weights
W_{1}
is at least
\mathbb{E}\left[\,b_{1}b_{2}/b\mid A^{\prime}\,\right]\geqslant(b-1)\delta/n
.
Summing up over all equivalence classes, and using the bound on
t
from (
12
),

\mathbb{E}\left[\,W_{1}\,\right]\geqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\,W_{1}\mid A^{\prime}\,\right]\geqslant\frac{\delta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left(|\mathcal{P}_{i}|-1\right)\geqslant\frac{\delta}{n}\left(|\mathcal{P}|-t\right)\geqslant\frac{\delta}{n}\left(|\mathcal{P}|-\pi_{\mathcal{S}}(s)\right).

(13)

Combining this with the estimate (
10
), the equality (
11
), and the definition (
9
) of
s
, leads to

2d\cdot|\mathcal{P}|\geqslant s\cdot\frac{\delta}{n}\cdot\left(|\mathcal{P}|-\pi_{\mathcal{S}}(s)\right)\geqslant 4d\cdot\left(|\mathcal{P}|-\pi_{\mathcal{S}}(s)\right).

Rearranging gives
|\mathcal{P}|\leqslant 2\cdot\pi_{\mathcal{S}}(s)
, so the statement follows from (
9
).
∎

Short Edge Lemma.

A standard form of the Short Edge Lemma assumes a polynomial bound on the dual shatter function, i.e.,
\pi^{*}_{\mathcal{S}}(m)=O(m^{r})
for some constant
r>1
,
and the resulting bound is expressed via the bound
O(m^{1/r})
on the inverse of
\pi^{*}_{\mathcal{S}}(m)
.
In our presentation of the Short Edge Lemma (
Lemma

A.3
) below we use a bound on the dual shatter function in a general from expressed as a function
f
, and the resulting bound is stated in terms of
f^{-1}
, the inverse of
f
. As before, by plugging in the bound
\pi^{*}_{\mathcal{S}}(m)=O(m^{r})
in
Lemma

A.3
one would recover the standard form of the lemma.
The proof is taken from
[
Mat10
]
with suitable adjustments.

Lemma A.3

(Short Edge Lemma, see
[
Mat10
, Lemma 5.18]
)
.

Let
X
be an
n
-element set,
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
be a strictly increasing function, and
d
be a natural number.
Let
(X,\mathcal{S})
be a set system of VC dimension
d
with
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}^{*}(m)\leqslant f(m)
for all
m\in[n]
.
Then, for any multiset
\mathcal{Q}
with elements from
\mathcal{S}
, there exist elements
x,y\in X
such that the edge
\{x,y\}
is crossed by at most

5d\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{Q}|}{f^{-1}(n/2)}

sets of
\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof.

We form a set system
\mathcal{D}
which is dual to
\mathcal{Q}
. That is, we consider the multiset
\mathcal{Q}
as the ground set (if some set appears in
\mathcal{Q}
several times, then it is considered with the appropriate multiplicity). For each
x\in X
, we let
D_{x}
be the set of all sets of
\mathcal{Q}
containing
x
, and we set

\mathcal{D}=\{D_{x}:x\in X\}.

The symmetric difference
D_{x}\Delta D_{y}
of two sets from
\mathcal{D}
consists of the sets in
\mathcal{Q}
crossing the pair
\{x,y\}
. We thus want to show that
D_{x}\Delta D_{y}
is small for some
x\neq y
. We may assume
D_{x}\neq D_{y}
for
x\neq y
, as otherwise we are done, and hence
|\mathcal{D}|=|X|=n
.

The primal shatter function
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}
is certainly no larger than the dual shatter function
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
, and hence
\pi_{\mathcal{D}}(m)\leqslant f(m)
by the assumption on
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}^{*}
. Suppose that any two sets
D_{x},D_{y}\in\mathcal{D}
have symmetric difference at least
\delta
, then the packing lemma (
Lemma

A.1
) implies

n=|\mathcal{D}|\leqslant 2\cdot\pi_{\mathcal{S}}^{*}\left(\left\lceil\frac{4d|\mathcal{Q}|}{\delta}\right\rceil\right)\leqslant 2f\left(\left\lceil\frac{4d|\mathcal{Q}|}{\delta}\right\rceil\right).

Since
f
is strictly increasing,
f^{-1}
is also strictly increasing. Therefore, we have

f^{-1}(n/2)\leqslant\left\lceil\frac{4d|\mathcal{Q}|}{\delta}\right\rceil\leqslant\frac{5d|\mathcal{Q}|}{\delta},

and therefore

\delta\leqslant 5d\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{Q}|}{f^{-1}(n/2)},

as claimed.
∎

Trees with low crossing number.

As with the Short Edge Lemma, the following result (
Theorem

A.4
) is stated with a general bound
f
on the dual shatter function and the resulting bound on the crossing number of a tree is expressed in terms of
f^{-1}
, the inverse of
f
.
The proof is taken from
[
Mat10
]
, which is very similar to Welzl’s original proof
[
Wel88
]
.
The statement in
[
Mat10
]
is for crossing number of matchings, and therefore it is slightly modified to work for trees as in
[
Wel88
]
.

Theorem A.4

(see
[
Mat10
, Lemma 5.17]
)
.

Let
X
be an
n
-element set,
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
be a strictly increasing function, and
d
be a natural number.
Let
(X,\mathcal{S})
be a set system of VC dimension
d
with
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}^{*}(m)\leqslant f(m)
for all
m\in[n]
.
Then there exists a tree
T
on
X
with crossing number at most

\log|\mathcal{S}|+5d\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{f^{-1}(j/2)}.

Proof.

We build
T
by selecting edges one by one according to the following strategy.

1.

Suppose
\{u_{1},v_{1}\},\ldots,\{u_{i},v_{i}\}
have already been selected.

2.

Define the weight
w_{i}(S)
of a set
S\in\mathcal{S}
as
2^{k_{i}(S)}
, where

k_{i}(S)
is the number of edges among
\{u_{1},v_{1}\},\ldots,\{u_{i},v_{i}\}
crossed by
S
. In particular,
w_{0}(S)=1
for all
S\in\mathcal{S}
.

3.

We select the next edge
\{u_{i+1},v_{i+1}\}
from
X_{i}=X\setminus\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{i}\}
as a pair of points with the total weight of sets crossing
\{u_{i+1},v_{i+1}\}
being the smallest possible.

4.

We continue in this manner until
n-1
edges have been selected.

Let
E:=\{\{u_{1},v_{1}\},\{u_{2},v_{2}\}\ldots,\{u_{n-1},v_{n-1}\}\}
. Then
T=(X,E)
is a tree.
Next, we will bound the crossing number
k
of the resulting tree
T
. To this end, we estimate the final total weight of all sets of
\mathcal{S}
, i.e.,

w_{n-1}(\mathcal{S})=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}w_{n-1}(S).

By definition of
w_{n-1}
we have
k\leqslant\log w_{n-1}(\mathcal{S})
.

Let us investigate how
w_{i+1}(\mathcal{S})
increases compared to
w_{i}(\mathcal{S})
.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{i+1}
denote the collection of the sets of
\mathcal{S}
crossing
\{u_{i+1},v_{i+1}\}
.
For each set in
\mathcal{S}_{i+1}
the weight increases by a multiplicative factor of two, and for the other sets it remains unchanged.
From this we get

w_{i+1}(\mathcal{S})=w_{i}(\mathcal{S})-w_{i}(\mathcal{S}_{i+1})+2w_{i}(\mathcal{S}_{i+1})=w_{i}(\mathcal{S})\left[1+\frac{w_{i}(\mathcal{S}_{i+1})}{w_{i}(\mathcal{S})}\right].

We now estimate
\frac{w_{i}(\mathcal{S}_{i+1})}{w_{i}(\mathcal{S})}
using the Short Edge Lemma (
Lemma

A.3
).
To begin we define a multiset
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
of sets by adding
S\cap X_{i}
to
\mathcal{Q}_{i}

with multiplicity
w_{i}(S)
for every
S\in\mathcal{S}
.
Thus

|\mathcal{Q}_{i}|=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}w_{i}(S)=w_{i}(\mathcal{S}).

Note that, by the construction of
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, the number of sets in
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
that cross
\{u_{i+1},v_{i+1}\}
is
w_{i}(\mathcal{S}_{i+1})
.
Furthermore, by our choice of edge in (
2
) and (
3
), the edge
\{u_{i+1},v_{i+1}\}
is crossed by the minimum number of sets in
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
.
Thus, by applying the Short Edge Lemma on the set system

(X_{i},\{S\cap X_{i}~:~S\in\mathcal{S}\})
to the multiset
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, and defining
n_{i}:=|X_{i}|=n-i
, we obtain:

w_{i}(\mathcal{S}_{i+1})\leqslant 5d\cdot\frac{w_{i}(\mathcal{S})}{f^{-1}(n_{i}/2)}.

Hence, we have
