Butyrate ameliorates ulcerative colitis through targeting STING-dependent ER stress signaling and limiting CD4+ TRM T cells accumulation
Authors: Tengfei Xiao, Jingjing Kang, Chuanxiang Zhao, Rong Zhu, Mingzhong Sun, Yungang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the role of butyrate in regulating STING-induced endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) and CD4+ tissue-resident memory (TRM) T cells responses during the progression of ulcerative colitis (UC). Our results demonstrated that butyrate significantly alleviated dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis, as evidenced by restored intestinal epithelial architecture, reduced inflammatory cytokine, and decreased CD4+ TRM T cells. These protective effects were likely mediated through modulation of the STING–ERS pathway. Using a CT26 cell model, we further confirmed that STING activation promotes ERS, leading to enhanced secretion of inflammatory factors and subsequent induction of CD4+ TRM T cells. Importantly, butyrate effectively suppressed this STING-initiated inflammatory cascade in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs). Our findings revealed a novel mechanism by which butyrate ameliorates UC through inhibition of the STING–ERS axis in IECs, highlighting its therapeutic potential for UC treatment.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12950-025-00475-5.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a heterogeneous autoimmune disorder characterized by chronic intestinal inflammation and lifelong cycles of relapse and remission [1]. Changes in dietary patterns and imbalances in the gut microbiota are directly linked to the development of ulcerative colitis (UC). Compared to healthy individuals, patients with UC exhibit reduced levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and a decreased abundance of SCFAs-producing gut bacteria. SCFAs play a crucial role in maintaining intestinal epithelial integrity—particularly butyrate, which serves as a major energy source for intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) and significantly contributes to immune regulation [2]. Upon encountering inflammation, butyrate’s anti-inflammatory effects are multifaceted and primarily mediated through epigenetic regulation and receptor-mediated signaling. Firstly, as a histone deacetylase inhibitor (HDACi), it promotes histone acetylation, thereby suppressing the activation of NF-κB and STAT1 [3, 4]. Secondly, by binding to GPCRs like GPR109A, butyrate suppresses the NF-κB pathway in immune and epithelial cells, curbing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 [5]. In addition, butyrate modulates the differentiation and function of various intestinal mucosal immune cells—such as neutrophils, T cells, and macrophages—thereby curbing the progression of intestinal inflammation [6–8]. However, the precise mechanisms through which butyrate modulates endoplasmic reticulum stress in ulcerative colitis remain unclear.

The accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins due to various stressors is known as endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS). In response, cells activate adaptive self-protective mechanisms—primarily through the unfolded protein response (UPR) and endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation (ERAD)—to enhance the endoplasmic reticulum’s protein-folding capacity and alleviate proteotoxic stress [9]. The UPR is initiated upon the dissociation of the chaperone protein BIP from three transmembrane sensors: IRE1α, PERK, and ATF6. Following UPR activation, PERK phosphorylates the downstream factor eIF2α to attenuate global protein synthesis. IRE1α enhances the endoplasmic reticulum’s protein-folding capacity through the unconventional splicing of XBP1 mRNA, producing the transcription factor XBP1s, which upregulates chaperones such as BIP. Meanwhile, ATF6 translocates to the Golgi apparatus with the assistance of BIP, where it is proteolytically processed to become an active transcription factor [10]. When the UPR is unable to alleviate endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), the ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway acts as a critical backup mechanism for protein quality control. In this process, misfolded proteins are recognized by the ERAD complex—whose core components include Sel1L and HRD1—ubiquitinated, and subsequently retrotranslocated into the cytoplasm for proteasomal degradation [11]. Previous studies have indicated that endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) in intestinal secretory cells—particularly goblet cells and Paneth cells—plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [12]. Moreover, UPR signaling components—such as IRE1α, PERK, and ATF6—represent important therapeutic targets for IBD treatment [13, 14]. As a key regulator of proteostasis, the ERAD pathway plays an essential role in maintaining intestinal epithelial cell (IEC) homeostasis. For instance, deletion of Sel1L, a central ERAD component, induces endoplasmic reticulum stress and leads to spontaneous colitis in experimental models [15]. Nevertheless, the roles of the integrated UPR and ERAD systems in the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis (UC) remain to be fully elucidated.

The intestinal lumen constitutes a complex symbiotic ecosystem inhabited by diverse gut microbiota, viruses, and toxins. As a key pattern recognition receptor in innate immunity, the cyclic GMP–AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) pathway has garnered significant attention for its role in various diseases, including cancer and inflammatory disorders [16]. Mouse models of colitis and human inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients consistently show elevated STING expression. Furthermore, mice carrying constitutively active STING alleles frequently develop spontaneous gut microbiota dysbiosis and colitis [17]. Moreover, STING activation has been shown to induce endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), resulting in elevated oxidative stress, enhanced inflammatory responses, and increased apoptosis in a murine model of lung ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury [18]. Similarly, STING has been shown to induce endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) in models of pulmonary fibrosis [19]. Furthermore, atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) was found to inhibit STING-driven ERS in a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced mouse model of colitis [20]. Therefore, we hypothesize that butyrate may attenuate the progression of ulcerative colitis by suppressing cGAS–STING-mediated endoplasmic reticulum stress.

Upon exposure to pathogenic microorganisms in the intestinal lumen of individuals with IBD, activated innate immune cells release a range of mediators that initiate adaptive immune responses. Continuously infiltrating T cells recognize pathogen-derived antigens and differentiate into various T helper subsets under inflammatory conditions [21]. As a subset of memory T cells, tissue-resident memory (TRM) T cells have gained increasing attention in recent years. Their defining features—including absence from systemic circulation and long-term persistence in local tissues—closely align with the recurrent pattern observed in colitis [22]. Tissue-resident memory (TRM) T cells are defined by the expression of CD69, which suppresses S1PR1-mediated tissue egress and promotes cellular retention within local sites. Another key marker, CD103 (integrin αE), forms a heterodimer with integrin β7 and facilitates epithelial infiltration through binding to E-cadherin [23]. Recent studies suggest that CD4+ tissue-resident memory (TRM) T cells may play a key role in driving the progression of ulcerative colitis (UC). Zundler et al. demonstrated that genetic deletion of Hobit and Blimp1—core transcription factors governing TRM cell development—significantly ameliorates experimental colitis [24]. Whether butyrate administration can mitigate ulcerative colitis (UC) by reducing CD4+ tissue-resident memory (TRM) T cells infiltration remains unreported.

In our study, we evaluated the effect of butyrate on the STING–ERS pathway using both a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced murine colitis model and CT26 cells. Our results demonstrated that butyrate suppressed inflammatory factor secretion in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) through the STING–ERS axis, thereby reducing CD4+ tissue-resident memory (TRM) T cells infiltration in ulcerative colitis (UC). These findings enhance our understanding of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) pathogenesis and offer promising therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Induction of DSS colitis and butyrate treatment in mice

Male BALB/c mice (approximately 20 g body weight) were purchased from Kavins Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China). The animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at a constant temperature (22 ± 2°C) on a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. After a 7-day acclimatization period, mice were randomly assigned to three experimental groups (n = 4 per group): (1) NC (Negative control): received regular drinking water, (2) DSS: received 2.5% (w/v) dextran sulfate sodium salt (DSS; MP Biomedicals, Cat# S2839) in drinking water, (3) DSS+Bu: received 2.5% (w/v) DSS plus 2% (w/v) sodium butyrate (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# MKCK9580) in drinking water. Colitis was induced according to the experimental timeline shown in Fig. 1A. DSS and butyrate were administered to mice via their drinking water, in which both compounds are soluble. Fresh solutions were prepared daily. Daily water consumption was monitored per cage to ensure equivalent DSS exposure across treated groups. Body weight, stool consistency, and fecal occult blood were assessed daily for 7 days to calculate the disease activity index (DAI). On day 7, the colon tissue was collected, photographed for documentation, and processed for subsequent experiments. Cell culture and treatment

The mouse colonic epithelial cell line CT26 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Considering the mouse source of CT26, the murine-specific STING agonist DMXAA (5,6-dimethylxanthine-4-acetic acid; MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-10964) and sodium butyrate (Sigma-Aldrich) are an ideal tool for studies using mouse-derived cell lines, such as the CT26 cells [25]. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized and seeded into appropriate plates. After attachment, they were divided into the following groups: (1) NC (Negative control): cells treated with vehicle control, (2) DMXAA: cells stimulated with DMXAA (10ug/mL), (3) DMXAA+Bu: cells co-treated with DMXAA and sodium butyrate (0.5 mM). Treatments were applied for the indicated time periods prior to subsequent analyses.

Histological, periodic acid-schif staining (PAS) and Masson analysis

Colon tissues from mice were processed into paraffin blocks using standard histological procedures, including PBS washing, dehydration, sectioning, and paraffin embedding. The embedded tissues were then sliced into approximately 5 μm-thick sections for subsequent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid–Schiff (PAS), and Masson’s trichrome staining, following the manufacturer’s protocols for each method. Immunohistochemistry

Following deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, paraffin-embedded sections were blocked with 5% BSA to prevent non-specific binding and then subjected to immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: (1) anti-STING (1:5000; Rabbit; 19851–1-AP; Proteintech, USA), (2) anti-GRP78/BIP (1:2000; Mouse; 66574–1-Ig; Proteintech, USA), (3) anti-Sel1L (1:1000; Rabbit; DF14696; Affinity Biosciences, UK). After washing, the sections were incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies: (1) Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (1:5000; SA00001-1; Proteintech, USA), (2) Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (1:5000; SA00001-2; Proteintech, USA). Subsequent steps included chromogenic substrate development, counterstaining, dehydration, and mounting. Stained sections were imaged at × 200 magnification to evaluate protein expression levels.

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) followed by chloroform phase separation, isopropanol precipitation, and washing with 75% ethanol. The RNA pellet was air-dried and dissolved in RNase-free water. cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix reverse transcription kit (R211, Vazyme Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711, Vazyme Biotech, China) on a QuantStudio® real-time PCR system. All reactions were run in triplicate under the following cycling conditions: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Western blot

Total protein was extracted from tissues and cells using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with PMSF and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010-1, Beyotime, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies (all from rabbit; Cell Signaling Technology unless noted): (1) Phospho-STING (Ser365) (D8F4W, 1:1000), (2) STING (D2P2F, 1:1000), (3) Phospho-TBK1 (Ser172) (D52C2, 1:1000), (4) TBK1 (D1B4, 1:1000), (5) Phospho-IRF3 (Ser396) (D6O1M, 1:1000), (6) IRF3 (D6I4C, 1:1000), (7) BIP (C50B12, 1:1000), (8) IRE1α (14C10, 1:1000), (9) NF-κB p65 (D14E12, 1:1000), (10) Sel1L (DF14696, Affinity Biosciences, UK, 1:1000). An HRP-conjugated β-actin antibody (Mouse, HRP-60008, Proteintech, USA; 1:10,000) was used as a loading control. Subsequently, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000, SA00001-2, Proteintech, USA) at room temperature for 1 hour. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Isolation of lamina propria cells and flow cytometry analysis

Single-cell suspensions from the colonic lamina propria were prepared by enzymatic digestion with collagenase type IV, followed by purification using a 40% Percoll gradient. A total of 1 × 106 cells were stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies for 30 minutes at 4 °C in the dark. The following antibodies were used: (1) anti-Mouse CD4-PerCP-Cy5.5 (E-AB-F1353J, Elabscience, China), (2) anti-Mouse CD69-APC (E-AB-F1187E, Elabscience, China), (3) anti-Mouse CD103-PE (E-AB-F1090D, Elabscience, China). After staining, cells were washed three times with PBS and analyzed on a flow cytometer. Data processing and visualization were performed using FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

CT26 cells were cultured on poly-D-lysine-coated sterile coverslips placed in 12-well plates. After reaching appropriate confluence, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. The coverslips were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: (1) Anti-GRP78/BiP (1:500; Mouse; 66574–1-Ig; Proteintech, USA), (2) Anti-Sel1L (1:500; Rabbit; DF14696; Affinity Biosciences, UK). After three washes with PBS, samples were incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature in the dark with species-matched secondary antibodies: (1) CoraLite488-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (1:500; SA00013-1; Proteintech, USA), (2) CoraLite594-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (1:500; SA00013-4; Proteintech, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and coverslips were mounted onto glass slides. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Nikon fluorescence microscope (Japan). Image merging and analysis were performed using ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

CT26 cell clusters were fixed overnight at 4 °C using electron microscopy fixative (G1102-100 ML, Servicebio, China). Secondary fixation was performed with 1% osmium tetroxide. Samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, treated with epoxy propane for transparency, and embedded in Quetol-812 epoxy resin. After polymerization, ultrathin sections (100 nm) were prepared and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Images were acquired using a HT7700 transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, Japan).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12 in the supernatant of CT26 cell cultures were measured using commercial ELISA kits (MultiScience, China; Catalog numbers: A28201031, A20600643, and A21281024, respectively). All procedures, including sample preparation, standard dilution, and assay steps, were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In vitro induction of spleen-derived CD4+ tissue-resident memory T cells

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from aseptically harvested mouse spleens following erythrocyte lysis using ACK buffer. Splenocytes were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and stimulated with anti-CD3 (5 μg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #16–0031-85), anti-CD28 (1 μg/mL; #16–0281-85), and recombinant IL-2 (2 ng/mL; #14–7021-81). Conditioned medium from CT26 cells primed with either DMXAA or a combination of DMXAA and sodium butyrate (Bu) was added at a 1:1 ratio to the T cell culture medium. After 72 hours of coculture, cells were harvested and subjected to surface staining with fluorescently conjugated antibodies against CD4, CD69, and CD103. The frequency of CD4+ CD69+ CD103+ &CD4+ CD69+ CD103- T cells was quantified by flow cytometry.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (version 1.1.2), which employs a semi-flexible docking approach with an estimated docking accuracy of 78%. The three-dimensional structure of butyrate was retrieved from the PubChem database, and the structure of the STING protein (UniProt ID: Q3TBT3) was obtained from the UniProt database. The STING protein was defined as the receptor and butyrate as the ligand.Protein and ligand structures were prepared using AutoDockTools (http://mgltools.scripps.edu/downloads). Steps included addition of hydrogen atoms, charge assignment, setting atomic types to the AutoDock 4 (AD4) format, and defining the docking grid box. The ligand was assigned flexible torsions and set as the root. Structural files were converted from PDB to PDBQT format to incorporate atomic partial charges and torsion tree definitions. Docking simulations were carried out using Vina, and binding affinity scores (in kcal/mol) were computed for the STING–butyrate complexes. The resulting docking poses were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL and Discovery Studio to examine three-dimensional interaction patterns and two-dimensional ligand–protein interaction diagrams.

Gene silencing of STING using small interfering RNA (siRNA)

STING-specific siRNA and negative control siRNA (NC-Si) were designed and synthesized by Genepharma (Shanghai, China). CT26 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected at appropriate confluence using siRNA-mate plus transfection reagent (Genepharma, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Four distinct siRNA sequences targeting STING were tested to optimize knockdown efficiency. After 48 hours of transfection, STING protein expression was evaluated by western blotting to confirm silencing efficacy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 5.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Differences among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA, and a P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Butyrate significantly ameliorated disease progression and pathological damage in DSS-induced murine colitis

During the DSS induction phase, water consumption in both the DSS and DSS/Bu groups decreased compared to the normal control (NC) group. However, no significant difference in daily water intake was observed between the DSS and DSS/Bu groups (Fig. S1). Administration of DSS successfully recapitulated key features of ulcerative colitis, including progressive weight loss, elevated disease activity index (DAI) scores, and reduction in colon length. Treatment with 2% sodium butyrate markedly attenuated these pathological manifestations (Fig. 1B–E). Histopathological evaluation further demonstrated that butyrate administration preserved crypt architecture, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, restored goblet cell numbers, and ameliorated collagen deposition in colon tissues of DSS-treated mice (Fig. 1F–H). These results indicate that butyrate effectively suppressed disease progression and exerted a protective effect in experimental colitis.

Butyrate inhibits STING phosphorylation in the colon of DSS-induced colitis mice

The inflammatory milieu in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is often characterized by the release of pathogens and endogenous double-stranded DNA, which is sensed by cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS), leading to activation of the STING pathway and subsequent nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling [26]. In DSS-induced colitis mice, immunohistochemical staining revealed a marked increase in STING protein expression within colon tissues (Fig. 2A). Consistent with this, western blot analysis demonstrated activation of the STING pathway, indicated by significantly elevated phosphorylation levels of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 (Fig. 2B, C). Butyrate alleviates endoplasmic reticulum stress in the colon of DSS-induced colitis mice

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) homeostasis is closely linked to the development of intestinal inflammation, particularly through its role in the regulated secretion of mucins by goblet cells and maintenance of the protective mucus layer [27]. Given the localization of STING at the ER, we assessed the expression of the ER stress marker BIP, the ERAD adapter Sel1L, and the unfolded protein response (UPR) signaling component IRE1α in colonic tissues. Results revealed a significant upregulation of BIP, Sel1L, and IRE1α in DSS-induced colitis tissues, accompanied by activation of downstream NF-κB signaling (Fig. 3A–C). This cascade promoted the production and accumulation of various pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ, and IL-17 (Fig. 3D). These findings indicate that ulcerative colitis is associated with loss of ER proteostasis and increased burden on the protein regulatory machinery in colonic cells. We next examined the functional relevance of this interaction using the mouse colonic epithelial cell line CT26. Butyrate attenuates STING-triggered endoplasmic reticulum stress in vitro

Existing evidence suggested a reciprocal causal relationship between STING activation and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in ulcerative colitis. Under inflammatory conditions, pathogenic DNA released into the intestinal mucosa can induce ER stress via STING pathway activation, while persistent chemical stimulation may provoke STING signaling as a consequence of ER stress [28]. In CT26 cells, STING activation by DMXAA induced significant ER stress. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed cystic and spherical dilatation of the ER, accompanied by a reduction in mitochondrial number (Fig. 6A). Consistent with these morphological changes, immunofluorescence and western blot analyses showed upregulation of the ER stress marker BIP, the ERAD adapter Sel1L, and the UPR-related kinase IRE1α following DMXAA treatment Furthermore, DMXAA led to activation of NF-κB signaling. Meanwhile, butyrate treatment markedly counteracted these STING-induced ER stress responses (Fig. 6B–E). To establish a causal link between STING activation and these effects, we performed STING knockdown (KD) experiments. Notably, STING KD effectively attenuated DMXAA-induced upregulation of BIP, Sel1L, and P65 as well as increased factor secretion of TNF-a, IL-6 and IL-12, confirming that these responses are STING-dependent (Fig. S2 D-F). In summary, STING activation by pathogenic stimuli disrupts ER homeostasis in colonic epithelial cells and contributes to UC progression. Butyrate likely alleviates intestinal inflammation through inhibition of the STING–ER stress axis.

Although our results indicated that STING activation induces endoplasmic reticulum stress and inflammatory responses in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), it remained unclear whether IECs could promote the induction and differentiation of CD4+ TRM T cells via cytokine secretion. We first measured inflammatory factors in the supernatant of CT26 cells stimulated for 24 hours with DMXAA or DMXAA plus butyrate (DMXAA/Bu). STING activation significantly increased the secretion of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-12 which was suppressed by butyrate co-treatment (Fig. 7B, Fig. S2 F). We observed that the supernatant derived from DMXAA-stimulated CT26 cells significantly increased the proportion of CD4+ CD69+ CD103+ T cells. Butyrate treatment effectively inhibited CD4+ CD69+ CD103+ T cells. (Fig. 7C–E). Discussion

Advances in microbial metabolomics have highlighted the critical role of gut microbiota-derived metabolites in the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis (UC), where they act as key mediators of host–microbiota interactions. A hallmark of UC is a marked reduction in gut microbial diversity, typically featuring an expansion of facultative anaerobes and a decline in obligate anaerobes [29]. Moreover, obligate anaerobes represent the primary source of butyrate—a metabolite with demonstrated potential in preserving intestinal barrier function, suppressing inflammation, and modulating immune responses [30]. The precise mechanism through which butyrate mitigates ulcerative colitis (UC)-associated inflammation via modulation of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) remains incompletely understood. Our findings suggest that ER stress may arise as a consequence of STING pathway activation. Data from this study demonstrated that butyrate alleviated experimental colitis by inhibiting STING-mediated ER stress (Fig. 8). Proposed mechanism of butyrate-mediated protection in ulcerative colitis via the STING–ER stress axis in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) and regulation of CD4+ tissue-resident memory T cells. Butyrate attenuates mucosal inflammation and damage in UC by suppressing the STING–ER stress-induced inflammatory response in IECs and inhibiting infiltration of CD4+ TRM T cells

Persistent exposure to pathogen-associated molecules resulting from intestinal mucosal damage in ulcerative colitis (UC) triggers the death of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) and promotes the infiltration of inflammatory factors, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12. The excessive cytoplasmic DNA released during cellular damage subsequently activates the cGAS–STING pathway, further disrupting intestinal homeostasis [31]. With advancing research, the STING pathway—a central cytosolic DNA-sensing mechanism—is now recognized not only for its classical antiviral functions but also for its significant involvement in the pathogenesis of certain autoimmune diseases. It is hypothesized that aberrant activation of the cGAS–STING pathway may represent a key pathogenic mechanism in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [32]. Emerging evidence confirmed that STING expression was significantly upregulated during the progression of colitis in both murine models and human patients [20, 33]. Furthermore, in DSS-induced murine colitis models, administration of STING agonists has been shown to exacerbate disease severity, whereas genetic or pharmacological inhibition of STING attenuates colitic pathology [34, 35]. In our study, the DSS-induced colitis model exhibited significant upregulation of STING expression and phosphorylation of downstream effectors TBK1 and IRF3 in colon tissues, consistent with previously reported findings. These results support the therapeutic potential of developing pharmacological STING inhibitors for ulcerative colitis (UC) treatment. Using molecular docking, we predicted potential binding sites between butyrate and STING. Butyrate administration effectively suppressed STING upregulation in colitic tissues and ameliorated inflammatory responses in vivo. Similarly, in CT26 cells, butyrate inhibited DMXAA-induced activation of the STING pathway. These data indicate that butyrate attenuates intestinal inflammation at least partially through suppression of the STING signaling cascade.

Given the established involvement of STING in the pathogenesis of colitis, the precise mechanisms through which it drives inflammatory progression require further elucidation. As an endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-resident protein, STING signaling is likely subject to regulation by ER homeostasis [36]. Particularly in secretory intestinal epithelial cells (IECs)—such as goblet and Paneth cells, which exhibit high protein secretion activity—a precisely regulated protein synthesis environment is essential. The maintenance of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) homeostasis depends on quality control systems including the unfolded protein response (UPR), ER-associated degradation (ERAD), and autophagy. When the burden of unfolded or misfolded proteins exceeds handling capacity and ER stress remains unresolved, IECs may initiate cell death programs and release pro-inflammatory factors, thereby exacerbating intestinal inflammation [28, 37]. Accumulating evidence underscores the significance of the interaction between STING and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in the pathophysiology of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). For instance, Wu et al. demonstrated that aberrant STING activation in T cells disrupts calcium homeostasis, resulting in persistent ER stress and ultimately T cell death [38]. Conversely, we also observed that endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress potently activates the STING–IRF3 signaling pathway, as demonstrated in models of alcoholic liver disease [39, 40]. Moreover, newly synthesized STING serves as a substrate of the Sel1L–HRD1 complex within the ER-associated degradation (ERAD) system, and the available pool of functional STING is constrained by ERAD-mediated degradation, thereby limiting its innate immune signaling capacity [41].

In our in vitro CT26 intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) model, the STING agonist DMXAA successfully induced phosphorylation of STING, triggering downstream activation of TBK1 and IRF3. Concurrently, STING activation provoked endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, as indicated by elevated expression of the chaperone protein BIP. In response, the unfolded protein response (UPR) and ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pathways—key mechanisms for mitigating ER stress—were activated, reflected by upregulation of IRE1α and Sel1L. Butyrate treatment effectively suppressed this entire cascade of STING-driven signaling events, as confirmed by immunoblotting and immunofluorescence analyses. Furthermore, morphological assessment of CT26 cells revealed DMXAA-induced cellular clustering, while electron microscopy illustrated cystic dilation of the ER and alterations in mitochondrial abundance. These structural abnormalities were markedly ameliorated by butyrate intervention. Similarly, in the DSS-induced colitis model, colonic tissues exhibited increased STING expression accompanied by signs of ER stress, both of which were alleviated following butyrate administration. Taken together, these findings suggest that the ER may serve as a critical hub through which STING exerts its regulatory functions in intestinal inflammation [42].

Inflammatory cytokines play a pivotal role in sustaining the development and persistence of ulcerative colitis (UC) and facilitate the transition from acute to chronic intestinal inflammation [43]. Intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) serve not only as a physical barrier but also as dynamic immune sentinels. In response to pathogenic or inflammatory stimuli, IECs secrete a variety of pro-inflammatory mediators—such as cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and antimicrobial peptides. Through this secretion, IECs orchestrate innate immunity by recruiting and activating neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes, thereby initiating and amplifying inflammatory responses to bolster host defense [44, 45]. Consistent with this, we observed elevated levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-γ, and IL-17 in colon tissues from DSS-treated mice, as well as increased secretion of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12 in the supernatant of DMXAA-stimulated CT26 cells. Butyrate treatment significantly suppressed the production and release of these inflammatory factors in both contexts. Furthermore, given the multifactorial pathogenesis of UC, colitic tissues often exhibit mucosal immune dysregulation. CD4+ T cells, in particular, are recognized as key drivers of disease progression in human UC [46, 47].

Tissue-resident memory T cells, a recently defined subset of memory T lymphocytes, are characterized by their long-term persistence within mucosal tissues without recirculating [48]. In alignment with their residency and survival properties, TRM T cells typically display surface markers such as CD69, CD103, CD49a, and CXCR3, which facilitate tissue retention and prevent egress into the systemic circulation [49–51]. Binfeng Chen et al. reported that CD4+ CD69+ CD103+ tissue-resident memory T cells were significantly upregulated in an experimental mouse model of colitis, and their abundance correlated positively with the disease activity index (DAI) [52]. This finding aligns with our observations in the DSS colitis model, where butyrate significantly suppressed the infiltration of CD4+ CD69+ CD103+ and CD4+ CD69+ CD103- T cells. In patients with ulcerative colitis (UC), CD4+ TRM T cells are also markedly expanded and represent a major source of pro-inflammatory cytokines—including TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-13, and IL-17A—within the intestinal mucosa [53–55]. Therefore, CD4+ tissue-resident memory T cells represent a promising therapeutic target for the treatment of ulcerative colitis [56]. The origin of CD4+ tissue-resident memory T cells in active colitis remains incompletely understood, and it was previously unclear whether damaged intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) contribute to their recruitment and infiltration. We observed a substantial release of inflammatory factors in the supernatant of DMXAA-stimulated CT26 cells, leading us to hypothesize that IEC-derived inflammatory mediators may play a crucial role in inducing CD4+ TRM T cells. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that conditioned medium from CT26 cells significantly promoted the induction of CD4+ CD69+ CD103+ T cells during T cell activation, an effect that was markedly suppressed by butyrate. Thus, we propose that during colitis, injured IECs may drive the induction of CD4+ CD69+ CD103+ T cells via paracrine signaling, thereby amplifying intestinal inflammation.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrated the therapeutic potential of butyrate in the treatment of ulcerative colitis (UC). Mechanistically, butyrate alleviates intestinal inflammation by suppressing the STING–ER stress axis in intestinal epithelial cells and inhibiting the recruitment of CD4+ tissue-resident memory T cells. These findings underscore the contribution of STING-mediated ER stress and CD4+ TRM T cells responses to colitis progression, highlighting their promise as potential therapeutic targets. The development of agents targeting this pathway may open new avenues for UC treatment (Fig. 8).
