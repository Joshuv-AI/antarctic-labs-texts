A rubric to assess generative AI-based feedback on student writing assignments
Authors: Daniel R. Rankins, Erick N. Tran, Valerie T. La, Dorothy M. Huang, Rachael M. Barry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGenerative artificial intelligence (GenAI) tools are an increasingly common resource used in the classroom and writing process. The landscape of available GenAI tools is rapidly evolving, so having a systematic and straightforward way to evaluate new tools for incorporation into the classroom is key. For example, in science writing education, GenAI tools can be used as a supplement to instructor feedback on student writing, allowing an additional opportunity for critique and revision by the student. Here, we describe a rubric we developed to enable instructors to assess differences between feedback provided by GenAI models based on five key areas: (i) accuracy, (ii) constructiveness, (iii) clarity and readability, (iv) recognition of strengths, and (v) original text. We show data comparing the feedback provided by multiple GenAI models on student work based on course guidelines. This rubric can serve as a resource for other instructors interested in evaluating various GenAI models for use as scientific writing feedback supplements in their own classrooms.

INTRODUCTION

Developing proficiency in discipline-specific writing is a key component of many undergraduate biology programs. While the mass adoption of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) models has given rise to new challenges in teaching and assessing student writing, it has also revealed new opportunities to leverage these tools to supplement existing instruction (1). This article presents a rubric to use as an evaluation tool for instructors who would like to incorporate GenAI models into their writing feedback process. This rubric provides a systematic approach to evaluate the quality of the output of each model and simplifies the comparison between different models. In light of the frequent release of new GenAI models, the utility of having a rubric to objectively and thoroughly assess the quality of the output of new models is demonstrable. We developed this rubric for working with feedback on upper-level research manuscripts in the biological sciences that follow the format used by journal articles, as these are the standard of discipline-specific writing in biology (2). However, we anticipate that it is discipline-agnostic and useful for other types of long-form writing assignments across levels from introductory courses to advanced topics.

This project arose from an investigation into the capabilities of various GenAI models to serve as supplemental sources of feedback on student writing assignments. The student work and feedback we assessed were part of upper-level undergraduate microbiology and biochemistry laboratory courses taught in the Department of Molecular Biology and Biochemistry at a large, public university in the United States. These in-person biology laboratory courses include a substantial writing component to help fulfill a university-wide upper-division writing requirement for the degree. These courses typically enroll biological sciences majors or majors from related disciplines (e.g., chemistry, pharmaceutical sciences) in their fourth year. Each course has a faculty instructor and multiple graduate student teaching assistants (TAs). The major writing assignment is a manuscript-style report on a project performed in the laboratory. Other laboratory courses in the department (e.g., molecular biology) assign the same kind of report as part of their curriculum.

Providing ample, timely writing feedback in these large classes (>100 students) is challenging. Instructional support and grading time are limited, with TAs completing most of the writing feedback and grading (Appendix A). TAs have a contract that sets a maximum number of hours for their position (110 h per quarter, an average of 10 h per week for a 20-student laboratory section). After accounting for the TAs’ various course responsibilities (e.g., class time, holding office hours, TA meetings, and preparing materials), a typical TA is often left with only 3–6 min per student per week to spend on grading (Appendix B). At odds with this constraint is the requirement by the university that these courses provide substantial opportunities to write, receive feedback, and revise discipline-specific writing assignments. Like many other institutions of higher education, our university requires students to complete some writing within their discipline, and scholarship in this area consistently highlights the need for opportunities for feedback and revision (for some examples, see [3, 4]). To balance these challenges, instructors for these courses often explore novel methods for providing feedback that can supplement what is provided by TAs. It is important to note that our goal is not to have this AI-based feedback replace TA or instructor feedback, nor is it to have the GenAI model grade the assignment. Instead, we hope resources such as this one will provide instructors with practical ways to integrate more rounds of feedback and revision, particularly in large courses or where instructional support is limited.

Science writing and communications is a core competency in the American Association for the Advancement of Science’s Vision and Change (5). An added benefit of leveraging GenAI-based tools in science writing education is that their increased prevalence and use in modern science and science writing suggests that GenAI literacy and skills will be important professional competencies in future STEM careers (6). Undergraduate students are using GenAI; one recent study found that 72% of students surveyed reported having used GenAI (7). In our course context, we asked students to create a reverse outline, an outline of a passage created after it is written that is often used to evaluate the structure of the piece and can identify issues with logical flow and other areas for improvement during revision (8, 9). These students were explicitly welcomed in the instructions to either use GenAI tools to create the reverse outline or create it themselves. Out of the 93 students who completed the reflection in 2024, 82 (88%) reported using GenAI tools to create the reverse outline. Another study at our institution with a similar group of students enrolled in general chemistry and organic chemistry labs found that the majority of students found AI-based feedback on their written assignments to be helpful (10). Recent work at other universities has shown that AI-generated writing feedback can have positive impacts on writing quality as well as student engagement and motivation (11). At the same time, other studies have highlighted that AI-generated feedback can have limitations (e.g., inconsistent quality, factual errors) and that students may not always have the expertise to discern the difference between appropriate and inappropriate AI-generated feedback (12, 13). Thus, teaching students how to effectively use GenAI to receive feedback as a structured part of the writing process may have positive impacts on their AI literacy and help them to hone their writing skills while keeping up with modern practices.

PROCEDURE

Safety issues

No safety issues are anticipated.

Rubric development and assessment

Our objective was to develop a rubric to assess GenAI-generated feedback on student writing. Two major considerations were that the feedback must be relevant to the assignment guidelines and helpful to the student. We also wanted to encourage students to do their own revisions, which motivated us to capture information about whether GenAI models tended to rewrite student work, providing them with finished text. Ultimately, we identified five key areas for distinguishing the quality of feedback received from the GenAI model: (i) accuracy, (ii) constructiveness, (iii) clarity and readability, (iv) recognition of strengths, and (v) original text (Table 1). We then developed a rubric to score the feedback based on each of these categories (Table 2). Members of the research team include an instructor of record for these courses (author R.M.B.) and graduate student TAs with experience across several biology and chemistry courses (authors D.R.R. and D.M.H.), as well as undergraduate students with experience taking these courses and completing these assignments (authors E.N.T. and V.T.L.). The rubric was primarily developed by authors D.R.R. and R.M.B., and scoring was done by authors D.R.R. or E.N.T. Each GenAI model was provided with the assignment guidelines and rubric (as a PDF file), an example of student work to provide feedback on (as text copied in), and a prompt requesting feedback (Appendix C; Table C1). We then used the rubric to score several different models that were available during late 2025 in the University of California, Irvine (UCI)-created GenAI ecosystem (ZotGPT) (Appendix C; Fig. C1 and C2).

GenAI feedback rubric

The ZotGPT platform allows use of several different GenAI models in a setting that is private within the university and does not share information with outside developers for model training. Most ZotGPT models can be used with student records data (14). For this work, text submitted to ZotGPT was separated from any student identifiers. Other institutions have developed similar in-house GenAI systems like ZotGPT (15). We strongly recommend that instructors who plan to use this rubric with student writing examples consult their institutional policies on GenAI use related to student work since they vary widely from institution to institution (reviewed in reference 16).

We have tested this rubric with examples of excellent, average, and below-average student work. These designations were based on the numerical score these reports received when originally graded by TAs. Below-average reports in this course are often missing sections of the manuscript, so only used complete assignments for our study, meaning we excluded those that were missing most or all of an introduction, results, or discussion section. Information about the numerical grades and quality designation (excellent, average, or below average) of these example reports is provided in Appendix D. We continue to test this rubric with more student writing and new or updated GenAI tools as they become available. We used two types of prompts: “neutral” and “no rewrite.” “Neutral” prompts asked the model to “provide feedback on my introduction using the uploaded guidelines as a basis for feedback.” “No rewrite” prompts asked the model to “provide feedback on my introduction using the uploaded guidelines as the basis for all feedback. Do not suggest any new text to add.” Previous instructor experience in these courses revealed that when students were using AI for their reverse outlines, the prompts used were relatively simple. Our use of relatively simple prompts here was meant to emulate this. In the future, should this be used as part of a student assignment, we recommend providing additional guidance or training in prompt engineering to students as this may improve the quality of the feedback they receive. For example, students could be introduced to the CRAFT framework (https://craftingaiprompts.org/) for prompt design or a customized prompt library could be developed. We predict that this rubric could be used in the testing of new prompts similar to how it has been used here to assess different models.

In the feedback that was provided, models were generally able to identify when aspects of the assignment requirements were missing. For example, some students were missing an explicit hypothesis or statement of purpose (examples of feedback are provided in Appendix E). In other aspects of the feedback, models diverged. We are able to evaluate the feedback capabilities of various models (Appendices C and D). In our context, ZotGPT’s Mistral Large 2 scored highest of the models tested (Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4, ChatGPT GPT-5, Google Gemini 2.5 Flash, Google Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Mistral Large 2) (Appendix C; Fig. C1 and C2). Google Gemini models (Flash and Pro) also received strong scores (Appendix C; Fig. C2). Several characteristics drove these scores, including output that was provided in an easy-to-read format, feedback that directly and accurately referenced the assignment guidelines, explicit references to strengths in the student’s writing, and a positive tone that we anticipate would be encouraging to students. Statistical comparisons between different models, prompts, and rubric category scores are provided in Appendices C and D. One limitation of Mistral Large 2 was that feedback frequently included direct rewrites of student work unless a “no rewrite” prompt was used. Google Gemini 2.5 Flash and Pro were added more recently to ZotGPT. With our examples, we found that the Gemini models provided comparable feedback to Mistral for average or excellent reports, but below-average reports were subject to worse quality feedback, particularly when using the “no rewrite” prompt. We theorize that this discrepancy may be due to the fact that the “no rewrite” prompt is an additional constraint that restricts and lowers the quality of the overall feedback more than it does for other models. Further inquiry into the nature of limitations is ongoing.

Limitations and plans for future use

Our goal here is to provide an example of how this assessment tool was used to systematically distinguish between GenAI models rather than propose Mistral as the universal first choice for all instructors and assignments. Models are updated frequently, and this data were collected in late 2025 on an institution-specific version of these models (ZotGPT) with one coder per model test. Previous work has shown that AI-generated feedback can cause issues due to factual errors introduced by the model or inconsistent feedback (17, 18). Therefore, we encourage instructors to implement the rubric to test multiple GenAI models with their own assignments and caution against using GenAI models as the sole source of feedback on writing assignments. We also encourage future users of the rubric to consider involving more than one scorer with experience in providing feedback on student work prior to making the final determination of which AI model is best for their particular assignment. Although the focus here was on the assessment of introduction sections, we have begun analysis of the AI-generated feedback of other sections (results and discussion) of student reports. Preliminarily, the feedback solicited for discussion sections appears consistent with the feedback provided for introductions. Our ability to assess the feedback provided on the results section was limited due to the fact that the models we had access to through ZotGPT lacked AI vision functionality. Because of this, results section feedback in these preliminary tests was limited to feedback on the results text and any information provided in tables. The models did not provide any feedback on images. If future users of the rubric use it with models capable of AI vision, we expect that they may not face this limitation. After testing, instructors can make recommendations in prompt design and model choice to students. We plan to use this rubric in the development of an assignment in which students will receive a suggested model and prompt(s) coupled with a self-reflection on the feedback’s impact on their writing.

CONCLUSION

We developed a rubric to assess the quality of student writing feedback from GenAI models. We anticipate that it could be used by other instructors interested in introducing AI-based feedback into the writing process. Assignments in which students learn to responsibly use AI tools to receive feedback may improve their AI literacy and provide an extra opportunity for feedback in classes that have limited instructor resources. After using this rubric to assess various GenAI models for their needs, instructors could recommend which model is most appropriate for student use. Instructors may also consider prioritizing tools that are free to students at their institution and do not use the data for training purposes to help improve equity and maintain privacy for students. This work provides a basis for the development of new assignments related to GenAI-based feedback during the writing and revision process in STEM courses.
