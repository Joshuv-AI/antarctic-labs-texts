Paying Less Generalization Tax: A Cross-Domain Generalization Study of RL Training for LLM Agents
Zhihan Liu, Lin Guan, Yixin Nie, Kai Zhang, Zhuoqun Hao, Lin Chen, Asli Celikyilmaz, Zhaoran Wang, Na Zhang

Abstract
Generalist LLM agents are often post-trained on a narrow set of environments but deployed across far broader, unseen domains. In this work, we investigate the challenge of agentic post-training when the eventual test domains are unknown. Specifically, we analyze which properties of reinforcement learning (RL) environments and modeling choices have the greatest influence on out-of-domain performance. First, we identify two environment axes that strongly correlate with cross-domain generalization: (i) state information richness, i.e., the amount of information for the agent to process from the state, and (ii) planning complexity, estimated via goal reachability and trajectory length under a base policy. Notably, domain realism and text-level similarity are not the primary factors; for instance, the simple grid-world domain Sokoban leads to even stronger generalization in SciWorld than the more realistic ALFWorld. Motivated by these findings, we further show that increasing state information richness alone can already effectively improve cross-domain robustness. We propose a randomization technique, which is low-overhead and broadly applicable: add small amounts of distractive goal-irrelevant features to the state to make it richer without altering the task. Beyond environment-side properties, we also examine several modeling choices: (a) SFT warmup or mid-training helps prevent catastrophic forgetting during RL but undermines generalization to domains that are not included in the mid-training datamix; and (b) turning on step-by-step thinking during RL, while not always improving in-domain performance, plays a crucial role in preserving generalization. Based on these findings, we offer practical guidance for agentic post-training when deployment domains are unknown: select or construct environments with higher state information richness and planning complexity, enable explicit reasoning, and apply light state randomization to improve generalization.

Introduction

Post-training for
generalist
large language model (LLM) agents is typically carried out on a narrow slice of tasks, while deployment spans far broader and shifting environments.
Even with well-maintained data flywheels, the full cycle of sampling, post-processing, and running large-scale training remains compute and time intensive. As a result, post-training can only incorporate a limited set of domains and tasks
(
Luo et al. 2024
)
. Empirically, this skew shows up when agents that look strong on popular leaderboards only deliver subpar user experience: recent studies find sizable gaps between benchmarked strengths and performance in real-world “office” tasks
(
Xu et al. 2024
;
Miller and Tang 2025
;
Jimenez et al. 2023
;
Jain et al. 2024
)
.
Therefore, when selecting or constructing post-training datasets, a key question is what types of task the agent should be trained on to ensure that the model not only achieves strong in-domain performance but also maintains generalization in unseen and broader scenarios.

On the other hand, online RL has become a principled tool for post-training LLMs, but it introduces its own constraints. Typical online RL training demands interactive environments that have reward signals and support the sampling of multiple rollouts. These simulation environments are costly to build and even infeasible for proprietary domains
(
Cao et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Samsami et al. 2024
)
. The state of public agentic environments reflects this difficulty: despite notable progress, most benchmarks remain narrow (e.g., specific websites, platforms, or workflows) and expose only slices of the broader task distribution
(
Deng et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Drouin et al. 2024
;
Boisvert et al. 2024
;
Le Sellier De Chezelles et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
. Furthermore, even for production models deployed in real traffic, the resulting flywheel data is predominantly single-rollout and off-policy with respect to the updated policy in RL. Therefore, we aim to understand what characteristics make a training environment effective in preserving broader out-of-domain performance, so that we can prioritize constructing such environments and tasks for LLM agent post-training
(
Guan et al. 2023
;
Hu et al. 2025
)
.

Overall, given the limited coverage in post-training data and the practical difficulty of constructing simulators at scale for online RL, we offer a data-centric perspective to study:

How can we strategically sample tasks or selectively build domain simulators for RL training that maximally preserves or transfers agentic capabilities of LLMs to unseen domains?

Before moving forward, we clarify that “generalization” has been assessed at different levels in prior work
(
Fu et al. 2025
;
Huan et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
. The first is in-domain: models are tested on held-out cases where the task space is similar but not identical to that seen in training (e.g., shopping on Amazon vs. eBay). The second is cross-domain generalization: transferring to tasks with substantially different tools, states, or goals (e.g., from shopping to mathematical problem solving). Much of the reported evidence for “RL generalization” concerns the first type, while our interest here lies in the second. In our preliminary experiments on multiple agentic domains, as RL training proceeds, performance on held-out (unseen) environments drops while in-domain scores improve. Therefore, this work is more prevention-focused: identify training- and generation-time choices that maximally preserve performance in environments not observed during post-training.

By performing RL training on four distinct environments (Table
1
), namely Webshop
(
Yao et al. 2022a
)
, Sokoban
(
Junghanns and Schaeffer 2001
)
, ALFWorld
(
Shridhar et al. 2021
)
, and SciWorld
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
, we find that two axes exhibit high correlation with
cross-domain performance
:

•

State information richness
: the volume of information that the agent must perceive and process from the state. Higher richness increases the
perception load
, forcing the policy to actively extract task-relevant signals from dense or noisy inputs.

•

Planning complexity
: the difficulty of composing long and contingent action sequences, which imposes a higher
reasoning load
. We estimate this via goal reachability under a base policy. Greater complexity exercises decomposition, progression, and potentially correction over a long horizon.

Given that state information richness and planning complexity are key factors for generalization, one natural follow-up question is whether we can make training environments more effective by manipulating either of these properties. In this work, we begin with state information richness as it is not intrinsic to a domain. Specifically, we apply state randomization by injecting small amounts of goal-irrelevant or domain-irrelevant content into the agent’s observations during training. This increases the information richness of states without altering other components in task space, and consistently improves cross-domain generalization. These results not only validate our hypothesis but also provide a practical method for augmenting any existing training environments.

Alongside the main experiments, we also examine two modeling choices relevant to generalization. First, we find that mid-training has a strong and lasting influence on generalization. Specifically, it deeply infuses knowledge into the model, such that performance on covered domains remains stable even after subsequent RL training on other domains. However, the performance drops significantly on domains not included in mid-training, revealing a clear trade-off: while mid-training consolidates knowledge and improves robustness to downstream training, it risks erasing prior knowledge in uncovered domains. Moreover, we find that enabling step-by-step thinking during both training and evaluation does not improve in-domain scores, but plays a key role in preserving generalization.
Taken together, all these findings offer concrete guidance for post-training when the target environment is unknown: select or construct training environments that have greater state information richness and planning complexity, enable explicit reasoning, and apply lightweight state randomization to better preserve generalization.

Contributions.
Our main contributions in this work are:

1.

Systematic investigation.
We systematically study cross-domain generalization in agentic RL and provide the first attempt to uncover its underlying environmental factors, showing that state information richness and planning complexity are important drivers of transferability to unseen environments.

2.

Causal validation and practical solutions.
We intervene on state information richness via a state randomization scheme that injects small amounts of goal- or domain-irrelevant content into state observations, consistently improving cross-domain robustness and providing causal validation of our findings. We further examine the effects of mid-training and explicit step-by-step thinking, uncovering their roles in generalization and the associated trade-offs.

Related Work

2.1
Multi-turn RL Training for LLM Agents

Recent works tackle the challenges of training LLMs as interactive agents via RL in multi-turn settings
(
Yao et al. 2022b
;
Sun et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2025c
)
. For example,
Wang et al. 2025c
and
Feng et al. 2025
extend the popular RL training framework VeRL
(
Sheng et al. 2024
)
from the standard single-turn settings (as in coding and math tasks) to the multi-turn settings. Besides,

Zhang et al. 2025a
introduce a scalable framework for agentic multi-turn RL that features an asynchronous generation-training pipeline and
Xi et al. 2025
propose a unified platform for training LLM agents in diverse environments.
Zhou et al. 2025
focus on the credit assignment problem in long dialogues. They develop a benchmark for collaborative multi-turn tasks such as team-based software development. These advances in LLM-agent frameworks, together with prior works such as AlphaGo
(
Silver et al. 2016
)
, demonstrate that with well-designed training environments and optimization strategies, RL agents can acquire highly specialized knowledge. Our work further expands the perspective of training specialized agents by investigating what types of environments are most effective for training generalist RL agents.

2.2
Cross-Domain Analysis for LLM Training

As LLMs are applied to a wide range of tasks, recent studies have examined how well RL fine-tuning generalizes to different domains.
Cheng et al. 2025
empirically measure the transferability of skills acquired in a training domain to other unseen domains. Besides,
Huan et al. 2025
compare the cross-domain generalization between SFT and RL on single-turn tasks and find that RL preserves more generalization than SFT.
These findings highlight the importance of understanding how an RL-tuned LLM performs on tasks beyond its training domains. Our work aligns with these perspectives, but with a particular emphasis on multi-turn agentic tasks, which are not well covered in previous studies.
Moreover, previous analyses largely focus on quantifying the correlation between training domains and performance on held-out domains. Our work moves beyond this by dissecting these correlations and explaining them through relevant environmental factors and modeling choices, which further informs the derivation of practical solutions for better generalization.

2.3
State Augmentation for Generalization and Robustness

Domain and state augmentation have long been explored as strategies to improve generalization, particularly in sim-to-real settings where agents trained in simulation must transfer to real-world deployments. These methods typically inject variations into the training environment by modifying background textures, object properties, lighting, or physics parameters
(
Tobin et al. 2017
;
Sadeghi and Levine 2017
;
Muratore et al. 2018
;
Loquercio et al. 2019
)
.
More recent work has applied similar ideas in visual RL by augmenting observations or introducing distractors to improve robustness across domains or simulator variants
(
Pinto et al. 2017
;
Packer et al. 2018
;
Hansen et al. 2021
;
Guan et al. 2021
;
Yue et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2025a
)
. It is important to note that our contribution lies beyond the technique of state randomization itself; we utilize it essentially as a tool to validate our systematic findings. Nevertheless, adapting it to LLM agent training is also meaningful: our work is the first to demonstrate semantic textual noise and controlled distraction degree are helpful for preventing OOD performance degradation in agentic RL.

3
Preliminary

3.1
RL Training for LLM Agents

Environment and Agent.

We consider a reinforcement learning framework in which a language model functions as an agent interacting with an environment to solve multi-step tasks described by a textual prompt
x\sim p(X)
.
At each discrete timestep
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, the agent perceives a state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
and produces a textual action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{V}^{n}
, where
\mathcal{V}
represents the token set and
n
is the maximum number of tokens generated.
The environment then emits a scalar feedback
r_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
and transitions to a subsequent state
s_{t+1}
.
An entire episode can be represented by the trajectory

\tau=\{(s_{1},a_{1},r_{1}),(s_{2},a_{2},r_{2}),\ldots,(s_{T},a_{T},r_{T})\}.

The agent’s behavior is dictated by a conditional policy
\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t},x)
parameterized by
\theta
, which defines the distribution over textual outputs given the current state and task prompt. The goal of the LLM agent is to find a policy
\pi
that maximizes the corresponding expected cumulative reward
\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)\mid\tau\sim\pi]
, where
R(\tau)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}
denotes the sum of rewards along the trajectory, and the expectation is taken with respect to the stochastic policy
\pi
.
In many practical agentic cases, the environment provides only binary success indicators at the end of an episode. As a result, the cumulative reward
R(\tau)
is also sparse, which enables direct adoption of single-turn optimization approaches like GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
.

Reinforcement Learning for LLM Agents.

Recent advancements in large-scale language model training have popularized a
group-based
strategy for reinforcement learning.
For a specific task prompt
x
, the agent samples
N
full-episode trajectories
\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{N}\}
with the latest policy.
Each trajectory
\tau_{i}
receives a scalar score
R(\tau_{i})
, representing the overall quality of the episode outcome.
Rather than estimating per-token value functions as in traditional actor–critic methods, group-based algorithms such as GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
derive normalized advantages using only the statistics computed from this group of trajectories:

A(a_{t}^{(i)})=\frac{R(\tau_{j})-\text{Mean}(\{R(\tau_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N})}{\text{Std}(\{R(\tau_{j})\}_{j=1}^{N})},

for all actions
\{a_{t}^{(i)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
that belong to a trajectory
\tau_{j}
.
And the RL optimization objective for GRPO is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p(X)}\,\mathbb{E}_{\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\left[\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min\!\left(\rho_{\theta}(a_{t}^{(i)})A(a_{t}^{(i)}),\,\mathrm{clip}\,\big(\rho_{\theta}(a_{t}^{(i)}),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\big)A(a_{t}^{(i)})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\parallel\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big),

where the importance sampling ratio between the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
and the sampling policy
\pi_{\text{old}}
is given by

\rho_{\theta}(a_{t}^{(i)})=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid s_{t}^{(i)},x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid s_{t}^{(i)},x)}.

Similar to many multi-turn RL works
(
Feng et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
, GRPO in its vanilla form can be seamlessly extended to multi-turn tasks with sparse rewards.

3.2
Cross-Domain Generalization of RL Training

In this subsection, we provide a definition of cross-domain generalization and clarify its connection to other types of generalization.
Let
e_{i}
denote a text-based agentic environment, and let
\mathcal{T}_{i}
be the set of tasks associated with
e_{i}
.
The most basic form of generalization evaluation tests a model’s performance on unseen tasks
within the same environment
.
Formally, for each environment
e_{i}
, we partition its task set into two non-overlapping subsets,

\mathcal{T}_{i}\;=\;\mathcal{T}_{i}^{\text{train}}\,\cup\,\mathcal{T}_{i}^{\text{val}},\qquad\mathcal{T}_{i}^{\text{train}}\,\cap\,\mathcal{T}_{i}^{\text{val}}\;=\;\emptyset.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{\text{env}}
be the index set of all environments. We then define the training and evaluation task collections

\mathcal{E}^{\text{train}}_{\text{ID}}\;=\;\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{env}}}\mathcal{T}_{i}^{\text{train}},\qquad\mathcal{E}^{\text{eval}}_{\text{ID}}\;=\;\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{env}}}\mathcal{T}_{i}^{\text{val}}.

We refer to this setup as
in-domain (ID) validation
.

Beyond this, one can evaluate a generalist agent on
unseen environments
that may differ substantially in states, actions, and dynamics.
Let
\mathcal{I}_{\text{env}}^{\text{train}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\text{env}}^{\text{eval}}
denote the index sets of environments used for training and evaluation, respectively, with

\mathcal{I}_{\text{env}}^{\text{train}}\,\cap\,\mathcal{I}_{\text{env}}^{\text{eval}}\;=\;\emptyset.

We define the corresponding sets of environments

\mathcal{E}^{\text{train}}\;=\;\{\,e_{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{env}}^{\text{train}}\,\},\qquad\mathcal{E}^{\text{eval}}\;=\;\{\,e_{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{env}}^{\text{eval}}\,\}.

We refer to this setup as the evaluation of
cross-domain generalization
or
out-of-domain (OOD) generalization
.
Due to the limited availability of public agentic domains, for cross-domain evaluation, we use one environment for training at a time and use the remaining for evaluation.

4
Analytical Framework

4.1
Domains and Metrics

We conduct our analysis on four representative agentic environments
(
Wang et al. 2025c
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
: WebShop
(
Yao et al. 2022a
)
, Sokoban
(
Junghanns and Schaeffer 2001
)
, ALFWorld
(
Shridhar et al. 2021
)
, and SciWorld
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
.
These domains cover distinct types of common agentic tasks, ranging from web navigation and information retrieval to spatial reasoning and embodied interaction.
While these environments vary across multiple dimensions—such as domain realism, action space size, and task horizon—our study identifies
state information richness
and
planning complexity
as the two primary factors influencing generalization. We will provide concrete definitions and measurements for these two axes in Section
5
to quantitatively analyze their correlation with out-of-domain performance.

•

WebShop

(
Yao et al. 2022a
)
: A text-based e-commerce simulator requiring the agent to navigate pages, filter attributes, and select products matching complex user queries.

•

Sokoban

(
Junghanns and Schaeffer 2001
)
: A classic grid-based puzzle where the agent needs to push boxes to targets while avoiding irreversible lock-states. Unlike the matrix representation used in prior work
(
Wang et al. 2025c
)
, we use explicit coordinate representations of walls, players, objects, and the target as the state representation. We find this necessary because RL training with
Llama-8B-instruct
fails to yield meaningful performance gains when using the original matrix representation
(
Wang et al. 2025c
)
.

•

ALFWorld

(
Shridhar et al. 2021
)
: A widely adopted benchmark that requires the agent to generate symbolic actions (e.g., “open fridge”, “take apple”) to interact with a household environment.

•

SciWorld

(
Wang et al. 2022
)
: An interactive simulator of a scientific laboratory. We include this as a significantly more challenging extension of ALFWorld. SciWorld features a larger action space and the requirements for agents to manipulate specialized tools and materials to execute complex, multi-stage experiments. Examples that compare between ALFWorld and SciWorld are provided in Appendix
B
.

Evaluation Protocol.

We perform RL training on one source domain and evaluate the performance on the remaining three to assess OOD generalization. Training runs for 150 steps, and we save checkpoints every 15 steps. We report the mean success rate (pass@1 per task) averaged over the last four checkpoints (i.e., checkpoints from steps 105 to 150). All experiments are repeated three times with different seeds.

4.2
Models

We adopt
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct

(
Grattafiori et al. 2024
)
as the base model. To establish a viable starting point for analyzing out-of-domain performance, the initial policy must exhibit non-trivial performance on all evaluation domains such that we can observe how the success rate changes over the course of subsequent RL on other training domains. However, the base model has a near-zero success rate in two domains, i.e., WebShop and SciWorld.

For WebShop, conducting a 20-step RL training on it can increase the in-domain success rate to 34.4%. The resulting policy, Checkpoint V1 (
Ckpt V1
), serves as an initial policy for all downstream experiments to be presented. However, for SciWorld, both the base model and
Ckpt V1
remain at a near-zero success rate even after extensive RL training. As a result, we resort to warming up the base model using SFT with expert demonstrations. This setup is similar to mid-training
(
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Mo et al. 2025
)
, which imparts sufficient prior knowledge for effective subsequent RL training. To ensure balanced initial performance on all domains, we also mixed self-generated trajectories from ALFWorld and WebShop into the SFT dataset. The details of the SFT dataset and training can be found in Appendix
A
. We refer to this mid-training checkpoint as Checkpoint V2 (
Ckpt V2
).

In summary,
Ckpt V1
and
Ckpt V2
serve as the two initial policies for our downstream experiments, with
Ckpt V2
additionally enabling us to investigate the influence of SFT warm-up or mid-training.

This section presents the initial experiment results based on the setup outlined in Section
4
. We aim to identify the properties of training environments that best align with the preservation of agent capabilities in unseen domains. We first report the performance of agents trained on each domain and evaluated on all others in Table
2
. Consistent with prior studiess
(
Luo et al. 2024
)
, while RL training significantly improves in-domain performance, it often leads to performance drops in OOD environments.

For a concise and clear comparison of how different training domains affect robustness, we introduce a ranking-based metric. For each training domain, we calculate its performance rank on every OOD domain (e.g., 1 represents the best performance compared against checkpoints trained on other training domains) and compute the sum of these ranks. We refer to this sum as OOD Ranking Score.

The aggregated results are shown in Table
2
, with detailed ranks provided in Table
8
in Appendix. Recall from Section
4.2
that
Ckpt V1
and
Ckpt V2
are applicable to different training domains due to SciWorld’s requirement for SFT warmup. Consequently, we report two sets of aggregated results: one based solely on Ckpt V2, and the other combining Ckpt V1 and Ckpt V2. The former compares all training domains (including SciWorld), while the latter compares the other three domains across a broader set of experimental trials. With
Ckpt V2
, SciWorld yields the most robust training outcome (ranking score: 3), closely followed by Sokoban (ranking score: 5). WebShop ranks third (ranking score: 6), while ALFWorld performs the worst (ranking score: 8). A similar pattern is observed in the combined results (
Ckpt V1
and
Ckpt V2
), where Sokoban is more effective (ranking score: 7) than both WebShop and ALFWorld, which are tied with a ranking score of 10.

Based on these rankings, our goal is to identify factors that correlate with stronger OOD performance. Given the complexity and the exploratory nature of such an analysis, we do not aim to exhaustively identify all factors, but rather to reveal at least some key insights that can inform the selection or construction of training domains.

Overall, we find two planning-related concepts below better explain the effectiveness of environments. For a quantitative analysis, we ground these two concepts into two quantifiable metrics, which are formally defined and measured as follows:

1.

State Information Richness:
State information richness refers to the volume of information contained in the environment states. A higher state information richness means that the agent must process a larger amount of content from the state to extract useful information for the current decision-making. We note that the observations in all four studied domains are highly compact, which convey each piece of state information in a similarly concise manner (see Appendix
B
for examples). In this case, a feasible way of quantifying information richness would be to use the
average character count of environment states
.
Practically, we employ
Ckpt V2
to collect 128 trajectories per domain with a maximum step limit
T_{\max}=50
and calculate the average length of the states within the sampled trajectories.

2.

Planning Complexity:
Planning complexity refers to the difficulty of composing a multi-step plan to solve the task. A higher planning complexity indicates that the task requires a longer, more sustained planning chain and has a lower goal reachability. Although planning complexity is often measured by properties such as branching factors or search depth in the classical planning literature
(
de Roo 2016
)
, these metrics are less applicable to LLM agents that leverage strong priors rather than relying on conventional search space traversal. Instead, we use
average trajectory length
as an empirical proxy of search depth to measure the
reasoning effort
in order to reach the terminal states.
Specifically, we also employ
Ckpt V2
to collect 128 trajectories per domain with a maximum step limit
T_{\max}=50
and compute this metric by averaging the trajectory lengths, where failed trajectories are assigned the maximum length
T_{\max}
to reflect the inability to solve the task within the budget. The average trajectory length in this case captures both task horizon and goal reachability.

Table
3
shows how the state information richness and the planning complexity are correlated to the robustness rankings. Specifically, Sokoban exhibits high state richness (3114 characters) and high planning complexity (44.0 steps), while SciWorld similarly combines substantial planning complexity (43.5 steps) with moderate-to-high information richness. This indicates that environments that demand both high perception loads and deep reasoning chains foster better generalization preservation.
In contrast, the two less effective domains, namely WebShop and AlfWorld, exhibit lower complexity in at least one dimension. Specifically, although WebShop has a richer state space characterized by lengthy product descriptions and feature lists, it demands relatively low planning complexity. A WebShop agent generally only needs to iterate through the products to identify a match based on attributes. Similarly, while AlfWorld involves more complicated task logic, its state space remains relatively simple and compact.

From Table

\Delta_{\text{ID}}
) over the course of RL. The results seem to suggest a naive trade-off: intuitively, the more specialized knowledge absorbed through RL training, the lower the model’s OOD performance. This raises the question of whether knowledge absorption is a more dominant factor than the environment properties discussed above. To answer this, we conducted an additional experiment where we extended the training steps for Sokoban and SciWorld to further increase their
\Delta_{\text{ID}}
, comparing the final checkpoints to the ALFWorld and WebShop checkpoints at 150 steps. As shown in Table

\Delta_{\text{ID}}
of Sokoban is increased to match or exceed that of ALFWorld and WebShop, its OOD performance remains higher. These results confirm the impact of the two environment properties and suggest that OOD performance is not simply determined by
\Delta_{\text{ID}}
. Additionally, we also identify several qualitative factors that have low correlations with OOD generalization. For instance, domain realism does not explain the performance differences. Sokoban, an abstract grid-world puzzle, enables strong generalization even to realistic domains like SciWorld. In contrast, ALFWorld, which simulates realistic household tasks, ranks lowest for cross-domain robustness.

6
From Correlation to Practical Solution for Preserving Generalization

The analysis in Section
5
identifies two environmental properties, namely planning complexity and state information richness, which align with cross-domain generalization. A natural next step is to intervene along either of these axes to test whether modifying them can causally enhance generalization. Since planning complexity is largely intrinsic to the task logic and difficult to manipulate without altering the environment’s underlying dynamics, we focus on increasing
state information richness
through a technique we call
State Information Augmentation
. This approach involves enriching observations with goal-irrelevant information while keeping task goals, action spaces, and reward functions unchanged. Intuitively, this approach forces the agent to more actively extract task-relevant signals while ignoring distractive noise, which reduces the model’s reliance on domain-specific patterns.

Formally, let
s
denote the original state and
g
the goal. For each state
s
, we sample a short goal-irrelevant text fragment
\delta
from a distribution of content that does not interfere with the original optimal policy conditioned on
g
. We thereby construct an augmented state
s^{\prime}=\mathrm{Augment}(s,\delta,\epsilon)
by injecting a controlled volume
\epsilon
of goal-irrelevant information
\delta
into the textual state. Note that the augmentation is applied only to the agent’s input; components such as transitions and rewards still operate on the original state.

Practically, there can be several options for coming up with valid augmentations that do not interfere with the original optimal plan. With minimal domain knowledge, one could inject observations from a dramatically different domain into the training environment. For instance, we can inject Webshop observation into an Alfworld state by appending “there is a monitor showing a shopping webpage: ¡random webshop state¿.” With slightly more understanding of a domain, one can come up with more contextual-relevant augmentation. For example, for the ALFWorld task “slice an apple”, valid augmentations could involve describing unrelated static objects (e.g., “the wall is painted blue”) or redundant features (e.g., “the apple is red”). Examples of augmentation for all training domains can be found in Appendix
C
. Nevertheless, we note that this study emphasizes verifying how increased state information richness can facilitate cross-domain generalization, instead of focusing extensively on the exact technique for constructing state augmentation.

Another practical consideration is setting the volume (
\epsilon
) of distractive information, which can also be quantified using the character count of the injected text. Our experiments reveal insufficient augmentation may not yield a noticeable change in OOD performance, while an excessive amount of noise can make the tasks overly difficult for the agent to learn, thereby negatively affecting ID performance. In practice, the appropriate
\epsilon
can be determined by interactively increasing its value until a significant drop in ID success rate is observed. Furthermore, we found it beneficial to stochastically apply the augmentation to only a portion of trajectories within a batch. This allows for better control over learning difficulty on top of adjusting the noise volume. In our experiments, we apply the augmentation to each trajectory with a probability of 50%, and the results suggest that this strategy helps maintain ID performance.

7
Other Influential Factors on Cross-domain Generalization

During the experiments on the environment-side intervention, we also determined two modeling-side design choices that have a significant influence on OOD performance. This section proceeds by first discussing how SFT warmup impacts knowledge retention and forgetting during the subsequent RL phase, followed by an analysis of the necessity of explicit reasoning for both ID and OOD performance.

7.2

8
Limitations and Future Work

This work establishes a practical framework for analyzing and improving cross-domain generalization in agentic post-training. Looking ahead, several promising directions merit further exploration.
First, expanding this study to a broader suite of domains and diverse model scales would help validate the universality of factors identified as critical for OOD performance. A larger-scale investigation may also reveal additional factors that influence generalization.
Second, there is room to further refine the empirical measures used to characterize effective training environments. Developing more formal and causally grounded definitions would support more principled environment design.
Methodology-wise, future work could investigate automated environment augmentation techniques, the relationship between reasoning structure and generalization, and adaptive SFT warmup schedules that dynamically balance knowledge consolidation and flexibility.
Lastly, connecting these empirical findings to theoretical interpretations of generalization and policy optimization remains an important step toward establishing quantitative design principles for scalable and robust agent training.

9
Conclusion

In this work, we take a data-centric perspective on RL post-training for
generalist
LLM agents, and probe how to strategically select or construct training domains so that agentic capabilities can be maximally preserved in unseen domains.
By analyzing correlations between cross-domain performance and properties of training environments, we find that realism and surface-level semantic similarity are not the main factors. Instead,
state information richness
and
planning complexity
, which respectively control perception load and reasoning load, align more with OOD performance.

Building upon these observations, we causally validate the importance of state information richness by directly intervening on this dimension. We introduce a state randomization method that injects small amounts of goal-irrelevant content into the agent’s observations, increasing information volume without changing the task space. Beyond environment-side designs, we also examine two important modeling choices, namely mid-training and step-by-step thinking. Our results reveal that mid-training or SFT warmup can deeply infuse domain knowledge into the policy model, and the infused knowledge can retain even after subsequent RL that is conducted on other domains. However, mid-training can also lead to larger generalization degradation on uncovered domains compared to RL. We further find that, while enabling step-by-step thinking during RL and evaluation does not increase ID success rates, it plays a crucial role in transferring agentic capabilities to unseen environments. Taken together, these results provide practical guidance for post-training when future test environments are unknown: select or construct training environments with higher state information richness and planning complexity, enable explicit step-by-step thinking, and apply lightweight state randomization to better maintain robustness and generalization.

The learning rate is 5e-6 with the cosine learning rate scheduler and warmup ratio 0.1. The batch size is 32. We train the model for 100 steps to best balance the performance on SciWorld and the performance on ALFWorld, Webshop, and Sokoban.

Appendix C
Information Augmentation Details

In this section, we provide the implementation details of state augmentation. We also cover the selection of information volume for ALFWorld, Webshop, and Sokoban.

C.1
ALFWorld

For ALFWorld, we increase state information richness by introducing synthetic distractor objects. To introduce distractor objects that do not interfere with the original tasks, we employ a multi-step synthesis process. First, we sample an object type from a predefined list:
{bowl, cup, pan, spoon, fork, knife, plate, mug, bottle, can, glass, basket, tray, vase, jar, container, jug, thermos, pitcher, dish, tumbler, sponge, towel, napkin, rack, coaster, utensil, lid}
. Adhering to ALFWorld’s naming convention of (
type, ID
), if the sampled object type currently exists in the scene, we will assign a distinct ID to ensure uniqueness.

Finally, we sample a “trivial” object descriptor from the list:
{cracked, dirty, slightly burnt, covered in dust, wrapped in foil, sticky, still warm, rusty, filled with water, emits a faint smell, has some liquid inside, tipped over, seems unused, greasy, wet on the surface, has a faint label, smudged, with faded color, shows fingerprints}
. The objects and their descriptors are combined using short natural-language templates such as “You notice a … that looks …” or “Near the sink, there is a … that is …”. Such distractive sentences are inserted at random positions in the textual states (i.e., observations).

The augmentation strength is controlled by an information volume parameter
\epsilon
. We estimate the length of each sentence as 12 tokens and inject
n_{\text{distractive}}=\lfloor\epsilon/12\rfloor
sentences per state. For example,
\epsilon=120
yields about 10 extra sentences, while
\epsilon=360
yields about 30.

C.2
Webshop

We consider injecting distractive information into two types of pages in WebShop, namely product-details pages and search-result pages.

On both page types, we inject irrelevant advertising information in the form of (
clothing categories
,
promotional descriptors
). For clothing categories, we sample from the following list:
{rompers, jumpsuits, blouses, cozy cardigans, pajamas, loungewear sets, button-down tops, long sleeve tees, casual jackets, knitwear, soft sweaters, thermal layers, fleece pullovers, v-neck tunics, red outfits, plus-size picks, cropped hoodies, layered looks, textured knits, everyday basics}
. For promotional descriptors, we sample from the following list:
{30% off, new arrivals, trending now, selling fast, limited edition, back in stock, editor’s pick, members-only deal, must-have looks, staff favorite, top-rated, recommended for daily wear, cozy & cute, perfect for layering, style spotlight, hand-picked for you, fan favorites, most loved by shoppers, inspired by you, perfect for your capsule wardrobe}
. Categories and descriptors are combined into short ad sentences via the template:
[AD] <descriptor> --- shop <category> today!
.

On product-detail pages, we introduce trivial features sampled from the following list: {
{fabric: machine wash cold}
,
{shipping note: ships within 5-7 days}
,
{sizing tip: runs half size large}
,
{model info: model is 5’8’’ wearing size medium}
,
{brand description: known for cozy minimalism}
,
{care label: made in Indonesia}
,
{origin: eco-conscious packaging}
,
{batch code: Spring 2024 release}
,
{warehouse tag: popular for travel & errands}
,
{return policy: soft fleece interior}
,
{manufacturing month: delicate stitching on seams}
,
{recommendation: designed for colder climates}
,
{intended use: do not iron over print}
,
{layering advice: packaged folded in bag}
}.

On search-result pages, we also create trivial products by sampling non-targeted product types paired with non-targeted features. The product types include
{romper, jumpsuit, blouse, tunic, pajama top, sweater, tank top, hoodie, cardigan, bodysuit, thermal shirt, dress, coverall, camisole}
. Non-targeted features include
{sleeveless, misleadingly tagged, overpriced, heavily discounted, out of stock, not available in large size, no reviews, size runs small, fabric mismatch, zipper broken, category mislabel, style mismatch, faded color, missing product images, customer complaints}
. The information is presented with templates such as
Women <desc> <obj> --- likely unsuitable
or
Warning: <desc> <obj> (cannot be selected)
.

The information volume
\epsilon
determines how many of such units are inserted. On each result page, we allocate up to 10 additional item slots and fill
k=\min(\lfloor(\epsilon/100)\,\alpha\cdot 10\rfloor,10)
of them with a mix of advertisements and trivial products (e.g., with
\epsilon=100
and
\alpha=0.5
, we insert
k=5
entries). On each detail page, we append
k=\max(1,\lfloor\epsilon/25\rfloor)
sentences about product features, and
k=\max(1,\lfloor\epsilon/30\rfloor)
sentences about advertisements.

C.3
Sokoban

Augmentation Implementation

For Sokoban, the augmentation operates at the level of the textual description of the grid. Given the true room dimensions
H\times W
, we sample coordinates that lie strictly outside the truly reachable locations and associate each with a random object drawn from the list:
{Broken crate, Sealed door, Pile of rubble, Faded sign, Leaking pipe, Loose tile, Stacked boxe}
. The locations are described using phrases from
{outside the room, beyond the wall, inaccessible, behind a collapsed wall, on a blocked corridor}
.

These elements are combined with the template
(r, c) shows a <obj> (<desc>; unreachable).
. The final sentences are appended as additional lines after the original textual state. Here, the information volume
\epsilon
directly controls the number of appended sentences: we estimate the length of each sentence as 10 tokens and generate
k=\max(1,\lfloor\epsilon/10\rfloor)
distractor lines (e.g.,
\epsilon=50
yields about 5 lines of unreachable locations, while
\epsilon=150
yields about 15). Again, all distractors are explicitly placed outside the grid.
