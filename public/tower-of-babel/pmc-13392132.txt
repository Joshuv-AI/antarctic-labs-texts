Quantum logic operations and algorithms in a single 25-level atomic qudit
Authors: Pei Jiang Low, Nicholas C F Zutt, Gaurav A Tathed, Crystal Senko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScaling quantum computers remains a substantial scientific and technological challenge. Leveraging the full range of intrinsic degrees of freedom in quantum systems offers a promising route towards enhanced algorithmic performance and hardware efficiency. We experimentally study the use of 137Ba+ ions for quantum information processing, achieving high-fidelity state preparation and readout of up to 25 internal levels, thus forming a 25-dimensional trapped-ion qudit. By probing superpositions of up to 24 states, we investigate how errors scale with qudit dimension d and identify the primary error sources affecting quantum coherence. Additionally, we demonstrate high-dimensional qudit operations by implementing a 3-qubit Bernstein-Vazirani algorithm and a 4-qubit Toffoli gate with a single trapped-ion. Our findings suggest that quantum computing architectures based on large-dimensional qudits hold significant promise.Subject terms: Quantum information, Qubits

Introduction

Efforts to scale quantum systems for fault-tolerant computation have focused on quantum analogues of binary computing1–4. However, trapped ions—a leading quantum computing platform5–7—have a richer energy-level structure than the 2 levels typically used for qubits. Recently, a qudit encoding approach, which moves to multi-valued logic for quantum algorithms, has gained traction8–11. Qudit-based encoding enables several useful applications, such as: directly working with base d qudits to scale computational space faster per ion12–14, relaxed error-correction thresholds15,16, defining multiple virtual qubits per ion17–19, using in situ measurement-free error correction by encoding qubit states in larger Hilbert spaces20,21, and directly simulating higher-dimensional systems22–24.

Trapped ion qudit-based quantum computing has been demonstrated with smaller dimensions (d ≤ 7)10,13,25,26, constrained by the number of (meta)stable states in the chosen ion species. Increasing d, though, only benefits the applications listed above. For N qudits, computational space scales as dN8. Bosonic system simulations benefit from larger truncations of the boson’s (infinite) Hilbert space24,27. Extra states enable qubit encodings that correct more known error sources28–30. These considerations motivate work with isotopes such as 137Ba+31,32, 43Ca+33, and 173Yb+34, whose large nuclear spins yield many (meta)stable states. Laboratory control of these complex energy-level structures will enable computation with up to tens of states per ion.

In this work, we demonstrate coherent control and readout of a 25-level 137Ba+ trapped ion qudit encoded in the S1/2 and D5/2 electronic levels—the maximum number of states discriminable in a single-shot measurement using known protocols. This is the largest digital encoding of a trapped ion qudit reported in the literature to date. We modify and extend the state preparation techniques in refs. 35,36 to show heralded state preparation and measurement for d = 25 with a fidelity of 99.51 ± 0.05%. We characterise the multi-level coherence in Ramsey-like experiments (which have been shown using similar measurements for d < 6 in other platforms37–39) for up to d = 24 states. Using coherence measurements and results from independent noise characterisation studies, we develop an experimentally validated physical error model for single qudit operations on high-dimensional qudits—with a detailed, device-level list of mechanisms and their impacts on system coherence. Performance of the Bernstein–Vazirani algorithm is a popular benchmark for quantum processors40, and we implement this algorithm on 2 and 3 virtual qubits in a single 137Ba+ ion. We also demonstrate an advantage of virtual qubit encodings in high-dimensional qudits by realising a CCCNOT gate on 16 basis levels with a 99.5 ± 0.2% success probability (compared to 92.2% in qubits41 and 88% in qutrits42). Our model projects that combined errors from these sources can be reduced to the 10−3 level or better for d ≤ 16 using demonstrated engineering improvements, which establishes the viability of scalable, high-dimensional encoding strategies in trapped-ion qudits.

Results

All results are measured with a single 137Ba+ ions confined in a hand-assembled Paul trap43 with secular motional frequencies of (1.27, 1.46, 0.21) MHz. Doppler cooling and fluorescence detection use 493 nm and 650 nm lasers driving the S1/2 − P1/2 and D3/2 − P1/2 transitions, respectively. A linewidth-narrowed 1762 nm shelving laser, incident with wavevector orthogonal to the weak trap axis, has its frequency tuned via arbitrary voltage waveforms applied to an electro-optic phase modulator. Its polarisation is set so that frequency adjustments alone can drive any quadrupole-allowed transition between the S1/2 and D5/2 states. Two (or 5) reference transitions are used to calibrate all shelving transition frequencies (or strengths) (“Methods” “Experimental setup”). A 614 nm laser repumps the D5/2 states via the P3/2 states to the S1/2 manifold.

Qudit encoding and SPAM

Qudits are encoded in the S1/2 and D5/2 magnetic sublevels44. We initialise according to a narrow-band optical pumping (NBOP) protocol, diagrammed in Fig. 1a, b, which is performed similarly for each target initial state in S1/2, F = 2. The sequence consists of: (1) flushing S1/2, F = 1 levels with 493 nm and 650 nm lasers; (2) pulsing four frequencies on the 1762 nm shelving laser to empty all S1/2, F = 2 levels except the target state; (3) repumping the D5/2 states with 614 nm light (tuned to preferentially excite P3/2, F = 3 states to minimise decay into S1/2, F = 1) and 650 nm light. This sequence is repeated until the targeted S1/2 state population saturates (Fig. 1c).

Whereas previous NBOP demonstrations have been limited to one target initial state, chosen due to their simplified repumping pathways35,45, our method allows for flexible initialisation in any of the S1/2, F = 2 states. As a result, in step (2), the 1762 nm transitions must be selected according to two crucial criteria. First, a detailed analysis of the re-pumping pathways through P3/2 (Supplementary Materials II) helps determine which states in D5/2 should be targeted for shelving. This selection is essential for the efficient implementation of the protocol. Second, the transitions employed to move the population to D5/2 must be sufficiently spectrally isolated to ensure initialisation with high fidelity.

To measure initialisation fidelity, we probe each initialised state separately. Six π-pulses of 1762 nm light shift population from the initialised S1/2, F = 2 state to distinct D5/2 states, followed by a fluorescence check with 493 nm and 650 nm light. A dark outcome indicates the ion is in D5/2 and corresponds to successful initialisation. This technique mitigates shelving pulse infidelity as a contributor to S1/2 state preparation and measurement (SPAM) error, at the expense of measuring only one basis state per shot. We measure a 98.6 ± 0.8% initialisation probability, averaged over all five states, at n = 80 sequence repetitions (Fig. 1d). Part of the observed error may arise from off-resonant 493 nm light driving population out of the target state. We also find evidence that off-resonant 1762 nm excitation to D5/2, for example via motional sidebands (Supplementary Materials II), contributes to infidelity.

The D5/2 state initialisation begins with NBOP to a chosen S1/2 level, usually the state with the strongest transition to the target D5/2 state (Fig. 2a). A single 1762 nm laser shelving pulse is then used to drive the ion to the desired state. The order of the encoded states follows their basis state numbers ∣i> and was mainly chosen to de-shelve the fastest transitions first, in order to mitigate off-resonant de-shelving error. We implement single-shot state measurement of the 25-level system by first checking for fluorescence in the encoded S1/2 state, then sequentially de-shelving each D5/2 state to S1/2 and checking for fluorescence after each pulse. The first bright outcome is assigned as the measurement result, and we measure 99.51 ± 0.05% SPAM fidelity when averaged over all 25 levels (Fig. 2b).

We detect initialisation faults with a heralding method similar to ref. 36, discarding data points with known failures (Fig. 2c). Following the shelving pulse, a fluorescence check of S1/2 results in a bright outcome for unsuccessful initialisation. To employ this method for ∣0⟩ state preparation, we shelve the state to the D5/2∣3⟩ state (Fig. 2a) using a 1762 nm transition with low π-pulse error. We then perform the heralding measurement, keep or discard the computation attempt accordingly, then de-shelve the population from D5/2 back to the initial S1/2 state. If no bright outcome is detected over the 25 levels, this indicates failure to de-shelve from D5/2, raising an error flag on that computation44,46, resulting in data loss (Fig. 2c). We report averaged data loss rates over the 25 basis states of 2.82 ± 0.05% from initialisation and 1.51 ± 0.03% from measurement.

Three dominant error sources impact this SPAM result: off-resonant driving, spontaneous decay from D5/2 → S1/2, and bright/dark discrimination error (fluorescence check infidelity). Due to the two heralding techniques used, the measured SPAM fidelity is robust against 1762 nm laser pulse errors, which are recast as data losses. The exception is preparation of ∣0>, where the initialisation herald is followed by a de-shelving pulse from the ∣3> encoded state. The ∣3⟩→∣0⟩ pulse infidelity introduces a 0.5% chance of misdiagnosing ∣0> as ∣3>. This sensitivity to pulse error explains the sharp rise in expected error from d = 3 to 4 in Fig. 2d, where this pulse error is included with other off-resonant driving effects. Beyond this, average infidelity can decrease as more spectrally isolated states are added, despite not every subset dimension being optimised. To verify the error model and study qudit SPAM error rates for d ≤ 25 (Fig. 2d), we re-analyse the data from Fig. 2b, including only the first d prepared states and reinterpreting heralding outcomes. The modelled errors agree with experimental data to within measurement precision and exhibit linear or sub-linear scaling with d (details in Supplementary Materials III).

This qudit SPAM result is competitive with qubit SPAM results on other platforms47–50. Through our error modelling (Supplementary Materials V.A), we project that heralded SPAM errors can be engineered to ~10−4 level for qudits with d ≤ 25 through optimised photon collection (NA = 0.8 51), shorter readout time (800 μs), and optimised magnetic field (∣B∣ = 7.13 G). This would make qudit results comparable to the best recent SPAM results in any qubit systems35,36.

Multi-level coherence

To demonstrate coherent control of this multi-level system, we have developed a protocol generalising qubit Ramsey interferometry techniques, similar to but distinct from refs. 37,52 in other platforms, to probe the mutual coherence of all d qudit states simultaneously. We study superpositions of up to d = 24 states.

For each dimension d, we select a different subset of physical states to encode the qudit. For d < 18, we optimise a cost function accounting for transition strengths and mutual magnetic-field sensitivities in sets of size d (“Methods” “State choices for encoding SPAM, qudits, and virtual qubits”). This optimisation is constrained to a “star” transition graph with one S1/2 state as the central node. Beyond this, no more D5/2 states connect to a single S1/2 level with usable transitions, so we extend the d = 17 encoding by adding D5/2 states that minimise the total probe sequence duration.

The protocol prepares an equal superposition of d basis states spanning the qudit, then probes how well the population can be re-phased into a single basis state. As sketched in Fig. 3a, after initialising to ∣0⟩, we apply quadrupole transition pulses with rotation angles

In an ideal noise-free scenario, when ϕ is an integer multiple of 2π/d, the ∣0⟩ population at the end of the protocol is either 100% if ϕ is a multiple of 2π, or 0% otherwise (derivation in “Methods” “Qudit Ramsey-type experiment—analytic expressions”). To obtain a global figure of merit for mutual coherence of the qudit system, we measure ∣0> population at ϕ = 0 and at ϕ = π for even d (or π(d − 1)/d for odd d). The difference, or contrast, is 100% in the ideal case, while decoherence and control pulse imperfections reduce this value.

Contrast measurements as a function of qudit dimension d (Fig. 3e) show how multipartite superpositions are coherently manipulated in this system. As more states are added, contrast decreases due to the inclusion of states with differing magnetic field sensitivities and longer pulse sequences. A sharp drop in contrast occurs for d > 17, where lengthier multi-pulse rotations are required. Independent measurements (Supplementary Materials IV) identify the primary error sources as magnetic field noise, laser frequency and power fluctuations, transition frequency and strength miscalibration, and A/C line-induced magnetic field changes, which dephase transitions. Phase coherence between an S1/2 state and a D5/2 state is sensitive to both laser frequency and magnetic field noise, while coherence between two states in the same manifold is unaffected by laser frequency noise, even when using multi-step 1762 nm transitions (Supplementary Materials IV). We measure coherence times within D5/2 up to two orders of magnitude longer than between the S1/2 and D5/2 manifolds.

We combine independent error measurements into a full physical error model with no free parameters, using shot-to-shot Monte Carlo sampling (“Methods” “Monte-Carlo simulation of noisy multi-level system”). The model yields results within experimental precision of the measured qudit contrasts (solid black line in Fig. 3e). This modelling identifies the dominant error sources for each qudit dimension d: pulse angle error dominates at lower d, while laser frequency noise and A/C line-induced magnetic field changes dominate at higher d. Our current trap apparatus lacks fibre noise cancellation and magnetic field shielding, both of which could mitigate these dominant errors.

Using literature-reported noise levels, our model projects contrast loss below 10−4 with demonstrated technologies for d ≤ 10 (star markers in Fig. 3e). Even at d = 16, we predict a (1.0 ± 0.2) × 10−3 contrast loss for these multi-level superpositions. These estimates assume 0.06 μG magnetic field fluctuations, 25 μG A/C line signal amplitude53, laser with spectral full-width at half-max of 1.2 Hz54,55 (Supplementary Table I), 10 Hz frequency calibration errors enabled by longer coherence times (Supplementary Materials I), and 0.1% pulse angle errors. In the Supplementary Text, we detail the engineering specifications required to reduce these modelled errors to 10−4 or better. While this pulse sequence does not reflect a full universal gate set, it indicates the errors achievable for single-qudit unitary operations manipulating all d states coherently.

Quantum information processing with virtual qubits

To demonstrate that these experimental tools extend to implementation and diagnostics of single-qudit gates and circuits, we focus on the scenario of encoding n virtual qubits using d = 2n basis states, which enables more direct comparisons with qubit-based platforms. We select an optimised subset of states (“Methods” “State choices for encoding SPAM, qudits, and virtual qubits”) and implement full control using the single 1762 nm phase- and frequency-controlled laser to drive quadrupole transitions between S1/2 ↔ D5/2. This allows any unitary operation (single- and multi-qubit gates) to be decomposed into sequences of Givens rotations between pairs of states56,57. This approach enables a greater number of possible transitions and reduced off-resonant driving, in comparison to RF control, though signal-multiplexed RF offers opportunities to shorten overall runtimes58.

We implement the Bernstein-Vazirani key finding algorithm59, given as a circuit diagram in Fig. 4a. The algorithm aims to determine an unknown n-bit key s, given an oracle implementing f: 0, 1n → 0, 1, where f(x) is x ⋅ s modulo 2 for secret s ∈ 0, 1n. Initialising the system in ∣0⟩⊗n for n ∈ 2, 3 amounts to preparing the ion in the appropriate ∣mf⟩ state in S1/2 (encodings in Fig. 4b). We find pulse decompositions implementing H⊗n using 5 Givens rotations for two virtual qubits and 21 for three (Supplementary Materials VI). The oracle ∣x⟩→(−1)f(x)∣x⟩ is implemented via virtual-Z gates, by adding π to the phase of rotations involving basis states ∣x> in the second H⊗n. This ensures the oracle is noiseless and instantaneous.

To minimise algorithm run-time for three virtual qubits, we abbreviate the initial H⊗n gate before the oracle by exploiting the fact that the system always starts in ∣0⟩n. This allows direct evolution to 12n∑02n−1∣x⟩ using 2n − 1 rotations. A full H⊗3 gate with 1.2 ms total pulse time is thus replaced by a “superposition pulse” of 7 rotations in 170 μs (Supplementary Materials VI).

This implementation achieves a successful key-finding probability of 97.9 ± 0.2% for 2 virtual qubits and 83.8 ± 0.8% for 3 virtual qubits (Fig. 4c, d). The difference in the success probabilities can be explained primarily by the longer execution time; for 2 and 3 virtual qubits, they are 380 μs and 1100 μs, respectively. These results are on par with recent multi-qubit implementations of the same algorithm40. We attribute the comparable fidelities in part to the lack of multi-ion gate errors, highlighting an advantage of the virtual qubit approach. Ordinarily, Hadamard gates H⊗n could be applied in parallel across separate qubits; in this system, they are an unfavourable gate type for virtual qubit encoding. We are currently limited to sequential Givens rotations, resulting in longer run times compared to parallel gates on separate ions, though this could be improved by simultaneous driving of transitions. No active noise mitigation techniques are used apart from triggering experiment start times to the A/C line signal. Using our error model, we find that A/C line magnetic field changes, despite triggering, dominate the error, causing the XXI-type errors in the 3 virtual qubit result (Fig. 4d). Based on the aforementioned noise levels (see Supplementary Table I), we predict a mean failure probability of (4.0 ± 0.3) × 10−3 for 3-bit key finding is attainable with current technology.

Unlike the relatively intensive H⊗n gates, we show how complex gates such as Toffoli or CCCNOT can be implemented using an optimised state encoding strategy to implement this gate in a single 26.6 μs pulse via a swap between ∣1110⟩ and ∣1111⟩ in the 4 virtual qubit encoding (Fig. 4e). The CCCNOT gate achieves the correct output state with a probability of 99.5 ± 0.2% averaged over the 16 basis states, limited mainly by SPAM errors, outperforming similar gates on multiple physical qubits or qutrits41,42,60. The way in which the multi-qubit basis states are encoded in this example enables a particularly simple gate implementation. Trade-offs between quantum encoding strategies (qubit, qudit, or multiple virtual qubits) are an active area of study8,61 and are vital for assessing multi-level computing advantages. In particular, as our CCCNOT implementation highlights, reducing two-ion gate counts may provide advantages to high-dimensional qudit architectures, despite the increased complexity of some single-qudit gates.

Discussion

The high-fidelity SPAM and coherent control demonstrated in this work are key primitives for quantum information processing62 and establish the viability of high-dimensional qudit encoding in 137Ba+. Notably, these capabilities are achieved without significant modifications to standard qubit trapped-ion hardware; they rely primarily on software-level control and laser parameters already present in typical architectures. The pulse decomposition scheme used for the Bernstein–Vazirani algorithm can be extended to a full single-qudit gate set63. As qudit dimension increases, the overall number of qudits and the number of entangling gates needed to implement a given algorithm decrease, at the expense of increased single-qudit gate complexity19. Further theoretical work is needed to fully understand these types of trade-offs in high-dimensional architectures. The calibration techniques discussed in Supplementary Materials I scale efficiently to multi-ion chains: rather than calibrating every transition frequency on every ion, all transition frequencies in a chain can be inferred from just two Ramsey-based frequency references measured on a single ion. With the operational building blocks now demonstrated, it is natural to view these results through the lens of universal and scalable computation. Universal qudit-based computation is possible through both native qudit entanglement10 and standard qubit Mølmer–Sørensen gates13,64, as recently demonstrated in 137Ba+65,66.

The remaining physical obstacle to developing a scalable quantum processor with high-dimensional qudits is demonstrating that the techniques generalise to multi-ion registers. The primary mechanism that could limit scalability to multiple qudits is spectral crowding of internal transitions due to the motional mode structure of a multi-ion register. To quantify this effect, we discuss estimated fidelity limits due to off-resonant driving for both individual and two-ion interactions in the context of large ion chains (“Methods” “Scaling to multi-ion registers”). We find that for experimentally feasible trap radial frequencies of ~4 MHz and magnetic fields of ~7.13 G, 25-level SPAM, single- and two-ion gate infidelities due to spectral crowding can be kept below 1 × 10−3 for at least 20 ions. This can be done while maintaining low-crosstalk individual addressing, either using AC light shifts to selectively tune ions into resonance65,66, or directly by focusing 1762 nm beams with high-NA lenses (we estimate that NA of 0.44 are sufficient for 2 μm beam waists). These considerations, and the coherence metrics and modelling presented above, show that key engineering targets for high-fidelity qudit processors are already in reach. Taken together, the primitives demonstrated here and the projected performance of qudits in multi-ion registers lay the groundwork for scalable quantum processors based on high-dimensional trapped ion qudits.

Methods

Experimental setup

We employ a two-step, isotope-selective ablation loading scheme using 554 nm and 405 nm excitation43 to confine single ions in our custom-built four-rod linear Paul trap. Further technical information about the ion trapping apparatus is described in ref. 44. In this work, the laser intensities are 13 mW/mm2 for 493 nm, 113 mW/mm2 for 650 nm, and 1.68 mW/mm2 for 614 nm. The applied magnetic field strength is ~4.209G. The magnetic field is supplied by an array of nickel-plated neodymium permanent magnets (Nd2Fe14B) held in 3D printed mounts fixed directly to the trap vacuum chamber.

Unless otherwise stated, all coherent operations initiated after state preparation is completed are triggered on the rising edge of the laboratory’s alternating current (A/C) power. This mitigates fluctuations in the magnetic field from shot-to-shot that stem from the 60 Hz A/C that powers all lab equipment, a well-known and documented effect in trapped ion setups67,68.

System calibration proceeds as follows (details in Supplementary Materials I): Frequencies used to drive the quadrupole S1/2 ↔ D5/2 transitions are calibrated by measuring two reference transitions, which enable calibration of magnetic field drifts as well as slow laser cavity drifts. Frequency calibration is typically run every 5–10 min during data acquisition. Transition strengths are calibrated by measuring the Rabi frequencies of five reference transitions, each with a unique Δm ∈ { − 2, − 1, 0, 1, 2}. These serve as a reference for every other transition with the matching Δm value, which are calculated from theory and scaled up/down according to the output of the reference measurement. These Rabi frequencies are typically calibrated once per day prior to data acquisition.

Narrow-band optical pumping

The NBOP approach introduced in Section “Qudit encoding and SPAM” involves several steps which each have opportunity for tuning/optimisation. Firstly, the S1/2, F = 1 states flushing step is sensitive to the power and pulse time of the 493 nm light used. A lower power, short pulse is desirable to reduce off-resonant excitation of S1/2, F = 2 states, as exciting these states would scramble the intended initial state. In the experiments shown in this work, the 493 nm light has an intensity of ~0.5 mW/mm2, and the flushing step has a duration of 1.5 μs.

In addition, the transitions chosen for NBOP initialisation, which vary depending on which of the 5 S1/2, F = 2 levels is initialised, must be chosen with care. We choose to repump population from D5/2 preferentially through P3/2, F = 3 such that decay from P3/2 to S1/2, F = 1 is suppressed by dipole selection rules, which also helps to reduce the number of NBOP cycles required before initialisation fidelity saturates. With this in mind, we have calculated the re-pumping path probabilities for all states in D5/2 through P3/2, F = 3 and decaying to S1/2, F = 2 in order to choose which transitions have the best chance of moving population toward the intended initial state efficiently. These repump path probabilities (shown in Supplementary Materials II) have a large impact on the initialisation efficiency and fidelity. To give just one example, if one intends to initialise ∣F=2,m=−2⟩ in S1/2, choosing to drive population in ∣F=2,m=2⟩ to the state ∣D5/2,F~=4,mF~=4⟩ would not work at all, as this state can only be pumped to ∣P3/2,F=3,m=3⟩ which in turn can only decay back to ∣S1/2,F=2,m=2⟩ (neglecting multi-step processes like repeated driving to P3/2 following decay back to D5/2 or D3/2). This would form a closed loop, preventing any population from getting to the desired initial state. Beyond completely forbidden transition paths, one must choose repump paths with care, as weak pathways to moving population to the desired initial state will unduly increase the number of repetitions needed. Given that our fluorescent readout completely scrambles the initial state of the next experiment, and therefore returns a mixed state of all levels in S1/2, this would severely limit the overall initialisation fidelity.

State choices for encoding SPAM, qudits, and virtual qubits

State preparation and measurement

For the d = 25 level SPAM result, all states in D5/2 are encoded, along with a single state in S1/2, F = 2. The ∣0⟩ state in S1/2 was determined to be the state with the shortest transition π-time from any state in D5/2. This ensures that, after shelving the population to D5/2 for the initialisation herald, the de-shelving pulse uses a strong transition and the infidelity of that π-pulse population transfer is minimised. The D5/2 that is used for the initialisation herald for ∣0⟩ is the ∣3⟩ state, which accounts for the population found in ∣3⟩ for the prepared ∣0⟩ outcomes in Fig. 2d. The encoded states ∣1⟩→∣24⟩ in D5/2 are labelled by the readout order, where the readout order is mainly determined by the de-shelving pulse transition strength, from highest to lowest. This ensures that the shortest de-shelving pulses are used earliest, to minimise off-resonant de-shelving of population in other D5/2 states during the sequential readout. Some changes to that ordering scheme were subsequently made to account for and mitigate off-resonant effects found empirically.

Multi-level encoding

Multi-level encoding for coherent operations follows a different method since the mutual coherence and connectivity of many states are the most important qualities to optimise over. Summing over all pairs of states in a given group, regardless of whether they are directly connected or not, and including mediating states, has the effect of introducing a rather steep penalty for state sets that do not have all participating states encoded (that is, where l > d). Nonetheless, for d ≤ 17, we impose a final restriction on the state sets that all states must be connected to the same, single state in S1/2, F = 2, a so-called “star-topology” graph, or tree with one node and d − 1 leaves. With these constraints and this cost function, we exhaustively search all possible sets of states of size d and pick the set that minimises C.

This connectivity graph enables the generation of equal superposition states, as in the qudit Ramsey-type measurements shown in Section “Multi-level coherence”, with one pulse per participating state ∣j≠0⟩, with the label ∣0⟩ assigned to the state in S1/2, F = 2.

Beyond d = 17, a star topology is no longer practical, as the transitions from any possible initial S1/2, F = 2 state to the remaining states in D5/2 are either too weak and magnetic field sensitive to be reliably used (see Supplemetary Materials I) or completely forbidden by quadrupole transition selection rules. As a result, some multi-pulse transitions are required in order to generate superpositions for d ≥ 18. The states used for d ≥ 18 include the states used for d = 17 (where ∣0⟩=∣S1/2,F=2,m=0⟩ state), with additional states found manually.

The 4, 8, and 16 state encodings used for basis states in the Bernstein–Vazirani algorithm, as well as the CCCNOT gate implementation, largely follow the state set finding results above. The two exceptions to this are states encoded for the CCCNOT gate, which are weakly connected to the ∣0⟩=∣S1/2,F=2,m=0⟩ state but allow higher data rates using initialisation and measurement via transitions to other S1/2, F = 2 states. Qudit Ramsey-type experiment—analytic expressions

In intermediate magnetic field strengths of order 1 G, in the context of maximally encoding the D5/2 states in 137Ba+, it is more practical to utilise the 1762 nm for coherent manipulation and connecting the qudit states as compared to driving the D5/2 to D5/2 transitions directly, i.e., with stimulated Raman transitions. This is because the closest frequency separation between all D5/2 to D5/2 transitions is in the order of 1 kHz in magnetic field strengths of order 1 G, which would limit the gate speed significantly to minimise off-resonant transition error. From our previous work, we have calculated that there are sets of S1/2 to D5/2 quadrupole transitions with sufficiently strong oscillator strengths to connect any two states in the S1/2 and D5/2 subspace44. Thus, in this work, we use the 1762 nm quadrupole transitions for complete coherent control of a 137Ba+ qudit.

Unitary decomposition with Star-Topology constraint

Based on state encoding choices (“Methods” “State choices for encoding SPAM, qudits, and virtual qubits”) we have one computational state in 6S1/2, ∣0⟩≡∣F=2,mF⟩6S1/2, while the D5/2 manifold supplies the remaining d − 1 states of the qudit (or virtual qubit) register, {∣1⟩,…,∣d−1⟩}≡{∣F,mF⟩}5D5/2. Coherent operations are driven by a set of 1762 nm electric-quadrupole transitions that couple ∣0⟩ in S1/2 to all the states in D5/2. Hence, the resulting interaction graph has a star topology with a central node at ∣0⟩.

The restricted connectivity invalidates standard QR-decomposition protocols, which assume full pairwise Givens rotations. We modify the algorithm so that all rotations act in a two-dimensional subspace span∣0⟩,∣i⟩ (i ∈ {1, …, d − 1}), ensuring that the decomposition can be physically implemented.

Scaling to multi-ion registers

Spectral crowding due to motional modes in multi-ion registers is expected to be a non-negligible contributor to control infidelity, particularly in the qudit context. We study these effects first for SPAM by performing a similar calculation as that presented in Fig. 2d for a chain of N = 20 ions. The full calculation is detailed in Supplementary Materials VII, and shows that infidelities due to off-resonant driving can be kept below 4 × 10−4 for d = 25 qudit SPAM in a multi-ion context.

We use those same results as a proxy for single qudit gate fidelities for multi-ion registers, given that SPAM and single qudit gates require the same transitions to be driven, for similar time scales. Other error channels for single qudit gates (such as those noise sources affecting coherence considered in Fig. 3e) are typically independent of the number of ions. Off-resonant driving, by contrast, is expected to grow with ion number, from being a negligible contribution to qudit Ramsey results for single ions (and therefore not considered in the noise model for Fig. 3e), to being a larger source in long ion chains. Our calculations show that under reasonable assumptions for trapping parameters (~3.8 − 4 MHz trap radial frequencies) and magnetic fields (~7.13 G), these off-resonant effects can be effectively mitigated.

Finally, using the experimental parameters obtained for high-fidelity SPAM and single qudit gates in the presence of many additional ions, we confirm (details in Supplementary Materials VII) that spectral crowding at those trap frequencies and magnetic fields is not anticipated to significantly degrade off-resonant effects for two-ion interactions, contributing at most 1 × 10−3 level errors under reasonable assumptions. Two-qudit gates in 137Ba+ will, of course, nonetheless be susceptible to the same error mechanisms that affect qubits today, but these calculations show that the unique complexities that these trapped-ion qudits bring with respect to spectral crowding are not insurmountable for high-fidelity, scalable control.
