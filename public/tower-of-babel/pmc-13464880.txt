Background

Arachnoid cysts (ACs) are sacs filled with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and represent the most prevalent brain cysts and account for 1% of all intracranial lesions. The overall prevalence of ACs appears to be higher among children with a rate of 2.6% compared to adults with a rate ranging from 0.3 to 1.7%. Furthermore, males have nearly twice the risk of developing ACs compared to females. Additionally, there is no established association between aging and developing ACs [1]. These cysts may be encountered accidentally during imaging modalities, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT), conducted for another medical concern [2]. Although most ACs are asymptomatic, some cases of ACs may be accompanied by a variety of symptoms which are usually determined by the size and location of the lesion [3]. While ACs are most commonly found in the middle cranial fossa and retrocerebellar area, their apparition in the orbital area is extremely rare, especially among children, with only a few cases reported in the literature to date [2, 3]. Most ACs tend to remain stable in size, therefore, the conservative management consistent with regular follow-up with MRI and CT is considered adequate for patients who do not exhibit any relevant symptoms, while in some cases, the surgical intervention may be irreplaceable for symptomatic patients to prevent irreversible neurological damage [4]. In this article, we present a case of a large intraorbital AC without visual impairment in a 3-year-old female with a concise literature review.

Case presentation

A 3-year-old Syrian female patient was referred to the Neurosurgery Department from an ophthalmologist due to a two-month history of left eye pain, which later progressed to proptosis over the last 15 days. On ophthalmic examination, the patient exhibited inferior and lateral strabismus, with preserved visual acuity of 20/20, and a normal optic disc upon fundoscopy. The patient had a history of congenital ptosis. Otherwise, her medical and pharmacological history was unremarkable. A comprehensive physical examination of vital organs revealed no abnormalities, and laboratory investigations, including blood tests, were within normal parameters.

A brain CT scan identified a suspected mass in the retrobulbar space, posterior to the left eyeball prompting further evaluation with MRI for characterization (Fig. 1).

Axial CT scan of the brain demonstrating a suspected retrobulbar mass located posterior to the left eyeball

Subsequently, an MRI examination was performed using a 1.5 Tesla closed scanner, with thin slices focused on the brain and orbit. Images were obtained in sagittal, axial, and coronal planes, incorporating various sequences including diffusion-weighted imaging and FLAIR. Findings revealed a large well-defined cystic lesion within the left orbital cone, measuring approximately 25.2 × 17.2 × 22.5 mm. The lesion exhibited low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and high signal intensity on T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences. It displaced adjacent muscular structures and the optic nerve, resulting in anterior displacement of the eyeball. The lesion extended toward the lateral cells of the left orbital cone in an angular manner. The brain parenchyma demonstrated normal morphology and signal intensity, with no evident masses or pathological lesions. Midline structures remained intact, with normal sulcal patterns and cerebellar tonsil positioning. No abnormalities were noted in the intracranial connections (Fig. 2).

MRI illustrating a well-demarcated lesion within the left orbital cone, consistent with an arachnoid cyst, displacing the optic nerve and adjacent structures (blue arrows). a Axial T2-weighted image; b axial T1-weighted image; c coronal view; d sagittal view

Based on these findings, the preoperative diagnosis of intraorbital AC was proposed, and the surgical intervention was deemed necessary to prevent the compression of the optic nerve. The surgery was performed by making a left pterional skin incision, followed by a supraorbital craniotomy with gentle retraction of the frontal lobe without opening the dura mater. The roof of the orbit was drilled using a high-speed drill to access the orbital contents behind the eyeball. The cyst was successfully identified, carefully dissected from surrounding neural and muscular structures, drained, and its wall destroyed (Fig. 3).

a Preoperative frontal view showing surgical planning markings over the forehead region, delineating intended incision lines and anatomical landmarks before arachnoid cyst intervention. b Intraoperative approach showing a left pterional skin incision, supraorbital craniotomy, and orbital roof drilling to access the retrobulbar space. The arachnoid cyst was identified, dissected, drained, and its wall destroyed to prevent recurrence

Following the surgical procedure, a sample of the fluid was collected and examined under the microscope. The analysis identified the presence of CSF, which was consistent with the diagnosis of the lesion as an AC (Fig. 4).

Microscopic analysis of the collected fluid confirmed the presence of cerebrospinal fluid

The diagnosis of intraorbital AC was supported by the gross examination of the cyst, relying on the neurosurgeon’s expertise, especially since our patient’s parents unfortunately had financial constraints and were unable to afford confirmatory histopathological examination. The surgical intervention led to complete resolution of the proptosis, while the congenital ptosis of the upper eyelid persisted (Fig. 5). The follow-up of our patient was continued for 2 months postoperatively, and an MRI conducted after 2 months of surgery revealed no signs of recurrence, and our patient’s condition remained stable. However longer-term follow-up was not available since our patient skipped her next follow-up appointments.

Discussion

ACs are non-cancerous, developmental abnormalities arising in the central nervous system and can be found in the spinal canal or the intracranial compartment. They are believed to stem from a congenital splitting in the arachnoid membrane, resulting in the confinement of the CSF in the aberrantly developed cavity [5]. In general, ACs are the most prevalent brain cysts and are most commonly found in the middle cranial fossa [2, 3]. However, their presence in the intraorbital area had rarely been reported in the literature [2]. We have incorporated a concise literature review of all cases of intraorbital ACs in adults and children (Table 1). To the best of our knowledge, 18 cases of intraorbital arachnoid cysts have been previously described in the literature, 5 of which involved pediatric patients. Among these pediatric cases, 4 presented with reduced visual acuity, while visual acuity was not documented in the remaining case. Optic disc abnormalities were noted in 3 of these 5 pediatric cases; the other two reports did not include an assessment of the optic disc. Furthermore, surgical decompression was performed in 3 of these 5 cases, but the outcomes either showed no improvement or were not reported. Therefore, we believe that the normal visual acuity and optic disc assessment in our patient is noteworthy. Although the epidemiological data regarding intraorbital ACs are very limited and mostly stem from individual cases, they tend to be reported in adults more than children. While ACs found in the middle cranial fossa and retrocerebellar area typically do not result in any symptoms, the rarer locations possess an increased potential of being symptomatic. The symptoms associated with ACs stem either from the exerted pressure on the surrounding structures or as a result of cyst rupture [3]. For instance, an AC located in the quadrigeminal plate cistern may exert pressure on the trochlear nerves and the quadrigeminal plate, potentially disrupting the trochlear nerve’s function. Furthermore, an AC exerting pressure on the optic nerve may result in visual impairment in the corresponding eye [3]. Remarkably, our patient had proptosis, but a normal visual acuity of 20/20 upon ophthalmic examination. Exophthalmos, also known as proptosis, is defined as the abnormal protrusion of the eye from the orbit, resulting from the increase in volume of contents within the bony orbital area. This condition typically arises due to inflammatory changes affecting the orbit, as in thyroid-related eye disease, the disruption of venous drainage from the orbital area as in orbital varices and cavernous sinus thrombosis, the presence of malignant or benign space-occupying intraorbital tumors such as neuroblastomas and capillary hemangiomas, and the introduction of foreign objects into the orbit, often resulting from trauma [6]. In our case, the intraorbital AC was relatively large, leading to apparent proptosis and causing eye pain. Multiple differentials may Intermix with the diagnosis of Intraorbital ACs, such as meningiomas, tuberculomas, dermoid and epidermoid cysts, gliomas, hematomas, orbital varix, and lymphangioma [2, 5]. However, imaging modalities such as MRI and CT provide valuable assistance in the diagnostic process since ACs feature distinct characteristics compared to other lesions. On CT, ACs appear as thin-walled, fluid-filled cysts, and though CT may be utilized sufficiently to detect ACs, MRI is the preferred investigation as it provides more information about the location and size of ACs with further evaluation of the adjacent anatomical structures [3]. On MRI, the signal intensity of ACs is consistent with the signal intensity of normal CSF on T1, T2, diffusion, and FLAIR, though there may be slight signal variations between normal CSF and the fluid within ACs as a result of CSF pulsations. Additionally, ACs do not exhibit contrast enhancement [3] CT and MRI scans of our patient effectively ruled out other differentials and supported the diagnosis of an AC within the left orbital cone, measuring approximately 25.2 × 17.2 × 22.5 mm. In general, most ACs remain stable in size, and the conservative management consisting of regular follow-up with MRI and CT imaging modalities is preferred over surgery for patients who do not exhibit symptoms of focal neurological deficits or elevated intracranial pressure, since the surgical interventions may carry the risk of developing serious complications, leading to increased morbidity [4]. However, in cases of symptomatic ACs, surgery may be irreplaceable to prevent irreversible neurological damage, and the specific approach is often determined by the location [3]. Due to the size of the AC in our case, the surgical intervention was deemed necessary to prevent the compression of the optic nerve, and the AC was carefully dissected from surrounding structures, drained, and its wall destroyed. In our case, the neurosurgeon recommended a pterional skin incision followed by a supraorbital craniotomy with orbital roof drilling, rather than a trans‑eyebrow supraorbital approach, given the anticipated need for wider bony exposure. We therefore considered the pterional incision preferable, as it would yield a more cosmetically acceptable scar concealed within the hairline. Additionally, various options for alternative approaches were considered; the first option was the anterior transorbital approach, which is generally suitable for lesions located in the anterior third of the orbit, and therefore was considered a far option in our case due to the deep location of the cyst. The second option was a lateral orbitotomy through the lateral orbital wall (Krönlein approach); although appropriate for deep orbital lesions within the intraconal space, our team has limited experience with this technique. The third option was a transcranial approach with orbital roof drilling, which provides safe access to posterior and deep orbital lesions and is particularly suitable for lesions located superior to the optic nerve, as in our patient, and our neurosurgeons have considerably better experience with this approach. For these reasons, we found this approach to be the most appropriate in our case. Subsequent follow-up with MRI indicated no further recurrence. The limitations for this study include the lack of confirmatory histopathological examination of the resected specimen, owing to the financial constraints of our patient’s parents, and the absence of long-term follow-up to evaluate potential delayed recurrence.

Conclusion

Intraorbital ACs may reach a relatively large size without signs of visual impairment. Proptosis can be the only symptom of intraorbital ACs, and further evaluation encompassing multimodal imaging should be considered in all patients with proptosis. Surgical intervention should be performed in cases of optic nerve compression to prevent irreversible neurological damage. Furthermore, surgery should be considered in cases of large intraorbital ACs even in the absence of visual impairment. CT and MRI remain sufficient for the diagnosis and follow-up of ACs.
