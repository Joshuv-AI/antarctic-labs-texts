Breakthrough SARS-COV-2 infection induces broad anti-viral T cell immunity
Authors: Katie Eireann Lineburg, Pauline Crooks, Jyothy Raju, Laetitia Le Texier, Panteha Khaledi, Kiana Berry, Srividhya Swaminathan, Archana Panikkar, Sweera Rehan, Kristyan Guppy-Coles, Michelle Anne Neller, Rajiv Khanna, Corey Smith
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryVaccines have curtailed the devastation wrought by COVID-19. Nevertheless, emerging variants result in a high incidence of breakthrough infections. Here we assess the impact of vaccination and breakthrough infection on severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) T cell immunity. We demonstrate that COVID-19 vaccination induces robust spike-specific T cell responses that, within the CD4+ compartment, display comparable IFN-γ responses to SARS-CoV-2 infection without vaccination. Vaccine-induced CD8+ IFN-γ responses however, were significantly greater than those primed by SARS-CoV-2 infection alone. This increased responsiveness is associated with induction of novel HLA-restricted CD8+ T cell epitopes not primed by infection alone (without vaccination). Despite these augmented responses, breakthrough infection still induced de novo T cell responses against additional SARS-CoV-2 CD8+ epitopes that display HLA-associated immunodominance hierarchies consistent with those in unvaccinated COVID-19 convalescent individuals. This study demonstrates the unique modulation of anti-viral T cell responses against multiple viral antigens following consecutive yet distinct priming events in COVID-19 vaccination and breakthrough infection.

Introduction

The worldwide roll-out of vaccines has had a significant impact on the incidence of severe COVID-19.1,2,3 Vaccination has been particularly effective at reducing severity in the elderly and in immunocompromised patients.4,5,6 While vaccine-induced antibody responses play a critical role in vaccine efficacy by neutralizing virus prior to infection of the host cells, the ongoing emergence of new variants of concern (VOC) has reduced the effectiveness of vaccine-induced neutralizing antibodies, leading to increased rates of breakthrough infection.7,8,9 While breakthrough infection associated with the emergence of VOC has led to increased morbidity and mortality, vaccines still provide significant protection against severe disease.2,3 Other adaptive vaccine-induced responses, including memory B cells and T cells, are likely to be critical mediators of protection. Vaccines are known to induce severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2)-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses that may mitigate some of the risks associated with breakthrough infection, helping to maintain some protection against disease severity.10,11

Extensive studies in COVID-19-convalescent individuals have led to a comprehensive understanding of both the CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses generated following SARS-CoV-2 infection.12,13,14 Immunodominance hierarchies following infection, particularly in CD8+ T cell populations, are defined by the recognition of dominant peptide epitopes associated with particular human leukocyte antigens (HLAs). These immunodominant epitopes are not confined to the spike (S) antigen, and are prevalent in structural proteins, particularly the nucleocapsid (N) and membrane proteins (M), and non-structural proteins, including ORF1AB and ORF3A.15,16 A number of recent observations have shown that although mutation, particularly in the S protein, can impact T cell-mediated recognition of SARS-CoV-2, the majority of immunodominant epitopes remain conserved in the VOC.17,18

COVID-19 vaccines are known to induce SARS-CoV-2-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses.10,19 These responses are likely to play a critical role in settings of B cell deficiency, particularly when patients with cancer or autoimmune disease are treated with B cell-depleting agents.20,21,22 They likely also mitigate some of the risks associated with breakthrough infection, helping to maintain some protection against disease severity. We therefore sought to understand the dynamics of the SARS-CoV-2-specific T cell response following multiple vaccine doses and after breakthrough infection. Using a cohort of healthy participants, we demonstrate that vaccine-induced T cell immunity remains relatively unchanged following the initial vaccine dose and is comparable in magnitude to the response generated following infection. We note the emergence of novel CD8+ T cell epitope responses following vaccination that were not detectable in individuals infected without vaccination. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the de novo generation of additional (non-spike) immunodominant SARS-CoV-2 responses were not impacted by the presence of vaccine-induced S-specific T cells. Indeed, vaccinated individuals generated immunodominant responses that were consistent with those detected in individuals infected without vaccination.

Results

S-specific immune responses following multiple vaccination doses

To study SARS-CoV-2 specific antibody and T cell responses following vaccination we recruited a cohort of 115 participants who received up to three doses of vaccine (Figure 1; Table 1). Blood samples were collected 10–14 days following the first dose, and 4–8 weeks after the second and third doses (i.e., post-vaccine 1 [PV1], post-vaccine 2 [PV2] and post-vaccine 3 [PV3] blood samples). Plasma was extracted and participants assessed for spike (S) specific IgG antibody titers (Figure 2A). S-specific IgG titers significantly increased with each vaccine dose (Figure 2A; Table S1). The exposure incidence within our cohort was determined by assessing antibody responses against SARS-CoV-2 N antigen. Data presented in Figure 2B show all participants had undetectable levels of antibody response to N antigen with the exception of a single PV3 participant who was undiagnosed and asymptomatic but returned a positive N antigen-specific antibody response. Vaccination induces CD8+ T cell responses not detected following SARS-CoV-2 infection alone

To determine the specificity of the T cell response generated following vaccination, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) from 40 individuals who had received 2–3 doses of vaccine (PV) were expanded for 14 days in the presence of IL-2 following stimulation with overlapping peptides from the S protein. CD4+ and CD8+ T cell reactivity was then assessed using an intracellular cytokine assay (Figure S1). Thirty-nine of the 40 vaccine recipients (97.5%) generated a detectable S-specific CD4+ T cell response above a threshold of 1% IFN-γ-producing cells (Figure 3A). In comparison with our previously published cohort of SARS-CoV-2 infected and unvaccinated (PI unvax) participants, we identified that both cohorts (PV and PI unvax) display a comparable S-specific IFN-γ response (Figure 3B). While vaccine responses were not impacted by cohort age or sex (Figure S2) S-specific CD4+ T cell responses within the infection cohort were significantly increased in association with increased age (Figure S3A), which is consistent with previously published data from this cohort.15 CD8+ T cell responses above the 1% IFN-γ producing cell threshold were observed in 29 (72.5%) of PV individuals (Figure 3C). The magnitude of this S-specific CD8+ T cell response, after at least two doses of vaccine, was significantly higher than the response detected in PI unvax samples where males displayed a significantly increased CD8+ S-response in comparison to females (Figures 3D and S3D). This increased PV response was identified in association with particular HLA alleles. HLA-B∗35:01/71+ vaccinated individuals displayed a significantly higher S-specific CD8+ T cell response compared to PI unvax individuals (Figure 3E). A similar trend was identified in HLA-B∗07:02+ individuals (Figure 3F). No differences were observed in responses associated with other common HLA alleles including HLA-A∗02:01 and HLA-A∗01:01 (Figure S4). To assess the epitope specificity of vaccine-induced responses, we assessed four distinct S-specific epitopes, previously mapped to either HLA-B∗35:01 or HLA-B∗07:02 (Table 2). LPF-specific T cell responses were detected in all eight HLA-B∗35:01 vaccine recipients assessed and were significantly increased in comparison to PI unvax HLA-B∗35:01 individuals, where LPF-specific T cell responses were undetectable (Figure 3G). This suggests that LPF-specific T cells represent a vaccine-induced response that is not induced by natural SARS-CoV-2 infection alone. In comparison, assessment of the B∗35:01-restricted IPF epitope yielded comparable responses in both groups. A similar trend was observed in HLA-B∗07:02+ individuals, with a higher proportion of vaccinated participants responding to the APH epitope in comparison to PI unvax, although APH-specific T cells were detectable in both cohorts (Figure 3H). Analysis of a second HLA-B∗07:02-restricted epitope, SPRR, again demonstrated no significant different between PV and PI unvax cohort responses (Figure 3H). Induction of de novo T cell responses in vaccinated individuals following breakthrough infection

To study the impact of breakthrough infection (PV + PI) on SARS-CoV-2 specific T cell immunity in vaccinated individuals, we first confirmed positive SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection using the same N-specific plasma antibody assessment described earlier, and demonstrated significantly increased N antibody reactivity at PV + PI in comparison to either PV2 or PV3 (Figure 4A). S-antibody concentrations were also assessed at PV + PI and displayed significantly increased titers in comparison to PV2 and PV3 (Figure 4B). We next assessed whole-blood cytokine responses in 32 participants with confirmed symptomatic breakthrough infection. To investigate the breadth of the SARS-CoV-2-specific T cell responses at PV + PI, we expanded our whole-blood assay to incorporate additional non-spike SARS-CoV-2 antigen pools: CoV-1 (containing ORFs 3A, 6, 10, and 9b), CoV-2 (ORFs 7A, 7B, 8, and Y14), and CoV-3 (nucleocapsid: N, membrane: M, and envelope: E). Using this assay, we assessed both spike and non-spike antigen responses in whole blood samples from participants following breakthrough infection (Figures 4C and 4D). As seen prior to infection, dominant IFN-γ and/or IL-2 responses were detectable against the Spike antigen following breakthrough infection (Figure 4C). However, this pre-existing vaccine induced S-specific immunity had no impact on the induction of new non-spike specific T cell responses, whereby IFN-γ and/or IL-2 responses against CoV-3 were detectable in 84% of PV + PI samples (Figure 4D). This is consistent with the response rates seen in unvaccinated infected individuals.15 IL-6 and IL-10 production were also detected following breakthrough infection in a high proportion of participants in response to both the Spike and CoV-3 pools. Breakthrough infection leads to broadening of the SARS-CoV-2 specific T cell response

To address the broader impact of breakthrough infection on SARS-CoV-2-specific T cell immunity, we expanded T cells from 32 individuals who developed infection following 2–3 doses of vaccine (PV). T cell responses were assessed against S, N, M, and ORF3A both prior to infection (PV) and 4 weeks post-infection (PV + PI). S-specific CD4+ T cell responses were detected in all individuals both pre-and post-infection (Figure 5A). However, breakthrough infection led to a significant increase in the S-specific CD4+ T cell response (Figure 5B). While some individuals displayed low frequency subdominant pre-existing CD4+ T cell responses to N, M, and ORF3a prior to infection, breakthrough infection led to a significant increase in CD4+ T cell responses against all SARS-CoV-2 antigens (Figure 5B). Here, non-spike (N- and M-specific) CD4+ T cell responses were detected in >90% of samples from participants that were both vaccinated and subsequently infected (PV + PI), with 50% of these samples also displaying ORF3A-specific CD4+ T cells (Figure 5C). Within the CD8+ compartment, breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection led to a broadening of antigen-specific T cell responses beyond S, to encompass N, M, and ORF3A, and a significant increase in the magnitude of all four of these CD8+ T cell responses (Figures 5D and 5E). Consistent with our observations in samples collected following infection without vaccination (PI unvax), the majority of vaccinated individuals generated CD8+ T cell responses against N and ORF3A following infection (Figure 5F). We also noted an increase in the proportion of individuals generating an above-threshold response against the S protein and a higher response in the PV + PI samples compared to the PI unvax samples. Is it likely that this increased response rate is due to hybrid immunity generated after vaccination and infection in the PV + PI cohort.

Breakthrough infection induces multi-epitope HLA class I-restricted CD8+ T cell immune responses

Our observations suggest that prior vaccination and the presence of spike-specific T cell responses does not prevent the induction of de novo responses to other antigens following infection. We next performed overlapping peptide matrix analysis to assess the CD8+ T cell responses to N epitopes post-infection. The N-specific CD8+ T cell responses demonstrated HLA-associated immunodominance hierarchies consistent with those previously observed following infection without vaccination,15,23 with uniform N-specific CD8+ T cell response patterns seen in HLA-B∗07:02+, HLA-B∗40:01+, HLAB∗35:01+, and HLA-A∗11:01+ participants (Figure 6A). Characterization of the epitope-specific responses identified in these participants (Table 2) demonstrated an immunodominance hierarchy, with the majority of responding N-specific CD8+ T cells displaying reactivity toward the same immunodominant epitopes: ATE, TPS, MEV, and SPR, previously identified in unvaccinated, infected individuals (Figure 6B). A similar trend was observed in participants who generated ORF3A-specific T cells, with responses identified in A∗02:01+ and A∗01:01+ individuals consistent with the responses previously identified in unvaccinated, infected individuals (Figure 6C). Discussion

This study demonstrates the longitudinal dynamics of T cell immunity in response to COVID-19 vaccination and the subsequent broadening of immunity that follows breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection. Moreover, it provides a direct comparison of the SARS-CoV-2-specific T cell immunity generated following two different forms of primary exposure: COVID-19 vaccination versus natural infection with SARS-CoV-2, and demonstrates the impact of HLA genetics on an individual’s propensity to generate T cell responses directed against immunodominant epitopes following two distinct SARS-CoV-2-specific T cell priming stimuli.

While earlier studies have demonstrated that sequence changes in SARS-CoV-2 variant strains lead to the evasion of neutralizing antibodies,9,24 T cell responses are less susceptible to this immune escape.10 As a consequence, antigen-specific T cells are likely to play an important role in providing long-term protection against severe COVID-19. SARS-CoV-2-specific T cell response profiling, based on the whole-blood assay, demonstrated a robust and stable S-specific T cell response following vaccination and is characterized predominantly by production of both IFN-γ and IL-2. However, the magnitude of this T cell response was not dramatically altered following multiple rounds of vaccination. Interestingly, following breakthrough infection, a significant increase in the expression of IL-6 was observed, which is indicative of virus-induced inflammation.25,26 Despite the presence of strong vaccine-induced S-specific T cell responses, 32 (27.8%) of participants went on to develop symptomatic breakthrough infection suggesting that vaccine-induced immunity is insufficient to prevent symptomatic disease in a significant proportion of the population. Symptomatic breakthrough infection was coincident with the broadening of SARS-CoV-2-specific T cell immunity to encompass non-spike antigens, consistent with responses previously observed following infection in the absence of vaccination.15,27,28

We previously reported that SARS-CoV-2 infection leads to a hierarchy of T cell responses in the CD4+ and CD8+ T cell compartments. We noted that while most individuals generate an S-specific CD4+ T cell response following infection, S-specific CD8+ T cell responses were less common and furthermore, immunodominant CD8+ responses were linked to the participant’s HLA type. This study identified expansion of spike-specific CD4+ T cell responses induced post-vaccination, that were consistent with the CD4+ spike-specific T cell responses observed following primary infection without vaccination,15 and displayed robust recall following breakthrough infection.29,30 In the CD8+ compartment this post-vaccination cohort displayed a significantly higher proportion of IFN-γ-producing S-specific CD8+ T cells, in line with other studies reporting enhanced CD8+ T cell responses following SARS-CoV-2 vaccination.11,31,32 However, we also noted that vaccination, in the absence of prior infection, led to distinct priming and the emergence of immunodominant CD8+ S-specific T cells, particularly against HLA-B∗35:01/71-restricted epitope LPF, that were not detectable in the individuals infected without prior vaccination. While previous studies have demonstrated that current COVID-19 vaccines serve to expand the spike-specific T cell memory pool29 and report that vaccine-induced CD8+ T cells play an important role in reducing COVID-19 disease severity,30 we hypothesize that in some instances, vaccine-induced CD8+ T cell immunity may be driven by an over-abundance of antigen following vaccination, which is not reflective of natural infection, and potentially rendering some vaccine-induced CD8+ T cell responses less efficient at controlling virus-infected cells.

The de novo emergence of non-spike specific T cell responses following breakthrough infection supports the contention that vaccine-induced T cell immunity may be less efficient in some individuals at preventing symptomatic disease. In the context of CD8+ T cell immunity, we observed the consistent emergence of HLA-restricted responses targeting immunodominant epitopes previously reported in convalescent individuals.23,28,33 These observations suggest that once infection is established, regardless of vaccine status, control of infected cells is mediated by a population of CD8+ T cells that target immunodominant epitopes, irrespective of any vaccine-induced S-specific T cell immunity. This is in keeping with recent studies that demonstrated the expansion of de novo SARS-CoV-2-specific CD8+ T cells associated with breakthrough infection29,34,35 and their correlation with peak SARS-CoV-2 RNA levels and the subsequent control of viral burden.30 This study identifies that a more thorough understanding of the differential protection afforded by vaccine-induced versus infection-induced CD8+ T cells may be critical for improving COVID-19 vaccine efficacy in populations who remain at high risk of disease, particularly those with B cell deficiencies who do not generate neutralizing antibody responses.20,21,36,37,38

Limitations of the study

Recruitment for our vaccinated cohort commenced with the initial roll out of COVID-19 vaccines in south east Queensland at a time where the majority of healthy individuals were only eligible for the AstraZeneca ChAdOx1-S (AZ) COVID-19 vaccine. As a result, the majority of vaccine-induced assessment in this study cohort was recipients of the AZ vaccine with only a small proportion having received an mRNA Pfizer vaccine from onset. Despite the limited number of Pfizer recipients available in this cohort the differential induction of novel S-specific vaccine-induced CD8+ T cell responses was confirmed in both AZ and Pfizer vaccinated individuals. While antibody responses specific to the SARS-CoV-2 antigens S and N were used to confirm vaccine-induced S-reactivity and the presence of absence of infection-induced N-reactivity these were the only two antibody titers measured in this study.

Experimental model and study participant details

All participants were over the age of 18 and provided informed consent to participate in this study. In total, 115 COVID-19 vaccine recipients were recruited for the study. The cohort comprised of 71 females and 41 males with a median age of 42 (24-79) years. Ancestry, race or ethnicity were not disclosed by study participants. These participants were all recruited in South-East Queensland, Australia between March and August 2021, prior to any significant COVID-19 outbreak in Queensland. Twenty-eight of the COVID-19 vaccine cohort donated peripheral blood prior to vaccination and were assessed for SARS-CoV-2 reactivity to establish baseline responses. From here, where possible, participants donated peripheral blood following each of their first three vaccine doses and again following any incidence of breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection (n = 32 participants). An additional cohort of 60 COVID-19 recovered participants recruited post-infection without prior vaccination. This cohort comprised of 36 females and 24 males with a median age of 49 (20-75) years.

This study was approved by the QIMR Berghofer Medical Research Institute Human Research Ethics Committee (Project 2282 and Project 3618) and was performed according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Method details

Whole-blood assay

The whole-blood cytokine assay used to assess SARS-CoV-2-specific T-cell immunity has been previously described.15 Briefly, 200 μL of heparinized blood was incubated with overlapping peptide pools, measuring a final concentration of 1 μg mL-1, corresponding to SARS-CoV-2-encoded proteins. Following overnight incubation at 37°C in 6.5% CO2 and 95% relative humidity, cultured blood samples were centrifuged, then plasma was harvested and stored at -80°C until required. A cytometric bead array (BD Biosciences) measuring IFN-γ, IL-2 IL-4 IL-5, IL-6, IL-10, IL-13, IL-17 and TNF was used to assess antigen-specific T-cell responses. Antigen-specific cytokine concentrations were determined following subtraction of the spontaneous cytokine release generated during incubation without antigen. A cytokine response following vaccination or infection was considered positive if it was greater than the 99% upper confidence interval of the control unvaccinated samples.

Measure of SARS-CoV-2 S- and N-specific antibodies in plasma samples

S-specific antibody levels were measured using the Alinity AdviseDx SARS-CoV-2 IgG II assay following the manufacturer’s protocol (Abbott Laboratories, Australia). This is a chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (CMIA) testing for the S-IgG immunoglobulin. It measures reactivity relative to a standard curve with a detection range spanning 22 – 25,000 assigned units (AU) per mL. N-specific IgG and IgM antibody titre was detected using the Cobas Elecsys® Anti-SARS-CoV-2 (Roche Diagnostics, USA) in vitro qualitative assay following the manufacturer’s protocol, with a cutoff index of 1.0 so that results are identified as reactive (>1.0) or non-reactive (<1.0). All in vitro antibody detection analysis was completed by Sullivan Nicolaides Pathology, Bowen Hills, Queensland, Australia.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells

PBMCs were separated from whole blood or buffy coats using density gradient centrifugation. PBMCs were used fresh or were cryogenically stored until use.

Expansion of SARS-CoV-2-specific T cells

The expansion of SARS-CoV-2-specific T cells has been described.23 PBMC were stimulated and incubated with 10μM SARS-CoV-2 overlapping peptide pools, and expanded for 14 days in RPMI-1640 supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin (Life Technologies) and 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Life Technologies). Cultures were supplemented with 120 IU/mL IL-2 every 2–3 days. SARS-CoV-2-specific peptide pools were supplied by JPT Technologies and Mimotopes. T cell lines were used freshly harvested, or were cryogenically stored for subsequent analysis.

Intracellular cytokine assay

Cultured T cells (5 × 105 per test) were stimulated separately with SARS-CoV-2 overlapping peptide pools (1 μg/mL of each peptide) or individual defined epitopes (1 μg/mL). Cells were incubated the presence of GolgiPlug (Brefeldin-A) and GolgiStop (Monensin) (BD Biosciences) and anti-CD107a-FITC (BD Biosciences) for 4 hours at 37°C. Following stimulation, cells were washed and stained with anti-CD8-PerCP-Cy5.5 (eBioscience), anti-CD4-Pacific Blue (BD Biosciences) and live/dead fixable near-IR dead cell stain (Life Technologies) for 30 minutes at 4°C before being fixed and permeabilized with Fixation/Permeabilization solution (BD Biosciences). After 20 minutes of fixation, cells were washed in BD Perm/Wash buffer (BD Biosciences) and stained with anti-IFN-γ-Alexa Fluor 700 or IFN-γ-PE (all BD Biosciences) for a further 30 minutes at 4°C. Finally, cells were washed again and acquired using a BD LSRFortessa with FACSDiva software. Post-acquisition analysis was performed using FlowJo software (TreeStar).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.2.1 (San Diego, CA). Box plots represent median (horizontal line), 25th and 75th percentiles (boxes) and minimum and maximum values (whiskers). Statistical comparisons between time points for cytometric bead array data was performed using a mixed-effects model with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. A Mann–Whitney U test was used to performed statistical comparisons between two datasets. Significant differences in CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses pre- and post-infection were performed using a Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
