Development of an interpretable machine learning model for predicting in-hospital mortality in ICU patients with sepsis: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Tianyu Zhao, Kexin Wen, Xumin Han, Ruimin Tan, Yongtao Wang, Quansheng Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis ranks among the primary causes of mortality in intensive care units (ICUs), often resulting in multiple organ dysfunction due to dysregulated systemic inflammation. Timely recognition of patients at high risk is essential, yet existing clinical scoring systems show limited predictive performance. We aimed to design and evaluate an interpretable machine learning model to predict the risk of in-hospital mortality in individuals with sepsis.MethodsThis retrospective study screened 503 sepsis patients who were admitted to the ICU at Hebei Provincial People's Hospital from January 2022 through January 2025. After excluding patients with ICU stay less than 24 h, incomplete clinical data, or pregnancy, 431 patients were included. The dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (n = 301) and a validation set (n = 130). Eight machine learning algorithms—including logistic regression, naive Bayes, support vector machine, single-layer neural network, random forest (RF), LightGBM, K-nearest neighbors, and decision tree—were trained and evaluated. Model performance was assessed by ROC curves (AUC), accuracy, F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, Brier score, expected calibration error, and decision curve analysis. The best-performing model was interpreted using SHAP values.ResultsAmong 431 patients, 198 (45.9%) died during hospitalization. Feature selection using the Boruta algorithm identified 12 key predictors. The RF model achieved the most balanced overall performance, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.804 (95% CI, 0.730–0.879) in the validation set, accuracy of 0.785, F1 score of 0.778, sensitivity of 0.817, and specificity of 0.757. SHAP analysis identified lactate dehydrogenase and urea as the most influential predictors, followed by age, APACHE II score, and procalcitonin. An interactive web application was developed as a research prototype to demonstrate the feasibility of model deployment.ConclusionsWe developed and internally validated an interpretable RF-based machine learning model for predicting in-hospital mortality in patients with sepsis. The model showed acceptable discrimination, reasonable calibration, and potential clinical net benefit in internal validation. SHAP-based interpretation provided insight into key predictive factors, while the web-based calculator demonstrated the feasibility of model deployment. However, external and prospective validation is required before its clinical utility and generalizability can be established.

Introduction

Sepsis occurs when an abnormal host response to infection causes life-threatening organ dysfunction and remains a major challenge in intensive care (1). Globally, it affects roughly 50 million individuals each year and is responsible for about 11 million deaths, accounting for nearly 20% of all mortalities, with the highest incidence and severity observed in intensive care units (ICUs) (2, 3). Despite advances in supportive care, early antimicrobial therapy, and organ support, sepsis-related mortality remains high, particularly among older patients and those with substantial comorbidity or severe organ dysfunction at admission (3, 4).

Sepsis pathophysiology reflects intricate interactions between pathogen virulence and the host immune system, leading to endothelial injury, microcirculatory collapse, and abnormal coagulation, which together increase the risk of multi-organ failure (5). This complexity renders early risk stratification difficult, as clinical signs may lag underlying systemic derangements. Traditional severity scores such as the SOFA, APACHE II, and SAPS II were developed to quantify illness severity and predict mortality risk (6–8). While widely adopted, these scoring systems have several limitations: they were not designed as individualized prognostic tools, often demonstrate only moderate discrimination in contemporary sepsis cohorts, rely on static threshold cutoffs rather than dynamic risk patterns, and their performance can vary substantially across different healthcare settings (9).

Given these limitations, there has been increasing interest in applying machine learning (ML) to prognostic modeling in sepsis, capitalizing on its ability to integrate large numbers of heterogeneous clinical variables, capture complex nonlinear interactions, and iteratively improve predictive performance (10, 11). However, many ML models remain insufficiently interpretable, often described as “black boxes,” which impedes clinical acceptance and integration into care pathways.

To address this gap, interpretable ML techniques such as SHAP and LIME have been introduced, enabling quantification of individual feature contributions and offering clinicians insights into how specific variables influence model predictions (12, 13). Recent studies have applied these approaches to sepsis prognosis. For example, Hu et al. developed SHAP-based interpretable models using MIMIC-IV data, while Park et al. evaluated explainable machine learning in a multicenter sepsis cohort (14, 15). Despite these advances, important limitations remain, including insufficient external validation, heterogeneity in predictor selection and modeling strategies, and uncertain generalizability across different healthcare settings. These concerns may be particularly relevant to Chinese tertiary ICUs, where patient characteristics and clinical practice patterns may differ from those of Western cohorts (16, 17). Therefore, in the present study, we systematically compared eight machine learning algorithms using routinely available variables collected within the first 24 h of ICU admission and further benchmarked the final model against SOFA and APACHE II scores.

In this context, there is a pressing need for locally validated, interpretable prognostic models that combine the predictive power of ML with transparency and actionable insights for bedside use. We conducted a retrospective cohort study of ICU patients with sepsis at Hebei Provincial People's Hospital, applying a suite of ML algorithms with robust feature selection and SHAP interpretability. Our goal was to develop and validate a model capable of accurately predicting in-hospital mortality, elucidate key driving risk factors, and provide a framework for clinical implementation that bridges predictive performance with interpretability.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in the Department of Critical Care Medicine of Hebei Provincial People's Hospital. Adult patients with sepsis admitted to the ICU between January 2022 and January 2025 were screened. Sepsis was diagnosed according to the Sepsis-3 criteria, defined as suspected or documented infection accompanied by an acute increase in SOFA score of at least 2 points (1). Patients were eligible if they met the Sepsis-3 diagnostic criteria. Patients were excluded if they were ≤18 years old, had an ICU stay <24 h, had incomplete required clinical data or unclear clinical outcomes, or were pregnant or lactating.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hebei Provincial People's Hospital (approval No. 2026-LW-002), and due to its retrospective design, the need for written informed consent was waived. Initially, 503 patients were screened, and after applying the exclusion criteria, 431 patients were included in the study, consisting of 233 survivors and 198 non-survivors. The cohort was randomly split into a training set (n = 301, 70%) and a validation set (n = 130, 30%). The patient selection and model development workflow is shown in Figure 1.

Outcome

The primary endpoint of the study was in-hospital mortality, defined as death from any cause occurring during the index hospitalization. Based on this outcome, patients were categorized into survivor and non-survivor groups.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the electronic medical record system. Candidate predictors included demographics, vital signs, comorbidities, laboratory measurements, treatment-related variables, and severity scores. ICU admission was defined as the index time, and all applicable candidate predictors were restricted to information available within the first 24 h after ICU admission. Demographic and vital sign variables included sex, age, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and mean arterial pressure. Comorbidities included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, respiratory disease, and malignancy. Treatment-related variables comprised vasoactive drug use, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and mechanical ventilation administered within the first 24 h after ICU admission. Severity of illness was represented by SOFA and APACHE II scores. Laboratory variables included procalcitonin, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, hemoglobin, platelet count, total protein, albumin, bilirubin indices, liver enzymes, renal function markers, lactate dehydrogenase, creatine kinase, creatine kinase-MB isoenzyme, lactate, oxygenation index, and coagulation parameters including PT, INR, APTT, and TT.

Missing data

The proportion of missing data was assessed for all candidate predictors before analysis. All included variables had less than 20% missingness and were therefore retained for subsequent analysis (Supplementary Table S1). Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with five imputations (m = 5). Imputation and preprocessing were performed within the training framework to minimize information leakage.

Feature selection and model development

A total of 39 candidate predictors were considered for feature selection. Boruta, a random forest–based selection algorithm, was applied only to the training set to identify predictors of in-hospital mortality. Variables confirmed by Boruta were retained for model construction. Eight supervised machine learning models were developed: logistic regression (LR), naive Bayes (NB), support vector machine (SVM), single-layer hidden-layer neural network (SHLNN), random forest (RF), Light Gradient Boosting Machine(LightGBM), K-nearest neighbors (KNN), and decision tree (DT). Feature selection, model training, and hyperparameter tuning were conducted using the training set, while the validation set was reserved solely for independent evaluation of model performance. During training, repeated 5-fold cross-validation with two repeats was performed, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used as the primary metric for model tuning and comparison. Hyperparameters were optimized within the training set during repeated 5-fold cross-validation, and the final settings for each algorithm are provided in Supplementary Table S2. The outcome distribution was relatively balanced; therefore, no class weighting, oversampling, undersampling, or synthetic sampling methods were applied during model training.

Model evaluation

Model performance was evaluated in the training and validation sets. In the training set, internal validation was based on out-of-fold predicted probabilities generated during cross-validation. Discrimination was assessed using ROC curves and AUC values, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the AUCs estimated using the DeLong method. To further assess the discriminative performance of the final model relative to established clinical severity scores, ROC curves for the SOFA and APACHE II scores were generated separately in the validation set. The AUCs of the final RF model, SOFA score, and APACHE II score were compared using paired DeLong tests. Classification performance was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, and F1 score. Calibration was assessed using calibration curves, Brier score, and expected calibration error (ECE), with ECE calculated using 10 fixed probability bins. Decision curve analysis was employed to evaluate clinical applicability. The final model was selected based on its overall balance across discrimination, classification performance, calibration, and clinical net benefit rather than on any single performance metric.

Model interpretation and web application

SHAP was applied to the best-performing model to evaluate feature importance, illustrate the impact and direction of each predictor, and provide individualized explanations for selected patients. To facilitate clinical use, the final model was deployed as an interactive web-based application using Python and Streamlit. Users can input selected clinical predictors through a browser-based interface and obtain the estimated probability of in-hospital mortality.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate, and categorical data as counts with percentages. Between-group differences were evaluated using the independent-samples t test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, chi-square test, or Fisher's exact test. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.0. Machine learning model development, validation, SHAP analysis, and web application deployment were performed using Python version 3.12.7.

Results

Patient characteristics and dataset partitioning

Baseline characteristics stratified by survival status are shown in Table 1. Non-survivors were older, had higher SOFA and APACHE II scores, and more commonly received vasoactive agents, CRRT, and mechanical ventilation. Regarding comorbidities, hypertension, coronary heart disease, and respiratory disease were more frequent in the non-survivor group. For laboratory variables, non-survivors had lower lymphocyte counts and higher aspartate aminotransferase, urea, creatinine, lactate dehydrogenase, and thrombin time levels. Procalcitonin also differed significantly between groups, with a higher median value observed in survivors. No significant differences were found in sex, MAP, diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease, malignant tumor, WBC, neutrophil count, hemoglobin, platelet count, total protein, albumin, direct bilirubin, ALT, CK-MB isoenzyme, lactate, oxygenation index, PT, INR, or APTT.

Feature selection

Feature selection considered 39 candidate predictors and was restricted to the training set to avoid information leakage. Boruta identified 12 confirmed predictors: lactate dehydrogenase, APACHE II score, urea, age, SOFA score, CRRT use, creatine kinase, thrombin time, INR, procalcitonin, prothrombin time, and vasoactive drug use. The Boruta feature selection results are shown in Figure 2.

Feature selection using the boruta algorithm. Boruta feature selection was performed in the training set to identify predictors associated with in-hospital mortality. Confirmed variables were retained for subsequent machine learning model construction.

Model performance and selection of the final model

Eight supervised machine learning models were developed and evaluated, including LR, NB, SVM, SHLNN, RF, LightGBM, KNN and DT. Model performance was assessed in both the training and validation sets using discrimination, classification, calibration, and clinical utility measures. Table 3 presents the classification and calibration performance of all models. In the validation set, RF showed the best overall balance, achieving an AUC of 0.804 (95% CI, 0.730–0.879), sensitivity of 0.817, specificity of 0.757, positive predictive value of 0.742, negative predictive value of 0.828, accuracy of 0.785, F1 score of 0.778, Brier score of 0.1858, and expected calibration error of 0.1082. Although LightGBM achieved the highest sensitivity and KNN had the lowest ECE, RF provided the most balanced overall performance across discrimination, sensitivity and specificity, classification accuracy, calibration, and clinical utility. Therefore, RF was selected as the final model based on its overall performance profile rather than superiority in any single metric.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of patients with sepsis admitted to the ICU at Hebei Provincial People's Hospital, we constructed and internally validated machine learning models to estimate the risk of in-hospital death using routinely collected clinical variables. Among 431 eligible patients, 198 died during hospitalization, indicating a high-risk critically ill population. Following random partitioning, 301 patients formed the training cohort and 130 formed the validation cohort. Among the eight machine learning algorithms assessed, RF provided the best balance of validation performance, yielding a sensitivity of 0.817, specificity of 0.757, accuracy of 0.785, F1 score of 0.778, Brier score of 0.1858, and expected calibration error of 0.1082. Importantly, the present study did not merely construct a prediction model, but further used Boruta-based feature selection and SHAP-based interpretation to translate model outputs into clinically understandable risk patterns. These findings suggest that an interpretable machine learning framework based on early ICU variables may support individualized mortality risk stratification in patients with sepsis.

Sepsis, defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection, remains a major contributor to global mortality despite advances in critical care (1, 2). The Surviving Sepsis Campaign highlights the importance of timely diagnosis, early initiation of antimicrobial treatment, effective source control, and appropriate hemodynamic resuscitation (18). However, early prognostic assessment remains challenging because sepsis is highly heterogeneous, and mortality risk is shaped by the interaction of infection burden, host vulnerability, organ dysfunction, hemodynamic instability, coagulation disorder, and treatment response. Traditional severity scores such as SOFA and APACHE II have been widely used to quantify organ dysfunction and physiological derangement (6, 7). Nevertheless, these scoring systems are based on predefined variables and weighting schemes, and may inadequately reflect nonlinear associations and higher-order interactions between clinical variables. Therefore, machine learning models may provide complementary value by integrating multidimensional information into individualized risk estimates.

Several studies have investigated the use of machine learning for predicting mortality in patients with sepsis. Hu and colleagues developed and validated interpretable models for the early estimation of in-hospital death among critically ill patients with sepsis, demonstrating that SHAP analysis can enhance model transparency (14). In postoperative sepsis, Yao and colleagues reported superior in-hospital mortality prediction with XGBoost compared with stepwise logistic regression and a baseline model, with coagulopathy, renal replacement therapy, urine output, and fluid–electrolyte imbalance ranking among the most influential predictors (19). Park et al. used nationwide multicenter data from Korea and found that machine learning models based on baseline clinical variables achieved better mortality prediction than SOFA components alone (15). Zeng et al. further showed that a blending machine learning model based on ICU sepsis patients outperformed SAPS II and SOFA in external validation using two large ICU databases (20). Compared with these studies, our work was based on a real-world ICU cohort from a Chinese tertiary hospital and focused on routinely available variables collected early during ICU care. Although the sample size was smaller than those of large public-database studies, this local cohort may better reflect institution-specific case mix, treatment patterns, and data structure, which is important for model implementation in real clinical settings.

The selection of RF was based on overall performance rather than superiority in any single metric. Although differences among several competing models were modest, RF provided a favorable balance of discrimination, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1 score, calibration, and clinical net benefit in the validation set. Although LightGBM achieved a slightly higher sensitivity and some competing models performed better on individual metrics, RF showed the most balanced overall performance profile. Therefore, RF was selected as the final model as a pragmatic choice based on its overall performance rather than as evidence of universal superiority over the other algorithms. The RF model also showed significantly better discrimination than SOFA or APACHE II alone in the validation set. This suggests that combining established severity scores with additional routinely available clinical variables may improve prognostic discrimination. However, external validation is still required before clinical superiority can be established.

The SHAP analysis provided further insight into the variables contributing to the RF model predictions. Lactate dehydrogenase and urea showed the highest global SHAP importance, while age was also among the leading predictors. Lactate dehydrogenase is a nonspecific marker of cellular injury and may reflect tissue hypoperfusion, metabolic stress, and multiorgan dysfunction; previous studies have also reported an association between elevated Lactate dehydrogenase and mortality in patients with sepsis (21). Although our model included serum urea rather than blood urea nitrogen (BUN), the prominence of this renal-related marker is consistent with previous evidence showing that BUN is independently associated with mortality in critically ill patients with sepsis (22). Age may further reflect differences in physiological reserve and host responses to severe infection, which may contribute to poorer outcomes in older patients with sepsis (23, 24).

SOFA and APACHE II also contributed to the model, supporting the importance of organ dysfunction and overall physiological severity in mortality risk assessment. Although some individual predictors overlap with clinical domains represented in SOFA and APACHE II, they may provide complementary information. Composite scores summarize physiological abnormalities using predefined thresholds, whereas individual continuous variables may retain more granular information and capture nonlinear patterns or interactions beyond these scoring categories. Random forest models can accommodate correlated predictors; however, overlapping variables may share or redistribute feature importance. Therefore, SHAP rankings should not be interpreted as independent or causal effects of individual predictors.

Among infection-related variables, procalcitonin was included as an informative predictor. Procalcitonin has been extensively evaluated as a biomarker for bacterial infection and sepsis, although its prognostic implications remain complex (25, 26). Unexpectedly, baseline PCT levels were higher in survivors than in non-survivors. This finding should be interpreted cautiously and should not be taken to indicate a protective effect of higher PCT. Several factors may partly explain this pattern, including differences in the timing of PCT measurement, infection etiology, prior antimicrobial exposure, immune responsiveness, and the stage of sepsis at ICU admission. In addition, the between-group comparison represents an unadjusted association, whereas Boruta selection and SHAP importance indicate only that PCT contributed predictive information within the multivariable model, potentially through nonlinear relationships and interactions with other variables. Because detailed microbiological characteristics and comprehensive immunosuppressive status were unavailable, these explanations could not be directly examined. Future studies incorporating infection etiology, immune status, and serial PCT measurements are needed to clarify this unexpected finding.

Other predictors, including creatine kinase, coagulation parameters, CRRT, and vasoactive drug use, provided additional information on the severity and systemic consequences of sepsis. Creatine kinase may reflect tissue injury, whereas abnormalities in TT, PT, and INR are consistent with the coagulation disturbances frequently observed in sepsis (27, 28). CRRT and vasoactive drug use should be interpreted primarily as indicators of severe renal dysfunction, hemodynamic instability, and the requirement for advanced organ support rather than as causal determinants of mortality. Thus, the SHAP findings collectively suggest that mortality prediction in sepsis reflects information across tissue injury, renal and coagulation dysfunction, physiological severity, inflammatory status, and organ-support requirements, rather than being driven by any single clinical factor.

The potential clinical value of this study lies in providing an interpretable framework for early risk stratification. If externally and prospectively validated, the web-based model may assist clinicians in identifying patients at higher estimated risk of in-hospital mortality. At present, however, the calculator should be regarded as a research prototype rather than a tool for routine clinical decision-making. Its clinical utility and impact on patient management require further evaluation in independent cohorts and prospective studies.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this was a single-center retrospective study conducted at Hebei Provincial People's Hospital, and the model may therefore reflect local patient characteristics, ICU admission criteria, treatment practices, and documentation patterns. Second, validation was limited to a random internal train-validation split. Although this approach provides an initial estimate of model performance, it does not establish transportability to other institutions or patient populations. Third, the total sample size was modest, with 431 patients and only 130 patients in the validation set, which may increase the uncertainty of performance estimates and limit model stability. Independent external validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is therefore required before the model can be considered for routine clinical use. Fourth, the model was based on variables collected during the early ICU period and did not incorporate dynamic trajectories, such as changes in lactate, SOFA score, vasopressor dose, renal function, oxygenation, or inflammatory biomarkers over time. Previous studies have suggested that dynamic vital sign patterns and longitudinal data may further improve prognostic prediction in sepsis (29). In addition, detailed microbiological characteristics and comprehensive information on immunosuppressive status were not consistently available, limiting our ability to further investigate the unexpected distribution of baseline PCT between survivors and non-survivors. Fifth, treatment-related variables, including vasoactive drug administration, mechanical ventilation, and CRRT, may primarily reflect underlying illness severity and clinician decision-making rather than directly modifiable causal determinants. Finally, SHAP analysis improves transparency by explaining the contribution of variables to model output, but it does not establish causality. Prospective validation, external testing, and clinical impact studies are required before this model can be recommended for routine bedside use.

In conclusion, this study developed an interpretable machine learning model for predicting in-hospital mortality in ICU patients with sepsis using routinely available clinical variables. The model showed acceptable discrimination and calibration, while SHAP analysis improved model interpretability. The accompanying web-based calculator serves as a research prototype demonstrating the feasibility of model deployment rather than an established clinical decision-support tool. Given the single-center design, modest sample size, and lack of external validation, these findings should be considered preliminary. Further multicenter and prospective validation is required before clinical application.
