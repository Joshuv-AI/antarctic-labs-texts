Exploring medical and nursing students’ experiences of interprofessional communication and teamwork after a simulation-based interprofessional education session
Authors: Hoang Minh Nguyen, Vu Quoc Huy Nguyen, Minh Tam Nguyen, Khanh Vinh, Kyung Hye Park, Yon Chul Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interprofessional education (IPE) is increasingly implemented to prepare health professions students for collaborative practice. This exploratory pilot examined how medical and nursing students experienced and applied interprofessional communication and teamwork in clinical practice after a simulation based IPE session. This exploratory mixed-methods pilot study involved eight third year medical and nursing students at a Vietnamese medical university. Students completed the Students’ Perceptions of Interprofessional Clinical Education–Revised (SPICE R2) questionnaire before and after the session and submitted reflective essays 3 months later. Quantitative data were analyzed descriptively using Wilcoxon signed rank tests, and essays were examined using inductive reflexive thematic analysis. SPICE-R2 scores increased across all domains from pre- to post-test. The total median score rose from 3.90 (interquartile range [IQR], 3.83–4.38) to 4.60 (IQR, 4.40–4.83) (p=0.01). Thematic analysis generated five themes describing movement from parallel to shared care, greater use of structured communication, clearer appreciation of roles, recognition of shared responsibility for patient safety, and persisting barriers related to hierarchy and workload. This study suggests that a simulation-based IPE session can support students’ interprofessional communication and teamwork, though barriers related to hierarchy and workload persisted. Future research with larger, multi-institution samples and richer data sources is needed to understand how these skills are sustained in practice.

Introduction

Interprofessional collaboration is a key determinant of healthcare quality, contributing to improved patient outcomes, more efficient use of resources, and reduced medical errors [1]. Effective collaboration relies on clear, structured communication, particularly in complex clinical environments requiring coordinated decision-making. The World Health Organization has highlighted collaborative practice as essential for safe and sustainable health systems [2].

To address this need, interprofessional education (IPE) has been increasingly integrated into health professions training worldwide to prepare students for collaborative practice [3,4]. In Vietnam, IPE has been introduced in several medical universities and has shown positive effects on students’ readiness for interprofessional learning and perceived collaborative competencies [5-7]. These initiatives mainly involve undergraduate students from medicine, nursing, pharmacy, and related disciplines, and are typically delivered through classroom-based modules, workshops, and pilot learning sessions embedded in curricula.

However, most existing studies have focused on outcomes within educational settings, such as attitudes and self-reported competencies, with limited insight into how these are applied in routine clinical practice [5-7]. This gap is particularly relevant to physician–nurse collaboration, which underpins daily patient care and is critical for communication, coordination, and patient safety [8-10]. Therefore, this exploratory pilot study aimed to examine how medical and nursing students experienced and applied interprofessional communication and teamwork in clinical practice following a simulation-based IPE session in Vietnam.

Methods

1. Study design and participants

This study employed an exploratory mixed-methods pilot design, combining a pre–post quantitative component with a prospective qualitative component. Students’ Perceptions of Interprofessional Clinical Education–Revised (SPICE-R2) questionnaire assessed changes in students’ perceptions of interprofessional learning before and after the session [11], while reflective essays collected 3 months later captured how students applied these experiences in clinical practice. This sequential combination was chosen to provide complementary insights: quantitative data established whether perceptual change occurred, and qualitative data explained the nature and contextual conditions of that change [12]. Reflective writing was selected as the qualitative instrument to allow students to articulate clinical experiences independently.

The study was conducted between May 2024 and February 2025 at University of Medicine and Pharmacy, Hue University, Vietnam. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling from third-year medical and nursing students undertaking clinical rotations at the affiliated teaching hospital. Eligibility criteria included current enrolment in a clinical rotation, limited prior exposure to structured IPE activities, and availability to complete the 3-month follow-up. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Eight students (four medical, four nursing) were recruited, consistent with the exploratory mixed-methods pilot nature of this study, in which quantitative data served as descriptive indicators of within-group trends and the qualitative component was guided by the information power principle [13].

2. Learning setting

The IPE session was developed by a multidisciplinary faculty team comprising physicians and nurses with experience in clinical education and patient safety. The full session structure, including duration and description of each component, is outlined in Table 1.

Prior to the session, pre-readings and instructional materials were distributed to all participants. The session began with a 15-minute ice-breaking activity in which each student introduced themselves by name, profession, and the specific roles and responsibilities of their discipline. A 15-minute briefing followed, in which instructors introduced the learning objectives, session structure, and simulation methodology.

The session included two 30-minute lectures. The first addressed the role of interprofessional teamwork in minimizing medical errors and enhancing patient safety. The second introduced communication frameworks and tools, including ISBAR (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation), for effective interaction between physicians and nurses.

Students were then divided into two interprofessional teams (each comprising two medical and two nursing students) and participated in a 45-minute simulation-based role-play involving simulated clinical scenarios. Students were required to identify clinical problems, share information, negotiate roles, and coordinate a team response, while faculty observed team interactions using a structured observation framework.

Each simulation was followed by a 15-minute structured debriefing using the GAS model (Gather–Analyze–Summarize model), facilitated to help students reflect on their communication strategies, examine how roles and decisions were negotiated, and identify areas for improvement. The session concluded with a 15-minute wrap-up in which facilitators summarized key takeaways and discussed interprofessional learning goals with participants.

3. Data collection

Participants completed the SPICE-R2 questionnaire immediately before and after the IPE session. This 10-item instrument measures perceptions across three domains—teamwork, roles and responsibilities, and patient outcomes—on a 5-point Likert scale, and was translated into Vietnamese using a forward–backward process with expert review and pilot testing to ensure linguistic and cultural appropriateness [14]. Three months after the session, all participants submitted a written reflective essay in Vietnamese, allowing sufficient time to observe and articulate clinical experiences [15]. Students were asked to recount a relevant clinical situation, describe how the IPE session influenced their behavior or thinking, and identify ongoing challenges. Essays were submitted electronically, anonymized, and labelled (Student A–H), with profession recorded for contextual interpretation.

4. Data analysis

1) Quantitative data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for total and domain scores of the SPICE-R2 at pre- and post-test. Given the small sample size and potential non-normal distribution, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to assess pre–post changes. Given the exploratory nature and small sample of this pilot study, quantitative findings are presented as descriptive indicators rather than definitive evidence of effect.

2) Qualitative data analysis

Reflective essays were analyzed using an inductive reflexive thematic analysis approach following Braun et al. [16], moving from familiarization through coding to iteratively refined themes. Reflexivity was considered an active component of the process: both researchers specialize in medical education, with one additionally holding a clinical background. Each researcher independently coded the full dataset before meeting to compare frameworks, discuss differences, and reach consensus through discussion. Final themes were defined collaboratively and illustrated with anonymized quotations. Essays were analyzed in Vietnamese, with selected excerpts translated into English by a bilingual educator and verified by a second researcher for semantic equivalence.

5. Ethics approval declaration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of University of Medicine and Pharmacy, Hue University (approval no., H2025/439). All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki [17]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

1. Changes in students’ perceptions after the IPE session

SPICE-R2 scores increased across all domains from pre- to post-test (Table 2). The total median score rose from 3.90 (interquartile range [IQR], 3.83–4.38) to 4.60 (IQR, 4.40–4.83), with a median difference of 0.70 (p=0.01). At the domain level, interprofessional teamwork increased from 3.83 (IQR, 3.58–4.37) to 4.42 (IQR, 4.25–4.83) (median difference, 0.59; p=0.01), and roles and responsibilities from 4.00 (IQR, 3.75–4.12) to 4.50 (IQR, 4.38–5.00) (median difference, 0.50; p=0.06). Patient outcomes showed a smaller increase from 4.50 (IQR, 4.00–4.62) to 5.00 (IQR, 4.50–5.00) (median difference, 0.50; p=0.25) (Table 2).

2. Qualitative themes from reflective essays

Reflexive thematic analysis of the eight reflective essays generated five themes that described how students experienced the simulation-based IPE session and how they later applied interprofessional communication and teamwork skills in clinical practice. Themes are summarized below and illustrated with quotations (Table 3).

1) Theme 1: Moving from parallel to shared care

Students described a shift from working in parallel to more actively sharing information and planning patient care together.

“Before the class, I usually concentrated only on my orders and did not ask much about the nurse’s work. After practicing together in the simulation, I tried to sit down with the nurse to clarify the care plan and ask how we could share the work so that nothing would be missed for the patient.” (Student C, medical)

After practicing together in the simulation, I tried to sit down with the nurse to clarify the care plan and ask how we could share the work so that nothing would be missed for the patient.” (Student C, medical)

2) Theme 2: Using structured communication to speak up

Students highlighted how structured communication (e.g., ISBAR) helped them organize information and speak up with more confidence.

“During my night shift, I had to call the doctor because the patient became more short of breath. I remembered the ISBAR steps from the simulation, so I introduced myself, clearly explained the situation and background, and then gave my assessment and recommendation. The doctor listened more carefully, and I felt less nervous compared with before.” (Student G, nursing)

I remembered the ISBAR steps from the simulation, so I introduced myself, clearly explained the situation and background, and then gave my assessment and recommendation. The doctor listened more carefully, and I felt less nervous compared with before.” (Student G, nursing)

3) Theme 3: Understanding and valuing each other’s roles

The scenarios and debriefings helped students better understand how doctors and nurses complement each other in assessing, monitoring, and making decisions for patients, and this clearer view reduced some of their earlier stereotypes or misunderstandings.

“When I saw how the nurses in our simulation monitored the patient continuously and noticed subtle changes, I realized that their observations are very important for making clinical decisions. Now, in the ward, I try to ask the nurse for their opinion instead of only checking the chart.” (Student A, medical)

Now, in the ward, I try to ask the nurse for their opinion instead of only checking the chart.” (Student A, medical)

4) Theme 4: Recognizing patient safety as a shared responsibility

Students associated interprofessional communication and teamwork with patient safety and described using lessons from the simulation to help prevent delays, errors, and other potential safety problems in practice.

“In one case, the nurse told me that the patient’s blood pressure was dropping during the night. Because we had talked in the simulation about speaking up early, I immediately went to see the patient and discussed with the nurse what might be happening. Together we called the senior doctor. I felt that our cooperation made the response faster and safer for the patient.” (Student B, medical)

5) Theme 5: Persisting challenges related to hierarchy and workload

Students also reported ongoing barriers to applying interprofessional skills, such as strong hierarchies, time pressure, and inconsistent responses from staff when they tried to communicate or join decision making.

“In some wards, the doctors are very busy and there is a strong hierarchy. Even when I try to use ISBAR, sometimes they interrupt me or only give brief answers. In those moments, I feel small again and hesitate to ask more questions.” (Student F, nursing)

Discussion

This study explored how third-year medical and nursing students experienced a pilot simulation-based IPE session and how they later described using communication and teamwork skills in clinical settings. Students’ accounts pointed to a shift from working in parallel toward more shared care, more frequent use of structured communication to speak up, and a growing sense of joint responsibility for patient safety. At the same time, barriers such as hierarchy and workload continued to influence how these skills were used in practice. Changes observed in SPICE-R2 scores followed a similar pattern, but given the small sample, these results should be interpreted with caution and viewed as supportive rather than definitive.

Students’ reflections on moving toward shared care and gaining a clearer understanding of each other’s roles suggest that the IPE session helped make the interdependence between doctors and nurses more visible in day-to-day clinical work. This finding is in line with previous studies showing that simulation-based IPE can improve role clarity and encourage collaborative practice [18]. However, unlike results from structured educational settings, our findings show that applying shared care in real clinical environments is uneven and shaped by context. Watching each other during simulation and engaging in joint reflection seemed to challenge some existing assumptions and encouraged students to involve colleagues from other professions more actively during clinical encounters. This supports the idea that learning develops through participation and reflection in real-world settings [19,20].

Students described structured communication tools such as ISBAR as both practical and empowering. These tools helped them organize information and provided a legitimate way to contribute in situations where hierarchy might otherwise limit participation. Previous research has highlighted the importance of communication and teamwork for safe and effective care [8,9]. In this study, structured communication also appeared to give students a sense of permission to speak, helping them navigate hierarchical relationships more confidently.

Reflections related to patient safety showed that students recognized the link between communication and the prevention of delays or errors. This aligns with earlier work emphasizing the importance of collaboration between physicians and nurses for improving patient outcomes [8,10]. However, students also noted that their ability to act on this understanding depended on the clinical environment. Time pressure, workload, and the attitudes of senior staff often limited opportunities to speak up, even when students felt more prepared. These findings suggest that while IPE can influence how students think about teamwork and communication, the extent to which these changes are expressed in practice depends on the surrounding context.

Overall, this pilot study suggests that simulation-based IPE can provide a useful reference point for students as they develop interprofessional skills. Previous studies also indicate that IPE can improve students’ readiness and perceptions of collaboration [5-7], and our findings add to this by showing how such learning may be carried into practice. However, sustaining these changes seems to depend on the clinical setting and the behaviors observed from senior staff.

1. Strengths and limitations

This study’s strength lies in its focus on how students experienced and applied interprofessional skills in real clinical settings several months after a simulation based IPE session. However, the small, single institution sample limits transferability, and the reflective essays relied on self report, which may be affected by recall bias, with no direct observation of clinical interactions.

2. Conclusion

This exploratory pilot study suggests that students developed greater awareness of shared responsibility, clearer role understanding, and more deliberate use of communication strategies, while ongoing challenges related to hierarchy and workload persisted. Future research should include larger, multi-institution samples and incorporate additional data sources to better understand how interprofessional communication and teamwork are enacted and sustained in clinical practice over time.
