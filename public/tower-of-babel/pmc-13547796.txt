Severe calciphylaxis in a post-kidney transplant patient with chronic allograft dysfunction and unmodifiable anticoagulation
Authors: Sivakajani Balakumar, Kelum Priyadarshana, Sandawana William Majoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalciphylaxis is a rare but life-threatening disorder characterised by progressive vascular calcification, thrombosis, and painful skin necrosis, with high morbidity and mortality. Although most cases are reported in patients with end-stage kidney disease receiving dialysis, it can also occur in kidney transplant recipients, particularly in the setting of chronic allograft dysfunction and persistent disturbances in mineral metabolism. We report the case of a 70-year-old man who developed biopsy-confirmed calciphylaxis during chronic allograft failure secondary to chronic T-cell-mediated rejection and BK virus nephropathy following deceased-donor kidney transplantation. Additional risk factors included hyperphosphataemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, long-term warfarin therapy for a mechanical aortic valve, and ongoing immunosuppression. Management involved multidisciplinary care, including cessation of warfarin with bridging intravenous unfractionated heparin, initiation of maintenance haemodialysis, optimisation of chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone disorder, intravenous sodium thiosulfate administered after each haemodialysis session, intensive wound care, pain management, and planned hyperbaric oxygen therapy. Despite these interventions, the patient developed varicella-zoster virus encephalitis and disseminated Trichosporon infection, resulting in progressive multisystem illness and death. This case highlights the multifactorial pathogenesis, diagnostic challenges, and poor prognosis of calciphylaxis in kidney transplant recipients with advanced allograft failure, particularly when anticoagulation cannot be safely discontinued because of a mechanical heart valve. Early recognition, histopathological confirmation, and prompt multidisciplinary management are essential; however, outcomes remain poor in patients with advanced graft dysfunction and ongoing immunosuppression.Keywords: calciphylaxis, calcific uraemic arteriolopathy, end-stage kidney disease, renal transplant, warfarin

Background

Calciphylaxis, also referred to as calcific uraemic arteriolopathy (CUA), is a rare but life-threatening disorder characterised by progressive microvascular calcification of small- and medium-sized vessels within the dermis and subcutaneous adipose tissue. Clinically, it typically presents with exquisitely painful, non-ulcerated violaceous or purpuric plaques forming a reticulated erythematous pattern, often associated with firm subcutaneous nodules and surrounding erythema. As the disease progresses, the lesions become increasingly indurated, hyperpigmented, and painful, ultimately evolving into necrotic ulcers with overlying black eschar.1 –3

Calciphylaxis lesions most commonly involve central areas of the body with abundant adipose tissue, such as the thighs, abdomen, and breasts; however, peripheral involvement has also been described in regions with minimal subcutaneous fat, including the lower limbs, feet, fingers, and penis.1,2,4 Although calciphylaxis has classically been associated with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), particularly in patients receiving long-term dialysis, its occurrence in renal transplant recipients has been recognised since early reports in the 1970s.1 –3,5

Subsequent case reports, series, and reviews have shown that calciphylaxis may develop early or late after kidney transplantation, often with declining graft function, persistent disturbances in mineral metabolism, immunosuppression, and exposure to precipitating factors such as vitamin K antagonists.6 –12 Importantly, calciphylaxis has also been reported in post-transplant patients with preserved or partially preserved renal function, supporting the concept of non-uraemic calciphylaxis.9 –11

Given this expanding clinical spectrum, the term calciphylaxis is now preferred over CUA, as it encompasses both uraemic and non-uraemic forms of the disease.2,9,11 The prognosis remains poor, particularly among patients with chronic kidney disease or graft dysfunction, with reported 1-year mortality rates approaching 80%, compared with approximately 45% in those without significant renal impairment.8,11,12 However, despite increasing recognition of calciphylaxis in kidney transplant recipients, there remains a paucity of evidence to guide optimal management in patients requiring ongoing post-transplant immunosuppression, and treatment decisions are largely based on extrapolation from the dialysis population and individual case reports. 4 Owing to its complex and incompletely understood pathogenesis, no standardised treatment strategy has been established through randomised controlled trials. Management therefore relies on a multidisciplinary approach incorporating meticulous wound care, aggressive infection control, optimisation of mineral and bone metabolism, and individualised modification of immunosuppressive and anticoagulant therapies.11,13

Here, we report a case of calciphylaxis in a post-renal transplant patient with declining graft function who was receiving warfarin therapy for aortic valve replacement (AVR) and atrial fibrillation.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old Caucasian man presented with a 3-week history of progressively worsening painful lower-limb skin lesions. He had undergone deceased-donor kidney transplantation in 2020 for ESKD secondary to diabetic nephropathy. His post-transplant course was complicated by biopsy-proven chronic active T-cell-mediated rejection and recurrent BK virus nephropathy, resulting in progressive chronic allograft dysfunction. Ultrasonography of the renal allograft demonstrated features consistent with chronic allograft nephropathy, including reduced graft size, increased cortical echogenicity, cortical thinning, and loss of corticomedullary differentiation without hydronephrosis. At presentation, serum creatinine was 417 μmol/L (baseline 280–320 μmol/L), corresponding to an estimated glomerular filtration rate (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) of approximately 12 mL/min/1.73 m2, consistent with advanced chronic allograft failure.

His medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic atrial fibrillation, and mechanical AVR requiring long-term warfarin anticoagulation. Additional risk factors for calciphylaxis included advanced chronic kidney disease, hyperphosphataemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and ongoing immunosuppression. The patient was not receiving maintenance haemodialysis before admission despite advanced graft dysfunction.

Physical examination demonstrated multiple exquisitely painful violaceous plaques and nodular lesions involving both lower limbs, with surrounding induration and evolving skin necrosis. Laboratory investigations revealed serum calcium 2.44 mmol/L, phosphate 2.16 mmol/L, calcium–phosphate product 5.27 mmol2/L2, parathyroid hormone (PTH) 129.5 pmol/L, and an international normalised ratio of 4.8 while receiving warfarin.

A skin biopsy was performed following dermatology review. Histopathological examination demonstrated calcification of small- and medium-sized dermal arterioles with intimal proliferation, luminal thrombosis, and ischaemic tissue necrosis, confirming calciphylaxis.

Pain management was individualised following review by the Acute Pain Service. Analgesia consisted of regular paracetamol (1 g four times daily) together with oxycodone administered as both regular and breakthrough doses according to pain severity, with doses adjusted based on daily pain assessment and tolerability. Opioid requirements were reviewed regularly and modified according to clinical response.

Immunosuppressive management

At presentation, the patient was receiving tacrolimus 1.5 mg twice daily (admission trough concentration 5.8 ng/mL), prednisolone 5 mg once daily, and everolimus 0.5 mg once daily. Everolimus had previously been introduced because of recurrent BK virus nephropathy.

Tacrolimus dosage was reduced to 0.5 mg twice daily with subsequent adjustment guided by serial trough concentrations to minimise immunosuppression while avoiding abrupt withdrawal. Everolimus was discontinued after progression to dialysis-dependent graft failure, whereas prednisolone 5 mg daily was continued throughout admission.

Graft nephrectomy was not considered because there was no clinical evidence of graft intolerance syndrome, and graft embolisation was not indicated.

Histopathological confirmation of calciphylaxis was obtained within 48 h of biopsy, and sodium thiosulfate (STS) therapy was commenced immediately thereafter. STS was administered intravenously after each haemodialysis session to minimise dialytic clearance. Following progression to end-stage graft failure, the patient commenced maintenance haemodialysis three times weekly and underwent approximately 12 haemodialysis sessions during his 4-week hospital admission. Consequently, he received 12 doses of STS, each administered following dialysis. No significant intradialytic hypotension occurred, and haemodynamic status remained stable throughout treatment.

Management also included intensive wound care, optimisation of calcium–phosphate balance, cessation of warfarin therapy, and multidisciplinary input from nephrology, dermatology, infectious diseases, cardiology, wound-care specialists, and the Acute Pain Service. Because of the patient’s mechanical aortic valve, anticoagulation management required careful balancing of thromboembolic and bleeding risks, with bridging anticoagulation individualised following cardiology consultation.

Given the patient’s mechanical aortic valve, interruption of warfarin therapy required careful consideration of thromboembolic risk. Warfarin was discontinued at presentation because of the increased bleeding risk associated with calciphylaxis and extensive skin necrosis. Following multidisciplinary discussion involving nephrology, cardiology, and haematology teams, bridging anticoagulation with intravenous unfractionated heparin (UFH) was initiated.

UFH was commenced with an initial bolus of 80 units/kg (approximately 5400 units for a body weight of 68 kg), followed by a continuous infusion of 18 units/kg/h (approximately 1200 units/h). The infusion rate was adjusted based on serial activated partial thromboplastin time monitoring, targeting the therapeutic range according to institutional protocol. Anticoagulation intensity was individualised, balancing the risk of mechanical valve thrombosis against the risk of bleeding complications and progression of calciphylaxis-related skin necrosis.

UFH bridging was continued during the period when therapeutic anticoagulation was considered clinically appropriate and was subsequently discontinued after clinical deterioration, development of severe opportunistic infections, and transition towards comfort-focused management.

Neck ultrasonography demonstrated diffusely enlarged hypoechoic parathyroid glands with oval morphology, consistent with secondary hyperparathyroidism. A sestamibi scan was not performed. Owing to frailty, multiple comorbidities, and rapid clinical deterioration, parathyroidectomy was not pursued.

Despite treatment, renal function continued to decline, necessitating ongoing thrice-weekly haemodialysis. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy was planned as adjunctive treatment but could not be commenced because of rapid clinical deterioration.

During the subsequent hospital course, the patient developed progressive confusion and altered mental status. Cerebrospinal fluid-polymerase chain reaction was positive for varicella-zoster virus (VZV), confirming VZV encephalitis. Subsequently, he developed disseminated Trichosporon infection, confirmed by positive blood cultures and wound cultures. Clinically, this was associated with persistent fever, progressive necrotic skin lesions, worsening systemic inflammatory response, and haemodynamic deterioration. Computed tomography demonstrated bilateral pulmonary nodular infiltrates and multifocal pulmonary consolidations compatible with disseminated fungal infection.

Despite aggressive multidisciplinary management, including antimicrobial therapy, haemodialysis, STS therapy, wound care, optimisation of metabolic abnormalities, and supportive care, the patient deteriorated because of severe opportunistic infections and progressive multisystem illness. After discussions about prognosis and goals of care, treatment was redirected to comfort-focussed management. The patient died approximately 4 weeks after the diagnosis of calciphylaxis.

Discussion

Calciphylaxis is a rare and frequently fatal disorder characterised by progressive microvascular calcification and thrombosis, culminating in tissue ischaemia and necrosis. 1 It predominantly affects patients with ESKD receiving long-term dialysis, particularly in the presence of severe disturbances in bone and mineral metabolism. However, an increasing number of cases have been described in kidney transplant recipients, especially those who develop allograft dysfunction and subsequently return to dialysis.2,3,5

In kidney transplant recipients, calciphylaxis typically manifests with clinical features indistinguishable from those observed in dialysis-dependent patients, including exquisitely painful purpuric skin lesions that evolve into necrotic, non-healing ulcers. These lesions most commonly involve adipose-rich regions such as the thighs and abdomen, although distal extremities may also be affected.2,5

The pathogenesis of calciphylaxis is complex and multifactorial, involving the interplay of disordered mineral metabolism, endothelial dysfunction, and a prothrombotic milieu.3,8 Secondary hyperparathyroidism, hyperphosphataemia, and an elevated calcium–phosphate product promote medial arterial calcification, while chronic inflammation and uraemic toxins exacerbate endothelial injury and vascular smooth muscle cell osteogenic transformation.3,8 In kidney transplant recipients who experience graft failure and return to dialysis, abrupt derangements in calcium–phosphate homeostasis, compounded by reactivation of secondary hyperparathyroidism, may significantly amplify susceptibility to calciphylaxis.3,5

Warfarin exposure constitutes a particularly important and well-established risk factor for calciphylaxis, especially in patients with mechanical prosthetic heart valves or chronic atrial fibrillation.6,10 Warfarin promotes vascular calcification by inhibiting vitamin K epoxide reductase, thereby preventing the activation of vitamin K-dependent proteins such as matrix Gla protein (MGP), a critical endogenous inhibitor of vascular and soft-tissue calcification. In the absence of functional MGP, vascular smooth muscle cells permit unchecked calcium deposition within the arterial media, accelerating medial arterial calcification and predisposing to calciphylaxis.6,10,13 Numerous case reports and observational studies have consistently demonstrated a strong association between long-term warfarin therapy and calciphylaxis in both dialysis and post-transplant populations.1,2,6,10

In the present case, the development of calciphylaxis can be understood as the convergence of several high-risk factors acting synergistically. Progressive allograft dysfunction necessitated a return to haemodialysis, reintroducing a uraemic environment characterised by impaired phosphate clearance, fluctuating calcium balance, and re-emergence of secondary hyperparathyroidism. Suboptimal solute clearance during the early phase of dialysis re-initiation may have further intensified the pro-calcific milieu. Concomitant abnormalities in PTH and phosphate levels provided a strong substrate for progressive vascular calcification.

The patient’s requirement for long-term warfarin therapy following mechanical AVR represented a critical, non-modifiable risk factor. Direct oral anticoagulants are contraindicated in patients with mechanical heart valves and are generally avoided in advanced kidney failure because of altered pharmacokinetics, drug accumulation, and increased bleeding risk. Consequently, warfarin remained the only feasible anticoagulation strategy despite its recognised role in inhibiting vitamin K-dependent calcification inhibitors such as MGP. Ongoing immunosuppression in the post-transplant setting likely compounded vascular vulnerability by promoting endothelial dysfunction, impairing tissue repair, and increasing susceptibility to infection.

Ongoing immunosuppression posed a significant therapeutic dilemma in this case. At presentation, the patient remained on tacrolimus, everolimus, and prednisolone despite progressive allograft failure. While the reduction of immunosuppression may theoretically decrease infection risk and improve wound healing, abrupt withdrawal carries the risk of graft intolerance syndrome and persistent inflammatory responses. Furthermore, there is currently no consensus regarding optimal immunosuppressive management in kidney transplant recipients who develop calciphylaxis. Consequently, treatment decisions must be individualised, balancing the risks of infection, allograft-related complications, and ongoing vascular injury. This case highlights an important gap in the current literature and underscores the need for evidence-based guidance in managing calciphylaxis in actively immunosuppressed transplant recipients.2,5

Management of calciphylaxis remains exceptionally challenging due to its rarity and the absence of standardised treatment protocols. The greatest therapeutic benefit has been observed with a multidisciplinary approach incorporating meticulous wound care, aggressive pain management, optimisation of mineral and bone metabolism, stringent infection control, and targeted pharmacological therapies.7,11,12 STS remains the most widely used adjunctive therapy and has demonstrated efficacy in reducing calcium deposition, alleviating pain, and promoting wound healing in both uraemic and post-transplant populations.7,11

Optimisation of secondary hyperparathyroidism is a critical component of management. Calcimimetics such as cinacalcet play an increasingly important role by suppressing PTH secretion, lowering serum calcium and phosphate levels, and reducing the calcium–phosphate product without increasing calcium load. Emerging evidence suggests that cinacalcet may stabilise or improve calciphylaxis lesions in selected patients, particularly when surgical intervention is contraindicated or delayed, although robust prospective data remain limited.4,8

In patients with severe, refractory secondary hyperparathyroidism, parathyroidectomy may be considered a definitive therapeutic option. While parathyroidectomy has been associated with rapid biochemical improvement and, in some cases, significant pain relief, its impact on lesion healing and overall survival remains variable. Current evidence suggests that parathyroidectomy may be most beneficial when performed early in the disease course, prior to extensive tissue necrosis, but it has not been conclusively shown to prevent the development of calciphylaxis or to uniformly improve survival outcomes.4,8

Despite aggressive multimodal therapy, mortality remains alarmingly high, with infection representing the leading cause of death.2,9,13 This case illustrates these challenges vividly, as the patient developed disseminated Trichosporon infection and VZV encephalitis, ultimately resulting in death despite initiation of haemodialysis and STS therapy. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy was planned as an adjunctive treatment; however, the patient deteriorated rapidly and died before therapy could be commenced. This outcome underscores the profound interplay between metabolic derangement, unavoidable anticoagulant exposure, and immunosuppression in precipitating calciphylaxis and its fatal complications in post-transplant patients.6,8,10

In addition to established therapies, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have recently emerged as a promising investigational treatment for calciphylaxis. MSCs exert multiple biological effects that directly target key pathogenic mechanisms involved in calciphylaxis, including the inhibition of vascular calcification, promotion of angiogenesis and tissue regeneration, modulation of inflammatory and immune responses, enhancement of wound healing, and reduction of oxidative stress. Furthermore, MSCs possess anti-infective and antithrombotic properties that may be particularly relevant in patients with advanced kidney disease and transplant recipients, who frequently experience concurrent immunosuppression and hypercoagulable states. Experimental and early clinical evidence suggests that MSC therapy may improve tissue repair and vascular health while addressing the multifactorial pathophysiology of calciphylaxis. Although current evidence remains limited and further clinical studies are required to establish efficacy and safety, MSC-based therapies represent a novel therapeutic avenue for patients with refractory calciphylaxis and may become an important adjunct to conventional treatment strategies in the future. 15

Several cases of calciphylaxis after kidney transplantation have been reported. Hanvesakul et al. described calciphylaxis in a renal transplant recipient with declining graft function, and Mitomo et al. reported penile calciphylaxis shortly after dialysis was initiated for graft dysfunction. Consistent with these reports, our patient had multiple recognised risk factors, including chronic allograft failure, hyperphosphataemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and long-term warfarin exposure. However, this case was notable for the need for ongoing anticoagulation because of a mechanical aortic valve, continued post-transplant immunosuppression, and the development of fatal opportunistic infections, including VZV encephalitis and disseminated Trichosporon infection. These features underscore the therapeutic challenges of managing calciphylaxis in immunosuppressed kidney transplant recipients with limited anticoagulation options.

Previous reports have described successful use of STS in kidney transplant recipients with calciphylaxis, although most patients retained partial graft function or developed calciphylaxis after transplantation without requiring dialysis. Hanvesakul et al. reported calciphylaxis in a renal transplant recipient with chronic allograft dysfunction treated with STS and multidisciplinary wound care. Similarly, Mitomo et al. described calciphylaxis after kidney transplantation managed with STS together with optimisation of mineral metabolism and withdrawal of precipitating factors. In contrast, our patient had irreversible chronic allograft failure requiring initiation of maintenance haemodialysis, allowing STS to be administered after each dialysis session to minimise dialytic clearance while correcting uraemia and disturbances in mineral metabolism. Despite this comprehensive approach, the outcome was poor because of advanced graft failure, multiple comorbidities, and fatal opportunistic infections, highlighting the markedly worse prognosis once calciphylaxis develops in the setting of severe chronic allograft dysfunction.

Conclusion

Kidney transplant recipients with graft failure who return to dialysis represent a uniquely vulnerable population at heightened risk for calciphylaxis. Early identification and aggressive correction of modifiable risk factors—particularly disturbances in mineral metabolism—alongside judicious use of calcimimetics, consideration of parathyroidectomy in appropriate candidates, and coordinated multidisciplinary care are essential to improving outcomes. However, despite timely diagnosis and multimodal treatment, mortality remains extremely high in this population because ongoing immunosuppression increases susceptibility to severe opportunistic infections and impairs wound healing. Further studies are required to refine risk stratification, define optimal therapeutic sequencing, establish evidence-based approaches to immunosuppression management, and explore safer anticoagulation strategies in this exceptionally high-risk group (Figures 1–3).
