Mitochondria‐Damaging Self‐Reporting Probe for Cancer Therapy
Authors: Hai Xu, Yura Lee, Sanghee Yoon, Yun Wang, Yejin Cho, Seongyu Choi, Lu Lu, Hua Zhang, Sun Choi, Ki Taek Nam, Juyoung Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMitochondrial damage induced by chemotherapeutic agents through disruption of the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm ) remains a central challenge in drug development and evaluation. However, the assessment of ΔΨm ‐targeting drugs using commercially available fluorescent probes is often unreliable, as these dyes can interfere with, mask, or artificially amplify drug‐induced mitochondrial dysfunction, frequently resulting in misleading conclusions and translational failure. Herein, we report a class of cationic chemotherapeutic small molecules (DPPs) possessing intrinsic fluorescence migration‐based self‐reporting capability, which enables direct and non‐invasive monitoring of drug action without the need for external probes. Among them, DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 disrupt mitochondrial function, trigger excessive reactive oxygen species generation, and induce highly selective apoptosis. Remarkably, both compounds exhibit concentration‐dependent mitochondrial‐to‐nuclear translocation, enabling the real‐time visualization of therapeutic progression at the subcellular level. In vivo studies further confirm their potent tumor growth inhibition and negligible systemic toxicity effects. This self‐reporting mitochondria‐targeted chemotherapeutic platform provides a highly promising strategy for integrated cancer diagnosis and precision therapy.

Introduction

Cancer cells exhibit rapid proliferation, abnormal energy metabolism, and resistance to apoptotic signals, making them highly resistant to conventional therapeutic agents [1, 2, 3]. As the central hub of energy production and signal regulation, mitochondria participate in adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, reactive oxygen species (ROS) modulation, and lipid metabolism, while also playing key roles in intrinsic apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, and DNA damage response [4, 5, 6, 7]; therefore, they have emerged as a crucial target of anticancer drug development strategies [8, 9]. In recent years, a variety of mitochondria‐targeted compounds have shown potent cytotoxicity toward multiple cancer cell types by disrupting the membrane potential, inducing ROS generation, or activating apoptotic proteins (Figure S1) [4, 10, 11, 12, 13]. These strategies can also partially overcome drug resistance, providing new opportunities for precision cancer therapy [6, 14].

At the same time, the rapid development of fluorescent probe technologies has enabled real‐time and dynamic monitoring of mitochondrial damage at the subcellular level [15, 16, 17]. In particular, probes responsive to changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm ) or to the accumulation of ROS have been widely employed to reveal the onset and progression of mitochondrial dysfunction [18, 19]. Furthermore, specialized probes capable of translocating from mitochondria to the nucleus have been developed to trace cross‐organelle damage signaling, thereby providing valuable insight into the dynamic interplay between mitochondrial impairment and apoptosis [20, 21].

To date, numerous studies have demonstrated that small‐molecule fluorescent probes can relocalize between mitochondria and the nucleus in response to cellular stress or perturbations of membrane potential [22, 23]. However, such fluorescence migration typically requires stimulation by external drugs or light, as the probes themselves do not possess intrinsic therapeutic activity (Figure S2) [21, 24, 25, 26]. Although these approaches offer powerful tools for probing early therapeutic responses and elucidating subcellular pharmacological mechanisms, fluorescence signaling and drug action remain fundamentally decoupled. Consequently, drug screening, mitochondrial damage assessment, and apoptosis evaluation are often treated as independent processes [19]. This separation frequently leads to asynchronous readouts, complicates mechanistic interpretation, and overlooks inter‐organelle signal transduction, ultimately limiting the efficiency of drug optimization and the rational design of combination therapies. Although several mitochondria‐targeted strategies have been reported for target damage detection, capturing early dynamic events and cross‐organelle signaling remains challenging [4, 12, 13, 27, 28]. An integrated diagnostic and therapeutic strategy based on fluorescence visualization of mitochondrial migration to other organelles can effectively bridge this gap. Currently, most fluorophore–drug conjugates are prepared by covalently linking an exogenous fluorophore to a therapeutic molecule, where the two components function independently [29, 30, 31]. Although such systems can be used for tracking and imaging applications, they may alter the pharmacokinetics or targeting properties of the drug and are prone to decoupling in vivo, leading to a mismatch between fluorescence signal and therapeutic efficacy. In contrast, in integrated fluorescent drugs, the fluorophore is directly embedded into the pharmacophore framework, generating intrinsic fluorescence while maintaining the pharmacological activity. This integrated structural design ensures superior stability and establishes a direct correlation between signal and function. Tracking the dynamic changes along the mitochondrial–nuclear signaling axis with integrated fluorescent drugs enables to achieve early evaluation of subcellular drug efficacy and precise anticancer drug development, providing a more accurate platform and theoretical foundation for integrated diagnosis and therapy [25, 32, 33].

To overcome these challenges, we developed a series of cationic fluorene derivatives (DPPs, DPP‐1 to DPP‐5) for self‐reporting cancer therapy by introducing different amino substituents at the 3‐, 6‐, and 9‐positions of the fluorene core [34, 35]. The substituent type was found to critically influence the extent of mitochondrial damage and the subsequent probe migration from mitochondria to the nucleus, an event strongly correlated with the cytotoxicity (Figure 1). Notably, DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 exhibited markedly higher cytotoxicity in cancer cells than in normal cells. Mechanistic analyses confirmed that mitochondrial dysfunction triggers apoptosis, and in vivo experiments further confirmed their therapeutic efficacy in tumor‐bearing mice. Overall, DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 enable real‐time visualization of inter‐organelle signaling during drug‐induced mitochondrial damage, providing new insights into the dynamics of cell death and a powerful tool for evaluating therapeutic responses at the subcellular level.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Spectral Properties of DPPs

A series of cationic fluorene derivatives bearing different amino substituents was synthesized as shown in Schemes S1–S5 (Figure 2a). All products were fully characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), confirming their expected structures (Figures S3–S17).

(d) Fluorescence intensity ratios (I max/I 0) of DPPs with varying ctDNA concentrations at 683 nm in Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.4). (e) Photophysical properties of DPPs. ε = molar extinction coefficient; Φ fl = fluorescence quantum yield (measured in ethanol).

As shown in Figure 2b,c, all DPPs exhibited similar absorption profiles in PBS buffer; however, their fluorescence emission intensities varied markedly. The sharp peak observed in the 300–500 nm region is likely an intrinsic characteristic absorption of the fluorene chromophore, which can be attributed to strong π–π* electronic transitions. Notably, DPP‐2 and DPP‐4, both bearing a tetrahydropyrrole substituent at the 9‐position, displayed substantially stronger fluorescence than the other derivatives, with quantum yields far exceeding those of DPP‐1, DPP‐3, and DPP‐5 (Figure 2e). This substantial enhancement can be attributed to the ability of the tetrahydropyrrole ring to effectively suppress twisted intramolecular charge transfer (TICT), thereby hindering non‐radiative relaxation pathways. Consistent fluorescence enhancement was also observed in other solvents (Figures S18 and S19), whereas DPP‐1, DPP‐3, and DPP‐5 exhibited pronounced fluorescence enhancement in glycerol, highlighting a clear structure–property relationship. In addition, the DPPs exhibited favorable molar extinction coefficients, large Stokes shifts (>118 nm), and bright near‐infrared (NIR) emission (Figure S20), enabling efficient fluorescence visualization in biological environments. These photophysical characteristics position DPPs as promising candidates for bioimaging and theranostic applications.

Previous studies have shown that aminofluorene‐based probes can bind to DNA and target the cell nucleus [35]. Therefore, we evaluated the potential DNA‐binding abilities of the five synthesized DPPs. Fluorescence titration experiments revealed that the fluorescence intensity of all DPPs increased with the gradual addition of calf thymus DNA (ctDNA); among them, DPP‐1, DPP‐2, and DPP‐5 exhibited particularly strong enhancements (Figures S21 and 2d). This observation suggests that DPP‐1, DPP‐2, and DPP‐5 possessed higher DNA affinity than the other derivatives. Subsequently, the DNA binding constants (K a) of these three probes were determined by fitting the fluorescence titration data using the Scatchard equation (Figure S22). The calculated K a values for DPP‐1, DPP‐2, and DPP‐5 (2.16×106, 1.24×106, and 0.61×106 M−1, respectively) were comparable to that of the conventional DNA intercalator ethidium bromide (≈ 106 M−1). These results indicate that certain DPPs can effectively interact with DNA, reflecting their nuclear localization tendency. We further investigated the effects of pH variations and representative biomolecules on the photophysical behavior of the compounds and found that changes in acidity or alkalinity exerted minimal influence on the fluorescence of DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 (Figure S23a). Moreover, DPP‐1 retained its strong DNA‐binding capability even in the presence of various biomolecules (Figure S23b), demonstrating its excellent selectivity and robustness under biologically relevant conditions.

Molecular Docking Analysis

To elucidate the binding modes of DPPs to DNA, molecular docking simulations were performed with DNA x‐ray crystal structures, for both intercalation (PDB id: 1Z3F) and minor groove binding (PDB id: 453D) modes.

As shown in Figure 3a, the fluorene core of DPP‐1, DPP‐2, and DPP‐5 successfully intercalated between the DNA base pairs, forming π–π stacking interactions with dG6 (in A chain), dC1, and dG2 (in B chain) with all three rings of the fluorene core. In addition, the N+ of the DPPs engaged in cation–π interactions with dC5 and dG6. Also, the pyrrolidine and pyrrolidinium groups of DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 made hydrophobic interactions with dC5, dG6, and dC1, while the dimethylamino and dimethyliminium groups of DPP‐5 made hydrophobic contact only with dC5. The diethylamino group of DPP‐1 exhibited hydrophobic interactions with four bases (dC1, dG2, dA3, and dG6), while the pyrrolidine group of DPP‐2 and the dimethylamino group of DPP‐5 interacted hydrophobically with two bases (dC1 and dG6). Together, these interactions contribute to the overall binding affinity, showing DPP‐1 the best docking score (−9.69) at this site, followed by DPP‐2 (−9.25) and DPP‐5 (−9.06). These hydrophobic interactions may also hinder the TICT process, accounting for the more pronounced fluorescence enhancement observed for DPP‐1 and DPP‐5 in DNA titration experiments.

For the minor groove binding mode, the DPPs formed hydrophobic interactions with dA6, dT7, dT8 (in chain A) and dA18, dT19, dT20, dC21 (in chain B), as shown in Figure 3b. Notably, DPP‐1 established an additional hydrophobic contact with dC9 via its diethylamino group, further stabilizing the complex, resulting in the best docking score (−9.88) at this site, followed by DPP‐2 (−9.68) and DPP‐5 (−8.99). The overall docking results for both the intercalation and minor groove binding modes of DPP‐1, DPP‐2, and DPP‐5 are consistent with the experimental findings.

On the contrary, DPP‐3 and DPP‐4 showed highly diverse conformations due to their flexible diethylamino side chains, resulting in the loss of π–π stacking and cation–π interactions and causing extensive steric clashes within the intercalation site, as shown in Figure S24. At the minor groove, they showed significantly high mean internal strain energies over all docked poses and less favorable binding affinity.

In summary, molecular docking analysis suggests that DPP‐1, DPP‐2, and DPP‐5 all possess significant DNA‐binding capabilities. These findings provide insight into the subsequently observed lack of nuclear translocation in DPP‐3 and DPP‐4, as well as the absence of fluorescence migration in DPP‐5, which, despite its DNA‐binding capability, exhibits relatively low cytotoxicity.

Imaging and Cytotoxicity Experiments

As the DPPs contain cationic units capable of electrostatic interactions with the mitochondrial inner membrane, they were initially expected to target mitochondria. The mitochondrial targeting behavior of the DPPs was examined by confocal laser scanning microscopy in HeLa cells through fluorescence co‐localization with the mitochondria‐specific probe MitoTracker Green. Unexpectedly, the imaging results revealed an entirely different behavior (Figure 4). DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 predominantly accumulated in the nucleus rather than in mitochondria, with almost no fluorescence detected in the green channel. In contrast, DPP‐3 and DPP‐4 exhibited low co‐localization coefficients, whereas only DPP‐5 showed clear mitochondrial localization.

Confocal fluorescence images of HeLa cells incubated with DPPs (2 µM) for 30 min, followed by staining with Hoechst 33342 (2 µg mL−1) and MitoTracker Green (100 nM) for 30 min. PCC = Pearson's correlation coefficient. DPPs: λ ex = 561 nm, λ em = 650–710 nm; MitoTracker Green: λ ex = 488 nm, λ em = 500–550 nm; Hoechst 33342: λ ex = 405 nm, λ em = 420–470 nm.

To clarify whether the apparent loss of mitochondrial staining originated from mitochondrial dysfunction rather than intrinsic targeting differences, we investigated the intracellular dynamics of DPP‐1. Time‐resolved imaging revealed that DPP‐1 rapidly entered cells and initially distributed in the cell within 4 min, followed by gradual accumulation in the nucleus with increasing fluorescence intensity (Figure 5a,b). Importantly, concentration‐dependent studies showed that DPP‐1 did not exhibit nuclear localization at low concentrations (0.25 and 0.5 µM), while pronounced nuclear accumulation occurred at higher concentrations (2 µM) (Figure 5c). Similar trends were observed for DPP‐2, whereas DPP‐3, DPP‐4, and DPP‐5 did not show significant nuclear localization even at elevated concentrations (Figure S25). These observations suggest a concentration‐dependent redistribution process. To test this hypothesis, colocalization experiments were conducted at low concentrations. As shown in Figure 5d, both DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 exhibited efficient mitochondrial localization at 0.25 µM, while DPP‐3 and DPP‐4 also demonstrated pronounced mitochondrial targeting at 0.5 µM (Figure S26). Interestingly, in normal L929 cells, mitochondrial‐to‐nuclear translocation required substantially higher concentrations of DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 (6 µM), indicating a clear cell‐type‐dependent behavior (Figure S27).

To determine whether mitochondrial damage alone is sufficient to trigger mitochondrial‐to‐nuclear translocation, cells were treated with the mitochondrial membrane potential uncoupler carbonyl cyanide m‐chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) or the well‐established apoptosis inducer hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) [32]. Upon CCCP treatment, the fluorescence of DPP‐1 (0.5 µM) redistributed from mitochondria to the nucleus and subsequently returned to mitochondria after removal of CCCP (Figure 5e) [36]. Similar reversible fluorescence migration behavior was observed for DPP‐2 and DPP‐5 (Figures S28 and S29). These results suggest that, at low concentrations, DPP‐1, DPP‐2, and DPP‐5 can undergo fluorescence translocation from mitochondria to the nucleus in response to ΔΨm disruption, thereby serving as potential indicators of mitochondrial damage. Consistently, treatment with H2O2 induced pronounced nuclear fluorescence of DPP‐5 (Figure S30), in agreement with the measured DNA‐binding affinities of the DPPs. In contrast, when cells were treated with oligomycin, an inhibitor of ATP synthase that does not directly dissipate the ΔΨm , no obvious mitochondrial‐to‐nuclear translocation was observed for DPP‐1, DPP‐2, or DPP‐5 (Figure S31), further supporting the critical role of mitochondrial membrane potential disruption in triggering fluorescence migration. Moreover, pretreatment with cyclosporin A (CsA), an inhibitor of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) that preserves ΔΨm , markedly suppressed the nuclear fluorescence of DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 (2 µM) (Figures 5f and S32). CsA effectively blocked mitochondrial‐to‐nuclear translocation, demonstrating that mitochondrial damage is a prerequisite for fluorescence migration. Collectively, these results demonstrate that DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 induce mitochondrial damage, which subsequently triggers their translocation from mitochondria to the nucleus (Figure 5g).

Consistent with these observations, cytotoxicity assays revealed that DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 exhibited the highest cytotoxicity toward HeLa cells, with median inhibition concentration (IC50) values of 1.12 and 3.27 µM, respectively (Figure 5h). Notably, these values exhibited a strong correlation with the concentrations at which mitochondrial‐to‐nuclear translocation occurred (Figure S33). Further evaluation across multiple cell lines showed that DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 exerted potent cytotoxic effects in cancer cells (MCF‐7 and 4T1), but were much less toxic to normal cells (L929 and NIH‐3T3), with IC50 values above 15 µM in NIH‐3T3 (Figures S34 and S35). This selectivity is likely attributed to the higher mitochondrial membrane potential in cancer cells, which facilitates the accumulation of cationic molecules [37, 38]. In contrast, DPP‐3, DPP‐4, and DPP‐5 displayed much weaker cytotoxicity in both cancerous and normal cells.

Identification of Anticancer Mechanism

To confirm that the DPPs induce mitochondrial damage–mediated cell death, we first evaluated the ROS generation activity of the DPPs using 2′,7′‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA) [39]. As shown in Figure 6a, the DPP‐1‐treated HeLa cells displayed intense green fluorescence, with a significantly higher intensity than those treated with other DPPs, confirming that DPP‐1 induced excessive ROS accumulation (Figure S36). We next monitored changes in ΔΨ m using Rhodamine 123 (Rh123) [40]. As shown in Figure 6b,c, control cells treated with PBS exhibited strong green fluorescence, whereas HeLa cells treated with DPPs (2 µM, 1 h) showed a marked fluorescence decrease, particularly in the DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 groups. Meanwhile, ΔΨ m was found to decrease concomitantly with increasing DPP‐1 concentration and its nuclear translocation (Figures 6d and S37). This result indicates that the DPPs effectively trigger mitochondrial membrane depolarization.

During the experiments, vesicle‐like structures, presumed to be apoptotic bodies, were observed in HeLa cells after incubation with DPP‐1 (8 µM) for 40 min (Figure S38). Flow cytometric analysis using Annexin V‐FITC/PI dual staining further confirmed that DPP‐1 predominantly induced apoptosis, with Annexin V+/PI− cells accounting for 64.4% of the population (Figure S39). Meanwhile, we observed that the apoptosis rate increased concomitantly with the nuclear translocation of DPP‐1, further supporting the self‐reporting nature of the mitochondrial damage (Figures 6e and S40). To elucidate the underlying mechanism, the expression of key mitochondrial apoptosis‐related proteins was examined by Western blotting and quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) analysis. As shown in Figures 6f and S41, DPP‐1 treatment markedly upregulated p53 and the pro‐apoptotic protein Bax while downregulating the anti‐apoptotic protein Bcl‐xL. The qRT‐PCR results revealed an even more pronounced regulatory trend. These findings show that DPP‐1 promotes apoptosis via a p53‐mediated mitochondrial pathway, through modulation of the Bax/Bcl‐xL signaling axis (Figure S42) [41]. Given that mitochondria serve as the cellular “powerhouse,” such dysfunction is expected to hinder ATP production [42]. In fact, Figure 6g shows that the intracellular ATP levels in DPP‐1‐ and DPP‐2‐treated HeLa cells decreased by approximately 80% and 55%, respectively, compared with the control. Notably, Bax and Bcl‐xL jointly regulate the permeability of the mitochondrial membrane, which is consistent with the observed loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and the reduction in intracellular ATP levels.

In Vivo Assessment of Therapeutic Effects

Given the selective cytotoxicity of DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 toward cancer cells, we further evaluated their in vivo antitumor efficacy (Figure 7a). After intratumoral injection, strong fluorescence signals were detected at the tumor site within 1 h and persisted for up to 24 h, indicating effective tumor accumulation and retention (Figure S43). After two treatment cycles, both the DPP‐1‐ and DPP‐2‐treated groups exhibited a pronounced reduction in tumor volume without significant changes in body weight, indicating their potent therapeutic efficacy and minimal systemic toxicity (Figure 7b,c). Meanwhile, following intravenous administration, biodistribution analysis showed that at 48 h post‐injection, DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 were predominantly accumulated in the liver, with partial distribution in the kidneys (Figure S44).

Histological analyses further confirmed these observations. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed extensive tumor cell death in the treatment groups (Figure 7d), whereas no apparent pathological abnormalities or inflammatory lesions were observed in the major organs (Figure S45). Moreover, TUNEL, cleaved caspase‐3, and caspase‐3 staining showed pronounced chromatin condensation, nuclear fragmentation, and apoptotic activation, with DPP‐1 exhibiting particularly strong cleaved caspase‐3 expression (Figure 7e,f). Collectively, these results confirm the potent in vivo antitumor efficacy and excellent biocompatibility of DPP‐1 and DPP‐2.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed mitochondria‐damaging self‐reporting probes that integrate cancer therapy and real‐time fluorescence monitoring capabilities. Among them, DPP‐1 and DPP‐2 selectively kill cancer cells by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS accumulation, and p53‐mediated apoptosis, while their mitochondria‐to‐nucleus fluorescence migration enables the visualization of therapeutic progression. In vivo studies confirmed their strong antitumor efficacy and minimal toxicity, demonstrating that the present self‐reporting strategy provides a promising platform for precise and controllable cancer therapy.
