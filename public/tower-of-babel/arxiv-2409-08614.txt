Abstract
. We prove that mean decay of the coefficient of Krein system is equivalent to the mean decay of the Fourier transform of its Szegő function.

1.
Introduction

Let
a\in L^{1}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be a complex-valued function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)
. The Krein system with the coefficient
a
is the following system of differential equations:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}P(r,\lambda)=i\lambda P(r,\lambda)-\overline{a(r)}P_{*}(r,\lambda),&\quad\,\,P(0,\lambda)=1,\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial r}P_{*}(r,\lambda)=-a(r)P(r,\lambda),&\quad P_{*}(0,\lambda)=1.\end{cases}

It was first introduced by M. Krein in
[
16
]
and played an important role in the studies of the spectral theory of differential operators. Krein systems are often used for transferring ideas from the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle to the spectral theory of self-adjoint operators with simple spectrum. Many of the important results on the orthogonal polynomials have their counterparts in the language of Krein systems.
For instance, continuous versions of the Bernstein-Szegő approximations, Baxter’s theorem, Szegő and strong Szegő theorems from the theory of orthogonal polynomials can be found in the survey
[
8
]
by S. Denisov among the key facts of the theory of Krein systems and spectral theory of Dirac operators, also see
[
7
]
for the continuous version of the Rakhmanov’s theorem and
[
12
]
for the ‘‘continuous’’ Máté-Nevai-Totik theorem.
In the present paper we focus on another classical theorem describing probability measures with exponentially small recurrence coefficients – the Nevai-Totik theorem
[
19
]
from 1989. The spectral version of Nevai-Totik theorem in the discrete situation (for Jacobi matrices) has been proved by D. Damanik and B. Simon in
[
6
]
.
The continuous setting remained open until recently. In
[
13
]
we described the class of Dirac operators with exponentially decaying entropy in terms of corresponding spectral measures. The main result of the present paper, see Theorem
1.1
below, can be regarded as a continuous version of the Nevai-Totik theorem in the superexponentially decaying situation. To formulate it, we need to recall the definitions of some basic objects in the spectral theory of Krein systems. We will use
[
8
]
as a main reference.

For any Krein system (
1.1
) there exists a unique Borel measure
\sigma
on the real line
\mathbb{R}
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+x^{2})^{-1}d\sigma(x)<\infty

and the mapping

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\colon f\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)P(r,\lambda)\,dr

is a densely defined isometry between the spaces
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\sigma)
. This measure is called the spectral measure of (
1.1
). If
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then
\sigma
belongs to the Szegő class on
\mathbb{R}
. The latter means
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\log w(x)|}{1+x^{2}}\,dx<\infty
, where
w
is the density of
\sigma
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. In this case the function

\displaystyle\Pi(\lambda)=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{s-\lambda}-\frac{s}{s^{2}+1}\right)\log w\,ds\right],\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}

is outer in
\mathbb{C}_{+}=\{\lambda\colon\mathop{\rm Im}\lambda>0\}
, satisfies
\Pi(i)>0
and
|\Pi(x)|^{-2}=w(x)
for Lebesgue almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, see Section 4 in
[
11
]
. The function
\Pi
is called the inverse Szegő function of system (
1.1
).

Given a function
a
, one can consider Krein systems with the coefficients
a_{r}\colon x\mapsto a(x+r)
for every
r\geqslant 0
. Denote the corresponding spectral measures by
\sigma_{r}
and let
w_{r}
be their densities with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. The entropy function of
a
is defined by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{a}(r)=\log\left(\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\sigma_{r}(x)}{x^{2}+1}\right)-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\log w_{r}(x)}{x^{2}+1}\,dx.

If
\sigma
belongs to the Szegő class then so does
\sigma_{r}
for every
r\geqslant 0
, see
[
3
]
. This means that
\mathcal{K}_{a}
is well-defined (the integrals in (
1.3
) converge) at least for
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. It is known, see Lemma 2.3 in
[
3
]
that
\mathcal{K}_{a}(r)\to 0
as
r\to\infty
.

Notation.

We will use the notation
\lesssim
and
\gtrsim
meaning that the corresponding inequality
\leqslant
or
\geqslant
holds with some multiplicative constant.
We will use the symbol
\approx
when both
\lesssim
and
\gtrsim
hold. Given a function
f
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\alpha>1
, we will write
f(r)=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(r)
if for some
c>0
we have
|f(r)|\lesssim e^{-cr^{\alpha}}
. The equality
f(r)=\varepsilon_{1}(r)
will be used when
f
is superexponentially decaying, i.e., when for every
\delta>0
we have
|f(r)|\lesssim e^{-\delta r}
.

Oscillating potentials.
For
\alpha\geqslant 1
consider the following subspace
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\colon\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx\text{ converges and }\int_{r}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(r)\right\}.

The assertion
\int_{r}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(r)
evidently holds when
f
has compact support or when
f(r)=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(r)
. It also holds for a wider class of rapidly oscillating functions of relatively weak decay, see Figure

.

Functions with the decaying Fourier transform.
Let us introduce the class

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha}=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\colon\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\left(\mathcal{F}f\right)\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}\text{ and }\int_{r}^{\infty}|(\mathcal{F}f)(\xi)|^{2}d\xi=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(r)\right\}

of the
L^{2}
functions with the decaying Fourier transform. Here
\mathcal{F}
stands for the isometric on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
Fourier transform initially defined on simple functions by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}f)(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-it\xi}f(t)\,dt.

As we will show in Lemma
4.2
, the class
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
consists of entire functions.
The following theorem is the main result of the present paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider Krein system (
1.1
) with the coefficient
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. For every
\alpha\geqslant 1
, the following assertions are equivalent:

(
A
)

a\in\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}
;

(
B
)

\sigma
is a. c. and
\frac{\Pi-\Pi(i)}{x-i}\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
;

(
C
)

\mathcal{K}_{a}(r)=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(r)
.

Moreover, if
\alpha>1
and
a\not\equiv 0
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then the above assertions are also equivalent to

(
D
)

for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
we have
P(r,z_{0})=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(r)
.

Let us give some additional remarks: we can change the point
i
in assertion
(B)
to an arbitrary
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, namely, in Proposition
4.5
we show that
(B)
is equivalent to

(
B’
)

\hskip-85.35826pt\sigma\text{ is a.\,c. and for some }z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\text{ we have }\frac{\Pi-\Pi(z_{0})}{x-z_{0}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha};

when
\alpha=1
, the implication
(D)

\Longrightarrow

(A)
still holds, see Proposition
4.6
, however the converse may fail; points
z_{0}
satisfying assertion
(D)
are exactly complex conjugate of resonances of the corresponding Dirac operator, see Section
3.3
.

It is widely known that the oscillation may compensate the growth of the potential and lead to the properties typical to the properties of decreasing potentials, see
[
18
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
22
]
and Appendix
2
to XI.
8
in
[
21
]
. The novelty of Theorem
1.1
is implication
(B)

\Longrightarrow

(A)
which allows to estimate the mean decay of the potential in terms of its spectral data; in comparison with the results from
[
13
]
, Theorem
1.1
has a more explicit condition for the coefficient
a
. Description for the class of compactly supported
L^{2}
potentials in terms of Szegő functions was established in the paper
[
15
]
by E. Korotyaev, similar result for the Schrödinger operator is proved in
[
1
]
by A. Baranov, Y. Belov, and A. Poltoratski. Spectral properties of superexponentially decaying potentials were studied in
[
10
]
,
[
14
]
.

2.
Orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle

2.1.
Basics of the theory

Let us introduce all the necessary concepts from the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle to formulate the Nevai-Totik theorem. We refer to the book
[
23
]
by B. Simon for the general background on the theory.

Let
\mathbb{D}=\{\omega\colon|\omega|<1\}
be a unit disk in the complex plane and
\mathbb{T}=\partial\mathbb{D}
be the unit circle. Consider a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{T}
which support is not a finite set, such measures are called nontrivial.
The functions
\{z^{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
are linearly independent in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mu)
and by the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization procedure, we can construct the sequence
\{\Phi_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
of monic polynomials orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mu)
. There are complex numbers
\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{D}
such that for
z\in\mathbb{C}
we have

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Phi_{n+1}(z)=z\Phi_{n}(z)-\overline{\alpha_{n}}\Phi_{n}^{*}(z),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Phi_{n+1}^{*}(z)=\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)-\alpha_{n}z\Phi_{n}(z),

where
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)=z^{n}\overline{\Phi_{n}(1/\overline{z})}
. These numbers are called the recurrence coefficients corresponding to
\mu
. The Szegő theorem states that
\sum_{n\geqslant 0}|\alpha_{n}|^{2}<\infty
if and only if
\mu
belongs to the Szegő class on
\mathbb{T}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\log\mu^{\prime}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, where
\mu^{\prime}
is the density of
\mu
with respect to the normalized Lebesgue measure
\mathop{\mathrm{m}}\nolimits
on
\mathbb{T}
. In this situation there exists an outer function
\Pi
in
\mathbb{D}
such that
\Pi(0)>0
and
{|\Pi(\zeta)|^{-2}=\mu^{\prime}(\zeta)}
for almost every
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
. The function
\Pi
is called the inverse Szegő function of
\mu
. Theorem 2.3.5 in
[
23
]
states that, for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
,
\Pi
satisfies the limit relation

(2.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)=\Pi(z)/\Pi(0).

2.2.
Nevai-Totik theorem

If
\mu=\mu^{\prime}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits
is an a. c. measure from the Szegő class on the unit circle let
r_{\Pi}
denote the radius of convergence of Taylor series of
\Pi
with center at
0
. Otherwise set
r_{\Pi}=1
. Nevai-Totik theorem, see Theorem 1 in the original paper by P. Nevai and V. Totik
[
19
]
or Chapter 7 in
[
23
]
, states

\displaystyle r_{\Pi}^{-1}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\alpha_{n}|^{1/n}.

When
r_{\Pi}=+\infty
, i.e., when
\mu
is a. c. from the Szegő class and
\Pi
is entire, Nevai-Totik theorem gives
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\alpha_{n}|^{1/n}=0
. In the next theorem we show that the order of
\Pi
can also be calculated in terms of the sequence
\alpha_{n}
. Theorem
1.1
can be considered as a version of this theorem for Krein systems.

Theorem 2.1
.

The following assertions are equivalent

(1)

the series
\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha_{n}z^{n}
defines an entire function of order
\rho
;

(2)

\mu
is a. c.  measure from the Szegő class and
\Pi
has an entire extension of order
\rho
.

Proof.

The proof is based on the relation between the order of the entire function and the asymptotic behaviour of its Taylor coefficients. Namely, let
f=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}f_{n}z^{n}
be an entire function then, see Lecture 1 in
[
17
]
, its order
\rho(f)
can be calculated by the formula

(2.4)

\displaystyle\rho(f)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\ln n}{-\ln|f_{n}|}.

By the Nevai-Totik theorem we already know that
\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha_{n}z^{n}
and
\Pi
are entire simultaneously. Hence further we can assume that both
\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha_{n}z^{n}
and
\Pi=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}c_{n}z^{n}
are entire and that
\mu
is a. c. from the Szegő class. Let us show that the orders
\rho_{\alpha}
and
\rho_{\Pi}
of these functions are equal.

First, we prove
\rho_{\Pi}\geqslant\rho_{\alpha}
. In the light of (
2.4
), we need to show

(2.5)

\displaystyle\rho=\rho_{\Pi}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\ln n}{-\ln|c_{n}|}\geqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\ln n}{-\ln|\alpha_{n}|}=\rho_{\alpha}.

If
\rho_{\Pi}=+\infty
this inequality is trivial and below we work with the case of finite
\rho_{\Pi}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
be the set of polynomials of degree not greater than
n
. Consider the minimization Christoffel function

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(z)=\lambda_{n}(\mu,z)=\inf\left\{\frac{\left\|P\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mu)}}{|P(z)|^{2}}\colon P\in\mathcal{P}_{n},\;P(z)\neq 0\right\},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

For the Christoffel function we have, see Chapter 2.2 in
[
23
]
,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(0)=\prod\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}(1-|\alpha_{k}|^{2}),\qquad\lambda_{\infty}(0)=\inf_{n}\lambda_{n}(0)=|\Pi(0)|^{-2}.

Let
h_{n}=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}^{n}c_{k}z^{k}
be the
n
-th Taylor polynomial of
\Pi
. We have
h_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
,
h_{n}(0)=\Pi(0)
and
d\mu(\zeta)=|\Pi(\zeta)|^{-2}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits
hence

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(0)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\left\|h_{n}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mu)}}{|h_{n}(0)|^{2}}=\frac{1}{|h_{n}(0)|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|h_{n}(\zeta)\right|^{2}d\mu(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Pi(0)|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|h_{n}(\zeta)\Pi^{-1}(\zeta)\right|^{2}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits(\zeta)=\frac{1}{|\Pi(0)|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|1+\frac{h_{n}(\zeta)-\Pi(\zeta)}{\Pi(\zeta)}\right|^{2}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Pi(0)|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}1+2\mathop{\rm Re}\left(\frac{h_{n}(\zeta)-\Pi(\zeta)}{\Pi(\zeta)}\right)+\left|\frac{h_{n}(\zeta)-\Pi(\zeta)}{\Pi(\zeta)}\right|^{2}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits(\zeta).

The function
\displaystyle\frac{h_{n}-\Pi}{\Pi}
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}
, therefore the second term vanishes after the integration. This implies

(2.8)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(0)\leqslant\frac{1}{|\Pi(0)|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}1

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{h_{n}(\zeta)-\Pi(\zeta)}{\Pi(\zeta)}\right|^{2}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits(\zeta)=\lambda_{\infty}(0)+\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|\frac{h_{n}(\zeta)-\Pi(\zeta)}{\Pi(\zeta)}\right|^{2}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits(\zeta).

For
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
we can write the uniform bound
\left|\Pi(\zeta)-h_{n}(\zeta)\right|\leqslant\sum_{m>n}|c_{m}|
. Formula (
2.4
) for
\Pi
implies that for every
\varepsilon>0
and large
n
the inequality
\rho+\varepsilon\geqslant n\ln n/(-\ln|c_{n}|)
holds. This is equivalent to
c_{n}\leqslant\exp\left({-\frac{n\ln n}{\rho+\varepsilon}}\right)
. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\left|\Pi(\zeta)-h_{n}(\zeta)\right|\leqslant\sum_{m>n}|c_{m}|\leqslant\sum_{m>n}e^{-\frac{m\ln m}{\rho+\varepsilon}}\lesssim e^{-\frac{n\ln n}{\rho+\varepsilon}}.

Moreover,
\Pi
is separated from
0
on
\mathbb{T}
, otherwise the assertion
\mu(\mathbb{T})<\infty
would fail. This means that the integral in (
2.8
) is
O\left(e^{-\frac{2n\ln n}{\rho+\varepsilon}}\right)
as
n\to\infty
. Then the relations in (
2.7
) give

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}(1-|\alpha_{k}|^{2})\leqslant\prod\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-|\alpha_{k}|^{2})+O\left(e^{-\frac{2n\ln n}{\rho+\varepsilon}}\right),\quad n\to\infty.

Therefore
|\alpha_{n}|=O\left(e^{-\frac{n\ln n}{\rho+\varepsilon}}\right)
as
n\to\infty
and (
2.5
) follows. This proves the inequality
\rho_{\Pi}\geqslant\rho_{\alpha}

The proof of the
\rho_{\alpha}\geqslant\rho_{\Pi}
is simpler and uses the same argument as in the proof of Theorem 1.1 from
[
24
]
.
For
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
we have

|\Phi_{n}^{*}(\zeta)|=|\zeta^{n}\overline{\Phi_{n}(1/\overline{\zeta})}|=|\Phi_{n}(\zeta)|
hence (
2.1
) implies

\displaystyle|\Phi_{n+1}(\zeta)|\leqslant|\zeta\Phi_{n}(\zeta)|+|\overline{\alpha_{n}}\Phi_{n}^{*}(\zeta)|=(1+|\alpha_{n}|)|\Phi_{n}(\zeta)|.

Inductively we deduce

\displaystyle|\Phi_{n}^{*}(\zeta)|=|\Phi_{n}(\zeta)|\leqslant\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1+|\alpha_{k}|)<\infty,\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{T}.

Therefore
z^{-n}\Phi_{n}
is bounded on
\mathbb{T}
uniformly in
n
. All
\Phi_{n}
are monic hence
z^{-n}\Phi_{n}(z)=1+o(1)
as
|z|\to\infty
. Now maximum modulus principle implies boundedness of
z^{-n}\Phi_{n}
in the domain
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{D}
. Therefore, by (
2.2
) we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\Phi_{n+1}^{*}(z)-\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)|=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|z\alpha_{n}\Phi_{n}(z)|\lesssim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|z^{n+1}\alpha_{n}|.

In particular, this means that
\Phi_{n}^{*}
converge on the compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
and (
2.3
) holds for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
.
Moreover, this gives the estimate
|\Pi(z)|\lesssim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|z^{n+1}\alpha_{n}|
. The inequality
\rho_{\Pi}\leqslant\rho_{\alpha}
follows and the proof is concluded.
∎

3.
Krein systems

Consider the Krein system (
1.1
), let
\sigma
be its spectral measure and
\Pi
be the corresponding inverse Szegő function. A simple calculation shows that for
r\geqslant 0
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle P(r,z)=e^{izr}\overline{P_{*}(r,\overline{z})},\qquad P_{*}(r,z)=e^{izr}\overline{P(r,\overline{z})}.

Furthermore, for every
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, the functions
P
,
P_{*}
satisfy the Christoffel-Darboux formula

(3.2)

\displaystyle P(r,\lambda)\overline{P(r,\mu)}-P_{*}(r,\lambda)\overline{P_{*}(r,\mu)}=i(\lambda-\overline{\mu})\int_{0}^{r}P(s,\lambda)\overline{P(s,\mu)}\,ds,

which is also proved by a straightforward calculation, see Lemma 3.6 in
[
8
]
. If we let
\mu=\lambda
then this becomes

(3.3)

\displaystyle|P_{*}(r,\lambda)|^{2}-|P(r,\lambda)|^{2}=2\mathop{\rm Im}\lambda\int_{0}^{r}|P(s,\lambda)|^{2}\,ds.

Krein theorem, see Section 8 in
[
8
]
or Section 3 in
[
26
]
, states that
\sigma
belongs to the Szegő class on the real line if and only if for every
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
we have
P(\cdot,\lambda_{0})\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. In this situation there exists a constant
\gamma\in[0,2\pi)
and a sequence of positive numbers
r_{n}\to\infty
such that the limit relation

(3.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{*}(r_{n},\lambda)=e^{i\gamma}\Pi(\lambda)=\Pi_{\gamma}(\lambda)

holds for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Convergence
\lim P_{*}(r,\lambda)=\Pi_{\gamma}(\lambda)
as
r\to\infty
takes place when
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, see Lemma
3.4
below.
Equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) together imply

(3.5)

\displaystyle|\Pi(\lambda)|^{2}=2\mathop{\rm Im}\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}|P(s,\lambda)|^{2}\,ds,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

Theorem 6.2 in
[
8
]
states that
|P_{*}(r,x)|^{-2}\,dx\to d\sigma(x)
in the weak -
\ast
sense. As a corollary of this convergence we get the following important lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
|P_{*}(r,x)|\to|\Pi(x)|
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}
then
\sigma
is absolutely continuous.

3.1.
Extremal problem and Christoffel functions

Let
PW_{[0,r]}
denote the Paley-Wiener space of entire functions
f
with the spectrum in
[0,r]
, in other words, the space of functions of the form
f=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\varphi
with
\varphi\in L^{2}([0,r])
.
Lemma 8.1 in
[
8
]
states that
PW_{[0,r]}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\sigma)
.
For
r>0
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
, define

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{r}(z)=\inf\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi|f(z)|}\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\sigma)}\colon f\in PW_{[0,r]},\;f(z)\neq 0\right\}.

The function
\mathbf{m}_{r}
is the analog of the Christoffel function
\lambda_{n}
from the theory of orthogonal polynomials, recall (
2.6
). Lemma 8.2 in
[
8
]
says

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{r}(z)=\left(\int_{0}^{r}|P(s,z)|^{2}\,ds\right)^{-1},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Moreover, the minimizer in (
3.6
) is unique up to the constant factor and is given by

(3.8)

\displaystyle k_{r,z}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{r}P(s,\lambda)\overline{P(s,z)}\,ds\in PW_{[0,r]}.

3.2.
Krein system with
L^{2}
coefficient

In the present paper we are interested in the case when the coefficient
a
of the Krein system (
1.1
) belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Three following results describe the properties of Krein system in this situation.

Theorem 3.2

(S. Denisov,
[
8
]
, Theorem 11.2)
.

If
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then
\sigma
belongs to the Szegő class on the real line,
\Pi
is well-defined and
\Pi_{\gamma}^{-1}=1+h
, where
h\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
is such that
\|h\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})}=\|a\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume that
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, the function
P_{*}(r,z)
is uniformly bounded for
r\geqslant 0
and
z
with
\mathop{\rm Im}z>\varepsilon
. Also for
z\in\mathbb{C}
we have

\displaystyle|P_{*}(r,z)|\leqslant\exp\left(\|a\|_{L^{1}([0,r])}+r(\mathop{\rm Im}z)_{-}\right)\lesssim\exp\left(r\|a\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}+r(\mathop{\rm Im}z)_{-}\right),

where
(x)_{-}
is the negative part of
x
, i.e.,
(x)_{-}=0
if
x\geqslant 0
and
(x)_{-}=-x
if
x<0
.

Proof.

The boundedness of
P_{*}
follows from Grönwall–Bellman inequality applied for the Krein system, for the details see the proof of Theorem 11.1 in
[
8
]
. Different application of Grönwall–Bellman inequality gives the bound

\displaystyle|P_{*}(r,z)|\leqslant\exp(\|a\|_{L^{1}([0,r])})=\exp\left(\|a\|_{L^{1}([0,r])}+r(\mathop{\rm Im}z)_{-}\right)

for
z
with
\mathop{\rm Im}z\geqslant 0
, see the proof of Theorem 12.1 in
[
8
]
. For
z
with negative imaginary part we can use the reflection formula (
3.1
) and the inequality
|P_{*}(r,z)|>|P(r,z)|
for

given by (
3.2
):

\displaystyle|P_{*}(r,z)|=\left|e^{izr}\overline{P(r,\overline{z})}\right|\leqslant e^{r(\mathop{\rm Im}z)_{-}}|P_{*}(r,\overline{z})|\leqslant\exp\left(\|a\|_{L^{1}([0,r])}+r(\mathop{\rm Im}z)_{-}\right).

The inequality
\|a\|_{L^{1}([0,r])}\leqslant\sqrt{r}\|a\|_{L^{2}([0,r])}\leqslant\frac{1+r}{2}\|a\|_{L^{2}([0,r])}

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

If
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then for some
\gamma\in[0,2\pi)
we have
\lim_{r\to\infty}P_{*}(r,\lambda)=\Pi(\lambda)
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

Proof.

Apply the Cauchy inequality to the differential equation for
P_{*}
and use the assertion
\|P\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}<\infty
from Krein theorem. We have

\displaystyle\|\frac{\partial}{\partial r}P_{*}(r,\lambda)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\leqslant\|a\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\|P(r,\lambda)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}<\infty

hence

converges as

. The limit coincides with
\Pi_{\gamma}
, recall (
3.4
).

∎

3.3.
Entropy function

Consider Krein system (
1.1
) with coefficient
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and let
J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
be the square root of the minus identity matrix and
Q=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-q&p\\
p&q\end{smallmatrix}\right)
be the matrix-valued function with
p(r)=-2\mathop{\rm Re}a(2r)
,
q(r)=2\mathop{\rm Im}a(2r)
. Krein system with the coefficient
a
is equivalent to the differential equation for the generalized eigenfunctions of the Dirac operator on the half-line

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{Q}=J\frac{d}{dr}+Q,

see Section 13 in
[
8
]
for the details. In particular, the spectral measure
\sigma_{a}
can be defined in terms of
D_{Q}
and the results for Krein systems, such as Theorem
1.1
, can be reformulated for the Dirac operator. When the inverse Szegő function of the Krein system or Dirac operator is entire one can speak of its zeroes – the scattering resonances, see the book
[
9
]
by S. Dyatlov and M. Zworski for the general theory. The exposition for specific case of the Dirac operator can be found in
[
15
]
, also see the references within. Theorem
1.1
allows us to study resonances of Dirac operators with oscillating potentials from
\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}
. Such studies will be presented elsewhere.

In the papers
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
R. Bessonov and S. Denisov described the class of canonical systems with the spectral measure from the Szegő class in terms of the so-called entropy function, also see
[
5
]
for the case of Dirac operators. Let us formulate this result on the language of Krein systems. Let
N_{a}
be the solution of

(3.9)

\displaystyle JN_{a}^{\prime}(r)+Q(t)N_{a}(r)=0,\quad N_{a}(0)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),\quad r\geqslant 0

and set

(3.10)

\displaystyle\qquad E_{a}(r)=\det\left[\int_{r}^{r+2}N_{a}^{*}(t)N_{a}(t)\,dt\right]-4.

Recall the definition (
1.3
) of the entropy function
\mathcal{K}_{a}
. We have
\mathcal{K}_{a}(0)<\infty
if and only if
\sigma_{a}
belongs to the Szegő class.

Theorem 3.5

(Theorem 1.2,
[
3
]
)
.

Assume that
a\in L^{1}_{\rm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and let
\sigma
be the spectral measure of the corresponding Krein system.
Then
\sigma
belongs to the Szegő class on the real line if and only if
\sum_{n\geqslant 0}E_{a}(n)<\infty
. More precisely, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{a}(0)\lesssim\sum_{n\geqslant 0}E_{a}(n)\lesssim\mathcal{K}_{a}(0)^{c\mathcal{K}_{a}(0)}

for some absolute constant
c
.

The paper
[
13
]
of the author is dedicated to the case when the series
\sum_{n\geqslant 0}E_{a}(n)
converges exponentially fast. When
E_{a}(r)=\varepsilon_{1}(r)
, Theorem 1.5 in
[
13
]
takes the following form.

Theorem 3.6

(Theorem 1.5,
[
13
]
)
.

Assume that
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then
E_{a}(r)=\varepsilon_{1}(r)
if and only if the spectral measure
\sigma
is a .c. and
\Pi
has an entire extension satisfying
\Pi(x-i\delta)/(x+i)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
for every
\delta>0
.

This theorem concerns an
\alpha=1
part of Theorem
1.1
. In Proposition
4.3
we show that the assertion
\Pi(x-i\delta)/(x+i)\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
is exactly the assertion
(B)
from Theorem
1.1
. Thus, the proof of Theorem
1.1
for
\alpha=1
requires the equivalence of
E_{a}(r)=\varepsilon_{1}(r)
and
a\in\mathcal{O}_{1}
. We formulate this in the following two results. Let
g_{a,r}(t)=\int_{r}^{t}a(s)\,ds
and define the variation of
a
by

(3.11)

\displaystyle D_{a}(r)=2\int_{r}^{r+2}|g_{a,r}(t)|^{2}\,dt-\left|\int_{r}^{r+2}g_{a,r}(t)\,dt\right|^{2}.

Theorem 3.7
.

If
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then
E_{a}(r)\approx D_{a}(r)
.

Theorem 3.8
.

If
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then
a\in\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}
if and only if
D_{a}(r)=\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(r)
.

R. Bessonov and S. Denisov, see
[
4
]
, established the connection between the entropy function and the Sobolev norm of the coefficient.

Theorem 3.9

(Theorem 4.1,
[
4
]
)
.

Assume that
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 0}E_{a}(n)\approx\|a\|_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|(\mathcal{F}a)(\xi)|^{2}}{1+\xi^{2}}\,d\xi,

where the quantity in the right-hand side is the definition of the norm in Sobolev space

and the constant in

.

Theorem
3.7
can be derived from the results in
[

but we give an independent proof. The proofs of Theorems

are mostly technical, we postpone them in the end of the present paper, Section

.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

4.1.
Equivalence of
(B)
and (
B’
)

To deal with the assertion
(B)
we need to examine the properties of the class
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
. From the definition (
1.4
) we see that
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\beta}
for
\alpha\geqslant\beta
. In particular,
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is the largest class. The following lemma will help us in showing that
\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha}
consists of entire functions.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function satisfying
f(r)=\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(r)
with
\alpha>1
. Let
g(x)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}f(n)e^{xn}
then
g(x)
is bounded for
x\leqslant 0
and there exists a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|g(x)|\lesssim\exp\left(c|x|^{\alpha^{*}}\right)
, where
\alpha^{*}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}
.

Proof.

When
x\leqslant 0
we have
|g(x)|\leqslant\sum_{n\geqslant 0}|f(n)|<\infty
because
f(n)=\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(n)
. Take
x>0
. From the definition of
\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}
we know that
f(n)\lesssim\exp(-n^{\alpha}/c_{1})
for some constant
c_{1}
. Hence we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 0}f(n)e^{xn}\lesssim\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\exp\left(-n^{\alpha}/c_{1}+xn\right).

Let
N_{0}=\left[\left(c_{1}(x+1)\right)^{1/(\alpha-1)}\right]+1
. Then for
n>N_{0}
we have
-n^{\alpha}/c_{1}+xn<-n
and

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant N_{0}}f(n)e^{xn}\lesssim\sum_{n\geqslant N_{0}}e^{-n}\lesssim 1.

On the other hand, if
n\leqslant N_{0}
then
-n^{\alpha}/c_{1}+xn<xN_{0}
hence

\displaystyle\sum_{n<N_{0}}f(n)e^{xn}\lesssim\sum_{n<N_{0}}e^{N_{0}x}\leqslant N_{0}e^{N_{0}x}.

The bound
N_{0}=O\left(x^{1/(\alpha-1)}\right)
as
x\to\infty
finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that
f\in\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha}
with some
\alpha\geqslant 1
. Then
f
has an entire continuation of order not greater than
\alpha^{*}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}
. Furthermore,
f
is bounded in every horizontal upper half-plane
\Omega_{\delta}=\{z\colon\mathop{\rm Im}z>-\delta\}
.

Proof.

let
\varphi=\mathcal{F}f
. We know that
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\varphi\subset{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
and
\int_{r}^{\infty}|\varphi(t)|^{2}\,dt=\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(r)
hence the integral
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(t)e^{izt}\,dt
converges for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
and defines an entire function. This entire function coincides with
f
on
\mathbb{R}
hence
f
is entire. Also we can write

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(t)e^{izt}\,dt\right|\leqslant\int_{0}^{\infty}|\varphi(t)|e^{-t\mathop{\rm Im}z}\,dt\leqslant\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\sqrt{\int_{n}^{n+1}e^{-t\mathop{\rm Im}z}\,dt}\sqrt{\int_{n}^{n+1}|\varphi(t)|^{2}\,dt}.

We have
\sqrt{\int_{n}^{n+1}|\varphi(t)|^{2}\,dt}=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(n)
and
\int_{n}^{n+1}e^{-t\mathop{\rm Im}z}\,dt\approx e^{-n\mathop{\rm Im}z}
hence the estimate of the order and the required boundedness follow from Lemma
4.1
.
∎

In other words,
\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\geqslant 1
consists of entire functions of order not greater than
\alpha^{*}
. We can formulate a different description of the class
\mathcal{S}_{1}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
f
be an entire function, then
f\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
if and only if
f\in H^{2}(\Omega_{\delta})
for every upper horizontal half-plane
\Omega_{\delta}=\{z\colon\mathop{\rm Im}z>-\delta\}
.

Proof.

Assume that
f
belongs to the Hardy space in
\Omega_{\delta}
for every
\delta>0
. Let
\varphi
be the Fourier transform of
f
and
\varphi_{\delta}
be the Fourier transform of
f(x-i\delta)
. Then for every
\delta>0
we have
\varphi_{\delta}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
\varphi_{\delta}=e^{\delta x}\varphi
. Therefore the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}e^{2\delta x}|\varphi^{2}(x)|\,dx
converges for every
\delta>0
, which is equivalent to
\int_{r}^{\infty}|\varphi(x)|^{2}\,dx=\varepsilon_{1}(r)
.

If
\int_{r}^{\infty}|\varphi(x)|^{2}\,dx=\varepsilon_{1}(r)
then
f(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(r)e^{irz}\,dr
, where the integral is absolutely convergent. This means
\|f\|_{H^{2}(\Omega_{\delta})}=\|f(x-i\delta)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|\varphi e^{\delta x}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}<\infty.

∎

In the light of Proposition
4.3
we can reformulate Theorem
3.6
in the following way.

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume that
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then
E_{a}(r)=\varepsilon_{1}(r)
if and only if
\sigma
is a. c. and for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
we have
\frac{\Pi-\Pi(z_{0})}{z-z_{0}}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
.

The description of the
\mathcal{S}_{1}
class given in Proposition
4.3
implies that the assertions
(B)
and (
B’
) of Theorem
1.1
are equivalent. In the following proposition we prove that the same is true for every
\alpha\geqslant 1
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
f
be an entire function and
\alpha\geqslant 1
. If the assertion
\frac{f-f(z_{0})}{z-z_{0}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
holds for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
then it holds for every
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

We have
\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha}\subset\mathcal{S}_{1}
hence
\frac{f-f(z_{0})}{z-z_{0}}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
. The characterization of
\mathcal{S}_{1}
from Proposition
4.3
implies
\frac{f-f(z_{1})}{z-z_{1}}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
for every
z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
. Let
\varphi=\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{f-f(z_{0})}{z-z_{0}}\right)
and
\psi=\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{f-f(z_{1})}{z-z_{1}}\right)
. We have
\varphi,\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and

(4.1)

\displaystyle f(z)=f(z_{1})+\frac{z-z_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(x)e^{izx}\,dx=f(z_{2})+\frac{z-z_{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(x)e^{izx}\,dx,

where the integrals are absolutely convergent for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Consider the functions

\displaystyle\Phi(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\varphi(x)e^{iz_{2}x}\,dx,\qquad\Psi(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\psi(x)e^{iz_{1}x}\,dx.

The proposition will follow from the equality

(4.2)

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\varphi(x)-i(z_{1}-z_{2})\Phi(x)e^{-iz_{2}x}.

Indeed, the assertion
\int_{r}^{\infty}|\varphi(t)|^{2}\,dt=\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(r)
implies
\Phi(t)=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(t)
by the integration by parts and the required
\int_{r}^{\infty}|\psi(t)|^{2}\,dt=\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(r)
then follows from (
4.2
).

Let us focus on (
4.2
). From (
4.1
) we get

\displaystyle f(z_{2})=f(z_{1})+\frac{z_{2}-z_{1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Phi(0),\qquad f(z_{1})=f(z_{2})+\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Psi(0),

(4.3)

\displaystyle\Psi(0)=\Phi(0)=\sqrt{2\pi}\frac{f(z_{2})-f(z_{1})}{z_{2}-z_{1}}.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(x)e^{izx}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(x)e^{iz_{2}x}\cdot e^{i(z-z_{2})x}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\Phi(x)e^{i(z-z_{2})x}\Big|_{0}^{\infty}+i(z-z_{2})\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi(x)\cdot e^{i(z-z_{2})x}\,dx

\displaystyle=\Phi(0)+i(z-z_{2})\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi(x)\cdot e^{i(z-z_{2})x}\,dx.

Similar transformation of
\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi(x)e^{izx}\,dx
in (
4.1
) gives

\displaystyle f(z_{1})

\displaystyle+\frac{\Phi(0)(z-z_{1})}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+\frac{i(z-z_{1})(z-z_{2})}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi(x)\cdot e^{i(z-z_{2})x}\,dx

\displaystyle=f(z_{2})+\frac{\Psi(0)(z-z_{2})}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+\frac{i(z-z_{1})(z-z_{2})}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi(x)\cdot e^{i(z-z_{1})x}\,dx.

Regrouping the terms, we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi}(f(z_{1})-f(z_{2}))

\displaystyle+z(\Phi(0)-\Psi(0))+(\Psi(0)z_{2}-\Phi(0)z_{1})

\displaystyle=i(z-z_{1})(z-z_{2})\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\Phi(x)e^{-iz_{2}x}-\Psi(x)e^{-iz_{1}x}\right)e^{izx}\,dx.

The left-hand side vanishes because of (
4.3
). Therefore we get
\Phi(x)e^{-iz_{2}x}-\Psi(x)e^{-iz_{1}x}=0
or
\Psi(x)=\Phi(x)e^{i(z_{1}-z_{2})x}
. By the definition of
\Phi
and
\Psi
we have
\Phi^{\prime}(x)=-\varphi(x)e^{iz_{2}x}
and
\Psi^{\prime}(x)=-\psi(x)e^{iz_{1}x}
. Taking the derivative in the previous equality, we get

\displaystyle-\psi(x)e^{iz_{1}x}=-\varphi(x)e^{iz_{2}x}\cdot e^{i(z_{1}-z_{2})x}+\Phi(x)\cdot i(z_{1}-z_{2})e^{i(z_{1}-z_{2})x},

which is equivalent to (
4.2
). The proof is finished.
∎

4.2.
Assertion
(D)
. Decaying solution of Krein system

In this subsection we prove that the assertion
(D)
of Theorem
1.1
implies assertions
(A)
and
(B)
and besides that gives other important information about
\Pi
. The following Lemma will be useful to us,

Proposition 4.6
.

Assume that
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
,
\alpha\geqslant 1
and
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
are such that
P(r,z_{0})=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(r)
. Then
\sigma
is a. c.,
a\in\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}
and
\Pi
has an analytic continuation into the whole complex plane such that
\Pi(\overline{z_{0}})=0
and
\frac{\Pi(z)}{z-\overline{z_{0}}}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Substitute
z_{0}
for
\mu
into the Christoffel-Darboux formula (
3.2
):

(4.4)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}P(s,\lambda)\overline{P(s,z_{0})}\,ds=i\frac{P_{*}(r,\lambda)\overline{P_{*}(r,z_{0})}-P(r,\lambda)\overline{P(r,z_{0})}}{\lambda-\overline{z_{0}}}.

We know that
|P(s,\lambda)|
is bounded by some exponential function in
s
by Proposition
3.3
and
P(s,z_{0})=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(s)
, hence the integral

(4.5)

\displaystyle F(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}P(s,\lambda)\overline{P(s,z_{0})}\,ds

converges absolutely for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and defines an entire function. In particular,
P(r,\lambda)\overline{P(r,z_{0})}\to 0
as
r\to\infty
. If
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
then from Lemma
3.4
we have
P_{*}(r,\lambda)\overline{P_{*}(r,z_{0})}\to\Pi_{\gamma}(\lambda)\overline{\Pi_{\gamma}(z_{0})}
as
r\to\infty
hence the right-hand side of (
4.4
) converges as
r\to\infty
and

\displaystyle F(\lambda)=\frac{i\Pi_{\gamma}(\lambda)\overline{\Pi_{\gamma}(z_{0})}}{\lambda-\overline{z_{0}}}=\frac{i\Pi(\lambda)\overline{\Pi(z_{0})}}{\lambda-\overline{z_{0}}},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

Therefore
\Pi(\lambda)=\frac{(\lambda-\overline{z_{0}})F(\lambda)}{i\overline{\Pi(z_{0})}}
is entire with
\Pi(\overline{z_{0}})=0
as claimed. Furthermore, for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
we get the limit relation

(4.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}P_{*}(r,\lambda)=\Pi_{\gamma}(\lambda).

Lemma
3.1
then implies that
\sigma
is absolutely continuous.
The estimate we used to establish the convergence of the integral in (
4.5
) is uniform in
\{\mathop{\rm Im}\lambda>-\delta\}
for every
\delta\geqslant 0
. Therefore
F
is bounded in every upper horizontal half-plane. In particular,
F
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
. Consider the set

(4.7)

\displaystyle M=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon|\Pi(x)|\leqslant 2\}.

We have
|F(z)|\lesssim|(z-\overline{z_{0}})^{-1}|
on
M
therefore
\|F\|_{L^{2}(M)}<\infty
. By Theorem
3.2
there exists
h\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
such that
\Pi_{\gamma}^{-1}=1+h
. If
x\notin M
then
|\Pi(x)|>2
and
|h(x)|>1/2
. Consequently, the Lebesgue measure of the set
\mathbb{R}\setminus M
is bounded by
\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
by the Chebyshev inequality. Therefore
\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus M)}\lesssim\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}<\infty
.
Hence
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and

\displaystyle\frac{\Pi(z)}{z-\overline{z_{0}}}=\frac{1}{i\Pi(z_{0})}F(z)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

To prove
\frac{\Pi(z)}{z-\overline{z_{0}}}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{S}}_{\alpha}
we need to show that
\mathcal{F}F
decays very rapidly.
We have

\displaystyle F(z)=\int_{0}^{r}P(s,z)\overline{P(s,z_{0})}\,ds+\int_{r}^{\infty}P(s,z)\overline{P(s,z_{0})}\,ds.

The first term is the function
k_{r,z_{0}}(z)\in PW_{[0,r]}
, recall (
3.8
). Let
f_{r}
be the inverse Fourier transform of the second term. We know that
f_{r}
and
\mathcal{F}F
coincide on
[r,\infty)
hence

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}^{-1}F\|_{L^{2}[r,+\infty)}=\|f_{r}\|_{L^{2}[r,+\infty)}\leqslant\|f_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\left\|\int_{r}^{\infty}P(x,z)\overline{P(x,z_{0})}\,dx\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

By the argument similar to the one we used to estimate
\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
we get

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{r}^{\infty}P(x,z)\overline{P(x,z_{0})}\,dx\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(r),\quad r\to\infty.

Let
M
be as in (
4.7
). The Lebesgue measure of
\mathbb{R}\setminus M
is finite hence

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{r}^{\infty}P(x,z)\overline{P(x,z_{0})}\,dx\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus M)}\lesssim\left\|\int_{r}^{\infty}P(x,z)\overline{P(x,z_{0})}\,dx\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=\varepsilon_{\alpha}(r).

On the other hand, on
M
we have
dz\lesssim|\Pi|^{-2}dz=d\sigma(z)
hence

. For

consider the functions
q_{r}(t)=\mathop{\rm Re}g_{a,r}(r+2t)
and
p_{r}(t)=\mathop{\rm Im}g_{a,r}(r+2t)
on
[0,1]
. We have
D_{a}(r)=4\mathbf{D}(p_{r})+4\mathbf{D}(q_{r})
. Hence
\mathbf{D}(p_{r})=\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(r)
and
\mathbf{D}(q_{r})=\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(r)
. In particular,
\mathbf{D}(p_{r}),\mathbf{D}(q_{r})\to 0
as
r\to\infty
.
Also we have
q_{r}^{\prime}(t)=2\mathop{\rm Re}a(r+2t)
and
p_{r}^{\prime}(t)=2\mathop{\rm Im}a(r+2t)
, therefore
\|p_{r}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}[0,1]},\|q_{r}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}[0,1]}\to 0
as
r\to\infty
. Lemma
5.1
then applies for
p_{r}
and
q_{r}
. It gives

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[r,r+2]}|g_{a,r}(s)|\leqslant\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|p_{r}(t)|+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|q_{r}(t)|\lesssim\mathbf{D}(p_{r})^{1/4}+\mathbf{D}(q_{r})^{1/4}=\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(r).

The assertion
a\in\mathcal{O}_{\alpha}
follows.
∎

5.2.
Ordered exponential. Reformulation of Theorem
3.7

Recall definition (
3.10
) of the entropy function
E_{a}
. The matrix
N_{a}
is a solution of
N_{a}^{\prime}(t)=JQ(t)N_{a}(t)
satisfying
N_{a}(0)=\mathcal{I}
, where
\mathcal{I}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix. Let us study this differential equation in more general form.

5.2.1.
Ordered exponential

Let
A
be a
2\times 2
matrix-valued function on
[0,1]
with entries from
L^{1}[0,1]
. Define
X_{A}
as the solution of

\displaystyle X_{A}^{\prime}(t)=A(t)X_{A}(t),\qquad X(0)=\mathcal{I}.

The matrix
X_{A}
is called the ordered exponential of
A
. It admits the following series representation:

(5.3)

\displaystyle X_{A}(t)=\mathcal{I}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}A(t_{1})\int_{0}^{t_{1}}A(t_{2})\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{m-1}}A(t_{m})\,dt_{m}\ldots dt_{3}\,dt_{2}\,dt_{1}.

Define the function
F_{A}
on
\mathbb{R}
and its Taylor coefficients
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
by

(5.4)

\displaystyle F_{A}(s)=\det\left(\int_{0}^{1}X_{sA}(t)X_{sA}^{T}(t)\,dt\right)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}a_{n}s^{n}.

Assume that
A
is of the form
A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-q&p\\
p&q\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where
p
and
q
are two functions from
L^{1}([0,1])
.
Let
J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, we have
J^{-1}=-\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
JAJ^{-1}=-A
.
Then

\displaystyle(JX_{A}(t)J^{-1})^{\prime}=JA(t)X_{A}(t)J^{-1}=(JA(t)J^{-1})(JX_{A}(t)J^{-1})=-A(t)JX_{A}(t)J^{-1},

hence
X_{-A}(t)=JX_{A}(t)J^{-1}
for every
t
. From formula (
5.4
) we see
F_{A}(s)=F_{-A}(s)
for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
. We also have
F_{-A}(s)=F_{A}(-s)
hence
F
is even and

a_{n}=0
when
n
is odd. Recall that for a function
f\in L^{2}([0,1])
we use the notation

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}(f)=\int_{0}^{1}f(s)^{2}\,ds-\left(\int_{0}^{1}f(s)\,ds\right)^{2}.

Lemma 5.2
.

We have
a_{2}=4\mathbf{D}(g_{p})+4\mathbf{D}(g_{q})
, where
g_{p}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}p(x)\,dx
,
g_{q}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}q(x)\,dx
.

Proof.

The proof is a calculation. We have

\displaystyle X_{sA}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}+s\int_{0}^{t}A(t_{1})\,dt_{1}+s^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}A(t_{1})A(t_{2})\,dt_{2}\,dt_{1}+o(s^{2}),

\displaystyle X_{sA}(t)X_{sA}(t)^{T}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}+s\int_{0}^{t}A(t_{1})\,dt_{1}+s\int_{0}^{t}A^{T}(t_{1})\,dt_{1}+s^{2}\int_{0}^{t}A(t_{1})\,dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}A^{T}(t_{1})\,dt_{1}

\displaystyle+s^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}A(t_{1})A(t_{2})\,dt_{2}\,dt_{1}+s^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}(A(t_{1})A(t_{2}))^{T}\,dt_{2}\,dt_{1}+o(s^{2}).

Since
g_{p}
and
g_{q}
are antiderivatives of
p
and
q
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}A(t_{1})\,dt_{1}=\int_{0}^{t}A^{T}(t_{1})\,dt_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}-g_{q}(t)&g_{p}(t)\\
g_{p}(t)&g_{q}(t)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}A(t_{1})\,dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}A^{T}(t_{1})\,dt_{1}=(g_{p}^{2}(t)+g_{q}^{2}(t))\mathcal{I}.

Next, we write

\displaystyle A(t_{1})A(t_{2})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-q(t_{1})&p(t_{1})\\
p(t_{1})&q(t_{1})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-q(t_{2})&p(t_{2})\\
p(t_{2})&q(t_{2})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}q(t_{1})q(t_{2})+p(t_{1})p(t_{2})&-q(t_{1})p(t_{2})+p(t_{1})q(t_{2})\\
q(t_{1})p(t_{2})-p(t_{1})q(t_{2})&q(t_{1})q(t_{2})+p(t_{1})p(t_{2})\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore
A(t_{1})A(t_{2})+(A(t_{1})A(t_{2}))^{T}=2\big(q(t_{1})q(t_{2})+p(t_{1})p(t_{2})\big)\mathcal{I}
. Also we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}q(t_{1})q(t_{2})\,dt_{2}dt_{1}=\frac{g_{q}(t)^{2}}{2},\quad\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}p(t_{1})p(t_{2})\,dt_{2}dt_{1}=\frac{g_{p}(t)^{2}}{2},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}A(t_{1})A(t_{2})+(A(t_{1})A(t_{2}))^{T}\,dt_{2}\,dt_{1}=(g_{q}^{2}(t)+g_{p}^{2}(t))\mathcal{I}.

Hence we have

\displaystyle X_{sA}(t)X_{sA}(t)^{T}=\mathcal{I}+2s\begin{pmatrix}-g_{q}(t)&g_{p}(t)\\
g_{p}(t)&g_{q}(t)\end{pmatrix}+2s^{2}(g_{q}^{2}(t)+g_{p}^{2}(t))\mathcal{I}+o(s^{2}).

Integrating and taking the determinant, we get

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=-4\left(\int_{0}^{1}g_{p}(t)\,dt\right)^{2}-4\left(\int_{0}^{1}g_{q}(t)\,dt\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+4\int_{0}^{1}g_{p}(t)^{2}\,dt+4\int_{0}^{1}g_{q}(t)^{2}\,dt=4\mathbf{D}(g_{p})+4\mathbf{D}(g_{q}).

∎

5.2.2.
Reformulation of Theorem
3.7

We see that the function
N_{a}
from (
3.9
) is an ordered exponential of the matrix function
JQ(t)
. Definitions (
3.10
) and (
5.4
) of
E_{a}
and
F_{JQ}
are similar, the only difference is the length of the integration segment. On
[0,1]
define
A_{r}(t)=2JQ(r+2t)
. Then we have

\displaystyle(N_{a}(r+2t))^{\prime}=2N_{a}^{\prime}(r+2t)=2JQ(r+2t)N_{a}(r+2t)=A_{r}(t)N_{a}(r+2t)

hence
X_{A_{r}}(t)=N_{a}(r+2t)
and

\displaystyle F_{A_{r}}(1)=\det\left(\int_{0}^{1}X_{A_{r}}(t)X_{A_{r}}^{T}(t)\,dt\right)=\det\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{r}^{r+2}N_{a}(t)N_{a}^{T}(t)\,dt\right)=\frac{1}{4}E_{a}(r)+1.

Thus, if we want to estimate
E_{a}
we can work with
F_{A_{r}}(1)-1
. We have
A_{r}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-q_{r}&p_{r}\\
p_{r}&q_{r}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
where
p_{r}=4\mathop{\rm Re}a(r+2t)
and
q_{r}(t)=4\mathop{\rm Im}a(r+2t)
, this follows from the definition of
A_{r}
and
Q=Q_{a}
. Hence
D_{a}(r)=\mathbf{D}(g_{p})+\mathbf{D}(g_{q})
.
Therefore
Theorem
3.7
follows from the following result.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
A
be a matrix-valued function on
[0,1]
of the form
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-q&p\\
p&q\end{smallmatrix}\right)
with
p,q\in L^{2}([0,1])
. Let
g_{p}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}p(x)\,dx
and
g_{q}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}q(x)\,dx
be the antiderivatives of
p
and
q
respectively. Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}(g_{p})+\mathbf{D}(g_{q})=\varepsilon,\qquad\|p\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}+\|q\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}=\delta.

Define
F_{A}
and
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
as in (
5.4
) then
\sum_{n\geqslant 4}|a_{n}|=o(\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon,\delta\to 0
and consequently
F_{A}(1)=1+a_{2}+o(\varepsilon)=1+4\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon,\delta\to 0
.

We will see from the proof of the theorem that the numbers

decay very fast and
F_{A}(s)=1+4\varepsilon s^{2}+o(\varepsilon)
holds for every

.

5.3.
Diagonal case

When the matrix
A(t)
is diagonal, the numbers
a_{n}
can be calculated explicitly and Theorem
5.3
can be proved very shortly.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
A(t)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-q&0\\
0&q\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is diagonal and
g(t)=\int_{0}^{t}q(s)\,ds
then we have

\displaystyle a_{n}=\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(g(x)-g(y))^{n}\,dx\,dy,\qquad n\geqslant 0.

Proof.

For
s\in\mathbb{R}
we can write

\displaystyle X_{sA}(t)=\exp\left(s\int_{0}^{t}A(x)dx\right)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-sg(t)}&0\\
0&e^{sg(t)}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle F_{A}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}e^{2sg(t)}\,dt\cdot\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2sg(t)}\,dt.

Expanding the Taylor series of the exponential, we get

\displaystyle F_{A}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{(2s)^{k}g^{k}(t)}{k!}\,dt\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{m\geqslant 0}\frac{(-2s)^{m}g^{m}(t)}{m!}\,dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(2s)^{k+m}}{k!m!}\int_{0}^{1}g^{k}(t)\,dt\int_{0}^{1}g^{m}(t)\,dt.

Changing the order of summation, we get the explicit formula for
a_{n}
:

\displaystyle a_{n}=2^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{l}}{l!(n-l)!}\int_{0}^{1}g^{n-l}(t)\,dt\int_{0}^{1}g^{l}(t)\,dt=\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(g(x)-g(y))^{n}\,dx\,dy.

In particular, we see that
a_{n}=0
if
n
is odd and

\displaystyle a_{2}=2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(g(x)-g(y))^{2}\,dx\,dy=4\int_{0}^{1}g(x)^{2}\,dx-4\left(\int_{0}^{1}g(x)\,dx\right)^{2}=4\mathbf{D}(g),

which is consistent with Lemma
5.2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.3
in the diagonal case.

From the formula established for
a_{n}
in Lemma
5.4
we get

\displaystyle|a_{n}|\leqslant\frac{2^{n-1}|a_{2}|}{n!}\sup_{x,y\in[0,1]}\left|g(x)-g(y)\right|^{n-2}\leqslant\frac{2^{n-1}\cdot 4\varepsilon}{n!}\delta^{n-2},

because
a_{2}=4\varepsilon
by Lemma
5.2
and
|g(x)-g(y)|\leqslant\|q\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\leqslant\|q\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}=\delta
.
The estimate
\sum_{n>2}{a_{n}}=o(\varepsilon)
follows.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&p\\
p&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
or, more generally, when

and

commute for almost every

.

5.4.
Auxiliary results

The proof of Theorem

in general situation requires technical details.
To simplify the exposition we introduce the notation

(5.5)

\displaystyle(f_{1}\ldots f_{n})_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{n-1}}f_{1}(t_{1})\ldots f_{n}(t_{n})\,dt_{n}\ldots\,dt_{1},

where we assume

and

. The following lemma will be used to bound terms with large indexes in (
5.3
).

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in L^{2}([0,1])
be real-valued functions on
[0,1]
and
F_{k}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f_{k}(s)\,ds
be their antiderivatives for
k=1,\ldots,n
. Assume that each
F_{k}
satisfies assertions from (
5.1
). Let
\gamma
be as in Lemma
5.1
then we have

\displaystyle|(f_{1}\ldots,f_{n})_{t}|\leqslant(8\gamma)^{m},\qquad m=[(n+1)/2].

If
n
is even and
f_{2i-1}=f_{2i}
for some
1\leqslant i\leqslant n/2
then the same inequality holds with
m=n/2+1
.

Proof.

Assume that
n
is odd,
n=2m-1
. We can change the order of integration so that

\displaystyle(f_{1}\ldots f_{n})_{t}=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\,dt_{2}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\,dt_{4}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{2m-2}}\,dt_{2m}\left(\prod_{l=1}^{m-1}f_{2l}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{m}\int_{t_{2k}}^{t_{2k-2}}f_{2k-1}\,dt_{2k-1}\right),

where
t_{0}=t
and
t_{2m}=0
.
For every
1\leqslant k\leqslant m
, by Lemma
5.1
, we have

(5.6)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{t_{2k}}^{t_{2k-2}}f_{2k-1}(t_{2k-1})\,dt_{2k-1}\right|=|F_{2k-1}(t_{2k-2})-F_{2k-1}(t_{2k})|\leqslant 8\gamma.

Therefore we can write

\displaystyle\left|\prod_{k=1}^{m}\int_{t_{2k}}^{t_{2k-2}}f_{2k-1}(t_{2k-1})\,dt_{2k-1}\right|\leqslant 8^{m}\gamma^{m},\qquad\left|(f_{1}\ldots f_{n})_{t}\right|\leqslant 8^{m}\gamma^{m}\prod_{l=1}^{m-1}\|f_{2l}\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}.

To finish the proof in the case of odd
n
notice that
\|f_{2l}\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}=\|F_{2l}^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\leqslant\delta<1
by (
5.1
). When
n=2m
is even we proceed similarly: take the outer integrals over
t_{2},t_{4},\ldots,t_{2m}
and the inner over
t_{1},t_{3},\ldots,t_{2m-1}
.

To obtain sharper inequality for the situation when
n
is even and
f_{2i-1}=f_{2i}=f
we let the outer integrals be over the variables
t_{2},t_{4}\ldots,t_{2i-2}
and
t_{2i+1},\ldots t_{2m-1}
. The product in the inner integral then becomes

\displaystyle\prod_{l=1}^{i-1}f_{2l}(t_{2l})

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{l=i}^{m-1}f_{2l+1}(t_{2l+1})\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{i-1}\int_{t_{2k}}^{t_{2k-2}}f_{2k-1}(t_{2k-1})\,dt_{2k-1}

\displaystyle\times\int_{t_{2i+1}}^{t_{2i-2}}\int_{t_{2i+1}}^{t_{2i-1}}f(t_{2i})f(t_{2i-1})\,dt_{2i}\,dt_{2i-1}\cdot\prod_{k=i+1}^{m}\int_{t_{2k+1}}^{t_{2k-1}}f_{2k}(t_{2k})\,dt_{2k}.

We see that there is only one new integral. We have

\displaystyle\int_{t_{2i+1}}^{t_{2i-2}}\int_{t_{2i+1}}^{t_{2i-1}}f(t_{2i})f(t_{2i-1})\,dt_{2i}\,dt_{2i-1}=\frac{(F(t_{2i-2})-F(t_{2i+1}))^{2}}{2},

which is not greater than
32\gamma^{2}
by Lemma
5.1
. To conclude the proof we use the same bound as in (
5.6
).
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
f,g\in L^{2}([0,1])
and
F(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(x)\,dx
,
G(t)=\int_{0}^{t}g(x)\,dx
. Assume that
F
and
G
satisfy the assertions in (
5.1
). Then we have

\displaystyle|(ffgg)_{t}|\leqslant 160\gamma^{4},\qquad|(fgg)_{t}|\leqslant 11\gamma^{3},\qquad|(ffg)_{t}|\leqslant 79\gamma^{3}.

Proof.

These bounds are sharper than the bounds in Lemma
5.6
in the degree of
\gamma
. Let us proceed more carefully. We have

\displaystyle(ffgg)_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})\cdot\left[\int_{t_{3}}^{t}\int_{t_{3}}^{t_{1}}f(t_{1})f(t_{2})\,dt_{2}\,dt_{1}\right]\cdot\left[\int_{0}^{t_{3}}g(t_{4})\,dt_{4}\right]\,dt_{3}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})\cdot\frac{(F(t)-F(t_{3}))^{2}}{2}\cdot G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{F(t)^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}-F(t)\int_{0}^{t}F(t_{3})g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}

(5.7)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}F^{2}(t_{3})g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}.

Let
C_{F}=\int_{0}^{1}F(x)\,dx
and
C_{G}=\int_{0}^{1}G(x)\,dx
. Lemma
5.1
gives

(5.8)

\displaystyle|C_{F}|\leqslant 2\gamma,\qquad\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|F(t)|\leqslant 4\gamma,\qquad\|F^{2}-C_{F}^{2}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\leqslant 6\varepsilon^{1/2}\gamma,

(5.9)

\displaystyle|C_{G}|\leqslant 2\gamma,\qquad\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|G(t)|\leqslant 4\gamma,\qquad\|G^{2}-C_{G}^{2}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\leqslant 6\varepsilon^{1/2}\gamma.

For the first integral in (

) we have

\displaystyle\frac{F(t)^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}=\frac{F(t)^{2}G(t)^{2}}{4},

\displaystyle\left|\frac{F(t)^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}\right|\leqslant 64\gamma^{4}.

Furthermore, rewrite

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}F(t_{3})g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}

\displaystyle=C_{F}\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}+\int_{0}^{t}(F(t_{3})-C_{F})g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{F}G(t)^{2}}{2}+\int_{0}^{t}(F(t_{3})-C_{F})g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}.

Inequalities in (
5.1
) imply
\|F-C_{F}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\leqslant\varepsilon^{1/2}
and
\|g\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\leqslant\delta
. Use this, Hölder inequality, the bounds from (
5.8
), (
5.9
) and
\varepsilon^{1/2}\delta\leqslant\gamma^{2}
to get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}F(t_{3})g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{|C_{F}G(t)^{2}|}{2}+\|F-C_{F}\|_{L^{2}}\|g\|_{L^{2}}\|G\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(2\gamma)\cdot(4\gamma)^{2}}{2}+\varepsilon^{1/2}\cdot\delta\cdot 4\gamma\leqslant 20\gamma^{3}.

Therefore, the estimate for the second term in (
5.7
) is

\displaystyle\left|\frac{F(t)}{2}\int_{0}^{t}F(t_{3})g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}\right|\leqslant 40\gamma^{4}.

Similarly, for the third integral we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}F^{2}(t_{3})g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}

\displaystyle=C_{F}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}+\int_{0}^{t}(F(t_{3})^{2}-C_{F}^{2})g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3},

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}F^{2}(t_{3})g(t_{3})G(t_{3})\,dt_{3}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{|C_{F}^{2}G(t)^{2}|}{2}+\|F^{2}-C_{F}^{2}\|_{L^{2}}\|g\|_{L^{2}}\|G\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(2\gamma)^{2}\cdot(4\gamma)^{2}}{2}+6\varepsilon^{1/2}\gamma\cdot\delta\cdot 4\gamma\leqslant 56\gamma^{4}.

Now the inequality
|(ffgg)_{t}|\leqslant 160\gamma^{4}
follows from (
5.7
). The inequalities for
(fgg)_{t}
and
(ffg)_{t}
are less technical. We have

\displaystyle(fgg)_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}f(t_{1})\left[\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}g(t_{2})g(t_{3})\,dt_{3}\,dt_{2}\right]\,dt_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}f(t_{1})G(t_{1})^{2}\,dt_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{G}^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}f(t_{1})\,dt_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}f(t_{1})(G(t_{1})^{2}-C_{G}^{2})\,dt_{1},

\displaystyle|(fgg)_{t}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C_{G}^{2}|F(t)|+\|f\|_{L^{2}}\|G^{2}-C_{G}^{2}\|_{L^{2}}}{2}\leqslant\frac{(2\gamma)^{2}\cdot 4\gamma+\delta\cdot 6\varepsilon^{1/2}\gamma}{2}\leqslant 11\gamma^{3}.

For
(ffg)_{t}
we write

\displaystyle(ffg)_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})\left[\int_{t_{3}}^{t_{1}}\int_{t_{3}}^{t}f(t_{1})f(t_{2})\,dt_{2}\,dt_{1}\right]\,dt_{3}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})(F(t)-F(t_{3}))^{2}\,dt_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{F(t)^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})\,dt_{3}-F(t)\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})F(t_{3})\,dt_{3}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})F(t_{3})^{2}\,dt_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{F(t)^{2}G(t)}{2}-F(t)\left[C_{F}\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})\,dt_{3}+\int_{0}^{t}g(t_{3})(F(t_{3})-C_{F})\,dt_{3}\right]+(gff)_{t}.

This gives
|(ffg)_{t}|=\frac{(4\gamma)^{2}\cdot 4\gamma}{2}+4\gamma[2\gamma\cdot 4\gamma+\delta\varepsilon^{1/2}]+11\gamma^{3}\leqslant 79\gamma^{3}
.
