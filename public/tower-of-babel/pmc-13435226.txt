RiSpy: a feature selection-based fingerprinting framework for accurate identification of genome-edited rice lines
Authors: Amin Zolfaghari, Marie-Alice Fraiture, Kevin Vanneste, Arno Stuyts, Julien Frouin, Anne-Cécile Meunier, Dieter Deforce, Nancy H C Roosens, Jolien D’aes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe European Union (EU) enforces strict regulations on the traceability and labeling of genetically modified organisms (GMOs), including genome-edited (GE) lines produced through new genomic techniques (NGTs). Identifying GE organisms created by single nucleotide variations (SNVs) is however challenging, as a single SNV alone cannot unambiguously define a GE line.Recently, we introduced the concept of generating a genetic fingerprint to distinguish a specific GE rice line. This proof-of-concept approach integrated whole-genome sequencing (WGS)-based characterization with the Illumina technology, the public 3 K Rice Genomes (3KRG) database, and statistical feature-selection tools, to select and combine key genetic elements, including GE on-target site(s) and cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes, into a unique genetic fingerprint.In the present study, we expand this concept into a generalized data-driven framework allowing identification of multiple rice lines. Supported by newly developed bioinformatics and statistical feature-selection-based pipelines, this optimized strategy enables the generation of genetic fingerprints irrespective of a rice cultivar’s inclusion in publicly available databases like 3KRG. In addition, this refined strategy can leverage WGS data generated from both Illumina and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) platforms for fingerprint generation and GE line identification. Using two distinct in-house GE rice lines from different cultivars, along with various publicly available WGS datasets, we demonstrated the robustness, scalability, and specificity of this approach for reliable GE rice line identification. Our findings provide a methodological foundation for data-driven traceability of GE rice lines, reinforcing regulatory compliance, supporting intellectual property (IP) protection, and contributing to the responsible implementation of EU GMO/NGT legislation.Keywords: genetic fingerprint, genome editing, statistical feature-selection, whole-genome sequencing (WGS), genetically modified organisms (GMOs), new genomic techniques (NGTs)

Introduction

The European Union (EU) enforces strict legislation on genetically modified organisms (GMOs) [1], including genetically modified (GM) food and feed products [2], as well as their import and market distribution. These regulations aim to protect the safety of food, feed, and the environment while ensuring consumer freedom of choice. In July 2018, the European Court of Justice (ECJ) ruled in Case C-528/16 that organisms produced via directed mutagenesis techniques, such as clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) [3, 4] and base editing (BE) [5], a CRISPR-derived approach that enables precise single-nucleotide changes without double-strand breaks or homology-directed repair, fall under the definition of GMOs according to Directive 2001/18 EC [6]. This decision clarified that new genomic techniques (NGTs) are subject to the same risk assessments, traceability, and labeling obligations as conventional GMOs, in which foreign genetic elements are introduced into the genome of the target variety [7]. Therefore, robust detection methods for genome-edited (GE) lines are needed to ensure compliance with traceability and labeling requirements [8, 9]. Beyond regulatory compliance, accurate identification of GE lines also provides critical support to biotechnology developers by protecting intellectual property (IP) associated with novel GE varieties and enabling responsible monitoring of proprietary lines.

While NGTs enable precise genome editing, identifying the resulting GE lines can be especially difficult when the modifications are minimal, and not unambiguously distinguishable from natural variations or from modifications introduced by techniques not regulated under EU GMO legislation [10], such as EMS (ethyl methanesulfonate) modifications. The nature of the genetic edits introduced by NGTs can vary considerably among GE lines; some carry multiple modifications, such as several single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and/or combinations of insertions and deletions (indels), while others may contain only a single, precise edit [11]. When multiple SNPs/indels are present, detecting the presence of a GE line can be done with relatively high confidence, as the likelihood of the same combination of edits occurring naturally at the exact same genomic positions is extremely low. Even so, such cases still remain technically demanding, requiring dedicated method development, as the nature of these edits differs fundamentally from those in conventional GMOs that involve the insertion of foreign genetic elements [12]. For GE lines where the genetic modification involves only a single SNP or a small indel, confidently distinguishing the GE line from natural variations becomes more difficult because such subtle changes could potentially arise spontaneously in non-GE lines or already exist in different cultivars, making it challenging to determine whether the mutation was introduced naturally or through targeted editing [13–15].

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based methods are the gold standard for the detection and unambiguous identification of conventional GMOs [11]. Recently it was shown that such PCR strategies can be used to detect a single nucleotide variant (SNV) of a GE line according to EU standards [11] and therefore can serve as a useful preliminary screening tool for detecting GE lines, particularly when prior knowledge of the target sequence exists. However, PCR-based approaches alone are not always optimal for unambiguously identifying GE lines. Detecting only the on-target site introduced by genome editing is not sufficient to confirm that a given variant was produced by an NGT rather than occurring naturally or through other non-NGT methods. Consequently, targeting additional genomic regions is required to accurately identify and confirm the target line, for which (q)PCR might not be the most well-suited technique. Therefore, while (q)PCR can support sample prioritization and quantification of GM events, sequencing-based approaches, such as whole-genome or targeted deep sequencing, are of added value to obtain sufficient sequence context for the accurate identification of GE lines carrying single or multiple modifications [13, 15].

To address previous limitations in GE line identification, we recently developed a novel strategy integrating whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data, public databases, and statistical feature-selection [10]. In a proof-of-concept study, this approach enabled the creation of a unique genetic fingerprint for a CRISPR-Cas9-edited Nipponbare rice line carrying a single indel. The fingerprint combined the on-target modification with cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes, comprising 145 combinations of 99 unique SNVs. While this initial study demonstrated the feasibility of identifying a GE line with high confidence, it was restricted to a single cultivar represented in public databases and relied exclusively on short-read sequencing and PCR-based detection [4, 12].

Building on this initial proof of concept [10], the present study generalizes the concept into a universal, data-driven framework for GE rice line identification. Here, we explored WGS as a single, integrative strategy compatible with both short- and long-read platforms, Illumina and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT). Three key genetic elements were considered: (i) the on-target modification introduced by NGTs [16–18], (ii) potential off-target modifications [19, 20], and (iii) cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes. The most stringent scenario, detection of a single-SNV edit, was used to evaluate the method’s robustness. This methodology is generally applicable to GE and wild-type (WT) rice cultivars, including those absent from public databases. By integrating short- and long-read sequencing technologies with statistical feature-selection–based fingerprint construction, we established a robust two-stage pipeline for generating the cultivar specific 2-SNV barcodes of a given GE rice line and for detecting these unique 2-SNV barcodes. Although additional work is needed to define the minimal 2-SNV barcode set for regulatory applications, our findings provide a methodological framework for accurate and traceable GE rice line identification, thereby strengthening traceability, regulatory compliance, intellectual property (IP) protection, and establishes a solid foundation for responsible innovation of GE lines together with the freedom of choice of EU consumers.

Results

In the present study, we developed an optimized workflow for the generation and detection of genetic fingerprints from Illumina and ONT WGS data (Fig. 1). This workflow includes quality control of WGS data, alignment to the reference genome and germline variant calling using two filtering strategies: a stringent filter applied to generate high-confidence 2-SNV barcodes for fingerprinting and a more lenient variant filter to evaluate barcode specificity, ensuring that the fingerprints were unique to the cultivar of interest and absent from other cultivars.

Workflow used in this study for generation and detection of genetic fingerprints from WGS data. Steps involved in fingerprint generation, fingerprint detection, and those shared between both processes are shown in blue, yellow, and black, respectively.

To challenge the workflow’s fingerprint generation and detection capabilities, we evaluated it using two case studies. The first involved a CRISPR-Cas9-edited Oryza sativa Japonica Nipponbare carrying an indel, on chromosome 8 at position 685,053, an insertion of an adenine (A), absent from the known O. sativa gene pool [10]. Based on our analysis, the difference between the reference Nipponbare genome used in the 3KRG dataset and our in-house WGS Nipponbare data is ~0.05% in the 160 K high quality SNP positions. The second case study examined a BEnCas9-edited O. sativa Japonica Kitaake line (KitGE) containing a single SNP (C/G to T/A), on chromosome 10 at position 22,451,559, that is naturally found in O. sativa Indica but absent from O. sativa japonica gene pool. The Kitaake cultivar is also not represented in the 3KRG database.

High-quality variant profiles could be generated from both Illumina and ONT-based whole-genome sequencing data

For both fingerprint generation and detection, germline variant calling was performed for all Kitaake and Nipponbare samples (Table 1). The total number of variants was substantially lower in Nipponbare samples compared to Kitaake samples, which was expected since the Nipponbare reference genome, the most complete rice reference genome available, was used for the read alignment of all samples. Additionally, across both cultivars, the total number of variants detected using the ONT platform was higher than in the Illumina datasets. A region-specific analysis of the variants uniquely identified by ONT and Illumina was conducted (data not shown), revealing that ONT outperformed in detecting variants located near indels and within complex genomic regions, such as repetitive sites.

A comparison of variants across WT and GE lines to identify shared and unique variations was performed (Supplementary Data, Fig. S1). First, for each Kitaake and Nipponbare cultivar, variants shared among all WT and GE lines were extracted to determine the common variants within each cultivar resulting from the shared genetic background of the used line. Next, for each of the two cultivars, variants present in the GE line but absent in the WT line were extracted to define those unique to the GE lines of each cultivar (Supplementary Data, Fig. S1). For 2-SNV barcode generation in the Kitaake rice lines, only SNVs consistently observed across all Kitaake wild-type (KitWT) replicates were retained to ensure cultivar specificity. Overall, GE lines generally exhibited more variants than their WT counterparts for both Kitaake and Nipponbare. An exception was observed in the KitGE ONT dataset, which showed fewer variants than KitWT, likely due to lower breadth of coverage in this sample (Table 1).

Optimization and extension of the genetic fingerprint generation framework to cultivars not represented in the 3KRG database

Based on the recently developed genetic fingerprint for a CRISPR-Cas9 GE O. sativa L. japonica Nipponbare line (NipGE), where the available Nipponbare WT (NipWT) genotype was used from the 3KRG database [10], we aimed to test whether a similar strategy, enhanced with novel WGS data, could be used to generate a fingerprint for the Kitaake cultivar. Since Kitaake is not included in the 3KRG database, this approach allowed to assess if a fingerprint could be generated without existing reference data. The resulting fingerprint was based on investigation of the main genetic elements described by Fraiture et al. (2025) [10], namely the on-target site, possible off-target sites, and cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes. To generate 2-SNV barcodes, genotype reconstruction was first performed to obtain high-confidence variant profiles from the sequencing data. This was followed by a feature selection step to identify informative SNVs suitable for distinguishing between cultivars. Finally, an exhaustive screening of all possible 2-SNV combinations was conducted to identify robust and unique 2-SNV barcodes.

Off-target modifications could not be detected

The presence of off-target CRISPR-Cas9 effects was assessed as one of the potential components of the genetic fingerprint for the identification of a GE line. Potential off-target sites were identified based on the PAM and protospacer sequences used for the BEnCas9 edit. In total, 24,704 potential off-target sites were computationally predicted for KitGE using sequence similarity to the BEnCas9 guide RNA. These regions were then screened using the set of KitGE-specific variants to identify mutations matching the specific nucleotide change introduced at the on-target site. Four such SNVs were detected in the ONT data, but none were observed in the Illumina datasets (Supplementary Data, Table S1). Further examination showed that all four ONT-specific SNVs were located within repetitive regions. However, since these candidate off-target SNVs were not confirmed in the Illumina WGS data, we opted to exclude them from the fingerprint.

Cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes generated based on an in-house WGS-based genotype

As Kitaake is not included in 3KRG, its variant profile was initially unknown. Therefore, a novel pipeline was implemented to generate cultivar-specific barcodes based on variant profiles derived from WGS data. The pipeline first established the Kitaake genotype at the 160 K SNP positions present in 3KRG, using Illumina and/or ONT WGS data, and then generated cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes following the strategy described in Fraiture et al. (2025) [10].

To assess the robustness and cross-platform applicability of the 2-SNV barcode generation approach, Kitaake-specific 2-SNV barcodes were generated using three different strategies: (i) variants called from the Illumina dataset, (ii) variants called from the ONT dataset, and (iii) variants shared between the Illumina and ONT platforms (Table 2). For generating the Kitaake-specific 2-SNV barcodes from the Illumina platform, two KitWT replicates were used (Table 1). Stringent filtering was applied to the shared variants in the two KitWT samples to reduce false positives as well as false negatives when identifying the Kitaake genotype across 160 K SNP positions in the 3KRG dataset.

Using the high-confidence SNP positions from the Illumina platform, Kitaake-specific 2-SNV barcodes were generated from 280 unique SNVs, producing 1453 barcodes, each consisting of a pair of SNVs that could uniquely distinguish Kitaake from other rice accessions (Table 2; Supplementary Data, Table S11). The same approach was applied to KitWT data from the ONT platform, resulting in 2018 2-SNV barcodes generated from 361 unique SNVs (Table 2; Supplementary Data, Table S12). Despite using the same filtering approach for variant quality and sufficient read depth, the ONT data yielded 340 more variants within the 160 K SNP positions used for 2-SNV barcode generation to the Illumina based dataset. Consequently, the ONT dataset based 2-SNV barcode set contains a substantially higher number of 2-SNV barcodes than the Illumina based dataset.

Subsequently, the same 2-SNV barcode generation approach was applied using only high-quality variants shared between both Illumina and ONT datasets to ensure robust detection across platforms. Although this strategy reduced the total number of generated 2-SNV barcodes, it increased the stringency of variant selection, thereby helping to minimize the risk of false-positive 2-SNV barcodes. This method generated 902 2-SNV barcodes containing 214 unique SNVs (Table 2; Supplementary Data, Table S13).

Each set of 2-SNV barcodes generated using these three approaches could cover at least 23 out of the 24 rice chromosome arms (Fig. 2), i.e. 23 arms when using Illumina data and the shared variants between both platforms, and all 24 arms when using only the ONT data, demonstrating the comprehensive representation of the genetic background achieved by these 2-SNV barcodes.

A comparison of the barcodes generated from each approach highlighted that a total of 730 2-SNV barcodes were common across all three approaches (Supplementary Data, Fig. S2). In addition, 123 2-SNV barcodes were found to be unique to the approach based on shared variants. This uniqueness can be explained by the feature selection process in the fingerprint generation pipeline, where the top 500 highest-ranked SNPs were selected. Although the SNVs forming these 123 2-SNV barcodes were present in both Illumina and ONT datasets, they did not rank among the top 500 SNPs when each platform was analyzed independently. Instead, platform-specific SNPs with higher rankings were selected in the Illumina- and ONT-based approaches, excluding these 123 shared 2-SNV barcodes.

Genetic fingerprints allow robust and specific detection of rice lines from both in-house and public WGS data

In this study, genetic fingerprints for the KitGE and KitWT lines were successfully generated using WGS data from multiple sequencing platforms, while an Illumina-based genetic fingerprint was previously designed for the NipGE and NipWT lines by Fraiture et al. (2025) [10]. These fingerprints can be used as unique identifiers to unambiguously identify the intended GE line. To assess the performance of this detection strategy, we evaluated whether the previously generated fingerprints for NipGE and NipWT and the fingerprints for KitGE and KitWT generated in the present study, could be reliably detected in WGS data from pure rice lines. This included in-house samples of Kitaake and Nipponbare sequenced using both Illumina and ONT technologies, as well as publicly available GE (distinct from the in-house GE line) and WT samples from both cultivars.

Prior to presenting the results, it is crucial to highlight the distinction between the fingerprint generation described in section 2.2 and the fingerprint detection addressed in this section (Fig. 1). In the previous section, three sets of fingerprints were generated through strict filtering of variants from Illumina, ONT, and variants shared between these two datasets. In contrast, this section focuses on detection of these fingerprints across various in-house and publicly available WGS datasets generated using both Illumina and ONT platforms, including both WT and GE lines, for which more lenient variant filtering criteria were applied.

Fingerprints were successfully detected in in-house samples

To evaluate the detection performance and technical reproducibility of the generated fingerprints, we tested in-house samples derived from the same lines used earlier for fingerprint generation (see section 2.2.2). High detection rates were expected in these samples, as they originated from the same lines employed to develop the fingerprints.

First, the ability of the Illumina and ONT variant-calling pipelines to detect the on-target modification was assessed in all KitGE and NipGE samples. The on-target modifications were successfully detected in all cases, and were confirmed to be present only in their respective GE lines and absent from their WT counterparts.

Subsequently, all GE and WT samples (Table 1) were evaluated to assess whether the generated cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes could be reliably detected across different sequencing platforms. Overall, our results demonstrated that all three sets of Kitaake-specific 2-SNV barcodes were detected at high rates across all samples and sequencing technologies (Fig. 3; Supplementary Data, Tables S2–S4). For all in-house samples sequenced using either the Illumina or ONT platform, the vast majority (>96%) of barcodes were successfully detected, demonstrating the cross-compatibility of fingerprints generated by both sequencing technologies. Overall, the average detection rates for barcodes generated using Illumina, ONT, and both platforms combined, were 99.59%, 99.36%, and 99.37%, respectively. The results suggest that barcodes generated using the Illumina platform exhibited a slightly higher detection rate compared to ONT, although the difference was minimal and primarily attributable to the lower read quality of ONT at those positions. However, no substantial improvement in detection rate was observed for 2-SNV barcodes generated from variants shared between both platforms. In all cases where a small proportion of 2-SNV barcodes remained undetected, this was attributed to variant filtering thresholds applied during fingerprint detection, such as minimum variant quality or read depth cutoffs. Specifically, the SNVs constituting these barcodes met the required quality and depth criteria in the replicates used for barcode generation, but in some of the other replicates used for detection of 2-SNV barcodes, a small proportion of SNVs failed to meet these thresholds.

As our previous study [10] employed only targeted Illumina sequencing for fingerprint detection, the Nipponbare-specific barcodes were assessed for detectability using WGS Illumina and ONT data. This analysis demonstrated that 100% of the Nipponbare cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes could be detected in all NipWT and NipGE samples (Supplementary Data, Table S5). Finally, none of the Kitaake 2-SNV barcodes were found in Nipponbare samples and none of the Nipponbare 2-SNV barcodes were detected in Kitaake samples, confirming the specificity of this approach.

Fingerprints were successfully detected in publicly available samples

To further assess the robustness and the general applicability of the fingerprinting approach including the generated cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes, multiple Kitaake and Nipponbare WGS datasets were downloaded from NCBI for an independent performance validation. Some NCBI projects included multiple replicates of the same line; the corresponding SRA IDs are provided in the Supplementary Data, Tables S6–S9. These samples were selected from different projects on NCBI to ensure broad representation of the Kitaake and Nipponbare backgrounds, encompassing multiple lines. Both GE and WT samples were included for each cultivar, with WGS data generated using Illumina and ONT platforms. Several GE lines were analyzed, which differed from the in-house NipGE and KitGE lines (Fig. 4). These GE lines were therefore characterized using cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes identified in this study, combined with their corresponding introduced on-target modification.

The Kitaake 2-SNV barcode sets were derived from Illumina and ONT sequencing data, as well as the set of shared variants between the two platforms (Illumina+ONT), while the Nipponbare 2-SNV barcode set was derived from Illumina data [10].

First, the on-target GE sites were successfully detected in the Nipponbare and Kitaake mutant samples for which GE site information was publicly available. In the Nipponbare line, the on-target edit was located on chromosome 3 at position 29 303 125 (GC to G). In the Kitaake line, on-target edits were detected on chromosome 4 at positions 23 362 019 (A to G) and 23 362 021 (A to G) of the same line. Consistent with previous studies [21, 22], no off-target effects were identified in those Kitaake and Nipponbare samples.

Second, across all Kitaake datasets, the vast majority of the generated 2-SNV barcodes could be detected (Fig. 4). Overall, an average of 99.95%, 99.15%, and 99.99% of 2-SNV barcodes generated from Illumina, ONT, and shared variants between both platforms, respectively, were detected in Kitaake WGS public data. Similar to the in-house datasets, 2-SNV barcodes generated on the Illumina platform could be identified in Kitaake lines sequenced with ONT, and conversely, 2-SNV barcodes from ONT sequencing were detectable in Illumina data.

For Nipponbare samples, the detection rate ranged from 47% to 100%. The lower detection rates observed in some samples were due to low depth and breadth of coverage of the WGS datasets, as detailed in the supplementary data (Table S9). Consequently, the majority of missing 2-SNV barcodes in the public data resulted from low variant quality scores (below 20 and 10 for Illumina and ONT data, respectively) and inadequate sequencing depth (below 5×). Additionally, a small proportion of 2-SNV barcodes was missing because the specific SNVs were absent. In some cases, the reference allele was present at the expected variant position instead of the alternative allele. These positions were manually inspected using IGV to ensure the missing SNVs were not due to limitations in our variant calling pipeline, confirming that the reference alleles were indeed present instead of the alternative alleles.

To further assess the specificity of the approach, nine negative control samples from Basmati, Aus, Indica, and Japonica cultivars were included from different NCBI projects. These downloaded WGS datasets were originally generated using Illumina or ONT technology (Supplementary Table S10). As expected, the analysis revealed that no Kitaake nor Nipponbare 2-SNV barcodes were detected in any of these samples. Furthermore, since the on-target BEnCas9-edited SNV of the KitGE line was known to naturally occur in Indica cultivars [23], we verified the presence of this SNV in the Indica samples. All Indica samples from both Illumina and ONT platforms were found to carry a natural mutation identical to the on-target BEnCas9-edited site of the KitGE line, highlighting the need for a cultivar-specific marker in addition to GE-site detection to identify a GE line with high confidence.

Sequencing quality thresholds for confident line identification

Based on the data presented in Supplementary Table S14 available online at http://bib.oxfordjournals.org/, we evaluated fingerprint detection performance across different sequencing depths for cultivars sequenced in-house using both Illumina and ONT platforms. Across platforms, these results indicate that an average sequencing depth of ≥11×, combined with ≥98% breadth of coverage and a Fold-80 base penalty ≤2, is sufficient for reliable detection of both SNP- and indel-based GE events. At this recommended depth, the pipeline consistently identified the on-target edit in over 95% of replicates (corresponding to the LOD95 threshold) and detected more than 79% of the 2-SNV barcodes.

To further validate that an average sequencing depth of 11× is sufficient for reliable fingerprint detection, we generated 2-SNV barcodes for 10 additional accessions from the 3KRG database and downloaded their corresponding WGS datasets from NCBI. These datasets were then down-sampled to 11× (Supplementary Table S15). Across all independent accessions, between 66% to 95% of cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes were successfully recovered, confirming that the recommended depth is adequate for robust fingerprint detection in diverse samples. Furthermore, we calculated the false-positive rate for these cultivars. With the exception of two cases, no false-positive 2-SNV barcodes were detected. The first case had a very low false-positive rate, corresponding to 0.46% Bhadoia 2-SNV barcodes detected in Pankhari. The second case had an even lower rate, where 0.06% Poenoet Hitam barcode was incorrectly identified in PANKHARI. Overall, these values are extremely low, and the vast majority of barcodes correctly identified their corresponding cultivars.

Discussion

The EU enforces strict regulations on the traceability and labeling of GMOs, including GE plants generated through NGTs. Consequently, robust analytical frameworks capable of distinguishing GE lines from conventionally bred or WT varieties, are essential to ensure regulatory compliance, market transparency, and consumer protection. However, the suitability and effectiveness of identification strategies vary substantially depending on the type and complexity of the underlying genetic modifications.

Large-scale insertions or deletions facilitate GE line identification because the likelihood of such precise events arising naturally is extremely low. In contrast, one of the most challenging scenarios arises when GE lines differ from their WT counterparts by only a single SNP or a small indel. In such cases, detecting the on-target edit alone is insufficient to confirm the GE nature of the line, as the same SNV may naturally occur in other rice accessions. This underscores the need to integrate additional key genetic elements to achieve confident identification.

The genetic fingerprinting concept combines two critical components: the GE-specific elements such as the on-target [5] and potential off-target modifications, and cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes. Together, these elements form a robust genetic fingerprint capable of distinguishing even single-SNV GE rice lines. However, developing accurate cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes requires access to a comprehensive genotype database that enables the identification of unique SNVs distinguishing the target cultivar [10]. While the 3KRG database provides extensive genomic data for 3024 accessions [24] spanning five major rice varietal groups, indica, aus/boro, basmati/sadri, tropical japonica, and temperate japonica, not all rice accessions are represented [25].

In this study, we expanded the concept of genetic fingerprinting into a generalized, data-driven framework for the identification of GE rice lines. Importantly, the framework was designed to generate de novo fingerprints directly from raw WGS data, without reliance on pre-existing genotype information from public resources such as 3KRG. This capability represents a key advance over previous approaches and substantially broadens applicability to uncharacterized or proprietary cultivars lacking reference genotypes. Beyond this, we have extended our framework to support cross-platform interoperability, allowing fingerprints generated from Illumina data to be reliably detected using ONT data and vice versa. This cross-technology compatibility is critical for real-world deployment, where sequencing platforms may vary between laboratories. Furthermore, through down-sampling and LOD95 analyses, we have established a practical threshold for minimum sequencing depth required for robust detection (~11×) and demonstrated scalability though evaluation of 10 additional cultivars. This was provided as an openly available command-line tool (RiSpy) that allows other researchers to perform fingerprint detection.

As a case study, a O. sativa L. japonica Kitaake line carrying a BEnCas9-introduced SNV was used to demonstrate the feasibility of reconstructing a rice genotype that is not represented in the 3KRG database, which can subsequently be used to generate cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes. Importantly, this edited line (KitGE) harbors a mutation that occurs naturally in the Indica gene pool, illustrating the strength of a cultivar-based 2-SNV barcode approach for distinguishing engineered variants from naturally occurring polymorphisms. Therefore, even when the edited SNV is shared across multiple varietal groups, the pipeline can still unambiguously identify the correct cultivar through its associated barcode profile. This illustrates the framework’s ability to distinguish engineered variants from naturally occurring polymorphisms. Additionally, the workflow supports the use of WGS data from different platforms, i.e., Illumina and ONT, for the fingerprint generation and detection, illustrating a flexible and scalable approach to GE rice traceability.

To evaluate the performance and robustness of this framework, we first generated and analyzed high-quality variant profiles from both Illumina- and ONT-based WGS data, which form the foundation for fingerprint construction. Due to ONT’s ability to span repetitive regions and structural variants that shorter Illumina reads may miss, the ONT dataset yielded a larger pool of variants, an observation consistent with the findings of Hall et al [26]. Subsequently, the obtained variant profiles were employed to generate the genetic fingerprint by examining the three key genetic components described by Fraiture et al. (2025) [10]: the modified on-target site, potential off-target effects, and the cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes. Regarding the potential off-target effects, although modern CRISPR-Cas9-based systems are designed to minimize unintended edits [27], off-target alterations were anticipated in the KitGE line due to the use of an older version of the CRISPR-Cas9-based system [28]. Indeed, four candidate off-target SNVs were exclusively identified with ONT WGS. Their absence in the Illumina dataset potentially reflects the ONT’s superior ability to resolve complex genomic regions [26]. This observation was consistent with previous studies demonstrating that long-read sequencing technologies improve the detection of Cas9 off-target effects, particularly in repetitive or structurally challenging regions [29]. However, experimental validation of these candidate sites was not feasible, due to the highly repetitive nature of their genomic context. Consequently, in the absence of supporting evidence, these potential off-target variants were excluded from the genetic fingerprints of Kitaake.

Next, the variant profiles were employed to generate the second component of the genetic fingerprint, i.e. the cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes that were combined with the on-target edit to generate the genetic fingerprint [10, 30, 31]. As ONT sequencing yielded a higher number of variants, it produced more 2-SNV barcodes than Illumina, and these 2-SNV barcodes spanned all chromosomal arms.

As a second major analytical step, the performance of the generated genetic fingerprints was assessed for their capacity to accurately identify the target rice lines. The fingerprint detection pipeline was applied to Kitaake and Nipponbare WGS datasets generated using both Illumina and ONT sequencing platforms. The on-target modifications in in-house NipGE and KitGE lines as well as in public datasets downloaded from NCBI, were successfully detected by the identification pipeline using either Illumina or ONT WGS data, underscoring the robustness of this approach even for single-nucleotide edits. With respect to the detection of the cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes, the vast majority of 2-SNV barcodes generated using Illumina and/or ONT datasets were successfully detected in in-house samples. This was anticipated, as the same line was used for 2-SNV barcode generation, confirming the reliability of the approach for cultivar-specific variant detection. Consequently, different replicates from the same line were expected to exhibit high concordance. To further evaluate performance, multiple public datasets from different Bioprojects (Supplementary Data, Tables S6–S9) were analyzed to assess detection rates in other Kitaake and Nipponbare lines. Together, these results support broader applicability of the framework across diverse datasets. However, full validation, particularly in routine wet-lab settings, will require testing on independently generated samples across the entire experimental and bioinformatics workflow. Overall, detection rates were consistently high across all lines. For Kitaake, the 2-SNV barcodes generated from variants shared between Illumina and ONT data exhibited slightly higher detection rates in public datasets, although detection for Illumina-only and ONT-only fingerprints also exceeded 99% on average, demonstrating the robustness of this statistical feature-selection-based approach across both sequencing platforms. It was initially hypothesized that the integrated 2-SNV barcode set derived from variants common to short- and long-read data, would enhance the reliability due to the stricter selection criterion. However, all three 2-SNV barcode sets showed high detection rates, indicating that platform-specific barcodes were already highly reliable.

Cross-platform analysis confirmed that 2-SNV barcodes generated on one platform were reliably detected on the other, showing the method accurately identifies the intended line regardless of sequencing technology.

Also for the public datasets of Nipponbare, the vast majority of the 2-SNV barcodes were detected, except for two lines showing lower detection rates of ~47% and 60%. These missed 2-SNV barcodes were attributable to low sequencing depth (median depths of coverage of 3 and 5), (Supplementary Data, Table S9). This confirms that sequencing quality and depths of coverage play important roles in fingerprint detection, while it also indicates that line identification remains feasible even at relatively low sequencing depths of coverage. Low sequencing breadth of coverage can also reduce 2-SNV barcode detection. Nevertheless, partial 2-SNV barcode recovery based on enough barcodes can enable discrimination, and our analysis further establishes that reliable detection of the on-target site, which is essential for NGT line identification, requires an average sequencing depth of ~11× with ≥98% breadth of coverage and a Fold-80 base penalty ≤2. At this coverage level, we are also able to detect the vast majority of the 2-SNV barcodes, ensuring robust fingerprint identification. These analyses provide practical guidance for future implementation and constitute an important step toward method standardization.

Although individual 2-SNV barcodes are theoretically unique, reliance on a single marker is insufficient for robust cultivar-specific identification. Indeed, one 2-SNV barcode may be shared by varieties not represented in the 3KRG database, and genetic variation can exist between lines of the same cultivar. For example, some public datasets labeled as Kitaake showed missing SNVs compared with the in-house Kitaake used for fingerprint generation. Therefore, the use of multiple independent barcodes introduces redundancy, significantly reducing the risk of false positives and enhancing the reliability of cultivar identification.

No Kitaake 2-SNV barcodes were detected in Nipponbare samples and vice versa, underscoring the specificity of the generated 2-SNV barcodes. In addition, negative control samples from diverse rice varieties, including Basmati, Aus, Indica, and Japonica, were incorporated into the analysis using public data from various Bioprojects and both short- and long-read sequencing platforms. The results showed that 2-SNV barcodes generated for Kitaake and Nipponbare were detected exclusively in their respective cultivars, with 0% detection in non-Kitaake and non-Nipponbare samples. In the broader false-positive analysis including 10 additional cultivars, the false-positive rate remained very low (<0.5%), demonstrating the high specificity of our approach (Supplementary Data, Table S10). Indica cultivars naturally carry the same mutation introduced by CRISPR–Cas9 in KitGE; however, none of the Kitaake-specific 2-SNV barcodes were detected in these samples, confirming that they are not KitGE. This highlights the importance of combining cultivar-specific barcodes with GE site information for accurate line identification. Overall, the workflow provides a flexible approach for the generation as well as robust, sensitive, and highly specific detection of rice genetic fingerprints, provided that the type and position of the on-target edit as well as the genetic background of the rice line are known.

Despite the potential of the proposed approach, some challenges remain. Specifically, implementation and interpretation of these approaches require basic familiarity with command-line tools which may limit its adoption by regulatory enforcement laboratories and other non-specialist stakeholders. Moreover, the current pipeline is currently optimized for pure, single-cultivar rice samples, and its application to mixed or contaminated samples is not yet supported. Additionally, extending the framework to polyploid genomes represents an important future direction, which will involve incorporating ploidy-aware adaptations in variant calling and barcode detection. We further acknowledge that the feature-selection step is influenced by the inherent class imbalance in the training data; however, independent public-dataset analyses demonstrated that the resulting 2-SNV barcodes are robust and support accurate identification. Moreover, the formal definition of the minimal number of 2-SNV barcode required for regulatory standardization remains unresolved, and addressing this limitation will require systematic benchmarking across substantially larger and more diverse germplasm collections. Additionally, rice, a diploid and highly inbred species, was employed as the case study, and the current pipeline is optimized for diploid, homozygous species with well-characterized public databases to support statistical feature-selection-based 2-SNV barcode generation. Although the workflow is currently optimized for rice, its application to other crops is expected to become increasingly feasible, as many species are now the focus of ongoing efforts to generate comprehensive reference databases. However, many important crop species are polyploid and/or cross-pollinating, and adapting the approach to these species will require considerable modifications to the pipeline. Hence, the broader significance of this study lies in establishing a general framework for sequence-based fingerprinting of GE plants. We consider this work an important step toward the development of future traceability and enforcement methodologies for NGT products, an area where validated detection and identification tools remain scarce.

Fingerprint generation is essential for developers submitting NGT-derived plants, as EU GMO legislation (Regulation (EC) 1829/2003 and 1830/2003) mandates that any genetically modified plant be accompanied by a method allowing its specific identification. Establishing a genetic fingerprint enables traceability, and provides a reliable record of the intended modifications. To ensure that detection remains possible, developers should provide WGS data and information on NGT modifications, which should be made accessible to competent authorities and enforcement laboratories. Sensitive detection of these fingerprints is equally critical for enforcement laboratories, as it enables affective monitoring for regulatory compliance, distinguish GE lines from conventional varieties, and maintain transparency across agricultural sectors, including the organic sector where the absence of genetically modified materials remains a fundamental principle.

At the time of writing, the European Council reached a provisional agreement on a new legal framework for NGT plants, introducing a distinction between different categories of NGT-derived organisms. Under this framework, NGT-edited lines carrying a limited number of genomic changes and/or modifications considered equivalent to those that could occur naturally or through conventional breeding are classified as Category 1. Certain exceptions, such as traits related to herbicide tolerance or the production of insecticidal substances, are proposed to be regulated and subject to authorisation, traceability and monitoring [32]. Nevertheless, even for Category 1 NGT plants, traceability obligations may persist for specific purposes, including labeling of seeds and other plant reproductive material, post-market monitoring, and coexistence measures. In this context, the availability of molecular fingerprints remains highly relevant, particularly for ensuring transparency and choice for operators along the value chain and for safeguarding the integrity of organic production systems, which may continue to require reliable analytical tools to verify compliance with organic standards irrespective of the regulatory status of Category 1 NGT plants.

By integrating cultivar-specific 2-SNV barcodes with information on the on-target NGT edits, even subtle modifications can be accurately identified, supporting both regulatory submissions and enforcement. Our study demonstrated that this approach can reliably detect NGT-derived lines, even the most challenging rice lines characterized by the presence of only one or a few SNVs, thus establishing a scalable, regulatory-relevant strategy for tracing, detecting, and identifying NGT derived plants in diverse agricultural and food systems.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

For the O. sativa L. japonica Kitaake line, both WT (KitWT) and GE lines using BEnCas9 (KitGE) were analyzed [23, 28, 33]. The KitGE line, which is not commercially available and was generated by our project partner CIRAD, carries a homozygous C-to-T single nucleotide variant (SNV) in the OsNRT1.1b gene. This line was edited using CRISPR-Cas9 with the protospacer sequence 5′-CGGCGACGGCGAGCAAGTGG-3′ and the PAM motif 5′-NGG-3′. This edit enhances nitrogen use efficiency in the GE line relative to its WT counterpart.

For the O. sativa L. japonica Nipponbare cultivar, as described by Fraiture et al. (2025) [10], a CRISPR-Cas9 GE line (NipGE, generated by our project partner CIRAD) and its parental line (NipWT) were used. The Nipponbare GE line, also not commercially available, contains a homozygous single adenosine insertion in the OsMADS26 gene. This edit was introduced using CRISPR-Cas9 with a protospacer sequence of 5′-GAACCGGGTTCTCGATGCGA-3′ and a PAM sequence of 5′-NGG-3′. Genomic DNA from all rice seeds was extracted and assessed as previously described [10].

Whole-genome sequencing

WGS was performed using Illumina and ONT platforms. For short-read sequencing, two technical replicates of KitWT and two KitGE were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 system in 150 bp paired-end mode, according to the manufacturer’s instructions at NXTGNT (Belgium). For NipGE and NipWT, Illumina data was already available from our previous study (BioProject accession: PRJEB84921 and PRJNA1370649) [10].

For long-read sequencing, DNA libraries were prepared using the ligation sequencing kit V14 (SQK-LSK114; Oxford Nanopore Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each DNA library was loaded on an individual R10.4.1 flow cell to be sequenced for 72 h using the GridION platform at Sciensano. Four flow cells for KitGE, five flow cells for KitWT, four flow cells of NipWT and five flow cells of NipGE were run. Generated sequencing data were then basecalled using Dorado (v0.8.1) with the duplex sup model (v5.0.0).

Sequencing reads from each flow cell were aligned to the Nipponbare reference genome and subsequently merged into single high-coverage BAM files for both KitGE and KitWT. The quality metrics are presented in Table 1. Following alignment, reads from the NipWT flow cells were merged into a single high-coverage sample, as were reads from the NipGE flow cells (Table 1).

Germline variant calling pipeline

Two bioinformatics pipelines were developed to process Illumina and ONT sequencing data, respectively. Each pipeline performs quality control, read alignment, calculation of depth and breadth of coverage, Fold80 calculation, variant calling, and variant filtering.

Quality control

For quality control of Illumina data, FastQC (v0.11.7) [34] was employed, while NanoPlot (v1.41.6) [35] was used for ONT samples. Following the initial quality assessment, low-quality reads were trimmed based on the quality control results. For Illumina data, Trimmomatic (v0.39) [36] was used to remove adapter sequences, overrepresented sequences, and low-quality bases using the following parameters: LEADING:10, TRAILING:10, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20, and MINLEN:40. For ONT data, Chopper (v0.8.0) [35] was applied with a quality cutoff of 10 and a minimum read length of 150 bp. The 150 bp length threshold for ONT reads was chosen to match the read length of Illumina data, ensuring comparable minimum read lengths across platforms. After trimming, the quality of the reads was reassessed using FastQC and NanoPlot for Illumina and ONT data, respectively, to confirm effective trimming and minimal loss of reads.

Alignment to Nipponbare reference genome

The rice reference genome (O. sativa cv. Nipponbare, RefSeq Accession GCF_034140825.1) [37] was used for alignment. For Illumina data, the reference genome was indexed using BWA (v0.7.17) [38] and SAMtools (v1.21) [39], while for ONT data, Minimap2 (v2.28) [40] and SAMtools were used. Illumina reads were aligned to the reference genome using bwa mem with the parameters -K 100000000 -M. For ONT data, alignment was performed with Minimap2 using the parameters -a -x ‘map-ont’. Subsequently, the SAM files were converted to BAM format and indexed using SAMtools for downstream analysis.

To assess the quality of read alignments and calculate coverage metrics, Qualimap (v2.2.2) [41] was used with the bamqc module and the parameter —java-mem-size = 100G for both Illumina and ONT datasets. To evaluate genome-wide coverage, SAMtools (v1.21) [39] depth was executed with the -H -a options, where the -a flag ensured that genomic positions with zero coverage were included. The generated depth output was then used to calculate the mean and median coverage values for each sample in Python. To determine the breadth of coverage, a custom Python script was used to analyze the SAMtools depth output, applying a depth threshold of 20× to calculate the proportion of the genome covered at or above this level. Additionally, SAMtools view with parameters -c -F 260 was used to count only the primary alignments in each BAM file, excluding secondary and supplementary alignments. As a final step in BAM file quality assessment, another in-house Python script was used to calculate the Fold-80 base penalty, providing insight into the uniformity of sequencing coverage. This calculation utilized the coverage_histogram.txt file generated by Qualimap. All quality metrics for these datasets are provided in Table 1.

Germline variant calling

BAM files from multiple ONT sequencing runs were merged to improve coverage, whereas Illumina runs were kept separate. Following this assessment, variant calling was performed using DeepVariant (v1.8.0) [42], which applies a deep-learning model to identify SNPs and indels with high accuracy, with the —model_type = WGS parameter applied for Illumina data and —model_type = ONT_R104 for ONT data. Post-calling, BCFtools (v1.21) [43] was used to filter out low-quality variants. For Illumina data, variants with a quality score below 20 and a depth below 5 were excluded. For ONT data, a more lenient quality cutoff of 10 was applied, while maintaining the same depth threshold of 5.

Detection of off-target effects

To investigate potential off-target effects of CRISPR-Cas9 editing in the KitGE lines, we conducted a genome-wide prediction and screening analysis, following the strategy described by Fraiture et al. (2025) [10].

Cas-OFFinder (v3.0.0) [44] was used on a GPU-enabled server to predict potential off-target sites. The analysis was performed using both DNA and RNA bulge sizes set to 2, a maximum of 3 mismatches, and the PAM sequence defined as 5′-NRG-3′ (where N represents any nucleotide and R represents A or G). The guide RNA protospacer sequence used as input was 5′-CGGCGACGGCGAGCAAGTGG-3′.

Following the prediction of off-target sites, an in-house Python script was used to screen the WGS variant data for the presence of the same SNV as the on-target CRISPR-Cas9 edit at these loci.

Generation of cultivar specific 2-SNV barcodes

The generation of a cultivar specific 2-SNV barcodes for the Kitaake line was performed following a similar statistical feature-selection approach described by Fraiture et al. (2025) [10], enhanced by the use of WGS data. Genotypic information for O. sativa was obtained from the 3KRG project [25]. The complete dataset, consisting of 32 million SNPs, was downloaded from the SNP-Seek database (Accessed in Dec 2022), along with a curated subset of ~160 000 high-quality SNPs [45]. Consistent with the observations by Wang et al. [31], one accession (IRIS_313–8921) was excluded from the analysis due to a high proportion of missing genotype data. Then the cultivar specific 2-SNV barcodes for the Kitaake line were generated based on three different variant profiles; variants from the Illumina platform, variants from the ONT platform, and variants shared among Illumina and ONT platforms.

Generation of cultivar specific 2-SNV barcodes from the Illumina platform

The variant profiles of two WT O. sativa Kitaake replicates, obtained through the Illumina variant-calling pipeline, were used to generate a unique set of cultivar specific 2-SNV barcodes capable of distinguishing the Kitaake cultivar from other rice accessions in the 3KRG dataset. As the Kitaake line was not represented in the 3KRG database, its genotype at the 160 K high-quality SNP positions had to be inferred.

To ensure the reliability of the inferred genotype, a stringent variant filtering strategy was applied independently to both replicates. To achieve this, different combinations of variant quality and depth cut-offs were tested in order to match the proportion of variants observed at the 160 K SNP positions in other Japonica accessions within the database. On average, Japonica cultivars in the 3KRG database carried alternative alleles at ~2.5% of these positions. Therefore, an appropriate set of filtering thresholds was selected for the Kitaake sample, also a Japonica cultivar, to achieve a similar proportion of variants, ensuring consistency with the broader Japonica group. Thus, only high-confidence SNVs that met the following criteria were retained: FILTER = ‘PASS’, TYPE = ‘snp’, QUAL ≥30, DP ≥ 50, and VAF ≥ 0.99. Filtering was performed using BCFtools filter (v1.21) [43]. Subsequently, BCFtools isec was used to extract the set of high-quality variants shared between both replicates, resulting in a final VCF file containing only the most robust SNVs for use in downstream 2-SNV barcode generation steps. Due to the stringent filtering criteria used to ensure high-confidence variant calls, false positive variants were minimized; however, this also increased the likelihood of false negatives.

To address this, an additional filtering step was implemented. Each SNV showing a reference genotype in the 160 K high-quality SNP positions was cross-checked against the variant profiles generated prior to filtering. If the SNV was found to have an alternative allele in either KitWT replicates before filtering, it was considered unreliable and excluded from the analysis. Following this filtering procedure, 2073, 2097, and 2916 positions were excluded from Illumina, ONT, and shared variants across both datasets, respectively.

Thus, a pipeline was developed to generate a set of cultivar specific 2-SNV barcodes for the Kitaake cultivar, beginning with the creation of a BED file representing the 160 K high-quality SNP positions. Since the 3KRG dataset was aligned to an earlier version of the Nipponbare reference genome (O. sativa cv. Nipponbare, RefSeq Accession GCF_001433935.1, downloaded from RefSeq), while our WGS data were aligned to the most recent and complete Nipponbare assembly (O. sativa cv. Nipponbare, accession GCF_034140825.1), a coordinate conversion (liftover) was performed using liftOver tool from UCSC to map the 160 K SNP positions to the newer reference genome version. Regarding the unmapped positions after liftover, any SNVs whose new coordinates could not be resolved were excluded from subsequent analyses.

Next, the strictly filtered variant calls from the KitWT replicates were cross-referenced with these curated SNP positions to identify high-confidence SNVs located within the 160 K set. Using this information, the genotype of Kitaake across the curated set of 160 K positions, after removing low-quality sites, was reconstructed, comprising both reference and alternative alleles.

Following this, feature selection was conducted as described by Fraiture et al. (2025) [10] to identify the 500 SNVs with the highest discriminatory power to identify the Kitaake cultivar ranked by chi-squared (χ2) scores. After evaluating several feature-set sizes, we selected the top 500 features because this threshold was computationally efficient and consistently produced enough informative 2-SNV barcodes for downstream analyses. A comparative analysis was then performed to determine how many rice accessions in the 3KRG dataset shared genotypes with Kitaake across these top 500 features. As no single SNV was found to uniquely differentiate Kitaake from all other accessions, the strategy shifted toward generating 2-SNV combinations (i.e., barcodes) to achieve specific discrimination.

An exhaustive screening of all pairwise combinations among the top-ranked SNVs was carried out, producing a list of candidate 2-SNV barcodes, with the condition that no 2-SNV barcode should contain two SNVs located on the same chromosome. Through this approach, a robust set of high-quality 2-SNV barcodes was generated, from the Illumina platform, capable of distinguishing the Kitaake cultivar from all other accessions in the 3KRG dataset (Supplementary Data, Table S11).

Generation of cultivar specific 2-SNV barcodes from the ONT platform

The same strategy used for generating the 2-SNV barcodes from Illumina data was applied to a single KitWT sample sequenced using ONT, using the combined data from all five KitWT flow cells. While the overall pipeline remained consistent, adjustments were made to account for differences in variant quality between sequencing platforms. Specifically, applying the same fixed variant filtering thresholds used for Illumina data would not be appropriate for ONT data due to inherent differences in their error profiles. Consequently, since the average depth of coverage in Illumina data is substantially higher than in ONT data (Table 1), a variant with the same depth and sequencing quality on both platforms would not yield the same variant quality score when processed by DeepVariant. Therefore, applying identical cut-offs for depth and variant quality to both platforms was not appropriate. Instead, we adopted an approach that selects the same proportion of high-quality variants from ONT data as observed in the Illumina dataset. This ensures that, for both platforms, the highest percentage of top-quality variants is consistently retained.

To address this, a statistically comparable filtering approach was adopted. First, for Illumina, the variant quality cutoff of 30 was determined to correspond to the 32nd percentile of the variant quality distribution. This quantile was then used to determine an equivalent threshold for ONT data, resulting in a variant quality cutoff of 38.8. Similarly, for sequencing depth, the Illumina cutoff of 50 corresponded to the 12th percentile, which translated to a depth cutoff of 13 for the ONT dataset.

Using these adjusted thresholds, a strict filtering step was applied to the ONT variant profile with these parameters: FILTER = ‘PASS’, TYPE = ‘snp’, QUAL ≥38.8, DP ≥ 13, and VAF ≥ 0.99. The downstream steps for 2-SNV barcode generation, including BED file preparation, liftover to the current Nipponbare reference genome, identification of high-confidence SNVs at the 160 K positions, genotype reconstruction, feature selection, and exhaustive screening of 2-SNV combinations, were carried out exactly as described for the Illumina-based pipeline (Supplementary Data, Table S12).

Generation of cultivar specific 2-SNV barcodes from the variants shared by the Illumina and ONT platforms

For the third set of barcodes, only high-quality variants shared among the two Illumina replicates and the WT ONT sample were used. Then the same strict filtering procedures described previously for both Illumina and ONT data were applied to each dataset independently. Following this, variants that passed the filtering criteria in all three samples were identified as shared high-confidence variants.

These shared variants formed the basis for the last 2-SNV barcode generation. The same in-house pipeline used in earlier steps was employed to produce the final set of Kitaake-specific 2-SNV barcodes based on this intersection of high-confidence variants from both sequencing platforms (Supplementary Data, Table S13).

Fingerprint detection

A pipeline was developed and tested for detection of the generated fingerprints for both Kitaake and Nipponbare using WGS data generated in-house (Table 1), as well as selected publicly available datasets (Supplementary Data). As described in 4.3, a pipeline was developed to generate high-quality variant profiles using less stringent filtering criteria compared to the fingerprint generation pipeline. The resulting variant profiles for each line, including the number and types of variants detected, are summarized in Table 1.

For each Kitaake set of 2-SNV barcodes (generated based on different sequencing platforms), two BED files were created; one for SNVs where the genotype was an alternative allele and one for SNVs with a reference allele. For the Nipponbare fingerprint, all 2-SNV barcodes consisted of reference alleles, thus only one BED file with reference alleles, was created. Next, filtered VCF files obtained with the variant calling pipeline described in 4.3 were compared to the BED files for the relevant set of 2-SNV barcodes, to confirm the presence of the SNV with the genotype predicted in the 2-SNV barcode. The list of detected SNVs was then used to derive the list of detected 2-SNV barcodes.

For the Nipponbare fingerprint, we excluded one of the 145 predicted 2-SNV barcodes, i.e. barcode number 134 [10], comprising SNV with rank ID 44. In the older Nipponbare reference genome used in our previous study [10], the base at the position of the SNV with rank ID 44 was a C, but in the new reference genome, the base at that position was updated to T. Due to this inconsistency and potential ambiguity, we decided to exclude 2-SNV barcode 134 from further analysis.

Determining the minimum sequencing quality metrics

The limit of detection (LOD95) was defined as the minimum sequencing depth, breadth of coverage and fold80 at which the fingerprint-detection pipeline identified the GE line in at least 95% of replicates. To estimate this threshold, multiple in silico pure replicates were generated from in-house WGS data (Supplementary Data, Table S14). LOD95 was evaluated for two GE rice lines: (i) a Kitaake line containing an on-target SNP, sequenced using both Illumina and ONT, and (ii) a Nipponbare line containing an on-target indel, also sequenced using Illumina and ONT.

This analysis established platform- and variant-specific depth requirements for confident detection of SNP- and indel-based GE lines. In addition, for each minimum-depth condition, we quantified the number of detected 2-SNV barcodes to assess whether barcode recovery at the LOD95 depth was sufficient to support reliable line identification.

Following the approach described in Shin et al. [46], Probit regression was applied to the replicate-detection data to estimate the LOD95 for each sequencing platform and variant type.
