ISAAF: an IoT security and attack prevention framework using AI-driven predictive analytics
Authors: Khaoula Karam, Abderrahmane Aqachtoul, Abderrahmane Elamrani, Mehdi Najib, Najat Rafalia, Idriss Moumen, Jaafar Abouchabaka, Mohamed Bakhouya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Internet of Things (IoT) is reshaping domains, such as healthcare, agriculture, and industry by enabling real-time connectivity among constrained devices. However, the lightweight Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) protocol exposes these systems to severe cyber threats, including DoS, Bruteforce, Malformed, Flood, and Slowite attacks. While machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models trained on simulated benchmarks, such as MQTTset, have shown promise, evaluation results indicate that these models achieved high accuracy in controlled environments but failed to generalize to real-world traffic. To address this limitation, MQTTEEB-D was introduced as a novel real-world intrusion dataset collected from an operational IoT testbed. Building on MQTTEEB-D, a layered and AI-driven security framework is introduced for real-time intrusion detection and automated mitigation. For instance, Decision Tree (DT) and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) accuracies, using MQTTset, dropped to 8% and 21% when tested on real data. However, after retraining both models on MQTTEEB-D, the results showed a noticeable improvement; DT reached 87% and GRU 86.5% accuracy. The framework was deployed and tested in real-sitting scenarios and experimental results demonstrated efficient attacks’ detection and mitigation with near-real-time responsiveness. Overall, the findings confirm that the proposed framework and its related services provide a scalable, deployable, and cross-domain security solution for real-world IoT applications.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of IoT has transformed sectors, such as healthcare, agriculture, and industrial systems by enabling real-time data exchange among interconnected devices1. While these advances deliver immense benefits, they also create new attack surfaces across interconnected devices and communication networks, particularly through lightweight communication protocols, like the MQTT. For instnace, MQTT is widely adopted, as an IoT communication protocol, for its low overhead and suitability for constrained devices, yet it remains highly vulnerable to cyber-attacks, such as Denial of Service (DoS), Bruteforce Authentication (Bruteforce), Malformed Data (Malformed), Slow DoS against Internet of Things Environments (Slowite), and MQTT Publish Flood (Flood). In contexts, such as healthcare, where both data confidentiality and service availability are critical, these vulnerabilities pose severe risks2–4. Traditional security mechanisms, often reactive in nature, struggle to prevent such evolving threats and are typically benchmarked only on simulated datasets, like MQTTset5. Although valuable, these datasets fail to capture the variability and noise of real-world IoT traffic, limiting the robustness and deployability of ML/DL-based intrusion detection systems. Recent studies have explored these challenges through advanced approaches, such as transformer-based intrusion detection and federated learning for MQTT and IoT environments3,6–10, highlighting the growing shift toward scalable and adaptive AI-driven security solutions.

To overcome these limitations, we propose a real-time IoT security framework (ISAAF) that integrates pre-trained ML and DL models for predictive attacks’ detection and automated prevention. Specifically, we focus on the two best-performing models identified during benchmarking: DT from the ML family and GRU from the DL family. Both were initially trained on MQTTset, but their deployment in real-sitting scenarios highlighted the need for realistic datasets. In this work, we developed MQTTEEB-D11, a real-world MQTT intrusion dataset capturing authentic traffic patterns and attacks’ traces within a healthcare IoT testbed. Retraining DT and GRU models on MQTTEEB-D significantly improved robustness, enabling accurate, proactive detection of DoS, Bruteforce, Malformed, Flood and Slowite attacks. ISAAF’s deployment was realized using Node-RED for orchestration and ThingsBoard for monitoring, ensuring seamless integration into IoT infrastructures without disrupting ongoing services. Remaining platform’s services continue to operate normally, as mitigation actions are applied only to the affected communication channel. This behavior is supported by the latency and jitter analysis presented in the section “Deployment and Real-Time Resilience”. In fact, obtained results demonstrate both ISAAF’s scalability by handling growth in devices and traffic as well as its cross-environment applicability in other domains, such as precision agriculture and environment monitoring. It is worth noting that the work conducted in this paper is part of the MGFARM project (Smart Stand-Alone Microgrids for Agricultural Electrification), where the main objective is to develop Smart Micro-Grids based on the production of renewable energy in order to support sustainable development of smart and energy-efficient farming systems12. The project aims to develop and deploy a blockchain-based IoT platform for secure, decentralized energy sharing and efficient resource management within interconnected microgrids13.

Related work

IoT ecosystems combine heterogeneous devices and protocols, which makes end-to-end security difficult to guarantee at scale. Resource-constrained endpoints limit the use of strong primitives (for example, advanced encryption, frequent patching, and mutual authentication), leaving systems exposed to eavesdropping, tampering, and unauthorized access22,23. MQTT, while popular for its lightweight publish–subscribe model, broadens the attack surface to DoS, Bruteforce, and Slowite24. Protocol hardening via TLS (Transport Layer Security), session resumption, selective payload encryption, OAuth 2.0, or mTLS improves confidentiality and integrity. These solutions by themselves do not, however, neutralize behaviorally subtle or network-level intrusions and remain insufficient for large-scale heterogeneous IoT environments25,26. This limitation has motivated extensive research into AI-driven intrusion detection and response, specifically for MQTT-centric IoT systems.

Recent reviews have emphasized the growing role of AI-driven and hybrid detection techniques in cybersecurity, highlighting both their strengths and limitations in evolving IoT environments6. The first wave of work focused on characterizing the vulnerabilities of MQTT. For instance, Soni and Makwana14surveyed MQTT weaknesses and operational pitfalls, clarifying protocol risks and motivating analytics-based defenses. However, they did not propose a concrete IDS/IPS architecture nor provide empirical validation, leaving open the challenge of building deployable defenses. Building on this groundwork, Hindy et al6. explored deep sequence modeling for MQTT traffic by combining CNN (Convolutional Neural Network) and LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory). Their work showed that temporal modeling improved recognition of evolving patterns in smart-home and IIoT settings, illustrating the promise of DL for MQTT telemetry. The main limitation was that evaluations were performed only on simulated traces without reporting latency or overhead, which made deployment implications unclear.

Alzahrani et al15. broadened the methodological scope by benchmarking KNN (K-Nearest Neighbors), LDA (Linear Discriminant Analysis), and CNN–LSTM across multiple MQTT datasets in smart cities, healthcare, and IIoT. They confirmed the superiority of ML/DL over rule-based baselines and highlighted trade-offs between shallow and deep models. However, like earlier work, their results suffered from the well-known generalization gap because most datasets were synthetic or semi-simulated, and the study did not incorporate a concrete mitigation loop. In response to robustness concerns, Siddharthan et al. proposed SENMQTT-SET16, an ensemble-based IDS with multi-cascade feature selection. This improved resilience and efficiency on simulated MQTT traffic in smart-home scenarios, but the reliance on synthetic traces left unresolved questions about performance under real noise, session churn, and broker-side stress.

Al Hanif and Ilyas7turned attention toward constrained devices by designing feature-engineering-centric pipelines that feed AI backends, targeting smart agriculture and IIoT. Their targeted feature selection improved detection accuracy under tight resource constraints, which was a practical advance for embedded devices. The tradeoff was nontrivial preprocessing cost, and the evaluation lacked validation in real-sitting scenarios with attacks’ detection and mitigate workflow. Most recently, Alzahrani et al3. proposed a lightweight AI framework, which is optimized for resource limited IoT nodes. Their design balanced detection accuracy and runtime efficiency and acknowledged the compute/memory constraints of IoT devices, but once again, validation relied heavily on synthetic traces and provided only a partial end-to-end view.

Recent 2025 studies have further advanced MQTT intrusion detection toward explainable and ensemble-based paradigms. For instance, Zeghida et al. (2025)17 introduced the XMID–MQTT framework, integrating explainable ML/DL models to enhance interpretability through SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations), while Solanki and Gupta (2025)18 proposed an ensemble-learning IDS combining diverse classifiers to improve detection robustness across IoT deployments. These approaches underline the increasing emphasis on transparency and hybridization within modern MQTT security research.

From this in-depth state-of-the-art review,, we have noticed three recurring gaps emerge: (i) many studies depend primarily on simulated datasets that ignore real traffic irregularities, timing jitter, and broker stressors, (ii) complete pipelines that start with capture and preprocessing and end with automated mitigation are rarely demonstrated, and (iii) critical QoS metrics, such as latency, jitter, and bottlenecks are underreported, making real-time viability difficult to assess. Parallel to model and framework development, multiple MQTT intrusion datasets have also been released. MQTTset (2020)5 became the community baseline, with over 11 million simulated samples covering DoS, Slowite, Malformed, Bruteforce, and Flood. Its strength is scale and availability, but it is entirely simulated. In the same year, Slowite19 targeted low-rate DoS scenarios, useful for protocol analysis but too small for models’ training. MQTT-IoT-IDS202020 extended the landscape by introducing flow-level features and diverse intrusions (DoS, MitM, generic intrusion). This dataset enabled the use of CNN–RNN–LSTM and hybrid models but still lacked real deployment irregularities because it was simulated. SENMQTT-SET (2022)16 provided 34M records with multi-cascade feature selection, advancing feature engineering but lacking realism. DoS/DDoS-MQTT-IoT (2023)21 captured live traces in a testbed, adding realism but focusing mainly on flooding attacks. Table 2 synthesizes these datasets together with our developed MQTTEEB-D.

In summary, prior studies advanced MQTT security using ML/DL but suffered from simulated datasets, incomplete pipelines, and limited deployment validation. The dual-dataset strategy adopted here using MQTTset for comparability and MQTTEEB-D for realism directly addresses these issues. Together, Table 1 and Table 2 position ISAAF as a deployment aware framework built on realistic data and motivate the following section, where the framework design is detailed.

ISAAF framework

ISAAF is a multi-layer, closed-loop framework designed for real-time detection and mitigation of cyberattacks in MQTT-based IoT environments. Each layer operates autonomously yet interacts continuously through Legitimate data, mitigation control, and adversarial traffic exchanges. This closed feedback loop ensures continuous adaptation and resilience in dynamic IoT scenarios. For instance, tn the left side of Fig. 1, the Cyberattack Execution block models the adversarial domain of the framework. Two sub-boxes Attackers and Attacks define the threat source and its operational scope. Cyber Attacks (DoS, Slowite, Malformed, Flood, and Bruteforce) are injected directly into the MQTT broker, which also manages Legitimate traffic, guaranteeing realistic coexistence between normal and adversarial flows during experimentation. All attack scenarios were developed and executed under controlled conditions, as detailed in the companion dataset paper11.Fig. 1Layered ISAAF architecture used in our deployment, showing end-to-end data capture, AI-driven analysis, prevention, and monitoring.

The Perception Layer handles in-field data streams and serves as the primary sensing and data-acquisition component of the proposed framework, ensuring continuous and reliable input from IoT devices. For instance, in our healthcare testbed, a MySignals board aggregates, via serial communication interfaces (i.e., serial port), a wide range of physiological multi-sensor signals, such as ECG, airflow, body temperature, EMG. A Raspberry Pi 4 hosts the Node-RED runtime, executing data stream flows by performing light filtering, normalization, and packet formatting before MQTT publication. It is worth noting that this design is domain-agnostic, replacing the biomedical sensors with agricultural or industrial ones requires no architectural change, thereby illustrating ISAAF’s modularity and adaptability.

At the core, the Network Layer acts as the data-processing and decision-making center. As depicted in Fig. 1, incoming streams first enter the AI-Driven Process module and processed through four sequential components: Traffic Capture, Parallel Processing, AI Model, and Decision-Making. Traffic Capture parses MQTT frames to extract discriminative protocol-level features; Parallel Processing distributes data batches across threads to minimize latency; the AI Model executes the deployed classifier (DT or GRU); and Decision-Making interprets predictions to generate prevention commands. In parallel, a System Monitoring module visualizes CPU load, memory use, and MQTT connectivity to ensure broker stability during stress. For instance, an average end-to-end cycle of 10–11 s (sensor-to-publication) was measured, including less than 4 s for inference and 1–3 s for mitigation values confirming nearly real-time responsiveness under operational conditions.

The Application Layer closes the loop by combining intelligent mitigation, visualization, and data storage. In fact,the Edge Mode integrates a Cassandra database for scalable time-series management and a REST API for automated mitigation actions. When an anomaly is detected, a REST call is triggered and instructs the broker’s firewall to block the malicious IP and the port 1883 connection, while Legitimate MQTT traffic continues uninterrupted. In parallel, processed information are forwarded to the dashboard for operator insight. The Actions panel confirms successful enforcement and restoration of stable operation. Real-time parameters, such as ECG_Rate, Airflow_Rate, Temperature_Value, and EMG_Rate are continuously displayed through the ThingsBoard interface, ensuring observability and auditability.

This organized structure visually captures ISAAF’s cyclic sensing–decision–action workflow. More formally, Algorithm 1 details this operational cycle. It encapsulates the eight key phases data collection, network connection, AI analysis, application use, attack simulation, detection, prevention, and administrator notification implemented as an autonomous loop. Each algorithmic step corresponds directly to a visual component in Fig. 1, ensuring one-to-one traceability between conceptual design and real deployment. This integration reinforces the reproducibility and transparency of the proposed architecture.

In summary, ISAAF delivers an interpretable, self-contained, and experimentally validated framework that unites perception, network, and application intelligence into a real-time adaptive defense loop. Its layered structure, explicit data and control pathways, and verified timing performance confirm its robustness and readiness for large-scale IoT cybersecurity applications.

Materials and methods

Data description and generation process

To rigorously evaluate the ISAAF framework for attack detection in MQTT-based IoT environments, two complementary datasets were employed: the benchmark MQTTset5 and our MQTTEEB-D dataset11. MQTTset ensures comparability with prior work, while MQTTEEB-D provides realistic traces from a physical testbed. Together, they form a dual foundation for standardized benchmarking and validation in real-sitting scenarios.

The MQTTset is a large-scale dataset, publicly available on Kaggle 1. It was mainly designed to emulate MQTT communications with both benign and adversarial traffic. MQTTset contains approximately 11.9 million records (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}1.6 GB) spanning Legitimate traffic and five attack categories: DoS, Slowite, Bruteforce, Malformed, and Flood. The dataset is distributed in several variants, including a reduced file (mqttdataset_reduced.csv) and pre-split train/test sets. We used the reduced version with an 80/20 split to balance computational feasibility and statistical robustness. To overcome the limitations of synthetic data and mirror operational conditions, we constructed MQTTEEB-D (11, 1), in a healthcare-oriented testbed with MySignals sensors, a Raspberry Pi 4 device, and a ThingsBoard MQTT broker. Legitimate data streams were continuously published, while adversarial scenarios targeted the broker with the same five attack types present in MQTTset. Traces were captured with multi-threaded PyShark and synchronized with metadata to ensure precise labeling. The final dataset contains 222,813 records with 13 features and a six-class target. Dataset preprocessing

To ensure the reliability of subsequent ML and DL modeling, both MQTTset and MQTTEEB-D were preprocessed using a unified pipeline designed to guarantee data quality, comparability, and reproducibility. This pipeline, which forms the data preparation layer of the ISAAF framework, addressed missing values, outliers, heterogeneous feature scales, class imbalance, and categorical encodings, while being guided by a systematic Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) process. In fact, by standardizing these steps across both simulated and real-world datasets, ISAAF ensured consistent inputs for the downstream intrusion detection modules.

Exploratory Data Analysis: was employed to guide feature selection and reduce redundancy. The process integrated three complementary layers: (i) univariate analysis, which inspected individual feature distributions through plotting and descriptive statistics; (ii) bivariate analysis, where categorical features were assessed using chi-squared statistics and numerical features with logistic regression coefficients to quantify their association with the target label; and (iii) multivariate analysis, where correlation matrices were used to detect collinearity and redundant variables. Encoding and class balancing: categorical attributes, such as protocol flags, were numerically encoded using label and binary schemes as appropriate. To address class imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied. This method generated synthetic samples along nearest-neighbor vectors, ensuring equitable class representation without distorting feature distributions.

ML/DL models training

Building on the preprocessed datasets and engineered features, we framed intrusion detection as a multiclass classification task covering DoS, Slowite, Bruteforce, Malformed/Flood variants, and Legitimate activity. To obtain a comprehensive view across modeling paradigms, we evaluated a diverse set of ML algorithms: DT, Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), AdaBoost (AdaB), CatBoost, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Naïve Bayes (NB), and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) as a neural baseline. This portfolio spans single-tree learners (DT) for interpretability, bagging ensembles (RF) for variance reduction, boosting families (GB, XGB, LightGBM, AdaB, CatBoost) for strong non-linear decision boundaries, instance-based methods (KNN), kernel-based margin maximizers (SVM), linear discriminants (LDA), and probabilistic generative baselines (NB). The MLP model provides a lightweight neural benchmark for non-linear hierarchies.

To ensure fair and effective comparisons, hyperparameters for each ML algorithm were tuned via grid search, combined with k-fold cross-validation, balancing then predictive accuracy against computational cost. Search ranges were defined per algorithm (e.g., tree depth, number of estimators, learning rate, neighbors, kernel type and C for SVM), with the best configurations selected by validation performance and, in case of ties, by lower runtime. The resulting optimal parameters are reported in Table 5. This tuning process enabled each family to demonstrate its strengths: efficient and explainable baselines (DT, LDA, NB), robust ensembles for high-dimensional and moderately imbalanced data (RF and boosting variants), and a neural reference point (MLP).

Following the ML experiments, we extended the study to DL architectures tailored for temporal IoT traffic analysis. Recurrent neural networks (RNN) were selected for their ability to capture sequential dependencies: GRU, LSTM, and simple RNN. These models were built using structured pipelines, which combined recurrent layers with dense layers, for feature compression, dropout for regularization, and softmax outputs for multiclass classification. Their detailed architectures, optimizers, loss functions, and training hyperparameters are summarized in Table 6.

Training of DL models followed the same rigor as ML: cross-validation with batch processing, careful tuning of epochs and minibatch sizes, and adaptive optimization. Regularization techniques, such as dropout and early stopping, were employed to mitigate overfitting, while learning-rate schedules stabilized convergence. All models were optimized under the sparse categorical cross-entropy loss to ensure comparability across architectures. Optimization was carried out using the Adam and AdamW optimizers, which apply adaptive moment estimation and decoupled weight decay to improve generalization in resource-constrained environments27.

Experimental results

Feature analysis and preprocessing

A thorough analysis of MQTT/TCP raw features was conducted to establish a reliable foundation for intrusion detection. At the transport layer, descriptors, such as tcp.flags, tcp.len, and tcp.time_delta captured packet-level behavior, while MQTT fields including mqtt.conack.flags, mqtt.conflag.cleansess, mqtt.conflags, mqtt.dupflag, mqtt.hdrflags, mqtt.kalive, mqtt.msg, and mqtt.qos provided insights into session state, delivery guarantees, duplicate handling, and keep-alive policies. Together with identifiers, like mqtt.msgid and mqtt.len, these attributes formed the backbone of the dataset, enabling the discrimination between Legitimate traffic and attack scenarios, such as DoS, Slowite, Bruteforce, Malformed, and Flood.

The initial exploration of raw numerical features revealed heavy-tailed distributions, notably in tcp.len, tcp.time_delta, and mqtt.msgid. Figure 4 illustrates this skewness, where extreme outliers could bias training. Outlier treatment using IQR-based capping mitigated these effects by clipping values beyond statistical bounds, reducing distortions while preserving informative ranges (Fig. 5). Normalization was then applied to scale numerical variables into the [0, 1] interval. As shown in Figs. 6 and 7, features that originally spanned highly unequal ranges became harmonized, ensuring that no single variable disproportionately influenced the model’s learning process. This was particularly effective for variables, such as tcp.time_delta, tcp.len, and mqtt.msgid, whose post-normalization profiles demonstrated comparability suitable bahaviour for gradient-based algorithms.

The exploratory data analysis confirmed these adjustments and revealed informative relationships with the target labels. Univariate plots (Figs. 8, 9) showed categorical imbalance: for example, tcp.flags was dominated by 0x00000010 and 0x00000018, while mqtt.msg concentrated on a few frequent message types. Numerical features, such as tcp.time_delta were clustered near zero with rare long delays, and mqtt.msgid exhibited a long tail. Bivariate comparisons with the target classes (Figs. 10 and 11) showed clear separability: tcp.len values tended to increase under DoS traffic, while mqtt.msgtype distinguished Bruteforce and Malformed scenarios. The correlation matrix (Fig. 12) highlighted structural dependencies, including a perfect inverse relation between tcp.flags and mqtt.hdrflags, a negative link between mqtt.dupflag and mqtt.msgtype, and positive associations between mqtt.qos and mqtt.retain. These findings informed feature pruning and clarified which attributes carried the strongest predictive signal.

Class imbalance represented another major challenge. Legitimate and DoS traffic heavily outweighed minority categories, such as Slowite, Bruteforce, Malformed, and Flood (Fig. 13A). To avoid bias toward the majority, the synthetic minority oversampling technique was applied, creating synthetic samples for underrepresented classes. The balanced histogram (Fig. 13B) demonstrates the effectiveness of this strategy, ensuring that models trained on the dataset achieve fair recall across all categories rather than collapsing onto dominant classes. SMOTE was applied solely to correct class imbalance and improve minority-class learnability; quantitative error trade-offs are examined later via the confusion-matrix analysis.

Integrating all diagnostics, we consolidated a final feature set that balances discriminative strength with parsimony. Twelve features were retained: tcp.flags, tcp.time_delta, tcp.len, mqtt.conack.flags, mqtt.conflag.cleansess, mqtt.conflags, mqtt.dupflag, mqtt.hdrflags, mqtt.kalive, mqtt.msg, mqtt.qos, and the target. This compact subset reflects the attributes most consistently informative across univariate, bivariate, and multivariate analyses, discarding redundant or weakly discriminative variables while preserving protocol relevance. By focusing on these features, the final schema ensures that ML/DL models are trained on a concise yet information-rich representation of MQTT/TCP traffic.

For the simulated dataset (MQTTset), this pipeline was implemented in a dedicated notebook that details every step from outlier capping to feature selection, ensuring reproducibility and transparency 1. For the real-world dataset (MQTTEEB-D), the same methodology was applied and validated, with results documented in11. Importantly, these insights guided the design of our curated dataset: instead of capturing all raw MQTT/TCP fields, we limited collection to the 12 most relevant features identified here and added a timestamp for temporal context. Performance evaluation on MQTTEEB-D

We assessed the generalization capability of pre-trained ML/DL models using the MQTTEEB-D dataset, which captures real-world IoT traffic. These models were initially trained on the simulated MQTTset dataset and evaluated on MQTTEEB-D to analyze their adaptability to real-world conditions. As discussed previously, the simplified nature of MQTTset limited the models’ ability to represent the complexity and variability inherent in real deployments. Consequently, a retraining phase on MQTTEEB-D was necessary to adapt to the nuanced characteristics of real data, leading to significant improvements across performance metrics.

The quantitative analysis for all confusion matrices in Fig. 16 follows the per-class error-rate formulation defined by Sokolova and Lapalme28, specifically Equations (6)–(8), which relate true/false positives and negatives to precision, recall, and F1-score. These normalized ratios provide a consistent interpretation of model behavior across all evaluated classes.

Conclusion and perspectives

This work presented ISAAF, an AI-driven framework for securing MQTT-based IoT systems through predictive analytics and automated prevention. By combining the benchmark MQTTset dataset with the newly developed real-world MQTTEEB-D dataset, we demonstrated the importance of realistic traffic for training robust intrusion detection models. While pre-trained models on MQTTset performed poorly when tested in real-sitting scenarios, retraining DT and GRU on MQTTEEB-D yielded consistent improvements in detection accuracy, confirming the dataset’s relevance as a benchmark for IoT security. More precisely, the experimental deployment demonstrated that the ISAAF framework can sustain secure and continuous IoT operation even under adversarial conditions. The layered design linking perception, network, and application components enabled efficient coordination between sensing, analysis, and mitigation while keeping normal MQTT communication active. During live attack scenarios, the prevention mechanism acted precisely by blocking the affected MQTT port, without interrupting Legitimate services, such as dashboard updates, user access, or background telemetry. This targeted action reflects the framework’s capacity to isolate threats rather than halt the entire system, allowing administrators to later restore blocked endpoints once verified as safe.

These behaviors confirm that ISAAF is not only accurate in detection but also operationally reliable in maintaining service availability. By integrating lightweight AI inference, real-time orchestration, and adaptive prevention within a closed feedback loop, ISAAF provides a practical and reproducible foundation for intelligent IoT defense. This balance between precision, responsiveness, and continuity demonstrates the framework’s potential as a dependable building block for scalable, real-time cybersecurity architectures. In fact, ISAAF demonstrates stable timing, negligible jitter, transparent resource awareness, and autonomous mitigation under diverse attack conditions, confirming its readiness for deployment in large-scale real-world IoT environments.

Future work will focus on reducing latency and enabling lightweight deployment via model compression and hardware–software co-design29,30. In parallel, Transformer-based models have shown potential for capturing long-range temporal dependencies in IoT intrusion detection8,9. However, their computational complexity limits immediate adoption on constrained devices; we therefore plan to study hybrid variants, suitable for edge integration. Explainable AI (XAI) is another priority for operational trust. Supported by recent explainable MQTT-IDS studies17,18, we will integrate post-hoc methods, such as SHAP and LIME to make feature contributions interpretable during operation.
