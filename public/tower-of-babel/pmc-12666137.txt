From knowledge to action: protocol for a mixed-methods evaluation of First Nations-led knowledge mobilisation on prenatal opioid exposure
Authors: Priscilla Medeiros, Agnes Mazzucco, Lauren Wilkinson, Marcia Altiman, Jennifer Glover, Ashley Stone, Bailley Taylor, Astrid Guttmann, Serene Kerpan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionFirst Nations communities in Canada are disproportionately impacted by prenatal opioid exposure (POE) and neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS). In response, we developed a research partnership with 13 First Nations communities in Ontario. Phase I of the research project, initiated in 2018, included the development of mixed-methods reports on the impact of POE for each community. This protocol outlines the evaluation of phase II, during which nine communities individually co-designed and implemented community-specific knowledge mobilisation (KMb) plans informed by findings from phase I. The evaluation aims to assess advisory working group engagement, KMb implementation and perceived community-level impacts.Methods and analysisThis mixed-methods evaluation integrates survey and qualitative data to assess First Nations-led KMb products and activities. The Public and Patient Engagement Evaluation Tool, a validated survey instrument, will be administered to advisory group members and analysed descriptively. Focus groups and interviews will be conducted to explore advisory working group members’ experiences and analysed using phenomenological methods. Qualitative findings will be mapped to the Engage with Impact framework to assess outcomes across engagement domains.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval has been granted by Vancouver Island University. All community contacts and advisory working group members will provide informed consent prior to data collection. Phase II activities are governed by formal community agreements. In alignment with First Nations Principles of OCAP (Ownership, Control, Access and Possession), First Nations community partners retain ownership of their KMb products and are actively involved in the design, implementation and dissemination of the project evaluation. Results will be shared through peer-reviewed publications, community reports and knowledge-sharing events.Keywords: Knowledge, Community-Based Participatory Research, Methods, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Introduction

The global opioid crisis has had profound consequences in many countries including Canada, with First Nations communities being disproportionately affected by opioid-related harms and deaths.1 This includes prenatal opioid exposure (POE), for which data from Ontario, Canada’s largest province, indicate just over 2% of all infants2 and 16% of First Nations infants are impacted.3 The majority (70%) of POE in First Nations infants is related to maternal treatment of opioid use disorder with medications such as methadone or buprenorphine, whereas in the general Ontario population, the majority is due to opioids for pain treatment or use of unregulated opioids.3 4 Neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS, also referred to as neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome or NOWS) is a withdrawal syndrome that is common in infants exposed to opioids prenatally, especially opioid agonist therapy.5 While NAS is a prevalent side effect of mothers being treated for opioid use disorder with opioid agonist therapy during pregnancy, there is strong evidence that treatment is a safer option than no treatment or the use of unregulated opioids for mothers and infants, including for the prevention of maternal drug toxicity.6 NAS has not been shown to be an independent risk factor for poor long-term outcomes. However, a number of systematic reviews have highlighted the association of POE with infant and child health problems including prematurity and longer term developmental issues.7,12 The evidence does not conclusively support a direct effect of opioid exposure versus the complex social and environmental factors related to opioid use in pregnancy with the documented longer term health and developmental outcomes.

POE project: phase I

In response to concerns raised by several First Nations communities in the lower half of Ontario regarding the health and development of school-age children with POE, we initiated a mixed-methods research project in 2017 in partnership with 13 First Nations communities. Participating First Nations communities were engaged in a community-based participatory action research (CBPAR) project designed to generate both quantitative and qualitative information related to POE and NAS. The goals of the research project were to (1) build strong relationships with First Nations communities, and (2) gather and share community and culturally specific information about the impacts of POE and NAS.13 Quantitative data were obtained from health records housed at ICES and governed by First Nations. ICES is an independent, non-profit research institute whose legal status under Ontario’s health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyse healthcare and demographic data for health system evaluation and improvement and with a strong Indigenous data governance framework. Community partners determined which ICES data elements were of interest and benefit to them. These included community-level information on the prevalence of POE and NAS, maternal and infant characteristics and outcomes, as well as trends in type of opioid use during pregnancy and by year. Concurrently, qualitative data were gathered through 19 focus groups and 32 interviews (n=51) across 11 communities. A diverse range of voices was included, such as elders, community members and leaders, service providers, educators and individuals with lived and living experience such as parents, grandparents and caregivers. The qualitative research focused on the impacts of POE on children, mothers, families and caregivers, service providers and educators, and communities as a whole; gifts and strengths that could be built on; and strategies to improve supports for families and to prevent POE. Community partners informed and shaped all aspects of the project and ensured that research processes were community and culturally appropriate. The findings of phase I of this project were summarised and shared through community-specific reports and presentations, each reviewed and approved by community partners. A public-facing aggregate report with findings from across the 13 communities provides further details on the project’s methodology and results.13

POE project: phase II

Phase II of this project is focused on the design, implementation and evaluation of KMb plans in participating First Nations communities. KMb is the process of moving research into action by making it accessible, understandable and applicable to diverse interest holders including researchers, policymakers and community members, through formats and dissemination channels tailored to their specific needs.14 KMb activities can include sharing research findings in plain language, co-developing resources with communities and supporting the integration of knowledge into policy, programmes and practice to improve outcomes. While the term is often used interchangeably with knowledge translation (KT) in the literature, KMb is broader in scope.15 KT is typically focused on applying health research to improve health outcomes, whereas KMb emphasises two-way knowledge exchange and collaboration.14 16 17 We use the term KMb in this project to reflect a wider range of activities in phase II of this work, while using KT when referring to the broader field.

In Indigenous health research, KT is understood more broadly as an inherently relational and contextual process grounded in community knowledge systems and cultural frameworks.18,20 Indigenous-led KT emphasises the ethical imperative to support community priorities, promote equity and respect and value Indigenous ways of knowing and doing in health systems and academic research.21 Smylie and colleagues defined KT in this context as the ‘Indigenously led sharing of culturally relevant and useful health information and practices to improve Indigenous health status, policy, services, and programs’.18

Despite growing recognition of the importance of culturally appropriate KT, there is limited research documenting or evaluating Indigenous-led KT processes, particularly in the context of substance use and maternal-child health.21,23 While research funding bodies increasingly require evidence of patient and community engagement, few studies have explored how KT is co-designed, implemented and assessed in ways that align with Indigenous frameworks of knowledge and action.21 24 This lack of evidence makes it difficult to identify best practices, support effective partnerships and ensure accountability to communities.

Phase II of the POE project is focused on a collaboration between the community partners and research team to co-develop community-specific knowledge mobilisation strategies aimed at addressing the impacts of POE within their respective communities. All 13 participating communities were invited to take part in phase II; 9 determined that they had the capacity to participate. The phase II research goals are to (1) continue to build strong research relationships with participating First Nations communities; (2) provide support and resources that enable participating First Nations communities to design, implement and evaluate community-specific KMb plans using the project findings from phase I; and (3) co-evaluate the effectiveness of the community-specific knowledge planning processes and impacts in ways that are meaningful and complementary to the ways that communities share knowledge.

Beginning in May 2024, participating communities were invited to identify a main contact(s) and form a local advisory working group to guide this process. These advisory working groups were community-based bodies composed of individuals with relevant expertise, lived and living experience, and cultural knowledge to provide leadership and direction on project activities. These groups played a central role in phase II by reviewing phase I findings and shaping KMb products and activities that were relevant and responsive to each community’s needs, context and capacity. To guide advisory working group members with the design, implementation and evaluation of their KMb products and activities, two members of the research team (PM and AM) conducted an orientation session. This included an introduction to phase I of the POE project, including key terms, research goals and community-specific findings, as well as an introduction to KMb and the Phase II goals, timelines and expectations. Community partners were invited to complete a KMb planning template adapted from the Public Health Agency of Canada’s Knowledge Translation Planning Primer.25 Throughout the phase II planning process, the research team provided ongoing consultation, collaboration, check-ins, resources and logistical support as determined by the main community contact or advisory working group members. A research team member with clinical expertise (AG) reviewed all KMb products for accuracy of health information. In addition, communities were offered dedicated funding to support the design, delivery and evaluation of their KMb products and activities. These methods were chosen to support First Nations communities’ rights to self-determination and Indigenous-led knowledge mobilisation.

The resulting KM initiatives were diverse and highly contextualised, reflecting each community’s goals and capacities. Activities included hosting community conferences, producing social media campaigns, developing a community declaration to address the opioid crisis and POE, and creating a video featuring a woman with lived experience highlighting a personal story of resilience and intergenerational healing. These activities were crafted to foster knowledge exchange in culturally safe and meaningful ways that brought community members together to raise awareness and promote local dialogue and action related to POE.

While KMb products and activities were led by participating First Nations communities, the research team supported advisory working group members in developing their own evaluations to assess the reach, awareness and next steps related to POE. These community-specific evaluations were designed to inform ongoing local KMb efforts. The focus of this paper is on the broader evaluation of processes and perceived impacts of co-designing, implementing and evaluating the KMb plans. In this context, co-design refers to a collaborative process led by First Nations communities, in which advisory working group members shaped the direction, content and implementation of their KMb plans, with support from the research team as requested. A mixed-methods approach will be used, drawing on validated, non-Indigenous engagement evaluation tools and qualitative inquiry.26,28 While the engagement evaluation tools we used are not grounded in Indigenous methodologies, they were selected to support a systematic assessment of engagement and outcomes, and to contribute to the growing literature on First Nations-led KMb. Findings from this study will contribute to the evidence on First Nations-led KMb and inform future practices in community-driven health research and KMb.

Methods

Study design

The evaluation integrates quantitative and qualitative data collection and analysis and is guided by a CBPAR approach.29,31 CBPAR is a form of decolonising research with Indigenous communities because it places much of the decision-making power with the community, which can help prevent ‘extractive’ research. Data sovereignty increases when the decision-making power resides with Indigenous communities. CBPAR respects local knowledge and cultural practices as legitimate and important forms of knowledge.32 This helps ensure that research is culturally relevant and grounded in the lived experiences of the community. Often, CBPAR, such as the case with this project, requires long-term engagement, which results in increased trust and respect for all research partners.33 34 This approach fosters community-centred action, research that is reflective and flexible, meaningful collaboration and collective decision-making.

This study will use a convergent parallel mixed-methods design to provide a comprehensive, balanced and efficient approach to understanding this research topic.35 In doing so, both qualitative and quantitative data are collected at the same time, analysed separately and then compared and integrated into the results of the project. This process allows us to examine how findings align, complement or differ from one another to strengthen the overall interpretation of results.36

Study setting and participants

Phase II of the project engaged 9 of the 13 First Nations communities that participated in phase I. Participating communities formed local advisory working groups to develop and guide the design, implementation and evaluation of KMb products and activities tailored to their phase I findings. These groups were composed of health and social service providers and educators; individuals with lived and living experience such as mothers who used opioids during pregnancy; and community members and leaders such as elders and council members. All advisory working group members involved in phase II will be invited to participate in the overall phase II project evaluation.

Data collection, including survey distribution and focus group and interview discussions, began in late June 2025 and will continue through September 2025. Data analysis will commence thereafter.

Quantitative component: Public and Patient Engagement Evaluation Tool (PPEET) questionnaire

The PPEET will be used to assess the planning, execution and perceived impacts of the KMb products and activities from the perspective of advisory working group members from the nine participating First Nations. The project version of the PPEET questionnaire was selected for its relevance to the advisory members’ roles and contributions in Phase II and was adapted in consultation with the original tool developers at McMaster University. PPEET is a validated instrument commonly used in health research to evaluate the quality and outcomes of patient, public and community engagement initiatives.27

The adapted one-time engagement questionnaire comprises 21 questions, including 15 closed-ended items using a five-point Likert scale (ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’) and six open-ended questions. The tool is based on module B of the PPEET framework, which focuses on assessing the implementation and quality of engagement activities.27 This module was selected because the evaluation is being conducted after the completion of the KMb planning and implementation phases of the project. As such, the questionnaire will be administered once, following the conclusion of all engagement activities.

In addition to the core PPEET items, three project-specific questions were added to expand on areas identified as important by the research team and community partners. These additions were included to collect further detail on the community partners’ experiences as advisory working group members and cover elements that may be more easily shared through a confidential survey than in a group discussion setting.

Advisory working group members will receive an email invitation with study details and a survey link. Before beginning the survey, advisory working group members were asked to complete six demographic questions, including their community affiliation, primary perspective in the advisory working group (eg, staff member, lived and living experience, elder, council member, community member), length of involvement, and whether they self-identify as First Nations, Métis or Inuk. The survey will be administered online using Hosted in Canada Surveys (https://www.hostedincanadasurveys.ca), a secure, Canadian-hosted data collection platform. While an online-only format offers the advantages of convenience and ease of access for individuals comfortable with digital tools, it may present challenges for members with limited experience using smartphones or computers, or for those without reliable internet access. To mitigate these barriers, the research team (PM and AM) will remain available to provide support and alternative arrangements as needed. All responses will be anonymous and stored on encrypted, password-protected servers at Vancouver Island University.

Qualitative component: focus groups and interviews

To expand on findings from the PPEET, focus group and interviews will be conducted with advisory working group members at the end of phase II. Across the nine participating First Nations communities, advisory working group members range in size from 1 to 12 members, with a total of 38 members overall. We anticipate conducting eight focus groups, one with each of the eight advisory working groups, and an individual interview with the First Nations community that has only a main contact. For advisory working group members who are unable to participate in the group discussion, an individual interview or second focus group will be offered, depending on the number of people from that community. Both main contacts and advisory working group members will be notified of the focus groups and interviews through an invitation email. Each advisory working group member who participates in a focus group or interview will receive a gift card honourarium in recognition of their time and contributions.

The focus group and interview guides were developed collaboratively by the research team and sessions are being facilitated by trained team members (PM and AM). In alignment with the project’s community engagement plan, MA, an advisory working group member with expertise in community-based evaluation, was invited to review the PPEET, and the focus group and interview guides. MA met with PM and AM to review the tools prior to their rollout, providing feedback on clarity, language, relevance and appropriateness of the questions.

While the PPEET captures structured feedback on engagement, it does not address deeper relational dynamics, perceived shifts in community knowledge and engagement, and reflections on the impacts of phase II activities. Focus group and interview questions were designed to align with three core principles of Indigenous KT, including relationality, reciprocity and relevance.18 21 37 Relationality recognises that knowledge is deeply connected to relationships between people, with the land and across generations, and that trust and respect are foundational to meaningful engagement. Reciprocity emphasises that knowledge sharing should be a two-way exchange where both communities and researchers benefit and learn from each other. Relevance means that KT activities should be meaningful to the community and shared in ways that are useful and culturally appropriate.18 38 By embedding these principles, the focus groups and interviews will provide a richer understanding of the community-led nature and impact of phase II activities, beyond what the survey data can capture. Focus groups will be offered as the primary method for their ability to reveal consensus and points of disagreement. Individual interviews will be available for those who do not feel comfortable in a group setting or are unable to attend the focus groups and want to share their insights.

Focus groups and interviews will be conducted virtually via Microsoft Teams. While this offers a convenient mode of participation, technological and relational challenges will need to be considered. The research team (PM and AM), with support from the main contacts for each advisory working group, will assist members in accessing the necessary technology within their communities. Prior research suggests that virtual focus groups can yield findings comparable to those conducted in-person groups, although the nature of interaction and attention to non-verbal cues may differ.39,41 The decision to host the focus groups and interviews online was informed by three primary considerations: the size of advisory working groups; their prior experience using online meeting platforms during earlier phases of the project; and the fact that advisory working group members are already familiar with one another. In addition to transcripts and video recordings of the focus groups and interviews, the facilitators (PM and AM) will record field notes and conduct debriefings immediately after each focus group and interview. These notes and discussions will capture their general observations, group dynamics and facilitator reflections and will be incorporated into the qualitative analysis. Field notes and debriefings are a recognised qualitative method that enhances analytical rigour by providing supplementary insights that may not be evident in audio recordings or transcripts alone.42 43

Patient and public involvement

Phase II of this project was a community-led research initiative that engaged 9 of the 13 First Nations communities who participated in phase I as partners in the research project. Communities established advisory working groups made up of a diverse range of perspectives including staff, people with lived and living experience, and community members and leaders. One advisory working group member (MA), and co-author on this protocol, reviewed the evaluation tools prior to data collection to ensure they were community and culturally appropriate.

Data analysis

Qualitative and quantitative data will be analysed independently but concurrently, allowing for a timely and integrated comparison of findings. With a convergent parallel design, both types of data will be equally valued and weighed in the interpretation of findings, with integration providing complementary insights and enhancing the robustness of the data.35

Quantitative data collected through the PPEET will be analysed using Microsoft Excel. Responses to Likert scale items will be summarised using measures of central tendency and dispersion, including means, medians, SD and IQRs. Categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and percentages.

Qualitative data from open-ended survey responses, focus groups and interviews will be analysed using a phenomenological approach in NVivo V.15. This method is used to explore how people make sense of their individual experiences, which is particularly relevant in Indigenous contexts where storytelling, relationality and experiential knowledge are central to meaning-making.44 Phenomenological analysis moves beyond surface-level descriptions to examine the deeper significance people attribute to their involvement in the project and creates space for culturally grounded narratives that honour Indigenous epistemologies.45 46

Two trained student researchers will conduct the initial coding of the qualitative data, with oversight and guidance from experienced team members (SK, PM and AM). The team will meet regularly to discuss emerging interpretations and maintain analytical consistency. Final qualitative analysis decisions will be reviewed by SK.

To complement the phenomenological qualitative analysis, results will be mapped onto the Engage with Impact Toolkit, a framework designed to evaluate outcomes of engagement in health research and system improvement initiatives.47 48 The toolkit outlines eight interconnected domains: (1) Knowledge and Skills, (2) Confidence and Trust, (3) Equity and Inclusion, (4) Priorities and Decisions, (5) Effectiveness and Efficiency, (6) Policy and System Change, (7) Culture and Relationships, and (8) Health and Well-being. This framework provides a structured lens to assess both individual and system-level impacts of the KMb activities and to identify how these efforts align with broader community goals.47

Evaluation findings will be shared with communities through an iterative, two-stage member-checking process. The first one will involve sharing the preliminary findings, and the second the draft manuscript. Advisory working group members will be invited to assess the language and content including any deidentified quotes, validate interpretations and provide additional input based on their experiences. This process reflects the research team’s commitment to the First Nations Principles of OCAP, respectful engagement and accountability to the participating First Nations communities.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval for this project has been granted by the Vancouver Island University Research Ethics Board (File No. 103106).

Informed consent will be obtained from all advisory working group members prior to data collection. For the online survey, consent will be collected electronically at the start of the survey. For interviews and focus groups, verbal consent will be obtained at the beginning of each session, and an additional confidentiality agreement will be reviewed with group members. All group members will be informed about the purpose of the evaluation, study procedures, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality and data protection measures, possible benefits and risks, compensation, and how the information will be used. Interview and focus group sessions will be recorded via Microsoft Teams, transcribed verbatim, deidentified and stored securely on a password-protected server at Vancouver Island University. No identifying information will be included in any dissemination materials.

This research adheres to the First Nations Principles of OCAP (Ownership, Control, Access and Possession), which affirm the rights of First Nations to govern how their data and information are collected, protected, used or shared (First Nations Information Governance Centre, 2020). In phase I, community research agreements were signed by chief and council or designated authorities to permit participation. For phase II, an addendum to the original agreements was signed by each of the nine participating First Nations communities. In alignment with OCAP, community partners will be actively engaged throughout the evaluation process, including the co-design of KMb products and activities. Each First Nation’s data will only be used for purposes for which written approval was granted.

Preliminary findings will be shared with main contacts and advisory working group members to validate interpretations, contribute to data interpretation and offer feedback stages. Group members will also have the opportunity to review and contribute to the final manuscript, be a coauthor or be acknowledged, and shape the dissemination plan. This strategy aims to ensure findings are accessible and meaningful to both academic and community audiences.

Final results will be disseminated primarily through peer-reviewed publications, academic conferences and other First Nations-centred knowledge sharing opportunities. Community partners will also be invited to co-present findings.
