A comparative analysis of food retail policy landscape in four Southeast Asian countries

ABSTRACT
SummaryChanging food retail environment in Southeast Asia has been linked with nutrition transition. While policy on food retail tends to focus on economic considerations, little is known about how nutrition considerations are integrated. This paper examines the landscape of policies addressing food retail and health in Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Thailand to inform global nutrition targets. Food retail policy landscapes in the study countries were thematically analyzed using Walt & Gilson's Policy Analysis Triangle framework. The analysis revealed that food retail policies were predominantly shaped by health, economic development, and politics, with nutrition maintaining low salience and being subsumed within food safety considerations. The study countries represented a complex food policy landscape requiring inter-sectoral collaboration and multi-level governance, yet formal monitoring mechanisms and evidence remained limited. This study recommends developing a comprehensive regional-level roadmap to support healthy food retail initiatives. By aligning nutrition priorities with existing economic and health governance systems, countries can better implement nutrition-sensitive retail policies.

Introduction

The Southeast Asia region is experiencing a double burden of malnutrition. Children aged under 5 years in this region have a higher prevalence of overweight (7.5%) than the global average (5.7%).1 The prevalence of stunting and wasting was the second highest compared to other subregions in Asia, and higher than the global average. Nearly 9% of women and men in the region lived with diabetes while 10.2% of women and 6.4% of men lived with obesity.1 More than half (55%) of the regional population cannot afford a healthy diet, which is higher than the global average (42%).2

Food environments and more specifically food retail environments play a vital role in shaping population diets and food system transformation.3 Food retail connects all of the key food system actors, including farmers, manufacturers, distributors, traders, and investors, with consumers. As food systems in Southeast Asia are transforming, it involves a rapid expansion of retail stores in particular modern retail that can increase consumer access to more processed foods in particular ultra-processed foods which tend to be high in calories, saturated and trans fats, sodium or sugar.4 Malaysia, the Philippines, Indonesia and Thailand have witnessed the largest growth in modern grocery retail market size in Southeast Asia, with figures in 2020 of $7910 million USD, $16,876 million USD, 19,684 million USD, and $25,747 million USD, respectively.5 This supermarket revolution is impacting dietary patterns and nutritional outcomes.6 Based on the 2023 data, the retail value (total sales) of ultra-processed foods per capita was highest in Thailand, followed by Malaysia, the Philippines and Indonesia (USD 175.6, 140.5, 125.1, and 90.0 per capita, respectively).2 Thailand has the highest adult population (47%) who consumed retail sugar-sweetened soft drinks by volume, which are generally known to be unhealthy, followed by the Philippines with 45%. Both figures significantly exceed the regional average of 21% for Southeast Asia.1

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) governments have indicated a high-level of commitment to take action on all forms of malnutrition and non-communicable diseases (NCDs), which is hampering social and economic development.7 As the growth of modern retail aligns with the growth of gross domestic product (GDP) per capita of these countries, cooperation between the Trade, Finance, Agriculture, Food safety, Commerce/industry and municipal governments is called for to stabilize the economic expansion while also prioritizing access to safe, healthy and nutritious foods.8 Reconciling tensions between these economic and health objectives is thus a potentially important step that involves bringing nutrition and health issues associated with the food retail to the attention of policy makers in the retail domain. More recently, there have been health sector recommendations for regulation of food retail in the interests of public health nutrition. As the role that the food retail industry plays in shaping dietary choices and overall nutritional wellbeing is increasingly recognized, the health sector continues to advocate for regulatory measures to ensure that the food retail policy landscape aligns with broader public health goals.9 However, their capacity to do so is limited by this lack of context specific policy evidence.

Food retail policy covers a wide range of government policies, regulations, and legislation that aim to promote and facilitate the sale of food and beverages to individuals for immediate or later consumption.10 The retail food environment encompasses food sold at public and privately owned venues, comprising a range of types of retail environments, which include standalone on-site and online retailing, such as modern trade, traditional, as well as licensed and unlicensed venues. Who is selling, what they provide, how they are accessed, and by whom are important factors.3 However, the determinants of the healthfulness of the retail food environments, including availability, affordability, and desirability of ultra-processed food products remain understudied. Although food retail policies have the potential to steer retailing across the food system in ways that enhance the healthfulness of the food environments, and in turn improve diets and nutrition outcomes,11 nutrition-sensitive policies in food retail represent an unmet opportunity, in particular in low- and middle-income countries.12

Understanding how nutrition is integrated and mainstreamed by diverse actors in food retail policy into their priorities is important to help move towards government's food retail policies that aim for better nutrition in the country's population. Understanding policy landscapes can inform long-term strategic planning. However, there has been limited analysis of the policy landscape in relation to food retail. Therefore, this paper aims to examine the public policy landscape relevant to food retail in four Southeast Asian countries, namely Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Thailand. These countries exhibit a high prevalence of obesity and diet-related non-communicable diseases, concomitant with ongoing food insecurity. Differences and commonalities in the policy landscape between the different countries are explored. Results from this study can help advance strategies and establish a foundational framework across Southeast Asian countries to strengthen the role that food retail policy can play in helping to achieve global nutrition targets.

Methods

Study design

This desk-based qualitative policy analysis of documents related to food retail policy for nutrition was conducted in Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines and Thailand. The researcher from each country (AFS, WST, EB, SP) led the data collection. The study analysed the content of existing policies related to promoting healthy food retail, their implementation mechanisms, and the involvement of policy actors during the development and implementation phases.

Theoretical framework

This study examined the policy landscape related to food retail in Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Thailand using Walt & Gibson's Policy Analysis Triangle13 to guide the systematic data extraction from policy documents into a matrix. The Policy Analysis Triangle draws on concepts from several disciplines to inform health policy reform in low- and middle-income countries. This model incorporates the concepts of policy content, actors, process and context. The term content refers to evidence-based content of policy. The triangular shape illustrate that none of these concepts exists in isolation; each concept is interrelated to the extent that it is influenced by other concepts.

Actors are influenced by the context within which they live and work. Context is affected by many factors, including political instability or uncertainty; by neo-liberal or socialist ideology; and by historical experience and culture. In turn actors influence the process of policy-making through their power, own values and expectations. These three concepts affect how the content of policy is shaped. The model is widely used for policy analysis in health and food and nutrition in many countries.14,15 However, its application to Southeast Asian countries has been limited, and the health sector in particular appears to have been neglected.

Case study selection

Key characteristics of countries selected in this study are presented in Table S1. Case studies selected from countries in Southeast Asia, were categorised as low- or upper-middle income in 2023,16 and had high prevalence of malnutrition and diet-related NCDs. From those countries that met these criteria, four were selected as case studies on the further criteria of feasibility of access to policy information, and allowing broader insights across countries with different economic and demographic backgrounds and forms of governance. The countries selected were Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Thailand, where three were upper-middle-income countries and one a lower-middle-income country. All countries had a documented presence of health and nutrition problems. These countries have a high prevalence of undernutrition (Indonesia), raised blood pressure (Indonesia), diabetes (Malaysia) and obesity (Thailand), and moderate or severe food insecurity (the Philippines). Governance systems vary across the four selected case study countries. Indonesia and the Philippines have non-monarchy systems (republican and presidential systems, respectively), while Malaysia and Thailand have constitutional monarchy systems. The form of legislature in all countries is bicameral.

Data sources

Data sources included government and non-governmental organisation (NGO) publications and websites, major country newspapers, and contacts with government officials and experts. Policies and actions during the last governing period were collected. The search was conducted from March to May 2023. Key search terms used included food retail policy, name of specific food products, government level, nutrition and name of country. The terms were modified for local language as seen appropriate. For data extraction, the research team developed an extraction matrix informed by Walt & Gibson's Policy Analysis Triangle13 to systematically extract relevant information from policy documents, which relates to: 1) framing of nutrition and food retail as a policy issue, 2) specific policy actions or provisions relevant to food retail, 3) resources and provisions relevant to implementation, and 4) mentions of key policy makers and other stakeholders involved in policy development in implementation.

The inclusion criteria were government policies that specifically address food retail with health and nutrition perspective aimed at improving nutrition outcomes. Policies unrelated to promoting nutrition in retail settings were excluded.

The food retailers included in this study were standalone on-site retailing, namely modern trade, traditional, licensed/unlicensed. Online-only retail and institutional food services (e.g., schools, hospitals, and hotels) were excluded as they operate under distinct systems and dynamics.

Data extraction

The researcher from each country extracted data from their respective policy documents and processed the data into a matrix with reference to the Policy Analysis Triangle.13 The matrix included themes, which were inductively determined for the indicators regarding policy content, context, processes and actors. The matrix of themes was developed for the deductive analysis of the policy documents to be conducted in local languages. Data were then translated into the English language by the country researchers.

Data analysis

The lead researcher (SP) led the thematic analysis of the processed data. The processed data for all countries were entered into a matrix using Microsoft Excel™ to enable cross-country and cross-theme analysis. Two researchers (SP, WS) analysed the data for each country, according to the coding framework (Table 1). Data coding was first undertaken by SP and WS, and then double-coded and cross-checked by AMT, PF, AFS, WST and EB. The data analysis of food retail policy followed a legislative hierarchy. The top of the hierarchy is always the legislation or acts (law). The Constitution serves as the paramount law, which is as the standard for the legislation and amendment of Acts and subordinate statutes of the country. This is followed by the more specific tool in relation to how to meet obligations under the Act, such as regulation, policy, and non-regulatory tools, such as standard, guidelines, code, and other documents, respectively. Then the data were examined according to the four elements of the Policy Analysis Triangle.

Context of food retail policy

Health context

The content of the policy documents (including laws, national action plans, government programs, news reports) indicated that policy making in food retail in all four study countries was being influenced by government recognition of health concerns, particularly the burden of obesity and NCDs (Table 2).

In some countries nutrition is less of a focus than food safety (Table 2). The policies in Indonesia and Thailand were first introduced in the context of government recognition of food safety, which focused on practices that need to safeguard the quality of food from production to consumption, with a particular focus on minimizing acute exposure to foodborne pathogens and toxins. Over time, these policies evolved to encompass the nutritional quality of food products across the food chain, including food retail. However, in other countries, such as Malaysia, separate policy documents address food safety and nutrition, enabling distinct focus areas and more targeted strategies in each domain. The government recognition of health context was largely driven by a global initiative to address issues related to obesity and NCDs that pose a significant threat to global population.

Economic context

Economic framing was another dominant type of framing across many policies in this study. There was clear framing regarding economic priorities, with reference to the national development strategy, in food retail policy documents in three countries (Table 2). The economic issues related to economic inequality, health care costs, tourism promotion, and improved grass root economies.

Malaysia's ‘Shared Prosperity Vision 2030’ document, which is a government blueprint, indicated a commitment to make the country achieve sustainable economic growth along with fair, equitable and inclusive economic distribution, across all income groups, ethnicities, regions, and supply chains (Table 2). Concern regarding the economic burden of unhealthy diets and NCDs was identified in the policy documents of government programs in Malaysia and Thailand as important to economic development goals. In Indonesia's policy documents, the regulation of food retailing in traditional markets was developed in order to support markets as tourist attractions, which can stimulate the growth of small, locally-owned businesses and contribute to regional economic growth by increasing consumption. Similarly, Thailand's ‘Street Food, Good Health’ policy aimed to frame healthy and safe street foods as national soft power to accelerate economic growth by attracting more foreign tourist to travel to Thailand.

Political context

In Malaysia and Thailand, the political context also shaped the development of food retail policy. In Malaysia's policy documents, the government commits to taking actions, including investment in good nutrition, in order to boost efforts to achieve the SDGs (Table 2). To achieve the aim of strengthening political stability underlying Shared Prosperity Vision 2030, Malaysia places high priority on children's nutrition to ensure that health policy can address health problems of this group more effectively.

The introduction section of some Thailand's policy documents included mentioning retail as political issue that was driven by particular interest of a political party (Table 2). In 2017, the cabinet in Thailand approved ‘Pracharat’ market. The Pracharat concept was initiated by the political party namely Palang Pracharat Party, a Thai civil-military political party, which later became the country government. There was also some mention in implementation of food retail with an aspiration to become the ASEAN community in 2015. Enhancing advocacy to promote production of safe and healthy food by producers is part of ASEAN Socio-Cultural Community, developed by ASEAN countries under the ASEAN Community framework.

Content and process of food retail policy

Policy design

Policy objectives

A total of nine policies, out of 13 policies, addressed nutrition targets together with other targets, in particular food safety, sanitation and hygiene, food security and relevant cost of living (Table 3). Four policies (two each from the Philippines and Thailand) had a sole objective of safeguarding the nutrition aspect in food retail. Of these four countries, they had varying focuses such as specific nutrients (sugar content), food products (junk foods, sugary drinks, products eligible for government food label), and settings (school, government office).

From the two Indonesian food retail policies, there was an emphasis on improving food safety, including nutritional quality of foods by following a voluntary food retail guideline for traditional markets, and mandatory guideline for processed food quality and safety assurance in all distribution chains. Three master policies of Malaysia, comprising both main policies and their sub-policies, illustrate the country's proactive stance on promoting healthy eating and active living in order to combat obesity and diet-related NCDs as well as strengthening food and nutrition security in general population. Three food retail policies with nutrition-specific objectives in the Philippines aimed to promote healthy eating habits through increasing availability and affordability of healthy, nutritious food/menus, and improving healthy food procurement. In Thailand, five food retail policies had an explicit nutrition goal. Some policies combined a nutrition objective with an objective of promoting good hygiene and safety management to food business operators and producers.

Type of policy endorsement

Food retail policies in the four case study countries were designed to be implemented on either a voluntary (n = 5) or mandatory (n = 6) basis (Table 3). Two policies (n = 2) are master policies, which include implementation of both mandatory and voluntary actions. Most mandatory and voluntary policies were initiated by governments, mainly through health ministries or food authorities.

In Indonesia, regulations are mandatorily enforced with the aim of protecting consumer health from the risk of unsafe food, ensuring food safety and quality. The sanctions can include fines, or closure of the business. Although these regulations are generally mandatory, several provisions allow for adjustments or flexibility. In Malaysia, policies relevant to food retail, such as those under the Food Act 1983 and Food Hygiene Regulations 2009, are mostly mandatory in nature. Voluntary initiatives, such as the Healthy Cafeteria Accreditation and Payung RAHMAH Initiatives, were introduced to promote good practices and support the availability and affordability of healthier foods. In the Philippines, policies are implemented as a combination of mandatory and voluntary measures. The local government also has the authority to craft local policies and regulate activities within their jurisdictions. In Thailand, the government's decisions were made to adopt all the policies on a voluntary basis.

Target retailers and products

The type of retailers targeted by the policies varied across countries, ranging from licensed retail to unlicensed retail and from traditional retail to modern trade retail (Table 3). Food retail policies in Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines mainly targeted retailers in the formal sector which registered their businesses with the government. The targeted licensed retailers include traditional retail markets, traditional retail or grocery stores, and modern trade retail markets, such as minimarkets, supermarkets, department stores and hypermarkets, as well as retail stores, restaurants, fast food outlets, and canteens or cafeterias. Policies that aimed at food retailers in the informal sector were observed especially in Thailand. The unlicensed retailers most frequently targeted were street food hawkers, stalls and carts.

The target products of food retail policy differed between policies. All countries implemented several policies targeting broad categories of food and beverage products such as fresh food, ready-to-eat, prepared meals and packaged foods. A few policies, particularly in the Philippines and Thailand, focused solely on junk food and/or sugar-sweetened beverages (either ready-to-drink or freshly made on-site, or both) as the objects of the policy.

Criteria for healthy food retail

Each policy has different standard procedures for assessing healthy food retail (Table 3). For example, the standards of healthy food retail in Indonesia are predominantly associated with food safety management and hygiene and sanitation, however, they differ in terms of scope and application. In Malaysia, retailers are required to adhere to the Food Act 1983 and supported by other regulations such as the Grading, Packaging, and Labelling Regulation 2008 for agricultural produce, and the Price Control and Anti-Profiteering Act 2011 for pricing standards. In the Philippines, the Pinggang Pinoy serves as the general nutrition guide. However, certain policies specify more detailed standards, such as the “green category,” which includes cut-off points for sugar, sodium, and trans fats. In Thailand, healthy food retail assessed nutritional quality of foods and beverages sold in retails in according to Department of Health's Healthy Menu Standard.

Stakeholder engagement

Multiple stakeholders were involved in policy making in all four countries. As policy development primarily occurs at the national level, key actors involved in policy decision making of food retail were ministries and government agencies (Table 3). All the countries have policy makers from the ministry responsible for the oversight of public health and nutrition in the country. In some countries the policy was made by policy makers from non-health sector such as Ministry of Education (the Philippines) and Ministry of Interior (Thailand). In Malaysia, non-health sectors also develop food retail policies with nutrition-sensitive approaches. For example, the Ministry of Domestic Trade and Costs of Living addresses food retail and affordable healthy foods as part of broader cost-of-living initiatives. Some policies are the result of collaborative efforts between multiple ministries, but are ultimately governed by the most relevant authority. Some policies in the Philippines were developed as sub-national policy, and thus, were made by local government. Across all four case study countries policy makers also engaged other stakeholders in the policy-making process, such as NGOs, civil society organizations, research institutes and universities and businesses.

Policy implementation

Governance

This study found that in all countries, the Ministry that is responsible for health matters designed and implemented policies using a top-down approach, relying on higher authority—central national government—to determine policy goals, and issue directives and orders to local agencies for implementation (Table 3). In the Southeast Asian context, local government systems in the four countries exhibit characteristics of both deconcentration (the shifting of administrative power to autonomous or elected subnational governments) and decentralisation (the transfer of authority from the central government to regional offices or units within ministries) (Table 4). Policy documents in the four countries indicated that their administration systems are highly centralized, equipped with an elaborate system of deconcentrated field offices of line agencies. Decisions for the most part are made by the central or national government while the role of subnational or local authorities is largely confined to implementation. The four countries also reported having devolved local self-government, having certain levels of fiscal and administrative autonomy. Monitoring and evaluation

Some policy documents included monitoring and evaluation (Table 3). These processes aimed to allow policy makers and responsible officers to assess how the policy evolves over time, and whether it delivers desired outcomes. Policies in all four countries include a process of verifying retailer's adherence to regulatory or policy requirements. For example, in Indonesia and the Philippines, government institutions mainly monitor food retail compliance with food safety and nutrition standard that are already in place. In Malaysia, monitoring indicators include retailer awareness, engagement, and availability of healthy retail food, while outcome indicators like population nutritional status and sugar intake reflect the combined implementation of relevant policies. Thailand focuses on food reformulation monitoring.

Despite this, there was no clear evidence showing whether the countries set a formal framework or mechanism to track policy performance on a regular basis. Moreover, the outcomes of monitoring and evaluation efforts are not consistently made publicly available.

Administration

Administration of food retail policies in all countries was led by governments to automate and streamline various tasks involved in managing the policies (Table 3). However, there was a lack of evidence describing how administrative mechanism is managed including resources used and involvement of stakeholders. In Indonesia, administration of food retail policies is predominantly government-led. However, level of government taking the lead as the administrator varies depending on the institution that issued the policy. In Malaysia, most health, food safety, and nutrition policies are administered by the Ministry of Health. In the Philippines, the administration of food retail policies is primarily led by government agencies, including the Department of Health, with support from local government units. In Thailand, all the policies were administered by health or interior ministries. The ministries serve as the main mechanism for overseeing administration, policies and practices.

Discussion

This study is the first multi-country study in Southeast Asia that analyzed the policy landscape in food retail, which is one of the key food environment domains for promoting healthy diets and reducing obesity and diet-related NCDs.3 Across the study settings, nutrition has low salience as a retail policy issue. The absence or limitation of ideas concerning nutrition in retail policy could at least partially stem from a productivist policy paradigm in the food and retail policy sectors that seeks to increase productivity and economic growth over nutrition objective.17 It is also possible that the complexity of nutrition/food and its link to retail make it difficult for creating a dialogue between policy makers and stakeholders on the topic.17 Furthermore, this study used the Policy Analysis Triangle Framework. This was a key strength of the study, as it enabled our systematic analysis of the policies by content, actors, context and process, thereby identifying gaps, barriers and opportunities to guide evidence-based policy improvement.

Most countries in the study followed multiple goals, rather than focus on a single nutrition objective. Most of their food retail policies concurrently strived to enhance food safety, sanitation, and hygiene, and improve nutrition. Multiple objectives can benefit governments in terms of alleviating tradeoffs to perform simultaneously multiple policy dimensions.18 However, they also create challenges in building coherence in the policy making, fitting policy objectives and designated policy instruments, and, as a consequence, lacking policy comprehensiveness,19 which can impose a huge cost on governments.18 Therefore, the governments need to balance the costs and benefits of multiple objectives. They may need a holistic, systemic approach with strong collaborative structures across policy dimensions, taking into account inclusion of diverse stakeholders and monitoring mechanism to ensure all the objectives being addressed simultaneously.

In line with previous evidence,3,7,20,21 this study found consistency in the framing of the health problems—particularly with reference to food safety and obesity, and in the economic considerations, such as health care costs, tourism promotion, and improved grass root economy. This study also identified the influence of political context in the development of food retail policy, such as SDG and ASEAN agenda. This is not surprising as governments globally have committed to developing coherent public policies to advance population nutrition.22,23 However, given the priority of economic growth, this could hold the food retail industry the most influence with country's decision makers, particularly demonstrating economic costs and benefits for the country. With its power, especially structural power (its economic influence and structurally privileged position), as well as instrumental power (through sponsorship, campaign and lobbying activities),24 this could pose a challenge for the governments to impose policies that limit their movement and operations for nutrition specific food retail. Therefore, taking corporate governance to a higher level with stakeholder-oriented ethics is a promising action to achieve combined economic and nutritional goals.

Implementation of food retail policies across the case study countries was a top-down implementation, focusing on high-level or central planning and decision making and delivering the tasks of lower-level policy actors. The countries also decentralize fiscal and administrative system to local government organizations. Under this structural context the policy implementation was characterized as either overlapping or as complementary with the roles and responsibilities of the different government spheres. For example, Thailand has two systems, which are deconcentrated central governments and decentralized local administration. Therefore, the local authority in implementing the policies is in hands of these provincial and local actors. However, these two actor groups are not completely separated. Consistent with previous evidence regarding decentralization systems,25 this can lead to the complementary, and often an overlapping in mandates (and policy areas), that may result in unfulfilled responsibilities or commitments, lack of enforcement of existing policies, and unfunded mandates. Therefore, national governments must play a role in establishing clear legal, regulatory arrangements to make clear assignment of tasks and functions between administrative levels, as well as incentives to improve local accountability, responsiveness, transparency and efficiency and to foster cooperation across local actors.

The study indicated absences of a formal monitoring mechanism to track policy performance on a regular basis, and evidence on performance outcome and impact. Overall, governments in the case study countries showed monitoring efforts in checking the policy performance. This reflects previous findings that in order to inform and strengthen accountability actions for food systems and governance it needs the monitoring.26 Moreover, dealing with constant change, increased complexity and uncertainty of food environments, they need to be monitored in a systematic way.27 While there is an increased attention to the monitoring of food environments, there is much less so in low and middle-income countries, where some of the nutrition burden (i.e. obesity and diet-related NCDs) is different and still exists.3 This may be due to technical and resource issues. Many governments among Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) members face shortage of capacities and capabilities to develop comprehensive strategies for policy evaluation, create demand for, and practical use of the evaluation results in policy process.28 Some governments face challenges in allocating resources to carry out the evaluations. Therefore, more government efforts, especially in Southeast Asian countries, for embedding the evaluation into policy planning, policy making and implementation processes in food retail are needed. As governance is intimately connected with issues surrounding accountability, food retail governance indicators is essential to be monitored.

This study acknowledges some limitations. The study focus was on the policy landscape analysis that specifically address food retail from a health and nutrition perspective and seek to improve nutrition outcomes. The findings of the study do not reflect the extent to which policies that do not promote nutrition outcomes in retail settings, or improve foods in supply chain. In addition, the study relied on policy documents available in the public domain, and thus limited access to non-published information such as meeting minutes or administrative reports. The analysis from other sources of information, such as in-depth interviews, may be useful to gain a more in-depth understanding of the policy landscape from the governments and key stakeholders. This study also limited the analysis to only four countries. However, they have different backgrounds in terms of economics and demography, health and nutrition outcomes and governance, and thus are likely to provide learnings that are relevant to other countries with similar backgrounds.

Conclusions

This study provides an overview of the Southeast Asia's food retail landscape by identifying policy content, policy process and key actors in four Southeast Asian countries. The study showed that food retail policies for nutrition in the case study countries are complex and inter-sectoral and has a multi-level governance. Across all countries, nutrition has low salience as a retail policy issue. Most countries followed multiple goals, rather than focus on a single nutrition objective. Development of the policies were mainly shaped by health problems, economic development and politics. The policies were governed in both deconcentrated and decentralized contexts, to promote sustainable management and participatory governance. Monitoring and evaluation efforts were observed, but presence of a formal monitoring mechanism and evidence on performance outcome and impact remained unclear. This study brings insights to inform strategic public health advocacy action to support healthy food retailing in Southeast Asia. Using multiple case studies could also support the development of a policy roadmap for the region that is rooted in a nuanced understanding of existing priorities and actions of governments and other key stakeholders.
