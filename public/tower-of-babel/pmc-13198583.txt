TiO2 nanolayer-assisted top-interface engineering for disturbance-free FeFETs: a blueprint for future van der Waals memory
Authors: Hyunjun Kang, Junhyeok Kwak, Giuk Kim, Sangho Lee, Yongyeon Kim, Sanghyun Park, Suhwan Lim, Kwangyou Seo, Wanki Kim, Daewon Ha, Jinho Ahn, Sanghun Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal-gate interlayer (G.IL)-ferroelectric (FE)-channel interlayer (Ch.IL)-Si (MIFIS) ferroelectric field-effect transistors (FeFETs) are attractive for large memory window (MW) and low-voltage FE NAND operation. Nevertheless, its fundamental operating principle also makes the device vulnerable to threshold voltage (Vth) shift under repeated disturb bias, which remains a major obstacle to array-level reliability. In this study, we employ a TiO2 nanolayer (NL) at the upper interface of the HZO FE layer to address this issue while preserving the low-voltage advantage of the MIFIS structure. The inserted TiO2 modifies the interfacial electrostatics and the ferroelectric switching characteristics at the same time. First, owing to its high dielectric constant and band alignment, it facilitates additional gate-side charge storage near the G.IL/FE interface. Second, it alters the switching nature of the underlying HZO toward a more abrupt response associated with enlarged effective domain size and improved remanent polarization. The proposed device with TiO2 NL operates below 15 V, while maintaining a large MW of 7.57 V, which is 18.9% higher than the reference device. Notably, the proposed device remains disturbance-free even after 105 cycles of 9 V/10 µs disturbance stress, whereas the counterpart experiences severe disturbance under the same conditions. Thus, we clarify that partial P switching acts as the primary driver of disturbances, as it precedes charge trapping and accelerates gate charge injection. Finally, while our top-interface engineering successfully optimizes gate-side dynamics, we propose that replacing the Si channel and bottom interlayer with emerging van der Waals (vdW) semiconductors and 2D insulators (e.g., h-BN) can fundamentally suppress channel-side charge injection (Qit). Combining this vdW-based bottom-interface with our TiO2 top-interface strategy presents a comprehensive blueprint to expand the MW and realize ultimate disturbance-free operation in next-generation computing architectures.Graphic abstract

Introduction

The integration density of 3D vertical NAND (VNAND) flash has been improved by increasing the number of stacked layers per string through continued scaling of word-line dimensions and inter-word-line spacing. However, further density enhancement in conventional charge-trap-flash (CTF)-based VNAND structures is becoming increasingly challenging. In particular, the high operating voltage (VOP) required for CTF operation intensifies the electric field across the dielectric region between adjacent word lines, thereby raising breakdown voltage (BV) concerns and limiting further structural scaling [1, 2].

To overcome these challenges, ferroelectric field-effect transistors (FeFETs) with hafnia-based ferroelectric (FE) material have attracted considerable attention as viable building blocks for next-generation FE NAND cells. It is mainly due to its superior scalability and excellent compatibility with CMOS processing, and FE NAND cells with hafnia FE can provide a wide memory window (MW) under relatively low VOP conditions [3–18]. In an FeFET, data are stored non-volatilely through the remanent polarization (Pr) of the FE layer, which modulates the threshold voltage (Vth) of the transistor. When a sufficiently high gate bias is applied, the polarization orientation of the FE layer is reversed, and the switched polarization state remains even after removal of the external bias. This retained polarization alters the surface potential of the channel, thereby producing distinguishable Vth states that can serve as the memory states.

In the early stage of FeFET development, a metal–FE–channel interlayer (Ch.IL)–Si channel (MFIS) gate stack was studied. However, MFIS FeFETs suffered from a limited MW because interface trap charges generated by charge injection (Qit) from the channel and trapped at the FE/Ch.IL interface compensates a substantial fraction of the FE polarization [19–22]. As a breakthrough to overcome these issues, metal–gate interlayer (G.IL)–FE–Ch.IL–Si (MIFIS) gate stack was proposed. As shown in Fig. 1a, introduction of an additional insulating layer between the gate electrode and the FE layer makes it possible to store gate-injected charge (Qit′) from the gate side, which induces a positive-feedback interaction between Qit′ and FE polarization [23–25]. The positive-feedback mechanism arises through the repeated sequence of the following processes. (1) During PGM/ERS operation, the applied gate bias bends the energy band of the G.IL, thereby facilitating charge injection from the gate electrode. (2) Consequently, the interface trap charge at the G.IL/FE interface, Qit′, increases. (3) The accumulated Qit′ compensates for the polarization-bound charge in the FE layer, which promotes polarization switching and transiently increases the FE capacitance. (4) This, in turn, strengthens the electric field across the G.IL and leads to more pronounced band bending. (5) The enhanced band bending then triggers further charge injection from the gate, and this sequence is repeatedly reinforced. Through this positive-feedback mechanism, the MIFIS FeFET structure can simultaneously enlarge the MW and lower the VOP [9]. However, this advantage of MIFIS FeFETs comes with a trade-off: They exhibit ΔVth even within relatively low voltage ranges, limiting their reliability in large-scale memory arrays (Fig. 1b). Under repeated PGM, READ, or PASS bias conditions, even a small amount of polarization variation in the FE layer can perturb the electric-field distribution across the stack, which is further exacerbated by a mutual reinforcement between ΔQit′ and ΔPS. This altered field profile promotes gate-side charge injection, and the injected charge in turn further strengthens the field acting on the ferroelectric layer. Once this feedback loop is triggered, the device gradually moves away from the initial erased state, leading to cumulative disturbance (Fig. 1c) [28, 29]. Therefore, the disturb problem in MIFIS FeFETs should not be interpreted simply as an independent trapping event, but rather as a coupled electrostatic process in which partial polarization response and interfacial charge generation continuously amplify one another. Although this coupling is beneficial for enlarging the memory window during normal operation, it becomes detrimental under repeated disturb bias and ultimately limits array reliability. [9]. Furthermore, such unintended ΔPS under disturb conditions is strongly associated with the subloop behavior arising from the polycrystalline nature of hafnia-based FE [30–40].

In polycrystalline FE films, the polarization response is governed by a multidomain structure, and the effective domain size reflects how broadly domains with different electrical characteristics, such as ∆P and EC, are distributed. When the domain size is relatively large, the variation among domains is smaller, resulting in a sharper switching transition of ∆P; in contrast, smaller domain sizes correspond to a wider spread of domain properties and thus a more gradual switching behavior. Since domain size strongly influences key FE device characteristics, including VPGM/VERS, incremental step pulse programming (ISPP) behavior, and disturbance immunity, developing practical and reliable strategies to control domain size is essential for fully realizing the potential of FE memory technologies. Such interface- and domain-sensitive behavior is expected to become even more critical in future ultrathin memory platforms, including those incorporating van der Waals (vdW) semiconductors and 2D dielectric layers.

Here, we focus on top-interface control as a practical means to suppress disturb behavior in MIFIS FeFETs without sacrificing the operating-voltage benefit of gate-injection-based programming. To this end, a thin TiO2 NL is inserted between the G.IL and the HZO FE layer. TiO2 layer simultaneously reshapes charge-storage conditions near the upper interface and modifies the ferroelectric switching response of the HZO beneath it. Through this dual action, the proposed structure maintains strong memory-window formation while substantially reducing unwanted threshold evolution during repetitive disturb stress. Part of this study was reported previously [10]; in the present manuscript, we expand that work by providing broader analysis of the operating mechanism, experimental characteristics, and reliability implications, and by discussing how this top-interface concept can be extended toward future memory architectures employing vdW semiconductors and 2D dielectric layers.

TiO2 NL improves the remanent polarization (Pr) value and uniformity of domain switching of the underlying HZO, which leads to low-voltage operation (< 15 V) and outstanding disturb immunity without compromising MW. Although a similar increase in Pr can be achieved by simply increasing the thickness of HZO, this approach requires a higher operating voltage to obtain the same Pr or MW. Furthermore, in FeFET fabrication, it increases the total thickness of the gate stack, which is disadvantageous from the perspectives of low-power operation and memory-cell integration density. Moreover, relatively small bandgap and high dielectric constant (~ 80) of TiO2 are advantageous for the intended device operation. Its small bandgap promotes a larger gate-injected charge by forming a potential well at the G.IL/FE interface, thereby generating additional charge beyond Qit′, as shown in Fig. 2a–b. The high capacitance value originating from the high dielectric constant of TiO2 enables the formation of a stable potential well without consuming a significant portion of the applied gate voltage, which should be allocated to interlayer band bending or HZO polarization switching for effective cell operation. The MIFIS FeFET with TiO2 NL exhibits a ΔVth of only 0.47 V at an amplitude of 9 V after 105 disturb cycles, while the control device exhibits a ΔVth of 3.57 V under the same conditions. In addition, the proposed device shows sufficient 10-year retention and endurance (~ 5 × 104 cycles) characteristics required for the FE NAND applications. While our current top-interface engineering successfully manages Qit′ and domain dynamics on a Si-channel platform, we propose that replacing the Si channel and bottom interlayer with emerging vdW semiconductors and 2D insulators can fundamentally suppress the destructive channel-side charge injection Qit. Methods/experimental

Fabrication process

The fabrication process of the MFM Capacitor was initiated by SPM cleaning of the SiO2 substrate. A 50 nm-thick TiN layer was first deposited as the bottom electrode by DC sputtering, on which a 10 nm-thick Hf0.5Zr0.5O2 (HZO) film was subsequently grown using plasma-enhanced atomic layer deposition (PEALD) at 320 °C, using tetrakis ethylmethylamido hafnium (TEMAHf) for Hf precursor, tetrakis ethylmethylamido zirconium (TEMAZr) for Zr precursor, and O2 plasma as an oxygen source, respectively. The Hf:Zr cycle ratio was controlled at 1:1 to achieve the target HZO composition. Then, another 50 nm-thick TiN layer was deposited to serve as the top electrode. The HZO film was crystallized by rapid thermal annealing (RTA) at 600 °C for 10 s in an N2 ambient. Lastly, the top electrode was defined through photoresist-based patterning, followed by TiN etching to finalize the structure.

For the MIFIS FeFET, the process began with boron ion implantation on a p-type silicon (100) wafer at 40 keV with a dose of 5 × 1012 cm−2 and a tilt angle of 7°. Then, a 500 nm-thick field oxide was grown by wet oxidation to electrically isolate each cell, followed by the definition of the active region and formation of the replacement gate using photoresist. A 10 nm-thick screening oxide was deposited by PEALD using bis-diethylamino silane (BDEAS) as a Si precursor, then replacement gate formation with photoresist and source/drain implantation was subsequently carried out using phosphorus at 50 keV with a dose of 5 × 1015 cm−2 and a tilt angle of 7°. After that, the replacement gate was removed, and RTA at 1000 °C for 30 s in an N2 ambient was performed to activate the source/drain region. The screening oxide was then removed using diluted HF (1:50), and the wafer was cleaned by SPM to form a 1.0 nm-thick channel Interlayer (Ch.IL), followed by an additional RTA at 1000 °C for 30 s in an N2 ambient. After that, a 10 nm-thick HZO FE layer was deposited using the same method as in the MFM fabrication, followed by 2.5 nm-thick TiO2 nanolayer (NL) deposition with titanium tetrachloride (TiCl4) precursor, and 4 nm-thick SiO2 gate Interlayer (G.IL) deposition with PEALD. Finally, a DC sputtering system was utilized to form a 50 nm-thick TiN gate electrode, and RTA was conducted at 600 °C for 10 s in an N2 ambient to crystallize the HZO.

Measurement methods

For the fabricated MFM Capacitors and MIFIS FE NAND cells, pulse-based measurements were performed using a semiconductor parameter analyzer (4200A-SCS, Keithley) and a pulse generator (81104A, Keysight), while transient current responses were captured with a current waveform analyzer (CX3324A, Keysight). All measurements were conducted on devices with a width (W) of 50 µm and a length (L) of 10 µm and were carried out at room temperature under ambient conditions.

The incremental step pulse programming (ISPP) evaluation, which is widely employed in current NAND flash technology for precise Vth control of each memory cell, was performed with a pulse width of 10 µs and a 0.2 V step. For pulse IV measurements, the READ operation was conducted with a rise time of 100 µs from − 3 to 7 V, and Vth was extracted by the constant current method at IDS of 1 µA. In addition, the drain voltage (VD) was fixed at 0.1 V throughout this study.

Since the devices include a 1.0 nm-thick SiO2 channel interlayer (Ch.IL), charge injection from the channel and the creation of interface trap charges (Qit) at the FE/Ch.IL interface can readily occur. Qit consists of approximately 90% stable and 10% unstable components, where the unstable component undergoes rapid detrapping after pulse application. Consequently, the MW extracted immediately after the pulse may differ from the intrinsic MW, a phenomenon referred to as read-after-write-delay (RAWD). Therefore, for all Vth shift-based analyses, a 100 ms delay was introduced between the PGM/ERS pulse and the READ pulse to ensure complete de-trapping of the unstable Qit prior to measurement.

Results and discussion

Detailed analysis on the MFM capacitor with TiO2 nanolayer

Firstly, the effects of the adjacent TiO2 NL on the HZO layer were experimentally verified in terms of material properties. To this end, phase-field simulations were performed for 10 nm-thick HZO films with various types of NL deposited on top. Figure 2c–d present the phase-field simulation results, and as shown in the figures, the average domain volume of HZO increased with a larger dielectric constant of the NL, which can be attributed to changes in the depolarization energy and domain-wall energy. As the dielectric constant of the NL increases, the domain-wall energy decreases, thereby leading to an increase in the domain volume of HZO. The 2.5 nm-thick TiO2 NL exhibited the most favorable domain size in the phase-field simulation; accordingly, the same thickness was employed in the subsequent device fabrication and experimental characterization. In addition, TiO2 NL stabilizes the o-phase of HZO by modulating the oxygen vacancy (VO) concentration in the HZO film. To verify this effect, grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) analysis for MFM stack with and without TiO2 NL was carried out (Fig. 2e). The o-phase and t-phase peaks can be distinguished around 2θ = 30.6°, and in the HZO film with the TiO2 NL, the GIXRD peak shifted toward lower angles, indicating an increased fraction of the o-phase. As shown in Fig. 2f, the extracted phase composition of HZO further supports the contribution of TiO2 to o-phase stabilization, with the TiO2-capped HZO exhibiting a higher o-phase fraction than the sample without NL. The improved o-phase fraction observed in the TiO2-inserted sample is consistent with the hypothesis that the TiO2 NL modifies the local oxygen environment of HZO. However, this mechanism is not directly proven by the present data. While GIXRD data clearly supports the phase evolution, it does not provide direct information on the chemical-state variation across the HZO thickness. Additional depth-resolved chemical characterization, such as XPS depth profiling, would be necessary to directly verify the proposed oxygen-vacancy modulation and to determine how far the effect of the TiO2 NL extends into the HZO layer.

Next, for the measurements with MFM capacitors, Fig. 3a–b shows the measured polarization–electric field (P-E) hysteresis subloops, depending on the presence of the superjacent TiO2 NL as outlined in each figure. The device with TiO2 NL exhibits improved ferroelectricity with 2Pr value of 34.2 μC/cm2, compared to the one without TiO2 NL with 2Pr value of 28.7 μC/cm2. Figure 3c depicts the extracted 2Pr values as a function of the electric field on the HZO layer. TiO2 NL effectively suppresses ΔPS below EFE of 1.3 MV/cm but enhances it above this level. These results demonstrate that TiO2 NL increases the orthorhombic phase (o-phase) content of the ferroelectric layer and makes the subloop behavior of the underlying HZO more abrupt. Figure 3d shows the time-dependent polarization switching properties (ΔP(t)/2PS) of each device. The symbols indicate experimental data, and the lines represent the fitted curves based on the nucleation-limited switching (NLS) model, which includes the Lorentzian distribution functions, as plotted in Fig. 3e. For the device with TiO2 NL, ΔPS is more suppressed at 1.5 V, but becomes larger at voltages above 2 V than its counterpart. Figure 3f presents the extracted half-width at half-maximum (ω) values of each curve from Fig. 3e. Although the physical domain size cannot be directly determined by the extracted ω value, the observed tendency toward more uniform switching is consistent with the behavior typically associated with domain enlargement. Comprehensive investigation of the MIFIS FeFET with TiO2 NL

To examine the performance enhancement attributed to the TiO2 NL, MIFIS FeFETs were fabricated. Figure 4a exhibits the gate stack of the proposed device containing the TiO2 NL positioned between G.IL and FE layer. Figure 4b presents the ISPP characteristics and the corresponding slope values of the devices. The device with TiO2 NL shows an 18.9% increase in MW at maximum VPGM, owing to the increased o-phase content in FE. Furthermore, a negligible ΔVth is observed up to VPGM of 11.6 V, whereas the original device presents a ΔVth onset at VPGM of 8.8 V. This indicates that TiO2 NL extends the disturb-free region, where any undesirable ΔVth does not occur. The ISPP slope characteristics were also extracted, and the device with TiO2 NL exhibits a higher maximum slope (~ 4.5), which is 1.88 times larger than that of the device without TiO2 NL, attributed to an abrupt ΔPS of HZO. While the device without TiO2 NL exhibits a considerable ΔVth across all amplitudes, the proposed device maintains a negligible ΔVth up to VPGM of 11 V. Figure 6a shows the measured pulse IDS-VGS curves for the MIFIS FeFET with TiO2 NL, where each curve corresponds to a cell programmed to a different state. Demonstration of stable 8-level states indicates the feasibility of triple-level-cell (TLC) operation. Notably, for the smaller initial MW, the breakdown of the device occurs at a larger number of cycles. This is due to reduced charge trapping and trap generation, which slows down the degradation of the gate stack. Thus, the proposed device has stable reliability characteristics for use as a FE NAND cell. While the proposed TiO2 top-interface engineering successfully optimizes gate-side dynamics and achieves remarkable disturbance immunity on a conventional Si-channel platform, the fundamental suppression of channel-side charge injection remains a critical milestone for ultimate device scaling. As inferred from our positive-feedback model, completely mitigating the destructive Qit at the bottom interface is essential for maximizing the MW and ensuring extreme long-term reliability. In this context, emerging vdW semiconductors and 2D insulators (e.g., h-BN), which inherently possess trap-free vdW gaps, present an ideal solution for replacing the Si channel and bottom interlayer. Therefore, the top-interface design principles established in this study serve as a crucial foundational blueprint. Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated a low-voltage operational and disturb-free MIFIS FeFET by implementing a top-interface engineering strategy that incorporates a multifunctional TiO2 NL between the G.IL and FE layer. Material analysis on the MFM capacitors revealed that the adjacent TiO2 NL increases the o-phase fraction of HZO, enlarges the effective domain size, and makes the subloop switching behavior more abrupt. In addition, owing to its high-k nature and favorable band alignment, the TiO2 NL promotes gate-side charge injection by forming a stable potential well at the G.IL/FE interface. Through these combined effects, the proposed MIFIS FeFET achieves a wide MW of ~ 7.57 V together with a low operating voltage below 15 V. Furthermore, the abrupt subloop behavior effectively suppresses unintended ΔPS under repetitive disturb conditions, enabling disturb-free operation up to 105 cycles by decoupling the mutual reinforcement between partial polarization switching and gate charge injection. The device also exhibits stable 8-level states, 10-year retention, and endurance of 5 × 104 cycles, demonstrating its potential for practical FE NAND applications. These results highlight that top-interfacial nanolayer engineering is an effective strategy for simultaneously controlling gate-side charge injection and FE switching. Building upon this success, we propose that integrating this top-interface optimization with emerging vdW semiconductors and 2D insulators at the bottom-interface will fundamentally suppress the destructive channel-side charge injection. Ultimately, this synergistic combination provides a comprehensive blueprint and practical design framework for realizing ideal, disturbance-free, and ultrathin next-generation memory platforms.
