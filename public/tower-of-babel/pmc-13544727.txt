Controlled hypotension therapy with isosorbide dinitrate for postoperative bleeding of transurethral resection of the prostate
Authors: Zihao Li, Hongliang Li, Zhenzhuo Chen, Li Wang, Tie Chong, Dong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Postoperative bleeding is a well-known complication of transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP). This study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of controlled hypotension using isosorbide dinitrate (ISDN) as a treatment for refractory postoperative bleeding after TURP when conventional non-surgical treatments have failed.Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted of eight patients who experienced postoperative bleeding following TURP and did not respond to standard non-surgical interventions. Patients were treated with an intravenous infusion of ISDN to induce controlled hypotension. The hemostatic effect was subsequently observed and evaluated once the mean arterial pressure was lowered to a target range of 66–73 mmHg.Outcomes:Hemostasis was achieved rapidly in all eight patients following the induction of controlled hypotension. The treatment demonstrated a highly satisfactory clinical effect, and no severe adverse events or complications were observed during or after the intervention.Conclusion:Controlled hypotension via intravenous ISDN is a safe, rapidly effective, and promising therapeutic alternative for managing refractory postoperative bleeding after TURP when conventional non-surgical measures fail.

Introduction

Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) is the most common surgical option for treating benign prostatic hyperplasia and has been proven to be a safe and effective treatment modality[1,2]. Despite advances in surgical techniques, a small number of patients still experience refractory postoperative bleeding due to inadequate intraoperative hemostasis, postoperative prostatic fossa spasms, or other physiological factors. Currently, standard conservative management for postoperative bleeding is largely limited to continuous bladder irrigation and the administration of hemostatic agents. Once conservative measures fail, patients are often forced to undergo a secondary surgery to achieve hemostasis. Controlled hypotension refers to the use of non-pharmacological anesthetic techniques and adjunctive medications to lower mean arterial pressure (MAP) and heart rate, thereby reducing perfusion to the surgical site while avoiding ischemia in vital organs[3]. Numerous studies have confirmed its reliable hemostatic efficacy[4,5]. However, there remains a paucity of reports regarding its application as a salvage treatment for postoperative bleeding following TURP. Physiologically, the bleeding rate from the prostatic fossa wound is closely correlated with the local vascular perfusion pressure[2]. For bleeding refractory to conventional treatments, inducing controlled hypotension can effectively lower the systemic MAP. This hemodynamic shift significantly decelerates local blood flow, creating an ideal microenvironment for platelet aggregation and early clot formation. Concurrently, the reduction in local mechanical blood wash-out mitigates the secondary activation of the fibrinolytic system, thereby promoting wound clot stabilization and endogenous hemostasis[6].

Isosorbide dinitrate (ISDN), a widely utilized vasodilator, exhibits unique pharmacological characteristics in achieving controlled hypotension. Compared to traditional potent antihypertensive agents that can easily trigger drastic blood pressure fluctuations, ISDN provides a more stable hypotensive effect and is easier to regulate clinically[7]. Furthermore, while effectively reducing systolic blood pressure (SBP), ISDN has a minimal impact on diastolic blood pressure and offers the distinct advantage of dilating coronary arteries to improve myocardial perfusion. Consequently, it maximizes the perfusion safety of vital organs, such as the heart, while successfully achieving the target blood pressure range for hemostasis[8].

Based on the aforementioned mechanisms and pharmacological advantages, this study retrospectively analyzed eight patients who experienced severe postoperative bleeding following TURP and remained unresponsive to standard non-surgical interventions. The study aimed to evaluate the hemostatic efficacy and clinical safety of controlled hypotension therapy with ISDN, with the ultimate goal of providing a safe, highly effective, and promising alternative treatment strategy for this challenging postoperative complication.

Methods

A retrospective case series was conducted involving eight patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia (mean age, 72.4 years; range, 68–79 years) who underwent TURP at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University between January 2022 and December 2023. Baseline clinical data included the mean three-dimensional prostate measurements determined by preoperative imaging (length × width × height). For patients receiving anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy, medications were routinely withheld for 14 days prior to surgery according to standard clinical protocols. Preoperative routine blood tests and coagulation profiles were within normal limits for all patients.

Inclusion criteria required the occurrence of severe postoperative bleeding, defined as persistently bright-red bladder irrigation effluent. “Failure of conservative treatment” was strictly defined as the inability to achieve hemostasis despite at least 90 minutes of maximum continuous bladder irrigation, intravenous administration of hemostatic agents, and continuous Foley catheter balloon traction. Patients who met these criteria and did not exhibit hemodynamic instability requiring immediate surgical exploration were included in this study.

Upon meeting the inclusion criteria, patients were treated with an intravenous infusion of 10 mg ISDN dissolved in 250 mL of 5% dextrose solution. The initial infusion rate was set at 16 drops per minute, with blood pressure monitored every 3–5 minutes. The rate was incrementally increased by 5–8 drops per minute based on the patient’s blood pressure response until the SBP was successfully lowered to a target range of 85–95 mmHg.

During controlled hypotension, the ISDN infusion rate was continuously titrated according to the color of the bladder irrigation effluent. Once the effluent became visibly clear and remained so for 5–15 minutes, the infusion rate was gradually tapered by 5–8 drops per minute until the infusion was completely discontinued. This case series is reported in accordance with the PROCESS guidelines.

Discussion

While TURP remains the gold-standard surgical treatment for benign prostatic hyperplasia[9], refractory postoperative bleeding is a severe complication[10,11]. When standard conservative measures – such as continuous bladder irrigation and Foley catheter balloon traction – fail, patients face the imminent risk of secondary surgical intervention. In this case series, we demonstrated that controlled hypotension with intravenous ISDN effectively achieved hemostasis in eight patients who were refractory to conservative management, providing a viable non-surgical salvage strategy.

The core mechanism underlying the efficacy of ISDN-induced controlled hypotension lies in hemodynamic alterations within the prostatic fossa. The postoperative bleeding rate is causally linked to local vascular perfusion pressure. By activating guanylate cyclase and increasing cGMP levels via nitric oxide release, ISDN induces systemic vasodilation[12]. Lowering the systemic MAP to a target of 66–73 mmHg directly decreases the local hydrostatic pressure at the resection site. This reduction in local mechanical blood-flow wash-out facilitates platelet aggregation, allows the formation of stable early clots, and suppresses the secondary activation of the fibrinolytic system, thereby achieving endogenous hemostasis[13].

The rationale for selecting ISDN over other potent vasodilators is based on the specific demographic profile of TURP patients, who are predominantly elderly and frequently present with cardiovascular comorbidities. The critical premise of controlled hypotension in this population is the maintenance of organ perfusion. ISDN effectively lowers SBP while exerting minimal impact on diastolic blood pressure[14]. This dynamic preserves coronary perfusion and myocardial oxygen supply, significantly mitigating the risk of myocardial ischemia[15]. However, this intervention carries potential systemic risks, including severe hypotension and inadequate end-organ perfusion. Therefore, continuous monitoring is mandatory to ensure that MAP is strictly maintained above 65 mmHg to avoid ischemic end-organ damage [16].

This study has inherent limitations. Given the small sample size (n = 8) and retrospective design, the universal efficacy and exact incidence of adverse reactions remain unknown and cannot be definitively extrapolated. Currently, determining the feasibility and safety of initiating controlled hypotension relies heavily on limited empirical clinical judgment. To overcome the constraints of small-scale observational studies, future clinical practice must incorporate advanced decision support systems. As recent global bibliometric analyses indicate, the integration of artificial intelligence into medical practice represents a major frontier in healthcare[17]. In the context of TURP complications, applying these advanced AI models to synthesize complex perioperative variables and massive electronic health records could accurately predict individual bleeding risks and precisely determine the feasibility of controlled hypotension for each patient[8]. This transition from small-sample empirical observation to AI-assisted precision medicine will optimize both the safety and efficacy of the intervention.

Conclusion

This research shows that controlled hypotension therapy with ISDN is a promising treatment for postoperative bleeding after TURP, which is effective and has no serious complications. Controlled hypotension could be used as an important therapy after conventional non-surgical treatment has failed. Although all patients with postoperative bleeding after TURP treated with controlled hypotension achieved satisfactory results, this research, involving few cases, is currently in the preliminary exploration stage. Further cases are needed to verify these findings.
