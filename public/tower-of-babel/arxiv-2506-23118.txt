Abstract
Distributed integrated sensing and communication (DISAC) systems are key enablers for 6G networks, offering the capability to jointly track multiple targets using spatially distributed base stations (BSs). A fundamental challenge in DISAC is the seamless and efficient handover of target tracks between BSs with partially overlapping fields of view, especially in dense and dynamic environments. In this paper, we propose a novel target handover framework based on belief propagation (BP) for multi-target tracking in DISAC systems. By representing the probabilistic data association and tracking problem through a factor graph, the proposed method enables efficient marginal inference with reduced computational complexity. Our framework introduces a principled handover criterion and message-passing strategy that minimizes inter-BS communication while maintaining tracking continuity and accuracy. We demonstrate that the proposed handover procedure achieves performance comparable to centralized processing, yet significantly reduces data exchange and processing overhead. Extensive simulations validate the robustness of the approach in urban tracking scenarios with closely spaced targets.



I
Introduction

The integrated sensing and communications paradigm leverages existing communication infrastructure for sensing and localization, either as a standalone service or as an auxiliary function to support communication
[
1
]
. In urban environments, scalability challenges in communication systems emerge from the high density of users. A common remedy is to partition the service area into cells
[
2
]
, each managed by a single base station, with users handed over from one cell to another
[
3
]
. Extending this divide-and-conquer strategy to integrated sensing and communications results in the concept of distributed integrated sensing and communications
[
4
,
5
]
.

The distributed integrated sensing and communications setup leads naturally to the multi-sensor multi-target tracking problem, which involves tracking multiple targets using multiple homogeneous sensors (base stations, in the context of distributed integrated sensing and communications), each with limited field of view
[
6
]
. multi-target tracking jointly estimates the number and states of a time-varying set of targets from noisy measurements, while addressing measurement origin uncertainty, missed detections, false alarms, and an unknown target count. Centralized processing of multi-sensor multi-target tracking can significantly outperform single-sensor approaches
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
, but distributed multi-sensor multi-target tracking without a fusion center introduces further challenges
[
11
]
.
Target handover
, where tracking responsibility shifts from one sensor to another as targets move across field of views, is an effective strategy to address these challenges
[
12
]
.

:
Example of a distributed integrated sensing and communications scenario with a central unit. Four base stations are shown, with finite and overlapping field of views. Target handover enables efficient tracking without needing to share raw data with the central unit.

Recent distributed integrated sensing and communications research has produced various target handover methods. A generalized framework for target mobility management, including dynamic sensing configuration handover, was presented in
[
13
]
. A primary base station handover within a virtual sensing cell—defined as three neighbouring cells—as well as a handover mechanism between virtual sensing cells was proposed in
[
14
]
. In
[
15
]
, a random finite set-based handover method has been developed. It achieves excellent performance but has poor scalability due to the need to maintain numerous global association hypotheses. In the multi-target tracking context, random finite set-based methods
[
16
]
are often contrasted with low-complexity belief propagation-based approaches, which scale linearly with the number of targets and measurements
[
17
]
. This motivates combining belief propagation with target handover.

In this paper, we propose a novel belief propagation-based handover mechanism for multi-target tracking in distributed integrated sensing and communications systems. Our specific contributions are as follows:
(i)
we introduce a new handover algorithm for belief propagation-based multi-cell multi-target tracking, incorporating a principled message-passing criterion based on target belief states;
(ii)
we formulate the handover process using factor graphs, enabling compatibility with existing belief propagation-multi-target tracking approaches while minimizing inter-base station communication;
(iii)
we systematically compare centralized, distributed, and handover tracking architectures, demonstrating that our approach matches the accuracy of centralized tracking at a fraction of the communication cost; and
(iv)
we release an open-source simulation framework to facilitate reproducibility and further exploration in the community. It is located at
https://github.com/BaiLiping/BPTargetHandover
.

II
System Model

In this section, we describe the kinematic state of the targets and the measurement model.

Target State Model

At discrete time
k
, each target
i
is represented by a kinematic state vector
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{i}
that comprises its position and velocity. The state transition density is given by
f(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{i}|\mathbf{x}_{k-1}^{i})
.
In this paper, we assume the constant velocity and stochastic acceleration kinematic model
[
18
]
,
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{i}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}_{k-1}^{i}+\mathbf{v}_{k-1}^{i}
, where
\mathbf{F}
is the state transition matrix and
\mathbf{v}_{k}^{i}
the additive zero-mean Gaussian process noise with covariance matrix
\mathbf{Q}
.

Measurement Model

In the distributed integrated sensing and communications system, there are
n_{s}>1
base stations with known positions and partially overlapping field of views. At time
k
, a set with
n_{m}
measurements

\mathbf{Z}_{k,s}=[\,\mathbf{z}_{k,s}^{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{k,s}^{n_{m}}\,],

is obtained by the base station
s
. Each element in the set can originate from a target or clutter, where clutter is modeled by Poisson point process with intensity
c(\mathbf{z})
.
We introduce a detection probability function on the kinematic state
p_{d}^{s}(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{i})\in[0,1]
. To model the field of view of base station
s
, we specify the following: if
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{i}\in\mathrm{FoV}_{s}
, then
p_{d}^{s}(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{i})>0
, and
p_{d}^{s}(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{i})=0
otherwise.
When a measurement
\mathbf{z}_{k,s}^{m}
originates from target
i
, the measurement
\mathbf{z}_{k,s}^{m}

is modeled as

\mathbf{z}_{k,s}^{m}=\mathbf{h}_{s}\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{i}\right)+\mathbf{w}_{k,s}^{i},

where
\mathbf{h}_{s}(\cdot)
is a nonlinear measurement function for base station
s
, and
\mathbf{w}_{k,s}^{i}
is the additive zero mean Gaussian measurement noise with covariance matrix
\mathbf{R}
. Measurement includes tuples comprising the time of arrival and angle of arrival of channel paths
[
19
]
, obtained through standard channel parameter estimation methods
[
20
]
.
The equivalent likelihood function for measurement originating from a known target is:
f(\mathbf{z}_{k,s}^{m}|\mathbf{x}_{k}^{i})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_{k,s}^{m};\mathbf{h}_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{i}),\mathbf{R})
.

III
belief propagation-Based Multi-Sensor Multi-Target Tracking

To introduce belief propagation-based multi-target tracking in a distributed integrated sensing and communications setup, we first briefly introduce factor graphs and the sum-of-product algorithm. Then, we discuss the case of a single-base-station belief propagation-multi-target tracking. Finally, we explain centralized and distributed processing in the context of multiple-base-station belief propagation-multi-target tracking.

III-A

Factor Graph and belief propagation Basics

Factor graphs convey the computational structure of a joint probability density function
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
and are widely used for marginal posterior computations in robotics, communication, sensing, and machine learning
[
21
]
. In a factor graph, factors (usually depicted as squares) and variables (traditionally shown as circles) are connected when a function takes that variable as input. belief propagation is a message passing method on a factor graph, whereby messages (functions) are computed and passed between factors and variables, which provides an efficient way to determine the marginals (also called the beliefs)
f(x_{i})
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

III-B

Single base station multi-target tracking

We first consider the case of a single base station. We will introduce an augmented state to capture the unknown number of targets. Then we detail the prior, the likelihood, the factorization of the joint density, and the beliefs, largely based on
[
17
, Sections IV and VIII]
. Our focus will be on one time step (from
k-1
to
k
), to simplify the notation, at the cost of some mathematical rigour.

III-B
1
Augmented States

To model the unknown and time-varying number of targets, we augment the kinematic state
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{j}
with an auxiliary binary variable
r_{k}^{j}
, where
r_{k}^{j}=1
indicates the existence of the potential target and
r_{k}^{j}=0
otherwise. The augmented state of a potential target is denoted by
\mathbf{y}_{k}^{j}=[\mathbf{x}_{k}^{j},r_{k}^{j}]
.
We distinguish between
new potential targets
and
legacy potential targets
, where every measurement is treated as an initial detection of a new potential target, and these new potential targets become legacy potential targets in subsequent time steps.
We denote the augmented state for new potential target by
\mathbf{\overline{y}}_{k}
, and the augmented state for legacy potential target by
\mathbf{\underline{y}}_{k}
. We denote the vector for all potential targets by
\mathbf{Y}_{k}
, with

\mathbf{Y}_{k}=[\mathbf{\underline{Y}}_{k},\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{k}]
.

III-B
2
Prediction and Prior

We denote the posterior (or the belief) of the
j
th potential target at time
k-1
by
f(\mathbf{\underline{y}}^{j}_{k-1})
. Each of the potential targets at time
k-1
corresponds to a legacy potential target at time
k
. The state transition density for the
j
th legacy potential target is

\displaystyle f(\underline{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{j}|\underline{\mathbf{y}}_{k-1}^{j})=\begin{cases}f_{d}({\mathbf{\underline{x}}}_{k}^{j})&\underline{r}_{k}^{j}=0,\underline{r}_{k-1}^{j}=0,\\
0&\underline{r}_{k}^{j}=1,\underline{r}_{k-1}^{j}=0,\\
(1-p_{s}({\mathbf{\underline{x}}}_{k-1}^{j}))\,f_{d}(\mathbf{\underline{x}}_{k}^{j})&\underline{r}_{k}^{j}=0,\underline{r}_{k-1}^{j}=1,\\
p_{s}(\mathbf{\underline{x}}_{k-1}^{j})\,f(\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j}|\mathbf{\underline{x}}_{k-1}^{j})&\underline{r}_{k}^{j}=1,\underline{r}_{k-1}^{j}=1,\end{cases}

(1)

where
p_{s}(\mathbf{\underline{x}}_{k-1}^{j})
is the survival probability of the potential target and
f_{d}(\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j})
is a dummy distribution (in the sense of being an arbitrary placeholder, that will be marginalized out when computing the beliefs). From the posterior of previous step
f(\mathbf{\underline{y}}^{j}_{k-1})
and the transition density
f(\underline{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{j}|\mathbf{\underline{y}}_{k-1}^{j})
, we can derive the prior probability density function for the
j
th legacy potential target at time
k
as

f(\mathbf{\underline{y}}_{k}^{j})=\int f(\mathbf{\underline{y}}_{k}^{j}|\mathbf{{\underline{y}}}_{k-1}^{j}){f}(\mathbf{\underline{y}}_{k-1}^{j})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{\underline{y}}_{k-1}^{j}}
.

The prior for the
m
th new potential target is set to be
f_{n}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{m})
, which in this paper is heuristically determined from the
m
th measurement
\mathbf{z}_{k}^{m}
.

III-B
3
Likelihood

To account for the unknown association between measurements and targets, we introduce two sets of discrete association variables: track-oriented association variables
\mathbf{a}_{k}
and measurement-oriented association variables
\mathbf{b}_{k}
. Specifically,
a_{k}^{j}=m
indicates that the
j
th potential target is associated with measurement
m
(while
a_{k}^{j}=0
indicates that the
j
th potential target is not detected), and
b_{k}^{m}=j
indicates that measurement
m
is associated with
j
th potential target (while
b_{k}^{m}=0
indicates that measurement
m
is associated with the new potential target).
To ensure that
\mathbf{a}_{k}
and
\mathbf{b}_{k}
are mutually consistent, there is a constraint
\Psi(\mathbf{a}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k})\in\{0,1\}
. The binary compatibility function
\Psi(\cdot)
can be factorized
[
17
, Eq. (21)]
, which makes it computationally efficient in the factor graph representation and the associated belief propagation.

We now introduce two so-called
pseudo-likelihood
functions for each legacy potential target (denoted by
q(\cdot)
for the
j
th legacy potential target) and for each new potential target (denoted by
v(\cdot)
for the
m
th new potential target).

These are defined as

\displaystyle q(\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j},\underline{r}_{k}^{j},a_{k}^{j};\mathbf{Z}_{k})=\begin{cases}\frac{p_{d}(\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j})}{{c}(\mathbf{z}_{k}^{m})}f(\mathbf{z}_{k}^{m}|\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j})&a_{k}^{j}=m\neq 0,r_{k}^{j}=1,\\
1-p_{d}(\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j})&a_{k}^{j}=0,r_{k}^{j}=1,\\
0&a_{k}^{j}\neq 0,r_{k}^{j}=0,\\
1&a_{k}^{j}=0,r_{k}^{j}=0.\end{cases}

(2)

and

\displaystyle v(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{m},r_{k}^{m},b_{k}^{m};\mathbf{z}_{k}^{m})

(3)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&b_{k}^{m}\neq 0,r_{k}^{j}=1,\\
\frac{\mu_{n}}{{c}(\mathbf{z}_{k}^{m})}f_{n}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{m})f(\mathbf{z}_{k}^{m}|\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{m})&b_{k}^{m}=0,r_{k}^{j}=1,\\
f_{d}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{m})&r_{k}^{j}=0.\end{cases}

where
\mu_{n}\geq 0
is the mean number of newborn targets, which is Poisson distributed. The clutter intensity
c(\mathbf{z})
is the expected number of clutter in an infinitesimal region around
\mathbf{z}
, and
c(\mathbf{z})=\mu_{c}\,f_{c}(\mathbf{z})
, where
f_{c}(\mathbf{z})
is the clutter probability density function.

4
Factorization

With the pseudo-likelihoods defined, we can factorize the joint probability density function at time step
k
based on conditional indepence as

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{Y}_{k},\mathbf{a}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k},\mathbf{Z}_{k})

(4)

\displaystyle\propto\Psi(\mathbf{a}_{k},\mathbf{b}_{k})\prod_{j=1}^{n_{p}}f(\mathbf{\underline{y}}_{k}^{j})q(\underline{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{j},a_{k}^{j};\mathbf{Z}_{k})\prod_{m=1}^{n_{m}}v(\overline{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{m},b_{k}^{m};\mathbf{z}_{k}^{m}),

The corresponding factorization is presented by the factor graph in Fig.
2
.

The Bayesian estimation process is expressed in the messages passed in the factor graph from Fig.

\mathbf{y}_{k}^{j}
will be a weighted combination of all the cases (associations). We refer the readers to

for the equations for each message.

III-B
5
Posterior and Beliefs

At time step
k
, the inputs to the factor graph are the beliefs from time
k-1
, as shown in Fig.
2
. For the
j
th legacy potential target, propagating its input beliefs through the loopy factor graph results in an approximated posterior density
f(\underline{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{j}|\mathbf{Z}_{k})
. From this approximated posterior, two updated beliefs at time
k
can be obtained: one over the target’s kinematic state
\tilde{p}(\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j})=\sum_{\underline{r}^{j}_{k}}f(\underline{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{j}|\mathbf{Z}_{k})
and one over its existence
\tilde{p}(\underline{r}_{k}^{j})=\int f(\underline{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{j}|\mathbf{Z}_{k})\mathrm{d}\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j}
. Analogous beliefs are computed for each new potential target.
The existence belief
\tilde{p}(r_{k}^{j}=1)
, also known as the posterior existence probability, is used to manage the potential targets: we declare the
j
th potential target to exist if

\tilde{p}(r_{k}^{j}=1)>P_{\mathrm{th}},

and we prune that potential target if

\tilde{p}(r_{k}^{j}=1)<P_{\mathrm{prune}},

where
P_{\mathrm{th}}
and
P_{\mathrm{prune}}
are predetermined thresholds for declaring a target exists and pruning, respectively.

III-C

Multi-base station multi-target tracking

Now, we extend our discussion from the single base station to multi-target tracking with
n_{s}>1
belief propagations. This is formally known as a multi-sensor multi-target tracking problem. Each base station
s
obtain a set with
n_{m_{s}}
measurements
\mathbf{Z}_{k,s}
. There are now two extreme strategies:

•

Distributed Processing:
Each BS operates independently and executes the belief propagation-multi-target tracking process outlined in Section
III-B
. This means that when potential targets leave the field of view of a BS, they are lost to that BS in the sense that no more measurements will be associated with it. In addition, if that target enters the field of view of another base station, its track must be initialized from scratch.

•

Centralized Processing:
In this case, there is a central unit (See Fig.
1
) to which all base stations send their measurements. There are several ways to proceed
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, but we present a simple sequential approach. In this sequential approach, the base stations are ordered and the central unit processes the measurements from each base stations sequentially, thereby generating new potential targets, to which the measurements from the subsequent base stations can be associated. Effectively, sequentially updating the state beliefs in a sequence of
n_{s}
base stations in one time is the equivalent of updating the state beliefs in
n_{s}
time in a distributed setup.

Example 1

(Fusion between two BSs)
.

To illustrate the sequential processing approach in the context of belief propagation-multi-target tracking, we discuss the procedure for one new target located within the overlapping field of view between base station1 and base station2. There are two measurements,

\mathbf{z}_{k,1}
at BS1 and
\mathbf{z}_{k,2}
at BS2, originating from the target:
\mathbf{z}_{k,1}
is first used to initiate the new potential target with posterior
f(\mathbf{\overline{y}}_{k}^{1}|\mathbf{z}_{k,1})
. Then
\mathbf{z}_{k,2}
is used to update the posterior
f(\mathbf{\overline{y}}_{k}^{1}|\mathbf{z}_{k,1})
to
f(\mathbf{\underline{y}}_{k}^{1}|\mathbf{z}_{k,1},\mathbf{z}_{k,2})
.

IV
belief propagation-Based Target Handover

In this section, we introduce the proposed target handover mechanisms. By target handover, we mean sharing the target information from one BS to one or more BSs, without deleting any information at transmission. The handover should not interfere with the belief propagation-multi-target tracking process and avoid double-counting of measurements.
We will present two target handover approaches: (i) with measurements exchange that exploits the overlapping field of view of base stations to improve target state estimation while minimizing the information exchanged between base stations (illustrated in Fig.
2
); (ii) without measurements exchange in order to highlight the benefits of sharing measurements in the numerical experiments.

We focus on a single pair of base stations for notational clarity, though the methods extend to any configuration. We denote the base station initiating the handover as base station-Tx (index
s_{t}
) and the base station receiving the information as base station-Rx (index
s_{r}
). We assume parallel processing between the two base stations. That is, the handover priors are received by the base station-Rx before prediction steps at time
k
, and the handover measurements are received by the base station-Tx before the updating steps at time
k
.

IV-A

Operation at the Transmitting BS (base station-Tx)

From BS-Tx, the handover process requires keeping track of several additional variables, as described below:

1.

BS-Tx runs BP as described in Section
III-B
. Hence, the target handover algorithm does not interrupt the belief propagation-multi-target tracking process at base station-Tx.
As a side-effect of running BP, we obtain the marginal association probabilities
f^{(s_{t})}(a_{k}^{j}|\mathbf{Z}_{k})
. We can then obtain the index of the most likely associated measurement for the
j
th potential target, denoted
m^{j}=\arg\max_{m}f^{(s_{t})}(a_{k}^{j}=m|\mathbf{Z}_{k})
. If
m^{j}=0
, the potential target is most likely to be miss-detected and no measurement is handed over. The notation
f^{(s_{t})}
indicates that this density is computed at BS
s_{t}
.

2.

For each PT
j
, we check if the target handover criterion
h_{c}^{j}\in\{0,1\}
is met:

\displaystyle h_{c}^{j}=\mathbb{I}\{f^{(s_{t})}(\underline{r}_{k}^{j}=1)>P_{\mathrm{th}}\}

(5)

\displaystyle\times\int p_{d}^{(s_{r})}(\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j})\,f^{(s_{t})}(\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j})\,\mathrm{d}\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j}>\Gamma,

where
f^{(s_{t})}(\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j})
is the marginal of the prior
f^{(s_{t})}(\underline{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{j})
over the kinematic state and
f^{(s_{t})}(\underline{r}_{k}^{j})
is the marginal of the prior over existence;
\mathbb{I}\{\mathsf{P}\}
is an indicator function valued
1
if
\mathsf{P}
is true and
0
otherwise;
p_{d}^{(s_{r})}(\underline{\mathbf{x}}_{k}^{j})
is the base station-Rx detection probability;
\Gamma
a predefined handover threshold.

3.

potential targets for which
h^{j}_{c}=1
and
m^{j}>0
are potential candidates for handover. To avoid repeated handovers of the same potential target, each potential target
j
is assigned a unique local label, say
L^{(s_{t})}_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
, at base station-Tx. Once a target has been handed over for the first time, its label is appended to a local list
\mathcal{T}^{(s_{t}\to s_{r})}
, where the subscript
(s_{t}\to s_{r})
indicates it is a list for handover labels from base station-Tx to base station-Rx.

4.

The actual handover now proceeds. If
h^{j}_{c}=1
and
L^{(s_{t})}_{j}\notin\mathcal{T}^{(s_{t}\to s_{r})}
, this is the first time we hand over this target from BS-Tx to BS-Rx. We hand over the prior
f^{(s_{t})}(\underline{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{j})
and append
L^{(s_{t})}_{j}
to
\mathcal{T}^{(s_{t}\to s_{r})}
. If
m^{j}\neq 0
, we also hand over
\mathbf{z}_{k}^{m_{j}}
from base station-Tx to base station-Rx. On the other hand, if
h^{j}_{c}=1
and
L^{(s_{t})}_{j}\in\mathcal{T}^{(s_{t}\to s_{r})}
, then this target was handed over before and we should not hand over the prior, to avoid double counting of information. Only the measurement
\mathbf{z}_{k}^{m_{j}}
is handed over, provided
m^{j}\neq 0
.

5.

When a potential target is pruned or leaves the field of view of base station-Tx, its label is removed from
\mathcal{T}^{(s_{t}\to s_{r})}
.

The target handover variant without measurements follows the same steps, but never hands over the measurements.

IV-B

Operation at the Receiving BS (base station-Rx)

Now we describe the target handover algorithm for base station-Rx. At time
k
, base station-Rx receives from base station-Tx a number of priors of potential targets and a number of measurements.
BS-Rx now incorporates this information as follows:

1.

The received priors are appended to the local priors, effectively increasing the number of legacy potential targets in the factor graph. This leads to an increased complexity due to the larger number of legacy potential targets, but allows BS-Rx to quickly detect new potential targets entering its field of view.
Each received potential targets is associated with a unique local label
L^{(s_{r})}
, which is added to the local list
\mathcal{R}^{(s_{r}\leftarrow s_{t})}
to prevent duplicate handovers.

2.

BS-Rx runs belief propagation sequentially (as detailed in Section
III-C
), first its local measurements and then using the handed over received measurements from other BSs.

3.

When a potential target is pruned or leaves the field of view of base station-Rx, its label is removed from
\mathcal{R}^{(s_{r}\leftarrow s_{t})}
.

The target handover without measurements for base station-Rx follows the same steps, omitting only the measurement-related sequential processing.

Remark 1
.

All base stations are acting as BS-Tx and BS-Rx at each time step and maintain transmission and reception lists with respect to all neighboring base stations and also share the information related to the field of view, in the form of the spatial detection probability.
It is also important to highlight that priors are handed over before BP update at time step
k
, while measurements are handed over after BP update at time step
k
.

V-C

Results and Discussion

The scores for both base station1 and base station2 are evaluated with estimates and true tracks within their respective field of view. We present only the results for base station1 (results for base station2 are available in the code).

The overall generalized optimal subpattern assignment score for base station1 is shown in Fig.
4
.
The proposed target handover with measurements algorithm achieves an overall generalized optimal subpattern assignment score comparable to that of the centralized approach, thanks largely to its reduction in missed‐detection error. Since the missed‐detection error dominates the generalized optimal subpattern assignment decomposition, the overall GOSPA curve closely mirrors the missed‐detection curve. In the following paragraphs, we will analyse each of the three contributors to the generalized optimal subpattern assignment score—localization error, missed‐detection error, and false‐alarm error—in detail.

The localization error component in the GOSPA for base station1, shown in Fig.
5
, demonstrates that within the overlapping field of view, the centralized algorithm achieves the lowest error, followed by the target handover with measurements algorithm. The handover without measurements approach yields only a slight error reduction at the start of the handover before its performance converges to that of the fully distributed method. This underscores the benefit of exchanging measurements during handover. All algorithms exhibit increased localization errors in the overlap region, where the reduced separation between targets makes data association more challenging.

The missed-detection error component for base station1 is shown in Fig.
6
. All algorithms exhibit elevated missed-detection errors in the initial frames, since it takes several steps for the posterior existence probability to exceed the detection threshold
P_{\mathrm{th}}
. In the fully distributed case, a pronounced spike in missed detections occurs when the target first moves into the field of view of base station1, because a new track must be initiated locally. Both the target handover without measurements and the target handover with measurements schemes achieve missed-detection performance comparable to that of the centralized algorithm.

The false alarm error component for base station1 is presented in Fig.
7
. We observe elevated false alarm rates at the boundary of the overlapping field of view for both the target handover with measurements and the target handover without measurements schemes. The timing of the target handover is determined by the handover threshold
\Gamma
. Increasing
\Gamma
would likely decrease false alarm errors at the cost of higher missed-detection errors. Within the overlapping field of view, the target handover with measurements approach achieves significantly fewer false alarms than the target handover without measurements case.

VI
Conclusions

We have proposed a novel belief-propagation-based target handover procedure for multi-target tracking in distributed integrated sensing and communications systems. Our approach addresses the need for scalable, decentralized tracking in scenarios where targets move across overlapping fields of view covered by spatially distributed base stations. By leveraging factor graph representations and message-passing principles, the proposed method enables efficient target handover with limited communication overhead.
Through detailed simulations, we have shown that the proposed handover mechanism achieves tracking performance comparable to centralized processing, while avoiding its high bandwidth and computational requirements. The algorithm maintains track continuity across base stations and is well-suited for dense urban environments where scalability and low latency are critical.
Future work will focus on extending the handover procedure to support more complex and dynamic sensing configurations and real-time operation over heterogeneous networks. In addition, integrating this method into standardized communication frameworks for ISAC will be an important step toward practical deployment in 6G systems.
