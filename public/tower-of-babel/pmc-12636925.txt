Physician and patient perspectives on PROM implementation barriers in spine care and pain management: a mixed-methods assessment
Authors: Fenan S Rassu, Sylvia M Johnson, Daniel S Barron, Claire Z Kalpakjian, Mary D Slavin, Daniel H Daneshvar, Zacharia Isaac
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) are vital for patient-centred care but face implementation challenges. Within the participating academic medical centre’s spine care and pain management clinics, PROMs were collected but underused, indicating a quality gap.ObjectiveTo identify and compare physician and patient perspectives on priorities, barriers and preferences for PROM implementation to inform a quality improvement initiative.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods evaluation (October 2024–December 2024) in two outpatient clinics. Data included quantitative surveys assessing priorities and challenges from physicians (N=8) and patients (N=35), and qualitative data from physician meeting field notes, patient interaction field notes and open-ended survey responses. Quantitative data were analysed descriptively; qualitative data underwent thematic analysis. Findings were integrated using triangulation.ResultsPhysicians and patients aligned on prioritising pain interference and physical function. However, patients prioritised pain severity and personal goals more highly than physicians. While 70% of patients found PROMs useful, only 24% reported discussing PROM findings with providers, and 75% of clinicians responded ‘not at all confident’ to a question about score interpretation. Implementation challenges diverged significantly: physicians universally (100%) cited perceived patient time burden as a barrier, but this concern was infrequently shared by patients (11.4%). Physicians also cited workflow integration as a barrier (87.5%), while patients primarily prioritised PROM format/design (37.1%) and relevance (28.6%). Five qualitative themes emerged across patients and physicians: (1) critiques of PROM content/fidelity; (2) disconnect between data collection and clinical integration; (3) prioritising function and patient-centred goals; (4) need for flexibility, customisation and communication and (5) system-level barriers influencing implementation.ConclusionsGaps exist between PROM collection and meaningful clinical use in this setting, driven by content limitations, workflow barriers, system issues and divergent stakeholder perspectives. Improving PROM implementation requires a multistakeholder approach prioritising function-focused, relevant measures integrated effectively into clinical workflows and support by system-level changes.Keywords: Patient Reported Outcome Measures, Pain Management, Qualitative research, Quality improvement, Implementation science

Introduction

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs)—standardised questionnaires completed by patients about their health status, function and quality of life1—are increasingly vital components of patient-centred healthcare. By capturing the patient’s perspective, PROMs enhance patient–provider communication,2 facilitate shared decision-making,3 4 track treatment effectiveness beyond clinical markers and improve health outcomes,5 particularly for persistent pain conditions.6 Despite growing international initiatives to integrate PROMs into clinical research and practice,7 8 successful implementation into routine workflows remains challenging across healthcare settings.9 10

Common barriers include hurdles with electronic health record (EHR) integration, workflow disruptions, difficulty interpreting data for action, time burden concerns and clinician scepticism about clinical utility.11,14 Overcoming these barriers requires dedicated resources and structured implementation strategies, particularly in specialised settings like spine care and pain management where understanding patient function and pain interference is paramount.

Within the participating academic medical centre, specific PROMs are routinely collected in outpatient spine care and pain management clinics and include a numerical pain rating scale of pain severity and the PROMIS Global Health-10 (PROMIS-10),15 supplemented with the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI)16 for patients with low back pain and the Neck Disability Index (NDI)17 for those with neck pain. However, despite the goal of routine collection, a significant gap between data acquisition and clinical application was observed. To address this quality gap, the current project was initiated as a quality improvement effort.

Successful implementation requires aligning stakeholder needs; thus, this project employed a multistakeholder approach involving both physicians and patients from the outset, guided by the PROM-cycle framework.18 Using a mixed-methods design, we identified and compared physician and patient perspectives on assessment priorities, barriers to PROM use and implementation preferences within the spine and pain clinics, providing a preliminary evidence base for subsequent measure selection and implementation.

Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive, cross-sectional study employed a convergent mixed-methods design, guided by implementation science principles and the PROM-cycle framework.18 This study occurred in two outpatient spine care and pain management clinics of the participating academic medical centre between October 2024 and December 2024.

Participants and recruitment

We aimed to recruit all attending pain management physicians (N=10) from the two participating spine care and pain management clinic sites. Physicians were invited via email from division leadership. Eight out of the 10 eligible physicians (80% participation rate) completed the physician survey component.

Patients were eligible to participate if they were adults (age >18 years) receiving care at one of two clinics. We recruited patients using consecutive sampling between October 2024 and December 2024, approaching them in waiting areas or exam rooms. Of 39 patients approached, 35 completed the survey (89.7% response rate).

Data collection

Quantitative data

Both surveys contained primarily closed-ended questions using Likert scales (1–5 point scales for importance ratings and confidence measures), multiple-choice questions for demographic characteristics and preferences, and categorical yes/no responses for current practices and perceived barriers. The physician survey assessed priorities, challenges and current PROM usage, while the patient survey assessed experiences with questionnaires, perceived usefulness and completion preferences. Each survey also included one open-ended question at the conclusion inviting additional comments or suggestions, which contributed to our qualitative dataset alongside field notes. The Physician Survey was administered online via REDCap in October 2024 for physician convenience, while the Patient Survey was administered on paper in clinic settings during December 2024 to enhance accessibility and completion during the clinic visit.

Qualitative data

We gathered qualitative data from three primary sources: (1) Detailed field notes recorded by the primary author (FSR) during two Spine Care and Pain Management Division meetings (attended by most participating physicians and other division members) where PROM implementation and preliminary project findings were discussed; (2) Field notes recorded by the lead author (FSR) during interactions with participating patients, capturing patients’ spontaneous comments and the clinical context surrounding survey administration and (3) open-ended written responses provided by participants to the final questions on both the physician and patient surveys. Field notes aimed to capture verbatim comments and contextual observations of stakeholder perceptions, barriers and the broader implementation context.

Data analysis

We analysed closed-ended survey items using descriptive statistics, calculating frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means/SD for Likert-scale items performed in R V.4.2.0.

Qualitative data were analysed manually using thematic analysis, following established principles.19 The primary analyst (FSR) employed an inductive approach without predefined themes or theoretical frameworks, allowing themes to emerge directly from the data. While the analysis was data-driven, the analyst remained mindful of the project’s overarching goals to identify PROM implementation barriers and stakeholder priorities without imposing predetermined categories.

The primary analyst (FSR) performed thematic analysis, using an inductive approach of data familiarisation, systematic coding and iterative theme development until a coherent set of key data patterns was achieved. Emerging themes were regularly discussed with coauthors (DB, MS and ZI) for critical review.

We used a convergent mixed-methods approach,20 analysing quantitative and qualitative data separately before integration during interpretation.21 Data were integrated by juxtaposing quantitative results and qualitative themes to interpret convergence and divergence.

Patient and public involvement

Patient involvement aligned with the IAP2 ‘Consult’ level.22 While not formal codesigners, patient input from surveys and field notes was integral to developing the qualitative themes and will guide future patient engagement in PROM selection.

Data anonymisation occurred at multiple stages: survey responses were collected without direct identifiers, field notes were recorded using generic descriptors (eg, ‘attending physician,’ ‘patient with chronic neck pain’), and all quotations were assigned anonymous participant codes (eg, physician 1, patient 1) during the analysis phase. Patients were first approached by an attending physician who introduced the research staff (FSR) in clinic waiting areas or exam rooms. Patients were explained that the survey was part of a quality improvement project aimed at understanding patient experiences with health questionnaires to improve care delivery. Patients were explicitly informed that participation was voluntary, that declining would not affect their care in any way, and that they could stop completing the survey at any point without consequences. Verbal consent was obtained after explaining that responses would be used anonymously to improve the clinic’s use of health questionnaires, that data would be securely stored, and that findings would be used to guide future quality improvement efforts. Physicians received email invitations from division leadership (ZI) explaining the project’s quality improvement purpose, voluntary participation and plans for using findings to inform future PROM implementation decisions. This manuscript adheres to the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence 2.0 guidelines.

Qualitative findings: integrated themes

Thematic analysis yielded five themes. To illustrate them, participant quotes are presented with anonymised identifiers (eg, physician 1) and have been lightly edited for clarity.

Theme 1: critiques of current PROM content and measurement fidelity

This theme captures widespread dissatisfaction with the content and structure of currently used PROMs. Physicians questioned the clinical fidelity of measures like the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) compared with functional assessment, while patients found generic questionnaires often irrelevant to their specific condition, pain location or functional limitations, and desired more specific or tailored questions.

Physicians noted the disconnect between NRS scores and observed function, with one remarking how they prioritise functional questioning:

Typically, when I document the note, I’m kind of doing PROMs indirectly… what can you do? What can you not do?… following [a procedure like] a branch block saying… ‘what could you do afterwards when you were numbed up that you couldn’t do beforehand? Can you stand longer?’… And I’ve been documenting that, and the patients are more in tune to that. So… ‘my pain went down to, like a six. But guess what? I could make dinner for… two hours and do dishes… and I had no pain.’ Okay, well, that’s great. It’s more important than just numbers are. (Physician 1)

Another physician highlighted the difficulty of using single scores for complex presentations:

…when we assess pain scores, we just do… a simple score, which might not… capture the whole picture. For instance… my patient who has bilateral pain… I do an intervention, and the pain on the right side is gone, but the pain on the left side still persists… that score… will not show the full picture… (Physician 2)

Patients echoed these concerns about measurement fidelity and relevance. One patient found the NRS “difficult… because I don’t know any frame… it feels very arbitrary” (Patient 1), while another critiqued the lack of specificity:

It’s kind of crazy, because [for] my pain, you have to pick neck. It doesn’t let you pick arm. And when you have cervical… nerve impingement… Your pain is usually radicular [in the arm]… So they should [let you pick arm]… (Patient 2)

Another patient pointed out the mismatch between questions asked and their specific condition:

A lot of times, [they’ll] ask you where your pain is, but then follow up questions don’t have anything to do with it. Or sometimes I’ll be seeing someone for… lateral pain here, but [the] questionnaires [are] geared toward… back or hip, so they don’t actually ask questions [relevant to my pain]… (Patient 3)

Theme 2: disconnect between PROM data collection and clinical integration

This theme highlights a significant gap between the collection of PROM data and its effective use in clinical care. Physicians face barriers related to time constraints, workflow disruption and technological hurdles (EHR access/display), leading to data often going unreviewed. Patients perceive this lack of integration, questioning the purpose of completing forms if they are not discussed or visibly used to inform care.

One physician noted the patient perception of redundancy when data is not reviewed beforehand:

After they’ve finished filling out the PROM… we don’t have time to look at them before we see them. So some patients have expressed… ‘well, I just told you all this stuff. Why am I going through this all again? What did I just fill all that stuff out for?’ (Physician 3)

Patients expressed this disconnect clearly, with one stating:

And I always have had the feeling that nobody ever reads them anyway… I mean, I just do it because… it’s the part of the E check in… (Patient 4)

Another patient questioned the direct benefit to their care:

Is that useful to me? Useful to them? I don’t know. I usually assume that when I’m doing these, it’s for the… purpose of the provider… So how useful do I feel these questions were in describing your health needs…? So it’s not at all useful on their face… (Patient 5)

Theme 3: prioritising function and patient-centred goals in assessment

A strong consensus emerged, particularly from physicians but echoed by patient desire for relevant functional content, that assessment should prioritise understanding a patient’s functional capacity, limitations in meaningful activities (work, hobbies, daily tasks), and progress towards personal goals. This patient-centred approach is viewed as more clinically relevant and valuable for guiding rehabilitation and pain management than relying solely on standardised pain severity metrics.

One physician explicitly noted the importance of patient-defined goals:

Maybe it should be like three goals, patient centric goals, and some sort of scale by which they’re meeting that those three goals, it’s golf, it’s walk my dog, it’s shop, whatever they’re saying… that’s what tracks over time that we’re looking at that goal. (Physician 4)

Patients also emphasised function over abstract pain scores:

Instead of measuring the pain, what are the things you cannot do anymore?… What’s difficult for you to do? Those kinds of questions might be more helpful… because I can’t measure pain… like when I came in today, they said, What’s my pain level? Well… I live with this every day, so my pain level is really zero, but it’s really not because there’s pain there… it’s hard to measure that. (Patient 6)

Theme 4: need for enhanced flexibility, customisation and communication in the PROM process

Both patients and physicians saw a need for significant improvements in how PROMs are administered and structured. Patients strongly desired more flexibility, including options to elaborate on answers (eg, open-ended fields), tailored questions relevant to their specific situation, various accessible completion methods (online, language options) and user-friendly formats. Physicians, while acknowledging current limitations, suggested potential technical solutions like Computerised Adaptive Testing or Artificial Intelligence-driven summaries to improve relevance and efficiency. Clearer communication about the purpose and use of PROM data was also needed.

Patients frequently expressed frustration with rigid formats:

I feel like some of the questions don’t allow the patients to elaborate… more like it’s just… this one or this one. And sometimes you’re like, well, it’s in between… So I feel like, if there’s… maybe… a little box where they can explain… be like, well, some days I can do this, but other days I can’t do this… (Patient 7)

Another patient stated simply:

I would like to explain my answers… (Patient 8)

The need for relevance and customisation was clear:

…there are certain… qualitative aspects… which are important for me, but when the questionnaire is generic for all patients… for example, the qualitative aspects which [are] most important, say, if I were a sports person… And if my level of participation goes down… I think that qualitative aspects sometimes don’t [get captured]… (Patient 9)

Physicians also recognised the need for better tools and approaches, with one suggesting:

I’m wondering if there’s a way to capture the data through our clinical encounter information. Especially I’ve been using the DAX, the AI tool… [it] would be able to pick up the information that you’re looking for in PROMs through the patient encounter… eliminate that problem of… redundancy, and then be able to get more of the granular features about pain… (Physician 3)

Theme 5: system-level factors influencing PROM implementation

The implementation and utility of PROMs were seen as significantly influenced by broader systemic factors. Challenges included inconsistent use of different PROM tools across various clinic sites, hindering longitudinal tracking, technical limitations or variations within the EHR system and external pressures from payers emphasising specific metrics (like NRS reduction) that may not align with perceived clinical priorities. Addressing these system-level inconsistencies was viewed as crucial for meaningful PROM implementation.

One physician highlighted the lack of standardisation:

How do we implement that in our system? Because in one clinic… it’s PROMIS 29, another one [it’s] PROMIS 10, another one it’s [Oswestry]… there’s no real longitudinal consistency as a result of that. (Physician 4)

Another noted EHR variations:

One thing I struggle with is that EPIC looks a little bit different to me at each of my five or six different locations… in some of my locations, the dashboard shows some of these PROMs… a little bit better than other locations. (Physician 4)

The influence of external mandates was also clear:

So a lot of our pragmatic situation is, did a person meet a certain level of numeric pain rating improvement to justify repeating the injection. And there are criteria for different procedures… (Physician 5)

Patients also perceived this institutional focus, with one understanding the push for PROMs coming from “…[the academic medical center] to incorporate more of these patient reported outcome measures into care, and it’s for insurance purposes… like Medicare. CMS is really pushing for incorporating more self report measures into care.” (Patient 10)

Discussion

This mixed-methods evaluation in spine and pain management clinics revealed a significant disconnect between PROM collection and meaningful clinical integration.2 3 11 14 This gap is quantitatively underscored by the findings that a majority of physicians lacked confidence in interpreting PROM scores,11 13 minimal use for clinical decision-making and infrequent PROM discussion reported by patients. The low confidence in score interpretation may reflect multiple factors such as lack of formal training on PROM interpretation,23 minimal integration of PROM education into medical education curricula, or absence of institutional support for score interpretation. Qualitatively, this was rooted in physicians’ lack of time for review3 14 24 (Theme 2: disconnect between collection and integration), which led patients to feel their input was disregarded, undermining the PROM’s perceived value. Alongside this integration challenge, a central finding was the critique of current PROM content and measurement fidelity (theme 1). Physicians questioned the NRS’s utility for capturing multidimensional pain, while patients found it arbitrary25 and other questionnaires irrelevant to their conditions. However, despite these shared critiques (theme 1) and a strong clinical preference for prioritising functional assessment (theme 3), a clear tension emerged: the NRS remains embedded in practice (theme 5), partially due to external payer requirements, which often mandate specific percentage reductions in NRS scores for treatment authorisation and reimbursement,26 regardless of the perceived limitations of the scale itself.

The current PROM measures were implemented as part of a health system-wide mandate to collect patient-reported outcomes across the enterprise. The PROMIS-10 was specifically selected to minimise burden on patients and clinicians. The ODI and NDI were added for condition specificity, triggered by patient-selected reason for visit (back pain or neck pain, respectively). The NRS is explicitly linked to reimbursement criteria for interventional procedures. This top-down selection process may partially explain the content and relevance concerns raised by both patients and providers in the current study.

The disconnect between data collection and clinical use raises important ethical considerations about data stewardship.27 When patients invest time providing data that workflow barriers prevent from being reviewed, it undermines both the therapeutic relationship and principles of data minimisation, creating an obligation for healthcare systems to ensure collected data serves its intended purpose of enhancing patient care rather than merely satisfying administrative requirements.27 28

Despite differing views on NRS, the strong quantitative alignment between clinicians and patients on the high importance of assessing physical function resonates strongly with the qualitative emphasis on functional impact over abstract numbers (theme 3: prioritising function). This convergence provides a clear mandate for function-based PROMs that reflect patient priorities like personal goals8 and pain severity. Perhaps most striking, however, was the divergence surrounding perceived implementation barriers. While physicians universally cited patient time burden as their top concern, this was not a primary challenge identified by patients. Instead, patients’ primary concerns centred on the format, relevance and volume of the questionnaires, aligning with qualitative critiques of content irrelevance and inflexibility (theme 1 and theme 4). While patients focused on the quality and relevance of questions (citing ‘volume’ over ‘time’), physicians’ focus on ‘patient time’ likely reflected their own workflow pressures (theme 2) rather than patient-voiced obstacles. Thus, implementation efforts focused on patient time burden may be misdirected from patient priorities like PROM relevance and design.

Patients expressed a strong desire for ways to elaborate on standardised responses29 and for content tailored to their specific conditions (theme 4: need for enhanced flexibility, customisation and communication). Physicians, while acknowledging current limitations, pointed towards potential technical solutions like Computerised Adaptive Testing or AI-driven summaries to improve relevance and efficiency.3 The strong patient preference for completing PROMs online pre-visit offers a practical workflow solution. However, successful implementation also requires addressing broader system-level factors (theme 5) like inconsistent PROM use across sites, EHR issues and payer mandates through coordinated strategies. Patient-generated measures like the Measure Yourself Medical Outcome Profile30 and the Measure Yourself Concerns and well-being31 directly address these content and flexibility concerns by allowing patients to nominate and score their own relevant concerns while maintaining the functional focus both groups prioritised.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strengths include its mixed-methods design allowing triangulation and the direct comparison of stakeholder perspectives. The small sample size of physicians (N=8, representing 80% of eligible physicians) limits generalisability, though qualitative data provided convergent insights. Additional limitations include the single-network setting and lack of demographic diversity analysis.

Implications for practice

These findings have direct operational implications for implementing PROMs in spine care and pain management. PROM selection should prioritise functional measures and patient-defined goals beyond NRS, using tools like patient-generated measures that allow customisation.8 30 31 Workflow integration requires previsit online completion, EHR modifications to display scores prominently during encounters, and dedicated time for score review and discussion.18 32 Training initiatives should focus on score interpretation and clinical application rather than just data collection. System-level changes must address PROM standardisation across clinic sites, EHR functionality improvements and alignment between payer requirements and meaningful outcomes through institutional advocacy.

Conclusions

This assessment revealed a critical disconnect between PROM collection and clinical use, with divergent physician and patient perspectives on content relevance, priorities and barriers highlighting the challenges of current practices. Successfully leveraging PROMs for quality improvement in this setting necessitates a multistakeholder approach that prioritises function-focused, flexible and relevant measures, coupled with workflow redesign and system-level changes to ensure data are effectively used at the point of care. Addressing these identified gaps through collaborative measure selection and implementation planning is essential to realising the potential of PROMs to enhance patient-centred care and outcomes.
