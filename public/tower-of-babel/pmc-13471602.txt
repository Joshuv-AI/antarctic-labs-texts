Effective deprescribing strategies for reducing potentially inappropriate medications and improving economic outcomes in community-based settings: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Honja Hama, Ramona Afzali, Johannes Lanzerstorfer, Lisa Lehner, Janina Kehr, Igor Grabovac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deprescribing is a valuable approach to reducing inappropriate medication use, particularly in older adults and patients concurrently using high numbers of medications (polypharmacy). While previous research has focused on specific drug classes or patient populations, the overall effects of deprescribing in a community-based setting remain unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to assess the effectiveness of deprescribing interventions in community-based settings regarding economic outcomes and patient safety, measured as the reduction of potentially inappropriate medication. We conducted a comprehensive search across Embase, Central, and Medline with studies published prior to 20th April 2025. Data was extracted and narratively summarized according to a standardized protocol, and a meta-analysis was performed with a selection of the included studies. Risk of bias was assessed with the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and the ROBINS-I tool for non-randomized controlled trials (NRCTs). The systematic review included 27 studies, of which nine were included in the meta-analysis. The meta-analysis showed significant results, indicating that deprescribing interventions reduced prescribing-related medication use, including potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) and specific medication types, in community-based settings, with an effect size of -0.3234 (SE = 0.1378). The descriptive outcomes further support the result that deprescribing interventions may lead to a reduction in PIMs. Additionally, eight studies assessing economic outcomes, like cost-effectiveness and cost-utility, consistently found that deprescribing interventions led to healthcare cost savings. This is the first meta-analysis to examine deprescribing in a broad, community-based setting without focusing on a specific patient group or medication type. The meta-analysis confirmed significant effects, and the descriptive findings, along with economic assessments, further support the benefits of deprescribing. However, further studies are needed to address remaining uncertainties regarding long-term outcomes and the scalability of deprescribing strategies across diverse healthcare systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14682-0.

Introduction

Polypharmacy, defined as the concurrent use of multiple medications, is a rapidly rising concern in resource-rich healthcare systems, affecting a significant proportion of the population across various age groups [1]. This trend is attributed to factors such as population aging, unhealthy lifestyles, and the increasing pharmaceuticalization of medicine (i.e., growing reliance on medications to manage health conditions) [2, 3]. Studies indicate that the prevalence of polypharmacy ranges from 26% to 40% of older adults, with a notable increase among individuals managing chronic diseases [4]. While medications play a crucial role in disease management, excessive medication use has been linked to adverse health outcomes, including cognitive impairment, hospitalizations, prolonged hospital stays, and mortality, largely due to drug-drug interactions and adverse drug reactions [5–9].

Deprescribing addresses these concerns by identifying and eliminating medications that may no longer provide benefits or that pose more harm than good to patients. The practice is defined as “the process of withdrawing an inappropriate medication, supervised by a health professional with the goal of managing polypharmacy and improving outcomes” [10]. It is generally recognized as a key strategy to reduce polypharmacy as it aligns medication regimens with individual health goals through careful evaluation and supervision [11]. Common medication classes targeted for deprescribing include benzodiazepines, proton pump inhibitors, and antipsychotics [12, 13]. On top of that, research suggests that certain deprescribing interventions can improve prescribing quality while being cost-effective by reducing medication-related hospitalizations and overall healthcare expenditures [14].

The process of deprescribing should ideally be understood as a continuous, patient-centered process that begins at the point of initial prescription, with clear communication about treatment duration and the need for regular review [15]. It typically involves a structured approach, including reviewing medication history, identifying potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs), and assessing whether and how they might be safely withdrawn, tapered, or dose-reduced [16].

Reducing PIMs through deprescribing has been shown to improve patient safety and to be cost-effective [17]. The Individual Deprescribing Intervention (IDeI) trial in Swiss nursing homes also showed that reducing PIMs leads to more safety benefits for patients, including reductions in adverse drug events, and increased medication appropriateness [18]. Concerning economic outcomes, studies demonstrate that PIM use among older adults contributes significantly to healthcare costs and increases the risk of adverse drug events, thus highlighting the economic burden of inappropriate prescribing for healthcare systems [19]. A study from Germany in 2018 that conducted a retrospective matched cohort study showed that older adults prescribed PIMs incurred €1,237 more in total healthcare costs during the first quarter post-prescription compared to those not on PIMs [20]. A cost-utility analysis from Ireland in 2014 similarly found that reducing inappropriate non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use by 49.8% would be cost-effective, with an estimated cost saving up to €1,971 per person at a willingness-to-pay threshold of €45,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) for the deprescribing intervention [21]. These findings collectively underscore the value of deprescribing not only as a clinical priority for patient safety but also as a cost-saving strategy for healthcare systems.

The practice of deprescribing is particularly well-suited to community-based settings, where general practitioners (GPs) often cultivate longstanding relationships with patients. The GP’s role as a trusted first point of contact enhances communication, supports shared decision-making, and increases the likelihood of successful deprescribing interventions [22]. Patient-centered care in this context has been shown to improve patient satisfaction, quality of life, and health outcomes [23]. It also positions the patient as an important source of information, not only about their medical history but also about their goals of care, which may increase the effectiveness of certain deprescribing interventions in turn [24]. For the purpose of this review, community-based settings refer to healthcare interventions delivered outside of hospital environments, including primary care clinics, general practices, and other outpatient or home-care services.

While the interest in the field of deprescribing around community-based settings is increasing, the evidence remains fragmented, with studies varying widely in target populations, intervention designs, outcomes measured, and healthcare contexts [11]. The objective of this systematic review was to assess the effectiveness of existing deprescribing strategies used in community-based settings for improving patient safety and economic outcomes, including cost-effectiveness and cost-utility. The main contribution of this review lies in its comprehensive approach, as it aims to present a broad perspective on the community-based healthcare setting. Unlike other reviews that concentrate on specific patient populations or particular medication groups, this review examines the community-based environment as a whole and analyzes the value of deprescribing strategies in a public health context of pharmaceuticalization and polypharmacy [25, 26]. Moreover, the review aims to synthesize evidence and provide an overview of different deprescribing strategies and their features in a community-based setting. Since definitions and approaches to deprescribing differ markedly [24], such a perspective aids a better and more comprehensive understanding of deprescribing as a technique for PIMs management as a whole, to further increase patient safety and the sustainability of healthcare systems.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist and statement [27]. The completed checklist is available in Supplementary Material 1. The review protocol is registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42024577809).

Search strategy

The researchers (IG & HH), aided by two research librarians at the Medical University of Vienna, jointly developed the initial search strategy and iteratively cross-checked with the most relevant literature on the topic to ensure rigorous procedures. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms to identify all eligible studies published up to 20th April, 2025. The key search terms included (1) deprescribing, (2) community-based setting, (3) original studies, and (4) non-tobacco cessation, explicitly excluding studies focused on tobacco cessation. The search strategy was tailored to each database, with searches conducted across Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Medline. In addition to the database search, a backward citation search was performed by reviewing the reference lists of all included studies to identify potentially eligible studies. Studies were excluded for the following reasons: ‘wrong intervention’ denoted studies that did not evaluate a deprescribing intervention, ‘wrong publication type’ denoted articles that were not original research, ‘wrong methodology’ denoted qualitative studies or studies lacking a control group, and ‘not community-based’ denoted studies conducted in non-community-based settings, such as hospitals or long-term care facilities. The full search string and PICO terms are available in Supplementary Material 2.

Criteria for study selection

Following the database search, all identified citations were imported into Zotero, where duplicates were removed both automatically and manually. Subsequently, four reviewers (RA, IG, HH & JL) working in teams of two, screened titles and abstracts for relevance using Rayyan [28], based on the four predefined eligibility criteria, which were operationalized through the key search terms listed above. Any conflicting decisions were discussed in pairs of two among the reviewers, and disagreements were resolved through consensus. Full-text eligibility was then assessed in collaborative discussion sessions by two researchers (RA & HH), one from each review team, to ensure consistency in study selection.

Data collection and quality assessment

Data collection was carried out by two independent reviewers (RA & HH) to ensure accuracy and reduce bias. Each reviewer was responsible for extracting key information from the included studies based on a pre-defined list of criteria in line with the review’s focus on deprescribing strategies, patient safety, economic outcomes, and community-based care settings. Patient safety was operationalized via prescribing-related indicators, primarily the reduction of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs), to enable consistent and quantifiable assessment across studies. Economic outcomes encompassed both cost-effectiveness and cost–utility analyses as reported in the included studies. These criteria included the following: objectives or purpose of the study/report, study design, inclusion and exclusion criteria, setting of intervention, mean age and percentage of male/female participants, patient characteristics, short description of the deprescribing intervention, controls, number of participants, primary and secondary outcomes, recruitment time or study duration, statistical methods used, level of analysis, type of metric (e.g., odds ratio, hazard ratio, mean difference), effect size, lower and higher 95% confidence interval, and any additional notes or other relevant information. The reviewers worked independently, and discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion or by consulting the third reviewer (IG) if necessary. If data was not available and the authors did not have the opportunity to access the data, attempts were made to contact the study authors to obtain the missing data. If no response was received, the studies were excluded from further quantitative analysis but were still included in the table.

Regarding quality assessment, different risk-of-bias assessment tools were used based on the design of the included studies. For quality assessment, the risk of bias was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and the ROBINS-I (Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions) tool for non-randomized controlled trials (NRCTs). These assessments were done by two reviewers (RA & HH) to assess the overall quality of the studies included in the meta-analysis. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion.

Data synthesis and analysis

Eligibility for data synthesis was determined through a structured process. Firstly, study characteristics, intervention type, and outcomes were tabulated and compared against the predefined inclusion criteria. Studies that met all eligibility requirements were included in the review. Furthermore, those with insufficient data or including only descriptive data were not included in the meta-analysis. A meta-analysis of nine included studies was conducted to synthesize current evidence on the effectiveness of deprescribing regarding the safety of medication reduction. Given the variability in individual study findings, a meta-analytic approach allows for a more comprehensive and quantitatively robust evaluation, aligning with the overarching goal of this review to provide an inclusive and evidence-based assessment of deprescribing strategies. In cases where summary statistics were missing, study authors were contacted for additional information.

A random-effects model (REML) was applied, as it accounts for both within-study and between-study variability, making it suitable given the expected heterogeneity across the community-based deprescribing interventions. The REML approach also provides more stable variance estimates compared to the DerSimonian-Laird and Maximum Likelihood methods [29]. Given the variability in study designs and populations, a random-effects model is adequate because it allows for differences in true effect sizes between studies, rather than assuming a single common effect size as with a fixed-effects model. This approach ensures a more generalizable and robust synthesis of the existing evidence on deprescribing interventions.

The results of the individual studies were systematically tabulated. For visualization, forest plots and funnel plots were used to summarize meta-analysis results and assess potential publication bias (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). Additionally, Egger’s test was conducted to examine the likelihood of bias. Heterogeneity was tested using I² statistics, with thresholds set at low (< 25%), moderate (25–50%), and high (> 50%) heterogeneity. Potential sources of heterogeneity were explored using subgroup analysis. Subgroups were only created if at least two studies per subgroup were included. Chi-squared statistics and p-values for subgroup differences were reported. Where meta-analysis was not possible because of incomplete or insufficient data, results are reported narratively.

To assess the robustness of the synthesized results, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding high-risk-of-bias studies, reanalyzing data with alternative statistical models (such as fixed-effects models), and testing the impact of different effect size calculations. One such approach included a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, where each study was sequentially removed from the meta-analysis to evaluate its influence on the overall effect estimate. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1, primarily utilizing the metafor and meta packages [30].

Results

The initial search resulted in 10,851 hits, with 4,277 duplicates removed, leaving 6,574 articles eligible for title and abstract screening. Of these, 6,515 were excluded, resulting in 59 publications for potential retrieval, with 11 unavailable. This resulted in 48 studies assessed for eligibility. During the final and full-text screening, 21 further studies were excluded, leaving a total number of 27 papers included in the review, eight oriented towards economic outcomes of deprescribing interventions and 19 towards patient safety defined as PIM reduction. Each study was categorized based on its primary outcome. Nine of the 19 safety-related papers were then included in a meta-analysis, as they reported comparable outcomes that allowed quantitative synthesis. The remaining 10 safety studies, as well as all cost-related studies, were not included due to outcome reporting, variability in economic evaluation methods, currencies, and reporting standards, which prevented meaningful aggregation. The citation search in the included studies did not yield any additional relevant literature, as all potentially eligible references had been included in the initial identification stage and were later excluded in the screening process. The whole study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow chart in more detail (Fig. 1).

Patient safety & effectiveness of PIM reduction

Nineteen studies were included in this review on the topic of patient safety and health-related outcomes, determining the effectiveness of deprescribing in terms of the reduction of PIMs. The majority of the studies included were conducted in the Global North, with the majority originating from the United States and none predating 2017. Exceptions were Bawazeer et al. [31] with a non-randomized interventional controlled study in Saudi Arabia, Silva et al. [32] with a randomized, open-label, non-blinded study in Brazil, and Yu et al. [33] evaluating the findings of a retrospective cohort study in Taiwan. Half of the studies follow an observational or quasi-experimental design in that the intervention was assigned but not fully randomized. Fried et al. [34], Kornholt et al. [35], Green et al. [36], Mangin et al. [37], Aharaz et al. [38], Jamieson et al. [39], Heisig et al. [40], Herrinton et al. [41], and Sheppard et al. [42] followed a RCT design. The review also included five pilot studies, given that such studies also focus on the feasibility of a deprescribing intervention that had not previously been done [36–38, 43, 44].

While all studies included focus on assessing deprescribing interventions, their objectives differ in medication focus and intervention type. For example, Fernandes et al. [45] focused on the effectiveness and safety of benzodiazepine discontinuation in a primary care setting, while Heisig et al. [40] looked into the effectiveness of an electronic decision-making tool to reduce long-term PPI intake. Studies varied in terms of scope and scale: Green et al. [36] had the largest scope with a total of 20 primary care clinics involved, whereas Silverii et al.’s study [46], for example, was designed as a single-center study. The number of patients involved, therefore, also differed significantly. Mangin et al. [37] conducted the smallest study, including only 37 patients − 18 in the intervention group and 19 in the control group. In contrast, Heisig and colleagues carried out a much larger study with a total of 2,387 patients, comprising 1,256 in the intervention group and 1,131 in the control group [40]. Yu et al. [33] evaluated the largest study, with a total sample size of 11,250 (n = 258 in the intervention group; n = 9,053 in the control group).

Some studies emphasize health impacts or specific mediation reductions, while others prioritize feasibility or integration into respective health systems. The studies included also tend to focus on one specific patient target group, such as elderly patients [36, 46–48] or people living with chronic illnesses [33, 41, 42, 49]. Overall, studies can be grouped into two types of interventions: ones that involve health care professionals (HCPs), such as pharmacists and GPs, directly (e.g., via consultations), and ones that are patient-centered and focus on educational interventions.

The HCP-led interventions involve decision-making support tools or algorithms that help HCPs or pharmacists to decide when to start deprescribing interventions. For example, in the case of McIntyre et al. [49], the authors developed a deprescribing tool for outpatients in a hemodialysis center, whereas Heisig and colleagues [40] focused on the reduction of PPI intake and the effectiveness of an electronic decision-making aid tool. HCP-led interventions also take the form of medication reviews, where pharmacists closely collaborate with GPs and may provide recommendations [31, 32, 35–37, 39]. Silverii et al. [46] analyze physician adherence to professional guidelines implemented in the Italian context, targeting deprescribing in elderly persons with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus.

More patient-centered interventions generally utilize more education-based approaches, including brochures, physician letters, or flyers about certain medication risks. For instance, the study of Le and colleagues [50] focused on the effects of the EMPOWER quality improvement initiative in which patients received an evidence-based educational brochure outlining the risks of long-term benzodiazepine use, along with guidance on tapering strategies. The intervention aimed to empower patients to initiate conversations with their healthcare providers about deprescribing [50]. In turn, the study by Fernandes et al. [45] implemented patient diaries containing educational content on medication risks and deprescribing strategies. In addition, one study [44] focused on the development and validation of a short-form questionnaire assessing patient perceptions and readiness for deprescribing, rather than evaluating a specific intervention.

Ten out of the 19 studies only provided descriptive statistics as raw estimates and percentages, without effect estimates, such as confidence intervals. For this reason, they could not be included in the meta-analysis as their results could not be pooled in the calculations. They will therefore only be described narratively. The narrative analysis of the ten studies not included in the meta-analysis shows that the interventions led to a higher rate of medication discontinuation compared to the control group [34, 36–38, 43, 45, 48, 49]. However, effect sizes varied, and not all findings were statistically significant [34, 46, 48]. Some studies reported changes in regimen complexity in terms of the Medication Regimen Complexity Index (MRCI) or specific drug classes such as PPI or diabetes medications [36, 40, 46], while others measured the reduction in the number of prescribed medications or dosage [45, 49]. Although sample sizes were small in some studies, their collective results suggest a consistent trend towards reduced medication use following deprescribing interventions.

Meta-analysis

In nine studies, the results suggested that deprescribing-related medication reduction was feasible, whether measured in terms of PIMs or specific medication types. The findings from these studies were synthesized in a meta-analysis, providing a quantitative estimate of the overall effect of deprescribing interventions on prescribing-related medication reduction.

The pooled effect size estimate was − 0.3234 (SE = 0.1378) with p = 0.0189, translating into a significant reduction in medication use (Fig. 2). The analysis showed considerable heterogeneity with an I² of 87,74%. This is also reflected by the tau² value, which is high at 0.1231. The REML estimator was deemed a reasonable fit with AIC = 14.1866 and BIC = 14.3455.

Publication bias was assessed using Trim-and-Fill, Egger’s test, and Begg’s test. The Trim-and-Fill method detected no missing studies, and both Egger’s test and Begg’s test showed no significant funnel plot asymmetry, indicating no strong evidence of publication bias (Fig. 3).

In the subgroup analysis by design, which distinguishes between RCTs and NRCTs, no significant differences in effect sizes were found (QM(df = 2) = 2.8450, p = 0.2411). This suggests that the study design does not significantly influence the overall effect estimate. In contrast, the variable, which differentiates between studies reporting OR, RR, or mean differences, significantly moderated the effect sizes (QM(df = 2) = 26.06, p < 0.0001). The model estimates for both OR and RR were statistically significant (p < 0.0001), indicating that the metric used to report the effect size had a substantial impact on the results. Furthermore, residual heterogeneity was low and not statistically significant (QE = 6.08, p = 0.41), so that the metric variable accounted for most of the variability across studies (R² = 95.13%). These results indicate that the design of the study does not account for the heterogeneity observed in the analysis, while the metric used to report effect sizes does. The number of participants does not have a significant impact on the effect size in this model and, therefore, does not serve as a meaningful moderator in this context. Additionally, the potential influence of the Risk of Bias variable, which categorizes studies as having low, moderate, or high risk of bias, was examined further, but there was no significant effect. A full breakdown of the Risk of Bias assessments is provided in Supplementary Material 3. Furthermore, the estimates for the different bias categories were relatively small, and their confidence intervals suggest considerable uncertainty, indicating that bias is not a significant factor in explaining the substantial heterogeneity observed in the model. Despite the high residual heterogeneity, no clear moderators emerged from these subgroup analyses.

As part of the sensitivity analysis, an influence analysis and leave-one-out analysis were conducted. The influence analysis revealed that Yu et al. [33] had a significant impact on the meta-analysis results. The study could be identified as influential with high leverage values and a large effect on the estimated coefficients. The results of the leave-one-out analysis confirmed the robustness of the model. However, the exclusion of Yu et al. [33] led to changes in heterogeneity measures. Both these results support the finding that Yu et al.’s study plays a crucial role in driving the overall results of the meta-analysis. Therefore, overall results and potential outliers should be interpreted carefully.

Based on these findings, another meta-analysis, excluding Yu et al. [33], was conducted (Fig. 4). Following exclusion, the effect size remained negative and statistically significant. The effect size indicates a moderate negative effect of deprescribing interventions on the total number of medications prescribed. While the effect may not be strong, there is still a statistically significant trend toward a negative impact. The heterogeneity decreased slightly but still stayed high.

This meta-analysis, based on five studies, had a moderate amount of total heterogeneity. The H² statistic (3.81) suggests that the total variability is just over three times the sampling variability, meaning that there is some unexplained variation between studies. Furthermore, the Q-test shows that the heterogeneity is significant. The fit statistics, however, suggest that it is a reasonable fit for the data with low values of AIC and BIC. The overall effect estimate is -0.29, with a standard error of 0.26, indicating that deprescribing was associated with a reduction in prescribing-related medication use in community-based settings, although this result did not reach statistical significance.

Economic outcomes

Eight studies fit the inclusion criteria and discussed the topic of economic outcomes in deprescribing. Of the eight studies included, the majority were conducted in the United Kingdom [51–54], the others in Ireland [55], the United States [56], and Hong Kong [57], respectively. All studies were conducted between 2019 and 2024. They evaluated cost-effectiveness based on a variety of deprescribing interventions, with Coyle et al. [52] and Bryant et al. [51] using an interventional single-arm cohort study design, in which the cost effects of a deprescribing intervention for a single group of participants were examined. Study designs overall varied, with some using a prospective approach [52, 54] while others, such as Jowett et al. [53], Turner et al. [58], and Gillespie et al. [55], conducted retrospective cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analyses based on outcomes from prior deprescribing trials. Full methodological details are summarized in Table 1.

The studies in this category focused on the reduction of polypharmacy and associated healthcare costs in primary care clinics and the impact of a nurse-led educational support program to reduce proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use in a community-based setting [27, 28]. Jowett et al. [53] explored the cost-effectiveness of deprescribing antihypertensive medications in older adults with normal blood pressure, providing insights into potential savings and health outcomes. Similarly, Gillespie et al. [55] evaluated the cost-effectiveness of the SPPiRE intervention, a general practitioner-led, web-based medication review tool, and found a trend towards cost savings and small QALY gains compared to usual care. The studies of Rea et al. [56] and Crawford et al. [54] examined pharmacist-driven interventions to reduce polypharmacy and analyzed their cost-effectiveness. Finally, the cost-effectiveness of the D-PRESCRIBE intervention [59] was evaluated by Turner et al. [58], offering insights into the economic impact of deprescribing.

Included studies varied in scope, delivery methods, and settings. Four studies implemented pharmacist- or nurse-led medication reviews within primary care clinics or GP offices, aiming to reduce unnecessary prescriptions and associated costs [51, 52, 54, 56], while others employed a health economic model to evaluate cost-utility from a public healthcare perspective [57, 58]. The cost-utility analysis of Turner et al. [58] estimated the costs and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) associated with the D-PRESCRIBE intervention in comparison to usual care over one year. The D-PRESCRIBE intervention is a community-based, pharmacist-led educational program for older adults and their primary care providers designed as a cluster randomized trial [60]. The studies all incorporate educational tools such as applications or brochures, but differ in terms of setting, the individuals responsible for delivering the education, and the scale of the intervention. Differences also included the number of patients reached, ranging from local trials to multi-regional or nationwide programs, thus reflecting differing levels of health system integration and resource investment.

All eight studies show that deprescribing not only reduces polypharmacy and increases patient safety but also has effects on cost savings. The intervention discussed by Turner et al. [58] was found to be both cost-saving and more effective in reducing polypharmacy compared to standard care. On average, it cost $1,392.05 less per person and resulted in a gain of 0.0769 additional QALYs. Similar effects can be seen in the MedOptimise intervention described by Bryant et al. [51]. The nurse-led educational support program described and analyzed by Coyle et al. [52] estimated annual cost-saving from prescriptions was £31,716.30. While in line with the other studies, Jowett et al. [53] demonstrate the cost-effectiveness of deprescribing but also highlight potentially negative medical implications. In the main analysis, reducing medications lowered costs by £185 per patient compared to the usual care group, but also led to slightly lower QALYs at the same time (0.062 less per patient over a lifetime). This means that standard care was more expensive but also more effective, costing £2,975 per QALY gained. Rea et al. [56] and Crawford et al. [54] examined potential cost avoidance and found that deprescribing interventions could result in annual cost savings of up to $184,221, offering a higher return on investment compared to the amount spent. Xie et al. [57] conducted a decision-analytic model in a hypothetical cohort, making it a model-based study that demonstrates stronger cost-effectiveness signals, whereas real-world trials face greater variability.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to provide a systematic overview of community-based deprescribing strategies and further assess the effects of deprescribing on cost-effectiveness and increased patient safety. The results of the meta-analysis confirmed that deprescribing interventions effectively reduced the number of prescribed medications compared to usual care. This finding supports the intended impact of such interventions and demonstrates their ability to achieve measurable reductions in medication use across diverse settings. The narrative review supports these findings and shows potential trends suggesting deprescribing could have a positive impact on cost-effectiveness and reduction of medication.

The meta-analysis assessed the effectiveness of deprescribing interventions on prescribing-related medication reduction, including outcomes related to PIMs and specific medication types. The results from the Random-Effects Model showed a statistically significant pooled effect size of -0.32 (95% CI: -0.59 to -0.05), indicating that fewer inappropriate or targeted medications were used following the intervention. However, the high heterogeneity (I² = 93%) highlights considerable variation in intervention strategies, patient populations, and outcome measures. The different intervention approaches varied from structured deprescribing protocols to ad-hoc physician decisions. Further, patient populations differed from only including older adults to including all persons taking multiple medications. While these study differences do not dismiss the potential benefits of deprescribing, they emphasize the need for context-specific approaches and further research to determine which interventions work best for which patient groups. They also point to a potential shift from attempts to standardize deprescribing definitions or approaches to attempts to standardize key criteria, effect, and outcome descriptions, and reporting strategies.

Our review and meta-analysis showed that deprescribing interventions had a moderate but statistically significant effect in reducing the use of PIMs in community-based settings, even in the absence of a specific target group or medication focus. Bloomfield et al. [61], in a systematic review focusing on RCTs among community-dwelling older adults, found fewer PIMs in the intervention group in nine out of thirteen trials. However, their review was more restrictive, including only RCTs. This might be a limitation in the context of deprescribing, where high variability in interventions and strong context-specific influences may not be fully captured by RCT-based evidence alone. Other reviews that focused on one specific substance group also reported high heterogeneity. In Soni et al.’s [62] analysis of ten studies conducting benzodiazepine deprescribing with 1,431 participants, heterogeneity in study design and outcomes was highlighted. The authors concluded that deprescribing benzodiazepines could be feasible depending on the method and support used [32], supporting the notion that individualized, well-structured interventions may enhance the success of discontinuation efforts. A study by Olesen et al. [63] further confirms the feasibility of integrating a medication review intervention focused on deprescribing into real-life primary care. The intervention led to 255 medication changes, with over 80% involving deprescribing, and these changes were sustained in nearly 90% of patients over 3–4 months and can be seen as an indicator for successful deprescribing interventions.

Furthermore, studies oriented towards economic outcomes consistently demonstrated the cost-effectiveness of deprescribing interventions. Our analysis suggests that reducing medications, particularly in populations with high medication burden or people living with chronic conditions, leads to significant cost savings, both in terms of direct healthcare expenditures (e.g., fewer hospitalizations, reduced medication costs) and indirect costs (e.g., improved patient productivity and QALYs) [51–58]. While these economic evaluations provide valuable insight and are helpful tools for policymakers, they do not fully address the wider health effects on patients, as can be seen in the different outcomes regarding the QALYs. The cost-effectiveness studies primarily emphasize the potential savings and financial impact of deprescribing. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that deprescribing is a viable and, in many cases, safe and cost-effective strategy within community-based settings, and that they can reduce unnecessary medication use without compromising patient safety. However, the findings are limited by high heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measures, preventing more meaningful comparisons. To provide a more comprehensive view, studies focusing on patient safety were also included, aiming to better understand the potential risks and benefits of deprescribing interventions. This underscores the importance of balancing both economic and health outcomes regarding new interventions. Despite the positive findings of the papers, it is important to note that the cost-effectiveness of deprescribing appears to be decisively context-dependent.

Strengths & limitations

This review has several strengths. It provides a comprehensive and up-to-date synthesis of community-based deprescribing interventions, incorporating both effectiveness and economic outcomes. By including a broad range of study designs and not restricting the analysis to specific patient groups or medication classes, this review reflects real-world clinical complexity and enhances the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the combination of meta-analysis and narrative synthesis allows for both quantitative and contextual interpretation of the evidence.

However, this review has a number of limitations. Firstly, it includes only English-language publications. Secondly, this review focused solely on community-based settings, so the effects of deprescribing for individuals in inpatient / intramural care (e.g., hospitals or long-term care) might differ. Thirdly, the potential for selective reporting and publication bias may have influenced our results by overrepresenting studies with positive or significant outcomes, while underreporting null or negative findings. Additionally, smoking cessation was explicitly excluded in the search strategy to maintain a focused scope on deprescribing interventions in community-based settings. However, this exclusion may have influenced the comprehensiveness of the review, as smoking cessation programs could share overlapping components or outcomes relevant to medication deprescribing, potentially skewing the findings or limiting the generalizability. We also summarized economic aspects related to the interventions to provide context on their financial implications narratively, but did not perform a formal cost-effectiveness analysis due to variability in reported data and heterogeneity across studies. Such an analysis would require standardized outcome measures and consistent economic reporting, which were lacking in the included studies. Finally, the conducted meta-analysis demonstrated high heterogeneity attributable to differences in study designs, populations, intervention characteristics, and outcome measures. This variability made direct comparisons and synthesis of results difficult, reducing the certainty with which conclusions can be drawn from pooled estimates. Furthermore, five out of the nine studies included in the meta-analysis had a high risk of bias, which may have additionally distorted findings.

Regarding the global generalizability of our findings, it is important to note that the majority of included studies were conducted in high-income countries with relatively high Socio-demographic Index (SDI) levels. This limits the applicability of the results to low- and middle-income countries, where healthcare systems, resources, and population health profiles may differ substantially. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings globally, and there is a clear need for more research specifically targeting diverse socio-economic and geographic contexts to better understand the impact across varying SDI levels.

In view of these limitations, future research should concentrate on the contextual and patient-specific complexity of deprescribing by examining it as a heterogeneous yet systemic strategy. This includes exploring how tailored approaches can be effectively implemented across diverse community-based settings, including those in low- and middle-income countries, and evaluating their sustained benefits and potential delayed risks over extended follow-up periods. Further investigation into the long-term effects of deprescribing is essential, as many studies included in this analysis had relatively short follow-up periods, which may not have fully captured the benefits or risks for patients associated with reducing medications over time, nor their long-term sustainability or health care system effects. Without longer-term data, it remains unclear whether reductions in PIMs are maintained over time or whether adverse outcomes emerge after medication withdrawal, although some studies suggest that reductions in PIM use may be sustained at follow-up periods of 6 to 12 months [61, 64]. Addressing these gaps requires longitudinal study designs that enable monitoring of both immediate and long-term effects, including economic outcomes, quality of life, and safety metrics. Furthermore, subgroup analyses exploring the effects of deprescribing in patients with specific conditions would assist in clarifying which groups might benefit most from deprescribing interventions. Following our findings, future research should furthermore concentrate on conducting more detailed economic evaluations, incorporating long-term cost-benefit analyses of deprescribing interventions across a range of healthcare settings and patient groups. Finally, exploring the impact of deprescribing on healthcare system sustainability and resource allocation will be important to better inform policy decisions. Integrating more complex system-level analyses can further support policymaking, where deprescribing interventions are emerging as a valuable tool for improving efficiency and reducing unnecessary medication use.

Despite these limitations, this meta-analysis is the first of its kind to focus on deprescribing interventions within a general community-based setting without targeting a specific patient group or medication type. This review highlights that deprescribing interventions in community-based settings can reduce potentially inappropriate medication use and show promising economic benefits, although findings are influenced by substantial heterogeneity. These results support the role of deprescribing as a relevant strategy in optimizing medication use, while emphasizing the need for context-specific implementation and further high-quality research. Furthermore, the review includes the newest publication on the topic of deprescribing that does not precede 2017, in the case of the economically oriented paper, even 2019, highlighting the timeliness and relevance of the findings for current deprescribing efforts.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that deprescribing interventions in community-based settings are effective in reducing potentially inappropriate medication use. The meta-analysis showed a moderate but statistically significant reduction in PIMs, although considerable heterogeneity across studies reflects variability in intervention design, populations, and outcome measures. In addition to these clinical findings, evidence from the included economic evaluations indicates that deprescribing interventions may contribute to cost savings and more efficient use of healthcare resources.

Taken together, these findings support the role of deprescribing as a relevant and applicable strategy to reduce medication burden and optimize prescribing practices in community-based care. They further highlight the potential of deprescribing interventions to contribute to both improved prescribing quality and more sustainable healthcare systems when appropriately implemented.

This meta-analysis contributes to the existing evidence on deprescribing by offering a broad perspective on its effects in the general community, which is necessarily complex. In light of the mounting emphasis on reducing inappropriate medication use, deprescribing remains a promising strategy that deserves further investigation and prudent implementation in routine healthcare practice.
