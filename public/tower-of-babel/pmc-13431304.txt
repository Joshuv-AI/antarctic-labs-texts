Stereo-electroencephalography–guided radiofrequency thermocoagulation in patients with implanted neuromodulation devices: patient series
Authors: Brin E. Freund, Venkateshwaran Vijayanarasimhan, Adrian Safa, Justyna Ekert, Filippo Emmanuele Colella, Cady Block, David Sabsevitz, Carlos Perez-Vega, Erik H. Middlebrooks, Seyed M. Mirsattari, Richard Byrne, William O. Tatum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThe authors aimed to evaluate the feasibility/safety of stereo-electroencephalography (SEEG) and SEEG-guided radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RFTC) in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy previously implanted with neuromodulation devices, using a retrospective cohort design evaluating patients treated at the Mayo Clinic Florida Epilepsy Center.OBSERVATIONSNine patients (mean age 36.3 ± 10.9 years, 33% female) with prior implantation of neuromodulation devices were included. A vagus nerve stimulator was present in 8 of 9 patients, and 4 patients had undergone previous intracranial neuromodulation. Five patients underwent SEEG-RFTC. The mean SEEG monitoring duration was 6.5 ± 2.9 days. No intraoperative or procedural complications occurred, including hardware-related issues. No permanent or significant neurological/cognitive deficits occurred. One patient experienced transient postoperative neurological symptoms attributed to perilesional edema. Seizure reduction ranged from 50% to 75% following SEEG-RFTC.LESSONSSEEG and SEEG-RFTC are feasible in patients who have implanted neuromodulation devices, including intracranial implants. These procedures may be performed without interference with existing hardware and provide meaningful seizure reduction in highly refractory populations. Further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to better define efficacy and patient selection. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26376

Epilepsy surgery is the standard of care and preferred method of treatment for drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE).1 In some cases, noninvasive data can guide surgical intervention. However, given the increasing complexity of DRE, many situations require intracranial electroencephalography, which involves subdural electrodes or ­stereo-electroencephalography (SEEG).2

SEEG is increasingly used during presurgical evaluation for DRE. SEEG provides data for planning surgery involving deep and superficial cortical locations and may even be considered in diffuse and multifocal epileptogenic zones.3,4 Because of the complexity of these cases, patients may have previously been implanted with neuromodulatory devices.5–7 SEEG in patients with extracranial8–14 and intracranial8,14–17 neurostimulators presents potential challenges and safety concerns.16 SEEG may be useful to help inform revision of neurostimulators17,18 or guide decisions on adding another device.5 However, these patients may also be candidates for SEEG-guided radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RFTC), especially considering that only a minority of patients are rendered seizure free with neuromodulation.19,20 Studies regarding SEEG-RFTC have demonstrated benefit in patients with > 1 epileptogenic focus/node or widespread epileptogenic networks.21–23 The rates of seizure freedom and reduction after RFTC are similar to those with neuromodulation with shorter time to effect.24 It is important to clarify the feasibility of SEEG-RFTC in those with implanted neurostimulators, as it would be a potential therapeutic option in patients with suboptimal responses.22,25 Although SEEG-RFTC is considered safe when done in accordance with best practices and manufacturer recommendations, the data available in this population of patients remain limited.26,27 Given recent FDA approval of a temperature-targeted system,28 cases referred for SEEG-RFTC with implanted neurostimulators are likely to increase. The goal of this study was to evaluate the data of patients previously implanted with intracranial and extracranial neurostimulators, who underwent SEEG with and without RFTC, focusing on the safety and feasibility of this approach.

Study Description

Methods

We conducted a retrospective observational study by accessing our institutional SEEG database. Patients who underwent electrode implantation between January 1, 2018, and October 31, 2025, were included. All cases that underwent any surgical interventions, including SEEG, were discussed at a multidisciplinary epilepsy surgery conference prior to consensus recommendations and subsequent surgical intervention, according to established protocols at our institution aligned with standards of care. A panel of experts in the fields of neurology, neurosurgery, neuroradiology, and neuropsychology review data for consensus regarding surgical planning.

SEEG Electrode Implantation and Monitoring

Prior to surgery, neuromodulatory devices (vagus nerve stimulator [VNS], deep brain stimulator [DBS], and responsive neurostimulator [RNS]) are interrogated for impedance and battery checks and to turn them off. After turning off the neuromodulatory devices, general anesthesia induction is performed using propofol, with insertion of a laryngeal mask airway, maintained with propofol infusion and halogenated gases. A stereotactic head frame is applied, and the patient is placed supine. An intraoperative CT scan is obtained and co-registered with the preplanned trajectory map for accurate neuronavigation. After sterile draping, the Renishaw robotic platform is brought into the surgical field and the semiautomatic arm advanced to the preplanned entry point. The cranium is twist-drilled, and an opening of 2.4 mm is made. The dura is opened using a stereotactic coagulator and cauterized, with an anchor bolt screwed into the cranium along the planned trajectory. A stylet is advanced 1 cm short of its target to create a tract for electrode insertion, implanted via the bolt-secured system. This is repeated in a serial fashion for all predetermined targeted sites. Intraoperative SEEG recordings are monitored to verify placement and function of electrodes. A postoperative CT study is performed to confirm positioning of the electrodes and to exclude immediate perioperative complications. Patients are admitted to the intensive care unit for 24 hours before transfer to the epilepsy monitoring unit. During admission, ictal and interictal SEEG recordings are performed, followed by functional brain mapping (FBM). Parameters of mapping include 50 Hz, pulse width of 200–1000 μsec, current intensities of 0.5–7 mA, and 5-second duration. Seizure provocation is performed with the following parameters for bipolar stimulation: 50 Hz, pulse width of 200–1000 μsec, stimulation intensities of 0.5–7 mA, and 5-second duration; and/or 1 Hz, pulse width of 200–1000 μsec, current intensities of 0.5–7 mA, and 10-second duration. Once sufficient data are derived regarding seizure localization and FBM, electrodes are explanted. If the patient is planned for bedside RFTC, this is performed with monitoring for at least 24 hours after the procedure, with explantation or repeat RFTC based on clinical assessment and EEG data.

RFTC Procedure

Bedside RFTC of the regions involved in seizure onset and propagation was performed using the NeuroOne RF ablation system and EVO electrodes with a contact diameter of 2 mm and 1.5–3.2 mm spacing between them for recording and performing RFTC with a bipolar (between two adjacent contacts on the same electrode) or monopolar technique. Patients underwent continuous video-EEG monitoring and bedside neuropsychological testing. Continuous pulse oximetry and cardiac telemetry were performed during the procedure. RFTC was carried out over 120–180 seconds, with temperatures ranging from 85°C to 89°C. Neuromodulatory devices were then interrogated for impedance and battery checks, with test stimulations being given and prior settings for stimulation and/or recording being restored. All patients had an MRI study within 24–48 hours of explantation of the SEEG electrodes as well as at follow-up 3–6 months after the procedure.

The median duration of inpatient SEEG monitoring was 5 days (IQR 4–9 days, range 3–11 days).

Five patients underwent SEEG-RFTC. Among patients with available follow-up, the median follow-up duration was 7.5 months (IQR 5.25–10.25 months, range 2–62 months). Three patients had dual devices: one patient had a VNS and bilateral anterior nucleus of the thalamus DBS (ANT-DBS), one had a VNS and DBS involving the bilateral centromedian nuclei (CM-DBS), and one had a VNS and bilateral temporal RNS. The dual-device neuromodulation cases (patients 6–8) are described in detail below to illustrate and highlight the role of RFTC in their management given the presence of intracranial neurostimulators. All devices were functional at last follow-up.

Patient 6

A 37-year-old man presented to an outside hospital 15 years prior to RFTC with 2 days of altered mental status, fever and headache, and recurrent focal impaired consciousness and focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. He was immediately transferred to our institution for evaluation and treatment. He underwent lumbar puncture, revealing lymphocytic pleocytosis, and negative viral, bacterial, and fungal studies and cultures. Three-tesla MRI of the brain showed right mesial temporal enlargement and T2 hyperintensity. He was diagnosed with febrile infection-related epilepsy syndrome and was sedated to control his seizures. He received intravenous steroids and trialed multiple antiseizure medications (ASMs). Video-EEG monitoring recorded both subclinical and clinical seizures, with poorly localized ictal patterns arising independently from both hemispheres. He was discharged to an inpatient rehabilitation facility 42 days after admission on levetiracetam, lamotrigine, and phenytoin.

Over a 15-year follow-up, he tried 8 additional ASMs. Bilateral tonic-clonic seizures occurred twice weekly, and focal impaired consciousness seizures occurred multiple times per week. Inpatient and ambulatory video-EEG monitoring revealing multiregional interictal discharges and ictal patterns. Three-tesla MRI of the brain demonstrated right mesial temporal sclerosis with concordant PET findings. A surgical epilepsy conference was convened 6 years after presentation and VNS implantation was recommended. Over 18 months, there was no significant improvement. The case was again presented at a surgical epilepsy conference, and it was decided to proceed with bilateral implantation of ANT-DBS. During a 2-year follow-up, there was a 50% reduction in seizures, but this waned. Genetic testing was unremarkable. Neuropsychological evaluation revealed global, severe cognitive impairment with evidence of decline.

After discussion with the patient and family, SEEG placement was performed with a plan for RFTC for palliative treatment. Twelve electrodes were placed targeting the bilateral temporal, orbitofrontal, middle and anterior cingulate regions;, right parietal region, and left motor cortex (Fig. 1). Interictal discharges were recorded in the bilateral amygdala, hippocampus, lateral temporal neocortex, and anterior cingulate cortex. Multiple electroclinical seizures were captured arising from the right lateral temporal neocortex, hippocampus, and amygdala with early spread and involvement in the right parietal region, anterior and middle cingulate cortices, and lateral frontal region (Fig. 1). Electroclinical seizures were also recorded from the left hemisphere evolving broadly, maximal in the mesial and lateral temporal lobe, orbitofrontal region, and middle and anterior cingulate cortices and spreading to the lateral frontal lobe (Fig. 2). Patient 7

A 50-year-old man with a history of developmental delay, autism spectrum disorder, and DRE was referred to our center 6 years prior to RFTC. Seizures began at 8 years of age, with semiology described as “petit mal,” “jack knife” tonic, and bilateral tonic-clonic. Neuroimaging demonstrated bilateral frontal subependymal nodular heterotopias. Genetic testing was previously performed and was reportedly ­unremarkable. He tried > 10 ASMs, all of which failed. A VNS was implanted 12 years prior to our evaluation. Two years after VNS implantation, he underwent SEEG followed by laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) of the left and right frontal nodular heterotopias. A bilateral ANT-DBS was implanted 1 year later, but he continued to have multiple hospitalizations per year for convulsive and nonconvulsive status epilepticus.

At our institution, 3T MRI of the brain confirmed bilateral ANT-DBS implantation and showed evidence of the prior LITT adjacent to the bilateral anterior horns of the lateral ventricle. Multiple inpatient video-EEG recording sessions demonstrated bihemispheric interictal epileptiform discharges maximal in the left-greater-than-right frontal regions and subclinical/subtle electroclinical seizures arising from these regions with semiologies of impaired consciousness and tonic and bilateral tonic-clonic seizures. Neuropsychological examinations showed exceptionally low performance across all cognitive domains. Recommendation was made for DBS explantation, followed by anterior two-thirds corpus callosotomy for palliative control of bilateral motor seizures. There was > 50% reduction in motor seizures, but focal impaired consciousness seizures persisted. There were multiple hospitalizations over the next 3 years for nonconvulsive status epilepticus averaging twice per month. It was then decided to proceed with replacement of the VNS battery followed by implantation of a CM-DBS. Unfortunately, there was no improvement in seizures over the next year. Repeat SEEG was proposed, with a plan for RFTC for palliative treatment. Electrodes were implanted in the left and right frontal and subcortical regions (Fig. 3). Multifocal interictal epileptiform discharges were recorded, maximal in the left supplementary motor area and anterior cingulate cortex. One right hemispheric focal impaired consciousness seizure was recorded (Fig. 3), and two poorly localized focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures were captured arising and evolving best from the left hemisphere (Fig. 4). Brief bilateral extensor tonic posturing occurred during sleep, although without ictal EEG. FBM was deferred due to low baseline neurological function, and RFTC was performed in the left and right fields of Forel and other areas in both hemispheres anatomically outside regions of eloquence The postoperative course was complicated by multiple focal seizures requiring ASM adjustments, and the patient was discharged on the 7th postoperative day and transferred to inpatient rehabilitation. Tonic-clonic seizures resolved for 2 months following the procedure. At last the follow-up, 10 months later, he had been admitted to the hospital three times for nonconvulsive status epilepticus in the setting of ASM noncompliance, with a reported 75% reduction in seizure frequency. Patient 8

A 25-year-old man with DRE presented initially to an outside facility with new-onset refractory status epilepticus 6 years prior to RFTC. The patient was diagnosed with viral encephalitis (Coxsackie B virus). He was admitted to the ICU, and video-EEG monitoring recorded multiple electrographic seizures arising from the left fronto-centro-temporal region and bilateral independent periodic discharges and ictal asystole requiring a pacemaker. He was discharged on 3 ASMs. Over 1 year, he tried multiple ASMs before referral to our center. At the time of our evaluation, focal impaired consciousness seizures occurred daily. Three-tesla MRI of the brain demonstrated left hippocampal sclerosis. Inpatient video-EEG demonstrated interictal epileptiform discharges in the left frontotemporal and right temporal regions, and focal impaired consciousness seizures arising from the left fronto-central-temporal region. Ictal SPECT demonstrated hypermetabolism in the left hemisphere, maximal in the frontocentral region. PET-CT was unremarkable. Intracranial EEG was pursued, with depth electrodes in the left temporal region and a subdural grid in the left frontoparietal region for FBM. Interictal epileptiform discharges were recorded in the left lateral frontal temporal neocortex. Five focal impaired consciousness seizures occurred, with ictal EEG patterns arising from near the left frontal eye field and motor cortex (face and hand). Eloquence was confirmed during FBM and resection was deferred. An RNS was implanted with two strips placed near the left primary motor area, complicated by infection requiring removal and replacement 2 months later.

The patient was then admitted for video-EEG 16 months later due to lack of clinical improvement. Multiple electroclinical seizures were recorded independently in the left frontotemporal and right regions. The VNS was implanted without benefit. Repeated inpatient video-EEG monitoring over 3 years demonstrated electrographic seizures arising from the left central/centrotemporal regions and the right hemisphere.

SEEG was recommended for RFTC. Electrodes were implanted in the left frontal and temporal lobes and the left occipital lobe, fields of Forel, and anterior, middle, and posterior cingulate cortices, as well as in the right hemisphere (Supplemental Fig. 1). Four electroclinical seizures were captured, with two different ictal EEG patterns: one arising from the lateral left frontal region spreading in the frontal neocortex, middle cingulate cortex, middle and posterior temporal neocortex, and left and right lateral occipital regions (Supplemental Fig. 1); and the other arising maximally in the left field of Forel and deep thalamic nuclei, and right lateral occipital cortex with spread to the left parietal region, right inferior posterior temporal cortex, left anterior and middle cingulate cortices, and lateral frontal lobe (Supplemental Fig. 2). FBM demonstrated eloquent language and motor function in some of the electrode contacts in the left temporal and frontal regions involved in the onset/early propagation of seizures. RFTC was performed outside of these regions, targeting the earliest involved electrodes during the recorded seizures: left lateral temporal, orbitofrontal, and frontopolar regions; left field of Forel; and right lateral temporal lobe. No acute neurological deficits or complications occurred and electrodes were explanted the next day. Five days later, the patient presented to the emergency department with mild right hemiparesis. CT showed evidence of prior RFTC with mild edema in the left frontal region, without hemorrhage or infarction. Deficits were believed to be due to edema from the RFTC involving contacts within the fields of Forel, and he recovered within 3 months with physical therapy. Based on postoperative imaging performed in follow-up, the median distance from the center of the ablation cavity to the closest point in one of the RNS electrodes was 5.8 cm (range 4–11.5 cm). At the 6-month follow-up, the patient was free of tonic-clonic seizures, but focal impaired consciousness seizures persisted, although reduced by 50%.

Informed Consent

The necessary informed consent was obtained in this study.

Discussion

Observations

We present data on the largest cohort published regarding SEEG-RFTC using the NeuroOne system in DRE patients, focusing on previously implanted neuromodulators. This is the first reported series of patients who underwent temperature-guided RFTC with implanted intracranial neurostimulators, demonstrating its potential feasibility, safety, and efficacy. This is also the first cohort, to our knowledge, reported to have undergone SEEG-RFTC targeting diffuse or multifocal epileptogenic networks using the NeuroOne system.21,29

Our cohort of 5 patients (4 previously implanted with intracranial neuromodulatory devices) who underwent SEEG had variable pathologies and epileptogenicity. Ictal and interictal data represented highly complex cases. Targets for RFTC included areas of seizure onset, and also early ictal involvement based on network connectivity with rapid spread or poorly localized patterns. This is similar when considering the mechanism of neuromodulation and may explain the potential benefit of RFTC in these cases. Although the patients who underwent RFTC continued to have seizures, their seizure burden was significantly reduced, as was utilization of healthcare resources. These are reasonable goals considering the complexity of these cases.

The mechanism of action of SEEG-RFTC has been previously described29,30 and involves generating heat to destroy epileptogenic tissue. Although LITT has been used in the setting of intracranial neuromodulatory devices,31 the outcome of RFTC in these patients has not been detailed. Specific to RFTC, electromagnetic interference (EMI) is a concern in the setting of intracranial neurostimulators. EMI may occur through radiation, or the induction or conduction of radiofrequency energy, resulting in unintended effects on electrical components of implanted hardware.32,33 Suspected interfacing between radiofrequency-generating sources and intracranial and extracranial neuromodulatory devices has been reported.34–41 These interactions could pose a risk of secondary tissue injury due to high temperatures generated near the neurostimulator and electrodes, not accounted for by the RFTC electrodes themselves.42–45 They may also generate radiofrequency-induced magnetic fields that could trigger spontaneous reactivation of the stimulator.45 In this cohort, 2 patients had a DBS implanted in the thalamus bilaterally with RFTC performed in the fields of Forel and the frontal and temporal lobes, and 2 patients had an RNS implanted with an electrode in the same lobe where RFTC was performed, demonstrating the potential feasibility of focused RFTC.

SEEG-RFTC could be an alternative treatment in those with ­diffuse network-related or multifocal epilepsies as in our cohort of patients.21,22 Although the outcomes may be modest in these cases, they may be substantial to individual patients, leading to improved quality of life.

The limitations of the study relate to its retrospective nature, relatively small cohort, and single-center series. Furthermore, the exact distance between RFTC and the intracranial leads for the neurostimulation devices was defined in only 1 case with RNS implanted, although cases with DBS implantation and cortical/subcortical RFTC had no complications. Finally, the short-term follow-up limits the ability to formulate a conclusion based on longer-term outcomes.

Lessons

SEEG-RFTC is an option for patients who derive suboptimal benefit from neurostimulators, with an emphasis on targeting network and regional epileptogenic hypotheses. Larger, prospective studies with longer-term follow-up would validate these preliminary findings, especially in patients undergoing SEEG-RFTC nearby implanted device leads. Given the increasing complexity of drug-resistant focal epilepsies and evolving options for treatment, neurostimulation should no longer be an “end of the road” therapy, as these patients may still be candidates for SEEG-RFTC.
