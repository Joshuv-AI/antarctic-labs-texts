Reduced antimicrobial use in weaned pigs through an intervention program supporting veterinarians’ adherence to the clinical practice Streptococcus suis guideline
Authors: Isaura Y A Wayop, Panagiotis Mallioris, Emely de Vet, Menno van Woerkom, Pim Sanders, Jaap A Wagenaar, David C Speksnijder
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVeterinary guidelines have been published in the Netherlands to promote antimicrobial stewardship. An evidence-based intervention program using Implementation Mapping and performance indicators was developed to enhance veterinarians’ adherence to the veterinary guideline “Streptococcus suis in weaned pigs”. The present study evaluates the effect of this eight-month intervention program in a multicenter, pragmatic, stepped-wedge cluster design trial. Of the 49 veterinarians that participated, 33 provided data from 125 S. suis problem farms. A baseline measurement (M0) before the intervention program, and two measurements during (M1) and partly after (M2) the intervention program were conducted. A significant reduction in farm-level antimicrobial use of 25% in M1 and 49% in M2 was achieved compared to M0. For the performance indicators at veterinarian level, a significant reduction (− 36%) was found for antimicrobial prescription in M2, a significant increase occurred in argumentation for 2nd choice antimicrobials (M2) and the usage of bacteriological examination (M1). Two other performance indicators (ratio of 1st to 2nd or 3rd choice antimicrobials and corticosteroids) did not change significantly. These results show how an evidence-based intervention program to foster adherence to an existing veterinary clinical practice guideline achieved a significant reduction in antimicrobial prescriptions by swine veterinarians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81155-x.Keywords: Implementation intervention, Veterinary guidelines, Antimicrobial stewardship, Antimicrobial resistance, Livestock, Performance indicatorsSubject terms: Bacterial infection, Outcomes research, Antimicrobial resistance

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance is an important threat to global health1, responsible for an estimated 1.27 million deaths worldwide and indirectly contributing to 4.95 million deaths in 20192. If no immediate measures are taken to reduce the increasing burden of antimicrobial resistance, it could contribute to up to 10 million deaths3 and US$ 1 trillion or more additional healthcare costs annually by 20504. A One Health approach is necessary to tackle antimicrobial resistance, as humans, domestic and wild animals, plants, and our ecosystems are closely linked and interdependent. To preserve the health advantages of antimicrobials, it is imperative to enhance their responsible use across all sectors.

In the Netherlands, antimicrobial sales for veterinary use have decreased by over 77% since 2009 after the introduction of various governmental and private regulations and measures5,6. However, considerable variation in antimicrobial usage persists across farms in all animal production sectors, and the Netherlands Veterinary Medicines Institute recommended focusing on farms with a persistently high antimicrobial usage and developing interventions for these farms. In terms of the national benchmarking system, in the Netherlands in 2019, almost 25% of farms with weaned pigs used (almost) no antimicrobials, but another 26% still used more antimicrobials than the action threshold and should take action to reduce their antimicrobial use, as indicated by the Netherlands Veterinary Medicines Institute7.

In the Netherlands, the “Streptococcus suis (S. suis) in weaned pigs clinical practice guideline” (S. suis guideline)8 was published in 2014 with the aim of improving antimicrobial stewardship (i.e., responsible use of antimicrobials), with recommendations about the prevention of, and antimicrobial use for, S. suis infections in commercial pig operations. S. suis infections cause severe clinical signs such as meningitis, septicemia, and sudden death, resulting in compromised animal welfare and economic losses9. There is no commercial vaccine available, and infections are considered a major reason for antimicrobial use in weaned pigs10,11. The S. suis guideline was developed based on evidence-based information where available and complemented with expert opinions. However, results of a survey in 2016 indicated a low adoption of the S. suis guideline in practice12. Adherence to the S. suis guideline was further studied using performance indicators, confirming that adherence to the guideline could be improved13.

It is known from human medicine that the implementation of clinical practice guidelines is complex, and research has shown that interventions tailored according to pre-identified barriers and drivers can optimize medical professionals’ guideline implementation and uptake14–16. In veterinary medicine, clinical practice guidelines are relatively new, and currently there are no evidence-based, tailored interventions to support adherence to veterinary clinical practice guidelines. Several intervention programs on antimicrobial stewardship for veterinarians have proven successful in reducing antimicrobial use17–19. Such programs were specifically tailored for veterinarians and contained various program activities, including veterinarians reflecting on their own behaviors, peer learning, and (online) training sessions20.

To support veterinarians’ adherence to the S. suis guideline for swine veterinarians, we developed an evidence-based intervention program using Implementation Mapping21. Implementation Mapping is a systematic step-by-step approach that guides the design of multi-level health promotion interventions and implementation strategies. To inform the intervention program, a qualitative study was conducted to get a better understanding of the perceived barriers to, and facilitators of, veterinarians’ adherence to the S. suis guideline22. To examine adherence to the guideline and, as starting point for the intervention program, we developed performance indicators to measure participating veterinarians’ compliance with the guideline’s recommendations13. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of the eight-month intervention program on antimicrobial use and other defined performance indicators in the 12 months following the baseline measurement.

Methods

Intervention

Between September 2020 and September 2022, a theory-based intervention program that aimed to support swine veterinarians’ adherence to the guideline on S. suis clinical practice in weaned pigs was implemented in the Netherlands. A detailed description of the development using Implementation Mapping and the content of the intervention program was described in a separate publication21. The intervention program was developed based on the existing S. suis guideline (published in 2014) without modifications and included three group-based peer-learning meetings, 1.5 h each, along with an e-learning module to be completed individually (1.5–2.5 h). Peer-learning meetings, chaired by an independent facilitator (from the Dutch Institute for Rational Use of Medicine with extensive experience with pharmacotherapeutic audit meetings (peer meetings of general practitioners and pharmacists in human medicine)), were held at locations familiar to the participants or online (because of COVID) and accredited by veterinary professional organizations. The three meetings took place two and six months apart and the total intervention lasted eight months (Fig. 1).

Study design

The effect of the intervention program was evaluated in a multicenter, pragmatic, stepped-wedge cluster design23. Data were collected over two years from September 2020 to September 2022, from six groups, divided into three measurement periods: measurement period 0 (M0)—the baseline measurement covering the 12 months before the intervention program started, measurement period 1 (M1) covering the first 6 months of the intervention program, and measurement period 2 (M2) covering the last 2–3 months of the intervention program and the follow-up period, three–four months after the intervention program ended (6 months in total). M0 spanned an exact 12 months of measurement. For M1 and M2, we extrapolated 6-month data to estimate the results over a period of 12 months. These measurement periods were selected to align with the intervention program meetings and to assess the program’s impact both during and after its implementation. M1 was necessary for ongoing program activities such as self-evaluation, feedback, and discussions during the last peer-meeting of the intervention. Figure 1 shows the timeframe of the intervention program.

Participants

When the study started, 253 veterinarians were registered as certified swine veterinarians in the Netherlands. It was estimated that roughly 150 swine veterinarians were at that time actively working in clinical practice and that most of them were working in a small number of specialized veterinary practices. Veterinary practices with a swine veterinarian, reflecting the distribution of swine veterinary practices across the Netherlands, were contacted by email and by phone to ascertain their willingness to participate in our intervention program. We decided on a minimum of six and a maximum of 12 participants per peer group, based on expert opinions and literature24,25.

A total of 49 swine veterinarians started participating in the intervention program, with 36 completing the entire program. Of the six peer-learning groups, the veterinarians in groups A, B, C, and D were already peers. By contrast, the veterinarians in groups E and F were not peers before the intervention program started.

The inclusion criterion for our study was that active Dutch swine veterinarians were supervising S. suis problem farms in the Netherlands and Belgium. In the Netherlands, the supervising veterinarian is responsible for prescribing antimicrobial use on the farm and is accountable for most of the farm visits. As a definition for an S. suis problem farm, we adopted the S. suis guideline’s definition, which states that an S. suis problem farm is a farm where antimicrobial use for treating weaned pigs with clinical symptoms of S. suis results in a level of AMU above the threshold or action value as set by the SDa (at that juncture, a defined daily dose per animal per year (DDDAF) of 20) and/or the use of 2nd choice antimicrobials. In the Netherlands, veterinary antimicrobials are classified as 1st, 2nd, and 3rd choice antimicrobials, where 1st choice antimicrobials can be prescribed empirically, 2nd choice antimicrobials can be prescribed if it is well reasoned and documented, and 3rd choice antimicrobials can be prescribed to individual animals only after susceptibility testing because of their importance for public health26. The maximum of S. suis problem farms per veterinarian to be included in our study was five. If a participant had more than five S. suis problem farms under their care, (s)he could choose the five farms with the biggest S. suis problem in terms of their antimicrobial use for S. suis problems.

Of the 49 veterinarians participating in the intervention program, 33 provided data on 136 farms that were considered to have an S. suis problem. The 33 veterinarians did not differ significantly from the 49 veterinarians in terms of basic characteristics. During the final analyses, it was noticed that 11 farms did not meet our inclusion criterion for an S. suis problem farm (despite being advised of our inclusion criterion before the intervention program started) and were therefore excluded. After the intervention program ended, 30 veterinarians provided data four months after (follow-up), covering a total of 106 S. suis problem farms. The most frequent reasons for farms dropping out were that the farmers had stopped farming or that the veterinarian had changed job and therefore dropped out of the program. Data collection

For every farm, the veterinarian provided us with the following data: the antimicrobial and other drug usage, the argumentation for the use of 2nd choice antimicrobials, and the numbers and results of bacteriological examinations. The veterinarian collected the data from the registered prescription reports mandatorily collected in centralized databases, laboratory reports, and records from the veterinary management systems. In the Netherlands, the level of farms’ antimicrobial use is quantified as the DDDAF, which is determined by first calculating the total number of treated kilograms for a farm for a particular period and then dividing this number by the average number of kilograms of animals present on the farm concerned6,27. The DDDAF collected in this study represents the annualized count of days of antimicrobial drug use for a weaned pig on the farm, incorporating the standardized weaned-pig average weight of 17.5 kg in the DDDAF formula. Besides the DDDAF, we measured outcomes of five established veterinarian-level performance indicators: antimicrobial use, the ratio of 1st to 2nd or 3rd choice antimicrobials, the argumentation for 2nd choice antimicrobials, the bacteriological examination of piglets, and the use of corticosteroids. These indicators were developed using a modified RAND/UCLA approach with six steps. The full description of their development and final content has already been published13. Statistical analysis

To analyze differences in farms’ antimicrobial usage, we employed a linear mixed-effects model with the two levels of the intervention program (i.e., two meetings) as fixed effects. Of the 125 S. suis problem farms, eight were removed because of missingness in both M1 and M2. As there were some outliers and to avoid heteroscedasticity of residuals, the DDDAF values were transformed using the natural logarithm but as in Eq. (1) to avoid zero values becoming minus infinity28:

Three nested random intercepts were used, as per the study design, to account for (1) the three measurements per farm, (2) the possible clustering effect of farms cared for by the same veterinarian (ranging from one up to five farms), and (3) the possible effect of grouping veterinarians in the peer-learning groups during the intervention program. A random slope for the different meetings was also added. Next, we examined whether a secular time effect was present by incorporating or not a time coefficient designating the year of each data point and assessing its significance and the change in the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) of the two models. A seasonal effect was assessed in a similar manner by assigning a value for months with a high risk for heat stress (i.e., June, July, August), months of extreme cold (September, October, November, January, March), and months of no particular risk29,30. Possible confounding effects of years of experience and gender of veterinarians were assessed using backward selection based on AIC. Furthermore, to assess whether the annualized/extrapolated DDDAF values of M1 and M2 would introduce any bias in the analysis, the final model was fitted also with just the baseline and the sum of the DDDAF of M1 and M2, which also lasted 12 months, and thus no extrapolation would be needed. As the results were similar, it was decided to extrapolate the DDDAF of M1 and M2 to provide an estimation of the effect size of both meeting periods and not of the intervention as a whole.

Finally, given the longitudinal nature of the data, the presence of Regression to the Mean effect was investigated in two ways; firstly, graphically by plotting a scatterplot of the percentage change difference of DDDAF from baseline to M1 with baseline values for observing unusual deviations31 (Supplementary File 1). Sensitivity analysis was performed, with and without observations, with the most extreme changes to see how the model’s coefficients would deviate. Secondly, the possible influence of a Regression to the Mean effect on the estimate for the intervention from M1 to M2 was assessed by adjusting or not for the mean centered baseline measurements in the model31. Overall, the Regression to the Mean effect did not appear to be an issue, as all the above analyses indicated no significant changes in the estimate of the intervention from M1 to M2 on the DDDAF values across the different models. For the benefit of assessing the average effect of both time periods as stated in our research question, it was decided to use the total three time points as outcome.

Next, we analyzed the performance indicators, ratio of 1st to 2nd or 3rd choice antimicrobials, the argumentation for 2nd choice antimicrobials, the bacteriological examination of piglets, and the veterinarians’ use of corticosteroids. As these four performance indicators were expressed in percentages including the two periods, we used a mixed-effects Beta Inflated regression with a log link. For the antimicrobial use performance indicator, we employed a mixed-effects Gamma regression with a log link, as the outcome was rightly skewed. Both types of models were fitted within the Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS) framework32. For the corticosteroids performance indicator, the farms in Belgium were excluded because Belgium has different legislation regarding the use of corticosteroids and therefore the veterinarians could not prescribe them in the same way as for farms in the Netherlands.

Because the Regression to the Mean, secular time, and seasonal effect were not found for total antimicrobial use, here only the possible confounding of gender and years of graduation was assessed, using backward selection with AIC as before. The random parts in these models were adjusted to two nested random intercepts, as the observational unit was not farms but veterinarians, to correct for the multiple measurements per veterinarian and possible clustering resulting from the groupings of veterinarians during the intervention program, as before. In all models, Bonferroni adjusted alpha levels were used to test significance (i.e., 0.05 divided by the number of covariates within each final model). For the linear mixed-effects model, we used the package lme433. For the other two models, we used the gamlss package32. The statistical analysis was performed using R v 4.2.334. The R script for the models can be found in Supplementary File 2.

Results

Of the 33 participants that provided data, 12 (36%) self-identified as female, and 21 (64%) self-identified as male. The median years since graduation as veterinarian was 18 (range: 0–37). The median of the veterinary practice size was 11 swine veterinarians (range: 1–19), and the median of the working hours as a swine veterinarian was 40 (ranged between 5 and 55). Of the participants, 52% (n = 17) had more than 25 swine farms under their care (ranging from 1 to 65), and 55% (n = 18) reported that they provided care to more than five S. suis problem farms (ranging from 1 to 26). The ratio between the performance indicators did not change substantially in M1 and M2 (Supplementary File 3). Although the antimicrobial prescriptions decreased, the ratio of 1st to 2nd or 3rd choice antimicrobials performance indicator did not change, meaning that there was a corresponding decrease in all categories of antimicrobials. Discussion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of an intervention program to support veterinarians’ adherence to the S. suis clinical practice guideline in improving antimicrobial stewardship in farm animal veterinary medicine. The effect was evaluated by measuring total antimicrobial use on farms and the veterinarians’ outcomes on five performance indicators. The results show a significant reduction in veterinarians’ antimicrobial prescriptions and farms’ antimicrobial use, an increase in argumentation for 2nd choice antimicrobials, and temporary improved application of bacteriological examination. For the other two performance indicators (ratio of 1st to 2nd or 3rd antimicrobials and the use of corticosteroids), the intervention program had no statistically significant effects.

That well-designed intervention programs informed by theory and based on evidence are useful is already known from research in human medicine35–37. In veterinary medicine, theory-informed intervention programs that include components comparable to our intervention program, such as reflecting on one’s own antimicrobial prescriptions and peer learning, have also proven successful17. With this theory-informed intervention program, we were able to reduce the antimicrobial use at S. suis problem farms; this confirms that the availability of a guideline alone does not guarantee the optimal outcome and that our supplementary approach contributes to improving the implementation of veterinary guidelines in daily practice.

Besides the impact of our intervention on antimicrobial prescriptions, the other performance indicators are important for evaluating the effect of the intervention program. Our intervention program also led to more argumentation for 2nd choice antimicrobials. The argumentation for 2nd choice antimicrobials does not need to be a goal in itself, but it initiates awareness at veterinarian level when they are prescribing 2nd choice antimicrobials. Although we found no significant change in the ratio of 1st to 2nd or 3rd antimicrobials performance indicator, we know that the use of 2nd choice antimicrobials decreased, as the ratio between all the performance indicators remained almost the same. This means that we can conclude that our intervention also had an impact on the absolute reduction in the use of 2nd choice antimicrobials.

For the bacteriological examination performance indicator, the results showed a significant effect in M1, but not in M2. Although a tentative S. suis diagnosis is often based on clinical symptoms and presentation of sick animals, bacteriological examination is paramount to confirm the diagnosis and necessary to perform susceptibility testing. In the Netherlands, orally administered trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole is the recommended 1st choice antimicrobial for group treatments, but resistance to this antimicrobial also exists30. To prevent treatment failure, susceptibility testing is necessary. However, some veterinarians in our intervention program mentioned that the S. suis guideline’s recommended frequency for sending animals in for postmortem examinations was too high (four animals recommended for S. suis problem farms), depending on the number of pigs at the farm and S. suis outbreaks and the farmers, as also suggested previously8,12,22. The results of the postmortem examination need to be monitored before they can convince the farmer to conduct another postmortem examination, and this takes more time. The number and frequency of postmortem examinations were identified as questions that needed to be answered in the S. suis guideline. As there is a lack of sufficient evidence, the recommendation relies primarily on expert opinions. It may be necessary to review this recommendation in the S. suis guideline; this could also explain why we have not yet observed an effect in M2 from our intervention program. However, to have a greater impact on this performance indicator, specific interventions should probably also be targeted at the farmer. To study whether the significant effect in M1 had remained, a longer follow-up period would have been necessary.

Our results also suggest that veterinary practice policies play a role in the routine of postmortem examinations and argumentation for 2nd choice antimicrobials. The veterinarians in the group (B) that scored higher for the bacteriological examination indicator mentioned during the intervention that performing postmortem examinations was a common routine procedure in their practice, to which their farmers were accustomed. This suggests that creating an environment where postmortem examinations can be performed easily, along with issuing reminders (such as a collection service for piglets or software system alerts when it is time), can have a significant impact, like methods described in various theories38,39. These differences in veterinary practice policies can also explain why groups E–F had a lower score for the argumentation for 2nd choice antimicrobials in M0 compared with the other groups, as they had no working relation before the intervention program started. This suggests that existing peer groups have an advantage in argumentation for antimicrobials because they provide one another with information. The success of peer-learning groups is also described in the literature40,41.

Regarding the corticosteroids performance indicator, some groups were motivated to prescribe more corticosteroids for clinical S. suis cases, but in the intervention program the veterinarians found that some of their farmers viewed using corticosteroids as additional time-intensive work without an effect. Some veterinarians were surprised by the relatively low corticosteroids prescription numbers, as they had expected to find that they prescribed more corticosteroids, as studied previously (self-reported bias13,42). In light of our results, we assume that, despite the determinants addressed in the intervention program to influence the farmer through the veterinarian, ultimately the farmers had a big influence on the results of this indicator in the same manner, as for postmortem examinations.

Our results show that farm-level antimicrobial prescription reduction can be achieved by targeting the veterinarian only, as farmers and other stakeholders did not directly participate in our intervention program. However, some indicators could potentially have been improved if we had targeted them too. Farmers and other stakeholders have a major—and sometimes crucial—influence, as they will in the end decide what will and what will not be implemented in daily management, as veterinarians cannot force them to comply with their recommendations22,43. We also know that veterinarians have been identified as farmers’ preferred and most trusted source of information regarding animal health and therefore can influence farmers’ decisions44,45. Our intervention program was designed for swine veterinarians, as it targeted implementation of a veterinary clinical practice guideline by veterinarians (who are the only users of the guideline), although we continuously involved farmers and stakeholders during the program’s development. On-farm antimicrobial stewardship programs have been shown to be effective in reducing antimicrobial use and can also be important starting points for developing future intervention programs46.

A wide variation in antimicrobial use can be observed between the six groups in our intervention program. Group E included veterinarians with farms in Belgium and was one of the two groups with a relatively high antimicrobial use before the intervention started compared with the other peer groups. This group showed an impressive decrease in antimicrobial prescriptions. The situation and monitoring regarding antimicrobial use at farms in Belgium is different from the situation in the Netherlands47,48. The Belgian veterinarians in our study (although also working in the Netherlands) were not (or only very limitedly) updated about the Dutch S. suis guideline before the intervention program. Therefore, it can be suggested that this was the first time that there was a trigger to lower their antimicrobial prescriptions and that these farms were the low-hanging fruit, as also experienced with the start of the Dutch antimicrobial reduction strategy5. On the other hand, there was no Regression to the Mean effect in our data and, during the meetings, the complexity of these farms was confirmed and discussed actively, thereby confirming that our intervention program could also work in Belgium. Besides, a decrease was observed in antimicrobial use in the other group (B, which had only Dutch farms) with a relatively high antimicrobial use before the intervention program started. Moreover, a significant decrease in antimicrobial prescriptions was seen in total, irrespective of the level of antimicrobial use in the baseline period (for example in group D, with a relatively low antimicrobial use at M0), suggesting that the intervention was not only effective in peer groups with an exceptionally high antimicrobial use. In addition to the difference in antimicrobial use between groups, it should be noted that there were big differences in antimicrobial prescription patterns between veterinarians within the same group. This suggests that there is an influence not only at the veterinary-practice level, but also at the individual veterinarian level within the same veterinary practices and practice collaborations, as also previously recognized22.

Frontrunner veterinarians can be expected to participate in the S. suis program, as this is common in voluntary antimicrobial stewardship initiatives49. However, we do not think that this is the case in our situation. Approximately 30% of the active Dutch swine veterinarians participated in our intervention program, and we contacted all major veterinary practices, all of which had veterinarians dealing with S. suis issues. The results show a diverse range of basic swine veterinarian characteristics, reflected in varying motivations to join our study. For instance, some sought education or updates on research, whereas others participated because of a personal drive to criticize the program or because of pressure from colleagues or employers. That 49 veterinarians in the intervention program participated but only 33 veterinarians (29 in the end) provided data was mostly because the excluded veterinarians had no S. suis problem farms or lacked time. Still, the basic characteristics did not differ significantly between these groups, and we had sufficient statistical power.

The use of a stepped-wedge design was necessary for logistical reasons (distribution of workload) and the relatively small size of our target group of approximately 150 active Dutch swine veterinarians. This number decreased further as several veterinarians did not meet our inclusion criterion (they had no S. suis problem farms). Consequently, opting for a randomized controlled trial where only half of the clusters received the intervention could have resulted in insufficient statistical power. Our results also demonstrated substantial differences in the groups (cluster-level effects), indicating the potential for greater statistical power using a stepped-wedge design50. This outcome was anticipated, as prior literature has described variations in veterinary practice policies51. However, we also experienced a familiar disadvantage of using a stepped-wedge design, as it took much effort to keep the intervention interval times the same for each group52. Because of people’s various calendars and the COVID situation, we encountered challenges in maintaining consistent intervals between the peer-group meetings. As a result, our fixed schedule meant that some participants were unable to attend. Because of COVID, we also had to schedule a lot of meetings online instead of the originally planned face-to-face group meetings at locations familiar to the veterinarians. Nevertheless, these differences in timing were relatively minor, and we ensured that they were accurately reported in the study and that all the meetings followed the same program, as is recommended53. Another disadvantage of the stepped-wedge design is the absence of a true control group, with the baseline measurement for each group serving as the reference point. This can introduce biases, such as temporal trends, although these were not observed in our data. Since this is an observational study, selection bias is always a concern. To mitigate this, veterinarians were randomly assigned for enrollment, reflecting the density of pig farms across the Netherlands.

The extrapolation of the M1 and M2 data to 12 months was necessary for practical reasons during the intervention program and to show the different impact in M1 and M2 of our intervention. Our results and other results of the monitoring of S. suis outbreaks in the Netherlands did not show a seasonal effect; this legitimates the assumption that the antimicrobial use in six months would be the same in 12 months with no other intervention30.

Performance indicators were used to evaluate the intervention program’s effectiveness in this study and constituted an integral component of the intervention itself. They proved helpful in both tasks. The results of the performance indicators enabled close monitoring of the veterinarians’ behavior and self-monitoring of behavior (keeping a record of a specified behavior as a method for changing behavior), which is known to have an effect in interventions54–56. Our performance indicators did not evaluate animal health because this was too time intensive and difficult to measure13. We know that the veterinary antimicrobial reduction approach in the Netherlands did not result in a decrease in swine farmers’ household income or in an increase in piglets’ mortality rate related to antimicrobial use57. We also know that better animal welfare was found to be associated with lower antimicrobial use58. However, further research on the effect of the performance indicators on animal health is necessary (e.g., other infectious diseases, piglets’ growth).

When one is considering an intervention program for antimicrobial stewardship, it is crucial to emphasize the complexity and underlying factors that influence antimicrobial prescriptions59. There is no one-size-fits-all intervention program. Each intervention needs to be carefully tailored according to scientifically validated theory-based framework/methods (such as Implementation Mapping60) and therefore each intervention will be context specific61. Our approach made tailoring possible and can be copied in other veterinary science intervention programs21.

Conclusion

A well-designed intervention program to foster adherence to an existing veterinary clinical practice guideline has been shown to support a significant reduction in antimicrobial prescriptions and can be regarded as a promising approach to be used for other (new) veterinary clinical practice guidelines.
