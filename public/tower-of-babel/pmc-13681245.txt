What Can Be Learned About Organizational Ethical Blindness When the Wishes of Inpatients in Acute Psychiatric Settings Are Embraced: An Observational Study
Authors: Lisbeth Hybholt, Susanne Winkel, Lene Lauge Berring
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe study explores how the co‐created non‐pharmacological intervention ‘Wishing Well’ may support patient autonomy and recovery‐oriented practice in an acute psychiatric ward. During the ‘Wishing Well’ meetings, inpatients were invited to express concrete wishes for improving daily ward life, which were then discussed collaboratively with peers and staff. The aim was to examine how wishes expressed by inpatients reflected tensions between everyday ward routines and recovery‐oriented values. Observations of 14 consecutive ‘Wishing Well’ intervention meetings over 7 months were analysed, with a focus on the content of wishes, staff–patient interactions, and subsequent actions. Reporting followed the COREQ guidelines. A total of 100 wishes were expressed. The wishes were categorized using the Safewards domains, which revealed that more than half of the wishes (53%) concerned the physical environment. The wishes also related to the staff team (20%), the regulatory framework (8%), the patient community (6%), patient characteristics (6%), and influences outside the hospital (5%). A small proportion (2%) was unclear and could not be categorized. The wishes concerning the staff team shed light on organizational routines that staff had not recognized as being restrictive or inconsistent with therapeutic ideals of recovery‐oriented practices. The findings illustrate that everyday ward routines may unintentionally restrict opportunities for patient autonomy and participation; however, during the meetings staff and patients discussed these routines and negotiated changes to practices. The findings suggest that the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention may provide opportunities for collaboration and reflection on everyday practices that created tensions in relation to recovery‐oriented values.Keywords: inpatients, mental health services, organizational culture, patient participation, psychosocial intervention

Introduction

Historically, a paternalistic approach has been used in the governance of psychiatric wards, whereby the psychiatric institution sets the conditions, rules, and restrictions that limit patients' autonomy. Local rules, including restrictions on phone calls, smoking, and contacting others, are examples of restrictions on social and individual rights that are common in psychiatric hospitals (Alexander and Bowers 2004; Berring, Georgaca, et al. 2024; Hallett, Duxbury, et al. 2024). From a professional perspective, such restrictions are commonly justified by the principle of beneficence, particularly in the form of weak paternalism. In these situations, healthcare professionals act in what they consider to be the patient's best interests when patients are assessed as lacking decision‐making capacity (Hempeler et al. 2024).

In this respect, some patients view hospitalization and restrictions as a necessary protection, whereas others perceive them as a violation of their autonomy (Silva et al. 2023). Patients may feel coerced, for example, when they feel excluded from accurate and reliable information (Berring, Georgaca, et al. 2024) or from decision‐making, and if they experience staff as unsupportive or disrespectful (Silva et al. 2024). This presents a challenge as societies aim to reduce the use of formal and informal coercive measures in psychiatry (World Health Organization 2023) and recognize that conflicts can arise when patients encounter a controlling attitude from staff (Papadopoulos et al. 2012; Szmukler and Appelbaum 2008).

All kinds of coercion ‐ formal, informal, and perceived ‐ seem to have primarily negative effects on the patients' possibilities for recovery (Berring, Georgaca, et al. 2024; Jordan and McNiel 2020; Silva et al. 2023, 2024). Further, experiences of coercion raise significant concerns related to ethical principles such as respect for patient autonomy, beneficence, non‐maleficence, and justice (Hallett, Whittington, et al. 2024; Hem et al. 2018). It is therefore important that patients are protected from having their rights violated and are not subjected to unnecessarily restrictive practices, and that staff demonstrate an increased awareness of safeguarding patients' rights to ensure that restrictive practices are used only when necessary and proportionate.

Background

The World Health Organization's guidelines call for a person‐centred, recovery‐oriented approach to mental health care that is grounded in human rights (Funk et al. 2025). However, research indicates that recovery‐oriented care supporting connectedness, hope, identity, meaning, and empowerment (Leamy et al. 2011) remains difficult to implement in acute psychiatric wards (Chatwiriyaphong et al. 2024; Leamy et al. 2023; Lorien et al. 2020). Competing demands, routines, and institutional constraints often take priority over recovery‐oriented values, thus limiting patients' autonomy, self‐determination, and opportunities to take the initiative in their own recovery process, despite staff ideals (Mathisen et al. 2016; Waldemar et al. 2018, 2019).

A narrative synthesis of patients' perspectives regarding activities in psychiatric wards found that patients often experienced boredom and a lack of involvement in the ward activities, which negatively affected their well‐being. The synthesis also highlighted limited involvement of service users and a lack of co‐design approaches in the implementation of ward activities (Foye et al. 2020). To address these issues, mental health organizations must rethink their approach and collaborate with service users on how to move away from unnecessary restrictions, for instance, by implementing Trauma‐Informed Care (Berring, Holm, et al. 2024).

To strengthen patients' voices and enforce recovery‐oriented values, a co‐operative inquiry group (Heron 1996) involving five staff members, four individuals with current lived experiences as inpatients at an acute psychiatric ward, and a researcher developed and pilot‐tested the non‐pharmacological intervention ‘Wishing Well’, which comprised regularly held meetings that were open to all current patients and staff. The purpose of the meetings was to provide a transparent, collective space in which patients could articulate concrete wishes to improve ward life in general, for example, regarding routines, daily activities, or the physical environment. Each wish was discussed through dialogue in the meetings. The wishes that the participants at the meeting considered to have collective relevance were taken forward for further action, with a designated person responsible for follow‐up. The ward's management had made a commitment to accommodate requests whenever possible and not to reject them without first exploring the available options (Psykiatrien Øst 2023).

Inviting patients to articulate and discuss wishes concerning everyday ward life may support a shift from a psychiatry in which staff is solely responsible and in charge of the ward milieu toward more transparent dialogue about how the ward milieu is experienced, negotiated, and potentially changed. The aim was to examine how wishes expressed by inpatients reflected tensions between everyday ward routines and recovery‐oriented values on acute psychiatric wards.

Methods

The study was guided by a qualitative descriptive approach (Sandelowski 2000, 2010), and the data were generated through systematic participant observations of the Wishing Well meetings as they unfolded in practice. This approach was chosen because it makes it possible to provide a practice‐near account of the wishes expressed by inpatients and of how these wishes were discussed and processed during and after the meetings.

Setting

The ‘Wishing Well’ intervention was carried out in Denmark in an adult acute psychiatric ward for individuals aged 18 to 75 years. The ward had 13 private patient rooms. The inpatients had a variety of diagnoses, such as affective disorder, schizophrenia, and personality disorder. Many patients had been readmitted to the ward and had thus become familiar with its routines and staff over time. Some inpatients were permitted to leave the ward for a few hours or overnight if the psychiatrist approved; others were not. The ward staff utilized various coercive measures as permitted by Danish legislation. The ward included voluntary and non‐voluntary patients as well as patients with a forensic psychiatric treatment order. The ward staff comprised 25 interdisciplinary staff members.

Participants and Data‐Generation

Data were generated through observations of a purposive, event‐based sample comprising 14 consecutive ‘Wishing Well’ intervention meetings over a seven‐month period from January to August 2022. Posters displayed in the ward invited inpatients to participate in the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention. Each meeting, held on a Monday evening every fortnight, lasted 45 min. The agenda was facilitated by ward staff and consisted of: (1) presentation and dialogue regarding new wishes, (2) status update on ongoing wishes, and (3) dialogue on what the participants could do together to meet the requests. The data set for this paper consisted of observations of the meetings, e.g., the number of participants, the background and content of the presented wishes. Furthermore, it was observed whether a wish was accepted for processing, how it was processed, and the reasons for rejection if it was not processed or fulfilled after processing. The observations were documented in detailed field notes taken during and immediately after each meeting (Bernard 2011, 256–305).

Analytical Strategy

The analyses followed a structured approach guided by the three concurrent analytical activities: (1) data condensation, (2) data display, and (3) drawing and verifying conclusions (Miles et al. 2020). The data condensation was inspired by descriptive coding, which is particularly suitable for non‐interview data, with the aim of summarizing the content of the data (Saldaña 2025). First, the authors discussed what the inpatients had wished for and what happened when inpatients made wishes for the general improvement of the ward, particularly in relation to ward routines and recovery‐oriented values. Several wishes concerned the acquisition of items and materials and could be realized with relatively limited financial or time resources, whereas others addressed issues that required changes in staff actions or ward routines. To organize the dataset, all three authors categorized each wish into one of the six domains of the Safewards model (Bowers et al. 2014): (1) the patient community, (2) the patient characteristics, (3) the regulatory framework, (4) the staff team, (5) the physical environment, and (6) outside the hospital. The domains concern different aspects of everyday life in psychiatric wards that have the potential to influence conflicts in the ward. Safewards is an organizational approach that encourages staff and clients to move away from coercive practices by working together to make the ward safer for everyone. ‘Safer’ means a calmer and more positive place (Bowers et al. 2014).

Based on the description of the domains, the authors individually assigned each wish to one of the six domains, then met to discuss disagreements and agree on a domain placement for the purpose of categorization and data display. Of the 100 wishes expressed, 54 were categorized as belonging to the same domain by all three authors. Two wishes were unclear and were not clarified at the ‘Wishing well’ intervention; thus, no data were generated about the meanings of these wishes. In 34 cases, two authors agreed, while the third disagreed. No individual author consistently dissented more than the others. In 10 cases, all three authors disagreed. For example, the wish: ‘A computer with a printer for the patients’ was placed in different categories: (1) as the physical environment, because it was a thing/object in the ward; (2) as a regulatory framework, because it was a part of the framework conditions and the house rules; (3) as outside the hospital, because the reason for the wish was to facilitate contact with authorities and employers, access the bank, and pay bills. Through discussion, it was agreed to place the wish in the domain ‘Outside the hospital’, as the aim was to reduce stressors from outside the hospital. A computer would make it possible to handle practical matters that may have severe consequences if left unattended. All such disagreements prompted dialogue about the authors' understanding of the domains until an agreement on how to categorize the wish was reached. Agreement was sought in order to be able to categorize the wishes and produce a data display (see Table S1).

Data display involved mapping the number and nature of wishes within each Safewards domain. This provided a structured overview that allowed us to identify which aspects of ward life the wishes related to. We found that some wishes highlighted potential flashpoints, that is, events or social circumstances closely tied to each domain that could trigger conflict when the wishes challenged established ward practices that staff did not notice or see as problematic. However, from the inpatients' perspective, these practices were considered to be incomprehensible or unnecessary restrictions.

We reached and verified our conclusions with a focus on interpreting and explaining what happened when patients proposed these kinds of wishes. We drew on the concept of organizational routines, understood as repeated, recognizable patterns of interdependent actions involving multiple actors. Organizational routines are essential because they ensure reliable and effective goal achievement over time, reduce uncertainty, ensure stability, and serve as sources of knowledge. However, they can also normalize and, unintentionally, maintain unethical practices (Howard‐Grenville et al. 2016; Kump and Scholz 2022; Parmigiani and Howard‐Grenville 2011). Using this theoretical lens, we examined how specific types of patients' wishes could help identify organizational routines that conflicted with recovery‐oriented practices.

Lived Experience Involvement and Reflexivity

The ‘Wishing Well’ intervention was developed and pilot‐tested through a co‐operative inquiry group (Heron and Reason 2008) that included individuals with current lived experience as inpatients. However, they did not want to be involved as co‐analysts in the present study and are therefore not included in the group of authors. The analysis reported in this paper should therefore be understood as a researcher‐led analysis of a co‐created intervention, based on observations made by the first author. The first author, a female nurse and experienced qualitative researcher, does not have lived experience as an inpatient. She was employed as an assistant professor and senior researcher (PhD) at the psychiatric hospital's research unit at the time of the study. She conducted the observations and engaged in moderate interaction, focusing on the wishes presented by inpatients, the dialogue during the meetings, and the subsequent processing of the wishes. Although she had been involved in developing the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention, she had no therapeutic or managerial responsibilities and had no authority over the staff facilitating the meetings. This position provided contextual familiarity with the intervention and the staff members. The co‐authors (SW and LLB) are both experts in the Safewards model. SW teaches the model to employees as part of her daily work, and as a supervisor, she is engaged in the daily practice at the wards. LLB has translated the Safewards model into Danish and has extensive expertise in Safewards and conflict prevention in psychiatric settings, including recent international work on Safewards fidelity measurement (Björkdahl et al. 2026). The interpretive claims regarding organizational routines, ethical blindness, and recovery‐oriented practice should therefore be understood as reflecting the analytical perspective the author team found productive for exploring tensions between everyday ward routines and recovery‐oriented values on acute psychiatric wards.

Ethical Considerations

The Danish Data Protection Agency (REG‐088‐2021) and the regional research ethics committee (EMN‐2021‐05549) were notified of the study, in accordance with Danish legislation. The study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association 2024) and the ethical conduct of nursing research (Northern Nurses' Federation 2003). Participants were informed about the research through a pamphlet that described the study's aim and clearly stated that participation in ‘Wishing Well’ was voluntary. Furthermore, the researcher who observed the meetings (LH) gave a brief presentation about herself and her interest in recovery‐oriented practices. She explained that she attended the ‘Wishing Well’ as an observer and outlined the type of data being generated. It was emphasized that she would not collect any data that could be linked to individuals; therefore, all participants in the intervention remained anonymous to the researcher.

Results

In all, 51% (74 out of 145: 24 males, 50 females) of the inpatients who were present in the ward participated in the meeting. Reasons for non‐participation were not collected. In total, the inpatients expressed 100 wishes, of which 53 were processed, and 21 were fulfilled during the observation period. Seven wishes were placed on a waiting list, and 40 were declined; the most common reasons for declining the wish were that the wish was combined with another wish (n = 13), that it already existed (n = 7), or that there was non‐consensus among the inpatients about the relevance of the wish (n = 6). Further reasons wishes were declined included security concerns, hygiene issues, or that the wish was already in progress (n = 14). Wishes were categorized into all six Safewards domains. Most of the wishes related to the physical environment, followed by wishes relating to the staff team, the regulatory framework, the patient community, patient characteristics, and finally outside the hospital. Together, these categories illustrate the diversity of inpatients' everyday concerns. However, wishes affecting the staff team were the most analytically interesting for the aim of this paper, as they highlighted tensions between routines and the patients' perceptions of incomprehensibility and unnecessary restrictions. Therefore, the following section focuses on this domain. The two examples below illustrate wishes in which patients pinpointed routines that staff had not considered and did not perceive as problematic.

Example 1: The Staffroom Door Notice

The wish was in written form and stated: ‘Feels like a bother because there is a note on the staffroom door saying, “do not disturb”. Wish for a new note and more approachability from the staff’. The sign on the staffroom door stated: ‘We have reports from 7:00–7:15 am, 8:00–8:15 am, 3:00–4:00 pm, and 11:00–11:30 pm. DO NOT DISTURB’. The patient who expressed the wish was present at the meeting and elaborated on how he would never interrupt the staff, as it was forbidden, even when he had many symptoms and was in great need of support from the staff. Other patients agreed, describing how the sign symbolized that the staff was unavailable and that patients were expected to manage on their own, even when they struggled to do so.

During the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention, staff acknowledged the experience and expressed surprise, emphasizing that this was not at all the intended message: the purpose was simply to ensure a smooth and undisturbed handover. One staff member suggested reformulating the wording, and together the participants at the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention agreed on the following: ‘The door is closed because we are handing over reports at the following times: 7:00–7:15 am, 8:00–8:15 am, 3:00–4:00 pm, and 11:00–11:30 pm. If you need anything, please feel free to knock!’. The new wording clarified that the door was closed for privacy during reporting, but that patients were still welcome to knock if they needed support. After the meeting, an unknown staff member added a shushing‐face emoji to the new sign. The intention behind this addition was not explored, but the symbol seemed to echo the earlier message of not wanting to be disturbed during the report. Regardless of intent, the revised sign continued to convey mixed signals about accessibility.

We interpreted this wish as revealing a potential flashpoint, a routine, long‐standing element of ward life that staff no longer noticed. For patients, however, this functioned as a perceived barrier that communicated exclusion and inaccessibility. It deprived them of seeking necessary support during periods of distress. The staff had not reflected on how the sign was received because it had become part of the everyday order of things at the ward. From a recovery‐oriented perspective, this lack of attention and the deprivation of autonomy to act in one's own best interest can itself be seen as ethically problematic. When routines are taken for granted, staff may stop asking how patients experience them. What appears to be a meaningful organizational structure may, through habituation and unawareness, reproduce subtle forms of unnecessary restriction and distance.

Example 2: Access to Coffee

Several wishes concerned access to coffee around the clock. This prompted a lively discussion among patients about both the noise from the coffee machine and the question of whether drinking coffee late at night would disturb sleep. Opinions were divided: some saw late‐night coffee as a normal part of their daily routine, which they appreciated; some found it calming to drink coffee when they felt bad and could not sleep; some had experienced that coffee kept them awake and therefore thought it would be unwise to drink coffee at night; and others preferred restrictions to ensure quietness and not being disturbed by the coffee machine.

The wish also revealed disagreement among staff members. Some considered it paternalistic, even contrary to basic human rights, to forbid coffee, whereas others argued that it was in the patients' best interest to restrict caffeine intake in the evening, mentioning disturbances to their sleep at night and the importance of sleep to mental health. Other staff expressed concern that unrestricted access to coffee might encourage many patients to stay up late, which might lead to a broader loss of control over ward routines and make the situation unmanageable. This reasoning reflected a long‐standing logic of care focused on maintaining order and deciding what is best for the patient rather than supporting autonomy, a view that once seemed responsible but clashes with the recovery‐oriented approach, which emphasizes the patient's experiences and expert knowledge about their own reactions and preferences. It was not possible to come to an agreement about fulfilling this wish at the ward, and eventually the chief nurse decided that coffee with and without caffeine should be available at all hours in noise‐free thermos pots to balance autonomy and quietness in the evenings.

We interpreted this wish as illustrating how practices once understood as caring can, over time, become routines, a source of ethical blindness. Restricting access to coffee has long been framed as a way to protect patients' sleep and maintain order and was taken for granted as a self‐evident part of good nursing. The practice was thus based on a source of professional knowledge, but it also implied that inpatients in psychiatry were not capable of deciding for themselves whether or not to drink coffee. The wish made the underlying assumptions visible and negotiable, and it also exposed disagreements among staff members: some viewed the restriction as paternalistic, whereas others defended it as an act of care. Within a recovery‐oriented framework, however, such practices risk reproducing unnecessary restrictions under the guise of care. The staff had not discussed the rule as a potentially unnecessary restriction until it was explicitly questioned by the patients. The wish thus revealed how well‐intentioned routines can persist beyond their ethical justification and, in doing so, unnecessarily limit patients' self‐determination.

Ordinary Practices With Unintended Consequences

Given the premise that organizational routines can be a source of unethical practices, both examples illustrate potential flashpoints that arise not from conflict or crisis but from routines the patients struggle with in silence and that have become so ordinary that staff no longer question them. The interaction between patients and staff was central to the flashpoints, as routines, interpretations, and unspoken agreements shaped much of daily life in the ward. When such routines are taken for granted, they can persist as organizational habits that seem neutral or even necessary to staff, but from a recovery‐oriented perspective, they may limit patients' possibilities for participation and self‐determination. During the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention, patients and staff discussed ordinary, taken‐for‐granted practices, thereby turning them into something that could be discussed and reflected upon together in ways that brought potential ethical blind spots into focus. In that sense, the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention created a space in which the routines of everyday ward practice could be brought forward and negotiated, instead of being silently reproduced as part of ‘how things are done here’.

Discussion

The study illuminated the potential of the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention to uncover organizational routines that may conflict with recovery‐oriented values (Leamy et al. 2011). Importantly, these tensions should not be understood as a simple opposition between inpatients and staff. Rather, the ‘Wishing Well’ meetings created a space in which different understandings of everyday ward routines could be articulated and discussed across positions. In some cases, inpatients and staff expressed similar concerns, whereas in other cases both groups disagreed internally about what should be understood as care, restriction, autonomy, or risk. In this way, patients' wishes may draw attention to ethical blind spots in everyday organizational routines and provide staff with opportunities for learning and critical reflection.

The analysis showed that organizational routines may sustain actions that initially appeared neutral or justified to staff, but which, through dialogue about patients' wishes, came to be seen as conflicting with recovery‐oriented ideals such as autonomy and empowerment. Such actions may be unethical not by intent but by omission, habits, organizational structures, and unawareness (Kump and Scholz 2022). In our findings, this was illustrated by routines that the staff members had not identified as problematic, but which some inpatients experienced as an unnecessary restriction of autonomy. By making the inpatients' wishes a topic for collective discussion, the intervention had the potential to expose tensions between recovery‐oriented ambitions and daily routines, but also to show that translation of wishes into change in everyday practice was not straightforward. This was exemplified by the addition of the shushing‐face emoji to the revised door sign. The ‘Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research’, which has been updated on the basis of user feedback, suggests that integration of new practices is influenced not only by the intervention itself but also by organizational culture and its compatibility with existing routines and workflows (Damschroder et al. 2022). Seen from this perspective, the example with the door sign may serve as an illustration of how existing understandings of order and acceptable behaviour remained influential despite attempts to create a more recovery‐oriented approach through listening to the inpatients.

According to literature on organizational routines, ethical blindness can be understood as occurring when repeated and taken‐for‐granted practices obscure the moral implications of staff actions (Howard‐Grenville et al. 2016; Kump and Scholz 2022; Parmigiani and Howard‐Grenville 2011). In this study, not allowing patients to drink coffee at a certain time of day is an example of this. Habits and old ward rules can limit the staff's ability to recognize situations as ethically problematic in relation to current best practice. Such minor ethical issues can escalate into more substantial moral problems that potentially contribute to the development of a corrupt organizational climate in which the needs of staff are prioritized over those of patients (B. Paterson et al. 2013), such as a sign on the door that tells patients not to disturb staff or maybe trying to ensure a quiet night for staff by not serving coffee. Although organizational routines may in some cases appear to be unethical, Paterson et al. (2025) argue that such interpretations risk obscuring the structural and relational dynamics that shape practice. Reducing the misuse of restrictive interventions requires genuinely whole‐organization strategies. Uni‐dimensional frameworks appear to be insufficient and should be replaced with a binary perspective that recognizes the imperative to safeguard the human dignity of both those receiving care and the staff providing it.

Further, the findings are interesting to understand in the light of perceived coercion, which is known to stand in contrast to the recovery‐oriented ideals of connectedness, hope, identity, meaning, and empowerment (Funk et al. 2025; Sampogna et al. 2019; Silva et al. 2023, 2024). Perceived coercion can be understood as the individual's subjective experience of being coerced, an experience regarded as valid, irrespective of the actual presence of formal or informal coercion (Lessard‐Deschênes et al. 2025). Around one in four non‐voluntary patients did not perceive their treatment as coercive, whereas a similar proportion of voluntary patients did report experiences of coercion (Newton‐Howes and Stanley 2012). This highlights that coercive experiences may be embedded in ordinary clinical routines and organizational practices that risk becoming normalized and therefore less visible to staff. The discussions about wishes to change routines made it apparent that both patients and staff interpreted the meaning of the routines in a variety of ways. Sharing these interpretations may provide an opportunity to explore patients' experiences of restrictions and to consider whether routines should be changed. This is important, as perceived coercion weakens the therapeutic alliance, reduces treatment engagement and satisfaction, and increases the risk of post‐discharge suicide attempts (Jordan and McNiel 2020; Silva et al. 2023, 2024). Because mental health professionals commonly use informal coercive practices (Hempeler et al. 2024; Puras and Gooding 2019), even without being aware of it, they also tend to underestimate the extent to which their actions may be perceived as restrictive by the patients, particularly when the pressure is subtle (Hempeler et al. 2024). The sharing of perspectives during the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention meetings provided opportunities to discuss how patients experienced ward routines, including whether they perceived them as coercive. Coercive measures can, in some instances, be carried out with less harm when staff remain present, supportive, and committed to upholding the individual's dignity (Berring, Georgaca, et al. 2024).

The examination of the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention helped us pinpoint routine practices in the organization that did not align with current ideologies for a beneficial ward milieu. The result revealed disagreements about the staff's approach regarding, for instance, what inpatients can decide for themselves. This awareness opened a space for critical reflection that, in the long term, may help to disrupt and challenge established practices. Furthermore, it became evident that the patients were able to articulate many of the same arguments as the staff; for example, both patients and staff had firm beliefs about coffee‐drinking practices. This shows that patients are concerned about the practices that guide the rules in psychiatry wards. However, reducing restrictive practices requires more than the introduction of a ‘Wishing Well’ intervention. It also requires ongoing organizational attention to whether such interventions are delivered as intended. The study by Björkdahl et al. (2026) in which active ingredients across the 10 Safewards interventions were identified, illustrates the importance of monitoring implementation fidelity to ensure that key therapeutic and relational components are not lost in everyday clinical practice. This is an important step in rethinking a psychiatry that embeds the human rights perspectives in its approach. If psychiatry is to move toward the co‐creation of the ward environment, approaches that bring inpatients and staff together to share their perspectives may offer a method for doing so.

In the Safewards model, a flashpoint refers to a critical moment in which rising tension creates a heightened risk of conflict and in which staff actions can determine whether the situation escalates or is defused (Bowers et al. 2014). It requires courage and professional judgement to draw on this knowledge within a ward, as it demands immediate, situationally attuned interventions: here and now encounters with the other, context‐sensitive actions that cannot be fully guided by protocols—actions in practice that can have serious consequences and may be judged as right or wrong. However, it is always possible to return to and develop a deeper understanding of clinical decisions through post‐occurrence reviews, as recommended by McKenna et al. (2024). Such reflective revisiting is essential for learning, professional development, and the refinement of relational practice. We argue that the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention has the potential to serve as an organizational mechanism for revisiting and critically reflecting on routine ward practices and rules that are often taken for granted in psychiatric care.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is that it was initiated in response to an explicit request from a psychiatric organization to explore how recovery‐oriented practice might be strengthened. The ‘Wishing Well’ intervention was jointly developed by patients and staff and was systematically structured and implemented over a two‐year period, which made possible a sustained examination of the processes of cultural and relational change. Although the intervention was developed and pilot‐tested with people who were inpatients at the time, lived experience collaborators were not involved in the categorization, analysis, or authorship of the present paper. This limits the scope of our interpretations, particularly as the study addresses autonomy, paternalism, and recovery‐oriented practice. However, the continuous involvement of staff and patients in the ‘Wishing Well’ meetings appeared to facilitate ongoing learning and reflection throughout the study. Instances in which staff reconsidered routine practices and assumptions were documented in the field notes and are reflected in the findings. This suggests that the intervention itself may have contributed to increased awareness of potential ethical blind spots in everyday clinical practice. Nevertheless, future studies of the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention should involve lived experience collaborators in the analytical process. A further limitation is that the findings reflect the ward context and practices during the data collection in 2022 and may not fully reflect current practices or circumstances.

The six Safewards domains provided a meaningful analytical framework for interpreting the expressed wishes and enabled a nuanced understanding of how everyday practices, interactions, and environmental factors shape experiences of recovery. We interpreted the data through a systematic qualitative analytical approach (Miles et al. 2020; Saldaña 2025), which supported our ability to move beyond specific events and generate higher‐order conceptual insights.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The involvement of two of the authors (L. H and S. W) in clinical practice may have shaped our interpretations, although we included an external researcher in the analysis and evaluation phases to broaden the range of interpretations considered. The study was conducted in a single ward, and the findings should therefore be understood within its particular context. Nonetheless, in‐depth qualitative investigations of this kind can contribute to an understanding of the cultural and contextual dynamics that influence practice change in mental health settings.

Conclusion

This study illustrates the ways in which the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention brought attention to organizational routines in the acute psychiatric ward studied that limited patients' autonomy and participation in daily life on the ward. Practices that staff perceive as neutral or necessary may be experienced by patients as restrictive or difficult to understand. During the ‘Wishing Well’ meetings, both patients and staff had the opportunity to share perspectives, reflect on existing routines, and negotiate changes in daily practices on the ward. The findings suggest that this process may help make potentially unnecessary restrictions visible and create opportunities to strengthen recovery‐oriented values, including autonomy and empowerment. The findings also suggest that well‐intentioned practices may sustain unnecessary restrictions if they are not regularly questioned and discussed. Overall, the findings suggest that the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention may support a more ethical and recovery‐oriented approach to psychiatric care by providing patients with a structured opportunity to contribute their perspectives on the ward milieu through dialogue and shared reflection.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

The findings suggest that the ‘Wishing Well’ intervention may provide a structured approach to involving patients in dialogue about ward life and everyday practices. Such involvement may support transparency, mutual understanding, and collaboration between staff and patients. By systematically listening to patients' wishes, staff may identify routine practices that unintentionally restrict autonomy or create tension and consider whether and how those practices may be changed. Co‐designed interventions of this kind may contribute to a more recovery‐oriented ward environment by providing patients with a formal avenue for expressing their perspectives and participating in discussions about ward practices.
