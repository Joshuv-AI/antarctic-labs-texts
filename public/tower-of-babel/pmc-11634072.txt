A commentary on concerns regarding ‘Metabolic syndrome and surgical complications: a systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 million individuals’
Authors: Yuquan Chen, Yanwei You, Qi Zhang, Qiang Cao
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

We commend the article ‘Metabolic syndrome and surgical complications: a systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 million individuals1’ for its insightful analysis of the risks metabolic syndrome (MetS) poses in surgical outcomes. Analyzing data from 63 studies involving 1 919 347 patients, the study highlights that patients with MetS face significantly higher risks of mortality, surgical site infections, cardiovascular complications, longer hospital stays, and increased readmission rates. It stresses the urgent need for targeted interventions across the surgical continuum, including preoperative screening, surgical prehabilitation, and postoperative management, to mitigate these risks. This extensive review underscores the critical impact of MetS on surgical safety and outcomes, advocating for a strategic focus on managing MetS in surgical patients to improve their care and prognosis. However, we wish to express some concerns and suggest areas for further clarification and improvement:

First, as one of the most crucial aspects of the PRISMA guidelines, the assessment of publication bias and its potential impact on the findings is not thoroughly discussed. For example, upon re-evaluating the authors’ assessment of publication bias for surgical site infection (SSI) across various subgroups, the results of publication bias by Egger’s test showed that the P values for the assessment of publication bias for Superficial SSI, Deep SSI, Organ Space SSI, and Wound Dehiscence were 0.4178, 0.7180, 0.0069, and 0.6823, respectively. The funnel plots for these four groups’ publication bias assessments, as shown in Figure 1, clearly indicate the presence of publication bias in the Organ Space SSI category. Additionally, when adjusting for bias using the Trim-and-fill method and subsequently re-evaluating with a random-effects model2, it was found that surgical patients with MetS showed no significant difference in the risk of organ space SSIs within 30 days of surgery compared to patients without MetS (OR 1.07, 95% CI: 0.83–1.38; P=0.619). However, the original authors reported a significant difference, stating that surgical patients with MetS were at 1.3 times the risk of organ space SSIs within 30 days of surgery compared to those without MetS. This highlights the importance of adjusting for publication bias, as failing to do so may lead to conclusions that contradict the actual trends3.

Funnel plot of publication bias of surgical site infections. (A) Superficial surgical site infection (SSI). (B) Deep SSI. (C) Organ space SSI. (D) Dehiscence.

Second, the inclusion criteria for the studies appear broad, potentially introducing heterogeneity that could affect the meta-analysis’s outcome. For instance, the variability in the definition of metabolic syndrome across studies and the diverse surgical procedures might have influenced the pooled risk estimates. A more detailed analysis that stratifies the results by type of surgery or specific components of metabolic syndrome could offer more nuanced insights.

Third, while the study endeavors to account for confounding factors, the extent to which adjustments were made for other risk factors (e.g. age, smoking status, or medication use) in the included studies is unclear. These factors could independently influence surgical outcomes and potentially confound the relationship between metabolic syndrome and complications.

Fourth, the clinical heterogeneity among the included studies regarding patient populations, healthcare settings, and outcome measures raises questions about the generalizability of the findings. Simultaneously, some crucial subgroups were not considered, possibly leading to higher heterogeneity. A subgroup analysis or sensitivity analysis exploring the effects of these variables on the overall results could enhance the study’s applicability to diverse clinical contexts. For instance, it would be beneficial to include subgroup analysis results categorized according to criteria such as study design and country.

Last but not least, while the study importantly highlights the association between metabolic syndrome and increased risk of surgical complications, it offers limited guidance on practical measures to mitigate these risks. Future research or an expanded discussion on preoperative optimization strategies for patients with metabolic syndrome would be highly beneficial for surgical practitioners.

In conclusion, we believe that addressing these issues would not only fortify the existing study but also pave the way for more targeted research in the future. Such endeavors could significantly impact clinical practice by enabling more personalized risk assessment and management strategies for surgical patients with metabolic syndrome.
