Elemental patterns in antarctic limpet shells indicate size and geographic differences based on gull pellet assemblages
Authors: Natalia Kurhaluk, Piotr Kamiński, Pavlo Khoyetskyy, Tomasz Hetmański, Agnieszka Włodarkiewicz, Ievgenii Aksonov, Halina Tkaczenko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Seabirds play a key role in connecting different parts of Antarctic ecosystems, yet the biogeochemical consequences of their interactions with marine invertebrates are not well understood. This study examines Nacella concinna shells recovered from kelp gull (Larus dominicanus) pellets across ten Antarctic islands, investigating how interactions between seabirds and limpets record elemental signatures shaped by organismal traits and environmental conditions. By analysing the levels of various elements (calcium, magnesium, sodium, phosphorus, manganese, iron, zinc, copper, cobalt, aluminium, nickel, tin and beryllium), we demonstrate that gull pellets provide a unique and underutilised record of marine-terrestrial nutrient transfer. This approach reveals how seabirds redistribute elements from coastal benthic habitats to terrestrial deposition sites, offering new insights into nutrient cycling and ecological connectivity in extreme polar environments. The elemental patterns in the shells reflect a combination of intrinsic and extrinsic factors. Shell size, used as a proxy for age, strongly influenced the concentrations of Mg, P, Mn, Be and Zn, indicating that there are age-related physiological controls on biomineralisation. Geographic location also modulated elemental composition, with elements such as copper and tin (Sn) showing pronounced spatial variability. This suggests that local environmental conditions shape elemental uptake prior to ingestion by gulls. The combined sensitivity of Mg, P, Sn and Ni to both size and geography highlights their potential as reliable indicators of ecological variability and environmental change across Antarctic islands. This study provides a novel framework for understanding how biological processes mediate nutrient redistribution in polar ecosystems by capturing the biogeochemical imprint of seabird–limpet interactions. Gull pellets are revealed to be not only biological remnants, but also focal points of elemental deposition and integrative records of marine-terrestrial linkages. These findings enhance our comprehension of ecosystem resilience in the swiftly evolving Antarctic region, emphasising the significance of seabird-mediated pathways in shaping nutrient dynamics and habitat functionality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42536-6.

Introduction

Antarctic ecosystems are characterised by extreme environmental conditions, and interactions between organisms intricately linked to adaptations that allow them to survive in these demanding environments1,2. Among these organisms, the Antarctic limpet Nacella concinna, and its predator, the kelp gull Larus dominicanus, play a key role in shaping benthic ecosystem dynamics. The shells of N. concinna serve as structural components of the benthic environment, while kelp gulls incorporate these limpets into their diet, thereby contributing to nutrient redistribution. Studying the interactions between these two species, particularly in terms of the elemental composition of limpet shells, provides important insights into ecosystem functioning and biogeochemical cycling in Antarctic environments3.

The Antarctic limpet N. concinna is a widespread benthic species and an important prey item for L. dominicanus in maritime Antarctica4,5. Due to its sensitivity to thermal stress and pollution, this species serves as an excellent bioindicator to study ecophysiological responses to environmental change6–8. Furthermore, morphological variation between habitats demonstrates a species’ ability to adapt to local conditions, influencing its resilience to stressors such as temperature variation, redox potential, electrolytical conductivity, organic matter content, ion compounds and pollutants. Understanding this variation provides a valuable opportunity to investigate adaptive mechanisms at both individual and population levels9,10. At the same time, the analysis of gull pellets provides a non-invasive approach to assess contaminant transfer within the food web, providing insights into trophic interactions and overall ecosystem condition. This method allows the assessment of bioaccumulation processes and provides critical data for conservation efforts in these ecologically sensitive environments11,12. Thus, a combined approach involving morphological, ecological and contaminant-related studies is essential for a comprehensive understanding of Antarctic marine ecosystems dynamics and potential responses to climate change.

An important and understudied aspect of these interactions is the biogeochemical transfer of elements from the ocean to terrestrial habitats, mediated by the deposition of seagull pellets containing undigested limpet shells and other organic material. The primitive soils of coastal Antarctica exhibit considerable variation in the composition and properties, creating highly contrasting conditions for plant and microbial colonisation. Gull nesting and feeding sites, often associated with accumulations of limpet shells, create unique microhabitats with enriched soil conditions, acting as nutrient-rich “oases” that promote primary succession and support microbial communities. These processes in turn influence soil development and ecological succession on Antarctic islands4,5,13.

The ingestion of small limpets by gulls is followed by regurgitation in the form of pellets, which are deposited either randomly or at specific feeding sites. Larger limpets, which cannot be swallowed whole, are transported to feeding sites where they are systematically broken down and consumed. Consequently, these shell accumulation areas serve as focal points for nutrient deposition, providing a crucial link between marine and terrestrial nutrient cycles4,5,13. The chemical and ecological significance of these regurgitated materials deserves further investigation, as they provide an essential perspective on the dynamics of nutrient fluxes and their role in shaping Antarctic ecosystems.

As benthic predators, kelp gulls play a vital role in controlling limpet populations, which in turn influences biogeochemical cycles, particularly through the redistribution of essential nutrients within the ecosystem. Changes in limpet population structure caused by predation can indirectly affect the cycling of essential elements, which could have consequences on the stability and composition of benthic communities9–12. The consumption of N. concinna by kelp gulls facilitates the transfer and redistribution of elements within the ecosystem14,15. Elements such as phosphorus, potassium and magnesium play a fundamental role in nutrient renewal and cycling in Antarctic environments. Metabolic processes within gulls further contribute to nutrient transport as excreta and guano deposits influence the chemical composition of surface waters and terrestrial substrates6–8. Such cycling processes are critical for the maintenance of primary production and biodiversity, highlighting the far-reaching implications of nutrient fluxes mediated by seabirds. Changes in the trophic structure of the ecosystem are particularly important, e.g. fluctuations in limpet populations due to predation by gulls can have cascading effects on competition between benthic organisms, ultimately altering ecosystem dynamics16. As observed by Branch17, changes in limpet abundance affect the availability and distribution of key elements within substrates, which in turn alter the biogeochemical properties of the ecosystem. This feedback loop highlights the complexity of nutrient cycling and predator-prey interactions in the Antarctic ecosystems.

In addition to ecological and trophic interactions, the chemical composition of limpets’ shells provides an alternative perspective on nutrient dynamics from a geochemical standpoint. The elemental composition of mollusc shells is governed by a combination of physicochemical and biological processes. The shells of marine calcifiers, including limpets, are made up of biogenic carbonates (mainly calcite and aragonite), and the way in which trace and major elements are incorporated into these minerals depends on environmental and physiological factors. Parameters such as seawater temperature, salinity, and saturation state influence the partitioning of elements during biomineralisation. Biological regulation, often referred to as ‘vital effects’, can modify these signals independently of the ambient environment18–21. Growth rate, shell microstructure and metabolic activity also play a critical role in determining trace element ratios, such as Mg/Ca, Sr/Ca and Ba/Ca, as well as rare earth element signatures. Understanding these controls is essential for interpreting geochemical proxies in terms of environmental change or nutrient dynamics. Integrating this geochemical perspective with the ecological framework of seabird–limpet interactions provides a more comprehensive basis for evaluating the influence of seabird-mediated nutrient inputs on shell composition in Antarctic coastal ecosystems. The temporal and spatial variation in the feeding behaviour of kelp gulls (Larus dominicanus), including their use of alternative food sources, may influence the population structure and size distribution of Antarctic limpets (Nacella concinna). These predator-prey interactions could indirectly affect limpet shell characteristics, such as growth patterns and elemental composition, by altering selective pressures and resource availability in their habitat15,16,22.

The broader significance of this research lies in its potential to deepen our understanding of nutrient redistribution in extreme environments and its implications for ecological resilience22. By analysing the elemental composition of limpet shells from gull pellets, this study bridges the gap between marine and terrestrial ecosystem processes. It also provides a critical understanding of how seabird-mediated nutrient cycling influences ecosystem dynamics in fragile Antarctic environments. The results have far-reaching implications for conservation strategies and management of Antarctic habitats, highlighting the interconnectedness of biotic and abiotic components in this unique ecosystem.

This study addresses an important yet understudied aspect of these interactions: the biogeochemical transfer of elements from the ocean to terrestrial habitats via seagull pellets containing undigested limpet shells and organic matter. Gulls ingest small limpets, which they then regurgitate as pellets at feeding and nesting sites. These areas of shell accumulation act as focal points for nutrient deposition, providing a crucial link between marine and terrestrial nutrient cycles. However, the chemical and ecological significance of these materials is not well understood and requires further investigation. This would provide valuable insights into the dynamics of nutrient fluxes and their role in shaping Antarctic ecosystems. Based on these ecological findings, the present study aims to analyse the concentration of selected trace and major elements (Ca, Mg, Na, P, Mn, Fe, Zn, Cu, Co, Al, Ni, Sn and Be) in N. concinna shells obtained from kelp gull pellets.

In light of these knowledge gaps, the aims of this study is to understand how biogeochemical signals are recorded in Nacella concinna shells found in kelp gull pellets on multiple Antarctic islands. To address this objective, the following scientific questions were formulated: (1) Does the elemental composition of N. concinna shells vary among islands with different ecological characteristics? (2) To what extent does shell size, used here as a proxy for age, influence the accumulation of major and trace elements? (3) Do geographic location and shell size jointly shape patterns of elemental variation, indicating potential interactions between environmental conditions and organismal traits? These questions provide the conceptual framework for the study and will guide the analyses presented in the subsequent sections.

Materials and methods

Study area and field collection in the argentine Islands archipelago

Fieldwork was conducted in the Argentine Islands archipelago, which is located in the Pacific sector of Antarctica (65°13’–65°16’ S, 64°10’–64°20’ W). The archipelago comprises ten islands: the Barchons, the Cruls Islands, Galindez Island, Petermann Island, the Forges Islands, Shelter Island, the Three Little Pigs, Black Island, Grotto Island and Moot Island. These islands are important ecological hotspots23(Fig. 1). The combination of rocky terrain, intertidal zones and seasonal environmental conditions makes this region crucial for understanding Antarctic food web dynamics and predator-prey interactions.

Samples of kelp gull (Larus dominicanus) pellets containing Nacella concinna shells were systematically collected from these islands. Sampling focused primarily on snow-covered areas to facilitate clear identification of individual pellets and minimise contamination from guano or aggregated materials. This approach enables reliable assessment of the elemental composition of individual pellets, providing robust ecological data.

The Argentine Islands archipelago, located near the Antarctic Peninsula, consists of numerous islands that boast diverse ecological characteristics. These islands provide essential habitats for the Antarctic limpet and the kelp gull. Notably, kelp gull pellets containing limpet shells collected from these islands have provided valuable insights into ecological system dynamics. The geographical location of the islands, extending from south-west to south-east along Graham Land on the Antarctic Peninsula, at a distance of 5–7.5 km from the mainland, further emphasises their biogeographical significance.

Galindez Island (area: 0.38 km2, perimeter: 4.7 km, maximum elevation: 51 m) has rocky intertidal zones that are home to large populations of limpets. Kelp gulls also commonly nest and forage in the area. Long-term faunal monitoring since the mid-1990s has provided valuable information on biodiversity and ecological processes.

Grotto Island (area: 0.12 km2, 2.83 km perimeter, 12 m elevation) provides nutrient-rich substrates that support limpets and attract kelp gulls. Petermann Island, which has a variety of habitats ranging from rocky shores to snow-covered highlands, also has significant limpet populations and serves as a nesting ground for kelp gulls during the breeding season.

Barchans, the largest island in the archipelago with an area of 0.83 km2 and a perimeter of 12.92 km, has wide intertidal zones that support abundant limpet populations. This makes it an important feeding ground for kelp gulls. The Forge Islands (0.12 km2, 3.13 km perimeter) and Shelter Island (0.03 km2, 0.91 km perimeter) also provide suitable habitats for limpets, and kelp gulls can be seen foraging along their rugged coastlines during the summer months. The Three Little Pigs (0.05 km2, perimeter 2.08 km), a group of small islets, offer ice-free conditions and accessible foraging areas supported by limpet-rich intertidal zones.

Black Island (0.06 km2, perimeter 1.79 km) and Moot Island, which have rocky shores and low elevations, provide habitats for limpets and nesting sites for kelp gulls during the breeding season. The nearby Cruls Islands support both limpet populations and nesting kelp gulls, providing important food resources for chick development.

During the 20th Ukrainian Antarctic Expedition (2018–2019), systematic collection of pellets was carried out along established monitoring routes, covering an area of approximately 20 km2 of the archipelago24. Pellets were collected from feeding platforms and the areas surrounding gull nests during the breeding season. A total of 81 pellets were collected, over 80% of which originated from the Argentine Islands, with the remainder coming from Barchans, Moot, Cruls and Petermann Islands. Most samples were collected in spring (April 2018 and March 2019) and summer (January-February 2019), as accessibility in winter was limited due to snowstorms. Only fresh, compact pellets exhibiting uniform shell presence were selected for analysis (Fig. 2). Monitoring was conducted daily, except during adverse weather conditions such as heavy precipitation or winds exceeding 20 m/s. Access to the islands followed strict safety protocols depending on the season and ice conditions25(Fig. 2).

The weight of each collected pellet was recorded, as was the number of Nacella concinna shells inside it. The length, width and height of individual shells were measured using digital callipers with an accuracy of ± 0.1 mm, and the shells were weighed on a laboratory balance with a precision of ± 0.001 g26. Shell length was used as a proxy for age, enabling variation in elemental accumulation across size classes to be assessed. Four size classes were defined based on shell length: 10–20 mm (size 1), 20–30 mm (size 2), 30–40 mm (size 3) and 40–50 mm (size 4) (Fig. 3 and reference12. All measurements were conducted under standardised laboratory conditions to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Chemical elemental analysis of limpet shells

Sample preparation

Prior to analysis, the shell samples (with an approximate dry weight of 500 mg) were mineralised to ensure complete decomposition and the preparation of a uniform solution. Microwave-assisted digestion was performed using concentrated nitric acid (69–70%) and hydrogen peroxide (30%), in accordance with standard protocols. For samples containing smaller quantities of material, the results were proportionally adjusted. The digested solution was diluted to 10 ml with deionised water prior to inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis. To ensure analytical reliability, each sample was measured in duplicate and procedural blanks were included to monitor potential contamination.

ICP-MS analysis

Quantitative analyses of Ca, Mg, Na, P, Mn, Fe, Zn, Cu, Se, Mo, Cr, Li, Co, Al, Ni, Sn, B, Ba, and Be were performed using an Agilent 7500CE ICP-MS instrument. Internal standards (45Sc, 89Y, 159Tb) were employed to compensate for matrix effects, and calibration was conducted using multi-elemental standards. Certified reference materials (Seronorm Trace Elements Serum and NCS ZC73016 chicken) were used to ensure quality control, achieving recoveries of 90–100% and measurement uncertainty below 10%. The detection and quantification limits were verified, and all results were above the detection threshold (Supplementary Material 1, SM1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistica 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, California, USA). The data were first screened for outliers and assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. Results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (S.D.), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of geographic location and shell size group on shell morphology and elemental composition, including potential interactions between these factors. Where Levene’s test indicated heteroscedasticity (p < 0.05), Welch’s correction was applied to univariate comparisons to ensure valid results.

For datasets that deviated from normality or failed the homogeneity test, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was applied, followed by pairwise post-hoc comparisons to identify significant differences between islands or shell size groups. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was then used to assess the relationship between shell size and elemental composition, providing insight into directional covariation.

Key metrics derived from MANOVA included the correlation coefficient (R), the coefficient of determination (R2), the adjusted R2 statistic which accounts for potential overfitting, and the standardised regression coefficients (β), which quantify the magnitude and direction of the effects of the predictors. This statistical framework enabled a robust evaluation of the factors influencing limpet shell morphology and elemental composition, offering valuable ecological and environmental insights.

Results

Size distribution of shell limpets

The size distribution of Nacella concinna shells varied between the surveyed sites. Size 2 limpets (20–30 mm) were the most abundant, accounting for 45–62% of the population at each site. Size 3 limpets (30–40 mm) ranged from 17% to 59% of the population. Size 1 limpets (10–20 mm) and size 4 limpets (40–50 mm) were less common, showing greater variability between sites. At Galindez Island (n = 135), size 2 individuals accounted for 48.9% of the total population, while size 1 individuals accounted for 18.5%. Forge Island had a predominance of size 3 limpets (58.7%). Barchans showed 62.3% size 2 individuals and 4.9% size 1 individuals. Black Island exhibited 55.7% size 3 individuals.

At Three Little Pigs, size 2 limpets represented 50.5% of the population, with the other size classes being more evenly distributed. Petermann, Shelter and Grotto Islands displayed similar distributions, with size 2 individuals accounting for 45–48% and the remaining size classes accounting for around 18% each. Overall, the intermediate size classes (2 and 3) were predominant across most sites, while the smallest and largest size classes (1 and 4) were less prevalent. This suggests variation in shell size composition across different sites.

Variation of elemental composition following size class and geographic location

Statistical analysis using ANOVA with Welch’s correction revealed significant differences in the elemental composition of Nacella concinna shells between islands and shell size classes. Significant differences were observed in calcium (Ca) (F39.707 = 1.593, p = 0.013), sodium (Na) (F39.707 = 4.344, p < 0.001), magnesium (Mg) (F39.707 = 10.747, p < 0.001) and aluminium (Al) (F39.707 = 6.239, p < 0.001) concentrations between groups. There were also significant differences for phosphorus (F39.707 = 10.717, p < 0.001), manganese (F39.707 = 7.881, p < 0.001), iron (F39.707 = 10.896, p < 0.001), copper (F39.707 = 10.368, p < 0.001) and zinc (F39.707 = 4.530, p < 0.001). Further significant differences were found for beryllium (Be; F39.707 = 6.402, p < 0.001), cobalt (Co; F39.707 = 11.582, p < 0.001), nickel (Ni; F39.707 = 13.628, p < 0.001) and tin (Sn; F39.707 = 36.125, p < 0.001). These results suggest that the elemental composition of shells varies significantly between islands and shell size classes.

Major element variability across sites and size classes

The elemental composition of Nacella concinna shells was analysed in relation to their geographical origin and size. A Kruskal-Wallis rank-based ANOVA was used to compare Ca concentrations between islands and shell size groups. This test revealed no statistically significant differences in Ca concentrations between the islands (H(39, N = 747) = 53.91, p = 0.057). However, significant differences in Ca levels were observed between size groups (H(3, N = 747) = 15.35, p = 0.0015). The elemental concentrations of Nacella concinna shells varied significantly with both geographical origin and shell size. Magnesium (Mg) showed strong spatial and size-related differences, with Kruskal–Wallis tests indicating significant variation among islands (H(39, N = 747) = 245.63, p < 0.001) and size groups (H(3, N = 747) = 97.29, p < 0.001), supported by MANOVA results (F = 12.20, p < 0.001; R = 0.64, R2 = 0.41). Sodium (Na) also differed significantly between islands (H(39, N = 747) = 146.31, p < 0.001) and size classes (H(3, N = 747) = 32.76, p < 0.001), with MANOVA confirming these effects (F = 4.15, p < 0.001; R = 0.44, R2 = 0.19). Phosphorus (P) exhibited pronounced spatial and size-related variability, with significant differences among islands (H(39, N = 747) = 299.76, p < 0.001) and size groups (H(3, N = 747) = 167.09, p < 0.001), further supported by MANOVA (F = 11.10, p < 0.001; R = 0.63, R2 = 0.39). Calcium (Ca) likewise varied significantly across islands and size classes, as indicated by Kruskal-Wallis and MANOVA analyses, with ANOVA results showing F = 1.49, p = 0.029 (R = 0.28, R2 = 0.08). Full statistical outputs are presented in Table 1.

Spatial and size-related variation in trace elements

The elemental composition of Nacella concinna shells exhibited clear spatial and size-related variability in terms of manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn) and aluminium (Al) (Tables 2 and 3).Significant differences in manganese concentrations were observed among islands (H(39, n = 747) = 204.85, p < 0.001) and size groups (H(3, N = 747) = 104.21, p < 0.001), with MANOVA confirming these effects (F = 7.80, p < 0.001; R2 = 0.31). Similarly strong variation was observed for iron across islands (H(39, N = 747) = 323.25, p = 0.001) and size classes (H(3, n = 747) = 107.55, p = 0.001), as supported by the results of the MANOVA (F = 11.39, p < 0.001; R² = 0.40). Copper concentrations also varied significantly between islands (H(39, n = 747) = 239.07, p < 0.001) and size groups (H(3, n = 747) = 59.11, p < 0.001). MANOVA indicated a strong effect (F = 11.92, p < 0.001; R2 = 0.41). Zinc concentrations differed significantly between islands (H(39, N = 747) = 138.35, p < 0.001) and size classes (H(3, n = 747) = 101.75, p < 0.001), as revealed by MANOVA (F = 4.15, p < 0.001; R² = 0.19). Aluminium concentrations also varied significantly according to geographical location (H(39, n = 747) = 233.32, p < 0.001) and shell size (H(3, n = 747) = 116.22, p < 0.001) (Tables 2 and 3).

The trace elements beryllium (Be), cobalt (Co), nickel (Ni) and tin (Sn) in Nacella concinna shells showed significant spatial and size-related variability (Table 3). Beryllium concentrations differed among islands (H(39, n = 747) = 208.12, p < 0.001) and size groups (H(3, n = 747) = 95.95, p < 0.001), with MANOVA indicating a moderate effect (R = 0.51, R2 = 0.26). Cobalt also varied significantly between islands (H(39, n = 747) = 185.57, p < 0.001) and size classes (H(3, N = 747) = 26.25, p < 0.001), supported by MANOVA results (R = 0.64, R² = 0.41). Nickel exhibited pronounced differences across islands (H(39, n = 747) = 348.18, p = 0.001) and size groups (H(3, n = 747) = 171.99, p = 0.001), with MANOVA confirming a strong effect (R = 0.66, R2 = 0.43). Tin showed similarly strong spatial and size-related variation (H(39, n = 714) = 309.48, p = 0.001; H(3, n = 714) = 190.16, p = 0.001), with MANOVA yielding the highest explanatory power among the analysed elements (R = 0.82, R2 = 0.68).

Thus, the MANOVA analysis shows that size and location significantly influence the elemental composition of limpet shells, with varying degrees of influence for each element. Elements such as tin, Ni and Mg showed the highest correlations, indicating strong relationships with the predictors. In contrast, elements such as Ca, Na and Zn had moderate correlations, suggesting that factors other than size and location may contribute to their distribution. The model successfully explains a substantial proportion of the variation for most elements, with the highest R2 values observed for Sn, Ni and Mg, highlighting the importance of these factors in determining the elemental composition of limpet shells.

Shell size and location as predictors of elemental composition

Multiple regression analyses were performed to examine the influence of shell size and geographical location on the elemental composition of limpets (Supplement 2, SM2). The results indicate that the effects of shell size and location varied among the studied elements. For Ca, shell size was negatively associated with content (β = − 0.09), whereas geographical location had no significant effect (β = − 0.01). Na content showed a positive relationship with shell size (β = 0.168), while location had no significant influence on the values. Mg content was negatively related to shell size (β = − 0.19), with location having no significant effect (β = 0.025). Al content was influenced by both factors, with negative associations for shell size (β = − 0.34) and location (β = − 0.09). P exhibited a negative correlation with shell size (β = − 0.42), while location had no significant effect (β = − 0.03). Mn content exhibited a positive relationship with shell size (β = 0.23), while location did not significantly contribute. Fe exhibited negative relationships with both shell size (β = − 0.17) and location (β = − 0.16). For Cu, only location was significant (β = − 0.15), while shell size was not (β = − 0.07). Zn displayed a negative association with shell size (β = − 0.17) and location had no significant effect (β = − 0.02). Be content was negatively related to shell size (β = − 0.17), with location remaining non-significant (β = 0.007). Co exhibited significant negative effects on both shell size (β = − 0.23) and location (β = − 0.16), yielding a regression model with an F-value of 34.40 and a p-value of < 0.0001. Ni exhibited weaker, yet still significant, relationships with shell size (β = − 0.12) and location (β = − 0.07) (F = 7.97, p < 0.05). Finally, Sn showed positive associations with both shell size (β = 0.246) and location (β = 0.117), with the model producing an F value of 32.06 and a p value of less than 0.0001.

Thus, the regression analyses revealed statistically significant associations for most elements, with shell size and location contributing to varying degrees depending on the element in question. The models accounted for a substantial proportion of the variation in elemental content for elements such as Co, Sn and Mg, while the associations with the predictors were moderate for other elements.

Correlation matrices for limpet shells of various sizes revealed different patterns of elemental association (Fig. 4). Cu and Zn also exhibited a strong correlation (r = 0.757, p < 0.001). Moderate correlations were observed between P and Cu (r = 0.616, p < 0.001), as well as between P and Mn (r = 0.555, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A). For medium-sized shells (size 2), the analysis revealed strong positive correlations between P and Zn (r = 0.661, p < 0.001) and between Cu and Zn (r = 0.561, p < 0.001). Ni and Co were positively correlated (r = 0.642, p < 0.001), while moderate correlations were found between Mg and Ni (r = 0.446, p < 0.001) and Cu and Ni (r = 0.446, p < 0.001). Co and Zn also displayed a strong correlation (r = 0.573, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

Shell size as a driver of elemental composition

This study demonstrates that the elemental composition of Nacella concinna shells collected from Antarctic islands is significantly influenced by shell size and geographic location. However, the strength of these effects varies among elements. Shell size was found to be the main factor influencing biogenic elements such as Ca, Mg, P and Zn, suggesting that intrinsic physiological processes play a dominant role in determining their concentrations. Smaller and medium-sized limpets, which represent active growth stages, exhibited higher concentrations of essential biomineralisation and metabolic elements, such as Mg and P. This is consistent with earlier observations of growth-related elemental incorporation in Antarctic limpets, and with broader patterns of biomineralisation control in marine invertebrates. Conversely, larger shells accumulated higher levels of certain trace metals, such as Mn and Sn, likely due to prolonged environmental exposure and age-related bioaccumulation. This pattern has also been reported in other Antarctic molluscs exposed to long-term environmental inputs15,39. These ontogenetic trends are consistent with studies showing that the incorporation of trace elements into calcified structures is influenced by both physiological regulation and environmental exposure18,19,21. The strong correlations identified between transition metals (Cu-Zn, Mg-Ni, and Fe-Al) suggest that there are shared uptake pathways or similar binding affinities within the shell matrix. This reflects both biochemical control and mineralogical constraints. Similar relationships between trace metals have been documented in other calcifying marine organisms, where shell microstructure and metabolic activity influence elemental partitioning18,21.

Geographical variation and environmental influences

The variation in elemental composition among the studied islands highlights the strong influence of local environmental conditions, including seawater geochemistry, substrate characteristics, and potential anthropogenic inputs. Islands such as Barchons, which exhibited elevated levels of calcium and magnesium, may be characterised by calcium-rich substrates or increased biological productivity. These patterns are consistent with the regional environmental variability previously documented in Antarctic coastal ecosystems1,3. In contrast, the lower elemental concentrations observed at Petermann Island may reflect reduced metal bioavailability or differences in feeding habitats, given that spatial heterogeneity in resource availability is well documented in Antarctic benthic communities9. Significant spatial differences in elements such as copper, cobalt and aluminium likely arise from local variability in lithogenic inputs and regional oceanographic circulation patterns. These factors strongly influence the distribution of trace metals in the Southern Ocean2,39. Although Antarctica is geographically isolated, trace metals can still reach its coastal ecosystems via long-range atmospheric transport, while local emissions from research stations may also contribute to the elemental load. Taken together, these patterns suggest that shell geochemistry integrates various environmental factors, offering insight into metal cycling, habitat conditions, and the biological processes that influence the incorporation of elements in Antarctic coastal ecosystems18,21.

As summarised in Fig. 5, the study revealed clear size- and location-dependent elemental patterns in Nacella concinna shells sourced from kelp gull pellets.

Ecological context and trophic interactions

Taking a broader ecological standpoint, the observed relationships between shell size, elemental composition and geographic location imply that Nacella concinna shells could serve as reliable indicators of environmental conditions and potential contamination in Antarctic coastal zones26. The strong correlations among transition metals indicate processes of co-accumulation driven by biological regulation and environmental factors. Limpets play a vital role in benthic ecosystems, contributing to nutrient cycling and serving as a key prey item for kelp gulls (Larus dominicanus)5,13. Size-dependent variation in elemental composition may influence predator exposure to essential and trace metals, particularly in regions where limpets constitute a major dietary component27–35. Regurgitated pellets containing indigestible shell fragments provide a non-invasive record of diet and bioaccumulation, enabling researchers to evaluate trophic interactions and the ecological ramifications of metal accumulation12. Further supporting the idea that shell traits mediate ecological interactions and influence elemental accumulation patterns are studies on shell morphology and growth11, climate-driven changes in shell structure8, and predator-prey dynamics4,9.

This study shows that the ecological importance of the observed patterns of elements in Nacella concinna shells is mainly due to differences in population structure and regulation at the level of organisms, rather than direct trophic transfer. Size-related variation reflects growth dynamics and metabolic activity, while geographic differences indicate habitat-level constraints that influence resource availability and environmental exposure. Taken together, these findings suggest that seabirds indirectly influence shell chemistry via demographic and physiological pathways. This emphasises the importance of interpreting elemental signatures within an ecological framework that considers environmental context and biological regulation.

Ecotoxicological implications and bioindicator potential

The interaction between seabirds and limpets plays a role in broader biogeochemical processes within Antarctic ecosystems. For instance, kelp gull predation facilitates the transfer of nutrients from marine to terrestrial systems by enhancing soil formation through the deposition of organic matter, such as guano and discarded shells. Studies have shown that seabird and marine mammal activity increases the concentration of P, As, Cd, Se, Sr and Zn in coastal soils, highlighting the role of biological activity in shaping trace element distributions36–38. The Nacella concinna species has been recognised as an effective biomonitor of environmental variability: sublittoral populations accumulate higher levels of Fe, Al and Zn, while intertidal populations exhibit stronger antioxidant defences15,39. Further research on Antarctic benthic organisms, including octopuses, shows that trace element accumulation reflects trophic position, dietary sources, and sampling depth40–67. This reinforces the importance of biological traits and environmental conditions in shaping elemental distributions. In this context, our findings demonstrate that elements such as Sn, Ni, Co and Cu are particularly sensitive to intrinsic and extrinsic factors, making them valuable indicators for ecotoxicological assessments.

Limitations and future directions

This study demonstrates how interactions between seabirds and limpets, as revealed through the analysis of Nacella concinna shells found in kelp gull (Larus dominicanus) pellets, can provide insight into elemental signatures influenced by size-related biological traits and geographic variation. While this approach provides a novel, non-invasive insight into predator–prey dynamics and nutrient transfer, there are opportunities to refine it further.

The study primarily focused on shell size and spatial distribution, while additional environmental variables, such as water temperature, salinity, pH, food availability and local pollution levels, were not directly measured. Incorporating these parameters in future research would clarify the environmental mechanisms driving elemental incorporation and strengthen the interpretation of size–geography interactions.

Although shell size served as a practical proxy for ontogenetic stage, growth rates in N. concinna vary across habitats. Combining pellet-derived shells with age-determination techniques (e.g. sclerochronology and isotopic profiling) in future studies would improve the resolution of ontogenetic patterns. Similarly, expanding the sample beyond gull pellets to include live-collected individuals would help to establish whether seabird prey selection influences the observed elemental signatures.

The geographical scope of this study was limited to a subset of Antarctic islands. Broader spatial coverage, combined with in situ environmental measurements and temporal replication, would enable more comprehensive assessments of regional variability. Overall, these approaches will enhance our understanding of how seabird-mediated processes shape elemental signatures in limpet shells, strengthening the use of pellet-derived biominerals as ecological and biogeochemical indicators in Antarctic coastal ecosystems.

Conclusions

This study provides comprehensive evidence for the first time that interactions between seabirds and limpets, as revealed by the analysis of Nacella concinna shells found in kelp gull (Larus dominicanus) pellets, can be used to trace elemental signatures shaped by a combination of biological traits and geographic context. By showing that shells derived from pellets retain clear size- and location-dependent geochemical patterns, this study introduces a novel, non-invasive approach to studying Antarctic coastal ecosystems.

A key strength of the study is showing that intrinsic factors, such as shell size, strongly influence biogenic elements (Ca, Mg and P), while several trace metals (Cu, Co and Sn) reflect spatial environmental variability. This integrated biological–environmental perspective improves our understanding of how elemental signals are formed in Antarctic limpets and highlights the value of combining predator–prey interactions with biomineral analysis.

Using gull pellets as a natural sampling pathway is another major contribution. This method captures ecological processes that would otherwise be difficult to observe, provides access to individuals from multiple microhabitats and enables multi-elemental assessments without disturbing the native fauna - an important advantage in protected polar regions.

Together, these findings establish N. concinna shells derived from pellets as a promising tool for ecological monitoring and biogeochemical research in Antarctica. Future studies that incorporate direct environmental measurements, broader spatial coverage and temporal datasets will clarify the mechanisms that govern elemental incorporation more clearly and strengthen the use of this approach for tracking environmental change in polar coastal ecosystems.
