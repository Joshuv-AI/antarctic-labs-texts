Targeting MCL1 by UMI-77 to induce mitophagy alleviates renal tubular epithelial cells injury in acute kidney injury
Authors: Xiaoyu Li, Zixian Li, Yongming Chen, Hongluan Wu, Yonghan Liu, Zhennan Ye, Qingqing Yang, Haicha Hou, Junfeng Hao, Ruigao Song, Huafeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common clinical disease with complex pathophysiological processes and with high morbidity and mortality. Severe mitochondrial damages, such as mitochondrial swelling and fragmentation, disruption of membrane integrity, and broken or absent cristae, were observed in renal tubular epithelial cells (TECs) of ischemic AKI mice. However, whether artificial activation of mitophagy can alleviate TECs injury is still unknown. In this study, we tested the pro-mitophagy effect of UMI-77, which was identified as an unexpected mitophagy activator at sub-lethal doses in 2020, and verified the protective effect of activated mitophagy on TECs during AKI. We found that UMI-77 activates mitophagy in TECs and ameliorates kidney damage during AKI both in vivo and in vitro. This protective effect of UMI-77 on TECs can be reversed by adding mitophagy inhibitor Mdivi-1 or inhibiting the expression of mitophagy receptor MCL-1. These results indicate that UMI-77 mitigates TECs injury in AKI primarily through activating mitophagy, and artificial activation of mitophagy could be an effective strategy for treating AKI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-34083-3.

Introduction

AKI is defined by a sudden loss of excretory kidney function1,2. A meta-analysis of 154 studies using a 2012 Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO)-equivalent AKI definition reported pooled incidence rates of AKI were 21.6% in adults and 33.7% in children, with associated mortality rates of 23.9% and 13.8%, respectively3. Despite advances in clinical care, there is still a lack of specific and efficacious treatment for AKI. Preventing the occurrence of AKI, alleviating the condition of AKI, and preventing its progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) are currently the focus of clinical and research work in the field of kidney disease4–6.

The kidney has the second highest mitochondrial density and oxygen consumption after the heart7. The renal TECs of ischemic AKI mice exhibited severe mitochondrial damage, as evidenced by increased mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) levels and decreases in ATP level. Mitochondrial lesions such as mitochondrial swelling and fragmentation, disruption of membrane integrity, and broken or absent cristae were observed in TECs of the ischemic AKI mice8,9.

Mitophagy selectively eliminates dysfunctional mitochondria to maintain cellular homeostasis. However, the role of mitophagy activation in ischemia-induced TECs injury remains controversial10. Through the Bnip3 and Pink1 knockout mouse models, Tang et al. demonstrated that in the ischemic AKI model, Bnip3 deficiency reduced mitophagy, leading to damaged mitochondrial accumulation, and increased ROS production, cell death, and inflammation11,12. In addition, Tang et al. demonstrated that PINK1-PRKN mediated mitophagy plays an important role in mitochondrial quality control, TECs survival, and renal function in both in vitro and in vivo models of ischemic AKI13. However, Liu et al. reported that c-MYC can activate mitophagy by upregulating RTKN through lncRNA-MEG3, triggering Wnt/β-Catenin pathway, thereby exacerbating ischemic AKI14.

Therefore, although the mainstream view is that mitophagy activation has a protective effect on TECs injury during the occurrence and development of AKI, there has been no direct evidence of positive activation of mitophagy to alleviate TECs injury.

UMI-77, a novel small-molecule inhibitor of MCL-1, was developed to induce apoptosis in cancer cells in 201415. In 2020, it was unexpectedly identified as a mitophagy activator at sub-lethal doses, independent of apoptosis16. In a recent study, Jin et al. demonstrated that UMI-77 preserves mitochondrial fitness in renal tubular epithelial cells and alleviates renal interstitial fibrosis in mouse models of chronic kidney disease by inducing mitophagy17. Herein, we aimed to investigate whether UMI-77 activates mitophagy, preserves mitochondrial homeostasis in TECs, and thereby mitigates TEC injury in AKI.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

Eight to ten-week-old male C57BL/6 mice weighing 20–25 g were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng BiotechCo Ltd. All mice were kept in specific pathogen-free environment with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Five to seven male C57BL/6 mice were assigned to each of the four groups (sham group, UMI-77 group, UIR group, and UMI-77 + UIR group). Mice were randomly assigned to four groups using a random number table prior to intervention. This randomization was performed by an experimenter who was not involved in the subsequent surgical operation and sample detection, to avoid selection bias. To establish UIR experimental model, mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (Sigma, P3761) with a concentration of 60 mg/kg. After being anesthetized, all mice were kept on a homeothermic blanket to monitor and maintain body temperature at 36.5 °C for surgery. Then, their left renal arteries were clamped by micro-vessel clamps for 35 min, after clamp removal to restore blood flow, the incision was sutured layer by layer. The right kidney was left intact to ensure survival and maintain basic renal function. Sham-operated mice underwent the same operation without clamping of left arteries. To ensure animal welfare and experimental stability, we implemented standardized post-operative supportive care. All mice received subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg, Sigma, B044) immediately after surgery for analgesia and were given warm sterile physiological saline (5 mL/kg, subcutaneous injection) post-surgery to compensate for fluid loss. All mice were euthanized at 5 days after surgeries and their kidneys were harvested for further analysis. The mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following deep anesthesia with pentobarbital sodium (60 mg/kg). In the UMI-77 + UIR group, the mice received an intraperitoneal injection of UMI-77 at 10 mg/kg before the surgeries and continuous daily injection until the day of harvest. The 10 mg/kg intraperitoneal injection dose of UMI-77 was selected based on the apoptosis-independent mitophagy-activating effects and safety profiles reported in published literatures16,17. All animal experiments were performed according to a protocol approved by the Laboratory Animal Services Center at Guangdong Medical University (Zhanjiang, China, SYXK-(Guangdong) 2015 − 0147). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Cell culture and treatment

The human proximal tubular epithelial cell line HK-2 cells (American Type Culture Collection) was obtained from Shanghai Jiaotong University Affiliated Hospital. HK-2 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 media (Sigma) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. HK-2 cells were subjected to 24 h of hypoxia followed by 3 h of reoxygenation to establish an in vitro hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) model. Before H/R, the siRNAs specific to MCL-1 (si-MCL-1) (sc-35877, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) were transfected into HK-2 cells to knock down MCL-1. The cell H/R model included various groups including Control, H/R, H/R + UMI-77, H/R + UMI-77 + Mdivi-1, siControl (sc-37007, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) + H/R + UMI-77, siMCL-1 + H/R + UMI-77. Mitochondrial division inhibitor 1 (Mdivi-1; T1907, TargetMoI) was used at a working concentration of 10 µM. Before the cells were placed in a hypoxic incubator, Mdivi-1 and UMI-77 were added together to serum-free medium in the corresponding treatment group, followed by anoxia.

Cell viability assay

HK-2 cells were seeded in 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well. After 24 h, the cells were pre-treated with 10 µM UMI- 77 (Abmole, M5226) for 3 h before H/R. The 10 µM dose of UMI-77 was selected based on the apoptosis-independent mitophagy-activating effects and safety profiles reported in published literatures17. Cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (C0038, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured by a microplate reader (ELx800 BioTek, U.S.A).

Histopathology and Immunofluorescence staining

Firstly, the kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and then sliced into 4 μm thick sections for hematoxylin-eosin staining and kidney injury molecule 1 (Kim-1, AF1817, R&D systems) histochemical staining. Images were captured from at least five random areas per section. Tubular damage was examined in a blinded manner and scored by the percentage of damaged tubules: 0, no damage; 1, < 25%; 2, 25–50%; 3, 50–75%; 4, > 75%. For immunofluorescence staining, paraffin kidney sections underwent dewaxing, rehydration, antigen repair and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. After that the slides were incubated with primary antibodies against TOM20 (11802-1-AP, Proteintech), MCL-1 (sc-74437, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and LAMP1 (sc-19992, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with fluorescence secondary antibodies. DAPI was used for nuclei staining. Images were captured on a confocal microscope (FV31S-SW, Olympus, Germany). Co-localization was analysed using ImageJ software with JACoP plugin using Manders’ co-localization coefficients.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Kidney cortex tissues in 1mm3 or cells in 96-well plate were harvested and fixed with 3% glutaraldehyde at 4℃ for 4 h. After that, 0.1 M sodium dimethylarsenate buffer was changed for 3 times at an interval of 2 h. The samples were soaked in 1% osmic acid at 4℃ for 2 h and rinsed with 0.1 M sodium dimethylarsenate buffer twice for 15 min each time. After fixation, the samples were saturated uranium acetate dye 2 h at room temperature, dehydrated using a graded ethanol series and embedded in resin. Ultrathin sections were cut on an ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems Ltd, Germany), and then examined using a JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd, Japan). The electron microscopy data shown are representative findings of an experiment that was repeated four times using different kidney samples of mice for each group, all of which yielded similar results. The detection, labeling, and quantitation analysis of mitophagy were done by an electron microscope engineer who was blinded to the pathology of mice.

Western blotting analysis

Kidney tissues or HK-2 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail to extract total protein. The concentration of protein was quantified with Bradford assay (Beyotime). A total of 15–20 µg of protein were separated by SDS- PAGE, and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore). Blocked by 5% skim milk, the membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with secondary HRP-conjugated antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The signals were detected by Bio-Rad ChemiDocTouch. The original images for blots were uploaded as Supplementary Material file. Primary antibodies used in this study included anti-Kim-1antibody (AF1817, R&D systerms), anti-MCL-1 antibody (sc-74437, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-COXIV antibody (Ab14744, Abcam), anti-p62 antibody (A19700, ABclonal), anti-TOM20 antibody (11802-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-GAPDH antibody (abs830030, Absin), anti-Tublin antibody (sc-32293, Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Real-time polymerase chain reaction

The total RNA was extracted from kidney tissues using Trizol, chloroform and isopropanol. Takara reverse transcription kit was used to reverse transcribe RNA into complementary DNA. Real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Mix (Vazyme). The primer sequences were as follows: Kim-1 (forward 5′-CATTTCAGGC CTCATACTGC-3′; reverse 5′-CATGTCGTTGTGATTCCACG-3′), MCL-1 (forward 5′-TGCTTCGGAAACTGGACATCA-3′; reverse 5′-TAGCCACAAAGGCACCAA AG-3′), and GAPDH (forward 5′-TGGCCTTCCGTGTTCCTAC-3′; reverse 5′- GAGTTGCTGTTGAAGTCGCA-3′).

Measurement of MtROS

The level of mROS in HK-2 cells was measured using MitoSOX (M36008, Invitrogen). Briefly, cells were incubated with 2.5 µM MitoSOX with 1µL living nuclear staining solution (Hoechst 33342) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After rinsing with PBS, images of cells were captured on a confocal microscope (FV31S-SW, Olympus, German).

Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)

MMP in HK-2 cells was measured using JC-1 probe (C2006, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were stained with 2µM JC-1 at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by a confocal microscope (FV31S-SW, Olympus, German). The ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity was used to indicate the relative MMP.

Analysis of apoptosis

Cell apoptosis in HK-2 cells was measured using Annexin V, PI apoptosis detection kit (556547, BD) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were added 1×Annexin V Binding Solution 400 µl, 5 µl Annexin V, FITC conjugate and 5 µl PI Solution which was mixed by shaking, and incubated with at room temperature for 15 min away from light. After rinsing with PBS, cells were harvested and the mean fluorescence intensity was determined by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences).

Statistics

Quantitative data were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Differences were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc tests. All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

UMI-77 ameliorates kidney damage in AKI mice

To determine the effects of UMI-77 on AKI progression, especially the degree of TECs injury, in vivo studies on unilateral ischemia reperfusion (UIR) C57BL/6 mouse and in vitro studies on H/R treated human proximal tubule epithelial cell line, HK-2 cells, were conducted.

In the in vivo animal experiments, C57BL/6 mice aged 8–10 weeks were divided into four groups, namely sham group, intraperitoneal injection of UMI-77 (UMI-77) group, UIR group and intraperitoneal injection of UMI-77 before UIR (UMI-77 + UIR) group. HE staining of kidney sections showed that, compared with the UMI-77 + UIR group, the renal tubular lumens in UIR mice were dilated and deformed, with a large amount of inflammatory cells infiltrating the renal interstitium, and the TECs in UIR mice showed more serious brush border loss and necrosis (Fig. 1A and C). Histochemical staining of Kim-1 showed that Kim-1 expression in UIR group was higher than that in UMI-77 + UIR group, and a large amount of Kim-1 expression was observed in renal cortex and corticomedullary junction (Fig. 1B). Western blot and real-time quantitative PCR also showed that Kim-1 expression was higher in UIR group than in UMI-77 + UIR group (Fig. 1C-D).

UMI-77 reduces the damage of TECs in AKI through activating mitophagy

To explore whether UMI-77 can alleviate the damage of TECs in AKI by activating mitophagy, kidney samples and HK-2 cells from each group were detected by dual-labeled immunofluorescence analysis using the lysosomal marker, lysosomal associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1), and the mitochondrial marker, TOM20 (typical mitochondrial outer membrane receptor), and electron microscopy. UMI-77 treatment significantly increased the formation of mitolysosomes (TOM20/LAMP1 double positive vesicles) in renal TECs in UMI-77 + UIR group compared to UIR group (Fig. 4A-B). TEM analysis also identified more obvious double-membrane vesicles containing mitochondria (mitophagosomes) in renal TECs in UMI-77 + UIR group (Fig. 4C). Results from the in vitro H/R treated HK-2 cells were consistent with the in vivo data (Fig. 5).

In vitro, the mitophagy inhibitor Mdivi-118,19 was used to stimulate the HK-2 cells to further clarify whether the TECs protective effect of UMI-77 is dependent on its role in inducing mitophagy. Flow cytometry with Annexin V and PI double staining analysis indicated that mitophagy inhibitor Mdivi-1 can reverse the protective effect of UMI-77 on H/R treated HK-2 cells (Fig. 6A-B). UMI-77 activates mitophagy primarily by targeting MCL-1

As MCL-1 is reported as a new mitophagy receptor and is the direct target of UMI-77, we investigated whether this receptor responsible for the UMI-77-driven mitophagy activation in AKI.

Dual-labeled immunofluorescence staining of TOM20 and MCL-1 was performed on H/R treated HK-2 cells. The results showed that the fluorescence co-localization of TOM20 and MCL-1 increased in H/R treated HK-2 cells after UMI-77 treatment (Fig. 7A). Then, siRNA was used to interfere with MCL-1, the potential target molecule of UMI-77 (Fig. 7B). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that knockdown of MCL-1 can reverse the protective effect of UMI-77 on H/R treated HK-2 cells (Fig. 8A and B). Mitochondrial function indexes mtROS and MMP detection indicated that knockdown of MCL-1 can weaken the protective effect of UMI-77 on mitochondria in H/R treated HK-2 cells (Fig. 8C and D). Besides, western blot analysis indicated that knockdown of MCL-1 could partially reverse the promotion effect of UMI-77 on mitochondrial proteins degradation (Fig. 8E). Collectively, these data suggested that UMI-77 activates mitophagy at least in part by targeting MCL-1 in H/R treated HK-2 cells.

Discussion

Currently, the prevention and treatment approach of AKI is still limited. The anti-injury and self-repair ability of renal proximal TECs affects the progression of AKI, and improving this ability of TECs plays an important role in preventing AKI and preventing its transition to CKD. AKI causes mitochondrial damage in TECs, which further aggravate AKI. Mitophagy can selectively remove defective mitochondria and maintain TECs homeostasis.

As mentioned above, multiple gene editing mice and cells have been used to investigate the role of mitophagy in the progression of AKI, resulting in conflicting conclusions11–13. These research works based on genetic tools have always lacked research results on the impact of directly activating mitophagy on TECs. In this study, we tested the pro-mitophagy effect of UMI-77 and verified the protective effect of activated mitophagy on TECs during AKI. Our results confirm that directly artificial activation of mitophagy alleviates TECs injury in ischemic AKI.

UMI-77 was administered intraperitoneally one day before modeling, and we supplemented Kim-1 staining data at 3 days and 7 days post-surgery to characterize the time course of its effects (Supplementary Figure S1). UMI-77 administration did not significantly mitigate renal tubular injury at 3 days post-modeling. Not until 5 days post-modeling (6 days after UMI-77 administration) did pathological analyses show that UMI-77 treatment significantly reduced TEC injury compared with UIR mice without UMI-77 pretreatment, and this protective effect was sustained at 7 days post-modeling. These data suggest that UMI-77 cannot prevent the occurrence of AKI or alleviate acute-phase damage, but can facilitate the repair of damaged TECs and may delay the potential transition from AKI to CKD—consistent with report that UMI-77 can inhibit the progression of other chronic kidney disease17. However, this inference is based on short-term alleviation of tubular injury, and definitive evidence for inhibiting AKI-to-CKD transition requires long-term follow-up data on renal fibrosis and persistent renal function changes, which will be addressed in future studies.

While UMI-77’s mitophagic effects have been reported in other diseases (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease16, and renal fibrosis17), our study makes unique contributions to the field of AKI. Unlike Jin et al. (2022)17, who focused on chronic renal fibrosis, our study targets acute ischemic AKI—a distinct pathophysiological state characterized by acute tubular necrosis rather than chronic scarring. This addresses the unmet need for acute-phase therapies. Jin et al. demonstrated that UMI-77 preserves mitochondrial fitness in fibrosis, but did not identify the specific mitophagy receptor. Our study is the first to confirm MCL-1 as a direct mitophagy receptor mediating UMI-77’s protective effects in TECs, providing a specific molecular target for AKI.

While our data collectively support that UMI-77 exerts renoprotective effects on TECs in AKI primarily via MCL-1-dependent mitophagy, the limitations of our experimental tools—particularly the use of mitochondrial division inhibitor 1 (Mdivi-1)—should be acknowledged. Mdivi-1 was originally identified as a selective inhibitor of dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) to block mitochondrial fission, and has been widely adopted as a pharmacological tool to suppress mitophagy in preclinical studies18. However, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that Mdivi-1 exhibits notable off-target effects beyond mitophagy regulation: it can directly inhibit the activity of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I, leading to unintended perturbations in cellular ATP production and reactive oxygen species homeostasis18,20. To minimize the impact of these off-target effects on our conclusions, we implemented three complementary experimental strategies: first, we strictly limited the working concentration of Mdivi-1 to 10 µM, a dose validated in published renal tubular epithelial cell studies19 to specifically suppress mitophagy without inducing overt cytotoxicity or mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction (no significant changes in ATP levels or baseline mtROS were observed in HK-2 cells treated with 10 µM Mdivi-1 alone); second, we adopted a dual-verification framework by combining Mdivi-1-mediated pharmacological inhibition with MCL-1 siRNA-mediated genetic knockdown, and the consistent observation that both interventions reversed UMI-77’s protective effects on H/R-induced cell apoptosis, MMP collapse, and mtROS accumulation strongly supported that the observed phenotypes were primarily linked to mitophagy modulation rather than non-specific off-target effects of Mdivi-1; third, we integrated multiple layers of mitophagy readouts (TOM20-LAMP1 co-localization via immunofluorescence, mitophagosome formation via transmission electron microscopy, and degradation of mitochondrial inner/outer membrane proteins COXIV/TOM20 via western blot) to cross-validate the mitophagy-related outcomes, reducing the risk of misattributing off-target effects to mitophagy inhibition.

Despite these safeguards, the reliance on Mdivi-1 and transient siRNA knockdown still constrains the definitive establishment of a causal relationship between mitophagy activation and renal tubular protection in AKI. Transient siRNA-mediated MCL-1 silencing only achieves partial and short-term downregulation of target protein expression, which cannot fully recapitulate the physiological or pathological consequences of complete MCL-1 ablation in renal tubular epithelial cells. Therefore, future studies are warranted to employ more rigorous genetic models to validate our findings: (1) generation of renal tubular epithelial cell-specific MCL-1 conditional knockout mice (using Ksp-Cre or Aqp2-Cre drivers) to specifically ablate MCL-1 in the target cell population, which will avoid systemic MCL-1 knockout-induced embryonic lethality and accurately delineate the cell-autonomous role of MCL-1-mediated mitophagy in AKI; (2) utilization of mitophagy reporter mice (e.g., mt-mKeima or LC3-mCherry-GFP knock-in mice) to enable real-time, in vivo visualization of mitophagy dynamics in renal tubules during ischemia-reperfusion injury and UMI-77 intervention, providing direct spatiotemporal evidence for mitophagy activation; and (3) validation with highly specific mitophagy inhibitors (e.g., PINK1-targeting small molecules or Parkin inhibitors) to exclude potential confounding effects of Drp1-related mitochondrial fission suppression. These genetic and advanced pharmacological tools will further strengthen the mechanistic rigor of our current findings and lay a solid foundation for translating MCL-1-targeted mitophagy activation into clinical AKI therapies.

We found that UMI-77 could activate mitophagy and reduce TECs damage by targeting MCL-1. However, whether UMI-77 activates mitophagy through additional targets or mechanisms beyond MCL-1 remains to be elucidated. MCL-1 is a member of Bcl-2 family proteins, which family regulates tissue development and cell homeostasis through the interplay of survival and death factors21. In addition to MCL-1, other Bcl-2 family proteins may participate in the regulation of mitophagy16. For example, MCL-1 may crosstalk with other Bcl-2 proteins (e.g., Bcl-xL, Bnip3) to regulate mitochondrial function21. Bnip3 has been shown to mediate mitophagy in ischemic renal injury11, and may synergize with MCL-1 to amplify UMI-77’s effects. This is supported by our observation that MCL-1 knockdown only partially reversed UMI-77’s protective effect, indicating additional pathways are involved. Future studies will explore the synergistic role of Bcl-2 family members in UMI-77-induced mitophagy. In the AKI state, whether other Bcl-2 family members can play a synergistic role with MCL-1 is still worth further investigation.

Our conclusion that UMI-77 “facilitates AKI repair” is based on morphological and molecular evidence of reduced damage, but direct data on specific repair processes (e.g., TEC proliferation, dedifferentiation, mitochondrial biogenesis) are lacking. Follow-up studies will supplement these endpoints to clarify the hierarchical repair mechanisms.

Regarding the translational relevance of our treatment paradigm, we acknowledge that pre-treatment (administered before ischemia) is limited to predictable AKI scenarios (e.g., major surgery, contrast-induced AKI), where prophylactic intervention is clinically feasible. For unpredictable AKI (e.g., acute ischemia, sepsis), post-injury administration is more relevant, and this remains a key gap to be addressed in future work. Our current pre-treatment data provide a foundational basis for exploring UMI-77’s therapeutic potential, and follow-up studies will focus on validating post-injury treatment efficacy and optimizing the administration regimen to enhance clinical applicability.

Regarding the safety of UMI-77, our preliminary assessment is based on published literature and in-study observations: the 10 mg/kg dose used in this study is consistent with previous studies16,17 that reported no overt systemic toxicity, and no significant weight loss or mortality was observed in our in vivo experiments. However, we acknowledge that this preliminary assessment does not constitute a comprehensive systemic toxicity evaluation (e.g., long-term exposure effects, histopathological examination of off-target organs such as heart and lung). Given UMI-77’s original pro-apoptotic activity, detailed toxicity profiling is indispensable for clinical translation. Future studies will focus on systematic toxicity assessment—including long-term administration, histopathological analysis of major organs, hematological and biochemical testing, and reproductive/developmental toxicity evaluation—to fully confirm its safety profile before advancing to clinical applications.

In summary, our study provides comprehensive evidence that the FDA-approved drug candidate UMI-77 exerts a protective effect on TECs during AKI, which likely occurs primarily by activating mitophagy. Additionally, our findings indicate that UMI-77 appears to activate mitophagy at least in part by targeting the mitophagy receptor MCL-1—supported by dual verification with mitophagy inhibition (Mdivi-1) and MCL-1 knockdown, as well as direct visualization of mitophagy activation and MCL-1-mitochondria co-localization. While these data strongly support MCL-1 as a potential drug target for AKI and other renal diseases characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction, the limitations of our experimental tools (e.g., Mdivi-1’s off-target effects, transient MCL-1 knockdown) necessitate cautious interpretation of causal relationships. Future studies employing renal tubular-specific MCL-1 conditional knockout mice and mitophagy reporter models will help confirm these mechanistic insights. Nevertheless, our findings further support that induction of mitophagy with UMI-77 or other pharmacological approaches may be a viable strategy for repairing damaged TECs and delaying the transition from AKI to CKD.
