Effects of HIV and azidothymidine on Alzheimer’s-like pathology and amyloid beta transporters in hu-PBL-NSG mice, brain endothelial amyloid beta uptake and endothelial barrier integrity
Authors: Biju Bhargavan, Narendran Annadurai, Georgette D. Kanmogne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite increased life expectancy with antiretroviral therapy, people with HIV (PWH) have significantly higher prevalence of comorbid diseases, including neurocognitive impairment. PWH and neurocognitive impairment often show features of Alzheimer’s-like pathologies, including increased brain amyloid-beta (Aβ) and phospho-Tau; it is not known if antiretroviral drugs contribute to the development of these pathologies or potentiate HIV effects. We aimed to investigate whether azidothymidine (AZT), a drug still used by PWH in many resource-limited countries, alters or potentiates HIV-induced Alzheimer’s-like pathologies.MethodsHu-PBL-NSG mice plasma/serum and brain tissues were analyzed to investigate the effects of HIV-1 infection and AZT treatment on viremia, immunosuppression, phospho-Tau, Aβ42, neuronal NeuN, endothelial claudin-5, ZO-1, and Aβ transporters [low-density lipoprotein receptor–related protein-1 (LRP1) and receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE)] transcription, expression, and proteolytic cleavage. In vitro, we assessed the effects of HIV-1 Tat and AZT on Aβ42 aggregation, endothelial LRP1 and RAGE expression, Aβ uptake and transport.ResultsHIV significantly increased brain phospho-Tau (serine199, threonine181, serine396), brain and plasma Aβ42, decreased LRP1, NeuN, claudin-5, ZO-1, and increased RAGE transcription and expression, increased soluble(s)LRP1 and decreased sRAGE. AZT treatment of infected animals decreased blood and brain viremia and reduced HIV-induced immunosuppression but had no effect on HIV-induced brain Aβ42, phospho-Tau, NeuN, claudin-5, ZO-1, LRP1 or RAGE transcription, expression, or cleavage. AZT treatment of non-infected animals significantly increased phospho-Tau in the brain somatosensory cortex, decreased LRP1, NeuN, claudin-5, and ZO-1, and increased RAGE transcription and expression, increased sLRP1 and decreased sRAGE. Tat decreased LRP1 and increased RAGE expression in brain endothelial cells, and AZT accentuated Tat-induced effects. AZT+Tat significantly increased Aβ aggregation and AZT significantly increased endothelial Aβ42 uptake/retention.ConclusionsHIV and AZT independently dysregulate NeuN, claudin-5, ZO-1, LRP1 and RAGE transcription, expression, and proteolytic cleavage in hu-PBL-NSG mice. Such dysregulation of neuronal nuclei, endothelial tight junction proteins, and Aβ transporters could contribute to increased neurovascular injury and altered Aβ clearance following HIV infection and/or AZT treatment. In the presence of Tat, AZT increased Aβ aggregation. AZT also increased endothelial Aβ uptake/retention, which suggests that AZT may contribute to brain endothelium impairment and dysfunction.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12987-026-00807-4.

Background

Successful combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) over the past decades has significantly increased the life expectancy of people with HIV (PWH). However, there is currently no cure for HIV, and compared to age-matched controls, PWH have a significantly higher prevalence of comorbid diseases, including central nervous system (CNS) complications [1–3]. These CNS comorbidities include HIV-associated neurological disorders (HAND), a constellation of behavioral, motor and cognitive abnormalities associated with increased blood-brain barrier (BBB) injury and dysfunction, neuronal injury, and neurodegeneration [3–6]. Although the prevalence of the most severe form of HAND (HIV-associated dementia) has decreased in the cART era, milder forms of HAND [asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment and mild neurocognitive disorders] are still highly prevalent and occur in up to 50% of the current 40.8 million PWH [3–5, 7].

Autopsy studies of HIV-infected people showed the presence of proteopathy and Alzheimer’s disease (AD)-like CNS pathologies, including increased amyloid-beta (Aβ) deposits, Tau hyperphosphorylation and the presence of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs)-like structures in brain tissues [8–13] associated with high viral loads [13] and neurocognitive impairment, including impairment in speed of information processing, attention, and working memory [12, 13]. These AD-like CNS pathologies are also present in people who have been on long-term cART, but it is not known whether antiretroviral drugs (ARVs) contribute to the development of these pathologies or potentiate the effects of HIV and viral factors.

Newer ARVs have been shown to induce less toxicity and adverse reactions compared to older HIV medications [14–16]; however, many long-term HIV survivors had been on older ARVs [15, 16] and it has been suggested that some comorbid diseases in PWH may be due to legacy effects from older HIV medications [16, 17]. Several such older ARVs are nucleoside/nucleotide reverse-transcriptase inhibitors, a drug class that constitute the backbone of cART and are used both for HIV treatment and pre-exposure prophylaxis. Drugs in this class approved for HIV treatment have included the nucleoside analogs azidothymidine (AZT), stavudine, zalcitabine, lamivudine, emtricitabine, didanosine, and abacavir; and the nucleotide analog tenofovir. Of these seven nucleoside analogs, three (zalcitabine, didanosine, and stavudine) have been withdrawn or discontinued, and four (AZT, lamivudine, emtricitabine, and abacavir) remain part of current FDA-approved HIV medicine [14]. Although AZT is now rarely used in developed countries [14, 18], it is still used in many resource-limited countries; the current World Health Organization (WHO) HIV treatment guideline list AZT-based regimens as preferred and alternative 2nd line cART for adults, adolescents, and children [19]. The goal of the present study was to investigate the effects of HIV infection and AZT treatment on CNS Aβ42, phospho-Tau, neuronal nuclei protein (NeuN), the brain endothelial tight junction proteins (claudin-5, ZO-1) and Aβ transporters low-density lipoprotein receptor–related protein-1 (LRP1), a receptor that mediates Aβ CNS efflux/clearance [20–22], and receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), a receptor that binds and transports circulating Aβ into the brain [21, 23, 24]. We further investigated the effects of HIV-1 Tat protein and AZT on Aβ aggregation, and using an in vitro BBB model consisting of primary human brain microvascular endothelial cells, we assessed the effects of Tat and AZT on endothelial LRP1 and RAGE expression, Aβ uptake and transport.

Methods

Hu-PBL-NSG mice model

Four-week-old NOD/scid–IL-2 Rγcnull (NSG) mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME), maintained in sterile microisolator cages under pathogen-free conditions in accordance with the University of Nebraska Medical Center (UNMC) and National Institutes of Health (NIH) ethical guidelines for the care of laboratory animals, and bred at the UNMC animal facility to expand the colony. This study was performed under a protocol approved by the UNMC Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Human peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBL) were obtained by countercurrent centrifugal elutriation of leukopheresis packs from HIV-1, 2, and hepatitis B seronegative donors, as previously described [25]. Mice (4 to 6 weeks old males) were engrafted by intra-peritoneal (i.p.) injection of human PBL (30 × 106 cells/mouse), and engraftment was confirmed a week later by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) quantification of human CD45+ cells in the animals’ blood (Fig. 1). Engrafted animals were randomly assigned into 4 groups (10 mice per group): PBS (non-treated and non-infected mice), AZT (non-infected mice treated with AZT); HIV (infected animals, untreated), HIV+AZT (infected animals treated with AZT). Animals were infected by i.p. injection of HIV-1ADA (single dose of 104 tissue culture infectious doses-50, 100 μl) (Fig. 1) as previously described [26–28]. Controls were mock-infected by i.p. injection of 100 μl phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Animals’ blood samples were collected weekly post-infection (p.i.) for 3 weeks and analyzed by FACS (Additional file 1). Animals were sacrificed at week 3 p.i., tissue samples were harvested (Fig. 1) and processed as previously described [26–28].Fig. 1Schematic overview of animal experiments. AZT preparation and treatment

AZT (CamberTM Pharmaceuticals, Piscataway, NJ) in 300 mg tablets were purchased from the UNMC pharmacy. One tablet was crushed and dissolved in 4 ml of a 1:1 mixture of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and sterile PBS, resulting in an initial stock concentration of 75 mg/ml (C1) that was stored in 600 µl aliquots at −80 °C and used within two days of preparation. For treatment, 500 µl of C1 stock solution was further diluted in PBS, yielding an intermediate working concentration of 15 mg/ml (C2). The C2 solution was then diluted in PBS to obtain a final (C3) concentration of 7.5 mg/ml. This C3 solution was passed through a 0.45 µm syringe filter to remove any undissolved drug excipients and 200 µl (60 mg/kg, 1.5 mg AZT per 25 g mouse) injected (i.p.) into each mouse twice/day. This dose and treatment schedule were based on recommended human AZT doses of 300 mg twice/day and the consensus human-mouse interspecies allometric scaling factor of 12.3 [29, 30]. Untreated animals were injected (i.p.) with 200 μl PBS containing an equal amount of DMSO as in the final AZT solution (C3) used for treatment.

Ethics and processing of brain tissues

This study was performed under a protocol approved by the UNMC Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol number 19–141-01-FC). Immediately following the animals’ sacrifice, each brain was collected and placed in ice-cold PBS and under a stereo zoom microscope, each lobe was split into three pieces: frontal cortex (FC), somatosensory cortex (SSC), and a 3rd piece consisting of a region encompassing the insular, auditory and visual cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, and hypothalamus. Animal samples were immediately frozen at −80 °C and subsequently used for protein extraction and Western blot analysis, RNA extraction and real-time PCR (Additional file 1).

Brain endothelial cells culture, treatment, and Aβ trans-endothelial transport

Primary human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMEC) were cultured as previously described [31, 32] (Additional file 1). For Aβ transport, confluent HBMEC cultured on collagen-treated tissue culture inserts (0.4-μm pore size; Corning, Lowell, MA) were exposed to human Aβ42 peptide (10 μM, added to the upper chamber of the transwell) in the presence or absence of AZT (1 μM) and/or high-affinity antagonists for LRP1 (500 nM, Kerafast, Boston, MA) and RAGE (200 nM, Tocris, Minneapolis, MN). Doses of LRP1 and RAGE inhibitors were selected based on previously published studies [26, 33] and manufacturers’ data showing that these concentrations blocked secretase activity and Aβ binding to LRP1 and RAGE without causing cellular toxicity. AZT used for in vitro studies was obtained from the NIH HIV Reagent Program (cat# 0952). The AZT dose used (1 μM) is within human plasma therapeutic ranges, has been shown to be nontoxic to human cells in vitro [34–36], and did not alter HBMEC viability (Fig. S1, Additional file 1). Controls included cells treated with DMSO (vehicle). After 24 h of culture, media from the transwell lower chamber were collected and any cell debris was removed by centrifugation (1000 ×g for 10 minutes at 4°C). HBMEC in the transwell upper chamber were harvested by trypsinization, washed three times with PBS, and lysed. Aβ42 levels in cell lysates and culture supernatants were quantified by ELISA. For LRP1 and RAGE expression, HBMEC cultured in six-well plates were exposed to HIV-1 Tat proteins (100 ng/ml) and/or AZT (1 μM) for 24 h and levels of LRP1 and RAGE quantified by immunoblotting (Fig. S2, Additional file 1).

ELISA and Thioflavin T (ThT) binding assay

Cellular, brain, and plasma levels of Aβ42, HIV-1 p24, soluble (s)LRP1, and sRAGE were quantified by ELISA (Additional file 1). For the ThT binding assay, 100 μl of Aβ42 (1 mg/ml in PBS) (AnaSpec, Fremont, CA) was sonicated for 10 minutes on ice using a 50 Sonic Dismembrator (Fisher Scientific), followed by a 30-seconds quick spin in a tabletop centrifuge. Sonicated Aβ42 was mixed with ThT (Biotium, Fremont, CA) in an ice-chilled Eppendorf tube, and 50 μl of this mixture (5 μM Aβ42 and 50 μM ThT in PBS) dispensed into a black, clear-bottom 96-well plate (Corning, Glendale, AZ). AZT (1 μM) and/or HIV-1 transactivator of transcription (Tat) (100 ng/ml) were then added, the plate sealed with an adhesive film and immediately placed in a SpectraMax M5e plate reader (configured to maintain constant shaking, with the measurement chamber temperature set to 37°C). Fluorescence was recorded hourly over 48 hours (excitation = 450 nm, emission = 482 nm) to assess ThT binding to aggregated Aβ. Controls included blank wells (without Aβ, Tat, or AZT) and wells containing only Aβ.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by Student’s t-test (two-tailed) or by one- or two-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test using GraphPad Prism 10.1.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Sample size was calculated based on previous studies showing an expected effect size ≥ 5 when comparing brain pathologies in non-treated and treated infected animals [26]; this achieved 90% power to detect at least 5 standard deviation (SD) differences in a single factor ANOVA study, the threshold for the significance level was 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± SD.

HIV and AZT dysregulate LRP1 and RAGE proteolytic cleavage

Proteolytic cleavage of full-length LRP1 or RAGE on the brain endothelial membrane release truncated isoforms (sLRP1 and sRAGE) into extracellular space and impair the ability of remaining cell-bound fragments to bind and transport Aβ [37–39]. Discussion

PWH show increased incidence and prevalence of HAND and other CNS comorbidities, compared to demographically matched controls [1–3]. HIV and viral proteins have been shown to damage brain cells and the BBB, and these viral-induced injuries contribute to the pathogenesis of HAND [4–6]. Although many PWH with HAND have controlled viremia and have been on long-term treatment, it is not known if cART or some ARV drugs are contributing factors. It has also been suggested that legacy effects from initial HIV-induced brain injury and older HIV medications such as AZT could be contributing factors [17]. The current WHO HIV treatment guideline list AZT-based regimens as preferred and alternative 2nd line cART for adults, adolescents, and children [19]. Over 120 low and middle income countries (87% of the 40.8 million PWH) follow WHO HIV treatment guidelines [40]; with persisting poor adherence, PWH failing 1st line cART remain a major issue, and those who fail 1st line therapy are on 2nd line cART, several of which are AZT-based regimens [19, 41, 42]. Thus, although currently recommended cART regimens in high income countries don’t include AZT [14, 18], this drug is still in use in numerous countries and study of its effects is important.

Review of CNS pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of different ARVs and cART regimens showed that AZT was among the ARVs with high CNS penetration and cerebrospinal fluid/plasma ratios [43, 44]. In a prospective study of PWH on various cART regimens, AZT-based regimens were associated with most adverse reactions, including CNS adverse effects [45]. Previous studies showed increased CNS proteopathy and AD-like pathologies in HIV-infected humans [8–11] and animal models [26], including increased accumulation of amyloid precursor protein, Aβ deposits and Tau hyperphosphorylation associated with neurocognitive impairment [8–11]. Our current study in a validated HIV animal model confirmed these findings and showed that AZT treatment did not significantly change CNS Aβ or phospho-Tau levels. AZT did not alter trans-endothelial Aβ transport, but it significantly decreased the transcription and expression of the neuronal nuclei marker NeuN, significantly decreased the transcription of the endothelial tight junction proteins claudin-5, also reduced claudin-5 and ZO-1 expression, and significantly increased endothelial Aβ uptake and retention; this could affect neuronal and brain vascular health and function. In fact, intracellular accumulation of Aβ42 induce apoptosis [46] and intracellular Aβ42 can further undergo oligomerization within cells, which accentuate Aβ-induced cellular toxicity [47]. Studies in humans and AD animal models showed that intracellular accumulation of Aβ42 is an early event in AD development and predicts synaptic dysfunction and neurodegeneration [47, 48]. The effects of AZT-induced increase in endothelial Aβ uptake/retention are not known. Our future studies will investigate whether such ARV-induced intracellular accumulation of Aβ42 in brain endothelial cells effect the brain endothelium properties and function. Our current study also showed that AZT increased Aβ aggregation in the presence of Tat. Tat proteins are secreted by infected cells in vitro and in vivo and can be detected in body fluids and tissues of HIV-infected humans [49–51]. The Tat concentration used in this study (100 ng/ml) has been used in previous studies [27, 50] and is within concentration ranges detected in PWH [49–51]. The fact that in the presence of Tat, AZT increased Aβ aggregation suggests an increased risk of Aβ deposit/accumulation in HIV infection and AZT treatment.

Our present study showed that increased CNS Aβ following HIV infection was associated with increased RAGE and decreased LRP1, two endothelial receptors that regulate Aβ BBB transport. Aβ is produced by brain cells and LRP1 binds CNS Aβ on the BBB abluminal side and induces its efflux/clearance to maintain brain Aβ homeostasis [20–22]. Aβ can also be produced by peripheral cells and RAGE binds peripheral Aβ on the BBB luminal side and induces its influx into the brain [21, 23, 24]. Studies in humans [20, 52–54] and AD animal models [20, 53] showed that AD was associated with drastic LRP1 reduction and major RAGE increase in brain tissues and capillaries, and this correlated with increased CNS Aβ and severe AD pathology [55, 56], increased Aβ influx into the brain, neuroinflammation, Tau hyperphosphorylation, and neurodegeneration [57, 58]. Our current data suggests that HIV and viral proteins may induce brain Aβ pathology by impairing brain endothelial Aβ transporters. HIV-induced decrease in LRP1 levels would impair and diminish CNS Aβ efflux and an increase in RAGE would increase influx of peripheral Aβ into the brain.

How AZT increases brain endothelial Aβ uptake/retention is not known. Dysregulation of endothelial Aβ transporters could be contributing factors. Although AZT treatment did not alter plasma or brain Aβ levels, it significantly decreased CNS LRP1 and increased RAGE and accentuated Tat-induced LRP1 decrease and RAGE increase in brain endothelial cells. AZT- or AZT+Tat- induced upregulation of RAGE (endothelial Aβ influx transporter) could contribute to increased endothelial Aβ uptake, impairment in BBB properties and function. AZT- or AZT+Tat- induced downregulation of LRP1 (endothelial Aβ efflux transporter) could further reduce endothelial Aβ CNS efflux and increase Aβ retention. Proteolytic cleavage of full-length LRP1 and RAGE releases sLRP1 and sRAGE into circulation, leaving truncated cell-bound LRP1 fragments that lack the LRP1 binding domain and cannot bind or transport/efflux Aβ [37, 59], and truncated cell-bound RAGE fragments that lack the extracellular domain essential for RAGE signaling and function [38, 39]. The current study showed that HIV infection drastically increased sLRP1 and decreased sRAGE, suggesting that dysregulation in LRP1 and RAGE proteolytic cleavage may contribute to the functional impairment of these brain Aβ transporters in HIV infection and increased CNS Aβ. Significantly, AZT treatment also drastically increased sLRP1 and decreased sRAGE in both infected and non-infected animals. sLRP1 can bind Aβ but loses the ability to endocytose and transport Aβ [37, 59]. Therefore, in addition to the fact that HIV- and AZT-induced decrease in LRP1 transcription and expression would reduce Aβ efflux, HIV- and AZT-induced increase in LRP1 proteolytic cleavage (sLRP1) will further reduce Aβ endocytosis, transport and CNS efflux. sRAGE antagonizes the activation and function of full-length RAGE and reduces Aβ BBB transport and CNS influx [60, 61], and inhibits Aβ aggregation [62]. Thus, in addition to the fact that HIV- and AZT-induced RAGE upregulation would increase Aβ CNS influx, HIV- and AZT-induced decrease in sRAGE could decrease the scavenging and clearance of circulating Aβ, and further increase the binding of peripheral Aβ to cellular RAGE and Aβ influx into the brain. However, in our current study, AZT-induced sLRP1 increase and sRAGE decrease were not associated with altered Aβ levels, and compared to non-treated animals, AZT-treatment of infected and non-infected animals did not change plasma or brain Aβ levels, suggesting that HIV infection was primarily responsible for the increased Aβ. It is not known if other transporters could have played a role. ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters such as P-glycoprotein/ABCB1, ABCG2, ABCA1, ABCA2, ABCA7, and ABCC1 have been shown to play a role in Aβ efflux and clearance [63, 64]. AZT has been shown to increase ABCB1, ABCC4, and ABCC5 in human macrophages [65], but it is not known if AZT also alter/increase these efflux transporters in brain cells, or whether these transporters may have facilitated CNS Aβ efflux in AZT-treated animals, despite increased RAGE and decreased LRP1.

Limitations

Because we did not have brain samples from PWH who had specifically been treated with AZT, we don’t know if AZT also dysregulates LRP1 and RAGE expression and proteolytic cleavage in humans. Soluble amyloid oligomers and protofibrils are considered the most toxic to cells, although the ThT fluorescence assay is a robust test for detecting amyloid aggregation, it cannot separately quantify amyloid fibrils and other forms of Aβ aggregates (oligomers and protofibrils). With the total number of samples from the four animal groups (8–10 animals per group) and analysis of 3 different regions (FC, SSC, 3rdBR) in each animal, we could not separately analyze more specific brain regions and had to combine several brain regions into the 3rdBR samples. HIV infection is now a chronic condition and PWH can live for decades on cART; thus, our current study of hu-PBL-NSG mice HIV-infected and AZT-treated for three weeks (equivalent of 2.5 human years) [66] may not fully recapitulate the effects of long-term/chronic infection and cART. Because we could not perform behavioral assessment, it is not known if any HIV- and/or AZT-induced effect observed in our current study could be associated with altered cognition. There are several other ARV drugs and different drug classes, and their effects on these Aβ transporters, CNS Aβ and Tau pathology remain to be examined. Our future studies will include older animals, longer-term HIV infection and treatment, including treatment with newer ARVs, and assessment of both pathological/cerebrovascular and behavioral effects.

Conclusions

Results of the current animals’ studies suggests that HIV increased CNS pTau and Aβ42, and blood Aβ42. Both HIV infection and AZT treatment independently decreased brain endothelial tight junction proteins (claudin-5 and ZO-1) and NeuN, and dysregulated LRP1 and RAGE transcription, expression, and proteolytic cleavage. This dysregulation of Aβ transporters could contribute to altered Aβ clearance in HIV infection (Fig. 11). In addition to decreasing tight junction proteins and neuronal marker NeuN in infected and treated animals, AZT increased Aβ uptake and retention; in the presence of Tat, AZT increased Aβ aggregation and accentuated Tat-induced dysregulation of endothelial LRP1 and RAGE.
