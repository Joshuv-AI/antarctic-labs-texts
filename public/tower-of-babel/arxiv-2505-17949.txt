Solving quadratic forms in restricted variables with the circle method
Mieke Wessel and Svenja zur Verth

Abstract
. Let f ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) f(\bm{x}) be a non-singular quadratic form with sufficiently many mixed terms and t t an integer. For a sequence of weights 𝒜 \mathcal{A} we study the number of weighted solutions to f ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) = t f(\bm{x})=t . In particular, we give conditions on both 𝒜 \mathcal{A} and f f such that we can use the circle method to count such solutions of bounded height.



1.
Introduction

Let
f(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}]
be a non-singular quadratic form and
\mathcal{A}
a sequence
(a_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{N}}
with
a_{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
such that the sum over
\mathcal{A}
diverges. The goal of this paper is to give general conditions on
\mathcal{A}
and
f
for which we can carry out the circle method to count the weighted number of solutions of bounded height to
f(\bm{x})=t
,
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, where the weights equal
\prod_{i=1}^{s}a_{x_{i}}
. In other words, we will give conditions under which we can determine an asymptotic formula for

R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A}):=\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{x}\leqslant X}\mathds{1}_{[f(\bm{x})=t]}\prod_{i=1}^{s}a_{x_{i}}.

For some specific choices of
\mathcal{A}
this counting function has already been studied thoroughly. For instance, if we choose
a_{x}=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{N}
the value of
R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A})
is the number of positive integral solutions to
f(\bm{x})=t
. This is a classical problem and it is known due to Heath-Brown
[
HB96
]
that an asymptotic formula exists whenever the number of variables
s
is greater or equal to
3
. By work of Baker
[
Bak06
,
Bak10
]
, the asymptotic formula for the case
a_{x}=\mu(x)^{2}
, which is the indicator function of the squarefree numbers, is known, also whenever
s\geqslant 3
. Another important example is motivated by the question if
f(\bm{x})=t
has any solutions in the primes. This has been researched by Liu and Zhao in
[
Liu11
]
and
[
Zha16
]
.
Rather than taking the indicator function for primes they considered the weights
a_{x}=\Lambda(x)
, where
\Lambda(x)
is the von Mangoldt function. They conclude that whenever
f
has sufficiently many mixed terms, the asymptotic formula can be determined. The meaning of sufficiently many is similar to our Condition

L
1

below, and consequently implies that
s\geqslant 9
or
s\geqslant 10
.

One thing that stands out about these examples is that in each case the sequence
\mathcal{A}
is far from random. It has a lot of arithmetic structure and the series
A(X):=\sum_{x=1}^{X}a_{x}
has a nice asymptotic expression as
X\in\mathbb{R}
grows. That raises the question, which minimal conditions on
\mathcal{A}
and
f
are sufficient to be able to apply the circle method to get an asymptotic formula for
R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A})
. To answer this question we build upon the work of Biggs and Brandes
[
BB23
]
, who studied a very similar question for several variations of Waring’s problem. In our case, however, we will consider forms that have many mixed terms.

To make the idea of ‘many mixed terms’ more precise we introduce
F=(F_{i,j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant s}\in M_{s\times s}(\mathbb{Z})
, the symmetric matrix of
f
such that

f(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}F_{i,j}x_{i}x_{j}=\bm{x}^{T}F\bm{x}.

Define
n=\lfloor s/2\rfloor
and let
F_{1}
be the
n\times n
matrix,
F_{2}
the
(s-n)\times n
matrix,
F_{3}
the
n\times(s-n)
matrix and
F_{4}
the
(s-n)\times(s-n)
matrix such that

F=\begin{pmatrix}F_{1}&F_{2}\\
F_{3}&F_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

Condition
\textup{L}_{1}
.

We say that
f
meets Condition
\text{L}_{1}
if we can permute the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}
such that

\rank(F_{2})\geqslant 5.

Remark 1.1
.

In particular Condition

L
1

implies that
s\geqslant 10
and that
f
has at least
5
mixed terms.

Apart from needing a positive non-singular solution to
f(\bm{x})=0
to guarantee positivity of the singular integral, Condition

L
1

will be the only condition on
f
. All the other conditions are on the sequence
\mathcal{A}
. From now on we will write
L
to denote
\log(X)
and we recall that
A(X)=\sum_{x=1}^{X}a_{x},X\in\mathbb{R}
.

Condition
\textup{L}_{2}
.

We say that
\mathcal{A}
meets Condition
\text{L}_{2}
if there exists a constant
c\geqslant 0
such that for all
X\geqslant 1
it holds that

\sum_{x=1}^{X}a_{x}^{2}\ll A(X)^{2}X^{-1}L^{c}.

Remark 1.2
.

Note that by Cauchy-Schwarz we have the lower bound

A(X)^{2}X^{-1}\leqslant\sum_{x=1}^{X}a_{x}^{2}.

Remark 1.3
.

When
a_{x}
is an indicator function we have that
\sum_{x\leqslant X}{a_{x}^{2}}=A(X)
and therefore,

A(X)\gg XL^{-c}

whenever Condition

L
2

holds.

To apply the circle method it is necessary that the weights
a_{x}
are distributed in a consistent way over residue classes modulo
q
as
X
grows. For
q\in\mathbb{N}
and the residue class
h
modulo
q
we define
A(q,h;X):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant x\leqslant X\\
x\equiv h\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})\end{subarray}}a_{x}
.

Condition D
.

Let
Q_{D}=Q_{D}(X)
be an increasing function of
X
. We refer to a set
\mathcal{A}
as distributed to the level
Q_{D}
when for all
X>1
and for each
q\leqslant Q_{D}
and
h\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})
there is a real number
\kappa(q,h)\geqslant 0
such that the asymptotic formula

A(q,h;X)=\kappa(q,h)A(X)+E(X)

is satisfied with an error term
E(X)=O(A(X)L^{-m})
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. The implicit constant is allowed to depend on
m
.

Remark 1.4
.

A similar condition was first stated in
[
Br“”u09
]
. Our condition is a bit stronger than the equivalent one in
[
BB23
]
– also called Condition (D) – where the error term
E(X)
is allowed to be of order
o(A(X))
.

Remark 1.5
.

Note that Condition
D
implies

\sum_{h\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})}\kappa(q,h)=1.

The following condition states that
\kappa
has some multiplicative structure, which is needed to interpret the constant of the asymptotic formula as a product of local densities.

Condition C
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is distributed to the level
Q_{D}=Q_{D}(X)
. Let
q,q^{\prime},h,h^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
. We say
\mathcal{A}
satisfies Condition C if the coefficients
\kappa(q,h)
satisfy

\kappa(qq^{\prime},qh^{\prime}+q^{\prime}h)=\kappa(q,q^{\prime}h)\kappa(q^{\prime},qh^{\prime})\text{ for all }(q,q^{\prime})=1\text{ such that }qq^{\prime}<Q_{D}.

Finally, our last condition states that
\kappa
is close to being uniformly distributed. We need this assumption to ensure that product of local densities converges.

Condition K
.

For
q\rightarrow\infty
there exists a constant
c\geqslant 0
independent of
q
such that

\kappa(q,h)\ll\frac{(\log q)^{c}}{q}.

Our main result states that under the above conditions we can give an asymptotic formula for
R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A})
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume Conditions
C
,
D
,
K
,

L
1

and

L
2

hold and that
A(X)
is smoothly approximable (see Definition
2.1
below). Furthermore, let
K>0
be any real number and define
\mathfrak{I}(X)
as in Equation (
4.3
). Then

R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A})=\mathfrak{S}\mathfrak{I}(X)+O(A(X)^{s}X^{-2}L^{-K}+\mathfrak{I}(X)L^{-K}).

Here
\mathfrak{S}
is a non-negative constant that can be interpreted as the product of local
p
-adic densities for all primes
p
.

Remark 1.7
.

A typical example one can think of for
A(X)
being smoothly approximable is
A(X)=XL^{k}+O(XL^{-m})
for
k\in\mathbb{R}
fixed and any
m
.

To also interpret
\mathfrak{I}(X)
we consider a slightly different related counting problem. Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}
and define
R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{B})
as the weighted number of solutions inside
X\mathfrak{B}:=\{X\bm{v}\mid\bm{v}\in\mathfrak{B}\}
, more precisely

R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{B}):=\sum_{\bm{x}\in X\mathfrak{B}}\mathds{1}_{\{0\}}(f(\bm{x})-t)\prod_{i=1}^{s}a_{x_{i}}.

Note that
R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A},[0,1]^{s})=R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A}).

Theorem 1.8
.

Assume we are in the same setting as in Theorem
1.6
and that
f(\bm{x})=0
has a solution in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}
. Then there are compact sets
\mathfrak{C}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{C}_{2}
such that for each
X
there exists
\mathfrak{B}
, a suitable compact set with
\mathfrak{C}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{B}\subset\mathfrak{C}_{2}
, for which the following holds.

Let
\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)
be as in Equation (
6.1
). Then

R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{B})=\mathfrak{S}\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)+O\left(A(X)^{s}X^{-2}L^{-K}\right),

where
\mathfrak{S}
has the same value as in Theorem
1.6
. Furthermore

A(X)^{s}X^{-2}\ll\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)\ll A(X)^{s}X^{-2},

and the value
\frac{X^{2}}{A(X)^{s}}\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)
can be interpreted as a local density over the reals inside the compact set
\mathfrak{B}
.

We will prove these results using the circle method. For
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, we write
a_{\bm{x}}
for
\prod_{i=1}^{s}a_{x_{i}}
. Hence,

(1.1)

R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A})=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{x}\leqslant X}a_{\bm{x}}e(\alpha(f(\bm{x})-t))\text{d}\alpha.

Indeed, one would then expect an asymptotic expression of the form
CX^{-2}A(X)^{s}
, where
C\geqslant 0
can be interpreted as a product over the densities of solutions in the reals and
p
-adics.

In Section
2
we set up the notation and discuss smooth approximability of
A(X)
.
In Section
3
we give a Weyl-like estimate for the exponential sum with which we can then bound the minor arcs. This is done under Conditions

L
1

and

2

, where the first condition ensures that we can apply a Cauchy-Schwarz inequality before we start Weyl-differencing and the second condition implies that applying Cauchy-Schwarz gives strong upper bounds.

In Section

we treat the major arcs in similar fashion as Biggs and Brandes did in
[

]
. Here we will need Condition

to make sure we have consistent behavior as

The analysis of the minor and major arcs already gives us an expression for

with the correct error term. To interpret the main term we consider the

-adic and real behavior of

separately in Sections

and

. First we interpret our so-called singular series as the product of

-adic densities using Conditions

and

. Then we use our bounds on

to conclude the claims made about

in Theorem

.

Finally, in Section

2.
Set-up and notation

We write
e(z)=e^{2\pi iz}
and
L=\log(X)
. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
define the exponential sum

S(\alpha):=\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{x}\leqslant X}a_{\bm{x}}e(\alpha f(\bm{x})).

Then
R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A})=\int_{0}^{1}S(\alpha)d\alpha.
We split up the interval
[0,1]
into major arcs, where we will show that
S(\alpha)
is big, and minor arcs, where we will show that
S(\alpha)
is small. For this we use the following major and minor arcs. Let
P
and
Q
be two fixed parameters and take
1\leqslant a\leqslant q\leqslant P
two integers such that
(a,q)=1
. We define the major arc
\mathscr{M}(a,q)
to be the interval
[\frac{a}{q}-\frac{1}{qQ},\frac{a}{q}+\frac{1}{qQ}].
The major arcs
\mathscr{M}
are then defined as the union over
a,q
as above, that is

\mathscr{M}=\cup_{1\leqslant q\leqslant P}\cup_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\mathscr{M}_{a,q}.

The minor arcs
\mathfrak{m}
are the complement of
\mathscr{M}
in
[0,1]
. It will suffice to take
Q=X^{2}P^{-1}
and
P=L^{B}
for some sufficiently large
B>0
depending on
K
and
c
of Condition

L
2

. In Section
4
we also consider the slightly enlarged major arcs
\mathscr{N}(a,q)
being the interval
[\frac{a}{q}-\frac{1}{Q},\frac{a}{q}+\frac{1}{Q}]
and their union
\mathscr{N}
.

Furthermore, we use the following notation throughout the entire paper:

Let
R
be some set. For
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})\in R^{s}
and any function
\phi
with domain
R
we write
\phi(\bm{x})
for
\prod_{i=1}^{s}\phi(x_{i})
. For example, for
\bm{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{s})\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{s}
we have
\kappa(q,\bm{h})=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\kappa(q,h_{i}).
Similarly, when writing
0\leqslant\bm{x}\leqslant X
we mean
0\leqslant x_{i}\leqslant X
for all
i
.

By
f(x)=O(g(x)),f(x)\ll g(x)
and
g(x)\gg f(x)
we mean that there exists some constant
C>0
such that for all
x
sufficiently large
|f(x)|\leqslant C|g(x)|
. We write
f(x)\asymp g(x)
if
f(x)\ll g(x)
and
f(x)\gg g(x)
.

The symbol
\varepsilon
denotes an arbitrarily small positive constant. The exact value of the constant may change between occurrences and even between lines.

We write
\mathop{{\sum}^{\ast}}\limits_{a\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})}
for the sum over integers
a
between
1
and
q
that are coprime to
q
.

We will write
\|x\|
to mean the minimal distance of
x
to an integer, in other words
\|x\|=\min_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|x-n|
.

Smooth approximability

For the treatment of the major arcs and the interpretation of the singular integral we need to work with a smoothed version of the weight function. For these purposes the naive idea of a connecting the dots function is not always good enough. This subsection is devoted to giving a suitable definition of smooth approximability.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that the function
X\mapsto A(X)
can be smoothly approximated if for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exists some smooth function
\Psi
with derivative
\psi
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

\Psi(X)=A(X)+O\left(\frac{A(X)}{L^{m}}\right),

(2)

\psi(X)\asymp\frac{A(X)}{X},

(3)

\int_{yX}^{zX}|\psi^{\prime}(u)|du\ll_{y,z}\frac{A(X)}{X},\textup{ where }y,z\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

Example 2.2
.

Some examples of such functions
\Psi(X)
are
X^{k}L^{\ell}
for all
k>0
and
\ell\in\mathbb{R}
and
X\log(\log(X))+XL^{-1}
.

In Section
8
we discuss the case where
\mathcal{A}
is indicator function of the primes.

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume that
A(X)
can be smoothly approximated by
\Psi(X)
. Then

\Psi(bX)\asymp_{y,z}\Psi(X)\text{ for all $b\in(y,z)$ where $y,z\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}$}.

Proof.

By Condition (1) and the monotonicity of
A(X)
we have

\Psi(yX)\ll A(yX)\leqslant A(bX)\ll\Psi(bX)\ll A(bX)\leqslant A(zX)\ll\Psi(zX).

It therefore suffices to show that
\Psi(X)\ll_{y}\Psi(yX)
and
\Psi(zX)\ll_{z}\Psi(X).
We may furthermore assume that
y<1
and
z>1
.
From Conditions (1) and (2) we know that there exist positive constants
c_{i}
such that for
X
big enough we have

c_{1}A(X)\leqslant\Psi(X)\leqslant c_{2}A(X)\text{ and }|\psi(X)|\leqslant c_{3}\frac{A(X)}{X}.

We first consider the asymptotic upper bound for
\Psi(zX)
. Let
z_{0}>1
.

\displaystyle\Psi(X)

\displaystyle=\Psi(z_{0}X)-\int_{X}^{z_{0}X}\psi(u)du

\displaystyle\geqslant\Psi(z_{0}X)-\int_{X}^{z_{0}X}|\psi(u)|du

\displaystyle\geqslant\Psi(z_{0}X)-c_{3}\int_{X}^{z_{0}X}\frac{A(u)}{u}du

\displaystyle\geqslant\Psi(z_{0}X)-c_{3}A(z_{0}X)\int_{X}^{z_{0}X}\frac{1}{u}du

\displaystyle\geqslant\Psi(z_{0}X)-\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}}\Psi(z_{0}X)\log z_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}}\log z_{0}\right)\Psi(z_{0}X)

Since
\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}}
is a positive constant, we can find
z_{0}>1
such that
1-\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}}\log z_{0}>0
.
Since for all
z>1
there exists a
k
such that
z\leqslant z_{0}^{k}
we can conclude by repeated application of the above inequality that

\Psi(zX)\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}}\log z_{0}}\right)^{k}\Psi(X)

and hence

\Psi(zX)\ll_{z}\Psi(X).

Similarly we obtain for the lower bound with
y_{0}<1
that

\displaystyle\Psi(y_{0}X)

\displaystyle=\Psi(X)-\int_{y_{0}X}^{X}\psi(u)du

\displaystyle\geqslant\Psi(X)-c_{3}\int_{y_{0}X}^{X}\frac{A(u)}{u}du

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1+\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}}\log y_{0}\right)\Psi(X).

Note that
\log y_{0}<0
.
Since
\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}}
is a positive constant, we can find
y_{0}<1
such that
1+\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}}\log y_{0}>0
.
Since for all
y>1
there exists a
k
such that
y\geqslant y_{0}^{k}
we can conclude by repeated application of the above inequality that

\Psi(yX)\geqslant\left(1+\frac{c_{3}}{c_{1}}\log y_{0}\right)^{k}\Psi(X)

and hence

\Psi(yX)\gg_{y}\Psi(X).\qed

3.
A Weyl-like estimate and the minor arcs

In this section we will prove the following Weyl-like estimate and use it to bound the contribution of the minor arcs.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
r:=\rank(F_{2})
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose Condition

L
2

holds and
\alpha=\frac{a}{q}+\lambda
for some
a\in\mathbb{Z}
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, then

S(\alpha)\ll\max_{i,j}(F_{i,j})(A(X)L^{C})^{s}\left(\frac{1}{X}+\frac{1}{q(1+X^{2}|\lambda|)}+\frac{q(1+X^{2}|\lambda|)}{X^{2}}\right)^{\frac{r}{2}},

where the implicit constant depends only on
s
and
r
and
C
is a constant depending on the constant
c
in Condition

L
2

.

Remark 3.2
.

The proof is strongly based on the proof of Lemma 3.7 in
[
Liu11
]
. For the sake of self-containedness we give a complete proof here.

Recall that
n=\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\rfloor
. We may express the vector
\bm{x}
as
(\bm{y},\bm{z})
, where
\bm{y}
is
n
-dimensional and
\bm{z}
is
s-n
dimensional. Then
f(\bm{x})
can be written as

f(\bm{x})=r(\bm{y})+y_{1}g_{1}(\bm{z})+\ldots+y_{n}g_{n}(\bm{z})+q(\bm{z}),

where
r
and
q
are quadratic forms and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
are linear forms. Similarly we can define linear forms
h_{1},\ldots,h_{s-n}
such that

f(\bm{x})=r(\bm{y})+z_{1}h_{1}(\bm{y})+\ldots+z_{s-n}h_{s-n}(\bm{y})+q(\bm{z}).

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose we are in the situation of Lemma
3.1
.
We have

|S(\alpha)|^{2}\ll(A(X)^{2}X^{-1}L^{c})^{s}\sum_{\bm{v}\leqslant X}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\min\left(X,\frac{1}{\|\alpha g_{j}(\bm{v})\|}\right),

and

|S(\alpha)|^{2}\ll(A(X)^{2}X^{-1}L^{c})^{s}\sum_{\bm{v}\leqslant X}\prod_{j=1}^{s-n}\min\left(X,\frac{1}{\|\alpha h_{j}(\bm{v})\|}\right).

Proof.

We rewrite the exponential sum
S(\alpha)
as

S(\alpha)=\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{y}\leqslant X}\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{z}\leqslant X}a_{\bm{y}}a_{\bm{z}}e(\alpha(f(\bm{y},\bm{z}))).

We can now start with applying Cauchy-Schwarz and Condition

L
2

to find that

\displaystyle|S(\alpha)|^{2}

\displaystyle\ll(A(X)^{2}X^{-1}L^{c})^{n}\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{y}\leqslant X}\left|\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{z}\leqslant X}a_{\bm{z}}e(\alpha f(\bm{y},\bm{z}))\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=(A(X)^{2}X^{-1}L^{c})^{n}\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{y}\leqslant X}\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{z}\leqslant X}\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{z}^{\prime}\leqslant X}a_{\bm{z}}a_{\bm{z}^{\prime}}e(\alpha(f(\bm{y},\bm{z}^{\prime})-f(\bm{y},\bm{z}))).

We define
\bm{v}
such that
\bm{z}^{\prime}=\bm{z}+\bm{v}
and note that

f(\bm{y},\bm{z}+\bm{v})-f(\bm{y},\bm{z})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}g_{j}(\bm{v})+q(\bm{z}+\bm{v})-q(\bm{z}).

Since
q(\bm{z})
and
q(\bm{z}+\bm{v})
do not depend on
\bm{y}
and the absolute value of
e(\alpha(q(\bm{z}+\bm{v})-q(\bm{z}))
equals
1
we find that

|S(\alpha)|^{2}\ll(A(X)^{2}X^{-1}L^{c})^{n}\sum_{|\bm{v}|\leqslant X}\sum_{\bm{z}}a_{\bm{z}}a_{\bm{z}+\bm{v}}\left|\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{y}\leqslant X}e\left(\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}g_{j}(\bm{v})\right)\right|,

where the sum over
\bm{z}
is taken such that both
\bm{z}
and
\bm{z}+\bm{v}
lie in the range
[0,X]
. Note that
\sum_{\bm{z}}a_{\bm{z}}a_{\bm{z}+\bm{v}}\leqslant\sum_{\bm{z}}a_{\bm{z}}^{2}
and recall the standard inequality

\sum_{0\leqslant y\leqslant X}e(\alpha yg)\ll\min\left(X,\frac{1}{\|\alpha g\|}\right).

Applying Condition

L
2

a second time we get

|S(\alpha)|^{2}\ll(A(X)^{2}X^{-1}L^{c})^{s}\sum_{|\bm{v}|\leqslant X}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\min\left(X,\frac{1}{\|\alpha g_{j}(\bm{v})\|}\right).

Analogously we can show that

|S(\alpha)|^{2}\ll(A(X)^{2}X^{-1}L^{c})^{s}\sum_{|\bm{v}|\leqslant X}\prod^{s-n}_{j=1}\min\left(X,\frac{1}{\|\alpha h_{j}(\bm{v})\|}\right).\qed

Lemma 3.4
.

Write
\bm{x}=(\bm{y},\bm{z})
as before and define
H_{j}(\bm{x}):=g_{j}(\bm{y})
for all

Lemma 3.6

4.
Treatment of the major arcs

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant x\leqslant X\\

Substituting this into the integral of (

\displaystyle h;u)\text{d}e(\lambda g(u))

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant\bm{x}\leqslant X\\

5.
Interpretation of the singular series

In this section we relate the singular series to the counting problem. Our goal is to show that we can interpret it as a product of local densities outside of infinity. For this we first show that the completed singular series can be written as an Euler product. We then recall the definition of weighted
p
-adic densities and show that each factor in the Euler product is actually a weighted
p
-adic density.

We define the exponential sum including the shift by
t
by

S_{t}(q,a):=\sum_{\bm{h}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})}\kappa(q,\bm{h})e\left(\frac{a}{q}(f(\bm{h})-t)\right),

where
\bm{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{s})
. Then we have that

B(q)=\mathop{{\sum}^{\ast}}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})\end{subarray}}S_{t}(q,a).

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume Condition
C
. Then the function
B(q)
is multiplicative.

Proof.

To show the multiplicativity of
B(q)
it suffices to show that for any coprime pair of natural numbers
(q,q^{\prime})
and any integers
a,a^{\prime}
we have

(5.1)

S_{t}(q,a)S_{t}(q^{\prime},a^{\prime})=S_{t}(qq^{\prime},qa^{\prime}+q^{\prime}a).

We start by working on the right hand side

S_{t}(qq^{\prime},qa^{\prime}+q^{\prime}a)=\sum_{\bm{h}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{qq^{\prime}})}\kappa(qq^{\prime},\bm{h})e\left(\frac{qa^{\prime}+q^{\prime}a}{qq^{\prime}}(f(\bm{h})-t)\right).

Writing
\bm{h}=q^{\prime}\bm{h}_{1}+q\bm{h}_{2}
we can replace the sum over
\bm{h}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{qq^{\prime}})
by two sums over
\bm{h}_{1}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})
and
\bm{h}_{2}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q^{\prime}})
and obtain

\sum_{\bm{h}_{1}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})}\sum_{\bm{h}_{2}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q^{\prime}})}\kappa(qq^{\prime},q^{\prime}\bm{h}_{1}+q\bm{h}_{2})e\left(\frac{qa^{\prime}+q^{\prime}a}{qq^{\prime}}(f(q^{\prime}\bm{h}_{1}+q\bm{h}_{2})-t)\right).

The multiplicativity of
\kappa
is given by Condition
C
. It hence matches the contribution of
\kappa
on the left hand side. It is left to show that the two exponential sums are equal.
Considering the contribution from the left hand side of (
5.1
), note that we can replace the summation over
\bm{h}_{1}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})
by
q^{\prime}\bm{h}_{1}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})
and the summation over
\bm{h}_{2}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q^{\prime}})
by
q\bm{h}_{2}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q^{\prime}})
since
(q,q^{\prime})=1
.
Therefore it suffices to show that

e\left(\frac{q^{\prime}a(f(q^{\prime}\bm{h}_{1})-t)+qa^{\prime}(f(q\bm{h}_{2})-t)}{qq^{\prime}}\right)=e\left(\frac{(qa^{\prime}+q^{\prime}a)(f(q^{\prime}\bm{h}_{1}+q\bm{h}_{2})-t)}{qq^{\prime}}\right).

This can be done by considering each monomial of
f
separately.
∎

We formally set

(5.2)

\tilde{\chi}_{p}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}B(p^{k})

and, using the multiplicativity of

rewrite the completed singular series defined in (

) as an Euler product

(5.3)

\mathfrak{S}=\prod_{p}\tilde{\chi}_{p}(\mathcal{A}).

Our goal is to show that this definition of
\tilde{\chi}_{p}(\mathcal{A})
matches the weighted
p
-adic density of solutions to
f(\bm{x})=t
. To help with the intuition of weighted
p
-adic densities, we first recall the classical definition.

Definition 5.2

(classical
p
-adic density)
.

Let
Q
be a subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{s}
. Consider the solutions to
r

s
variables:

V:=\{\bm{x}\in Q\mid\ f_{1}(\bm{x})=t_{1},\ldots,f_{r}(\bm{x})=t_{r}\}.

Let

V(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z}):=\{\bm{x}\in Q/p^{m}\mathbb{Z}\mid\ f_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv t_{1},\ldots,f_{r}(\bm{x})\equiv t_{r}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})\}.

Denote the number of solutions modulo
p^{m}
by
v_{m}(p)
and the number of inputs modulo
p^{m}
by
q_{m}(p)
,

\displaystyle v_{m}(p)

\displaystyle:=|V(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})|

\displaystyle q_{m}(p)

\displaystyle:=|Q\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})|.

Note that there are
p^{mr}
possible choices for
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})
.
We then define the density of
V
with respect to
Q
modulo
p^{m}
by

\mu(p,m)=\frac{p^{mr}}{q_{m}(p)}v_{m}(p).

The
p
-adic density is defined as

\chi_{p}(V):=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\mu(p,m).

In analogy to this we can now incorporate the weights.

Definition 5.3

(weighted
p
-adic density)
.

Let
Q
be a subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{s}
. Consider the solutions to
r
polynomial equations in
s
variables:

V:=\{\bm{x}\in Q\mid\ f_{1}(\bm{x})=t_{1},\ldots,f_{r}(\bm{x})=t_{r}\}.

Let there be weights for
x\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})
given by
\kappa(p^{m},x)
.
Denote the weighted number of solutions modulo
p^{m}
by
v_{\kappa,m}(p)
and the number of weighted inputs modulo
p^{m}
by
q_{\kappa,m}(p)
,

\displaystyle v_{\kappa,m}(p)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\bm{x}\in V(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})}\kappa(p^{m},\bm{x})

\displaystyle q_{\kappa,m}(p)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\bm{x}\in Q\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})}\kappa(p^{m},\bm{x}).

Note that there are still
p^{mr}
possible choices for
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})
.
We define the weighted density of
V
with respect to
Q
modulo
p^{m}
by

\mu_{\kappa}(p,m)=\frac{p^{mr}}{q_{\kappa,m}(p)}v_{\kappa,m}(p).

We define the weighted
p
-adic density as

\chi_{\kappa,p}(V):=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{\kappa}(p,m).

With this definition in mind we compute
\chi_{\kappa,p}(V)
in our case. Observe that for us

and hence the number of possible outcomes is
p^{m}
. Note furthermore that
Q=\mathbb{Z}^{s}
and hence the size of

is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{x}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})}\kappa(p^{m},\bm{x})=1,

since for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant s
we have
\sum_{x_{i}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})}\kappa(p^{m},x_{i})=1
.
It is left to determine the size of
V(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})
.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
M(p^{m})
be the weighted count of solutions to our original counting problem modulo
{p^{m}}
, i.e. the
\bm{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{m}\mathbb{Z})^{s}
such that

weighted by
\kappa(p^{m},\bm{x})
. As a formula,

M(p^{m})=\sum_{f(\bm{x})\equiv t\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})}\kappa(p^{m},\bm{x}).

Hence the weighted
p
-adic density is in our case given by

(5.4)

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}p^{m}M(p^{m}).

To conclude this section we show that (
5.4
) matches our definition of
\tilde{\chi_{p}}(\mathcal{A})
in (

).

Lemma 5.5
.

The weighted

-adic density equals
\tilde{\chi}_{p}(\mathcal{A})
, i.e.

\tilde{\chi}_{p}(\mathcal{A})=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}p^{m}M(p^{m}).

Proof.

From

\sum_{a\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})}e\left(\frac{a}{p^{m}}x\right)=\begin{cases}p^{m}&\text{if }x\equiv 0\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}

we obtain by definition of
M(p^{m})
and

that

p^{m}M(p^{m})=\sum_{a\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})}S_{t}(p^{m},a).

We now group the numbers modulo
p^{m}
by their greatest common divisor with
p^{m}
.

p^{m}M(p^{m})=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\mathop{{\sum}^{\ast}}\limits_{a\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{k}})}S_{t}(p^{m},p^{m-k}a).

Now using that

\kappa(q,h)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{qq^{\prime}})\\
b\equiv h\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{q})\end{subarray}}\kappa(qq^{\prime},b)

we have

\displaystyle S_{t}(p^{m},p^{m-k}a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{h}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})}\kappa(p^{m},\bm{h})e\left(\frac{ap^{m-k}}{p^{m}}(f(\bm{h})-t)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{h}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{k}})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{b}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{m}})\\
\bm{b}\equiv\bm{h}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{k}})\end{subarray}}\kappa(p^{m},\bm{b})e\left(\frac{a}{p^{k}}(f(\bm{h})-t)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{h}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{k}})}\kappa(p^{k},\bm{h})e\left(\frac{a}{p^{k}}(f(\bm{h})-t)\right)

\displaystyle=S_{t}(p^{k},a).

Hence we obtain

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}p^{m}M(p^{m})=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\mathop{{\sum}^{\ast}}\limits_{a\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p^{k}})}S_{t}(p^{k},a)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}B(p^{k})=\tilde{\chi}_{p}(\mathcal{A}).\qed

This allows us to conclude that our singular series is indeed the product of the local densities outside of infinity.

Remark 5.6
.

In general it is not clear that this density is positive. We might need extra conditions, like for example Condition
B
in Section
8
or the no bad primes condition in
[
Liu11
]
.

6.
Interpretation of the singular integral

Recall that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}(X)=\int_{-1/Q}^{1/Q}I(\lambda,X)e(-\lambda t)\text{d}\lambda=\int_{-1/Q}^{1/Q}\int_{[0,X]^{s}}\psi(\bm{u})e(\lambda f(\bm{u}))e(-\lambda t)\text{d}\bm{u}\text{d}\lambda.

We do a change of variables
\bm{u}\rightarrow X\bm{u},\lambda=X^{-2}\gamma
and
t_{0}=X^{-2}t
to reduce the dependency on
X
of the singular integral. Using that
f(X\bm{u})=X^{2}f(\bm{u})
and
X^{2}/Q=P=L^{B}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}(X)=

\displaystyle\int_{-P}^{P}\int_{[0,X]^{s}}\psi(X\bm{u})e(X^{-2}\gamma f(X\bm{u}))e(-X^{-2}\gamma X^{2}t_{0})X^{s}X^{-2}\text{d}\bm{u}\text{d}\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\penalty\ \frac{\Psi(X)^{s}}{X^{2}}\int_{-P}^{P}\frac{X^{s}}{\Psi(X)^{s}}\int_{[0,1]^{s}}\psi(X\bm{u})e(\gamma f(\bm{u}))e(-\gamma t_{0})\text{d}\bm{u}\text{d}\gamma.

We would like to show three things. First, that integrating
\gamma
over
(-\infty,\infty)
instead of
(-P,P)
gives an admissible error. Second, that this new integral grows asymptotically like
\frac{\Psi(X)^{s}}{X^{s}}
. Finally, that the constant in front of this asymptotic has an interpretation as the local density of solutions over the reals. This will be done in the next three subsections respectively. However, this only works if for
\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{s}
the equation
f(\bm{x})=t_{0}
has a solution and not too much curvature. To slightly generalize our results we consider the integral
\mathfrak{I}(X)
over some box
\mathfrak{B}=\prod_{i=1}^{s}[y_{i},z_{i}]\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}
instead of
[0,1]^{s}
. Hence, we define

(6.1)

\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X):=X^{s-2}\int_{-P}^{P}\int_{\mathfrak{B}}\psi(X\bm{u})e(\gamma f(\bm{u}))e(-\gamma t_{0})\text{d}\bm{u}\text{d}\gamma.

Furthermore, let

\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X):=\int_{\mathfrak{B}}\psi(X\bm{u})e(\gamma f(\bm{u}))d\bm{u}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{J}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B}):=\int_{\mathfrak{B}}e(\gamma f(\bm{u}))d\bm{u}.

We get that

\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)=X^{s-2}\int_{-P}^{P}\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)e(-\gamma t_{0})d\gamma.

The main consequence of changing to the box
\mathfrak{B}
is that the weighted solutions we are now counting are those inside of
X\mathfrak{B}
, rather than
[0,X]^{s}
.

Completing the singular integral

In this part we complete the integral by first bounding
\mathcal{J}
and then comparing
\frac{X^{s}}{\Psi(X)^{s}}\mathcal{I}
with
\mathcal{J}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and assume that Condition

L
1

holds, then

\mathcal{J}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B})\ll\min(\textup{Meas}(\mathfrak{B}),|\gamma|^{-2-\varepsilon}).

Proof.

It is immediate that
\mathcal{J}
is upper bounded by
\textup{Meas}(\mathfrak{B})
by taking the absolute value within the integral. For the second upper bound we consider the sequence
b_{x}=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{N}
. This sequence meets Condition

L
2

with
c=0
and therefore we can apply Lemma
3.1
on
T(\alpha):=\sum_{0\leqslant\bm{x}\leqslant X}b_{x}e(\alpha f(\bm{x}))
to see that

T(\lambda)\ll X^{s}L^{\frac{s}{4}}\left(\frac{1}{X}+\frac{1}{1+|\gamma|}+\frac{1+|\gamma|}{X^{2}}\right)^{\frac{r}{2}},

where we have taken
a=0,q=1
and
\gamma=X^{2}\lambda
and, because
c=0
, we know
C\leqslant 1/4
. Furthermore, the function
X
is a smooth approximation for the sequence
b_{x}
and it has an error of
O(1)
. Therefore, by a similar argument as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we have that

T(\lambda)=X^{s}\mathcal{J}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B})+O(\gamma X^{s}L^{-m^{\prime}}),

where we may take
m^{\prime}
as big as we like. For our purposes any
m^{\prime}>\frac{3}{2}s
will suffice, as we will see later on.
Combining the two facts about
T(\lambda)
we find that

\mathcal{J}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B})\ll|\gamma|L^{-m^{\prime}}+L^{\frac{s}{4}}\left(\frac{1}{X}+\frac{1}{1+|\gamma|}+\frac{1+|\gamma|}{X^{2}}\right)^{\frac{r}{2}}.

Since the left hand side does not depend on
X
, we may choose
X
depending on
\gamma
. Let
X
be so that
|\gamma|=L^{D}
where
D>0
is some constant such that
\frac{s}{4}-(\frac{r}{2}-2)D<0
and
m^{\prime}>3D
. We find that

\mathcal{J}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B})\ll L^{-2D-\varepsilon}=|\gamma|^{-2-\varepsilon}.

Note that in particular such a
D
always exists if
m^{\prime}>\frac{3s}{2r-8}
. This holds whenever
m^{\prime}>\frac{3}{2}s
by Condition

L
1

.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose that
\Psi(X)
is the smooth approximation of
A(X)
and that Condition

L
1

holds. Then

\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)=\frac{\Psi(X)^{s}}{X^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{X^{s}}{\Psi(X)^{s}}\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)e(-\gamma t_{0})d\gamma+O\left(\frac{\Psi(X)^{s}}{X^{2}L^{B}}\right).

Proof.

Recall that

\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)=\int_{y_{1}}^{z_{1}}\psi(Xu_{1})\cdots\int_{y_{s}}^{z_{s}}\psi(Xu_{s})e(\gamma f(\bm{u}))d\bm{u}.

Define

\Phi_{1}(\gamma,u_{1}):=\int_{y_{1}}^{u_{1}}\int_{y_{2}}^{z_{2}}\psi(Xu_{2})\cdots\int_{y_{s}}^{z_{s}}\psi(Xu_{s})e(\gamma f(w_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{s}))du_{s}\cdots du_{2}dw_{1}.

Then, by partial integration in the variable
u_{1}
, we find

\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)=\left[\psi(Xu_{1})\Phi_{1}(\gamma,u_{1})\right]^{z_{1}}_{y_{1}}-\int_{y_{1}}^{z_{1}}X\psi^{\prime}(Xu_{1})\Phi_{1}(\gamma,u_{1})du_{1}.

Thus,

\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)\ll\sup_{u_{1}\in(y_{1},z_{1})}|\Phi_{1}(\gamma,u_{1})|\left(|\psi(Xz_{1})|+|\psi(Xy_{1})|+\int_{y_{1}}^{z_{1}}|X\psi^{\prime}(Xu_{1})|du_{1}\right).

By the properties of Definition
2.1
and by Lemma
2.3
each term within the brackets is asymptotically bounded by
\Psi(X)X^{-1}
and we have

\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)\ll\sup_{u_{1}\in(y_{1},z_{1})}|\Phi_{1}(\gamma,u_{1})|\Psi(X)X^{-1}.

Let
1\leqslant i\leqslant s
, write
\bm{v}_{i}
for
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{i-1},w_{i},u_{i+1},\ldots,u_{s})
and define

\Phi_{i}(\gamma,u_{1},\ldots,u_{i}):=\int_{y_{i}}^{u_{i}}\int_{y_{i+1}}^{z_{i+1}}\psi(Xu_{i+1})\cdots\int_{y_{s}}^{z_{s}}\psi(Xu_{s})e(\gamma f(\bm{v}_{i}))du_{s}\cdots du_{i+1}dw_{i}.

In the following we will write
\Phi_{i}
to mean
\Phi_{i}(\gamma,u_{1},\ldots,u_{i})
.
Let
2\leqslant i\leqslant s
then, by partial integration of the integral with respect to
u_{i}
, we get

\displaystyle\Phi_{i-1}=\int_{y_{i-1}}^{u_{i-1}}\left(\left[\psi(Xu_{i})\Phi_{i}\right]_{u_{i}=y_{i}}^{z_{i}}-\int_{y_{i}}^{z_{i}}X\psi^{\prime}(Xu_{i})\Phi_{i}du_{i}\right)dw_{i-1}.

This shows that

\Phi_{i-1}\ll\int_{y_{i-1}}^{u_{i-1}}\sup_{u_{i}\in(y_{i},z_{i})}|\Phi_{i}|\left(|\psi(Xz_{i})|+|\psi(Xy_{i})|+\int_{y_{i}}^{z_{i}}|X\psi^{\prime}(Xu_{i})|du_{i}\right)dw_{i-1},

which by the same argument as above gives us

\Phi_{i-1}\ll\int_{y_{i-1}}^{u_{i-1}}\sup_{u_{i}\in(y_{i},z_{i})}|\Phi_{i}|\Psi(X)X^{-1}dw_{i-1}.

By induction we then find that for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant s-1
we have

\sup_{u_{1}\in(y_{1},z_{1})}|\Phi_{1}|\ll\Psi(X)^{i}X^{-i}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{j}\in(y_{j},z_{j})\\
\textup{for }1\leqslant j\leqslant i\end{subarray}}|\Phi_{i+1}|.

Combining this with our estimate for
\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)
from above, we get

\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)\ll\sup_{\bm{u}\in\mathfrak{B}}|\Phi(\gamma,\bm{u})|\Psi(X)^{s}X^{-s},

where

\Phi(\gamma,\bm{u})=\int_{y_{1}}^{u_{1}}\cdots\int_{y_{s}}^{u_{s}}e(\gamma f(\bm{t}))dw_{s}\cdots dw_{1}.

Lemma
6.1
showed that
\Phi(\gamma,\bm{u})\ll\min(1,|\gamma|^{-2-\varepsilon})
, from which we deduce that

\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)\ll\Psi(X)^{s}X^{-s}|\gamma|^{-2-\varepsilon}.

Hence,

\int_{P}^{\infty}X^{s}\Psi(X)^{-s}\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)d\gamma\ll\int_{P}^{\infty}|\gamma|^{-2-\varepsilon}d\gamma\ll P^{-1}

and the same holds for the integral between
-\infty
and
-P
. This shows that

\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)=\frac{\Psi(X)^{s}}{X^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{X^{s}}{\Psi(X)^{s}}\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)e(-\gamma t_{0})d\gamma+O\left(\frac{\Psi(X)^{s}}{X^{2}P}\right).\qed

Bounds for the singular integral

Now that we have completed the singular integral we will show that it has the expected asymptotic upper and lower bounds of the form
\frac{\Psi(X)^{s}}{X^{2}}
. To do so we study the following object

\tilde{\mathfrak{I}}_{\mathfrak{B}}(Y,X)=\int_{-Y}^{Y}\mathcal{I}(\gamma,\mathfrak{B},X)e(-\gamma t_{0})d\gamma

for a suitable box
\mathfrak{B}
. In fact, our choice for
\mathfrak{B}
will depend slightly on the height
X
, because it will depend on
t_{0}=\frac{t}{X^{2}}
. The following lemma implies that counting the weighted solutions within the box
X\mathfrak{B}
is lower bounded and upper bounded by the total of weighted solutions in the boxes
X\mathfrak{C}_{1}
and
X\mathfrak{C}_{2}
respectively, where
\mathfrak{C}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{C}_{2}
do not depend on
X
. Since we assume
A(X)
to be smoothly approximable, we know that asymptotically the weighted measure of the three boxes is the same.

Lemma 6.3
.

Assume that
f(\bm{x})=0
has a solution in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}
. Whenever
\eta>0
is sufficiently small there exist boxes
\mathfrak{C}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{C}_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}
, such that for all sufficiently large
X
there is a solution
\bm{x}^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}
to
f(\bm{x})=t_{0}
for which

\mathfrak{C}_{1}\subset\bm{x}^{\ast}+[-\eta,\eta]^{s}\subset\mathfrak{C}_{2}
.

Proof.

Write
\bm{x}_{0}
for the positive real solution to
f(\bm{x})=0
, that we have by our assumptions. Since
f
is non-singular we know that whenever
\bm{x}
is sufficiently close to
\bm{x}_{0}
we have

f(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(\bm{x}_{0})(x_{i}-x_{0,i})+O(G_{2}),

where
G_{2}
is a degree
2
form in
(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})
and we may assume without loss of generality that
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}(\bm{x}_{0})\neq 0.
Hence, for any
\delta>0
we can take
t_{0}
sufficiently small such that there exists a solution
\bm{x}^{\ast}
to
f(\bm{x}^{\ast})=t_{0}
and
|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}^{\ast}|<\delta
. Suppose that
\eta>0
is such that
\bm{x}_{0}+[-2\eta,2\eta]^{s}\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{s}
. Taking
\delta=\eta/2
gives us, that whenever
X
is sufficiently large, there exists a solution
\bm{x}^{\ast}
to
f(\bm{x}^{\ast})=t_{0}
for which
|\bm{x}^{\ast}-\bm{x}|<\eta/2
. We define

\mathfrak{C}_{1}:=\bm{x}_{0}+[-\eta/2,\eta/2]^{s},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathfrak{C}_{2}:=\bm{x}_{0}+[-2\eta,2\eta]\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \textup{ and }\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathfrak{B}:=\bm{x}^{\ast}+[-\eta,\eta]^{s}.

We will prove that
\mathfrak{C}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{B}\subset\mathfrak{C}_{2}
. Let
\bm{y}_{1}\in\mathfrak{C}_{1}
and
\bm{y}_{2}\in\mathfrak{B}
. We find

|\bm{x}^{\ast}-\bm{y}_{1}|\leqslant|\bm{x}^{\ast}-\bm{x}_{0}|+|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{y}_{1}|\leqslant\eta/2+\eta/2=\eta

and

|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{y}_{2}|\leqslant|\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}^{\ast}|+|\bm{x}^{\ast}-\bm{y}_{2}|\leqslant\eta/2+\eta<2\eta.

Thus,
\bm{y}_{1}\in\mathfrak{B}
and
\bm{y}_{2}\in\mathfrak{C}_{2}
and we are done.
∎

Definition 6.4
.

Let
\bm{x}^{\ast}
and
\eta>0
be as in Lemma
6.3
. We define
\mathfrak{B}:=\bm{x}^{\ast}+[-\eta,\eta]^{s}
.

Proposition 6.5
.

Suppose that
A(X)
is smoothly approximable and that
f(\bm{x})=0
has a solution in
\mathbb{R}^{s}_{>0}
. Let
X
be fixed and define
\mathfrak{B}
as above. Then

I_{0}(X):=\lim_{Y\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\mathfrak{I}}_{\mathfrak{B}}(Y,X)\asymp X^{-s}A(X)^{s}.

Proof.

We start with the expression for
\tilde{\mathfrak{I}}_{\mathfrak{B}}(Y,X)
above and integrate over
\gamma
to find that

\tilde{\mathfrak{I}}_{\mathfrak{B}}(Y,X)=\int_{\mathfrak{B}}\frac{\sin(2\pi Y(f(\bm{u})-t_{0}))}{\pi(f(\bm{u})-t_{0})}\psi(X\bm{u})d\bm{u}.

Our first objective will be to rewrite our integral such that we can use Fourier’s Theorem, see Section 9.7
[
WW21
]
, which states

\lim_{Y\rightarrow\infty}\int_{[a,b]}\frac{\sin(2\pi Yw)}{\pi w}K(w)=K(0),

whenever
K(w)
is a real function on
[a,b]
of bounded variation.

Write
\bm{x}^{\ast}=(x_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,x_{s}^{\ast})
for the non-singular positive real solution to the equation
f(\bm{x})=t_{0}
, which we know exists by the proof of Lemma
6.3
. Then we know that

d_{j}:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}(\bm{x}^{\ast})\neq 0\text{ for some }1\leqslant j\leqslant s.

Without loss of generality we assume that
j=1
and
d_{1}=1
.

We do a change of variables to
\bm{v}=\bm{u}-\bm{x}^{\ast}
such that our domain of integration changes to
[-\eta,\eta]^{s}
:

\tilde{\mathfrak{I}}_{\mathfrak{B}}(Y,X)=\int_{[-\eta,\eta]^{s}}\frac{\sin(2\pi Y(f(\bm{x}^{\ast}+\bm{v})-t_{0}))}{\pi(f(\bm{x}^{\ast}+\bm{v})-t_{0})}\psi(X(\bm{x}^{\ast}+\bm{v}))d\bm{v}.

To be able to replace
f(\bm{x}^{\ast}+\bm{v})
by something linear, we consider the Taylor expansion of
f
near
\bm{x}^{\ast}
:

f(\bm{x}^{\ast}+\bm{v})=v_{1}+d_{2}v_{2}+\ldots+d_{s}v_{s}+G_{2}(\bm{v}),

where
G_{2}(\bm{v})
is a form of degree
2
. We define
w=f(\bm{x}^{\ast}+\bm{v})-t_{0}
and will do a change of variables from
v_{1}
to
w
. By inverting the previous relation, we have that

v_{1}=t_{0}+w-d_{2}v_{2}-\ldots-d_{s}v_{s}+G(w,v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}),

where
G
is a multiple power series starting with terms of degree at least
2
. Hence, our Jacobian equals

\frac{\partial v_{1}}{\partial w}=1+G_{1}(w,v_{2},\ldots,v_{s})

for some multiple power series with terms of degree at least
1
. We will write
G_{1}
instead of
G_{1}(w,v_{2},\ldots,v_{s})
to keep our notation uncluttered. Since we have taken
\eta
to be sufficiently small, we know that there exists some positive
\vartheta=\vartheta(\eta)
such that whenever
v_{1},\ldots,v_{s}
are in
[-\eta,\eta]
we have that
|w|<\vartheta(\eta)
and that
|G_{1}|<\frac{1}{2}
. Whence,

\tilde{\mathfrak{I}}_{\mathfrak{B}}(Y,X)=\int_{w\in[-\vartheta,\vartheta]}\frac{\sin(2\pi wY)}{\pi w}K(w)dw,

where

\displaystyle K(w)=\int_{[-\eta,\eta]^{s-1}}(1+

\displaystyle G_{1})\psi(X(x_{2}^{\ast}+v_{2}))\psi(X(x_{3}^{\ast}+v_{3}))\cdots

\displaystyle\psi(X(x_{s}^{\ast}+v_{s}))\psi(X(x_{1}^{\ast}+w-d_{2}v_{2}-\ldots-d_{s}v_{s}+\varepsilon))dv_{2}\cdots dv_{s},

and
\varepsilon
comes from
G
. The function
K(w)
has bounded variation, because
|\psi(X)|\geqslant c_{1}A(X)X^{-1}
for some
c_{1}>0
which shows that
\psi(X)
cannot equal
0
for any
X
and thus cannot change sign. We can now apply Fourier’s Theorem from which we conclude that
I_{0}=K(0)
. Therefore it suffices to show that
K(0)\asymp A(X)^{s}X^{-s}.

Because
\frac{1}{2}<1+G_{1}<\frac{3}{2}
for all
v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}
we may ignore this factor. For the remaining factors we use the mean value theorem to find
c_{2},\ldots,c_{s}\in[-\eta,\eta]
such that

K(0)=(2\eta)^{2s}\psi(X(x_{1}^{\ast}-d_{2}c_{2}-\ldots-d_{s}c_{s}+\varepsilon))\prod_{i=2}^{s}\psi(X(x_{i}^{\ast}+c_{i})).

Write
c_{1}=-d_{2}c_{2}-\ldots-d_{s}c_{s}+\varepsilon
. We find that
\bm{x}^{\ast}+\bm{c}\in\mathfrak{C}_{2}
and therefore, using the properties of being smoothly approximable and Lemma
2.3
, that

I_{0}\asymp\frac{\Psi(X(\bm{x}^{\ast}+\bm{c}))}{X(\bm{x}^{\ast}+\bm{c})}\asymp\frac{\Psi(X)^{s}}{X^{s}}\asymp\frac{A(X)^{s}}{X^{s}}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Volume interpretation of the singular integral

By Proposition
6.5
we can write
I_{0}(X)
as
\sigma_{\infty}A(X)^{s}X^{-s}
, where
\sigma_{\infty}>0
is depending on
X
but can be lower and upper bounded independent of
X
. Combining this with Lemma
6.2
yields that the main term of
\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)
equals
\sigma_{\infty}A(X)^{s}X^{-2}.
We will show that this
\sigma_{\infty}
can be interpreted as the local density of real solutions to
f(\bm{x})=t_{0}
inside of
\mathfrak{B}
. This is done in a similar way as in Section 5 in
[
BB23
]
.

First, we change the measure to the
\mathcal{A}
-normalized measure

d\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(z)=\frac{X}{\Psi(X)}\psi(Xz)dz.

We denote
d\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{u})=\prod_{i=1}^{s}d\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(u_{i})
.
Note that this is a probability measure for all
X
, since
\mu_{\mathcal{A}}([0,1])=1
.
For the singular integral we obtain

\tilde{\mathfrak{I}}_{\mathfrak{B}}(Y,X)=\frac{\Psi(X)^{s}}{X^{s}}\int_{-Y}^{Y}\left(\int_{\mathfrak{B}}e(\gamma(f(\bm{u})-t_{0}))d\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{u})\right)d\gamma.

Let

\displaystyle V_{\mathcal{A}}(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{B}}\mathds{1}_{[f(\bm{u})-t_{0}=h]}d\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\vol\{\bm{u}\in\mathfrak{B}:f(\bm{u})-t_{0}=h\}.

Here
\vol
denotes the volume with respect to the measure
\mu_{\mathcal{A}}
. Note that
V_{\mathcal{A}}(0)
counts the
\mu_{\mathcal{A}}
-weighted solutions to
f(\bm{u})=t_{0}
. We now explain how to relate this to our singular integral. For this we consider the inverse Fourier transform of
V_{\mathcal{A}}
at
\gamma
:

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}V_{\mathcal{A}}\right)(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{\mathcal{A}}(h)e(\gamma h)dh

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathfrak{B}}\mathds{1}_{[f(\bm{u})-t_{0}=h]}d\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{u})e(\gamma h)dh

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{B}}e(\gamma(f(\bm{u})-t_{0}))d\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{u}).

Note that this is exactly the inner integral of
\tilde{\mathfrak{I}}_{\mathfrak{B}}(Y,X)
. Hence, now taking the forward Fourier transform on both sides and formally evaluating at 0, we obtain

\displaystyle V_{\mathcal{A}}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathfrak{B}}e(\gamma(f(\bm{u})-t_{0}))d\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{u})d\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{X^{s}}{A(X)^{s}}I_{0}(X)=\sigma_{\infty}.

This shows that we may interpret
\sigma_{\infty}
as the volume of solutions to
f(\bm{x})=t_{0}
for
\bm{x}\in\mathfrak{B}
, where we have weighted the volume with
\mu_{\mathcal{A}}
. By smooth approximability we have
d\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(z)\asymp 1
for all
z\in\mathfrak{B}
. This additionally shows that
\sigma_{\infty}
is asymptotically lower and upper bounded by the non-weighted volume of solutions to
f(\bm{x})=t_{0}
for
\bm{x}\in\mathfrak{B}
.

Remark 6.6
.

As
X\rightarrow\infty
we have that
t_{0}
goes to
0
. Hence the box
\mathfrak{B}
, as defined in the proof of Lemma
6.3
, converges to
\bm{x}_{0}+[-\eta,\eta]^{s}
. From the above we can then conclude that
\sigma_{\infty}
converges to the weighted volume of solutions to
f(\bm{x})=0
inside of
\bm{x}_{0}+[-\eta,\eta]^{s}
.

Proof of the main theorems

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

It follows from Equations (
1.1
), (
4.1
), (
4.4
) and Lemma
3.6
that

R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A})=\mathfrak{S}(P)\mathfrak{I}(X)+O(A(X)^{s}X^{-2}L^{-K}).

By Equation (
4.6
) we may complete the singular series to get

R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A})=\mathfrak{S}\mathfrak{I}(X)+O(A(X)^{s}X^{-2}L^{-K}+\mathfrak{I}(X)L^{-\frac{B}{2}}).

Here
\mathfrak{S}
is the same constant as in Equation (
4.5
) which by Equation (
5.3
) and Lemma
5.5
can indeed be interpreted as the product of local densities over the
p
-adics for all the primes
p
. Taking
B\geqslant 2K
completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

The proof that

R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{B})=\mathfrak{S}\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)+O(A(X)^{s}X^{-2}L^{-K}+\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)L^{-\frac{B}{2}})

goes analogous to the proof above. It remains to show the results about
\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)
.
By Lemma
6.2
we may also complete this singular integral at the cost of
O(A(X)^{s}X^{-2}L^{-B})
.
Since
B\geqslant 2K
we find

R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{B})=\mathfrak{S}I_{0}+O\left({A(X)^{s}}{X^{-2}}L^{-K}+I_{0}L^{-K}\right),

and by Proposition
6.5
we know that

I_{0}\asymp A(X)^{s}X^{-2}.

Finally, the interpretation of

\frac{X^{s}}{A(X)^{s}}\mathfrak{I}_{\mathfrak{B}}(X)

as the local density over the reals follows from the last subsection of Section
6
.
∎

Remark 7.1
.

We only use Condition
C
to interpret the constant
\mathfrak{S}
. Hence, it would also be possible to state a theorem without this condition that would give the same asymptotic formulae but without the local density interpretation.

\mathcal{A}

1.6
in action. We do not claim that the theorems below are in any sense optimal, since, by using the specific arithmetic structure of the sequences, one would expect to be able to get better error terms. In both cases we consider a non-singular quadratic form

s

For our first example we choose the weights to be the indicator function of primes. We show this example because it gives a non-trivial case of smooth approximability.

For the second example we consider the indicator function of the
k
-free numbers. This example shows that our conditions on the sequence
\mathcal{A}
are reasonable and gives some insight on the information about
\mathcal{A}
that is needed.

Note that the case of squarefree numbers has already been investigated by Baker in
[
Bak06
]
and
[
Bak10
]

k
-free numbers.

Primes

If the weights
a_{x}
are the indicator function of primes the prime number theorem gives us

A(X)=\sum_{x\leqslant X}\mathds{1}_{\text{prime}}(x)=XL^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}k!L^{-k}+O(A(X){L^{-m}}).

From this it is easy to check that for a given
m
in Definition
2.1
the series

\Psi(X)=XL^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}k!L^{-k}
.

Furthermore we know that
\kappa

\kappa(q,a)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if $(q,a)>1$}\\
\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

Here

Conditions

,

[

]
, Lemma 7.1 for Condition

Theorem 8.1
.

Consider
f
such that Condition

L
1

is satisfied and
f(\bm{x})=0
has a solution in
\mathbb{R}^{s}_{>0}
. Fix
X
and define
\mathfrak{B}
as in Definition
6.4
.
Let
R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{B})
be the number of solutions in primes in
X\mathfrak{B}
to the equation
f(\bm{x})=t

R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{B})=\#\{\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})\in X\mathfrak{B}\mid x_{i}\text{ is prime }\forall\ 1\leqslant i\leqslant s,\ f(\bm{x})=t\}.

be any real number. Then

R_{f,t}(X,\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{B})=\mathfrak{S}\sigma_{\infty}\frac{X^{s-2}}{L^{s}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}k!L^{-k}\right)^{s}+O(X^{s-2}L^{-K}).

Here

-free numbers

Theorem 8.3
.

Consider
f

is satisfied and
f(\bm{x})=0

We can furthermore provide a condition and argument for the singular series to be lower bounded. This allows us to conclude that the main term in the above theorem dominates the error term, i.e. that the constant

For all primes

there exists a

f(\bm{x})\equiv t\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{(2\det F)^{2k-1}})\text{ and }p^{k}\nmid x_{i}\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,s.

Let

Here

is positive. The constant
\mathfrak{S}
can be interpreted as the product of
p
-adic densities and
\sigma_{\infty}
can be interpreted as the local density of real solutions to

Before we start checking the required conditions we start with the general setup.
A
k
-free number is by definition not divisible by
n^{k}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote their indicator function by

\mu_{k}(n)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if $n$ is $k$-free}\\
0&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

Note that for the squarefree numbers we have
\mu_{2}(n)=\mu(n)^{2}
.
In this example we choose our weights
to be

.
Using the asymptotics of the
k
-free numbers as for example stated in
[
Br“”u+98
]

A(X)=\sum_{x\leqslant X}\mu_{k}(x)=\frac{X}{\zeta(k)}+O(X^{\frac{1}{k}}).

We hence set our error term to be
E(X)=X^{\frac{1}{k}}
, which obviously satisfies
E(X)=O(A(X)L^{-m})
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Note furthermore that Condition

L
2

is satisfied since our weights

are an indicator function and

.

The next step is to find the asymptotics for
k
-free numbers in arithmetic progressions.
More explicitly: for Condition
D
we need to find
\kappa(q,a)
and a constant
Q_{D}=Q_{D}(X)
such that for all
q\leqslant Q_{D}(X)
and

we have

A(q,a;X)=\kappa(q,a)A(X)+O(X^{1/k}).

The expression for

can be found in
[
Br“”u+98
]

The definition given below is derived from their definition of

Let the prime factorization of

Then

\kappa(q,a)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ there exists $p$ s.t. }p^{k}\mid(q,a)\\
\frac{1}{q}\prod_{p|q}(1-\frac{1}{p^{k}})^{-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|q\\
p\nmid\frac{q}{(q,a)}\end{subarray}}(1-p^{e_{p}-k})&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

In

Remark 8.5
.

The error term
O(X^{1/k})
for
k
-free numbers in arithmetic progressions is not the best possible. It was already known to Prachar
[
Pra58
]
for the case of coprime residue class and modulus. It has since been improved, but this is irrelevant for us.

Condition
C
follows from noting that for
(q,q^{\prime})=1
we have

(qq^{\prime},qh^{\prime}+q^{\prime}h)=(q,q^{\prime}h)(q^{\prime},qh^{\prime}).

It is left to show Condition
K

c\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\kappa(q,a)\ll\frac{(\log q)^{c}}{q}
.
If
\kappa(q,a)
vanishes the statement is trivial. Note furthermore that
e_{p}-k\leqslant-1
. Hence
(1-p^{e_{p}-k})<1
for all
p
. We obtain

\displaystyle\kappa(q,a)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{q}\prod_{p|q}(1-\frac{1}{p^{k}})^{-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{q}\left(\frac{2^{k}}{2^{k}-1}\right)^{\omega(q)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{q}\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^{\omega(q)}

where
\omega(q)
counts the number of distinct primes dividing

By the Hardy-Ramanujan Theorem we know that

\omega(q)=\log\log q+O(\log\log(q)^{1/2+\varepsilon}.

Hence
\kappa(q,a)\ll\frac{\log q}{q}
and Condition

.
Having checked all the conditions we can hence conclude Theorem
