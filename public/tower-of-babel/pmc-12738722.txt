Irritability in autism examined through network analysis of phenotypic and physiological correlates
Authors: Sara Alatrash, Tithi Paul, Brendan F Andrade, Suneeta Monga, Jessica Brian, Evdokia Anagnostou, Melanie Penner, Atena Roshan Fekr, Azadeh Kushki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSymptoms of irritability are commonly reported in autism, yet correlates of this domain remain poorly understood. While prevalence estimates in the literature vary considerably, they range up to 80%. Irritability can interfere with daily functioning, social relationships, and academic performance. Despite years of research and the availability of approved medications, intervention outcomes for irritability remain highly variable. To advance understanding and inform more targeted, personalized interventions, the present study aimed to clarify the correlates of irritability in autism using network analysis, an analytical tool suited to capture complex associations across interconnected variables. We examined demographic, phenotypic, and physiological factors in a sample of autistic and neurotypical children. Our findings identified strong direct associations between irritability and externalizing behaviors, emotion dysregulation, autism features, and negative affect. Physiological responses, including heart rate reactivity and variability, were indirectly connected to irritability through links with self-regulation abilities and ADHD traits. These results highlight the importance of conceptualizing irritability in autism as part of a broader, interconnected network of influences rather than an isolated symptom. Background

Irritability can be a significant challenge for many autistic individuals, often manifesting as a reduced ability to control one’s temper resulting in frequent verbal or behavioral outbursts1. The reported prevalence of clinically significant irritability symptoms in autism varies widely, with rates ranging from 19% to 80%2–4. Appearing early and often persisting into later childhood5,6, irritability can significantly affect daily functioning and quality of life as it can disrupt social interactions and academic performance7. Medications approved to treat symptoms of irritability in autism can have mixed outcomes and significant adverse effects8, such as weight gain, sedation, extrapyramidal symptom, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease9–11. In this context, there is a pressing need to understand the demographic and phenotypic correlates and predictors of irritability to inform other intervention approaches and care.

The definition of irritability in the existing literature has been ambiguous and inconsistent and often conflated with related terms like anger and aggression1. In the context of this paper, we use the definition proposed by Snaith and Taylor, which encompasses both the emotional and behavioural dimensions of irritability: “a feeling state characterized by reduced control over temper which usually results in irascible verbal or behavioral outbursts” 1. Although irritability may trigger aggressive behaviors12, it is essential to differentiate between the two as irritability can be expressed through internal distress rather than overt aggression13.

In addition to differentiating between emotional and behavioural manifestations of irritability, it is also important to differentiate among the traits, emotion, and mood related to irritability. In particular, irritability can be viewed as a trait, indicating a stable predisposition to experience anger across various situations13, with anger considered a conscious emotion that drives behavior without awareness14. Irritability as a mood, however, represents a more diffuse, prolonged affective state characterized by a persistent tendency toward anger or annoyance, often without a specific or immediate trigger15. Moods are typically longer lasting than emotions, less tied to specific events, and can be recognized through subjective feelings and observable signs, such as facial expressions or irritable behavior13. However, the ability to distinguish among these levels is often constrained by available measurement tools. For example, a commonly used measure in autism research, the Aberrant Behavior Checklist – Irritability subscale (ABC-irritability)16, captures observable behaviors associated with irritability (e.g., aggression, self-injury, temper outbursts), but does not explicitly differentiate whether these reflect state-based, mood-based, or trait-level irritability. These measurement limitations underscore the need for approaches, such as the present study, that examine irritability within a broader multidimensional framework to clarify how different levels and correlates of irritability are expressed in autism.

The predictors of irritability in autism remain largely unknown17, although emerging evidence suggests that irritability can be associated with emotion dysregulation, sensory differences, demographic characteristics, and mental health challenges such as anxiety and depression17. The gaps in understanding these predictors are particularly notable in two areas17. First, physiological correlates of irritability, such as autonomic arousal, are critically unexplored. This is an important area of investigation as differences in autonomic arousal modulation have been linked with difficulties in domains that overlap with irritability (e.g., emotion dysregulation and anxiety18,19. Furthermore, arousal modulation may potentially provide an intervenable target for irritability. Atypical patterns of autonomic arousal have been reported in autism including autonomic hyperarousal and dampened reactivity to stressful tasks20–22. Additionally, research on the pathophysiology of irritability conceptualizes it as an aberrant response to frustration or threat19, suggesting that physiological reactivity may be a meaningful contributor to individual differences in irritability expression. To address this gap, this study examined whether autonomic arousal was associated with irritability symptoms. This approach advances our understanding of physiological mechanisms underlying irritability and provides a foundation for future investigations exploring physiology as a potential target or marker in the context of irritability.

The second literature gap in understanding predictors of irritability is the siloed study of irritability correlates. This is a challenge as many of these correlates, including demographic and phenotypic features, can be highly interconnected. For example, sex and mental health conditions are suggested to be associated with irritability; however, sex and mental health are also correlated with each other, as females are more likely to experience certain mental health challenges, such as anxiety and depression, which can, in turn, contribute to higher levels of irritability23. Similarly, sensory processing and emotion dysregulation are closely associated with irritability24. At the same time, disruptions in sensory processing can lead to heightened emotional responses, making it more difficult for individuals to regulate their emotions, thereby intensifying both emotion dysregulation and irritability25. This interconnectedness suggests that the correlates of irritability do not exist in isolation but rather influence one another in complex ways, necessitating the use of analytical approaches that can capture this complexity. To this end, the present study characterized the multidimensional association among irritability symptoms and demographic, phenotypic, and physiological factors. In particular, we employed network analysis26, an analytical approach used to examine the associations among an interconnected set of variables. Network analysis results in a visual and statistical representation of how various factors relate to each other, highlighting which variables are most central and influential within the network.

Methods

Participants

The data used for the present analysis were collected in a study funded by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) and conducted at the Bloorview Research Institute. The study was approved by the Clinical Trials Ontario-Qualified Research Ethics Board through Holland Bloorview Kids Rehabilitation Hospital, and was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was provided by participants deemed to have a capacity for consent, while assent from children and written consent from their legal guardians were obtained for all other participants. Parents completed all questionnaires about their child.

The sample included children aged 7–16, with an IQ ≥ 85. The participants had a diagnosis of autism or were neurotypical. The autism group was recruited from the Province of Ontario Neurodevelopmental Disorders Network (POND), a multi-site and interdisciplinary network of researchers across Ontario. Typical controls were recruited from POND and the community. Autism diagnosis was supported by the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R) and/or the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) (57 participants completed both ADOS and ADI-R and 3 participants completed only one of these measures). The ADOS data were obtained from the POND dataset, where Modules 1 through 4 may be used depending on participants’ language abilities. Module selection followed standard ADOS administration guidelines: nonverbal participants were administered Module 1, those with phrase speech Module 2, and verbally fluent participants Modules 3 or 4, with age also informing the decision between Modules 3 and 4. Children in the neurotypical group did not have a diagnosis of autism or any other developmental, neurological, or psychiatric disorder. Children on beta-blockers were excluded from the study as these affect autonomic function. We have limited our autism sample to those with IQ ≥ 85. This threshold corresponds to one standard deviation below the population mean (mean = 100, standard deviation = 15). While we recognize the importance of studying autonomic function in those with IQ below this cut-off, this decision was made to minimize the confounding effects of IQ on autonomic measures as the neurotypical sample is not expected to present with intellectual disability.

Establishing a clear threshold for what constitutes impairing irritability remains a clinical challenge as the prevalence and expression of irritability varies significantly across different age groups and contexts13. Instead of relying on rigid cutoffs, viewing irritability on a continuum can provide a more accurate understanding, acknowledging its diverse manifestations across populations. Adopting this dimensional view of irritability, we included both autistic and neurotypical children to investigate irritability as a continuous construct27. Additionally, including both groups helped to provide a wider numerical range for the variables of interest.

Study design

The study protocol is shown in Fig. 1. To elicit physiological responses, participants played five video games, preceded and followed by movie watching baseline activities. Descriptions of the video games are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Three of the five video games were positively modulated by providing rewards, whereas two were negatively modulated to elicit frustration. Negative modulation was achieved through simulated software malfunctions (e.g., the computer not responding correctly to the game controller). Each game was three minutes long, with two minutes of positive or negative modulation preceded and followed by 30 s of regular gameplay. After each game, participants provided self-reports of their levels of enjoyment (1: disliked to 5: liked very much), sense of ease (1: very easy to 5: very hard), and frustration (1: not at all frustrating to 5: extremely frustrating). The baseline period consisted of a 15-minute video composed of short (3–4 min) clips from the animated movies Cars, Wall-E, The Good Dinosaur, Finding Nemo, Iron Giant, and Ratatouille. All participants viewed the same compilation, which was edited to exclude continuous plotlines, reducing the potential for narrative engagement or disruption at the end of the viewing period. Prior to the first baseline period, study staff demonstrated each game type for 30 s followed by a one-minute training interval.

Physiological measures

Physiological measures were collected while the participants partook in the video game activities. Electrocardiogram (ECG) signals were measured using the Thought Technology ProComp Infiniti System and sampled at 2048 Hz frequency via a three-lead setup, that was adhered to cleaned skin. The electrodes were positioned with one just below the right collarbone, one just below the left collarbone, and the third on the lower side of the abdomen near the sternum. The quality control (QC) process consisted of visually inspecting and identifying noisy segments of the ECG signals. Noisy segments without clearly defined R-peaks failed the QC pipeline and participant data records that contained more than 5% noise were excluded from the analysis. ECG signals were processed using a bandpass filter, allowing frequencies between 5 and 15 Hz, to maximize QRS energy and remove artifact noise including baseline wandering, motion artifacts, and electrical noise. Two indices of cardiac activity were computed: heart rate (HR) and heart rate variability (HRV). HR was computed as the inverse of the number of beats per minute using the inter-beat interval (IBI) series extracted using the Pan-Tompkin algorithm28,29. HRV was indexed by respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) and computed by sampling the IBI sequence uniformly at 8 Hz using cubic interpolation, and removing the variance associated with activities at frequencies lower than spontaneous breathing by filtering it with a third-order 21-point polynomial filter18. The filtered signal was subtracted from the original resampled sequence, and the power spectral density of this residual sequence was estimated using Welch’s averaged modified periodogram method. The RSA was calculated as the natural logarithm of the total power in the frequency band 0.24 to 1.04 Hz30.

In addition to baseline values, we calculated HR and HRV reactivity and recovery for each game as the difference between HR and HRV values and the preceding and proceedings baseline values. Reactivity and recovery values were averaged across the positive and negative games separately, resulting in a total of 16 columns that reflect HR and HRV reactivity and recovery for both positive and negative games.

Behavioural measures

Irritability was quantified using the Aberrant Behavior Checklist - Community (ABC; irritability subscale)16, a symptom checklist for assessing problem behaviors of children and adults with developmental disabilities. The checklist includes five subscales with items scored on a scale of 0 to 3 (higher scores indicate greater severity of symptoms): irritability (15 items), lethargy/social withdrawal (16 items), stereotypic behavior (7 items), hyperactivity/noncompliance (16 items), and inappropriate speech (4 items). The ABC has good to excellent internal consistency for every factor (irritability alpha 0.92; lethargy/social withdrawal alpha 0.89; stereotypic behavior alpha 0.85; hyperactivity/noncompliance alpha 0.93), except for inappropriate speech that had an acceptable level (alpha 0.77)31, and it established validity in samples of autistic children31.

For phenotypic features, we used parent-reported measures of autism features, intellectual functioning, emotion dysregulation, anxiety, attention and hyperactivity symptoms, externalizing symptoms, and temperament. Autism features were quantified using the Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ) – Lifetime32. The SCQ contains 40 yes/no items that are completed by the parent or primary caregiver and used to characterize autism symptoms. Sensory characteristics were quantified using the Short Sensory Profile (SSP), a 38-item caregiver report questionnaire for autistic and non-autistic children33. Behaviors are scored on a 1–5 rating scale based on their frequency (1 being “Always” and 5 being “Never”) where lower scores indicate higher frequency34. Intelligence Quotient (IQ) was measured using the full-scale IQ (FSIQ) from the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence-Second Edition (WASI-II)35. The Screen for Child Anxiety-Related Disorders (SCARED)36 was used to quantify anxiety symptoms. The SCARED is a brief questionnaire developed to identify anxiety symptoms in children and adolescents. The coefficient alpha values for the total score were approximately 0.90 36. It has moderate to excellent test-retest reliability, with ICC for parent-report ranging from 0.74 to 0.86 and consistency with an alpha of 0.9036,37. The Emotion Dysregulation Inventory (EDI) - reactivity full38, a 30-item parent-report questionnaire used to evaluate emotion dysregulation, providing subscale scores of Reactivity and Dysphoria. The EDI has demonstrated strong psychometric properties in autistic youth, with factor analysis loadings for reactivity ranging from 0.76 to 0.90, and for dysphoria, from 0.72 to 0.9139. The Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL)40 was used to characterize attention deficit/hyperactivity and externalizing problems. The CBCL consists of 113 questions scored on a three-point Likert scale (0: absent, 1: occurs sometimes, 2: occurs often) and has strong psychometric properties including internal consistency, criterion validity, and test-retest reliability (Pearson correlation coefficients ranged from 0.72 for the activities scale to 0.97 for total score, with a mean of 0.89)41. Temperament was quantified using the Temperament in Middle Childhood Questionnaire (TMCQ)42 and the Early Adolescent Temperament Questionnaire (EATQ)43. The subdomains assessed included surgency, effortful control, and negative affect, according to the participants’ age level. TMCQ assesses temperament traits in children aged 7 to 10 through parent and caregiver responses, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.71 to 0.92. EATQ evaluates temperament in adolescents aged 9 to 15, with Cronbach’s alpha for self-report scales ranging from 0.70 to 0.87.

It is important to note that ABC irritability, CBCL externalizing problems, and EDI reactivity and dysphoria share some construct overlap, particularly in assessing outbursts, sudden mood changes, and intense emotional reactions. The ABC irritability and CBCL externalizing problems both capture constructs related to aggression and irritability, while the ABC irritability and EDI share a focus on intense, prolonged emotional reactions and depressed mood. However, there are also unique aspects captured by each scale. For example, the ABC irritability captured self-injurious behaviors and urgent, irritable demands, which are not directly covered by the CBCL or EDI44,45. The CBCL, on the other hand, captures a broader range of externalizing behaviors, such as rule-breaking behavior. Additionally, the EDI provides insights into extreme mood shifts and difficulties with emotional regulation, particularly in calming down.

Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (R Studio, version 2023.3.0.386)46. Baseline and reactivity for heart rate and heart variability were transformed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA)47 to reduce the dimensionality of the data and the correlations among these measures. The analysis was performed in two ways, with and without the physiological variables, given that physiological data was incomplete for some participants.

Network analysis was used to analyze the interconnectedness of the considered variables. In the network, each node represents a variable including age, sex, ABC-irritability, SCQ, SSP, CBCL-externalizing problems, CBCL-ADHD, EDI-reactivity, EDI-dysphoria, anxiety, negative affect, surgency, effortful control, IQ, physiology baseline and physiology reactivity. An edge between a pair of nodes (variables) represents the presence and strength of the association between these two variables. An edge can be either directed or undirected; Directed edges indicate an influence from one node to another, as is common in causal models48. Undirected edges, which do not imply directionality, are frequently used in exploratory analyses to examine how variables are interrelated without assuming causation26. Edges can also be classified as weighted or unweighted. Unweighted edges indicate the presence or absence of a relationship, while weighted edges convey the strength of the association26. Weighted undirected networks, such as those used in this study, are especially useful when the focus is on exploring the intensity and density of associations among variables without presupposing a directional effect.

Network analysis was conducted using the ‘bootnet’ package (1.6 CRAN version)49 in R. We estimated the network using the ‘estimateNetwork’ function, which takes as inputs the dataset, the estimation method, the correlation method, and the tuning parameter (default set at 0.5).

The function internally computes an adjacency matrix, which identifies connections between variables while controlling for the influence of other variables. These partial correlations range from − 1 to 1, where values closer to ± 1 indicate stronger associations, and values near zero indicate weaker or no unique associations. The resulting output is a visualization that depicts the statistically significant associations among the variables in the dataset. We selected the ‘EBICglasso’ method for network estimation26, which is particularly useful for sparse networks. This method combines the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) and the graphical least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (glasso) regularization technique. Glasso generates a set of models by estimating a sparse inverse covariance matrix, which represents the partial correlations between variables and reveals conditional dependencies50. EBIC then selects the best-fitting model from this set by adding a penalty for model complexity, helping avoid overfitting by discouraging overly dense networks51.

Centrality analysis49 was conducted to assess the importance of nodes in the network. Centrality estimates the influence of each node within the network, helping to identify key variables that are most interconnected and potentially play a central role in the overall structure. Node strength reflects the sum of absolute connections a node has with other nodes. It is particularly useful for identifying which variables are most highly connected in the network. Network stability estimation was performed through bootstrapping to evaluate the reliability of strength centrality measures and edge weights. The Correlation Stability coefficient (CS-coefficient) was used to assess stability by determining the maximum proportion of cases that can be removed while still maintaining, with 95% confidence, a correlation with the original centrality above 0.7 26.

We applied the Clique Percolation Method with weights (CPMw)52,53 to identify network communities or clusters (Supplementary Table 2). This was done by locating overlapping cliques that share one or more nodes (i.e., cliques are subsets of nodes where each node connects directly with every other node in the subset). This overlapping structure allows nodes to participate in multiple communities, capturing complex, densely connected substructures within the network​.

The network showed relatively high density, meaning that a substantial proportion of all possible connections between variables were present, indicating a strongly interconnected system. Specifically, 50 out of 91 possible connections observed, representing about 55% of the total. In the context of regularized psychological networks, where sparse models are typically expected49, this indicates a strongly interconnected system of demographic and phenotypic variables. The high density suggests complex interrelations among variables, consistent with the multidimensional nature of irritability in autism. Three distinct clusters of variables (communities) were identified, representing groups of variables that were more strongly interconnected with each other than with the rest of the network. One of these communities contained irritability, which had a direct positive correlation with externalizing problems, emotion dysregulation (reactivity, dysphoria), autism features (SCQ), temperament (negative affect), and anxiety. The second community included autism features (SCQ, sensory profile), temperament (negative affect), and anxiety. The third community included temperament (effortful control), externalizing problems, ADHD features, and autism features (sensory profile). IQ, sex, and age were not assigned to any community.

To conduct network analysis with the addition of physiology, PCA was applied to reduce the dimensionality of the physiology variables. This analysis identified two principal components explaining 65% of the variance in the physiological data (Supplementary Fig. 2). Component 1 captured baselines arousal (HR and HRV) while component 2 corresponded to HR and HRV reactivity. Using these PCA components and the demographic and phenotypic variables, network analysis revealed a network of 16 nodes with an average edge weight of 0.0377 (Fig. 2d-f). This network has a moderate density, with 45 out of 120 possible connections (38% of the total). The most central nodes in this network were autism features (sensory profile), ADHD features, effortful control, autism features (SCQ), emotion dysregulation (reactivity), externalizing problems, negative affect, and irritability. The network was stable (CS-coefficients > 0.5). Detailed results of the network estimation and stability analyses are presented in Supplementary Table 4.

Community analysis showed that irritability clustered with physiology (baseline), ADHD features, temperament (effortful control), and autism features (sensory profile) and as before with emotion dysregulation (reactivity), externalizing problems, temperament (negative affect), autism features (SCQ), and anxiety. In this community, irritability was indirectly associated with physiology (baseline), meaning that physiology (baseline) had weak positive correlations with effortful control and ADHD features, which in turn were directly linked to irritability.

The second community included emotion dysregulation (reactivity and dysphoria) and sex whereas the third community included autism features (SCQ), sex, and effortful control. Unlike moderation effects, where one variable alters the strength or direction of the relationship between two others, network analysis does not model interaction terms explicitly. Instead, the clustering of variables into communities suggests shared associations that may reflect underlying common influences. Temperament (surgency), IQ, age, and physiology (reactivity) were not assigned to any community; However, physiology (reactivity) had a weak positive association with autism features (sensory profile).

Discussion

To enhance the understanding of the interconnected and multidimensional correlates of irritability in autism, the present study used network analysis to characterize the constellation of demographic, phenotypic, and physiological variables associated with irritability. In summary, our network analysis identified that irritability in autism is most strongly linked with externalizing behaviors and emotion dysregulation, with additional connections to autism features, negative affect, and physiological markers related to self-regulation and ADHD traits.

Our analysis revealed that irritability symptoms show the strongest direct associations with externalizing problems and emotion dysregulation, and to a lesser degree, with autism features (SCQ) and negative affect, although the interpretation of these findings may be limited due to the partial overlap among these constructs. From a statistical perspective, direct associations are analogous to bivariate correlations, where two variables exhibit an association without necessarily implying causality. Indirect associations, on the other hand, are conceptually similar to mediation, where an intermediate variable (e.g., effortful control) may partly account for the connection between two other variables (e.g., irritability and physiology). However, unlike mediation analysis, which tests specific causal pathways, network analysis is exploratory and identifies structural associations within the data.

It is not surprising that irritability was closely associated with externalizing problems and emotion dysregulation given the significant overlap of these constructs. For example, irritability may be associated with impulsive or aggressive behaviors, which are key components of externalizing problems6. Furthermore, externalizing problems may act as a bridge between irritability and ADHD, as individuals with ADHD are more prone to impulsivity and hyperactivity, which in turn increase the likelihood of externalizing behaviors. This connection is supported by research showing that ADHD symptoms, such as poor emotional control and impulsivity, often coexist with irritability, contributing to heightened risks for aggression and defiance54,55. Moreover, decreased flexibility, inhibitory control, and problem-solving skills further hinder effective emotional responses, increasing irritability56. However, it is important to note that some of these associations may partly reflect conceptual and measurement overlap across commonly used questionnaires, as many scales (e.g., ABC–irritability, CBCL-externalizing problems, and EDI-reactivity) include items that capture related aspects of irritability, externalizing behaviors, and emotion dysregulation.

Within the same constellation of features, emotion dysregulation also emerged as a highly interconnected domain with irritability. In particular, irritability was positively associated with reactivity, defined as intense, quickly escalating, and poorly managed negative emotional reactions to stress or frustration57. Our analysis of physiological variables revealed that baseline hyperarousal may also belong to this constellation of features, alongside effortful control, a component of temperament which reflects an individual’s capacity to manage their attention, emotions, and behavior in the face of challenges or frustration58,59. In particular, irritability’s association with physiological factors appears to be bridged by its connections to self-regulation (effortful control) and ADHD. Previous research has demonstrated that aggressive responses to perceived threats are associated with both emotional and physiological hyperarousal60. When individuals engage in frustration-inducing tasks, physiological markers such as heart rate tend to increase, while heart rate variability decreases60,61. This aligns with findings that autistic individuals and individuals with ADHD may experience heightened frustration reactivity, which is accompanied by increased cardiovascular arousal62,63.

Overall, our findings suggest that irritability is among a cluster of features related to effective regulation of emotions and responses. This finding is consistent with previous research, which suggests that individuals with higher irritability struggle to control their emotional and behavioral responses, particularly in situations where their goals are blocked, or expectations are unmet64. Among these features, emotion dysregulation may be a potential target for non-medication interventions. Future studies are needed to investigate the impact of emotion dysregulation intervention on irritability outcomes. Additionally, our findings demonstrate the significant role of physiological factors in irritability, emphasizing the need for future research to explore these biological mechanisms further. Integrating physiological assessments with behavioral and emotional measures can provide deeper insights and inform more comprehensive intervention approaches.

In addition to the above constellation of features, we found a second set of features related to increased propensity of states of negative feelings. These features included negative affect and surgency from the temperament domain and dysphoria from the emotion dysregulation domain (persistent feelings of sadness, dissatisfaction, and emotional discomfort57. The features were also interconnected with anxiety which is a common, persistent, and disabling co-occurring condition in autistic individuals, affecting them across all levels of functioning and throughout their lifespan65,66. The association between negative affect and irritability is consistent with previous literature, which indicates that individuals with higher negative affect are more prone to irritability64.

Anxiety in turn was linked with autism features, showing a direct association with the social communication (SCQ) and sensory differences, with SCQ serving as a bridge between these differences and irritability. Previous research indicates that the prevalence of irritability is particularly elevated in autistic individuals67 and suggests that irritability in autism may be linked to several mechanisms, including social communication challenges, difficulties with sensory processing, and problems with self-regulation56. The inability to accurately process social and emotional cues, such as recognizing and labeling emotions in others, may contribute to irritability, as difficulty in reading these cues can lead to misunderstandings and frustration68,69. Additionally, increased irritability may stem from the cognitive inflexibility, which can hinder adaptation to new situations and the ability to recognize ineffective strategies69,70. Furthermore, heightened sensory sensitivities often lead to frequent overstimulation and frustration when sensory environments are overwhelming, while rigidity makes transitions particularly challenging, further escalating irritability24,56. These constellations of features may inform additional targets for irritability intervention, including management of anxiety, and supports and accommodations for sensory differences. Future research is needed to further investigate these avenues.

Clinically, these results underscore the importance of targeting emotion dysregulation and externalizing behaviors in interventions aimed at reducing irritability in autistic individuals. Additionally, addressing co-occurring anxiety and supporting sensory sensitivities may provide further benefits. Tailored, multi-faceted approaches that consider the diverse factors contributing to irritability could improve outcomes and enhance quality of life. Future studies should explore the efficacy of such personalized interventions.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is that our findings reveal associations rather than causality, and they must be interpreted within that context. In particular, our results do not determine whether these variables predict irritability or whether irritability influences these variables, which limits the ability to draw conclusions about the directionality of these relationships. Another limitation is the small sample size, particularly after excluding participants with missing data, which constrains the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the short duration of each designed game may not have been sufficient to fully capture prolonged physiological or emotional responses, and the artificial frustration created by game glitches may not accurately reflect real-world experiences. Moreover, the exclusion of individuals with intellectual disability further limits the generalizability of results to the broader autistic population.

Finally, although the ABC-irritability is one of the most widely used and validated measures of irritability in autism research, it does not distinguish between irritability as a transient emotional state, a persistent mood, or a stable trait. More broadly, there is also conceptual and item-level overlap across many questionnaires commonly used in the field, which can complicate interpretation and reduce precision in distinguishing between related constructs (e.g., irritability, emotion dysregulation, aggression).

Future work is needed to develop or apply measurement approaches that more precisely capture distinct dimensions of irritability, including its expression as a transient state, a persistent mood, and a stable trait, while minimizing redundancy across tools. Incorporating this dimensionality into future network analyses would allow researchers to examine whether state-, mood-, and trait- level irritability occupy different roles in the broader network of behavioral, emotional, and physiological correlates. Such work could clarify which dimensions are most influential, how they relate to co-occurring features such as anxiety or emotion dysregulation, and which may represent the most appropriate intervention targets.

Conclusions

This study provides a critical step toward understanding the complex network of demographic, phenotypic, and physiological factors associated with irritability in autism. By using network analysis, we uncovered connections between irritability and externalizing behaviors, emotion dysregulation, temperament traits, and autism features. These findings underscore the importance of examining irritability as part of an interconnected system rather than in isolation.
