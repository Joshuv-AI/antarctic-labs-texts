From Exploration to Exploitation: A Two-Stage Entropy RLVR Approach for Noise-Tolerant MLLM Training
Donglai Xu, Hongzheng Yang, Yuzhi Zhao, Pingping Zhang, Jinpeng Chen, Wenao Ma, Zhijian Hou, Mengyang Wu, Xiaolei Li, Senkang Hu, Ziyi Guan, Jason Chun Lok Li, Lai Man Po, Independent Researcher The Chinese University of Hong Kong City University of Hong Kong

Abstract
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) is highly dependent on high-quality labeled data, which is often scarce and prone to substantial annotation noise in real-world scenarios. Existing unsupervised RLVR methods, including pure entropy minimization, can overfit to incorrect labels and limit the crucial reward ranking signal for Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). To address these challenges and enhance noise tolerance, we propose a novel two-stage, token-level entropy optimization method for RLVR. This approach dynamically guides the model from exploration to exploitation during training. In the initial exploration phase, token-level entropy maximization promotes diverse and stochastic output generation, serving as a strong regularizer that prevents premature convergence to noisy labels and ensures sufficient intra-group variation—enabling more reliable reward gradient estimation in GRPO. As training progresses, the method transitions into the exploitation phase, where token-level entropy minimization encourages the model to produce confident and deterministic outputs, thereby consolidating acquired knowledge and refining prediction accuracy. Empirically, across three MLLM backbones-Qwen2-VL-2B, Qwen2-VL-7B, and Qwen2.5-VL-3B—spanning diverse noise settings and multiple tasks, our phased strategy consistently outperforms prior approaches by unifying and enhancing external, internal, and entropy-based methods, delivering robust and superior performance across the board.

Introduction

Recently, Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has gained recognition for its effectiveness, as evidenced by its superior generalization compared to supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
[
5
]
, its ability to elicit reasoning potential, and its ease of implementation. A notable example is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
[
22
]
, applied by Deepseek-R1
[
22
]
, which exemplifies these strengths. RLVR has demonstrated significant success across a wide range of domains, including mathematical reasoning
[
39
,
30
]
, formal verification
[
37
,
28
]
, and code generation
[
36
]
. Moreover, RLVR has been extended to multimodal tasks, significantly enhancing the reasoning capabilities of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). These applications span image classification and object grounding
[
24
,
14
,
31
,
2
]
, image segmentation
[
23
]
, medical reasoning
[
16
]
, video understanding
[
33
,
7
]
, and graphical user interface (GUI) reasoning
[
25
,
26
]
. Despite these advancements, a critical challenge remains: RLVR methods typically rely on high-quality labeled data to compute verifiable rewards. In real-world scenarios, datasets are frequently accompanied by annotation noise, posing a significant barrier to effective RLVR implementation.

To address the challenge of applying RLVR to datasets with annotation noise, recent methodologies can be grouped into three primary categories:

1.
External-Signal-Based Methods
: These approaches utilize external verifiable signals to guide RLVR training, such as compilers for code generation
[
20
]
, Large Language Models (LLMs) as evaluators (e.g., LLM-as-a-Judge)
[
10
]
, and Test-time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL)
[
35
,
43
]
. While versatile, these methods exhibit inconsistent performance due to variations in LLM capabilities across domains, and tools like compilers are often task-specific, limiting their applicability.

2.
Internal-Signal-Based Methods
: These methods derive rewards directly from model outputs, such as random rewards or format rewards, eliminating reliance on labeled data or external tools
[
39
,
29
]
. By computing rewards from output queries or fixed functions, these approaches offer flexibility. However, their effectiveness is constrained, as the reward functions are often not closely aligned with task-specific objectives.

3.
Entropy-Based Methods
: These methods leverage generation uncertainty to guide training. For example,
34
proposed a one-shot RL scheme that achieves significant improvements in mathematical reasoning using entropy loss alone. Similarly,
42
employed intrinsic self-certainty signals, while EMPO
[
41
]
minimized predictive entropy on unlabeled questions. However, these approaches often overemphasize entropy reduction, potentially overlooking the dynamic role of entropy across different training stages.

To investigate the robustness of RLVR under varying data conditions, we evaluate the performance of MLLMs trained with different RL methods on two visual tasks: GUI grounding and fine-grained classification. We systematically vary the proportion of mislabeled data while maintaining a fixed training set size. The results for the GUI grounding task are presented in Figure
1
. Our key observations regarding the three methodological categories are as follows:

1. As the proportion of mislabeled data decreases, model accuracy generally increases, indicating that external-signal-based methods, such as TTRL
[
43
]
, have an upper performance bound determined by the accuracy of training samples and the capability of the MLLM used for pre-labeling.

2. With a small proportion of correctly labeled data, standard GRPO training outperforms internal-signal-based methods, such as those relying on spurious rewards
[
29
]
.

3. Augmenting GRPO with entropy-based losses
[
41
]
consistently yields superior performance compared to using GRPO alone. Similar trends are observed across other vision tasks.

Based on the observation, we found that external-signal-based or internal-signal-based methods might be improved by generation-entropy-based methods. However, if the optimization objective is naively reduced to either only entropy maximization or only entropy minimization, the learning dynamics can become problematic. Entropy maximization only leads to policies that is hard to converge, while exclusive minimization may trap the model in sub-optimal deterministic behaviours, especially facing the label noise. We argue that an the entropy optimization should be scheduled and switch between the two regimes, which could offer controlled trade-off between exploration and exploitation without sacrificing convergence stability.

Specifically, we propose a two-stage entropy-guided RLVR training method. During the early phase of training, we encourage the model to output more diversely by maximizing token-level entropy. This promotes exploration and prevents the model from overfitting to noisy data. As the training is processed, the model has captured most of the information from the datasets. We then proceed to the second stage, where entropy minimization is applied to encourage more confident and deterministic outputs. By explicitly guiding the model from exploration to exploitation, this two-stage method enhances the model’s ability to learn from noisy datasets. For instance, by applying the two-stage entropy optimization to Qwen2.5-VL-3B
[
1
]
with 50% noise labels, the method further boosts performance from 76% to 80.2% on ScreenSpot dataset
[
4
]
, with similar gains observed across other levels of label noise (e.g., from 71% to 75.8% for 100% noisy labels, and from 82.2% to 83.6% for 0% noisy labels), as shown in Figure 1. It also consistently outperforms solely maximizing or minimizing the entropy
[
41
]
.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We conduct comprehensive experiments across multiple dimensions: 1) varying annotation rates, 2) diverse model architectures and scales (Qwen2-VL-2B, Qwen2.5-VL-3B, Qwen2-VL-7B), and 3) multiple task types (GUI grounding and fine-grained classification), to systematically evaluate the impact of noisy labels on RLVR.

•

We demonstrate that RLVR-trained models with a certain proportion of noisy labels can achieve performance comparable to those trained with clean data, highlighting the robustness of RLVR to annotation noise.

•

We introduce a two-stage entropy-guided optimization method for RLVR, which first maximizes and then minimizes entropy, significantly improving model performance on partially labeled or unlabeled data.

Related Works

2.1
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards

RLVR leverages verifiable signals to compute rewards, particularly for tasks with well-defined correctness criteria, such as mathematical reasoning and code generation
[
30
,
17
,
13
,
32
]
. Unlike traditional reinforcement learning approaches that rely on learned reward models, RLVR employs rule-based verification functions, such as exact answer matching, to mitigate the complexities and potential biases associated with learned rewards. This characteristic has enabled RLVR to achieve state-of-the-art reasoning capabilities in LLMs, as exemplified by DeepSeek-R1
[
11
]
. The GRPO algorithm and its variants
[
30
]
have further extended RLVR to multimodal scenarios, including image classification
[
24
]
, geometry reasoning
[
14
]
, GUI grounding
[
26
]
, and multi-step reasoning tasks such as search
[
15
]
. Despite these successes, RLVR’s effectiveness is predominantly limited to domains with reliable verifiable signals and high-quality annotations, posing challenges in scenarios with noisy data.

2.2
Reinforcement Learning without Human Annotation

In scenarios where explicit or external rewards are unavailable, unsupervised reinforcement learning has gained traction as an alternative approach. A prominent method, LLM-as-a-Judge
[
40
]
, utilizes the LLM itself as a reward function, eliminating the need for human feedback. This concept has been extended to verify the correctness of generated training data
[
38
]
. More recently, TTRL
[
43
]
employs majority voting across diverse model outputs to generate pseudo-ground-truth signals, which serve as verifiable rewards to enhance mathematical reasoning through RL training. Additionally, research on spurious rewards
[
29
]
has explored unsupervised signals, such as format rewards, random rewards, and incorrect label rewards, revealing that LLMs exhibit varying reasoning priors depending on the reward type. However, the majority of unsupervised RL studies have focused on mathematical reasoning and code generation tasks. In this work, we systematically evaluate the impact of these unsupervised reward signals on multimodal tasks under noisy supervision.

2.3
Entropy in Reinforcement Learning

Entropy minimization has long been a cornerstone of unsupervised learning, with early work by
9
demonstrating that minimizing entropy on unlabeled data enhances classification accuracy. This principle has been adapted to the LLM domain to improve generation confidence and downstream task performance
[
42
,
34
]
. For instance,
42
utilized self-certainty as a sole reward signal in RL training, achieving superior out-of-domain performance and matching standard GRPO training on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. Similarly, the EMPO framework
[
41
]
minimizes the entropy of output sequences, leveraging internal model consistency as an effective reward signal. Additionally, Seed-GRPO
[
3
]
employs entropy to modulate the magnitude of policy updates, enhancing training stability. However, existing entropy-based approaches primarily focus on fully unsupervised settings or partially labeled datasets with clean annotations. In contrast, this work investigates the role of entropy-based mechanisms in RLVR for multimodal tasks under noisy supervision, providing a more comprehensive understanding of their applicability in challenging real-world scenarios.

3
Preliminary

3.1
Group Reward Policy Optimization (GRPO)

RLVR leverages external verification signals to provide binary rewards for policy optimization, circumventing the complexities of learned reward models. Unlike traditional reinforcement learning approaches that rely on human feedback or learned preference models, RLVR employs rule-based verification functions such as exact answer matching, compiler feedback, or mathematical correctness checks to determine reward assignment.

GRPO serves as the primary algorithm for RLVR training. The GRPO training process begins by sampling
K
responses
\{y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{K}\}
from the current policy
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)
for each input
x
. Each response
y_{i}
is evaluated using a verifiable reward function
\mathcal{R}(y_{i},y^{*})
that returns a binary signal based on correctness verification. The key innovation of GRPO is its group-wise advantage estimation that normalizes rewards within each group to reduce variance.
For a given group of
K
responses with rewards
\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{K}\}
, GRPO computes the advantage for each response as:

A_{i}=\frac{r(y_{i})-\text{mean}(r(y_{1:K}))}{\text{std}(r(y_{1:K}))},

(1)

where
\text{mean}(r(y_{1:K}))
and
\text{std}(r(y_{1:K}))
are the mean and standard deviation of rewards within the group, respectively.

The policy gradient objective becomes:

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}A_{i}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|y_{i,<t},x)\right].

(2)

In practice, to constrain policy online updates and prevent overly aggressive parameter changes, the surrogate loss is proposed:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},i\in{1,2,...,K}}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min\Bigg(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|y_{i,<t},x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|y_{i,<t},x)}A_{i},

(3)

\displaystyle\text{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|y_{i,<t},x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old},x}}(y_{i,t}|y_{i,<t})},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)A_{i}\Bigg)\Bigg].

4
Methodology

4.1
Token-Level Entropy

The foundation of our approach lies in leveraging token-level entropy as a granular measure of uncertainty in text generation. Unlike sequence-level entropy, which captures the overall uncertainty of an output, token-level entropy quantifies the predictability of each token at every generation step. Formally, for an input sequence
x
and partially generated tokens
y_{<t}
, the model produces a conditional probability distribution
\pi_{\theta}(v\mid x,y_{<t})
over vocabulary
V
. The per-token entropy is computed as:

\mathcal{H}_{t}(x,y)=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{\theta}(v\mid x,y_{<t})\log\pi_{\theta}(v\mid x,y_{<t}).

(4)

The token-level entropy for the entire sequence is then computed by averaging over all
T
tokens in the trajectory:

\mathcal{H}_{\text{token}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{H}_{t}(x,y).

(5)

The corresponding entropy loss is then defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}=-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathcal{H}_{\text{token}}(x,y_{i})\right].

(6)

where
K
is the number of responses sampled per input
x
.

4.2
Two-Stage Entropy-Guided GRPO

Revisiting Entropy as A Reward Signal for
Unsupervised RLVR.

The role of entropy in learning with unlabeled data has been examined from two perspectives.
Early work in semi-supervised classification
[
18
,
9
]
argues that optimizing the predictive distribution towards low entropy transforms unlabeled inputs into effective constraints on the classification decision boundary.
Deep reinforcement learning
[
12
]
literature, by contrast, argues for maximizing policy entropy to support exploration until the optimal behavior is reliably discovered. Existing RLVR studies inherit one of these viewpoints in isolation.
EMPO
[
41
]
and one-shot RL
[
34
]
, minimized predictive entropy on unlabeled questions during training, while CLIP-Cov
[
6
]
prevent policy from
entropy collapse thus promoting exploration.

Both choices may break down under of noisy supervision. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}
be the token-level entropy loss defined in Eqs. (6). GRPO using
-\lambda{L}_{\text{entropy}}
as a regularization term in total loss may drive the model to place unjustified confidence on potentially incorrect labels and simultaneously suppresses the diversity that GRPO’s group-wise normalization requires for stable advantage estimation. Regularizing with
+\lambda{L}_{\text{entropy}}
alleviates over-confidence and preserves alternative candidate for GRPO responses diversity, yet the policy now struggles to converge because probability mass is never allowed to concentrate under entropy maximization. Therefore we argue that the entropy optimization direction should not be fixed, but should be evolved in schedule. As in Figure 3, it should be high early on to resist noisy rewards and to provide GRPO with informative rank orderings, and low later so that the correct answer could consolidate from exploration and evolve to exploitation.

Based on the above intuition, we propose a two-stage token-level entropy optimization framework for RLVR
training, thereby realizing the exploration-to-exploitation trajectory. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}
denote the standard GRPO loss derived from the group-wise advantage formulation, and let
\lambda(\tau)
be a scalar coefficient that varies with the training step
\tau
. The unified objective function is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}+\lambda(\tau)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{entropy}}.

(7)

We define the schedule for
\lambda(t)
as a simple piecewise function:

\lambda(\tau)=\begin{cases}\;\;\lambda_{\max},&\text{if }\tau\leq\tau_{\text{switch}}\quad\text{(Stage 1: exploration)},\\[4.0pt]
-\lambda_{\min},&\text{otherwise}\quad\text{(Stage 2: exploitation)},\end{cases}

(8)

with fixed positive hyper-parameters
\lambda_{\max}
and
\lambda_{\min}
.
During Stage 1, the positive coefficient instantiates a entropy maximization variant of GRPO, which encourages diverse sampling. The switch is triggered when the running average of
H_{\text{token}}
saturates, which occurs after roughly
80\%
of the total training steps empirically (denoted as
\tau_{\text{switch}}
).
Stage 2 flips the same term so that entropy is now minimized, which direct the model towards confident and deterministic outputs, consolidating knowledge gained during exploration. The adaptive scheduling ensures the model fully benefits from both regimes. The full pipeline pseudo code can be found in Algorithm
1
. Here, we only use a simple piecewise function and achieve great empirical performance. It demonstrates the potential of our two-stage transition design. More sophisticated schedulers, such as a linear-decay schedule, are also discussed in the appendix.

Experimental Setup

Datasets and Training.

We use GRPO
[
22
]
to train base model with UI-R1 framework
[
26
]
for GUI grounding and Visual-RFT framework
[
24
]
for fine-grained classification. For GUI grounding task, we randomly select 500 samples from ScreenSpot
[
4
]
as a training set, with an equal distribution between mobile, web, and desktop. For fine-grained classification task, we utilize Pets37
[
27
]
with 4-shot setting.

Evaluation.

For evaluation of the GUI grounding task, we select 500 samples from ScreenSpot as a test set, which is different from the training samples but with the same platform distribution. For the fine-grained classification task, we use the corresponding test dataset of Pets37 for evaluation. We adopt the grounding or prediction accuracy as our evaluation metric, which is calculated by bounding box match for GUI grounding task and label text match for fine-grained classification.
We compare five configurations: (1) Base pretrained model without RL (Base Model), (2) Standard GRPO (GRPO), (3) GRPO training using entropy minimization as reward signal (GRPO w. Min.), (4) GRPO training using entropy maximization as reward signal (GRPO w. Max.), and (5) Our proposed two-stage entropy-guided method (GRPO w. Two.).

Imperfect Supervision Simulation.

For the GUI grounding task, we simulate noisy labels by randomly generating a new bounding box in the image with the same size as the original ground truth bounding box, ensuring no overlap between them, and using it as the new target. We reward the response if the grounding point is within the ground truth bounding box.
For the fine-grained classification task, to create noisy annotations, we randomly replace the correct label with an incorrect one drawn from the remaining label set. We reward the response if the predicted label matches the ground truth label. Across both tasks, we generate datasets with noise levels
\{100\%,80\%,60\%,50\%,40\%,20\%,0\%\}
.

Main Results

Quantitative Analysis.

Table
1
presents GUI grounding results, revealing three key findings. First, the proposed two-stage entropy optimization method maintains 80.2% accuracy at 50% noise, just 2% below clean-data performance—demonstrating remarkable noise tolerance. Second, our method consistently outperforms alternatives across all noise levels, with particularly strong improvements (4.0-4.8% absolute gains) at high noise (100%). Third, the 8.2-14.8% gains over base Qwen2.5-VL-3B highlight RLVR’s effectiveness even with noisy supervision. These results validate our core hypothesis that strategic entropy modulation enhances model performance under noisy data settings.
Table
1
also shows fine-grained classification results with similar trends. The two-stage method achieves 68.4% accuracy at 50% noise, outperforming alternatives by 0.6-1.4% absolute. Notably, entropy minimization performs best at 100% noise (59.3%), while maximization excels at 0% noise (69.8%). Our method balances these regimes, delivering robust performance across noise levels. This confirms the task-agnostic benefits of phased entropy optimization.

Qualitative Analysis.

Figure 2 provides an illustrative comparison of how the three entropy regimes shape both the sampled reasoning traces and the final predictions. For GRPO with entropy minimization, the policy collapses almost immediately onto a single confident decoding path. All GRPO rollouts verbalize an almost identical chain of thought, so noisy rewards are propagated unchecked and the model converges to the same incorrect coordinate at inference. In contrast, pure entropy maximization generates diverse reasoning paths that include at least one trajectory consistent with the true label, thereby diluting the impact of noisy rewards, yet the lack of consolidation leaves its accuracy short of the best. For our two-stage method, the reasoning traces remain diverse enough to resist noise, but also coherent enough to pinpoint the correct GUI region.

Base Model Scaling.

Table

(GUI grounding) report results for three Qwen‐VL backbones with different parameter count (2B, 3B, and 7B). Our two-stage method delivers consistent gains across most model sizes and noise levels.

Noisy Data Scaling.

To further investigate the impact of noisy data on GRPO training, we fixed 500 right samples and added 50 wrong samples each time to train models. In Table
4
, we see that when there are 100 wrong samples adding to 500 right samples, it achieves the best performance. After that, the accuracy starts to drop. Our two-stage method maintains 81.4-81.8% accuracy across noise levels, demonstrating superior stability. The noise effect is most pronounced for entropy maximization at +50 samples (82.2%), but degrades with additional noise. Our method’s consistent performance confirms that phased entropy optimization effectively harnesses additional data benefits while mitigating its risks.

Entropy Training Dynamics.

For our proposed two-stage entropy optimization method, Figure

The order of exploration and exploitation matters more than the per-subset assignment of coefficients.

Table 6 compares four possible ways of combining entropy maximization and minimization under 100%, 50% and 0% label noise.

Across all noise levels, “Max. then Min.” outperforms “Min. then Max.” by 5.6% at 100% noise, 3.4% at 50% noise and 3.8% on clean data. Beginning with minimization drives the policy to over-confidently imitate whichever labels right or wrong happen to be present, leaving little room for later correction. Conversely, starting with maximization supports the diversity needed for GRPO’s group-wise ranking to isolate truly informative trajectories, and the subsequent minimization phase then consolidates those findings.

When maximizing entropy is restricted to the noisy subset only (“LF. Max. LT. Min.”), performance is better than the flipped order but still inferior to the unified “Max. then Min.” schedule. Isolating exploration to bad data deprives the model of useful variance on good data, which is necessary for learning long-tail correlations under partial supervision. Symmetrically, “LT. Max. LF. Min.” works well at 5% and 0% noise, because half or all of the data are reliable, yet it suffers under 100% noise when no clean labels exist to guide exploitation.

Generalizable Findings Across Different Tasks.

GRPO tolerance to Data Noise.

Fig
1
and Table
1
reveal that vanilla GRPO degrades gracefully as the label-noise ratio increases. With 50% noisy GUI‐grounding labels, Qwen2.5-VL-3B trained only with GRPO attains 76.2% accuracy, only six percentage points below the clean-data ceiling. This stems from GRPO’s group-relative advantage normalization. If all
K
roll-outs for a mislabeled sample happen to converge on the true answer, every response in the group receives the same binary reward, yielding zero advantage and therefore no harmful gradient.
This self-gating effect establishes a robust baseline on top of which entropy scheduling can operate.

Limitations.

We find that entropy works best when the model has an original ability on target task. If the base model’s zero-shot ability on the target task is very poor, early entropy maximization may amplify erroneous modes before any correct trajectory is found. The phenomenon is visible when Qwen2.5-VL-3B tackles fine-grained classification with fully noisy supervision in Table
1
.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we explore the effectiveness of RLVR under noisy supervision for multimodal reasoning tasks. We propose Two-Stage Entropy-Guided GRPO that first maximizes and then minimizes token-level entropy during training. This strategy encourages early exploration and later exploitation, leading to improved robustness against label noise. Through extensive experiments with Qwen-series model, we demonstrate that our method maintains high performance even under substantial annotation noise. Notably, the two-stage method contributes to more stable convergence and better generalization. Our findings highlight the potential of entropy-aware policy optimization as a powerful tool for learning from imperfect data in multi-modal scenarios.

Entropy Optimization Schedule

Why Training Starts with Entropy Maximization.

Our two-stage schedule begins with token-level entropy maximization because diversity is the currency that GRPO relies on to compute meaningful advantage signals. Maximization enlarges the variance of responses within each group, sharpening the relative ranking and, consequently, the gradient. At the same time, it regularizes the policy against premature converge to spurious labels. When the correct supervision is missing or wrong, a more diverse distribution prevents the policy from overfitting to the noisy target. Empirically, this exploration phase already yields a non-trivial improvement over either entropy minimization or the plain GRPO baseline (e.g. 77.8% vs. 76.2% at 50% noise on ScreenSpot).

Why Training ends with Entropy Minimization.

Exploration alone is insufficient. Once the policy has discovered high-reward regions, it must consolidate. After token entropy plateaus, the sign of the entropy coefficient is flipped. Minimizing entropy concentrates probability mass on the best trajectory identified earlier, reduces variance at inference time and sharpens predictions. The switch consistently achieves improvements across all noise levels, confirming that exploitation effectively complements exploration.
