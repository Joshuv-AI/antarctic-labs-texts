Azolla reshapes rhizosphere microbiomes and nutrient cycling in paddy fields
Authors: Rong Zhao, Ping Huang, Changbing Pu, Feiyu Zhu, Changfeng Wang, Can Cai, Ni Xiang, Mengting Ren, Qinqin Ma, Jinmeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoil quality is a critical determinant of agricultural productivity and sustainability. The symbiotic nitrogen fixation by Azolla plays a key role in enhancing soil quality. However, despite its potential as a green manure for enhancing soil quality, the role of Azolla in paddy systems remains inadequately characterized. This study aims to elucidate the effects of Azolla on soil quality by examining nutrient cycling dynamics and microbial community composition, along with their interactions.ResultsWe integrated soil physicochemical analyses, enzyme activity assays, bacterial community profiling, co-occurrence network analysis, and correlation assessments to evaluate the effects of Azolla on soil microbial ecology. Rice monoculture (R) and rice–Azolla co-cultivation (RA) systems were established. RA significantly increased activities of carbon- and nitrogen-cycle-related enzymes by 3–44% (P < 0.05), while phosphorus-cycle-related enzyme activities decreased by 12–42%. Under high nitrogen fertilization, Azolla altered bacterial community structure and reduced alpha diversity. Notably, Azolla recruited specific functional taxa—including Haliangium, SC-I-84, Candidatus_Solibacter, Anaerolinea, and Sphingomonas-whose relative abundances were 1.03–1.33 times higher in RA than in R.ConclusionsThis study elucidates the interactions between soil properties and microbial communities under Azolla application and uncovers the mechanisms by which Azolla enhances soil quality through nutrient cycling. Our findings demonstrate that Azolla, as a green manure, not only elevates soil nutrient content but also improves soil quality by driving microbe-mediated nutrient recycling. These results underscore the potential of Azolla as a sustainable alternative to conventional fertilization practices, offering novel insights into biofertilizer strategies for agricultural soil enhancement.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-026-00903-w.Keywords: Green manure, Paddy soil, Azolla, Co-cultivation, Soil enzymes, Bacterial community structure

Background

In response to the challenges of a growing global population and increasing food demand, agricultural production has relied on the excessive application of chemical fertilizers to achieve high crop yields [1, 2]. However, soil acidification, decreased health indices, and disrupted nutrient cycling resulting from excessive fertilizer application must not be underestimated [3, 4]. Soil quality, fundamentally defined as the capacity of soil to function within ecosystem boundaries to sustain biological productivity, is paramount and is crucial for promoting nutrient cycling and maintaining agricultural productivity [5, 6]. It encompasses physical, chemical, and biological properties that affect nutrient availability, water retention, and biodiversity [7]. Appropriate fertilization regimes not only effectively enhance soil quality, and multifunctionality but also improve crop yields and nutrient uptake efficiency [8–10]. Furthermore, the integration of reduced chemical fertilizer use with organic fertilizers has been shown to significantly improve soil quality [11].

Beyond rational fertilization, the integration of green manures represents a powerful tool for soil quality restoration. For instance, legumes like milk vetch, through symbiotic nitrogen fixation with rhizobia, can significantly reduce dependence on synthetic nitrogen fertilizers while improving soil quality. [12, 13]. While common green manures such as alfalfa and rapeseed are well-studied, the potential of the aquatic fern Azolla remains relatively underexplored [13]. Emerging evidence positions Azolla as a promising natural biofertilizer; its symbiotic association with the nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterium can effectively supplement the nitrogen demands of paddy systems [14, 15]. For example, Zhu et al. [15] achieved a significant increase in nitrogen use efficiency by rice roots and a reduction in ammonia volatilization in a rice monoculture system by integrating lateral and deep fertilization with Azolla. Yang et al. [16] also demonstrated that the integration of Azolla and urease inhibitors in a rice monoculture system reduced ammonia volatilization and enhanced nitrogen use efficiency and rice yield. Despite these agronomic benefits, the mechanistic underpinnings of how Azolla influences the cycling of key elements, particularly carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P), within the complex paddy ecosystem are still poorly resolved.

As the primary driving force behind C, N, and P cycling in farmland ecosystems, soil microorganisms play a crucial role in activating soil nutrients, facilitating nutrient exchange, and constructing microecological communication networks [17, 18]. A growing body of research confirms the critical function of the soil microbiome in enhancing soil quality [19]. For instance, Liu et al. [20] investigated the impacts of long-term organic fertilizer application on microbial communities in paddy soil and discovered that the structured bacterial communities enhanced nutrient cycling within the soil microbial ecology by modulating the activities of key soil enzymes, thereby further increasing rice yield. In addition, Dong et al. [8] confirmed the importance of soil bacterial diversity for soil multifunctionality in their study on the combination of biochar and nitrogen fertilization in a rice monoculture system. Han et al. discovered that the incorporation of green manure enhanced soil quality by increasing the abundance of dominant bacterial taxa while reducing bacterial diversity [21]. These studies have shown that bacteria are related to soil nutrient cycling, multifunctionality, and soil quality. In addition, soil enzyme activities serve as sensitive indicators of microbial metabolic constraints and are pivotal biomarkers for assessing the efficiency of C, N, and P cycling, indirectly reflecting the dynamic status of soil nutrients [22]. Nevertheless, significant knowledge gaps persist. A comprehensive understanding of the composition and diversity of bacterial communities and their interactive effects on nutrient cycling is still elusive. This is particularly true within complex integrated rice cultivation systems, where the interplay between novel amendments like Azolla, soil microbiota, and enzymatic profiles remains a critical frontier for research [20, 23].

While these studies demonstrate the benefits of organic management on soil microbes, Azolla is a unique aquatic green manure [24]. The rhizosphere micro-ecological environment it creates when co-cultured with rice is fundamentally different from that of monoculture systems or systems amended with other organic fertilizers [24]. To date, no study has systematically deciphered the assembly rules of the microbial community and its functional coupling with the C-N-P cycling network in the unique rice-Azolla co-cultivation system. By designing four fertilization strategies and comparing two rice cultivation patterns (rice monoculture (R) and rice-Azolla co-cultivation (RA) systems), we comprehensively analyzed the interactions between soil nutrient cycling and rhizosphere bacterial microecological communities in rice polyculture systems. The specific objectives of the study were (1) to analyze the effects of Azolla on the activities of key enzymes involved in soil carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus cycling; (2) to explain the mechanism by which the addition of Azolla affects the diversity and complexity of soil bacterial communities; (3) to investigate the interactions between the bacterial community and the soil nutrient cycling network under Azolla. We hypothesize that: (1) Azolla enhances soil nitrogen cycling through its biological nitrogen fixation capacity; and (2) by serving as a green manure, it amplifies bacterial diversity and network complexity by facilitating microbial dispersal and strengthening potential interactions across soil microhabitats [25].

Materials and methods

Study site and experimental design

The field trial was conducted during 2023–2024 at the Modern Agricultural Science and Technology Innovation Demonstration Station of the Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (30°40′N, 103°54′E) (Fig. 1A), spanning two rice-growing seasons. The study site is located in southwestern China and is characterized by a subtropical humid monsoon climate. Soil type is classified as permeable paddy soil sub-class. The topsoil properties are as follows: pH = 5.83, soil water content = 29.7%, organic matter content = 26.36 g kg−1, total phosphorus (TP) = 0.60 g kg−1, and total nitrogen (TN) = 1.92 g kg−1. In this study, the rice variety "Yongyou 4949" and the rape variety "Chuan Youyan 33" were used. Rice was planted in equal rows with a row spacing of 30 cm and hole spacing of 18 cm, with 3–4 seedlings per hole. The plots were separated by a film-covered ridge (ridge width = 40 cm) to prevent water and fertilizer interference between the plots (Fig. 1B). The experimental plot is 3 m long and 4 m wide. It was replicated three times and a completely random design was adopted.

The rice monoculture system (R) served as the control group, while the rice-Azolla co-cultivation system (RA) (Fig. 1C) served as the comparison group. Based on this setup, different reduced nitrogen gradients were established in combination with local cultivation practices (nitrogen fertilizer application), as follows: − 0% (180 kg N ha−1, N180), − 25% (135 kg N ha−1, N135), − 75% (45 kg N ha−1, N45), and − 100% (0 kg N ha−1, N0). Add 0.7 t ha−1 of Azolla at the seedling stage of rice into the RA. The climatic conditions during the experimental period are shown in Fig. 1D. The fertilizers applied were urea, potassium oxide, and superphosphate. Detailed information on the treatments, fertilizer application rates, and agronomic measures used during the experiment is provided in Table 1. Rice seedlings were raised in mid-May each year, transplanting began in early June, and harvesting occurred in late September of the same year, with a growth period of 120–130 days.

Soil sampling and preservation

Five soil samples were collected by the stainless-steel soil corer from the surface layer (0–20 cm) of each treatment at rice maturity. After removing the gravel and impurities, the samples were thoroughly mixed and divided into two portions. One portion was immediately placed in a dry ice container and subsequently transferred to a − 80 ℃ freezer for long-term storage. The other portion was air-dried at room temperature, finely ground using a mortar, and sieved for further analysis of soil enzyme activity.

Soil enzyme measurements

Soil enzyme activities [amylase (G0318W), sucrase (G0302W), β-D-cellobiosidase (CBH, G0323W), urease (G0301W), and acid phosphatase (ACP, G0304W)] were measured using the corresponding analytical kits (Grace Biotechnology, Suzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Sequencing and analysis of soil microorganisms

First, 1.0 g fresh soil samples were used for microbial DNA extraction following the instructions provided with the E.Z.N.A.® SOIL DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, U.S.A.). The DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop NC2000 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, U.S.A.). The 16S rRNA gene of bacteria was amplified using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), targeting the V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene. The PCR procedure involved 94 °C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 94 °C for 45 s, 40 °C for 60 s, and 72 °C for 90 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR amplification and sequencing were performed by Personalbio Gene Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The raw sequences were processed using Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME2, version 2.1) software for dereplication, filtering, and analysis, with sequence denoising conducted according to the DADA2 workflow. Operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering was performed at 100% similarity using Mothur software (version 1.48.0, http://www.mothur.org/) based on the Greengenes2 database (http://ftp.microbio.me/greengenes_release/). All sequences underwent stringent quality filtering to remove chimeras and non-target sequences prior to the microbial community composition and diversity analyses. Bacterial classification and diversity analyses were conducted using Parson’s Biological Companies platform (https://www.genescloud.cn/).

Statistical analysis

Soil enzyme activity was analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). α-diversity was characterized using the Shannon and Simpson indices for species diversity within habitats; Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity (PD), Allen’s H, and Rao quadratic entropy indices for evolutionary-based diversity; Pielou’s evenness index for evenness; and Good’s coverage index for coverage. The Shannon and Simpson indices were used to reduce dimensionality and quantify bacterial diversity. β-diversity was assessed using the Bray–Curtis distance method. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) maps were generated using the R package "phyloseq". Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) integrates non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) to identify significant differences in key bacterial taxa. In each treatment, for the amplified sequence variants (ASVs) with relative abundance greater than 0.5%, we used the R software packages "WGCNA" and "igraph", and set the filtering parameters of |r|> 0.6 and P < 0.001 to conduct the co-occurrence network analysis. The packages "Hmisc", "RcolorBrewer", "igraph", "psych", "RColorBrewer" and "grDevices" were used to select ASVs with a relative abundance greater than 1% in each treatment, and to analyze their correlation with soil enzyme activity. The filtering parameters of |r|> 0.6 and P < 0.001 were set for constructing the co-occurrence network. Network visualization was performed in the Gephi software. Mantel’r analysis was conducted to interpret the relationships among the top-ten bacterial phyla based on their relative abundance, microbial community attributes, and soil enzyme activities. All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, U.S.A.) and R (version 4.4.3; http://www.r-project.org/).

Results

Soil enzyme activity

During the experimental period, Azolla application increased the pH in 2024 but decreased it in 2023, while enhancing soil SOC, TN, and TP contents in both years (Table S1). Soil enzyme activities were affected to some extent by the planting patterns. Under the different nitrogen fertilizer concentrations, Azolla addition significantly increased soil amylase activity by 7–26% (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A); CBH activity was significantly enhanced by 32–44% only under high nitrogen fertilizer concentrations (135 and 180 kg ha−1) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C); and sucrase activity did not show significant changes (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Compared with the R treatment, Azolla addition increased urease activity by 3–37%, with significant effects observed only at N45 and N180 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D). Additionally, ACP activity was unaffected by nitrogen fertilizer concentration but was significantly reduced by 29% and 42% after Azolla addition in the N45 and N135 treatments (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2E). The incorporation of Azolla significantly altered the activities of key enzymes involved in C, N, and P cycling. Specifically, it stimulated C and N turnover while suppressing the P activation, thereby differentially regulating these nutrient pathways.

Soil bacterial composition and diversity

The addition of Azolla influenced the soil bacterial composition and diversity in the experimental paddy fields. The five dominant bacterial phyla across all the treatments were Proteobacteria, Chloroflexi, Acidobacteriota, Actinobacteriota, and Myxococcota (Fig. 3A and Table S2). The major bacterial genera identified were KD4-96 and Anaeromyxobacter (Fig. 3B and Table S3). Compared with the R treatment, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria decreased in the RA treatment, and that of Gemmatimonadota decreased significantly (P < 0.05), whereas the relative abundance of Actinobacteriota decreased with increasing nitrogen fertilizer concentrations(Fig. 3A and Table S2).The Shannon index indicated that the diversity of the RA treatment was significantly higher than that of the R treatment under the N45 treatment, whereas the opposite trend was observed under the N135 treatment (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3C). Under low nitrogen fertilizer concentrations, compared with the R treatment, the Simpson index of the RA treatment increased in the N0 and N45 treatments, whereas, with the exception of the N0 treatment, the opposite trend was observed in the N135 and N180 treatments (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3D). The trend in the bacterial diversity index was consistent with that of the Simpson index (Fig. 3E). All soil samples were analyzed using PCoA based on the Bray–Curtis distance. The first two principal coordinates explained 14.7% and 11.1% of the total variation in the bacterial community structure, respectively, with the samples clustered separately, except for the RAN45 and RAN135 treatments (Fig. 3F). Azolla inoculation fundamentally reshaped the bacterial community structure, as evidenced by PCoA where treatments largely clustered by cultivation pattern (Fig. 3F). Crucially, the effect of Azolla on bacterial diversity was contingent on nitrogen availability. It promoted diversity under low nitrogen (N0 and N45) but suppressed it under high nitrogen (N135 and N180) (Fig. 3E). This nitrogen-dependent effect challenges the simplistic view of biodiversity response and indicates that Azolla’s ecological impact is mediated by soil nutrient context.

Key soil bacteria taxa

To identify the specific bacterial taxa that were enriched, LEfSe analysis was performed based on Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, with a significance threshold of 0.05 and an LDA score threshold of 3.85. The RN45 treatment group had the most abundant key bacterial groups (Fig. 4A). The results of the relative abundance heatmap and evolutionary clustering analyses for the top 20 bacterial genera revealed that the bacterial communities in RAN0, RAN45, and RN135 were highly similar, whereas those in RAN45 and RN135 were significantly different (Fig. 4B). In the RAN180 and RN180 treatments, the most closely related bacterial genera, Haliangium versus Thiobacillus, Gemmatimonas versus S0134_terrestrial_group, Anaeromyxobacter versus Bacteroidetes_vadinHA17, and Sideroxydans versus Candidatus_Koribacter showed opposite trends. Additionally, Anaerolinea and ADurb.Bin063-1, which are closely related, displayed opposing abundance trends in the RN135 and RAN135 treatments. Furthermore, the bacterial genera Candidatus_Solibacter and SBR1031, as well as Sideroxydans and Candidatus_Koribacter, showed contrasting trends under the RN45, RAN180, and RAN135 treatments. LEfSe analysis and heatmap results demonstrated that Azolla incorporation enriched specific key bacterial taxa, with this enrichment effect varying significantly across nitrogen gradient treatments.

The co-occurrence network analysis of ASVs with relative bacterial abundances greater than 0.5% (Fig. 5, Tables 2) shows that the proportion of positive edges exceeded that of negative edges in the networks for all treatments (Table 2). The bacterial co-occurrence networks for the RA treatment indicated that network complexity—as represented by nodes, edges, average degree, and density—increased with increasing nitrogen concentration. Compared with the R treatment, the complexity of the bacterial co-occurrence network decreased in the low-nitrogen fertilizer treatments after Azolla application but exhibited the opposite trend in the high nitrogen fertilizer concentration treatments (Table 2). Classification of the nodes at the phylum level revealed that the dominant bacterial phyla included Proteobacteria, Acidobacteriota, Chloroflexi, Gemmatimonadota, and Actinobacteriota (Table S4). In the RA treatment, the proportions of Bacteroidota and Actinobacteriota were higher in low-nitrogen treatments compared to high-nitrogen treatments. Similarly, the proportion of Gemmatimonadota was higher under the low-nitrogen conditions and was reduced by Azolla application under the same nitrogen concentration. The proportions of Gemmatimonadota, Chloroflexi, and Desulfobacterota were highest in the N0 treatment, the proportion of Proteobacteria was highest in the N45 treatment, and the proportion of Nitrospirota was highest in the N135 treatment (Table S4). Co-occurrence network analysis revealed that Azolla addition significantly altered bacterial interaction patterns, simplifying network structure under low nitrogen but enhancing its complexity under high nitrogen. This finding links macroscopic community changes to microscopic interspecific interactions.

Relationships between key bacterial taxa and environmental factors

RDA, Mantel’r, and co-occurrence network analyses elucidated the relationships between key bacterial taxa in the soil and soil enzyme activity (Fig. 6). Axes 1 and 2 of the RDA explained 29.73% and 20.79% of the total bacterial variation in the R treatment, and 47.31% and 13.87% in the RA treatment, respectively (Fig. 6A, B). Mantel’r analysis revealed that bacterial complexity was correlated with urease activity in the R treatment and with amylase activity in the RA treatment. Bacterial composition and complexity were strongly associated with environmental factors in the RA treatment (Fig. 6C, D). The co-occurrence network was used to visualize interactions between bacterial phyla with relative abundances greater than 1% in the soil and soil enzyme activities (Fig. 6E, F). In the R treatment, the correlation between bacterial phyla and soil enzymes was predominantly negative under low nitrogen fertilizer concentrations but positive under high nitrogen fertilizer concentrations. Furthermore, the number of edges and nodes was lower in the low nitrogen fertilizer concentration treatment than in the high nitrogen fertilizer concentration treatment, whereas the opposite trend was observed for the RA treatment. Among all the treatments, Proteobacteria had the highest number of nodes, followed by Chloroflexi, Acidobacteriota, Myxococcota, and Gemmatimonadota. Amylase was the soil enzyme most frequently involved in the co-occurrence networks (Table S5). Mantel’r and network analyses jointly confirmed that the RA significantly strengthened the associations between the bacterial community and environmental factors such as soil enzyme activities. This indicates that the introduction of Azolla enhances the coupling between the microbial community and nutrient cycling functions, making the bacterial micro-ecology respond to and participate in soil biochemical processes more directly. This result organically connects the aforementioned community changes and network structure with ecosystem functions (nutrient cycling), helping to explain how the Azolla affects soil ecological functions.

Discussion

This study underscores the critical roles of bacterial properties and soil nutrient cycling in shaping soil microbial ecology. Our results demonstrate that the addition of Azolla influenced soil nutrient cycling by enhancing carbon and nitrogen cycling while suppressing phosphorus cycling. Simultaneously, the treatment altered the composition, diversity, and network complexity of rhizosphere bacterial communities. Specifically, the addition of Azolla increased bacterial diversity and reduced network complexity under low-nitrogen conditions, whereas the opposite trend was observed under high-nitrogen conditions. Overall, our findings reveal that the addition of Azolla not only promotes soil nutrient cycling but also reshapes the bacterial microecological structure and interaction network in the rhizosphere, which has significant implications for advancing the sustainable development of paddy ecosystems.

Soil key bacterial taxa in the RA treatment played a positive role in improving soil quality

Soil microorganisms play an important role in soil composition and quality by participating in nutrient cycling and organic matter decomposition [26]. We investigated the effects of Azolla addition on key taxa within the rhizosphere soil bacterial communities. Proteobacteria, Acidobacteriota, Chloroflexi, Gemmatimonadota, and Actinobacteriota were dominant across all treatments, which is consistent with previous studies [27]. Compared with the R treatment, the RA treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Actinobacteriota while decreasing that of Gemmatimonadota (P < 0.05). This is attributed to the input of Azolla, which elevated carbon and biologically fixed nitrogen, thereby favoring copiotrophic Actinobacteria. These bacteria exhibit metabolic diversity and antibiotic‑producing traits that enhance their competitiveness in nutrient‑enriched environments [28]. In contrast, Gemmatimonadota are adapted to oxidized and phosphorus‑limited conditions [29]. Their decline aligns with the Azolla‑driven shift toward more reduced soil conditions and alleviated phosphorus limitation [24]. Compared with R treatment, RA significantly increased the relative abundance of Haliangium by 1.28 times (P < 0.001). It is speculated that RA has a higher C cycling capacity (Fig. 3B) [30]. Compared with R, the Azolla accumulates SC-I-84, Candidatus_Solibacter genus, Anaerolinea genus and Sphingomonas genus involved in the N cycling process. Among them, Candidatus_Solibacter and Sphingomonas are nitrogen-fixing microorganisms (Fig. 3B) [31–35]. Nitrogen-fixing microorganisms convert atmospheric N2 into usable ammonia through biological nitrogen fixation, providing nitrogen sources for the surrounding environment and improving soil quality [36]. It is reported that Anaerolinea can promote the phosphorus cycle by facilitating the migration of phosphorus [37] (Fig. 3B). The application of Azolla increased the abundance of this bacterial genus. The Azolla accumulates bacterial genera related to C-N-P cycling in the soil, helping to improve soil quality, which is consistent with our hypothesis.

Co-occurrence network analysis facilitates a deeper understanding of the complexity of microbial communities, their relationships with environmental factors, and the role of microorganisms in maintaining the network structure [38, 39]. The commonly used parameters for evaluating the complexity of soil microbial networks include the nodes, edges, modularity, and average degree of network topology [40]. In the RA treatment, network complexity increased with increasing N fertilizer concentration (Fig. 5B), as soil bacteria were less nitrogen-limited, and nitrogen fertilizer enhanced bacterial interactions, thereby increasing the co-occurrence network complexity. This is consistent with the findings of Tian et al. [41]. Nitrogen addition has been shown to increase the availability of soil nutrients, promote microbial cooperation, and improve energy conversion efficiency [42, 43]. Positive correlations within microbial networks have also been reported to indicate community instability, because communities with more positive correlations may generate positive feedback and co-oscillations in response to environmental disturbances, leading to instability [44]. Negative correlations reflect limited environmental resources and high competition for these resources; when environmental resources are abundant and competition decreases, the proportion of negative correlations in the co-occurrence network decreases [45]. Compared to the R treatment, Azolla application increased community stability under low nitrogen fertilizer concentrations and reduced resource competition under high nitrogen fertilizer concentrations. (Table S4). During its growth process, Azolla synthesizes a large amount of N. After decomposition, it can bring C to the environment. Therefore, it can enrich C and N cycling-related microorganisms in the paddy field, changing the microbial composition and function, and thereby affecting the co-occurrence network. In summary, RA increases the community stability of the low-nitrogen fertilizer concentration treatment group, reduces the resource competition of the high-nitrogen fertilizer concentration treatment group, and increases the relative abundance of beneficial microbial groups and improves soil quality.

Soil bacteria microbial ecology promoted nutrient cycling under the RA treatment

The specific environment formed during the growth of Azolla alters the microbial ecology of soil bacteria. The addition of Azolla led to an increase in functional bacteria related to nutrient cycling, and enhanced the correlation between bacteria and soil enzyme activity, resulting in a decrease in diversity in the RA treatment group but an improvement in soil quality. (Fig. 4A and Table S5). The enrichment effect and functional enhancement of key microorganisms by leguminous cover crops have also been confirmed in other studies [46]. Enzymatic activity is widely used as an indicator of soil quality because of its sensitivity to soil management practices; however, assessing soil quality based on the activity of a single enzyme is often inaccurate [47, 48]. Therefore, a suite of enzymes, including amylase, sucrase, CBH, urease, and ACP, are essential for evaluating soil quality and providing a more comprehensive assessment. The RDA identified five enzymes as critical factors influencing key bacterial genera in the studied soils (Fig. 6A, B). It has been reported that mineral fertilization may reduce the activities of C-, N-, and P-related hydrolases due to decreased soil heterotrophic respiration [49]. Compared with the R treatment, the RA treatment increased the activities of enzymes related to C and N cycling but decreased the activities of enzymes related to P cycling. Compared with the R treatment, Azolla application in paddy fields significantly promoted soil amylase activity (P < 0.05) and CBH activity under high nitrogen fertilizer concentrations (P < 0.05) but had no significant effect on sucrase activity, suggesting a stronger C cycling ability in the RA treatment (Fig. 2A–C). Compared with the R treatment, Azolla addition enhanced urease activity. There was, however, no significant difference in urease activity across the different nitrogen fertilizer concentrations in the RA treatment, likely attributable to the nitrogen supplied by the endosymbiotic cyanobacteria in Azolla mitigating the nitrogen concentration differences between the treatments (Fig. 2D) [50, 51].

Additionally, Mantel’r analysis revealed that the RA treatment increased the correlations between bacterial microbial ecology, soil enzymes, and key microbial groups compared to the R treatment. The microbial ecology under the RA treatment regulated soil nutrient cycling (Fig. 6C, D). The increase in soil microbial diversity and the decrease in acid phosphatase activity (Fig. 3C, D; Fig. 2E) were inconsistent with the findings of other studies [26, 52]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the reduced substrate concentration caused by P absorption during Azolla growth [37, 53]. The co-occurrence network between key bacterial taxa and soil enzyme activity indicated that Azolla had a modulating effect (Fig. 6E, F). Crucially, recent studies have demonstrated that increasing soil enzymatic activity promotes soil nutrient cycling, thereby improving soil quality [42, 54]. The decomposition of Azolla significantly increased SOC and TN content, shifting microbial nutrient status towards reduced phosphorus limitation [55]. Under these conditions, extracellular phosphorus acquisition became metabolically inefficient, leading to a decline in ACP activity, which aligns with predictions from ecological stoichiometry. This shift coincided with increased activities of carbon- and nitrogen-cycling enzymes and elevated abundance of microbial taxa associated with carbon and nitrogen transformation, indicating a reallocation of microbial energy investment away from phosphorus mineralization pathways. Notably, the RA treatment exhibited a higher nutrient cycling efficiency and enriched more functional bacterial taxa than the R treatment, further enhancing soil quality. Under high nitrogen fertilizer concentrations, Azolla addition reduces bacterial diversity but improves soil quality [21].

Limitations

As highlighted by previous studies, co-occurrence networks alone do not provide definitive evidence of ecological interactions and should be integrated with additional evidence [56]. Furthermore, given that this study spanned only two years, a longer-term experiment is required to validate the reported findings. In the future, analyses of the rhizosphere metabolome and microbiome could be combined to further investigate the effects of Azolla on paddy soil health and cultivation. And further study the interaction mechanisms among Azolla, nitrogen fertilizers, and microbial communities under different nitrogen concentration gradients.

Conclusions

Our results demonstrate that a rice-Azolla co-cultivation system enhanced soil nutrient cycling, reshaped the rhizosphere microecological structure, and further improved soil quality by modulating bacterial diversity. The incorporation of Azolla optimized soil quality by regulating rhizosphere bacterial microbial ecology and enhancing soil nutrient-cycling efficiency. Our findings underscore the pivotal roles of soil bacterial microbial ecology and soil enzyme activity in these processes. The application of Azolla increased the complexity of the bacterial network while reducing its stability under high nitrogen fertilizer conditions, whereas it stabilized the bacterial network under low nitrogen fertilizer conditions. Notably, compared with the rice monoculture system, the addition of Azolla facilitated soil C and N cycling and slowed P cycling in the soil rhizosphere. Overall, this study demonstrates the critical role of Azolla in paddy field systems, offering a robust evidence base for quantifying and interpreting its effects. Importantly, the results suggest that the rice-Azolla co-cultivation represents an effective agricultural practice for promoting healthy and sustainable soil development.
