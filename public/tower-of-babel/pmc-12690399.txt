Implementing a Multidimensional Nursing Protocol in Dabie Bandavirus‐Induced SFTS With Critical Complications
Authors: Yongping Qiu, Minrui Li, Tingting Li, Jing Liu, Xiaodan Zhu, Shaoqin Zheng, Sha Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case demonstrates that a comprehensive, multidimensional nursing protocol—encompassing intensive monitoring, complication‐specific management, and rigorous infection control—is associated with favorable outcomes in this critically ill severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) patient with complex comorbidities like Parkinson's disease.

Parkinson's disease

Background

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is a tick‐borne infectious disease first identified in rural areas of China, particularly in Hubei and Henan, during 2009–2010 [1]. As an emerging hemorrhagic fever, SFTS is caused by a novel bunyavirus, now classified as the SFTS virus or Dabie bandavirus [2]. With four major stages of incubation, febrile phase, multi‐organ failure, and convalescence, SFTS is characterized by clinical manifestations of severe fever, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, and multi‐organ dysfunction, usually accompanied by gastrointestinal symptoms [1]. SFTS is transmitted mainly through tick bites but can also spread through direct contact with infected blood, blood‐containing secretions, excreta, or contaminated objects [3], posing a public health threat due to regional outbreaks [4]. With distinct seasonality, SFTS occurs predominantly between April and October, peaking from May to July. Individuals at the highest risk are farmers with frequent exposure to tick habitats, particularly those working in hilly or mountainous areas of endemic regions [5]. Notably, the case fatality rate of SFTS has declined from initial reports of 30% to approximately 5% due to improved diagnosis and management in China [6, 7, 8]. Despite this progress, its mortality remains high among severe cases, necessitating enhanced surveillance, and epidemiological research.

Furthermore, acting as a common neurodegenerative disorder, Parkinson's disease (PD) occurs dominantly in middle‐aged and elderly individuals. Patients may frequently experience motor symptoms and non‐motor symptoms [9]. Clinically, there is an exceptionally rare co‐occurrence of SFTS and PD. Such patients often present with numerous complications, posing complicated management challenges in the clinical setting. There is currently a paucity of reports on such cases both domestically and internationally, resulting in a significant lack of relevant nursing experience, presenting substantial challenges for clinical treatment and care. In October 2024, our department treated an elderly PD patient who developed Dabie bandavirus‐induced SFTS. Successfully, the patient recovered and was discharged after 22 days of comprehensive clinical treatment and meticulous nursing care, sustained positive recovery was observed during 3 months of post‐discharge follow‐up (1 week, 1 month, and 3 months).

Case Presentation

A 79‐year‐old male farmer developed fever, vomiting, and altered consciousness after falling into the water while fishing on October 8, 2024. On October 13, the patient was admitted to the People's Hospital of a certain county for treatment. Physical examination of the patient observed a tick bite on the left side of the waist, with complete removal of the tick (Figure 1). A blood test was ordered by contacting the local Center for Disease Control, showing a positive nucleic acid test for Dabie bandavirus, with a cycle threshold (Ct) value of 20.38. And the patient was transferred to the Emergency Department of our hospital for resuscitation due to disease progression. On October 14, he was admitted to our department with a preliminary diagnosis of “tick bite, 5‐day fever, 1‐day altered consciousness, and Dabie bandavirus infection”. The diagnosis of SFTS was confirmed by metagenomic next‐generation sequencing (mNGS) of a blood sample, which detected a high viral load of Dabie bandavirus (35,827 reads). The patient appeared confused, with an acute illness appearance, systematic tremors (notably in the lower jaw and limbs), significant shaking, and markedly increased limb muscle tone. Meanwhile, scattered pale red maculopapular rashes were noted on the patient's chest, upper limbs, and thighs, as well as bite marks of around 1 × 1 cm and 0.5 × 0.5 cm visibly on the lateral aspect of the left thigh, the left calf gastrocnemius muscle, and the left waist, with notable neck stiffness and a positive Babinski sign.

Furthermore, the patient underwent a physical examination on admission, with a body temperature of 37.5°C, a pulse of 74 beats/min, a respiratory rate of 21 breaths/min, and a blood pressure of 129/84 mmHg. Chest CT showed pneumothorax in the posterior pleural space of the lower lobes of both lungs, and small amounts of pleural effusion bilaterally. This patient with preexisting PD presented with SFTS following a tick bite, complicated by multi‐organ dysfunction, involving upper gastrointestinal bleeding, infectious toxic encephalopathy, pulmonary infection, hemothorax, respiratory failure, rhabdomyolysis, abnormal liver function, hypoproteinemia, right renal agenesis, peritonitis, heart failure, hypoproteinemia, thrombocytopenia, and moderate anemia.

Nursing Assessment & Interventions

Formation of a Specialized Nursing Team for Coordinated Care

Given the patient's critical condition and advanced age, a multidisciplinary case management team was promptly established upon admission. Led by a nursing team leader (associate chief nurse specialized in infection control), the team included infection control and intensive care nurses, along with specialists in rehabilitation, nutrition, and psychological support. Using a “dual syndrome cross‐management” model, the team conducted daily joint rounds to analyze symptom correlations, perform comprehensive assessments, and formulate prioritized nursing interventions. This dual syndrome cross‐management model integrated infectious disease control protocols with PD‐specific care strategies, facilitating coordinated management of SFTS‐related complication prevention and acute PD symptom control.

Comprehensive Health Assessment and Identification of Key Nursing Issues

On admission, the patient was moderately nourished, arrived via stretcher, and presented with an acutely ill appearance. A focused nursing assessment revealed impaired consciousness, poor mental status, and severe whole‐body tremors—including prominent jaw and limb shaking—along with markedly increased limb muscle tone. Cooperation during the physical examination was limited. Maculopapular pale red rashes were noted on the chest, upper limbs, and thighs, alongside insect bite marks (approximately 1 × 1 cm and 0.5 × 0.5 cm) on the left thigh, left calf, neck, and left lumbar region.

Initial psychological assessment was unfeasible due to impaired consciousness. Posttreatment, the patient regained consciousness and was informed of his condition. Both the patient and family exhibited significant psychological distress, particularly regarding financial concerns, though family cooperation improved after counseling and health education. Social assessment revealed limited support as the elderly patient relied on financially constrained children with limited care capacity.

A tailored nursing model—including close monitoring, holistic care, and emotional support—enhanced the patient's confidence and ability to cope with treatment challenges.

Blockage of Transmission Routes and Prevention of Cross Infection

Given the high transmissibility of the virus, strict infection control measures were implemented to ensure the safety of healthcare workers and family members and prevent nosocomial spread. The patient was isolated in a single room, and both the patient and family received education regarding disease prevention and control. Daily environmental disinfection included peracetic acid for surfaces and 1000 mg/L chlorine‐based disinfectant for floors. Air disinfection was performed every 6 h for 60 min using an automated machine. Contaminated textiles were collected in red water‐soluble bags, further sealed within double‐layered yellow infectious waste bags, disinfected, labeled for contact isolation, and disposed of as infectious waste.

Vomit was managed using hydrogen peroxide‐based absorbent towels followed by disinfection with 1000 mg/L chlorine‐based solution. Strict personal protection protocols were enforced: all individuals in contact with the patient wore Level‐I protection, with double gloves and face shields required during procedures. PPE was doffed in a designated buffer zone with hand hygiene performed upon exit. All patient specimens were sealed, disinfected with 75% ethanol, placed in biohazard bags, and transported in secure specimen boxes by trained personnel to designated laboratories. Bodily fluids were pretreated with 2000 mg/L chlorine‐based solution for 30 min before disposal. Terminal disinfection after discharge involved spray disinfection with 500 mg/L chlorine‐based solution, ozone treatment for the bed unit, and use of an advanced disinfection machine.

Initiation of Intensive Care and Symptomatic Treatment

The patient's critical condition, advanced age, PD, and communication challenges necessitated an intensive care mode. As family involvement was limited during the acute infectious phase, the patient received 24‐h monitoring. Body temperature was regularly assessed and managed through physical cooling methods—such as sponging and cooling blankets—as well as antipyretic medications as prescribed. Infection markers were closely monitored to evaluate disease progression and treatment efficacy.

All antimicrobial agents, including meropenem, voriconazole, and ribavirin, were administered strictly as prescribed. Nursing care focused on ensuring medication safety through close monitoring for potential adverse effects, such as renal function with meropenem, hepatic function and visual disturbances with voriconazole, and hemolytic anemia with ribavirin. We vigilantly observed the patient's clinical response and promptly reported any changes to the attending physician. Neurological status was assessed through frequent interaction and observation for Parkinson's symptoms like tremors and rigidity. Levodopa was administered accurately and on schedule. The patient was assisted with in‐bed activities, regular repositioning, and chest physiotherapy. As tolerance improved, rehabilitation activities were gradually introduced, including bed‐to‐chair transitions, standing, and ambulation [10].

Prevention and Management of Potential Complications to Ensure Patient Safety

Upon admission, the patient exhibited decreased blood oxygen saturation and oxygen partial pressure, along with intermittent seizures accompanied by loss of consciousness and convulsions. Airway management was prioritized, with regular assessment of airway patency to prevent respiratory distress. Lung auscultation was performed to detect crackles or moist rales indicating mucus accumulation. Humidification was applied to facilitate sputum expulsion. If the patient was unable to cough autonomously, sputum was gently cleared from the oropharynx using a gauze‐wrapped hemostat. Once conscious and cooperative, the patient was encouraged to expectorate independently; suctioning was performed when necessary.

Continuous monitoring included observation for tachypnea, respiratory distress, oxygen saturation, and arterial blood gases. Any abnormalities were promptly reported, and oxygen or high‐flow oxygen therapy was administered as ordered. Given the risk of multi‐organ bleeding in SFTS, the patient was closely monitored for bleeding signs, including changes in skin ecchymoses, petechiae, characteristics of gastric drainage, and hematuria. Laboratory results were reviewed regularly to evaluate bleeding status and treatment response. During bleeding episodes, fasting and fluid restriction were enforced. Medications including sulfamethoxazole‐trimethoprim (compound sulfamethoxazole, a broad‐spectrum antibiotic for prevention of secondary bacterial infection), aminocaproic acid (for bleeding prevention), esomeprazole (for gastrointestinal protection), and somatostatin (for upper gastrointestinal bleeding control) were administered as prescribed, along with nutritional and electrolyte solutions. Close monitoring of clinical responses was continued, with timely reporting of abnormalities to the attending physician.

Provision of Timely Psychological Support to Alleviate Fear in the Patient and Family

Given the rarity of SFTS and the patient's comorbid PD—which contributed to motor and cognitive impairments—effective communication with the family was essential. Initially, the family had mistaken the condition for a common mosquito bite and underestimated its infectivity. Admission heightened fear yet increased awareness of the disease's severity.

A psychological support team led by the head nurse and involving experienced infection care nurses was formed. From admission, multiple counseling sessions were provided to the patient and family under psychological guidance. Detailed explanations covered disease etiology, treatment plans, and potential challenges. Family members received education on infection prevention to enhance care capability and reduce anxiety. Throughout hospitalization, concerns were actively listened to and addressed, and daily living needs were attentively supported. Outcomes from similar cases were shared to strengthen confidence in recovery.

Recurrent education was provided on disease management and precautions. Special attention was paid to the family's concern regarding reinfection; additional targeted guidance was provided by the medical and nursing team. These interventions markedly improved the family's understanding and cooperation, enabling them to provide informed and supportive care post‐discharge.

Implementation of Post‐Discharge Continuity of Care

The patient was followed up via telephone regularly (1 week, 1 month, and 3 months post‐discharge) by our case management team members to monitor the recurrence of SFTS symptoms (fever, bleeding tendency) and the progression of PD (tremors, and medication adherence). Based on the “Technical Guidelines for the Prevention and Control of Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome (2024 Edition)” issued by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention [11], a series of health education measures were developed, such as the “Tick Prevention Health Guidance Manual” for family members. A WeChat‐based real‐time consultation channel was established to guide home‐based rehabilitation exercises, thus ensuring seamless continuity of care between healthcare providers and family members. Following intervention, the patient had negative viral tests, stabilized PD symptoms, and significantly improved family caregiving capabilities. No recurrence or readmission was observed during follow‐up.

Evaluation of Outcomes

Following 22 days of hospitalization and multidimensional nursing care, the patient achieved negative Dabie bandavirus nucleic acid test results and demonstrated significant functional recovery. Key improvements included: mobility progressing from bedbound to ambulating with assistance; dietary intake advancing from nasogastric tube feeding to an oral soft diet; and oxygen requirement decreasing from noninvasive ventilation to room air at discharge. No nursing‐sensitive adverse events occurred, and the patient and family reported a satisfaction score of 9.8/10 with the nursing care. Follow‐up via telephone and WeChat at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months post‐discharge confirmed a sustained recovery with no readmissions or recurrence of SFTS symptoms. Furthermore, the structured health education effectively enhanced the family's care capacity and health literacy, reducing anxiety and mitigating the risk of future reinfection.

Discussion

Development of Nursing Team and Preparation for the Admission of Relevant Patients at Any Time

Dabie bandavirus infection can cause symptoms such as fever, headache, muscle pain, and fatigue, and even hemorrhagic fever, shock, and death in severe cases. The condition can deteriorate rapidly, especially in elderly patients and those with underlying medical conditions after infection, potentially resulting in multi‐organ failure and high mortality. Nursing care for such patients is especially difficult given the diversity of symptoms and the rapid onset of the disease, requiring a high level of vigilance and adaptability by healthcare providers. Clinically, beyond heavy work and psychological stress that induce physical and mental exhaustion, healthcare workers are the major exposure population to Dabie bandavirus infection during nursing, encountering high infection risks and prolonged periods of intensive care.

Therefore, nursing staff should participate in training and learning activities regularly organized by departments to enhance their understanding of Dabie bandavirus and improve their nursing skills. Additionally, their abilities to manage patients with the infection should also be enhanced based on the optimization of facilities, equipment, and personnel.

Health Education on Dabie Bandavirus Infection

Infection with Dabie bandavirus represents an emerging infectious disease characterized by distinct seasonal prevalence, primarily occurring between April and September each year [12, 13]. Post‐infected patients and their families often lack adequate knowledge about the disease, leading to fear and anxiety that may impair treatment compliance. Hospitals should strengthen public health education focused on the epidemiological features of the infection to reduce tick bite‐related transmission. Detailed explanations should be provided to patients and families upon admission to alleviate psychological distress. Such efforts can enhance family involvement in care, reduce the burden on healthcare staff, and promote more patient‐centered nursing practices. Furthermore, upon discharge, patients and families should receive individualized guidance on protective measures against tick bites and precautions for outdoor activities during high‐risk seasons to prevent reinfection.

Follow‐Up on the Pathogenesis and Drug Research Progress of Dabie Bandavirus

Currently, symptomatic and supportive care are the primary therapeutic options for patients infected with Dabie bandavirus, as no antiviral therapy has been definitively proven effective, leading to increased complexity and uncertainty in nursing care. This patient we reported also had PD concurrently. Existing studies have documented that infection with Dabie bandavirus itself can affect patients' central nervous system and cause severe complications [14, 15], leading to symptoms such as confusion, seizures, and coma. Therefore, it highlights the importance of closely monitoring patients' condition during treatment to determine whether neurological symptoms are complications of SFTS and PD. Healthcare professionals should stay updated on research progress regarding the pathogenesis and treatment of Dabie bandavirus.

Conclusion

In summary, this case of SFTS complicated by PD highlights critical nursing considerations and suggests that a comprehensive, multidimensional nursing management approach is essential. Successful management was supported by active collaboration among healthcare providers, patients, and their families, manifested through a dedicated multidisciplinary team, protective isolation, inhospital special‐grade nursing, and post‐discharge continuous care. This experience provides a valuable reference for nursing recommendations and improvements when caring for similar complex cases.
