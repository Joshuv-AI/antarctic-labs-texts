Evaluating 360-degree virtual reality live streaming for remote workplace inspections: a cross-sectional pilot study
Authors: Hajime Ando, Shizuka Kurosaki, Yuta Akiyama, Ryutaro Matsugaki, Akira Ogami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn Japan, occupational health physicians are required to conduct workplace inspections to assess health hazards, but resource constraints pose challenges. Although certain physician duties, such as worker interviews, can be remotely performed under specific conditions, workplace inspections must still be conducted in person. We evaluated the feasibility of virtual reality (VR) live streaming in 360-degree video as a potential solution, addressing prior limitations, such as restricted viewing angles and transmission delays.MethodsA cross-sectional experimental study was conducted in a simulated factory environment with four occupational health physicians. Three conditions were assessed: (1) on-site inspection with remote VR guidance, (2) remote VR viewing of on-site filming, and (3) remote personal computer (PC) viewing of on-site filming. A 360-degree camera (RICOH THETA X) and VR headset (Meta Quest 2) were used for 4K live streaming at 5 fps.ResultsVideo latency was reduced to 1–3 s, markedly improved from 15 s in prior studies. Visual acuity decreased to 0.1–0.2 for all participants when using VR. Participants identified an average of 21.0 inspection points (out of 25) using VR headset compared with 19.25 via PC.ConclusionsVR live streaming is a viable approach for remote workplace inspections. Although reduced visual acuity remains a limitation, it may help balance workplace visibility with confidentiality. However, environmental factors, such as noise, odor, temperature, humidity, and glare, were not adequately represented, necessitating the use of supplementary measurement tools. Further research is needed to evaluate its applicability across diverse settings and long-term effectiveness.

Introduction

In Japan, occupational hygiene efforts have historically been led by physicians, partly because infectious diseases, such as tuberculosis, were a major challenge in workplaces1). Since 1972, this approach has been codified in the Japanese Industrial Safety and Health Act2), placing the responsibility for assessing workplace health risks on physicians3) — a role that may be performed by occupational hygienists in other countries, such as the United States.

Regular occupational health inspections play a critical role in identifying and mitigating factors that may pose health risks. However, the current number of dedicated occupational health physicians in Japan is insufficient to meet demand4). Their scope of tasks is extensive, yet the resources available to them and allocated time for each workplace are limited4). Therefore, it is necessary to perform the required activities as efficiently as possible.

With the Japanese working-age population projected to decline5), more efficient occupational health practices are necessary. Among the activities performed by occupational health physicians, worker interviews can be conducted online under specific conditions, a practice that became particularly important during the coronavirus disease pandemic6,7). Nonetheless, concerns, such as decreased satisfaction and other challenges, have been reported8). In contrast, online workplace inspections are currently not permitted, likely owing to the perceived difficulty of comprehensively assessing workplace conditions remotely. One potential limitation of online inspections is a restricted field of view, which may hinder the identification of occupational hazards. A broad visual perspective is essential for detecting hazardous factors; however, conventional online systems do not adequately provide this capability. To address this issue, in 2019, we conducted a preliminary trial using virtual reality (VR) live streaming with a 360-degree video feed to enable a wider field of view9). Although the system allowed visualization of designated pointers within the VR environment, a key limitation was an average latency of 15 s9). The trial utilized Facebook Live, a platform widely employed in medical education10,11). Several factors may account for the delay, including Facebook Live being a United States-based service, which may result in slower communication from Japan. Moreover, the platform likely implements a buffer period to ensure stable communication.

In recent years, with advancements in technology, lower-latency streaming is becoming increasingly viable. Successful applications using WebRTC technology have been reported in the context of low-latency video streaming12). However, its feasibility for VR-based workplace inspections remains unclear. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility of 360-degree VR-based workplace inspections with reduced latency to address prior limitations, such as restricted viewing angles and transmission delays.

Methods

Research design and participants

This cross-sectional experimental study was conducted in February 2024 at a multipurpose simulated factory (7 m×13.7 m) at the University of Occupational and Environmental Health, Japan. The simulated factory is designed to resemble the structure of a typical Japanese factory, is currently used as a lecture space, and includes various personal protective equipment, posters, and signs on display. A simulated workplace inspection environment was established, and remote inspections via VR live streaming were performed.

The study participants included four physicians specializing in occupational medicine, with experience ranging from 2 to 20 years. Among them, three were residents in social medicine (primary specialization: occupational medicine), and one was a board-certified supervisory physician for public health and social medicine, a senior occupational health physician certified by the Japan Society for Occupational Health, and an Industrial Health Consultant. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Medical Research, University of Occupational and Environmental Health, Japan (approval no. ER23-054), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Experimental setup

The inspection area (5.5 m×10 m) contained 25 designated inspection points, including areas requiring attention and examples of best practices. These points were determined through consultation between two researchers, both of whom are senior occupational health physicians, certified by the Japan Society for Occupational Health, and Industrial Health Consultants. To evaluate visual acuity, a 5-m visual acuity chart using Landolt C optotypes, which adhere to international standards, such as JIS T 7309 and ISO 8596, was installed, and distance measurements were performed using a laser rangefinder (GLM50C; Robert Bosch GmbH, Gerlingen, Germany). Room brightness and other conditions were not controlled, and this applied to all measurements. To simulate real-world working conditions, background noise (a composite of various machine sounds) was continuously played from a speaker within the inspection area. As a standardized reference point for setting the volume, the A-weighted sound level (time-weighting: Fast) was measured to be 60–80 dBA at a distance of 1 m from the speaker using an NL-42 (RION Co., Ltd., Kokubunji, Japan). This was intended to replicate a common level of workplace noise that would not prevent verbal communication. The designated inspection points also included an item to assess whether the ambient environmental noise could be perceived through the remote system. Equipment and system configuration

For remote live streaming, we used the RICOH THETA X (Ricoh Company, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) as a 360-degree camera, connected to an external battery (Anker 511 Power Bank; Anker Innovations, Changsha, China) (Figure 2). A custom ABS-made jig was designed and three-dimensionally printed to securely hold the camera and battery. The Meta Quest 2 (Meta Platforms, Inc., Menlo Park, CA, USA) was used as the VR headset for viewing the streamed footage (Figure 3).Fig. 2. VR headset for viewing VR videos

For video streaming, we employed the RICOH 4K Live Streaming API, with dedicated software developed for the personal computer (PC), Quest 2, and THETA360 using Node.js, Unity, and Kotlin, respectively. The video stream was set to 4K resolution, 5 fps, and 10 Mbps.

The network environment consisted of a three-carrier automatic connection mobile router (AiR-2; AIR-U Inc., Tokyo, Japan) on the filming side, while the viewing side was connected to the university Local Area Network, simulating real-world usage. For audio communication, we used bone conduction earphones and microphones (Shokz OpenRun Pro; Shokz, Shenzhen, China) on the filming side, and a PC microphone and speakers on the viewing side. Communication was facilitated through Google Meet (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA).

Experimental procedure

The experiment was conducted based on the following three design patterns:

Pattern 1 — On-site inspection with remote guidance: The participant performed an on-site workplace inspection and filmed, while the researcher (supervisor) provided guidance remotely via VR headset. This design reflected the Japanese legal requirement that workplace inspections are to be conducted on-site, at least once a month3). Moreover, it ensured that the participants were familiar with the workplace before remote inspections were conducted according to Patterns 2 and 3.

Pattern 2 — Remote viewing via VR headset: The researcher filmed on-site, and the participant viewed the footage remotely using VR headset.

Pattern 3 — Remote viewing via PC: The researcher filmed on-site, and the participant viewed the footage remotely on a PC screen.

A crossover comparison design was used. All participants started with Pattern 1, reflecting the Japanese legal requirement3). The order of Patterns 2 and 3 was then randomized. To ensure participant safety while using VR headset, participants were seated in rotatable chairs and instructed to adjust their viewpoint by rotating their head and chair. Additionally, researchers were present throughout the experiment to monitor safety.

Evaluation metrics

After each experiment, participants rated the following parameters on a scale of 1 to 10: (1) audio latency, (2) audio quality, (3) image latency, (4) image quality, (5) overall communication, and (6) overall evaluation. Additionally, the number of inspection items identified was recorded, along with free-text comments from participants. Visual acuity was assessed during each inspection, with binocular vision measured both on-site and remotely (using a PC or VR headset). For distance vision, binocular vision was evaluated while viewing the transmitted video using the participant’s usual corrective lenses.

Statistical analysis

The number of inspection points identified in the PC and VR conditions was compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, as the data were not expected to be normally distributed due to the small sample size. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using a commercial software package (Stata/SE 18.0; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Transmission performance evaluation

The video latency generally ranged from 1 to 3 s. However, multiple interruptions occurred during the experiment, requiring reconnection each time. Following reconnection, latency remained stable within the same range.

Visual acuity evaluation

All participants in the field test had a visual acuity of 1.2 under normal conditions. Nonetheless, when visual acuity was assessed using VR images on a PC screen or through VR headset, it decreased to 0.1–0.2 for all participants. This reduction in visual acuity persisted even when the zoom function was used on the PC screen.

Subjective evaluation

For audio transmission, the average latency and quality scores when viewing on a PC were 7.0 and 7.25, respectively. When using VR headset, these scores were 8.75 and 7.25, respectively. For video transmission, the average latency and quality scores for PC viewing were 7.0 and 5.5, respectively, whereas for VR headset, the scores were 8.75 and 5.75, respectively. For communication adequacy, the average score was 9.25 for PC viewing and 9.0 for VR headset viewing. The overall system evaluation yielded an average score of 7.67 when viewed on a PC and 6.0 when using VR headset.

Visibility of inspection points

Of the 25 predefined inspection points, an average of 19.25 were visible when viewed on a PC screen, whereas an average of 21.0 were visible when using VR headset; this difference was not statistically significant (p>0.05).

A review of the individual inspection points revealed differences in detection rates for several items between the two remote viewing methods. For instance, for “damaged ground terminal” (item 18), the number of participants who identified it was higher in the VR condition (two participants) than in the PC condition (zero participants). Similarly, for “lacquer spray” (item 13), the number of identifiers was higher in the VR condition (four participants) than in the PC condition (two participants). However, for the majority of items (19 out of 25), no notable differences in detection rates were observed.

Qualitative evaluation

In the free-text comments, participants cited several advantages of VR headset, including “the floor is easy to see”, “there is little VR sickness”, and “the low frame rate is not noticeable”. Meanwhile, disadvantages included “block noise is noticeable when moving”, “small text is difficult to read”, and “some colors are inaccurate”.

For PC screen viewing, reported advantages included “the zoom function can be used” and “the field of vision is wide”, whereas disadvantages included “image quality is insufficient”, “there is a time lag or disconnection”, and “it is difficult to search for new elements”.

Additionally, participants identified system-wide limitations, such as “the effects of noise are reduced more than they actually are”, “smell cannot be perceived”, and “the eye level differs from usual”. Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the feasibility of VR live streaming for workplace inspections from both the technical performance and practicality perspectives.

Regarding technical performance, we successfully reduced video latency to 1–3 s, eliminating the tens-of-seconds delay observed in our previous study9). The distribution platform used in this study is based on WebRTC technology, which is characterized by low latency12). Previous research has reported latencies of less than 1 s for VR live streaming13,14). When using mobile networks, LTE signaling has been identified as a potential bottleneck15). However, in this study, latency remained sufficiently low. Nonetheless, when relying on mobile connections, interruptions in streaming and block noise were observed due to communication instability.

While the 1–3 s latency is a notable improvement, it may still pose operational risks. For instance, the on-site person filming is not visually obstructed by the equipment and maintains a clear field of view; however, in scenarios where a remote expert (the viewer) identifies a risk that the on-site person is unaware of, the communication delay could prevent a timely warning to avert danger. To mitigate this risk, on-site filming should be conducted by a person with sufficient knowledge and experience in workplace safety, such as a local safety officer who would typically accompany an in-person inspection.

In the visual acuity assessment, the vision of all participants decreased from 1.2 to 0.1–0.2 when viewing VR video. This decline is likely attributable to the resolution of the transmitted images (4K, 3840×1920), as the visual angle per pixel in a 360-degree panoramic shot is approximately 0.1 degrees, closely matching theoretical visual acuity values. A similar reduction in visual acuity when using VR has been reported16), consistent with our findings. At this resolution, while an overall view of the workplace and equipment can be obtained, reading text such as documents remains challenging. Therefore, VR may help maintain corporate confidentiality by obscuring fine details but also carries the risk of overlooking critical hazards, such as the text on warning labels. Although the on-site person can be directed to move closer to an object for detailed inspection, interpreting a lower-resolution image to identify potential risks requires a high level of expertise. This suggests that the system is most effective when used by experienced occupational health physicians who already possess a thorough understanding of the specific workplace, as was the case in our experiment. Future improvements in communication technology may re-introduce confidentiality risks, but it may also present an opportunity to find an optimal balance, as the streaming resolution can be intentionally adjusted (eg, lowered) or specific information masked to meet the security needs of a given workplace.

Another advantage of VR can be explained by the difference in the mode of visual exploration between the two modalities when re-inspecting a familiar environment. PC-based inspection requires conscious, deliberate actions to move the mouse to pan across the field of view, whereas VR-based inspection relies on natural, intuitive head movements, potentially causing PC users to miss hazards located in areas not fully scanned.

While participants noted in their comments that the zoom function of the PC is a distinct advantage for detailed examination, this benefit may have been less critical in the specific context of this study, where the inspection points were already known. In contrast, the one-to-one mapping of head movements to the virtual viewpoint in VR facilitates a more thorough and systematic visual scan than that achieved using PC-based scanning. This difference in the completeness of visual scanning, rather than an advantage in detailed analysis, could be a key factor in the apparently better performance of VR observed in this small pilot study, although the difference was not statistically significant.

Limitations were identified in conveying certain environmental factors, such as noise and lighting, through video and audio processing. In particular, noise levels were automatically adjusted to optimize voice communication clarity. Similarly, the auto-exposure function of the camera corrects for ambient lighting, which made accurate assessment of illumination levels difficult. Furthermore, sensory information, such as the smell of chemicals, temperature, humidity, and glare, could not be transmitted at all. Accurate evaluation of workplace noise exposure is an aspect emphasized as crucial in the American College of Occupational and Environmental Medicine guidelines17) for preventing noise-induced hearing loss. While noise exposure assessment is a limitation in remote inspections, recent studies have demonstrated that noise levels can be measured with a reasonable degree of accuracy using smartphones18), suggesting that integrating such tools into VR-based workplace inspections could enhance their effectiveness.

Educational potential

A key feature of this VR system is its ability to share the same visual feed across multiple devices simultaneously. This capability suggests potential applications in education, such as enabling supervisors and trainees to conduct workplace inspection training while viewing the same video feed in real time. VR technology is already being utilized in the medical field19), as well as in health and safety education20,21). Additionally, a distinctive advantage of VR-based workplace inspection training is the ability to assess the inspector’s own adherence to health and safety protocols. Using 360-degree video, trainees can evaluate their use of personal protective equipment and other safety measures, making this a potentially more practical and immersive educational tool.

Limitations and future directions

This study has some limitations. First, the evaluation was conducted with a small number of participants and in a simulated environment, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, our methodological design intentionally required all participants to complete an on-site inspection first. This design leveraged participants’ memory of the workplace, a factor we believe is necessary to compensate for the technical limitations of the system, such as reduced visual acuity. Consequently, the number of inspection points identified in the remote conditions is likely higher than what would be found in a “first-time” remote inspection. Furthermore, the initial on-site experience may have systematically influenced the subjective ratings. Third, as all three design patterns were conducted consecutively on the same day, carryover effects, such as fatigue, may have been present. While the order of the two remote conditions (VR and PC) was randomized, future studies should employ a more rigorous research design, such as randomizing the order of all conditions and providing sufficient intervals between them, to better isolate these effects. Further validation in diverse, real-world workplace settings is also required.

Conclusions

This study explored the potential application of VR live-streaming technology for workplace inspections. A key improvement over previous studies was the reduction of video latency to 1–3 s, enabling real-time communication during inspections. The visual acuity of VR video was approximately 0.1–0.2, a characteristic that may help both maintain corporate confidentiality and facilitate an overview of the workplace. Nevertheless, certain technical challenges were identified, including the instability of the communication environment and limitations in evaluating environmental factors. To ensure the effective implementation of workplace inspections, a more stable communication environment and higher streaming resolution are necessary. This issue is expected to be mitigated with the widespread adoption of 5G. In addition, given the importance of noise exposure assessment in occupational health, the use of sound measurement equipment, such as sound level meters is recommended.

The VR system allows multiple remote participants to share video in real time, making it a promising educational tool for training young occupational health physicians in workplace inspections. Future research should focus on further technological advancements and validation in diverse workplace settings, with a particular emphasis on quantitatively assessing the educational impact of the system and advancing its practical implementation.
