FLoPS : Semantics, Operations, and Properties of P3109 Floating-Point Representations in Lean
Tung-Che Chang, Sehyeok Park, Jay Lim, Santosh Nagarakatte

Abstract
The upcoming IEEE-P3109 standard for low-precision floating-point arithmetic can become the foundation of future machine learning hardware and software. Unlike the fixed types of IEEE-754, P3109 introduces a parametric framework defined by bitwidth, precision, signedness, and domain. This flexibility results in a vast combinatorial space of formats — some with as little as one bit of precision — alongside novel features such as stochastic rounding and saturation arithmetic. These deviations create a unique verification gap that this paper intends to address. This paper presents FLoPS , F ormalization in L ean o f the P 3109 S tandard, which is a comprehensive formal model of P3109 in Lean. Our work serves as a rigorous, machine-checked specification that facilitates deep analysis of the standard. We demonstrate the model’s utility by verifying foundational properties and analyzing key algorithms within the P3109 context. Specifically, we reveal that FastTwoSum exhibits a novel property of computing exact “overflow error” under saturation using any rounding mode, whereas previously established properties of the ExtractScalar algorithm fail for formats with one bit of precision. This work provides a verified foundation for reasoning about P3109 and enables formal verification of future numerical software. Our Lean development is open source and publicly available.

1
Introduction

The IEEE P3109 standard
[
17
]
introduces a
flexible framework for low-precision, binary floating-point (FP)
formats tailored for machine learning. In contrast to the fixed types
supported by IEEE-754, a P3109 format is defined by four parameters:
bitwidth (
i.e.
, the total number of bits), precision (
i.e.
, how
accurately each real value can be represented), signedness (
i.e.
,
positive or negative), and domain (finite or extended - presence of
special values). This parametric approach results in a large space of
potential formats with distinct encoding schemes for each
configuration. Furthermore, the set of real values representable by a
given P3109 configuration (value set) deviates from IEEE-754
conventions: all formats contain a single zero, single
NaN

(not a number), and optional infinities. These features are designed
to maximize the number of real values representable with low bitwidth
encodings.
To address the limited precision and dynamic range of low bitwidth
formats, the P3109 standard provides diverse specifications for
mapping real numbers to their floating-point counterparts. P3109
expands the rounding modes supported by IEEE-754 (
i.e.
, round-down
(
\rd
), round-up (
\ru
), round-toward-zero (
\rz
),
round-to-nearest-ties-to-even (
\rne
)) with stochastic rounding
(
\sr
) and round-to-odd (
\ro
), which are utilized to enhance
convergence and stability in machine learning
training
[
15
]
.
Unlike standard floating-point arithmetic, which typically rounds to
infinity upon overflow, P3109 operations are subject to various
saturation semantics that choose between infinity and the largest
representable value with a finite magnitude.

Formal verification of IEEE-754 floating-point arithmetic is a
well-established field
[
5
,
6
]
.
Mainstream FP verification literature assumes a ”reasonably large”
precision, an assumption that could fail in the context of machine
learning formats where precision may drop to as little as one bit.
Therefore, existing formalizations of floating-point arithmetic under
IEEE-754 need to be significantly modified to handle various
properties unique to P3109.

FLoPS.
This paper seeks to develop a formalization bespoke to
P3109 that would be appealing to verify future applications of its
formats and operations. We describe,
FLoPS
, a

F
ormalization in
L
ean
o
f the
P
3109

S
tandard. We chose the Lean 4 theorem prover to develop this
framework for the following reasons. First, Lean enables us to use the
extensive mathematical libraries from
Mathlib
. The P3109
standard defines floating-point operations via mappings to

closed extended reals
, a set comprising real numbers,
positive and negative infinity, and
NaN
.
Mathlib

provides a robust theory of real and extended real numbers
(
EReal
), thus enabling a direct and faithful encoding of
these reference semantics. Second, Lean’s tactic language also helps
with managing the complexity of floating-point proofs. In particular,
the
calc
tactic allows for structured, readable arithmetic
proofs, which are useful for verifying chains of inequalities common
in arithmetic analysis.
Our primary objective with
FLoPS
is to establish a mathematically
precise ground truth for the P3109 standard, which can serve as a
reference model for future efforts involving P3109 implementations.

The P3109 standard specifies the semantics of the format using integer
bit-strings and the real values encoded by them. We formalize finite
P3109 values as an algebraic data type consisting of an integer
significand and an exponent. These pairs are strictly constrained by
the format parameters: the significand must occupy at most
P
bits,
and the exponent must be within the format’s dynamic range (from

emin
to
emax
). Furthermore, our encoding explicitly
enforces that these valid finite pairs map to bit-patterns distinct
from those reserved for special values, such as infinities and
Not-A-Number (
NaN
).
We use this intermediate algebraic model to avoid the mechanization
overhead and complexity associated with raw bit-level
reasoning. Attempting to verify properties directly on bit-strings
forces the formalization to rely on complex piecewise definitions to
distinguish between finite numbers, infinities, and
NaN
s, which
increases the cases in the proofs, making them difficult and any
automation infeasible. Our algebraic model can utilize Lean’s type
system to enforce constraints and perform high-level mathematical
reasoning on the friendlier model.
We show our formalization’s faithfulness to the standard by
establishing a three-way equivalence between the set of integer
bit-patterns, our algebraic model, and the closed extended real value set
of each format (Section
3
).

To highlight our model’s practical utility, we formalize the
properties of the
FastTwoSum

[
14
]
algorithm in the
context of P3109 (Section
4
). Under the
round-to-nearest rounding mode,
FastTwoSum
computes the exact
rounding error of floating-point addition using only floating-point
operations. Through our formalization, we discovered that

FastTwoSum
exhibits a novel property in the presence of
saturation. When saturation occurs,
FastTwoSum
computes
exactly the ”overflow error”, the difference between the real
arithmetic result and the saturated result, regardless of the active
rounding mode. We extend our analysis of
FastTwoSum
to
develop the first formal analysis of the floating-point splitting
technique
ExtractScalar

[
27
]
. Our
analysis reveals that previously established properties of

ExtractScalar
do not extend to P3109 formats with one bit
precision (Section
5
). The
FLoPS
framework is open
source and publicly available
[
8
]
. While developing

FLoPS
, we discovered multiple errors in the draft P3109 standard and
reported it to the P3109 working group, which subsequently have been
fixed.

Background

IEEE-754 Standard.
Floating-point formats as defined by the
IEEE-754 standard for binary floating-point
arithmetic
[
18
]
represent subsets of real numbers,
augmented by special values such as
\pm\infty
and
NaN
. The
set of finite real values in a format
f
is determined by three
parameters: precision (
P
), the maximum exponent (
emax
), and the
minimum exponent (
emin
). Given a binary indicator
s
for the
sign, a finite real
x
in the value set of
f
can be expressed as
x=(-1)^{s}\times m\times 2^{e-P+1}
where
m
and
e
each represent

x
’s significand and the canonical exponent. For a non-zero
x
,
e=\text{max}(\lfloor\text{log}_{2}(x)\rfloor,\texttt{emin})
. If
x=0
,

e=\texttt{emin}
. For
x
to be a member of
f
’s value set, its
significand and exponent must satisfy
m,e\in\mathbb{Z}
,
0\leq m<2^{P}
, and
\texttt{emin}\leq e\leq\texttt{emax}
. IEEE-754 additionally
requires that if
e>\texttt{emin}
, then
m\geq 2^{P-1}
. Based on this
requirement, the standard further divides finite reals into two
categories. A number
x
is
normal
if
|x|\geq 2^{\texttt{emin}}

(
i.e.
,
m\geq 2^{P-1}
) and
subnormal
otherwise.

Figure 1:

For a finite
x
,
t
represents the
P-1
trailing bits of
x
’s
significand
m
.
B
and
W
each represent the exponent bias and
bitwidth.

For each value in a given format, the standard assigns a corresponding
binary encoding. An encoding contains three fields, each designated for the
sign, exponent, and significand respectively. For a finite
x
, the significand
field stores the
P-1
trailing bits of
x
’s significand. Given

K
-bits for the full encoding (
e.g.
,
64
for double-precision), with
1
-bit
and
P-1
-bits reserved for the sign and significand respectively, the exponent
field occupies the remaining
W=K-P
bits. Using the
W
-bits available,
the exponent field stores the sum of
x
’s exponent and the format’s exponent
bias
B
, which is defined as
B=\texttt{emax}
(see Figure
1
).

Of the
2^{W}
possible values for the exponent field,
2^{W}-2
are
designated for the biased exponents of normal values. The extremal encodings

E=0
and
E=2^{W}-1
(
i.e.
, exponent field containing only ones) are
reserved for subnormal numbers and special values respectively. Consequently,
the maximum exponent
emax
maps to the second largest exponent encoding

E=2^{W}-2
. If
E=2^{W}-1
and the significand field consists entirely
of zeroes, the encoding represents an infinity, with the sign being determined
by the encoding’s leading bit. Conversely, if
E=2^{W}-1
and the
significand field is non-zero, the encoding represents a
NaN
value.
The value in the significand field subsequently determines the type of the

NaN
. Given that encodings store sign bits explicitly, another notable
property of IEEE-754 formats is that there exist two zeroes
\pm 0
.
For any given IEEE-754 format with
K
-bit-encodings,
+0
and
-0
each
correspond to the encodings
0
and
2^{K-1}
.

A floating-point format
f
can only represent numbers using
P
-bits of
precision. However, the real arithmetic result of an operation on
floating-point numbers may not be exactly representable with
P
precision bits
(
e.g.
,
1+2^{-P}
). Therefore, floating-point operations must augment their
real arithmetic counterparts with a rounding function
\circ:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{f}
that maps real numbers to the target format
f
’s
value set. Rounding in turn can incur errors due to loss of precision.
To mitigate errors, a rounding function must
ideally possess certain properties, the most notable of which are monotonicty
and
faithfulness
. Given a target value set
\mathcal{V}_{f}
and a
number
r\in\mathbb{R}
, the largest
v\in\mathcal{V}_{f}
such that

v<r
is
r
’s
floating-point predecessor
(
pred(r)
). Similarly,
the smallest
v\in\mathcal{V}_{f}
such that
v>r
is
r
’s

floating-point successor
(
succ(r)
). Collectively,

pred(r)
and
succ(r)
are referred to as
r
’s floating-point neighbors.
A rounding function is
faithful
if for any
r\in\mathbb{R}
,

\circ(r)=r
when
r\in V_{f}
and
\circ(r)\in\{pred(r),succ(r)\}

otherwise.

For binary formats, the IEEE-754 standard supports four rounding modes

\rd
,
\ru
,
\rz
, and
\rne
. All four rounding modes are faithful, with
each mode enforcing specific rules for choosing between floating-point
neighbors when a number
r
is not representable in the target format.
For such cases,
\rd
always chooses
pred(r)
while
\ru
chooses
succ(r)
.
A rounding function
\circ
is thus also faithful if

\forall r\in\mathbb{R},\circ(r)\in\{\rd(r),\ru(r)\}
.

\rz
applies
\rd
when
r>0
and
\ru
otherwise.

\rne
, on the other hand, applies the round-to-nearest rule and thereby always
chooses the floating-point neighbor closest to
r
. If
r
is at an exact
midpoint between its neighbors (
i.e.
,
|pred(r)-r|=|succ(r)-r|
),
\rne

breaks the tie based on floating-point neighbors’ parity. In such cases,
\rne

as specified by IEEE-754 chooses the neighbor that has an even
significand.

Given the maximum representable exponent
emax
, the maximum representable
finite magnitude in a IEEE-754 format is
M_{hi}=2^{P}-1\times 2^{\texttt{emax}-P+1}
. Numbers
r\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|r|>M_{hi}
are subsequently
in the
overflow
range. When handling overflow, the IEEE-754 standard
applies rounding using the aforementioned rounding modes to
decide between
M_{hi}
(or
-M_{hi}
) and
\infty
(or

-\infty
). For example, any
r
such that
r<-M_{hi}
will round to

-\infty
under
\rd
while numbers
r>M_{hi}
round to
M_{hi}
.

IEEE P3109 Standard.
Unlike the IEEE-754 standard, which
prescribes a fixed set of interchange formats (
e.g.
, FP32, FP64), P3109
allows for the derivation of a format
f
via a tuple of four
parameters: bitwidth (
K
), precision (
P
), signedness (
\Sigma
),
and domain (
\Delta
). The bitwidth
K
specifies the total storage
size and must be at least 3 bits. The precision
P
must be at least
1.
P
is further constrained by the signedness parameter
\Sigma\in\{\texttt{Signed},\texttt{Unsigned}\}
:
P
must be strictly less
than
K
for
\Sigma=\texttt{Signed}
and must be no greater than
K

otherwise.

While IEEE-754 defines the exponent bias
B
using the
maximum representable exponent
emax
, P3109 instead directly
defines
B
using the available bitwidth and precision. For

Signed
formats,
B=2^{K-P-1}
; for
Unsigned

formats,
B=2^{K-P}
. Given the exponent field width
W
, which is

K-P
for
Signed
formats and
K-P+1
otherwise, the bias of a
P3109 format subsequently determines the maximum exponent. Unlike
IEEE-754, P3109 does not reserve the highest exponent field entirely
for special values such as
NaN
and
\pm\infty
.

\Sigma
determines the inclusion of
negative reals in a format’s value set. Likewise, the domain parameter
\Delta\in\{\texttt{Finite},\texttt{Extended}\}
determines the inclusion of
infinities. Formats in the
Finite
domain represent only finite numbers
and
NaN
, while those in the
Extended
domain include
infinities. Specifically, an
Extended
format includes both
\pm\infty
if it is also
Signed
and only positive
\infty
otherwise.
The bit integer encodings for these special values are parametric and vary
across formats depending on
K
,
\Sigma
, and
\Delta
. We present the

For arithmetic operations, P3109 supports a superset of IEEE-754’s
standard rounding modes. P3109 additionally provides round-to-odd
(
\ro
) and stochastic rounding (
\sr
), which can improve the
stability of training for machine learning. While sharing several
rounding modes with IEEE-754 counterparts, P3109 formats can exhibit
unique rounding behaviors due to their low precision. For example, the
conventional definition of
\rne

.
For

, parity-based rounding (
e.g.
,
\rne

)

.
Moreover, P3109 extends IEEE-754’s precision-based

, which explicitly
define the rounding decision for numbers in the overflow range.
The

standard overflow behavior.

preserves infinities, but

We describe our
FLoPS
framework for the formalization of low-precision
representations in the P3109 standard. To bridge the gap between
low-level bit-patterns used in the P3109 standard with high-level
mathematical reasoning,
FLoPS
has three primary parts: (1) bit-level
encodings, (2) an algebraic model with inductive types, and (3) a
semantic domain of value sets (
i.e.
, closed extended reals). To
facilitate rigorous proofs, we define the P3109 formats and values as
inductive types. This layer abstracts away from bit-level details to
enforce the standard’s parametric constraints on bitwidth, precision,
and special value encoding directly in the type system. By treating
floating-point numbers as algebraic structures rather than as bit
patterns, we simplify the formalization of operations and predicates.

Triangle isomorphism.
To ensure our model is a faithful
representation of the P3109 standard, we establish a three-way formal
equivalence relation, which we call
Triangle Isomorphism
as
shown in Figure
2
. Specifically, it
involves showing (1)
encoding equivalence
and (2)

semantic equivalence
. The encoding equivalence step ensures a
verified bijection between the bit-level encodings and our algebraic
model. The semantic equivalence step also provides a proof of bijection
between the algebraic model and the set of closed extended
real values in the value set of a given P3109 format. These two steps
guarantee that any property proven within our algebraic model
automatically holds for the actual bit-level implementation described
by the standard.

FLoPS
also includes a formalization of the core projection pipeline
that maps closed extended real values back into the P3109 domain.
This includes a bespoke implementation of rounding modes, specifically
handling the unique “ties-to-even” logic required for
P=1
formats,
and the saturation semantics, which is useful to verify high-level
properties of foundational FP algorithms in
Section
4
.

The P3109 Algebraic Model

The P3109 algebraic type is indexed by a P3109 format as detailed in
Section

(exponent), supplemented with the
constraints they should satisfy according to the index format. Note that
because

is not explicitly scaled with respect to the precision,

e
represents the exponent of the significand’s
least significant bit
.

We define a format

,

(

Unlike

The default definition
\texttt{emax}=2^{W}-1-B
, for which the encoding

formats deviate from this definition.
When

, the trailing bits of the

i.e.
, no significand field). Moreover,
the largest binary encodings are reserved for the special values.
For example, for an

format such that

, and thus

The
p3109
type is categorized into
p3109_infinity
,

p3109_nan
, and
p3109_finite
. For infinite values
of type
p3109_infinity
,

must be
Extended
, and
its signedness must also coordinate with the representable values.

For
(m,e)\in f
to be

, it must be a
canonical
floating-point number that satisfies the signedness constraint:

must be non-negative when

is
Unsigned
. A floating-point
value
(m,e)
is
canonical
if it satisfies the

predicate and is either normal or subnormal. A value
(m,e)
is

if its significand is representable in the available precision and
its exponent is not below the minimum allowed value:
|m|<2^{P}\land\texttt{emin}_{lsb}\leq e
. A value is normal if it is bounded and its significand’s
magnitude is large enough to assume the standard normalized form:
2^{P-1}\leq|m|
. Additionally, a normal value must satisfy
e\leq\texttt{emax}_{lsb}
. For
formats where
P>1
, the bit-patterns of normal numbers must not conflict
with those of special values. For example, the largest binary encoding for

Signed Extended
formats is reserved for
\infty
, thereby requiring
|m|<2^{P}-1
when

. A value is subnormal if
it is bounded,
e=\texttt{emin}_{lsb}
, and
|m|<2^{P-1}
.

Lastly, we define a mapping
[\![\cdot]\!]
from the P3109
algebraic model to the set of closed extended reals. We express closed
extended reals as a sum type of extended reals for finite values and
the unit type for special values. Finite P3109 values defined by the
pair
(m,e)
map to
m\times 2^{e}
. For
Extended
formats,

+\infty
and
-\infty
map to
\top
and
\bot

respectively.
NaN
maps to the unit, right injected. We
formally define the value set
\mathcal{V}_{f}
of a format
f
as the
range of the evaluation function:
\mathcal{V}_{f}=\{v\mid\exists a\in\texttt{p3109}_{f},[\![a]\!]=v\}
. This
construction guarantees that every semantic value in
\mathcal{V}_{f}

has at least one algebraic representative. To ensure model
correctness, we must confirm that this mapping is invertible: the
algebraic structure fully captures the semantics without information
loss. We establish invertibility in the next section, in which we
prove the correctness of the formalization.

3.2
Ensuring Model Fidelity: The Triangular Isomorphism

A central challenge in formalizing IEEE P3109 is ensuring that our
inductive model (the
p3109
ADT) is a
faithful

representation of the standard’s specification. The standard specifies
formats by mapping integers (
i.e.
, binary encodings) to closed extended
real values (
i.e.
, the value set
\mathcal{V}_{f}
). Our formalization
introduces an intermediate algebraic layer to facilitate proof
automation. To establish trust, we prove isomorphism between three
domains:

1.

Bit-Level:
The concrete machine representation (
n\in\text{Fin}(2^{K})
).

2.

Algebraic (ADT):
The structured
p3109
inductive type, optimized for case analysis.

3.

Semantic:
The value set
\mathcal{V}_{f}\subset\text{EReal}\cup\{\texttt{NaN}\}
as defined by a given format
f
.

To establish the full isomorphism between the three structures, we must first
verify that the algebraic bounds defining our ADT are mathematically sound.
The most volatile bound in the P3109 standard is the maximum exponent. As
discussed in Section
3.1
, formats with precision

P\leq 2
reserve the highest exponent field encodings for special values,
dynamically lowering the maximum representable finite exponent.

The definition of
n_{fmax}
, the encoding of the maximum
finite value in a given format.

Given our definition of
emax
for each format, we must
formally guarantee that this abstraction matches the actual encoding.
We decode the exponent directly from the maximum finite number
encoding
n_{fmax}
, which we define in Figure
4
,
and prove its equality with the
emax
defined in
Figure
3
. Under case analysis, the special cases of

emax
contain enough information about the format parameters
to prune all but one branch in
n_{fmax}
for each case. Establishing
equivalence is thus trivial for such cases. Handling the special cases
also addresses cases where
P\leq 2
. For the default case of

\texttt{emax}=2^{W}-1-B
, the exclusion of formats with
P\leq 2

makes the logic for decoding the exponent field from
n_{fmax}

independent of the format parameters. The integer division

n_{fmax}/2^{P-1}
returns the expected exponent encoding
2^{W}-1
for
all remaining formats.

Isomorphism between bit-level encodings and our algebraic
ADT.
Our correctness argument is founded on the strict isomorphism
between our algebraic ADT and the bit-level representation. Unlike
IEEE-754 formats where special values occupy a static, reserved
exponent field, P3109 dynamically shifts the boundary between finite
and special values based on the bitwidth (
K
), signedness (
\Sigma
),
and domain (
\Delta
). Using these parameters, we formally define the
bit-to-ADT decoding function
\Phi:\text{Fin}(2^{K})\rightarrow\text{p3109}_{f}
as a piecewise mapping as shown in
Figure

For integers mapping to the finite domain, the decoding function extracts the
significand (
m
) and canonical exponent (
e
) via Euclidean division. Given a
target precision
P
and exponent bias
B
, let the trailing significand
T

and the biased exponent
E
be defined as
T=n\bmod 2^{P-1}
and

E=\lfloor n/2^{P-1}\rfloor-B
respectively. The helper function

\Phi_{fin}
(see Figure

finite constructor while distinguishing between subnormal (including zero) and

Mechanizing injectivity.

The explicit piecewise formulation of
\Phi
is necessary for a computable
definition, but it introduces severe mechanization overheads. To prove
injectivity, we demonstrate that each input to
\Phi
is decoded to a
unique P3109 value, lying in one of three disjoint regions
\mathcal{S}_{NaN}
,

\mathcal{S}_{Inf}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{Finite}
.

Lemma 1

(
\Phi
is injective)
.

\forall n_{1},n_{2}\in\text{Fin}(2^{K}),\quad\Phi(n_{1})=\Phi(n_{2})\implies n_{1}=n_{2}

In Lean 4, the branching in
\Phi
triggers a combinatorial explosion in the
proof state, which can be partitioned into four cases:

1.

Inter-Region Disjointness:
Addressing this case proves that
a decoded bit-pattern never collides with values outside of its region.
Lean automates the pruning process of contradictory branches.

2.

Sign Disjointness:
Proving this property affirms that
positive finite encodings (
\Phi_{fin}
) and negative finite encodings
(
-\Phi_{fin}
) never collide. Because P3109 allocates IEEE-754’s
bit-pattern for
-0
(
i.e.
,
2^{K-1}
) to
NaN
, negative finite
encodings always decode to strictly negative significands (
i.e.
,
m<0
).
When the proof state assumes a collision, it equates a non-negative
significand with a strictly negative one, exposing an immediate contradiction.

3.

Implicit Bit Disjointness:
We also prove normal and
subnormal subregions are semantically disjoint. When Lean generates an
equality assumption between normal and subnormal encodings, the normal
decoding path adds the implicit leading bit (
2^{P-1}
) to its significand
(
i.e.
, the trailing bits of the encoding) while the subnormal path does
not. This forces the assumption
T_{sub}=T_{norm}+2^{P-1}
.
Because
T_{sub}
is defined as
n\bmod 2^{P-1}
, it is strictly bounded
by
T_{sub}<2^{P-1}
, thus exposing a direct mathematical contradiction.

4.

Intra-Region Injectivity:
For two bit-patterns within the
same subregion, we apply the fundamental theorem of Euclidean
division
n=E\cdot 2^{P-1}+T
to establish injectivity
(
e.g.
,
\forall n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{Normal},\Phi(n_{1})=\Phi(n_{2})\implies n_{1}=n_{2}
).

Mechanizing surjectivity.
The second requirement for the bijection is

surjectivity
: every valid value in the
p3109
ADT must
correspond to at least one realizable bit-pattern. Formally, we prove the
following:

Lemma 2

(
\Phi
is surjective)
.

\forall b:\text{p3109}_{f},\quad\exists a\in\text{Fin}(2^{K})\quad\textit{s.t.}\quad\Phi(a)=b

The proof proceeds by structural induction on
b
. For each constructor of the

p3109
type, we explicitly construct a witness
a
and prove it decodes
back to
b
. For values constructed as
p3109_nan
or
p3109_infinity
, the witness construction directly relies on the
definitions from the P3109 standard (see Table
1
).
For
NaN
, we select the format’s designated encoding (e.g.,
2^{K-1}

for
Signed
formats). The decoding function maps this specific integer
directly to
p3109_nan
, trivially satisfying the requirement. For
infinities, we select the bit-pattern corresponding to the format’s signedness
and the requested sign (e.g.,
2^{K-1}-1
for
\infty
in
Signed
Extended
formats). As is the case for
NaN
, the bit-patterns
immediately map back to the corresponding infinities.

For finite values, the complexity lies in the
\texttt{p3109\_finite}(m,e)

constructor. For any valid finite number
(m,e)
permitted by a given format,
we must demonstrate that we can mathematically construct a
K
-bit integer
n

that decodes back to
(m,e)
. We construct the candidate integer
n

using the inverse of the decoding formula. For a subnormal value

(m,\texttt{emin}_{lsb})
, its bit-level encoding is just
m
.
For a positive normal value, the bit-pattern is defined as

n=(m-2^{P-1})+(e+P-1+B)\cdot 2^{P-1}
.
The term
m-2^{P-1}
represents the significand minus the implicit leading
bit. The term
(e+P-1+B)\cdot 2^{P-1}
represents the biased
exponent for
m
’s MSB, shifted into the exponent field. We first establish
that proofs for the negative value encodings can be reduced to their positive
counterparts.

Lemma 3

(Reduction)
.

If format is
Signed and

0<n<2^{K-1}
, then
-\Phi(n)=\Phi(n+2^{K-1})

Without loss of generality, we must show that an integer
n
constructed for a
positive value does not enter any branches in
\Phi
outside of the
positive case of
\Phi_{fin}
, which includes branches for
NaN
or
negative numbers. We demonstrate this guarantee for the two aforementioned cases using proof by contradiction.

We first prove that the bit-pattern
n

constructed for a
positive normal
number is distinct from the bit
pattern reserved for
NaN
in
Signed
formats:
n_{\text{NaN}}=2^{K-1}
. Suppose there exists a valid normal pair
(m,e)
such that its
bit-pattern is
n_{\text{NaN}}
(
i.e.
,
(m-2^{P-1})+(e+P-1+B)\cdot 2^{P-1}=2^{K-1}
).
To check if this equality is satisfiable, we solve for the exponent term. The
term
(m-2^{P-1})
represents the trailing significand bits and is strictly
less than
2^{P-1}
. Dividing the bit-pattern
2^{K-1}
by
2^{P-1}
thus
isolates the exponent field, from which we extract the exponent
e=2^{K-P}-B-P+1
.

We then compare
e=2^{K-P}-B-P+1
to
\texttt{emax}_{lsb}
, the maximum
allowable exponent for a normal
(m,e)
. For
Signed
formats,

\texttt{emax}_{lsb}=2^{W}-1-B-P+1=2^{K-P-1}-1-B-P+1
. From
the previously established equality
e=2^{K-P}-B-P+1
, we derive
e=2\cdot 2^{K-P-1}-B-P+1>2^{K-P-1}-1-B-P+1=\texttt{emax}_{lsb}
. The
inequality
e>\texttt{emax}_{lsb}
contradicts the premise that
(m,e)
is a
valid P3109 normal number where
e\leq\texttt{emax}_{lsb}
. Therefore, no
valid normal number in a
Signed
format can be encoded into the

NaN
bit-pattern.

We now prove that the bit-pattern
n
constructed for a
positive

number does not fall into the range reserved for
negative
numbers,
which is
2^{K-1}<n<2^{K}
for
Signed
formats. We demonstrate the
impossibility of this collision by analyzing the following inequality:

2^{K-1}<n=(m-2^{P-1})+(e+P-1+B)\cdot 2^{P-1}
. By relaxing
this inequality using the known bounds of
m
(
i.e.
,
m<2^{P}
) and

e
(
i.e.
,
e\leq\texttt{emax}_{lsb}
), we deduce that the constructed integer

n
is strictly less than
2^{K-1}
. Thus,
n
is strictly
constrained to the positive partition of the encoding space.

While we only detail cases involving
NaN
and negative bounds, the full
mechanization of surjectivity for positive normal values requires handling the
rest of the branches in
\Phi
. For the remaining cases, Lean generates the
relevant proof obligations to confirm that our constructed normal bit-pattern
does not collide with
\pm\infty
,
NaN
in
Unsigned
formats
(
i.e.
,
n_{\text{NaN}}=2^{K}-1
), or the subnormal boundary. Crucially, each of these
exfalso
proofs acts as a rigorous stress test for the normal P3109 predicate. If the invariants defining a canonical normal value
(
i.e.
,
e\leq\texttt{emax}_{lsb}
and
m\geq 2^{P-1}
) are even slightly too
loose, the constructed bit-pattern would incorrectly enter an adjacent
partition.
Because P3109 shifts these boundaries dynamically based on the format
parameters, manually verifying these bounds is highly error-prone. By
successfully closing these contradictory branches, our mechanization formally
certifies that the P3109 ADT invariants optimally bound the combinatorial
encoding space of the standard.

To further validate the correctness, we also performed differential testing on the
\Phi
function. Based on the specifications detailed in
Section
2
, we implemented the standard’s decoding function
in C. For each P3109 format defined by parameters (
K,P,\Sigma,\Delta
),
the test (i) iterates through all integers
n\in\text{Fin}(2^{K})
, (ii)
interprets the bit-string via the standard decoding function, and (iii)
compares the output against that of
\Phi
. Our test shows a one-to-one
correspondence between
\Phi
and the standard’s decoding function for all
formats.

Semantic soundness.
The second part of our triangular
isomorphism connects the algebraic model to the semantic
domain. Unlike standard approaches that define the value set
abstractly, we define the value set
\mathcal{V}_{f}

constructively
as the image of the algebraic model under the
semantic evaluation function. For these two domains, we define two
mapping functions:

•

[\![\cdot]\!]
:
\texttt{p3109}_{f}\to\text{EReal}\oplus\text{Unit}
.
This function evaluates an algebraic term to its semantic value (where
\oplus\text{Unit}
handles
NaN
s).

•

encode
:
\mathcal{V}_{f}\to\texttt{p3109}_{f}
.
This function maps a value in the value set back to the algebraic structure.

The value set
\mathcal{V}_{f}
is defined as the range of the evaluation
function. Hence, its output is
naturally in the value set. Given these functions, we establish the
round-trip identity - the composition of
[\![\cdot]\!]
and
encode
is the identity function.

Theorem 4

(Round-Trip Identity)
.

For any algebraic value
a\in\texttt{p3109}_{f}
, converting it to its semantic value and re-encoding it restores the original algebraic term exactly:

\forall a\in\texttt{p3109}_{f},\ \texttt{encode}([\![a]\!])=a

Theorem
4
confirms that the evaluation function
is injective on the domain of valid
p3109
terms and that
encode
serves as its left inverse. Moreover, the function is
trivially surjective because of the definition of the value set. Therefore,
the evaluation function of
\texttt{p3109}_{f}
is also a bijection between
the P3109 ADT and the semantic domain.

The correctness of the full bit-level encoding is established by
composing bit-to-ADT and ADT-to-semantic bijections.
Since both the bit decoding function
\Phi:\text{Fin}(2^{K})\to\text{p3109}_{f}
and the evaluation function
[\![\cdot]\!]
:

\text{p3109}_{f}\to\mathcal{V}_{f}
are bijective,
their composition also establishes an isomorphism
between the bit-pattern encodings
\text{Fin}(2^{K})
and the P3109
semantic value set
\mathcal{V}_{f}
.

3.3
Projection

Floating-point operations in the P3109 standard are defined
mathematically on the set of closed extended reals and their results
are projected back into a representable value in the target
format. This projection is a three-step process:
rounding
a
real number to a precision
P
with unbounded exponents,

saturating
based on the format’s finite range, and

encoding
the closed extended real back to a P3109 value. The
standard defines a projection specification
\pi
as a pair consisting
of a rounding mode and a saturation mode:
\pi=(Rnd_{\pi},Sat_{\pi})

Rounding.
The
RoundToPrecision
function as defined by the
standard converts a real number
x
to a value representable with precision
P

and an unbounded exponent. P3109 supports multiple modes for

RoundToPrecision
, including the IEEE-754 standard modes
(
e.g.
,
\rne
,
\rz
),
\ro
, and three variants of
\sr
.

RoundToPrecision
relies on the scaled significand of a real number
x
,
which is its real number significand scaled by the canonical exponent
it would have if it were representable in
P
-bits
(
i.e.
, the scaled significand is
x/2^{\text{max}(\lfloor\text{log}_{2}(x)\rfloor,\texttt{emin})-P+1}
).
Our implementation of
RoundToPrecision
is parameterized by a rounding
operator that dictates how the scaled significand is rounded to an
integer. For each rounding function, we provide an
axiomatic definition and prove the correctness of the implementation.
The operators range from simple directed functions like floor and
ceiling (
e.g.
,
\rd
and
\ru
) to parity-based operators for
\rne
and
\ro
.

We separate the unified typeclass that the operators must satisfy in
Flocq
[
5
]
, a library that formalizes floating-point
arithmetic in Rocq, into two typeclasses Faithful and
Monotonic. While all the P3109 rounding modes are faithful, the stochastic
rounding operators are not monotonic. Using only faithful rounding, however, we
can still establish a weaker version of monotonicity where one side of the
inequality is already representable. In fact, this weaker monotonicity property
is sufficient to complete arithmetic proofs in the following sections.

To accommodate P3109’s low-precision formats, our formalization of rounding
diverges from that of existing libraries. In Flocq, which applies rounding
based on IEEE-754, a rounding operator has type
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}
, and
using
\rne
when
P=1
is ill-defined. This is because
\rne
conventionally
relies on the
parity of significands
. In contrast, P3109 requires
information about the exponent to make tie-breaking decisions when
P=1
. Therefore, our rounding function is parameterized by the operator

rnd
:
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}
,
where the first integer argument represents the canonical exponent.
For rounding modes where this context is irrelevant (
e.g.
,
\rd
,
\ru
,
\rz
),
the
rnd
function simply ignores the first argument. We explicitly
avoid partially applying this exponent to match the standard Flocq signature,
as doing so would render the rounding function dependent on the input’s binade.
Such a function would essentially change the rounding behavior at power-of-two
boundaries and make the proof of global monotonicity impossible.

Saturation.
Using the saturation modes described in
Section
2
, the
Saturation
function handles values
exceeding the format’s finite range
[M^{lo},M^{hi}]
. The output of applying

RoundToPrecision
and
Saturation
must be a value in the target
value set. We prove this through case analysis on whether the original input
value is already in the finite range. If it is, the result of

RoundToPrecision
and
Saturation
is guaranteed to be in the
value set due to the aforementioned monotonicity property. For values greater
than
M^{hi}
, we prove that the saturated result is either
M^{hi}
or

+\infty
. Similarly, we prove that values smaller than
M^{lo}
saturate to
either
M^{lo}
or
-\infty
.

Encode.

Encode
is a stand-alone function that takes a closed extended real
value
x
along with the proof that it is in the value set of the target
format (
i.e.
,
x\in\mathcal{V}_{f}
) and returns the corresponding algebraic
P3109 value. Formalizing
Encode
requires
casting
the
real-valued significand to
\mathbb{Z}
. However, there is no coercion
from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{Z}
in Lean. We instead utilize the existential
proposition of
x\in\mathcal{V}_{f}
and prove that a finite P3109 value
extracted using an axiom of choice has the same integer significand as the
real-valued significand. This approach poses another challenge, as it could
discard all information about the original significand prior to applying

Encode
. To address this issue, we design a compound return type that
bundles the P3109 value and the proof of equivalence between the real-valued
significand and the finite significand, if it exists. The formalization of

Encode
can then simply take the first component of this intermediate
construct.

Projection.

We assemble the three steps to form the
Projection
function.
We present key properties of
Projection
that are relevant
to our analysis in Sections
4
and
5
.

Lemma 5

(Projection equals to rounding if already in range)
.

If an extended real value
r
is in the finite range
M^{lo}\leq r\leq M^{hi}

then
Projection
and
RoundToPrecision
produce the same outputs
in the extended real domain.

Given the relaxed monotonicity property, the output of
RoundToPrecision
for a finite real is also finite. Under
all saturations modes,
Saturation
subsequently returns the rounded
value as it is already in the finite range. Since the value is in the real
domain,
Encode
also preserves the rounded output.

Lemma 6

(Projection is ”faithful”)
.

Projecting a real value
r
under any
(Rnd_{\pi},Sat_{\pi})
produces
r
if
r\in\mathcal{V}_{f}
,

pred(r)
or
succ(r)
otherwise.

Lemma
6
signifies that projected outputs are
always faithful roundings of the input.
If the input value is already in the finite range
[M_{lo},M_{hi}]
,
then the faithfulness of
Rnd_{\pi}
immediately guarantees faithfulness for
the projected output. If the value is larger than
M^{hi}
, then

Saturation
produces either
M^{hi}
or
+\infty
. Both numbers are
faithful roundings (
i.e.
,
\rd(r)=M_{hi}
and
\ru(r)=+\infty
for
r>M^{hi}
). By symmetry, the projected outputs for values smaller than
M^{lo}

will always be
M^{lo}
or
-\infty
, both of which are faithful roundings for numbers below
M_{lo}
when the format is
Signed
.

FastTwoSum

lower-precision values. Given the low-precision nature of P3109 formats,
formalizing floating-point splitting techniques for P3019 representations
is of practical use to the design of future algorithms.

Lean Development

We developed
FLoPS
using the Lean 4 theorem prover. The entire Lean
development is open-source and publicly available
[
8
]
.
The entire Lean development is approximately fifteen thousand lines of
Lean code. It includes the P3109 bit-encodings, our intermediate
algebraic inductive types, the semantic model, the isomorphism proofs,
and analysis of
FastTwoSum
and
ExtractScalar
algorithms.
We extensively used
Mathlib
’s robust theory of real and
extended real numbers (
EReal
). We also used Lean’s tactic
language to manage the complexity of proofs especially the use of

calc
tactic structured, readable arithmetic proofs, which are
useful for verifying chains of inequalities common in arithmetic
analysis. Unlike the Rocq ecosystem, which offers libraries with

mathematical specification rather than an executable reference

clarity over direct execution.
Natural language specifications—even those as carefully drafted as
IEEE standards—are susceptible to ambiguities, particularly when
defining interactions between complex features like saturation and
rounding. By encoding the P3109 standard in a theorem prover, we
provide a reference model where behaviors are strictly defined.

Related Work

The formalization of floating-point
arithmetic
[
21
]
has a rich history.
One of the earliest comprehensive efforts in Coq was the PFF
library
[
12
,
7
,
3
]
. PFF provided a high-level, axiomatic
formalization of IEEE-754, focusing on proving standard properties of
floating-point formats. However, PFF relied on an axiomatic
definition of rounding as a relation rather than a computable
function. This design made it excellent for abstract proofs but
difficult to use for executable specifications or automated
verification. The state-of-the-art in this domain is
Flocq
[
5
]
, which unified the axiomatic
strengths of PFF with executability. Flocq abstracts floating-point
numbers as predicates on their corresponding real values via the
”Generic Format”, separating the mathematical value from its binary
representation. While Flocq is the gold standard for IEEE-754, and is
actively being ported to the

FloatSpec

[
2
]
library in
Lean, it cannot be directly adapted for P3109 due to two critical
structural mismatches. First, Flocq is based on IEEE-754 rounding
modes, which are both faithful and monotonic, encompassed by the
typeclass
Valid_rnd
. However, stochastic rounding is
faithful but not monotonic, and splitting
Valid_rnd
could
have a cascading effect on the files that depend on it.
Second, the most significant barrier to using Flocq for P3109 is the
definition of the
IsEven
predicate used for tie-breaking
under
\rne
. Flocq defines the rounding operator, which maps a
scaled real number to an integer, as a context-insensitive function
with the signature
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}
. This abstraction
assumes that ”evenness” can be determined solely from the
significand. For P3109 formats such that
P=1
, however, the
definition of ”evenness” required for tie-breaking is not purely a
property of the real value but also dependent on the exponent.
Because Flocq’s rounding operator does not accept the exponent as an
argument, it cannot implement P3109’s tie-breaking logic. Modeling
this correctly requires changing the rounding signature to
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}
(where the first argument is the
exponent), which allows the rounding operator to access the context
necessary for
IsEven
. Because rounding is the cornerstone of
any floating-point library, changing the definition requires
significant changes.

Closest to our work,
Wintersteiger
et al.

[
31
]
have
formalized the operational specification of P3109 using Imandra. In
contrast to the operational specification, our work provides a more
comprehensive framework to reason about the P3109 specification along
with deeper exploration of the P3109 format’s numerical algorithms
such as
FastTwoSum
, and
ExtractScalar
.

There are numerous tools to perform rounding-error analysis and
rewriting of expressions. Gappa

[
13
]
is designed to automatically
verify bounds and numerical errors in floating-point code. It
operates by computing interval enclosures for expressions, certifying
that values remain within safe limits. However, Gappa and the most
recent error analysis tool VCFloat2
[
1
]

based on Flocq are misaligned with P3109 due to their treatment of
overflow. They assume
non-overflow
, which requires assertions
that a computed value
x
strictly resides within the representable
range
[-M_{hi},M_{hi}]
. In contrast, P3109 incorporates

saturation arithmetic
, where overflow is a valid,
non-exceptional state that could result in clamping to the maximum
finite magnitudes. Gappa lacks the semantics to model this clamping
behavior; it cannot reason about the
result
of saturation,
only the
absence
of it. Consequently, neither Gappa nor
VCFloat2 can verify P3109 algorithms where saturation is an intended
behavior.

Other related work to our effort include
CR-Libm
[
10
]
and
CORE-MATH
[
29
]
projects, which have
pioneered correctly rounded libraries using
Sollya
[
9
]
and Gappa. The
RLibm
[
20
,
24
]
project has
demonstrated that rounding to an intermediate high precision format
via round-to-odd retains sufficient information to make
double-rounding innocuous for all standard rounding modes in the
context of creating math libraries. Recently they have analyzed
FastTwoSum in the context of
round-to-odd
[
25
]
, which is relevant to
P3109 because it explicitly adopts round-to-odd as a rounding mode.
For automated optimization, Herbie
[
23
]

applies heuristic rewriting to minimize error, while
Daisy
[
11
]
provides sound static analysis
via affine arithmetic. In safety-critical domains, NASA’s
PRECiSA
[
30
]
analyzes control flow divergence
(unstable guards) caused by round-off errors, ensuring stability that
meets rigorous industry standards.

8
Conclusion

The
FLoPS
framework provides a machine-checked specification of the
upcoming IEEE P3109 standard for low-precision representations in Lean
while addressing the challenges created by new features such as
stochastic rounding and saturation. We show a three-way equivalence
between the bit-level encodings, our algebraic inductive type, and the
semantic value set of closed extended reals. We show the usefulness of

FLoPS
by analyzing foundational FP algorithms such as
FastTwoSum

and the FP splitting technique
ExtractScalar
. Our verification of

FastTwoSum
revealed a novel property specific to P3109: under
saturation, the algorithm computes the exact ”overflow error” regardless of the
rounding mode. Our analysis for
ExtractScalar
shows that previously
known properties fail for formats with one bit of precision,
highlighting the necessity of bespoke verification for ultra-low
precision domains.
