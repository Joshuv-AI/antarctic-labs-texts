Establishing a community event-based surveillance system in Kadoma City, Zimbabwe: a mixed-methods study protocol
Authors: Clayton Munemo, Daniel Chirundu, Tsitsi Juru, Gerald Shambira, Addmore Chadambuka, Notion Gombe, Gibson Mandozana, Mufuta Tshimanga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe public health surveillance and response systems in Kadoma primarily rely on indicator-based surveillance (IBS), contributing to delayed detection and response to outbreaks. This protocol outlines the establishment of a Community Event-Based Surveillance (CEBS) system using the One Health approach to complement the existing IBS and enhance early identification and response to public health threats in Kadoma.Methods and analysisA mixed-methods approach will be used to establish and evaluate a CEBS system in Kadoma. The system set-up will follow the Africa Centre for Disease Control (Africa CDC) Event-Based Surveillance Framework guidelines. Healthcare personnel, community health workers (CHWs) and representatives from the animal health and environmental sectors will be trained in signal detection, triage, reporting and response. A preliminary evaluation will be conducted 5 months after implementation to assess system performance, including the frequency and verification of signals, the types of events detected, response timeliness and system acceptability. The full evaluation of the CEBS system is scheduled for September 2026, 3 years postimplementation, guided by the updated CDC guidelines for evaluating public health surveillance systems.Ethics and disseminationAuthority to establish, evaluate and publish the protocol for the CEBS system in Kadoma was sought and obtained from the Kadoma City Health Department and its Institutional Review Board (IRB) (IRB number: Mun/08/23). The surveillance system set-up process will be conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the Belmont Report and in compliance with local ethical standards. Findings will be shared with stakeholders through formal reports and community feedback channels.

Introduction

The International Health Regulations (2005) require all countries to establish Early Warning and Response (EWAR) systems capable of rapidly detecting, reporting and responding to public health events that pose significant risks to human health.1 A functional EWAR system is essential for the early detection of outbreaks and the timely implementation of control measures.2 To be effective, EWAR systems should be integrated into existing national surveillance frameworks to ensure a coordinated and comprehensive response to emerging threats. Two core components of the EWAR system are Indicator-Based Surveillance (IBS) and Event-Based Surveillance (EBS).3 IBS involves the routine and systematic collection of structured data on diseases and conditions, using standardised case definitions and reporting tools, typically from health facilities.2 4 This enables the monitoring of trends in notifiable diseases over time. In contrast, EBS is defined by the WHO as ‘the organised and rapid capture of information about events that pose a potential risk to public health5’. It includes unstructured information such as rumours, informal reports, media alerts and community observations, transmitted through both formal and informal channels.5 These pieces of information, referred to as signals, may indicate unusual or unexpected public health events that require verification and response.

A key strength of EBS is its focus on the immediate detection and rapid reporting of signals.3 6 EBS complements IBS by capturing events that may not be detected through routine surveillance, particularly those requiring urgent attention.7 When effectively implemented, EBS provides a flexible and adaptable model that can be tailored to different epidemiological and infrastructural settings.2 7 Integrating EBS with IBS enhances the sensitivity and responsiveness of public health surveillance systems.3 Building on these principles, Community EBS (CEBS) has emerged as a model for operationalising EBS at the community level.8 9 CEBS engages community members and community/village health workers (CHWs) in the early identification and reporting of public health threats. It facilitates the rapid detection of events that might otherwise go unnoticed by facility-based surveillance systems.10 11 CEBS has been successfully implemented in diverse settings, including Vietnam, Ghana, Kenya, Sierra Leone and Afghanistan, where it has strengthened early warning systems, particularly during complex emergencies such as the Ebola and COVID-19 outbreaks.2 9 11 12

In the African context, both IBS and EBS operate within the broader Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) strategy, developed bythe World Health Organization Regional Office for Africa (WHO AFRO) in 1998 to strengthen countries’ capacity to detect, report and respond to priority diseases.13 CEBS has gained traction as a strategy to improve early detection of public health events at the community level, especially in resource-limited settings where IBS coverage is weak.3 11 During the Ebola outbreak in Sierra Leone, CEBS significantly improved community-level reporting of the cases and played an important role in interrupting transmission through rapid localised responses.10 11

Zimbabwe adopted IDSR in 2003, which led to the integration of multiple vertical surveillance systems, improved data quality and the establishment of outbreak response teams at national and subnational levels.13 The IDSR framework strengthened IBS implementation, primarily through the weekly disease surveillance system, which improved reporting of priority diseases. However, recurrent public health emergencies, including cholera, typhoid, measles and COVID-19, have highlighted the need for systems capable of detecting threats early, particularly at the community level.1416 Although progress has been made in enhancing IBS, gaps remain in the timely detection of unusual events and community-level signals, especially in rapidly growing urban and peri-urban settings where informal settlements heighten vulnerability to outbreaks.17 Kadoma City, in particular, has experienced multiple outbreaks, including cholera (2008, 2018, 2023), COVID-19 (2020–2022) and measles (2022), many of which were detected late, resulting in delayed responses and increased health risks. A risk assessment conducted in July 2023 during a cholera outbreak revealed that surveillance in Kadoma relied mainly on health facility-based case reporting, limiting its early warning capacity. Additionally, the potential role of CHWs in identifying and reporting unusual health events had not been fully leveraged.

To address these gaps, Kadoma City proposed the establishment of a CEBS using the One Health approach to complement the existing IBS system. By engaging CHWs and applying a One Health approach, the system aims to enhance the timeliness and sensitivity of public health response in Kadoma. This paper presents a protocol for establishing a CEBS system in Kadoma City, Zimbabwe.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study will use a mixed-methods design. The CEBS system will be developed in alignment with the Africa Centre for Disease Control (CDC) EBS framework.7 Its performance will be evaluated at two time points: a preliminary evaluation at 5 months and a comprehensive evaluation at 3 years postimplementation, guided by the updated CDC guidelines for evaluating public health surveillance systems.18

CEBS system set-up phase

Study setting

The surveillance system will be set up in Kadoma City, which is situated in Sanyati District, Mashonaland West Province, Zimbabwe. According to the 2022 Population and Housing Census, Kadoma had 17 administrative wards with a total population of 117 380.19 Health service providers include two uniformed forces clinics, six municipal health centres and one general hospital managed by the Ministry of Health and Childcare (MoHCC). Community-based surveillance is supported by 70 CHWs, with each ward having at least two CHWs.

Kadoma City operates within Zimbabwe’s IDSR framework, which integrates multiple categorical surveillance and response systems. Under IDSR, health facilities report notifiable and non-notifiable diseases using standardised case definitions and reporting tools. Data are submitted through the district health information office into the district health information system-2(DHIS-2) platform coordinated by MoHCC. The IDSR framework has primarily emphasised IBS, focusing on routine reporting of predefined priority diseases. At present, community-based surveillance in Kadoma mainly supports the IBS component of IDSR. CHWs identify and report suspected cases of priority diseases such as cholera, measles, malaria and polio using agreed-upon lay case definitions and standardised tools. Although this approach has strengthened the detection of specific notifiable diseases, it remains largely event-blind as it does not systematically capture unstructured community information, rumours or signals.

Training in CEBS

Initial training for CHWs, healthcare personnel and representatives from the animal health and environmental sectors was conducted in August 2023, with implementation of the CEBS system beginning in September 2023. The training was a 2-week programme (10 working days) designed to capacitate all cadres involved in CEBS implementation.

Week 1: healthcare workers and representatives from the animal health and environmental sectors were trained on CEBS using the One Health Approach. The training covered disease surveillance frameworks, CEBS implementation principles, risk assessment and response to signals, coordinated surveillance and considerations for CEBS implementation. The training also included a module on media scanning, which involved systematic reviews of newspapers, radio, television, social media channels and community WhatsApp groups to identify rumours or reports of unusual health events that may signal emerging public health threats. During this phase, participatory discussions were held to codevelop simplified community case definitions and event descriptions for priority public health threats, guided by the Africa CDC EBS Framework and the IDSR Guidelines.7 20 These definitions were consolidated into a localised signal list that included both notifiable and non-notifiable events across human, animal and environmental domains. Examples of human health signals include sudden clusters of diarrhoeal illness within a household, unexplained swelling below the ears and jaws and reports of cigarette stubs or syringes found in unusual places at schools as potential indicators of drug or substance abuse. Animal health signals include unusual illness or deaths among livestock or poultry and unexplained deaths among domestic or wild animals. Environmental signals include contaminated or unsafe water sources, recurrent veld fires in residential or peri-urban areas and excessive cutting down of trees. A comprehensive initial list of signals developed is provided in online supplemental appendix 1. Reporting tools, including CHW notebooks, signal verification forms and facility signal registers, were adapted and pretested to ensure user-friendliness and alignment with the CEBS workflow. The finalised tools were then disseminated to all participating facilities and CHWs through the Kadoma City Health Department prior to full system rollout.

CEBS process and flow of information

At the community level, CHWs will detect or receive signals from various sources, including community informants, churches, schools, markets and social media platforms. These signals will be documented in CHW notebooks and reported immediately to the nearest health facility or the Kadoma City Health Department via phone call, short message services or in-person visits. At the health facility or Kadoma City Health Department, the signals will be logged manually into the signal register and triaged to avoid duplicate reporting and to determine whether the reported information constitutes a public health event. Triaging will be conducted by trained health workers or surveillance officers using the standard signal verification tool adapted from the Africa CDC EBS Framework. Signals with animal health or environmental implications will be communicated to relevant authorities, including the Veterinary Services Department and Environmental Management Agency (EMA). These sectors will participate in verification and response to zoonotic or environmental public health threats. Verified signals will be classified as events and escalated to the Rapid Response Team (RRT). The RRT will conduct risk assessments to determine the public health significance of verified events and guide timely response actions. Data management and reporting

Once signals are reported by CHWs, community informants or detected through media scanning, the RRT will be responsible for collecting relevant data through field investigations. A designated data entry clerk will capture all CEBS-related data using a questionnaire in Epi Info. The data will be analysed collaboratively with public health officials on a weekly, monthly and quarterly basis. Reports will be disseminated to the Director of Health Services for Kadoma City, the clinical services manager, the environmental health manager, the veterinary service department, EMA, the district health authorities and the Provincial Medical Directorate (PMD).

Frequency of data collection

Data on signals, events and responses will be collected daily. This continuous process will enable real-time monitoring and allow early detection of public health threats.

Feedback

To ensure continuity and support for the CEBS system, routine feedback will be provided to the sources and communities from where signals have originated. Reports will be disseminated using the same platforms used for initial signal transmission, including WhatsApp and community meetings.

Quality assurance and system supervision

Quality assurance for the CEBS system will be integrated across all stages of data collection, verification and reporting. Standardised tools, including CHW notebooks, signal verification forms, facility signal registers and supervisory checklists, will be used to ensure consistency and completeness of data. CHWs will receive routine supervision from public health officers and facility surveillance focal persons, who will review notebooks and verify reported signals during weekly data quality checks. At the facility level, verification forms and registers will be cross-checked before data are submitted to the Kadoma City Health Department. To maintain data quality, weekly data checks, quarterly supervisory visits and review meetings will be held with CHWs and health facility staff to identify reporting challenges, provide feedback and implement corrective actions. Insights from these quality assurance activities will guide continuous system improvement and strengthen overall CEBS performance.

Evaluation of the CEBS

Evaluation of the CEBS system in Kadoma will be conducted in two phases to support continuous system refinement and long-term assessment. A preliminary evaluation will be conducted 5 months after implementation (January 2024). This phase will focus on assessing system performance, including the frequency and verification of signals, the types of events detected, response timeliness and system acceptability. Both quantitative and qualitative methods will be used to assess the timeliness of event detection and reporting, as well as the acceptability of the CEBS system among CHWs and healthcare staff. Findings from this phase will inform early adjustments to strengthen the functionality and responsiveness of the system. A comprehensive evaluation will be conducted 3 years postimplementation (September 2026), following the updated CDC guidelines for evaluating public health surveillance systems. This evaluation will assess core attributes of the CEBS system, including usefulness, simplicity, stability, acceptability, representativeness, data quality and timeliness (table 1).

Data sources will include routinely collected CEBS data from CHW notebooks, signal verification forms, facility signal registers and the Epi Info database. Furthermore, in-depth interviews will be conducted with purposively selected key informants (KIs) involved in CEBS implementation, including nurses, environmental health officers (EHOs), surveillance staff and representatives from the animal health and environmental sectors. Trained data collectors will conduct face-to-face interviews in private settings using a semistructured interview guide designed to explore experiences, facilitators and barriers to CEBS implementation. Interviewers will take detailed notes during discussions to ensure accurate capture of participants’ responses.

Sample size for evaluation

The minimum sample size for the evaluation was calculated using the Dobson formula (n=z2 p (1 p)/d2). Based on a study by Stone et al11 on CEBS during the Ebola outbreak in Sierra Leone, which reported an acceptability rate of 93% among community volunteers, and using a 95% confidence level with a 5% margin of error, the calculated minimum sample size was 100 participants.11

Study population

The evaluation will target key stakeholders involved in the implementation and operation of the CEBS system in Kadoma City. These will include CHWs, who are responsible for detecting and reporting signals within the community, and KIs with knowledge and experience related to the system. The KIs will comprise nurses, environmental health technicians, EHOs, public health surveillance personnel, data entry clerks and representatives from the animal health and environmental management sectors.

Sampling methods

The sampling frame for the evaluation of the CEBS set-up in Kadoma will comprise the entire population of CHWs in Kadoma. Random sampling will be used to select CHWs from the complete list of those trained and participating in the CEBS system. Purposive sampling will be used to select KIs based on their roles and involvement in the CEBS set-up and operations.

Data collection

Data collection will be carried out in three stages of setting up the CEBS system. The first stage (preimplementation phase) will involve obtaining baseline data through a review of resource needs, training reports and existing surveillance records to assess system readiness, available human and material resources and existing gaps in the current IBS system.

The second stage (implementation phase) will include routine data collection using CHW notebooks, signal verification forms and facility signal registers. These tools will document the number and types of signals detected, verification outcomes, timeliness of reporting, response actions and feedback provided to reporters. This process will generate continuous monitoring data to track CEBS functionality and reporting performance.

The third stage (implementation evaluation phase) will assess the CEBS system’s performance attributes, including acceptability, usefulness, simplicity, stability, timeliness, data quality and representativeness. Data will be collected using a structured checklist and interviewer-administered questionnaires for both healthcare workers and CHWs and KI interview guides for supervisors and managers. Quantitative tools will be adapted from the CDC Guidelines for Evaluating Public Health Surveillance Systems and previously validated CEBS assessment instruments used in Vietnam, Côte d’Ivoire and Sierra Leone.8 11 21 The qualitative KI interview guide will be developed to explore perceived facilitators, barriers, system feasibility and contextual factors influencing CEBS implementation.

All data collection tools will be pretested with a small sample of CHWs and health workers from non-study facilities to evaluate clarity, relevance and completion time. Feedback from the pretest will inform refinements before field deployment. Data collection will be conducted in person by trained data collectors from the Kadoma City Health Department.

Set-up costs

A cost analysis will be conducted to estimate the resources used in establishing the CEBS system in Kadoma City. This assessment will cover various components, including human resource costs related to staff training, signal reporting and response activities. It will also account for the purchase of airtime to support real-time reporting, costs incurred during the investigation and response to signals and the procurement of software to digitise data collection and reporting tools. A contingency budget will be included to accommodate unforeseen expenditures. The cost estimation will be informed by a review of financial and administrative records, consultations with key stakeholders and technical experts and quotations obtained for the required equipment and services.

Data analysis

Data collected through the CEBS system will be routinely analysed to support continuous monitoring and improvement of surveillance activities. Information on reported signals, verified events and corresponding response activities will be collected from CHW notebooks, signal verification forms and facility signal registers. These data will be analysed using Microsoft Excel and Epi Info V.7 to generate descriptive statistics, including frequencies, proportions, means and medians. Geographic Information Systems will be used to assess the spatial distribution of verified events. Routine data analysis will be conducted on a weekly, monthly and quarterly basis, with summary reports disseminated to key stakeholders, including the Director of Health Services, Clinical Services Manager, Environmental Health Manager, District Health Authorities and the PMD, as part of the regular feedback and decision-making process. Data analysis from the comprehensive evaluation scheduled for September 2026 will be done to assess the CEBS system performance based on the updated CDC surveillance system evaluation guidelines. Core attributes such as usefulness, simplicity, stability, acceptability, representativeness and timeliness will be evaluated. Representativeness will be assessed by examining the data sources and sectors contributing to signal reporting. Additional document review of surveillance reports, meeting minutes and CEBS-detected events will complement the analysis to ensure a comprehensive understanding of system functionality.

Qualitative data from the interviews will be transcribed from notebooks into Microsoft Word and analysed thematically to identify emerging patterns and key themes related to system functionality, acceptability and contextual feasibility. Findings will be triangulated with quantitative data to enhance interpretation and validity. Data collection will continue until thematic saturation is reached.

Patients and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of our research.

Discussion

This protocol presents the implementation of a CEBS system in Kadoma. Repeated outbreaks of cholera (2008, 2018, 2023), measles (2022) and COVID-19 (2020–2022) highlighted systemic deficiencies in the IBS system, including delayed case detection, limited community engagement and underutilisation of local information sources. These challenges highlighted the urgent need for a more responsive and inclusive surveillance model. The proposed CEBS system will address these gaps by using unstructured, community-generated information, known as signals, to detect unusual health events earlier and trigger faster public health actions.

The proposed CEBS system in Kadoma aligns with established frameworks from the Africa CDC and WHO.5 7 Comparable systems implemented in Vietnam, Ghana, Kenya and Uganda have demonstrated improvements in early outbreak detection and response.122123 Notably, in Sierra Leone and Kenya, CEBS was reported to be more effective than traditional IBS in identifying outbreaks, particularly in underserved and remote settings.11 22 This is especially relevant for Kadoma, where rapid urban growth has outpaced health service capacity, and its sole reliance on IBS has delayed outbreak detection.

A key feature of the CEBS system in Kadoma is its focus on adaptability and inclusivity. By operationalising the One Health approach, the system integrates human, animal and environmental health signals, allowing for the detection of both notifiable and non-notifiable events, such as sudden animal deaths, unexplained community deaths and environmental hazards.2 3 Additionally, the participatory development of localised signal lists and targeted training of CHWs promotes community ownership and system sustainability. This strategy mirrors successful CEBS implementation in Afghanistan and Sierra Leone, where strong community engagement contributed to system resilience.911

A methodological strength of this protocol is its mixed-methods evaluation design, which will assess system performance and contextual feasibility using both quantitative and qualitative data. However, the use of paper-based reporting tools during the initial implementation phase may compromise data timeliness and quality. Kenya and Nigeria have mitigated similar challenges by incorporating mobile surveillance platforms, such as the m-Dharura system.22 24 Additionally, while broad signal definitions enhance sensitivity, they may increase false positives and verification workload, a challenge previously noted in Ghana and Vietnam.12 21 Addressing these limitations through digital innovation, a refined signal list and sustained funding will be key to ensuring system scalability and institutionalisation.

Communication and dissemination of results

Throughout implementation, regular data analysis of signals and events will inform protocol refinements. Updates, such as revised signal lists or data tools, will be communicated to CHWs and stakeholders. Findings from the evaluations will be shared through stakeholder meetings, routine feedback platforms and future publications to support continuous learning and system improvement.
