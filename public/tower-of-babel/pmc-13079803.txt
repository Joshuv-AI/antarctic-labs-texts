An interpretable piecewise model for human mortality: the Christopher–Samba model
Authors: Christopher Lalromawia, Samba Siva Rao Pasupuleti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Modelling mortality schedules has a long history in demographic research, with many models developed over time. This study proposes a new mortality model—the Christopher–Samba (C–S) model—that captures mortality patterns across the entire age spectrum. The proposed piecewise model integrates two components: The first part is an exponential function describing mortality up to age 5, while the second part focuses on mortality above age 5. The parameters of the second component, a reparametrized version of the classical logistic model, reflect the ages at which the annual probability of death reaches 1% and 10%, representing the benchmark ages for the onset of increased mortality and severe mortality, respectively. These thresholds naturally vary across populations. Mortality data from the Human Mortality Database were used to demonstrate the application of the proposed C–S model. The C–S model fits well across countries with diverse mortality schedules. Fitted parameters reveal substantial variation in the ages at which the annual probability of death reaches 1% and 10%, underscoring population differences in mortality dynamics. In summary, with its simplicity, strong fit, and meaningful parameter interpretations, the proposed C–S model is a valuable addition to mortality modelling literature.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40550-2.

Introduction

Understanding how the risk of mortality changes with age is of fundamental importance in public health policy, actuarial science, and demography. While empirical life tables provide a valuable snapshot of mortality at a specific point in time, their raw data often contain irregularities—especially at the youngest and oldest ages—which limit their analytical utility. To overcome this, mortality schedules can be modelled parametrically. This process transforms unprocessed data into a robust analytical framework by using a succinct set of parameters to represent the entire risk profile from birth to the most advanced ages1. These models smooth erratic data, estimate missing values, and enable the creation of standardized mortality schedules for subpopulations.

The power of this approach is exemplified by models like the Heligman–Pollard model2, which moves beyond simple description to provide a flexible and reliable basis for analysis. The smoothed mortality functions generated by these models enable direct comparison across populations and time periods. Crucially, comparisons based on model parameters are far more effective than those using raw life tables, enabling researchers to objectively assess and contrast the mortality regimes of various countries or track a single population’s evolution over decades3.

Ultimately, the parameters of a mortality model are more than just mathematical abstractions; they capture the underlying biological, environmental, and societal determinants of survival. Shifts in these parameters often reflect broad public health and ageing trends, from the historical decline in infectious disease mortality to contemporary challenges posed by senescent mortality in ageing populations4. Therefore, mortality modelling is not merely a statistical exercise. It is a crucial tool for identifying public health priorities, informing social policy, and deepening our understanding of human longevity.

Despite their significance, mortality modelling remains complex. Data quality is also uneven, especially in developing countries, limiting the reliability of many models5. Classical parametric models have long been used to smooth mortality schedules, summarise observed patterns, and provide the foundation for life table construction6. An effective graduation method must eliminate random fluctuations while preserving the true age pattern of mortality2. However, mortality trajectories are inherently nonlinear, challenging the predictive accuracy of traditional approaches7. Furthermore, many models rely on mathematically convenient parameters that lack demographic interpretability, restricting their value for comparative and policy-oriented analysis8.

These drawbacks highlight the importance of models that are simple, have a good fit, and have interpretable parameters. From a demographic perspective, interpretability allows for direct comparison of mortality across populations and time, making results more useful for research and policy. In this context, the present study introduces a new interpretable piecewise threshold-based mortality model, the Christopher–Samba (C–S) model, designed to balance simplicity, interpretability, and effectiveness. The C-S model consists of two piecewise components that capture mortality dynamics in childhood age of up to 5 years —dominated by neonatal conditions, infections, and nutrition, and marked by a rapid decline in the early years9—and in the remaining age groups- where there is a gradual rise in mortality for many years before accelerating at older ages. The proposed model consists of only four parameters. Variations in the parameters across populations or over years reflect shifts in corresponding mortality patterns.

Graduation of mortality schedules

Across populations, the pattern of mortality progression with age is remarkably consistent. Generally speaking, mortality rates are high in infancy, drop precipitously in early childhood, rise once more in adolescence because of the so-called “accident hump,” and then rise exponentially in adulthood. Using mathematical functions with a small number of parameters, parametric mortality models seek to replicate this distinctive pattern10.

John Graunt11established the first systematic review of death records in London, which laid the groundwork for later actuarial and demographic research and the origins of mortality analysis. Abraham De Moivre12expanded on this early realization by introducing the uniform distribution of deaths model, a theoretical breakthrough that significantly streamlined annuity calculations. A biological explanation of mortality was offered by Benjamin Gompertz in 1833, a century later13. He suggested that the force of mortality grows exponentially with age. As Haberman14points out, the Gompertz formulation provided a long-lasting mathematical basis for contemporary mortality modelling, marking a significant turning point in actuarial science.

Gompertz’s work was later improved by Makeham15, who included a constant term to account for background mortality. This allowed the model to better capture mortality trends over a larger age range16. Thiele17later expanded this methodological framework by adding the idea of competing risks to create a more complete model that included mortality dynamics over the course of a person’s life17,18.

Many methods of mortality graduation were developed in the decades that followed. Perks’s19 logistic model and its variations were significant among them. Despite some drawbacks, the well-established Gompertz and Weibull20models continued to be used extensively. Building on Perks’s research, later advancements like the Gamma–Makeham model21and the frailty models22were crucial in understanding patterns of late-age mortality. Another significant step was the Siler model23, which included a background mortality term to successfully capture mortality across a wide age range. However, it was unable to accurately depict the mortality that occurs in early adulthood.

The work of Heligman and Pollard2represented a significant advancement in mortality modelling, as they put forth an eight-parameter model that could fully describe the age-specific mortality trajectory from infancy to old age. Their formulation achieved a high level of accuracy and flexibility by combining distinct functional forms for senescent, infant, and accidental mortality. For this reason, it continues to be among the best parametric models for fitting mortality data in a variety of populations24. However, its complexity together with a comparatively large number of parameters motivates researchers to try for other simple alternatives.

In response to these limitations, researchers developed alternative formulations, such as the Extreme-Value model25and the Log–Logistic and Log–Normal models26. These models broadened the analytical toolkit for mortality research. In recent years, notable progress has been made by Bergeron-Boucher et al.27, de Beer and Janssen28, Pascariu et al.29, Basellini et al.30, Castellares et al.16, and Dyrting & Taylor18, who have further advanced parametric approaches to capture the complex dynamics of human mortality.

In particular, the CoDe model proposed by de Beer and Janssen28offers a flexible framework for modelling mortality by explicitly capturing compression and delay through a ten-parameter structure, combining time-invariant and time-varying components. Its piecewise formulation, consisting of five terms, effectively represents distinct mortality phases across the full age range. Motivated by the life-stage–specific nature of mortality patterns, this study proposes a simpler and more interpretable alternative piecewise model with smooth graduation across ages. Further, as shown by Basellini et al.30, re-parameterisation enhances interpretability, reduces parameter correlation, and improves estimation stability and sensitivity, strengthening demographic analysis, as also noted by Pasupuleti and Pathak8. In this study, we contribute to this ongoing work by proposing a simple model characterized by fewer, demographically interpretable parameters.

Christopher-samba model (C–S model)

Exploring contemporary mortality schedules from the Human Mortality Database (HMD) reveals two things:

In early life, especially up to age 5, mortality declines sharply in an exponential-like pattern, reflecting the sharp reduction in the probability of death in early childhood.With increasing age, after age 5, mortality after merely remaining constant for many years until mid-30s rises steadily but slows in later years, reflecting the distinctive late-age deceleration commonly observed in human mortality2,9,13,15,31. This overall pattern—the early stability and the late-age deceleration—can be more appropriately captured by a logistic model, which follows a stretched S-shaped curve32,31.

The 1% annual probability of death serves as a marker of the onset of increased mortality, indicating the point of age at which mortality begins to deviate from the very low levels of early adulthood. It marks the transition into an age range where mortality starts to exert a noticeable influence on population change. In contrast, the 10% probability of death reflects a much later stage of ageing, where frailty is pronounced, and the risk of death increases rapidly. From here on, we refer to the age of 10% annual probability of death as the age of severe mortality. Indeed, empirical evidence from across countries suggests that fewer than 4% of individuals survive beyond the age of 10% annual probability of death (Table A2 in the Appendix), with almost all of the remaining survivors also dying within the next 10–15 years. So, together, these two threshold ages provide clear and meaningful benchmarks for tracing the progression of mortality risk across the lifespan.

By anchoring the C-S model parameters at these interpretable reference points, the model not only enhances comparability across populations and time periods but also throws light on the speed at which populations move from the 1% to the 10% mortality levels. This speed could serve as an indicator of the rate of mortality increase or the pace of ageing, enabling researchers to assess ageing dynamics, detect shifts in mortality trends, compare population health across regions or cohorts, and support health policy planning and preventive strategies.

We know the primary goal of any mortality model is to provide an alternative to empirical data. But, usually, good-fitting models with fewer parameters that have clear demographic or biological interpretations are preferred over the complex models33. In this regard, the C-S model stands out for its simplicity and interpretability. Unlike many other models, which contain several complex terms, the C-S model has only two components, making it easier to apply while still capturing key mortality patterns across the entire lifespan. Its second component offers flexibility to choose specific benchmark ages to study a wide range of growth and decay behaviours across populations or periods. The model also allows estimation of other benchmark ages of achieving certain (e.g., 5%, 50%, 90%, etc.) annual probability of death, providing intuitive thresholds for comparison.

Application of the C–S model for graduation of mortality schedules

In this section, the proposed C-S model is applied to graduate mortality schedules from multiple countries at two distinct time points. All analyses and graph plotting were conducted using SAS OnDemand for Academics34, a freely available online platform. Mortality data were obtained from the Human Mortality Database35. For each country, the most recent available year and the earliest year from 1921 onward were selected. The study includes: Australia (1921, 2021), Canada (1921, 2023), Chile (1992, 2020), Germany (1990, 2020), Hong Kong (1986, 2023), Italy (1921, 2022), Japan (1947, 2023), Korea (2003, 2023), the United Kingdom (1992, 2022), and the United States (1933, 2023). These countries represent diverse demographic structures and geographic regions. Across these periods, each country experienced substantial changes in mortality patterns. Through this analysis, we aim to demonstrate how the C-S model helps to explore mortality patterns over time and across countries, highlighting the flexibility of the proposed C-S model in capturing these variations.

The fit of the C-S model for various countries at two distinct time points is shown in Fig. 1, and the fitted parameter estimates are presented in Table 1. It is clear from Fig. 1 that the fit of the C-S model is good for all the considered countries. We can see from Table 1 that the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:A$$\end{document}, which reflects the probability of death during infancy, exhibits substantial variation across countries and over time. Countries such as Canada and Italy experienced more than 12% chance of death during infancy in 1921, and this chance has decreased to around 0.3–0.5% by around 2023, aligning closely with socioeconomic development and public health achievements in these countries. In recent years, Hong Kong (0.0015), Japan (0.0018), and Italy (0.0025) have exhibited the lowest probability of death during infancy, which is indicative of exceptionally low infant mortality and advanced healthcare systems in these countries.

Comparison of the C-S model fit with other commonly used mortality models

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the C-S model in graduating mortality schedules, the C-S model fit was compared with a few commonly used mortality models: Gompertz model, Makeham Model, Kannisto-Makeham Model, and Heligman Pollard Model (see Table A1 in the Appendix for more details about these models).

The parameters of the proposed C–S model were estimated using nonlinear least squares, with initial values chosen from observable features of the mortality schedules such as infant mortality levels and approximate ages where empirical annual probability of death is close to 1% and 10%. Owing to its parsimonious structure, the model exhibited low sensitivity to reasonable variations in starting values and converged reliably across all populations and time periods under standard nonlinear routines. In contrast, the Heligman–Pollard model, which involves a substantially larger number of parameters, was more sensitive to initial values and did not converge under the same estimation framework, requiring the use of a specialized fitting package. While a formal comparison of convergence rates was beyond the scope of this study, these observations highlight the numerical stability and practical advantages of the proposed model.

The fit of all these models, measured using the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) was presented in Table 2. This comparative assessment, based on SSE values and graphical evaluations (Figure A1 in the Appendix), shows that the C–S model achieves a level of fit comparable to classical models. While SSE provides an intuitive measure of overall fit, it does not account for differences in model complexity. Therefore, we additionally evaluated model performance using Mean Squared Error (MSE), the Akaike Information Criteria (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC). These criteria penalize over-parameterization and allow a more balanced comparison across models. The results, reported in the Supplementary file (Tables B1, B2 and B3), show that the C–S model consistently achieves competitive fit while maintaining parsimony.

To demonstrate its usefulness, the proposed C-S model was fitted to the mortality schedules of several countries that differ in terms of population size and mortality patterns, including Australia, Canada, Chile, Germany, Hong Kong, Italy, Japan, Korea, UK, and USA. For each of these countries, the C-S model was fitted for two different time points, one for the latest year and the other for the earliest available year after 1920. Mortality data from the HMD were used for this purpose. In all these cases, the proposed model provided a satisfactory fit, and the fit is mostly comparable with other commonly used mortality models.

C-S model fit results showed, infant mortality exceeded 12% in countries such as Canada and Italy in 1921 but declined to around 0.3–0.5% by 2023 in most populations, reaching some of the lowest levels in Hong Kong, Japan, and Italy. Childhood survival also improved markedly, with year-to-year mortality reductions exceeding 90% in recent decades. The age at onset of increased mortality (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G$$\end{document}) shifted substantially—for example, by nearly 21 years in Australia between 1921 and 2021—while cross-country differences persist in the pace of progression toward severe mortality (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H$$\end{document}). These findings illustrate the ability of the C–S model to capture meaningful changes in mortality timing and ageing dynamics across populations.

These shifts in the ages at onset of increased and severe mortality (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G\:\text{ a}\mathrm{n}\text{d }\:H$$\end{document}) are consistent with long-term epidemiological transitions and policy-related developments37. Sustained declines in cardiovascular mortality, advances in medical technology, and expanded access to healthcare have likely contributed to the postponement of elevated mortality risks to older ages, providing an important interpretive context for the variation captured by the C–S model parameters38.

The proposed C–S model is developed and evaluated using single-year age-specific mortality schedules from the Human Mortality Database (HMD), which provides high-quality and well-validated data. A natural question therefore arises regarding the suitability of the model for mortality schedules in developing-country settings. In many such contexts, including India and China, mortality information is typically available only in abridged age groups and is affected by incomplete death registration and age misreporting, particularly at older ages39,40. Even when smoothing methods are applied to reconstruct single-year schedules, these data limitations may distort fine-age mortality patterns and, consequently, affect parameter stability and model fit.

To explore the applicability of the C–S model in settings characterized by uneven mortality data quality, as is common in many developing countries, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses using smoothed mortality schedules for India and China. These analyses highlight that the model’s performance varies across contexts, performing reasonably well in China but less satisfactorily in India (Figure A2 in the Appendix). Such differences likely reflect a combination of factors, including structural heterogeneity in mortality patterns, limitations in underlying data quality, and potential artefacts introduced by the smoothing procedures required to reconstruct single-year age schedules. These findings underscore that the applicability of the C–S model in developing-country settings is context-dependent rather than universal. Comprehensive validation using high-quality mortality data from a wider range of developing countries therefore remains an important direction for future research. Further extensions of the C–S framework—potentially incorporating an additional demographically interpretable component for young and adult ages—may enhance its flexibility and applicability in such contexts. Overall, the C–S model is best viewed as a parsimonious and interpretable complement to existing mortality models, particularly in populations with reliable age-specific mortality data.
