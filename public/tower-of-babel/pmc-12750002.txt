Efficacy of laparoscopic bailout surgery for moderate and severe acute cholecystitis
Authors: Nao Kitasaki, Tomoyuki Abe, Tsuyoshi Kobayashi, Naruhiko Honmyo, Shintaro Kuroda, Michinori Hamaoka, Daisuke Takei, Takashi Onoe, Toshihiko Kohashi, Koichi Oishi, Hideki Ohdan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
“Bailout surgery” (BOS) refers to specific surgical maneuvers implemented to safely manage difficult acute cholecystitis (AC) cases and prevent complications, including massive bleeding or bile duct injury (BDI). This study validated laparoscopic BOS (lap-BOS) in moderate to severe AC cases requiring BOS. Overall, 547 patients with moderate and severe AC diagnosed per Tokyo Guideline 2018 (TG18) were retrospectively analyzed from 2020 to 2023. Of these, 129 underwent BOS, and 418 conventional laparoscopic cholecystectomy (Lap-C). Perioperative outcomes between Lap-C and BOS were compared. Patients who underwent BOS were divided into lap-BOS and conversion to open surgery (CO) groups. Patients in the BOS group were older and had higher Charlson comorbidity index scores than those in the Lap-C group. Intraoperative blood loss and operative times the rate of postoperative bile leakage were worse in the BOS group than Lap-C group. Among BOS group, the Lap-BOS group had shorter operative times, less blood loss and lower rates of complications than CO groups. BDI, postoperative bile leakage and the modified Nassar difficulty grading scales were comparable between the Lap-BOS and CO groups. Although, no differences were observed in BDI or postoperative bile leakage among Lap-C and Lap-BOS. Lap-BOS would be beneficial in avoiding common bile duct injury as for TG18 moderate and severe AC. In difficult AC cases, Lap-BOS is an alternative strategy to prevent bile duct injury of common bile duct requiring surgical intervention postoperative bile leakage and reduce intraoperative time and intraoperative bleeding rather than CO.

Introduction

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (Lap-C) is a widely performed surgical procedure1. The benefits of Lap-C over open cholecystectomy are well established, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter postoperative hospital stay, and lower incidence of postoperative complications2,3. According to the Japan Society for Endoscopic Surgery database, Lap-C is one of the most common laparoscopic surgeries performed4. Recently, the Tokyo Guidelines 2018 (TG18) proposed that Lap-C should be considered a safe and effective treatment option for acute cholecystitis (AC), including the severe AC cases at advanced laparoscopic centers4.

Nevertheless, in clinical situations, complete Lap-C in some AC cases is challenging owing to the pronounced adhesion of Calot’s triangle, which causes complications such as bile peritonitis or bile duct injury (BDI). In such cases, conversion to open surgery (CO) and bailout surgery (BOS) are necessary5. Several reports have indicated that CO is significantly associated with prolonged hospital stays, longer operative time, and greater intraoperative bleeding compared to Lap-C2,3,6. BOS refers to a set of techniques initially developed to prevent massive bleeding and BDI. These techniques, as outlined in TG18, include the fundus-first approach, subtotal cholecystectomy, and CO5,7. However, the optimal surgical approach for BOS in AC remains unclear. This study aimed to assess the clinical utility of BOS and analyze procedure-specific outcomes associated with its application in moderate (grade II)/severe (grade III) AC.

Methods

Patient population and selection

This study enrolled 129 patients who underwent BOS and 418 who underwent conventional Lap-C for moderate or severe AC, diagnosed according to the TG18 criteria from May 2020 to August 2023. The data were retrospectively collected from the Hiroshima Surgical study group of Clinical Oncology (HiSCO) database, which comprises seven hospitals. There were at least 10 primary surgeons and senior surgeons involved. Primary surgeons had a minimum of 3 years of post-graduate training, whereas senior surgeons had 10 or more years of post-graduate training in gastroenterological surgery. The modified Nassar difficulty grading scale was used to assess intraoperative difficulty8. Variables measured were sex, age, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA-PS), and Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) scores9. Of the patients, 513 and 34 were diagnosed with grade II and grade III AC, respectively. Perioperative factors were compared between the BOS and Lap-C groups, as well as between the Lap-BOS and CO groups.

Laboratory and computed tomography (CT) findings were collected preoperatively. Eleven preoperative CT parameters for AC were analyzed using previously reported measurements: poor contrast of the gallbladder wall, gallbladder neck wall thickness, and gallbladder neck wall with poor contrast. Duodenal edema was defined as the thickening of the intestinal wall confined to the area adjacent to the gallbladder10. Two surgeons interpreted the CT images.

Emergency magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography was routinely performed to observe the anatomy of the extrahepatic bile duct, prevent intraoperative injury to the common bile duct (CBD), and detect the accessory hepatic duct. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) was performed in suspected concomitant acute cholangitis cases. The study was approved by the local review board (E2023-0050), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Confirming that all experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

For patients with incidental GB cancer, Our strategy for curative resection included simple cholecystectomy, extended cholecystectomy with regional lymph node (LN) dissection, and wedge resection of the gallbladder fossa of the liver. In the event of invasion of the cystic duct or hepatoduodenal ligament, or in the presence of suspicious metastatic hepatoduodenal LNs, extrahepatic bile duct resection was performed. Regional LN dissection included the common hepatic artery (LN8), proper hepatic artery (LN12a), bile duct (LN12b), portal vein (LN12p), and retropancreatic LNs (LN13). We recommended additional radical surgery if tumor invasion was T2 or higher after simple cholecystectomy11.

Intraoperative difficulty assessment (using modified Nassar difficulty grading scale)

We retrospectively reviewed the surgical videos of the enrolled patients and assessed surgical difficulty using the modified Nassar difficulty grading scale (grades 1–5)8. The scale, published in 2020, evaluates operative findings based on the gallbladder, cystic pedicle, and associated adhesions. The scale is as follows:

Grade 1:

Floppy, non-adherent gallbladder; thin, clear cystic pedicle; simple adhesions up to the neck/Hartmann’s pouch.

Grade 2:

Mucocele or gallbladder packed with stones; fat-laden cystic pedicle; simple adhesions up to the body.

Grade 3:

Gallbladder in deep fossa, acute cholecystitis, contracted or fibrotic, Hartmann’s pouch adherent to CBD; abnormal or obscured cystic pedicle anatomy; dense adhesions involving the hepatic flexure or duodenum.

Grade 4:

Obscured gallbladder, empyema, gangrene, or mass; cystic pedicle impossible to clarify; dense, fibrotic adhesions wrapping the gallbladder and adjacent structures.

Grade 5:

Mirizzi Syndrome type 2 or higher, cholecysto-cutaneous, cholecysto-duodenal or cholecysto-colic fistula.

The grading system provides an overall summary of operative conditions, using the worst factor found in any category to determine the final grade.

Surgical procedures

BOS consists of the fundus-first approach associated with subtotal cholecystectomy, subtotal cholecystectomy, CO, and fenestration5. The consultant surgeon determined the type of surgical procedure—namely, either Lap-BOS and CO groups. In this study, “Lap-BOS” is defined as Lap-C initiated and completed laparoscopically with BOS techniques, whereas “CO” denotes procedures that began laparoscopically but were converted to open cholecystectomy during BOS. Generally, the first choice of treatment is Lap-C, which is a widely accepted four-port procedure. Priority should first be given to achieving the Critical View of Safety (CVS). CVS is an important milestone to prevent BDI during Lap-C. It ensures that the cystic duct and cystic artery are correctly identified before they are clipped and divided5. In cases where CVS could not be secured, the decision to convert from Lap-C to BOS was made by the senior surgeon. Gallbladder neck reconstruction was performed during subtotal cholecystectomy using a clipping, stapling device or sutures. In cases of severe inflammation around the GB neck, stapling was used as 1st choice rather than suturing. In our group, we also combined the fundus-first approach with subtotal cholecystectomy if the cystic duct was difficult to process; this technique is referred to as “combination of procedures” in this review. The surgical procedure factors were compared among patients in the Lap-BOS and CO groups.

Morbidity and complications

BDI was classified according to the Strasberg classification12. The Strasberg classification system remains one of the most widely adopted frameworks for categorizing BDI, particularly in the context of laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Originally proposed by Strasberg et al., this schema provides a systematic approach to identifying and stratifying BDIs based on anatomical disruption and clinical significance. It encompasses both minor bile leaks (Types A–D) and major transections (Type E), with the latter further sub-classified (E1–E5) according to the location and extent of injury relative to the hepatic duct confluence.

Type A: Bile leak from the cystic duct stump or small bile ducts in the liver bed.

Usually managed conservatively or with endoscopic drainage.

Type B: Occlusion of an aberrant right hepatic duct without bile leak. May go undiagnosed initially; surgical correction might be needed.

Type C: Transection of an aberrant right hepatic duct with bile leak. Recognized by postoperative bile leak; requires drainage and possible reconstruction.

Type D: Lateral injury to a major bile duct without complete transection. Can often be repaired primarily or with stenting.

Type E: Complete transection and/or excision of the common hepatic duct, classified further into E1–E5.

Complications were defined according to the methods described by Clavien et al.13. Postoperative complications were defined as grade II or higher. Postoperative mortality was defined as death occurring within 90 days of surgery.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians and ranges, whereas categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages). Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Data were analyzed using a single-factor analysis of variance. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The cutoff values for albumin (Alb) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were estimated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences for Windows (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Comparison of characteristics among the Lap-C and BOS groups in patients with grade II and grade III AC

Background and perioperative factors were analyzed for the BOS and Lap-C groups (Tables 1, 2). The number of patients aged > 80 years was significantly higher in the Lap-C group than in BOS group (P = 0.002). The CCI score was higher in BOS group than Lap-C group (P = 0.008). Preoperative Alb (P = 0.002) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels (P = 0.030) were significantly better in the Lap-C group. Preoperative CT findings showed that there were significantly more inflammatory findings in the gallbladder and surrounding area in the BOS group compared with the Lap-C group. There were significant differences in operative time (P < 0.001), blood loss (P < 0.001), and postoperative hospitalization days (P < 0.001). Regarding Clavien–Dindo (CD) grade III complications, the BOS group included 8 cases: postoperative abscess (n = 4), empyema (n = 3), and aspiration pneumonia (n = 1). The Lap-C group included 13 cases: postoperative cholangitis (n = 6), postoperative abscess (n = 4), and empyema (n = 3). As for CD grade IV complications, both cases were diagnosed as sepsis. Three patients died due to preoperative aspiration pneumonia and sepsis-associated acute cholangitis. However, no significant differences were observed in terms of BDI of Strasberg classification type D & E and postoperative complications, excluding postoperative bile leakage. The modified Nassar difficulty grading scale revealed significantly more grade 4 cases in the BOS group than in the Lap-C group (P < 0.001).

Comparison of perioperative characteristics between the Lap-BOS and CO groups in patients with grade II and grade III AC requiring BOS

Tables 3 and 4 summarize the characteristics of patients with grade II and grade III AC after laparoscopic BOS (Lap-BOS) (n = 91) and CO (n = 38) groups. Among the preoperative factors, no significant differences were observed between the two groups except for the TG18 Grade (P = 0.034). The Lap-BOS group showed shorter operative time (P = 0.002) and less intraoperative blood loss (P < 0.001) than the CO group. Remnant cystic duct syndrome, including residual cholecystitis and gallstone formation after subtotal cholecystectomy, was observed in three patients (3.3%) in the Lap-BOS group and one (2.6%) in the CO group. No significant differences were observed in the modified Nassar difficulty grading scale between the two groups. One patient (1.1%) experienced intraoperative BDI of B5 in the Lap-BOS group (Strasberg classification type C). Postoperative bile leakage was identified in 6 patients (4.7%), all of whom were successfully treated with endoscopic nasobiliary drainage without the need for reoperation. Most postoperative bile leaks, particularly those classified as Strasberg classification type A. One patient died within 90 days of surgery (0.8%) due to exacerbation of duodenal cancer that had existed prior to surgery.

Discussion

BDI is one of the most significant complications associated with cholecystectomy, and the implementation of comprehensive measures to prevent its occurrence is of paramount importance. This ultimately results in conditions that require significant liver resection or transplantation. The consequences of BDI are biliary stricture, hepatic atrophy, recurrent cholangitis, portal hypertension, and biliary cirrhosis. The incidence of BDI remains relatively constant at 0.1–1.4%, which is largely attributable to the misinterpretation of biliary anatomy14. This study included emergency surgical cases for grade II/III AC. The BOS group had poorer preoperative CT findings, background factors, and blood test results compared with the Lap-C group. Perioperative factors such as blood loss and operative time were better in the Lap-C group. On the other hand, BDI of Strasberg classification type D & E, and postoperative complications were not significantly different in the two groups, which included more than 50% of patients with the modified Nassar difficulty grading scale of grade 4, indicating that BOS techniques may contribute to avoiding BDI of Strasberg classification type D & E despite the need for reoperation.

In our comparative analysis of the Lap-BOS and CO groups, preoperative factors showed no significant differences other than TG18 Grade. However, the Lap-BOS group demonstrated superior perioperative outcomes, in terms of operative time, intraoperative blood loss and length of postoperative hospital stays than the CO. Furthermore, gangrenous cholecystitis was notably more prevalent in the CO group. The incidence of BDI in Lap-BOS (1/91, 1.1%) in this study was comparable to that reported in previous studies (0.4–1.5%)3,15. These findings underscore that while anatomical distortion due to gangrene or hemorrhage may necessitate CO in select cases, it is fundamentally important to actively utilize BOS techniques in order to complete the procedure laparoscopically.

Consequently, many centers opt for laparoscopic surgery as the primary approach, even in cases of severe AC. Compared to the Lap-C group, patients in the BOS group demonstrated significantly greater pericholecystic inflammatory spread and a notably higher incidence of gallstones impacted at the GB neck (exceeding 60%). In this study, combination of procedures (the fundus-first approach with subtotal cholecystectomy) were employed in 20% of the Lap-BOS group. The fundus-first approach was used owing to the challenging nature of visualizing the gallbladder neck in the presence of gallstones. Subsequently, a subtotal procedure was performed to address the difficulty in accessing the gallbladder duct due to adhesions around the neck. This approach may have contributed to the avoidance of BDI, as evidenced by the growing body of literature suggesting the efficacy and safety of a fundus-first strategy followed by subtotal cholecystectomy as an alternative to immediate CO15.

With respect to postoperative complications and avoidance of CO, several recent reports have indicated that the fundus-first approach is more effective than the retrograde approach not only in routine surgical procedures but also in the context of AC16,17. However, in clinical practice, surgeons frequently encounter situations where a serosal incision at the gallbladder neck is initiated, necessitating conversion to BOS owing to the inherent challenges associated with conventional surgical manipulation. In this study, the modified Nassar difficulty grading scale was used to assess intraoperative difficulty. Although this scale is used an intraoperative assessment, it was difficult to make a reference to the choice of the BOS technique. Various criteria have been proposed to assess surgical difficulty based on intraoperative findings18,19. The surgical difficulty score, developed by Asai et al., evaluates indications for BOS based on intraoperative grades20. Egawa et al. demonstrated that this scoring system was correlated with the operative time, blood loss, and conversion to subtotal cholecystectomy21. However, none of these have been applied specifically to BOS groups. Our team plans to adopt and refine these criteria for future evaluations.

Postoperative bile leakage was observed in 1.3% (7/547) of cases, and all BDI were successfully treated with ERCP without necessary of reoperation. Previous reports showed that bile leakage was more common after laparoscopic subtotal cholecystectomy than after open surgery1–3. However, the rates of BDI, postoperative complications, reoperation, and mortality were lower22,23. In the case of subtotal cholecystectomy, complications such as postoperative bile leakage and gallbladder malignancy have been recognized. Previous studies reported that gallstones recurred at long-term follow-up after subtotal resection in approximately 4–5% of patients22,23. The risk of gallbladder cancer development has also been reported, although the incidence is low11,22. In this study, three patients (3.3%) in the lap-BOS group had residual cystic duct syndrome, which is consistent with existing reports. Residual cystic duct syndrome was completely cured using antibiotic treatment. Further studies should be conducted to evaluate the influence of BOS on long-term complications such as remnant gallbladder stone formation and remnant gallbladder cancer.

A 2017 survey of experts in Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan indicated that only 17.5% of respondents said that CO would make operations easier24. OC should be prioritized over lap-BOS to mitigate the risks of BDI and massive bleeding in facilities or by surgeons who are highly proficient in managing potential hemorrhage. The choice of BOS method must be determined by the safest and most established technique within a given institutional context. A recent study reported the use of the Pringle maneuver during Lap-C to limit blood flow from the liver pedicle, thereby controlling bleeding and providing a clear surgical field25. Although the CO rates have decreased, it is questionable whether the Pringle maneuver can be performed in patients with bleeding. Practically, it is important to control bleeding during Lap-C, and further research is needed to determine procedures that should be performed in cases of actual bleeding. This study revealed longer operative times and greater blood loss in patients undergoing CO.

This study had some limitations. First, this was a multicenter study, and the timing of the transition from Lap-C to BOS depended on the judgment of each surgeon. Second, comprehensive and appropriate surgical evaluation is difficult because long-term complications are not tracked. Third, the timing of cholecystitis onset was not analyzed in this study. The results of surgical intervention early in the development of cholecystitis are good, and all centers were committed to early surgical intervention when possible26–28. However, in practice, the safety of Lap-C has been reported for cholecystitis more than 3 days after onset, and both cases were included in this study29. Further studies are needed to elucidate the preoperative and intraoperative factors that require conversion to BOS.

In conclusion, although BOS tends to increase operative time and intraoperative blood loss, among these procedures Lap-BOS would be beneficial for avoiding bile duct injury in Strasberg type D & E for TG18 grade II or higher AC.
