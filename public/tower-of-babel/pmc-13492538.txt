Socialising artificial intelligence in diagnostic cellular pathology
Authors: Peter Johnston
Published: 2025

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) is growing quickly in high-tech, people-dependent sectors, including healthcare. There is potential application in diagnostic cellular (anatomic) pathology as shown by the article by Jaeckle et al.1 In this paper, the authors describe a novel interpretable AI tool which analyses scanned whole slide histopathological images to diagnose coeliac disease. The tool can provide pathologists with quantitative estimates of relevant data—crypt to villus ratio, proportion of intraepithelial lymphocytes—to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Although not a rare diagnosis, coeliac disease is challenging because the criteria in question tend to be inconsistently determined by eye.

While the development the paper reports is promising, are there things we should stop and think about as AI technology is adopted? Pathology investigations are integral to patient progress along clinical pathways with benefit in improved patient outcomes following appropriate diagnosis and personalised treatment. At the same time, there is growing pressure in cellular pathology, because more patients are being tested and more information is needed, increasingly based on tiny fragments of tissue. Jaeckle et al1 show their tool has utility in the study context, potentially easing pressure on pathologist time—AI helping pathologists has appeal.

Adopting technology is a tradition in pathology to improve the quality of reports. The flip side is that technology brings more data to the interpretation—and so increases the intellectual effort and time pathologists expend on reporting. Whilst AI tools have the potential to add data, automation might begin to rebalance the wicked disparity between workload and shortfall in available workforce. AI has proven capability in genomic studies of tumour biology (eg, Tiwari et al2) with helpful implications for patients. Moving into the diagnostic setting, however, AI requires advanced digital capability with access to scanned whole slide images, all requiring massive and sustained investment. This is a long way off as a universal solution.

Laboratory medicine has expertise in consistent accuracy, relying on skilled staff and robust systems designed for safety, internal and external controls and quality assurance processes. These are surrounded by regulation of devices, standards of practice, professional regulation and laboratory accreditation to ensure reliability. Frameworks around AI must also ensure confidentiality (not open access) and acceptability by professionals, patients and the public.3 As laboratory data increasingly form the backbone for safe, effective treatment, the need for oversight of AI used in this context becomes ever more apparent.

A recent investigation showed that 90% of authors of research papers use or would use AI with disclosure4 (although what disclosure means is less clear). Estimates suggest only a small percentage of AI users do explain this in publications. This observation highlights the need in clinical laboratory practice for detailing AI technologies used in reporting, ascribing professional responsibility for signing out these results as, for example, with immunocytochemistry.

But healthcare is all about people—how will AI influence the pathologists who will interact with it? How do pathologists feel about using it? Studies show pathologists need to be involved in the design, clinical value and implementation of AI tools, their functionality and embeddedness to enable development of trust.5 Further, the focus should be on workflow, noting that value will differ by specialty.6 In clinical radiology, there are concerns about the effects AI tools have on radiologist performance with worries about undermining confidence.7 Pathologists must learn to trust AI tools, but how these affect their performance in the clinical setting is little understood. Illustrating this, an endoscopy study reports adenoma detection rate of standard colonoscopy decreased significantly from 28·4% to 22·4% after 3 months using AI as a procedural aid.8 Another issue is cognitive deskilling, which reduces critical thinking skills,9 and dependence on technology can diminish human capability, bringing risk to clinical acumen. This, in turn, might affect self-esteem.

The contrasting clinical impact and fragility of pathology services highlight the value of pathologists. Supporting their human resources is important. To do this, work is needed exploring pathologists’ interactions with their new AI associates. Tools need to fit the needs of clinical practice and avoid requiring adapting practice to fit the tools. What effects does AI have on pathologists’ practice and on their understanding of what they do and how well they do it? Can pathologists trust AI and rely on it—and how will pathologists and their reports be viewed within healthcare? The uncertainties of clinical medicine are many, and dealing with them is what senior doctors do well. Pathologists are often faced with being unable to provide a definitive diagnosis because there is insufficient material on which to base it or where findings are discordant—the correct action is to be honest and ask for more tissue to enable diagnostic accuracy. Can we therefore learn to evaluate AI’s plausible answers in this situation?

To benefit most from the potential of AI, work is needed to ensure appropriate partnership with pathologists, sensible implementation of technology and safety processes supporting pathologists as well as patients. The human interactions and nuances are challenges around which many questions remain unanswered.
