John Schulenberg as a developmental scholar and mentor: Personal reflections
Authors: Jennifer L. Maggs
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractJohn Schulenberg has had significant impact on developmental science. His conceptual writing and empirical research, grounded in developmental contextualism, emphasized the critical role that developmental transitions play in shaping health risks, especially substance use, across the life course. Schulenberg's integration of developmental concepts with large‐scale epidemiological studies, particularly through his leadership in the Monitoring the Future study, provides key insights into how significant life changes—such as school transitions, employment, and relationships—interact to influence well‐being across adolescence and early adulthood. He was a fierce advocate for adolescence and young adulthood being critical phases of life, deserving of focused attention and support. John was also a devoted mentor to the next generation of developmental scientists. This commentary reflects on John Schulenberg's legacy, highlighting five of his foundational concepts (developmental tasks, transitions, trajectories, turning points, and timing) and celebrating his role as a generative and joyful mentor. He fostered open intellectual dialog, promoted and celebrated career development, and took pleasure in life inside and outside work, helping early career scientists to develop innovative and impactful research programs. Schulenberg's commitment to positive relationships and celebrating success is an enduring model for future generations of developmental scientists and mentors.

John Schulenberg championed a developmental perspective for understanding adolescence and the transition to adulthood. His rich developmental thinking provided the foundation to his conceptual, empirical, and leadership achievements, as well as to his joyful and generative mentoring of the next generations of research scientists. His love for evocative conceptual ideas and metaphors, his deep understanding of the transition to adulthood, and his commitment to the careers of others shape his impactful legacy.

Schulenberg and colleagues' collaborative conceptual writing and empirical work emphasize how health risks such as substance use are rooted in common developmental transitions and experiences across adolescence and young adulthood (e.g., Schulenberg & Maggs, 2002). Significant life changes across many domains, including puberty, school transitions, first jobs, friendships, and romantic relationships, play critical roles in shaping substance use and well‐being in adolescence (Brown et al., 2008; Schulenberg et al., 2014) and in early adulthood (Schulenberg et al., 2000). Correspondingly, substance use and mental health impact young people's timing, success, and satisfaction with achievements and social role transitions (e.g., Maggs et al., 2015; Schulenberg, Sameroff, & Cicchetti, 2004; Staff et al., 2010). Because multiple domains of life are fundamentally interconnected, they need to be considered together.

Rooted in a developmental contextual approach (e.g., Lerner & Kauffman, 1985), Schulenberg's work explained, developed, and applied many tenets of life‐span developmental psychology (e.g., Baltes, 1987) and life‐course sociology (e.g., Elder et al., 2003). A powerful advocate for adolescence and young adulthood as critical junctures in the life course, John expounded the value of large population‐based samples to colleagues in developmental science. He also brought an individually focused, deep developmental understanding to his population level work in the Monitoring the Future (MTF) study, applying developmental concepts, methods, and questions to large‐scale epidemiological data. Through his many contributions, John Schulenberg's impactful legacy continues in many careers and lines of research, a selection of which are illustrated by the papers in this special issue.

The academic summary above of John's contributions, however, does not do proper justice to his insightful and passionate understanding of what it is like to be an adolescent and to make the transition to adulthood. John got it, he cared about it, and he found joy in it. Many titles and quotes in his published work illustrate his affinity for this time of life. As an example, many years ago my sister was approached by a someone at a wedding who told her “Hey, I'm 21. I'm in my prime drinking years and I plan to take advantage of that tonight.” While she was unimpressed, John loved this story and kept returning to it. At the time, we were writing a paper applying a developmental perspective to college drinking for a National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism Task Force (Schulenberg & Maggs, 2002). This wedding guest's off‐hand remark resonated so strongly with John because it sums up what can be so great about being 21: Culturally, in the US middle class, this is supposed to be the fun and crazy time of life, a phase that adults look back on with nostalgia (Maggs, 1997). And the immediate wedding reception context was ideal: Open bar, potential new partners, culturally sanctioned liminal space. But the statement also captures what can be so tough about this transitional time of life: The feelings of frantic urgency, fears of missing out, anxieties about falling behind. This duality is reflected in John and colleagues' empirical paper titled “Taking hold of some kind of life” (Schulenberg, Bryant, & O'Malley, 2004). These tensions remain ever true for young people now as wages stagnate, the cost‐of‐living increases, education and work transitions remain challenged by the pandemic, many struggle with mental health, and inequities increase (National Academy of Sciences, 2023).

In this commentary, I highlight a small selection of John Schulenberg's conceptual and empirical contributions, with brief reference to some examples from this special issue that carry these traditions forward. Second, I describe John's joyful and generative mentoring of next generations of scientists seeking to understand and support the transition to adulthood. A central thread linking John's achievements and impact is his fundamentally developmental approach which permeated his published work, mentoring, and leadership.

CORE DEVELOPMENTAL CONCEPTS: JOHN'S PASSION FOR BIG IDEAS

John Schulenberg was drawn to big conceptual ideas. Many of these concepts emerged from his training in the 1980s during the heyday of the newly articulated developmental contextual perspective (Lerner & Kauffman, 1985). Focusing on adolescence and young adulthood as critical junctures in the life course, Schulenberg and colleagues' body of work articulated how developmental transitions are fundamentally linked with health risks. He was an important advocate of bringing together population‐based methods with the study of adolescent development. This short commentary cannot cover the many developmental concepts that John explained, applied, and advocated for. I focus here solely on five “T”s that John frequently returned to in his writing: developmental tasks, transitions, trajectories, turning points and timing. Many more ideas and insights are can be found by revisiting John's rich papers.

Historical context of John's developmental education and perspective

In the mid‐20th century, many organismic developmental theories and models proposed “normative” qualitative stages assumed to be universal and innate, despite being initially derived from small, non‐representative convenience samples. With the advent of behaviorism and social learning theory, universal laws of behavior were proposed, and the causal emphasis shifted toward how our environments shape what we do. Developmental change was increasingly seen as quantitative and incremental, and similarities between individuals in the sequence of observed developmental changes were increasingly explained by forces outside the person.

When John began his PhD in human development at Penn State in the 1980s, organismic, innate stage conceptions and mechanistic, law‐based theories were giving way to more expansive contextual models. This was a foundational time for life‐span developmental science (e.g., Baltes, 1987; Lerner, 1982/2002), and John was privileged to work with many leaders of developmental contextualism, including Anne Petersen, John Nesselroade, Fred Vondracek, and Richard Lerner. Not only did he develop his outstanding mentoring skills, but he also studied alongside Penn State graduate students with whom he established lifelong relationships, many of whom also became esteemed life‐span developmental scholars.

Developmental‐contextual models were more inclusive and expansive than prior organismic and mechanistic approaches (Bronfenbrenner, 1979; Lerner & Kauffman, 1985). In response to the big questions (Is development qualitative or quantitative? Active or passive? Universal or idiosyncratic? Genetic or environmental? Continuous or discontinuous?), developmental contextual models answered Yes, It depends, and It's probably more complicated than that (Lerner, 1982/2002). This new framework pointed to multidimensional and multidirectional development, across the entire life span, as active/reactive individuals interacted dynamically within their active/reactive contexts (e.g., Baltes, 1987; Lerner & Kauffman, 1985; Sameroff, 1987; Zucker et al., 1995).

Interest in other disciplines and their methods was keen and growing: Age‐period‐cohort models, new methods for modeling trajectories, tracking individual variability intensively. All of these were part of John's formative education. As students in this tradition, we learned that life‐span developmental psychology's aim was to understand inter‐individual differences, intra‐individual change, and inter‐individual differences in intra‐individual change (Baltes et al., 1988). With some practice, eventually we learned to say and think this phrase without hesitation. More importantly, we could explain its meaning: People change, and they don't all change the same. Understanding how, and why, and under what conditions things could go better for more people was the goal.

A new inter‐disciplinary approach applying developmental models and methods to understand change and variability across the entire life span was emerging (Baltes et al., 1988), and John became an important proponent applying developmental contextual thinking to adolescence and young adulthood.

Developmental transitions are fundamentally linked with health risks

John Schulenberg's conceptual and empirical work emphasized how developmental transitions impact health risks, particularly substance use, across adolescence and young adulthood (Schulenberg et al., 1997; Schulenberg & Maggs, 2002). Significant life changes such as puberty, school transitions, first jobs, and higher education all play critical roles in shaping substance use and well‐being. Some personal and role changes commonly coincide with increases (e.g., becoming a college student) while others typically co‐occur with decreases (e.g., becoming a spouse; Schulenberg & Maggs, 2002; Staff et al., 2010). Some, like puberty, have more varied patterns as a function of timing, gender, and other factors. As well, risk behaviors and mental health clearly impact people's timing, success, and satisfaction with role transitions, such as achieving educational goals or building rewarding relationships. The many domains of life are all interconnected, and therefore researchers, educators, preventionists, and parents need to consider them together in all their complexity to understand development and well‐being across the transition to adulthood (Brown et al., 2008; Crockett et al., 2023; Schulenberg et al., 1997).

How developmental changes and transitions in one domain reverberate and combine across other areas of life are the focus of many empirical papers in this special issue, including puberty, cognition, family, peers, work, and community service (e.g., Benner et al., 2024; Chaku, Waters, & Ahmed 2024; Staff & Mortimer; Leer et al., 2024; Wray‐Lake et al., unpublished). Conceptually, explore and expand Schulenberg et al.'s (2003) contention that transitional periods where the relations between individuals and their contexts change dramatically may lead to continuity or discontinuity in development. This rich paper focuses on puberty and middle school as two key developmental transition domains, linking the historical context underlying many of these conceptual propositions with important remaining research questions still to be addressed.

Adolescence and transition to adulthood are critical junctures in the life course

The early years of childhood set a key foundation on which future development and health build (e.g., Ramey & Ramey, 1998). John Schulenberg and colleagues argued for similar emphasis on adolescence and the transition to young adulthood as two pivotal phases of the life span (Schulenberg & Maslowsky, 2015) that should indeed be considered together (Crockett et al., 2023). Due to profound biologically and contextually driven changes in every domain of life, as well as increasing autonomy and heterogeneity in life paths, adolescence and young adulthood are fundamental shapers of individual trajectories and long‐term outcomes (Schulenberg & Maggs, 2008; Schulenberg et al., 2003, 2018; Schulenberg & Schoon, 2012; Staff et al., 2010). Across his career, Schulenberg illuminated how this developmental period, characterized by numerous life changes and role transitions, can profoundly impact health and well‐being (e.g., Schulenberg et al., 2015). In this special issue, Staff and Mortimer (unpublished) take a long view on sequelae of the quality of adolescent part‐time work experiences, demonstrating long‐term linkages with adult work quality across three decades.

Developmental science and population studies should work together

As a life span developmental scientist and as principal investigator of the panel MTF study, Schulenberg advocated for taking both a long view—and a wide view—on development. To his colleagues in public health and survey methodology, he advocated for high‐quality, long‐term data tracking individuals across significant parts of the life span. To developmental psychologists, he championed the value of broad‐scale, representative and large samples, not only to be more generalizable but also to identify diverse and complex developmental patterns. Linking developmentally rich longitudinal data with large representative samples allows researchers to ask important questions about interindividual differences in intraindividual change (Baltes et al., 1988), leading to a deeper understanding of both epidemiology and etiology (Schulenberg et al., 2014, 2021).

John advanced and advocated for population‐based longitudinal studies through his leadership in the MTF study, Presidency of the Society for Research on Adolescence, teaching developmental psychology at the University of Michigan, and involvement in national panels such as the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism's Task Force on College Drinking (collegedrinkingprevention.gov) and the Committee on Improving the Health, Safety, and Well‐Being of Young Adults (National Research Council, 2015). The combination of developmental science with population samples has been part of a significant paradigm shift in developmental psychology research which previously focused on primarily smaller, local samples with more intensive measurement (Davis‐Kean et al., 2015; Harris et al., 2019). As an example of this approach, Wray‐Lake, Dunn, Freund, & Kloska (unpublished) used data from 20 national cohorts of high school seniors in MTF to document how less steep declines in community service across young adulthood are associated with lower substance use, healthier behaviors, and higher psychological well‐being in middle adulthood.

Development as a journey: The five Ts

John had a knack for using “approachable and engaging language” (Fosco et al., 2024). This section summarizes five developmental concepts that John elaborated in many papers, each consistent with viewing development as a journey. By the mid‐1990s, when I began to work with John, the expansive, multi‐level breadth of some developmental contextual models led some of us to question whether any (close to) universals remained. With growing recognition that identities, contexts, cultures, and historical time as well as economics, politics, policy, and global forces were all fundamental (and also interacting) determinants of individual development, the simple researcher could feel overwhelmed. I remember sitting next to John at a conference when Urie Bronfenbrenner stood up after listening to a research presentation that incorporated many multi‐level influences. He asked, somewhat rhetorically, whether we, as a field, had forgotten development itself. Where was the individual person developing inside the many circles of his ecological model? In this scholarly context, John and I debated the value of five historic developmental concepts, all characterizing life as a journey or voyage. Four are metaphors of the nature of developmental changes: Tasks, Trajectories, Transitions, and Turning Points. The last is Timing, also key during any trip.

Developmental tasks

Traditionally, developmental tasks had been defined as socially and biologically prescribed psychosocial tasks that “should” be accomplished during specific sensitive periods (Schulenberg & Maggs, 2002). But who defines the “should”? In 1986, Neugarten, an early proponent of the related concept of developmental timetables, argued that historic distinctions between life periods were blurring, with “changes in the traditional rhythms and timing of events in the life cycle” (Neugarten & Neugarten, 1986, p. 34). Was the concept of developmental tasks as employed by Erikson (1950) and Havighurst (1952) outdated? With increased, long‐overdue recognition of diversity in life paths, contexts, and goals, narrow definitions of developmental tasks were clearly too prescriptive (Schulenberg, Bryant, & O'Malley, 2004). However, people growing up in a culture or context may still feel there are things they should achieve, whether in a certain order or by a certain age. After a lot of discussion, we argued that developmental tasks and timetables remain salient organizers to people's own personal biographies, and thus continue to have meaning in developmental descriptions of periods of the life course. However, scholars and commentators need to be extremely cautious not to jump thoughtlessly from could to ought (Schulenberg et al., 2003; Schulenberg & Maggs, 2002).

Developmental transitions

Developmental transitions are major changes within persons and in social roles and contexts, as well as how they interact together (Schulenberg, Sameroff, & Cicchetti, 2004). Common examples are puberty, the transition to college, becoming a worker, and marriage. But how common must a developmental transition be to be considered normative, that is, seen as fundamental to the nature of the age period? Is normative even a useful concept? We agreed that biologically driven changes such as puberty were relatively easily considered as developmentally normative, given their strong age‐graded nature and close to universality, even across species (Schulenberg & Maggs, 2002). On the other hand, rigid expectations to complete education, marry, and start a family (in that order) in one's 20s were not (Schulenberg & Schoon, 2012). But we pondered, what about educational achievements? Employment? Financial independence? Romantic and sexual relationships? At what level of prevalence are major developmental changes and social roles so common that they come to define an age or phase of life, or can be evoked as explanations for why and how people are doing?

With many caveats and “it depends” disclaimers, John and colleagues argued and demonstrated that, despite much wider inter‐individual differences in the timing, sequence, and occurrence of developmental transitions and role changes across recent decades (Schulenberg & Schoon, 2012), transitions and roles remain important to individuals in how they construct and interpret their lives. They are also important determinants of concurrent and subsequent health and well‐being, as well as success and satisfaction with subsequent changes in life (Maggs et al., 2012; Schulenberg, Bryant, & O'Malley, 2004). In this special issue, links between developmentally normative social roles and heavy drinking are innovatively illustrated in a diverse sample of young adults followed across 24 months (Fairlie, Calhoun, Walukevich‐Dienst, Janson, Patrick, & Lee, 2024). Heavy episodic drinking was less likely in months people were in serious romantic relationships and months they were living with parents. This study of micro‐transitions (Patrick et al., 2018) provides insight into how young adults construct their lives within changing opportunities and constraints shaped by social roles and positions.

Developmental trajectories

The third metaphor—developmental trajectories—is a mathematical one. Trajectories visualize developmental change in one domain (e.g., height, adjustment, cannabis use) as quantitative (i.e., a change in amount, not in fundamental nature), cumulative, and predictable. Notably, trajectories are more than a metaphor, because they are modeled statistically using data. Trajectory models conceptualize developmental change as following a linear or more complex pattern of systematic and predictable change that is modeled along one or multiple arcs, curves, or other piece‐wise lines. Developmental trajectories can be modeled as normative or as multiple/taxonomic (Maggs & Schulenberg, 2004).

John is well known for his early empirical work distinguishing multiple trajectories of alcohol and other substance use across the transition to adulthood. In the 1990s, using longitudinal MTF data following 10 nationally representative cohorts from age 18–24, he published two companion pieces on developmental trajectories of frequent binge drinking (Schulenberg, O'Malley, et al., 1996; Schulenberg, Wadsworth, et al., 1996). The first paper demonstrated how the well‐known average trend of substance use—initiation in adolescence, escalation through the early 20s, and declines for many but not all early in adulthood—concealed a range of subgroups with distinct patterns or trajectories of frequent binge drinking. Building on prior work differentiating developmental types and patterns in adulthood (e.g., Zucker, 1987), the second companion paper drew attention to the challenge that it was frustratingly difficult to predict at age 18 which subsequent binge drinking trajectory a person would take through age 24. That is, which rarely binge drinking youth (“Rare” binge drinkers) would remain infrequent binge drinkers into their mid‐20s, who would escalate their frequent binge drinking temporarily (“Fling” group), and who would continue to escalate (“Increased”)? Similarly, among age 18 frequent binge drinkers, it was difficult to distinguish subsequent “Decreased” from “Chronic” heavy drinking groups. Something must be happening in the interim, that is, during the transition, that could explain the divergent paths.

Identification of clinically distinct groups with vastly different long‐term risk that were difficult to distinguish in advance was important for epidemiology, etiology, and prevention. Epidemiologically, the prevalence at any one snapshot of time will not identify who is a greatest long‐term risk. Etiologically, these highly cited papers highlighted a need to understand emergent causes of binge drinking that occur during the transition to adulthood, and not in childhood or adolescence. Similarly, prevention programs targeting childhood or adolescence needed to be supplemented with efforts after high school graduation, for example within college, work or community settings or via policy.

Turning points

A fourth developmental concept is also a metaphor related to life as a journey. Turning points emphasize discontinuity in development rather than predictable change. As John and colleagues wrote: “some young people who falter and flounder during the transition come from the ranks of well‐functioning adolescents; like‐wise, some troubled adolescents demonstrate a turnabout through successful experiences with the new opportunities and tasks of this transition” (Schulenberg, Bryant, & O'Malley, 2004, p. 1120). Developmental scientists often model, in the aggregate, how prior successes or challenges across developmental domains accumulate across the life span, in virtuous or vicious circles. However, focusing on turning points aims to understand why and how people exhibit significant, apparently unexpected changes. For example, Benner, Shen and Kloska (2024) find a majority of adolescents making the transition from high school to college show substantial continuity in friends' descriptive alcohol use norms. However, important smaller groups show significant shifts in peer norms as they make new friend groups. These adolescents may be using this developmental transition to re‐invent themselves (Benner, 2011).

In these many ways, John's empirical and conceptual work drew attention to the variety of patterns hidden within means and traditional trajectory models, encouraging researchers to ask what is happening during adolescence and early adulthood that might explain the diverging paths. While other scholars advocated for the importance of early childhood experience and intervention in setting the stage for positive development (e.g., Ramey & Ramey, 1998), John was one of many in the Society for Research on Adolescence and other groups championing the idea that adolescence and young adulthood also represent pivotal turning points for many (e.g., Rutter, 1996). This insight has been very fruitful, with others taking it further. For example, in this special issue, Cauffman, Beardslee, Sbeglia, Frick, and Steinberg document differential experiences in the justice system as developmental snares that have lasting impact on future offending versus desistance.

Schulenberg's work focuses on understanding and predicting longer‐term patterns of change, while also cautioning that modeling a mathematical trajectory as the average for individuals, groups, or populations does not imply they were “shot out of a cannon” beginning in childhood (Schulenberg & Maslowsky, 2015). Rather, individuals have agency, contexts change, and (semi)random events can divert people into new directions (Schulenberg & Maggs, 2002). Prevention and intervention efforts seek to capture, in positive ways, this potential for change in discontinuity because what happens during adolescence and the transition to adulthood can dramatically change the trajectories of individuals (Fosco, Fang, Chen, Feinberg, & Spoth, 2024).

Timing

The final concept of developmental timing also builds on decades of prior articulation by developmental scientists. Chronological age may be an empty variable for explanatory purposes, but age does organize many opportunities and experiences (e.g., eligibility to start kindergarten, get a driving license, be employed). Further, age norms also structure expectations about appropriate behaviors and desirable timing of achievements (e.g., dating, graduations, residential independence), with wide variations within social classes and cultural groups. And, age is a useful metric we use to organize our data. The concept of developmental timing draws attention to when, in development (often defined by age or grade), risk or protective factors are most impactful (e.g., Maslowsky et al., 2014; Schulenberg et al., 2018). What critical or sensitive periods have especially strong influence on which outcomes? Are distal or more proximal factors more important? How do distal factors shape proximal factors (see e.g., Troop‐Gordon, Thomas, Brigham, Xu, & Rudolph, 2024). Despite prior challenges, what later experiences can facilitate positive change? When is the most effective time to intervene to support positive development in an enduring way?

Application of the five Ts today

Challenges and losses due to the Covid pandemic clearly illustrate the current value of the five Ts—developmental tasks, transitions, trajectories, turning points, and timing—for understanding adolescence and the transition to adulthood today. When schools, activities, and everything else closed abruptly in Spring 2020, immediate concerns for adolescents were informed by expert developmental knowledge of teens' needs for contact with friends, support from non‐parental adults, physical activity, relief from boredom, independence. Similarly, it was known that many young adults, due to their developmental and social position in the life course, might be lonely and isolated, struggle with moving back in with parents, or lack money for rent. While each of these human concerns is relevant at all ages, some are heightened by the developmental phase of life. For that reason, major historical and personal events may impact people differently based on their position along their own developmental journey.

As an example, imagine an adolescent in high school 5 years ago. She hopes to become the first in her family to move away from home for higher education. She is building a portfolio toward a competitive college application to study graphic design. But in March of her grade 11 year, suddenly everything is interrupted by the Covid pandemic. When her school is closed, her studio and software access are interrupted. She cannot see her friends. Her parents and siblings are stressed. In the fall, guidance counselors do their best, but they are overwhelmed supporting students and unable to provide their typical hands‐on college application support.

This adolescent's developmental task of applying to colleges with amazing fine arts programs is challenged. She feels discouraged because her portfolio is unfinished, and she misses some application deadlines. As a result, she does not receive her hoped‐for scholarship and her developmental transition to college is postponed. Will her educational and professional trajectory be simply delayed? Will she find her way back on track toward achieving her dreams? More positively, what new opportunities will she encounter? What support and resources will she marshal to navigate her transitions toward adulthood? Will this apparent setback become a salutary turning point toward exploration of new fulfilling interests? Perhaps a part‐time job will suggest different or broader vistas and interests. Perhaps the additional time living with her family will prepare her better for independent living. Perhaps she will emerge with greater strength and compassion, having weathered these hurdles when her life did not go as planned. Life, and development, are hard to predict. Finally, consider developmental timing. If this adolescent had been born 5 years earlier or later, the meaning of the immediate experience and the possible long‐term impacts of this major historical event would be different in specific content. However, the same developmental concepts—the five Ts—could help us understand its impact.

This single, briefly developed example is designed to illustrate some of the complexity of the developmental metaphors and concepts John wrote about so extensively and passionately. The papers in this special issue demonstrate John Schulenberg's ongoing contributions in much greater depth. Furthermore, his impact also continues with vigor in the careers of the students and early career researchers he mentored and through the ongoing work of his collaborators.

JOYFUL AND GENERATIVE MENTORING

Fostering open dialogue and intellectual exchange

As a mentor, John Schulenberg fostered the next generation of scientists by cultivating open intellectual dialogue and encouraging his mentees to explore emergent ideas in the field. John and I started working together in the mid‐1990s during my postdoctoral work at the University of Michigan. I was thrilled when he agreed to work with me, at first on a yet‐to‐be‐defined project which became an edited volume (Schulenberg et al., 1997). From the outset, John fostered an environment where ideas flourished. I always felt he took me and my ideas seriously. For example, my dissertation had illustrated how college students experienced alcohol use purposefully and positively rather than as a risk to be avoided (Maggs, 1997). While myriad pleasures of alcohol had been extolled for millennia in literature, music, and cultural lore, positive consequences of drinking were rarely acknowledged in the developmental, family, or prevention literatures. Developmental scientists were particularly negative about adolescent and young adult risk behaviors. Most still assumed that adolescent substance use was rooted in psychopathology, psychological or social vulnerability, or pernicious (typically deviant peer) social influences. Only a few provocative scholars were exploring the idea that risk taking could also serve constructive functions (e.g., Baumrind, 1987; Chassin et al., 1989; Jessor, 1987; Shedler & Block, 1990; Silbereisen & Noack, 1986; Zucker et al., 1995).

John was cautiously open to the idea of perceived benefits and positive experiences of substance use. Like many of us, he nostalgically remembered some possibly dangerous episodes during his younger years. But at the same time, he pushed me to ponder how acknowledging perceived and real benefits of risky behaviors could be integrated responsibly into etiological and prevention models. We debated what constructive roles drinking—even binge drinking—could play in young people's lives given significant acute and chronic risks. We wrote about the challenges posed by the paradox that substance use and other risky behaviors were prevalent, subjectively rewarding, and inconsequential in the long‐run for many—while contributing to the downfall of others (e.g., Maggs et al., 1997; Schulenberg et al., 1997, 2003; Schulenberg & Maggs, 2002). Of course, many things in life serve developmental or other important functions yet pose the probabilistic potential for harm. For example, during the transition to adulthood, this includes dating, student loans, and moving for jobs. My conversations with John pushed me to tackle this line of research more rigorously, for example, in intensive repeated measures designs (e.g., Maggs et al., 2011; Patrick et al., 2015) and nationally representative longitudinal studies (e.g., Maggs et al., 2015; Maggs, Feinstein, & Patrick, 2008).

Others had the same experience over the years, going on to develop their distinctive research programs that continue to reflect ideas and insights they developed, in part, in discussion with John. Just two examples of many possible eminent scholars are Justin Jager (e.g., Jager et al., 2015, 2023) and Julie Maslowsky (e.g., Maslowsky et al., 2021). Similarly, many scientists I had the privilege to mentor are conducting incredibly innovative, impactful epidemiological, etiological, and prevention research on substance use across the transition to adulthood, all of it influenced by the developmental perspective described here.

Promoting career development and celebrating success: “Spread your wings and fly”

John took transitions and well‐being seriously in his mentoring. He was committed to fostering a vibrant academic community. The overarching theme was building positive, collegial relationships, for which he set a great example: By expressing genuine happiness encountering old friends and colleagues at conferences. By communicating difficult or critical feedback kindly and constructively, so the author or colleague or student felt respected and supported. By talking with joy about family, friends, and things outside work.

I learned a great deal from John while we worked on our edited volume, Health Risks and Developmental Transitions During Adolescence, published by Cambridge University Press in 1997: To actively seek out colleagues who are brilliant, knowledgeable, and insightful (but also who are collaborative, fun, and will respond pleasantly to feedback and be reasonable with deadliness; see author list). To keep decent records of agreements (yes, dear editor, you did agree to “give” a hardcover copy to every chapter author). And, sometimes, to take risks ourselves (we never did discover who owned the copyright to the cover illustration of two children sharing a moment smoking).

Over the years, John promoted the careers of many early career researchers. He shared opportunities, put our names forward, and spoke highly of us to others. He was genuinely enthusiastic about our successes and believed in our potential to make significant contributions to the field. He listened. He praised with specificity rather than vague and automatic habit. For example, when I was a new associate professor, John commented after a conference presentation how much he liked my new more confident, off‐the‐cuff speaking style. This was not surprising, as he preferred more informal and spontaneous presentations, often saying he didn't want to over‐practice so he could “keep it fresh.” Occasionally, with a good deal of caution, he shared a kind and quiet word of insight that helped us as his mentees and colleagues not make important mistakes.

John supported mentees' developmental progression in academia, transitioning from great mentor to valued colleague. He expected and celebrated the successes of earlier career scholars he worked with. A true believer in development, he expected us to become full‐fledged independent scientists. Over the years, many of my faculty colleagues expressed surprise when my own children grew taller after a few years (an observation my older kid always found amusing, asking If they are specialists in development, why are they so surprised I grew?). John, however, never made this mistake about the people he mentored: He expected us to grow in our careers, to become more accomplished, and to leave the nest to establish our own independent projects and collegial relationships.

John's legacy as a mentor is marked by his ability to smoothly shift from guiding young scholars to collaborating as colleagues, a natural progression where he was happy for us to move on and equally pleased to collaborate with us when it made sense. His example of fostering intellectual curiosity, supporting career development, and maintaining a balance between professional and personal life serves as an inspiration for aspiring scholars and mentors.

Conclusion: Influence on the field and current scholars

John Schulenberg's contributions to life‐span developmental science are enduring, extending beyond his scholarly output to his profound influence on the field and future generations of scholars. His involvement in many collaborative projects, including the recent APA Handbook of Adolescent and Young Adult Development (Crockett et al., 2023) exemplifies his commitment to advancing scholarly discourse and integrating diverse perspectives. His mentorship legacy continues to shape the impactful careers of many brilliant scholars who now mentor the next generation. John's supporting mentoring emphasizing the importance of intellectual curiosity, collaboration, and taking joy in both work and life provides us an inspiring model as we seek to advance our understanding of developmental processes across the lifespan and to improve the lives of adolescents and young adults.
