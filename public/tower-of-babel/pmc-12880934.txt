Utilization of Antiviral Treatment for Hospitalized Patients With Mild-to-moderate COVID-19 at High Risk for Disease Progression
Authors: Michael P. Iannetti, Katie Barnes, Stephen Varga, Suzanne Kemper, Elaine D. Mattox
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Clinicians frequently encounter patients in the hospital with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 who are at high risk for disease progression. Antiviral drugs reduce the rate of progression to severe COVID-19 when given to these patients within 5–7 days of symptom onset. To look at the utilization of antiviral medications in hospitalized patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19, a detailed chart review was conducted of 500 randomly selected patients from the Charleston Area Medical Center COVID-19 Registry. We identified 124 patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 who were eligible for antiviral treatment. Only 41 of the 124 patients (33.1 %) received antiviral treatment. Providers listed mild symptoms as the most common reason (74.7 %) that antiviral treatment was not started. These findings show that antiviral medications are underutilized in treating high-risk hospitalized patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 to prevent disease progression.

Introduction

Clinicians encounter patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 in the hospital setting more frequently than they did early in the pandemic.1 Patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 illness may exhibit nonspecific signs and symptoms of upper or lower respiratory tract disease during clinical assessment but have oxygen saturation >94 % on room air.2 These patients may have acquired SARS-CoV-2 in the hospital and present early in their disease course or may have tested positive for COVID-19 upon presentation to the hospital for another condition after which they developed symptomatic COVID-19.3 Many of these patients are at high risk for disease progression based on age and comorbid conditions.4,5

Guidelines from the Infectious Disease Society of America (IDSA) and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) recommend that clinicians treat these hospitalized patients with remdesivir if their symptoms started within the last 7 days.6,7 This recommendation is based on aggregate data from multiple trials showing a faster recovery time in high-risk patients treated with remdesivir,7–11 as well as a large retrospective study showing reduced mortality in immunocompromised hospitalized patients receiving remdesivir.7,12 Additionally, the IDSA and the NIH suggest that either nirmatrelvir/ritonavir or molnupiravir be given to high-risk patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 hospitalized for reasons other than COVID-19 if symptoms started within 5 days, with nirmatrelvir/ritonavir being the preferred agent.6,7

While antiviral medications have been recommended for treating mild-to-moderate COVID-19 during the care of hospitalized patients since February 2022,6 implementation of this practice had not been examined. The purpose of this study was to evaluate care of hospitalized patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 and explore the reasons why antiviral treatments may or may not have been given.

Methods

We randomly selected 500 hospitalized patients from a total of 1473 non-ICU patients (age >50 years) who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 by PCR at Charleston Area Medical Center (CAMC) between March 1, 2022 and August 27, 2023 with the goal of identifying patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 eligible for treatment. We assigned each patient a unique identifier and utilized the Microsoft Excel = RAND() function to achieve a random sample. A sample size of 500 (34 % of the total 1473 eligible patients) was chosen due to the extensive time required for detailed manual chart review, which was necessary to extract clinical information not reliably captured in structured data fields. This approach enabled a comprehensive review while maintaining feasibility within the study’s time and resource limitations. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 124 patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 were identified and included in the final analysis.

Symptoms of COVID-19 were defined as fever, cough, sore throat, malaise, headache, muscle pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, loss of taste or smell, and dyspnea. Patients were excluded if they were in the ICU at the time of SARS-CoV-2 testing, had an oxygen saturation <94 % at the time of testing, were on long-term supplemental oxygen therapy, received antiviral or monoclonal antibody treatment prior to hospitalization, or had tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 within the past 90 days. Patients who were asymptomatic and tested positive for surveillance purposes or as a pre-condition for discharge to long-term care were also excluded.

Antiviral treatments were defined as treatment with nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, remdesivir, or molnupiravir. Patients were considered treated if they received antiviral medication in the hospital or were prescribed antiviral medication on hospital discharge.

When a patient with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 within 5 days of symptom onset did not receive antiviral treatment, we sought to determine the reasons why antiviral therapy was not given and classified each patient using the following criteria: 1) the patient/family declined treatment; 2) antiviral medication was not offered because of concerns about drug interactions; 3) antiviral medication was not offered due to concerns about the patient’s liver or kidney function; 4) antiviral medication was not offered due to determination of mild disease; or 5) no reason documented.

The study was approved by the CAMC/WVU-Charleston Division Institutional Review Board.

Results

Of 500 patients with symptomatic COVID-19, 124 patients (24.8 %) met the inclusion/exclusion criteria and were identified as having mild-to-moderate COVID-19 and being eligible for antiviral treatment (Table 1). The 124 patients who met the inclusion criteria were cared for by 58 different attending physicians. Each provider cared for 1–8 patients, with a median 2 patients per provider (interquartile range 1–3).

One-third of these patients (41 of 124 patients; 33.1 %) received antiviral treatment; 37 were treated with remdesivir, and 4 were treated with nirmatrelvir/ritonavir. The majority of eligible patients (83 patients, 66.9 %) did not receive antiviral treatment. Although 37 of 63 fully vaccinated patients received antiviral treatment, only 4 of 20 unvaccinated patients received this care (p = 0.09). Of the 124 patients, 9 treated patients (22.0 %) and 25 untreated patients (30.1 %) were hospitalized for conditions other than COVID-19 (p = 0.40). The most common reason documented as to why antiviral medication was not prescribed was that the patient had mild symptoms (62 patients, 74.7 %) (Table 2).

Discussion

A substantial percentage of hospitalized patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 who were eligible for antiviral treatment did not receive treatment despite being evaluated within 5 days of symptom onset and being at high risk for disease progression. The study group represented a subset of our broader patient population, reflects real-world prescribing decisions across 58 different attending physicians and provides insight into treatment practices for mild-to-moderate COVID-19 in hospitalized, high-risk patients. Based on our chart reviews, clinicians typically did not prescribe antiviral medications because only mild symptoms were present when the patient tested positive for COVID-19. Because we did not observe a significant difference in antiviral prescribing between patients hospitalized for conditions other than COVID-19 and those admitted primarily for COVID-19, the primary reason for hospitalization may not have been a major driver of prescribing decisions. Additionally, the findings were unlikely to be driven by the practice patterns of any single provider or small subset of providers because most providers cared for fewer than 4 patients each.

Our study did not query why clinicians may believe that patients with minimal symptoms do not need antiviral treatment for COVID-19. This practice may be due to an incomplete understanding that patients can progress from mild disease to more severe disease or that antiviral treatment can reduce the risk of disease progression. Furthermore, clinicians may be unfamiliar with current guidelines recommending antiviral treatment for eligible patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19. Future investigations into this question are warranted to improve patient care.

Importantly, questions remain about the efficacy of COVID-19 antivirals in populations with immunity from prior SARS-CoV-2 infections or vaccination, but currently guidelines still recommend antiviral use because of a lack of data suggesting otherwise.6,7 Of note, many hospitalized patients may be immunocompromised and unable to mount an adequate immune response to vaccination or prior infection.7 Furthermore, immunity can wane after vaccination or prior infection providing less protection to individuals.13,14

Although not statistically significant in this cohort, patients who did not receive antiviral treatment experienced higher rates of in-hospital death, 30-day readmission, and progression to severe COVID-19 (Table 1), which is consistent with prior studies.8–12 Moreover, we observed a trend suggesting that a greater proportion of unvaccinated patients did not receive antiviral treatment, even though this group has been more extensively studied and shown to benefit from treatment. These differences further highlight missed opportunities to give antivirals to eligible patients.

Several strategies could be considered to increase the use of antiviral treatment in this population. These include educating providers through continuing medical education and offering targeted lectures to trainees to improve awareness of when antivirals are indicated. In addition, incorporating antiviral medications into electronic medical record order sets, along with clinical-decision support tools that notify providers when patients meet eligibility criteria, could help standardize care and reduce missed opportunities for treatment.

A major limitation of our study is that it was conducted within a single healthcare system in West Virginia, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other geographic regions or healthcare settings with different patient populations or practice patterns when treating patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19. However, the 124 patients were cared for by 58 different attending physicians, with most providers caring for only 1–3 patients, reducing the likelihood that our findings reflect the prescribing habits of any one provider or small group. The relatively small sample size limited the statistical power, particularly for outcome comparisons, such as mortality or progression to severe disease, so these findings should be interpreted descriptively rather than inferentially. In addition, as a retrospective chart review, the study was subject to selection bias. It was also subject to missing data and misclassification both when the data was entered into the registry and when the data was extracted for the study by the reviewer. Some treatment decisions may not have been fully documented or may have been influenced by factors that were not captured in the record. Finally, we were unable to adjust for confounding variables, such as vaccination status, prior infection history, specific comorbidities, or provider specialty, that may influence both prescribing behavior and clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, our study suggests that inpatient providers are not treating many hospitalized patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 who are eligible for antiviral treatment. This suggests a need to increase the utilization of antiviral medications in preventing disease progression in high-risk hospitalized patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19.
