Standfirst

‘Scorpion sting envenomation meets all the criteria to be recognised as a neglected tropical disease, yet it remains overlooked and excluded from the official WHO list of neglected tropical diseases. The lack of formal recognition is, in itself, a barrier to achieving global health equity and preventing avoidable deaths in vulnerable communities, particularly among children in tropical and subtropical regions across the globe’, argue Carlos Castillo-Salgado and colleagues.

Scorpion sting envenomation (SSE) is a life-threatening medical condition caused by the venom of certain scorpion species, posing a significant public health burden in tropical and subtropical regions. Each year, SSE leads to thousands of deaths and severe complications, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations with limited access to timely treatment, especially children under 10 years of age.1 2 The WHO defines neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) as ‘a diverse group of conditions caused by a variety of pathogens (including viruses, bacteria, parasites, fungi and toxins) associated with devastating health, social and economic consequences’.3 NTDs primarily affect impoverished communities in tropical regions and impact more than one billion people worldwide. Their epidemiology is complex, often influenced by environmental, geographical and social conditions, making public health control efforts challenging.3 In 2018, Benin and Morocco proposed SSE for recognition as an NTD by WHO.4 The request was reviewed by a three-member subcommittee of the WHO Strategic and Technical Advisory Group (STAG) for NTDs and the WHO secretariat. Two members concluded that SSE did not meet the NTD criteria, citing ‘extremely rare mortality, lack of long-term disability and difficulties in defining a broad control strategy’. Consequently, STAG did not recommend its inclusion as an NTD.4 However, new evidence challenges these previous conclusions, allowing for a reassessment of SSE’s classification. Today, robust and compelling data demonstrate that SSE meets all WHO criteria for NTD recognition. This letter presents evidence-based arguments for the urgent reconsideration of SSE as an NTD, considering new data that addresses previous objections and underscores its significant and underestimated public health impact.

Scorpions are found on every continent except Antarctica, some islands and boreal regions. Their adaptability allows them to thrive in a wide range of environments (arid, tropical and temperate regions). Scorpions exhibit notable resilience to pesticide treatments, and certain species of sanitary significance (eg, Tityus serrulatus) can withstand extreme environmental conditions, including prolonged periods of starvation lasting over a year, which complicates efforts to control SSE.2 5 Additionally, some of these species reproduce through parthenogenesis, allowing them to rapidly establish and expand populations.5 This combination of widespread distribution and survival capabilities puts more than 30% of the global population at risk of SSE.2 6 Fourteen scorpion genera (figure 1) and >150 species (table 1) are considered medically relevant worldwide, with key taxa distributed in 44 countries, where severe/lethal SSE cases have been reported, spanning five of the six WHO Health Regions: the Americas (13 countries: genera Centruroides, Tityus, Jaguajir), Eastern Mediterranean (18 countries: Androctonus, Buthus, Mesobuthus, Parabuthus, Odontobuthus, Nebo, Hottentotta, Hemiscorpius, Leiurus, Olivierus), African (7 countries: Androctonus, Buthus, Hottentotta, Leiurus, Parabuthus), European (2 countries: Aegaeobuthus, Androctonus, Nebo, Hottentotta, Leiurus) and South-East Asian (3 countries: Hottentotta).2 5 7 The following are three key aspects used by WHO to classify NTDs, as applied to the context of SSE.3

SSE, scorpion sting envenomation.

Criterion 1: SSE causes severe health consequences. SSE is a medical emergency with devastating health impacts. Venom components primarily act on voltage-gated ion channels in excitable tissues and receptors involved in immune responses.7 Systemic manifestations, generally species-specific in the case of the scorpion family Buthidae, are characteristic of autonomic stimulation, both adrenergic and cholinergic, and cause systemic inflammation associated with the release of proinflammatory mediators from affected tissues.7,9 This can lead to increased blood pressure and cardiac output, followed by left ventricular dysfunction, hypotension, cardiac arrhythmias, respiratory distress, myocardial injury (myocarditis and myocardial ischaemia), pancreatitis, liver injury, multisystem organ failure, coma and death.7,11 Anaphylactic shock (eg, after Jaguajir rochae envenomation)12 and infections (eg, cellulitis, abscesses, necrotising fasciitis and rarely fungal infections) following scorpion stings can occur but are uncommon.10 13 Additional complications include seizures, stroke, paralysis (eg, Parabuthus leiosoma), coagulopathy (eg, Nebo hierichonticus and Hemiscorpius species), local tissue necrosis, haemolytic uraemic syndrome (eg, Hemiscorpius species) and acute kidney injury (after envenoming by several noxious species prevalent throughout the African, Eastern Mediterranean, European and South-East Asian WHO regions).7,10 Despite its severe acute clinical outcomes, long-term complications of SSE are also recognised, including progression from acute kidney injury (with cortical and tubular necrosis involvement) to chronic kidney disease on dialysis,8 14 chronic relapsing pancreatitis,11 15 pancreatic microcalcifications16 and stroke (haemorrhagic and thrombotic), which was observed in 7.1% of patients in an Indian case series (eg, Hottentotta tamulus).9 17 In a case series of 489 patients envenomed by Hemiscorpius lepturus, more than 95% had cutaneous manifestations (eg, erythema, bullae, necrosis and ulcers, or a combination of these); the latter two may result in extensive permanent scarring with physical, psychological and social consequences that impair quality of life.9 18 19 Additional reported severe complications include persistent cerebral visual loss due to cerebral infarction,20 and cerebellar and cerebral infarcts with corpus callosum involvement and bilateral cerebral atrophy with subdural haemorrhage.21 Moreover, recent experimental findings indicate that scorpion venom persistence in peripheral nerves may impair remyelination, delay functional recovery, reduce motor axonal regeneration and downregulate genes and ion channels essential for regeneration, highlighting the risk of long-lasting deficits beyond the acute phase.22 The above complications likely contribute significantly to disability-adjusted life years, yet they remain poorly studied.23 The supposed absence of long-term disabilities following SSE, as cited by the STAG subcommittee, more plausibly reflects not a true lack of sequelae, but rather the limited availability of longitudinal data; largely due to the under-resourced health systems serving the poorest and most vulnerable populations. Recognising SSE as an NTD could help address these systemic gaps by mobilising attention, funding and coordinated research and clinical efforts.

Criterion 2: SSE disproportionately affects impoverished and rural communities. SSE disproportionately affects impoverished and rural communities, primarily in Latin America, Africa, the Middle East and Asia, with cases also occurring in urban areas of the southern USA, Central America and South America.1 2 5 7 24 Globally, more than 1.5 million cases and over 3250 deaths are reported annually.2 6 However, these figures are likely underestimations due to under-reporting, lack of epidemiological surveillance systems in poor resource settings and the fact that mild cases often go undocumented.5 The extent of underreporting remains unknown. Although STAG previously considered SSE mortality to be ‘extremely rare’,4 this is not the case in certain regions. Case fatality rates (CFR) vary widely: in the USA and Saudi Arabia, CFR is <0.01%, in Mexico, it is 0.01%, in Morocco 0.18%, and in India, it ranges from 1% to 10.7%.10 25 In Brazil, SSE had a higher CFR than dengue in 2023 (0.07% vs 0.04%).26 27 In Mexico, among children aged 1–4 years, SSE was the fourth leading cause of death among all notifiable conditions under epidemiological surveillance, as defined in the Mexican Official Standard NOM-017-SSA2-2012 for epidemiological surveillance; according to official 2021 mortality data, it ranked below COVID-19 but above tuberculosis, rickettsiosis, HIV, meningitis and influenza.28 SSE is a growing public health issue in Brazil and Mexico.5 25 In 2023, SSE cases accounted for approximately 60% of all venomous animal incidents in Brazil, nearly six times the number of snakebite cases.26 In Mexico, during the same period, SSE cases were 74 times more common than snakebite cases.29

Criterion 3: SSE presents a complex epidemiology and faces significant barriers to public health control. Mexico accounts for 25% of all SSE cases globally, with fatalities concentrated in resource-poor regions where timely access to medical care is limited. Vulnerable populations bear a disproportionate burden: indigenous communities account for approximately 30% of SSE-related deaths, and children under five represent 67% of total fatalities nationwide.24 25 30 These groups are often subjected to systemic marginalisation and remain under-represented in political decision-making, which contributes to the ongoing neglect of this public health threat. SSE-related child mortality results in immense suffering, psychological trauma and significant economic hardship for affected families. Accurately assessing the true burden of SSE is further hindered by the widespread lack of death registration—nearly 40% of global deaths go unrecorded, with the figure even higher among children; in low-income countries, only 8% of reported deaths have documented causes.31 Additional barriers to effective SSE control include limited production of species-specific antivenoms, the use of antivenoms with unknown paraspecificity, uneven distribution, recurrent supply shortages and inadequate training of healthcare personnel, all of which contribute to preventable morbidity and mortality.5 25 As scorpions generally exhibit high levels of endemism and typically narrow distributions, they are vulnerable to environmental changes. Climate change is expected to exacerbate the SSE situation by expanding scorpion species into previously unsuitable areas due to shifts in temperature and precipitation. These changes may facilitate the spread of parthenogenetic and synanthropic medically important species into new regions, particularly those with poor housing infrastructure, thereby increasing the risk of SSE in already vulnerable communities.5 32

Recognising SSE as an NTD is crucial for mobilising global health action. The STAG subcommittee previously advised against its inclusion, stating that ‘it was difficult to visualise a broad control strategy’.4 However, the lack of formal recognition itself has hindered the development of effective solutions. Without NTD designation, securing funding for SSE research, treatment development and public health interventions remains challenging. Official recognition would catalyse coordinated research efforts, support targeted interventions and enable resource mobilisation to reduce SSE’s burden. Given that both SSE manifestations and venom composition are largely species-specific—and that significant antigenic interspecies venom divergences have been documented33 —research driven by NTD designation could enhance antivenom quality by broadening neutralising spectra or developing new immunobiologicals, while also promoting studies on therapeutic anti-inflammatory alternatives and aiding in the identification of cryptic culprit species. SSE is both preventable and treatable; public education and timely access to safe, effective and affordable antivenoms have the potential to significantly reduce morbidity and mortality.5 10 24

SSE shares common risk factors and social determinants with other NTDs.1 25 Yet, its exclusion from the NTD list has allowed neglect to persist, exacerbating health disparities. The recognition of snakebite envenoming as an NTD demonstrated how coordinated global action can mobilise resources and improve public health outcomes.34 A similar approach for SSE would lead to advances in treatment accessibility, research and prevention strategies.

A call to action. Addressing SSE as an NTD is a crucial step towards achieving global health equity and preventing avoidable deaths in vulnerable communities, particularly among children. By acknowledging its burden and integrating it into the global health agenda, we can work towards the Sustainable Development Goals and ensure that no population continues to suffer from this disease.
