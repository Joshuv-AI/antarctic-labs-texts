On the local Maxwellians solving the Boltzmann equation with boundary condition
Théophile Dolmaire

Abstract
We derive the expressions of the local Maxwellians that solve the Boltzmann equation in the interior of an open domain. We determine which of these local Maxwellians satisfy the Boltzmann equation in a regular domain with boundary, without assuming the boundedness of the domain. We investigate separately, on the one hand, the case of the bounce-back boundary condition in any dimension, and on the other hand the case of the specular reflection boundary condition, in dimension d = 2 d=2 and d = 3 d=3 . In the case of the bounce-back boundary condition, we prove that the only local Maxwellians solving the Boltzmann equation with boundary condition are the global Maxwellians. In the case of the specular reflection, we provide a complete classification of the domains for which only the global Maxwellians solve the Boltzmann equation with boundary condition, and we describe all the local Maxwellians that solve the equation for the domains presenting symmetries.



Keywords.
Boltzmann equation; Local Maxwellian; Boundary condition.

1
Introduction

In 1872, Ludwig Boltzmann
[
1
]
introduced the following equation, in order to describe dilute gases composed with particles that interact with each other via binary collisions:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\int_{v_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}B\big(|v-v_{*}|,\left|\frac{v-v_{*}}{|v-v_{*}|}\cdot\omega\right|\big)\left[f(v^{\prime})f(v^{\prime}_{*})-f(v)f(v_{*})\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v_{*}.

(1.1)

Here, the unknown
f=f(t,x,v)
of the Boltzmann equation (
1.1
) is a probability density for all time
t
on the space
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni(x,v)
(the particles constituting the gas are assumed to evolve in the domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d
is an integer, representing the dimension of the physical space that is considered), and:

\displaystyle f(t,x,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v

(1.2)

represents the number of particles of the gas lying in the infinitesimal volume
x+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
and moving with a velocity in
v+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v
at time
t
.
v^{\prime}
and
v_{*}^{\prime}
, the
post-collisional velocities
, are defined as:

\displaystyle v^{\prime}=v-(v-v_{*})\cdot\omega\omega,\hskip 28.45274ptv_{*}^{\prime}=v_{*}+(v-v_{*})\cdot\omega\omega,

(1.3)

\omega
is the
angular parameter
, and
B\big(|v-v_{*}|,\left|\frac{v-v_{*}}{|v-v_{*}|}\cdot\omega\right|\big)
, the
collision kernel
, describes the rate at which collisions involving two particles with relative velocities
v-v_{*}
and colliding with an angular parameter
\omega
take place.

For instance, if we assume that the particles interact as hard spheres, the angular parameter
\omega
represents the direction of the line joining the respective centers of the two colliding particles, and in such a case the collision kernel
B
is:

\displaystyle B(|v-v_{*}|,\left|\frac{v-v_{*}}{|v-v_{*}|}\cdot\omega\right|)=|(v-v_{*})\cdot\omega|.

(1.4)

To complete the description of the model, boundary conditions need to be added to (
1.1
). We will assume that the domain
\Omega
is an open set, with a boundary
\partial\Omega
regular enough such that at any point
x\in\partial\Omega
we can define an outgoing unitary normal vector
n(x)
to the boundary. In the present article, we will consider the two following boundary conditions: the bounce-back boundary condition, defined as

\displaystyle\forall t\in[0,T],\,x\in\partial\Omega,\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\hskip 8.53581ptf(t,x,v)=f(t,x,-v).

(1.5)

and the specular reflection boundary condition, defined as

\displaystyle\forall t\in[0,T],x\in\partial\Omega,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},f(t,x,v)=f(t,x,v^{\prime}),

(1.6)

with

\displaystyle v^{\prime}=v-2\left(v\cdot n(x)\right)n(x).

(1.7)

These two conditions, written at the mesoscopic level (that is, for the unknown
f
of the Boltzmann equation (
1.1
)), reflect the model chosen to describe the dynamics of the particles that compose the gas, when such a particle collides with the boundary
\partial\Omega
. In the case of the bounce-back boundary condition, the velocity
v
of a particle about to leave the domain
\Omega
is immediately changed into
v^{\prime}=-v
, while in the specular reflection case, the reflected velocity
v^{\prime}
is defined by (
1.7
). For other possible boundary conditions and discussions concerning the relevance of the laws (
1.5
) and (
1.6
), the reader may refer to
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
and
[
13
]
.

The collision law (
1.3
) describes elastic collisions: momentum and kinetic energy are conserved when two particles collide. These conserved quantities can be recovered at the level of the Boltzmann equation: if
f
is a solution of (
1.1
) (at least, in the absence of boundary), then

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\int_{x}\int_{v}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
v\\
|v|^{2}\end{pmatrix}f(t,x,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0.

(1.8)

More surprisingly, if we define the
entropy

H
as the functional:

\displaystyle H(f)(t)=\int_{x}\int_{v}f(t,x,v)\ln f(t,x,v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(1.9)

then if
f
is a solution of the Boltzmann equation, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}H(f)(t)=-\int_{x}D(f)(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq 0,

(1.10)

with
D
the
entropy production
defined as

\displaystyle D(f)=\frac{1}{4}\int_{x}\int_{v}\int_{v_{*}}\int_{\omega}B\left[f(v^{\prime})f(v^{\prime}_{*})-f(v)f(v_{*})\right]\ln\frac{f(v^{\prime})f(v^{\prime}_{*})}{f(v)f(v_{*})}.

(1.11)

In addition, we have that
\int_{x}D(f)(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0
if and only if there exist three functions
\rho,a:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that:

\displaystyle f(x,v)=\rho(x)\exp\left(-a(x)|v-u(x)|^{2}\right).

(1.12)

These two statements constitute the celebrated
H-theorem
, discovered by Boltzmann (
[
1
]
). In addition, let us observe that any function of the form:

\displaystyle m(t,x,v)=\rho_{0}\exp\left(-a_{0}|v-u_{0}|^{2}\right),

(1.13)

where
\rho_{0},a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are constant, is a solution to the Boltzmann equation. A function of the form (
1.13
) is called a
global Maxwellian
, whereas a function of the form:

\displaystyle m(t,x,v)=\rho(t,x)\exp\left(-a(t,x)|v-u(t,x)|^{2}\right),

(1.14)

with
\rho,a:I\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
u:I\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
I\subset\mathbb{R}
), is called a
local Maxwellian
.

If we can show that the only local Maxwellians solving the Boltzmann equation (
1.1
) are global Maxwellians, then the H-theorem would indicate the long-time behaviour of the solutions of the Boltzmann equation: we would expect that any solution
f
converges towards the only global Maxwellian that has the same mass, momentum and kinetic energy as
f
. For this reason, determining the local Maxwellians that solve the Boltzmann equation is of central importance in order to understand the long-time behaviour of the solutions of (
1.1
).

Since a local Maxwellian cancels the collision term of the Boltzmann equation (that is, the right hand side of (
1.1
)), a local Maxwellian
m
solves the Boltzmann equation if and only if
m
solves the free transport equation, that is:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}m+v\cdot\nabla_{x}m=0.

(1.15)

It is well-known how to determine the local Maxwellians that solve the free transport equation, and such a result was already obtained by Boltzmann himself
[
2
]
. The problem is also discussed by Cercignani in
[
4
]
. In this case, an external field acting on the particles is also considered.

As a matter of fact, in the case of a domain without boundary, many local Maxwellians, that are not global, solve the free transport equation. More generally, the question of the long-time behaviour of the solutions of the Boltzmann equation is a very challenging problem, that remains an open question nowadays. The difficulties arise from the mixing of the effects of the collision term of the Boltzmann equation on the one hand, and of the transport term
-v\cdot\nabla_{x}
on the other hand. Under the action of the collision term, the solutions converge to local Maxwellians (as it can be rigorously proved in the case of the homogeneous Boltzmann equation). However, the collision term is local in
x
, whereas the transport term
-v\cdot\nabla_{x}f
acts on the two variables
x
and
v
of the phase space, introducing major complications.

Nevertheless, let us mention that important results were obtained in
[
8
]
concerning the long-time behaviour of the solutions of the non-homogeneous Boltzmann equation (
1.1
). For more information on the Boltzmann equation, the reader may refer to the classical references
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
and
[
13
]
.

Back to the question of determining the local Maxwellians solving (
1.1
), the case of a domain with a boundary reduces dramatically the possibilities. A discussion concerning the geometry of the boundary can already be found in the article
[
9
]
of Grad. A classification of the boundaries as well as the complete proof of this classification can be found in
[
6
]
. Essentially, the result is that if the boundary of the domain does not present any symmetry, then only the global Maxwellians solve the free transport equation in a bounded domain. However, the results of
[
6
]
are obtained under the assumption that the domain
\Omega
is bounded. Besides, to the best of our knowledge, there exists no analogous result for more general domains. Therefore, and considering the central role of the local Maxwellians in the trend to converge to equilibrium for solutions of the Boltzmann equation, it seemed relevant to provide a discussion similar to the one presented in
[
6
]
in the case when the domain
\Omega
is not necessarily bounded.

In the present article, we determine completely the local Maxwellians that solve the Boltzmann equation in a general domain, not necessarily bounded. In particular, when such a domain presents a boundary, we perform the study in the case of the bounce-back boundary condition, and in the case of the specular reflection boundary condition.

The plan of the article is the following. In the second section, we derive the expressions of the local Maxwellians that solve the free transport equation in the interior of an open domain. This section follows the references
[
4
]
and
[
6
]
. In the third section, we study the local Maxwellians solving (
1.1
) in a domain with a boundary, considering the bounce-back boundary condition. In particular, we prove that only the global Maxwellians are admissible solutions. Finally, in the fourth section we turn to the case of the specular reflection boundary condition. In this section we determine local Maxwellians solving the Boltzmann equation that were not discussed in
[
6
]
.

2
Obtaining the expressions of the admissible local Maxwellians

In this section we will derive the expression of a local Maxwellian, that solves the Boltzmann equation in dimension
d\geq 2
, on the time interval
[0,T]
, in the interior of a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\Omega
is open and regular.

The expression of a local Maxwellian is written as:

\displaystyle m(t,x,v)=\rho(t,x)\exp\left(-a(t,x)\left|v-u(t,x)\right|^{2}\right),

(2.1)

where we assume that
\rho,a:\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
are strictly positive almost everywhere, and
u:\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume also that
\rho
,
a
and
u
are differentiable.

It is well known that
Q(m,m)=0
if and only if
m
is a local Maxwellian (see for instance
[
1
]
or
[
13
]
), that is, of the form of (
2.1
). Therefore, a local Maxwellian solves the Boltzmann equation in the domain
\Omega
if and only if

\displaystyle\forall\,(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\hskip 14.22636pt\partial_{t}m+v\cdot\nabla_{x}m=0,

(2.2)

that is, the local Maxwellian
m
solves the free transport equation. It is a classical result (
[
2
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
6
]

[
11
]

(Local Maxwellians solving the free transport equation)
.

Let
m:[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a local Maxwellian of the form:

\displaystyle m(t,x,v)=\rho(t,x)\exp\left(-a(t,x)\left|v-u(t,x)\right|^{2}\right),

with
\rho
,
a
and
u
differentiable and

\rho
and

\displaystyle\partial_{t}m+v\cdot\nabla_{x}m=0.

Then, there exist four real number

\displaystyle\forall\,(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\hskip 5.69054ptm(t,x,v)

\displaystyle=r_{0}\exp\Big(-\alpha|x-tv|^{2}+\beta(x-tv)\cdot v-\gamma|v|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+2\left(\Lambda_{0}(x-tv)\right)\cdot v-2w_{1}\cdot(x-tv)+2w_{2}\cdot v\Big).

Let us observe that the expression can be simplified as:

\displaystyle m(t,x,v)

\displaystyle=r_{0}\exp\Big(-\alpha|x-tv|^{2}+\beta(x-tv)\cdot v-\gamma|v|^{2}+2\left(\Lambda_{0}x\right)\cdot v-2w_{1}\cdot(x-tv)+2w_{2}\cdot v\Big),

Fourth, combining the previous expression of

Remark 2
.

It is a classical and elementary result to establish that if a smooth vector valued function
W:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is such that its Jacobian matrix is everywhere skew-symmetric, then
W
is an affine mapping. Such a result means that the movement described by the mapping
W
corresponds to a rigid motion, since
W
can be seen as an infinitesimal rotation.

Let us observe that such a result can be seen as the “simplest possible” version of a Korn-type inequality:

\displaystyle|\hskip-0.7113pt|\text{Jac}(W)|\hskip-0.7113pt|_{L^{p}}\leq C_{p}|\hskip-0.7113pt|\text{Jac}^{s}(W)|\hskip-0.7113pt|_{L^{p}},

(2.20)

where
\text{Jac}^{s}(W)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\text{Jac}(W)+\hskip 0.28453pt^{t}\hskip-1.42262pt\text{Jac}(W)\right]
is the symmetric part of the Jacobian matrix of
W
.

One of the first versions of such a result can be found in
[
10
]
, and was obtained in order to study the deformation of elastic materials. Korn’s inequalities are supplemented with appropriate boundary conditions, motivated by physical considerations.

It is worthwhile to mention that versions of Korn’s inequality have been established in kinetic theory (such as in
[
7
]
, or more recently in
[
3
]
), and used in a crucial manner to study the long-time behaviour of the solutions of the Boltzmann equation
[
8
]
.

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}}\left(\Lambda_{0}x\right)\cdot\left(\Lambda_{0}x\right)-\sigma_{0}x\cdot\left(\frac{\Lambda_{0}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}x\right)=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}}(\penalty\ ^{t}x)\left(\penalty\ {}^{t}\Lambda_{0}\right)\Lambda_{0}x-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}}\penalty\ ^{t}x\Lambda_{0}^{2}x=0.

Now, let us note that:

\displaystyle 2\sigma_{0}\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}^{\prime}}{2\sigma_{0}}\right)^{2}+\sigma_{0}\left(\frac{\sigma_{0}^{\prime}}{2\sigma_{0}}\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0}\left[\frac{8\alpha^{2}t^{2}+8\alpha\beta t+2\beta^{2}-4\alpha^{2}t^{2}-4\alpha\beta t+4\alpha\gamma-2\beta^{2}}{(2\sigma_{0})^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha,

so that we have:

\displaystyle m(t,x,v)

\displaystyle=r_{0}\exp\left(-\sigma_{0}|v|^{2}-\alpha|x|^{2}+2\left(\Lambda_{0}x\right)\cdot v+\sigma_{0}^{\prime}x\cdot v+2\sigma_{0}C\cdot v-2w_{1}\cdot x\right).

(2.41)

Finally, using (
2.33
) and (
2.34
) we have:

\displaystyle-\sigma_{0}|v|^{2}-\alpha|x|^{2}+\sigma_{0}^{\prime}x\cdot v+2\sigma_{0}C\cdot v-2w_{1}\cdot x

\displaystyle=-\left(\alpha t^{2}+\beta t+\gamma\right)|v|^{2}-\alpha|x|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\left(2\alpha t+\beta\right)x\cdot v+2(w_{1}t+w_{2})\cdot v-2w_{1}\cdot x

\displaystyle=-\alpha|x-tv|^{2}+\beta v\cdot(x-tv)-\gamma|v|^{2}-2w_{1}\cdot(x-tv)+2w_{2}\cdot v.

It is therefore possible to deduce that if a local Maxwellian solves the free transport equation, it can be written in the form given by (
2.4
), which concludes the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

Thanks to Theorem
1
, we can now describe completely the local Maxwellians that solve the Boltzmann equation, inside particular domains in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(even
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the bounce-back case), depending also on the choice of the boundary condition we prescribe. This is the object of the two following sections.

3
Local Maxwellians and boundary condition I: the bounce-back case

Let us start with the case of the
bounce-back boundary condition
. We consider a gas evolving in a regular open set
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This boundary condition, which may not look natural at the first glance, is defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Bounce-back boundary condition (BBBC))
.

Let
\Omega
be a regular, open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
f:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any function.
f
is said to satisfy the
bounce-back boundary condition
(abbreviated as
BBBC
) on the boundary
\partial\Omega
of
\Omega
if:

\displaystyle\forall t\in[0,T],\,x\in\partial\Omega,\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\hskip 8.53581ptf(t,x,v)=f(t,x,-v).

(3.1)

At the level of the particles, this means that the particles are reflected on the boundary of the domain
\Omega
, in such a way that the particles travel back, following backwards exactly the path they took before they collided with the wall.

Let us now characterize the local Maxwellians solving the free transport equation with bounce-back boundary condition.

Theorem 2

(Characterization of the local Maxwellians solving the free transport equation in a domain with bounce-back boundary condition (BBBC))
.

Let
T
be a strictly positive number (possibly
+\infty
), let
\Omega
be a regular open set of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a non empty boundary, and let us consider a local Maxwellian
m
, that is, a function of the form (
2.4
), which solves the transport equation (
2.2
) on
[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with the bounce-back boundary condition, that is, such that

\displaystyle\forall t\in[0,T],x\in\partial\Omega,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\hskip 8.53581ptm(t,x,v)=m(t,x,-v).

(3.2)

Then, there exist two real numbers
r_{0}
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that:

\displaystyle\forall t\in[0,T],x\in\Omega,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\hskip 8.53581ptm(t,x,v)=r_{0}\exp\left(-\gamma|v|^{2}\right)\hskip 14.22636pt.

(3.3)

Remark 3
.

(
3.3
) shows in particular that for a mass and a kinetic energy fixed, there exists only one local Maxwellian that solves the free transport equation with bounce-back boundary condition. Such a Maxwellian has zero bulk (the momentum of
m
is zero), and it is actually global (none of its coefficient depends on the time
t
nor on the position variable
x
).

Remark 4
.

In the literature, it is usual to find the assumption that the domain
\Omega
is bounded (see for instance
[
6
]
,
[
8
]
or
[
11
]
). This assumption simplifies the proof of Theorem
2
, but it is not necessary, in the sense that it is possible to obtain the same result, without any assumption concerning the boundedness of the domain.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Writing the local Maxwellian
m
in its general form (
2.2
), the bounce-back boundary condition (
3.1
) writes:

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)\exp\left(-a(t,x)\left|v-u(t,x)\right|^{2}\right)=\rho(t,x)\exp\left(-a(t,x)\left|-v-u(t,x)\right|^{2}\right)

for all
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in\partial\Omega
, and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This implies that
u(t,x)
has to be zero for all time
t
, and all
x
that belongs to the boundary
\partial\Omega
of the domain.

Remembering now that we obtained, along the proof of Theorem
1
, that
u(t,x)
writes:

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}(t)}\Lambda_{0}x+\frac{\sigma_{0}^{\prime}(t)}{2\sigma_{0}(t)}x+\frac{tw_{1}+w_{2}}{\sigma_{0}(t)},

(see in particular (
2.25
) page
2.25
and (
2.34
) page
2.34
), and using the fact that
\sigma_{0}(t)
was the polynomial
\alpha t^{2}+\beta t+\gamma
of degree
2
in
t
(see (
2.33
) page
2.33
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}x+(\alpha t+\beta/2)x+tw_{1}+w_{2}=0,

(3.4)

which implies that the first order coefficient (in
t
) in (
3.4
) has to vanish, that is, we have:

\displaystyle\alpha x+w_{1}=0

(3.5)

for all point
x\in\partial\Omega
. If
\alpha\neq 0
, (
3.5
) cannot hold, because
\partial\Omega
was assumed to be non empty, and so it cannot be reduced to a single point by regularity. Therefore, we have
\alpha=0
.

Using then the expression (
2.4
) and applying again the boundary condition, we obtain the new equation:

\displaystyle 4v\cdot\left(\Lambda_{0}x\right)+4v\cdot w_{2}+4tv\cdot w_{1}+2\beta v\cdot x=0,

for all
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in\partial\Omega
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We deduce immediately the new condition:

\displaystyle 2\Lambda_{0}x+2w_{2}+2tw_{1}+\beta x=0,

(3.6)

for all
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in\partial\Omega
. The first order term in
t
provides directly that

\displaystyle w_{1}=0,

(3.7)

while the constant term gives:

\displaystyle 2\Lambda_{0}x+\beta x+2w_{2}=0,

(3.8)

everywhere on the boundary
\partial\Omega
of the domain. Let us now notice that (
3.8
) cannot hold if
\beta\neq 0
: indeed, in that case, we would have that the mapping
\phi:x\mapsto 2\Lambda_{0}x+\beta x
is invertible because for any non-zero vector
x
, we find:

\displaystyle x\cdot\phi(x)=2x\cdot\left(\Lambda_{0}x\right)+\beta|x|^{2}=\beta|x|^{2}\neq 0.

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\beta=0.

(3.9)

But then, (
3.8
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}x+w_{2}=0,

(3.10)

for all
x\in\partial\Omega
. Let us show that if
\Lambda_{0}\neq 0
, we obtain a contradiction. In that case, the matrix
\Lambda_{0}
is not trivial, and so its kernel cannot be of dimension
d
. Let us fix a point
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}x_{0}+w_{2}=0,

and so, for any other point
x\in\partial\Omega
, we have by linearity:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}(x-x_{0})=0,

that is,
x-x_{0}
is contained in the kernel of
\Lambda_{0}
, or again:

\displaystyle\partial\Omega\subset x_{0}+\text{Ker}\Lambda_{0}.

(3.11)

This last equation shows already that if the boundary
\partial\Omega
is not an affine hyperplane of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then we cannot have
\Lambda_{0}\neq 0
. Let us show that even when the boundary of the domain
\Omega
is flat, we obtain a contradiction anyway, due to the dimension of the kernel of
\Lambda_{0}
.

Let us assume that
\Lambda_{0}
is not the zero matrix. We will prove that the dimension of the kernel of
\Lambda_{0}
is at most
d-2
.

The assumption that
\Lambda_{0}\neq 0
implies that its spectrum cannot be reduced to zero. Indeed, a skew-symmetric matrix is a particular case of a normal matrix (that is, it commutes with its Hermitian conjugate
\Lambda_{0}^{*}
), so there exists a unitary matrix
U
and a diagonal matrix
D
(both with complex entries) such that: such that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}=UDU^{*}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636ptUU^{*}=I_{d}.

Therefore, there exists a non-zero eigenvector
z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
of
\Lambda_{0}
associated to a non-zero eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Let us decompose
z
into its real and imaginary parts:

\displaystyle z=u_{1}+iu_{2}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{with}\hskip 14.22636ptu_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

\Lambda_{0}
being skew-symmetric, we know in addition that
\lambda
has to be purely imaginary. Besides,
z
being non zero, either its real part
u_{1}
or its imaginary part
u_{2}
is non-zero. Actually both
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are non-zero. Indeed, if
u_{2}
would be zero,
z
would be a real-valued vector, which would lead to a contradiction, because
\Lambda_{0}
is also real-valued and
\lambda\in i\mathbb{R}
. In the same way,
u_{1}
cannot be zero.

Therefore, the real and imaginary parts
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
of the eigenvector
z
are both non zero. They are in addition linearly independent in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let us indeed assume that there exists a real number
\mu
(necessarily non zero) such that
u_{2}=\mu u_{1}
. Using that
\Lambda_{0}
is a real matrix, we obtain another eigenvector, associated to a different eigenvalue, considering simply the eigenequation:

\displaystyle\overline{\left(\Lambda_{0}\cdot z\right)}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{0}\cdot\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\overline{\left(\lambda z\right)}=\overline{\lambda}\overline{z}.

We would have then:

\displaystyle z=u_{1}+iu_{2}=(1+i\mu)u_{1},\text{ and }\overline{z}=u_{1}-iu_{2}=(1-i\mu)u_{1}.

But this would imply that
z
and
\overline{z}
are linearly dependent (in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
), which cannot be, since they are associated to the respective distinct eigenvalues
\lambda
and
\overline{\lambda}
. Therefore, the real and imaginary parts of
z
are linearly dependent.

In the end, observing that:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}u_{1}=\Lambda_{0}\left(\frac{z+\overline{z}}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda z+\overline{\lambda}\overline{z}\right)=\mathfrak{Re}(\lambda z)=-\text{sgn}(\lambda)|\lambda|u_{2}\neq 0,

and

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}u_{2}=\overline{\Lambda}_{0}\left(\frac{z-\overline{z}}{2i}\right)=\frac{1}{2i}\left(\lambda z-\overline{\lambda}\overline{z}\right)=\mathfrak{Im}(\lambda z)=\text{sgn}(\lambda)|\lambda|u_{1}\neq 0.

We can therefore conclude that
\text{Span}(u_{1},u_{2})
is of dimension
2
, and that this space is in direct sum with
\text{Ker}\Lambda_{0}
. In other words, the kernel of
\Lambda_{0}
has a dimension that is at most
d-2
. Therefore, in the case
\Lambda_{0}\neq 0
, (
3.11
) can never hold, regardless the dimension
d
, or the geometry of the boundary
\partial\Omega
, because no hypersurface can be contained in a vector space of codimension
2
.

This implies, according to (
3.10
), that
w_{2}=0
, and so Theorem
2
is proved.
∎

Remark 5
.

This is equation (
3.11
) that is used by Desvillettes in
[
6
]
to deduce that if
\Omega
is bounded, then we obtain a contradiction if
\Lambda_{0}\neq 0
. Our addition here is the discussion concerning the dimension of the kernel of

, which enables to obtain the result of Theorem

, without using any boundedness assumption on

.

4
Local Maxwellians and boundary condition II: the specular reflection case

Let us now turn to the case of the specular reflection, which is much more relevant regarding the physical motivation. In this case, the particles are assumed to be reflected against the boundary of the domain, exactly as a billiard ball would do: during the collision, the velocity of the particle is reflected, that is, is obtained as the orthogonal symmetry, with respect to the tangent hyperplane to the obstacle, at the point of bounce.
As for the bounce-back boundary condition, this assumption on the behaviour of the particles is translated into an equality on the density function
f
.

Definition 2

(Specular reflection boundary condition (SRBC))
.

Let
\Omega
be a regular, open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
f:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any function.
f
is said to satisfy the
specular reflection boundary condition
(abbreviated as
SRBC
) on the boundary
\partial\Omega
of
\Omega
if:

\displaystyle\forall t\in[0,T],x\in\partial\Omega,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},f(t,x,v)=f(t,x,v^{\prime}),

(4.1)

where
v^{\prime}
is defined as:

\displaystyle v^{\prime}=v-2\left(v\cdot n(x)\right)n(x),

(4.2)

with
n(x)
a unitary normal vector to the boundary
\partial\Omega
of the domain, at
x\in\partial\Omega
.

Remark 6
.

Note that we did not specify completely the normal vector
n(x)
we used in (
4.2
). The reason is that changing
n(x)
into
-n(x)
leaves the expression (
4.2
) of
v^{\prime}
unchanged.

We will now see that, contrary to the bounce-back boundary condition, there can be much more local Maxwellians solving the free transport equation with these new boundary condtions. More precisely, we will see that, the more the domain is “symmetric”, the more we can find local Maxwellians verifying the free transport with SRBC.

We will proceed first when the dimension

is
2
, and then when
d=3
. However, the starting point, namely, exploiting the boundary condition (
4.1
), is independent from the dimension, and provides the equation:

\displaystyle\left|v-u(t,x)\right|^{2}=\left|v^{\prime}-u(t,x)\right|^{2}

(4.3)

for all
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\partial\Omega
(where
v^{\prime}
is defined in (
4.2
)),
as a consequence of the expression (
2.1
). The last equation can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\left(v\cdot n(x)\right)n(x)\cdot u(t,x)=0,

(4.4)

for all
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in\partial\Omega
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since
v
can be chosen freely in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have:

\displaystyle n(x)\cdot u(t,x)=0\hskip 14.22636pt\forall t\in[0,T],x\in\partial\Omega,

which we rewrite again, taking advantage of the explicit expression of

we derived in (
2.25
) page

, (
2.34
) page

, and (

) page

:

\displaystyle n(x)\cdot\left[\Lambda_{0}x+\left(\alpha t+\beta/2\right)x+tw_{1}+w_{2}\right]=0\hskip 14.22636pt\forall t\in[0,T],x\in\partial\Omega.

(4.5)

Equation (
4.5
) will then be the central object of our study in the present section.
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Lambda_{0}
skew-symmetric, all have to be determined, depending on the shape of the domain, and the dimension

.

In the case of the specular reflection case, the condition (

) we obtained on the boundary

is less easy to study than (

) (obtained in the BBBC case). Indeed, this time the information provided by (

, which depends itself on

.
In order to establish the proof of Theorems
3
and
4
, we will make use of the following results, that describe solutions of some particular linear ODEs in general dimensions.

These results will be applied to describe which type of curves the boundary
\partial\Omega
of an open set
\Omega
can contain, if one knows particular conditions of the form of (

) holding on the normal vector
n(x)
to the boundary

can already be found in

Lemma 8
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\alpha\neq 0
, and let
w_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with
d=2
or
d=3
).

Then the solution

of the the following Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}x(t)&=&\alpha x(t)+w_{1},\\
x(0)&=&x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{array}\right.

is globally well-defined and is given by the following expression:

\displaystyle x(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\alpha x_{0}+w_{1}\right)e^{\alpha t}-\frac{w_{1}}{\alpha}\hskip 14.22636pt\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.8)

Lemma 9
.

Let
\Lambda_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
be a non-zero skew-symmetric matrix, let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
be a real number, and let
w_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with

or

).

Then the solution
t\mapsto x(t)
of the the following Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}x(t)&=&\Lambda_{0}x(t)+\frac{\beta}{2}x(t)+w_{2},\\
x(0)&=&x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{array}\right.

is globally well-defined. In addition, the solution

can be described explicitely as follows.

—

If
\beta\neq 0
or if the dimension
d
is equal to
2
, the matrix
\Lambda_{0}+\frac{\beta}{2}I_{d}
is invertible, and the solution

is given by the expression:

\displaystyle x(t)=e^{\frac{\beta}{2}t}e^{t\Lambda_{0}}\left(x(0)+y\right)-y,

(4.11)

where
y
is the preimage of the vector
w_{2}
by the matrix
\Lambda_{0}+\frac{\beta}{2}I_{d}
.

In such a case, the graph of

is a logarithmic spiral inscribed in a plane that contains the point
-y
if
\beta\neq 0
, and a circle inscribed in a plane if
\beta=0
.

—

If

\beta=0

\displaystyle x(t)=e^{t\Lambda_{0}}\left(x(0)+y\right)-y+\lambda ut,

where

, with

\Lambda_{0}x=z\wedge x
for all

, and

,

, with:

\displaystyle w_{2}=\lambda u+w=\lambda\frac{z}{|z|}+w,\hskip 8.53581pt\text{with}\hskip 8.53581ptw\perp u.

In such a case, the graph of
t\mapsto x(t)
is an helix of axis
\text{Span}(u)=\text{Span}(z/|z|)

Proof.

In the case when
\beta\neq 0
, the matrix
\Lambda_{0}+\frac{\beta}{2}I_{d}

\begin{pmatrix}0&-a\\

, with

(because
\Lambda_{0}
was assumed to be not zero), which is also invertible. In both cases, we can define the vector

, preimage of
w_{2}
by the matrix
\Lambda_{0}+\frac{\beta}{2}I_{d}
. Therefore, posing:

\displaystyle\varphi(t)=(x(t)+y)e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}t},

(4.14)

we find:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\Lambda_{0}x(t)+\frac{\beta}{2}x(t)+w_{2}\right)e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}t}-\frac{\beta}{2}\left(x(t)+y\right)e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}t}

\displaystyle=\left(\Lambda_{0}x(t)+\Lambda_{0}y\right)e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}t}=\Lambda_{0}\varphi(t),

(4.15)

hence

\displaystyle\varphi(t)=e^{t\Lambda_{0}}\varphi(0)=e^{t\Lambda_{0}}\left(x(0)+y\right).

(4.16)

We deduce then the expression (
4.11
).

In the second case, since
\Lambda_{0}
was assumed to be non-zero and the dimension

is assumed to be equal to

, there exists a non-zero vector

such that the action of
\Lambda_{0}
can be represented by the cross product with the vector

. Denoting

by

\displaystyle w_{2}=\lambda u+w,

(4.17)

for some

and

. Such a decomposition satisfies that

belongs to the image of
\Lambda_{0}
, let us denote by

).

This time, we pose:

\displaystyle\varphi(t)=x(t)+y-\lambda ut,

(4.18)

and in this case we find:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{0}x(t)+w_{2}-\lambda u

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{0}x(t)+\Lambda_{0}y=\Lambda_{0}\left(x(t)+y-\lambda ut\right)

(because

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\varphi(t)=\Lambda_{0}\varphi(t).

(4.20)

.

. More precisely, in the

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}^{2k}=(-a^{2})^{k}I_{2}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\Lambda_{0}^{2k+1}=(-a^{2})^{k}\Lambda_{0}\hskip 14.22636pt\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.21)

Then, we obtain:

\displaystyle\exp\left(t\Lambda_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}(-a^{2})^{k}I_{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}(-a^{2})^{k}\Lambda_{0}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}a^{2k}&\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}a^{2k+1}\\
-\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}a^{2k+1}&\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}a^{2k}\end{pmatrix}

The

4.2
Local Maxwellians and specular reflection, in dimension

We conclude this study with the second physically relevant case (and maybe the most relevant of the two): the

Before stating the result, we start with introducing definitions concerning particular symmetries that we will use in the following.

Definition 4

(Rotational symmetry, spatial version)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a

\mathcal{S}

Remark 10
.

A surface
\mathcal{S}
that presents a rotational symmetry is the union of circles that are all inscribed in planes orthogonal to the axis of symmetry

Definition 5

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
surface of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
\mathcal{S}
is said to present an

if there exists an affine straight line

of the space, orientated by a unit vector

, and a real number

such that, for any point

Remark 11
.

In the case when a surface

presents an helical symmetry, the surface is the union of helices, that all have the same axis and the same shift (that is, the same distance between two consecutive coils).

In the case when
\lambda=0
, the notion of helical symmetry coincides with the notion of rotational symmetry.

We introduce a precise nomenclature for remarkable surfaces that will appear in the main result of this section.

Definition 6

(Generalized cylinder)
.

A surface
\mathcal{S}
of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is said to be a

if there exists a family

of straight lines

any unit vector that orientates any of the lines

Remark 12
.

A generalized cylinder is in particular a ruled surface.

Definition 7

(Cylinder of revolution)
.

A surface
\mathcal{S}
of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is said to be a

if
\mathcal{S}
is the union of a family of parallel straight lines obtained from all the possible rotations of a given line

Remark 13
.

In particular, a cylinder of revolution is a generalized cylinder such that all the lines

intersect a given circle inscribed in a plane orthogonal to the axis
\Delta
.

Finally, we will make use of the two following results concerning surfaces that are generalized cylinders or presenting an helical symmetry.

The first result describes the intersection between the class of the surfaces presenting an helical symmetry, with the class of the generalized cylinders.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
connected surface of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, that is a generalized cylinder. Then, if in addition
\mathcal{S}
presents an helical symmetry:

—

if the shift
\lambda
of the helical symmetry is zero,
\mathcal{S}
is either a plane, or a cylinder of revolution,

—

if the shift
\lambda
of the helical symmetry is non-zero,
\mathcal{S}
is a cylinder of revolution.

Proof.

By assumption,
\mathcal{S}
presents an helical symmetry (say, of axis
\Delta
), and contains at least one straight line

.

Our definition of helical symmetry covers also the case of a rotational symmetry. Let us start with the case, that is, we assume in a first time that the shift of the helical symmetry is zero.

Let us assume first that

intersects the axis of symmetry

, in which case these two lines are either orthogonal or parallel, or none of these two cases. In the two first cases,
\mathcal{S}
is a plane, or a cylinder of revolution. In the latter case, that is, when

The case of an helical symmetry with a zero shift is then completely addressed.

Let us now assume that the helical symmetry has a non-zero shift. We will make use of the following result, that the reader may find for instance in

\Delta
, which is not the case. For the same reason, its direction cannot be orthogonal to
\Delta
, because the helicoid would contain a plane that is orthogonal to
\Delta

\Delta

Since we covered all the possible cases, the proof of Lemma

y+\text{Span}(n)
, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
n\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

The following cases lead to a direct contradiction:

\displaystyle\alpha\neq 0,\hskip 56.9055pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\Lambda_{0}\neq 0\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679pt\beta\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt],\hskip 56.9055pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\Lambda_{0}=0\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679pt\beta\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt].

(4.77)

In particular, we deduce that
\alpha=\beta=0
.

In the case when
w_{1}\neq 0
, we deduce that
w_{1}
has to be colinear to
n
. If
\Lambda_{0}\neq 0
, represented by the vector
z
,
\partial\Omega
presents an helical symmetry of axis
-y^{\prime}+\text{Span}(z)
, where
w_{2}=-\Lambda_{0}y^{\prime}+pn
. Therefore, this implies
z=an
for a certain
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\neq 0
, and
\Lambda_{0}y^{\prime}=\Lambda_{0}y
, so that:

\displaystyle w_{2}=-\Lambda_{0}y+pn.

(4.78)

If finally
\Lambda=0
and
w_{2}\neq 0
, then
w_{2}
is colinear to
n
. In general,
\Lambda_{0}
and
w_{2}
have then the forms:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}x=an\wedge x\hskip 8.53581pt\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\hskip 8.53581pt\text{and}\hskip 8.53581ptw_{2}=-an\wedge y+\ell_{2}n,

(4.79)

for
a,\ell_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Therefore, if
\partial\Omega
is the union of one or two coaxial cylinders of revolution, any local Maxwellian solving the Boltzmann equation with the boundary condition (
4.1
) is necessarily of the form (
4.64
).

\partial\Omega
is a sphere.
Let us assume that the sphere
\partial\Omega
is centered on
y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In this case, the following assumptions lead to a direct contradiction:

\displaystyle\alpha\neq 0,\hskip 42.67912pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\alpha=0\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679ptw_{1}\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt],\hskip 42.67912pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\Lambda_{0}\neq 0\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679pt\beta\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt],

\displaystyle\hskip 34.1433pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\Lambda_{0}=0\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679pt\beta\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt],\hskip 42.67912pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\Lambda_{0}=0,\hskip 4.2679pt\beta=0,\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679ptw_{2}\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt].

(4.80)

We deduce in particular that
\alpha=0
,
\beta=0
,
w_{1}=0
, and if
\Lambda_{0}=0
, then
w_{2}=0
.

In addition, if
\Lambda_{0}\neq 0
, represented by the vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, according to Lemma
9
,
\partial\Omega
presents an helical symmetry, of axis
y+\text{Span}(z)
, with
w_{2}=-\Lambda_{0}y+pn
. In this case, since
\partial\Omega
is a sphere, the shift of the helical symmetry has to be zero, so that:

\displaystyle w_{2}=-\Lambda_{0}y.

(4.81)

Therefore, if
\partial\Omega
is a sphere, only the local Maxwellians of the form (
4.65
) can solve the Boltzmann equation with the boundary condition (
4.1
).

\partial\Omega
presents an helical symmetry, but is not a sphere nor a generalized cylinder.
Let us denote by
y+\text{Span}(n)
the axis of the helical symmetry of
\partial\Omega
, with
y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
n\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and let us denote by
2\pi p
the shift of this helical symmetry, with
p\in\mathbb{R}
. As for the previous case, the following cases lead to a direct contradiction:

\displaystyle\alpha\neq 0,\hskip 42.67912pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\alpha=0\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679ptw_{1}\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt],\hskip 42.67912pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\Lambda_{0}\neq 0\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679pt\beta\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt],

\displaystyle\hskip 34.1433pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\Lambda_{0}=0\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679pt\beta\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt],\hskip 42.67912pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\Lambda_{0}=0,\hskip 4.2679pt\beta=0,\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679ptw_{2}\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt].

(4.82)

We deduce then that
\alpha=0
,
\beta=0
,
w_{1}=0
, and if
\Lambda_{0}=0
and
\beta=0
, then
w_{2}=0
as well.

According to Lemma
11
, since
\partial\Omega
is not a plane (which is a particular case of a generalized cylinder), nor a cylinder of revolution (another particular case of a generalized cylinder), nor a sphere, we deduce that
\partial\Omega
presents a single helical symmetry. Therefore, the direction of
z
, representing
\Lambda_{0}
is fixed by
n
, and the component of
w_{2}
along
n
is fixed as well by the shift of the helical symmetry. More precisely, if
\Lambda_{0}\neq
, there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\neq 0
, such that:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}c=an\wedge x\hskip 5.69054pt\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(4.83)

and

\displaystyle w_{2}=-\Lambda_{0}y+apn=a\left(-n\wedge y+pn\right).

(4.84)

As a consequence, if
\partial\Omega
presents an helical symmetry, but is not a sphere nor a generalized cylinder, then only the local Maxwellians of the form (
4.67
) solve the Boltzmann equation with the specular reflection boundary condition.

\partial\Omega
is a generalized cylinder which does not present an helical symmetry.
Let us assume that the direction of boundary
\partial\Omega
, as a generalized cylinder, is given by a vector
n\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. In the present case, the following cases lead to a direct contradiction:

\displaystyle\alpha\neq 0,\hskip 34.1433pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\Lambda_{0}\neq 0\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679pt\beta\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt],\hskip 34.1433pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\Lambda_{0}\neq 0\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679pt\beta=0\hskip 2.84526pt],\hskip 34.1433pt[\hskip 2.84526pt\Lambda_{0}=0\hskip 4.2679pt\text{and}\hskip 4.2679pt\beta\neq 0\hskip 2.84526pt].

(4.85)

In particular, we deduce that
\alpha=0
,
\beta=0
and
\Lambda_{0}=0
. In addition, if
w_{1}
or
w_{2}
are not zero, then they have to be colinear to
n
. Hence the conclusion: only the local Maxwellians of the form (
4.67
) can be solutions of the Boltzmann equation with the boundary condition (
4.1
).

\partial\Omega
does not present an helical symmetry, and is not a generalized cylinder.
In this final case, if any of the parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\Lambda_{0}
,
w_{1}
or
w_{2}
is not zero, we obtain a contradiction. Therefore, only the parameter
\gamma
can be non-zero if a local Maxwellian
m
solves the Boltzmann equation with the specular reflection boundary condition in such a domain, hence (
4.68
).

All the cases were investigated, concluding the proof of Theorem
4
.
