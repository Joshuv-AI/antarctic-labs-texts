Comments on “Interlaminar Contralateral Endoscopic Foraminotomy Reduces Postoperative Dysesthesia in Patients With Degenerative Lumbar Deformity: A Multicenter Comparative Cohort Study”
Authors: Fatih Kaplan, Bilge Kurnaz Kaplan
Published: 2026

We read with interest the study by Van Isseldyk and colleagues comparing transforaminal endoscopic lumbar foraminotomy (TELF) with interlaminar contralateral endoscopic lumbar foraminotomy (ICELF) in patients with degenerative lumbar deformity. 1 The marked difference in postoperative dysesthesia (POD) is clinically provocative; however, two aspects warrant clarification before ICELF is interpreted as reducing POD while preserving equivalent functional efficacy. First, the stated primary-outcome definition and the reported event timing appear discordant. POD was defined as new-onset burning leg pain appearing 3 to 7 days after surgery, whereas POD onset in the TELF group was reported to range from 2 to 6 days. 1 Thus, at least one reported event appears to have begun outside the stated diagnostic window, unless the day-2 value is a reporting error or the operational definition differed from that described in the Methods. Because only nine POD events underpin the reported 17.4% versus 1.9% rates, odds ratio of 11.16, 89% relative risk reduction, and number needed to treat of 6.4, clarification of how the day-2 event was classified is important. We would therefore welcome confirmation of the operational POD definition and whether all reported effect estimates were calculated using that definition.

Second, the interpretation of functional outcomes appears stronger than the statistical analysis permits. The authors describe 12-month functional outcomes as “equivalent” and state that the reduction in dysesthesia does not come at the expense of decompressive efficacy. 1 However, the reported comparisons of VAS, ODI, and modified MacNab outcomes are conventional between-group tests of difference; no equivalence margin or equivalence/noninferiority analysis is specified. Moreover, the authors acknowledge that the study may be underpowered to detect differences in secondary outcomes. 1 Failure to detect a statistically significant difference is not evidence of equivalence, particularly when power is limited. 2 The data therefore support that no statistically significant between-group functional difference was detected, rather than that functional efficacy was equivalent. This distinction is clinically relevant because the conclusion extends to approach selection, suggesting that ICELF may be preferable across multiple dimensions. A more cautious interpretation would preserve the important observed association—substantially fewer recorded POD events in the ICELF cohort—while distinguishing it from evidence that functional efficacy is equivalent. Clarification of the POD case definition and moderation of the equivalence language would align the clinical inference more closely with the study design and analysis.
