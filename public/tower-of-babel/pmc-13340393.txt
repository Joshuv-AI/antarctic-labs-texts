Real-world medical and long-term care service utilization patterns among older adults with complex needs in China: a latent class analysis
Authors: Peng Zhang, Bifan Zhu, Yunwei Zhang, Jiajie Xu, Hansheng Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder adults with complex needs (CN), commonly defined as the coexistence of multiple chronic conditions and functional limitations, are associated with high levels of medical and long-term care (LTC) utilization. However, evidence on real-world patterns of joint medical and LTC service use among this population in China remains limited.MethodsThis cross-sectional study utilized data from 177,807 individuals aged ≥ 60 years who underwent LTC insurance assessment in Shanghai between January and May 2023. CN was defined as having three or more chronic conditions with at least one limitation in activities of daily living. Within an integrated care framework, latent class analysis (LCA) was applied to identify patterns of medical and LTC service utilization based on 10 indicators informed by the Andersen Behavioral Model of Health Services Use. Multinomial logistic regression was used to examine the association between CN status and class membership, adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors.ResultsOlder adults with CN (n = 42,277) differed significantly from those without CN (n = 135,530) across demographic, socioeconomic, health status, and service utilization. Six latent classes of medical and LTC service utilization were identified: Low Medical & Low Care, Moderate Medical & Low Care, High Medical & All Care, High Medical & Informal Care, High Medical & Formal Care, and High Inpatient & Formal Care. Compared to non-CN individuals, CN individuals had higher probabilities of belonging to high-utilization classes, particularly High Medical & All Care, High Medical & Informal Care, and High Medical & Formal Care classes, with the Low Medical & Low Care Class as the reference. These associations remained significant after adjusting for covariates.ConclusionsOlder adults with CN in China showed heterogeneity in patterns of medical and LTC service utilization and were more frequently represented in intensive and multi-sector service use profiles. Early identification of CN individuals and the development of risk-stratified integrated care models may help inform more coordinated and people-centered service delivery approaches.

Introduction

Complex needs (CN) status is commonly characterized by the coexistence of multimorbidity with three or more chronic conditions and any functional limitations, particularly among older adults [1–6]. Specifically, in the U.S., older adults constitute the predominant CN subgroup, with approximately 81% are aged ≥ 50 years and 55% aged ≥ 60 years among the CN population [7]. In China, nationally representative data indicate that over 50% of individuals aged 60 years and above experience multimorbidity accompanied by varying levels of functional limitations, highlighting their high risk of CN [8–10]. CN individuals pose substantial challenges to service delivery systems due to disproportionately high utilization of health and long-term care (LTC) services, significant financial burdens, and worse health outcomes [1–3], though they comprise only about 5% of the population in high-income countries [5, 7, 11]. In nationally representative data from the United States, adults with CN in the general population had approximately 2.7-fold higher outpatient visits, 3.4-fold higher emergency department visits, and 5-fold higher inpatient admissions compared with adults without CN [7]. Meanwhile, CN individuals largely overlap with high-need, high-cost populations (the top 5% of healthcare spenders), which account for approximately 40–60% of total healthcare expenditures in OECD countries [7, 11–13].

From a theoretical perspective, the Andersen Behavioral Model of Health Services Use gives explanations by considering multimorbidity and functional limitations as key need factors, which have an influence on both the intensity and type of service utilization [14]. Specifically, multimorbidity increases treatment complexity and the need for continuous medical monitoring [15], whereas functional limitations generate long-term care dependency that extends beyond episodic medical treatment [16, 17]. When these conditions coexist, individuals may simultaneously require intensive medical management and sustained supportive care, and this dual burden has been empirically shown to result in greater-than-additive increases in emergency department visits, outpatient visits, and hospitalizations compared with either condition alone [4, 18, 19]. In China, no prior studies have integrated multimorbidity and functional limitations to directly identify CN individuals. For individuals with multiple chronic conditions, studies show that they exhibit higher levels of outpatient visits, hospital admissions, healthcare expenditures, and medication use [20–22], as well as higher levels of the LTC service needs and utilization [16, 23]. For individuals with limitations in daily activities, they also increased the demand and utilization of medical services [24, 25] and LTC services [26–28]. Consequently, there is room for jointly identifying the CN older adults and exploring the patterns of medical and LTC service utilization among them.

Traditional applications of the Andersen model have predominantly focused on single-sector outcomes, offering limited insight into how individuals with complex needs navigate multiple care systems simultaneously. To address this, the SELFIE framework (Sustainable intEgrated chronic care modeLs for multi-morbidity: delivery, FInancing, and performancE) emphasizes aligning medical services and LTC services around the individual’s needs across the life course and providing integrated care across service sectors, rather than fragmented, single-system responses [3, 29–31]. This framework provides the conceptual foundation for examining medical and LTC services as a unified pattern of service utilization. International reforms have placed people-centered integrated services at the core for older adults with CN, aiming to establish interdisciplinary teams and provide transitional, integrated care that links healthcare institutions with social resources [32] and to extend beyond single-disease treatment or isolated long-term care provision [1, 33]. Empirically, however, evidence remains limited on how older adults with CN utilize healthcare and LTC services in combination, especially in non-Western settings. Previous studies mainly focused solely on medical service utilization among CN populations [2, 6, 33, 34], and a few have investigated LTC needs and utilization within CN subgroups either with multimorbidity or functional limitations [35, 36], lacking direct evidence that jointly examines healthcare and LTC utilization patterns among CN older adults.

Against the backdrop of rapid aging, China has expanded its LTC service system since the launch of the Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI) pilot in 2016 [37]. Population-based studies have explored determinants of LTC use, showing strong associations with disability severity, multimorbidity, and socioeconomic factors [16, 23, 27, 28, 37]. Recent work has increasingly emphasized the need for better integration between medical care and LTC services due to the fragmented delivery structure between healthcare and social care sectors in China [37–39], highlighting the importance of providing a comprehensive depiction of the CN population and exploring their service utilization patterns [40].

This study conceptualizes CN as a key need factor consistent with Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Health Services Use [14], and then examines medical and long-term care services as a unified pattern of utilization from an integrated care perspective informed by the SELFIE framework [29]. Within this theoretical framework, three specific objectives were pursued. First, to describe and compare the demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related characteristics of older adults with and without CN in China, reflecting differences in predisposing, enabling, and need factors. Second, to identify real-world patterns of combined medical and LTC service utilization using latent class analysis (LCA), which allows for the identification of heterogeneous and unobserved utilization profiles across multiple service sectors [41, 42]. Third, to examine the association between CN status and these utilization patterns after adjusting for relevant covariates. By doing so, this study provides empirical evidence relevant to the development of coordinated, people-centered care strategies for older adults with CN in China.

Method

Study design and data source

This study was a population-based observational study using administrative assessment data derived from the LTCI system in Shanghai, China. Shanghai LTCI, administered by the Shanghai Healthcare Security Bureau, has covered all basic medical insurance enrollees aged 60 years and above since its implementation. As part of the LTCI eligibility determination process, all applicants are mandatorily assessed using a standardized official questionnaire, the Unified Needs Assessment Form for Elderly Care Questionnaire (with its development and validation process described in detail in former studies [43–45]). This study used anonymized data derived from a cross-sectional face-to-face investigation from January to June 2023 in this routine assessment process, and its secondary analysis was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Health Development Research Center (Approval Number: 2020001) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

The study included older adults aged 60 years and above who applied for LTCI during the study period and completed the standardized LTCI needs assessment. Application for LTCI benefits is voluntary; however, once an application is submitted, participation in the assessment is mandatory and not subject to purposive selection by assessors or researchers. This administrative-based design reduces intentional selection bias within the assessed population. Inclusion criteria were: (1) applied for LTCI and completed the assessment process during the study period; (2) older adults aged 60 and above; and (3) provided valid responses to related questions with clear consciousness or assistance, and the written informed consent was obtained.

A total of 179,141 records were initially extracted from the administrative LTC insurance assessment database. Restricting the sample to assessments conducted between January and May 2023 led to the exclusion of 1,330 records outside the predefined study period, leaving 177,811 records. 4 individuals didn’t meet the age eligibility criterion (< 60 years), and all included participants had completed informed consent procedures and were able to provide assessment information. After data cleaning and verification of key analytic variables, the final analytic sample included 177,807 older adults.

Assessment procedure and data collection

The assessments were conducted by trained personnel officially appointed by the Shanghai Healthcare Security Bureau, including healthcare professionals (primarily general practitioners familiar with the applicant’s health status) and certified social insurance assessors. All assessors completed standardized training on questionnaire administration and functional assessment prior to data collection to ensure consistency and accuracy across assessments. Assessments were conducted face-to-face in the participants’ homes using a standardized electronic system, based on direct observation, structured interviews with participants and caregivers when appropriate, and review of available medical information. All data were reviewed and verified by qualified professionals prior to upload into a centralized database. Completion of all core items required for eligibility determination was mandatory for finalizing the assessment, resulting in minimal item non-response due to the structured, assessor-administered design. This standardized process was implemented to ensure consistency and accuracy across assessments.

Measures

Complex needs of the study population

CN status was operationalized as a binary construct based primarily on conceptual and clinical considerations and distributional properties. Before defining CN status, the distributions of chronic condition counts and ADL limitation measures were examined, both of which demonstrated right-skewed distributions (Table 1). Identification of CN in this study followed a three-step approach.

First, chronic conditions were assessed via self-reported physician diagnoses collected during the survey. Trained interviewers recorded all reported diagnoses, and a list of chronic diseases was developed by intersecting two authoritative sources (in Table S1) [31, 33]. Based on this list, individuals were classified as having multimorbidity if they reported three or more chronic conditions, as the widely used threshold to identify CN [1–5].

Second, functional limitations were measured using questionnaire items adapted from the Barthel Index, a validated tool covering all ADL domains, including bowel and bladder control, grooming, toileting, feeding, transfers, mobility, dressing, stair climbing, and bathing [46]. Certain domains, such as dressing and toileting, were operationalized with more detailed items to capture long-term care needs more precisely. Scores were categorized as Normal (≥ 20.0), Mild (12.1–19.9), Moderate (8.1–12.0), and Severe (0–8.0) [47].

Finally, participants meeting both the multimorbidity and ADL limitation criteria were classified as CN, and the remaining individuals were designated as non-CN.

Medical and long-term care service utilization and the LCA indicators

Building on the principles of integrated people-centered care [3, 29–31], medical and LTC services were conceptualized as an integrated service pattern, with the following primary outcome variables as the basis for the LCA.

Medical services were classified into outpatient and inpatient care following Andersen’s Behavioral Model to reflect the pathways and intensities of service utilization in response to health-related needs [14]. Respondents reported the number of outpatient visits, hospital admissions, and total hospital days in the past 12 months. From these data, four sets of indicators were derived: service occurrence (any outpatient or inpatient use), utilization frequency (counts of visits/admissions), high-intensity use (binary, frequency above the sample median), and prolonged hospitalization (binary, total hospital days above the median). The median was chosen as a robust threshold to minimize skewness effects, and values above the 99th percentile were truncated, preserving their relative ranking to minimize the influence of extreme values and produce reasonably sized groups for pattern identification.

LTC services were categorized into informal and formal care [48, 49], based on prior studies applying Andersen’s model to LTC [50–52]. Participants reported the average monthly frequency of informal care and the frequency and cost of formal care over the preceding three months, extrapolated to annual values. Five sets of indicators were derived: care occurrence (any informal/formal care), care frequency (counts of episodes), high-intensity care (frequency above median), formal care expenditure (annual cost in USD, converted from CNY using 2024 rate 1 USD = 7.1217 CNY), and high-cost formal care (annual expenditure above median).

In total, ten LCA indicators captured the occurrence, intensity, and cost of service utilization: medical indicators—outpatient visit, intensive outpatient, inpatient visit, intensive inpatient, prolonged hospitalization; LTC indicators—informal care, intensive informal care, formal care, intensive formal care, high-cost formal care.

Other related factors

Both descriptive and model-based analyses accounted for a comprehensive set of covariates spanning demographic, socioeconomic, and health domains. Demographic variables included age (60–74, 75–89, ≥ 90 years), sex (male/female), and ethnicity (Han vs. other minorities). Socioeconomic variables comprised marital status (married vs. unmarried), educational attainment (illiterate, primary, secondary, university, other), primary source of income (yes/no), and living arrangement (home, nursing home, or healthcare institution). Household co-residence type, as a structural indicator of living arrangement and potential availability of informal care, was operationalized based on the types of cohabiting persons, including spouse/partner, children, grandchildren, and professional caregivers, which was further grouped into four levels: none, one type, two types, and three or more types of co-residence [53, 54]. Health status was assessed via self-rated health, categorized as very good, good, moderate, poor, or very poor.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed to summarize and compare the characteristics of the overall sample, the CN group, and the non-CN group. Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for continuous or ordinal variables to assess group differences.

Then, Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was applied, as a model-based clustering method to exploit conditional independence across multiple observed indicators and identify unobserved subgroups [55], which is particularly well-suited for capturing the heterogeneous characteristics or behaviors in the healthcare area [41, 42]. Using Mplus software [56], models with one to seven classes were estimated based on the ten LCA indicators and were evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (ABIC), Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRT), bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and entropy [57]. To assess the robustness of the association of CN status and latent classes, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a reduced set of six indicators: outpatient visits, inpatient visits, inpatient expenditure, informal care episodes, formal care episodes, and formal care expenditures with quartile-based thresholds to test the impact of cut-off selection on class assignment.

Finally, multinomial logistic regression examined the association between CN status and latent classes, adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related covariates [58]. Analyses were performed in Stata 15.0.

Comparison of Characteristics Between Older Adults with and without Complex Needs

Significant differences were observed between the two groups across most characteristics. Compared with non-CN individuals, older adults with CN were more likely to be aged 75–89 years and less likely to be aged 90 years or above. The proportions of males and Han Chinese were higher in the CN group. Regarding socioeconomic characteristics, CN individuals were less likely to be illiterate and more likely to be married and to report having a fixed income. Although most participants in both groups lived at home, a slightly higher proportion of CN individuals resided in nursing homes. CN individuals also reported a more complex household co-residence type, with fewer living alone.

In terms of health status, CN individuals exhibited substantially worse functional status, with 43.0% experiencing severe ADL limitations. All CN individuals had three or more chronic conditions: 60.2% had exactly three, and nearly 40% had four or more. In contrast, 94.80% of non-CN participants had fewer than three chronic conditions. The mean number of chronic diseases was significantly higher in the CN group than in the non-CN group (3.50 vs. 1.25). CN individuals also reported poorer self-rated health, with 63.6% and 15.3% rating their health as “poor” or “very poor,” compared with 54.6% and 8.5% among non-CN individuals.

Regarding medical service utilization, CN individuals were more likely to have used outpatient services in the past year (64.4% vs. 55.3%) and had a higher mean number of visits (6.79 vs. 4.99). They were also more likely to experience hospitalizations (39.1% vs. 29.0%), with higher mean numbers of admissions (0.57 vs. 0.40) and longer average lengths of stay (5.25 vs. 4.94 days).

With respect to long-term care (LTC) services, CN individuals were more likely to receive both informal and formal care. They reported higher mean numbers of informal and formal care episodes (251.88 and 135.98, respectively) compared with non-CN individuals. In addition, the average annual expenditure on formal care was higher among CN individuals ($1,405.42 vs. $1,182.98).

Latent class analysis of medical and ltc service utilization in older adults

As shown in Table 2, to determine the optimal number of latent classes, models were fitted and compared using fit indices. The AIC, BIC, and ABIC values consistently decreased when comparing models from two to six classes, and the LMRT and BLRT p-values were statistically significant (p < 0.001), suggesting that each additional class significantly improved model fit. The LMRT and BLRT became non-significant for the seven-class model, implying that the seventh class did not contribute meaningfully to the model. Based on the model fit statistics, statistical tests, and interpretability, the six-class solution was identified as the most appropriate model for subsequent analysis. Although entropy values approached 1.000, this should be interpreted as reflecting strong indicator separation rather than perfect individual-level classification, as evidenced by the distribution of posterior class probabilities (in Table S4). The mean posterior probability for the assigned class exceeded 0.90 across all latent classes, while probabilities for non-assigned classes remained substantially lower, supporting the interpretation that the identified latent classes reflected robust and substantively distinct service utilization patterns.

The Multinomial Logistic Regression of the Association between CN and Latent Classes

After adjustment for covariates, CN status was significantly associated with different latent classes. Compared with non-CN individuals, older adults with CN had higher relative likelihoods of being classified into the High Medical & All Care (RRR = 1.46), High Medical & Informal Care (RRR = 1.13), and High Medical & Formal Care (RRR = 1.16) classes, whereas non-CN individuals were more likely to belong to the High Inpatient & Formal Care class (RRR = 0.84).

Several sociodemographic and health-related factors were also associated with latent class membership. Advanced age of 75–89 years old, female sex, and non-Han ethnicity were associated with higher probabilities of being in five higher-utilization classes. Those aged 90 + were more likely to be in Class 5 and 6 with more formal care and less likely to be in Class 4 with more informal care. Higher educational attainment and being married were associated with higher likelihoods of membership across all higher-utilization classes, whereas having a fixed income was associated with lower likelihoods of membership relative to Class 1. Residence in nursing homes was correlated with classes characterized by formal care use, including the High Medical & All Care Class, the High Medical & Formal Care Class, and the High Inpatient & Formal Care Class, while residence in healthcare institutions was not significantly associated with these. Compared to individuals living alone, those with co-residence support showed lower likelihoods of membership in higher-utilization classes. Poor or very poor self-rated health was consistently associated with a higher likelihood of belonging to classes 2 to 6, compared to those with very good health.

Sensitivity analyses using the six-indicator LCA and alternative quartile-based thresholds produced three class structures and similar distributions (in Table S2-S3 and Figure S1). These findings indicated that the identification of latent classes and their association with CN status are robust to variations in indicator selection and cut-off definitions.

Discussion

This study compared characteristics of CN and non-CN groups among 177,807 older adults and identified six real-world patterns of combined medical and LTC service utilization using LCA. Older adults with CN were more frequently clustered in profiles characterized by intensive and diversified use of medical services together with informal and/or formal care, and these patterns remained significantly associated with CN status in regression analyses. The findings indicated substantial heterogeneity in joint service utilization and showed that CN status was associated with a higher likelihood of membership in several high-utilization service patterns.

From a theoretical perspective, these findings can be interpreted through the Andersen Behavioral Model and the SELFIE framework. Within Andersen’s model, CN status represents cumulative need complexity arising from the coexistence of multimorbidity and functional limitations. This cumulative need burden may increase both the frequency of healthcare contacts and the range of services required across medical and LTC systems. This mechanism helps explain why older adults with CN were distributed across more service-intensive classes characterized by High Medical utilization combined with varying levels of LTC utilization. These observations are consistent with prior studies showing that CN populations tend to have higher resource consumption, higher healthcare expenditure, and greater service utilization [1, 7, 11–13]. The concentration of CN individuals in multi-sector and high-intensity utilization classes, therefore aligns with a needs-driven pathway of service use rather than isolated disease-specific care seeking. Conversely, the non‑CN individuals were more likely to fall into the Low Medical & Low Care class, and had slightly higher proportions in classes 2 and 5 than the CN group. This suggests that non‑CN individuals occasionally experienced episodes of high medical service utilization, which tended to be less severe and more transient [2], often manageable through self‑care or short-term formal care [24].

The SELFIE framework further helps contextualize how individuals with CN navigate healthcare and social care systems simultaneously. The identified utilization patterns are consistent with the framework’s emphasis on coordinated, multi-sector service engagement among individuals with CN [3, 27–29]. Rather than substituting for one another, this study conceptualizes medical services and LTC services as interconnected components of a unified service utilization pattern rather than as independent domains [29]. The six latent classes identified in this study reflect substantial heterogeneity in how older adults combine medical and care services, ranging from low overall utilization to intensive and diversified use of both healthcare and LTC. International studies identified subtypes of the characteristics among the high-need population [13, 34, 50], but very few have jointly examined their utilization of both medical and care services. By integrating both domains, our analysis captured probabilistic patterns that summarize relative tendencies in service use and underscore the importance of moving beyond a single-service dimension. It provides new evidence on the real-world patterns of combined medical and LTC service utilization, rather than care preferences and needs for discrete or exclusive services in earlier Chinese studies [23, 24, 26].

The observed associations between CN status and latent classes can also be interpreted within Andersen’s Model, which posits that healthcare utilization is shaped by the interaction of predisposing, enabling, and need factors, with need factors being the most proximal drivers of service use [50–52]. Consistently, CN status remained associated with high-utilization patterns after adjustment. Other factors, including older age (75–89 years), female sex, minority ethnicity, lower income stability, living alone, and poorer self-rated health, were also associated with higher service utilization patterns [21, 36, 48, 51, 59]. These findings suggest that older adults with similar clinical complexity may still interact differently with healthcare and LTC systems depending on broader contextual factors.

This study highlights that older adults with CN constituted a higher-risk population, exhibiting marked differences from the non-CN across demographic, socioeconomic, and health status [1, 2, 12]. In line with prior studies, these findings demonstrated that the co-occurrence of chronic conditions and functional impairments is associated with more complex and resource-intensive service use [1, 7, 11–13], as well as substantially poorer self-rated health [2]. Building on prior Chinese research that examined multimorbidity or functional limitations separately [23, 26, 40], this study extends existing evidence by considering CN as their combined presence, which may support earlier identification of populations with elevated service needs [1, 2, 33].

In the sensitivity analysis, the identified association of CN and the patterns of medical and LTC service utilization was robust. The LCA in this study was initially based on ten indicators capturing the frequency, intensity, and cost of medical and LTC service utilization [18, 28, 60]. The sensitivity analyses using a reduced set of six indicators and a quartile-based threshold was conducted, which remained highly consistent with the primary result. The results indicated that the identification of distinct service utilization patterns is robust to both indicator selection and threshold definitions. Among these indicators, inpatient care showed a greater function to further distinguish the subtypes of medical utilization, as it better reflects disparities in health status and CN [24], and a complementary rather than substitutive relationship between informal versus formal care was indicated [49, 50]. A positive alignment between high-frequency hospitalizations and high-frequency and high-cost formal care utilization was also observed [35].

This study has several important policy implications. First, early identification of CN populations may support more targeted resource allocation and stratified integrated care planning [50], including the use of comprehensive geriatric assessment and multidisciplinary care models aligned with integrated care principles [1, 29]. Second, the observed association between CN status and intensive medical and LTC utilization highlights the importance of strengthening coordination between healthcare and social care sectors [1, 2, 32]. Care models may need to be tailored according to need profiles, with some CN individuals potentially benefiting from integrated medical-nursing facility models, while others may be supported through enhanced home- and community-based services [50].

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and associations between CN status and service utilization should be interpreted cautiously. Second, this study relied on secondary administrative data, which limited the precision of measures such as ADL items and the accuracy of self-reported disease status. Future research using primary data and validated measurement tools may help validate these findings. Third, although latent class analysis relied on ten carefully selected indicators with sensitivity analyses confirming robustness, the choice of indicators and thresholds may influence the classification of subgroups. Finally, although the study utilized the full sample collected in Shanghai, the unmeasured factors, such as regional healthcare accessibility, may influence observed service use patterns and limit generalizability.

Conclusion

This study provides empirical evidence on the heterogeneity of combined medical and long-term care service utilization among older adults with and without CN in China. Using latent class analysis in a large representative sample, six distinct patterns of service use were identified, with CN status associated with higher probabilities of membership in high-intensity utilization classes. These findings suggest a heavy service burden among older adults with CN and may inform the development of early identification approaches, risk stratification strategies, and more coordinated models of medical and long-term care delivery.
