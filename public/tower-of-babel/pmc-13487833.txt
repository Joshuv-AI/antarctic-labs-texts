Evaluating the probiotic potential of Bacillus pumilus HFS2 isolated from the gut microbiota of Heteropneustes fossilis for aquaculture applications
Authors: Md. Sakhawat Hossain, Md Imtiaz Ahamed, Ashoka Paul Sudipta, Ummul Muhosnin, Mohammad Matiur Rahman, Md. Rafiqul Islam Sarder, Muhammad Tofazzal Hossain, A. K. Shakur Ahammad, Mariom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProbiotics are increasingly used in aquaculture to enhance fish survival, growth, and health, offering a sustainable approach to fish farming.ObjectiveThis study assessed the probiotic properties of Bacillus pumilus HFS2, isolated from the digestive tract of Heteropneustes fossilis.MethodAmong fourteen isolates, HFS2 was characterized using physiological, biochemical, and 16S rRNA analyses, and it's in vitro probiotic properties as well as in vivo safety were evaluated.ResultsThe isolate was Gram-positive, rod-shaped, motile, and aerobic, showing positive results for methyl red, catalase and Voges–Proskauer tests, and a negative result for the indole test. It fermented dextrose, lactose, maltose, and mannitol with acid production but did not ferment sucrose. In vitro probiotic assessments showed that the strain tolerated a temperature range of 4–35 °C, survived at pH 2–5, and tolerated bile concentrations of 2.5–7.5%. The isolate demonstrated promising adhesion-related properties, including autoaggregation (66%), cell surface hydrophobicity (69% with xylene and 68% with toluene), and coaggregation with Lactococcus lactis (53%) and Staphylococcus arlettae (49%). It also exhibited inhibitory activity against fish pathogens such as Lactococcus garvieae, Aeromonas caviae, A. jandaei, A. veronii, and A. hydrophila. The isolate exhibited γ-hemolytic activity, strong biofilm-forming ability, and susceptibility to commonly used antibiotics, including ampicillin, vancomycin, erythromycin, tetracycline, chloramphenicol, and ciprofloxacin, while resistance observed only against cefotaxime. In vivo safety evaluation through intraperitoneal injection and immersion challenge at 108 CFU/mL indicated no pathogenic effects in H. fossilis.ConclusionThese results suggest that B. pumilus HFS2 is a resilient and promising potential probiotic candidate for aquaculture applications.

Introduction

Heteropneustes fossilis (Stinging catfish) is a nutrient-rich and economically significant freshwater fish in widely distributed across Southeast Asia, with a notable presence in Bangladesh. It is rich in iron and calcium and is well adapted to low-oxygen environments, making it suitable for intensive aquaculture.1, 2 Although natural populations have declined due to habitat degradation and environmental stress,3 its farming has expanded in ponds and biofloc systems. According to the statistics of the Department of Fisheries,4 it contributed 0.113 MMT (2.57%) to Bangladesh's inland fish production and is currently listed as “Least Concern” by the International Union for Conservation of Nature.5

Aquaculture is growing rapidly on a global scale and serves as a key contributor to fulfilling the rising demand for animal protein.6 However, as aquaculture systems have become more intensive, infectious diseases have become more prevalent, leading to massive financial losses.7 Although antibiotics have traditionally been used to control disease outbreaks, their excessive and indiscriminate application has contributed to the development of antibiotic-resistance, environmental contamination, and potential public health concerns.8, 9, 10, 11 These challenges highlight the urgent need for sustainable and environmentally friendly alternatives, such as probiotics, for effective disease prevention and health management in aquaculture.12, 13, 14, 15

Probiotics are live microorganisms that provide health benefits to the host when consumed in adequate amounts.16 Effective probiotic strains must be genetically characterized, non-pathogenic, and capable of surviving processing, storage, and gastrointestinal conditions.17 In aquaculture, probiotics enhance growth performance, improve feed efficiency, modulate immune responses, and inhibit pathogen colonization, thereby serving as promising alternatives for disease prevention.18 Notably, host-derived (autochthonous) strains exhibit superior intestinal adhesion and functional efficacy compared to non-native strains.19, 20, 21, 22

Members of the genus Bacillus are widely utilized as probiotics and biocontrol agents in both human and animal systems.23, 24 These bacteria can serve as effective probiotic candidates, as they exhibit key attributes such as safety, functional efficacy, and technological suitability.25 In aquaculture, Bacillus spp. confer multiple advantages, like enhanced growth performance, improved feed utilization efficiency, stress mitigation, and water quality management through the degradation of organic matter and nitrogenous wastes.26, 27, 28 Furthermore, Bacillus spp. are recognized for their ability to produce a wide range of antimicrobial compounds, including subtilin, bacillin coagulin, mycosubtilin, bacillomycin, megacin, and toximycin which contribute to the inhibition of pathogenic microorganisms, thereby enhancing disease resistance and modulating host immune responses.29, 30 Several non-pathogenic species, including B. coagulans, B. pumilus, B. clausii, B. subtilis, B. cereus, and B. licheniformis, are widely used in biotechnology and food fermentation due to their antioxidant and antimicrobial activities.31, 32 The expanding global demand for probiotic-based interventions has stimulated ongoing research to isolate and characterize novel Bacillus strains possessing enhanced functional traits and host-specific efficacy for sustainable aquaculture.

Research on host-derived probiotics for H. fossilis is still relatively scarce. Previous studies, however, have reported several probiotic candidates from this species. In our recent study, Lactococcus lactis HFL8 was successfully isolated from the gut of H. fossilis.33 Another study34 identified multiple Bacillus strains, including B. safensis (HF8), B. altitudinis (HF10), B. pacificus (HF19), and B. cereus (HF20 and HF21). Similarly,35 documented the occurrence of diverse bacterial species such as B. subtilis, Staphylococcus succinus, Macrococcus caseolyticus, Glutamicibacter mysorens, and B. cereus from the gut microbiota of the same host.

To address this gap, we screened and characterized intestinal bacteria from healthy H. fossilis to identify strains having probiotic potential. Among 14 isolates, a strain of Bacillus pumilus (HFS2) was identified and confirmed through physiological, biochemical, and molecular analyses. Its probiotic potential was further validated using in vitro and in vivo assessments. This study thus provides promising candidate strains for the development and application of probiotics in H. fossilis aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Experimental fish procurement and intestinal microbiota isolation

The experimental procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1. Healthy H. fossilis specimens (average weight: 20 ± 0.08 g; average length: 15.8 ± 0.5 cm) were obtained from a local fish market near Bangladesh Agricultural University, Mymensingh. Seven fish (n = 7) were selected and starved for 24 h to allow complete gut clearance. The fish were then anesthetized with 60 mg/L MS-22236 and their external surfaces were disinfected using 75% ethanol to reduce external microbial contamination. Under sterile conditions, the midgut (including its contents) was aseptically removed and serially diluted in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). A volume of 0.1 mL from each dilution was plated onto nutrient agar (NA) and incubated at 28 °C for 24–72 h. The midgut was specifically targeted for bacterial isolation because it harbors a higher microbial load than the foregut and hindgut.37 After incubation, individual colonies were isolated and purified by repeated streaking on NA plates until axenic cultures were achieved. The purified strains were then preserved in nutrient broth (NB) supplemented with 25% (v/v) glycerol at −20 °C for subsequent analyses.Fig. 1Overview of the experimental procedures.Fig. 1

Preliminary screening of probiotic candidates

Hemolytic assay

Hemolytic assay of the bacterial isolates was assessed following the method described by38 and the strains were categorized as alpha, beta, or gamma hemolytic according to their hemolytic patterns.39

Evaluation of inhibitory activity of bacterial isolates

The antagonistic activity of the bacterial isolates against selected fish pathogens, Lactococcus garvieae strain HFL1 (PQ870415), Lactococcus formosensis strain HFL6 (PQ870420), Aeromonas caviae strain HFS3 (PQ870425), Aeromonas jandaei strain HFS7 (PQ870429), Aeromonas veronii strain HFS8 (PQ870430), and Aeromonas hydrophila strain HFS9 (PQ870431) was determined using the agar well diffusion technique.40 The antimicrobial assay was performed using the cell-free supernatant.

Morphological profiling of the bacterial isolates

The morphological characteristics of the bacterial isolates were examined following the established methodology. Gram staining, oxygen requirements, and motility were assessed, and colony features including shape, size, elevation, margin, and color were recorded.

Biochemical profiling of the bacterial isolates

Catalase activity, methyl red, indole production, and Voges–Proskauer (VP) tests, along with carbohydrate fermentation profiles, were performed in accordance with the protocols described in Bergey's Manual of Systematic Bacteriology.41

Molecular identification of the probiotic bacteria

The bacterial isolates were molecularly identified by genomic DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from freshly cultured bacterial strains using the phenol–chloroform method.42 The 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) as the forward primer and 1492R (5′-TACGGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) as the reverse primer.43 Following gel purification, the PCR products were sequenced (Wuhan Tianyi Huayu Gene Technology Co., Ltd., China). The obtained sequences were analyzed using BioEdit software (Version 7.2). Identification of bacterial genus and species was carried out by comparing the sequences with those in the NCBI database using the BLAST tool. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using MEGA software (Version 12.1) with the neighbor-joining method. Bootstrap values, calculated from 1,000 replicates, are shown alongside the branches and represent the number of base substitutions per site.44

In vitro analysis of probiotic efficacy

Assessment of auto- and coaggregation abilities

The autoaggregation and coaggregation abilities of the test strains were assessed following the methods of,45, 46 respectively. For coaggregation assays, Lactococcus lactis HFL8 (PQ870422) and Staphylococcus arlettae HFL5 (PQ870419), previously isolated from the gut of H. fossilis, were used. Assessment of biofilm formation

Biofilm formation was assessed following the method.48 Black, dry, crystalline colonies indicated biofilm formation, while red colonies represented non-biofilm-forming strains.49

Resistance of probiotic isolates to varying pH levels

Resistance to low gastric pH was evaluated following33 to determine the suitability of the probiotic strains for oral application. Briefly, bacterial isolates were inoculated into nutrient broth adjusted to different pH levels (2, 3, and 5) using HCl or NaOH. After incubation at 37 °C for 3, 6, 9, and 24 h, the optical density (OD) of each culture was measured at 600 nm to assess bacterial survival and growth.

Resistance of probiotic isolates to gastric bile

Bile salt tolerance was assessed to evaluate the ability of the probiotic strains to survive under intestinal bile conditions, following the method described by.50 Briefly, pure bacterial cultures were inoculated into nutrient broth containing crude bile at 2.5%, 5.0%, and 7.5%. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C, and growth was monitored by measuring OD at 600 nm after 3, 6, 9, and 24 h to assess bile tolerance.

Assessment of salt and temperature tolerance

Temperature tolerance (4 °C, 15 °C, 25 °C, 30 °C, and 35 °C) and salt tolerance (1%, 2%, 4%, 6%, and 8% NaCl) of the isolates were evaluated following the method described by.23

Antibiotic resistance profile of the isolate

The antibiotic resistance profile of the identified isolates was evaluated using the disc diffusion method51 against commonly applied antibiotics: Vancomycin (Glycopeptide, 30 μg), Ampicillin (Penicillin, 10 μg), Erythromycin (Macrolide, 15 μg), Tetracycline (Tetracycline, 30 μg), Chloramphenicol (Protein synthesis inhibitor, 30 μg), Cefotaxime (Cephalosporin, 30 μg), and Ciprofloxacin (Fluoroquinolone, 5 μg). Antibiotic discs were procured from Thermo Scientific™ Oxoid™ (Oxoid Limited, UK) and supplied locally via Ubuy Bangladesh.

Evaluation of in vivo safety of the identified probiotic strain

Formulation and standardization of B. pumilus HFS2 probiotic suspension

To obtain the required cell density, a pure culture of B. pumilus HFS2 was inoculated into 100 mL of nutrient broth and incubated for 24 h. The culture was then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 2 min, and the resulting pellet was washed three times with 0.87% PBS to remove residual medium. Finally, the cells were resuspended in 0.87% PBS and adjusted to a concentration of 1.0 × 108 CFU/mL using a McFarland densitometer (DEN-1, Grant-bio, England) for use in the challenge experiment.

Intraperitoneal injection of B. pumilus HFS2

A total of 180 stinging catfish (H. fossilis) fingerlings (mean length: 12.53 ± 1.19 cm; mean weight: 13.28 ± 0.18 g) were obtained from Sabir Agro Farm, Mymensingh, Bangladesh. Fish were randomly distributed into six 80 L aquaria (30 fish per aquarium), comprising one control and one treatment group with three replicates each. Prior to the trial, fish were acclimatized for one week and fed twice daily (07:00 and 15:00 h). In the treatment group, fish received a single intraperitoneal injection of B. pumilus HFS2 (100 μL per fish; 1.0 × 108 CFU/mL), equivalent to approximately 1.0 × 107 CFU/fish, while control fish were injected with 100 μL sterile PBS. Post-injection, fish were monitored daily for 14 days to record clinical signs and mortality. No histopathological examinations of liver or intestinal tissues were performed in the present study; however, no visible behavioral abnormalities or external lesions were observed during the observation period.

Immersion challenge with B. pumilus HFS2

In a separate pathogenicity assessment, 180 fingerlings of H. fossilis were randomly distributed into control and treatment groups, each in triplicate across six 80 L glass aquaria (30 fish per tank). The treatment group was exposed to B. pumilus HFS2 via immersion at a final concentration of 1.0 × 108 CFU/mL, obtained by directly introducing the bacterial suspension into the aquarium water, corresponding to an estimated exposure of ∼108 CFU/fish based on tank volume and stocking density. The control group received sterile PBS at an equivalent volume. The in vivo safety evaluation was conducted at this single concentration, which was selected based on commonly used probiotic dosages in aquaculture studies.52, 53 Fish were monitored daily for 30 days post-exposure for clinical signs and mortality, while maintaining water quality through regular removal of uneaten feed and waste. No histopathological examination of liver or intestinal tissues was performed in the present study; however, no visible external or behavioral abnormalities were observed during the observation period.

In both the immersion and intraperitoneal challenge trials, fish survival was recorded daily throughout the experimental period.

Statistical evaluation of data

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (Version 2024) and OriginPro (Version 2025b; 10.25). Differences in probiotic potential were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD post hoc test. All assays were performed in triplicate, with each experiment including three independent biological replicates and technical replicates as appropriate. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Prior to analysis, the normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene's test to confirm the suitability of parametric analysis before applying one-way ANOVA.

Physiological and biochemical profiling of bacterial isolates

Among the tested isolates, HFS2 exhibited non-hemolytic activity and the highest antibacterial activity against the tested fish pathogens. Therefore, this isolate was selected for further physiological, biochemical, and molecular identification. The colony morphology of HFS2 were cream-colored, opaque, and medium sized, with circular to slightly irregular shape, entire to undulate margin, and a dry, smooth to slightly rough surface. The bacterial isolate was characterized as Gram-positive, rod-shaped cells occurring singly or in short chains (Table 2). The bacterium was motile and exhibited strictly aerobic growth requirements (Table 2). Biochemical tests indicated positive reactions for catalase, methyl red (MR), and Voges–Proskauer (VP), while the indole test was negative (Table 2). In vitro analysis of probiotic efficacy

Assessment of cell surface hydrophobicity, auto- and coaggregation abilities

The adhesive capabilities of the probiotic B. pumilus HFS2 were evaluated by measuring its autoaggregation, coaggregation and cell-surface hydrophobicity (Fig. 3). The autoaggregation percentage increased progressively over time, from 28.09 ± 1.84% at 3 h to 45.64 ± 3.41% at 6 h, reaching a maximum of 65.93 ± 1.02% at 9 h, demonstrating a time-dependent increase in autoaggregation. The strain displayed notable cell-surface hydrophobicity against the tested solvents. Hydrophobicity values reached 69.11 ± 0.55% with xylene and 68.17 ± 1.21% with toluene after 9 h of incubation. The coaggregation ability of the isolate was assessed against two bacterial strains at three different time points (3, 6, and 9 h). The coaggregation potential of B. pumilus HFS2 with L. lactis and S. arlettae was observed from 3 h, with values of 24.55 ± 1.73% and 29.25 ± 0.79%, respectively. B. pumilus HFS2 was able to produce biofilms. Strong biofilm formation was indicated by the isolate's production of black colonies that looked dry and crystalline.

Discussion

Over the past decade, Bacillus species have received increasing attention as effective biological control agents due to their versatile functional attributes and well-documented efficacy. Among these, B. pumilus is commonly employed in agriculture, aquaculture and animal husbandry, has been shown to have a number of advantageous qualities, such as boosting crop productivity, fostering plant growth, and improving animal intestinal health.54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60 Recent studies have demonstrated that probiotics, particularly host-associated Bacillus spp., can exert significant immunomodulatory effects in aquatic animals in addition to improving gut health and disease resistance. Several Bacillus strains have been reported to enhance innate and adaptive immune responses through increased lysozyme activity, respiratory burst activity, complement activity, antioxidant defense, cytokine regulation, and immunoglobulin production in fish.61, 62, 63 For example, B. velezensis supplementation significantly enhanced lysozyme and complement activity in common carp, indicating improved nonspecific immunity.63, 64 Similarly, a host-derived B. pumilus strain improved intestinal immune responses and reduced oxidative stress in catfish,65 while B. subtilis supplementation upregulated anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β in Oreochromis niloticus.66, 67 These findings suggest that host-derived probiotic strains may contribute not only to gut colonization and pathogen inhibition but also to immune homeostasis, stress mitigation and immune gene expression in aquaculture species.66, 67 Although the present study did not directly evaluate immune parameters, the strong probiotic characteristics and safety profile exhibited by B. pumilus HFS2 indicate its potential as a functional probiotic candidate with possible immunostimulatory benefits for aquaculture applications. Future in vivo studies should therefore, investigate its effects on immune gene expression, antioxidant responses, and disease resistance in fish.

This study characterized a novel probiotic candidate strain, B. pumilus HFS2 based on its colony morphology, physiological attributes, and biochemical properties. These characteristics were used to distinguish the isolate from other bacterial strains within the same Bacillus genus. Furthermore, the identity of the isolate was confirmed through partial 16S rRNA gene sequencing and phylogenetic analysis.

The isolated bacterium HFS2 was characterized as Gram-positive and rod-shaped. Its colonies were cream-colored, opaque, and medium-sized, exhibiting circular to slightly irregular shapes, with margins ranging from entire to undulate, and surfaces that were dry and smooth to slightly rough. These morphological traits are consistent with typical characteristics of B. pumilus, which are Gram-positive, rod-shaped bacteria with cells occurring singly or in short chains, motile, and aerobic.49, 56

Biochemical analyses revealed that HFS2 tested positive for catalase, methyl red (MR), and Voges–Proskauer (VP), while showing a negative result for the indole test. In carbohydrate fermentation assays, the isolate fermented dextrose, lactose, maltose, and mannitol, producing acid, but did not ferment sucrose. Collectively, these morphological and biochemical characteristics indicated close similarity to species within the genus Bacillus (Table 4).68, 69 Molecular analysis further confirmed this identification, as HFS2 exhibited 99.65% 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity with B. pumilus. Generally, 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity of >95% is considered indicative of the same genus, whereas similarity of >97% supports species-level identification.70 In the present study, isolate HFS2 exhibited 99.65% sequence similarity with Bacillus pumilus, confirming its assignment to both the genus and species levels. However, 16S rRNA gene sequencing alone has limited discriminatory power for resolving closely related strains within the same species. As this is the first report of B. pumilus isolated from Heteropneustes fossilis, the isolate was designated as B. pumilus strain HFS2. Nevertheless, definitive strain-level taxonomic identification requires higher-resolution molecular approaches, such as multilocus sequence analysis (MLSA) based on housekeeping genes (e.g., gyrB and rpoB) or whole-genome sequencing coupled with Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) analyses.71, 72

The assays for autoaggregation and cell surface hydrophobicity offer crucial information about the probiotic candidates' adhesion potential, which is vital for their proper colonization and persistence in the host gut.15, 73 In the present study, B. pumilus HFS2 exhibited a progressive increase in autoaggregation over time, rising from 28.09 ± 1.84% at 3 h to 65.93 ± 1.02% at 9 h. This steady increase suggests enhanced intercellular interactions and a potential to form stable microbial communities. Such time-dependent self-aggregation is advantageous for the formation of microcolonies, which may help the bacteria resist intestinal shear forces and facilitate long-term colonization of the gastrointestinal environment.74

Cell surface hydrophobicity, a key physicochemical property linked to microbial adhesion to host epithelial cells, showed strong strain-specific characteristics. In this study, B. pumilus HFS2 exhibited high hydrophobicity, with values of 69.11 ± 0.55% (xylene) and 68.17 ± 1.21% (toluene) after 9 h of incubation, exceeding the >67% threshold associated with promising colonization potential.77 Coaggregation, the ability of probiotic bacteria to bind with pathogens, is an important trait that can inhibit pathogen colonization and proliferation in the gut.78 In this study, B pumilus HFS2 exhibited notable coaggregation after 9 h of incubation, with values of 53.32 ± 0.43% with L. lactis and 49.16 ± 0.91% with S. arlettae, consistent with previous reports describing strong coaggregation ability in B. pumilus.79, 80 Collectively, the hydrophobicity, autoaggregation, and coaggregation properties of B. pumilus HFS2 suggest potential adhesion-related functionality and underscore its potential as a promising probiotic strain.

Probiotics must be able to withstand intestinal bile and acidic environments in order to improve gut health by promoting beneficial microbes, aiding digestion and absorption, and preventing pathogenic microbes.81, 82 In pH and bile tolerance tests, B. pumilus HFS2 survived at low pH and high bile concentrations which is aligned with previous studies and draws the spotlight to its probiotic effectiveness, as resistance to stomach acidity and intestinal bile is crucial for effective colonization.33, 50

Probiotics' adaptability and efficacy in aquaculture systems can be ascertained by assessing them in various salinity conditions. Since probiotic bacteria require specific environmental conditions for optimal growth, assessing their salinity tolerance is essential, as some strains adapt well to varying salinity levels while others show reduced survival beyond their tolerance range. The isolate B. pumilus HFS2 demonstrated tolerance to a wide range of salinity levels, with optimal growth observed at 2% NaCl and minimal growth at 8% NaCl. This pattern indicates the halotolerant nature of the strain, as it maintained growth across all tested salt concentrations.83 Such tolerance is consistent with previous reports describing the adaptive capacity of Bacillus spp. to withstand saline stress.23, 84 These findings highlight the ecological adaptability of B. pumilus HFS2 and support its potential application in environments with fluctuating salinity.85

The study demonstrated that B. pumilus HFS2 grew across a broad temperature range, with optimal growth observed at 35 °C, consistent with previous findings (Purivirojkul & Maketon, 2005; Wekesa et al., 2022). The isolate maintained growth at 4, 15, 25, 30, and 35 °C, indicating considerable thermal tolerance. Such adaptability highlights the resilience of this bioactive bacterium and supports its suitability for potential probiotic candidate and biocontrol applications in aquaculture.23 The temperature tolerance assay was limited to 35 °C; therefore, the upper thermal growth limit of the strain could not be determined, and further studies are required to evaluate its growth performance at higher temperatures. The observed optimal growth of B. pumilus HFS2 at 35 °C and 2% NaCl aligns well with the natural habitat conditions of H. fossilis, which commonly inhabits warm, shallow freshwater environments in tropical regions where temperatures frequently range between 28 and 35 °C. Such thermal preference suggests that the strain is well adapted to the physiological conditions of its host environment. Similarly, the tolerance and optimal growth at 2% NaCl indicate moderate halotolerance, which is advantageous in aquaculture systems where slight salinity fluctuations may occur due to feed input, mineral supplementation, or environmental variation. Overall, these physiological traits support the suitability of the strain for application in H. fossilis culture systems under typical farm conditions.

Safety assessment is crucial, and a lack of hemolytic activity is considered an important selection criterion.87 Hemolysis is associated with bacterial virulence, and non-hemolytic strains are considered safe, minimizing the risk of opportunistic pathogenicity.77 In this study, B. pumilus HFS2 showed no hemolytic activity, indicating its non-virulent nature in vitro. Similar non-hemolytic properties have also been reported in other probiotic B. pumilus strains.49, 79

A crucial factor in choosing safe probiotic strains is antibiotic susceptibility.88 In this study, B. pumilus HFS2 was susceptible to most commonly used antibiotics in fish farming. These results are consistent with previous reports: B. pumilus DX24 exhibited susceptibility to multiple antibiotics, including gentamicin, ciprofloxacin, ampicillin, and tetracycline,75 while other B. pumilus strains were sensitive to kanamycin, tetracycline, azithromycin, ampicillin, erythromycin, penicillin-G, and vancomycin, with intermediate resistance to polymyxin-B and oxacillin.79 Antibiotic resistance in probiotics poses a safety concern due to the potential transfer of resistance genes to pathogens.89 Thus, the broad susceptibility of B. pumilus HFS2 supports its safety as a potential probiotic candidate.49, 90 The resistance of B. pumilus HFS2 to cefotaxime may represent an intrinsic characteristic reported in certain Bacillus species and is therefore, considered less likely to be associated with horizontally transferable resistance. However, the observed resistance phenotype alone is insufficient to establish its genetic basis or biosafety. Although intrinsic resistance generally poses a lower risk of dissemination than acquired resistance, further molecular characterization is required to determine the underlying resistance mechanism, confirm the absence of transferable antibiotic resistance genes, and comprehensively assess the biosafety of the strain before its potential application as a probiotic.

Biofilm formation is a key probiotic trait that enhances colonization and inhibits pathogen establishment.91 In this study, B. pumilus HFS2 demonstrated strong in vitro biofilm formation, highlighting its capacity to persist and confer protective effects on the host mucosa. Similar traits have been observed in other Bacillus species, where biofilm formation facilitates the sustained activity and incorporation of probiotic bacteria.49, 92, 93

In this study, B. pumilus HFS2 effectively inhibited the growth of fish pathogens, including L. garvieae HFL1 (PQ870415), L. formosensis HFL6 (PQ870420), A. caviae HFS3 (PQ870425), A. jandaei HFS7 (PQ870429), A. veronii HFS8 (PQ870430), and A. hydrophila HFS9 (PQ870431). Antagonistic activity of B. pumilus against fish pathogens has been reported previously, highlighting its potential to reduce infection risk in aquaculture.49, 56, 79 The antimicrobial assay was performed using the cell-free supernatant after pH neutralization to rule out the effect of organic acids. This suggests that the observed inhibitory activity is more likely associated with bioactive compounds such as antimicrobial peptides (e.g., bacteriocin-like substances) rather than acidity alone. Effective pathogen inhibition requires that probiotic strains also survive and colonize the gastrointestinal tract. Consistent with related studies, bacterial isolates from fish intestines have demonstrated the dual ability to inhibit pathogens while persisting in the gut environment.94, 95

Safety evaluation is a fundamental requirement for probiotic candidates intended for aquaculture, as strains must not induce disease or physiological stress in the host. The in vivo biosafety assessment confirmed that B. pumilus HFS2 is non-pathogenic to H. fossilis. Neither intraperitoneal injection nor immersion challenge at 1 × 108 CFU/mL resulted in mortality, external lesions, or internal organ abnormalities. These findings demonstrate that the strain poses no pathogenic threat to the host species. The absence of pathogenic effects observed in this study is consistent with previous reports indicating that several Bacillus spp. used as probiotics exhibit high biosafety profiles in fish when tested through both intraperitoneal and immersion challenge models.23, 53, 96 However, histopathological examination of liver and intestinal tissues would provide additional evidence regarding the absence of sub-clinical tissue alterations and should be considered in future studies to further validate the biosafety of the strain. Therefore, the non-pathogenic nature of B. pumilus HFS2 further supports its suitability as a safe probiotic candidate for aquaculture applications.

The probiotic properties of B. pumilus HFS2 are consistent with those reported for other Bacillus spp. and B. pumilus strains used in aquaculture. The strain demonstrated tolerance to a broad range of temperatures, acidic pH conditions, and elevated bile salt concentrations, indicating strong resilience to both gastrointestinal and environmental stresses, similar to previously described B. pumilus probiotics.75, 86 HFS2 also exhibited high autoaggregation, cell surface hydrophobicity, and coaggregation capacities, traits that are associated with effective intestinal colonization and competitive exclusion of pathogens. Furthermore, the strain showed antagonistic activity against several important fish pathogens, including Aeromonas spp. and L. garvieae, in agreement with findings for other probiotic B. pumilus isolates.24, 56, 65, 79 Safety evaluation revealed that HFS2 was γ-hemolytic, susceptible to most tested antibiotics, and produced no adverse effects in H. fossilis during in vivo assessment, comparable to other reported probiotic strains.80, 97 Notably, the combined presence of strong stress tolerance, adhesion-related properties, biofilm-forming ability, broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, and confirmed in vivo safety distinguishes HFS2 as a potential probiotic candidate for H. fossilis aquaculture.

Conclusion

Bacillus pumilus HFS2, isolated from the digestive tract of Heteropneustes fossilis, demonstrated multiple desirable probiotic characteristics, indicating its potential for application in aquaculture. The strain's beneficial traits highlight its promise as a potential probiotic candidate for improving fish health management. Further studies are recommended to evaluate its efficacy and stability under farm-scale conditions before practical application.
