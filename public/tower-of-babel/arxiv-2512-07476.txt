An Analysis of Decision Problems for Relational Pattern Languages under Various Constraints
Klaus Jansen, Dirk Nowotka, Lis Pirotton, Corinna Wambsganz, Max Wiedenhöft

Abstract
Patterns are words with terminals and variables. The language of a pattern is the set of words obtained by uniformly substituting all variables with words that contain only terminals. In their original definition, patterns only allow for multiple distinct occurrences of some variables to be related by the equality relation, represented by using the same variable multiple times. In an extended notion, called relational patterns and relational pattern languages, variables may be related by arbitrary other relations. We extend the ongoing investigation of the main decision problems for patterns (namely, the membership problem, the inclusion problem, and the equivalence problem) to relational pattern languages under a wide range of individual relations. It is shown show that - even for many much simpler or less restrictive relations - the complexity and (un)decidability characteristics of these problems do not change compared to the classical case where variables are related only by equality.

Introduction

A
pattern
(with variables) is a finite word that consists only of symbols from a finite set of
(terminal) letters

\Sigma=\{\mathtt{a}_{1},...,\mathtt{a}_{\sigma}\}
and from an infinite but countable set of
variables

X=\{x_{1},x_{2},...\}
with
\Sigma\cap X=\emptyset
. By applying terminal preserving morphisms, called
substitutions
, to a pattern, we can obtain words that consist only of terminal letters. The
language
of a pattern is then just the set of all words that can be obtained through the application of arbitrary substitutions.

Originally, pattern languages as defined and introduced by Angluin
[
1
]
only considered words that are obtained by what is now called
non-erasing substitutions
, i.e., substitutions where each variable is substituted by a word of at least length one. Hence, these languages are now also called
non-erasing (NE) pattern languages
. Shinohara
[
34
]
extended this notion by allowing for the empty word to be used to substitute a variable, introducing so called
extended/erasing (E) pattern languages
.

For example, consider the pattern
\alpha=x_{1}\mathtt{a}\mathtt{a}x_{1}\mathtt{b}x_{2}
. Using the non-erasing substitution
h
that sets
h(x_{1})=\mathtt{b}\mathtt{b}
and
h(x_{2})=\mathtt{a}
, we obtain the word
h(\alpha)=\mathtt{b}\mathtt{b}\mathtt{a}\mathtt{a}\mathtt{b}\mathtt{b}\mathtt{b}\mathtt{a}
. If we also considered the E-pattern language of
\alpha
, we could set all variables to the empty word and obtain the word
\mathtt{a}\mathtt{a}\mathtt{b}
which we could not obtain in the NE pattern language of
\alpha
.

As patterns form a natural and compact device for the definition of formal languages and due to their practical and simple definition, they occur in numerous areas in computer science and discrete mathematics. Among others, these include for example the areas of unavoidable patterns in words
[
17
,
21
]
, word equations
[
21
]
, algorithmic learning theory
[
1
,
7
,
35
]
, database theory
[
10
,
33
]
, or the theory of extended regular expressions with back references
[
11
]
. Furthermore, many practical areas such as machine learning, database systems, or bio-informatics deal with the question of identifying or describing patterns in (sets of) strings
[
23
]
, for example in the analysis of protein data
[
2
]
, in pattern matching
[
4
]
, or the design of algorithms for program synthesis
[
25
]
.

Three main decision problems emerge when it comes to the investigation of patterns and pattern languages. These are the general
membership problem
and its variations
[
12
,
13
,
8
]
, the
inclusion problem
, and the
equivalence problem
, all of which are considered in the non-erasing (NE) as well as erasing (E) cases. The membership problem determines whether a word belongs to a pattern’s language. It has been shown to be generally NP-complete in the non-erasing as well as erasing cases
[
1
,
17
]
. The inclusion problem checks whether the language of a pattern is included as a subset in another pattern’s language. It has been shown to be generally undecidable by Jiang et al.
[
18
]
. Their result has been improved by Freydenberger and Reidenbach in
[
9
]
as well as by Bremer and Freydenberger in
[
3
]
, where it has been shown to be undecidable for all alphabets
\Sigma
of fixed size
|\Sigma|\geq 2
, for erasing and non-erasing pattern languages. The equivalence problem determines whether the languages of two given patterns are equal. In Angluin’s original work
[
1
]
, it has been shown to be trivially decidable for all NE pattern languages. For erasing pattern languages, however, the general decidability of this problem has been the
most significant open problem
in the field of decision problems for patterns
[
18
,
30
,
29
,
28
,
31
]
. In the special case of terminal-free patterns (containing only variables), the membership problem remains unchanged in both erasing and non-erasing cases, as does the non-erasing equivalence problem. However, inclusion for terminal-free E-pattern languages is NP-complete, as it reduces to checking for a morphism between patterns
[
18
,
6
]
, while equivalence is decidable. In contrast, Saarela
[
32
]
showed that inclusion for terminal-free NE pattern languages is undecidable over a binary alphabet.

Various extensions to the notion of patterns and pattern languages have been introduced over time either to get closer to an answer for the remaining open problems or to obtain additional expressibility that is usable in some practical context. Some examples are the bounded scope coincidence degree, patterns with bounded treewidth,
k
-local patterns, or strongly-nested patterns (see
[
5
]
and references therein). Koshiba
[
20
]
introduced so called
typed patterns
where the substitutions of variables can be restricted by arbitrary recursive languages, so called
types
. This has been recently extended by Geilke and Zilles in
[
14
]
to the notion of
relational patterns
and
relational pattern languages
. Here, other relations than equality between variables may be used to restrict the valid substitution of patterns.

This model enhances expressiveness, includes all previous models, but also includes arbitrary other constructions that involve relations between variables in a pattern. Consider, for example, the pattern
\beta=x_{1}\mathtt{c}\mathtt{c}x_{2}
. Now let’s say that the variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
should be the substituted by the reversal of each other, i.e.,
(h(x_{1}),h(x_{2}))\in r_{rev}
, where
h
is a substitution and
r_{rev}
is the reversal relation defined by
r_{rev}:=\{\ (w,w^{R})\mid w\in\Sigma^{*}\ \}
(where
w^{R}
is defined to be the reversal of a word
w
). Then, e.g., a substitution
h
with
h(x_{1})=\mathtt{a}\mathtt{b}
and
h(x_{2})=\mathtt{b}\mathtt{a}
is said to be valid regarding the pattern
\beta
, we get
h(\beta)=\mathtt{a}\mathtt{b}\mathtt{c}\mathtt{c}\mathtt{b}\mathtt{a}
, and we say that
h(\beta)
is in the relational pattern language of
\beta
under the relational constraints containing the pair that relates
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
by
r_{rev}
. If we considered a relation
h^{\prime}
, however, in which
h^{\prime}(x_{1})=\mathtt{a}\mathtt{b}
and also
h^{\prime}(x_{2})=\mathtt{a}\mathtt{b}
, then clearly
h^{\prime}(x_{1})\neq h^{\prime}(x_{2})^{R}
and therefore
h^{\prime}
is not a valid substitution in this case, resulting in
h^{\prime}(\beta)
not being in that relational pattern language of
\beta
.

Geilke and Zilles
[
14
]
show some properties of relational pattern languages in general. Holte et al.
[
15
]
investigated relational pattern languages under the equal length or reversal relations, respectively. They show that inclusion, by that also equivalence, under equal length is decidable in polynomial time for ternary alphabets. In a very recent paper, Mousawi and Zilles
[
23
]
extended this from the perspective of learning theory, introducing the notion of
positive characteristic sets
. Their results carry over and it is shown that inclusion, therefore also equivalence, in the erasing and non-erasing cases under equal length is decidable in polynomial time for binary alphabets. Also, for the reversal relation, their result implies that in the non-erasing case, equivalence is decidable. Additionally, also in two recent papers, in the cases of two special kinds of relations/constraints, namely regular constraints
[
26
]
or length constraints
[
27
]
, Nowotka and Wiedenhöft have shown, among other results, that the still open equivalence problem for erasing pattern languages actually becomes undecidable if these constraints are allowed in addition to equality between variables. This sets a first upper bound of constraints necessary to obtain undecidability for that problem. In comparison to many other relations, however, these constraints appear to be very strong, hence leaving the question of what happens in cases of weaker relations.

In total, we see that the field of relational pattern languages remains largely unexplored, leaving room to study various other or weaker relations restricting variable substitutions. This paper considers
equality
(
r_{=}
),
equal length
(
r_{|w|}
),
subsequence
(
r_{ssq}
),
abelian equivalence
(
r_{ab}
),
alphabet permutation
(
r_{perm}
),
reversal
(
r_{rev}
), (nonempty)
commutation
(
r_{com^{+}}
,
r_{com^{*}}
), and the
star relation
(
r_{*}
). We study the membership, inclusion, and equivalence problems under these relations, providing a comprehensive overview of many cases (Table
3
) and showing that hardness and (un)decidability persist even for weaker relations than equality. In particular, it can be shown that the membership problem is NP-complete for all considered relations in all cases. Regarding the inclusion problem, it is shown that undecidability persists even for the relations
r_{ssq}
,
r_{ab}
,
r_{rev}
, and
r_{*}
in the erasing and non-erasing cases. Finally, in the case of the equivalence problem, we show decidability in polynomial time in the non-erasing case for a class of relations with specific properties, containing
r_{ab}
and
r_{com^{+}}
.

Section 2 covers all the necessary notation and models needed to follow the results of the paper. In Section 3, we present the results for the membership problem. This is followed in Section 4 with an overview of the results for the inclusion problem. After that, in Section 5, we briefly discuss the results regarding the equivalence problem. Finally, in Section 6, we summarize the paper and provide a brief outlook with regards to open questions. Due to space constraints, some proofs were moved into the appendix. The corresponding statements are marked with (✂).

Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the natural numbers
\{1,2,3,\dots\}
and let
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
For
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
set
[m,n]:=\{k\in\mathbb{N}\mid m\leq k\leq n\}
.
Denote
[n]:=[1,n]
and
[n]_{0}:=[0,n]
.
An
alphabet

\Sigma
is a non-empty finite set whose elements are called
letters
.
A
word
is a finite sequence of letters from
\Sigma
.
Let
\Sigma^{*}
be the set of all finite words over
\Sigma
and set
\Sigma^{+}:=\Sigma^{*}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
.
We call the number of letters in a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}

length
of
w
, denoted by
|w|
.
Therefore, we have
|\varepsilon|=0
. Denote
\Sigma^{k}:=\{w\in\Sigma^{*}\mid|w|=k\}
.
For
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, let
w[i]
denote
w
’s
i^{th}
letter for all
i\in[|w|]
.
For compactness reasons, we denote
w[i]\cdots w[j]
by
w[i\cdots j]
for all
i,j\in[|w|]
with
i<j
.
Set
\operatorname{alph}(w):=\{\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma\mid\exists i\in[|w|]:w[i]=\mathtt{a}\}
as
w
’s alphabet.

Let
X:=\{x_{1},x_{2},...\}
be a countable set of variables such that
\Sigma\cap X=\emptyset
.
A
pattern
is then a non-empty, finite word over
\Sigma\cup X
.
The set of all patterns over
\Sigma\cup X
is denoted by
Pat_{\Sigma}
.
For example,
x_{1}\mathtt{a}x_{2}\mathtt{b}\mathtt{a}x_{2}x_{3}
is a pattern over
\Sigma=\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b}\}
with
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in X
.
For a pattern
\alpha\in Pat_{\Sigma}
, let
\var(\alpha):=\{\ x\in X\ |\ |p|_{x}\geq 1\ \}
denote the set of variables occurring in
p
.
A
substitution of
\alpha
is a morphism
h:(\Sigma\cup X)^{*}\to\Sigma^{*}
such that
h(\mathtt{a})=\mathtt{a}
for all
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
and

h(x)\in\Sigma^{*}
for all
x\in X
.
If we have
h(x)\neq\varepsilon
for all
x\in\var(p)
, we call
h
a
non-erasing substitution
for
\alpha
.
Otherwise
h
is an
erasing substitution
for
\alpha
. The set of all substitutions w.r.t.
\Sigma
is denoted by
H_{\Sigma}
.
If
\Sigma
is clear from the context, we may write just
H
.
Given a pattern
\alpha\in Pat_{\Sigma}
, it’s erasing pattern language
L_{E}(\alpha)
and its non-erasing pattern language
L_{NE}(\alpha)

are defined respectively by

\displaystyle L_{E}(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\{\ h(\alpha)\ |\ h\in H,h(x)\in\Sigma^{*}\text{ for all }x\in\var(\alpha)\},\text{ and }

\displaystyle L_{NE}(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\{\ h(\alpha)\ |\ h\in H,h(x)\in\Sigma^{+}\text{ for all }x\in\var(\alpha)\}.

Let
RegPat_{\Sigma}:=\{\alpha\in Pat_{\Sigma}\mid x_{i}\in\operatorname{alph}(\alpha)\cap X,|\alpha|_{x_{i}}=1\}
denote the set of all regular patterns, i.e., the set of all patterns where each variable occurs only once. Let
R
be some set of relations and denote for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
by
R_{n}\subset R
the subset of
n
-ary relations in
R
. Define
Rel_{R}:=\{(r,x_{i_{1}},...,x_{i_{n}})\mid r\in R_{n},x_{i_{1}},...,x_{i_{n}}\in X,i_{1},...i_{n}\in\mathbb{N},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
to be the set of all combinations to relate variables in
X
over the set of relations
R
. Then, a pair
(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\in RegPat_{\Sigma}\times Rel_{R}
is a relational pattern over the terminal alphabet
\Sigma
and the set of relations
R
. We denote the set of all relational patterns over
\Sigma
and
R
by
\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
. For some
(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
and
h\in H
, we say that
h
is a

-valid substitution if for all
(r^{\prime},x_{i_{1}},...,x_{i_{n}})\in r_{\alpha}
we have
(h(x_{i_{1}}),...,h(x_{i_{n}}))\in r^{\prime}
, i.e., all variables are substituted such that the resulting terminal words are related to each other regarding the relation
r^{\prime}
.
The set of all
r_{\alpha}
-valid substitutions w.r.t.
\Sigma
is denoted by
H_{\Sigma,r_{\alpha}}
.
If
\Sigma
and
r_{\alpha}
are clear from the context, we may also just write
H_{r_{\alpha}}
or

.
We extend the notion of pattern languages by the following. For any
(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
we denote by

L_{E}(\alpha,r_{\alpha}):=\{\ h(\alpha)\ |\ h\in H,h(x)\in\Sigma^{*}\text{ for all }x\in\var(\alpha),h\text{ is }r_{\alpha}\text{-valid}\ \}

the
erasing relational pattern language
of
(\alpha,r_{\alpha})
and by

L_{NE}(\alpha,r_{\alpha}):=\{\ h(\alpha)\ |\ h\in H,h(x)\in\Sigma^{+}\text{ for all }x\in\var(\alpha),h\text{ is }r_{\alpha}\text{-valid}\ \}

the

of
(\alpha,r_{\alpha})
.

In this paper, we primarily investigate the considered decision problems for relational pattern languages for binary relations, i.e.,
|R|=1
and if
R=\{\tilde{r}\}
, then
\tilde{r}
is 2-ary/binary. In particular, we are interested in the following selection of specific customary binary relations. Let
\mathcal{R}
be the set containing all of the following nine relations.

1.

r_{=}
: equality of words, i.e.,
(u,v)\in R_{=}
iff

.

2.

: length equality of words, i.e.,
(u,v)\in R_{|w|}
iff
|u|=|v|
.

3.

r_{ssq}
: subsequence relation, i.e.,
(u,v)\in R_{ssq}
iff
u\in\SubSeq(v)
.

4.

r_{ab}
: abelian equivalence of words, i.e.,
(u,v)\in R_{ab}
iff
u\equiv_{ab}v
.

5.

r_{perm}
: alphabet permutation of words, i.e.,
(u,v)\in R_{perm}
iff
u
can be obtained by

with a permutation on the alphabet of its letters.

6.

r_{rev}
: reversal of words, i.e.,
(u,v)\in R_{rev}
iff
u=v^{R}
.

7.

r_{com^{*}}
: commutation between words, i.e.,
(u,v)\in R_{com^{*}}
iff there exists
z\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
u,v\in\{z\}^{*}
.

8.

r_{com^{+}}
: nonempty commutation between words, i.e.,
(u,v)\in R_{com^{+}}
iff there exists
z\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
u,v\in\{z\}^{+}
.

9.

r_{*}
: word in star of the other word, i.e.,
(u,v)\in r_{*}
iff
u\in\{v\}^{*}
.

In the proofs of the main results regarding the inclusion problem, we make use of two different automata models that have undecidable language emptiness properties. The first one is the so called
nondeterministic 2-counter automaton without input
(see, e.g.,
[
16
]
). The second one is a very specific universal Turing machine
U
with 2 symbols and 15 states (see
[
24
]
). As the technical proofs are very lengthy and based on the constructions provided in other papers, the formal definitions of the models can be found in Appendix
0.B.1
and Appendix
0.B.2
respectively. For the intuitions of the proofs provided in the main body, it suffices to know that these respective models have undecidable language emptiness properties, that all terminating/accepted computations are encoded using finite binary words, and that the number of existing and not existing outgoing transitions per state are bound by a constant. This last property results in a finite number of properties of words that are not encodings of accepting computations.

The Membership Problem

In this section, we discuss the membership problem for relational pattern languages for the relations in
\mathcal{R}
. In addition to that, we also consider certain special cases of one-sided relations mentioned at the end of
[
1
]
that do not immediately translate to the framework of relational pattern languages considered here. As to be seen, the membership problem remains NP-complete for all considered cases. We will begin by covering the cases arising for all relations in
\mathcal{R}
.

For the case of
r_{=}
, we recall the original result by Angluin in
[
1
]
in which it is shown that the membership problem of non-erasing pattern languages is NP-complete. In the erasing case, the same result has been shown by Jiang et al. in
[
17
]
. The following theorem states the main result of this section. It is the NP-completeness of the membership problem for relational pattern languages over any of the relations in
\mathcal{R}
. The rest of this section serves as its proof.

Theorem 3.1

Let
r\in\mathcal{R}
and assume
R=\{r\}
. Given
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
, deciding whether
w\in L_{X}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})
for
X\in\{E,NE\}
is NP-complete.

Indeed, the NP-containment of the membership problems for any of the relations follows by a rather similar argument as can be made for the case of the equality relation
r_{=}
(see Appendix
0.A
for more details).

Proposition 1
(✂)

Let
r\in\mathcal{R}
and
R=\{r\}
. Given
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
, deciding whether
w\in L_{X}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})
for
X\in\{E,NE\}
is in NP.

4
The Inclusion Problem

In this section, we continue with the inclusion problem for relational pattern languages. We recall that the inclusion problem for non-erasing as well as erasing pattern languages over
r_{=}
is generally undecidable for all alphabets
\Sigma
of size
|\Sigma|\geq 2
, as shown and refined in
[
18
,
9
,
3
]
. As we will see in this section, for several of the considered relations in
\mathcal{R}
, we also obtain that this problem is undecidable in the non-erasing or erasing cases.

In fact, if we consider, first, the relations
r_{ssq}
and
r_{*}
, we observe that we can simulate the exact behavior of
r_{=}
, similar to the NP-hardness proofs in the membership section. Meaning, if we take any two variables
x,y\in\var(\alpha)
, for some relational pattern
(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
, and any of the three relations above, we see that relating these variables in both direction effectively forces two variables to be substituted equally in
r_{\alpha}
valid substitutions, i.e., if
(x,y),(y,x)\in r_{\alpha}
, then
h\in H_{r_{\alpha}}
if and only if
h(x)=h(y)
. By the fact that this problem is undecidable for
r_{=}
, this results in the following, for which the formal proof can be found in Appendix
0.B.3
for reference.

Corollary 1

[
18
,
9
,
3
]

Let
\Sigma
be any finite alphabet with
|\Sigma|\geq 2
. Let
\tilde{r}\in\{r_{=},r_{ssq},r_{*}\}
and let
R=\{\tilde{r}\}
. Given
(\alpha,r_{\alpha}),(\beta,r_{\beta})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
, it is undecidable to answer whether
L_{\mathcal{X}}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\subseteq L_{\mathcal{X}}(\beta,r_{\beta})
, for
\mathcal{X}\in\{E,NE\}
.

Now, we continue with the analysis for relations for which we cannot immediately infer the undecidability from such trivial relationships.

4.1
The Non-Erasing Cases

In the non-erasing case, an interesting observation regarding
r_{ab}
can be found. Reconstructing the reduction from
[
3
]
in the setting of relational pattern languages, it can be shown that the inclusion problem for non-erasing relational pattern languages under
r_{ab}
is actually also undecidable, even though
r_{ab}
generally implies a significantly less restrictive behavior than
r_{=}
.

Proposition 5
(✂)

Let
R:=\{r_{ab}\}
and
|\Sigma|\geq 2
for some alphabet
\Sigma
.
It is undecidable whether
L_{NE}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\subseteq L_{NE}(\beta,r_{\beta})
for
(\alpha,r_{\alpha}),(\beta,r_{\beta})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
.

Proof

This can be shown by reducing the problem of deciding whether the universal Turing machine
U
has any valid
computations to the problem of whether the NE-relational pattern language of one relational pattern under abelian
equivalence is included in the NE-relational pattern language of another relational pattern under abelian equivalence.
It is done by constructing two such relational patterns
(\alpha,\alpha_{R})
and
(\beta,\beta_{R})
for which
we have that
L_{NE}(\alpha,R)\subseteq L_{NE}(\beta,\beta_{R})
if and only if
U
has no valid computation.

As mentioned above, this result follows from the same reduction as in
[
3
]
for the inclusion problem of NE-pattern languages over the equality relation. Only some technical modifications have to be made to formally get it to work in the setting of relational pattern languages under
r_{ab}
. The proof covering the technical adaptions can be found in Appendix
0.B.4
for reference. ∎

As the construction used to show Proposition
5
only related variables to either enforce equality between length 1 substitutions (letters) or equality between unary strings, it also not only translates to the case of
r_{ab}
, but also to the reversal relation
r_{rev}
. Indeed, the reversal of a unary word
w
is
w
itself and the reversal of a letter
\mathtt{a}_{i}
is clearly also just the letter
\mathtt{a}_{i}
. So, we can enforce the same behavior to select only one
x_{i,1}
to become the letter
\#
in valid substitutions while all other
x_{j,i}
take the letter
0
, as
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
,
b_{1}
, and
b_{2}
are always substituted only to unary words that are powers of the letter
0
. With that, the
v
parts will look exactly the same. Further, the counter values that are represented in simple predicates with the variables
\hat{x}_{i,j}
will also only take the shape of unary words, as do the variables in the third predicate
\pi_{3}
in the remaining cases not covered by the predicates
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
. So, using an analogous construction as for Proposition
5
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 2

Let
R:=\{r_{rev}\}
and
|\Sigma|\geq 2
for some alphabet
\Sigma
.
It is undecidable whether
L_{NE}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\subseteq L_{NE}(\beta,r_{\beta})
for
(\alpha,r_{\alpha}),(\beta,r_{\beta})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
.

4.2
The Erasing Cases

We continue with the additional cases for erasing relational pattern languages. First, it can be shown that the inclusion problem under the reversal relation
r_{rev}
is also undecidable. We can use the same framework as in the result from Freydenberger and Reidenbach
[
9

that shows undecidability in the erasing case under

\Sigma

|\Sigma|\geq 2

Theorem 4.1
(✂)

Let

\Sigma

|\Sigma|\geq 2

L_{E}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\subseteq L_{E}(\beta,r_{\beta})

4.3
Discussion and Challenges Regarding the Other Relations

Currently, we know that for relational patterns under the relations
r_{=}
,
r_{ssq}
,
r_{ab}
,
r_{rev}
, and
r_{*}
the inclusion problem is undecidable. This mainly comes from the fact that either the relations can be used to simulate the behavior of
r_{=}
generally (as in the case of
r_{ssq}
and
r_{*}
) or it comes from the fact that essential properties used in relevant reductions can also be emulated using other relations (enforce equal letters or equal unary words using
r_{ab}
or
r_{rev}
).

When it comes to the remaining relations
r_{|w|}
,
r_{perm}
,
r_{com^{*}}
, and
r_{com^{+}}
, we see that these properties are not trivially obtainable. The relations
r_{|w|}
and
r_{perm}
both do not force equal letters or equal unary words in alphabets that are at least of binary size. The relations
r_{com^{*}}
and
r_{com^{+}}
do not even force equal length between variables in valid substitutions, making, for example, the comparison of counter values impossible in the way it has been done in the previous reductions. Hence, it remains an worthwhile open question whether the inclusion problem becomes decidable at some point, for very structurally weak relations.

Question 1

Let
R=\{\tilde{r}\}
for some relation
\tilde{r}\in\{r_{|w|},r_{perm},r_{com^{*}},r_{com^{+}}\}
. Let
(\alpha,r_{\alpha}),(\beta,r_{\beta})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
. Is the inclusion problem
L_{\mathcal{X}}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\subseteq L_{\mathcal{X}}(\beta,r_{\beta})
, for
\mathcal{X}\in\{E,NE\}
, generally decidable?

5
The Equivalence Problem

In this section, we briefly discuss some results regarding the equivalence problem for non-erasing relational pattern languages. As we will see, we will obtain a series of small results that builds up to the main theorem of this section, stating that the equivalence problem for all non-erasing pattern languages over a binary equivalence relation that is antisymmetric on elements of length 1 (i.e., has equality for single letters) is decidable in polynomial time (covering the relations
\{r_{=},r_{ab},r_{con^{+}}\}
from this paper’s discussion).

We define
[r_{\alpha}]
to be the transitive, reflexive and symmetric closure on some set of binary related variables
r_{\alpha}
. Additionally, for this section, we assume that variables are always introduced inside a pattern with increasing indices starting from
1
(i.e.,
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, and so on). Due to space constraints, the technical proofs of almost all steps can be found in Appendix
0.C
, while the steps themselves are supposed to give some intuition on why the main result of this section, Theorem
5.1
, holds. We begin with a result regarding
[r_{\alpha}]
in the case of
r_{\alpha}
being an equivalence relation.

Lemma 3

Let
R:=\{\tilde{r}\}
with
\tilde{r}
being an equivalence relation and let
(\alpha,r_{\alpha})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
.
Then we have
L_{NE}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})=L_{NE}(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}])
.

Proof

(
\Rightarrow
): Let
w\in L_{NE}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})
.
Then there exists
h\in H
such that
h((\alpha,r_{\alpha}))=w
.
Let
(\tilde{r},x,y)\in r_{\alpha}
for
x,y\in\var(\alpha)
.
Then
(h(x),h(y))\in\tilde{r}
by definition.
As
\tilde{r}
is an equivalence relation, we also have
(h(y),h(x))\in\tilde{r}
,

(h(x),h(x))\in\tilde{r}
, and if there exists
(\tilde{r},y,z)\in r_{\alpha}
such that
(h(y),h(z))\in\tilde{r}
then also
(h(x),h(z))\in\tilde{r}
.
So,
h
satisfied all constraints set by
[r_{\alpha}]
.
Hence,
w\in L_{NE}(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}])
.

(
\Leftarrow
): Let
w\in L_{NE}(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}])
and let
h\in H
such that
h((\alpha,[r_{\alpha}]))=w
.
As
r_{\alpha}\subseteq[r_{\alpha}]
, all constraints in
r_{\alpha}
must be satisfied.
Thus, we also have
w\in L_{NE}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})
.
∎

Following on that, we can also obtain the following relationship.

Lemma 4
(✂)

Let
R:=\{\tilde{r}\}
with
\tilde{r}
being some equivalence relation and assume
|\Sigma|\geq 2
.
Let
(\alpha,r_{\alpha}),(\beta,r_{\beta})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
.
If
L_{NE}(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}])=L_{NE}(\beta,[r_{\beta}])
, then
\alpha=\beta
.

Using the previous lemma, we can show the following result.

Lemma 5
(✂)

Let
R:=\{\tilde{r}\}
with
\tilde{r}
being an equivalence relation that is antisymmetric on words of length 1 (i.e., equality for letters) and assume
|\Sigma|\geq 2
.
Let
(\alpha,r_{\alpha}),(\beta,r_{\beta})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
be two relational patterns.
Then
L_{NE}(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}]])=L_{NE}(\beta,[r_{\beta}])
if and only if
\alpha=\beta
and
[r_{\alpha}]=[r_{\beta}]
.

Finally, taking all previous lemmas into account, we obtain the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.1
(✂)

Let
R:=\{\tilde{r}\}
with
\tilde{r}
being an equivalence relation that is antisymmetric on words of length 1 (i.e., equality for letters) and assume
|\Sigma|\geq 2
.
Let
(\alpha,r_{\alpha}),(\beta,r_{\beta})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
be two relational patterns.
Then
L_{NE}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})=L_{NE}(\beta,r_{\beta})
if and only if
(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}])=(\beta,[r_{\beta}])
.

Hence, for the considered relations in this paper, we obtain the following.

Corollary 3
(✂)

Let
R=\{\tilde{r}\}
for some
\tilde{r}\in\{r_{=},r_{ab},r_{con^{+}}\}
. Given the relational patterns
(\alpha,r_{\alpha}),(\beta,r_{\beta})\in\mathtt{RelPat}_{\Sigma,R}
, we can check in polynomial time whether
L_{NE}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})=L_{NE}(\beta,r_{\beta})
.

6
Conclusion

We considered the three main decision problems membership, inclusion, and equivalence for relational pattern languages under various typical binary relations, i.e.,
r_{=}
,
r_{ssq}
,
r_{ab}
,
r_{perm}
,
r_{rev}
,
r_{com^{+}}
,
r_{com^{*}}
, and
r_{*}
.

In particular, for the membership problem, we observed that in all considered cases, that is, for all relations in
\mathcal{R}
and for erasing as well as non-erasing relational pattern languages, the problem turns out to be NP-complete, even for very simple relations such as
r_{|w|}
. As the reduction relies on rather simple properties to hold between related areas inside the constructed patterns, we expect the problem to be NP-hard for generally almost all typical relations that could also be considered. This allows for the assumption that searching for words in sets of texts that are compactly described by, e.g., a relational pattern of almost any kind, is generally a computationally hard problem.

For the inclusion problem, we observed that many cases remain undecidable. In the erasing as well as the non-erasing cases, the problem remains undecidable for the structurally less restrictive relations
r_{ssq}
,
r_{ab}
, and
r_{*}
as well as for the relation
r_{rev}
. In the cases of
r_{ssq}
and
r_{*}
, we can abuse the property that relating two variables in both directions results basically in
r_{=}
. For the other cases, we observe that the key for the reductions to work is, first, finding ways to force certain variables to be substituted by equal single letters to realize structural properties and, second, to force equality of longer unary words to realize the comparison of counter values. It would be interesting to know whether similar results can be obtained using the other relations that enforce even less structure in valid substitutions.

Regarding the equivalence problem, we obtained decidability in polynomial time for
r_{ab}
and
r_{com^{+}}
in the non-erasing case, similar to
r_{=}
. We conjecture that this should hold for many more relations in the non-erasing case by checking some set of minimal length substitutions. For the erasing cases, everything except
r_{|w|}
remains open and we expect the decidability question to be generally rather difficult to answer, as it is the main open question for
r_{=}
.

0.B.1
Definition of Nondeterminstic 2-Counter Automata without Input

A
nondeterministic 2-counter automaton without input
(see e.g.
[
16
]
) is a 4-tuple
A=(Q,\delta,q_{0},F)
which consists of a set of states
Q
, a transition function
\delta:Q\times\{0,1\}^{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(Q\times\{\-1,0,+1\}^{2})
, an initial state
q_{0}\in Q
, and a set of accepting states
F\subseteq Q
. A
configuration
of
A
is defined as a triple
(q,m_{1},m_{2})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
in which
q
indicates the current state and
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
indicate the contents of the first and second counter. We define the relation
\vdash_{A}
on
Q\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
by
\delta
as follows. For two configurations
(p,m_{1},m_{2})
and
(q,n_{1},n_{2})
we say that
(p,m_{1},m_{2})\vdash_{A}(q,n_{1},n_{2})
if and only if there exist
c_{1},c_{2}\in\{0,1\}
and
r_{1},r_{2}\in\{-1,0,+1\}
such that

1.

if
m_{i}=0
then
c_{i}=0
, otherwise if
m_{i}>0
, then
c_{i}=1
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
,

2.

n_{i}=m_{i}+r_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
,

3.

(q,r_{1},r_{2})\in\delta(p,c_{1},c_{2})
, and

4.

we assume if
c_{i}=0
then
r_{i}\neq-1
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Essentially, the machine checks in every state whether the counters equal
0
and then changes the value of each counter by at most one per transition before entering a new state. A
computation
is a sequence of configurations. An
accepting computation
of
A
is a sequence
C_{1},...,C_{n}\in(Q\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})^{n}
with

for all

, and

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n>0
.

We
encode
configurations of
A
by assuming
Q=\{q_{0},...,q_{e}\}
for some
e\in\mathbb{N}
and defining a function
\enc

:

Q\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\{0,\#\}^{*}
by

\enc(q_{i},m_{1},m_{2}):=0^{x+i}\#0^{c_{1}+y_{2}\cdot m_{1}}\#0^{c_{2}+y_{2}\cdot m_{2}}

for some numbers
x,c_{1},y_{2},c_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. The values for these numbers depend on the construction of the respective proofs and are not specified here. This is extended to encodings of computations by defining for every
n\geq 1
and every sequence

\enc(C_{1},...,C_{n}):=\#\#\ \enc(C_{1})\ \#\#\ ...\ \#\#\ \enc(C_{n})\ \#\#.

Encodings of this kind are used to prove different undecidability results regarding the inclusion problem for erasing relational pattern languages for various considered relations, in particular the results in Subsection
4.2
.
For some nondeterministic 2-counter automaton without input

and let

The emptiness problem for deterministic 2-counter-automata is undecidable (cf. e.g.
[

22

18

So, there exists
(\tilde{r},x_{i},x_{j})\in[r_{\alpha}]
such that
(\tilde{r},x_{i},x_{j})\notin[r_{\beta}]
.
Select
h\in H_{r_{\beta}}
such that
h
substitutes all variables in
\beta
to some letter, i.e.,
|h(x)|=1
for all
x\in\var(\beta)
.
In particular, let
h(x_{i})=h(x_{k})=\mathtt{a}
, for some
\mathtt{a}\in\Sigma
and all
(\tilde{r},x_{i},x_{k})\in[r_{\beta}]
.
Finally, set
h(x_{j})=h(x_{k^{\prime}})=\mathtt{b}\neq\mathtt{a}=h(x_{i})
, for all other variables
x_{k^{\prime}}\in\var(\beta)
which are not already
set to
h(x_{i})=\mathtt{a}
and some letter
\mathtt{b}\in\Sigma
with
\mathtt{a}\neq\mathtt{b}
.
Then, we have that
h(\beta)\in L_{NE}(\beta,[r_{\beta}])
but
h(\beta)\notin L_{NE}(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}])

as there cannot exist any substitution
h^{\prime}\in H_{r_{\alpha}}
with
h^{\prime}(x_{i})\neq h^{\prime}(x_{j})
as
(\tilde{r},x_{i},x_{j})\in[r_{\alpha}]
and by the assumption that
\tilde{r}
is an equivalence relation. This is a contradiction to
L_{NE}(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}]])=L_{NE}(\beta,[r_{\beta}])
.
So, we must have
[r_{\alpha}]=[r_{\beta}]
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
:

Proof

(
\Rightarrow
): Assume
L_{NE}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})=L_{NE}(\beta,r_{\beta})
.
As
\tilde{r}
is an equivalence relation, we get
by Lemma
3
that
L_{NE}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})=L_{NE}(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}])

and
L_{NE}(\beta,r_{\beta})=L_{NE}(\beta,[r_{\beta}])
.
Hence,
L_{NE}(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}])=L_{NE}(\beta,[r_{\beta}])
, and we get by Lemma
5

that
\alpha=\beta
and
[r_{\alpha}]=[r_{\beta}]
. Thus, we have
(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}])=(\beta,[r_{\beta}])
.

(
\Leftarrow
): Assume
(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}])=(\beta,[r_{\beta}])
.
Then, by Lemma
5
we have
L_{NE}(\alpha,[r_{\alpha}])=L_{NE}(\beta,[r_{\beta}])

and by Lemma
3
we get that
L_{NE}(\alpha,r_{\alpha})=L_{NE}(\beta,r_{\beta})
using the same arguments from the other direction.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

3
:

Proof

First, we notice that all relations in
\{r_{=},r_{ab},r_{con^{+}}\}
are equivalence relations that are antisymmetric on elements of length
1
.
Now, we normalize
(\alpha,r_{\alpha})
and
(\beta,r_{\beta})
such that all variables are introduced with an index in increasing manner, starting with
x_{1}
in both relational patterns. Then, we check in linear time whether
\alpha=\beta
. If that is the case, we check whether
[r_{\alpha}]=[r_{\beta}]
by constructing the sets representing reflexive, symmetric, and transitive closure of related variables. This can be done in linear time in the lengths of the patterns by scanning them and filling up the resulting sets. The total number of elements in these sets will not exceed the total number of variables in the patterns. If these sets are equal for both relational patterns, we return true, applying
Theorem

5.1
. In any other case, we return false. ∎
