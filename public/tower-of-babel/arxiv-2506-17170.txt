Partition function for several Ising model interface structures
Alessio Squarcini, Piotr Nowakowski, Douglas B. Abraham, Anna Maciołek

Abstract
We employ a procedure that enables us to calculate the excess free energies for a finite Ising cylinder with domain walls analytically. This procedure transparently covers all possible configurations of the domain walls under given boundary conditions and allows for a physical interpretation in terms of coarse-grained quantities such as surface and point tensions. The resulting integrals contain all the information about finite-size effects; we extract them by careful asymptotic analysis using the steepest descent method. To this end, we exactly determine the steepest descent path and analyse its features. For the general class of integrals, which are usually found in the study of systems with inclined domain walls, knowledge of the steepest descent path is necessary to detect possible intersections with poles of the integrand in the complex plane.

I
Introduction

Two crucial advances in the theory of phase transitions were made in 1936 and 1944. In the first, Peierls
Peierls 1936
demonstrated that taking the Ising model on a square lattice and fixing the boundary spins to be all
+
would cause the average magnetisation of a central spin to be strictly positive for low enough temperatures, no matter how large the system is. There was a slight lacuna in this work which was fixed independently by Dobrushin
Dobrushin 1968
and by Griffiths
Griffiths 1964
;
Griffiths 1971
.

The second advance was made in 1944, by Onsager
Onsager 1944
. He showed that in the two-dimensional Ising model there is a critical temperature
T_{\mathrm{c}}
at which the specific heat diverges as
\ln\left|(T_{\mathrm{c}}-T)/T_{\mathrm{c}}\right|
(provided the lattice is infinite in extent).
Further, Onsager gave a definition of surface tension which was carefully tailored to make it amenable to the techniques he had developed. It is based on a grain boundary of reversed bonds, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(a)
, and assumes the existence of phase separation. The incremental free energy associated with this is strictly non-zero per unit length for all
T<T_{\mathrm{c}}
(the temperature at which the free energy is singular).
Another idea for introducing phase separation lines was that of Gallavotti, Martin-Löf and Miracle-Solé
Gallavotti et al. 1973
:
suppose we take the Peierls setup and impose the additional restriction that
N_{+}+N_{-}=N
, where
N_{+}

and
N_{-}
are the number of up
and down spins, respectively, and these are fixed. Then we might hope for the picture as the one shown in Fig.
1
(b)
. The
+
phase is pinned to the boundary, the
-
phase has a fluctuating boundary and “floats around” embedded in the
+
phase as shown in Fig.
1
(b)
. The boundary
\partial_{-}
fluctuates as
N^{1/2}
as shown in remarkable work by Gallavotti
Gallavotti 1972
;
Gallavotti 1999
.

The leverage of Dobrushin boundary conditions enables geometrically controlled tuning of interface configurations, while preserving exact solvability.

Closed-form expressions for the incremental free energies in systems in which the interface is pinned by

Recently, interest in incremental free energies for certain interface configurations has gained new momentum, as they have proven essential for understanding the emergence of long-range order in the Ising “network” model introduced in Refs
Abraham et al. 2014
;
Abraham et al. 2017
;
Abraham et al. 2024

The purpose of this paper is to provide in some detail the exact solution for incremental free energies associated with the interfacial structures shown in Fig.
2
, which were not presented before.
Specifically, we consider the edge-to-edge transfer matrix for the strip shown in Fig.
2
and for a strip with the same lower edge, but whose upper edge has all spins fixed at
+
(see Figs
3
and
4
, respectively).
The construction of an exact solution in these cases is feasible because the block of spin rotation operators that flips the spins at the strip edge between positions
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
is quadratic in the lattice fermions.
The action of the transfer operator along the cylinder axis, propagates this boundary condition throughout the system; the direction of the propagation

(0,1)
is equivalent to the time direction for the two fermions emitted by the boundary.
The resulting phase separation lines, due to topological constraints, cannot end in the middle of the system but at its boundary, as shown in Fig.
2
. Technically, the solution can be obtained by employing a generalised version of Wick’s theorem in which the in and out states are not the same
Abraham 2012
. Within this framework, the Wick contractions of lattice fermions can be handled by means of diagrammatic techniques and the classification of the resulting graphs is in one-to-one correspondence with the interfacial structures shown in Fig.
2
.
Anticipating some results, the Wick contraction for the cross-connected interfaces—not represented by Peierls contours—will acquire an overall minus sign due to the fermionic statistics under particle exchange.

The advantage in adopting the transfer along
(0,1)
is that the final result for the incremental free energy can be expressed as a single integral involving a function that depends on the type of interfacial configuration,
e.g.
, a single interface as in Fig.
2
(a)
or a single interface as in Fig.
2
(c)
. This is a significant simplification over the approach based on the transfer along
(1,0)
. The latter would yield the excess free energy in the form of a summation over a discrete set of momenta, which can ultimately be calculated by employing a summation kernel technique, as known for the Casimir effect
Abraham and Maciołek 2010
;
Abraham and Maciołek 2013
. In this sense, the generalised Wick theorem within the Dobrushin program allows to perform the mode summation in an implicit way. The resulting single integral contains all information about finite size effects that eventually can be extracted by a careful asymptotic analysis.

In order to compute a single integral giving a incremental free energy or to evaluate this integral asymptotically, we will need to perform saddle point calculations; the latter requires knowledge of the steepest descent path (SDP), which was never explored before. We will show that the SDP can be found exactly and in closed form.

II
Formulation

We consider the planar Ising model on a square lattice
Onsager 1944
with the Hamiltonian given by

E=-J_{1}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\sigma_{i,j}\sigma_{i+1,j}-J_{2}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\sigma_{i,j}\sigma_{i,j+1},

(1)

with
J_{1},J_{2}>0
and spin variables
\sigma_{i,j}=\pm 1
, where the sums are restricted to the nearest neighbouring pairs of sites in a square lattice.
We wrap the lattice into a cylinder of circumference
M
and height
N
with axis along the (vertical) lattice direction
(0,1)
.
We introduce domain walls into the system by setting the value of the boundary spins equal to
\sigma_{i,j}=+1
everywhere, except for the segments where the spins are
\sigma_{i,j}=-1
, as shown in Fig.
3
.

The incremental free energy associated with the insertion of these domain walls is given by
-\ln(Z_{+-}/Z_{++})
, where
Z_{+-}(N,M|s,s_{1})
is the partition function of the system depicted in Fig.
3
and
Z_{++}(N,M)
is the partition function for the cylinder with no reversed spins on both edges.
We show below that this quantity includes contributions from the interface configurations shown in Fig.
2
. Taking the limit
M\rightarrow\infty
followed by
s_{1}\rightarrow\infty
eliminates possible interactions between the two domains and allows us to determine the incremental free energy for only one of them. We denote

\zeta_{\mathrm{A}}\left(N,s\right)=\lim_{s_{1}\to\infty}\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{Z_{+-}\left(N,M|s,s_{1}\right)}{Z_{++}\left(N,M\right)}.

(2)

A completely different type of boundary-induced effects can be expected when only the lower edge has a finite segment of reversed spins, as illustrated in Fig.
4
. This induces a domain wall whose endpoints are attached to the lower edge and separated by
s
lattice sites. We denote

\zeta_{\mathrm{B}}\left(N,s\right)=\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{Z_{+-}\left(N,M|s\right)}{Z_{++}\left(N,M\right)},

(3)

where
Z_{+-}(N,M|s)
is the partition function for the lattice shown in Fig.
4
, which gives us the associated incremental free energy.

Next, we use a transfer matrix formalism with cyclic boundary conditions in the
(1,0)
direction in order to derive the quantities
\zeta_{\mathrm{B}}
and
\zeta_{\mathrm{A}}
.

We introduce the transfer operator
\mathsf{V}_{1}

\mathsf{V}_{1}=\exp\left[-K_{1}^{\star}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sigma_{m}^{z}\right],

(4)

which corresponds to the transfer between neighbouring rings of the cylindrical lattice
Abraham 1971
. The reduced coupling
K_{1}
is defined by
K_{1}=\beta J_{1}
,
K_{1}^{\star}
is the dual coupling defined by
\tanh(K_{1}^{\star})=\exp(-2K_{1})
. The operator
\sigma_{m}^{\mu}
, defined by

\sigma_{m}^{\mu}=\underbrace{\mathsf{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{I}}_{m-1}\otimes\sigma^{\mu}\otimes\mathsf{I}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{I}\,,\qquad\mu=x,y,z\,,

(5)

acts as a Pauli spin operator
\sigma^{\mu}
on site
m
and as the identity elsewhere. Following the convention of
Schultz et al. 1964
, we choose a representation for Pauli spin matrices in which
-\sigma^{z}
—for historical reasons—is identified with the spin flip operator and
\sigma^{x}
is the magnetization operator. Strictly speaking, the transfer operator
\mathsf{V}_{1}
is defined up to an overall factor, which however simplifies in the computation of partition function ratios, and for this reason such a factor is not shown here. The transfer within each horizontal row in the cylindrical lattice is taken into account by the operator
\mathsf{V}_{2}
,

\mathsf{V}_{2}=\exp\left[K_{2}\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}\sigma_{m}^{x}\sigma_{m+1}^{x}+K_{2}\sigma_{M}^{x}\sigma_{1}^{x}\right],

(6)

with
K_{2}=\beta J_{2}
. The occurrence of the term
\sigma_{M}^{x}\sigma_{1}^{x}
is signalling the fact that each horizontal row is closed, forming a ring of circumference
M
. The edge with spins locked all up or down, respectively, is indicated with
|\pm\rangle
. The latter is an eigenstate of
\sigma_{m}^{x}
such that
\sigma_{m}^{x}|\pm\rangle=\pm|\pm\rangle
for
1\leqslant m\leqslant M
. The fixed spin states
|\pm\rangle
are constructed as follows:

|\pm\rangle=2^{-M/2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\left(1\pm\sigma_{m}^{x}\right)|0\rangle,

(7)

where
|0\rangle
is an eigenstate of the spin flip operator with eigenvalue
+1
,
i.e.
,
-\sigma_{m}^{z}|0\rangle=|0\rangle
for
1\leqslant m\leqslant M
.

A boundary configuration with pointing down spins between
s_{1}\geqslant 1
and
s_{2}>s_{1}
, with
s_{2}\leqslant M
, is given by

|s_{1},s_{2};+\rangle=\prod_{j=s_{1}}^{s_{2}}(-\sigma_{j}^{z})|+\rangle,

(8)

which has the property

\sigma_{s}^{x}|s_{1},s_{2};+\rangle=\begin{cases}-|s_{1},s_{2};+\rangle&\text{if}\quad s_{1}\leqslant s\leqslant s_{2},\\
+|s_{1},s_{2};+\rangle&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(9)

Using this notation we can write the partition function ratio for the lattice in Fig.
4

\frac{Z_{+-}\left(M,N|s\right)}{Z_{++}\left(M,N\right)}=\frac{\langle+|\left(\mathsf{V}_{1}\mathsf{V}_{2}\right)^{N-1}\mathsf{V}_{1}|1,s+1;+\rangle}{\langle+|\left(\mathsf{V}_{1}\mathsf{V}_{2}\right)^{N-1}\mathsf{V}_{1}|+\rangle}.

(10)

The calculation proceeds by introducing the symmetrised product
\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}_{2}^{1/2}\mathsf{V}_{1}^{\vphantom{1/2}}\mathsf{V}_{2}^{1/2}

that, in order to diagonalize the symmetrized forms of the transfer operators, it is a good idea to introduce the Jordan–Wigner transformation and the lattice spinors
\Gamma_{m}
. These are introduced in the following together with Fermi lattice fields
f_{m}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}
and

defined by

(11)

where
\sigma_{m}^{\pm}
are the raising/lowering operators
\sigma_{m}^{\pm}=(\sigma_{m}^{x}\pm{\operatorname{i}}\sigma_{m}^{y})/2
, and

\mathcal{P}_{m}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\left(-\sigma_{\ell}^{z}\right)

(12)

is the Jordan–Wigner tail. We note that Jordan–Wigner tail operators satisfy
\mathcal{P}_{M}|+\rangle=|-\rangle
. It then follows that Fermi lattice fields satisfy the anti-commutation relations

\left[f_{n},f_{m}\right]_{+}=0,\qquad\left[f_{n}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}},f_{m}^{{\dagger}}\right]_{+}=\delta_{n,m},

(13)

as required for fermions. The state
|0\rangle
is a
f
-vacuum, namely
f_{m}|0\rangle=0
for
1\leqslant m\leqslant M
. Then, we introduce Kaufman spinors by means of

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2m-1}

\displaystyle=f_{m}^{{\dagger}}+f_{m}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}=\mathcal{P}_{m-1}\sigma_{m}^{x},

(14a)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2m}

\displaystyle=-{\operatorname{i}}\left(f_{m}^{{\dagger}}-f_{m}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\right)=-{\operatorname{i}}\mathcal{P}_{m-1}\sigma_{m}^{y},

for

\mathcal{P}_{0}\equiv\mathsf{I}

\left[\Gamma_{n},\Gamma_{m}\right]_{+}=2\delta_{n,m}.

(15)

|+\rangle

4

\mathcal{P}_{s_{1}-1}\mathcal{P}_{s_{2}}=\prod_{j=s_{1}}^{s_{2}}(-\sigma_{j}^{z}).

(16)

Therefore the state in Eq. (
8

|s_{1},s_{2};+\rangle=\mathcal{P}_{s_{1}-1}\mathcal{P}_{s_{2}}|+\rangle.

(17)

By bringing in Kaufman spinors, the above becomes

|s_{1},s_{2};+\rangle=-\mathcal{P}_{s_{1}-1}\sigma_{s_{1}}^{x}\mathcal{P}_{s_{2}}\sigma_{s_{2}+1}^{x}|+\rangle\\

R(s_{1},1+s_{2})

|+\rangle

In terms of fermion operators, the block rotation operator becomes

R\left(s_{1},1+s_{2}\right)={\color[rgb]{0.4961,0,0.4961}-}\left(f_{s_{1}}+f_{s_{1}}^{{\dagger}}\right)\left(f_{1+s_{2}}+f_{1+s_{2}}^{{\dagger}}\right).

(19)

|+\rangle
is an eigenstate of
\mathsf{V}_{2}

\mathsf{V}_{2}|\pm\rangle=\exp(MK_{2})|\pm\rangle

\operatorname{e}^{{\operatorname{i}}K\Gamma_{n}\Gamma_{m}}\Gamma_{m}\operatorname{e}^{-{\operatorname{i}}K\Gamma_{n}\Gamma_{m}}=\operatorname{e}^{2{\operatorname{i}}K\Gamma_{n}\Gamma_{m}}\Gamma_{m},

\frac{Z_{+-}\left(M,N|s\right)}{Z_{++}\left(M,N\right)}=\frac{\langle+|\mathsf{V}^{N}R\left(1,1+s\right)|+\rangle}{\langle+|\mathsf{V}^{N}|+\rangle}.

We then express the hard edge states
|\pm\rangle
in terms of the (degenerate) maximal eigenvectors of
\mathsf{V}_{2}
, namely

|\pm\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|\Phi_{+}^{0}\rangle\pm|\Phi_{-}^{0}\rangle\right).

(22)

By using projection operators

\mathcal{P}\left(\pm\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1\pm\mathcal{P}_{M}\right),

(23)

we can write

\mathsf{V}_{2}=\mathcal{P}\left(+\right)\mathsf{V}_{2}\left(+\right)+\mathcal{P}\left(-\right)\mathsf{V}_{2}\left(-\right),

(24)

where
\mathsf{V}_{2}(\pm)

\mathsf{V}_{2}=\exp\left[{\operatorname{i}}K_{2}\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}\Gamma_{2m}\Gamma_{2m+1}-{\operatorname{i}}K_{2}\mathcal{P}_{M}\Gamma_{2M}\Gamma_{1}\right],

with the appropriate eigenvalue of the parity operator

\pm 1

, where
\mathsf{V}\left(\pm\right)
is the corresponding symmetrised transfer matrix. Since the limit
M\rightarrow\infty
is performed, both the terms are asymptotically identical. We will therefore focus on one of the terms in the following analysis:

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{Z_{+-}\left(M,N|s\right)}{Z_{++}\left(M,N\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\Phi_{+}^{0}|\left[\mathsf{V}(+)\right]^{N}R\left(1,1+s\right)|\Phi_{+}^{0}\rangle}{\langle\Phi_{+}^{0}|\left[\mathsf{V}(+)\right]^{N}|\Phi_{+}^{0}\rangle}.

III
Steepest descent path

Integrals of the type (
27
) and (
38
) belong to a much more general class that is often found in the calculation of correlation functions and excess free energies in the Ising model. Generally, these integrals can be brought to the form

I\left(M,N\right)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\operatorname{d}\omega}{2\pi}g\left(\omega\right)\operatorname{e}^{-N\gamma\left(\omega\right)+{\operatorname{i}}M\omega},

(39)

where
g\left(\omega\right)
is some function whose actual expression depends on the problem at hand. The geometrical parameters
N
and
M
are usually considered to be large compared to the lattice spacing, and the aim is to extract the asymptotic behaviour of
I\left(M,N\right)
when
N,M\gg 1
with fixed ratio
M/N
. It is clear that this task brings us to the realm of the asymptotic analysis of integrals. In particular, the analysis of
I\left(M,N\right)
requires the application of standard techniques such as the steepest descent path method and complex analysis
Bleistein and Handelsman 1986
. Here we address this problem in full generality without relying on any specific choice for the function
g\left(\omega\right)
. We only require that
g\left(\omega\right)
is an analytic complex function for
\operatorname{Re}\omega\geqslant 0
and
-\pi\leqslant\operatorname{Im}\omega\leqslant\pi
that satisfies the conditions

\displaystyle g\left(-\pi+{\operatorname{i}}v\right)

\displaystyle=g\left(\pi+{\operatorname{i}}v\right),

\displaystyle\text{for any }v\geqslant 0,

(40a)

\displaystyle g\left(\omega\right)

\displaystyle\to 0,

\displaystyle\text{for }\operatorname{Im}\omega\to+\infty.

(40b)

The analysis will proceed through the following stages: First, we determine the location of the saddle point and the shape of steepest descent path. Second, we modify the integration contour to incorporate the steepest decent path, and third, we calculate asymptotic expansion of the integral for large size of the system
M
and
N
. Finally, we will apply our approach to the specific cases of incremental free energies as provided by

\zeta_{\mathrm{A}}
and
\zeta_{\mathrm{B}}
.

III.1
Saddle point and steepest descent path

We begin the analysis of Eq. (
39
) by locating the saddle point of the function in the exponent. As anticipated,
M
and
N
are taken to be large with a finite ratio. Thus, we have
M,N\to\infty
with the ratio parametrized by the angle
\vartheta
as follows:
M=[N\tan\vartheta]
,
\vartheta\in[0,\pi/2)
, where the notation
[\cdot]
stands for the integer part. The exponential part of the integral (
39
) containing the large parameters can be written as
\exp\left[-N\gamma\left(\omega\right)+{\operatorname{i}}M\omega\right]\equiv\exp\left[-NL\left(\omega\right)\right]
with
L\left(\omega\right)=\gamma\left(\omega\right)-{\operatorname{i}}\omega\tan\vartheta
. The saddle point
\omega_{\mathrm{s}}
is determined as the solution of
L^{(1)}\left(\omega\right)=0
,
i.e.
,

\gamma^{(1)}\left(\omega_{\mathrm{s}}\right)={\operatorname{i}}M/N={\operatorname{i}}\tan\vartheta,

(41)

where the superscript denotes the first derivative with respect to
\omega
. It is straightforward to check (see Sec.
1
of Supplementary Information (SI)) that the saddle point is
unique
and it is located on the imaginary axis,
\omega_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)={\operatorname{i}}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
with positive
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
. The function
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
is known for any
\vartheta\in\left[0,\pi/2\right)
and arbitrary couplings
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, the expression is derived in SI, see Eq. (
S4b
). In Sec.
1
of SI we also prove that
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
is strictly monotonically increasing for
\vartheta\in\left(0,\pi/2\right)
with the following limiting values:
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(0\right)=0
and
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\pi/2\right)=\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)
, where
\hat{\gamma}\left(\omega\right)
is
\gamma\left(\omega\right)
given by Eq. (
28
) with
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
interchanged.

We are now in the position to discuss the steepest descent path (SDP) and its main properties. By definition, the imaginary part of
L\left(\omega\right)
stays constant along the steepest descent path. Since
\operatorname{Im}\left(L\left(\omega\right)\right)
is zero at the saddle point, along the SDP the following condition is satisfied

\operatorname{Im}\left[N\gamma\left(\omega\right)-{\operatorname{i}}M\omega\right]=0,

(42)

and consequently, the exponential factor in Eq. (
39
) is purely real along the SDP.

Finding the analytic expression for the SDP is a conceptually simple exercise, although in the case of arbitrary
\vartheta
it requires cumbersome calculations. A detailed account on the analytic expression of the SDP, its derivation and properties, are provided in Sec.
3
of SI. Here, we outline the main ideas underlying the exact determination of the SDP and, on the way, we will present its salient features.
We introduce the complex wave number
\omega=u+{\operatorname{i}}v
with
u
and
v
real numbers and parametrize a point on the SDP with the coordinates
\left(u,v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)\right)
for
u\in\left(-\pi,\pi\right)
. The Onsager function becomes

\gamma\left(u+{\operatorname{i}}v\right)=\gamma_{1}\left(u,v\right)+{\operatorname{i}}\gamma_{2}\left(u,v\right),

(43)

where
\gamma_{1}\left(u,v\right)
and
\gamma_{2}\left(u,v\right)\in\mathbb{R}
are the real and imaginary parts of
\gamma\left(u+{\operatorname{i}}v\right)
, respectively. By equating real and imaginary parts of
\cosh\gamma\left(u+\mathrm{i}v\right)
obtained from

\cosh\gamma\left(u+{\operatorname{i}}v\right)=c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}-s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\cos\left(u+{\operatorname{i}}v\right),

(44)

we find the pair of equations

\displaystyle\cosh\gamma_{1}\cos\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}-s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\cos u\cosh v\equiv\mathcal{A}\left(u,v\right),

(45a)

\displaystyle\sinh\gamma_{1}\sin\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\sin u\sinh v\equiv\mathcal{B}\left(u,v\right).

(45b)

Since, along the SDP the imaginary part of the function
L\left(\omega\right)=\gamma\left(\omega\right)-{\operatorname{i}}\omega\tan\vartheta
vanishes,

\gamma_{2}\left(u,v\right)=u\tan\vartheta.

(46)

Thus, the SDP is implicitly given via

\frac{\mathcal{A}^{2}\left(u,v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)\right)}{\cos^{2}\left(u\tan\vartheta\right)}-\frac{\mathcal{B}^{2}\left(u,v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)\right)}{\sin^{2}\left(u\tan\vartheta\right)}=1.

(47)

In Sec.
3
of SI we solve this equation in a closed form and provide analytical expression for the SDP. Here, we only analyse the simple and instructive case of isotropic lattice (
K_{1}=K_{2}\equiv K
) and
\vartheta=\pi/4
.
In this situation, the function
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)
is given by the solution of

\cos u\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)=cc^{*}/2,

(48)

where
c=\cosh 2K
,
c^{*}=\cosh 2K^{*}
,
-\pi/2<u<\pi/2
, and the saddle point is located at
u=0
and
\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(0\right)=\cosh v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\pi/4\right)=cc^{*}/2
. From the exact solution (
48
) we observe that the SDP is symmetric around the imaginary axis. Moreover, the SDP is convex and it diverges to infinity for
u\to\pm\pi/4
.

In SI we show that the mirror symmetry,
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)=v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(-u\right)
, is a general property valid for any
\vartheta
. Furthermore, the function
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)
diverges for
u\to\pm u_{\rm ex}\left(\vartheta\right)
with
u_{\rm ex}\left(\vartheta\right)=\pi/(1+\tan\vartheta)<\pi
. This means that SDP does not intersect the side lines
u=\pm\pi
. Additionally, it is possible to verify numerically that
\operatorname{d}v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)/\operatorname{d}u>0
for
u>0
—this property implies that the saddle point is located in the minimum of
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)
.

In Appendix
B
, we present a typical behaviour of the function
L\left(\omega\right)
on the complex plane.
In Fig.
5
we show typical shape of SDP for a fixed temperature and various angles
\vartheta
. The figure illustrates the location of the saddle point and the divergence of SDP upon approaching the angle-dependent exit point
u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
.

Incremental free energies: leading asymptotics

Let us see how the above framework can be effectively used to evaluate certain partition functions in finite geometries. Strictly speaking, the expressions (

IV
Point tension

We can now provide an interpretation of the quantity (

(68)

To the above we can associate an incremental free energy
\mathcal{F}
such that
\zeta_{\mathrm{A}}=\exp\left(-\mathcal{F}\right)

With this identification, it is clear that

is the energetic cost of a straight interface with inclination angle
\vartheta
and length
\ell
. The point tension
\tau_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\vartheta\right)
is the energy stemming from each pinning point where the interface touches the wall. Lastly, the factor
\zeta_{\rm f}
takes into account the thermal fluctuations around the straight interface connecting the pinning points. This term can be interpreted as an entropic contribution.

(71)

The point tension

Rattling effect for incremental free energies

In Section

\zeta_{\mathrm{A}}
and

V.1
Inclined interface

Let us first consider the expression for

with

g_{\mathrm{A}}^{(n)}\left(\omega\right)=\frac{2}{1+\cos\delta^{*}\left(\omega\right)}\left[-\tan^{2}\left(\delta^{*}\left(\omega\right)/2\right)\right]^{n}.

(73)

Each term in the above series is an integral that is of the form given in Eq. (
39
). Therefore, the framework of Section
III

Absolute convergence of series in Eq. (

for an inclined interface

where

and

III.1
for each term in the series (

). It is clear that now we have a
sequence of saddle points
parametrized by the odd integer
2n+1

n

Comparing the above relation with Eq. (

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we describe in detail the exact calculation of the incremental free energies associated with domain walls induced by Dobrushin boundary conditions in a ferromagnetic planar Ising lattice.

We have given closed-form expressions for the incremental free energies and presented their coarse-grained interpretation in terms of the “rattling phenomenon” in which the domain wall undergoes multiple reflections from the walls, an analogous effect has been pointed out in the context of the Lorentz gas or Ehrenfest wind-tree model (see
Abraham and Gates 1974
and references therein). This analysis shows that the configurations presented in Fig. 2(a) and (c) are topologically distinct, since they involve an even and odd number of reflections, respectively.

The asymptotic forms of the incremental free energies obtained using the steepest descent method are given in terms of the angle-dependent surface tension
\tau(\vartheta)
, the point tension
\tau_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\vartheta\right)
, the point tension for the fluctuating points of contact
\tau_{\mathrm{fcp}}\left(\vartheta\right)
, and an entropic term. We have studied the main properties of
\tau(\vartheta)
, such as its maximum, minimum, and inflection points for the general case of lattices with anisotropic nearest neighbour interactions. Among the various results, we found a relationship between
\tau_{\mathrm{p}}
and
\tau_{\mathrm{fcp}}
, showing that when one of the two diverges the other one must vanish.

This paper also provides a comprehensive description of the steepest descent path method previously used in the analysis of the asymptotic correlation function for the planar Ising model. We then specialize this method for the correlation functions of boundary operators involved in the calculation of incremental free energies. In particular, we illustrate how the generalized Wick theorem in Dobrushin’s program allows us to obtain the incremental free energies in the form of a single integral whose saddle point and asymptotic behaviour are handled exactly, as shown in the illustration. This approach offers a valuable perspective.

Saddle point

This section provides a summary of properties of the saddle point of the function
L\left(\omega\right)=\gamma\left(\omega\right)-\mathrm{i}\omega\tan\vartheta
for arbitrary angle
0\leqslant\vartheta\leqslant\pi/2
and arbitrary couplings
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
. The interested reader can find previously existing results on Wulff construction and equilibrium shapes of Ising crystals and related models in Refs.
Abraham 1984
;
Rottman and Wortis 1981
;
Avron et al. 1982
, in the review articles
Abraham 1986
;
Rottman and Wortis 1984
;
Pfister 2009
;
Pfister 2010
, and references therein.
The saddle point, given by the solution of Eq. (
41
), is located on the imaginary axis
\omega_{\mathrm{s}}=\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
, where the function
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
fulfils the condition

F(v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta))=\tan\vartheta,

(S1a)

where the function
F\left(v\right)=-\mathrm{i}\gamma^{\left(1\right)}\left(\mathrm{i}v\right)
is given by

F(v)=s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\frac{\sinh v}{\sinh\gamma({\operatorname{i}}v)},\qquad\text{with }\cosh\gamma(\mathrm{i}v)=c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}-s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\cosh v,

(S1b)

as can be calculated from Eq. (
28
). For completeness, we recall the definition of the simplified notation that we use in this manuscript:
s_{i}=\sinh 2K_{i}
,
s^{\star}_{i}=\sinh 2K_{i}^{\star}
,
c_{i}=\cosh 2K_{i}
,
c^{\star}_{i}=\cosh 2K_{i}^{\star}
, for
i=1,2
.

Lemma 1

(Existence, uniqueness and differentiability of
v_{\mathrm{s}}
)
.

For
\vartheta\in\left[0,\pi/2\right]
the condition (
S1
) has exactly one real solution and the defined function
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
is differentiable and it is strictly increasing upon increasing
\vartheta
.

Proof.
We start from analysing the properties of the Onsager
\gamma\left(\omega\right)
function for imaginary arguments. As follows from Eq. (
S1b
), upon increasing
v
the value of
\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v\right)
decreases from
\gamma\left(0\right)=2K_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}-2K_{1}^{\star}
for
v=0
to
0
for
v=\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)=2K_{2}^{\star}-2K_{1}^{\vphantom{\star}}
, where
\hat{\gamma}\left(\omega\right)
is given by

\cosh\hat{\gamma}(\omega)=c_{1}^{\vphantom{\star}}c_{2}^{\star}-s_{1}^{\vphantom{\star}}s_{2}^{\star}\cos\omega,

(S2)

i.e.
, it is Onsager
\gamma\left(\omega\right)
function with
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
interchanged. Moreover, the function
\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v\right)
is differentiable and, therefore, also
F\left(v\right)
is differentiable for
0\leqslant v<\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)
.

Since
\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v\right)
is a decreasing function of
v
, also
\sinh\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v\right)
is a decreasing function of
v
, and from Eq. (
S1b
) it follows that the function
F\left(v\right)
is a strictly increasing function of
v
. As we have calculated,
F\left(0\right)=0
and
\lim_{v\to\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)^{-}}F\left(v\right)=+\infty
, and therefore Eq. (
S1b
) has exactly one solution
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
, for
0\leqslant\vartheta\leqslant\pi/2
.

Finally, we note that it can be easily checked that
\operatorname{d}F/\operatorname{d}v>0
, and from the implicit function theorem it follows that
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
is differentiable and it is an increasing function of its argument.
\square

The above calculations allow us to determine the limiting values of
v_{\mathrm{s}}

v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(0\right)=0,\qquad\lim_{\vartheta\to\pi/2}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)=\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right).

(S3)

The formula for the saddle point
v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)
can be derived in a closed form for any value of
\vartheta
and for arbitrary couplings; this is the result of the following

Lemma 2

(Formula for
v_{\mathrm{s}}
)
.

For
\vartheta=\pi/4
the saddle point location is given by

\cosh v_{\mathrm{s}}(\pi/4)=\frac{(s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}})^{2}+(c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}})^{2}-1}{2s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}}\,.

(S4a)

For
0\leqslant\vartheta<\pi/2
and
\vartheta\neq\pi/4
the saddle point location
v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)
is given by

\cosh v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)=\frac{-V(\vartheta)+\sqrt{V^{2}(\vartheta)-U(\vartheta)W(\vartheta)}}{U(\vartheta)}\,,

(S4b)

where

\displaystyle U(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=(s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}})^{2}\bigl[1-\tan^{2}\vartheta\bigr],

(S5a)

\displaystyle V(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\tan^{2}\vartheta,

(S5b)

\displaystyle W(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\tan^{2}\vartheta-(c_{1}^{\star}c_{2})^{2}\tan^{2}\vartheta-(s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}})^{2}.

(S5c)

Proof.
Eq. (
S4b
) can be obtained by taking the square of Eq. (
S1a
) and expressing
\sinh^{2}v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)
,
\sinh^{2}\gamma({\operatorname{i}}v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta))
in terms of
\cosh v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)
and its powers. The result is the quadratic equation in the variable
\cosh v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)

U(\vartheta)\cosh^{2}v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)+2V(\vartheta)\cosh v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)+W(\vartheta)=0,

(S6)

with
U(\vartheta)
,
V(\vartheta)
, and
W(\vartheta)
given by (
S5
). The sign “
+
” in front of the square root in Eq. (
S4b
) can be determined by requiring the consistency with values of
v_{\mathrm{s}}
for
\vartheta=0
and
\vartheta\to\pi/2
given in Eq. (
S3
). Alternatively, one can consider the critical point for the symmetric lattice (for which
ss^{\star}=1
and
cc^{\star}=2
). In this case Eq. (
S4b
) yields
\cosh v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)=1
, meaning that
v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)=0
for all possible values of
\vartheta
.

In the special case of
\vartheta=\pi/4
,
U\left(\vartheta\right)=0
and the quadratic equation (
S6
) reduces to a linear expression in
\cosh v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)
and Eq. (
S4a
) follows straightforwardly.
\square

2
Angle-dependent surface tension

The angle-dependent surface tension
\tau(\vartheta)
, defined by Eqs. (
50
), is given by

\tau(\vartheta)=\gamma({\operatorname{i}}v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta))\cos\vartheta+v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)\sin\vartheta.

(S7)

Formally, this definition is valid only for
0\leqslant\vartheta<\pi/2
,
i.e.
, within domain of the function
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
. Motivated by the physical interpretation of
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
as a surface tension of the interface forming an angle
\vartheta
with the vertical direction of the lattice, we naturally extend this definition to an arbitrary angle by requiring that
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
is symmetric around
\vartheta=0
and
\vartheta=\pi/2
, and by assuming that it is continues for
\vartheta=\pi/2
. This leads to the following conditions

\tau\left(\vartheta\right)=\tau\left(-\vartheta\right),\qquad\tau\left(\pi/2+\vartheta\right)=\tau\left(\pi/2-\vartheta\right),\qquad\tau\left(\pi/2\right)=\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right).

(S8)

We note that the two symmetries of the surface tension follow from invariance of the lattice under reflections along vertical and horizontal directions.

Lemma 3

(Differentiability of
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
)
.

The function
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
defined for arbitrary angle
\vartheta
via Eqs. (
S7
) and (
S8
) is continuous and differentiable.

Proof
. For
\vartheta\in\left(0,\pi/2\right)
the differentiability of
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
follows directly from the properties of
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
, proved in Lemma
1
. The symmetries in Eq. (
S8
) guarantee that this function is also differentiable for
\vartheta
located in mirror reflections of this segment:
\left(-\pi/2,0\right)
,
\left(\pi/2,\pi\right)
, and
\left(\pi,3\pi/2\right)
.

The only points that need to be checked separately are the points of symmetry
\vartheta=0
and
\vartheta=\pi/2
. From the definition of
\tau
we get

\lim_{\vartheta\to 0^{\pm}}\tau\left(\vartheta\right)=\tau\left(0\right)=\gamma\left(0\right),\qquad\lim_{\vartheta\to\pi/2^{\pm}}\tau\left(\vartheta\right)=\tau\left(\pi/2\right)=\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right),

(S9)

and therefore the function is continuous in these points. From Eq. (
S7
), using the relation (
S1a
), for
0<\vartheta<\pi/2
we derive

\tau^{(1)}(\vartheta)=-\gamma({\operatorname{i}}v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta))\sin\vartheta+v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)\cos\vartheta,

(S10)

and thus

\lim_{\vartheta\to 0^{+}}\tau^{(1)}\left(\vartheta\right)=0,\qquad\lim_{\vartheta\to\pi/2^{-}}\tau^{(1)}\left(\vartheta\right)=-\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)\right)=0.

(S11)

The derivatives from opposite directions can be calculated using the symmetries (
S8
). We conclude that for
\vartheta=0
and
\vartheta=\pi/2
the function
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
is continuous and its left- and right-side derivatives are equal to
0
, and therefore

\tau^{(1)}\left(0\right)=\tau^{(1)}\left(\pi/2\right)=0

(S12)

and the function is differentiable for arbitrary angle
\vartheta
.
\square

Lemma 4

(Behaviour of
\tau\left(\theta\right)
)
.

For
T<T_{\mathrm{c}}
the maximal and minimal values of the surface tension depend on the lattice couplings
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
in the following way:

1.

If

s_{1}\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)\geqslant s_{2}\sinh\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)

(S13a)

the maximal value of surface tension is
\tau\left(\pi/2\right)=\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)
, the minimal value is
\tau\left(0\right)=\gamma\left(0\right)
, and for
0<\vartheta<\pi/2
the function
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
is strictly increasing.

2.

If

s_{2}\gamma\left(0\right)\geqslant s_{1}\sinh\gamma\left(0\right)

(S13b)

the maximal value of surface tension is
\tau\left(0\right)=\gamma\left(0\right)
, the minimal value is
\tau\left(\pi/2\right)=\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)
, and for
0<\vartheta<\pi/2
the function
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
is strictly decreasing.

3.

If none of the above inequalities holds, the function
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
has two local minima,
\tau\left(0\right)=\gamma\left(0\right)
and
\tau\left(\pi/2\right)=\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)
, and the lower of them is the global minimum. Between these minima there is a single maximum (that is also a global maximum) located at
\vartheta=\vartheta_{\mathrm{m}}
, given by

\tan\vartheta_{\mathrm{m}}=\frac{v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta_{\mathrm{m}}\right)}{\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta_{\mathrm{m}}\right)\right)},\qquad 0<\vartheta_{\mathrm{m}}<\pi/2,

(S13c)

where this equation has exactly one solution. The surface tension is strictly increasing function for
0<\vartheta<\vartheta_{\mathrm{m}}
and strictly decreasing function for
\vartheta_{\mathrm{m}}<\vartheta<\pi/2
.

The behaviour of
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
for angles outside of the range
\left[0,\pi/2\right]
follows directly from the symmetries (
S8
). For completeness, we recall that for
T<T_{\mathrm{c}}
,
\gamma\left(0\right)=2K_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}-2K_{1}^{\star}
and
\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)=2K_{1}^{\vphantom{\star}}-2K_{2}^{\star}
.

Proof
. We start from the condition
\tau^{(1)}\left(\vartheta\right)>0
, which guarantees that the surface tension is a strictly increasing function of
\vartheta
. For
0<\vartheta<\pi/2
, from Eq. (
S10
) it follows that this condition is equivalent to

\tan\vartheta<\frac{v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)}{\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right)}.

(S14)

Using Eq. (
S1
), after some algebra we get

s_{2}\frac{\sinh v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)}{v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)}<s_{1}\frac{\sinh\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right)}{\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right)}.

(S15)

We now note that
\sinh x/x
is a strictly increasing function for
x>0
. Since, as we have proven in Lemma
1
,
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
is also strictly increasing for
0<\vartheta<\pi/2
, left-hand-side of inequality (
S15
) is also a strictly increasing function of
\vartheta
. On the other hand, the function
\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v\right)
is a strictly decreasing function of

and, as a result, right-hand-side of inequality (
S15
) is a strictly decreasing function of
\vartheta
. Therefore, if inequality (
S15
) is satisfied for
\vartheta\to\pi/2
, it must be satisfied for all
\vartheta\in\left(0,\pi/2\right)
and, thus, the surface tension is strictly increasing. By applying the limit

, after short calculations (using identity

), and proves first point of the Lemma.

The above reasoning can be repeated for the condition

is strictly decreasing function of
\vartheta

s_{2}\frac{\sinh v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)}{v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)}>s_{1}\frac{\sinh\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right)}{\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right)}.

(S16)

Like in the previous case, left-hand-side is an increasing function and right-hand-side is a decreasing function of

\vartheta\to 0

) and proves second point of the Lemma.

Finally, when inequality (

) is not fulfilled for

, and inequality (

, there must be points where both sides are equal,

, where

is strictly increasing, and for

\square

S13c
) it is straightforward to check that
\vartheta_{\mathrm{m}}=\pi/4
. Using Lemma
4
, we have therefore shown that the minima of
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
are located at
\vartheta=0
and
\vartheta=\pi/2
, and maximum is located at
\vartheta=\pi/4
.

In order to show that
\tau\left(\pi/4\right)<\sqrt{2}\tau\left(0\right)
, from Eqs. (
S17
) and (
S18
) we derive

v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\pi/4\right)=\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\pi/4\right)\right)=\ln\sinh 2K,

(S19)

and therefore from (
S7
) we get

\frac{\tau\left(\pi/4\right)}{\tau\left(0\right)}=\frac{\sqrt{2}\log(\sinh(2K))}{2K-\ln\coth(K)}\equiv f\left(K\right)<\sqrt{2},

(S20)

where we have introduced the function
f\left(K\right)
. The plot of this function is presented in Fig.
S1
. We leave the proof of inequality
f\left(K\right)<\sqrt{2}
for
T<T_{\mathrm{c}}
(
i.e.
,
K>K_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1+\sqrt{2}\right)
) as a simple exercise.

.

We now show that the surface tension for symmetric lattice is symmetric around
\vartheta=\pi/4
,
i.e.
,
\tau\left(\pi/4+\vartheta\right)=\tau\left(\pi/4-\vartheta\right)
, or equivalently
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)=\tau\left(\pi/2-\vartheta\right)
. To this end, we recall the function
F\left(v\right)
defined in Eq. (
S1b
). Simple calculation for symmetric lattice gives

F\left(\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right)\right)=\frac{\sinh\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right)}{\sinh\gamma({\operatorname{i}}\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right))}=\frac{\sinh\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right)}{\sinh v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)}=1/F\left(v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right)=1/\tan\vartheta=\tan\left(\pi/2-\vartheta\right),

(S21)

where we have used the definition (
S1a
) of
v_{\mathrm{s}}
and identity
\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v\right)\right)=v
. Comparing Eq. (
S21
) with Eq. (
S1a
) proofs that for symmetric lattice and
0<\vartheta<\pi/2

v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\pi/2-\vartheta\right)=\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right),

(S22)

and therefore, from Eq. (
S7
) we can derive

\tau\left(\pi/2-\vartheta\right)=\gamma({\operatorname{i}}v_{\mathrm{s}}(\pi/2-\vartheta))\cos\left(\pi/2-\vartheta\right)+v_{\mathrm{s}}(\pi/2-\vartheta)\sin\left(\pi/2-\vartheta\right)=v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)\sin\vartheta+\gamma({\operatorname{i}}v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta))\cos\vartheta=\tau\left(\vartheta\right).

(S23)

This proofs the postulated symmetry of the surface tension.
\square

The above Lemma allows to understand the meaning of the three possible scenarios of behaviour of
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
presented in Lemma
4
. Since
\sinh x/x\geqslant 1
, the condition (

) in the first point of Lemma
4
can only be satisfied when

. Similarly, condition (

) in the second point of Lemma
4
can only be satisfied when
K_{2}<K_{1}
. Lemma
5
shows than on the plane
\left(K_{1},K_{2}\right)

in which the surface tension has a non-trivial maximum (point 3 of Lemma

). Outside of this region, for

the surface tension

is an increasing function for

), and for

). We present the plots of the surface tension for different lattices in Fig.

Lemma 6

(Bounds for
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
)
.

For
T<T_{\mathrm{c}}
for any couplings
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, and any angle
\vartheta
the surface tension satisfies inequalities

\min\left\{\gamma\left(0\right),\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)\right\}\leqslant\tau\left(\vartheta\right)<\left[\gamma^{2}\left(0\right)+\hat{\gamma}^{2}\left(0\right)\right]^{1/2}.

(S24)

Proof
. Due to the symmetries (
S8
) of
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
, it is enough to prove the inequalities for
0\leqslant\vartheta\leqslant\pi/2
.

The lower bound follows directly from Lemma
4
: no matter which of the three possible cases is applicable, minimal value of the surface tension is equal to the smaller of
\gamma\left(0\right)
and
\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)
.

To prove the upper bound, we first note that

v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\leqslant\lim_{\vartheta\to\pi/2}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)=\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right),\qquad\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right)\leqslant\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(0\right)\right)=\gamma\left(0\right),

(S25)

where the inequalities follow from the fact that
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
is strictly increasing and
\gamma\left(\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)\right)
is strictly decreasing function of
\vartheta
. These inequalities allow us to estimate

\tau\left(\vartheta\right)=\gamma({\operatorname{i}}v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta))\cos\vartheta+v_{\mathrm{s}}(\vartheta)\sin\vartheta<\gamma(0)\cos\vartheta+\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)\sin\vartheta\leqslant\left[\gamma^{2}\left(0\right)+\hat{\gamma}^{2}\left(0\right)\right]^{1/2}.

(S26)

We note that
\tau\left(\vartheta\right)
cannot reach the above upper bound because inequalities (
S25
) cannot be both satisfied for a single value of
\vartheta
. We leave the calculation of maximal value of
\gamma(0)\cos\vartheta+\hat{\gamma}\left(0\right)\sin\vartheta
as a simple mathematical exercise.
\square

3
Steepest descent path

In this section we prove basic properties of the steepest descent path (SDP) for
0\leqslant\vartheta<\pi/2
for arbitrary
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
. In the considerations below we use some well-known properties of SDPs, for completeness we discuss them in next section of this document.

In our problem, the SDP is defined as a curve on a complex plane of variable
\omega
that goes horizontally through the saddle point
\omega=\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
and satisfies condition (
42
):

\operatorname{Im}\left[N\gamma\left(\omega\right)-\mathrm{i}M\omega\right]=0.

(S27)

We parametrize the SDP with
\omega_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)=u+\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)
, with the parameter
-\pi<u<\pi
(the exact domain for
u
is discussed below). As we have shown in the main text (see Eqs. (
47
) and (
45
)), the function
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)
is given implicitly by

\frac{\mathcal{A}^{2}\left(u,v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)\right)}{\cos^{2}\left(u\tan\vartheta\right)}-\frac{\mathcal{B}^{2}\left(u,v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)\right)}{\sin^{2}\left(u\tan\vartheta\right)}=1,

(S28a)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\left(u,v\right)

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}-s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\cos u\cosh v,

(S28b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(u,v)

\displaystyle=s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\sin u\sinh v.

(S28c)

Proposition 1

(Symmetry of SDP)
.

The SDP is symmetric under reflection around the imaginary axis.

Proof.
The SDP is symmetric under reflections around imaginary axis when
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)=v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(-u\right)
. It is straightforward to check that if
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)=v_{0}
is a solution of Eq. (
S28
), also
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(-u\right)=v_{0}
solves this equation.
\square

Proposition 2

(Range of
u
)
.

The SDP does not touch the side lines
u=\pm\pi
.

Proof.
In order to prove this property it is enough to check that for
u=\pi
Eq. (
S28
) cannot be satisfied. Since
\mathcal{B}(\pi,v)=0
, Eq. (
S28a
) gives

\left(c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}+s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(\pi\right)\right)^{2}=\cos^{2}\left(\pi\tan\vartheta\right),

(S29)

or equivalently

\left[\cosh{\gamma}(\pi)+s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\left(\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(\pi\right)-1\right)\right]^{2}=\cos^{2}\left(\pi\tan\theta\right).

(S30)

Since
\cosh\gamma\left(\pi\right)>1
and
(\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(\pi\right)-1)>0
, the left-hand-side of Eq. (
S30
) is bigger than one. Since, the right-hand-side of Eq. (
S30
) does not exceed one, there is no solution for
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(\pi\right)
.
\square

Proposition 3

(Asymptotes of SDP)
.

The SDP approaches the vertical asymptotes
\omega=\pm\mathrm{i}u_{\mathrm{ex}}(\vartheta)
with
u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)=\pi/\left(1+\tan\vartheta\right)
,
i.e.
,
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)
diverges to infinity for
u\to u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
.

Proof.
Due to the symmetry of SDP, it is enough to consider only the case of
u>0
. For
v\to+\infty
the functions
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
exhibit the following asymptotic behaviour

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\left(u,v\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\left(\cos u\right)\operatorname{e}^{v}+\operatorname{O}\left(\operatorname{e}^{-v}\right),

(S31a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\left(u,v\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\left(\sin u\right)\operatorname{e}^{v}+\operatorname{O}\left(\operatorname{e}^{-v}\right).

(S31b)

By inserting the above into Eq. (
S28a
), we find

\left(s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\right)^{2}\sin\left[u\left(-1+\tan\vartheta\right)\right]\sin\left[u\left(1+\tan\vartheta\right)\right]=\sin^{2}(2u\tan\vartheta)\operatorname{e}^{-2v}.

(S32)

Since for
v\to\infty
right-hand-side vanishes, the asymptote of the SDP is located at the smallest positive
u
for which the left-hand-side also vanishes. This proves that

u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)=\frac{\pi}{1+\tan\vartheta},

(S33)

and when
u
approaches
u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
from below, the SDP approaches asymptotically the vertical asymptote
\omega=\mathrm{i}u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
, with
u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)<\pi
.
\square

Proposition 4

(SDP for
\vartheta=\pi/4
and symmetric lattice)
.

The SDP for
\vartheta=\pi/4
and symmetric lattice is given by

v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)=\operatorname{arccosh}\left(\frac{\cosh v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\pi/4\right)}{\cos u}\right).

(S34)

For
u\in(0,\pi/2)
the above function
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)
is convex, increases monotonically and diverges for
u\to\pi/2
.

Proof.
For symmetric lattice
K_{1}=K_{2}
, and therefore
s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}=s^{\star}s=1
,
c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}=cc^{\star}
. In this case, for
\vartheta=\pi/4
Eq. (
S28a
) simplifies to

\cos u\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)=cc^{\star}/2.

(S35)

Since
\cosh v_{s}(\pi/4)=cc^{\star}/2
, the above equation is equivalent to Eq. (
S34
). The first and second derivatives of
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}
with respect to
u
can be computed straightforwardly:

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}}{\operatorname{d}u}v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)

\displaystyle=\tan u\coth v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right),

(S36a)

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{d}^{2}}{\operatorname{d}u^{2}}v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\coth v_{\rm sdp}(u)}{\cos^{2}u}.

(S36b)

Both functions are positive and diverge for
u\rightarrow\pi/2
(in agreement with Property
3
), which proves the postulated properties of
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}
.
\square

Theorem 1

(Analytic expression for SDP)
.

The SDP
\omega=u+\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)
for
-u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)<u<u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
, with
u_{\mathrm{ex}}
given by Eq. (
S33
), can be calculated from the following analytic formulae:

For
\vartheta=\pi/4
the steepest descent path
v_{\rm sdp}(u)
is given by

\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)=\frac{\left(c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\right)^{2}+\left(s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\right)^{2}\cos^{2}u-\cos^{2}u}{2s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\cos u};

(S37)

for
\vartheta\neq\pi/4
the steepest descent path is given by

\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)=\frac{-\mathcal{Q}\left(u,\vartheta\right)\pm\sqrt{\mathcal{Q}^{2}\left(u,\vartheta\right)-\mathcal{P}\left(u,\vartheta\right)\mathcal{R}\left(u,\vartheta\right)}}{\mathcal{P}\left(u,\vartheta\right)},

(S38)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\left(u,\vartheta\right)

\displaystyle=(s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{}})^{2}\sin\left[u\left(1-\tan\vartheta\right)\right]\sin\left[u\left(1+\tan\vartheta\right)\right],

(S39a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}\left(u,\vartheta\right)

\displaystyle=s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{}}c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{}}\cos u\;\sin^{2}\left(u\tan\vartheta\right),

(S39b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\left(u,\vartheta\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{}}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\left(u\tan\vartheta\right)-\left(s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{}}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}u\;\cos^{2}\left(u\tan\vartheta\right)+\sin^{2}\left(u\tan\vartheta\right)\cos^{2}\left(u\tan\vartheta\right).

(S39c)

The sign before square root in Eq. (
S38
) is chosen as follows: for
0<\vartheta<\pi/4
it is “
+
”, for
\pi/4<\vartheta<\pi/2
and
0<u\leqslant u_{0}\left(\vartheta\right)
it is also “
+
”, and for
\pi/4<\vartheta<\pi/2
and
u_{0}\left(\vartheta\right)<u<u_{\mathrm{ex}}(\vartheta)
it is “
-
”. Here
u_{0}\left(\vartheta\right)=\pi/\left(2\tan\vartheta\right)
.

Proof
. Equation (
S28a
) can be transformed to the following quadratic equation in the variable
\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)

\mathcal{P}\left(u,\vartheta\right)\cosh^{2}v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)+2\mathcal{Q}\left(u,\vartheta\right)\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)+\mathcal{R}\left(u,\vartheta\right)=0,

(S40)

with the functions
\mathcal{P}\left(u,\vartheta\right)
,
\mathcal{Q}\left(u,\vartheta\right)
, and
\mathcal{R}\left(u,\vartheta\right)
as defined in Eq. (
S39
). The expression for the SDP for
\vartheta=\pi/4
, given in Eq. (
S37
), follows by noting that
\mathcal{P}\left(u,\pi/4\right)=0
.

In order to establish the sign in front of the square root, we consider various limiting cases. We begin by examining the limit
u\rightarrow 0
, in which we must retrieve the formula for the saddle point. It is straightforward to check that

\lim_{u\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathcal{P}\left(u,\vartheta\right)}{u^{2}}=U\left(\vartheta\right),\qquad\lim_{u\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathcal{Q}\left(u,\vartheta\right)}{u^{2}}=V\left(\vartheta\right),\qquad\lim_{u\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathcal{R}\left(u,\vartheta\right)}{u^{2}}=W\left(\vartheta\right),

(S41)

and thus, Eq. (
S40
) reduces to the equation for
v_{\mathrm{s}}
(Eq. (
S6
)) in the limit
u\to 0
. The steepest descent path
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u=0\right)
yields the saddle point
v_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
provided the sign “
+
” is chosen in Eq. (
S38
).

The above analysis is valid on the imaginary axis
u=0
. In the following, we examine what happens away from the imaginary axis. We proceed by examining separately two cases:
0<\vartheta<\pi/4
, and
\pi/4<\theta<\pi/2
.

Case 1.
For
0<\vartheta<\pi/4
we look at Eq. (
S38
) in the limit
u\to u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)=2/\left(1+\tan\left(\vartheta\right)\right)
. Since for
0<\vartheta<\pi/4
we have
\cos u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)<\cos\left(\pi/2\right)=0
, and in this limit
\mathcal{P}\to 0
and
\mathcal{Q}<0
. Using this result in Eq. (
S38
) we get
\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\to\infty
for “
+
” sign, and
\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\to-\mathcal{R}/2\mathcal{Q}
for “
-
” sign. Since for
u\to u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
SDP approaches to its vertical asymptote, only the result with “+” sign is correct.

For
0<u<u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
, in order to establish the correct sign in Eq. (
S38
), we use the properties of SDP. Since
v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)
must be continuous, the sign can change only when the discriminant
\Delta=\mathcal{Q}^{2}-\mathcal{P}\mathcal{R}=0
. In Lemma
7
below, we show that
\Delta
is non-negative, and it is equal to 0 only for
u=u_{n}\left(\vartheta\right)=\left(2n+1\right)\pi/\left(2\tan\vartheta\right)
, where
n=0,1,2,\ldots
. For
0<\vartheta<\pi/4
we have
u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)<u_{n}\left(\vartheta\right)
and, therefore, the discriminant
\Delta
is strictly positive for
0<u<u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
and the sign cannot change. This shows that in this case “+” is the correct sign in Eq. (
S38
).

Case 2.
For
\pi/4<\vartheta<\pi/2
from the Lemma
7
below it follows that the first zero of the discriminant
0<u_{0}\left(\vartheta\right)<u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
, and therefore, the sign in Eq. (
S38
) can change at
u=u_{0}\left(\vartheta\right)
. We note that for
n\geqslant 1
,
u_{n}\left(\vartheta\right)>u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
and thus the other zeros of discriminant are still not relevant.

When
0<u<u_{0}\left(\vartheta\right)
the continuity of SDP requires that the sign in Eq. (
S38
) is not changing. Therefore it must be the same as for
u=0
, i.e., “+”.

When
u_{0}\left(\vartheta\right)<u<u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
the sign can be establish by considering the limit
u\to u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)
. Since for
\pi/4<\vartheta<\pi/2
we have
\cos u_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\vartheta\right)>\cos\left(\pi/2\right)=0
, in this limit
\mathcal{P}\to 0
and
\mathcal{Q}>0
. Using this result in Eq. (
S38
) we get
\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\to-\mathcal{R}/2\mathcal{Q}
for “
+
” sign, and
\cosh v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\to\infty
for “
-
” sign. Therefore, in this case the sign “
-
” gives the correct result in Eq. (
S38
).

Finally, we note that when
u=u_{0}\left(\vartheta\right)
, since the discriminant is zero, both posible signs in Eq. (
S38
) give the same, correct result.
\square

In order to prove Theorem
1
it is useful to introduce the following lemma.

Lemma 7

(Non-negativity and zeros of the discriminant)
.

For
0<\vartheta<\pi/2
the discriminant

\Delta\left(u,\vartheta\right)=\mathcal{Q}^{2}\left(u,\vartheta\right)-\mathcal{P}\left(u,\vartheta\right)\mathcal{R}\left(u,\vartheta\right)

(S42)

is non-negative for any
u\in(0,\pi)
and vanishes only for
u=u_{n}\left(\vartheta\right)
with

u_{n}\left(\vartheta\right)=\frac{2n+1}{2}\frac{\pi}{\tan\vartheta},\qquad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(S43)

The functions
\mathcal{P}\left(u,\vartheta\right)
,
\mathcal{Q}\left(u,\vartheta\right)
, and
\mathcal{R}\left(u,\vartheta\right)
are defined in Eq. (
S39
).

Proof.
After some algebra, we get the formula for the discriminant

\Delta\left(u,\vartheta\right)=\frac{\left(s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}\right)^{2}}{4}\cos^{2}\left(u\tan\vartheta\right)\left[X_{1}\left(u,\vartheta\right)+X_{2}\left(u,\vartheta\right)\sin^{2}u\right],

(S44)

where

\displaystyle X_{1}\left(u,\vartheta\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\cos\left(2u\right)-\cos\left(2u\tan\vartheta\right)\right]^{2},

(S45a)

\displaystyle X_{2}\left(u,\vartheta\right)

\displaystyle=4\left(c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\left[u\tan\vartheta\right]+2\left[\left(s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\right)^{2}-1\right]\left[\cos\left(2u\tan\vartheta\right)-\cos\left(2u\right)\right].

(S45b)

We start our analysis from the factor in square brackets in Eq. (
S44
). We transform the formula to

X_{1}\left(u,\vartheta\right)+X_{2}\left(u,\vartheta\right)\sin^{2}u=\left(s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\right)^{2}x^{2}+y^{2}+xy\left[\left(s_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}+s_{2}^{2}\right],

(S46)

where we have introduced

x=1-\cos\left(2u\right),\qquad y=1-\cos\left(2u\tan\vartheta\right),

(S47)

and used identity

\left(c_{1}^{\star}c_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\right)^{2}-\left(s_{1}^{\star}s_{2}^{\vphantom{\star}}\right)^{2}-1=\left(s_{1}^{\star}\right)^{2}+s_{2}^{2}.

(S48)

Since for
0<u<\pi
,
x>0
and
y\geqslant 0
, the factor (
S46
) is strictly positive and, therefore, the discriminant (
S44
) is non-negative.

Finally, from Eq. (
S44
) it follows that the zeros of the discriminant are given by solutions of
\cos\left(u\tan\vartheta\right)=0
,
i.e.
,
u=u_{n}\left(\vartheta\right)
given by Eq. (
S43
).
\square

4
General properties of the steepest descent path

In this section we discuss some basic properties of the SDP which were necessary to prove the theorems in the previous section of this document. Even though they are well-known and even considered obvious, we were not able to find their systematic proofs. For completeness of our manuscript we provide these proofs.

In the following we assume that
f\left(z\right)
is a holomorphic function defined on an open subset
D
of the complex plane and we will look at the steepest descent line defined by the condition

\operatorname{Im}f\left(z\right)=0.

(S49)

Theorem 2

(smoothness of SDP)
.

If there is no saddle point in
D
, the steepest descent line is continuous and differentiable. Also the line cannot have endpoint inside
D
.

Proof.
Let
z_{0}
be any point on the steepest descent path. Since it is not a saddle point,
f^{\prime}\left(z_{0}\right)\neq 0
and from holomorphic inverse function theorem [N. Bleistein and R. A. Handelsman, “Asymptotic Expansions of Integrals”], there exists an open set
D_{1}\subset D
around
z_{0}
on which
f
can be inverted, and the inverse function
f^{-1}\left(z\right)
is also holomorphic. Let us now consider the domain of
f^{-1}\left(z\right)
, the open set
A=f\left(D_{1}\right)
. Of course,
f\left(z_{0}\right)\in A\cap\mathbb{R}
. Let
t_{1}<f\left(z_{0}\right)<t_{2}
be two real numbers, such that the segment
B=\left(t_{1},t_{2}\right)\subset A
. The steepest descent path around
z_{0}
can be parametrized
\gamma\left(t\right)=f^{-1}\left(t\right)
for
t_{1}<t<t_{2}
. This proves that steepest descent path is continuous, differentiable, and
z_{0}
is not its endpoint.
\square

We note that the continuity and smoothness of SDP was crucial in the proof of Theorem
1
, where we used these properties to determine the correct sign in Eq. (
S38
).

Theorem 3

(intersection of SDP)
.

Two steepest descent paths can intersect only in the saddle point.

Proof.
Let as assume that two steepest descent lines intersect in
z_{0}
and it is not a saddle point. Since
f^{\prime}\left(z_{0}\right)\neq 0
from inverse function theorem, in the vicinity of
z_{0}
there exists inverse function
f^{-1}\left(z\right)
.

At the same time, in any open neighbourhood of
z_{0}
we have four distinct curves starting in
z_{0}
, along which
f\left(z\right)
is real (a pair for each intersecting steepest descent path).
If along any of these curves real
f\left(z\right)
is non-monotonic, we can find two points
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
for which
f\left(z_{1}\right)=f\left(z_{2}\right)
. If, on the other hand,
f\left(z\right)
is monotonic along all these curves, we can still find two of them along which
f\left(z\right)
has the same monotonicity (is strictly increasing or decreasing); and along these two paths we can find two points
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
for which
f\left(z_{1}\right)=f\left(z_{2}\right)
. This shows that
f\left(z\right)
is not injective in the vicinity of
z_{0}
, and therefore, the inverse function does not exist, which is in contradiction with the inverse function theorem.
\square

The two above theorems draw a picture of steepest descent paths connecting, across the complex plane, saddle points or points in infinity (and possibly ending in non-analyticity points). However, they do not exclude cases when infinitely long SDP occupies a finite open subset of the complex plane, like for example a logarithmic spiral. Impossibility of such a scenario follows from the theorem below.

Theorem 4

(no ending of SDP)
.

Let
\gamma\left(t\right)
be a non-intersecting differentiable contour inside
D
. If there is no saddle point in
D
, and the steepest descent path crosses
\gamma\left(t\right)
and enters the region inside the contour, there exists a second point where the steepest descent path crosses
\gamma\left(t\right)
and leaves this region.

Proof.
Let
z_{0}=\gamma\left(t_{0}\right)
be the point in
D
where the steepest descent path and the contour
\gamma\left(t\right)
intersect. We now look at the function
\operatorname{Im}\left[f\left(\gamma\left(t\right)\right)\right]
. Clearly this continuous function has a zero for
t=t_{0}
. If there is another zero for some
t=t_{1}
, the point
\gamma\left(t_{1}\right)
is where the steepest descent path leaves the region inside the contour and the theorem is proven. We therefore assume that
\operatorname{Im}\left[f\left(\gamma\left(t\right)\right)\right]
does not have any other zeros.

If
\operatorname{Im}\left[f\left(\gamma\left(t\right)\right)\right]
has only one zero, since the function is defined on a closed contour, it must have a maximum or a minimum at
t=t_{0}
. This means that the derivative of
\operatorname{Im}\left[f\left(z\right)\right]
at
z=z_{0}
in the direction tangent to the contour is zero. At the same time, derivative of
\operatorname{Im}\left[f\left(z\right)\right]
at
z=z_{0}
in the direction tangent to steepest descent line is also zero. If these two directions are different, the derivative in any direction must be zero and via Cauchy–Riemann equations it is straightforward to show that
z_{0}
is a saddle point—a contradiction with the assumptions of the theorem.

If, on the other hand, the steepest descent path enters the interior of the contour in
z_{0}
being tangent to the contour (like
y=x
and
y=\tan x
in the origin), we slightly modify the contour to make these two curves not tangent in
z_{0}
. This can be done using holomorphic inverse function theorem: We first use
f\left(z\right)
to map both curves. Now, the steepest descent path lies on the real axis and the contour is some curve intersecting it in a tangent manner. Second, we modify the contour keeping it continuous and differentiable, such that is intersects real axis in
f\left(z_{0}\right)
with non-zero angle. Third, we map both curves back using
f^{-1}\left(z\right)
. This way, the modified contour intersects with steepest descent path with non-zero angle, in which case the theorem has already been proven. Here, we skip the details of this construction.
\square

Theorem 5

(intersection of SDP in saddle point)
.

If at some saddle point
z_{0}
inside
D
,
f^{\prime\prime}\left(z_{0}\right)\neq 0
, two steepest descent paths intersect at right angles in
z_{0}
.

Proof.
We prove this theorem using the Morse lemma for holomorphic functions
Żołądek 2025
. In
z_{0}
we have
\operatorname{Im}f\left(z_{0}\right)=0
,
f^{\prime}\left(z_{0}\right)=0
, and
f^{\prime\prime}\left(z_{0}\right)\neq 0
, and therefore, there exist holomorphic map
\varphi\left(z\right)
such that

\varphi\left(z_{0}\right)=0,\quad f\left(\varphi^{-1}\left(w\right)\right)=f\left(z_{0}\right)+w^{2},

(S50)

for
w
from some open neighbourhood of
0
. By taking
w=\varphi\left(z\right)
, we get

\operatorname{Im}f\left(z\right)=\operatorname{Im}\varphi^{2}\left(z\right),

(S51)

for
z
in the open neighbourhood of
z_{0}
. The steepest descent paths
\operatorname{Im}f\left(z\right)=0
are equivalent to

\operatorname{Im}\varphi\left(z\right)=0,\qquad\text{or }\operatorname{Re}\varphi\left(z\right)=0,

(S52)

so from Cauchy–Riemann equations it follows that there are two perpendicular steepest descent lines intersecting in
z_{0}
.
\square

The above theorem proves that in the saddle point
\omega=\mathrm{i}\nu_{\mathrm{s}}\left(\vartheta\right)
we have intersection of two perpendicular SDPs. Since one of them is located along the imaginary axis, the second SDP must go from the saddle point in the direction parallel to the real axis. This justifies (at least for small
u
) the parametrisation of SDP
\omega=u+\mathrm{i}v_{\mathrm{sdp}}\left(u\right)
used in Section
3
.
