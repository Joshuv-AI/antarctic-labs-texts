Noise sensitivity in last-passage percolation
Daniel Ahlberg, Malo Hillairet, Ekaterina Toropova

Abstract
The study of noise sensitivity of Boolean functions was initiated in a seminal paper of Benjamini, Kalai and Schramm, published in 1999. While this study has revealed fascinating phenomena in the context of Bernoulli percolation, few results have been obtained regarding other random spatial processes. In this paper we prove the first instance of noise sensitivity for a spatial growth process associated to the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang class of universality. More specifically, we show that travel times in geometric last-passage percolation are noise sensitive with respect to a perturbation acting on a Bernoulli encoding of the geometric weights. Our method of proof includes a generalisation of the celebrated Benjamini-Kalai-Schramm noise sensitivity/influence theorem, and precise bounds on the probability of a given vertex being on a geodesic, which we believe to be of independent interest. Keywords: Noise sensitivity; last-passage percolation; BKS theorem; KPZ universality. Funding: Research in part supported by the Swedish Research Council through grant 2021-03964.



1
Introduction

In the study of random spatial processes and structures there is an abundance of models with a simple probabilistic formulation at a microscopic level that give rise to highly complex behaviour at macroscopic scales. It is believed that such models to a large extent can be divided into classes, referred to as universality classes, where all models in one class exhibit a similar large-scale behaviour, although their local description differs. This large scale behaviour is characterised by deterministic quantities such as power-law exponents describing the scaling of different features at the critical point. Two prominent examples of universality classes are those of planar Bernoulli percolation on the triangular lattice, described by work of Schramm
[
46
]
and Smirnov
[
49
]
, and the KPZ universality class for
1+1
dimensional growing interfaces, introduced in work of Kardar, Parisi and Zhang
[
37
]
, and made rigorous in work of Baik, Deift and Johansson
[
6
,
35
]
.

The most classic model associated to the KPZ universality class was introduced in the 1960s in work of Eden
[
24
]
and Hammersley and Welsh
[
32
]
, and is referred to as first-passage percolation. In its most simple setting, the edges of the
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
nearest-neighbour lattice are equipped with i.i.d. non-negative random weights, inducing a random metric on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.
A central objective is to understand the asymptotic behaviour of distances, balls and geodesics in this random metric space.
Despite its simple formulation, there is no rigorous mathematical analysis proving that first-passage percolation satisfies the predictions of KPZ universality.
However, there are closely related models, whose formulation is amenable to favourable calculations, that tie them to the KPZ class.
One such model is last-passage percolation, in which non-negative random weights are assigned to the vertices instead of edges, and where weight is maximised over directed paths as opposed to minimised over all paths.
In the case of exponentially or geometrically distributed weights, this models has serendipitous connections to random matrix models via combinatorial identities, allowing for a precise analysis of fluctuations of distances and geodesic, in agreement with the predictions of KPZ universality
[
35
,
7
]
.

In parallel, the exploration of noise sensitivity, introduced in seminal work of Benjamini, Kalai and Schramm
[
12
]
,
has highlighted a remarkable phenomenon in Bernoulli percolation.
In this context, noise sensitivity refers to the surprising property that a small random perturbation of a critical percolation configuration is sufficient in order to completely decorrelate its connectivity properties. More precisely, critical Bernoulli bond percolation on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
is noise sensitive in the sense that if
\omega
is a configuration of 0s and 1s encoding the states of the edges (i.e. open or closed), the perturbation
\omega^{\varepsilon}
is obtained from
\omega
by independently resampling each entry of
\omega
with probability
\varepsilon
, and
f_{n}
is the function encoding the existence of a horizontal crossing of open edges of an
n\times n
square, then

{\mathbb{E}}[f_{n}(\omega)f_{n}(\omega^{\varepsilon})]-{\mathbb{E}}[f_{n}(\omega)]^{2}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(1)

Noise sensitivity is straightforwardly extended to the framework of Boolean functions, that is, functions of the form
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
. Again we sample an element
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{n}
according to (a possibly biased) product measure, and obtain a perturbation
\omega^{\varepsilon}
of
\omega
by independently resampling each coordinate with probability
\varepsilon
. A sequence
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of Boolean functions is said to be
noise sensitive
if it satisfies (
1
). The mathematical techniques for the study of noise sensitivity have been extensively developed in the context of Boolean functions
[
29
,
41
]
. These methods include the study of influences, Fourier decomposition and randomized algorithms. These methods have led to a precise description, in planar Bernoulli percolation, of the transition from a stable regime, where the noise is too weak to alter percolation crossings, to a noise sensitive regime
[
45
,
28
,
50
]
. For Bernoulli site percolation on the triangular lattice, where critical exponents are known explicitly, Garban, Pete and Schramm
[
28
]
showed that the transition occurs for the noise strength
\varepsilon
scaling with
n
as
\varepsilon\asymp n^{-3/4}
.

In related work, Schramm and Smirnov
[
44
]
proved that the scaling limit of Bernoulli percolation is a black noise – a notion introduced by Tsirelson
[
51
]
– referring to the property that observables cannot be expressed through linear functions or averages, which are the most stable to noise. Thus, being a black noise established a form of noise sensitive behaviour for the scaling limit, although it is not known if noise sensitivity of a percolation model implies that its scaling limit is a black noise, or vice versa.
Recently, Himwich and Parekh
[
33
]
showed that also the directed landscape is a black noise. The directed landscape was introduced by Dauvergne, Ortmann and Virag
[
21
]
as the supposed scaling limit of models in the KPZ class of universality. This indicates that models in the KPZ class can be expected to be noise sensitive.

To this point, noise sensitivity has not been established for a single model associated to KPZ universality. In fact, the only prior evidence for noise sensitivity in the context of spatial growth appears to be work of Ahlberg and de la Riva
[
2
]
, elaborated upon by Dembin and Elboim
[
22
]
. Their work shows that ‘being above the median’ is a noise sensitive property in a simplified model of first-passage percolation exhibiting Gaussian fluctuations. One difficulty in studying noise sensitivity in the context of spatial growth is that central observables are not Boolean functions, which limits the existing techniques. Another difficulty is that large regions need to be explored in order to identify the weight maximising path between two points, rendering exploration-based algorithm methods inadequate.

In this paper we establish noise sensitivity for a model known to belong to the KPZ class. More precisely, we prove that planar last-passage percolation with geometric weights exhibits noise sensitivity, via the method of influences. In order to accommodate the method to the present context, we extend the BKS influence theorem from Boolean to real-valued functions, and present precise bounds on the probability that a vertex is visited by the weight-maximising path between two given points.

1.1
Main result

We consider planar last-passage percolation with geometrically distributed weights, which we describe next. To the integer points of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
we assign independent geometrically distributed weights
\omega=(\omega_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
of parameter
p\in(0,1)
.

1

1

We work with the convention that
{\mathbb{N}}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
and that
X
is geometrically distributed with parameter
p\in(0,1)
if
{\mathbb{P}}(X=k)=p(1-p)^{k}
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

We introduce a partial order on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and write
u\leq v
for
u=(u_{1},u_{2})
and
v=(v_{1},v_{2})
if
u_{1}\leq v_{1}
and
u_{2}\leq v_{2}
. Let
\mathbf{e}_{1}:=(1,0)
and
\mathbf{e}_{2}:=(0,1)
denote the coordinate vectors. Given
u\leq v
, a
directed path

\gamma
from
u
to
v
is an upwards-rightwards sequence of vertices
x_{0}=u,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}=v
where
x_{i}-x_{i-1}\in\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}\}
. For
u\leq v
, we define the
last-passage time
, also referred to as the
travel time
, from
u
to
v
as

T(u,v):=\max\big\{T(\gamma):\gamma\text{ is a directed path from $u$ to $v$}\big\},\quad T(\gamma):=\sum_{x\in\gamma}\omega_{x}.

(2)

We refer to a path attaining the maximum in the definition of
T(u,v)
as a
geodesic
from
u
to
v
. Since the travel time is integer-valued, there may be multiple geodesics between two points, and we let
\pi(u,v)
denote the set of points which belong to at least one of the geodesic from
u
to
v
, that is,

\pi(u,v):=\big\{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:\exists\text{ a geodesic }\gamma\text{ from }u\text{ to }v\text{ such that }x\in\gamma\big\}.

For ease of notation we occasionally write
T_{n}:=T(0,n\mathbf{e}_{+})
and
\pi_{n}:=\pi(0,n\mathbf{e}_{+})
, where
\mathbf{e}_{+}:=\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{2}=(1,1)
. In addition, we shall write
T_{n}(\omega)
when we want to make explicit (as we often do) that
T_{n}
is a function of the weight configuration
\omega
.

We next introduce a random perturbation of the weight configuration, and to do so we begin with an encoding of the geometric weights via Bernoulli random variables.
For each
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and
i\in{\mathbb{N}}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, let
X_{v,i}
be independent Bernoulli distributed random variables with parameter
p\in(0,1)
, and set

\omega_{v}:=\min\{i\geq 0:X_{v,i}=1\}.

(3)

Hence,
(\omega_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
are independent and geometrically distributed with parameter
p
.

We now introduce the perturbation. For
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
X^{\prime}_{v,i}
be Bernoulli distributed with parameter
p
and let
U_{v,i}
be exponentially distributed with parameter
1
(i.e. with density
t\mapsto e^{-t}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
[0,\infty)
). We assume
X_{v,i},X^{\prime}_{v,i},U_{v,i}
to be independent of each other, and independent between different
v
and
i
. For
t\geq 0
, let

X_{v,i}^{t}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} X_{v,i}&&\text{if }t<U_{v,i};\\
X^{\prime}_{v,i}&&\text{if }t\geq U_{v,i}.\end{aligned}\right.

Finally, we define
\omega^{t}
, for
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and
t\geq 0
, as

\omega_{v}^{t}:=\min\{i\geq 0:X^{t}_{v,i}=1\}.

(4)

Note that for every
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
we have
\omega_{v}^{0}=\omega_{v}
, and that for
t\geq 0
we have that
\omega_{v}^{t}
is geometrically distributed with parameter
p
, and that the variables
(X^{t}_{v,i})
are obtained from
(X_{v,i})
by resampling each bit with probability
\varepsilon=1-e^{-t}
. For small
t>0
, one may hence think of
\omega^{t}
as a small perturbation of
\omega
.

Our main theorem states that the sequence
(T_{n})_{n\geq 1}
exhibits noise sensitivity, and provides the first instance of noise sensitivity in a model associated to the KPZ class of universality.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n\geq 1
, let
T_{n}=T_{n}(\omega)
denote the last-passage time from
(0,0)
to
(n,n)
with respect to the weight configuration
\omega
.
Planar last-passage percolation with geometric weights is noise sensitive in the sense that, for every
p\in(0,1)
and
t>0
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Corr\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\omega^{t})\big)=0.

We shall proceed below with an outline of the method of proof, and the properties of last-passage percolation needed for the method to work. We shall also comment upon the fact that when extending the notion of noise from a Boolean to a more general context, there are various ways in which this can be done; we show that two natural ways this can be done are roughly equivalent. We end the introduction with a brief discussion of related work on the concept of chaos, and why this work suggests that the transition from stability to noise sensitivity is expected to occur at
t\asymp n^{-1/3}
.
Our proof of Theorem
1.1
shows that the conclusion of the theorem holds as long as
t\gg 1/\log n
.

1.2
Noise sensitivity and influences

Noise sensitivity was introduced by Benjamini, Kalai and Schramm
[
12
]
in the context of Boolean functions
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, but extends straightforwardly to square-integrable functions
f:\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
To define things properly, let
X=(X_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a family of independent Bernoulli random variables of parameter
p
, and let
X^{\prime}=(X^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be an independent copy of
X
. Moreover, let
U_{i}
be exponentially distributed with parameter
1
, and define, for
t\geq 0
, a perturbation
X^{t}
of
X
as

X^{t}_{i}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} X_{i}&&&\text{if }t<U_{i};\\
X^{\prime}_{i}&&&\text{if }t\geq U_{i}.\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

We say that a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of square-integrable functions
f_{n}:\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
noise sensitive
if for every
t>0
we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Corr\big(f_{n}(X),f_{n}(X^{t})\big)=0.

(6)

Note that the perturbed configuration
X^{t}
is obtained from
X
by resampling each bit independently with probability
\varepsilon=1-e^{-t}
. We occasionally refer to the parameter
t
as the noise strength. The reason for this parametrization is that
t
will later be interpreted as time, as
(X^{t})_{t\geq 0}
is recast as a time-homogeneous Markov process under that parametrization. Note also that noise sensitivity as expressed in the context of last-passage percolation in Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to noise sensitivity as defined in (
5
), via the Bernoulli encoding of geometric variables.

It appears, through the identity

\textup{Var}(f)-\Cov\big(f(X),f(X^{t})\big)=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(f(X)-f(X^{t})\big)^{2}\big],

that noise sensitivity of
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
means that,
in the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
f_{n}(X^{t})
is not even slightly more concentrated around
f_{n}(X)
than an independent copy, contrary to what one could expect for small values of
t
, for which
X
and
X^{t}
are very similar. Note that in the case where the
f_{n}
are Boolean functions of variance bounded away from zero (such as planar percolation crossings at criticality), noise sensitivity as defined above coincides with noise sensitivity in the sense of (
1
).

An important quantity in the study of noise sensitivity is the influence of bits. The relevance of influences to tackle noise sensitivity problems can be conceived via the heuristic that, in order to understand the effect of randomly changing the values of a tiny proportion of bits, it would be useful to investigate the effect of changing exactly one bit. In the context of Boolean functions, the influence of bit
i
is defined as the probability that changing the input coordinate
i
changes the output of the function. In extending this notion to functions taking values in
{\mathbb{R}}
, it seems reasonable to also take the amount of change into account.
For
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the
influence
of
i
on
f:\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
as

\I_{i}(f):={\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(X)\big|(X_{j})_{j\neq i}\big]-f(X)\big|\big].

(7)

For Boolean functions, the definition of influences given in (
7
) coincides with the classical definition up to a factor
2p(1-p)
, as explained in Section
2
. For real-valued functions, the definition is analogous to that used in
[
34
,
17
]
, but differs from the
L^{2}
-notion used e.g. in
[
4
,
3
]
.

The first result justifying the relevance of the heuristic that “in order to understand the effect of randomly changing the values of a tiny proportion of bits, it would be useful to investigate the effect of changing each bit individually” is due to Benjamini, Kalai and Schramm
[
12
]
. The authors famously proved that a sufficient condition for a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of Boolean functions, each depending on finitely many coordinates, to be noise sensitive is that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\I_{i}(f_{n})^{2}=0.

(8)

This result has come to be referred to as the BKS theorem. The authors further noted that for monotone functions, the condition in (
8
) is also necessary. In many settings in which noise sensitivity has been studied – including Bernoulli percolation, but also first- and last-passage percolation – key observables correspond to monotone functions. As a consequence, the condition in (
8
) is of fundamental importance, since it is not possible to establish noise sensitivity without, at least indirectly, also establishing (
8
).

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we extend the BKS theorem, from
[
12
]
, to real-valued function on the Boolean cube. Our result is quantitative, and applies to pairs of functions from
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
p\in(0,1)
and
t>0
there exists
\theta=\theta(p,t)>0
such that for all functions
f,g
from
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
having finite variance, we have

\Cov\big(f(X),g(X^{t})\big)\leq\sqrt{\textup{Var}(f)\textup{Var}(g)}^{1-\theta}\bigg(\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\I_{i}(f)\I_{i}(g)\bigg)^{\theta}.

The statement holds for
\theta(p,t)=\tanh(\rho t/2)
, where
\rho=1
for
p=1/2
and
\rho=2\frac{2p-1}{\log(p)-\log(1-p)}
otherwise.

There exist a few versions of the BKS theorem in the literature in the case of a single function
f
. The original theorem was proved for Boolean functions and uniform measure (
p=1/2
) in
[
12
]
. The theorem was extended to biased measures (
p\neq 1/2
) independently by Keller and Kindler
[
38
]
and Ahlberg, Broman, Griffiths and Morris
[
1
]
; the former of which providing a quantitative version. Very recently, and in parallel to the following paper being written, Caravenna and Donadini
[
17
]
proved a version of Theorem
1.2
for noises including the one in (
5
), with methods different from ours.
In
[
17
, Theorem 2.17]
the proof uses hypercontractivity estimates in a Fourier framework, inspired by
[
38
]
. Our proof of Theorem
1.2
combines hypercontractivity and a Markov semigroup framework, closer to related results of van Handel, as outlined in
[
43
]
, and Ivanishvili and Zhang
[
34
]
. We deviate, however, from these proofs by replacing a martingale argument by the use of a semigroup property. The extension to two functions
f
and
g
, instead of just one, has not been considered before, and will come to use in forthcoming work.

1.3
Bounding the influences

The first evidence of KPZ-scaling was established for the longest increasing subsequence of a random permutation, in work of Baik, Deift and Johansson
[
6
,
36
]
. Analogous results were soon after obtained for planar last-passage percolation with geometric weights by Johansson
[
35
]
and Baik, Deift, McLaughlin, Miller and Zhou
[
7
]
.
The approach developed in these works is analytic, exploiting combinatorial identities to enable techniques from the study of random matrices. A few years later, a probabilistic approach was developed by Cator and Groeneboom
[
18
]
and Balázs, Cator and Seppäläinen
[
9
]
. The probabilistic approach combines coupling techniques and queueing theory for the study of a stationary version of the last-passage process, revealing remarkable independence and large deviation properties.

KPZ-scaling refers to the scaling of so-called shape fluctuations and transversal fluctuations. For last-passage percolation this entails that the travel time
T_{n}
fluctuates of order
n^{1/3}
around its mean, and the geodesic
\pi_{n}
fluctuates transversally of order
n^{2/3}
away from the line segment connecting its endpoints; see
[
35
,
7
]
. In particular, as first proved in
[
7
, Corollary 1.3]
, there exist constants
0<c,C<\infty
such that for all large
n
we have

cn^{2/3}\leq\textup{Var}(T_{n})\leq Cn^{2/3}.

(9)

Due to Theorem
1.2
, completing the proof of Theorem
1.1
reduces to bounding
\sum_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}I_{v,i}(T_{n})^{2}
, where
I_{v,i}(T_{n})
is the influence on the travel time
T_{n}
of bit
i
used to encode the geometric variable a vertex
v
, and proving that this quantity is
o(n^{2/3})
when
n
tends to infinity.

Note that flipping the variables encoding the weight at a vertex
v
from zero to one has an effect on the travel time
T_{n}
only if that vertex is on some geodesic between the origin and
n\mathbf{e}_{+}
. On a heuristic level,
this suggests that the sum of influences of the bits associated to a vertex
v
are roughly proportional to the probability of this vertex being on the geodesic, and hence that

\sum_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}I_{v,i}(T_{n})^{2}\approx\sum_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(v\in\pi_{n})^{2},

where we recall that
\pi_{n}
is the set of points that are on some geodesic from the origin to
n\mathbf{e}_{+}
. Indeed, we shall later see that this is the case, with a small error in the exponent. This reduces the computation of influences to bounds on the probability of a given vertex being on the geodesic.

As mentioned, the geodesic between the origin and
n\mathbf{e}_{+}
is known to fluctuate within a range of order
n^{2/3}
from the straight line connecting the two points. Vertices at distance further than
n^{2/3}
from the diagonal should therefore contribute very little to the total influence. In addition, one could expect the contribution from vertices within distance
n^{2/3}
to be roughly equal, and thus roughly of order
n^{-2/3}
.

Given
v=(v_{1},v_{2})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, we let
|v|_{1}=|v_{1}|+|v_{2}|
denote the
\ell_{1}
-norm of
v
. We shall present two bounds on the probability of a given vertex being on the geodesic. While both bounds apply to all vertices in the square of side length
n
, the first will be relevant for vertices ‘far’ from the main diagonal, whereas the second will be relevant for vertices ‘close’ to the main diagonal. ‘Close’ here refers to a vertex
v=(v_{1},v_{2})
such that
|v_{2}-v_{1}|
being at most order
s|v|_{1}^{2/3}
and ‘far’ refers to
|v_{2}-v_{1}|
being at least
s|v|_{1}^{2/3}
, for some
s
growing slowly with
|v|_{1}
. (We later set
s=C(\log|v|_{1})^{1/3}
.)

The theorem we prove is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists
0<c,C<\infty
such that for all
n\geq 1
and
v\in[0,n]^{2}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
such that
|v|_{1}\leq n
then

(i)

\displaystyle{{\mathbb{P}}\big(v\in\pi(0,n\mathbf{e}_{+})\big)\leq C\exp\left(-c\frac{|v_{2}-v_{1}|^{3}}{|v|_{1}^{2}}\right)};

(ii)

\displaystyle{{\mathbb{P}}\big(v\in\pi(0,n\mathbf{e}_{+})\big)\leq C\left(\frac{\log|v|_{1}}{|v|_{1}}\right)^{2/3}}.

While the correct order of fluctuations have been known since the work of Baik, Deift and Johansson
[
6
,
36
]
, refinements of these methods able to produce upper bounds on the probability of visiting a given vertex are more recent.
We will derive the first part of Theorem
1.3
from known large deviation bounds on so-called exit points in stationary last-passage percolation. Such bounds were obtained for exponential last-passage percolation by Basu, Ganguly and Zhang
[
10
]
via the analytic approach, and by Elnur, Janjigian and Seppäläinen
[
26
]
via coupling techniques of the probabilistic approach. The latter was extended to geometric last-passage percolation by Groathouse, Janjigian and Rassoul-Agha
[
30
]
. We deduce part
(i)
of Theorem
1.3
from Theorem
3.6
, which is a slight reformulation of a theorem from
[
30
]
.

The second part of Theorem
1.3
requires a careful analysis of the probability of a given vertex close to the line segment connecting the origin to
n\mathbf{e}_{+}
being on the geodesic.
Basu, Hoffman and Sly
[
11
]
obtained bounds of this type from coalescence estimates obtained via the analytic method.
Shortly after, Balázs, Busani and Seppäläinen
[
8
]
obtained similar bounds using the coupling technique of stationary last-passage percolation processes. This technique consists in reducing the calculation of the probability of a vertex close to the main diagonal being on a geodesic, to a calculation of a random walk staying above zero for a given number of steps. We shall here opt for this more probabilistic approach.

In
[
8
]
, the authors considered exponential last-passage percolation. Their approach was later extended to geometric last-passage percolation by Groathouse, Janjigian and Rassoul-Agha
[
30
]
. Although the precise statement of the above theorem is new, these works obtained versions of this theorem. Our Theorem
1.3
refines the estimates in these papers, and extends them to cover the full
n\times n
square, as opposed to merely apply for vertices in the centre of the square, as in all of the above mentioned works. Indeed, it will be crucial to obtain bounds for vertices also close to the corners in order to bound the sum of influences, and hence prove Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 1.4
.

We have, in Theorem
1.3
, considered diagonal crossings of squares only. However, the same proof extends to diagonal crossings of rectangles. More precisely, it is possible to show that for every
\delta>0
there exists constants
0<c,C<\infty
such that for all
0\leq v\leq u
such that
u_{2}/u_{1}\in(\delta,1/\delta)
and
|v|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}|u|_{1}
we have

(i)

\displaystyle{{\mathbb{P}}\big(v\in\pi(0,u)\big)\leq C\exp\left(-c\frac{|v_{2}-v_{1}u_{2}/u_{1}|^{3}}{|v|_{1}^{2}}\right)};

(ii)

\displaystyle{{\mathbb{P}}\big(v\in\pi(0,u)\big)\leq C\left(\frac{\log|v|_{1}}{|v|_{1}}\right)^{2/3}}.

Remark 1.5
.

As an immediate corollary of Theorem
1.3
we obtain the well-known bound on the transversal fluctuation exponent
\xi=2/3
; see Corollary
4.9
for the precise statement.
This result is, of course, well-known from
[
35
,
7
]
. The novelty here is the observation that the lower bound
\xi\geq 2/3
can be obtained via the coupling method of stationary last-passage times, avoiding the use of integral probability. (In
[
48
, Remark 5.4]
this was stated as unknown.) It is possible that this observation has been made before, but it seems plausible that the observation has not yet appeared in print.

1.4
Discussion of the notion of noise

When perturbing a uniformly chosen element in
\{0,1\}^{n}
, which acts independently and homogeneously on the coordinates, there is only one choice for a given noise strength. In the presence of an underlying geometry, other notions of perturbation may be natural, such as the exclusion dynamics studied in
[
16
]
, but these would not preserve the independence between coordinates.

When extending the notion of a perturbation beyond the Boolean setting, there are various ways to do this, maintaining the independence between coordinates. We have in Theorem
1.1
opted for a Bernoulli encoding of the geometric variables, and perturbed the configuration by resampling a small proportion of the bits in this encoding, much like in the Boolean setting. In the discussion that follows, we will refer to this perturbation, defined in (
4
), as the
bit-resampling noise
.

Since geometric random variables are usually introduced in the context of Bernoulli trials, the bit-resampling noise becomes a natural notion of noise in this setting. For other specific distributions, other notions of noise may seem natural. For instance, in the case of the Gaussian distribution, a notion of noise considered in the literature is by updating the Gaussian variables independently according to Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes; see e.g.
[
19
]
. The bit-resampling noise and the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck dynamics have in common that the corresponding Markov semi-group is hypercontractive, enabling an analysis of noise sensitivity via influences, as in Theorem
1.2
; see Theorem
2.7
.

For other distributions, such as the exponential, under which last-passage percolation remains exactly solvable, these notions of noise may also be applied. Indeed, any real-valued random variable can be encoded using a countable family of Bernoulli random variables. Moreover, an exponential can be expressed as the sum of two independent Gaussian variables squared. Hence, in an implicit way, Theorem
1.2
does not only loosen the condition on the codomain of the functions under consideration, but also on the domain.
Arguably, this generalisation of the input space is only in appearance, as we are limited to consider noise sensitivity with respect to the bit-resampling noise applied via an abstract encoding, which for most distributions would feel artificial.

In a setting like last-passage percolation, where weights are assigned to the sites of the square lattice, another candidate for noise is to resample each weight entirely with a small probability. This notion has the advantage of remaining equally natural for distributions other than the geometric one.
Let
(\omega_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
and
(\omega^{\prime}_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
be two independent collections of independent geometrically distributed random variables, with parameter
p\in(0,1)
. Let also
(U_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
be exponentially distributed with mean 1, and independent from the remaining variables. We obtain a perturbation
\tilde{\omega}^{t}=(\tilde{\omega}^{t}_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
of
\omega=(\omega_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
via

\tilde{\omega}_{t}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} \omega_{v}&&&\text{if }t<U_{v};\\
\omega^{\prime}_{v}&&&\text{if }t\geq U_{v}.\end{aligned}\right.

(10)

We refer to the perturbation in (
10
) as the
site-resampling noise
.

One would expect that the property of being noise sensitive should not depend on the specific notion of noise under consideration.
However, the site-resampling noise is (in general) not hypercontractive, so trying to extend our Theorem
1.2
to site-resampling of the geometric distribution fails; see Section
2.5
. Consequently, it remains an open problem to establish noise sensitivity of geometric last-passage percolation with respect to the site-resampling noise.

We next state a result which provides a comparison between the bit-resampling noise defined in (
4
) and the site-resampling noise defined in (
10
). This shows that our approach just barely fails to establish noise sensitivity also with respect to the site-resampling noise.

Proposition 1.6
.

For every
p\in(0,1)
there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
0<t_{n}\leq 1/(C\log n)
, we have that if

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Corr\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\omega^{t_{n}})\big)=0,

then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Corr\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{C\,t_{n}\log n})\big)=0.

In particular, if the travel times
T_{n}
of geometric last-passage percolation are noise sensitive for the bit-resampling noise along a sequence
t_{n}=n^{-\alpha+o(1)}
, then they are also noise sensitive for the site-resampling noise along the sequence
t^{\prime}_{n}=C\,t_{n}\log n=n^{-\alpha+o(1)}
. Inspecting our proof of Theorem
1.1
, which is based on Theorem
1.2
, shows that the conclusion of the theorem holds for the bit-resampling noise for sequences
t_{n}\gg 1/\log n
. Hence, our approach just barely fails to establish noise sensitivity for site-resampling.

1.5
From stability to chaos in last-passage percolation

Closely related to noise sensitivity is the notion of chaos, first explored in the context of Gaussian disordered systems by Chatterjee
[
19
]
. In the language of disordered systems, chaos differs from noise sensitivity in that it asks for the sensitivity of the so-called ‘ground state’ of a disordered system, as opposed to the sensitivity of the ‘energy’ of the ground state. Instead of making these terms precise, let us simply explain what they correspond to in the context of last-passage percolation. Thinking of the weight configuration
\omega
as a random potential, and the weight accumulated along a path as the energy of the path in the random potential, then geodesics play the role of ground states, i.e. energy-optimising paths, and their travel times corresponds to the ground state energies. Chaos would hence correspond to sensitivity of the geodesic
\pi_{n}
as the weight configuration is exposed to a slight perturbation.

As discussed above, there are various possible ways to extend the notion of a perturbation when moving from a Boolean to a more general setting. In order to align the discussion below with prior work on the topic, we shall here consider the site-resampling noise. Hence, we shall say that geometric last-passage percolation exhibits
chaos
if for every
t>0
, as
n\to\infty
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|\pi_{n}(\omega)\cap\pi_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{t})\big|\big]=o(n).

(11)

Chatterjee
[
19
]
studied certain Gaussian disordered systems, including random polymers, random matrices and spin-glasses, and showed that statements analogous to (
11
) hold under Ornstein-Uhlenbeck dynamics. Later, Bordenave, Lugosi and Zhivotovskiy
[
15
]
established a sharp transition at
t\asymp n^{-1/3}
, from a stable regime to a chaotic regime, for the largest eigenvector of an
n\times n
Wigner matrix. In the context of Brownian last-passage percolation, Ganguly and Hammond
[
27
]
established a sharp transition from stability to chaos, again at
t\asymp n^{-1/3}
. A weaker form of this transition was later established for a large family of first- and last-passage percolation models by Ahlberg, Deijfen and Sfragara
[
4
,
3
]
, occurring at
t\asymp\frac{1}{n}\textup{Var}(T_{n})
, which under KPZ-scaling is equivalent to
t\asymp n^{-1/3}
. In particular, it was shown in
[
3
]
that for geometric last-passage percolation under site-resampling, as
n\to\infty
, we have

Stability:

for
t\ll n^{-1/3}
we have
\Corr\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{t})\big)=1-o(1)
;

Chaos:

for
t\gg n^{-1/3}
we have
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big|\pi_{n}(\omega)\cap\pi_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{t})\big|\big]=o(n)
.

Note that the above result considers different observables in the two regimes, contrary to
[
27
]
which considers the expected overlap of geodesics in both regimes. Altogether, these results give evidence that the transition from stability to chaos, for models adhering to KPZ-scaling, should occur at
t\asymp n^{-1/3}
. We expect that the transition from stability to noise sensitivity scales similarly, leading to the following conjecture, stated also in
[
3
]
.

Conjecture 1.7
.

Consider planar last-passage percolation with geometric weights. The transition from stability to noise sensitivity occurs at
t\asymp n^{-1/3}
in the sense that, as
n\to\infty
,

(i)

for
t\ll n^{-1/3}
we have
\Corr\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{t})\big)=1-o(1)
;

(ii)

for
t\gg n^{-1/3}
we have
\Corr\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{t})\big)=o(1)
.

Further support for the above conjecture comes from a recent approach to noise sensitivity developed by Tassion and Vanneuville
[
50
]
. They showed, in the context of Bernoulli site percolation on the triangular lattice, that stability of the pivotal set for
t\ll n^{-3/4}
implies noise sensitive behaviour for
t\gg n^{-3/4}
, thus reproving the occurrence of the noise sensitivity threshold at
t\asymp n^{-3/4}
, as established already in
[
28
]
. This suggests, indeed, that the thresholds for noise sensitivity and chaos coincide.

We emphasise that the first part of Conjecture
1.7
was proved in
[
3
]
. Theorem
1.1
takes a first step towards a proof of the second part of the conjecture. We provide a list of further open problems at the end of this paper.

2
A generalization of the BKS theorem

In this section we prove a version of the BKS theorem, which is stated as Theorem
1.2
of the introduction, for pairs of functions on the hypercube that take values in
{\mathbb{R}}
instead of
\{0,1\}
. The approach we take involves interpreting the bit resampling dynamics as a Markov semigroup, and understanding its behaviour with respect to certain differential operators.

A key ingredient in the different proofs of the BKS theorem, as well as related results such as the Kahn-Kalai-Linial theorem and Talagrand’s inequality, is an hypercontractive inequality. This is for example discussed in
[
29
,
41
]
. Benjamini, Kalai and Schramm
[
12
]
used hypercontractivity in a spectral setting, and their proofs rely on the fact that Boolean functions are bounded and can take only two values.
It has been known for some time that interpolation methods relying on calculus with Markov semigroups and differential operations can produce noise sensitivity-relevant results. For instance, the hypercontractive properties of the semigroup associated to resampling bits of the discrete hypercube at constant rate was used to prove Talagrand’s inequality by Cordero-Erausquin and Ledoux
[
20
]
.
A similar approach was used by Chatterjee
[
19
]
to establish an equivalence between superconcentration and chaos for certain Gaussian disordered systems, such as spin glasses and directed polymers, based on the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup.
Related results have been obtained also by Eldan and Gross
[
25
]
.

Our approach, on the Boolean hypercube, will follow the exposition of related results presented in lectures held by Ramon van Handel, and transcribed by Rosenthal
[
43
]
.
Notably, we interpret Lemma 11 of
[
43
]
as a generalization of the BKS theorem. Our proof differs in that we extend the argument to biased coin flips and to pairs of functions
f
and
g
defined in terms of a countable sequence of bits, and we replace a martingale argument using properties of the Markov semigroup.
We mention once again that results of a similar flavour have been obtained in parallel by different methods in work of Caravenna and Donadini
[
17
]
.

2.1
Noise sensitivity in a semigroup framework

Let
p\in(0,1)
be fixed, and let
X=(X_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of independent Bernoulli random variables of parameter
p
. Let
L^{2}
denote the set of functions
f:\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f(X)^{2}
has finite expectation.
2
2

2

The space which we call
L^{2}
thus depends on
p
.
For
f\in L^{2}
, we write
{\mathbb{E}}[f]
and
\textup{Var}(f)
for the expectation and variance of
f(X)
.

We introduce the
bit-resampling process

\mathbf{X}:=(X^{t})_{t\geq 0}=(X^{t}_{i})_{t\geq 0,i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as the continuous-time stochastic process on
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
started from
X
and where the dynamics is described by independently resampling the bits according to independent Poisson clocks of rate 1. Hence,
X^{0}_{i}=X_{i}
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and at every time
t
the Poisson clock assigned to
i
rings,
X_{i}^{t}
is resampled according to a Bernoulli distribution of parameter
p
independently of everything else. Note that the bit-resampling process defined here differs from the bit-resampling noise as defined in (
5
) in that

\mathbf{X}\text{ is Markov, reversible and invariant for the law of X}.

Note, however, that the joint distribution of the pair
(X,X^{t})
is equal in the two definitions. Since the result we aim to prove is a result about the joint distribution of this pair, we may henceforth work with the bit-resampling process as defined here. The advantage for doing so is that we will be able to exploit the framework of Markov semigroups.

Given
x\in\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
, we will denote by
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{x}
the conditional probability and expectation corresponding to
\mathbf{X}
with initial configuration
X=x
.
We introduce the semigroup family
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
associated to the Markov process
\mathbf{X}
, where for
t\geq 0
we let
P_{t}:L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}
be defined, for
f\in L^{2}
and
x\in\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
, as

P_{t}f(x):={\mathbb{E}}_{x}[f(X^{t})]={\mathbb{E}}[f(X^{t})|X=x].

The family
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the
semigroup property
, which is a way of expressing the Markov property:

P_{t+s}=P_{t}\circ P_{s}\quad\text{for all }s,t\geq 0.

(12)

By stationarity and reversibility of
\mathbf{X}
we have for every
t\geq 0
that the operator
P_{t}
is symmetric:

{\mathbb{E}}[fP_{t}g]={\mathbb{E}}[P_{t}fg]\quad\text{for all }f,g\in L^{2}.

(13)

Using the semigroup framework, and properties (
12
) and (
13
), we may rewrite central quantities of interest as

{\mathbb{E}}[f(X)g(X^{2t})]={\mathbb{E}}[f(P_{2t}g)]={\mathbb{E}}[fP_{t}(P_{t}g)]={\mathbb{E}}[(P_{t}f)(P_{t}g)].

Subtracting
{\mathbb{E}}[f]{\mathbb{E}}[g]
from both sides gives

\Cov\big(f(X),g(X^{2t})\big)=\Cov(P_{t}f,P_{t}g).

(14)

We may also recast the notion of influence in terms of difference operators
(\nabla_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
acting on
f
. Given
f\in L^{2}
,
x\in\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
and
\xi\in\{0,1\}
, the difference in the outcome of
f
induced by changing the value of bit
i
to
\xi
in
x
is
f\circ\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(x)-f(x)
, where

\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(x):=(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},\xi,x_{i+1},\dots).

Whenever a bit changes value in the process
(X^{t})_{t\geq 0}
, its new value is decided by a Bernoulli random variable of parameter
p
. We introduce the difference operators
\nabla_{i}
, for
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, acting on
L^{2}
by sampling
\xi\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)
and letting

\nabla_{i}f(x):={\mathbb{E}}^{\xi}\big[f\circ\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(x)\big]-f(x),

(15)

where
{\mathbb{E}}^{\xi}
means the expectation is taken with respect to the random variable
\xi
only. We now note that the notion of influence, introduced in (
7
), can be expressed in therms of the difference operators as

\I_{i}(f)={\mathbb{E}}\big[|\nabla_{i}f|\big].

(16)

We can make the expression of
\nabla_{i}f
more explicit by computing the expectation with respect to
\xi
and using that
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
to write
f(x)=x_{i}f(\sigma_{i}^{1}(x))+(1-x_{i})f(\sigma_{i}^{0}(x))
, yielding

\nabla_{i}f(x)=(p-x_{i})\big(f\circ\sigma_{i}^{1}(x)-f\circ\sigma_{i}^{0}(x)\big).

(17)

Since
|f\circ\sigma_{i}^{1}(X)-f\circ\sigma_{i}^{0}(X)|
only depends on
(X_{j})_{j\neq i}
, the influence of
i
on
f
can then be expressed as

\I_{i}(f)=2p(1-p){\mathbb{E}}\big[|f\circ\sigma_{i}^{1}(X))-f\circ\sigma_{i}^{0}(X)|\big].

(18)

In the case where
f
is Boolean, we recover the classical definition of influences up to the factor
2p(1-p)
.

Ahlberg, Sfragara and Deijfen
[
3
]
worked in a similar setting with an
L^{2}
notion of influence, referred to as the ‘coinfluence’:
{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{i}f(X)\nabla_{i}f(X^{t})]
. Bounding the coinfluences will be central also here, and we shall do so by reducing it to a statement of
L^{1}
influences, as defined in (
7
) and (
16
).
Indeed it is common that the
L^{1}
norm of a gradient appears in
L^{1}-L^{2}
estimates such as Talagrand’s inequality. A reason for that, which will become clear in the proof, is that the hypercontractive inequality gives appropriate control of the coinfluence
{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{i}f(X)\nabla_{i}f(X^{t})]
in terms of
{\mathbb{E}}[|\nabla_{i}f|]
.

2.2
Operator properties

In this subsection we explore some key properties of the semigroup and difference operators introduced above, in preparation for the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
At occasions it will be useful to work with local functions, that is functions depending on finitely many coordinates:
We say that
f\in L^{2}
is
local
and write
f\in L^{2}_{loc}
if and only if there exists
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
f(x)=f(y)
whenever
x_{j}=y_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq m
.

Note that, for any
f\in L^{2}
, we can define the sequence of functions
(f_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
by

f_{k}(x)={\mathbb{E}}[f(X)|X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{k}=x_{k}]\,,

The sequence
(f_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is then a sequence of local functions which converges to
f
in
L^{2}
. Any time we invoke ‘density of
L^{2}_{loc}
in
L^{2}
’, it can be thought of as referring to an argument where we approximate functions of
L^{2}
by functions of
L^{2}_{loc}
in this way and let
k
go to
+\infty
.

Our first lemma expresses the commutativity of the operators
P_{t}
and
\nabla_{i}
. It is a consequence of independence of the bits with respect to each other.

Lemma 2.1

(Commutativity)
.

For every
t\geq 0,i\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
f\in L^{2}
,

\nabla_{i}P_{t}f=P_{t}\nabla_{i}f.

Proof.

By density of
L^{2}_{loc}
in
L^{2}
it is sufficient to prove
\nabla_{i}P_{t}f=P_{t}\nabla_{i}f
for every
f\in L^{2}_{loc}
. Let
f\in L^{2}_{loc}
and
m
be such that
f
only depends on the first
m
coordinates. Let
i\in\{0,\dots,m\}
,
x\in\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
and
t\geq 0
be fixed. Let
\xi
be a
\mathrm{Ber}(p)
-distributed random variable. When
\mathbf{X}
is started from
\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(x)
,
X^{t}_{i}
is equal to
\xi
with probability
e^{-t}
and it is an independent
\mathrm{Ber}(p)
random variable with probability
1-e^{-t}
,
so that under
{\mathbb{E}}^{\xi}{\mathbb{E}}_{\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(x)}
,
X^{t}_{i}
is a
\mathrm{Ber}(p)
random variable independent of
(X^{t}_{j})_{j\neq i}
. For
j\neq i
,
X^{t}_{j}
has the same distribution under
{\mathbb{P}}_{\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(x)}
as under
{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
. Therefore, the law of
X^{t}
under
{\mathbb{E}}^{\xi}{\mathbb{E}}_{\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(x)}
is the same as the law of
\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(X^{t})
under
{\mathbb{E}}^{\xi}{\mathbb{E}}_{x}
. As a consequence,

{\mathbb{E}}^{\xi}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(x)}\big[f(X^{t})\big]\Big]={\mathbb{E}}^{\xi}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{x}\big[f\circ\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(X^{t})\big]\Big].

Subtracting
P_{t}f(x)
on both sides and using the definition of
P_{t}
, it follows that

{\mathbb{E}}^{\xi}\big[\big(P_{t}f\big)\circ\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(x)\big]-P_{t}f(x)={\mathbb{E}}_{x}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}^{\xi}\big[f\circ\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(X^{t})\big]-f(X^{t})\Big].

Finally, we obtain from the definition of
\nabla_{i}
and
P_{t}
that the left-hand side is
\nabla_{i}P_{t}f(x)
and the right-hand side is
P_{t}\nabla_{i}f(x)
.
∎

The next lemma is an expression for the infinitesimal generator of
P_{t}
. We refer to this as a heat equation due to the infinitesimal generator
\sum_{i}\nabla_{i}=\sum_{i}\nabla_{i}\circ\nabla_{i}
being a discrete version of the Laplacian operator. It is helpful to keep in mind this analogy with a partial differential equation for interpreting other operator properties. It could also be referred to as a ‘quenched’ dynamical Margulis-Russo formula, whereas an ‘annealed’ version would be, for instance, the case of monotone functions in
[
50
, Lemma 3.2]
. It would be tedious to express in which sense this formula holds for general
L^{2}
functions, and we circumvent this difficulty by going through local functions.

Lemma 2.2

(Heat equation)
.

Let
f\in L^{2}_{loc}
. For every
x\in\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
,
t\mapsto P_{t}f(x)
is differentiable and its time-derivative satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}P_{t}f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\nabla_{i}P_{t}f.

Proof.

Using the assumption that
f
is local, let
m
be an integer such that
f
depends only on the first
m
coordinates. This allows us to consider
f
as being a function from
\{0,1\}^{m}
to
{\mathbb{R}}
. Note that
f
is bounded since
\{0,1\}^{m}
is finite. Fix
x\in\{0,1\}^{m}
from now on. By linearity of the expectation, for all
0\leq s<t
we have

P_{t}f(x)-P_{s}f(x)={\mathbb{E}}\big[f(X^{t})-f(X^{s})|X=x\big].

Let
E_{s,t}
be the random set

E_{s,t}:=\{i\in\{0,\dots,m\}:(X_{i})\text{ is resampled between $s$ and $t$}\},

and for
0\leq i\leq m
let
R_{i}(s,t)
be the event

R_{i}(s,t):=\{E_{s,t}=\{i\}\}.

By definition of ‘resampling at rate 1’, for every
i
the number of times at which
(X_{i})
is resampled between
s
and
t
is a Poisson random variable of parameter
t-s
and these are independent for distinct
i
. The function
f
is bounded, the powerset of
\{0,1\}^{m}
is finite and
f(X^{t})-f(X^{s})
is 0 on
\{E_{s,t}=\emptyset\}
. Summing over the possible issues of
E_{s,t}
yields, as
|t-s|\to 0
,

{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(X^{t})-f(X^{s})\big|X=x\big]=\sum_{i=0}^{m}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(f(X^{t})-f(X^{s})\big)\mathds{1}_{R_{i}(s,t)}\big|X=x\big]+o(|t-s|).

The event
R_{i}(s,t)
is independent of
X^{s}
and has probability
(t-s)(1+o(1))
. Moreover, the distribution of
X^{t}
given
X^{s}
and
R_{i}(s,t)
is that of
\sigma_{i}^{\xi}(X^{s})
where
\xi
is a
\mathrm{Ber}(p)

In the heat equation analogy, the following lemma is an integration by parts identity. Once again, this relies on the independence of the bits with respect to each other, and also on the stationarity and reversibility of the process.

Lemma 2.3

(Integration by parts)

For every

The following lemma expresses that the effect of changing a single bit at time 0 decays exponentially in time. We shall mainly use it for large

Lemma 2.4

(Time-decorrelation)

Let

be the first time at which

2.3
Hypercontractivity

By Jensen’s inequality, the operator

is a contraction from

Lemma 2.5

(Hypercontractivity)
.

Let
\rho:=2\frac{2p-1}{\log(p)-\log(1-p)}
if
p\neq\frac{1}{2}
and
\rho:=1
if

. For all

and

,

{\mathbb{E}}\big[(P_{t}f)^{2}\big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[|f|^{1+e^{-2\rho t}}\big]^{\frac{2}{1+e^{-2\rho t}}}.

Combining hypercontractivity with Hölder’s inequality, we can obtain the following corollary, see also
[
43
, Corollary 10]
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
f\in L^{2}
and let
\rho=\rho(p)
be as in the statement of Lemma

. For all

,

{\mathbb{E}}_{p}\big[(P_{t}f)^{2}\big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{p}\big[f^{2}\big]^{1-\tanh(\rho t)}{\mathbb{E}}_{p}\big[|f|\big]^{2\tanh(\rho t)}.

Proof.

Let
t>0
and
f\in L^{2}
and let
\rho
be as required. By Lemma
2.5
,

{\mathbb{E}}\big[(P_{t}f)^{2}\big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[|f|^{1+e^{-2\rho t}}\big]^{\frac{2}{1+e^{-2\rho t}}}.

Applying Hölder’s inequality to the functions

,
|f|^{1-e^{-2\rho t}}
and to the conjugate exponents

,
(1-e^{-2\rho t})^{-1}
, we obtain

{\mathbb{E}}\big[|f|^{1+e^{-2\rho t}}\big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[|f|^{2}\big]^{e^{-2\rho t}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[|f|\big]^{1-e^{-2\rho t}}.

Combining these last two inequalities yields

{\mathbb{E}}\big[(P_{t}f)^{2}\big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[|f^{2}|\big]^{\frac{2e^{-2\rho t}}{1+e^{-2\rho t}}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[|f|\big]^{2\frac{1-e^{-2\rho t}}{1+e^{-2\rho t}}},

which is the desired inequality.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

{\mathbb{E}}\big[(P_{s}\nabla_{i}f)(P_{s}\nabla_{i}g)\big]\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}[(P_{s}\nabla_{i}f)^{2}]{\mathbb{E}}[(P_{s}\nabla_{i}g)^{2}]}.

We end this section by discussing the limitations in extending the above proof to other semigroups (say, of Markov reversible processes). A key property of the bit-resampling dynamics is that it acts independently on the bits, which is at the core of the proofs of the commutativity (Lemma

For a semigroup satisfying a version of hypercontractivity with corrections, one could possibly extract a noise sensitivity criterion similar to the BKS theorem. In the case of independent Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes, hypercontractivity is known, so the following theorem follows with the same proof as Theorem

2.5
Non-hypercontractive semi-groups

The site-resampling semigroup for the geometric distribution is not hypercontractive. We sketch an argument to prove this for functions of a single geometric variable. We will omit a few computations related to computing the moments of a geometric random variable, and we will exchange derivative and expectations without justification.

Let

(0,1)
with positive derivative

q^{\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{p(1-p)}{2\sqrt{(1-p)\lambda(1-\lambda)}\big(\sqrt{\lambda}+\sqrt{(1-p)\lambda(1-\lambda)}\big)^{2}}.

(22)

Hence,
q(\lambda)
is a bijection from
(0,1)
to
(0,p)
. Finally, we set

p_{H}(\lambda):=q(\lambda)\quad\text{and}\quad p_{V}(\lambda):=1-\frac{1-p}{1-q(\lambda)}.

We note that
p_{H}(\lambda)
is increasing (from
0
to
p
) and
p_{V}(\lambda)
is decreasing (from
p
to
0
) in
\lambda
.

Next, we construct the set of boundary weights
\{\omega_{x}^{H}(\lambda),\omega_{x}^{V}(\lambda):x\in u+{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}\}
through an auxiliary process
L_{x}^{\lambda}
defined for
x\in u+{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
\{\omega_{u+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}^{V}(\lambda):j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
be i.i.d.
\textrm{Geom}(p_{V}(\lambda))
and independent of the bulk weights
(\omega_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
that define
T(x,y)
. Set, for
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

L_{\lambda}(u)=0\quad\text{and}\quad L_{\lambda}(u+j\mathbf{e}_{2})-L_{\lambda}(u+(j-1)\mathbf{e}_{2})=\omega^{V}_{u+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}(\lambda).

(Note that
L_{\lambda}(u+j\mathbf{e}_{2})<0
for
j<0
.) For
x\in u+{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
we set

L_{\lambda}(x):=\sup_{j\leq x_{2}-u_{2}}\big[L_{\lambda}(u+j\mathbf{e}_{2})+T(u+\mathbf{e}_{1}+j\mathbf{e}_{2},x)\big].

(23)

(The supremum is attained for finite
j
since the weights along the boundary stochastically dominate the weights in the bulk.) Finally, for
x\in u+{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
we define boundary weights by

\displaystyle\omega^{H}_{x}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=L_{\lambda}(x)-L_{\lambda}(x-\mathbf{e}_{1}),

(24)

\displaystyle\omega^{V}_{x}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=L_{\lambda}(x)-L_{\lambda}(x-\mathbf{e}_{2}),

and the
boundary travel time
or
last-passage time
for
u\leq x\leq y
as

T(\lambda;x,y):=\max\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega^{H}_{x+i\mathbf{e}_{1}}(\lambda)+T(x+k\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{2},y)\bigg\}\bigvee\max\bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\omega^{V}_{x+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}(\lambda)+T(x+\mathbf{e}_{1}+\ell\mathbf{e}_{2},y)\bigg\}.

(25)

We remark that we could equivalently define the boundary travel time by first defining the travel time of a directed path
\gamma
from
x
to
y
as

T(\lambda;\gamma):=\sum_{z\in H_{x}\cap\gamma\backslash\{x\}}\omega_{z}^{H}(\lambda)+\sum_{z\in V_{x}\cap\gamma\backslash\{x\}}\omega_{z}^{V}(\lambda)+\sum_{z\in R_{x+\mathbf{e}_{+},y}\cap\gamma}\omega_{z},

(26)

and then maximising over all directed paths from
x
to
y
.

As mentioned before, boundary travel times are here defined with respect to a base vertex
u
, but can be extended in a consistent way to the full plane. For this reason,
u
is not explicit in the notation.

Several useful properties of the boundary travel times are straightforward from the construction. First, note that any path attaining the supremum in (
23
) must visit either
x-\mathbf{e}_{1}
or
x-\mathbf{e}_{2}
, and hence that

L_{\lambda}(x)=\max\{L_{\lambda}(x-\mathbf{e}_{1})+\omega_{x},L_{\lambda}(x-\mathbf{e}_{2})+\omega_{x}\},

which can be rewritten as the identity

\omega_{x}=\min\{\omega_{x}^{H}(\lambda),\omega^{V}_{x}(\lambda)\}.

(27)

An analogous argument shows, for
u\leq x\leq y
, that

L_{\lambda}(y)=\max_{1\leq i\leq y_{1}-x_{1}}\big\{L_{\lambda}(x+i\mathbf{e}_{1})+T(x+i\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{2},y)\big\}\bigvee\max_{1\leq j\leq y_{2}-x_{2}}\big\{L_{\lambda}(x+j\mathbf{e}_{2})+T(x+\mathbf{e}_{1}+j\mathbf{e}_{2},y)\big\}.

Subtracting
L_{\lambda}(x)
from both sides gives, using (
24
) and (
25
), that

T(\lambda;x,y)=L_{\lambda}(y)-L_{\lambda}(x),

and hence that the boundary travel times are additive, meaning that for
x\leq y\leq z
in
u+{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}
we have

T(\lambda;x,z)=T(\lambda;x,y)+T(\lambda;y,z).

(28)

More remarkable properties of the above construction can be obtained via techniques from queuing theory. Indeed, interpreting the boundary weights
(\omega^{V}_{u+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}(\lambda))_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
as inter-arrival times and the bulk weights
(\omega_{u+\mathbf{e}_{1}+j\mathbf{e}_{2}})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
as service times, the weights
(\omega^{V}_{u+\mathbf{e}_{1}+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}(\lambda))_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
can be interpreted as inter-departure times and
(\omega^{H}_{u+\mathbf{e}_{1}+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}(\lambda))_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
as the sojourn times of a single server queue at equilibrium. This connection is by now classical, and we summarise the consequences thereof in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every
p\in(0,1)
,
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
u\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, we have for every
x\in u+{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}
that

(i)

(stationarity)
\{T(\lambda;x,x+y):y\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{T(\lambda;u,u+y):y\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}\}
;

(ii)

(distribution)
\omega^{H}_{x}(\lambda)\sim\textrm{Geom}(p_{H}(\lambda))
and
\omega^{V}_{x}(\lambda)\sim\textrm{Geom}(p_{V}(\lambda))
;

(iii)

(independence) the collection
\{\omega^{H}_{x+i\mathbf{e}_{1}},\omega^{V}_{x+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}:i,j>0\}
consists of mutually independent variables; the same holds for
\{\omega^{H}_{x-i\mathbf{e}_{1}},\omega^{V}_{x-j\mathbf{e}_{2}}:0\leq i<x_{1}-u_{1},0\leq j<x_{2}-u_{2}\}
.

Proof.

We refer the reader to
[
30
, Theorem A.2]
for details; part (i) is their (a.v), part (ii) is their (d) and part (iii) is their (c). Note that our notation corresponds to their notation in that
q(\lambda)=1-\overline{p}(\lambda,1-\lambda)
with
r=1-p
.
∎

Additivity of the boundary travel times (
28
), together with the distributional properties of the above theorem, show that

{\mathbb{E}}[T(\lambda;0,x)]=x_{1}{\mathbb{E}}[\omega^{H}_{0}(\lambda)]+x_{2}{\mathbb{E}}[\omega^{V}_{0}(\lambda)]=x_{1}\frac{1-q(\lambda)}{q(\lambda)}+x_{2}\frac{1-p}{p-q(\lambda)}.

From (
27
) we further obtain that

{\mathbb{E}}[T(0,x)]\leq\inf_{\lambda\in(0,1)}{\mathbb{E}}[T(\lambda;0,x)]=\frac{1}{p}\Big[(1-p)(x_{1}+x_{2})+2\sqrt{(1-p)x_{1}x_{2}}\Big].

In the limit, one may in fact show that the above gives an equality, and thus gives an explicit expression for the shape function
\psi(x)
, in that

\psi(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}[T(0,nx)]=\frac{1}{p}\Big[(1-p)(x_{1}+x_{2})+2\sqrt{(1-p)x_{1}x_{2}}\Big].

Just as in
[
8
,
30
]
, for the analysis below, we shall be required to consider boundary travel times for pairs of values of the parameter
\lambda
as a time. For this purpose we shall extend the above construction into a simultaneous construction of boundary weights for two different values of
\lambda
. More precisely, given
0<\lambda<\lambda^{\prime}<1
, let
(\omega^{V}_{u+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}(\lambda))_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be as above. Next, let
(\tilde{\omega}_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be i.i.d.
\textrm{Geom}(p_{V}(\lambda^{\prime}))
, and take
(\omega^{V}_{u+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}(\lambda^{\prime}))_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
to be the inter-departure times of a single server queue with inter-arrival times given by
(\tilde{\omega}_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and service times given by
(\omega^{V}_{u+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}(\lambda))_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
. It follows from standard result of queuing theory that
(\omega^{V}_{u+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}(\lambda^{\prime}))_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is i.i.d.
\textrm{Geom}(p_{V}(\lambda^{\prime}))
, and hence that in the simultaneous construction the boundary travel times, for
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
, individually satisfy the properties of Theorem
3.1
. In addition to the above, the joint process satisfies a few additional properties that we summarise in the next theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

For every
p\in(0,1)
,
0<\lambda<\lambda^{\prime}<1
and
u\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, we have for every
x\in u+{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}
that

(i)

(stationarity)
\{(T(\lambda;x,x+y),T(\lambda^{\prime};x,x+y)):y\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{(T(\lambda;u,u+y),T(\lambda^{\prime};u,u+y)):y\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}\}
;

(ii)

(monotonicity)
\omega_{x}\leq\omega^{H}_{x}(\lambda^{\prime})\leq\omega^{H}_{x}(\lambda)
and
\omega_{x}\leq\omega^{V}_{x}(\lambda)\leq\omega^{V}_{x}(\lambda^{\prime})
;

(iii)

(independence)
\{\omega^{V}_{x+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}(\lambda):j>0\}
and
\{\omega^{V}_{x+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}(\lambda^{\prime}):u_{2}-x_{2}<j\leq 0\}
are independent; the same holds for
\{\omega^{H}_{x+i\mathbf{e}_{1}}(\lambda):i>0\}
and
\{\omega^{V}_{x+i\mathbf{e}_{1}}(\lambda^{\prime}):u_{1}-x_{1}<i\leq 0\}
.

Proof.

We refer the reader to
[
30
, Theorem A.2]
for details; part (i) is their (a.v), part (ii) is their (a.iv.2) and part (iii) is their (b).
∎

Remark 3.3
.

For future reference, we also point out that it is immediate from construction that the boundary weights
\omega^{H}_{x}(\lambda)
and
\omega^{V}_{x}(\lambda)
are measurable with respect to the weights
\{\omega^{V}_{u+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}:j\leq x_{2}-u_{2}\}
and
\{\omega_{y}:y\leq x\}
. Similarly, the coupled weights
\omega^{H}_{x}(\lambda)
,
\omega^{V}_{x}(\lambda)
and
\omega^{H}_{x}(\lambda^{\prime})
,
\omega^{V}_{x}(\lambda^{\prime})
are measurable with respect to the weights
\{\omega^{V}_{u+j\mathbf{e}_{2}}:j\leq x_{2}-u_{2}\}
,
\{\tilde{\omega}_{j}:j\leq x_{2}-u_{2}\}
and
\{\omega_{y}:y\leq x\}
.

We proceed with a brief explanation of how to extend the above construction of boundary passage times on
u+{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}
to the full plane
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. We reiterate that this extension is not essential for what is to come, but will allow us to talk about semi-infinite geodesics, and relate them to the boundary weights.

To extend the construction, we note that the configuration
\{(\omega_{x},\omega^{H}_{x},\omega^{V}_{x}):x\in u+{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}\}
induces, for every
u\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, a measure on
({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}})^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
, equipped with the product topology and Borel sigma algebra. As noted in
[
30
]
, taking
u=(-n,-n)
, and sending
n\to\infty
, results in a limiting measure
{\mathbb{P}}^{\prime}
, due to the stationarity in Theorem
3.1
, which restricted to
u+{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}
is equal in distribution to the construction above, and in particular enjoys the properties stipulated in Theorem
3.1
. In a similar manner we may extend the simultaneous construction for two values
\lambda<\lambda^{\prime}
.

Although the above extension is not essential, we shall henceforth assume it is in force, and for that reason make no mentioning of a base vertex
u
with respect to which the boundary travel times are defined. Moreover, whenever we are considering boundary travel times for two different parameter values, we shall assume the coupling construction to be in force. We will continue to use the notation above.

3.3
Geodesics for boundary travel times

Recall that
\pi(u,v)
denotes the set of vertices belonging to some geodesic for
T(u,v)
. Similarly, we let
\pi(\lambda;u,v)
denote the set of vertices belonging to some geodesic for
T(\lambda;u,v)
, i.e.

\pi(\lambda;u,v):=\{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:\exists\text{ a $\lambda$-geodesic }\gamma\text{ from }u\text{ to }v\text{ such that }x\in\gamma\},

where we by
\lambda
-geodesic refer to a path
\gamma
satisfying
T(\lambda;\gamma)=T(\lambda;u,v)
, where
T(\lambda;\gamma)
is as in (
26
).

In order to be able to talk about an ‘upmost’ and ‘downmost’ geodesic between two points, we need a notion of order between paths. Given two (finite, directed) paths
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
, we say that
\gamma
is
above

\gamma^{\prime}
if and only if for every vertical line
V
intersecting both
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
, the minimum of
\gamma\cap V
is larger or equal to the minimum of
\gamma^{\prime}\cap V
(these minima exist because
V
is a fully ordered set and paths are finite); see Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
The directed path
\gamma
is above
\gamma^{\prime}
.

Equivalently, for every horizontal line
H
intersecting both paths, the maximum of
\gamma\cap H
is lesser or equal to the maximum of
\gamma^{\prime}\cap H
.

For
u\leq v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, we now define the
upmost
geodesic from
u
to
v
to be the geodesic from
u
to
v
that lies above all other, and denote this by
\pi_{+}(u,v)
and
\pi_{+}(\lambda;u,v)
, depending on whether we refer to geodesics with respect to
T(u,v)
or
T(\lambda;u,v)
. We define the
downmost
geodesic between
u
and
v
analogously, and use the notation
\pi_{-}(u,v)
and
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
.

From (
27
) we note that boundary weights dominate bulk weights, and it may therefore be favourable for the geodesic associated to
T(\lambda;u,v)
to remain longer at the lower-left boundary of
R_{u,v}
than the geodesic associated to
T(u,v)
. This will provide us with a comparison between geodesics for travel times with or without boundary. Let
E_{\rightarrow}(\lambda;u,v)
be the event that the first step of
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
is to the right and
E_{\uparrow}(\lambda;u,v)
that the first step of
\pi_{+}(\lambda;u,v)
is up, so

\displaystyle E_{\rightarrow}(\lambda;u,v)

\displaystyle:=\{u+\mathbf{e}_{1}\in\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)\},

\displaystyle E_{\uparrow}(\lambda;u,v)

\displaystyle:=\{u+\mathbf{e}_{2}\in\pi_{+}(\lambda;u,v)\}.

We then have the following lemma establishing monotonicity in the positions of geodesics with respect to
\lambda
-geodesics provided
E_{\rightarrow}(\lambda;u,v)
or
E_{\uparrow}(\lambda;u,v)
is satisfied.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all
u\leq v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, on the event
E_{\rightarrow}(\lambda;u,v)
the path
\pi_{-}(u,v)
is above
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
, and on
E_{\uparrow}(\lambda;u,v)
the path
\pi_{+}(\lambda;u,v)
is above
\pi_{+}(u,v)
.

Proof.

The two statements of the proposition are equivalent by symmetry, so we only prove the first one. Let
u\leq v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and let
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
Let
k
be the largest integer such that
u+k\mathbf{e}_{1}\in\pi_{-}(u,v)
and let
k_{\lambda}
be the largest integer such that
u+k_{\lambda}\mathbf{e}_{1}\in\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
. (These random integers
k
and
k_{\lambda}
are in this context often referred to as ‘exit times’.) We prove two things: on
E_{\rightarrow}(\lambda;u,v)
we have
k\leq k_{\lambda}
, and if the geodesics
\pi_{-}(u,v)
and
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
intersect in the bulk, then they coincide after their first point of intersection.

We first prove
k\leq k_{\lambda}
on
E_{\rightarrow}(\lambda;u,v)
. It is clear if
u_{2}=v_{2}
, because the geodesics are then both the horizontal line from
u
to
v
. It is also clear if
k=0
, so we can assume that
u_{2}<v_{2}
and
k>0
. For
j\geq 0
, let
\pi_{j}
be the downmost geodesic from
u+j\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{2}
to
v
. We then have that
\pi_{-}(u,v)
is the concatenation of
(u,u+\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,u+k\mathbf{e}_{1})
and
\pi_{k}
, and
k
is the largest maximizer of
\{\sum_{i=1}^{j}\omega_{u+i\mathbf{e}_{1}}+T(\pi_{j}):j\geq 1\}
. Thus, for any
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{j}\omega_{u+i\mathbf{e}_{1}}+T(\pi_{j})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{u+i\mathbf{e}_{1}}+T(\pi_{k}).

By (
27
) we have
\omega_{u+i\mathbf{e}_{1}}\leq\omega^{H}_{u+i\mathbf{e}_{1}}
for all
i
. Applying this equality for
j+1\leq i\leq k
in the above, we deduce for all
1\leq j\leq k

\sum_{i=1}^{j}\omega_{u+i\mathbf{e}_{1}}^{H}+T(\pi_{j})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{u+i\mathbf{e}_{1}}^{H}+T(\pi_{k}).

(29)

Moreover, on the event
E_{\rightarrow}(\lambda;u,v)
, the
\lambda
-geodesic
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
does not pick up weight on the left boundary, so that
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
is the concatenation of
(u,u+\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,u+k_{\lambda}\mathbf{e}_{1})
and
\pi_{k_{\lambda}}
, and
k_{\lambda}
is the largest maximizer of
\{\sum_{i=1}^{j}\omega_{u+i\mathbf{e}_{1}}^{H}+T(\pi_{j}):j>0\}
. Therefore, as
k>k_{\lambda}
would contradict (
29
), we deduce
k\leq k_{\lambda}
on
E_{\rightarrow}(\lambda;u,v)
.

It remains to prove that if
\pi_{-}(u,v)
and
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
intersect outside of the boundary, they coincide from that point onwards. Assume
\pi_{-}(u,v)
visits
x\in R_{u+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}
. By maximality of
\pi_{-}(u,v)
and the fact that it is downmost, we have
\pi_{-}(u,v)\cap R_{x,v}=\pi_{-}(x,v)
. Similarly, if
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
visits
x\in R_{u+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}
, since the restriction of
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
to
R_{x,v}
is maximal for
T(x,v)
, as well as downmost, we have
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)\cap R_{x,v}=\pi_{-}(x,v)
. Therefore, if
\pi_{-}(u,v)
and
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
visit the same
x\in R_{u+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}
, then they coincide after
x
.

This concludes the proof as
k\leq k_{\lambda}
means that
\pi_{-}(u,v)
is above
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
until these geodesics leave the boundary, and the coalescence property implies that
\pi_{-}(u,v)
cannot visit a point strictly below
\pi_{-}(\lambda;u,v)
, inside the rectangle
R_{u+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}
.
∎

The next lemma establishes that
\lambda
-geodesics ending in the same point are consistent with each other in the sense that changing the starting point does not change the geodesic in the region where both geodesics are defined. This is a rather straightforward consequence of the additivity of the boundary travel times.

Lemma 3.5
.

Consider
u,u^{\prime},v
in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
such that
u,u^{\prime}<v
, and let
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Then
\pi(\lambda;u,v)
and
\pi(\lambda;u^{\prime},v)
coincide in
R_{u+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}\cap R_{u^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}
.

Proof.

Fix
u,u^{\prime}<v
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Let
y
denote the bottom-left corner of the rectangle
R_{u,v}\cap R_{u^{\prime},v}
.
Let
x\in R_{y,v}
. Using additivity of the boundary travel times (
28
) we find that

T(\lambda;u,x)=T(\lambda;u,y)+T(\lambda;y,x)

and that

T(\lambda;u^{\prime},x)=T(\lambda;u^{\prime},y)+T(\lambda;y,x).

Combining the two equations leads to the identity, for all
x\in R_{y,v}
,

T(\lambda;u,x)-T(\lambda;u,y)=T(\lambda;u^{\prime},x)-T(\lambda;u^{\prime},y).

(30)

For
x\neq y
on the bottom-left boundary of
R_{y,v}
, let
\tilde{x}
be the neighbor of
x
that is inside
R_{y+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}
. Now, let
\gamma
be a
\lambda
-geodesic from
u
to
v
. This geodesic visits the bottom-left boundary of
R_{y,v}
, and leaves it at a point
x_{0}
, so that
T(\lambda;\gamma)=T(\lambda;u,x_{0})+T(\tilde{x}_{0},v)
. Moreover, since
\gamma
is a geodesic,

x_{0}\in\mathrm{argmax}\{T(\lambda;u,x)+T(\tilde{x},v):x\in R_{y,v}\backslash R_{y+\mathbf{e}_{+},v},x\neq y\}.

By (
30
), note that
T(\lambda;u,x)
equals
T(\lambda;u,y)-T(\lambda;u^{\prime},y)+T(\lambda;u^{\prime},x)
for
x
in
R_{y,v}\backslash R_{y+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}
, and since the difference
T(\lambda;u,y)-T(\lambda;u^{\prime},y)
does not depend on
x
, we have

\displaystyle x_{0}\in

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{argmax}\{T(\lambda;u,y)-T(\lambda;u^{\prime},y)+T(\lambda;u^{\prime},x)+T(\tilde{x},v):x\in R_{y,v}\backslash R_{y+\mathbf{e}_{+},v},x\neq y\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmax}\{T(\lambda;u^{\prime},x)+T(\tilde{x},v):x\in R_{y,v}\backslash R_{y+\mathbf{e}_{+},v},x\neq y\}.

We deduce that there exists a
\lambda
-geodesic
\gamma^{\prime}
from
u^{\prime}
to
v
going through
x_{0}
and
\tilde{x}_{0}
, and by replacing the segment of
\gamma^{\prime}
from
\tilde{x}_{0}
to
v
by the corresponding segment of
\gamma
if necessary (which does not change the fact that it is a geodesic, since both paths must be geodesics from
\tilde{x}_{0}
to
v
), we can assume that
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
coincide in
R_{y+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}
. This concludes the proof as any element of
\pi(\lambda;u,v)\cap R_{y+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}
thus belongs to
\pi(\lambda;u^{\prime},v)\cap R_{y+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}
, and vice versa.
∎

The backwards compatibility of geodesics, established in the previous lemma, allows us to define the
infinite backwards

\lambda
-geodesic
\pi(\lambda;-\infty,v)
as the union

\pi(\lambda;-\infty,v):=\bigcup_{\underset{u<v}{u\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:}}\big(\pi(\lambda;u,v)\cap R_{u+\mathbf{e}_{+},v}\big).

The above union is increasing due to Lemma
3.5
. We also let
\pi_{+}(\lambda;-\infty,v)
and
\pi_{-}(\lambda;-\infty,v)
denote the upmost and downmost infinite backwards
\lambda
-geodesics. This definition of infinite backwards geodesics is convenient to describe how
\lambda
-geodesics behave geometrically. The next theorem connects the
\lambda
-geodesics to the direction
\mathbf{u}_{\lambda}=\lambda\mathbf{e}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{e}_{2}
. Moreover, it gives a precise quantitative bound on how unlikely it is that a
\lambda
-geodesic deviates significantly from that direction; see also Figure
2
. Given
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
we let
\lfloor x\rfloor
denote
(\lfloor x_{1}\rfloor,\lfloor x_{2}\rfloor)

3.4
Boundary weights and Busemann functions

As an aside, which will not be used further in this paper, we end this section by interpreting the boundary weights introduced above in terms of so-called Busemann functions. We refer the reader to the survey
[
42
]
for further references to the literature.

Given a semi-infinite geodesic
\pi=(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots)
, directed ‘backwards’ in that
v_{0}\geq v_{1}\geq...
, we define the Busemann function
B_{\pi}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
of
\pi
as the limit

B_{\pi}(x,y):=\lim_{k\to\infty}[T(v_{k},x)-T(v_{k},y)].

(The limit exists almost surely for all
x
and
y
in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.)

It follows from a well-known compactness argument that for every
\lambda\in(0,1)
and every
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
there exists an almost surely unique upmost backwards-infinite geodesic
\gamma_{\lambda}(x)
with asymptotic direction
-\mathbf{u}_{\lambda}
. Moreover, for every
x
and
y
, the geodesics
\gamma_{\lambda}(x)
and
\gamma_{\lambda}(y)
coalesce almost surely. Indeed, Theorem
3.6
says that
\pi_{+}(\lambda;-\infty,x)
is the upmost geodesic in direction
-\mathbf{u}_{\lambda}
.

By definitions of boundary weights (
24
) and the additivity of boundary travel times (
28
), we have that

\omega^{V}_{x}(\lambda)=T(\lambda;x,x-\mathbf{e}_{2})=T(\lambda;u,x)-T(\lambda;u,x-\mathbf{e}_{2}).

Since geodesics coalesce, we may for every
x
find a point
z\in\pi_{+}(\lambda;-\infty,x)\cap\pi_{+}(\lambda;-\infty,x-\mathbf{e}_{2})
. Due to Lemma
3.5
, the finite geodesics from
u<z
coincide with the infinite geodesics on
R_{u+\mathbf{e}_{+},x}
, so that
z\in\pi_{+}(\lambda;u,x)\cap\pi_{+}(\lambda;u,x-\mathbf{e}_{2})
. In particular,

\displaystyle T(\lambda;u,x)

\displaystyle=T(\lambda;u,z)+T(z,x),

\displaystyle T(\lambda;u,x-\mathbf{e}_{2})

\displaystyle=T(\lambda;u,z)+T(z,x-\mathbf{e}_{2}),

and hence

\omega_{x}^{V}(\lambda)=T(z,x)-T(z,x-\mathbf{e}_{2})=B_{\pi(\lambda;-\infty,0)}(x,x-\mathbf{e}_{2}).

A similar argument shows that
\omega^{H}_{x}(\lambda)=B_{\pi(\lambda;-\infty,0)}(x,x-\mathbf{e}_{1})
.

In conclusion, it is possible to construct the boundary weights from Busemann functions, given the existence of a unique geodesic in direction
-\mathbf{u}_{\lambda}
. Indeed, this gives a simultaneous construction of the boundary weights for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
. However, this construction does not identify the distributional properties of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
, for which the queuing theory seems to be essential.

4
Bounds on the probability of being on the geodesic

In this section, we prove upper-bounds on the probability that a vertex
v\in R_{0,n\mathbf{e}_{+}}
belongs to the set
\pi(0,n\mathbf{e}_{+})
of vertices belonging to some geodesic between the origin and
n\mathbf{e}_{+}
. We will prove two bounds, that together amount to Theorem
1.3
. The first bound is a large deviation estimate on transversal fluctuations, which we derive from Theorem
3.6
. Since the transversal fluctuation exponent is
2/3
, this bound will be nontrivial only for vertices at least this distance from the main diagonal.

The second bound provides a precise bound on the probability of being on the geodesic for vertices within distance
n^{2/3}
of the main diagonal. We may expect that points within distance
n^{2/3}
from the diagonal are roughly equally likely to be visited by the geodesic, resulting in a heuristic upper bound of order
n^{-2/3}
. The second bound verifies this with a logarithmic correction.

The bound for vertices ‘far’ from the main diagonal we derive from Theorem
3.6
, which in turn was deduced from precise bounds on so-called exit times in stationary last-passage percolation obtained in
[
30
]
. The bound for vertices ‘close’ to the main diagonal will follow an approach from
[
8
]
, also employed in
[
30
]
, which reduced the event of being on the geodesic to an estimate of a random walk being non-negative for order
n^{2/3}
steps.

In both cases a notable difference from the above mentioned works is our bounds hold for all vertices in the square
R_{0,n\mathbf{e}_{+}}
, and not only for vertices at a macroscopic distance from the boundary.

4.1
Vertices ‘far’ from the diagonal

Given a directed path
\gamma
and a vertex
v
, we say that
\gamma
is
above

v
if every point in
\gamma\cap V_{v}
is above or equal to
v
. Similarly,

Theorem 4.1
.

There exists

Note that, by symmetry with respect to the main diagonal, this translates to a similar bound for
v

v_{1}\leq v_{2}

|v|_{1}

4.2
Vertices ‘close’ to the diagonal

We proceed in this section with an argument that provides a better bound on the probability of being on the geodesic for vertices close to the main diagonal.

Let
v\in R_{0,n\mathbf{e}_{+}}
be fixed. We start by shifting the plane so that
v
is mapped to the origin. By translation invariance, we have that

{\mathbb{P}}\big(v\in\pi(0,n\mathbf{e}_{+})\big)={\mathbb{P}}\big(0\in\pi(-v,n\mathbf{e}_{+}-v)\big).

(39)

We next note that if the geodesic between
-v
and
n\mathbf{e}_{+}-v
visits the origin, then it also visits either
\mathbf{e}_{1}
or
\mathbf{e}_{2}
. That is, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\big(0\in\pi(-v,n\mathbf{e}_{+}-v)\big)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\big(0,\mathbf{e}_{1}\in\pi(-v,n\mathbf{e}_{+}-v)\big)+{\mathbb{P}}\big(0,\mathbf{e}_{2}\in\pi(-v,n\mathbf{e}_{+}-v)\big).

(40)

Since the two terms in the above right-hand side can be handled analogously, or by referring to symmetry with respect to the main diagonal, we shall henceforth only consider the former of the two. Indeed, we shall prove the following bound on the probability of visiting the origin and
\mathbf{e}_{1}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

There exist constants
0<c,C<\infty
such that the following holds. For all
n\geq 1
,
s\geq 1
and
v\in R_{0,n\mathbf{e}_{+}}
such that
|v|_{1}\leq n
,
\min\{v_{1},v_{2}\}\geq C
and
|v_{2}-v_{1}|\leq s|v|_{1}^{2/3}
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\big(0,\mathbf{e}_{1}\in\pi(-v,n\mathbf{e}_{+}-v)\big)\leq C\Big(s^{2}|v|_{1}^{-2/3}+\exp(-cs^{3})\Big).

In order to prove this theorem we will follow the approach from
[
8
]
, also employed in
[
30
]
, which boils down to an estimate of a random walk staying non-negative for a certain number of steps.

In order to facilitate the notation, we shall for the remainder of this section let
w=n\mathbf{e}_{+}-v
. We are hence interested in the geodesic
\pi(-v,w)
. Note that

T(-v,w)=\max\limits_{i}\big[T(-v,i\mathbf{e}_{2})+T(i\mathbf{e}_{2}+\mathbf{e}_{1},w)\big],

(41)

where the maximum is over
-v_{2}\leq i\leq w_{2}=n-v_{2}
. Note that some geodesic from
-v
to
w
leaving the vertical axis at the origin is equivalent to the maximum in (
41
) being attained for
i=0
. For
-v_{2}\leq i\leq w_{2}
, let

D_{i}:=\big[T(-v,0)+T(\mathbf{e}_{1},w)\big]-\big[T(-v,i\mathbf{e}_{2})+T(\mathbf{e}_{1}+i\mathbf{e}_{2},w)\big].

(42)

Then
D_{i}
denotes the difference in travel time when traveling from
-v
to
w
leaving the vertical axis at the origin as opposed to at
i\mathbf{e}_{2}
. Then, the maximum in (
41
) being attained for
i=0
is equivalent to the event that
D_{i}\geq 0
for all
-v_{2}\leq i\leq w_{2}
. This leads to the identity

{\mathbb{P}}\big(0,\mathbf{e}_{1}\in\pi(-v,w)\big)={\mathbb{P}}\big(D_{i}\geq 0\text{ for }-v_{2}\leq i\leq w_{2}\big)

to be negative for

for

and

Next, we introduce the notation

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}

\displaystyle:=T(-v,j\mathbf{e}_{2})-T(-v,(j-1)\mathbf{e}_{2}),

(44)

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=T(\mathbf{e}_{1}+(j-1)\mathbf{e}_{2},w)-T(\mathbf{e}_{1}+j\mathbf{e}_{2},w).

Note that since any directed path from

. By a similar argument we conclude that also

. Note further that, by a telescoping argument, we have

T(-v,0)-T(-v,i\mathbf{e}_{2})=\left\{\begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{i}-\Delta_{j}&&\text{for }i\geq 0;\\
\sum_{j=i+1}^{0}\Delta_{j}&&\text{for }i<0.\end{aligned}\right.

In addition, we have

T(\mathbf{e}_{1}+i\mathbf{e}_{2},w)-T(\mathbf{e}_{1},w)=\left\{\begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{i}-\Delta^{\prime}_{j}&&\text{for }i\geq 0;\\
\sum_{j=i+1}^{0}\Delta^{\prime}_{j}&&\text{for }i<0.\end{aligned}\right.

In this notation, we may express the differences

in terms of the incremental differences
\Delta_{j}
and
\Delta^{\prime}_{j}
as

D_{i}=\left\{\begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{i}(-\Delta_{j}+\Delta^{\prime}_{j})&&\text{for }i\geq 0;\\
\sum_{j=i+1}^{0}(\Delta_{j}-\Delta^{\prime}_{j})&&\text{for }i<0.\end{aligned}\right.

(45)

We next note that the sequence
(\Delta_{j})_{j}
is independent of the sequence

It does not imply that the variables within each sequence

That is, for

is approximately equal in distribution to a zero-mean random walk with i.i.d. increments. Consequently, the probability in (

\sum_{j=i+1}^{0}(\Delta_{j}-\Delta^{\prime}_{j})\leq\sum_{j=i+1}^{0}Z_{j}&&&\text{for }-k\leq i<0.\end{aligned}\right.

As we shall see, in Lemma
4.8
below, the variables in each of the sequences
(Y_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and

are i.i.d. with positive mean of order

. In addition, the two sequences are in fact mutually independent. The sequence
(D_{i})_{-k\leq i\leq k}
can thus be bounded by two independent random walks with a slight positive drift. In light of (

), it will suffice to bound the probability that these random walks remain non-negative for distance
k=\lfloor 2s|v|_{1}^{2/3}\rfloor+1
. That is, we will need a bound on the non-recurrence of a random walk with a positive drift. Recurrence bounds of this type for random walks without drift are classical; see e.g.
[
39
, Lemma 5.1.8]
. The following proposition is an extension to random walks with non-negative drift, and is proved similarly.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. random variables with mean
\mu\geq 0
, variance
\sigma^{2}>0
satisfying
\delta:={\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}\geq 1)>0
. Set
S_{0}=0
and let
S_{k}=X_{1}+\ldots+X_{k}
for
k\geq 1
. Then

{\mathbb{P}}\Big(S_{k}\geq 0\text{ for }k=1,2,\ldots,N\Big)\leq\frac{4\sigma}{\delta\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\mu}{\delta}.

For
\sigma
and
\delta
bounded away from zero and infinity, and with the mean varying with
N
, the bound can be expressed as
C\max\{1/\sqrt{N},\mu\}
for some constant
C<\infty
.

Proof.

Let
q_{N}:={\mathbb{P}}(S_{k}\geq 0\text{ for }k=1,2,\ldots,N)
, and note that
q_{N}
is non-increasing in
N
. We have, moreover, that

{\mathbb{P}}(S_{1}\geq 1,\ldots,S_{N}\geq 1)\geq{\mathbb{P}}(X_{1}\geq 1){\mathbb{P}}(S_{k}-X_{1}\geq 0\text{ for }k=2,3,\ldots,N)=\delta q_{N-1}\geq\delta q_{N}.

(49)

For
N\geq k\geq 1
let
J_{k,N}:=\{S_{k+1},\ldots,S_{N}\geq S_{k}+1\}
, and note (from (
49
)) that

{\mathbb{P}}(J_{k,N})={\mathbb{P}}(S_{1}\geq 1,\ldots,S_{N-k}\geq 1)\geq\delta q_{N-k}\geq\delta q_{N}.

Let
m_{N}
and
M_{N}
denote the minimal and maximal values, respectively, of
S_{k}
over the range
k=0,1,\ldots,N
. Then, since the number of times the process can jump up by one, and remain above that level until time
N
, is bounded by the range of
S_{k}
, that is that

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf 1}_{J_{k,N}}\leq M_{N}-m_{N},

we obtain that

N\delta q_{N}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\mathbb{P}}(J_{k,N})\leq{\mathbb{E}}[M_{N}-m_{N}].

(50)

Next, we define
S_{k}^{\prime}:=S_{k}-\mu k
by removing the drift from
S_{k}
. Let
m_{N}^{\prime}
and
M_{N}^{\prime}
denote the minimal and maximal values, respectively, of
S_{k}^{\prime}
over the range
k=0,1,\ldots,N
. Since
\mu\geq 0
by assumption, we have
m_{N}\geq m_{N}^{\prime}
and
M_{N}\leq M_{N}^{\prime}+\mu N
. Consequently, we have

{\mathbb{E}}[M_{N}-m_{N}]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[M_{N}^{\prime}-m_{N}^{\prime}]+\mu N\leq 2{\mathbb{E}}\big[\max\{|S_{k}^{\prime}|:k=0,1,\ldots,N\}\big]+\mu N.

(51)

Kolmogorov’s maximal inequality gives that

{\mathbb{P}}\big(\max\{|S_{k}^{\prime}|:k=0,1,\ldots,N\}\geq t\big)\leq\frac{\textup{Var}(S_{N}^{\prime})}{t^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}N}{t^{2}},

and hence that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\max\{|S_{k}^{\prime}|:k=0,1,\ldots,N\}\big]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\big(\max\{|S_{k}^{\prime}|:k=0,1,\ldots,N\}\geq t\big)\,dt

(52)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma\sqrt{N}+\int_{\sigma\sqrt{N}}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma^{2}N}{t^{2}}\,dt=\sigma\sqrt{N}+\frac{\sigma^{2}N}{\sigma\sqrt{N}}=2\sigma\sqrt{N}.

Finally, putting together (
50
), (
51
) and (
52
), we obtain that

q_{N}\leq\frac{1}{\delta N}{\mathbb{E}}[M_{N}-m_{N}]\leq\frac{1}{\delta N}\Big(2{\mathbb{E}}\big[\max\{|S_{k}^{\prime}|:k=0,1,\ldots,N\}\big]+\mu N\Big)\leq\frac{1}{\delta N}\Big(4\sigma\sqrt{N}+\mu N\Big),

as required.
∎

and
Z_{j}
in (
48
).

.

There exist constants

4.4
The transversal fluctuation exponent via the coupling method

We here derive as a corollary to Theorem
1.3
that the transversal fluctuation exponent
\xi=2/3
. This corollary will not be used in the remainder of the paper. In order to emphasise the fact that Theorem
1.3
applies to vertices all the way to the corners, we consider here a sharper definition of the fluctuation exponent, which considers the whole segment of the geodesic.

For
\alpha>0
and integers
\ell,k,n\geq 1
we let

\displaystyle I(\alpha,k,n)

\displaystyle:=k\mathbf{e}_{+}+\big[-\min\{k,n-k\}^{\alpha}\mathbf{e}_{2},\min\{k,n-k\}^{\alpha}\mathbf{e}_{2}\big],

\displaystyle B(\alpha,\ell,n)

\displaystyle:=\big\{v\in R_{0,n\mathbf{e}_{+}}:|v_{2}-v_{1}|\leq\min\{|v|_{1},|n\mathbf{e}_{+}-v|_{1}\}^{\alpha}+\ell\big\}.

That is, for
k\leq n/2
,
I(\alpha,k,n)
is an interval of width
2k^{\alpha}
around the diagonal, and
B(\alpha,\ell,n)
is the set containing every point
v
at distance at most
|v|_{1}^{\alpha}+\ell
from the diagonal.
We now set

\displaystyle\xi_{-}

\displaystyle:=\sup\big\{\alpha>0:\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\sup_{n\geq 2\ell}\max_{\ell\leq k\leq n-\ell}{\mathbb{P}}\big(\pi_{n}\cap I(\alpha,k,n)\neq\emptyset\big)=0\big\},

\displaystyle\xi_{+}

\displaystyle:=\inf\big\{\alpha>0:\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\inf_{n\geq\ell}{\mathbb{P}}\big(\pi_{n}\subseteq B(\alpha,\ell,n)\big)=1\big\}.

Corollary 4.9
.

For planar last-passage percolation with geometric weights we have
\xi_{-}=\xi_{+}=2/3
.

Proof.

By definition, we have
\xi_{-}\leq\xi_{+}
, so it will suffice to show that
\xi_{+}\leq 2/3
and
\xi_{-}\geq 2/3
. We begin with the former of the two.

Take
\alpha>2/3
. By part
(i)
of Theorem
1.3
, and by symmetry with respect to the transverse diagonal, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\big(\pi_{n}\not\subseteq B(\alpha,\ell,n)\big)\leq\sum_{v\in B(\alpha,\ell,n)^{c}}{\mathbb{P}}(v\in\pi_{n})\leq\sum_{v\in B(\alpha,\ell,n)^{c}:|v|_{1}\leq n}2\exp\bigg(-\frac{(|v|_{1}^{\alpha}+\ell)^{3}}{|v|_{1}^{2}}\bigg).

Discriminating with respect to the
\ell_{1}
-norm gives the further upper bound

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{v\in B(\alpha,\ell,n)^{c}:|v|_{1}=k}2\exp\big(-k^{3\alpha-2}-\ell^{3}\cdot k^{-2}\big)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}2\ell\exp\big(-\ell\big)+\sum_{k=\ell}^{n}2k\exp\big(-k^{3\alpha-2}\big)

We then set
\beta=\min\{1,3\alpha-2\}

C<\infty
such that

{\mathbb{P}}\big(\pi_{n}\subseteq B(\alpha,\ell,n)\big)=1-{\mathbb{P}}\big(\pi_{n}\not\subseteq B(\alpha,\ell,n)\big)\geq 1-\frac{2C}{\ell}-2C\sum_{k=\ell}^{\infty}k^{-2},

and so that

Now, take

By symmetry with respect to the transverse diagonal, it follows that

\max_{\ell\leq k\leq n-\ell}{\mathbb{P}}\big(\pi_{n}\cap I(\alpha,k,n)\leq 2C(\log\ell)^{2/3}\ell^{\alpha-2/3},

for all sufficiently large

∎

For
k
fixed, we split the sum over vertices in
R_{0,n\mathbf{e}_{+}}
with
|v|_{1}=k
into two sums, depending on whether
v
is ‘close’ or ‘far’ from the main diagonal. Let
C_{k}=\{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}:|v|_{1}=k,|v_{2}-v_{1}|\leq k^{2/3+\delta}\}
and
F_{k}=\{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{2}:|v|_{1}=k,|v_{2}-v_{1}|>k^{2/3+\delta}\}
. This gives the further bound

\sum_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(v\in\pi_{n})^{2-\delta}\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigg[\sum_{v\in C_{k}}{\mathbb{P}}(v\in\pi_{n})^{2-\delta}+\sum_{v\in F_{k}}{\mathbb{P}}(v\in\pi_{n})^{2-\delta}\bigg].

Part
(i)
of Theorem
1.3
gives for
v\in F_{k}
that
{\mathbb{P}}(v\in\pi_{n})\leq C\exp(-ck^{3\delta})
, and part
(ii)
of the same theorem gives for
v\in C_{k}
that
{\mathbb{P}}(v\in\pi_{n})\leq Ck^{-2/3+\delta}
, for some constants
0<c,C<\infty
. Consequently, we obtain that

\sum_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(v\in\pi_{n})^{2-\delta}\leq 2C\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigg[\sum_{v\in C_{k}}k^{-(2-\delta)(2/3-\delta)}+\sum_{v\in F_{k}}\exp\big(-2ck^{3\delta}\big)\bigg],

which is upper bounded by

4C\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[k^{2/3+\delta}k^{-(2-\delta)(2/3-\delta)}+k\exp\big(-2ck^{3\delta}\big)\Big]\leq 4C\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^{-2/3+4\delta}+C\leq Cn^{1/3+4\delta}+C.

This shows, via (
59
), that (
58
) holds, as required.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

The sum of influences squared is smaller than the variance by a polynomial margin. Examining the exponent
\theta(p,t)=\tanh(\rho t/2)
, where
\rho
is a constant depending on
p
, we note that for any sequence
(t_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that
t_{n}\log n\to\infty
, the above proof gives that

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\Corr\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\omega^{t_{n}})\big)=0.

6
Comparison between bit-resampling and site-resampling noise

In Section
5
we proved noise sensitivity of the travel times
T_{n}
for the bit-resampling noise
(\omega_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. We shall here compare the bit-resampling noise to the site-resampling noise .
On the heuristic side, for a typical vertex site-resampling results in a larger perturbation than bit-resampling. However, vertices with very large weight are more likely to be affected by bit-resampling than site-resampling at a fixed noise level.
In order to establish a covariance inequality, comparing site-resampling to bit-resampling, we note that there exists a constant
C
such that in a square of side length
n
, none of the geometrically distributed weights will attain a value greater than
C\log n
with probability tending to one. This is why we in Proposition
1.6
obtain a comparison between the two noises at noise strengths that differ by a log-factor.

6.1
Monotonicity with respect to resampling

Before we attend to the proof of Proposition
1.6
, we first formalise the idea that resampling more variables leads to a lower covariance in a general setting.
Let
I
be a finite set and
Y=(Y_{i})_{i\in I}
and
Y^{\prime}=(Y^{\prime}_{i})_{i\in I}
two independent vectors of independent random variables, all drawn from some common distribution. For every subset
S\subseteq I
we define another vector
Y^{S}=(X_{i}^{S})_{i\in I}
where

Y^{S}_{i}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} Y^{\prime}_{i}&&&\text{if }i\in S,\\
Y_{i}&&&\text{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

The idea that resampling more variables leads to a smaller correlation is captured by the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{I}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be any function such that
{\mathbb{E}}[f(Y)^{2}]<\infty
. Let
\mathscr{S}
and
\tilde{\mathscr{S}}
be random subsets of
I
, independent of
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
. Suppose that
\mathscr{S}\subseteq\tilde{\mathscr{S}}
almost surely, then

\Cov\big(f(Y),f(Y^{\mathscr{S}})\big)\geq\Cov\big(f(Y),f(Y^{\tilde{\mathscr{S}}})\big).

Proof.

By equality in law of
Y
,
Y^{\mathscr{S}}
and
Y^{\tilde{\mathscr{S}}}
, we have
f(Y)
,
f(Y^{\mathscr{S}})
and
f(Y^{\tilde{\mathscr{S}}})
are equal in mean, so

\Cov\big(f(Y),f(Y^{\mathscr{S}})\big)-\Cov\big(f(Y),f(Y^{\tilde{\mathscr{S}}})\big)={\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)f(Y^{\mathscr{S}})\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)f(Y^{\tilde{\mathscr{S}}})\big].

(60)

Summing over all
S\subseteq\tilde{S}\subseteq I
, using that
\mathscr{S}\subseteq\tilde{\mathscr{S}}
a.s. and that
(Y,Y^{\prime})
and
(\mathscr{S},\tilde{\mathscr{S}})
are independent,

{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)f(Y^{\mathscr{S}})\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)f(Y^{\tilde{\mathscr{S}}})\big]=\sum_{S\subseteq\tilde{S}}{\mathbb{P}}\big((\mathscr{S},\tilde{\mathscr{S}})=(S,\tilde{S})\big)\Big({\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)f(Y^{S})\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)f(Y^{\tilde{S}})\big]\Big).

(61)

We now claim that for fixed sets
S\subseteq\tilde{S}\subseteq I
we have

{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)f(Y^{S})\big]\geq{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)f(Y^{\tilde{S}})\big],

(62)

which via (
60
) and (
61
) proves the lemma.

Below we shall write
Y_{S}
for the collection of variables
(Y_{i})_{i\in S}
. Since
\tilde{S}^{c}\subseteq S^{c}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)|Y_{\tilde{S}^{c}}\big]={\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)|Y_{S^{c}}\big]\big|Y_{\tilde{S}^{c}}\big],

which via Jensen’s inequality implies that

{\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)|Y_{\tilde{S}^{c}}\big]^{2}\Big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)|Y_{S^{c}}\big]^{2}\Big].

(63)

Finally, since
Y_{S}
and
Y^{\prime}_{S}
are independent and identically distributed, and independent of
Y_{S^{c}}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)f(Y^{S})\big]={\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)f(Y^{S})|Y_{S^{c}}\big]\Big]={\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)|Y_{S^{c}}\big]{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y^{S})|Y_{S^{c}}\big]\Big]={\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(Y)|Y_{S^{c}}\big]^{2}\Big].

Together with (
63
), this proves (
62
), as required.
∎

6.2
Proof of Proposition
1.6

We shall construct a coupling of the bit-resampling and site-resampling noises, defined in (
4
) and (
10
) respectively. In the case of bit-resampling, the construction below will be identical to that in (
4
), whereas for site-resampling, the construction below will differ from the construction in (
10
) in order to create a suitable coupling between the two.
Before we begin we fix a constant
C<\infty
such that for all
n\geq 1

{\mathbb{P}}\big(\exists v\in R_{0,n\mathbf{e}_{+}}:\omega_{v}\geq C\,{\log n}\big)\leq Cn^{-3}.

(64)

This can be done since the geometric distribution has an exponential tail.

Let
(X_{v,i},X^{\prime}_{v,i},U_{v,i})_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a family of mutually independent random variables such that
X_{v,i}
and
X_{v,i}^{\prime}
are Bernoulli distributed with parameter
p\in(0,1)
, and
U_{v,i}
is exponential distributed with parameter
1
, for all
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Given
n\geq 1
, and with
C
as in (
64
), we let
M:=\lfloor C\log(n)\rfloor
and set, for
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
,

\tilde{U}_{v}:=M\Big(\min\limits_{0\leq i\leq M-1}U_{v,i}\Big),

which again is exponentially distributed with parameter
1
. For
t\geq 0
we let

X^{t}_{v,i}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} X_{v,i}&&&\text{if }t<U_{v,i};\\
X^{\prime}_{v,i}&&&\text{if }t\geq U_{v,i};\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{X}_{v,i}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} X_{v,i}&&&\text{if }t<\tilde{U}_{v};\\
X^{\prime}_{v,i}&&&\text{if }t\geq\tilde{U}_{v}.\end{aligned}\right.

Finally, we specify
(\omega^{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\tilde{\omega}^{t})_{t\geq 0}
by

\displaystyle\omega^{t}_{v}

\displaystyle:=\min\big\{i\in{\mathbb{N}}:X_{v,i}^{t}=1\big\},

(65)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{v}^{t}

\displaystyle:=\min\big\{i\in{\mathbb{N}}:\tilde{X}_{v,i}^{t}=1\big\}.

We emphasise that the construction of
(\omega^{t})_{t\geq 0}
is identical to that in (
4
) but that the construction of
(\tilde{\omega}^{t})_{t\geq 0}
differs from that given in (
10
) in order to create a simultaneous construction of the two processes on a common probability space. The process
(\tilde{\omega}^{t})_{t\geq 0}
, as defined in (
65
), is nevertheless equal in distribution to that given in (
10
).

Given a configuration
\omega=(\omega_{v})_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
we let
\omega\wedge M
be the configuration defined by
(\omega\wedge M)_{v}=\min\{\omega_{v},M\}
for all
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.
Note that, for each
t>0
, every bit that has been resampled in
(X_{v,i}^{t})_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},i<M}
has also been resampled in
(\tilde{X}^{Mt}_{v,i})_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},i<M}
. Lemma
6.1
, applied to
I=R_{0,n\mathbf{e}_{+}}\times\{0,\dots,M-1\}
,
\mathscr{S}=\{(v,i)\in I:t\geq U_{v,i}\}
and
\tilde{\mathscr{S}}=\{(v,i)\in I:Mt\geq\tilde{U}_{v,i}\}
, thus gives that

\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega\wedge M),T_{n}(\omega^{t}\wedge M)\big)\geq\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega\wedge M),T_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{Mt}\wedge M)\big).

(66)

We next claim that

\big|\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\omega^{t})\big)-\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega\wedge M),T_{n}(\omega^{t}\wedge M)\big)\big|=o(1),

(67)

and that the analogous expression for
\tilde{\omega}^{Mt}
, instead of
\omega^{t}
, also holds. From (
67
), for
\tilde{\omega}^{Mt}
, it follows that

\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{Mt})\big)\leq\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega\wedge M),T_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{Mt}\wedge M)\big)+o(1),

which via (
66
) gives

\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{Mt})\big)\leq\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega\wedge M),T_{n}(\omega^{t}\wedge M)\big)+o(1).

Another application of (
67
) now gives

\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{Mt})\big)\leq\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\omega^{t})\big)+o(1),

which would complete the proof of the proposition.

It remains to show that (
67
) holds as stated, as well as for
\tilde{\omega}^{Mt}
instead of
\omega^{t}
. Since the computations are similar for
\omega^{t}
and
\tilde{\omega}^{Mt}
, we present them for
\omega^{t}
only. Let
A_{t}
denote the event that there exists
v\in R_{0,n\mathbf{e}_{+}}
for which
\omega_{v}^{t}\geq M
. We write
A
instead of
A_{0}
for brevity.
Since
T_{n}
only depends on the weights associated to
v\in R_{0,n\mathbf{e}_{+}}
, we have on the event
A
that
T_{n}(\omega)=T_{n}(\omega\wedge M)
. Using that
T_{n}(\omega)-T_{n}(\omega\wedge M)\leq T_{n}(\omega)
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we deduce that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[T_{n}(\omega)\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[T_{n}(\omega\wedge M)\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(T_{n}(\omega)-T_{n}(\omega\wedge M)\big)\mathds{1}_{A}\big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[T_{n}(\omega)\mathds{1}_{A}\big]\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{P}}(A)\,{\mathbb{E}}[T_{n}^{2}]}.

Via the identity
a^{2}-b^{2}=(a-b)(a+b)
and the inequality
T_{n}(\omega\wedge M)\leq T_{n}(\omega)
, we deduce further that

{\mathbb{E}}\big[T_{n}(\omega)^{2}\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[T_{n}(\omega\wedge M)^{2}\big]\leq 2\,{\mathbb{E}}[T_{n}]\sqrt{{\mathbb{P}}(A)\,{\mathbb{E}}[T_{n}^{2}]}\leq 2\sqrt{{\mathbb{P}}(A)\,{\mathbb{E}}[T_{n}^{4}]}.

An analogous argument gives also that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[T_{n}(\omega)T_{n}(\omega^{t})\big]

\displaystyle-{\mathbb{E}}\big[T_{n}(\omega\wedge M)T_{n}(\omega^{t}\wedge M)\big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[T_{n}(\omega)T_{n}(\omega^{t})\mathds{1}_{A\cup A^{t}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{P}}(A\cup A_{t})\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[T_{n}(\omega)^{2}T_{n}(\omega^{t})^{2}\big]}\leq 2\sqrt{{\mathbb{P}}(A)\,{\mathbb{E}}[T_{n}^{4}]},

and hence that

\big|\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega),T_{n}(\omega^{t})\big)-\Cov\big(T_{n}(\omega\wedge M),T_{n}(\omega^{t}\wedge M)\big)\big|\leq 4\sqrt{{\mathbb{P}}(A)\,{\mathbb{E}}[T_{n}^{4}]}.

By (
64
) we have
{\mathbb{P}}(A)\leq Cn^{-3}
, and since

{\mathbb{E}}[T_{n}^{4}]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\Big(\sum_{v\in R_{0,n\mathbf{e}_{+}}}\omega_{v}\Big)^{4}\bigg]\leq 15n^{2}\,{\mathbb{E}}[\omega^{4}],

we conclude that (
67
) holds, as required.

Remark 6.2
.

The comparison between bit-resampling and site-resampling exhibited in Proposition
1.6
is not exclusive to last-passage percolation. A similar statement would hold for any sequence
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of functions
f_{n}:{\mathbb{N}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
which takes as an input independent geometrically distributed random variables, as long as the ratio
\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}[f_{n}^{4}]}/\textup{Var}(f_{n})
and the number of geometric variables
f_{n}
depends on grow at most polynomially in
n
.

7
Open problems

We end this paper with a few suggestions for further research. We begin with the obvious problem, discussed already in the introduction, of pinning down the correct threshold for noise sensitivity to occur.

Problem 7.1
.

Prove part (ii) of Conjecture
1.7
, i.e. that geometric last-passage percolation is noise sensitive, as
n\to\infty,
for
t\gg n^{-1/3}
.

Our main theorem shows that geometric last-passage percolation is noise sensitive with respect to the bit-resampling noise. Although we come close, as discussed in Section
6
, our approach is not enough to establish noise sensitivity with respect to the site-resampling noise.

Problem 7.2
.

Show that for every
t>0
we have
\Corr\big(T_{n}(\omega)),T_{n}(\tilde{\omega}^{t})\big)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

One way to approach the above problem would be to prove a version of Theorem
1.2
with respect to the site-resampling noise, as defined in (
10
). It is possible that such a result would only hold for a subclass of functions, since hypercontractivity fails in general; see Section
2.5
.

Problem 7.3
.

Prove a version of the BKS theorem for a suitable class of function under site-resampling.

Closely related to noise sensitivity is the question about exceptional times in dynamical percolation. In dynamical Bernoulli percolation, edges (or sites) are resampled according to independent Poisson processes. The central question is whether, at critical, there exist exceptional times at which an infinite connected component appears. (In dimensions
d=2
and
d\geq 11
, at a fixed time, an infinite connected component is known not to exist at criticality, almost surely.) Dynamical percolation was introduced by Häggström, Peres and Steif
[
31
]
, who showed that no exceptional times exist in dynamical percolation in sufficiently high dimensions. Interestingly, exceptional times were show to exist in two dimensions by Schramm and Steif
[
45
]
and Garban, Pete and Schramm
[
28
]
; see also
[
50
]
.

A natural candidate for an exceptional event in the context of spatial growth, substituting the occurrence of an infinite connected component, is the existence of bi-infinite geodesics, or bigeodesics. A
bigeodesic
is a bi-infinite directed nearest-neighbour path
(x_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
with the property that every finite segment is a geodesic between the endpoints. Bigeodesics always exist, as every horizontal or vertical line is trivially a bigeodesic. However, non-trivial bigeodesics are known not to exist. This was first proved by Basu, Hoffman and Sly
[
11
]
for exponential last-passage percolation, and an alternative proof was later given in
[
8
]
. The argument of the latter was later extended to rule out non-trivial bigeodesics in geometric last-passage percolation in
[
30
]
.

The question of exceptional times at which non-trivial bigeodesics exist was recently considered in work of Bhatia
[
13
,
14
]
. The existence of exceptional times at which a bigeodesics appears was not confirmed in this work, but bounds were given that shows that if exceptional times do not exist, then they just barely do not exist. At the same time, the author conjectured that if there are exceptional times at which bigeodesics exist, then the Hausdorff dimension of this set of times is zero.

Problem 7.4
.

Determine whether there exist exceptional times at which the origin is contained in a non-trivial bigeodesic.

In loose terms, a sequence of Boolean functions is noise sensitive if we cannot tell the outcome of the function when only partial information, selected randomly, is available. Benjamini, Kalai and Schramm
[
12
]
also posed some questions regarding partial information selected deterministically. A question of this type in the context of last-passage percolation would be the following. Consider again the encoding in (
3
), and suppose that for all
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
we are told the value of all bits in the encoding of the weight at
v
except for the first one. That is, for all vertices
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, we are told the value of the weight at
v
in case it is non-zero, but we do not know if it is non-zero or not. Based on this information, what can we say about
T_{n}
?

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all bits
(X_{v,i})_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},i\geq 1}
. The information obtained by observing
\mathcal{F}
can then be measured by
\textup{Var}\big({\mathbb{E}}[T_{n}|\mathcal{F}]\big)
. If
\textup{Var}\big({\mathbb{E}}[T_{n}|\mathcal{F}]\big)
is small in comparison to
\textup{Var}(T_{n})
, then observing
\mathcal{F}
gives little information about
T_{n}
.

Problem 7.5
.

Show that
\textup{Var}\big({\mathbb{E}}[T_{n}|\mathcal{F}]\big)=o\big(\textup{Var}(T_{n})\big)
as
n\to\infty
.

Last-passage percolation with geometric weights is one out of a few exactly solvable models known to belong to the KPZ class of universality. As mentioned in the introduction, it is possible to encode an exponential random variable either via a Bernoulli encoding or a Gaussian encoding, and hence prove noise sensitivity via Theorem
1.2
or Theorem
2.7
, for the respective notion of noise. One could argue that the exponential distribution is more naturally perturbed via site-resampling. We expect that proving noise sensitivity of exponential last-passage percolation via site-resampling is roughly equivalent to solve Problem
7.2
.
A more interesting extension could be to establish noise sensitivity for a continuum model of last-passage percolation, such as Poisson last-passage percolation (also known as the Hammersley process) or the related longest increasing subsequence problem of a random perturbation.

Let
\eta
be a unit Poisson point process on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and let
L_{n}(\eta)
denote the maximal number of points than one may collect when travelling from
(0,0)
to
(n,n)
along an up-right directed path. For
t>0
we let
\eta^{t}
be obtained from
\eta
by first thinning
\eta
by parameter
1-t
and then super-positioning an independent Poisson point process
\eta^{\prime}
of intensity
t
, sometimes written as
\eta^{t}=(1-t)\eta+t\eta^{\prime}
.

Problem 7.6
.

Show that Poisson last-passage percolation is noise sensitive in the sense that for every
t>0
we have
\Corr\big(L_{n}(\eta),L_{n}(\eta^{t})\big)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Let us finally propose a problem asking for a limiting distribution for the transversal fluctuations of a geodesic. It was proved in
[
35
]
that there exist constants
a
and
b
such that
an^{-1/3}(T_{n}-bn)
converges in distribution to the Tracy-Widom law. While the correct scaling of transversal fluctuations is also known, a similar limiting distribution remains unknown. One way to address this is in terms of the last position on the vertical axis visited by some geodesic from
-n\mathbf{e}_{+}
to
n\mathbf{e}_{+}
. That is, since

T(-n\mathbf{e}_{+},n\mathbf{e}_{+})=\max_{i}\big[T(-n\mathbf{e}_{+},i\mathbf{e}_{2})+T(i\mathbf{e}_{2}+\mathbf{e}_{1},n\mathbf{e}_{+})\big],

we may consider

Z_{n}:=\max\big\{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}:T(-n\mathbf{e}_{+},n\mathbf{e}_{+})=T(-n\mathbf{e}_{+},i\mathbf{e}_{2})+T(i\mathbf{e}_{2}+\mathbf{e}_{1},n\mathbf{e}_{+})\big\},

where we take the maximum since the equality may hold for various values of
i
.

Problem 7.7
.

Determine the limiting distribution, if it exists, of
n^{-2/3}Z_{n}
as
n\to\infty
.

It is well-known, since the work of
[
36
,
7
]
, that
Z_{n}
is typically of order
n^{2/3}
.
