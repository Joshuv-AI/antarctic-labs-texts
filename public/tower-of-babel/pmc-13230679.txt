Spatiotemporal antimalarial drug resistance saturation and emerging artemisinin tolerance in western Kenya during a pilot multiple first-line treatment study
Authors: Cole O. Andrew, Omolo Bernard, Chege Tim, Ochola-Oyier L. Isabella, Magudha John, Kinyanjui Sam, Ongas Magdalene, Abbas Ishmael, Onyango O. Boniface, Kandie Regina, Kibor Keitany, Kokwaro Gilbert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emergence of partial resistance to artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) threatens malaria control globally. This pilot multiple first-line treatment (MFT) for uncomplicated malaria study was conducted across three Western Kenya sites namely, Homa Bay County Mainland (intervention sites), Mfangano Island in Homa Bay County (intervention sites) and Migori County (control sites). The MFTs used were artemether plus lumefantrine (AL), dihydroartemisinin plus piperaquine (DHA + PIP), artesunate plus amodiaquine (AS + AQ) and artesunate plus pyronaridine (AS + PYD). The study was a serial cross-sectional analysis (September 2020–September 2022, extended to January 2024 for Mfangano Island) to characterize spatiotemporal resistance patterns following MFT deployment thus: (a) AL at baseline, then DHA + PIP for 8 months, followed by AS + AQ for 8 months, then AL for 8 months in Homa Bay County Mainland; (b) AL at baseline followed by AS + PYD for 36 months in Mfangano Island; (c) AL for 24 months in Migori County. Dried blood spots (n = 310) from malaria patients were analyzed for resistance markers (Pfdhfr, Pfdhps, Pfmdr1, Pfk13) using targeted amplicon deep sequencing. Deep sequencing generated 9,288,278 reads with mean complexity of infection 2.62 and 63% showing polyclonal infections. Core dhfr markers (N51I, S108N) showed saturation (100% non-wildtype), with emerging I164L detected at 12.2%. Dominant IRNI haplotype occurred in 93.7% of samples. For dhps, A437G and K540E reached fixation (100%), while S436H showed 33.4% prevalence. The mdr1 Y184F mutation demonstrated rapid temporal escalation in Homa Bay County Mainland (22.9% to 56.2%, P = 0.005). WHO-validated artemisinin resistance marker k13 A675V was detected (0.5%) and A578S, a k13 polymorphism not yet WHO-validated as a marker of artemisinin partial resistance, occurred in 7.8% of samples. Machine learning models achieved > 90% accuracy with genotype showing strongest predictive power. These findings suggest antimalarial resistance saturation in Western Kenya with emerging artemisinin resistance markers, indicating potential need for enhanced surveillance and alternative treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46982-0.

Introduction

Malaria burden and ACT context

Malaria remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), with Plasmodium falciparum (Pf) responsible for most of the clinical disease and death, particularly in children under 5 years and pregnant women1. Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) have served as the cornerstone of uncomplicated malaria treatment across SSA for two decades, and the World Health Organization (WHO) currently recommends six ACTs for this indication: artemether-lumefantrine (AL), artesunate-sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (AS + SP), artesunate-pyronaridine (AS + PYD), artesunate-amodiaquine (AS + AQ), artesunate-mefloquine (AS + MQ), and dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (DHA + PIP)1. Sustained investment in malaria control has yielded measurable progress; however, the continued efficacy of ACTs is now under threat from emerging parasite resistance, underscoring the urgency of resistance surveillance and treatment optimization2.

Health systems context and treatment efficiency

Sustaining effective malaria treatment in SSA requires not only appropriate drug selection but also efficient use of available resources within increasingly constrained health financing environments. Kenya’s transition from low- to lower-middle-income status has intensified pressure to optimize antimalarial treatment efficiency amid shifting donor support, as external funding for malaria control programs is progressively reduced3. In this context, prolonging the therapeutic life of existing ACTs, through strategies such as multiple first-line therapy (MFT), represents both a clinical and health systems priority, as premature ACT failure would impose substantial additional costs on already resource-constrained programs4–6.

Artemisinin partial resistance: definitions, distinctions, and spread

A critical distinction must be drawn between artemisinin partial resistance and ACT treatment failure as these terms though related, are not interchangeable. Artemisinin partial resistance refers specifically to prolonged parasite clearance time mediated by mutations in the Plasmodium falciparum kelch-13 gene (Pfk13), which reduces the susceptibility of ring-stage parasites to artemisinin derivatives without necessarily resulting in clinical or parasitological treatment failure7,8. ACT treatment failure on the other hand is defined as the recurrence of parasitemia and clinical symptoms following a complete treatment course, and is a distinct outcome that, while plausible in the context of established partial resistance, has not yet been validated in SSA in the manner documented in the Greater Mekong Subregion9,10. Nonetheless, artemisinin partial resistance is of serious concern because it subjects the non-artemisinin partner drug in the ACT to disproportionately higher parasite biomass, progressively eroding the partner drug’s protective role and creating conditions favorable to the eventual emergence of full treatment failure2. Partial resistance has been well-characterized in the Greater Mekong Subregion9,10 and is now an established concern in East Africa, with validated Pfk13 mutations, including R561H in Rwanda and A675V in Uganda and Kenya, detected at increasing prevalence8,11,12. Surveillance data from Uganda recorded Pfk13 resistance markers exceeding 20% prevalence by 2021–2022, representing a concerning regional trajectory13,14. Mathematical modelling projections suggest that widespread artemisinin resistance could result in more than 116,000 additional deaths and over USD 385 million in additional treatment costs annually15, reinforcing the imperative for proactive surveillance and adaptive treatment strategies.

Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine resistance: ongoing relevance beyond SP treatment

Although SP is no longer recommended as a treatment for uncomplicated malaria in SSA, surveillance of SP resistance markers remains highly relevant for three reasons. First, SP continues to be used for intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) and seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) across SSA, including in Kenya, where resistance evolution directly threatens the protective efficacy of these public health interventions1,16. Second, key SP resistance markers, mutations in the dihydrofolate reductase (Pfdhfr) and dihydropteroate synthase (Pfdhps) genes, occur against a background of continued drug pressure from IPTp and SMC programs, making their trajectories essential indicators of ongoing selection pressure in the population, independent of treatment context17. Third, the Pfdhfr triple mutant (N51I/C59R/S108N) and Pfdhps double mutant (A437G/K540E), which together constitute the quintuple mutant associated with SP treatment failure, remain dominant across East African parasite populations, and their further evolution toward higher-order mutants such as the sextuple mutant (quintuple + Pfdhps A581G) represents an escalating threat to both preventive and curative SP-based interventions17. Multidrug resistance protein 1 (Pfmdr1) mutations provide additional modulation of ACT partner drug susceptibility and are therefore also included as part of a comprehensive resistance surveillance panel18. Continuous monitoring of these markers is essential to inform evidence-based treatment and prevention policies7.

MFT rationale, surveillance gap, and molecular approaches

One strategy proposed to prolong the useful therapeutic life of current ACTs is the deployment of multiple first-line therapies (MFT), whereby more than one ACT is simultaneously or sequentially deployed as first-line treatment within a defined population4,5,19. The theoretical basis for MFT is that diversifying drug pressure across a parasite population reduces the selective advantage of any single resistance genotype, thereby delaying the emergence and fixation of resistance to any individual ACT6. Three approaches to anti-malarial drug resistance surveillance exist: therapeutic efficacy studies (TES), in vitro/ex vivo parasite susceptibility assays, and molecular surveillance of known resistance markers20. While TES provide the most direct clinical evidence, they are resource-intensive and logistically demanding21, and in vitro/ex vivo assays require substantial laboratory infrastructure 22. Molecular surveillance of resistance markers offers a standardized, scalable, and sensitive approach capable of detecting early resistance emergence, tracking spread, and characterizing within-host genetic diversity through single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and gene copy number variations (CNVs)23–25. High-resolution approaches such as targeted amplicon deep sequencing further enhance this capability by accurately resolving polyclonal infections and rare minor variant detection in high-transmission settings26. Western Kenya and the coastal region are the country’s primary malaria-endemic areas and therefore critical sites for resistance surveillance, given their high transmission intensity and exposure to multiple anti-malarial interventions27. Previous spatiotemporal molecular analyses in coastal Kenya28,29 and targeted amplicon deep sequencing in Western Kenya26 have documented concerning resistance trends, but comprehensive spatiotemporal analysis using high-resolution molecular approaches in the context of MFT deployment has not, to our knowledge, been undertaken.

Study aim

This study aimed to undertake a comprehensive spatiotemporal analysis of anti-malarial drug resistance markers using high-resolution molecular approaches following the deployment of MFTs in a malaria-endemic area of Western Kenya. The study was conducted as part of a 2-year adaptive cycling MFT pilot study previously described30. It employed targeted amplicon deep sequencing to characterize resistance patterns in Pfdhfr, Pfdhps, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13 across Migori County (control sites) and Homa Bay County, including Mfangano Island (intervention sites), over the MFT deployment period (September 2020-September 2022, extended to January 2024 for Mfangano Island). The extended surveillance on Mfangano Island, where AS + PYD was deployed continuously for 36 months, provided a unique opportunity to monitor resistance dynamics associated with the newest ACT registered in Kenya, offering molecular resolution of resistance evolution within a geographically defined, high-transmission closed environment during the implementation of an adaptive cycling MFT approach.

Methods

Study sites

This was a serial cross-sectional study conducted from September 2020 to September 2022, with extension for Mfangano Island to January 2024, across twenty-eight health facilities in Western Kenya ( ten in Mfangano Island , nine in Homa Bay County Mainland and nine in Migori County ).

Study drugs

The following ACTs were used as MFTs:artemether-lumefantrine (AL), artesunate-pyronaridine (AS + PYD), artesunate-amodiaquine (AS + AQ), and dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (DHA + PIP).

Study design (Fig. 1)

Graphical representation of the sampling time-points T1, T2, T3, and T4 (vertical silver bands) superimposed on the MFTs adaptive cycling and timelines. The horizontal bars (blue for AL, black for AS + PYD, red for DHA + P, and purple for AS + AQ) represent the cycling of ACTs in Migori County (control), Mfangano Island, and Homa Bay County Mainland, respectively. The red vertical line separates the baseline study period on the left and MFT deployment period on the right.

Intervention in Homa Bay County Mainland: AL was deployed for the first 8 months followed by the deployment of DHA + PIP for 8 months, then AS + AQ for 8 months. Finally, AS + AQ was replaced with AL to complete the cycling.

Intervention in Mfangano Island: AL was deployed for the first 8 months followed by the deployment of AS + PYD for 36 months. Finally, AS + PYD was replaced with AL to complete the cycling.

Control in Migori County: AL was deployed in the neighboring Migori County throughout the 24-month study period.

The 8-month rotation interval was informed by the adaptive cycling protocol in the parent MFT implementation study30, upon which this study is based. The protocol balanced operational feasibility with the minimum duration considered necessary to exert meaningful selection pressure on the parasite population while maintaining alignment to the parent study.

Sample collection and processing

Filter paper dried blood spots (DBS) were collected from malaria-positive, de-identified and anonymized laboratory samples across four time points (T) as follows: T1 (September 2020), which was the baseline when AL was still being used in all health facilities (Fig. 1), T2 (August-October 2021), which was 8 months following deployment DHA + PIP in Homa Bay Mainland, and 12 months following deployment of AS + PYD in Mfangano Island, T3 (May–July 2022) which was 8 months after replacement of DHA + PIP with AS + AQ in Homa Bay Mainland and 24 months after deployment of AS + PYD in Mfangano Island, and T4 (November 2023-January 2024), which was 8 months after replacing AS + AQ with AL in Homa Bay Mainland and 36 months after deployment of AS + PYD in Mfangano Island. T4 sampling was extended at health facilities on Mfangano Island due to the unique circumstance of having a new ACT (AS + PYD) in this closed environment to facilitate monitoring of antimalarial drug resistance markers for longer durations during which AS + PYD was in use. This asymmetry in time-point sampling necessitated balancing time-point analysis approaches for cross-site comparisons. Malaria diagnosis was confirmed by mRDT. All included samples were from symptomatic patients with confirmed P. falciparum mono-infection. Patients who reported prior antimalarial drug intake within 1-month preceding presentation to healthcare facilities were not systematically excluded at the time of sample collection.

DNA extraction and amplicon sequencing

DNA extraction: Four spots were punched from each DBS (a total of 316 samples) using a 6 mm puncher into sterile 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. For each sample, parasite DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, catalogue number: 51106) following manufacturer protocols, eluted in 70 μL of elution buffer and stored at -20 °C until PCR amplification.

PCR amplification: Targeted amplicons spanning the ama1 (PF3D7_1133400, nucleotides 465-923 bp), dhfr (PF3D7_0417200, 7-515 bp), dhps (PF3D7_0810800, 1439–1946 bp), mdr1 (PF3D7_0523000, 183–719 bp), and two fragments for k13 (PF3D7_1343700, 1276-1786 bp and 1630-2141 bp) genes were generated from each sample using previously described primers26, with slight modifications to genotype specific mutations within the k13 propeller domain.

Rationale for marker panel selection

The resistance marker panel was designed prior to study commencement to enable standardized cross-site baseline surveillance applicable regardless of intervention arm assignment. The selected markers (Pfdhfr, Pfdhps, Pfmdr1, and Pfk13) represent the most widely used and validated molecular indicators of antimalarial resistance in SSA, and their inclusion follows the standardized target product profile recommended by Nsanzabana20. Regarding the rotated ACTs specifically: validated resistance markers for artemether-lumefantrine include Pfmdr1 N86Y and copy number variation, both partially addressed by the current panel; Pfcrt K76T and plasmepsin II-III CNVs are the established markers for DHA + PIP resistance but were not included in the original panel design; Pfcrt K76T is additionally relevant to amodiaquine tolerance, extending this gap to the AS + AQ deployment period. Resistance markers for AS + AQ also overlap substantially with Pfmdr1 and Pfkelch13 content already assessed; and no validated molecular resistance markers currently exist for artesunate-pyronaridine, reflecting the novelty of this ACT. The absence of Pfcrt and plasmepsin II-III CNVs is acknowledged as a limitation and is discussed accordingly.

Barcoding and sequencing: All amplicons were barcoded using the Oxford Nanopore Technologies native barcoding kit (SQK-NBD114.96) and pooled into a single tube. Amplicons for k13 (targeting codons 469 and 675) and ama1 were pooled and sequenced in one run, while dhps, dhfr and mdr1 were pooled for a second run. Deep sequencing of the final pools was conducted on the SpotON Flow Cell R10.4.1 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, catalogue number FLOW-MIN114).

Bioinformatics analysis: SeekDeep v3.0.131 was used to assess sequence quality, cluster microhaplotypes, and identify variants. A “microhaplotype” was defined as a set of alleles co-occurring on a single DNA amplicon. To reduce the risk of false positives, only samples with a minimum read depth of 250 (SeekDeep’s default threshold) were included in the analysis. Alleles were filtered out if their frequency was below 5%, unless they were detected separately in other samples and exceeded the 5% threshold. Chimeric reads were considered as PCR artefacts and were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Allele frequency calculation: Allele frequencies at each codon were calculated by dividing the number of reads supporting each allele by the total number of reads spanning that codon within each sample and expressing them as percentages to enable the quantification of within-host genetic variability. Infections were classified as wild-type (alleles that match the reference), mutant (mono-allelic variants differing from the wild-type), or mixed (having both wild-type and mutant variants at the same codon position).

Haplotype analysis: The relative frequency for each microhaplotype was calculated by dividing its count in the population by the total count of microhaplotypes and expressing it as a percentage. This approach helped identify common and rare haplotypes, crucial for understanding genetic diversity and evolution patterns.

The complexity of infection (COI) estimates which are derived from these approaches are reported in the Results section.

Temporal and geographic analyses: Temporal trends were assessed using the Cochran-Armitage test for trend and balanced temporal analysis (T1-T3) was conducted for cross-site comparisons to ensure validity, while extended analysis (T1-T4) was performed for Mfangano Island-specific trends. Geographic comparisons employed chi-square tests for overall differences in categorical variables and Fisher’s exact tests for intervention effects.

Machine learning models: Machine learning models (logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Gaussian Naive Bayes) were implemented for resistance prediction using demographic and geographic variables as covariates.

Ethical review and approvals

This study was conducted as part of a health systems implementation program and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with the accession number NCT07362498. Ethical approval (number SU-IERC0624/20) was obtained from the Strathmore University Institutional Scientific and Ethics Review Committee (SU-ISERC) on February 27, 2020. A research permit (number NACOSTI/P/20/4427) was obtained from the Kenya National Commission for Science, Technology, and Innovation (NACOSTI) on April 1, 2020. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Informed consent procedures

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in the parent implementation study. For participants under 18 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians. Individual identifiers were removed from samples prior to molecular analysis to ensure confidentiality.

All participants reporting to the facility laboratory for malaria diagnosis were requested for voluntary informed consent (or ascent for those with sick children < 14 years old) in person before participating in the study. A study information sheet was provided to participants, and the study was described in detail with questions and clarifications actively sought after from participants. Individually identifiable information needed to contact and recruit participants (e.g., names, email addresses) was not linked to interview data. Consent was provided in Kiswahili and the local language (Dholuo).

Sequencing performance and complexity of infection

Sequencing metrics

Targeted amplicon sequencing generated 9,288,278 total reads across all targets: ama1 (1,709,435 reads), k13 fragment 469 (1,170,039 reads), k13 fragment 675 (1,043,228 reads), mdr1 (589,992 reads), dhps (1,117,760 reads), and dhfr (3,657,824 reads). Successful amplification rates were 72.9% (dhfr), 70.0% (dhps), 35.5% (mdr1), 57.4% (ama1), and 55.5–60.6% (k13 fragments). The comparatively lower amplification success for mdr1 may reflect the longer amplicon size targeted (183–719 bp, spanning 536 bp), which was the longest fragment of all amplicons that ranged between ama1 (458 bp) and mdr1 (536 bp). Re-sequencing of failed samples was not feasible given financial constraints and possible exhaustion of archived DNA eluate. We also considered whether adjusting the minimum read depth threshold below SeekDeep’s default of 250 reads might recover a larger proportion of mdr1 samples; however, reducing this threshold would have substantially increased the risk of false-positive variant calls and inconsistent mixed infection classifications, undermining the uniform quality control framework applied across all gene targets. The original threshold was therefore retained, and mdr1-derived findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution given the reduced sample size. Detailed complexity of infection (COI) analysis based on targeted amplicon deep sequencing revealed a mean COI of 2.62 (95% CI: 2.32–2.92, range 1–11) across 176 successfully sequenced samples. The primary COI determination using ama1 genotyping across all 310 samples yielded a mean of 2.0 (range 1–11). Significant geographic variation was observed (Kruskal–Wallis H = 6.34, P = 0.042), with Mfangano Island exhibiting the highest mean COI (3.06), followed by Homa Bay County Mainland (2.61) and Migori County (2.10). This gradient correlated with known transmission intensity patterns.

Monoclonal infections (COI = 1) comprised 36.9% (65/176) of samples, with the remainder showing polyclonal infections: 39.8% had low polyclonality (COI 2–3), 11.4% moderate (COI 4–5), and 11.9% high (COI ≥ 6). Notably, Mfangano Island had the highest proportion of high-complexity infections (16.9%), consistent with intense endemic transmission in this fairly closed setting (Supplementary Table S1).

COI by site and overall

Complexity of infection determination: COI was assessed using two complementary approaches to ensure robustness. First, ama1 genotyping was performed on all successfully sequenced samples (n = 310) by counting the maximum number of distinct alleles detected at highly polymorphic positions, yielding an overall mean COI of 2.0 (range 1–11). Second, for samples with sufficient sequencing coverage across all target genes (n = 176), COI was determined by direct haplotype counting from deep sequencing reads, yielding a mean COI of 2.62 (median 2.0, range 1–11). The concordance between these approaches (both indicating COI ~ 2 with range 1–11) validates the estimates. The slightly higher mean from direct haplotype counting (2.62 vs 2.0) may reflect the increased sensitivity of deep sequencing to detect minor clones that might be missed by single-locus ama1 genotyping.

Infections were classified as monoclonal (COI = 1) when only a single distinct haplotype was detected, or polyclonal (COI > 1) when multiple distinct haplotypes were present. Polyclonal infections were further stratified as low (COI 2–3), moderate (COI 4–5), or high (COI ≥ 6) complexity. Statistical comparisons of COI distributions across sites were performed using Kruskal–Wallis test with post-hoc Dunn’s test for pairwise comparisons, and Bonferroni correction for multiple testing (α = 0.017 for three comparisons).

Antimalarial resistance marker frequencies

Individual mutation analysis

Individual mutation analysis (Table 2) revealed critical stratification between saturated and emerging resistance markers. Four markers reached complete or near-complete fixation, rendering them unsuitable for future resistance differentiation: dhfr N51I (95.0% pure mutant + 4.5% mixed = 99.5% non-wildtype), dhfr S108N (99.5% non-wildtype, identical to N51I), dhps A437G (100% mutant), and dhps K540E (100% mutant). These saturated markers indicate that SP-based resistance is effectively universal in this population, with wild-type parasites representing < 1% of infections for dhfr core mutations and completely absent for dhps critical positions. In contrast, six markers demonstrated prevalence patterns suitable for ongoing surveillance. The dhfr resistance marker I164L occurred at 2.7% pure mutant plus 9.5% mixed infections (12.2% total), representing an emerging threat associated with high-level pyrimethamine resistance when combined with the triple mutant. The dhfr C59R mutation showed 87.8% mutant plus 9.5% mixed (97.3% total), approaching but not yet reaching complete fixation. For dhps, the S436H mutation demonstrated intermediate prevalence (13.6% mutant + 19.8% mixed = 33.4% total) with substantial wild-type persistence (66.7%). The mdr1 Y184F mutation showed complex infection patterns with 42.1% pure mutant, 22.6% wild-type, and notably 35.3% mixed infections, suggesting substantial polyclonal infection with both sensitive and resistant clones co-circulating. Critically, mdr1 N86Y remained completely wild-type (100%), despite Y184F prevalence. Individual mutation geographic patterns

The dhfr triple mutant IRNI showed near-universal prevalence (91.7–100%) across all sites with no significant differences (P = 0.389). However, the emerging super-resistance marker I164L demonstrated geographic variation with highest prevalence in Mfangano Island (12.9%) compared to Homa Bay County Mainland (11.7%) and Migori County (9.5%).

The dhps S436H mutation showed remarkable consistency across sites (31.3–35.3%, P = 0.926), suggesting uniform selection pressure despite varied intervention contexts. In contrast, dhps A437G and K540E demonstrated complete fixation (100%) at all sites, rendering these markers unsuitable for future geographic surveillance. The mdr1 Y184F mutation exhibited moderate geographic homogeneity (56.0–62.5%, P = 0.887), with slightly higher but non-significant prevalence in the control site (Migori, 62.5%) compared to intervention sites.

Artemisinin resistance markers showed low-frequency geographic restriction, with k13 A578S (unvalidated polymorphism) mixed infections detected primarily in Mfangano Island (9.6%) compared to Homa Bay County Mainland (4.0%) and Migori (6.3%) (P = 0.402). The single k13 A675V mutant was detected in Migori county, representing 0.9% of successfully sequenced samples from that site.

Overall geographic comparison

Despite varied intervention across sites, no statistically significant differences in resistance prevalence were detected between sites (Table 4). dhfr resistance ranged from 64% (Homa Bay County Mainland) to 72% (Migori) (χ2 = 1.481, P = 0.477) (Fig. 2D), consistent with previous surveillance in Western Kenya showing uniform resistance distribution26. When intervention areas (Homa Bay County Mainland and Mfangano Island) were combined, they showed 65% dhfr resistance compared to 72% in the control area (Migori). The general resistance patterns were consistent across all sites, with dhfr showing the highest prevalence, followed by mdr1, then dhps. Notably, an inverse relationship emerged between dhfr and dhps resistance levels across all locations, with Migori County showing the highest dhfr (72%) but lowest dhps (4%) resistance simultaneously (Fig. 3A). However, even though we have a strong negative correlation, the small sample size of 3 made it insignificant (r = − 0.866, P = 0.333).

dhfr temporal trends

The dhfr resistance marker I164L demonstrated temporal emergence specifically in Mfangano Island (0% at T2 to 13.2% at T3 to 18.1% at T4, P = 0.340), not reaching statistical significance, while remaining relatively stable at lower frequencies in Homa Bay County Mainland (2.9 to12.5%, P = 0.126) and Migori County (0–3.6%, P = 0.355). This site-specific emergence of I164L in Mfangano Island coincided with exclusive AS + PYD deployment.

dhps temporal trends

For dhps mutations, A437G and K540E remained at complete fixation (100%) throughout the study period, while S436H showed moderate, non-significant temporal fluctuations without clear directional trends (P > 0.05 all sites). Artemisinin resistance markers showed potentially concerning upward trajectories despite low absolute prevalences, with k13 A578S (unvalidated polymorphism) mixed infections increasing in Mfangano Island from 0% (T2) to 7.9% (T3) to 11.1% (T4), though not reaching statistical significance (P = 0.452).

mdr1 temporal trends

The mdr1 Y184F mutation exhibited significant temporal escalation documented in this study. In Homa Bay County Mainland, Y184F increased from 22.9% (T1) to 45.5% (T2) to 56.2% (T3) over 24 months (P = 0.005), representing a 33.3 percentage point increase. By comparison, Migori County showed a non-linear pattern from 7.9 to 25.0% to 18.2% (P = 0.221), without statistical significance. Interestingly, Mfangano Island displayed a decreasing Y184F prevalence pattern (66.7 to 63.2% to 54.2%, at T2, T3, and T4 respectively P = 0.345) although this finding was not statistically significant.

k13 temporal trends

Artemisinin resistance markers showed consistently low but potentially notable prevalences across all timepoints (Table 8). The unvalidated k13 A578S polymorphism, detected exclusively in mixed infections, showed a non-significant upward trajectory in Mfangano Island from 0% (T2) to 7.9% (T3) to 11.1% (T4) (P = 0.452), and remained at low levels in Homa Bay County Mainland (0–3.1%) and Migori (0–3.0%). The WHO-validated A675V marker was detected as a pure mutant in a single sample from Migori County at T3 (1/33, 3.0%), yielding a non-significant temporal trend (P = 0.680).

Combined trends

When aggregated across balanced timepoints (T1-T3), combined dhfr resistance (defined as presence of N51I, I164L, or triple mutant IRNI) increased from 47.9 to 85.0%, representing a 37.1 percentage point rise (P < 0.001). However, this combined metric masks the critical distinction between fixed markers (N51I, S108N at 100%) and emerging resistance (I164L). The margin spread between the aggregated values at all loci for dhfr and mdr1 was generally maintained throughout T1-T3, while the aggregated values at all dhps loci showed divergent patterns with the largest difference occurring at T2 (Fig. 4A). Age, sex and site-level population characteristics

Age and sex-stratified analysis revealed two distinct resistance patterns within the traditionally defined pediatric population. Highest resistance occurred in toddlers < 2 years (85.7%), while the 2–5-year age group showed markedly lower resistance (71%) similar to older age groups (Fig. 2B). Adults > 50 years showed 76.5% resistance, while the reproductive age group 15–50 years demonstrated lower resistance (60.5%) (Fig. 2B). The youngest group (< 2 years) also had the lowest sample size (n = 7) after random down-sampling which would invalidate chi square comparison. Males demonstrated higher dhfr resistance prevalence (70% vs 66%) despite comprising a smaller study population (35.8% vs 63.2%) (Fig. 2C). Migori (control county) had the youngest mean age (18 years old) and highest proportion of females (67%). Most of the participants in Migori County were females of reproductive age attending focused antenatal clinic (FANC) where SP prophylaxis is routinely administered (Fig. 2D). The margin spread between dhfr and dhps was approximately 55% for intervention locations regardless of adaptive cycling strategy (6–8-month vs 12 month crowding out) or ACT type used, while Migori showed a larger 68% margin spread with exclusive AL use.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive spatiotemporal molecular analysis of antimalarial drug resistance markers conducted during MFT deployment in sub-Saharan Africa. These findings are consistent with previous reports of antimalarial resistance saturation in Western Kenya and carry implications for malaria treatment and control strategies. The near-complete elimination of wild-type dhfr alleles (99.5% non-wild-type) and complete absence of wild-type dhps haplotypes indicate resistance saturation exceeding previously reported levels in sub-Saharan Africa17,32. The 37.1 percentage increase in combined dhfr resistance over the study period is notably high compared to rates reported from Southeast Asia during the height of multidrug resistance emergence10. This temporal trend, combined with uniform geographic distribution despite varied intervention contexts, suggests powerful regional selection pressures overwhelming local intervention effects and will be a useful point of monitoring during the implementation of MFT.

Mutation-specific resistance dynamics and surveillance priorities

Individual mutation analysis revealed critical differentiation between saturated and emerging resistance markers, with direct implications for surveillance priorities. The fixation of four core resistance markers is consistent with near-universal SP-based resistance in this population, a finding that appears to exceed previously reported saturation levels in SSA, with direct implications for both treatment policy and surveillance design, as these markers can no longer differentiate resistant from sensitive parasites.

The rapid rise of mdr1 Y184F from 22.9 to 56.2% in Homa Bay Mainland and the inconsistent pattern from 7.9 to 25.0%, to 18.2% in Migori over just 16–24 months represents one of the complex resistance realities for this marker, suggesting intense cross-resistance selection pressure possibly related to residual chloroquine use or ACT partner drug effects.

Age-stratified resistance insights: The discovery of distinct resistance patterns within the pediatric population represents a change in basic assumptions for policy considerations. The < 2 years age group showed exceptionally high resistance (85.7%) compared to the 2–5 years group (71%), challenging current policy frameworks that treat < 5 years as a homogeneous group. This observation may potentially be explained by the proximity of < 2-year-olds to their intrauterine and perinatal life, given the likelihood that their mothers received SP prophylaxis during focused antenatal care (FANC). Additionally, owing to high fertility rates in the study area, mothers may continue receiving SP during subsequent pregnancies while still nursing, potentially affecting resistance selection in lactating children. The fact that the sample size of this age group was very small (n = 7) lends credence to the use of SP prophylaxis as it may explain the small sample size if we assume that numbers would have likely been higher in the absence of SP prophylaxis. In essence, SP prophylaxis is working as intended to reduce acquisition of malaria. Moreover, pyrimethamine is excreted in breast milk33, creating conditions for SP resistance marker selection in children < 2 years. A study in Senegal reported a general trend of an increase in the prevalence of triple Pfdhfr haplotypes after Intermittent Preventive Treatment of infants and children (IPTi/c) over just 1 year34. This suggests that current IPTi/c and IPTp strategies may inadvertently contribute to resistance selection in vulnerable populations, necessitating more targeted research to clarify these observations and policy review. This is because the current recommended dose for SP IPTp is not based on pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of the drug35. Therefore, this dose may be inadequate to produce blood drug concentrations to prevent malaria parasite replication36. The 2–5-year age group, likely weaned from lactation, showed resistance prevalence resembling older groups (71% vs 76.5% in > 50 years), negating the effect of maternal SP exposure.

Complexity of infection and methodological considerations

The observed mean COI of 2.62 in our study substantially exceeds the MOI of 0.27–0.32 recently reported by Bungei et al.,32 in Western Kenya, reflecting fundamental differences in study design and epidemiological context. Bungei et al. included both malaria endemic (Kisumu, 20% prevalence) and malaria epidemic-prone highland (Kisii, 1% prevalence) regions, with their low overall MOI driven substantially by samples from the low-transmission epidemic region. In contrast, our study focused exclusively on high-transmission endemic areas, where elevated COI is expected due to frequent superinfection with multiple parasite clones.

The geographic gradient in COI (Mfangano Island > Homa Bay County Mainland > Migori County) suggests that transmission intensity, rather than intervention status alone, drives polyclonal infection rates. Paradoxically, intervention sites showed higher COI than the control site, potentially reflecting that MFT deployment was appropriately targeted to highest-transmission areas if the COI realities pre-dated MFT and was not, instead, a consequence of it. Mfangano Island’s elevated COI (the highest values of which were located at one same facility during T4) may also reflect its geographic isolation creating a relatively closed transmission network with intense local cycling of multiple parasite clones. The substantial proportion of polyclonal infections validates the necessity of deep sequencing technology for accurate haplotype determination in endemic settings, as conventional Sanger sequencing would misclassify mixed infections as single haplotypes.

Gender and demographic patterns: Males demonstrated higher dhfr and mdr1 resistance prevalence along with lower dhps resistance prevalence despite comprising a smaller study population though none of these findings were statistically significant (P = 0.475, P = 0.123 and P = 0.232 for dhfr, mdr1 and dhps, respectively). This may reflect a chance finding.

Emerging artemisinin resistance concerns: Perhaps most concerning is the detection of k13 A675V artemisinin resistance mutations, identified in a single sample from Migori county. While prevalence remains low, this WHO-validated biomarker’s detection is consistent with recent surveillance detecting this along with other low-frequency artemisinin resistance mutations (C469Y, P553L, A675V) in asymptomatic school-age children in Kenya37. The concurrent detection of A578S (unvalidated polymorphism) mixed infections (7.8% overall, 9.6% in Mfangano Island) alongside other k13 polymorphisms including N489K suggests active genetic changes at the k13 artemisinin resistance locus in this endemic setting. Critically, these k13 mutations are emerging against a backdrop of near-complete SP resistance saturation (dhfr N51I, S108N, dhps A437G, K540E all at 100%) creating concerning prospects for multidrug resistance development, a pattern with some parallels to the sequence of resistance evolution documented in South-east Asia, though the contexts differ substantially. The detection of these artemisinin resistance markers in an MFT deployment context despite theoretical benefits of multiple first-line therapies in delaying resistance underscores the potential need for enhanced surveillance where the insights gained by assessing the genetic markers provide a tool for surveillance during MFT roll out, and emphasize the need for molecular surveillance as well as whole genome sequencing for the early detection of genetic changes in the parasites as drugs are cycled, potential modification of MFT strategies, and development of novel therapeutic approaches beyond current ACT paradigms.

Mfangano Island’s unique parasite resistance trajectory

The unique pattern observed in Mfangano Island requires mutation-specific interpretation. The apparent “decline” in combined dhfr resistance was better elucidated with individual mutation analysis revealing the fact that this combined metric masked important dynamics:

Core dhfr markers (N51I, S108N) remained at complete fixation (100%) across all Mfangano Island timepoints (T2-T4), showing no change while Y184F and I164L revealed useful prevalence patterns. The combined metric “decline” reflects: (1) stable proportions of fixed mutations, and (2) emergence of I164L resistance biomarker though the lack of sample representation at T1 and the small sample size at T2 (n = 3) make related conclusions unreliable. This paradox—an apparent “decline” in combined resistance concurrent with rising resistance illustrates why individual mutation reporting is essential for accurate surveillance interpretation and warrants longer, more targeted studies for comprehensive causal elucidation.

More critically, Mfangano Island showed divergent mdr1 Y184F patterns compared to other sites. While Homa Bay County Mainland experienced Y184F escalation, Mfangano Island displayed a decreasing prevalence (67% at T2, 63.2% at T3, 54.2% at T4, P = 0.345). These dynamics (though not statistically significant) occurred alongside a numerically notable, though similarly non-significant (P = 0.340), I164L increase suggesting AS + PYD deployment may have been associated with different selection pressures compared to AL/DHA + PIP/AS + AQ cycling used elsewhere. The inherent meaning of these patterns and findings warrant elucidation through longer studies and genome-wide studies that allow more selective clarity to decrease ambiguity.

The Island’s geographic isolation, combined with highest mean COI (3.06 vs 2.61 and 2.10 elsewhere, P = 0.042), creates a closed transmission network where ACT-specific selection effects may be more readily detectable. The exclusive 36-month AS + PYD deployment, the longest continuous use of a single ACT in this study, provides unique insights: while unable to prevent I164L emergence (likely reflecting ongoing SP exposure through IPTp in the region), AS + PYD may have been associated with altered mdr1 selection pressure compared to other ACT regimens. This observation, if validated in longer studies, could inform ACT selection strategies, particularly for vulnerable populations with high baseline dhfr resistance.

Intervention effect analysis

The absence of clear associative interventional effects across individual mutations may reflect: (1) short pilot duration (24 months for balanced analysis, 48 months for extended follow-up on Mfangano Island) insufficient for population-level resistance shifts; (2) brief ACT rotation periods (8 months Homa Bay County Mainland, 12 months Mfangano Island initial phase) during adaptive cycling; (3) regional resistance networks operating across geographically distinct areas, potentially through human migration or mosquito dispersal; or (4) pre-existing high baseline resistance limiting detectable changes. Notably, the margin spread between dhfr and dhps resistance markers showed narrower ranges in intervention sites (~ 55%) compared to control (68%), potentially suggesting subtle intervention effects that might become statistically apparent with longer observation or alternative MFT strategies.

Predictive modeling implications and clinical significance

Model performance: Machine learning model comparison revealed Gradient Boosting as the best-performing model with 0.957 prediction accuracy. The dominance of genotype in predictive models potentially positions biomarkers with granulated genotypes as great covariate features in modeling for resistance monitoring and validation efforts. The importance of age also validates the clinical significance of age-stratified resistance patterns revealed in this study.

These findings may indicate the potential utility of age-stratified treatment protocols and genotype-aware surveillance strategies, particularly for the vulnerable < 2 years population showing exceptional resistance burden. One way of realizing this important stratification would be to include them in the adaptive cycling MFT strategy which would modify it to a more hybrid method of applying MFT. The predictive model results suggest a potential clinical significance of the distinct pediatric resistance patterns identified.

Study limitations

This analysis acknowledges limitations including observational design limiting causal inference, the unbalanced sampling design (T4 only for Mfangano Island) required analytical adjustments potentially affecting temporal trend interpretation, and potential selection bias from healthcare facility-based sampling. The absence of Pfcrt and plasmepsin II-III copy number variant analysis represents a recognized gap, as these are validated markers of piperaquine resistance relevant to DHA + PIP use in Homa Bay County Mainland, and Pfcrt K76T is relevant to the AS + AQ deployment period via its role in amodiaquine cross-resistance. Additionally, the short pilot study duration (24 months) and brief ACT rotation periods may have limited intervention effect detection. Furthermore, the lack of detailed individual drug history data including pre-dose antimalarial intake prevented direct assessment of exposure effects and may have introduced confounding resistance pattern interpretation, though population-level patterns provided valuable insights. The potential correlational effects of weather patterns were also not considered for each study site.

Public health implications

The findings suggest that policy responses across multiple domains may be useful. First, the observed near-complete fixation of core dhfr and dhps resistance markers suggests substantially compromised SP treatment efficacy in this population, warranting evaluation of alternative treatment protocols. Second, enhanced artemisinin resistance surveillance is critical following k13 mutation detection. Third, obstetric SP prophylaxis may benefit from revision given extreme resistance levels in vulnerable populations, particularly considering the distinct nested < 2 years versus 2–5 years resistance profiles as related to the potential knock-on effects of IPTP.

The potential benefits of AS + PYD observed in Mfangano Island warrant investigation for broader implementation, particularly for post-discharge malaria treatment in high-resistance areas. Regional coordination is essential given uniform resistance distribution across geographically distinct areas, suggesting resistance networks transcending local boundaries. Investment in novel therapeutic approaches, including predictive, feature aware mathematical modeling, next-generation antimalarials and combination strategies, should be prioritized alongside traditional vector control measures.

Conclusions

This comprehensive spatiotemporal analysis is consistent with antimalarial resistance saturation in Western Kenya, with near-complete wild-type allele elimination and emerging artemisinin resistance markers. The rapid resistance escalation (37.1 percentage points over three years) combined with uniform geographic distribution indicates regional resistance networks requiring coordinated intervention strategies beyond current approaches.

The finding of distinct resistance patterns in children < 2 years versus 2–5 years raises questions about current policy frameworks and suggests that maternal SP prophylaxis strategies may require reassessment. The unique dhfr resistance prevalence pattern observed with AS-PYD use in Mfangano Island provides hope for alternative treatment strategies, particularly in vulnerable pediatric populations.

The detection of WHO-validated artemisinin resistance mutations is consistent with an emerging transition point that warrants enhanced surveillance and potential prioritization of alternative treatment development. These findings provide crucial evidence for malaria control programs and policy makers to develop evidence-based responses to the evolving resistance landscape in sub-Saharan Africa, with particular emphasis on age-stratified treatment protocols inclusion in MFT approaches and regional coordination strategies.
