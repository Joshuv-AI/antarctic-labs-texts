Global thermodynamics for isothermal fluids under gravity Journal: Journal of Statistical Physics
Naoko Nakagawa, Shin-ichi Sasa, Takamichi Hirao, Tsuyoshi Shiina, Kyosuke Tachi, Akira Yoshida

Abstract
We develop a formulation of global thermodynamics for equilibrium systems under the influence of gravity. The free energy for simple fluids is extended to include a dependence on ( T , V , N , m ​ g ​ L ) (T,V,N,mgL) , where L L represents the vertical system length in the direction of gravity. A central idea in this formulation is to uniquely fix the reference point of the gravitational potential, ensuring a consistent thermodynamic framework. Using this framework, we derive the probability density of thermodynamic quantities, which allows us to define a variational function for determining equilibrium liquid-gas coexistence under gravity. The resulting free energy landscape, derived from the variational function, reveals the local stability of liquid-gas configurations. Specifically, the liquid phase resides at the lower portion of the system due to gravity, while the inverted configuration (with liquid on top) is also locally stable in this landscape. Furthermore, we characterize the transition between these liquid-gas configurations as a first-order phase transition using the thermodynamic free energy of ( T , V , N , m ​ g ​ L ) (T,V,N,mgL) . Finally, we validate the predictions of global thermodynamics through molecular dynamics simulations, demonstrating the applicability and accuracy of the proposed framework.

Introduction

Equilibrium thermodynamics characterizes fluids using a limited set of extensive and intensive variables, such as
(T,V,N)
, based on the assumption of spatial uniformity
Callen
;
Landau-Lifshitz-Statphys
.
Local descriptions are generally favored to account for the non-uniformity and time evolutions of fluids
with local thermodynamic quantities such as local particle density
\rho(\bm{r})
, temperature
T(\bm{r})
, and pressure
p(\bm{r})

Landau-Lifshitz-Fluid
;
Prigogine-Kondepudi
.
We refer to the former description as “global thermodynamics,” contrasting the latter with “local thermodynamics,” typically represented by hydrodynamics.

Recent research has proposed that global thermodynamics could extend equilibrium thermodynamics to non-equilibrium systems
Global-PRL
;
Global-JSP
;
Global-PRR
;
KNS
;
Global-modelB
. For instance, heat conduction systems can be described using global thermodynamic variables, regardless of their non-uniformity. Although this global description neglects spatial inhomogeneity, it accurately predicts phenomena such as the deviation of the liquid-gas interface temperature from the equilibrium transition point and the stabilization of metastable local states. Thus, global thermodynamics offers a profound yet effective framework for describing thermodynamic phenomena in heat conduction systems.
However, the predictions of global thermodynamics should be validated based on microscopic dynamics. Nonequilibrium statistical mechanics, though, remains too formal to enable quantitative calculations of macroscopic quantities
Zubarev
;
Mclennan
;
Maes-rep
.

Equilibrium systems under gravity show a notable resemblance to heat conduction systems: both exhibit one-dimensional structures, such as gradients in density, pressure, or temperature. Without detailed measurements of local temperature or pressure, the density distributions of equilibrium systems under gravity and those of heat conduction systems may appear indistinguishable. This resemblance suggests that equilibrium systems under gravity could potentially be described using global thermodynamics in a manner similar to heat conduction systems.

Historically, fluids under gravity have been studied primarily within a hydrodynamic framework, where the global perspective is often disregarded due to its lack of spatial detail.
It is worth noting, however, that many fundamental experiments in equilibrium thermodynamics are conducted under the influence of gravity.
This might have led to the assumption that “weak” gravity has no effect on global thermodynamics.
While this may be true in simple gases or liquids, the situation for liquid-gas coexistence is significantly complex.
This raises an important question: Can systems under gravity be “comprehensively” described from a global thermodynamic viewpoint?
In this context, the extension of thermodynamics includes the reformulation of the variational principles, such as entropy maximization and free energy minimization.
It is well known that gravity plays a crucial role in determining the configuration of liquid and gas,
while the variational principles without gravity determine the distribution of liquid and gas.
This suggests a potential discrepancy between the phenomena observed in the presence of gravity and the results predicted by conventional variational principles.
By revising these variational principles, we can examine whether the gravitational force modifies the balance condition between liquid and gas.
This approach also enables a comparison of the effect of gravity and heat flow on equilibrium conditions of liquid-gas systems.

In this paper, we aim to incorporate weak gravitational effects into the equilibrium thermodynamics of liquid-gas coexistence.
A useful example of such an extension is found in magnetic fluids, where the thermodynamic state is expressed by
(T,V,N,H)
, with the fundamental relation given by
dF=-SdT-pdV+\mu dN-MdH
.
Here,
H
denotes the applied magnetic field, and
M
represents the magnetization
Kubo
.
Similarly, extending thermodynamics to include gravitational effects requires identifying the appropriate conjugate pairs of extensive and intensive variables.
Unlike heat conduction systems, equilibrium statistical mechanics can be directly applied to systems under gravity in a straightforward manner.
This provides a robust framework for addressing non-uniform states, whether in liquid, gas, or liquid-gas coexistence, and facilitates the identification of new conjugate pairs of extensive and intensive gravity-related variables.

It is important to note that variational functions in equilibrium thermodynamics correspond to the logarithm of the probability densities of macroscopic fluctuations, which can be derived from canonical distributions
Einstein
;
Landau-Lifshitz-Statphys
.
The minimum (or maximum) value of a variational function determines the thermodynamic free energy (or thermodynamic entropy) for the entire system, for which the fundamental relation of thermodynamics remains valid.
Consequently, any newly identified conjugate pair would be incorporated into this framework.
The consistency among thermodynamic functions, variational functions, and the probability densities of macroscopic fluctuations serves as a guiding principle for extending the thermodynamic framework to non-uniform systems under gravity.
Moreover, the probability densities of macroscopic fluctuations underpin the Onsager relations and non-equilibrium thermodynamics
Onsager1931
;
Groot-Mazur
.
An extension of thermodynamics that preserves consistency among these three elements could pave the way for the development of non-equilibrium thermodynamics under gravity.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents an overview of the main results derived from equilibrium statistical mechanics. The setup for equilibrium statistical mechanics is introduced in Section 3. Section 4 defines the free energy that characterizes the thermodynamic state of a system under gravity. In Section 5, a formulation of global thermodynamics under gravity is proposed based on this free energy. Sections 6 and 7 focus on constructing a variational principle for determining the spatial configuration of liquid and gas. The variational equations are then solved in Section 8. Section 9 is for the stability of the solutions. The free energy is compared among respective solutions in Section 10.
The free energy landscape and thermodynamic free energy are presented in Section 11 with a singularity at
g=0
exhibiting the feature of the first-order transition. Numerical verification of these theoretical results is provided in Section 12, followed by concluding remarks in Section 13.

Main results

We focus on a single component fluid of
N

At constant temperature

,

\displaystyle dF=-SdT-pdV+\mu dN.

(2.1)

From the extensive property, we define the free energy density as

\displaystyle f(T,\rho)=F(T,V,N)/V,

(2.2)

where
\rho=N/V
. Similarly, we have the entropy density
s(T,\rho)=S(T,V,N)/V
, etc.
These densities form the basis of the local thermodynamics adopted in the hydrodynamic description.

Impose weak gravity in the opposite direction to the
x
-axis.
With the mass
m
of each particle and gravitational acceleration

, the gravitational potential is
mg(x-x_{\mathrm{o}})
with an arbitrary reference point at
x_{\mathrm{o}}
.
Suppose the cuboid container has a vertical length
L
in the
x
-direction and a horizontal area
A
.
The positions of the bottom and the top are
x_{\mathrm{b}}
and
x_{\mathrm{t}}
, respectively,
where
L=x_{\mathrm{t}}-x_{\mathrm{b}}
.
All walls are made of the same materials and interact with particles in a short range.
In the thermodynamic limit, the equilibrium state becomes horizontally uniform under gravity, and all local quantities are functions of
x
, such as
f(T,\rho(x))
, where
T(x)=T
everywhere.

Set a dimensionless parameter for representing the effect of the gravity acceleration as

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\frac{mgL}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}.

(2.3)

This paper concentrates on weak gravity cases and neglects the contribution of
O(\varepsilon^{2})
.
Our first result is the extension of thermodynamics obtained from equilibrium statistical mechanics.
It is revealed that an extra pair of extensive and intensive variables is required, such that

\displaystyle(T,V,N)\rightarrow(T,V,N,mgL)

(2.4)

with a fundamental relation of global thermodynamics as

\displaystyle dF_{g}=-SdT-\bar{p}dV+\bar{\mu}dN-N\frac{x_{\mathrm{m}}-X}{L}d(mgL).

(2.5)

Here,
F_{g}
is the free energy under gravity, which is given by

\displaystyle F_{g}

\displaystyle=A\int_{x_{\mathrm{b}}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}+L}dx~[f(T,\rho(x))+mg(x-x_{\mathrm{m}})\rho(x)]

(2.6)

with the local free energy density
f(T,\rho(x))
.

x_{\mathrm{m}}
is the midpoint of the system,

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{m}}=\frac{x_{\mathrm{b}}+x_{\mathrm{t}}}{2},

(2.7)

and
X
is the center of mass,

\displaystyle X=\frac{A}{N}\int_{x_{\mathrm{b}}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}+L}dx~x\rho(x).

(2.8)

Due to gravity, the pressure and chemical potential become dependent on
x
,
while they are homogeneous without gravity.
Then
p
and
\mu
in (
2.1
) are replaced by the spatial averages

\displaystyle\bar{p}\equiv\frac{1}{L}\int_{x_{\mathrm{b}}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}+L}dx~p(x),\qquad\bar{\mu}\equiv\frac{1}{L}\int_{x_{\mathrm{b}}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}+L}dx~\mu(x)

(2.9)

in (
2.5
).
We remark that fluids in a single-phase state, such as pure gas or pure liquid, follow thermodynamics without gravity, characterized by
(T,V,N)
, because
X=x_{\mathrm{m}}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
.
At the phase coexistence,
X\neq x_{\mathrm{m}}
as the liquid is placed below the gas.
The new term appearing in (
2.5
) becomes essential.

The second result is a free energy landscape for determining the equilibrium configuration of the liquid-gas coexistence under gravity.
In the light of global thermodynamics with
(T,V,N,mgL)
in (
2.5
),
we restate the common phenomenon that liquid is placed below gas as the emergence of the heterogeneous structure via a first-order transition.
From this point of view, the system without gravity is just at the transition point.
Let
\mathrm{\ell}
and
\mathrm{u}
be the respective phases occupying the lower and upper regions in the container,
and
{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}
and
{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}
be the number of particles and the volume of the phase
\mathrm{\ell}
.
We derive the variational function for determining the equilibrium state in liquid-gas coexistence,

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{g}({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}};T,V,N,mgL)=F(T,{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}})+F(T,V-{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},N-{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}})+\frac{NmgL}{2}\left(\frac{{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{V}-\frac{{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{N}\right).

(2.10)

The third term in (
2.10
) coincides with the spatial integration of the gravitational term in (
2.6
).
The landscape of
{\cal F}_{g}
in the space of
({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}})
possesses two local minima:
one is the configuration
(\mathrm{\ell},\mathrm{u})=(\mathrm{L},\mathrm{G})
where the liquid is below the gas.
The other is
(\mathrm{\ell},\mathrm{u})=(\mathrm{G},\mathrm{L})
where the liquid is above the gas.
Comparing the values of
{\cal F}_{g}
for the two configurations, we find that
(\mathrm{\ell},\mathrm{u})=(\mathrm{L},\mathrm{G})
is the equilibrium state corresponding to the global minimum for
g>0
due to the extra third term in (
2.10
).
The reverse configuration
(\mathrm{\ell},\mathrm{u})=(\mathrm{G},\mathrm{L})
becomes the metastable state belonging to the local minimum.
When changing the value of
mgL
from positive to negative, we observe the interchange of the two minima
at
mgL=0
, providing a sudden change in the configuration as the upside-down of liquid and gas.
This phenomenon, while well known, is formulated in global thermodynamics as a first-order transition.

Global thermodynamics provides a consistent result with hydrodynamics, showing that all local states, including the vicinity of the liquid-gas interface, are thermodynamically stable.
The variational equations, which are satisfied by both the global and local minima, can be expressed as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial{\cal F}_{g}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}\right)_{T,V,N,mgL}=0,\qquad\left(\frac{\partial{\cal F}_{g}}{\partial{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}\right)_{T,V,N,mgL}=0,

(2.11)

which result in the continuity of the pressure profile with

\displaystyle p_{\theta}=p_{\mathrm{s}}(T).

(2.12)

Here,
p_{\theta}
is the pressure at the liquid-gas interface, and
p_{\mathrm{s}}(T)
is the saturation pressure at temperature
T
.
This demonstrates that the coexistence conditions in conventional equilibrium thermodynamics are satisfied locally at the interface.

3
Equilibrium statistical mechanics

3.1
Setup

Let
\Gamma=({\bm{r}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{r}}_{N},{\bm{p}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{p}}_{N})
denote the microstate of an
N
-particle system.
The Hamiltonian without gravity is given by

\displaystyle H(\Gamma;x_{\mathrm{b}},x_{\mathrm{t}},A,N)=H_{0}(\Gamma;N)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{\rm w}({\bm{r}}_{i};x_{\mathrm{t}},x_{\mathrm{b}},A),

(3.1)

where
H_{0}(\Gamma;N)
includes the kinetic energy and potentials describing interactions among particles.
The term
\phi_{\rm w}({\bm{r}}_{i};x_{\mathrm{t}},x_{\mathrm{b}},A)
represents the interaction potential between a particle and the walls.
We assume that these interactions are short-ranged.
Given the symmetry in the
y
- and
z
-directions, the parameters specifying the positions of the four vertical walls are simplified and characterized by the horizontal area
A
.
For simplicity, we further assume symmetry between the bottom and top walls, such that
\phi_{\rm w}({\bm{r}}_{i};x_{\mathrm{t}},x_{\mathrm{b}},A)=\phi_{\rm w}({\bm{r}}_{i};x_{\mathrm{b}},x_{\mathrm{t}},A)

Any breakdown of this spatial symmetry is attributed solely to the presence of gravity.

The Hamiltonian under gravity is expressed as

\displaystyle H_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}(\Gamma;x_{\mathrm{b}},x_{\mathrm{t}},A,N,mg)=H(\Gamma;x_{\mathrm{b}},x_{\mathrm{t}},A,N)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}mg(x_{i}-x_{\mathrm{o}}),

(3.2)

where
x_{\mathrm{o}}
is an arbitrary reference point for the gravitational potential, such as the position of the ground.
We consider an isothermal system at temperature
T
under gravity.
The equilibrium statistical mechanics framework provides the canonical distribution, partition function, and the corresponding free energy as

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}(\Gamma)=\frac{1}{Z_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}}e^{-\beta H_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}(\Gamma)},

(3.3)

\displaystyle Z_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}=\int d\Gamma~e^{-\beta H_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}(\Gamma)},

(3.4)

\displaystyle F_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}=-k_{\mathrm{B}}T~(~\ln Z_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}-\ln N!~),

(3.5)

where
\beta=1/k_{\mathrm{B}}T
is the inverse temperature, and

denotes the Boltzmann constant.

3.2
Total differential of
F_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}

The quantity
F_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}
, defined from statistical mechanics, may be regarded as free energy. However, it depends on several macroscopic parameters

To explore the physical measning of

\displaystyle X=\Bigl\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\Bigr\rangle.

(3.9)

The pressure in the system depends on the height

Operationally, we consider the pressure

While the pressure varies with height

Using (

\displaystyle p_{\rm b}A=\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial H_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{b}}}\Bigr\rangle=\left(\frac{\partial F_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)_{T,x_{\mathrm{t}},A,N,mg},

(3.10)

\displaystyle p_{\rm t}A=-\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial H_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{t}}}\Bigr\rangle=-\left(\frac{\partial F_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}}{\partial x_{\mathrm{t}}}\right)_{T,x_{\mathrm{b}},A,N,mg},

It should be noted that the system is not extensive in the direction of gravity but remains extensive and homogeneous in directions perpendicular to gravity.
This property is expressed as

\displaystyle F_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}(T,x_{\mathrm{b}},x_{\mathrm{t}},\lambda A,\lambda N,mg)=\lambda F_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}(T,x_{\mathrm{b}},x_{\mathrm{t}},A,N,mg),

\displaystyle\mu_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}\equiv\left(\frac{\partial F_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}}{\partial N}\right)_{T,x_{\mathrm{b}},x_{\mathrm{t}},A,mg}.

(3.15)

Summarizing the above relations, the total differential of

\displaystyle dF_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}=

\displaystyle-SdT+p_{\rm b}Adx_{\mathrm{b}}-p_{\rm t}Adx_{\mathrm{t}}-\bar{p}LdA+\mu_{g}^{\mathrm{o}}dN+N(X-x_{\mathrm{o}})d(mg).

While

4.18
), we obtain

\displaystyle dF_{g}=-SdT+p_{\rm b}Adx_{\mathrm{b}}-p_{\rm t}Adx_{\mathrm{t}}-\bar{p}LdA+\mu_{g}dN+N(X-x_{\mathrm{m}})d(mg)-Nmgdx_{\mathrm{m}}.

(4.19)

Using
L=x_{\mathrm{t}}-x_{\mathrm{b}}
and (
4.6
), we have
x_{\mathrm{b}}=x_{\mathrm{m}}-L/2
and
x_{\mathrm{t}}=x_{\mathrm{m}}+L/2
.
Thus,

\displaystyle p_{\rm b}Adx_{\mathrm{b}}-p_{\rm t}Adx_{\mathrm{t}}=-p_{\mathrm{m}}AdL+Nmgdx_{\mathrm{m}},

(4.20)

where

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{m}}=\frac{p_{\rm b}+p_{\rm t}}{2}.

(4.21)

The total differential (
4.19
) is now expressed as

\displaystyle dF_{g}=-SdT-p_{\mathrm{m}}AdL-\bar{p}LdA+\mu_{g}dN-N(x_{\mathrm{m}}-X)d(mg).

(4.22)

Remarkably, (
4.22
) is free from the arbitrary reference point
x_{\mathrm{o}}
and is also unaffected by the container’s shift, represented by changes in
x_{\mathrm{m}}
.
Only the difference
X-x_{\mathrm{m}}
is relevant from a thermodynamic perspective.

The midpoint
x_{\mathrm{m}}
shifts when
x_{\mathrm{t}}
or
x_{\mathrm{b}}
changes.
Equation (
4.12
) implies that the updated value of
x_{\mathrm{m}}
must be adopted for the free energy to accurately characterize the thermodynamic state.
General operations altering
T
,
L
,
A
, or
mg
do not change the position of
x_{\mathrm{m}}
, but they may shift
X
.
Such shifts result in
\Delta F_{g}\neq 0
, reflecting a change in the thermodynamic state.

5
Global thermodynamics under weak gravity

We further transform (
4.22
) into

\displaystyle dF_{g}=-SdT-\bar{p}dV+\mu_{g}dN-\Psi_{g}d(mgL)+(\bar{p}-p_{\mathrm{m}}+mg\bar{\rho}(x_{\mathrm{m}}-X))AdL,

(5.1)

where
V=AL
,
\bar{\rho}=N/V
, and

\displaystyle\Psi_{g}=N\frac{x_{\mathrm{m}}-X}{L}.

(5.2)

Because no particle currents flow in equilibrium, forces balance at any local point
x
, and, therefore, the pressure profile
p(x)
becomes continuous throughout the system.
With this constraint, we have

\displaystyle\bar{p}-p_{\mathrm{m}}=mg\bar{\rho}(X-x_{\mathrm{m}})+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

(5.3)

as shown in Sec.
5.2
.
Then, the relation (
5.1
) forms a fundamental thermodynamic relation as

\displaystyle dF_{g}=-SdT-\bar{p}dV+\mu_{g}dN-\Psi_{g}d(mgL),

(5.4)

with an error of
O(\varepsilon^{2})
.
The fourth term is newly added and is essential for describing the nonuniformity of the local states brought about by gravity.
We thus extend the equilibrium thermodynamic variables as

\displaystyle(T,V,N)\rightarrow(T,V,N,mgL)

(5.5)

for equilibrium systems under gravity.

In a pure liquid or pure gas,
X-x_{\mathrm{m}}=O(\varepsilon)
.
Accordingly, in the single-phase state, we have
\Psi_{g}=O(\varepsilon)
, and therefore, the fourth term of (
5.4
) is negligible.
The fundamental relation (
5.4
) then becomes equivalent to the fundamental relation of thermodynamics without gravity.
In liquid-gas coexistence,
X-x_{\mathrm{m}}
becomes
O(\varepsilon^{0})
, where
X<x_{\mathrm{m}}
for
g>0
and
X>x_{\mathrm{m}}
for
g<0
.
The fourth term in (
5.4
) remains
O(\varepsilon)
because
\Psi_{g}=O(\varepsilon^{0})
.
Thus, it is essential in the liquid-gas coexistence to extend thermodynamics as in (
5.5
).

5.1
Scaling relation

The scaling properties connecting thermodynamic functions of different system sizes are anisotropic due to gravity.
Consider two systems with
(L,A,N)
and
(\lambda_{1}L,\lambda_{2}A,\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}N)
, with arbitrary positive constants
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
.
The fundamental relation (
5.4
) indicates

\displaystyle F_{g}(T,\lambda_{1}L,\lambda_{2}A,\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}N,mg/\lambda_{1})=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}F_{g}(T,L,A,N,mg),

(5.6)

where the variables are denoted as
(T,L,A,N,mg)
instead of
(T,V,N,mgL)
to clarify the point.
The standard scaling property
(N,V,F_{g})\rightarrow(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}N,\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}V,\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}F_{g})
holds only when the magnitude of gravity is changed as
g\rightarrow g/\lambda_{1}
in accordance with the change
L\rightarrow\lambda_{1}L
.
See Fig.
3
.

To aid understanding, let us compare two operations to change the shape of the container with
V
fixed, as shown in Fig.
4
.
We set
(T,V,N,mgL)
such that liquid coexists with gas under gravity.
We change the aspect ratio of the container by a factor
\lambda
such that the volume
V
of the container remains unchanged, i.e.,
V=LA=(\lambda L)(A/\lambda)
.
In this change, the scaling relation (
5.6
) is written as

F_{g}(T,\lambda L,A/\lambda,N,mg/\lambda)=F_{g}(T,L,A,N,mg)
.

The value of
F_{g}
may change during the operation shown in Fig.
4
(a) but remains unchanged in the operation shown in Fig.
4
(b).
Suppose
\lambda<1
.
When the vertical length becomes shorter with
g
fixed, as shown in Fig.
4
(a), the pressure difference
p_{\rm b}-p_{\rm t}
decreases.
This change causes the system’s state to deviate from the initial state; for instance, the liquid may evaporate, or the gas may condense.
Correspondingly, (
5.4
) leads to
\Delta F_{g}\neq 0
in the operation in Fig.
4
(a).

The fundamental relation (
5.4
) shows that
\Delta F_{g}
can vanish only if the operation is accompanied by a change in gravitational acceleration.
In the operation shown in Fig.
4
(b), with
g\rightarrow g/\lambda
, the fourth term in (
5.4
) does not contribute to
\Delta F_{g}
.
In this case,
p_{\rm b}
,
p_{\rm t}
, and the liquid-gas distribution remain unchanged from the initial state.

).

5.2
Derivation of (
5.3
)

When the system is in the single-phase state, (
5.3
) holds trivially because we have
X=x_{\mathrm{m}}+O(\varepsilon)
and
\bar{p}=p_{\mathrm{m}}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
.
These results can be derived by applying the trapezoidal rule to the nearly linear profiles of
\rho(x)
and
p(x)
.

We now focus on the liquid-gas coexistence.
Assuming that the liquid-gas interface is single and flat, we label the phases below and above the interface as
\mathrm{\ell}
and
\mathrm{u}
, respectively, and use superscripts
\mathrm{\ell}
and
\mathrm{u}
for the quantities in each phase, such as
N^{\mathrm{\ell}}
,
N^{\mathrm{u}}
,
X^{\mathrm{\ell}}
,
X^{\mathrm{u}}
, etc.
Under weak gravity, the density and pressure profiles in
\mathrm{\ell}
and
\mathrm{u}
are approximately linear with respect to
x
.
Thus,

\displaystyle X^{\mathrm{\ell/u}}=x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{\ell/u}}+O(\varepsilon),\qquad\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell/u}}=p_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{\ell/u}}+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

(5.7)

Because the force balance holds at every point
x
, the pressure
p(x)
is continuous across the liquid-gas interface.
Let
p_{\theta}
denote the pressure at the interface.
The definitions of
\bar{p}
and
p_{\mathrm{m}}
are as follows:

\displaystyle\bar{p}=\frac{1}{L}\int_{x_{\mathrm{b}}}^{x_{\mathrm{t}}}dx\,p(x)=\frac{V^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{V}\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}+\frac{V^{\mathrm{u}}}{V}\bar{p}^{\mathrm{u}},

(5.8)

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{m}}=\frac{p_{\rm b}+p_{\rm t}}{2},

(5.9)

where
p_{\rm b}=p(x_{\mathrm{b}})
and
p_{\rm t}=p(x_{\mathrm{t}})
.
Subtracting the above expressions and using (
5.7
), we find

\displaystyle\bar{p}-p_{\mathrm{m}}=\frac{V^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{2V}(p_{\theta}-p_{\rm t})+\frac{V^{\mathrm{u}}}{2V}(p_{\theta}-p_{\rm b}),

(5.10)

where we used
p_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{\ell}}=(p_{\rm b}+p_{\theta})/2
and
p_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{u}}=(p_{\rm t}+p_{\theta})/2
.
The force balance, as expressed in (
4.2
), holds in each region:

\displaystyle p_{\rm b}-p_{\theta}=\frac{N^{\mathrm{\ell}}mg}{A},\qquad p_{\theta}-p_{\rm t}=\frac{N^{\mathrm{u}}mg}{A}.

(5.11)

Substituting these relations into the expression for
\bar{p}-p_{\mathrm{m}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{p}-p_{\mathrm{m}}=-\frac{mgL}{2}\frac{N}{V}\left(\frac{N^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{N}-\frac{V^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{V}\right),

(5.12)

where we used
V^{\mathrm{\ell}}+V^{\mathrm{u}}=V
,
N^{\mathrm{\ell}}+N^{\mathrm{u}}=N
, and
V=AL
.

Next, consider the center of mass
X
and the midpoint
x_{\mathrm{m}}
,

\displaystyle X=\frac{N^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{N}x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{\ell}}+\frac{N^{\mathrm{u}}}{N}x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{u}},

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{m}}=\frac{V^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{V}x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{\ell}}+\frac{V^{\mathrm{u}}}{V}x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{u}}.

(5.13)

Subtracting these equations gives

\displaystyle\frac{x_{\mathrm{m}}-X}{L}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{N^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{N}-\frac{V^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{V}\right),

(5.14)

where we used
x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{u}}-x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{\ell}}=L/2
.

Combining (
5.14
) and (
5.12
), we recover (
5.3
).

6
Effective Hamiltonian for the distribution of particles

By coarse-graining the canonical distribution (
4.17
), we derive the probability density for the particle number in a specified region.
The most probable value corresponds to the equilibrium state, determining the system’s equilibrium configuration.

We virtually divide the fluid in the container with a section at an arbitrary position
x=x_{\mathrm{b}}+l
, defining the regions below (
x<x_{\mathrm{b}}+l
) and above (
x>x_{\mathrm{b}}+l
) this section as “
\mathrm{blw}
” and “
\mathrm{abv}
,” respectively. These regions may contain liquid, gas, or a coexistence of both (see Fig.
5
).
Because the wall at
x=x_{\mathrm{b}}+l
is virtual, the number of particles in the region
x<x_{\mathrm{b}}+l
fluctuates.
The probability density for observing the particle number
{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}
is formally given by

\displaystyle\rho({\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}};l)=\int d\Gamma~\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}(\Gamma)\delta({\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}-\hat{N}^{\mathrm{blw}}(\Gamma;l)),

(6.1)

where
\rho^{\mathrm{eq}}(\Gamma)
represents the canonical distribution in (
4.17
), and
\hat{N}^{\mathrm{blw}}(\Gamma;l)
is the particle number in the region
x<x_{\mathrm{b}}+l
for a given microstate
\Gamma
, expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{N}^{\mathrm{blw}}(\Gamma;l)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{x_{\mathrm{b}}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}+l}dx~\delta(x_{i}-x).

(6.2)

We define an effective Hamiltonian
{\cal F}_{g}({\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}};l)
to represent the probability density
\rho({\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}};l)
as

\displaystyle\rho({\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}};l)=e^{\beta(F_{g}-{\cal F}_{g}({\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}};l))}

(6.3)

with

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{g}({\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}};l)=F_{g}^{\mathrm{blw}}(T,l,A,{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}},mg)+F_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}(T,L-l,A,N-{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}},mg)+\frac{mg}{2}(lN-L{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}),

(6.4)

as derived in Appendix A.
Here,
F_{g}
,
F_{g}^{\mathrm{blw}}
, and
F_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}
denote the free energies for the total system and respective regions, while contributions of
o(N)
terms are neglected.
The functional dependencies of
F_{g}^{\mathrm{blw}}
and
F_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}
are consistent with the total differential in (
4.22
).

Three variants of setting up a virtual wall.
The thermodynamic states of the lower and upper regions depend on the position of the virtual wall.

Let
{N}^{\mathrm{blw}}
represent the most probable value of (
6.3
), i.e.,

\displaystyle N^{\mathrm{blw}}(l)=\argmin_{{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}}{\cal F}_{g}({\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}};l),

(6.5)

which implies

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial{\cal F}_{g}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}}\right)_{*}=0,

(6.6)

where
(\cdot)_{*}
denotes a truncated expression under the condition
(\cdot)_{T,V,N,mgL}
.
The equilibrium state is specified by
{N}^{\mathrm{blw}}(l)
, and the thermodynamic free energy corresponds to the minimum value

\displaystyle F_{g}={\cal F}_{g}(N^{\mathrm{blw}}(l);l).

(6.7)

As illustrated in Fig.
5
, the equilibrium states are independent of
l
.
Consequently, the free energy
F_{g}
in (
6.7
) satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{dF_{g}}{dl}=0~~\Leftrightarrow~~\left(\frac{\partial{\cal F}_{g}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}}\right)_{*}\frac{dN^{\mathrm{blw}}}{dl}+\left(\frac{\partial{\cal F}_{g}}{\partial l}\right)_{*}=0.

(6.8)

Substituting (
6.6
) into (
6.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial{\cal F}_{g}}{\partial l}\right)_{*}=0.

(6.9)

The relations (
6.6
) and (
6.9
), combined with (
6.4
), yield

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial F_{g}^{\mathrm{blw}}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}}\right)_{*}+\left(\frac{\partial F_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}}\right)_{*}-\frac{mgl}{2}=0,

(6.10)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial F_{g}^{\mathrm{blw}}}{\partial l}\right)_{*}+\left(\frac{\partial F_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}}{\partial l}\right)_{*}+\frac{mgN}{2}=0.

(6.11)

The total differential relations (
4.22
) for the respective regions are expressed as

\displaystyle dF_{g}^{\mathrm{blw}}

\displaystyle=-p_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{blw}}Adl+\mu_{g}^{\mathrm{blw}}d{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}},

(6.12)

\displaystyle dF_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}

\displaystyle=-p_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{abv}}Ad(L-l)+\mu_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}d{\cal N}^{\mathrm{abv}}

(6.13)

with
T
,
A
, and
mg
fixed.
These lead to specific expressions for (
6.6
) and (
6.9
) as

\displaystyle\mu_{g}^{\mathrm{blw}}-\mu_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}=\frac{mgL}{2},

(6.14)

\displaystyle(p_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{blw}}-p_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{abv}})A=\frac{mgN}{2}.

(6.15)

These two equations provide the necessary conditions for the equilibrium state.

6.1
Continuity of the pressure profile provided by (
6.15
)

Let
p_{-}
and
p_{+}
denote the pressures immediately below and above the divided position
x=x_{\mathrm{b}}+l
, respectively.
The mean pressures in the lower and upper regions are given by

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{blw}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{\rm b}+p_{-}}{2},\qquad p_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{abv}}=\frac{p_{\rm t}+p_{+}}{2}.

(6.16)

Substituting these expressions into (
6.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle p_{\rm b}-p_{\rm t}+p_{-}-p_{+}=\frac{mgN}{A}.

(6.17)

By applying the force balance condition (
4.2
), it follows that

\displaystyle p_{-}=p_{+}.

(6.18)

Since (
6.18
) holds for any arbitrarily chosen value of
l
, the pressure profile
p(x)
must be continuous throughout the system. This continuity is independent of whether the dividing position
l
coincides with the liquid-gas interface. The result is consistent with the mechanical equilibrium condition assumed in Sec.
5
in the absence of fluid flow.

6.2
Linearity of the chemical potential profile concluded from (
6.14
)

Profile of the chemical potential
\mu(x)
under gravity, shown as the red solid line.
The slope of
\mu(x)
is consistently
-mg
regardless of whether the system is in a pure liquid, pure gas, or liquid-gas coexistence state.
The difference between these equilibrium states is reflected only in the value of
\mu_{g}=\bar{\mu}=\mu(x_{\mathrm{m}})
.

Combining the fundamental thermodynamic relation (
5.4
) with (
6.7
),
we find

\displaystyle\mu_{g}=\left(\frac{\partial{\cal F}_{g}}{\partial N}\right)_{*}.

(6.19)

Using (
6.4
), this can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mu_{g}=\mu_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}+\frac{mgl}{2}.

(6.20)

Substituting (
6.20
) into (
6.14
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mu_{g}+mgx_{\mathrm{m}}=\mu_{g}^{\mathrm{blw}}+mgx_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{blw}}=\mu_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}+mgx_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{abv}},

(6.21)

where
x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{abv}}-x_{\mathrm{m}}=l/2
and
x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{blw}}-x_{\mathrm{m}}=(l-L)/2
have been used. The relation (
6.21
) holds irrespective of whether the liquid-gas interface exists within the defined regions.

Except at points corresponding to the liquid-gas interface,
the pressure profile
p(x)
is smooth, and the force balance can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{dx}=-mg\rho,

(6.22)

which can be rewritten using the Gibbs-Duhem relation
d\mu=dp/\rho
at constant temperature as

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu}{dx}=-mg.

(6.23)

Here, the local chemical potential is defined by the free energy density as
\mu(x)\equiv\partial_{\rho}f(T,\rho(x))
. Equation (

) indicates that
\mu(x)
is linear with a constant slope in
x
.

To examine the continuity of
\mu(x)
at the discontinuities of
\rho(x)
, we divide the system into regions separated by these discontinuities and denote each region by a superscript “
{\rm i}
”. Within each region
{\rm i}
, which is in a single-phase state, the local thermodynamic quantities at the midpoint
x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\rm i}
determine the chemical potential, satisfying
\mu_{g}^{\rm i}=\bar{\mu}^{\rm i}=\mu(x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\rm i})
. Combining this result with the slope
-mg
in (

), the profile in each region
{\rm i}
is expressed as

\displaystyle\mu^{\rm i}(x)=-mgx+\mu_{g}^{\rm i}+mgx_{\mathrm{m}}^{\rm i}.

(6.24)

The intercept
\mu_{g}^{\rm i}+mgx_{\mathrm{m}}^{\rm i}
corresponds to the term appearing in (
6.21
). Therefore, the equality in (
6.21
) ensures that the functional forms of
\mu^{\mathrm{abv}}(x)
and
\mu^{\mathrm{blw}}(x)
are equivalent, including both the slope and the intercept.

Thus, the chemical potential profile, irrespective of the liquid-gas interface, is universally described by

\displaystyle\mu(x)=-mg(x-x_{\mathrm{m}})+\bar{\mu}

(6.25)

for
x_{\mathrm{b}}<x<x_{\mathrm{t}}
, where

\displaystyle\mu_{g}=\bar{\mu}\equiv\frac{1}{L}\int_{x_{\mathrm{b}}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}+L}dx~\mu(x).

(6.26)

The formula (
6.25
) applies to both single-phase states and liquid-gas coexistence. As (

\bar{\mu}=\mu(x_{\mathrm{m}})
. Figure
6
illustrates the relationships among

,
\mu_{\mathrm{c}}(T)
, and
\mu_{g}
.

Finally, we emphasize that the linear profile (

does not coincide with the saturation chemical potential

, where
\mu^{\mathrm{L}}
and

are the equilibrium chemical potentials for the liquid and gas phases, respectively.

7
Variational principle for the phase separation under gravity

The effective Hamiltonian
{\cal F}_{g}({\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}};l)
in (
6.4
)
has established the continuity of
p(x)
and the linearity of
\mu(x)
.
Since both

and
\mu(x)
are linear, they lack any special point in
x
, rendering them incapable of identifying the interface position.
While the pressure profile
p(x)
may exhibit piecewise linearity due to the liquid-gas configuration,
Eqs. (
6.14
) and (
6.15
) do not specify the local pressures at the interfaces.
This section examines the case where the number of liquid-gas interfaces is at most one, constructing a variational principle that leads to consistent equations with (
6.14
) and (
6.15
).
The equilibrium conditions derived are then transformed into the chemical potential balance at the interface.
The case of multiple interfaces is discussed in Appendix B.

7.1
Constraints to refine each region in a single-phase state

Construction of the variational principle to determine liquid and gas distribution.

To this point, the dividing position
x_{\mathrm{b}}+l
has been set arbitrarily.
We now impose the constraint

\displaystyle X^{\mathrm{blw}}=x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{blw}}+O(\varepsilon),\qquad X^{\mathrm{abv}}=x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{abv}}+O(\varepsilon),

(7.1)

which, referring to (
5.7
), implies that both regions are in a single-phase state.
This section examines this implication in detail.

Starting with the fundamental relation (
5.4
),
the following equations hold for each region above or below the virtual section:

\displaystyle dF_{g}^{\mathrm{blw}}

\displaystyle=-S^{\mathrm{blw}}dT-\bar{p}^{\mathrm{blw}}d{\cal V}^{\mathrm{blw}}+\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{blw}}d{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}+{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}\frac{X^{\mathrm{blw}}-x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{blw}}}{l}d(mgl),

(7.2)

\displaystyle dF_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}

\displaystyle=-S^{\mathrm{abv}}dT-\bar{p}^{\mathrm{abv}}d{\cal V}^{\mathrm{abv}}+\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{abv}}d{\cal N}^{\mathrm{abv}}+{\cal N}^{\mathrm{abv}}\frac{X^{\mathrm{abv}}-x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{abv}}}{L-l}d(mg(L-l)).

(7.3)

Here,
{\cal V}^{\mathrm{blw}}=Al
and
{\cal V}^{\mathrm{abv}}=A(L-l)
.
Equations (

) and (

) yield Maxwell relations, such as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{blw}}}{\partial(mgl)}\right)_{T,{\cal V}^{\mathrm{blw}},{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}}=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}}\left({\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}\frac{X^{\mathrm{blw}}-x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{blw}}}{l}\right)\right)_{T,{\cal V}^{\mathrm{blw}},mgl},

(7.4)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{abv}}}{\partial(mg(L-l))}\right)_{T,{\cal V}^{\mathrm{abv}},{\cal N}^{\mathrm{abv}}}=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{abv}}}\left({\cal N}^{\mathrm{abv}}\frac{X^{\mathrm{abv}}-x_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{abv}}}{L-l}\right)\right)_{T,{\cal V}^{\mathrm{abv}},mg(L-l)}.

(7.5)

These relations elucidate how
\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{blw}}
and
\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{abv}}
respond to gravity.

Under the constraint (
7.1
), the right-hand sides of (

) and (

) are

.
Thus, the Maxwell relations indicate that
\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{blw}}
and
\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{abv}}
do not contain

terms.
Together with (

) and (

), the constraint (

) refines
\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{blw}}
and
\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{abv}}
as functions of
(T,v^{\mathrm{blw}})
and
(T,v^{\mathrm{abv}})
by neglecting

terms, where

and
v^{\mathrm{abv}}={\cal V}^{\mathrm{abv}}/{\cal N}^{\mathrm{abv}}
.
Moreover, the constraint (
7.1
) simplifies the free energies of the respective regions

\displaystyle F_{g}^{\mathrm{blw}}=F(T,{\cal V}^{\mathrm{blw}},{\cal N}^{\mathrm{blw}}),\qquad F_{g}^{\mathrm{abv}}=F(T,{\cal V}^{\mathrm{abv}},{\cal N}^{\mathrm{abv}}),

(7.6)

where

denotes the free energy at

When

7.2
Reformulation of the variational principle

The effective Hamiltonian

Subsequently, the arbitrariness of

We reformulate these statements under the constraint (

Hereafter, the indices

Specifically, there are four possible configurations,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{\ell},\mathrm{u})=(\mathrm{L},\mathrm{G}),~(\mathrm{G},\mathrm{L}),~(\mathrm{L},\mathrm{L}),~(\mathrm{G},\mathrm{G}),

where

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{g}}{\partial\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}\right)_{*}=0,\qquad\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}_{g}}{\partial\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}\right)_{*}=0.

(\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{\ell}},\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{\ell}})

7.3
Balance of pressure and chemical potential at the interface

Substituting (
7.8
) into (
7.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F(T,\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{\ell}})}{\partial\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}+\frac{\partial F(T,V-\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},N-\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{\ell}})}{\partial\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}+\frac{NmgL}{2V}=0,

(7.11)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F(T,\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{\ell}})}{\partial\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}+\frac{\partial F(T,V-\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},N-\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{\ell}})}{\partial\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}-\frac{mgL}{2}=0.

(7.12)

Because
F(T,\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{\ell}})
and
F(T,V-\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},N-\mathcal{N}^{\mathrm{\ell}})
represent the free energies in single-phase states, (
7.11
) and (
7.12
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle(\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}-\bar{p}^{\mathrm{u}})A=\frac{mgN}{2},\qquad\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{\ell}}-\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{u}}=\frac{mgL}{2}.

(7.13)

Since
\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}=p_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{\ell}}
and
\bar{p}^{\mathrm{u}}=p_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{u}}
, the first equality in (
7.13
) leads to the continuity of pressure

\displaystyle p_{\theta}=p_{+}=p_{-}.

(7.14)

From the force balance in (
5.11
), we find

\displaystyle\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}=p_{\theta}+\frac{p_{\mathrm{b}}-p_{\theta}}{2}=p_{\theta}+\frac{mgN^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{2A},

(7.15)

\displaystyle\bar{p}^{\mathrm{u}}=p_{\theta}+\frac{p_{\mathrm{t}}-p_{\theta}}{2}=p_{\theta}-\frac{mgN^{\mathrm{u}}}{2A}.

(7.16)

Because the chemical potential at
g=0
depends on
(T,p)
, we write

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{\ell}}=\mu^{\mathrm{\ell}}(T,\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}),\qquad\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{u}}=\mu^{\mathrm{u}}(T,\bar{p}^{\mathrm{u}}),

(7.17)

where
\mu^{\mathrm{\ell}}(T,p)
and
\mu^{\mathrm{u}}(T,p)
represent the chemical potentials for liquid or gas phases.

Substituting (
7.15
) and (
7.16
) into (
7.17
) and expanding
\mu^{\mathrm{\ell}}
and
\mu^{\mathrm{u}}
in terms of
mg
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{\ell}}=\mu^{\mathrm{\ell}}(T,p_{\theta})+\frac{mgl}{2},\qquad\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{u}}=\mu^{\mathrm{u}}(T,p_{\theta})-\frac{mg(L-l)}{2},

(7.18)

where we have used
\left(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial p}\right)_{T}=1/\rho
.
Thus, the second equality in (
7.13

\displaystyle\mu^{\mathrm{\ell}}(T,p_{\theta})=\mu^{\mathrm{u}}(T,p_{\theta}),

(7.19)

indicating the continuity of the chemical potential at the interface.

In Appendix B, we extend the above arguments to systems with two or multiple interfaces, demonstrating that the thermodynamically stable state contains at most one interface.

Thermodynamics at

We begin by considering the thermodynamic states when

Let the equation of state be

By integrating

The single-phase state becomes unstable in the region

The free energy of the liquid-gas coexistence is depicted as the dotted line in Fig.

\displaystyle f^{\mathrm{hetero}}(T,v)\equiv\frac{N_{0}^{\mathrm{L}}}{N}f(T,v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{L}}(T))+\frac{N_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}}{N}f(T,v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)),

The number of particles and volumes,

\displaystyle V_{0}^{\mathrm{L}}=v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{L}}(T)N_{0}^{\mathrm{L}},\quad V_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}=v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)N_{0}^{\mathrm{G}},\quad N_{0}^{\mathrm{L}}+N_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}=N,\quad V_{0}^{\mathrm{L}}+V_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}=V,

\displaystyle\frac{N_{0}^{\mathrm{L}}}{N}=\frac{v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)-\bar{v}}{v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)-v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{L}}(T)},\quad\frac{V_{0}^{\mathrm{L}}}{V}=\frac{v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{L}}(T)}{v}\frac{v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)-v}{v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)-v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{L}}(T)},

(8.3)

\displaystyle\frac{N_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}}{N}=\frac{\bar{v}-v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{L}}(T)}{v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)-v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{L}}(T)},\quad\frac{V_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}}{V}=\frac{v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)}{\bar{v}}\frac{\bar{v}-v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{L}}(T)}{v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)-v_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{L}}(T)}.

(8.4)

Thus,
(N_{0}^{\mathrm{L}}/N,V_{0}^{\mathrm{L}}/V)
and
(N_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}/N,V_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}/V)
are uniquely determined for a given
(T,v)

In Fig.
8
(b),

f(T,v)

Clearly,

We can then derive

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta N}{N}\equiv\frac{mgL}{2}\left[\alpha^{\mathrm{L}}\frac{v^{\mathrm{L}}_{\mathrm{c}}(T)}{\bar{v}}\left(\frac{N^{\mathrm{L}}_{0}}{N}\right)^{2}+\alpha^{\mathrm{G}}\frac{v^{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{c}}(T)}{\bar{v}}\left(\frac{N^{\mathrm{G}}_{0}}{N}\right)^{2}\right]\frac{1}{v^{\mathrm{G}}_{\mathrm{c}}(T)-v^{\mathrm{L}}_{\mathrm{c}}(T)},

(8.15)

In

\displaystyle N^{\mathrm{\ell}}_{(\mathrm{G},\mathrm{L})}=N_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}+\Delta N,\quad V^{\mathrm{\ell}}_{(\mathrm{G},\mathrm{L})}=V_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}+\Delta V,

Stability of each solution

The variational equations provide necessary conditions for the equilibrium states. Here, we examine the thermodynamic stability of each solution. Let us denote the solution of (

. The probability of a fluctuation
(\delta{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}},\delta{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}})
is formally represented by the Einstein formula
Einstein
;
Onsager1931

\displaystyle\rho(\delta{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}},\delta{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}})\propto e^{-\beta{\delta{\cal F}_{g}}},

(9.1)

which is equivalent to (

), derived from equilibrium statistical mechanics.

The corresponding fluctuation of the free energy is expressed as

\displaystyle\delta{\cal F}_{g}(\delta{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}},\delta{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}})={\cal F}_{g}(N^{\mathrm{\ell}}+\delta{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}},V^{\mathrm{\ell}}+\delta{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}};T,V,N,mgL)-{\cal F}_{g}(N^{\mathrm{\ell}},V^{\mathrm{\ell}};T,V,N,mgL).

(9.2)

Since the linear terms of
O(\delta{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}},\delta{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}})
cancel out, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta{\cal F}_{g}(\delta{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}},\delta{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}})=\delta^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}}),

(9.3)

neglecting higher-order terms. Here,
F^{\mathrm{\ell}}=F(T,{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}})
and
F^{\mathrm{u}}=F(T,V-{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},N-{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}})
, while

\displaystyle\delta^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})=\begin{pmatrix}\delta({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)&\delta({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial{({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)}^{2}}\right)_{*}&\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)\partial({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)}\right)_{*}\\[5.0pt]
\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)\partial({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)}\right)_{*}&\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial{({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)}^{2}}\right)_{*}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\delta({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)\\[5.0pt]
\delta({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)\end{pmatrix}.

(9.4)

The formula (
9.1
), combined with (
9.3
), indicates that the solution
(N^{\mathrm{\ell}},V^{\mathrm{\ell}})
is locally stable in the space of
({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}})
if

\displaystyle\delta^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})>0.

(9.5)

The matrix elements in (
9.4
) can be expressed in terms of the isothermal compressibilities for each phase,
\alpha^{\mathrm{\ell}}
and
\alpha^{\mathrm{u}}
. The first diagonal element transforms as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial{({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)}^{2}}=N^{2}\frac{\partial\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}+N^{2}\frac{\partial\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{u}}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{u}}}=-N^{2}\left(\frac{{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{\partial{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}-N^{2}\left(\frac{{\cal V}^{\mathrm{u}}}{{\cal N}^{\mathrm{u}}}\right)^{2}\frac{\partial\bar{p}^{\mathrm{u}}}{\partial{\cal V}^{\mathrm{u}}},

(9.6)

where the second equality follows from the Gibbs-Duhem relation

\displaystyle{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}\frac{\partial\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}={\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}\frac{\partial\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}.

(9.7)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial{({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)}^{2}}\right)_{*}=\frac{N^{2}}{\alpha^{\mathrm{\ell}}V^{\mathrm{\ell}}}\left(\frac{{V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{{N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}\right)^{2}+\frac{N^{2}}{\alpha^{\mathrm{u}}V^{\mathrm{u}}}\left(\frac{{V}^{\mathrm{u}}}{{N}^{\mathrm{u}}}\right)^{2}.

(9.8)

The second diagonal element is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial{({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)}^{2}}=-V^{2}\frac{\partial\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{\partial{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}-V^{2}\frac{\partial\bar{p}^{\mathrm{u}}}{\partial{\cal V}^{\mathrm{u}}},

(9.9)

and can be expressed as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial{({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)}^{2}}\right)_{*}=\frac{V^{2}}{\alpha^{\mathrm{\ell}}V^{\mathrm{\ell}}}+\frac{V^{2}}{\alpha^{\mathrm{u}}V^{\mathrm{u}}}.

(9.10)

The off-diagonal element is transformed as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)\partial({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)}=-NV\frac{\partial\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}-NV\frac{\partial\bar{p}^{\mathrm{u}}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{u}}}.

(9.11)

To rewrite this expression, we utilize the fact that the pressure is an intensive quantity, which implies the following relation for each phase:

\displaystyle{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}\frac{\partial\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{\partial{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}+{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}\frac{\partial\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{\partial{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}=0.

(9.12)

Applying this result to each phase, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)\partial({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)}=NV\frac{{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}\frac{\partial\bar{p}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{\partial{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}+NV\frac{{\cal V}^{\mathrm{u}}}{{\cal N}^{\mathrm{u}}}\frac{\partial\bar{p}^{\mathrm{u}}}{\partial{\cal V}^{\mathrm{u}}}.

(9.13)

Thus, the off-diagonal element becomes

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)\partial({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)}\right)_{*}=-\frac{NV}{\alpha^{\mathrm{\ell}}N^{\mathrm{\ell}}}-\frac{NV}{\alpha^{\mathrm{u}}N^{\mathrm{u}}}.

(9.14)

The eigenvalues of the matrix in (
9.4
) are given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{+}=A+\sqrt{A^{2}-D},\qquad\lambda_{-}=A-\sqrt{A^{2}-D},

(9.15)

using the half-trace
A
and determinant
D
of the matrix, where
A^{2}>D\geq 0
.
Explicitly,
2A
and
D
are calculated as

\displaystyle 2A

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial{({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial{({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)}^{2}}\right)_{*}=\frac{N^{2}}{\alpha^{\mathrm{\ell}}V^{\mathrm{\ell}}}\left(\left(\frac{V}{N}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{V^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{N^{\mathrm{\ell}}}\right)^{2}\right)+\frac{N^{2}}{\alpha^{\mathrm{u}}V^{\mathrm{u}}}\left(\left(\frac{V}{N}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{V^{\mathrm{u}}}{N^{\mathrm{u}}}\right)^{2}\right),

(9.16)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial{({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial{({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)}^{2}}-\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(F^{\mathrm{\ell}}+F^{\mathrm{u}})}{\partial({\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/N)\partial({\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}/V)}\right)^{2}\right)_{*}=\frac{N^{2}V^{2}}{\alpha^{\mathrm{\ell}}\alpha^{\mathrm{u}}V^{\mathrm{\ell}}V^{\mathrm{u}}}\left(\frac{V^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{N^{\mathrm{\ell}}}-\frac{V^{\mathrm{u}}}{N^{\mathrm{u}}}\right)^{2}.

(9.17)

\displaystyle F(T,V^{\mathrm{L}},N^{\mathrm{L}})+F(T,V-V^{\mathrm{L}},N-N^{\mathrm{L}})

\displaystyle=F(T,V_{0}^{\mathrm{L}},N_{0}^{\mathrm{L}})+F(T,V-V_{0}^{\mathrm{L}},N-N_{0}^{\mathrm{L}})

\displaystyle=F_{g}(T,V,N,mgL=0).

(11.5)

Since
F_{g}(T,V,N,mgL=0)=F(T,V,N)
, we conclude

\displaystyle F_{g}(T,V,N,mgL)=F(T,V,N)-m|g|LN\psi_{g}^{(\mathrm{L},\mathrm{G})}(T,V/N).

(11.6)

Thus,
F_{g}(T,V,N,mgL)
becomes a continuous but non-smooth function of
mgL
at
g=0
, as illustrated in Fig.
13
(b).
These results confirm that
g=0
is a first-order transition point.
Consequently, thermodynamic properties in liquid-gas coexistence can exhibit singular behavior in the small-gravity limit
|g|\rightarrow 0
.

11.2
Relation between
F_{g}
and
F

Using (
11.4
), Eq. (
11.6
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle F_{g}(T,V,N,mgL)=F(T,V,N)-mgL\Psi_{g}(T,V,N),

(11.7)

despite the singularity at
g=0
.
The total differential of
F_{g}
forms the fundamental relation of thermodynamics, derived in (
5.4
), as

\displaystyle dF_{g}=-SdT-\bar{p}dV+\bar{\mu}dN-\Psi_{g}d(mgL),

(11.8)

which implies

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial F_{g}}{\partial mgL}\right)_{T,V,N}=-\Psi_{g},

(11.9)

even though
\Psi_{g}\propto g/|g|
is singular at
g=0
.

Note that (
11.7
) provides a Legendre transformation.
Applying (
11.7
) to (
11.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle dF=-SdT-\bar{p}dV+\tilde{\mu}dN+NmgL\,d\left(\frac{x_{\mathrm{m}}-X}{L}\right).

(11.10)

Here, we replaced
\bar{\mu}
with a global chemical potential defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}\equiv\frac{A}{N}\int_{x_{\mathrm{b}}}^{x_{\mathrm{b}}+L}dx\,\mu(x)\rho(x),

(11.11)

with which
\bar{\mu}
is expressed as

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}=\tilde{\mu}+mg(X-x_{\mathrm{m}}),

(11.12)

derived from the local relation (
6.25
).

Since
F
is the free energy of the system without gravity,
we rewrite (
11.10
) as

\displaystyle dF=-S_{0}dT-p_{0}dV+\mu_{0}dN,

(11.13)

where

\displaystyle S_{0}=S(T,V,N,mgL)-NmgL\left(\frac{\partial(x_{\mathrm{m}}-X)/L}{\partial T}\right)_{V,N},

(11.14)

\displaystyle p_{0}=\bar{p}(T,V,N,mgL)-NmgL\left(\frac{\partial(x_{\mathrm{m}}-X)/L}{\partial V}\right)_{T,N},

(11.15)

\displaystyle\mu_{0}=\tilde{\mu}(T,V,N,mgL)+NmgL\left(\frac{\partial(x_{\mathrm{m}}-X)/L}{\partial N}\right)_{T,V}.

(11.16)

Note that
S_{0}
,
p_{0}
, and
\mu_{0}
are independent of
g
and should correspond to
S(T,V,N)
,
p(T,V,N)
, and
\mu(T,V,N)
derived from
F(T,V,N)
for the case
g=0
.
In the phase coexistence state,
S_{0}
,
p_{0}
, and
\mu_{0}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=S(T,V_{0}^{\mathrm{L}},N_{0}^{\mathrm{L}})+S(T,V_{0}^{\mathrm{G}},N_{0}^{\mathrm{G}}),

(11.17)

and

\displaystyle p_{0}=p_{\mathrm{s}}(T),\quad\mu_{0}=\mu_{\mathrm{c}}(T).

(11.18)

This argument provides expressions for

,
\bar{p}
, and
\bar{\mu}
in terms of
(T,V,N,mgL)
using (
11.14
), (
11.15
), and (
11.16
).

12
Numerical demonstration of global thermodynamics

In this section, we aim to verify the validity of global thermodynamics for liquid-gas coexistence under gravity.
We present a formula predicted by global thermodynamics, namely Maxwell’s relation.
We then perform molecular dynamics simulations on the liquid-gas coexistence and compare the numerical results with the theoretical predictions.

12.1

The fundamental relation (

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\bar{p}}{\partial(mgL)}\right)_{T,V,N}

by substituting (

Here, we used

.
From (

\displaystyle\bar{p}(T,\bar{\rho},mgL)=p_{\mathrm{s}}(T)+\frac{|mgL|}{2}\frac{\bar{\rho}^{2}-\rho_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{L}}(T)\rho_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)}{\rho_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{L}}(T)-\rho_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{G}}(T)},

(12.2)

using

at

Note that (

) clearly shows the singularity at

(12.8)

12.2
Fluctuation-response relation

Let the center of mass and its deviation from
x_{\mathrm{m}}
be

\displaystyle\hat{X}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i},\qquad\hat{Y}\equiv\frac{x_{\mathrm{m}}-\hat{X}}{L}.

(12.9)

Their means are denoted by
X=\langle\hat{X}\rangle
and
Y=\langle\hat{Y}\rangle
.
Define a response

\displaystyle\chi_{g}(T,V,N,mgL)\equiv\frac{\partial Y}{\partial(mgL)},

(12.10)

which can be expressed as
\chi_{g}(T,\bar{\rho},mgL)
because of its intensive nature. Using

\displaystyle\chi_{g}=\frac{\partial}{\partial(mgL)}\int d\Gamma\frac{\hat{Y}}{Z_{g}}e^{-\beta H(\Gamma)}e^{\beta NmgL\hat{Y}},

(12.11)

and the relations

\displaystyle\left.\left(\frac{\partial Z_{g}}{\partial(mgL)}\right)_{T,L,A,N}\right|_{mgL}=Z_{g}\beta N\langle\hat{Y}\rangle,

(12.12)

\displaystyle\left.\left(\frac{\partial^{2}Z_{g}}{\partial(mgL)^{2}}\right)_{T,L,A,N}\right|_{mgL}=Z_{g}\beta^{2}N^{2}\langle\hat{Y}^{2}\rangle,

(12.13)

we derive a fluctuation-response relation,

\displaystyle\chi_{g}(T,\bar{\rho},mgL)=\beta N\sigma_{Y}^{2}(T,L,A,N,mgL),

(12.14)

where
\sigma_{Y}
is the standard deviation of
\hat{Y}
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{Y}(T,L,A,N,mgL)\equiv\sqrt{\langle(\hat{Y}-\langle\hat{Y}\rangle)^{2}\rangle}.

(12.15)

The relation (
12.14
) is expressed as

\displaystyle\sigma_{Y}(T,L,A,\bar{\rho},mgL)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{LA}}\sqrt{\frac{k_{B}T\chi_{g}(T,\bar{\rho},mgL)}{\bar{\rho}}}.

(12.16)

The formula (
12.16
) indicates that fluctuations can be ignored in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
,
L\to\infty
,
A\to\infty
with
mgL
and
\bar{\rho}
fixed.
Note that
L\to\infty
should be taken with
mg\to 0
to keep
mgL
at a small finite value.

13
Concluding remarks

We have formulated global thermodynamics for equilibrium systems under gravity. Thermodynamic variables were extended from
(T,V,N)
to
(T,V,N,mgL)
by adopting the midpoint
x_{\mathrm{m}}
of the system as the reference for the gravitational potential. The free energy landscape revealed the presence of both global and local minima in the phase coexistence states. The principle of minimum free energy identified the global minimum and characterized the inversion of the liquid-gas configuration as a first-order transition at
g=0
. The numerical simulation results aligned well with the predictions of global thermodynamics. Before concluding this paper, we present three remarks.

First, in this paper, we focused on isothermal systems under gravity at fixed
T
. It is possible to formulate global thermodynamics in other scenarios. For instance, consider energy-conserved systems where the total energy
E=U+mg(X-x_{\mathrm{m}})
is fixed. The maximum entropy principle determines the equilibrium state. Define entropy as a variational function in fixed
(E,V,N,mgL)

\displaystyle{\cal S}({\cal E}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}};E,V,N,mgL)\equiv S({\cal E}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}})+S({\cal E}^{\mathrm{u}},V-{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},N-{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}),

(13.1)

where
{\cal E}^{\mathrm{\ell}}
and
{\cal E}^{\mathrm{u}}
are the total energies in the lower and upper regions, respectively. These energies satisfy
{\cal E}^{\mathrm{\ell}}={\cal U}^{\mathrm{\ell}}
and
{\cal E}^{\mathrm{u}}={\cal U}^{\mathrm{u}}
. Since the reference points for the gravitational potential differ between
\mathrm{\ell}
and
\mathrm{u}
, they are related by:

\displaystyle{\cal E}^{\mathrm{\ell}}+{\cal E}^{\mathrm{u}}=E+mgL\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{V}-\frac{{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{N}\right).

(13.2)

Thermodynamic entropy corresponds to the maximal value of
{\cal S}
as

\displaystyle S(E,V,N,mgL)=\max_{{\cal E}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}}}{\cal S}({\cal E}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal V}^{\mathrm{\ell}},{\cal N}^{\mathrm{\ell}};E,V,N,mgL).

(13.3)

This satisfies a fundamental relation of thermodynamics:

\displaystyle dS=\frac{dE}{T}+\frac{\bar{p}}{T}dV-\frac{\bar{\mu}}{T}dN-\frac{N}{T}\frac{X-x_{\rm m}}{L}d(mgL),

(13.4)

where the maximum entropy principle leads to
p_{\theta}=p_{\mathrm{s}}(T)
and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T(E,V,N,mgL)}=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\right)_{V,N,mgL}.

(13.5)

Second, we discuss a possible non-equilibrium extension of the results presented in this paper. Our previous studies proposed global thermodynamics as a framework to extend conventional thermodynamics to heat-conducting systems
Global-PRL
;
Global-JSP
;
Global-PRR
;
KNS
. One key prediction is the deviation of the interface temperature, proportional to the heat flux, from the equilibrium transition temperature. This paper has formulated the equilibrium version of global thermodynamics, ensuring consistency with conventional thermodynamic principles, and has demonstrated its validity. Building on this foundation, we aim to extend global thermodynamics to systems with heat conduction under gravity.

For example, consider a system where heat baths at higher and lower temperatures are attached at
x=0
and
x=L
, respectively. Without gravity (
g=0
), a liquid region emerges near
x=L
in the liquid-gas coexistence state. An intriguing question arises: What happens when gravity
g>0
is introduced? If the gravitational force is sufficiently strong, the liquid phase shifts near
x=0
. Conversely, when gravity is weak, the liquid remains near
x=L
. This behavior suggests a transition as gravitational strength changes. Numerical simulations of molecular dynamics
ANS
and related studies
Wagner
provide insight into this transition. Extending global thermodynamics under gravity to include heat conduction could offer quantitative predictions for such transitions
Global-Heat-Gravity
.

Here, one might wonder whether the predictions of global thermodynamics are consistent with those of a continuum hydrodynamic description based on the free energy functional

\displaystyle{\cal F}([\rho])=\int_{\mathcal{D}}d^{3}{\mathbf{r}}\left[f(T,\rho(\mathbf{r}))+mgx\rho(\mathbf{r})+\frac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla\rho|^{2}\right],

(13.6)

where
[\rho]
denotes a smooth density profile
\rho(\mathbf{r})
in a region
\mathcal{D}
,
f
is a mesoscopic free energy density, and
\kappa
corresponds to the surface tension at mesoscopic scales. For equilibrium systems under gravity, the continuum theory provides the same thermodynamic behavior as global thermodynamics formulated in the present paper. However, for heat conduction systems without gravity (
g=0
), it is known that global thermodynamics predicts that the pressure at the interface deviates from the saturation pressure under heat conduction, whereas the continuum theory does not. Understanding this discrepancy remains an important and open question.

The final remark addresses this question. We begin by reviewing the continuum description of phase coexistence states, originally proposed by van der Waals
vanPress
. One significant challenge lies in achieving a microscopic understanding of the effective Hamiltonian. This difficulty arises because, for systems far from the critical point, the liquid-gas interface width is microscopic, rendering the density gradient too sharp for the van der Waals theory to apply. Interface fluctuations have been extensively studied through statistical mechanics
Kirkwood_Buff
;
BLS
;
Weeks
;
Rowlinson_Widom
;
Stillinger
;
Napiorkowski_Dietrich
;
Mecke_Dietrich

Weeks
, while non-analytic behavior of long-range fluctuations in the direction parallel to the steep liquid-gas interface was observed, depending on the type of molecular interactions. These fluctuations cannot be described by the van der Waals theory
Napiorkowski_Dietrich
;
Mecke_Dietrich
.
Experiments
Daillant
;
Daillant2
;
Rice
and molecular dynamics simulations
Chacon_Tarazona
;
Chacon_Tarazona2
further suggest limitations in the van der Waals theory. Moreover, in heat conduction states, the microscopic singularity at the liquid-gas interface becomes evident. For instance, numerical simulations have reported a microscopic temperature gap at the interface
Muscatello
, suggesting a breakdown of the continuum description in this region.

Despite these uncertainties, it is anticipated that the van der Waals theory can effectively capture universal characteristics, provided that macroscopic behaviors involving interfaces are independent of microscopic details.
For example, it is well established that the canonical distribution with the effective Hamiltonian
{\cal F}([\rho])
reproduces the critical exponents near the liquid-gas critical point
CP
, and furthermore, that phenomenological arguments using
{\cal F}([\rho])
successfully reproduce dynamic universality classes
Hohenberg_Halperin1977
.
However, outside these successful examples, the validity of this approach should be reconsidered by conducting a detailed comparison with experimental results.
In such cases, a universal framework and corresponding mesoscopic models should also be proposed to better understand the phenomena under study.
In this context, our recent study is worth highlighting. In Ref.
Global-modelB
, we investigated a discrete fluctuating hydrodynamics of a density field under a particle current driven by a battery.
By applying non-equilibrium statistical mechanics

;
Mclennan
;
Maes-rep
, we found that thermodynamic properties deviate from those predicted by the continuum description with the van der Waals theory but align with the predictions of global thermodynamics. Notably, this deviation arises only when the liquid-gas interface is assumed to be singular; it disappears when the density profile at the interface becomes smooth.
These results suggest that global thermodynamics is the only valid framework for systems with a singularly thin interface. Constructing a consistent narrative that bridges microscopic and macroscopic scales remains a fascinating and challenging endeavor.
