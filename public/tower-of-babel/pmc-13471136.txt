The Infant Feeding History: An Evidence-Informed 9-Item Structured History for Detecting Serious Illness in the Emergency Department
Authors: Joel Berezow, Chrysalis Mandell, Ellery H Schlingmann, Cheryl Vance, Robert A Dudas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeriously ill infants frequently present to emergency departments with vague or nonspecific symptoms, making early identification of these patients challenging. Changes in feeding behavior are among the most reliable early indicators of pathology. This article outlines an evidence-informed, structured approach to the infant feeding history, proposing a 9-item tool to aid frontline providers in detecting life-threatening conditions. This tool utilizes the feeding history to evaluate an infant’s vigor and hydration, and aids in the identification of red flags for serious or life-threatening conditions. Normative feeding patterns by age are provided to contextualize deviations. This evidence-informed framework is intended to standardize and strengthen feeding history assessment; prospective studies are needed to validate its diagnostic performance and determine whether a scoring threshold or abbreviated version is feasible. By integrating this tool into clinical practice, providers may be able to improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce missed diagnoses, and enhance parental counseling and anticipatory guidance at discharge from the emergency department.Keywords: infant feeding, serious illness identification, pediatric triage, emergency department

Introduction

Infants (<1 year old) account for more than 1 in 10 visits to community hospital emergency departments (EDs) and have the highest ED visit rate of any age group.1,2 One of the greatest challenges in emergency medicine is the ability to identify serious illnesses in this nonverbal population who often present with vague and nonspecific complaints. Because many infants who present for care are not seriously ill, vital sign abnormalities alone lack adequate sensitivity and specificity, and additional historic clues are often needed.

Infants have limited physiologic reserve and may deteriorate rapidly. Unlike adults, whose illness may manifest with a wide range of symptoms, infants have a narrow behavioral repertoire limited to feeding, sleeping, voiding, and stooling. Feeding, their primary activity, is often the first behavior to change in systemic illness. Global data show that poor feeding is a strong clinical sign of severe illness in young infants and may be more sensitive than fever, hypothermia, tachypnea, or lethargy.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Accordingly, poor feeding is included as a risk sign in both NICE guidance for febrile illness and the World Health Organization’s Integrated Management of Childhood Illness.11

Yet clinicians lack a standardized, ED-ready method to obtain and interpret the feeding history, leading to inconsistent assessments and missed opportunities to detect time-sensitive diagnoses. To address this gap, we propose an evidence-informed, 9-item Infant Feeding History Tool that standardizes how emergency clinicians assess vigor (frequency, initiation, suck strength, interruptions), hydration (method, volume/duration, urine output), and red flags (bilious emesis, bloody stool, feed-associated dyspnea). The tool pairs age-based norms with actionable phrasing and quantitative thresholds, eg, ≥50% reduction from baseline intake, to translate parental observations into clearer triage, diagnostic, and disposition decisions.

The Clinical Challenge

Serious illness in infants can be subtle and, therefore, easily missed or delayed in diagnosis. Up to 25% of pediatric sepsis cases may be missed by clinician judgment alone.12 Similarly, volvulus is often not recognized early because bilious vomiting may be intermittent, may occur only once, and may resolve temporarily, while the infant is still in the emergency department. In some infants, intermittent emesis or poor feeding may be the only clue.13 Pediatric myocarditis is also difficult to diagnose because early symptoms are often nonspecific and may mimic viral illness or sepsis. Feed-associated fatigue, reduced suck, or pauses during feeding may be among the earliest signs of poor cardiac output.14 Additionally, necrotizing enterocolitis and botulism are both characterized by subtle changes in feeding patterns which precede overt, catastrophic illness.15

Importantly, the feeding history can also identify “red flags” for life-threatening conditions, such as bilious vomiting (volvulus and intussusception), feeding-associated shortness of breath (myocarditis and anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery), less vigorous suck or decreased feeding (botulism) or bloody stools (necrotizing enterocolitis).

The 9-Item Infant Feeding History Tool

We performed a narrative review of the literature for evidence to support the role of the infant history in identifying serious illness in infants. We searched the following bibliographic databases: PubMed (National Library of Medicine) and Embase (Elsevier). We used the search terms of “poor feeding,” “infant feeding behavior,” “serious bacterial infection,” “sepsis,” “myocarditis,” “volvulus,” “intussusception,” “necrotizing enterocolitis,” and “botulism.” We prioritized systematic reviews, clinical guidelines, and observational studies conducted in emergency or acute care settings. Serious illnesses were defined as invasive bacterial infections which commonly include sepsis, bacteremia, urinary tract infections, meningitis and bacterial pneumonia. Based upon this review of the literature, current pediatric sepsis guidelines and expert consensus, we developed a 9-item infant feeding history tool (Table 2). This tool assesses infant vigor, risk of dehydration, and highlights several red flags which can suggest serious illness.

Existing structured feeding assessments, such as the Neonatal Eating Assessment Tool, validate that quantifiable feeding behaviors (eg, vigor, efficiency) are critical markers of infant health.16 Additionally, Quantifying changes (eg, “50% reduction in intake”) improves predictive value for severe illness.17 Our 9-item tool adapts similar principles for emergency settings, focusing on red flags for acute illness.

Key Red Flags

Bilious Vomiting

Bilious Vomiting: Green/yellow vomit mandates emergent imaging (UGI for volvulus in infants <4 months; ultrasound for intussusception in older infants).

Providers should ask specifically about vomiting and clarify whether emesis was green or predominantly yellow. Even if vomiting has resolved or the infant appears well, a history of bilious emesis is concerning for intestinal obstruction. In infants younger than 4 months, any green or predominantly yellow vomit should be treated as bilious and should prompt urgent surgical consultation and imaging for volvulus. In infants 4 months or older, green vomit is still bilious and should prompt evaluation for intussusception or other obstruction. For infants in the transitional age of between 3 and 4 months old, BOTH volvulus AND intussusception should be considered.

Shortness of Breath During Feeds

Shortness of Breath During Feeds: May indicate cardiac or respiratory pathology, including myocarditis, anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA), bronchiolitis, or pneumonia.

Mild feed-associated dyspnea may be overlooked by caregivers if the infant still completes most feeds. When taking the history, first establish the infant’s usual feeding pattern, then ask whether the child now pauses more than usual, tires during feeds, or appears short of breath while feeding. If any part of the feeding history raises concern here, consider the possibility of myocarditis, and for infants still <6 months old also consider ALCAPA.

Frank Blood in Stool

Frank Blood in Stool: Requires evaluation for necrotizing enterocolitis, coagulopathy, or obstruction.

Gross blood in the stool is a red flag and warrants evaluation for necrotizing enterocolitis, coagulopathy, or obstruction. Small streaks or flecks of blood are more commonly seen with benign conditions such as anal fissure, infectious diarrhea, or milk-protein allergy, but gross blood should not be dismissed and could be an early sign of necrotizing enterocolitis (in newborns and very young infants), intussusception, volvulus, thrombocytopenia, or other coagulopathy.

Implementation and Discussion

The 9-item Infant Feeding History Tool is intended as a structured clinical framework rather than a validated scoring system. The significance of any abnormal response depends on the nature of the abnormality, the infant’s age, the degree of change from baseline, and associated symptoms. In practice, a single high-risk finding such as bilious emesis, bloody stool, marked reduction in intake, weak suck, or feed-associated dyspnea may warrant urgent evaluation even if other items are normal. Conversely, smaller changes in multiple domains may increase concern and prompt closer assessment, observation, or diagnostic testing. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether age-specific thresholds, a total score, or an abbreviated version can be validated for bedside use.

The tool directs the clinician to ask about all 9 items in 2 time frames: the infant’s usual preillness baseline and the current illness period. This distinction helps clinicians identify change from normal feeding behavior, which is often more informative than the absolute feeding pattern alone. A useful transition is, “I know your infant has been having these symptoms now, but I’d like to ask about their usual behavior before they became ill,” followed by the same questions framed around the current illness.

This article is based on narrative review and expert synthesis rather than prospective validation, and future studies are needed to test reliability, sensitivity, specificity, and clinical impact.

Quantifiable measures may improve risk stratification by correlating directly with illness severity, enabling earlier identification of high-risk infants. Additionally, clear benchmarks derived from normative feeding patterns enhance anticipatory guidance for parents, helping them recognize concerning deviations and to seek timely care. By integrating these elements, the tool bridges the gap between clinical assessment and actionable decision making in emergency settings.

Conclusion

The literature supports the infant feeding history as an important element in the evaluation of potentially ill infants, but no standardized framework currently exists for frontline use. We present a 9-item, evidence-informed history tool designed to organize and strengthen feeding assessment in the emergency department. Prospective studies are needed to validate its diagnostic performance, determine whether age-specific thresholds or a scoring approach are useful, and assess whether its use improves early identification of serious illness. Our hope is that integrating age-based norms and red flags into a standardized format will improve recognition of life-threatening conditions in this vulnerable population.
