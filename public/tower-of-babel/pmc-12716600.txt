PINK1 dysfunction in hepatocellular carcinoma fosters immune evasion and disease progression by promoting neutrophil infiltration
Authors: Zhiyun Gu, Tongwang Yang, Yuanli Ni, Yuanze Zhou, Cheng Qian, Na Zhuang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite breakthroughs in immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), tumor immune evasion remains a major limitation to immunotherapy efficacy. PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) has highlighted roles in immune system regulation and tumor progression, but its specific role in HCC and impact on ICB response remains unclear. This study aimed to elucidate how PINK1 dysfunction enables HCC adaptation to ICB-induced immune attacks and identify potential therapeutic targets.MethodsOnline patient dataset analyses were performed to evaluate the impact of reduced PINK1 expression on clinical prognosis in HCC. An orthotopic HCC tumor cell mouse model was established. Single-cell RNA sequencing and flow cytometry were employed to explore the immune characteristics and remodeling of the tumor microenvironment in PINK1-deficient HCC. Neutrophils and T cells were isolated, stimulated, and/or cultured for ex vivo functional assays. Gene Expression Omnibus database analyses were performed to identify key genes involved in HCC resistance to ICB.ResultsWe identified PINK1 as a key mediator of ICB resistance in HCC. We found that PINK1 expression was significantly reduced in ICB-resistant HCC and a reduced PINK1 signature was associated with poorer clinical outcomes in patients with HCC. Importantly, PINK1 deficiency serves as a predictive biomarker for diminished responsiveness to immunotherapy. Function experiments have revealed that PINK1 regulates HCC progression and infiltration and activation of neutrophils and T cells. Mechanistically, the tumor cell-intrinsic downregulation of PINK1 activated the production of chemokines involved in neutrophil recruitment, and tumor-infiltrating neutrophils inhibited T cell activity. Additionally, a neutrophil-depleting antibody resensitized tumors to antiprogrammed death-ligand 1 therapy in orthotopic HCC models, further demonstrating that neutrophils are the primary population responsible for the adaptation of cancer cells with reduced PINK1 to ICB-induced immune attacks.ConclusionsOur study uncovered PINK1 as both a predictor of the ICB response and a key mediator of immune evasion by promoting neutrophil infiltration. These results highlight that the therapeutic targeting of neutrophils may represent a viable strategy to overcome ICB resistance in HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for 75%–85% of all primary liver tumors and is currently the sixth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide.1 2 HCC responds poorly to standard chemotherapy or targeted therapy, and immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies have demonstrated strong antitumor activity in a subset of patients with HCC and have become the standard of care as a first-line treatment for advanced HCC.3 Despite this breakthrough, a significant proportion of patients with HCC do not benefit from this new standard of care because of primary or acquired resistance.4 Dysfunctional interactions between the tumor and immune system can facilitate tumor immune evasion by impairing antigen recognition and creating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).5 6 Given the dynamic nature of tumor-immune interactions, adaptive immune responses in tumors may clinically manifest as either primary or acquired resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI).7 Nevertheless, our understanding of how cancer cells adapt to ICB-induced immune attacks remains incomplete, particularly in the context of the HCC TME.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that tumor-infiltrating immune cells play crucial roles in the tumor response to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) immunotherapy. Tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) are a significant component of the TME and exhibit dual functions. In the early stages of tumor development, TANs primarily mediate antitumor effects; however, as the disease progresses, these neutrophils undergo a functional transition towards immune suppression and pro-tumor activities.810 A previous study found that increased neutrophil counts and elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios were associated with a lack of response to ICIs.8 One potential mechanism by which tumors evade the immune system is the induction of T cell exhaustion, particularly in CD8+ T cells. Research has shown that neutrophils can inhibit T cell proliferation and contribute to immune suppression by producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), nitric oxide, and arginase 2.11 12 Furthermore, recent studies have reported that neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) promote T cell exhaustion within the TME.13 However, the specific role of neutrophils in HCC immune evasion and their contribution to resistance to anti-PD-L1 therapy remains largely unclear. In this study, we aimed to gain deeper insights into how the interplay between neutrophils and T cells drives the immunosuppressive TME and contributes to resistance to immunotherapy.

As a mitochondrial serine/threonine kinase, PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) regulates various biological functions, including mitophagy, ROS production, and oxidative phosphorylation.1416 As a critical mediator of mitophagy, PINK1 plays an essential role in the onset and progression of Parkinson’s disease (PD). Accumulating evidence suggests that PINK1 is also involved in regulating the TME and immune system. Specifically, PINK1 has been shown to influence T cell metabolism and determine T cell fate.17 Furthermore, dendritic cells (DCs) rely on PINK1-mediated phosphorylation of BCKDE1α to promote fatty acid oxidation, which is vital for maintaining their immune function.18 Ziegler et al found that downregulation of PINK1 inhibits the expression of cell surface MHC I and the activation of CD8+ T cells, thereby impeding antitumor immunity.19 Evidence also indicates that PINK1 can mitigate inflammatory responses, with PINK1 deficiency leading to increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines.20 In glioblastoma, PINK1 functions as a tumor suppressor by antagonizing cell growth and the Warburg effect. Notably, overexpression of PINK1 inhibits the proliferation of glioblastoma cells harboring PTEN mutations.21 22 Additionally, elevated levels of PINK1-mediated phosphorylation of p53 contribute to the prevention of the progression of hepatic tumors from benign to malignant states.23 Conversely, PINK1 expression is elevated in certain malignant tumors, indicating potential pro-tumorigenic activity.24 25 Supporting this notion, PINK1 has been shown to activate Akt through the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 2, enhancing cell growth, cancer cell invasiveness, and stem cell renewal independently of Parkin.2628 Moreover, deletion of PINK1 reduces tumor-associated properties, such as cell proliferation, colony formation, and invasiveness, while sensitizing cancer cells to various stressors. In contrast, PINK1 overexpression promotes cancer cell survival and confers protection against chemotherapeutic agents.2931 Although PINK1 has highlighted roles in immune system regulation and tumor development, its involvement in HCC and the regulation of responses to ICIs remains largely unexplored. Thus, further investigation is needed to elucidate the mechanisms by which PINK1 influences the HCC response to ICB therapy.

In this study, we analyzed transcriptional data from anti-PD-1 sensitive and resistant tumors obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, identifying PINK1 as a crucial mediator of ICB resistance in HCC. Additionally, immunohistochemistry (IHC) results from our mouse tumor samples demonstrated that PINK1 expression was significantly reduced in ICB-resistant HCC. Our integrative in vivo and in vitro experiments revealed that PINK1 regulates HCC progression, with tumors exhibiting reduced PINK1 expression evading therapeutic pressure from PD-L1 blockade through remodeling of the immunosuppressive TME. Mechanistically, the tumor cell-intrinsic downregulation of PINK1 activates the production of chemokines involved in neutrophil recruitment, thereby promoting neutrophil expansion. We further demonstrated that neutrophils are the primary cell population that facilitates the adaptation of cancer cells with reduced PINK1 levels to ICB-induced immune attacks. Importantly, targeting neutrophils can avert ICB resistance in orthotopic HCC models. In summary, our study enhances our understanding of the role of PINK1 in cancer progression and highlights the significant contribution of neutrophils in HCC adaptation to ICB therapy.

Results

PINK1 is downregulated in clinical HCC samples, and reduced PINK1 expression is associated with worse prognosis in patients with HCC

To reveal the role of PINK1 in HCC, we first applied data from TCGA to determine the expression levels of PINK1 across various cancer types. Our analysis revealed that PINK1 expression was significantly downregulated in most tumors compared with that in normal tissues (figure 1A,B). Additionally, we compared PINK1 expression in our collected liver cancer tissues with adjacent non-cancerous tissues. In accordance with TCGA data, our results revealed that for most HCC samples, PINK1 expression was reduced at both messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein levels in HCC tissues compared with matched adjacent non-tumor tissues (n=22) (figure 1C–E). Immunohistochemical staining further revealed predominant PINK1 expression in epithelial and endothelial cells, with negligible detection in immune cells (online supplemental figure S1A). Consistent with these findings, analysis of the human dataset GSE14961432 and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from our mouse HCC model demonstrated a highly concordant cell type-specific expression pattern of PINK1. Violin plots illustrated prominent PINK1 enrichment in tumor cells and endothelial cells while its expression remained low or undetectable in other components of the TME, particularly in immune cell populations (online supplemental figure S1B,C). We also applied immunofluorescence staining to visualize the subcellular localization of PINK1 and observed strong colocalization between PINK1 and mitochondria (online supplemental figure S1D). To further investigate the clinical significance of PINK1 expression in patients with HCC, we performed Kaplan-Meier survival analyses to assess the correlation between PINK1 expression and survival in liver cancers. Specimens were classified into two groups based on PINK1 expression, revealing that reduced PINK1 expression was negatively correlated with overall survival (OS), progression-free survival, recurrence-free survival, and disease-specific survival in patients with HCC (figure 1F).

To predict the potential clinical ICB response in patients with PINK1high vs PINK1low HCC, we conducted tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) analysis, which integrates expression signatures of T cell dysfunction and T cell exclusion to predict ICB response. Our findings indicate that patients with HCC and low PINK1 expression were significantly more susceptible to ICB resistance (figure 1G). This susceptibility is evidenced by higher TIDE scores, elevated T cell exclusion, tumor-associated macrophage M2 (TAM-M2), and myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) signature scores along with lower microsatellite instability (MSI) and CD8 signature scores when compared with patients with PINK1high HCC (figure 1H). These collective findings suggest that PINK1 downregulation is common in human cancers and is associated with poor prognosis. Moreover, PINK1 may serve as a potential predictive biomarker of ICB tolerance in HCC.

Reduced PINK1 expression promotes HCC progression and TIME remodeling

To elucidate the role of PINK1 in HCC progression, we generated stable PINK1-knockdown (KD) and control sublines in mouse HCC (H22 and Hepa1-6) cells via short hairpin RNA (shRNA), and validated the KD efficiency through reverse-transcription-PCR (RT-PCR) (figure 2A and online supplemental figure S2A). For orthotopic tumor implantation, we injected PINK1-KD or control HCC cells into the left liver lobe of male mice aged 8–9 weeks. Mice were sacrificed on day 14 postimplantation, and tumor weights were measured. Tumors derived from PINK1-KD cells were significantly larger than those derived from the control cells (figure 2B and online supplemental figure S2B,C). To comprehensively explore the function of PINK1, we infected H22 and Hepa1-6 cells with PINK1-expressing lentiviruses, which resulted in high expression of PINK1 in HCC cells (figure 2C and online supplemental figure S2D). Subsequent orthotopic implantation and tumor weight measurements revealed that tumor cell-intrinsic PINK1 overexpression significantly suppressed tumorigenicity, consistent with the KD phenotype (figure 2D and online supplemental figure S2E,F).

To investigate the tumor immune microenvironment associated with PINK1 dysfunction, we leveraged scRNA-seq on H22 PINK1-KD and control tumors derived from BALB/c mice for cell type-specific profiling. After quality control, 17 408 cells with a median of 25 348 genes detected per cell were subjected to uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) dimensionality reduction and unsupervised graph-based clustering (figure 2E). We identified 14 distinct immune clusters that were further grouped into seven major cell populations, namely B cells, macrophages, DCs, monocytes, neutrophils, natural killer (NK) cells, and T cells based on their canonical marker gene expression (figure 2F). Analysis of immune cell proportions revealed that H22 PINK1-KD tumors exhibited a decreased proportion of NK and B lymphocytes and an increased proportion of T cells and neutrophils compared with control tumors (figure 2G). Taken together, these results illustrate that PINK1 expression reshapes the composition of tumor-infiltrating immune cells in the HCC microenvironment.

Tumor-infiltrating T cells exhibit an exhausted phenotype in PINK1-knockdown tumors

T cells are pivotal in the adaptive immune response and play crucial roles in tumor immune surveillance and clearance. To elucidate their intrinsic characteristics, we reclustered T cells from control and PINK1-KD tumors, and identified six distinct clusters (γδT, CD4+ naïve T cells, CD4+ T helper (Th)1, CD4+ Th17, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and CD8+ T) (figure 3A) based on key marker genes (figure 3B,C). Notably, PINK1 deficiency led to a pronounced expansion of interleukin (IL)17+ γδT cells concomitant with a reduction in CD8+ T cell infiltration, with no significant alterations in other T cell subsets (figure 3D). IL17+ γδT cells have the potential to promote tumor growth.33 34 Additionally, studies suggest that IL17+ γδT cells contribute to immunosuppression by inhibiting CD8+ T cell activity, facilitating the activation of MDSCs, or directly enhancing inflammation.35 36 To characterize the functional states of tumor-infiltrating T cells, we analyzed the expression of specific key molecules. In addition to the observed changes in T cell subset abundance following PINK1 downregulation, we observed alterations in T cell functional states. The violin plot showed that T cells in PINK1-KD tumors expressed higher levels of exhaustion markers, including CTLA4, PDCD1, LAG3, SLAMF7, and TOX, suggesting a potential drive towards irreversible T cell exhaustion. Conversely, there was a notable reduction in the expression of the antitumor immune molecule CD226, which potentially diminished antitumor immunity and sensitivity to ICB therapy (figure 3E). These findings imply that T cells within PINK1-KD tumors exhibit a predominantly high exhaustion and low effector phenotype.

PINK1 dysfunction facilitates neutrophil infiltration and enhances their T cell-suppressive activity

Neutrophils constitute a major component of the TME and play key roles in regulating tumor progression and metastasis. The scRNA-seq data indicated that PINK1 dysfunction promotes increased neutrophil infiltration. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further revealed that pathways related to neutrophil chemotaxis and migration are negatively correlated with PINK1 protein levels (figure 4A). Ligand-receptor analysis of our scRNA-seq data between tumor cells and neutrophils demonstrated heightened interactions that facilitate neutrophil chemotaxis, particularly the chemokine signaling axis formed by Cxcl1/Cxcl2-Cxcr2 exhibited significantly enhanced interaction intensity on PINK1-KD, suggesting potentiated chemotaxis of neutrophils (figure 4B). RT-quantitative PCR (qPCR) results confirmed that inhibition of PINK1 significantly upregulated Cxcl1/Cxcl2/Cxcl5/Spp1/Lgals9/Fn1 mRNA levels. Conversely, overexpression of PINK1 in HCC tumor cells significantly downregulated these neutrophil-related proteins (figure 4C,D). To quantify immune cell infiltration, we employed fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Consistent with scRNA-seq findings, PINK1-KD significantly increased the intratumoral neutrophil population (figure 4E and online supplemental figure S3A). This increase was further validated by IHC staining of the tumor tissues, which showed elevated S100A9-positive neutrophils (figure 4F). In contrast, PINK1 overexpression reduced neutrophil infiltration (figure 4G,H and online supplemental figure S3B). To investigate whether the Cxcl1/Cxcl2/Cxcl5-Cxcr2 axis mediates neutrophil recruitment on PINK1 inhibition, we conducted Cxcr2 blockade experiments. Transwell chemotaxis assays confirmed that neutrophils exhibited enhanced migration toward PINK1-KD Hepa1-6 cells compared with control cells. Importantly, blocking Cxcr2 significantly suppressed this neutrophil chemotaxis, to a greater extent in the PINK1-KD group (figure 4I), indicating that PINK1 depletion promotes neutrophil recruitment primarily through the Cxcl1/Cxcl2/Cxcl5-Cxcr2 signaling axis.

We further characterized the tumor-infiltrating neutrophils (TINs) in both PINK1-KD and control groups. Neutrophils were reclustered into five distinct subpopulations, Neu_Ccl4, Neu_Apoa2, Neu_Mmp8, Neu_Ifit1, and Neu_S100a10 (figure 5A) based on key marker genes (figure 5B). Notably, PINK1 deficiency led to an elevated proportion of the intratumoral Neu_Mmp8 subset and a reduced proportion of Neu_Ccl4 and Neu_Ifit1 subsets, with no significant changes in other subsets (figure 5C). Pseudotime analysis by CytoTRACE2 and Monocle2 revealed that Neu_mmp8 subset maintained a low-differentiation state with progenitor-like characteristics, while Neu_Ccl4 and Neu_Ifit1 subsets exhibited more advanced differentiation status (online supplemental figure S4A,B). Pathway enrichment analysis further demonstrated that both Neu_Ccl4 and Neu_Ifit1 subsets were enriched in immune-related pathways, including tumor necrosis factor signaling and Nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain(NOD)-like receptor signaling. In contrast, the Neu_Mmp8 subset was involved in NET formation (online supplemental figure S4C). Numerous studies have shown that tumor progression and metastatic dissemination are positively correlated with NETs.13 37 38 NETs contain double-stranded DNA, histones, and granule proteins, including neutrophil elastase, cathepsin G, and myeloperoxidase,39 which play an important role in inducing T cell exhaustion and dysfunction within the TME, facilitating immune escape and promoting tumor growth.40 In our scRNA-seq analysis, we generated UMAP plots to illustrate the expression of NET-related genes within the neutrophil populations. We observed that the Neu_Mmp8 subset was highly enriched for NET-related genes (figure 5D). To demonstrate the direct regulatory effect of PINK1 on neutrophils, we isolated neutrophils from the spleens of mice and co-cultured them with either PINK1-KD or control tumor cells. After 24 hours, NET-related gene expression was measured in the neutrophils. Compared with neutrophils cultured alone, those co-cultured with tumor cells exhibited elevated NET-related gene expression. Notably, co-culture with PINK1-KD tumor cells further enhanced this expression (figure 5E).

We next applied CellChat to profile intercellular communication networks between neutrophils and T cell subsets. Strikingly, the Neu_Mmp8 subset and T cell subsets demonstrated increased and stronger interactions in the PINK1-KD groups (figure 5F), suggesting a more profound regulatory effect on T cells. Conversely, interactions involving other neutrophil and T cell subsets were comparable between the two groups (online supplemental figure S5). To further validate the role of PINK1 in regulating TINs and their immunosuppressive effects on T cells, we isolated neutrophils from PINK1-KD tumors, control tumors, and spleens. These neutrophils were co-cultured with purified splenic CD8+ T cells. The co-cultures revealed that TINs were more potent than splenic neutrophils in inducing CD8+ T cell apoptosis and inhibiting T cell proliferation. Critically, PINK1-KD in tumors significantly enhanced this T cell suppression mediated by TINs (figure 5G,H). Collectively, these findings show that PINK1 regulates the immunosuppressive function of TINs. Therefore, neutrophils are likely key mediators of PINK1-dependent HCC tumor growth.

Tumor-intrinsic downregulation of PINK1 orchestrates neutrophils to resist ICB therapy

Our data from both mouse models and human samples highlight the potential role of tumor-intrinsic downregulation of PINK1 in immune evasion, an essential process for ICB resistance. To further confirm that reduced PINK1 inhibits antitumor immune responses and restricts HCC sensitivity to ICB therapy, we established luciferase-labeled PINK1-KD and control HCC cells. This enabled the real-time monitoring of tumor growth using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). We orthotopically injected these cells into the liver capsule and applied IVIS to track tumor progression from days 0 to 12 postanti-PD-L1 treatment (figure 6A). By normalizing the bioluminescent signal at each time point to the initial signal, we observed that control tumor-bearing mice displayed significantly reduced tumorigenicity on ICB treatment. In contrast, intrinsic PINK1 inhibition in tumor cells limited their response to the anti-PD-L1 therapy (figure 6B). Mice were sacrificed on day 12 postanti-PD-L1 treatment. Liver imaging showed that decreased PINK1 limited the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy in HCC, consistent with the bioluminescence imaging signals (figure 6C and online supplemental figure S6A). Quantification of total liver weight further confirmed this result (figure 6D and online supplemental figure S6B). Building on our earlier findings of increased neutrophil infiltration in PINK1-KD tumors, we investigated the functional role of neutrophils in PINK1 deficiency-induced ICB resistance. To achieve this, we treated H22 PINK1-KD tumor-bearing mice with an anti-Ly-6G antibody. Neutrophil blockade alone modestly reduced tumor growth; however, when combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy, it markedly enhanced efficacy and induced significant tumor regression (figure 6E–H). These findings suggest that adaptive downregulation of PINK1 in tumor cells orchestrates neutrophils to mediate ICB resistance.

PINK1 expression is significantly reduced in immune checkpoint blockade-resistant cancers

To further validate the clinical relevance of our findings, we analyzed the publicly available Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets GSE224237 and GSE181946. GSE224237 contains gene expression profiles of orthotopic Hepa1-6 tumors,41 whereas GSE181946 comprises the mRNA profiles of patients with HCC with progressive disease (PD-1R) and partial response (PD-1S).42 In this study, we used the full GSE181946 dataset along with transcriptome data of anti-PD-1 responsive and non-responsive groups from GSE224237 to probe the involvement of PINK1 in ICB therapeutic evasion. Volcano plot analysis identified 1216 upregulated and 2737 downregulated genes in anti-PD-1-resistant tumors from patients with HCC. In resistant orthotopic Hepa1-6 tumors, 342 genes were upregulated and 919 were downregulated (figure 7A,B). The intersection of differentially expressed genes between the two datasets yielded 134 common genes. Subsequent correlation analysis with clinical outcomes in patients with HCC revealed nine genes associated with OS and disease-free survival (DFS) (figure 7C). Online supplemental figure S7 shows the OS and DFS curves associated with the expression of these nine genes. Hierarchical clustering analysis of these nine genes across the two datasets highlighted PINK1, PHF19, GJA4, and RAPM3 as exhibiting consistent upregulation or downregulation trends (figure 7D,E). Furthermore, we predicted the immunotherapy effects of these genes using the website https://rocplot.com/immune and discovered that only reduced PINK1 expression was significantly associated with non-response to immunotherapy, which aligned with the survival outcomes and heatmap trends (figure 7F). In our ICB-resistant liver cancer mouse model, IHC and RT-PCR analyses confirmed decreased PINK1 expression in ICB-resistant HCC tumors (figure 7G,H). These collective findings support the involvement of PINK1 in therapeutic evasion of ICB.

Discussion

Despite considerable advancements in the diagnosis and treatment of HCC, its incidence and mortality rates remain alarmingly high. There is an urgent medical need to develop life-prolonging therapies. In recent years, significant breakthroughs have been achieved in tumor immunotherapy; however, their efficacy against solid tumors such as liver cancer remains limited. The emergence of tumor resistance is a critical factor that restricts the effectiveness of immunotherapy. Although considerable efforts have been made to develop combination therapies aimed at enhancing immunotherapy outcomes, the overall prognosis for patients with liver cancer remains unsatisfactory. Understanding tumor cell adaptation to ICB therapy may present new opportunities to obviate the emergence of refractory cell states and enable specific targeted agents to improve clinical outcomes for patients.40

While research on PINK1 has predominantly focused on its role in PD, it also significantly modulates various physiological and pathophysiological processes in cancer cells, including cytoplasmic homeostasis, cell survival, and cell death.43 Studies have indicated that PINK1 exhibits a context-dependent dual role in cancer biology, displaying both tumor-suppressive and oncogenic properties. For instance, PINK1 serves as a tumor suppressor by regulating the Warburg effect21 and iron-mediated immunometabolism,44 while conversely functioning as an oncogenic promoter in lung,45 breast,30 and esophageal46 cancers. These evidences verify that dysregulated PINK1 expression is associated with tumor progression, underscoring its critical involvement in multiple cancer-related processes. However, the expression pattern and function of PINK1 in HCC and its tolerance to ICB remain poorly defined. Moreover, the antagonistic duality of PINK1 in cancer biology suggests that further attention is warranted to better understand which patterns of PINK1 expression may be therapeutically beneficial for HCC.

In this study, we demonstrated that PINK1 dysfunction contributes to ICB tolerance in HCC based on transcriptomic data from anti-PD-1 responsive and resistant tumors in TCGA database, as well as our established ICB-resistant HCC xenografts. Moreover, we systematically characterized PINK1 expression in clinical HCC specimens and found that PINK1 was downregulated in primary HCC tumor tissues compared with matched adjacent tissues. We further revealed that a reduced PINK1 signature is associated with poor clinical prognosis in patients with HCC, and PINK1 inhibition predicted low responsiveness to immunotherapy in patients with cancer. Furthermore, we investigated the association between PINK1 expression and tumor response to ICB therapy, demonstrating that diminished PINK1 expression inhibits antitumor immune responses and restricts HCC sensitivity to ICB therapy. These collective findings identify PINK1 as a promising predictive biomarker for ICB resistance in HCC.

In our established HCC models with PINK1 overexpression and KD, we found that PINK1 downregulation promoted HCC progression. scRNA-seq results implied that PINK1 inhibition remodels the TME, which is characterized by reduced NK and B lymphocyte infiltration along with increased T cell and neutrophil accumulation. Consistently, qPCR indicated that chemokines that promote neutrophil recruitment were inversely correlated with PINK1 protein levels. In vivo FACS demonstrated that decreased PINK1 markedly expanded intratumoral neutrophil populations. Collectively, these findings establish TANs as active promoters of HCC progression and as key mediators of immune evasion in PINK1-dysfunctional tumors.

Apart from modulating immune cell abundance, PINK1 downregulation also alters the functional state of these immune cells. NETs drive T cell exhaustion and dysfunction within the TME to facilitate immune escape and tumor progression.13 In our study, H22 PINK1-KD tumors harbored more NET-rich neutrophils, and PINK1 inhibition enhanced the expression of NET-related genes in neutrophils. Furthermore, TINs promoted T cell apoptosis and suppressed T cell proliferation, and PINK1 deficiency significantly enhanced such T cell suppression. We also observed that T cells from H22 PINK1-KD tumors expressed higher exhaustion markers CTLA4, PDCD1, LAG3, SLAMF7, TOX, and lower antitumor immune molecule CD226, indicating terminal exhaustion with limited rejuvenation capacity. Collectively, these data demonstrate that PINK1 deficiency augments the immunosuppressive function of TINs via T cell suppression, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment in HCC. Zhu et al have shown that increased mitochondrial DNA release activates the cyclic GMP-AMP Synthase-Stimulator of Interferon Genes (cGAS-STING) signaling pathway and upregulates PD-L1.47 Accumulating evidence indicates that the cGAS-STING pathway contributes significantly to tumor immunity by sustaining type I interferon production and chronic inflammation.48 Such persistent inflammation stimulation coupled with high PD-L1 expression promotes T cell exhaustion and recruits immunosuppressive cells including MDSCs and Tregs, collectively creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment. In our study, PINK1 inhibition in Hepa1-6 cells led to impaired mitophagy, reduced mitochondrial oxygen consumption, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, and increased ROS production (online supplemental figure S8), underscoring the essential role of PINK1 in maintaining mitochondrial function in HCC. We thus propose that PINK1 deficiency causes the accumulation of damaged mitochondria and subsequent mitochondrial DNA release, which in turn activates the cGAS-STING pathway. This cascade drives chronic inflammation and facilitates the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation.

Previous studies have shown that elevated neutrophil counts and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios are correlated with resistance to ICIs.8 However, the specific contribution of neutrophils to anti-PD-L1 therapy resistance in HCC remains unclear. Our study established that PINK1 dysfunction critically mediates neutrophil recruitment to tumors and promotes their T cell-suppressive function. By treating H22 PINK1-KD tumor-bearing mice with anti-Ly-6G antibody, we found that blockade of neutrophils slightly reduced tumorigenicity and led to remarkable tumor regression when combined with anti-PD-L1 antibody, suggesting that adaptive downregulation of PINK1 in tumor cells orchestrates neutrophils to resist ICB therapy. Therefore, selectively targeting neutrophils represents an attractive strategy to overcome the immunosuppressive nature of the TME by releasing immune effector cells for tumor eradication.

In conclusion, we first established a link between PINK1 expression and immunotherapy tolerance in HCC, demonstrating a crucial role for TINs in PINK1-mediated ICB resistance. However, one limitation of our study was the orthotopic mouse model using a hepatoma cell line to test for anti-PD-L1 therapy. We recognize that the insights gained from this model may not fully recapitulate the cellular heterogeneity of the human HCC TME. Moreover, the precise role of TINs in mediating immunosuppressive activity within the context of HCC ICB tolerance warrants further investigation, which will be explored in future studies. Collectively, our findings deepen our understanding of the intrinsic mechanisms underlying resistance to ICB therapy in HCC and provide further insights into potential immunotherapy targets.

Conclusion

Our study identified PINK1 as a key mediator of immunotherapy resistance in HCC, and PINK1 expression was significantly reduced in ICB-resistant tumors. Reduced PINK1 signature correlates with poorer clinical outcomes in patients with HCC and predicts low immunotherapy responsiveness. Tumor cell-intrinsic downregulation of PINK1 activates chemokine production, which is involved in neutrophil recruitment, driving neutrophil expansion. Subsequently, TINs promoted T cell exhaustion and suppressed T cell activity, ultimately enabling cancer cells with reduced PINK1 expression to evade immune attacks induced by ICB therapy. Critically, targeting neutrophils averts ICB resistance in orthotopic HCC models, demonstrating that neutrophils are the primary population responsible for the adaptation of PINK1-deficient cancer cells to ICB-induced immune attacks.

Materials and methods

Human specimens

To analyze the expression of PINK1 in HCC, fresh HCC specimens were obtained from patients with informed consent.

Cell culture

The mouse HCC cell line H22 was purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology (Wuhan, China). The mouse HCC cell line Hepa1-6 and HEK293T cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. H22 cells were routinely cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (GIBCO-BRL, New York, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 mg/mL streptomycin (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Gaithersburg, Maryland, USA); Hepa1-6 and HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37°C. All cell lines were tested and authenticated for genotypes by DNA fingerprinting, confirmed to be mycoplasma-negative prior to experimental use and passaged for <6 months after resuscitation.

Analysis of GEO

We downloaded data from GSE149614, GSE181946, and GSE224237 from the GEO database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE149614 dataset contains single-cell transcriptome data for 10 patients with HCC. The GSE181946 dataset contains RNA sequencing data from tumor tissues of patients with HCC with PD and PR. The GSE224237 dataset comprises the gene expression profiles of orthotopic Hepa1-6 tumors. In this study, we selected all data from GSE149614 to draw violin plots and selected all data from GSE181946 and transcriptome data from the anti-PD-1-responsive group (GSM7018116, GSM7018117, and GSM7018118) as well as the non-responsive group (GSM7018113, GSM7018114, and GSM7018115) within the GSE224237 dataset to draw volcano plots and different expression heatmaps.

Expression and survival curve of PINK1 in TCGA cohort

The SRplot web resource was used to analyze clinical data from TCGA database (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/). We employed the SRplot tool to compare differences in PINK1 expression across various cancers relative to normal tissue controls. Additionally, transcriptome and survival data of HCC from TCGA were obtained from the University of California, Santa Cruz (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma(LIHC) samples were stratified into high-expression and low-expression groups according to the mRNA levels of each gene. The Surv function of the survival package was performed to assess the correlation between the expression of each candidate gene and survival rates in liver cancers, and survival curves were plotted using the ggplot2 package. Kaplan-Meier plots (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) were performed to assess the correlation between PINK1 expression and survival rates in liver cancers based on HRs and log-rank p values.

Online patient dataset analysis

To evaluate the associations between PINK1 and neutrophil chemotaxis or migration, GSEA (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea) was performed. Enrichment scores were computed with a positive or negative value to compare the enrichment level of neutrophil chemotaxis or neutrophil migration signatures between the high-expression and low-expression groups. Gene sets with a nominal p<0.05 and a false discovery rate <0.25 were considered significant. To explore the potential role of PINK1 in immunotherapy responsiveness, TIDE, T cell exclusion, MDSCs, MSI, TAM-M2, and CD8 signatures were calculated by TIDE algorithm (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) according to the developer’s instructions. The cut-off value of 0 in TIDE score was used to predict the immunotherapy response of each tumor sample. To study the correlation between PINK1 and survival in ICB-treated patients, patients were classified into high-expression and low-expression groups based on the optimal cut-off threshold for each individual ICB cohort via z-score in Cox proportional hazard model.

Flow cytometry analysis

Primary single cells were isolated from fresh tumor tissues by chopping and digesting them in type II collagenase (1000 U/mL in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), Worthington) and dispase II (11 U/mL in PBS, Gibco) at 37°C for 30 min as described previously.49 Cells were collected, washed, and then incubated with an Fcγ blocking antibody for 15 min at 4°C. For cell surface marker staining, cells were directly stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies at optimum concentration for 30 min at 4°C. The Pharmingen PE Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lake, New Jersey, USA) was used to detect cell apoptosis following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were washed three times before being subjected to flow cytometric analysis (Beckman CytoFLEX LX). Data were analyzed by CytExpert software (V.2.5.0.77, Beckman Coulter, Brea, California, USA).

RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR

RNA was extracted with a Total RNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, 1 µg of total RNA was subjected to reverse transcription to complementary DNA using a Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Takara, Beijing, China), and real‐time PCR was performed to assess the mRNA expression level of the gene of interest using TB Green PCR master mix (Takara) on a LightCycler 480 Instrument II (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). All samples were subjected to semi-quantitative analysis using the ΔΔCt method with at least three biological replicates, and mouse β-actin and human GAPDH expression were used for normalization. Primers used are listed in table 1.

Construct PINK1 overexpression and knockdown plasmids

The coding sequence of mouse PINK1 was cloned into the lentiviral vector pLVX-Puro to obtain the pLVX-PINK1-Puro plasmid. The following primers were used: PINK1-F (ATGGCGGTGCGACAGGCACT) and PINK1-R (TCATGGGGCTGCCCTCCAGG). Cell transfection, lentivirus packaging, and virus infection

Cell transfection was performed with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lentivirus packaging vectors plasmid Rous Sarcoma Virus promoter-driven Rev expression vector (pRSV-Rev), plasmid Rev Response Element (pRRE), and plasmid Vesicular Stomatitis Virus Glycoprotein expression vector (pVSVG) were co-transfected into HEK-293T cells along with PINK1 shRNA/sh-scramble or PINK1 overexpression plasmids by Calcium Phosphate Transfection Kit (CAPHOS‐1KT, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA). The culture medium of 293 T cells was collected after 48 and 72 hours, filtered with a 0.45 μm filter, and titrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lentiviruses were incubated with target cells in media supplemented with polybrene (TR‐1003, Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 hours. After transduction, puromycin at the appropriate concentration was added for selection over 3–5 days, and the transfection efficiency was determined by qPCR.

Animal studies

BALB/c and C57BL/6N mice were purchased from the Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). For the orthotopic tumor mouse model, 2×105 H22 cells resuspended in 20 μL PBS/Matrigel Basement Membrane Matrix (Corning; Cat#356234) were injected into the left liver lobe of male BALB/c mice aged 8–9 weeks as previously described.50 Hepa1-6 cells (5×106), resuspended in 25 μL PBS/Matrigel, were injected into the left liver lobe of male C57BL/6N mice aged 8–9 weeks. After 2–3 weeks, the mice were sacrificed, liver images and liver weights were recorded, and tumor tissues were collected for primary cell isolation and subsequent immune profiling analysis by multicolor flow cytometry as well as for scRNA-seq and IHC staining.

To investigate the role of tumor-intrinsic PINK1 in determining HCC ICB resistance, luciferase-labeled PINK1-KD or control HCC cells were intrahepatically injected into the liver capsule of male mice. The kinetics of tumor growth were monitored using bioluminescence in vivo imaging (IVIS Spectrum System; PerkinElmer). Mice with equivalent luciferase signals were selected and treated with anti-PD-L1 (10F.9G2, 200 μg, BioXcell) antibodies via intraperitoneal injection on days 7, 10, 13, and 16. The mice were sacrificed on day 19, and liver images and liver weights were recorded.

To investigate the role of neutrophils in PINK1 dysfunction-mediated resistance to ICB, PINK1-KD H22 cell orthotopic tumor-bearing mice were treated with anti-Ly-6G antibody (clone 1A8, 200 μg, BioXcell) alone or in combination with anti-PD-L1 antibody via intraperitoneal injection on days 7, 10, 13, and 16. The mice were euthanized on day 19 post-tumor cell inoculation, and liver image and liver weight were recorded.

To establish an anti-PD-L1-resistant HCC subcutaneous tumor model, 2×105 H22 cells were injected into either flank of the mice. When the tumor reached a size of approximately 0.5 cm in diameter, the mice were randomly divided into two groups: (i) control (isotype control antibody) and (ii) 200 μg anti-PD-L1 antibody. All animals received intraperitoneal injections of antibodies every 3 days. After 21–22 cycles of in vivo selection, tumors that displayed resistance to the anti-PD-L1 antibody were collected and referred to as anti-PD-L1-resistant tumors.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was conducted as previously described.51 Briefly, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded specimens were cut into 4 μm thick sections, followed by deparaffinization, rehydration, and blocking with 3% H2O2 for 15 min. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating tissue sections in a pressure cooker for 150 s. After cooling to room temperature, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the indicated primary antibodies. Subsequently, the sections were stained with a secondary antibody against rabbit and mouse immunoglobulins according to the kit protocol (DAKO, Copenhagen, Denmark). For visualization, 5% 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride was used as the chromogen. After washing with water, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. The sections were then dehydrated through a series of solutions: 75% ethanol, 90% ethanol, 100% ethanol, and xylene, before being mounted with DPX (Avantor). The following primary antibodies were used: PINK1 (sc-517353, Santa Cruz, 1:100) and S100A9 (#73425, CST, 1:800). In all assays, the primary antibody in the negative control slides was replaced with PBS.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

scRNA-seq data alignment and data processing

Raw reads from scRNA-seq were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) and processed using the Cell Ranger pipeline (V.4.0.0, 10x Genomics), resulting in matrices of gene counts per cell barcode. These gene-barcode matrices were further analyzed using the Seurat R package (V.3.2.3). For subsequent analyses, cells expressing at least 200 genes and genes detected in at least three cells were retained. All samples were then combined into a single Seurat object and subjected to additional filtering criteria: cells had to contain between 500 and 4000 features (nFeature-RNA), <0.2% mitochondrial gene content, and total Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) counts (nCount-RNA) ranging from 800 to 10 000. This refined dataset was used for the downstream analyses.

Clustering and dimension reduction

The filtered Seurat object was normalized and scaled, with the percentage of mitochondrial genes regressed. The top 2000 most variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function (Seurat V.3.2.3) and were subsequently used for principal component analysis. The nearest neighbors for graph-based clustering were determined using the FindNeighbors function in Seurat, and cell subtypes were defined using the FindClusters function. To eliminate batch effects, the Harmony algorithm from the Harmony R package was applied for batch correction before clustering analysis. Cells were partitioned into major cell types, including B cells, macrophages, DCs, monocytes, neutrophils, NK cells, and T cells. Then these major types were further subdivided into distinct subtypes.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes

To identify differentially expressed genes between two specific groups, the FindMarkers function in Seurat was employed. Non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were conducted to derive p values for comparisons, and Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust the p values for all genes in the dataset.

Ex vivo neutrophil and T cell functional assays

For the ex vivo neutrophil assay, naïve spleen-derived neutrophils were isolated using an antimouse LY6G MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec) and cultured with PINK1-KD or control HCC cells. After 24 hours, the expression levels of NET-related genes in neutrophils were measured by quantitative RT-PCR. Transwell migration assay (3 µm pore size, Corning) was used for neutrophil chemotaxis analysis. PINK1-KD or control Hepa1-6 cells were placed in the lower chamber, naïve spleen-derived neutrophils (with one group having the Cxcr2 blockade antibody) were incubated in the upper chamber for 12 hours. After incubation, cells were stained using crystal violet (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and the number of migrated neutrophils was assessed using ImageJ software.

For the ex vivo T cell assay, CD8+ T cells were purified from naïve spleens using antimouse CD8 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec), labeled with carboxy fluorescein succinimidyl ester, and co-cultured with neutrophils isolated from PINK1-KD or control tumors or spleens in the presence of CD3/CD28 dynabeads (Invitrogen) and mouse recombinant IL-2 (R&D) for 3 days. T cells stimulated with dynabeads served as positive controls. The proliferation and survival of T cells were determined and calculated by FlowJo software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism for Windows (V.5.04, GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). Results are presented as mean±SD. Variations between the control and treated groups were assessed using Student’s t-test. Tumor growth data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance. All experiments were repeated at least three times and p values denoted different levels of statistical significance.
