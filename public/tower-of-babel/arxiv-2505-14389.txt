Accelerating Diagonal Methods for Bilevel Optimization: Unified Convergence via Continuous-Time Dynamics
Radu I. Boţ, Enis Chenchene, E. Robert Csetnek, David A. Hulett

Abstract
We analyze fast diagonal methods for simple bilevel programs. Guided by the analysis of the corresponding continuous-time dynamics, we provide a unified convergence analysis under general geometric conditions, including Hölderian growth and the Attouch–Czarnecki condition. Our results yield explicit convergence rates and guarantee weak convergence to a solution of the bilevel problem. In particular, we improve and extend recent results on accelerated schemes, offering novel insights into the trade-offs between geometry, regularization decay, and algorithmic design. Numerical experiments illustrate the advantages of more flexible methods and support our theoretical findings.



Key Words.
bilevel optimization, Tikhonov regularization, Nesterov momentum, convergence rates, Lyapunov analysis, diagonal scheme

Introduction

Bilevel optimization is an increasingly active area in optimization that consists in finding a minimizer of an
outer
function among the minimizers of an
inner
function. It originated in economic game theory in the 50s
[
16
]
, and found enormous application in modern applied mathematics, such as in hyperparameter optimization
[
22
,
12
]
, meta-learning
[
32
]
, and reinforcement learning
[
6
]
, see
[
16
]
and the references therein for a comprehensive account.

In this paper, we consider a class of
bilevel
optimization problems, where the lower-level problem is independent of the upper-level decision variable, within the broader framework of
hierarchical optimization
that read as:

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle H(x):=h(x)+\hat{h}(x),

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle x\in\argmin_{z\in\mathcal{H}}\ F(z):=f(z)+\hat{f}(z),

where
\mathcal{H}
is a real Hilbert space,
\hat{h},\hat{f}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
are proper, convex and lower semicontinuous functions, and
h,f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
are convex and Fréchet differentiable functions with
L_{\nabla h}
- and
L_{\nabla f}
- Lipschitz continuous gradients, respectively. We further assume that
\argmin F
, the set of global minimizers of
F
, is nonempty and denote by
\min F
the infimal value of
F
. Moreover, we assume that
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{H}}H(x)>-\infty
, that the set of solutions to (
1
) is nonempty, and denote its optimal value by
\min_{\argmin F}H
.

A classical approach to tackle (
1
) is by an approximation argument, the so-called
viscosity method

[
2
,
1
]
. This method consists of replacing (
1
) with the regularized optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathcal{H}}\ F(x)+\varepsilon H(x),\quad\text{for}\ \varepsilon>0,

(2)

and recovering a solution to (
1
) by considering (weak) cluster points of minimizers of (
2
) as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This classical approach appears across a wide range of mathematical disciplines, including the calculus of variations
[
18
]
, numerical linear algebra
[
13
]
, optimal transport
[
15
]
, control theory and partial differential equations
[
14
]
, among others. Its origins trace back to the work of Tikhonov
[
37
]
in the context of ill-posed linear problems, where the regularization term takes the form
H:=\|\cdot\|^{2}
. While in the so-called
exact penalization
setting
[
19
]
, solving (
2
) for
\varepsilon
small enough actually yields solutions to (
1
), such a threshold is usually not known a-priori. Thus, successive solutions of (
2
) with
\varepsilon\equiv\varepsilon_{k}
and
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
as
k\to+\infty
are needed to approach a solution to (
1
). Quite surprisingly, it turns out that we can replace these successive minimization problems by a
single
iteration of a minimization algorithm. This defines the so-called
diagonal
or
Tikhonov
methods.

The first breakthrough in this direction was proposed by Cabot in
[
10
]
, who replaced the minimization oracle (
2
) with a proximal step on (
2
) and highlighted the role of a slow-vanishing behavior of
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Later, Solodov provided the first gradient-type algorithm
[
34
]
designed for solving (
1
) with
\hat{h}=0
and
\hat{f}=\iota_{D}
, where
D\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is a nonempty, convex and closed set and
\iota_{D}
denotes its
indicator function
. In the smooth setting, i.e.,
\hat{h}=\hat{f}=0
, these methods can be understood as a discretization of the dynamical system

\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))+\varepsilon(t)\nabla h(x(t))=0,\quad\text{for}\ t\geq t_{0},

(3)

where
t_{0}\geq 0
and
\varepsilon:[t_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a
vanishing regularization function
. Observe that indeed (
3
) can be seen as a gradient flow applied to (
2
) with a vanishing parameter function
t\mapsto\varepsilon(t)
. The convergence of (
3
) has been first established by Attouch and Czarnecki in
[
4
]
, who showed that, under a certain growth condition on
f
(Assumption
2
),
x(t)
converges weakly to an optimal solution of (
1
) with a convergence rate for
f(x(t))-\min f
expressed in terms of
\varepsilon(t)
, cf.
[
4
, Theorem 5.1]
. More recently, by replacing
\nabla f
with a general monotone and Lipschitz continuous operators and setting
h:=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
, (
3
) and some of its discretizations have gained renewed interest for their ability to yield strong convergence toward the minimal norm solution, as well as their connection to Halpern-type methods
[
33
,
24
,
39
]
, see also
[
8
,
7
]
.

Building on the work of Cabot and Solodov, Peypouquet proposed in
[
31
]
an adaptation of the Attouch–Czarnecki condition
[
4
]
for gradient systems, establishing weak convergence of the generated sequence to a solution of (
1
). In finite-dimensional settings, convergence results can still be obtained under milder assumptions, e.g., coercivity of
H
, even in the general framework of (
1
). For example,
[
25
]
, which complements and extends
[
21
]
, highlights an inherent trade-off between inner and outer function performance, similar to (
58
). Recently, backtracking strategies have been developed for functions with merely locally Lipschitz continuous gradients
[
23
]
. Additional notable extensions—without claiming to be exhaustive—include bundle methods
[
35
]
, cutting plane approaches
[
11
]
, and algorithms tailored for variational inequality inner problems
[
21
]
.

Building on this rationale, Merchav, Sabach, and Teboulle
[
26
]
were the first to observe that applying a single iteration of Nesterov’s fast gradient method—or FISTA in the composite (smooth
+
non-smooth) setting—to (
2
) can accelerate convergence, while still generating sequences that converge weakly to solutions of (
1
). In continuous time, these methods can be interpreted as discretizations of the dynamical system
[
36
]

\ddot{x}(t)+\frac{\alpha}{t}\dot{x}(t)+\nabla f(x(t))+\varepsilon(t)\nabla h(x(t))=0,\quad\text{for}\ t\geq t_{0},

(4)

where
t_{0}\geq 0
and
\alpha>3
. This system is known to provide a theoretical foundation for analyzing corresponding discrete numerical algorithms, including those involving non-smooth proximal terms. Building on the extensive body of work developed over the past decade on second-order continuous and discrete-time dynamical systems—greatly shaped by Attouch’s influential contributions—and guided by the analysis of the continuous-time systems (
3
) and (
4
), we propose two new flexible and fast numerical algorithms. Our approach allows us to i) devise more general numerical schemes than in
[
26
,
23
]
, see Algorithms
1
and
2
, and ii) significantly improve several convergence results therein, yielding, among other results, little-
o
rates and weak convergence of trajectories in a Hilbert space setting, see Section
1.1
for a complete overview. In doing so, we unveil an intriguing one-to-one correspondence on the convergence behavior of (
3
) and (
4
) and the corresponding numerical algorithms, which shows that the techniques for their analysis are fundamentally equivalent.

1.2
Relation to previous work

Accompanied with Section
4
, our comprehensive analysis complements and improves the recent works
[
25
,
23
,
26
]
. First, all results are formulated in a infinite dimensional Hilbert space setting, in line with
[
31
,
4
]
, where the case
\eta=1
and
\hat{h}=\hat{f}=0
is analyzed with a general regularization sequence/parameter. However, only weak convergence to an optimal solution of (
1
) is provided (in both continuous and discrete time), along with a convergence rate for the inner function (in continuous time only).

In the case
\eta=1
, that is, for Algorithm
1
—which coincides with the proximal-gradient method introduced in
[
23
]
—we provide a convergence analysis by choosing the regularization sequence
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
as in (
5
) and making the geometric assumptions of Figure
1
, but allowing larger step sizes. As discussed in Section
4
, our analysis can be extended to accommodate milder assumptions—such as the coercivity of
F
or
H
—and more general parameter choices. However, without stronger assumptions, convergence rates are typically only available in the
ergodic
or
best-iterate
sense, and convergence to solutions to (
1
) can generally only be characterized by the vanishing distance to the solution set of (
1
), and stated in finite-dimensional settings; see Section
4
for further details. We obtain best-iterate results similar to
[
25
, Theorem 3]
, but assuming coercivity on either function
F
or
H
instead of
H
, and for the method in Algorithm
1
, instead of the iterative scheme in
[
25
]
. Furthermore, our analysis can be extended to accommodate general parameter sequences (see Section
4
). However, we find little motivation to do so, and numerical results—e.g. those reported in
[
8
]
—seem to indicate that polynomial choices tend to give the best performance in Tikhonov methods.

In the case
\eta=2
, that is, for Algorithm
2
, the only method available for a fair comparison is that of
[
26
]
. Our results substantially extend those in
[
26
]
in several directions. First, we introduce a more general algorithmic framework. Second, we allow for
\rho
-Hölderian growth with any
\rho\in(1,2]
, rather than restricting to the quadratic case
\rho=2
. Third, our analysis is carried out in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space setting. Fourth, for
\delta\in(\frac{2}{\rho^{\star}},2)
, we refine the convergence rates established in
[
26
]
, improving the simultaneous bounds from
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
and
\mathcal{O}(k^{\delta-2})
to

and

as

, respectively, by building on techniques from
[
3
]
. In addition, we complement the results of
[
26
]
by treating the case
\delta=2
under the Attouch–Czarnecki assumption, which is weaker than the Hölderian growth condition, and by establishing weak convergence of the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in this setting. Finally, our analysis departs fundamentally from prior work in that all results are derived from the dissipation of a single energy functional—the so-called
Lyapunov function
.

We also emphasize that each of our results admits a continuous-time counterpart, presented in Appendix
B
, which in fact guided the convergence analysis of the corresponding numerical methods.

1.3
Preliminaries and notation

For a proper function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
we denote by
\dom f:=\{x\in\mathcal{H}\ :\ f(x)<+\infty\}
its
domain
, by
\min f
its
infimal value
, and by
\argmin f:=\{x\in\mathcal{H}\ :\ f(x)=\min f\}
its
set of minimizers
. For a proper, convex and lower semicontinuous function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
we denote by
f^{*}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
its
Fenchel–Moreau conjugate
, defined by
f^{*}(z):=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{H}}\{\langle x,z\rangle-f(x)\}
. The
proximity operator
of
f
with modulus
s>0
is defined for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
by

\prox_{sf}(x):=\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{H}}\Big\{f(y)+\frac{1}{2s}\|y-x\|^{2}\Big\}.

If
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and Fréchet differentiable with a
L_{\nabla f}
-Lipschitz continuous gradient, we say that
f
is
L
-smooth
. We denote by
\iota_{C}
the indicator function associated to a nonempty, convex and closed set
C\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, by
\sigma_{C}:=\iota_{C}^{*}
the support function of the set
C
, and by
N_{C}:=\partial\iota_{C}
its normal cone.

In our geometric setting, we assume Hölderian error bound for the inner function, and a weaker summability condition due to Attouch and Czarnecki in
[
4
]
. We will now present both assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Hölderian growth condition)
.

A proper, convex and lower semicontinuous function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
with
\argmin f\neq\emptyset
is said to satisfy a
Hölderian growth condition with exponent
\rho\in(1,2]
if for some
\tau>0
we have

\tau\rho^{-1}\dist(x,\argmin f)^{\rho}\leq f(x)-\min f\quad\text{for all}\ x\in\mathcal{H}.

(8)

Assumption 2

(Attouch and Czarnecki,
[
4
]
)
.

A proper, convex and lower semicontinuous function
f:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
with
\argmin f\neq\emptyset
is said to satisfy the
Attouch–Czarnecki condition
with respect to
\eta\in\{1,2\}
if for any solution
x^{*}
to the bilevel problem (
1
), for all
p\in N_{\argmin f}(x^{*})
, and
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\geq 0}
defined as in (
5
) we have that

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}k^{\eta-1}\bigg[(f-\min f)^{*}\left(\varepsilon_{k}p\right)-\sigma_{\argmin f}\left(\varepsilon_{k}p\right)\bigg]<+\infty.

(9)

The Attouch–Czarnecki condition is known to be weaker than the Hölderian growth condition, since, whenever
f
satisfies Assumption
1
,

0\leq(f-\min f)^{*}(z)-\sigma_{\argmin f}(z)\leq\tau^{1-\rho^{\star}}\frac{1}{\rho^{\star}}\|z\|^{\rho^{\star}}\quad\text{for all}\ z\in\mathcal{H},

(10)

where the first inequality is a consequence of the Young–Fenchel inequality, and the second can be derived as in
[
31
, Section 3.2]
. Here, the dual conjugate exponent
\rho^{\star}
is given by

\frac{1}{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{\star}}=1.

(11)

Therefore, as
\varepsilon_{k}\simeq k^{-\delta}
, Assumption
1
implies Assumption
2
when
\frac{\eta}{\rho^{\star}}<\delta
. A further mild technical assumption we will require is a qualification condition.

Assumption 3

(Qualification condition)
.

The functions
H
and
F
in (
1
) are such that

\partial(H+\iota_{\argmin F})(x^{*})=\partial H(x^{*})+N_{\argmin F}(x^{*})\quad\text{for any solution}\ x^{*}\ \text{to \eqref{eq:bilevel_problem}}.

(12)

The qualification condition is known to be fulfilled if either there exists
x^{\prime}\in\dom H\cap\argmin F
such that
H
is continuous at
x^{\prime}
or if
0\in\text{int}(\dom H-\argmin F)
. This assumption proves to be crucial for controlling the (sign-less) residual of (
2
) with
\varepsilon>0
in terms of its value at an optimal solution to (
1
). Indeed, picking
p^{*}\in N_{\argmin F}(x^{*})
such that
-p^{*}\in\partial H(x^{*})
given by Assumption
3
, we get that for all
x\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle-\Bigl[F(x)-F(x^{*})+\varepsilon(H(x)-H(x^{*}))\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq-(F(x)-\min F)+\varepsilon\langle p^{*},x-x^{*}\rangle

(13)

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\langle\varepsilon p^{*},x\rangle-(F-\min F)(x)\Bigr]-\langle\varepsilon p^{*},x^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq(F-\min F)^{*}(\varepsilon p^{*})-\sigma_{\argmin F}(\varepsilon p^{*}),

where we used
\langle\varepsilon p^{*},x^{*}\rangle=\sigma_{\argmin F}(\varepsilon p^{*})
. Although the Attouch–Czarnecki condition in Assumption
2
might appear abstract, (
13
) shows that it comes naturally in the analysis of Tikhonov-type methods.

In this section, we present our numerical experiments, which are performed in Python on a 12thGen. Intel(R) Core(TM) i7–1255U, 1.70–4.70 GHz laptop with 16 Gb of RAM and are available for reproducibility at
https://github.com/echnen/fast-bilevel-methods
.

5.1
Considered algorithms

To begin, we briefly present the numerical algorithms utilized for comparison to test the performance of Algorithms
1
and
2
.
To the best of our knowledge, these are the only approaches that can cope with the generality of (
1
), so we refrain from discussing other variants requiring, e.g., strong convexity of the outer function.

Fast Bi-level Proximal Gradient.

The
Fast Bi-level Proximal Gradient method
(FBi-PG) was recently introduced by Merchav, Sabach and Teboulle in
[
26
]
. It can be understood a special case of Algorithm
2
with
\gamma=\alpha-1
,
c=1
, and
\beta=\gamma
. Specifically, although the method is introduced in
[
26
, (2.4)–(2.6)]
with
t_{k}
picked as in the original Nesterov’s method, the convergence results are only stated with
t_{k}:=\frac{k+a}{a}
for some
a\geq 2
. This choice yields a momentum parameter
\alpha_{k}:=t_{k}^{-1}(t_{k-1}-1)
that can be obtained from our general choice in Algorithm
2
by setting
\gamma=\alpha-1
and
a:=\alpha-1
. Being a special case of Algorithm
2
, its convergence properties are specified in Theorem
3.3
.

Static Bilevel Method.

The
Static Bilevel Method
(staBiM) was introduced by Latafat, Themelis, Villa and Patrinos in
[
23
]
. While the method is designed for an arbitrary slowly vanishing, i.e., not summable, regularization sequence
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, in these experiments we always consider
\varepsilon_{k}
as in (
5
) with
\delta\in(0,1)
. For a fixed
\tilde{\theta}\in(0,1)
and
\eta_{0}>0
, the method iterates for all
k\geq 0

x_{k+1}:=\prox_{\theta_{k+1}(\hat{f}+\varepsilon_{k}\hat{h})}\big(x_{k}-\theta_{k+1}(\nabla f(x_{k})+\varepsilon_{k}\nabla h(x_{k}))\big),

(62)

where
(\theta_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is the sequence defined by
\theta_{k+1}:=\frac{\tilde{\theta}}{\eta_{k+1}L_{\nabla h}+L_{\nabla f}}
, where
\eta_{k+1}
can be picked arbitrarily in
\big[\frac{3}{4}\eta_{k},\eta_{k}\big]
for all
k\geq 0
. Observe the similarity with Algorithm
1
: While allowing for general regularization sequences
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, and originally designed in the more general context of locally Lipschitz gradients, it requires a non-stationary step size
\theta_{k}
that, picking
\eta_{k+1}=\frac{3}{4}\eta_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, asymptotically behaves like
\theta_{k}\simeq\frac{\tilde{\theta}}{L_{\nabla f}}
with
\tilde{\theta}\in(0,1)
. In contrast, our analysis suggests that indeed, we can choose i) stationary and ii) twice larger step sizes as
\theta_{k}=\frac{\tilde{\theta}}{L_{\nabla f}}
and
\tilde{\theta}\in(0,2)
for all
k\geq 1
.

Bi-Sub-Gradient method.

The
Bi-Sub-Gradient method
in its two different variants was introduced by Merchav and Sabach in
[
25
]
. In this paper, we focus on the so-called Variant II, (Bi-SG-II), which, for
\varepsilon_{k}:=\frac{c}{(k+1)^{\delta}}
,
\delta\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
,
c\leq\min\{\frac{1}{L_{\nabla h}},1\}
, and
\theta\leq\frac{1}{L_{\nabla f}}
, iterates for all
k\geq 0

\left\{\begin{aligned} &y_{k+1}:=\prox_{\theta\hat{f}}(x_{k}-\theta\nabla f(x_{k})),\\
&x_{k+1}:=\prox_{\theta\varepsilon_{k}\hat{h}}(y_{k+1}-\theta\varepsilon_{k}\nabla h(y_{k+1})).\end{aligned}\right.

(63)

The method alternates a proximal-gradient step in the inner problem (with step size
\theta
) and in the outer problem (with step size
\theta\varepsilon_{k}
). The benefit of this formulation lies in the fact that it does not require computing the proximity operator of
\theta\hat{f}+\theta\varepsilon_{k}\hat{h}
, which for staBiM and Algorithm
1
requires a product-space trick as suggested in
[
17
]
.

5.2
Linear inverse problem with simulated data

To test the dependency of the proposed method on the algorithm’s parameter, we consider the following bilevel problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \|x-\hat{x}\|_{1}\quad\text{subject to}\ x\in\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(z)\,,

(64)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the quadratic function defined, for
1<J<d
, by

f(x):=\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}-1)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=2}^{J}(x_{j-1}-x_{j})^{2},\quad\text{for}\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(65)

and
\hat{x}:=(50,\dots,50)
. The inner function (
65
) is a standard benchmark to test first-order methods, usually revealing their sublinear rates of convergence
[
27
]
. In particular, it is not strongly convex, but satisfies Assumption
1
with
\rho=2
. Since
J<d
, the set of minimizers of
f
is
\argmin f=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ :\ x_{1}=\dots=x_{J}=1\}
and thus the unique solution to (
64
) is
x^{*}:=(1,\dots,1,50,\dots,50)
.

In our first experiment, we compare the performance of Algorithms
1
and
2
with
\beta=10
,
\gamma=20
,
c=10
, and
\alpha=4
against the methods described in Subsection
5.1
. For fair comparison, for each of them we set
95\%
of the largest possible step size, and consider the same initial point
x^{0}:=0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, we let the algorithms run for
10^{4}
iterations with
20
different choices of
\delta\in(1,2)
, sampled randomly—for Algorithm
1
, staBiM and Bi-SG-II we use
\delta/2
instead of
\delta
to ensure convergence. We measure inner and outer residual values and show the results in Figure
2
.

Although the objective of this paper is not to compete with existing algorithms, we can observe that the improved flexibility of Algorithm
2
plays a crucial role in performance, yielding results that clearly surpass those of FBi-PG. In this example, specifically from Figure

, we can also observe that the first-order methods Algorithm
1
, staBiM and Bi-SG-II may indeed only achieve the rate

on the inner objective function decrease. On the other hand, the two second-order methods employed, i.e., Algorithm
2
and FBi-PG, show a much faster convergence behavior. Further, Figure

confirms the inherent trade-off between inner and outer function performance as

Logistic regression

In this experiment, we consider a copy of the UCI ML Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic) dataset
[
38
]
freely available online
[

]
. It contains

30

y_{i}=1
or malign
y_{i}=0

a\in\mathbb{R}^{30}
yields
1
if
\mathfrak{s}(\hat{a}^{T}x)>\frac{1}{2}
and
0
else, where
\hat{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the lifted feature vector of
a
that now belongs to a
d=5456

\ell^{1}
norm by solving the following bilevel problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \|x\|_{1}\quad\text{subject to}\ x\in\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(y_{i}\log(\mathfrak{s}(\hat{a}_{i}^{T}z))+(1-y_{i})\log(1-\mathfrak{s}(\hat{a}_{i}^{T}z))\bigg).

To train the model, we run the three numerical algorithms presented in Subsection
5.1
and Algorithms
1
and
2
for
5\cdot 10^{4}
iterations with the
\delta=1.9
for the second-order methods, and
\delta/2=0.95
for the first-order ones. Specifically, for Algorithm
2
we set
\gamma=\beta=1
,
\alpha=4
,
c=10^{2}
,
s=\frac{0.95}{L_{\nabla f}}
. For FBi-PG,
\alpha=4
, and
s=\frac{0.95}{L_{\nabla f}}
. For Algorithm
1
we set
c=10^{2}
,
s=\frac{1.95}{L_{\nabla f}}
and
\beta=\gamma=1
, while for staBiM
\gamma=1
,
c=10^{2}
and for Bi-SG-II,
s=\frac{1}{L_{\nabla f}}
, and
c=10^{2}
. Along the iterations, we measure the inner and outer objective values and the norm of the difference of consecutive iterates and show the results in Figure

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

The following straightforward result encodes an important inequality that is contained, e.g., in
[
25
]
, and is used repeatedly in our paper.

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
3
hold. Then for any solution
x^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
to (
1
), and
p^{*}\in N_{\argmin F}(x^{*})
such that
-p^{*}\in\partial H(x^{*})
it holds

H(x^{*})-H(x)\leq\|p^{*}\|\left(\frac{F(x)-F(x^{*})}{\tau\rho^{-1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho}}\quad\text{for all}\ x\in\mathcal{H}.

(67)

Proof.

Using the definitions of the convex subdifferential and of the normal cone, for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
it holds

\displaystyle H(x^{*})-H(x)

\displaystyle\leq-\langle p^{*},x^{*}-x\rangle\leq-\bigl\langle p^{*},P_{\argmin F}(x)-x\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|p^{*}\|\bigl\|P_{\argmin F}(x)-x\bigr\|=\|p^{*}\|\dist(x,\argmin F)\leq\|p^{*}\|\left(\frac{F(x)-F(x^{*})}{\tau\rho^{-1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho}},

(68)

which yields the claim.
∎

For the proof of the following classical lemma, see, e.g.,
[
36
]
.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\hat{\Phi}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, convex and lower semicontinuous function and
{\Phi}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
L_{\nabla\Phi}
-smooth function. For
s>0
and
y\in\mathcal{H}
, define

G(y):=\frac{1}{s}\Bigl[y-\prox_{s\hat{\Phi}}(y-s\nabla\Phi(y))\Bigr].

(69)

Set
\Psi:=\Phi+\hat{\Phi}
. Then, for all
x,y\in\mathcal{H}
it holds

\Psi(y-sG(y))\leq\Psi(x)-\frac{s}{2}(2-sL_{\nabla\Phi})\|G(y)\|^{2}+\langle G(y),y-x\rangle-\frac{1}{2L_{\nabla\Phi}}\|\nabla\Phi(x)-\Phi(y)\|^{2}.

(70)

The following result follow from simple integral estimates.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, for all
k_{0}\geq 2
and all
k\geq k_{0}
it holds

•

If
r<0
, then
\sum_{\ell=k_{0}}^{k}\frac{1}{\ell^{r}}\in\left[\frac{1}{1-r}\Bigl(k^{1-r}-(k_{0}-1)^{1-r}\Bigr),\frac{1}{1-r}\Big((k+1)^{1-r}-k_{0}^{1-r}\Bigr)\right].

•

If
r=1
, then
\sum_{\ell=k_{0}}^{k}\frac{1}{\ell}\in\Bigl[\ln(k+1)-\ln(k_{0}),\ln(k)-\ln(k_{0}-1)\Bigr].

•

If
r\geq 0
and
r\neq 1
, then
\sum_{\ell=k_{0}}^{k}\frac{1}{\ell^{r}}\in\left[\frac{1}{1-r}\left(\frac{1}{(k+1)^{r-1}}-\frac{1}{k_{0}^{r-1}}\right),\frac{1}{1-r}\left(\frac{1}{k^{r-1}}-\frac{1}{(k_{0}-1)^{r-1}}\right)\right].

•

In particular, if
1-r>0
, then
\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\ell^{r}}\leq\frac{1}{1-r}\Bigl[(k+1)^{1-r}-(k_{0}-1)^{1-r}\Bigr].

A special case (i.e., when
t_{k}=k
) of the following lemma can be found in the literature, for example, in
[
9
, Lemma A.5]
. However, that version requires the sequence
(\varphi_{k})_{k\geq 0}
to be bounded, which we cannot assume. This is important, since when applying this lemma we do not know beforehand if
(\varphi_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is bounded or not.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
(\varphi_{k})_{k\geq 0},(t_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be sequences of real and positive numbers respectively, and such that
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{t_{k}}=+\infty
. Then,

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\left(\varphi_{k+1}+t_{k}(\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k})\right)=L\in\mathbb{R}\quad\implies\quad\lim_{k\to+\infty}\varphi_{k}=L.

(71)

Proof.

For all
k\geq 0
, denote
\ell_{k}:=\varphi_{k+1}+t_{k}(\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k})
and suppose that
\ell_{k}\to L
as
k\to+\infty
. Consider the positive sequence
(\mu_{k})_{k\geq 0}
defined recursively as

\mu_{0}:=\frac{1}{t_{0}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mu_{k+1}:=\frac{\mu_{k}(1+t_{k})}{t_{k+1}}\quad\text{for all}\ k\geq 0.

(72)

We denote by
P_{k}:=\mu_{k}t_{k}
for all
k\geq 0
. Then,
P_{0}=1
and for all
k\geq 0

P_{k}>0,\quad\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mu_{i}=P_{k+1}-1\quad\text{and}\quad P_{k+1}=\prod_{j=0}^{k}\bigg(\frac{1}{t_{j}}+1\bigg)\geq 1+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{1}{t_{j}},\quad\text{thus}\quad P_{k}\to+\infty,

(73)

where the first three statements follow from (
72
), and the fourth statement from the summability assumption on
(t_{k})_{k\geq 0}
. Multiplying
\ell_{k}
by
\mu_{k}
yields

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}t_{k+1}\varphi_{k+1}-\mu_{k}t_{k}\varphi_{k}

\displaystyle=(\mu_{k+1}t_{k+1}-\mu_{k}t_{k})\varphi_{k+1}+\mu_{k}t_{k}(\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k})

(74)

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}\varphi_{k+1}+\mu_{k}t_{k}(\varphi_{k+1}-\varphi_{k})=\mu_{k}\ell_{k}.

Therefore, by summing (
74
) from
0
to
k
, we obtain for all
k\geq 0

\varphi_{k+1}=\frac{\varphi_{0}}{P_{k+1}}+\frac{1}{P_{k+1}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mu_{i}\ell_{i}=\frac{\varphi_{0}}{P_{k+1}}+\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mu_{i}\ell_{i}}{\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mu_{i}+1}.

(75)

In light of (
73
), applying the Stolz–Cesáro Theorem leads to
\varphi_{k}\to L
as
k\to+\infty
.
∎

.
