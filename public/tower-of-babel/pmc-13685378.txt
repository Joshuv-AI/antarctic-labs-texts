S. carcinoma arising in a breast implant: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Gustavo Adolfo Villegas-Gomez, Valentina Pérez-Ariza, Marcela Mejía-Arango, María Isabel Rizo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast implants are widely used in plastic surgery and generally considered safe. However, recent reports of implant-associated malignancies, including anaplastic large-cell lymphoma and squamous-cell carcinoma, have raised concern. This report presents a case of squamous-cell carcinoma arising in a silicone breast implant and reviews the current literature on its association with breast implants.Case presentationA 48-year-old Hispanic female presented with pain and asymmetry of the left breast. Persistent symptoms and imaging findings led to the identification of a cystic mass associated with a silicone implant. Histopathological analysis confirmed squamous-cell carcinoma arising from the periprosthetic capsule. The patient underwent implant removal, and after radiologic surveillance and diagnosis of recurrence, an attempted oncoplastic approach was converted intraoperatively to a radical mastectomy due to extensive tumor infiltration. No adjuvant therapy was administered. At 12-month follow-up, the patient remains disease-free without evidence of recurrence.ConclusionsThe diagnosis and management of breast-implant-associated squamous-cell carcinoma require a multidisciplinary approach due to its unpredictable behavior. Increased awareness, early detection, and individualized treatment strategies are essential to improve outcomes in this rare but aggressive entity.

Background

Breast reconstruction and augmentation with implants are among the most common plastic surgery procedures, exceeding one million cases annually worldwide [1, 2]. Breast implants are categorized as silicone gel or saline-filled, with silicone-based implants being the most frequently used [3]. These procedures are generally considered low risk, with capsular contracture (fibrous scar tissue tightening around the implant) and implant rupture/leakage being the most common complications [1, 4].

Although breast implants have not been linked to an increased risk of primary breast cancer, [1, 5] implant-associated malignancies, such as anaplastic large-cell lymphoma (ALCL) and squamous-cell carcinoma (SCC), have raised concern [2, 6]. ALCL, a T-cell lymphoma, was first reported in 1997 and recognized by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2011 as a rare risk associated with textured implants [3, 5]. More recently, in September 2022, the FDA issued a safety communication warning to healthcare professionals and patients about SCC cases arising within the capsule surrounding breast implants [7]. Primary squamous-cell carcinoma of the breast is exceptionally rare, accounting for approximately 0.04–0.1% of all breast malignancies [8–10]. Its diagnosis requires exclusion of primary skin SCC, metastases from extramammary sites, and metaplastic breast carcinoma [8].

Reports indicate that SCC can develop in association with both silicone and saline implants, often presenting as an aggressive disease with poor prognosis [8]. Implant-associated SCC remains poorly understood, with limited data available regarding its clinical behavior, diagnostic challenges, and optimal management strategies. To date, approximately 20 cases of implant-capsule-associated SCC have been documented in the plastic surgery literature [7]. Of these cases reported, 12 involved silicone implants. Therefore, our aim is to report a case of a 48-year-old female patient with SCC arising in the setting of a silicone implant, managed with radical surgery alone and achieving short-term disease-free survival. This case adds to the limited literature on breast-implant-associated SCC (BIA-SCC) and highlights the importance of early multidisciplinary evaluation, even in the absence of adjuvant therapy.

Case presentation

In November 2022, a 48-year-old Hispanic woman (weight 62 kg, height 1.59 m, body mass index [BMI] 24.52) presented with left breast pain and asymmetry (Fig. 1A). Her past medical history was unremarkable, with no chronic diseases, prior malignancies, or autoimmune conditions. She denied a family history of breast cancer, implant-associated malignancies, or other hereditary cancer syndromes. Breast ultrasound (US) revealed implant malposition and a delayed periprosthetic seroma, an accumulation of serous fluid in the periprosthetic space that, although rare, may be associated with implant-related malignancies. A complex cystic mass measuring 62 × 56 mm (estimated volume 72 cc) was identified in the upper inner quadrant of the left breast, containing multiple mural nodules and located near the pectoralis major muscle. Suspicious lymphadenopathy was also observed in the left internal mammary chain (19 × 12 mm), leading to a Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) 4C classification. Subsequent breast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Fig. 1B) confirmed a multilobulated cystic lesion with mural nodularity and left internal mammary lymphadenopathy, supporting a revised BI-RADS 5 classification due to the high suspicion of malignancy.

Fine-needle aspiration and immunohistochemistry were conducted to rule out anaplastic large-cell lymphoma (ALCL), with cytology of the periprosthetic fluid returning negative for ALCL. The patient subsequently underwent explantation of the breast implant, and a histopathological examination of the capsule was performed. As detailed in Fig. 2, histopathological examination revealed a continuous morphological spectrum comprising four key stages: (1) areas of squamous metaplasia without dysplasia, characterized by epithelial stratification and chronic stromal inflammation; (2) progression to moderate and severe epithelial dysplasia, showing architectural disarray, nuclear atypia, and loss of polarity; (3) epithelial protrusion into the stroma without evidence of stromal invasion, consistent with advanced intraepithelial neoplasia; and (4) invasive well-differentiated squamous-cell carcinoma, forming keratinizing nests of atypical cells with parakeratosis, prominent nucleoli, and intercellular bridges infiltrating the adjacent adipose tissue. These findings demonstrate a histologic continuum from benign metaplastic change to overt malignancy, underscoring the stepwise transformation driven by chronic inflammation within the implant capsule. Following implant removal and confirmation of SCC, the patient was placed under close clinical and radiological surveillance. At that time, there were no signs of residual disease or metastasis, and given the apparent complete resection and lack of alarming imaging findings, no immediate additional treatment was initiated. However, during the ensuing months, the patient experienced recurrent enlargement of the left breast. Repeat imaging studies and a core needle biopsy revealed a highly infiltrative SCC with a cystic component, suggesting local progression. An axillary lymph node biopsy was negative for malignancy.

Given these findings, a second surgical procedure was indicated. Initially, an immediate reconstruction using an oncologic mammoplasty technique was planned on the basis of preoperative imaging and the patient’s breast anatomy. However, intraoperative exploration revealed a large, infiltrative mass involving the pectoralis major muscle, findings that were not apparent in prior imaging studies. Due to the unexpected extent of disease, the surgical team proceeded with a radical mastectomy to ensure complete oncologic resection with clear margins, prioritizing oncologic safety over breast conservation.

The delay between implant removal and definitive surgery was due to the need for expert pathological review and further diagnostic evaluation, including high-resolution breast MRI, to assess residual disease. Given the diagnostic complexity and rarity of BIA-SCC, this multidisciplinary reassessment was crucial to guide appropriate surgical planning.

At the 10-day postoperative follow-up, the patient demonstrated adequate wound healing with a functioning vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) system and no complications. She was subsequently scheduled for delayed breast reconstruction with a microvascular flap from the femoral region, in collaboration with the plastic surgery team. Since then, she has remained under consistent clinical and radiological follow-up and, at 12 months after the operation, shows no evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis.

Discussion

BIA-SCC is an exceptionally rare malignancy with poor prognosis and limited understanding due to data scarcity. Primary squamous-cell carcinoma of the breast itself accounts for less than 0.1% of all breast cancers [11], and its association with breast implants is even more uncommon, with about 20 documented cases in the literature (Table 1). Pathophysiology

BIA-SCC is thought to arise from a prolonged process of chronic inflammation and irritation, leading to malignant transformation of epithelial cells [2]. While its precise histogenesis remains unclear, one hypothesis suggests that silicone implants may induce squamous metaplasia of mammary duct epithelium, which, under persistent inflammatory conditions, progresses to dysplasia and eventually carcinoma [6]. This pathological sequence resembles Marjolin’s ulcer, a malignancy that arises in chronically inflamed or injured tissue and may evolve over a period of 10–20 years, mirroring the median latency between breast augmentation and SCC diagnosis [23].

Chronic inflammation appears to be the central driver of BIA-SCC, potentially exacerbated by foreign body irritation, antigenic stimulation, micro-leaks from the implant, and chronic T-cell activation [12]. Other contributing factors may include genetic susceptibility and bacterial biofilm growth [13, 24, 25]. Additionally, the presence of microscopic epithelial fragments within the implant capsule could facilitate metaplasia and malignant transformation [13].

Interestingly, many reported cases involve intact implants, challenging the assumption that implant rupture is a prerequisite for cellular inflammation and carcinogenesis [24]. A notable finding in our case was the simultaneous presence of squamous metaplasia, dysplasia, and invasive carcinoma within the same capsule. Although metaplasia, dysplasia, and invasive carcinoma represent sequential stages of squamous malignant transformation, they may coexist within the same lesion when evaluated histologically [26]. In chronic inflammatory environments such as breast implant capsules, epithelial changes frequently develop in a field-effect pattern, whereby areas of benign squamous metaplasia progressively evolve into dysplasia and ultimately invasive carcinoma. This results in a heterogeneous lesion in which multiple phases of transformation are present simultaneously [27]. As in the progressive changes described in chronic-inflammation-associated malignancies, including Marjolin’s ulcers, this process supports a continuum of epithelial alteration rather than an abrupt malignant shift [23]. Consistent with previous reports of breast-implant-associated squamous-cell carcinoma, our specimen demonstrated adjacent zones of benign metaplasia, high-grade dysplasia, and invasive squamous carcinoma infiltrating the stroma, reinforcing the concept that BIA-SCC may arise through a gradual chronic-inflammation-driven pathway [1, 6, 12, 17, 18].

Immunohistochemical analysis (Fig. 3) further supported this inflammation-driven, stepwise transformation. Areas of metaplastic epithelium without atypia exhibited wild-type p53 expression restricted to basal cells and low Ki-67 proliferation indices. In contrast, dysplastic regions showed diffuse nuclear p53 overexpression and markedly increased Ki-67 positivity across all epithelial layers. These molecular findings align with the concept that progressive genetic dysregulation and proliferative activation accompany the histologic continuum from metaplasia to dysplasia and invasive carcinoma, reinforcing the role of chronic inflammation and p53 pathway disruption in the pathogenesis of BIA-SCC.

Clinical features

A systematic review by Niraula analyzed 19 cases of BIA-SCC, revealing a median age of 52 years at diagnosis (interquartile range [IQR] 46–60) and a strong female predominance (94.7%) [6, 24] The most common presenting symptoms were swelling (84.2%), pain (73.7%), and erythema (21.1%)[6, 24]. Notably, 73.7% of cases occurred in patients who had undergone breast augmentation for cosmetic reasons, suggesting a potential association between aesthetic procedures and BIA-SCC development [6, 24].

Regarding implant characteristics, 58.8% of cases involved silicone-based implants, with 42.9% of these having a textured surface [2, 6, 11]. The median latency period from implantation to SCC diagnosis was 20 years (IQR 15–35), emphasizing the prolonged disease course and the need for long-term surveillance [2] Histopathological confirmation of BIA-SCC is typically obtained after surgical excision, with most cases exhibiting extracapsular spread at diagnosis, reinforcing the aggressive nature of the disease [2].

Our case closely mirrors these findings: the patient was 48 years old, slightly below the median age reported in the literature; the patient had her most recent implant exchange approximately 5 years before symptom onset, which is slightly shorter than the median latency reported in the literature but still within the range of previously described revision-related cases. Her original implants were placed in 2006, suggesting a total implant exposure of 16 years. Her initial symptoms included pain and breast asymmetry, consistent with the most frequently reported clinical presentations. Additionally, the characteristics of the implant, being silicone-based, are consistent with the reported literature, as this type of implant has shown a greater incidence of SCC. These similarities support the notion of a consistent clinical pattern in BIA-SCC and reinforce the need for clinical suspicion in patients presenting with late-onset symptoms long after implantation.

Diagnostic work-up

The diagnosis of BIA-SCC requires a comprehensive approach integrating imaging, cytology, and histopathology. Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) of periprosthetic fluid typically reveals a thick, cloudy, white effusion containing sheets of atypical keratin-producing cells expressing p63+ and CK5/6+ (immunohistochemical markers commonly expressed in squamous epithelium and used to support the diagnosis of squamous-cell carcinoma) [24]. However, peri-implant effusions are not usually detected on imaging, and no cases of sentinel lymph node involvement have been reported, underscoring the uniqueness of BIA-SCC’s presentation [14].

In our case, the patient initially demonstrated a delayed periprosthetic seroma, whereas most reported cases of BIA-SCC do not exhibit a peri-implant effusion at presentation and instead manifest as a solid or cystic mass without significant fluid collection [2, 6, 24]. This distinction is clinically relevant, as late seromas are classically associated with BIA-ALCL, reinforcing the importance of not dismissing effusions as benign or lymphoma-specific findings. Additionally, the presence of mural nodules within the cystic lesion on ultrasound and MRI further raised suspicion for malignancy, aligning with rare reports where nodularity within a fluid-containing lesion suggested invasive behavior [1, 3, 13].

Another notable feature in our case was the identification of internal mammary lymphadenopathy on imaging. While most published cases report either no nodal involvement or findings limited to axillary nodes, internal mammary chain involvement has been seldom described, highlighting the need for comprehensive regional nodal assessment [2, 3]. Moreover, preoperative imaging underestimated the extent of tumor infiltration, as intraoperative evaluation revealed invasion into the pectoralis major muscle despite no definite evidence of muscular involvement on MRI, a discrepancy previously noted in other cases where deep tissue invasion became apparent only during surgery [3, 11]. These points emphasize that BIA-SCC can mimic a range of implant-related complications and may demonstrate imaging–surgical discordance, underscoring the importance of maintaining high diagnostic suspicion, thorough radiologic assessment, and multidisciplinary decision-making even when findings do not fully align with expected patterns.

Current surveillance guidelines from the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) recommend ultrasound or MRI starting 5–6 years after silicone implantation to assess for implant rupture or structural changes [14]. However, these protocols were not designed to detect malignancy. Given the range of BIA-SCC presentations, including cases with intact implants and no effusion, the sensitivity of such surveillance strategies remains limited. Broadening the clinical triggers for imaging, beyond the presence of effusion, may enhance early recognition, as the FDA Communication on BIA-SCC emphasized [7].

Ultrasound and MRI are the preferred initial imaging modalities, with findings that may include fluid accumulation, cystic or solid masses, nodules, calcifications, and inflammatory changes [3, 6]. Despite the variable imaging presentation, peri-implant effusions are often absent, and hematomas may be suspected in some cases [6]. Given the potential for locoregional metastasis, whole-body imaging is recommended once the diagnosis is established [3]. The American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) has proposed preliminary guidelines emphasizing the use of ultrasound and MRI for patients presenting with late-onset breast tenderness or enlargement [28]. If a mass or seroma is detected, further evaluation with biopsy or FNA is necessary, including histological and cytological examination, culture, and flow cytometry to differentiate malignancy, such as anaplastic large-cell lymphoma (ALCL), and to rule out infection [6]. Morphologically suspicious findings may require additional immunohistochemical staining to exclude other sources of squamous-cell carcinoma [4].

For patients with confirmed BIA-SCC, positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) scans are recommended prior to surgery to assess disease extent and rule out distant metastases, as they offer greater sensitivity than computed tomography (CT) or ultrasound alone [27]. This step is critical, as many patients present with extracapsular spread at the time of surgery, necessitating more extensive surgical interventions. Imaging plays a crucial role in identifying the presence of fluid, edema, and significant masses [4]. These modalities align with ASPS guidelines for early detection [28]. The spectrum of metastatic involvement varies from localized disease to extensive regional and distant organ spread, highlighting the need for thorough preoperative staging. Histopathological analysis of the capsule is essential to confirm diagnosis and exclude other neoplasms. Squamous-cell carcinoma presents a wide range of differentiation, from well-differentiated to poorly differentiated tumors, making immunohistochemistry a valuable diagnostic tool.

While the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) has established guidelines for breast-implant-associated ALCL, specific recommendations for BIA-SCC remain underdeveloped. However, the increasing number of reported cases has led the ASPS to outline a preliminary framework for diagnosis and management.

Treatment and outcomes

The treatment of BIA-SCC is highly variable, with surgical intervention being the cornerstone of management. Implant removal and capsulectomy have been performed in over 60% of reported cases, while more than half of the patients undergo mastectomy, and a significant proportion require chest wall excisions [2]. Adjuvant therapies such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy are commonly employed, particularly in cases with extracapsular spread at diagnosis [11].

In our case, adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy was not administered. This decision was based on the current lack of evidence supporting adjuvant treatment for BIA-SCC and the specific features of our patient’s case. A radical surgical resection was performed, and postoperative pathology confirmed complete tumor removal with clear margins, no extracapsular extension, and no axillary involvement. Given the absence of high-risk features or residual disease and considering that surgery remains the mainstay of treatment in reported cases, the multidisciplinary tumor board determined that no additional therapy was necessary.

Importantly, the decision was accompanied by a plan for strict radiologic and clinical surveillance, recognizing the aggressive potential of BIA-SCC and the current lack of standardized treatment protocols. The patient’s favorable clinical course, with 12 months of disease-free survival to date, supports the adequacy of this tailored approach in selected cases. Nonetheless, longer-term follow-up remains essential to monitor for late recurrence and to guide future therapeutic decisions.

Despite aggressive treatment approaches, prognosis remains unpredictable. Some patients develop metastatic disease within 5 months of follow-up, whereas others achieve long-term survival extending up to 8 years after radical mastectomy and adjuvant radiotherapy [6, 24]. This variability is reflected in survival statistics, with a median follow-up of 9 months showing an 80.8% 1-year overall survival rate and a 53.0% 1-year disease-free survival rate [2]. Mortality within 6–12 months after treatment is not uncommon, often due to disease progression or complications such as malignant pleural effusion [24]. In contrast, our patient remains disease-free at 12 months after the oncologic mammoplasty, with no clinical or radiologic evidence of recurrence. This favorable outcome reinforces the potential benefit of early multidisciplinary intervention and careful surgical planning in selected cases without extracapsular spread. Although long-term data are lacking, her case contributes to the small subset of patients achieving short-term remission and highlights the need for extended follow-up to assess sustained disease control.

Chemoradiation regimens vary significantly across cases. Reported treatment combinations include epirubicin, cyclophosphamide, docetaxel, and gemcitabine with carboplatin, with capecitabine added in select cases. Treatment strategies are highly individualized, considering factors such as prior malignancies and pregnancy status [12, 13, 28].

In the absence of standardized guidelines, surgical management remains aggressive, prioritizing complete tumor resection to prevent disease progression. Adjuvant therapies are critical in eradicating residual disease, yet patient outcomes remain highly heterogeneous, ranging from long-term remission to rapid disease progression and death within a year.

Given the aggressive nature of BIA-SCC, regular follow-up and imaging surveillance are crucial for early detection of recurrence or metastasis. The American Society of Plastic Surgeons recommends MRI or ultrasound 5–6 years after silicone implantation, followed by subsequent screenings every 2–3 years to assess implant integrity and detect potential complications [13]. This proactive monitoring is essential, as some patients may remain asymptomatic even in the presence of implant rupture or disease progression. In our case, adherence to close clinical and radiologic follow-up enabled timely detection of disease progression and guided an effective surgical strategy. The patient’s 12-month disease-free status at the time of publication underscores the importance of sustained surveillance even after initial treatment, particularly in rare malignancies with unpredictable behavior such as BIA-SCC.

Conclusions

This case of squamous-cell carcinoma (SCC) associated with a breast implant in a 48-year-old female patient underscores the critical aspects of breast-implant-associated squamous-cell carcinoma (BIA-SCC), a rare and aggressive malignancy. Given its multifactorial etiology, involving chronic inflammation, implant characteristics, and potential genetic predisposition, heightened awareness and vigilance are essential for early detection and management. Pathological examination of all extracted capsules is crucial to identify tumors, as well as precursor lesions such as squamous metaplasia and dysplasia, to better understand the disease process. The diagnosis and management of BIA-SCC present significant clinical challenges, requiring a multidisciplinary approach that integrates surgical intervention, radiological assessment, pathological evaluation, and systemic therapies to address both local and metastatic spread. The wide variability in patient outcomes highlights the unpredictable nature of this malignancy, emphasizing the need for individualized treatment strategies and long-term surveillance. In selected non-metastatic cases, early radical surgery may be sufficient to achieve disease control, although long-term surveillance remains essential due to the limited data on recurrence patterns.
