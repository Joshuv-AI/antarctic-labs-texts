There exist infinite cube-free words over any sequence of binary alphabets
Vuong Bui, Matthieu Rosenfeld

Abstract
We prove that for any sequence of binary alphabets 𝒜 1 , 𝒜 2 , … \mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\dots , there exists a cube-free word c 1 ​ c 2 ​ … c_{1}c_{2}\dots so that c 1 ∈ 𝒜 1 , c 2 ∈ 𝒜 2 , … c_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1},c_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{2},\dots . In particular, for every n n , there are at least 1.35 n 1.35^{n} cube-free words in 𝒜 1 × 𝒜 2 × ⋯ × 𝒜 n \mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}\times\dots\times\mathcal{A}_{n} . We also prove that if the list of alphabets is computable then one of these words is computable and its n n th letter can be computed in time polynomial in n n .

In the current article, we consider a natural variant of the question. Since cubes are avoidable over the binary alphabet, is it possible to avoid cubes if all of the lists are of size

Theorem 1

For any sequence of binary alphabet

In fact, we do not prove this result directly, but we prove two different stronger results, each implying Theorem

These two proofs share a key idea: we can use an automaton to study the variant of the problem where we only forbid cubes of length at most

The delicate part is then to prove that if

The idea behind the first proof is to show that the growth rate of the language is not far from the growth rate of the regular approximation of the language which yields the following result.

Our approach is to bound the difference between the number of cube-free words and the number of words that avoids all cubes up to a given length

3
A polynomial-time algorithm to construct cube-free words

For any word
u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}
, we define the weight
\omega(u)
of
u
as follows,

\omega(u)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\text{ such that: }\\
uv\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\\
uv=xyyy\text{ with }|y|\geq 1,|v|<|y|\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\Gamma(uv)}\beta^{-|v|}\,,

where
p=12
,
\alpha=1.457
and the
\lambda_{\Gamma(uv)}
are defined in Lemma
5
. This quantity is parameterized by
\beta
, and the particular choice of
\beta
will be fixed later.
Note that
v
can be
\varepsilon
here.

Intuitively, this weight attempts to evaluate how difficult it will be to avoid cubes when extending
u
. For this, we consider cubes that are obtained by extending the word while remaining in
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}
(in some sense, it measures how likely it is that a long cube appears if we randomly follow the automaton). We only consider cubes such that at least two periods of the cube are contained in the suffix of
u
(which will allow us to use Corollary
8
). The more letters we have to add to create a specific cube, the less weight we want to give to this cube since we will have more opportunities to avoid it, which motivates the
\beta^{-|v|}
term. On the other hand, a word
u
with a larger coefficient
\lambda_{\Gamma(u)}
has more extensions in
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}
, hence probably more extensions in
\mathcal{C}
as well.
Multiplying by this coefficient
\lambda_{\Gamma(u)}
allows taking into account the fact that some words should be more costly to forbid than others because they have more extensions. Although we try to justify it by some intuitions, this definition is not simply a well-chosen heuristic, but a precise definition where every detail is crucial to the proof and can only be justified by the proof itself.

Let us state the first simple property of this weight function.

Lemma 9
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{C}
, and
a
be a letter such that
ua\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}
. Then
\omega(ua)<\lambda_{\Gamma(ua)}
implies
ua\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

If
u\in\mathcal{C}
and
ua\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\setminus\mathcal{C}
, then
ua
admits a cube as a suffix. Hence, considering the first term of the sum in the definition of
\omega(ua)
(for
v=\varepsilon
) yields

\omega(ua)\geq\lambda_{\Gamma(ua\varepsilon)}\beta^{-|\varepsilon|}=\lambda_{\Gamma(ua)}\,

as desired.
∎

Note that
\omega(\varepsilon)=0<\lambda_{\varepsilon}
(due to Lemma
5
). The following lemma will be used to construct inductively a sequence
w
where all prefix
u
of
w
is such that
u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}
and
\omega(u)<\lambda_{\Gamma(u)}
so the whole sequence
w\in\mathcal{C}
by the previous lemma.

Lemma 10
.

Suppose
\beta>1
is such that

\beta+\frac{\beta^{1-p}}{\beta-1}\leq\alpha.

Let
u\in\mathcal{C}
be such that
\omega(u)<\lambda_{\Gamma(u)}
, then for any alphabet
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
of size at least
2
there exists
a\in\mathcal{A}
such that
ua\in\mathcal{C}
and

\omega(ua)<\lambda_{\Gamma(ua)}\,.

Proof.

Let
u
and
\mathcal{A}
be as in the theorem statement. By the definition of
\Gamma
, followed by Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathcal{A}\\
uc\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\Gamma(uc)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathcal{A}\\
\Gamma(u)c\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\Gamma(\Gamma(u)c)}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\Sigma\in\binom{\mathbb{N}}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\Sigma\\
\Gamma(u)c\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\Gamma(\Gamma(u)c)}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\lambda_{\Gamma(\Gamma(u))}=\alpha\lambda_{\Gamma(u)}\,.

On the other hand, we consider the sum of the weights of the extensions of
u
by a single letter:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathcal{A}\\
uc\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\end{subarray}}\omega(uc)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathcal{A}\\
uc\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\text{ such that: }\\
ucv\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\\
ucv=xyyy\text{ with }|y|\geq 1,|v|<|y|\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\Gamma(ucv)}\beta^{-|v|}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v^{\prime}\text{ such that: }\\
uv^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\\
uv^{\prime}=xyyy\text{ with }|y|\geq 1,|v^{\prime}|\leq|y|\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\Gamma(uv^{\prime})}\beta^{1-|v^{\prime}|}\,.

This inequality is a direct consequence of the fact that if
cv
is one of the summands in the first sum then it is a summand
v^{\prime}=cv
in the second sum. Splitting this last sum according to whether or not
|v|<|y|
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathcal{A}\\
uc\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\end{subarray}}\omega(uc)

\displaystyle\leq\beta\omega(u)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\text{ such that: }\\
uv\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\\
uv=xyyy\text{ with }|y|\geq 1,|v|=|y|\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\Gamma(uv)}\beta^{1-|v|}.

We have for all
u

\displaystyle\{v:uv\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)},uv=xyyy\text{ with }|y|\geq 1,|v|=|y|\}=\{y:uy\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)},u=xyy,|y|>p\}\,.

This set contains at most one element of each size larger than

Moreover, by Corollary

\lambda_{\Gamma(uy)}=\lambda_{\Gamma(u)}
.

This implies

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathcal{A}\\
uc\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\end{subarray}}\omega(uc)

\displaystyle\leq\beta\omega(u)+\sum_{i>p}\lambda_{\Gamma(u)}\beta^{1-i}

\displaystyle<\lambda_{\Gamma(u)}\left(\beta+\frac{\beta^{1-p}}{\beta-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\Gamma(u)}\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c\in\mathcal{A}\\
uc\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(p)}\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\Gamma(uc)}\,,

where the third inequality is by our theorem hypothesis, and the last inequality was proven earlier in this proof.
This implies that there exists

In particular, with

can be computed in time

Note that the
O(n^{3})
bound is not optimal. In fact, given

to find the smallest corresponding

as it is equivalent to finding the largest suffix of

of period

Our argument implies something slightly stronger than Theorem
3
. Suppose that for every
n
, you can learn
\mathcal{A}_{n+1}
only after having chosen
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, then we can still apply the exact same technique. Indeed, in our proof when we choose the letter
a_{n}
, we never make any assumption on the following alphabets, which is consistent with the aforementioned setting (in particular, note that the weight
\omega
does not depend on any of the alphabets, but only on the current word). Note that our second proof can also be adapted to say something about this setting, but it is not worth the extra technicalities.

In fact, the conclusion of Lemma
10
can be reformulated as saying that there is at most one letter
a\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\omega(ua)\geq\lambda_{\Gamma(ua)}
. This leads to an even stronger variant of the problem: instead of choosing the next letter an adversary provides an alphabet
\Sigma
, we forbid one letter
a\in\Sigma
allowing the adversary to append any chosen letter from
\Sigma\setminus a
to the current word. Our argument directly implies that it is possible for us to ensure that the resulting word avoid cubes for any strategy of the adversary. Furthermore, in this new setting, if
\Sigma
is finite, the next move can always be computed.

4
Cube-free words grow exponentially

This section is devoted to proving Theorem

\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{2},\dots
. For all

For all

We first prove an inequality about the size of these sets.

Lemma 11

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
