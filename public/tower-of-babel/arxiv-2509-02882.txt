Power Laws for the Favard Length Problem in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d}
Caleb Marshall

Abstract
. We prove a power law for the asymptotic decay of the Favard length of neighbourhoods of certain self-similar sets in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} with d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . These self-similar sets are generalizations of the so-called four-corner Cantor set to higher dimensions, as well as to a more general class of rational digit sets. When d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 , our estimates are the first such non-trivial asymptotic upper bounds for the Favard length problem. The extension to a new class of digit sets (which is new even when d = 2 d=2 , but holds for d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 generally) uses the work of G. Kiss, I. Łaba, G. Somlai and the author on vanishing sums of roots of unity and divisibility by many cyclotomic polynomials.

1.
Introduction

For
d\geq 2
, let
\pi_{\theta}(x)=x\cdot\theta
denote the standard dot product of a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a unit vector
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. It is well known that the mapping
\pi_{\theta}
can be naturally identified with the
orthogonal projection
of points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto the linear subspace
L_{\theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is spanned by
\theta
. The
Favard length
of a compact set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the quantity

\textrm{Fav}(E):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\pi_{\theta}(E))d\sigma^{d-1}(\theta)

where
\sigma^{d-1}
denotes the standard surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. The Favard length thus detects the average length of the orthogonal projection of
E
onto linear subspaces
L_{\theta}
.

The
Besicovitch-Federer theorem
(see
[
25
, Theorem 18.1]
) states that a compact set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
which satisfies
0<\mathcal{H}^{1}(E)<+\infty
is
purely 1-unrectifiable
if and only if
\textrm{Fav}(E)=0
. Since
\pi_{\theta}
is continuous and
E
is compact, this is equivalent to stating that there exists some monotone-decreasing function
\psi:[1,\infty]\rightarrow[0,\infty)
which satisfies
\psi(t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, and such that the
\delta
-neighbourhood
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(E)
of
E
satisfies

\textrm{Fav}\big(\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(E)\big)\leq\psi(\delta^{-1}),\textrm{ for all }\delta\in(0,1).

Our main result is an asymptotic upper bound on the function
\psi
for two distinct, countably-infinite families of self-similar sets
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying the hypothesis of the Besicovitch-Federer theorem. All of these self-similar sets are higher dimensional analogoues of the
rational product Cantor sets
which were studied in
[
3
,
22
]
. Our most general asymptotic estimates are motivated and discussed in Section
2
, with our most general Favard length estimates summarized in Theorem
2.4
. However, for this introductory section, we begin by constructing a simple canonical example of a rational product Cantor set to which our main results apply. This is the subject of the next section.

1.1.
A higher dimensional variant of the four-corner Cantor set

Let
d\geq 2
and set
A:=\{0,2^{d}-1\}\subset\mathbb{Z}
. For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
we define a multiscale digit set

(1.1)

A^{N}:=2^{-d}A+2^{-2d}A+\cdots+2^{-Nd}A.

and then consider the following union of
2^{Nd}
-many hypercubes with edge-length
2^{-d}
, which is obtained by writing

(1.2)

\mathcal{K}_{d}^{N}:=\underbrace{(A^{N}\times\cdots\times A^{N})}_{d\textrm{ times }}+[0,2^{-d}]^{d}.

Geometrically, one can think of obtaining this set by iterating the following pruning process: first, partition the unit hypercube
[0,1]^{d}
into
2^{d^{2}}
-many sub-hypercubes of side-length
2^{-d}
; then, delete all cubes which do not touch a vertex of the hypercube, leaving behind
2^{d}
-many sub-hypercubes; to continue, repeat this process on each hypercube preserved by the previous step in the pruning process to obtain
\mathcal{K}_{d}^{2},...,\mathcal{K}_{d}^{N}
. We then let

(1.3)

\mathcal{K}^{\infty}:=\bigcap_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}_{d}^{N}

and we refer to this set as the
2^{d}
-
corner Cantor set
.

The
2^{d}
-corner Cantor set is a compact, purely
1
-unrectifiable set. Moreover, it is also a
self-similar set
which satisfies the open set condition (see
[
25
, Chapter 4]
), and so satisfies
0<\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathcal{K}_{d}^{\infty})<+\infty
. Thus, as a consequence of the Besicovitch-Federer projection theorem, we know that the
\delta
-neighbourhood of
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}
satisfies

\textrm{Fav}\big(\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(\mathcal{K}_{d}^{\infty})\big)\leq\psi(\delta^{-1}),\textrm{ for all }\delta\in(0,1).

for some function
\psi
as described previously.

{remark}

When
d=2
, the following construction produces the
four-corner Cantor set
, a simple and well known example of a purely
1
-unrectifiable self-similar set in the plane. One can see
[
35
]
for an exposition on this set and its relevance to projection problems in geometric measure theory.

Our first main result is an asymptotic upper bound on this function.

1.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and suppose that
\mathcal{K}_{d}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the
2^{d}
-corner Cantor set. Then, there exists some
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and an absolute constant
C>0
such that for all
N\gg 1

(1.4)

C^{-1}N^{-1}\leq\textrm{Fav}\big(\mathcal{N}_{2^{-dN}}(\mathcal{K}_{d}^{\infty}\big)\leq CN^{-\epsilon},

We note that the asymptotic lower bound in (
1.4
) follows from an earlier result due to P. Mattila
[
24
, Theorem 4.1]
. Our main contribution is the asymptotic upper bound.

When
d=2
(so that
\mathcal{K}_{d}^{\infty}
is the the standard four-corner Cantor set in the plane), the estimate (
1.4
) was
already established by F. Nazarov, Y. Peres, and A. Volberg in
[
27
]
, with the explicit estimate
\epsilon>\frac{1}{6}
. Due to certain number theoretic complexities discussed in Section
3.1
, we are unable to calculate a precise lower bound on the value of the exponent
\epsilon
associated to the
2^{d}
-corner Cantor set. This is because, when
d=2
, the polynomial
K(X)=X^{3}+1
(which is clearly associated to the digit set
A=\{0,3\}
) has a particularly simple collection of roots on the unit circle, whereas the polynomial
A(X)=X^{2^{d}-1}+1
(which is associated to the digit set
A=\{0,2^{d}-1\}
) for
d\geq 3
has a more complicated collection of such roots.

{remark}

In fact, while we do not calculate a concrete value of
\epsilon\in(0,1)
as in
[
27
]
, we are able to entirely characterize the dependency of the exponent
\epsilon\in(0,1)
upon the number theoretic structure of the digit set
A:=\{0,2^{d}-1\}
. This is accomplished in Proposition
3.5
, with our explicit characterization being entirely new to the literature. Our characterization also strengthens the bound of Nazarov, Peres, and Volberg to
\epsilon>\frac{1}{4}
(which was known in
[
27
]
but not explicitly proven). We discuss exactly how the calculation of this exponent is accomplished in Section
3.5
.

Estimates from above and below by powers of
N
as in (
1.4
) are known colloquially as
power laws for the asymptotic decay of the Favard length
(or, simply,
power laws for the Favard length problem
). As far as the author is aware, this is the first such upper bound for
any
compact and purely
1
-unrectifiable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with
d\geq 3
) which satisfies the hypothesis of the Besicovitch-Federer projection theorem and also does not have its
\mathcal{H}^{1}
measure concentrated on any two-dimensional affine subspace. To understand what this means, notice that we could easily embed the standard four-corner Cantor set
\mathcal{K}_{2}^{\infty}
into (say)
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by letting

\widetilde{\mathcal{K}_{2}^{\infty}}:=\mathcal{K}_{2}^{\infty}\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

a then obtain the upper bound (
1.4
) by applying the method of
[
27
]
. If we ignore these trivial cases, then Theorem
1.1
is the first example of a power law for the asymptotic decay of the Favard length of a purely
1
-unrectifiable set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 3
.

1.2.
Higher dimensional estimates for more general self-similar sets

Theorem
1.1
will, in fact, follow from a much more general Favard length estimate (see Theorem
2.1
combined with Example
2.2
in Section
2
). By more general, we mean that the asymptotic estimate (
1.4
) in fact holds for a countably-infinite class of self-similar sets
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(i.e. certain
rational product Cantor sets
with amenable number theoretic structure). The structure of these sets and their associated Favard length estimates are the main topic of Section
2
(with all of our Favard length estimates are summarized together in Theorem
2.4
).

The asymptotic decay of the Favard length of general rational product Cantor sets was first studied in the plane by M. Bond, I. Łaba and A. Volberg in
[
3
]
. This work extended the estimate of Nazarov, Peres and Volberg in
[
27
]
to a wider (but still finite) class of self-similar sets in
[
3
]
. This was then followed by the work of I. Łaba and the author in
[
22
]
, who proved that the methods applied to a countably-infinite class of self-similar sets in the plane.

The extension proven by Łaba and the author in
[
22
]
was accomplished by examining the structure of
vanishing sums of roots of unity
and their connection to digit sets associated to Cantor sets; this is discussed in much further detail in Section
2.3
. The articles
[
3
]
and
[
22
]
represent two successive expansions upon the original proof methodology of
[
27
]
. To prove our Theorem
1.1
, we show that this methodology for proving power laws for the asymptotics of the Favard length in planar setting generalizes completely to the
d\geq 3
case.

In fact, the methodology we develop is flexible enough so as to allow a further extension of the estimate (
1.4
), to a new class of self-similar sets which were previously unknown to the literature even in the planar setting. These are related to the so-called
fibered sets
which were introduced by G. Kiss, I. Łaba, G. Somlai and the author in
[
17
, Section 3]
. We define this new class of self-similar sets in Section
2.3
, and also provide concrete examples of such sets in the plane whose power laws for the Favard length were not implied by the previously-established results of
[
3
]
or
[
22
]
.

In the planar setting, there is a broad literature examining the asymptotic behaviour of the Favard length of neighbourhoods of fractal sets (see, for example,
[
1
,

,

,

,

,

,

]
, and an overview of the planar case is provided in the review articles
[

]
and
[

]
. There is also a deep connection to quantitative estimates for the Favard length and analytic capacity, which is formalized via Vitushkin’s conjecture, and this connection was examined in
[

]
, with the most recent progress towards variations upon Vitushkin’s conjecture occurring
[

(we also discuss the application to Vitushkin’s conjecture briefly in Section

2.
The Favard Length of Rational Product Cantor Sets

We will prove Theorem
1.1
by establishing asymptotic upper bounds for the Favard length of
rational product Cantor sets
(RPC sets), which are introduced in Section
2.1
. Section
2.2
communicates the main number theoretic tools needed: mask polynomials and their cyclotomic factorizations. Section
2.3
then explains the extension we obtain by introducing
fibered sets

A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
. These are sets of integers which have a certain special structure relative to their cyclotomic divisors. The formal statements of our Favard length estimates are summarized as Theorem
2.4
. In particular, Theorem
1.1
follows by combining Theorem
2.1
and Example
2.2
from this section. We conclude the section with an outline of the remaining parts of the paper.

2.1.
Rational product Cantor sets and their cyclotomic structure

If
L\geq 3
and
A\subset[L]
is a given set of integers with
\#A\geq 2
, we will refer to
A
as a
digit set
. Define for each
N\geq 1
the
N
-th scale digit set

A^{N}:=\{a\in\mathbb{Q}:a=\alpha_{1}L^{-1}+\cdots+\alpha_{N}L^{-N},\,\alpha_{n}\in A\}.

Given
d\geq 2
and digit sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}\subset[L]
, we then let

\mathcal{S}^{N}:=\underbrace{(A_{1}^{N}\times\cdots\times A_{d}^{N})}_{\mathcal{A}^{N}}+[0,L^{-N}]^{d}

which is a subset of the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We then let
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}:=\bigcap_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}^{N}
. Sets such as
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are colloquially known as
rational product Cantor sets
and are standard examples of self-similar sets. If
s\in[0,d]
satisfies

s=\frac{\log(\#A_{1})+\cdots+\log(\#A_{d})}{\log L}

then one can easily verify that
\dim_{H}A=\dim_{S}A=s
, where
\dim_{H}(\cdot)
and
\dim_{S}(\cdot)
denote the
Hausdorff
and
similarity dimension
, respectively. It is also clear that the RPC sets
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}
satisfy the open set condition. Hence, if

\prod_{i=1}^{d}\#A_{i}=L

then
\dim_{S}\mathcal{S}^{\infty}=1
, and so
0<\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathcal{S}^{\infty})<+\infty
. One can see
[
25
, Chapter 4]
for a standard reference on these topics.

{remark}

While the Cantor iterates

\mathcal{S}^{N}

are not the same as
\mathcal{N}_{L^{-N}}(\mathcal{S}^{\infty})
, the
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
norms are comparable, so one always has that

(2.1)

\textrm{Fav}\big(\mathcal{N}_{L^{-N}}(\mathcal{S}^{\infty})\big)\approx_{d}\textrm{Fav}\big(\mathcal{S}^{N}).

In particular, to prove power laws for the Favard length, one can work with whichever set is the most amenable to the present argument.

2.2.
Mask polynomials and cyclotomic factorizations

The main method of proof for establishing upper bounds for asymptotics of the Favard length of rational product Cantor sets is estimating (both from above and below) integrals of the form

(2.2)

\int_{L^{-m}}^{1}\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}|\phi_{(t_{1},...,t_{d-1})}(L^{j}\xi)|^{2}d\xi,

where
(t_{1},...,t_{d-1})\in[0,1]^{d-1}
and

\phi_{(t_{1},...,t_{d-1})}(\xi):=\frac{1}{L}\sum\limits_{(a_{1},...,a_{d})\in A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{d}}e^{2\pi i\xi(a_{1}+t_{1}a_{2}+\cdots+t_{d-1}a_{d})}.

The integrand in (
2.2
) is an example of a
lacunary Riesz product
—a multiscale trigonometric polynomial written as linear combinations of coefficients of powers of some common base frequency
L>1
. These have a long history within Fourier analysis and one can read
[
26
, Chapter 13]
or
[
42
, Chapter 5]
for more background on the general theory.

The product structure of the set
\mathcal{A}:=A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{d}
allows us to write

(2.3)

\phi_{t}(\xi)=\phi_{A_{1}}(\xi)\phi_{A_{2}}(t_{1}\xi)\cdots\phi_{A_{d}}(t_{d-1}\xi),

where, for each
i=1,...,d
, we have

(2.4)

\phi_{A_{i}}(\xi):=\frac{1}{\#A_{i}}A_{i}(e^{2\pi i\xi}),\textrm{ where }A_{i}(X):=\sum_{a\in A_{i}}X^{a}.

The assumption that our digit sets
A_{i}
consist of only non-negative integers implies that
A_{1}(X),...,A_{d}(X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
. In fact, these polynomials
A_{i}(X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
are all
mask polynomials
; that is, single-variable polynomials with coefficients in
\{0,1\}
.

We utilize the following factorization of the mask polynomials
A_{1}(X),...,A_{d}(X)
, which (at least relative to the Favard length problem) was first introduced in
[
3
]
. For each
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
\Phi_{s}(X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
denote the
s
-
th cyclotomic polynomial
. That is, the unique, monic and irreducible polynomial whose roots are the
s
-th roots of unity.

{definition}

If
A\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
is a mask polynomial, we define its
cyclotomic factorization

A(X)=A^{(1)}(X)A^{(2)}(X)A^{(3)}(X)A^{(4)}(X),

where each factor is defined as follows:

(1)

A^{(1)}(X)=\prod_{S_{A}^{(1)}}\Phi_{s}(X)
,
S_{A}^{(1)}:=\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\Phi_{s}(X)\mid A(X)\textrm{ and }(s,\#A)\neq 1\}

(2)

A^{(2)}(X)=\prod_{S_{A}^{(2)}}\Phi_{s}(X)
,
S_{A}^{(2)}:=\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\Phi_{s}(X)\mid A(X)\textrm{ and }(s,\#A)=1\}

(3)

A^{(3)}(X)
is the product of those irreducible factors of
A(X)
that have at least one root of the form
e^{2\pi i\xi_{0}}
and
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
.

(4)

A^{(4)}(X)
has no roots on the unit circle.

We further denote polynomials
A^{\prime}(X)
and
A^{\prime\prime}(X)
of
A(X)
as

A^{\prime}(X):=A^{(1)}(X)A^{(3)}(X)A^{(4)}(X),\quad A^{\prime\prime}(X):=A^{(2)}(X),

and we will refer to the set
S_{A}^{(2)}
and the polynomial the
A^{\prime\prime}(X)
as the
accumulating zeroes
and
accumulating factor
, respectively.
We will also use the notations
\phi_{A}^{(j)}(\xi)=A^{(j)}(e^{2\pi i\xi})
, with
\phi_{A}^{\prime}(\xi)
and
\phi_{A}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)
defined similarly.

Most of our analysis concerns the accumulating zeroes of our digit sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}
. This is because of the associated behaviour of their roots on the complex unit circle, which we now briefly describe. If
\zeta_{s}\in\mathbb{C}
is an
s
-th primitive root of unity and
R\geq 1
, one readily checks that if
\gcd(s,R)=1
one obtains

\Phi_{s}\big(\zeta_{s}\big)=\Phi_{s}\big(\zeta_{s}^{R}\big)=\cdots=\Phi_{s}\big(\zeta_{s}^{R^{N}}\big)=0,

for any
N\geq 1
. As
N\rightarrow\infty
, we thus see that the roots of the function
\Phi_{s}(X^{R^{N}})
contain all roots of the functions
\Phi_{s}(X^{R^{n}})
for all
0\leq n\leq N-1
(and, in fact, this containment is strict). We illustrate this idea in the following simple example.

{example}

Let
A=\{0,2,3,4,6\}
, so that

\displaystyle A(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X^{6}+X^{4}+X^{3}+X^{2}+1=\underbrace{(X^{4}+X^{3}+X^{2}+X^{1}+1)}_{\Phi_{5}(X)}\underbrace{(X^{2}-X+1)}_{\Phi_{6}(X)}=

Clearly, then, we have that

S_{A}^{(1)}:=\{5\},\,\,S_{A}^{(2)}:=\{6\}.

Now, by definition, we know that
\Phi_{6}(z)=0
if and only if

z\in\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:\zeta=e^{\frac{2\pi ik}{6}}\textrm{ for some }k\in\mathbb{Z}\textrm{ with }\gcd(k,6)=1\}

which is just the set of primitive
6
-th roots of unity. Now,
\Phi_{6}(z^{5})=0
if and only if

z\in\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:\zeta=e^{\frac{\pi ij}{15}+\frac{2\pi ik}{5}}\textrm{ for }j\in\{1,5\}\textrm{ and some }k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

In particular, by setting
(j,k)=(5,0)
followed by
(j,k)=(1,4)
in the previous, we see that that all roots of
\Phi_{6}(X)
are repeated as roots of
\Phi_{6}(X^{5})
, and so the function
\Phi_{6}(X^{5})
accumulates the roots of
\Phi_{6}(X)
.

The calculation along
S_{A}^{(1)}
is simpler. Again,
\Phi_{5}(z)=0
if and only if

z\in\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:\zeta=e^{\frac{2\pi ik}{5}}\textrm{ for some }k\in\mathbb{Z}\textrm{ with }\gcd(k,5)=1\}

which are the primitive
5
-th roots of unity. Since
\Phi_{5}(X^{5})=\Phi_{25}(X)
, we know that
\Phi_{5}(z^{5})=0
if and only if

z\in\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:\zeta=e^{\frac{2\pi ik}{25}}\textrm{ for some }k\in\mathbb{Z}\textrm{ with }\gcd(k,25)=1\}.

One can readily check that these sets of roots of unity are disjoint; hence, the mapping
\Phi_{5}(X)\mapsto\Phi_{5}(X^{5})
does not preserve the roots of
\Phi_{5}(X)
, even though it does introduce many new primitive
5^{2}
-roots of unity.

As the previous discussion and example show, we essentially have the opposite behaviour of the roots
S_{A_{i}}^{(1)}
and
S_{A_{i}}^{(2)}
under the exponentiation map
\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{R_{i}^{k}}
(where
R_{i}=\#A_{i}
and
k\geq 1
). Relative to the Favard length problem, the roots induced by
S_{A}^{(1)}
are completely understood (this is due to the analysis in
[
3
, Section 4]
). It is the accumulating roots induced by
S_{A}^{(2)}
which are still not fully understood.

If we assume that the accumulating roots from
S_{A}^{(2)}
are not too complicated (i.e. that the elements do not have too many prime divisors) then we can, in fact, still prove our Favard length estimate. (Note that, in general, this observation was first made by I. Łaba and the first author in
[
22
]
). Our first main contribution is fully generalizing this number theoretic machinery to the case where
d\geq 3
.

2.1
.

Let
A_{1},...,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
and define

s_{A_{i}}:=\textrm{lcm}\big(S_{A_{i}}^{(2)}\big)\in\mathbb{N}.

If each one of the
A_{i}
satisfies at least one of the following two conditions:

(1)

\#A_{i}\leq 10
; or else,

(2)

s_{A_{i}}
has (at most) two distinct prime factors,

then there exists an
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, which may depend upon
A_{1},...,A_{d}
and
d\geq 2
, such that

(2.5)

\textrm{Fav}(\mathcal{N}_{L^{-N}}(\mathcal{S}^{\infty}))\lesssim N^{-\upsilon(\epsilon,N)}

where

\upsilon(\epsilon,N):=\begin{cases}\epsilon,\textrm{ if }A_{i}^{(3)}\equiv 1\textrm{ for each }i=1,...,d\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\epsilon}{\log\log N},\textrm{ otherwise }\end{cases}

The special case of
\max(\#A_{1},\#A_{2})\leq 6
was proven in the plane by M. Bond, I. Łaba and A. Volberg in
[
3
]
. Łaba and the author then improved this result in the plane to Theorem
2.1
(1) by examining the explicit classifications of vanishing sums of roots of unity given in
[
10
]
and
[
29
]
. The hypothesis of Theorem
2.1
.(2) may appear incongruous when compared to the required size-bound of Theorem
2.1
.(1). However, the two-prime-factors assumption essentially forces the set
S_{A}^{(2)}
associated to the accumulating factor
A_{i}^{\prime\prime}(X)
from Definition
2.2
to be less complicated (this is made precise in
[
22
, Section 5]
). With this simplifying assumption on
S_{A}^{(2)}
, Łaba and the author demonstrate a size bound on the set
A_{i}
, which acts as a substitute for the assumption
\#A_{i}\leq 10
. One can see
[
22
, Theorem 1.2]
for the precise statement of this size bound.

To see that the power law of Theorem
1.1
does genuinely follow from Theorem
2.1
, we begin with the following slightly-weaker estimate for the
2^{d}
-corner Cantor set.

{example}

[A Higher-Dimensional Four-Corner Set]
Let
d\geq 2
and
\mathcal{K}_{d}^{\infty}
be the
2^{d}
-corner Cantor set defined in equations (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). Since
\#A_{i}=2\leq 10
, Theorem
2.1
already furnishes some
\epsilon\in(0,1)
such that

\textrm{Fav}\big(\mathcal{N}_{2^{-dN}}(\mathcal{K}_{d}^{\infty})\big)\lesssim_{d}N^{-\frac{\epsilon}{\log\log N}}.

However, we can improve upon this by noticing that the mask polynomials

A_{i}(X):=1+X^{2^{d}-1}

together satisfy
A_{i}(\zeta)=0
if and only if

\zeta=e^{\frac{i\pi}{2^{d}-1}+\frac{2\pi ir}{2^{d}-1}}

for some
r\in\mathbb{Z}
. In particular, each
A_{i}
has only roots of unity on the complex unit circle (equivalently,
A_{i}^{(3)}(X)\equiv 1
for all
i=1,...,d
), and so Theorem
2.1
now furnishes an
\epsilon\in(0,1)
such that

\textrm{Fav}\big(\mathcal{N}_{2^{-dN}}(\mathcal{K}_{d}^{\infty})\big)\lesssim_{d}N^{-\epsilon},

as required.

2.3.
Favard length estimates and fibered digit sets

In this section, we state the extension of Theorem
2.1
for a special class of digit sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}
which are related to the so-called
fibered sets
studied by G. Kiss, I. Łaba, G. Somlai and the author in
[
17
]
. The main advancement is that we are able to drop the assumption that
s_{A_{i}}:=lcm(S_{A_{i}})
has at most two prime factors if, instead, we assume that our digit sets
A_{i}
admit some subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
which is highly structured mod
s
for each
s\in S_{A_{i}}^{(2)}
. We introduce the basic notations and definitions needed to state our result, and remark that a thorough treatment is given in
[
17
]
.

Letting
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
be fixed, we set
S=S_{A}^{(2)}
and
M=\textrm{lcm}(S)
. Because
s|M
we must have that
\Phi_{s}(X)\mid(X^{M}-1)
, and so

\Phi_{s}(X)\mid A(X)\Leftrightarrow\Phi_{s}(X)\mid A(X)\bmod X^{M}-1.

One can readily check (or see from
[
17
, Section 2]
) that

(2.6)

A(X)\equiv\sum\limits_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}}w_{A}^{M}(a)X^{a}\mod X^{M}-1

where

(2.7)

w_{A}^{M}(a):=\#\{a^{\prime}\in A:a^{\prime}\equiv a\bmod M\}.

Hence we see that divisibility by
\Phi_{s}(X)
in the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[X]
is equivalent to divisibility of the induced polynomial (
2.6
) in the polynomial quotient ring
\mathbb{Z}[X]\setminus(X^{M}-1)
. This leads us to consider the following more general paradigm.

{definition}

A
weight
is any non-trivial function
w:\mathbb{Z}_{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
. Given a weight, its associated
multiset
on
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
is the collection of ordered pairs

B:=\{(y,w(y))\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}\times\mathbb{Z}\}.

We let
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{Z}_{M})
denote the collection of all multisets on
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
. We also let
\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}_{M})
denote the collection of all multisets whose weights satisfy
w\geq 0
uniformly on
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
. The
mask polynomial

B\bmod M
is the unique polynomial
B(X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]\setminus(X^{M}-1)
which satisfies

B(X)\equiv\sum\limits_{y\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}}w(y)X^{y}\mod X^{M}-1.

If
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
is a non-empty set and
M\geq 2
is any integer, then our previous discussion shows that

A\bmod M:=\{(y,w_{A}^{M}(y)):y\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}\textrm{ and }w_{A}^{M}(y)\textrm{ is as in }\eqref{eq:weightfunction}\}.

The associated mask polynomial of
A\bmod M
then has equation

A(X)\equiv\sum\limits_{y\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}}w_{A}^{M}(y)X^{y}\mod X^{M}-1,

and, if
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then
(A\bmod M)\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}_{M})
for any
M\geq 2
. We also always have that

\#A=A(1)=\sum\limits_{y\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}}w_{A}^{M}(y).

One can see
[
22
, Section 4]
or
[
17
, Section 2]
for a more thorough introduction to multisets and their mask polynomials.

We work with sets
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
whose induced multisets
A\bmod s
have a special structural property called
fibering
, which was first studied in
[
17
]
. To this end, let

M:=\textrm{lcm}(S)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}^{n_{k}},

where
p_{1},\dots,p_{K}
are distinct primes and
n_{1},\dots,n_{K}\in\mathbb{N}
. Notice that, since we are assuming that
S_{A}=S_{A}^{(2)}
, the hypothesis of Theorem
2.1
.(2) assumes that
K\leq 2
. However, we now make no such assumption moving forward.

If
s\mid M
and
p_{k}\mid s
, we say that
F\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}_{M})
is an
s
-fiber in the
p_{k}
direction
if the multiset
F\bmod s
satisfies

F(X)\equiv(\underbrace{1+X^{s/p_{k}}+X^{2s/p_{k}}+\cdots+X^{(p_{k}-1)s/p_{k}}}_{F_{k}^{s}(X)}\mod X^{s}-1.

We will always use the notation
F_{k}^{s}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}_{M})
to denote an
s
-fiber in the
p_{k}
direction. When
s
and
p_{k}
are assumed from context, we simply call such sets
fibers
.

While we will not use the following test for cyclotomic divisiblity in this work, we state it to explain how fibers are related to cyclotomic divisibility. The following is known as the
Bruijn-Rédei-Schoenberg theorem
on the structure of vanishing sums of roots of unity (see
[
15
,
21
,
23
,
30
,
31
,
33
]
).

2.2
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
have mask polynomial
A(X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
and suppose
s=\prod_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}
for some distinct prime numbers
p_{1},...,p_{K}
and exponents
\alpha_{k}\geq 1
. Then,
\Phi_{s}(X)\mid A(X)
if and only if there exist coefficient polynomials
P_{1}(X),...,P_{K}(X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
such that

(2.8)

A(X)\equiv\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}P_{k}(X)F_{k}^{s}(X)\mod X^{s}-1,

where, we remark, the coefficient polynomials
P_{k}(X)
have integer, but not necessarily non-negative, coefficients.

We want to work with sets
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
which admit a subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
whose induced multisets
A^{\prime}\bmod s
all have a certain rigid structure for every
s\in S_{A}^{(2)}
. This structure is known as
fibering
and was first introduced in
[
17
, Section 3]
relative to the study of the Coven-Meyerowitz conjecture for integer tilings.

{definition}

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
and

S\subseteq S_{A}:=\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\Phi_{s}(X)\mid A(X)\}

be some set of cyclotomic divisors of
A
. Set
M=\textrm{lcm}(S)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}^{n_{k}}
. Fix some
s\mid M
(and
s\neq 1
) and suppose that
p_{k}\mid s
. We say that
A
is
fibered in the
p_{k}
-direction at scale
s
if there exists a polynomial
P_{k}(X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
such that

A(X)\equiv P_{k}(X)F_{k}^{s}(X)\mod X^{s}-1.

An
assignment function
is any function
\sigma:S\rightarrow\{1,...,K\}
such that

\sigma(s)\in\{i:p_{i}\mid s\}.

Given an assignment function
\sigma
, we say that
A
is
(S,\sigma)
-
fibered
if, for every
s\in S
, the associated multiset
A
mod
s
is fibered in the
p_{\sigma(s)}
direction on the scale
s
.

We also introduce the following definition (which is new to the literature, but is based upon the work of G. Kiss, I. Łaba, G. Somlai and the author in
[
17
]
). This definition allows us to work with sets which may not, themselves, be fibered, but which at least contain a fibered subset along the those scales
S_{A}^{(2)}\subset S_{A}
which are associated to the accumulating zeroes of
A(X)
.

{definition}

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
We say that
A

admits a fibered subset
if there exists some
\emptyset\neq A^{\prime}\subseteq A
such that
A^{\prime}
is an
(S_{A}^{(2)},\sigma)
-fibered set.

We have the following new Favard length estimate for rational product Cantor sets whose digit sets
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
admit a fibered set. Note that this result is entirely new to the literature, even in the well-studied case of planar rational product Cantor sets.

2.3
.

Let
A_{1},...,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
and suppose that each
A_{i}
admits a fibered subset (see Definitions
2.3
and
2.3
).
Then there exists an
\epsilon
(which may depend upon
A_{1},...,A_{d}
and
d\geq 2
) such that

\textrm{Fav}(\mathcal{N}_{L^{-N}}(\mathcal{S}^{\infty}))\lesssim N^{-\upsilon(\epsilon,N)}

where

\upsilon(\epsilon,N):=\begin{cases}\epsilon,\textrm{ if }A_{i}^{(3)}\equiv 1\textrm{ for each }i=1,...,d\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\epsilon}{\log\log N},\textrm{ otherwise }\end{cases}\,\,.

To understand why one might expect to have a Favard length estimate for such digit sets, consider the following lower bound on the cardinality of fibered multisets, which was recently proven by G. Kiss, I. Łaba, G. Somlai and the author in
[
17
]
.

{proposition}

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
and set
S:=S_{A}^{(2)}
and
M:=\textrm{lcm}(S)
. Choose some fixed assignment function
\sigma_{0}:S\rightarrow\{1,...,K\}
and, for each
k=1,...,K
, let

\hbox{\sf EXP}_{k}(S,\sigma_{0}):=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}:\exists\,s\in S\textrm{ with }(s,p_{k}^{n_{k}})=p_{k}^{\alpha}\textrm{ and }\sigma_{0}(s)=k\}.

Let
E_{k}(S,\sigma_{0}):=\#\hbox{\sf EXP}_{k}(S,\sigma_{0})
and define a quantity

\hbox{\sf FIB}(S,\sigma_{0}):=p_{1}^{E_{1}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdots p_{K}^{E_{K}(S,\sigma_{0})}.

Then, if
A
is
(S,\sigma)
-fibered, then we have the size estimate

(2.9)

\#A\geq\min_{\sigma}\hbox{\sf FIB}(S,\sigma)

where the minimum is taken over all possible assignment functions
\sigma:S\rightarrow\{1,...,K\}
.

Of course, we immediately obtain the following.

{corollary}

Suppose that
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
admits a fibered subset. Then,

\#A\geq\min_{\sigma}\hbox{\sf FIB}(S_{A}^{(2)},\sigma),

where the minimum is taken over all possible assignment functions
\sigma
on
S_{A}^{(2)}
.

Critically: the definition of
admitting a fibered subset
requires that the subset
A^{\prime}
is
(S,\sigma)
-fibered with
S=S_{A}^{(2)}
, thus allowing us to obtain a non-trivial lower bound on
\#A
which depends upon the accumulating roots of
A
. This cardinality estimate is the key starting point for our constructions in Section
5.2
.

{remark}

It is reasonable to ask why one would even think to study digit sets which have this
(S,\sigma)
-fibering property. To help explain how such a structure arises, observe that whenever
\Phi_{s}(X)\mid A(X)
, we have to have that
A\bmod s
is a linear combination of fibers (this is just the Bruijn-Rédei-Schoenberg theorem, stated as Theorem
2.2
in this work). Hence, the
(S,\sigma)
-fibered sets are exactly those which have the
simplest allowable structure relative to these fibers
at all
s\in S
.

In principle, this observation suggests that fibered sets are an ideal test class when dealing with constructions which are sensitive to complicated configurations of roots of unity. Indeed, it is exactly the simple structure of fibered sets which enable the size bound of Proposition
2.3
to hold in full generality. For more complicated sets, such estimates can fail (which is a nontrivial fact, see
[
17
]
for a thorough discussion of this phenomenon).

2.3.1.
Examples of digit sets which admit fibered subsets

To explain why Theorem
2.3
represents genuine progress on the Favard length problem, we give two examples of digit sets
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
which fails to satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem
2.1
but which admit a persistently fibered subset. Because of Theorem
2.3
, we can still obtain a power law for the associated RPC sets
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}
which are associated to the Cantor iterates

\mathcal{S}_{d}^{N}:=\underbrace{(A^{N}\times\cdots\times A^{N})}_{d\textrm{ times}}+[0,(\#A)^{-dN}]^{d}.

We remark that one can find similar styles of examples in
[
17
, Section 8]
and
[
22
, Section 7]
, where a more geometric interpretation of these constructions is developed. However, these two examples which we present are new to the literature.

The first example is intentionally simple, providing the reader with an intuition for the type of structured digit sets which Theorem
2.3
addresses. We work in
\mathbb{Z}_{30}
because
30=2\times 3\times 5
is the smallest possible integer for which the two-prime hypothesis of Theorem
2.1
fails.

{example}

Consider the set
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
with mask polynomial

A(X)=(1+X+X^{5})F_{3}^{30}(X)+X^{2}F_{2}^{30}(X)\mod X^{30}-1,

or, if one prefers,

A=(\{0,10,30\}\cup\{1,11,21\}\cup\{5,15,25\})\cup\{2,17\}.

Then
\#A=11
and since
A(X)\bmod X^{30}-1
is the linear combination of fibers, Theorem
2.3
implies that
\Phi_{30}(X)\mid A(X)
. In fact, one can also verify by direct calculation that

A(X)=(1+2X)F_{3}^{6}(X)+X^{2}F_{2}^{6}(X)\mod X^{6}-1

so that
\Phi_{6}(X)\mid A(X)
.
Moreover, we know that
\Phi_{s}(X)\nmid A(X)
for any
s\not\in\{6,30\}
, via standard arguments regarding fibers (see, for example, either
[
17
, Section 2]
or
[
22
, Section 5]
for examples). In particular, then,
S_{A}^{(2)}=\{6,30\}
so that
s_{A}=\textrm{lcm}(S_{A}^{(2)})=30
has three distinct prime divisors. Nevertheless, we see that
A
admits at least one (and, in fact, several) persistently fibered subset, for example
A^{\prime}:=\{0,10,20\}
.

For the second example, we construct a digit set
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

•

A
admits a persistently fibered subset;

•

\#A
can be taken as large as we wish; and also,

•

s_{A}
can have arbitrarily-many prime factors.

This highlights that the hypothesis of Theorem
2.3
is a distinctive structure from those assumed in the two hypotheses of Theorem
2.1
.

{example}

Let
R\geq 1
and
K\geq 2
be two given integers, and suppose that
p_{1},...,p_{K-1},q
are distinct prime numbers. Let
M=p_{1}\cdots p_{K-1}q^{R+1}
and
N=p_{1}\cdots p_{K-1}
. We then consider the polynomial

A(X):=\underbrace{F_{q}^{M}(X)F_{q}^{M/q}(X)\cdots F_{q}^{M/q^{R}}(X)}_{B(X)}+\underbrace{X\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{K-1}F_{p_{k}}^{M}(X)}_{D(X)}\mod X^{M}-1.

The set
B\subset\mathbb{Z}_{M}
with mask polynomial
B(X)
is known as a
long fiber
(see
[
17
, Section 6]
) and the set
D\subset\mathbb{Z}_{M}
with mask polynomial
D(X)
is known as a
plane
(see
[
21
, Section 2]
). In particular, since
\gcd(a^{\prime},q)=q
for any
a\in A^{\prime}
, whereas
\gcd(a^{\prime\prime},q)=1
for any
a^{\prime\prime}\in A^{\prime\prime}
, we see that
B\cap D=\emptyset
. Now, if
1\leq r\leq R
, then a direct calculation shows that

A(X)=q^{r}F_{q}^{M/q^{r}}(X)\cdots F_{q}^{M/q^{R}}(X)+X\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{K-1}F_{p_{k}}^{M/q^{r}}(X)\mod X^{M/q^{r}}-1,

and so
\Phi_{M}(X)\cdots\Phi_{M/q^{R}}(X)\mid A(X)
. We also clearly have that

\#A=A(1)=q^{R}+p_{1}\cdots p_{K},

so that
\gcd(\#A,M/q^{r})=1
for each
1\leq r\leq R
while also
\#A>q^{R}
. In particular, we may take
\#A
as large as we would like by letting
R\rightarrow\infty
. Furthermore, if
s\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies
s\mid M
but
q\nmid s
, then

F_{q}^{M/q^{r}}(X)\cdots F_{q}^{M/q^{R}}(X)=q^{R}=q^{R}X^{0}\mod X^{s}-1,

so that
\Phi_{s}(X)\nmid A(X)
for all such
s
.

Altogether, this implies that

S_{A}^{(2)}=\{M,M/q,...,M/q^{R}\}=\{p_{1}\cdots p_{K-1}q^{R+1},...,p_{1}\cdots p_{K-1}q\}

so that
s_{A}:=\textrm{lcm}(S_{A}^{(2)})=M
, which clearly has
K
-many prime factors. Finally, we observe that both the sets
B
and
D
are
(S_{A}^{(2)},\sigma)
-fibered sets. In particular,
B
is an
(S_{A}^{(2)},\sigma_{q})
-fibered set, with assignment function
\sigma(s)=q
for all
s\in S_{A}^{(2)}
; whereas,
B
is an
(S_{A}^{(2)},\sigma_{p})
-fibered set, for any assignment function with
\sigma_{p}(s)\in\{p_{1},...,p_{K-1}\}
for
s\in S_{A}^{(2)}
.

Our previous examples show that Theorem
2.3
is new and tangible progress towards power laws for rational product Cantor sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
associated to arbitrary digit sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
. However, the key ingredient in our proof of Theorem
2.3
is the lower bound (
2.9
), which
fails to hold
for arbitrary sets of integers
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This is actually a complicated (and rather surprising) property of multisets, which is proven by direct construction in
[
17
, Theorem 1.3]
. Hence, any progress beyond that of Theorem
2.3
would require significant new ideas in the study of vanishing sums of roots of unity and cyclotomic divisibility. The author is currently investigating this, with the hope of addressing this in a sequel to this work.

2.4.
Summary of Favard length estimates for rational product Cantor sets

We include the following summary of the results of Theorem
2.1
and Theorem
2.3
to obtain the following.

2.4
.

Let
A_{1},...,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
and suppose that each
A_{i}
satisfies one of the following conditions:

(1)

\#A_{i}\leq 10
;

(2)

The integer
s_{A_{i}}:=\textrm{lcm}(\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\Phi_{s}(X)\mid A_{i}(X)\textrm{ with }\gcd(s,\#A_{i})=1\})
has at most two prime factors; or else,

(3)

A_{i}
admits a fibered subset (see Definitions
2.3
and
2.3
).

Then there exists an
\epsilon
(which may depend upon
A_{1},...,A_{d}
and
d\geq 2
) such that

\textrm{Fav}(\mathcal{N}_{L^{-N}}(\mathcal{S}^{\infty}))\lesssim N^{-\upsilon(\epsilon,N)}

where

\upsilon(\epsilon,N):=\begin{cases}\epsilon,\textrm{ if }A_{i}^{(3)}\equiv 1\textrm{ for each }i=1,...,d\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{\epsilon}{\log\log N},\textrm{ otherwise }\end{cases}

Notice that each of the digit sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}
need not satisfy the
same conditions
in order for our Favard length estimate to hold. Rather, it is enough that
at least one
of the three aforementioned conditions is satisfied for each
A_{i}
. This is a consequence of how we construct a certain special set (i.e. the
set of large values
) associated to the sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}
. This set of large values defined in Section
3
and constructed in Section
5
.

As a final comment, we remark that while Conditions
(1)
,
(2)
and
(3)
represent three genuinely distinct assumptions upon the digit sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
, they all allow us the same leverage when attacking the Favard length problem. In particular, these hypotheses all tell us how large the cardinality of the digit sets
A_{i}
can be relative to the complexity of their set of accumulating zeroes
S_{A}^{(2)}
. This is exemplified in the construction of the SLV set given in
[
22
, Section 3]
as well as in Section
5.2
of this work.

3.
The Counting Function and Associated Riesz Products

This section introduces the main techniques which are needed to prove Theorem
2.1
.
In Section
3.1
, we introduce the tools which support our main calculations in Section
4
. These are the
set of small values property
(SSV property) and the
set of large values property
(SLV property), which were first introduced in the plane in
[
3
]
. Our usage of these tools, which occurs in Section
4
and Section
5
, requires significant generalization of this machinery. In Section
3.3
, we define the
counting function
and provide a framework of the general proof method for obtaining Favard length estimates from
L^{2}
estimates on this object. Section
3.4
shows that these aforementioned
L^{2}
estimates for the counting function are reducible to both lower and upper bounds on the
L^{2}
norms of Riesz products over
almost-unit
intervals. Section
3.5
is a discussion of the dependencies of the exponent
\epsilon\in(0,1)
appearing in the statement of Theorem
2.4
upon the SSV and SLV properties introduced in this section.

3.1.
The Set of Small Values (SSV) property

In this section, we introduce two statements about the sublevel and superlevel sets of the lacunary Riesz product, which was previously introduced in (
2.2
).

{definition}

We say that
\phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
has the
Set of Small Values (SSV)

property
with
SSV function

\psi=\psi_{c_{1}}(m)
if, for all
m\gg 1
, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}>0
which are independent of
m
and which satisfy
c_{3}\gg c_{2}
such that the set

(3.1)

\textrm{SSV}_{\psi}:=\bigg\{\xi\in[0,1]:\bigg\lvert\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\phi(L^{k}\xi)\bigg\rvert\lesssim\psi_{c_{1}}(m)\bigg\}

is contained in
L^{c_{2}m}
-many intervals each of size
L^{-c_{3}m}
. If

(1)

\psi(m)=L^{-c_{1}m}
, we say that
\phi
has the
SSV property
;

(2)

\psi(m)=L^{-c_{1}m\log m}
, we say that
\phi
has the
log-SSV property
;

(3)

\psi(m)=L^{-c_{1}m^{2}}
, we say that
\phi
has the
square-SSV property
.

The constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and
c_{3}
are assumed to be independent of all scale parameters, but may depend upon the function
\phi
. In our applications, the function
\phi
will be defined relative to the digit sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and so the constants
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
will depend upon these sets (and thus also the ambient dimension
d\geq 2
). Observe that, as one transitions from conditions
(1)-(3)
in Definition
3.1
, the sets
\textrm{SSV}_{\psi}
are nested decreasing: satisfying Condition
(1)
implies satisfying Condition
(2)
which, in turn, implies satisfying Condition
(3)
. We note that, in our calculations, the parameter
m\gg 1
will be chosen sufficiently large as a function of
N\gg 1
(something like
m\approx\log N
will do), where
N\sim\mathcal{S}^{N}
is the current level of construction for our rational product Cantor iterates. The precise choice of
m
is made in Definition
3.4
which appears later in this section.

We remark here that, at least relative to proving power laws for the Favard length problem, the set of small values machinery for the single-variable oscillatory functions
\phi_{A_{1}}^{\prime},...,\phi_{A_{d}}^{\prime}
is both
as general as possible
and also
sharp
(see
[
3
, Section 4]
for a discussion of these facts). For this reason, we will essentially black-box the SSV property
for said functions
as the following two lemmas (which were proven in
[
3
, Section 4]
).

{lemma}

Assuming
m\gg 1
, and regardless of the choice of digit sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the functions
\phi_{A_{1}}^{\prime},...,\phi_{A_{d}}^{\prime}
all have the log-SSV property. Moreover, if each mask polynomial
A_{1}(X),...,A_{d}(X)
has only roots of unity on the unit circle (equivalently,
A_{i}^{(3)}\equiv 1
for each
i\in\{1,...,d\}
), then the functions
\phi_{A_{1}}^{\prime},...,\phi_{A_{d}}^{\prime}
each have the SSV property.

The constants
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
are allowed to depend upon
A_{1},...,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
d\geq 2
, but will not depend upon
m
. If
\lambda=\lambda_{A_{1},...,A_{d};d}\in(1,\infty)
then we may always assume that
c_{3}\geq\lambda c_{2}
, perhaps at the expense of allowing
c_{1}
to depend upon
\lambda
.

Proof.

This is a combination of Proposition 4.1 and Proposition 4.3 in
[
3
, Section 4]
.
∎

{lemma}

If
\phi_{A_{1}}^{\prime},...,\phi_{A_{d}}^{\prime}
have the log-SSV or SSV property with constants
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
, then
\phi_{A_{1}}^{\prime},...,\phi_{A_{d}}^{\prime}
continue to have the log-SSV or SSV property with constants
(c_{1},c_{2}+\tau,c_{3}+\tau)
for any
\tau\in(1,\infty)
which may depend upon
A_{1},...,A_{d}
and
d\geq 2
, but not
m
.

The following lemma is used implicitly in
[
3
]
, but we include a proof (since it is an exceedingly simple one).

Proof.

This is a simple covering argument. Indeed, if a set
U\subset\mathbb{R}
is covered by
L^{c_{2}m}
many intervals of length
L^{-c_{3}m}
, then there exists a covering by smaller intervals of length
L^{-(c_{3}+\tau)m}
of which we can assume there are at most
\approx L^{(c_{2}+\tau)m}
simply by counting how many smaller intervals of length
L^{-(c_{3}+\tau)m}
were contained in our original interval of length
L^{-c_{3}m}
.
∎

Hence, the previous lemmas give us a precise, quantitative control of the sublevel sets of the functions
\phi_{A_{1}}^{\prime},...,\phi_{A_{d}}^{\prime}
. This forms a key tool for our later analysis in Section
4
. The result was first proven by M. Bond, I. Łaba and A. Volberg in
[
3
, Section 4]
.

{remark}

The fact that
c_{1}
is chosen large-enough relative to the digit sets
A_{1},..,A_{d}
to guarantee that
c_{3}\gg c_{2}
is, in fact, an extremely lossy part of this argument. In fact, it is one of the main reason why we are unable to calculate precise values of the exponent
\epsilon\in(0,1)
appearing in our calculations. This is discussed further in Section
3.5
, with the exact dependence of
\epsilon
upon the SSV property of the function
\phi
being stated as Proposition
3.5
, which is the first result to state the precise functional relationship between the SSV property and the finer asymptotic decay of the Favard length.

3.2.
The Set of Large Values (SLV) property

The second major pillar of our analysis is a statement regarding the arithmetic structure of the superlevel sets of our Riesz product. This structure will allow an application of the so-called
Salem trick
to the integral of our Riesz product on the almost-unit interval
[L^{-m},1]
. We refer the reader to
[
3
, 359]
for a discussion of this classical idea.

{definition}

Let
\phi:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with
L\geq 2
and
m\gg 1
. Say that
\phi
is
Set of Large Values (SLV)-structured
if there exists a Borel set
\Gamma\subset[0,1]
, a parameter
\eta\in(0,1)
and constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
(which do not depend upon
m
or
\eta
) such that:

(3.2)

\Gamma-\Gamma\subset\bigg\{\xi:\bigg|\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\phi(L^{k}\xi)\bigg|\geq L^{-C_{1}m}\bigg\}

(3.3)

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\Gamma\big)\geq C_{2}L^{-(1-\eta)m}.

In this article, we prove that to establish power laws for the Favard length of RPC sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it suffices to demonstrate that a certain oscillatory function associated to our digit sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}
is SLV structured. (See Proposition
4.3
for the precise statement).

In the plane, this implication (i.e. existence of SLV sets associated to RPC sets
\Rightarrow
power laws for the Favard length of RPC sets) was first utilized in
[
3
]
in the special case where
\max(\#A_{1},\#A_{2})\leq 6
, and then further leveraged in more generality by I. Łaba and the author in
[
22
]
. We will prove this implication explicitly in Section
4
for all rational product Cantor sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, we show the following where, recall that

S_{A}^{(2)}:=\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\Phi_{s}(X)\mid A(X)\textrm{ with }\gcd(s,\#A)=1\}.

{proposition}

Let
A_{1},...,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
be digit sets with each
A_{i}
satisfying at least one of the following conditions:

(1)

\#A_{i}\leq 10
; or,

(2)

\textrm{lcm}(S_{A_{i}}^{(2)})
has at most two distinct prime divisors; or,

(3)

A_{i}
admits an
(S_{A_{i}}^{(2)},\sigma_{i})
-fibered subset (see Definition
2.3
).

Then, the function

\phi_{t}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)=\phi_{(t_{1},...,t_{d-1})}^{\prime\prime}(\xi):=\phi_{A_{1}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\phi_{A_{2}}^{\prime\prime}(t_{1}\xi)\cdots\phi_{A_{d}}^{\prime\prime}(t_{d-1}\xi),

is SLV-structured for all
t\in[0,1]^{d-1}
, with all involved constants independent of
t
. One can see Definition
2.2
for a precise definition of the oscillatory functions
\phi_{A_{i}}^{\prime\prime}
.

In the plane, Proposition
3.2
.(1) was proven by M. Bond, I. Łaba and A. Volberg in
[
3
]
when
\max(\#A_{1},\#A_{2})\leq 6
, whereas Proposition
3.2
.(1)-(2) were both proven by I. Łaba and the author in
[
22
]
. Proposition
3.2
.(3) is entirely new to the literature, even in the planar case. When
d\geq 3
, all parts of Proposition
3.2
are new to the literature. We will prove Proposition
3.2
in Section
5
.

3.3.
The counting function and exceptional set of angles

In this section, we define the
counting function
, a key analytical tool for detecting when the orthogonal projections of the set
\mathcal{S}^{N}
can be small. In order to make later calculations neater, we define the counting function relative to
\ell^{2}
balls (rather than the
\ell^{\infty}
balls (i.e.
hypercubes
) which appear in the definition of the Cantor iterates
\mathcal{S}^{N}
). So, we let
c=c_{d}>1
be the optimal constant so that

(3.4)

(z_{j}+[0,L^{-N}]^{d})\subset B(z_{j},cL^{-N}),\quad\forall z_{j}\in\mathcal{A}^{N},

and then define
Q_{j}:=B(z_{j},cL^{-N})
.
A Vitali-type covering argument shows that

\textrm{Fav}(\mathcal{N}_{L^{-N}}(\mathcal{S}^{\infty}))\approx_{d}\textrm{Fav}(\mathcal{S}^{N})\approx_{d}\textrm{Fav}(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{N}),\quad\forall N\gg 1

where
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{N}:=\bigcup_{z_{j}\in\mathcal{A}^{N}}B(z_{j},cL^{-N})
.
In particular, since the implicit constant is independent of the scale
N\gg 1
, this does no harm to our calculations.

{definition}

Given
N\geq 1
and
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, the
counting function
is the mapping

\mathbb{R}\ni x\mapsto f_{N,\theta}(x):=\sum\limits_{z_{j}\in\mathcal{A}^{N}}\mathbf{1}_{\pi_{\theta}(Q_{z_{j}})}(x),

where
Q_{z_{j}}
are the
\ell^{2}
balls defined in (
3.4
).

Because the set
\mathcal{S}^{N}
is essentially the
L^{-N}
-neighbourhood of the self-similar set
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}
, any ball
Q^{N}
at scale
N
will have a a parent relationship with each child ball
Q^{N+1}
at scale
N+1
. This just means that either

Q^{N+1}\subset Q^{N}\quad\textrm{or else}\quad Q^{N+1}\cap Q^{N}=\emptyset.

We will use this idea often and in much more detail in Section
6
, where we essentially show that the self-similarity of this set is equivalent to a large failure of injectivity for the counting function at many points
x\in\mathbb{R}
. As a simple consequence of this parent-child relationship, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that either

f_{N,\theta}(x)\leq f_{N+1,\theta}(x)\quad\textrm{ or else }\quad f_{N+1,\theta}(x)=0,

and so it makes sense to define a maximal version of the counting function

f_{N,\theta}^{*}(x):=\max_{1\leq n\leq N}f_{n,\theta}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

which encodes the maximal stacking which occurs up to a given scale
N\geq 1
. The maximal counting function is then utilized to define the set of
low multiplicity directions

E_{N,K}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Intuitively, these are the directions where the projection mapping mostly fails to be
K
-to-one. To make this notion rigorous, we define a
stacking parameter

K=K(N)
which is a function of the maximal scale
N
, and whose value is determined by the cyclotomic factorization of the mask polynomials
A_{1},...,A_{d}
.

{definition}

Given some
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
to be chosen later (but independent of
N
) define a parameter
K
, which depends upon
A_{1},...,A_{d}
,
N
and
\epsilon_{0}
in the following manner:

(1)

If
A_{i}^{(3)}\equiv 1
for each
i\in\{1,...,d\}
, we let
K=N^{\epsilon_{0}}
.

(2)

Otherwise, we choose
K=N^{\epsilon_{0}/\log\log N}
.

Using this choice of
K
, we now define the set of low multiplicity directions.

{definition}

Given
N\gg 1
and its associated parameter
K
(see Definition
3.3
), we let

G=G_{N,K,\theta}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:f_{N,\theta}^{*}(x)\geq K\}

denote the level set of
f_{N,\theta}^{*}
at height
K
. If
\rho\in(3,\infty)
is some parameter to be chosen later, then we let

(3.5)

E_{N,K}:=\{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{N,K,\theta})\leq K^{-\rho}\}

denote the
set of low multiplicity directions
for the counting function.

In practice, we can take
\rho\rightarrow 3^{+}
, which is proven in Section
6
, so that our proof methodology is valid for any choice of
\rho>3
.

{remark}

The choice of
K^{-\rho}
appearing in the definition of
E_{N,K}
is
intentionally different
than the choice of
K^{-3}
which appear in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
, since the combinatorial estimates do require that this exponent be chose strictly-larger than
3
(see specifically the proof of Lemma
6.1
). This also highlights a portion of the general methodology which we believe to be
sub-optimal
, and thus in need of improvement if one wants to obtain finer control on the power law exponent
\epsilon\in(0,1)
appearing in Theorem
2.4
.

The following lemma makes precise the notion that the set of low multiplicity directions are those where we do
not
observe a large failure of injectivity in the stacking function
f_{N,\theta}^{*}
.

{lemma}

If
N\gg 1
and
K
is its associated stacking parameter (see Definition
3.3
), then for each
\theta\in E_{N,K}^{C}
we necessarily have that

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{S}^{NK^{\rho}})\big)\lesssim\frac{1}{K}.

We will colloquially refer to Lemma
3.3
as a
propagation inequality
because, while our low multiplicity set of directions
E_{N,K}
is defined at scale
N
, the structure of our analysis forces us to consider the Favard length at the later-stage of construction
NK^{\rho}
. Using this estimate, then, we obtain the following reduction.

{proposition}

To establish our power laws for the Favard length problem as in Theorem
2.4
, it is enough to show that there exists some
\beta\in(0,1)
such that

(3.6)

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(E_{N,K}\big)\lesssim K^{-\beta},\quad\forall N\gg 1,

where
K=N^{\nu(\epsilon_{0},N)}
is the stacking parameter from Definition
3.3
.

Proof.

This follows from the simple calculation

\displaystyle\textrm{Fav}(\mathcal{S}^{NK^{\rho}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\bigg(\int_{\theta\in E_{N,K}}\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{S}^{NK^{\rho}})\big)d\theta+\int_{\theta\in E_{N,K}^{C}}\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{S}^{NK^{\rho}})\big)d\theta\bigg)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\bigg(K^{-1}+\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(E_{N,K}\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle N^{-\beta\nu(\epsilon_{0},N)}

where in the final step we used the definition of the stacking parameter
K=N^{\nu(\epsilon_{0},N)}
.
∎

In fact, over the course of our analysis, we will show that
\beta
can be taken arbitrarily-close to
1
.

Hence, our plan-of-attack for proving our Favard length upper bounds is to demonstrate that the low multiplicity set of directions
E_{N,K}
cannot be too large in measure. We set up the general analytic framework for proving the bound (
3.6
) in the next section.

{remark}

Notice that the mismatch in scales between the left-hand and right-hand side of the previous calculations essentially has the final effect of weakening the value of
\epsilon\in(0,1)
appearing in Theorem
2.1
and Theorem
2.3
by a multiplicative factor
(1+\rho\nu(\epsilon_{0},N))^{-1}
. This is one main reason we do
not
track the value of
\epsilon\in(0,1)
in the statement of our Favard length estimates too carefully. However, we do encourage the reader to see Proposition
3.5
for a discussion of how this value of
\epsilon
depends upon the SSV and SLV parameters, as well as the (unfortunately, sub-optimal) choice of
\rho>3
.

3.4.
The counting function and trigonometric polynomials

Similar to
[
3
]
and
[
27
]
, one of our main analytic tools is the following
L^{2}
estimate, which holds for directions in our low multiplicity set
E_{N,K}
. While this result was already known in the plane (first in
[
27
]
and then with an alternative proof given
[
2
, Section 5]
), we will prove the analogous version for all
d\geq 2
in Section
6.2
.

{lemma}

If
\theta\in E_{N,K}
, then

(3.7)

\max_{1\leq n\leq N}||f_{n,\theta}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\leq CK

We will now convert this upper bound on the counting function into an upper bound on a Riesz product of the form (
2.2
). This involves taking the Fourier transform of the left-hand side of (
3.7
). However, we need to write the counting function in a manner which is amenable to an application of Plancherel’s identity. We accomplish this with a suitable change of variables defined below.

If
\theta=(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d})\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d-1}
, then there exists a unique choice of
\rho_{1},...,\rho_{d-1}\in[0,\pi]
such that

\displaystyle(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\cos\rho_{d-1}\cdots\cos\rho_{1},\,\cos\rho_{d-1}\cdots\cos\rho_{2}\sin\rho_{1},

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\cos\rho_{d-1}\cdots\cos\rho_{3}\sin\rho_{2},\,\cdots\,,\,\cos\rho_{d-1}\sin\rho_{d-2},\,\sin\rho_{d-1}).

This is just the representation of
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
in spherical coordinates. By further using the symmetry of the unit cube as in
[
27
]
and
[
3
]
, we may assume that
\rho_{1},...,\rho_{d-1}\in[0,\frac{\pi}{4}]
.

{remark}

To be precise, in order to take advantage of this reflection symmetry described above, we should translate our set
\mathcal{S}^{n}
so that its convex hull is the unit cube with centre at the origin. This amounts to working with the set
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{n}:=[\mathcal{S}^{n}-(1/2,...,1/2)]
. However, one can check that this amounts to composing the counting function
f_{n,\theta}
by a constant translation factor
\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}+\cdots+\frac{\theta_{d}}{2}
, which will not influence our estimates.

Having made this reduction, each
\rho_{j}
then corresponds to the unique value of
t_{j}\in[0,1]
which satisfies the equation

\arctan(t_{j})=\rho_{j}.

Utilizing that for each
v\in[0,1]
we have

\cos(\arctan(v))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+v^{2}}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\sin(\arctan(v))=\frac{v}{\sqrt{1+v^{2}}},

and then performing the change of variables

(3.8)

(\rho_{1},...,\rho_{d-1})\mapsto(\arctan(t_{1}),...,\arctan(t_{d-1}))

in the equation (
3.4
), we obtain that each
\theta\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d-1}
satisfies the matrix equation

(3.9)

\theta=\textrm{diag}(T_{1},T_{2},...,T_{d})(1,t_{1},...,t_{d-1})^{T},

where
\textrm{diag}(T_{1},...,T_{d})
denotes a certain
d\times d
diagonal matrix whose entries depend upon the choice of
t\in[0,1]^{d-1}
, but which satisfy
T_{i}\approx_{d}1
for each
i=1,...,d
. Intuitively, then, we know that the contribution from each of the
T_{i}
should be negligible (since we do not track constants which depend only upon the ambient dimension). We will make this idea precise in a moment, after a short example.

{example}

To illustrate the change of variables the specific case where
d=3
. We begin by writing

\mathbb{S}_{+}^{2}\ni\theta=(\cos\rho_{2}\cos\rho_{1},\cos\rho_{2}\sin\rho_{1},\sin\rho_{2})

for some
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in[0,\frac{\pi}{4}]
. Under the change of variables (
3.8
), this then becomes

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t_{1}^{2}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t_{2}^{2}}},\frac{t_{1}}{\sqrt{1+t_{1}^{2}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t_{2}^{2}}},\frac{t_{2}}{\sqrt{1+t_{2}^{2}}}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\underbrace{(1+t_{1}^{2})^{-1/2}(1+t_{2}^{2})^{-1/2}}_{T_{1}}&0&0\\
0&\underbrace{(1+t_{1}^{2})^{-1/2}(1+t_{2}^{2})^{-1/2}}_{T_{2}}&0\\
0&0&\underbrace{(1+t_{2}^{2})^{-1/2}}_{T_{3}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
t_{1}\\
t_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

The bound
T_{i}\approx_{d}1
then follows since
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\leq(1+t_{i}^{2})^{-1/2}\leq 1
whenever
t_{i}\in[0,1]
and so
2^{-(d-1)/2}\leq T_{i}\leq 1
for each
i=1,2,3
.

So, at least up to the uniformly bounded anistropic scaling described above, for any
1\leq n\leq N
and
t\in[0,1]^{d-1}
, the counting function satisfies

(3.10)

\mathbb{R}\ni x\mapsto f_{n,t}(x)=L^{N}\big(\nu_{n,t}*\mathbf{1}_{[-L^{-N},L^{-N}]}\big)(x),

where
\nu_{n,t}
is build up scale-by-scale by writing

(3.11)

\nu_{n,t}:=\ast_{k=1}^{n}\tilde{\nu}_{k,t}

and letting for each
1\leq k\leq n

(3.12)

\tilde{\nu}_{k,t}:=L^{-1}\sum\limits_{(a_{1},...,a_{d})\in A_{1}\times\cdots A_{d}}\delta_{L^{-k}(a_{1}+t_{1}a_{2}+\cdots+t_{d-1}a_{d})}.

In fact, we should be a bit more transparent in explaining why we are allowed to ignore this anisotropic rescaling (especially since this is a key distinguishing feature between the planar and multidimensional case). To this end, for each
t\in[0,1]^{d-1}
, consider the associated linear functional

p_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R};\quad p_{t}(z_{1},...,z_{d}):=z_{1}+t_{1}z_{2}+\cdots+t_{d-1}z_{d}.

For any
N\gg 1
and any choice of
t\in[0,1]^{d-1}
, there necessarily exist constants
c_{-},c_{+}
which depend only upon
|\textrm{diag}(T_{1},...,T_{d})|
such that for each
Q_{j}:=B(z_{j},L^{-N})\subset\mathcal{S}^{N}
, we have

p_{t}(c_{-}Q_{j})\subset\pi_{\theta}(Q_{j})\subset p_{t}(c_{+}Q_{j}),

and
c_{\pm}Q_{j}:=B(z_{j},c_{\pm}L^{-N})
is a concentric rescaling of the
Q_{j}
. As we have seen that
|\textrm{diag}(T_{1},...,T_{d})|\approx_{d}1
, this choice of
c_{\pm}
can be taken independent of
t
, and so in fact only depends upon the ambient dimension
d\geq 2
. As
N\gg 1
, this rescaling is harmless, and so we take (
3.10
) as the definition of our counting function moving forward. One can check that when
d=2
, this description of the counting function agrees with that of
[
3
]
. However, in the planar setting, the rescaling is
isotropic
, and so we took extra care in the
d\geq 3
case to justify this step of the argument.

Moving forward, we now freely change variables between
\theta
and
t
(at the cost of modifying the radii of our
\ell^{2}
balls in the definition of the counting function by an implicit dimensional constants). As there is a bijective correspondence between our directions
\theta=(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d})\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and the parameters
t=(t_{1},...,t_{d-1})\in[0,1]^{d-1}
, we will often freely change between the parameters
\theta
and
t
when necessary. For example, if we write
t\in E_{N,K}
, we mean
t\sim\theta\in E_{N,K}
(and so on).

Recall that Lemma
3.4
then shows that, for each
\theta\in E_{N,K}
, we have that

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f_{N,\theta}(x)|^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{f_{N,\theta}}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\gtrsim_{d}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|\widehat{\nu_{N,t}}(\xi)(L^{N}\widehat{\mathbf{1}}_{[-L^{-N},\,L^{-N}]})(\xi)\big|^{2}d\xi

where we include the dependence on
d\geq 2
in the second inequality because of the anisotropic rescaling. When
N\gg 1
, one can verify that the Bessel function
\psi(\xi):=L^{N}\widehat{\mathbf{1}}_{[-L^{-N},\,L^{-N}]}(\xi)
satisfies
\lvert\psi(\xi)\rvert\gtrsim 1
so long as
|\xi|\leq L^{N/2}
. Hence, by applying the estimate for
\psi
in the interval
[0,L^{N/2}]
and averaging the above inequality over
E_{N,K}
, we see that

(3.13)

\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K})}\int_{t\in E_{N,K}}\bigg[\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N/2}\int_{L^{n-1}}^{L^{n}}|\widehat{\nu_{N,t}}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\bigg]dt\leq K.

We now restrict ourselves to a certain subset of the
N/2
-many intervals appearing in the inner integral of (
3.13
). This corresponds to ignoring the low-frequency part of the region of integration. To make this choice precise, we will use a pigeonholing argument relative to the following parameter.

{definition}

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be a parameter which depends upon
A_{1},...,A_{d}
,
N
and
\epsilon_{0}
in the following manner:

(1)

If
A_{i}^{(3)}\equiv 1
for each
i\in\{1,...,d\}
, we let
m=\lceil\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}\log_{L}N\rceil
;

(2)

Otherwise, let
m=\lceil\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}\frac{\log_{L}N}{\log_{L}\log_{L}N}\rceil
.

If one compares the above with the stacking parameter
K
introduced in Definition
3.3
, one can readily see that
m
is chosen so as to guarantee that
K=L^{\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}m}
. Moreover, we have the following characterization of the set of small values function
\psi
(see Definition
3.1
).

{lemma}

Suppose that
m
is as given in Definition
3.4
and
\psi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is as defined in Definition
3.1
and that
N\gg 1
. Then there exist a choices of
\kappa^{-},\kappa^{+}\in(c_{1}/2,c_{1}]
such that

(3.14)

N^{-\kappa^{-}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}\lesssim\psi(m)\lesssim N^{-\kappa^{+}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}.

where
c_{1}
denotes the set of small values constant from Definition
3.1
.

In practice, we will usually only write
\psi(m)\approx N^{-\kappa\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}
, with the implicit understanding that the value of
\kappa\in[c_{1}/2,c_{1}]
may need to change line-by-line in order for the asymptotic inequalities to be made precise. Note that this consideration is not an issue in the SSV case (as the following proof shows).

Proof.

If
\phi_{t}^{\prime}
has the SSV property, then
m=\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}\log_{L}N
, and so

\psi(m)=L^{-c_{1}m}:=L^{-\log_{L}N^{c_{1}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}}=N^{-c_{1}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}},

and so we take
\kappa^{-}=\kappa^{+}=c_{1}
and obtain (
3.14
).

If
\phi_{t}^{\prime}
instead has the log-SSV property, so that then
m=\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}\frac{\log_{L}N}{\log_{L}\log_{L}N}
, then

\displaystyle\psi(m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{-c_{1}m\log m}:=L^{-\frac{\log_{L}N^{c_{1}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}}{\log_{L}\log_{L}N}\times\log_{L}\bigg(\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}\frac{\log_{L}N}{\log_{L}\log_{L}N}\bigg)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N^{-c_{1}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}\bigg(\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}\frac{\log_{L}N}{\log_{L}\log_{L}N}\bigg).

Here, we critically use that
\epsilon_{0}
is independent of
N
, and thus we can choose
N\gg 1
large enough so that there are
\kappa^{-},\kappa^{+}\in[c_{1}/2,c_{1}]
satisfying (
3.14
). We remark that we can always assume
c_{1}>2
so that
\kappa^{-},\kappa^{+}>1
(this follows from the SSV argument in
[
3
, Section 4]
).
∎

By pigeonholing inequality (
3.13
) relative to this choice of
m
, there exists a corresponding choice of integer
n\in[N/4,N/2]
which satisfies

(3.15)

\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K})}\int_{E_{N,K}}\bigg[\int_{L^{n-m}}^{L^{n}}|\widehat{\nu_{N,t}}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\bigg]dt\leq\frac{CKm}{N}.

This allows us to pass to a modified low multiplicity set of directions

E_{N,K}^{*}:=\big\{t\in E_{N,K}:\int_{L^{n-m}}^{L^{n}}|\widehat{\nu_{N}}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi\leq\frac{2CKm}{N}\big\},

(which is more suitable to a reduction of frequencies, as we shall see shortly). In order to justify passing to this subset of the low multiplicity directions, we notice that in the definition of
E_{N,K}^{*}
, we have that

\int_{E_{N,K}^{*}}\bigg[\int_{L^{n-m}}^{L^{n}}\lvert\widehat{\nu_{N,t}}(\xi)\rvert^{2}d\xi\bigg]dt\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})\bigg[\frac{2CKm}{N}\bigg]\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K})\bigg[\frac{CKm}{N}\bigg]

where the last inequality is given by (
3.15
). In particular, we have

\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})\geq\frac{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K})}{2}.

As noted previously, for
t\in E_{N,K}^{*}
, we can also reduce the upper frequencies of our Riesz product, so that

\int_{L^{n-m}}^{L^{n}}|\widehat{\nu_{N,t}}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi:=\int_{L^{n-m}}^{L^{n}}\bigg|\prod_{k=1}^{N}\phi_{t}(L^{-k}\xi)\bigg|^{2}d\xi\approx\int_{L^{n-m}}^{L^{n}}\bigg|\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi_{t}(L^{-k}\xi)\bigg|^{2}d\xi.

This follows because
\phi_{t}(L^{-k}\xi)\rightarrow 1
rapidly as
k\rightarrow\infty
whenever
x\in[L^{n-m},L^{n}]
. Hence, for each
t\in E_{N,K}^{*}
, one can say that

\int_{L^{n-m}}^{L^{n}}\lvert\widehat{\nu_{n,t}}(L^{-k}\xi)\rvert^{2}d\xi\leq\frac{2CKm}{N}

We also now make the change of variables
\xi\mapsto L^{n}\xi
, so that the above becomes

(3.16)

\int_{L^{-m}}^{1}\big\lvert\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi_{t}(L^{k}\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi\lesssim\frac{Km}{L^{n}N}

and so that our region of integration is now the (almost unit) interval
[L^{-m},1]
. We now state the strategy of proof for our Favard length estimates.

{proposition}

Let
0<\epsilon_{0}\ll 1
be sufficiently small and suppose that

2\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})\geq\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K})>K^{-\beta},

for any
\beta\in(0,1)
.
Then, there exists a
t\in E_{N,K}^{*}
and an constant
\alpha=\alpha_{A_{1},...,A_{d}}
, which is independent of
\epsilon_{0}
and
\beta
, such that

(3.17)

\int_{L^{-m}}^{1}\big\lvert\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi_{t}(L^{k}\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi\gtrsim\frac{K}{L^{n}N^{\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}}

Moreover, if one wishes to make this choice of
\alpha
explicit, then we may take

(3.18)

\alpha_{A_{1},...,A_{d}}=\begin{cases}c_{1}+C_{1},\textrm{ if }A_{i}^{(3)}\equiv 1\textrm{ for all }i=1,...,d\\[4.30554pt]
3c_{1},\textrm{ otherwise }\end{cases}

where the constant
C_{1}>0
is taken from the SLV property (see Definition
3.2
and the constant
c_{1}\geq 2
is taken from the SSV property (see Definition
3.1
) for the functions
\phi_{A_{1}},...,\phi_{A_{d}}
.

We assume that Proposition
3.4
holds and prove the main result of this article.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

This is essentially a comparison of the inequalities (
3.16
) and (
3.17
), which together give

\frac{K}{L^{n}N^{\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}}\lesssim\int_{L^{-m}}^{1}\big\lvert\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi_{t}(L^{k}\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi\lesssim\frac{Km}{L^{n}N}.

for any
N\gg 1
chosen large enough. Canceling like terms, this becomes

N^{1-\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}\lesssim m.

Now, regardless of which choice of
m
and was required (recall: this choice was determined in Definition
3.4
) and using that
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
we always have that
m\lesssim\log N
. Recalling that
\alpha
does not depend upon
\epsilon_{0}
, we choose

(3.19)

\epsilon_{0}>\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}

so that

N^{\alpha^{\prime}}\lesssim\log N,

for some
\alpha^{\prime}>0
. This is clearly false if
N
is taken large enough. Since both (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) can hold for at most finitely-many
N
, we must have that

\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K})\leq\frac{1}{K^{\beta}},

and Theorem
2.1
follows from Proposition
3.3
.
∎

We will give the proof of Proposition
3.4
in the following Section. We remark, however, that this is explicitely a proof by contradiction, since we are
assuming
that the modified set of low multiplicity is large, so that

\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})>\frac{1}{2K^{\beta}},\textrm{ for some arbitrary }\beta\in(0,1),

and then using this information to arrive at the “impossible” lower bound (
3.17
).

3.5.
A consideration of the exponent

We conclude this section by giving some consideration of the dependencies of the exponent
\epsilon\in(0,1)
appearing in Theorem
2.4
. We first state our result (which is necessarily somewhat technical, but fully elucidates these dependencies) before providing a more plain-language explanation. A proof then follows.

{proposition}

Given
\rho\in(3,\infty)
and
\beta\in(0,1)
, there exists a continuous function
\delta=\delta_{\beta,\rho}:[1,\infty)\times(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,1)
which satisfies, for any fixed
s_{0}\in[1,\infty)
and
t_{0}\in[0,\infty)
, the two conditions

(1)

The single-variable functions
\delta(s,t_{0})
and
\delta(s_{0},t)
are both strictly-decreasing functions; and also

(2)

\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\delta(s,t_{0})=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\delta(s_{0},t)=0

and such that, if
A_{1},...,A_{d}
are any digit sets which satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
2.4

and whose mask polynomials satisfy
A_{i}^{(3)}\equiv 1
for each
i=1,...,d
(see Definition
2.2
), then the associated rational product Cantor set
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
has the following power law decay

\textrm{Fav}\big(\mathcal{N}_{L^{-N}}(\mathcal{S}^{\infty})\big)\lesssim N^{-\delta(c_{1},C_{1})}

where the constants
c_{1}
and
C_{1}
are derived from the SSV and SLV properties of the function
\phi_{t}=\phi_{A_{1}}\cdots\phi_{A_{d}}
(see Definition
3.1
and Definition
3.2
).

Moreover, this function
\delta
does not depend in any way upon the digit sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}
, nor does it depend upon the ambient dimension
d\geq 2
, but rather depends solely upon the parameters
\rho\in(3,\infty)
and
\beta\in(0,1)
.

Recall that the constants
c_{1},C_{1}
essentially control the size of the sublevel (in the case of
c_{1}
) and superlevel (in the case of
C_{1}
) sets of our lacunary Riesz products. Indeed, if we re-examine these two level sets defined in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we notice that the SSV and SLV properties become
easier to satisfy
as the associated constants
c_{1}
and
C_{1}
become larger. Hence, condition
(2)
is perhaps not surprising, as we expect
better estimates
on the Favard length as our estimates for Riesz products become stronger.

Proposition
3.5
, tells us that the final exponent
\epsilon\in(0,1)
which we obtain is, in fact, sensitive to
exactly to what level of strength
our functions
\phi_{t}^{\prime}
and
\phi_{t}^{\prime\prime}
satisfy the SSV and SLV property (respectively). While this does not provide any concrete estimate upon the size of our exponent
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, this at least elucidates the finer relationship between these special properties for the level sets of lacunary Riesz products and the exacty power law decay for the asymptotics of the Favard length problem. Moreover, it strongly suggests that, in order to prove more delicate statements (such as those required by the hypothesis of
Vitushkin’s conjecture
—see
[
13
]
,
[
38
]
for some discussion) one must do a significantly finer analysis of the SSV and SLV property in order to make any reasonable progress.

{remark}

Proposition
3.5
is the first
explicit
description in the literature of the dependencies of the exponent
\epsilon\in(0,1)
upon the SSV and SLV properties for general rational product Cantor sets.

Proof.

The assumption that
A_{i}\equiv 1
allows us to assume that
K=N^{\nu(\epsilon_{0},N)}=N^{\epsilon_{0}}
. Then, from the proof of Proposition
3.3
, see that

\textrm{Fav}\big(\mathcal{S}^{NK^{\rho}})=\textrm{Fav}\big(\mathcal{S}^{N^{1+\rho\epsilon_{0}}}\big)\lesssim N^{-\beta\epsilon_{0}},

where
\rho\in(3,\infty)
was the parameter used to define the set
E_{N,K}
and
\beta\in(0,1)
controls the measure of the low multiplicity set of directions.
Making the change of variables
M=N^{1+\rho\epsilon_{0}}
so that
N=M^{\frac{1}{1+\rho\epsilon_{0}}}
, we see that

\textrm{Fav}(\mathcal{S}^{M})\lesssim M^{-\frac{\beta\epsilon_{0}}{1+\rho\epsilon_{0}}}.

We now need to do some work unwinding what the parameter
\epsilon_{0}
depends upon. When we assumed Proposition
3.4
and proved Theorem
2.4
, we assumed that

\epsilon_{0}>\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}=\frac{1}{(c_{1}+C_{1})^{2}},

where we used the explicit representation of
\alpha=\alpha_{A_{1},...,A_{d}}
given in Proposition
3.4
. So, let us choose

\epsilon_{0}=\frac{\beta^{-1}}{(c_{1}+C_{1})^{2}},

so that we have that

\frac{\beta\epsilon_{0}}{1+\rho\epsilon_{0}}=\frac{(c_{1}+C_{2})^{-2}}{1+\beta^{-1}\rho(c_{1}+C_{2})^{-2}}=\frac{1}{(c_{1}+C_{2})^{2}+\beta^{-1}\rho}

In particular, if we take

\delta(s,t):=\frac{1}{(s+t)^{2}+\beta^{-1}\rho},

then we obtain Proposition
3.5
.
∎

Proposition
3.5
in fact illustrates a key point of the Nazarov, Peres and Volberg machinery for the four-corner Cantor set in
[
27
]
. Namely, since the mask polynomial of the digit set
A:=\{1,3\}
satisfies

A(X):=1+X^{3}=(1+X)(X^{2}-X+1)=\Phi_{2}(X)\Phi_{6}(X),

we see that
A(X):=A^{(1)}(X)
, so that
A^{(i)}\equiv 1
for all
i=2,3,4
. In particular, we can take
C_{1}=0
(as the SLV property is vacuously satisfied for such a digit set) and, as part of the trigonometric polnomial estimates in
[
27
, top of pg. 90]
, we can also take
c_{1}=1
. In particular, for any
u\in(1/4,\infty]
, there exist corresponding choices of
\rho=\rho(u)\in(3,\infty)
and
\beta=\beta(u)\in(0,1)
such that

\textrm{Fav}\big(\mathcal{N}_{4^{-N}}(\mathcal{K}^{\infty})\big)\lesssim N^{-\delta(1,0)}=N^{-\frac{1}{4}+u}

which, in fact, is concretely better than the exponent obtained in
[
27
, Theorem 1]
. However, this improvement was at least known to the authors, who mention it in the remark immediately following this Theorem in
[
27
]
. Nevertheless, this verifies that our Proposition
3.5
recovers the best possible power law decay for the four-corner set.

Proposition
3.5
also explains the discrepancy between the concrete value of exponent appearing in
[
27
]
and the more nebulous values appearing in our work, as well as
[
3
]
and
[
22
]
. Moreover, it demonstrates that to have any reasonable improvement over the bound of Nazarov, Peres and Volberg in
[
27
]
for general self-similar sets, one must
simultaneously
have finer estimates for the constants associated to the SSV/SLV properties, while also having a more-effective combinatorial argument than the one given by Lemma
3.3
and presented in Section
6
.

4.
Lower Bounds on Riesz Products

In this Section, we prove Proposition
3.4
(and, by extension, Theorem
2.1
) assuming all intermediate results of the previous Section
3
. The remaining unproven results—the set of large values construction and the combinatorial lemmata—are then proven in Section
5
and Section
6
. The parameters
N,K,m,n,\beta,\rho
defined in Section
3
continue to have the same meaning and interrelationships as before, as do the SSV property constants
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
and the SLV property constants
(C_{1},C_{2})
.

Section
4.1
leverages the set of small values property to obtain a good bound on the high-frequency portion of our Riesz product over the covering intervals for the sublevel set of our Riesz product. Section
4.2
proves an estimate for the high-frequency portion of our Riesz product over the short interval
[0,L^{-m}]
. Section
4.3
then combine these two estimates for the high frequency portion of our Riesz product to prove our main reduction, which is Proposition
3.4

To clarify what we mean by the
high frequency portion
of our Riesz product, we introduce the following frequency decompositions of the integrand in (
3.17
). Write

\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi_{t}(L^{k}\xi):=\bigg(\underbrace{\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\phi_{t}(L^{k}\xi)}_{P_{1},t(\xi)}\bigg)\cdot\bigg(\underbrace{\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\phi_{t}(L^{k}\xi)}_{P_{2,t}(\xi)}\bigg)

so that
P_{1,t}
and
P_{2,t}
correspond to the low- and high-frequency parts of the Riesz product. We will, as it becomes necessary in the proof of Proposition
3.4
, further factor these two trigonometric polynomials
P_{1,t}
and
P_{2,t}
relative to the sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}
and also their further factorizations
A_{i}^{\prime},A_{i}^{\prime\prime}
introduced in Definition
2.2
.

4.1.
The set of small values estimate

For this section, we encourage the reader to review the definition of a set of small values (Definition
3.1
) which is associated to a size function
\psi(m)
which depends upon the parameter
m
introduced in Definition
3.4
, and which also depends upon certain associated parameters
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
. To each
t\in[0,1]^{d-1}
we associate the following sublevel set

SSV=SSV_{\psi}(t):=\bigg\{\xi\in[0,1]:\bigg\lvert\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\phi_{t}^{\prime}(L^{k}\xi)\bigg\rvert\lesssim\psi(m)\bigg\}.

We can assume that
\lvert P_{2,t}^{\prime}(\xi)\rvert\gtrsim\psi(m)
(perhaps by slightly enlarging the constant
c_{1}
) for
\xi\not\in SSV_{\psi}
. We can also freely assume that
c_{2}>c_{d}
for some dimensional constant
c_{d}\geq 2
, perhaps by subdividing our original family of covering intervals for the set of small values and making the change-of-parameters
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})\mapsto(c_{1},c_{2}+c_{d},c_{3}+c_{d})
. This set-up is identical to that of
[
3
]
. We then prove the following.

{proposition}

Suppose that
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
is chosen small enough, and that

\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})\geq\frac{1}{2K^{\beta}}.

Then, we have the estimate

\mathsf{J}:=\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}\bigg[\int_{SSV(t)\cap[L^{-m},1]}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi\bigg]dt\leq\frac{C_{0}}{4}KL^{-n}.

Furthermore, there exists a
t_{0}\in E_{N,K}^{*}
such that

(4.1)

\int_{SSV(t_{0})\cap[L^{-m},1]}\big\lvert P_{1,t_{0}}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi\leq C_{0}KL^{-n}

Proposition
4.1
is an exact generalization of
[
3
, Proposition 3.1, Corollary 3.2]
, and our proof follows a similar strategy (with the added difficulty of needing to handle the multiple integrations in the variables
t_{1},...,t_{d-1}
as opposed to a single integration in
t=t_{1}
in the case where
d=2
).

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

For the purpose of our analysis, we assign to each of the functions
\phi_{A_{1}}^{\prime},...,\phi_{A_{d-1}}^{\prime}
an associated sublevel set

SSV_{A_{i}}(t):=\bigg\{\xi\in[0,1]:\bigg\lvert\prod_{k=0}^{m}\phi_{A_{i}}^{\prime}(L^{k}\xi)\bigg\rvert\lesssim\psi(m)\bigg\}.

and observe that because
||\phi_{A_{i}}||_{\infty}\leq 1
, we have

(4.2)

SSV(t)\subset SSV_{A_{1}}(t)\cup\cdots\cup SSV_{A_{d}}(t).

Since for any
t=(t_{1},...,t_{d-1})\in[0,1]^{d-1}
, we have

\phi_{t}(\xi)=\phi_{A_{1}}(\xi)\phi_{A_{2}}(t_{1}\xi)\cdots\phi_{A_{d}}(t_{d-1}\xi).

we can factor the trigonometric polynomials
P_{1,t}
and
P_{2,t}
as

P_{1,t}(\xi):=\prod_{i=1}^{d}P_{1,t}^{(i)}(\xi),\,P_{2,t}(\xi):=\prod_{i=1}^{d}P_{2,t}^{(i)}(\xi)

where

\displaystyle P_{1,t}^{(1)}(\xi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\phi_{A_{1}}(L^{k}\xi)=(\#A_{1})^{-(n-m)}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert A_{1}(e^{2\pi iL^{k}\xi})\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle P_{2,t}^{(1)}(\xi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\phi_{A_{1}}(L^{k}\xi)=(\#A_{1})^{-m}\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\lvert A_{1}(e^{2\pi iL^{k}\xi})\rvert^{2}

and for each
2\leq i\leq d
, we have

\displaystyle{P}_{1,t}^{(i)}(\xi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\phi_{A_{i}}\big(L^{k}(t_{i-1}\xi)\big)=(\#A_{i})^{-(n-m)}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert A_{i}(e^{2\pi iL^{k}t_{i-1}\xi})|^{2}

\displaystyle P_{2,t}^{(i)}(\xi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\phi_{A_{i}}\big(L^{k}(t_{i-1}\xi)\big)=(\#A_{i})^{-m}\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\lvert A_{i}(e^{2\pi iL^{k}t_{i-1}\xi})\rvert^{2}

Using this factorization together with the containment (
4.2
), we see that

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}\bigg[\int_{SSV(t)\cap[L^{-m},1]}\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\rvert^{2}d\xi\bigg]dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\bigg[\int_{SSV_{A_{i}}(t)\cap[L^{-m},1]}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\,\lvert P_{1,t}^{(i)}(\xi)\rvert^{2}d\xi\bigg]dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\bigg[\underbrace{\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}\int_{SSV_{A_{i}}(t)\cap[L^{-m},1]}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\,\lvert P_{1,t}^{(i)}(\xi)\rvert^{2}d\xi}_{\mathsf{J}_{i}}\bigg]dt,

so that each of the expressions
\mathsf{J}_{1},...,\mathsf{J}_{d}
consists of an outer integration in
t\in[0,1]^{d-1}
as well as an inner integration over
SSV_{A_{i}}(t)
. We will show that

(4.3)

\mathsf{J}_{1}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{4d}KL^{-n}

and (as the calculations for
\mathsf{J}_{2},...,\mathsf{J}_{d}
are essentially the same, up to a reparametrization of the sphere) we thus obtain that:

\mathsf{J}\leq\mathsf{J}_{1}+\cdots+\mathsf{J}_{d}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{4}KL^{-n}

which is the first estimate appearing in Proposition
4.1
.

We now recall that Lemma
3.1
states that each of the functions
\phi_{A_{1}}^{\prime},...,\phi_{A_{d}}^{\prime}
has (at worst) the log-SSV property.
In particular, the set
SSV_{A_{1}}
is contained in
L^{c_{2}m}
-many intervals of length
L^{-c_{3}m}
, with
c_{3}\gg c_{2}
(of course, the choice of
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
depend upon
A_{1}
, but we suppress this dependence). Letting
\ell=c_{3}m
, we let
a_{j}\in[L^{-m},1]
denote the left-hand endpoints of these covering intervals for
SSV_{A_{1}}
. Replacing the
\xi
region of integration with these covering
SSV_{A_{i}}
intervals and then changing the order of integration gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\sum\limits_{j}\int_{a_{j}}^{a_{j}+L^{-\ell}}\lvert P_{1,t}^{(1)}(\xi)\rvert^{2}\bigg(\int_{[0,1]^{d-1}}\prod_{i=2}^{d}\,\lvert P_{1,t}^{(i)}(\xi)\rvert^{2}dt\bigg)d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\sum\limits_{j}\int_{a_{j}}^{a_{j}+L^{-\ell}}\bigg[\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{1}}(\xi)\rvert^{2}\times\big(\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{2}}(L^{k}t_{1}\xi)dt_{1}\rvert^{2}\bigg)\times\cdots

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{d}}(L^{k}t_{d-1}\xi)\rvert^{2}dt_{d-1}\bigg)\bigg]d\xi

We now wish to separate the variables
\xi
and
t
. This is accomplished by writing

(\xi,t_{1},...,t_{d-1})\mapsto(\xi,u_{2},...,u_{d})

where
u_{i}:=t_{i-1}\xi
so that
dt_{i-1}=du_{i}/\xi
for each
i=2,3,...,d
. Since
\xi\in[L^{-m},1]
and our integrand is non-negative, we can slightly enlarge the region of integration in each of the
u_{i}
variables to obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\bigg(\sum\limits_{j}\int_{a_{j}}^{a_{j}+L^{-\ell}}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{1}}(\xi)\rvert^{2}\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{d-1}}\bigg)\times\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{2}}(L^{k}u_{2})\rvert^{2}du_{2}\bigg)\times\cdots

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{d}}(L^{k}u_{d})\rvert^{2}du_{d}\bigg)

Now that we have decoupled our variables
\xi
and
t_{1},...,t_{d-1}
, we can evaluate the integrals in the
u_{i}
variables by using the following simple size bound, which is
[
3
, Lemma 3.3]
.

{lemma}

Let
s\in[m,n-1]
and
i=1,2,...,d
. Then, for every
u_{0}\in[0,1]
, we have the estimate

(4.4)

\int_{u_{0}}^{u_{0}+L^{-m}}\bigg\lvert\prod_{k=m}^{s}A_{i}(e^{2\pi iL^{k}u})\bigg\rvert^{2}du\lesssim(\#A_{i})^{s-m}L^{-m}

Proof.

This follows from the fact that

(4.5)

\prod_{k=m}^{s}A_{i}(e^{2\pi iL^{k}u})=\sum\limits_{(a_{0},...,a_{s-m})\in A_{i}^{s-m+1}}e^{2\pi i\big(L^{m}a_{0}+L^{m+1}a_{2}+\cdots+a_{s-m}L^{s}\big)\xi}

where

A_{i}^{s-m+1}:=\underbrace{A_{i}\times\cdots\times A_{i}}_{s-m+1\textrm{ many times}}.

Since we assumed that
\max A_{i}\leq L-1
, we know the frequencies which appear on the right-hand side of (
4.5
) are distinct. Moreover, it is clear from (
4.5
) that the integrand in (
4.4
) is a periodic function with period
L^{-m}
. The bound (
4.4
) then follows from a simple integration, where only the diagonal frequency terms survive and one is integrating over an interval of length
L^{-m}
.
∎

Lemma
4.1
really only claims that, so long as we integrate along an interval which is strictly-larger than the period of our trigonometric polynomial, there is almost-exact cancellation in all off-diagonal terms. Notice that, since

\lvert\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\phi_{A_{i}}(L^{k}u_{i})\rvert^{2}:=(\#A_{i})^{-2(n-m)}\lvert\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}A_{i}(e^{2\pi iL^{k}u_{i}})\rvert^{2}

an application of Lemma
4.1
for each
i=2,...,d
shows that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\lvert\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\phi_{A_{i}}(L^{k}u_{i})\rvert^{2}du_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\#A_{i})^{-2(n-m)}\int_{0}^{1}\lvert\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}A_{i}(e^{2\pi iL^{k}u_{i}})\rvert^{2}du_{i}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(\#A_{i})^{-(n-m)}.

Applying this estimate to each of the
u_{i}
integrals then gives that

\mathsf{J}_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\frac{(\#A_{1})^{n-m}}{L^{n-m}}\bigg(\sum\limits_{j}\int_{a_{j}}^{a_{j}+L^{-\ell}}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{1}}(\xi)\rvert^{2}\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{d-1}}\bigg)

We now turn to estimating the integral in
\xi
, which is taken over the covering intervals for
SSV_{A_{1}}
. First, since we know that
a_{j}\geq L^{-m}
, we always have that
\xi^{-(d-1)}\leq L^{(d-1)m}
, so that

\mathsf{J}_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\frac{(\#A_{1})^{n-m}L^{(d-1)m}}{L^{n-m}}\bigg(\sum\limits_{j}\int_{a_{j}}^{a_{j}+L^{-\ell}}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{1}}(\xi)\rvert^{2}d\xi\bigg)

Now, let
a_{*}\in\{a_{j}\}
be a left-hand endpoint of a maximizing interval from the collection of covering intervals given by the SSV property. This just means that
a_{*}
is chosen so that,

\int_{a_{j}}^{a_{j}+L^{-\ell}}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{1}}(\xi)\rvert^{2}d\xi\leq\int_{a_{*}}^{a_{*}+L^{-\ell}}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{1}}(\xi)\rvert^{2}d\xi,\textrm{ for all }j=1,...,\lceil c_{2}m\rceil.

Using this maximization bound (and recalling that
SSV_{A_{1}}
is contained in
L^{c_{2}m}
-many intervals of the form
[a_{j},a_{j}+L^{-c_{3}m}]
) gives

\mathsf{J}_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\frac{(\#A_{1})^{n-m}L^{(d-1)m}L^{c_{2}m}}{L^{n-m}}\bigg(\int_{a_{*}}^{a_{*}+L^{-\ell}}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{1}}(\xi)\rvert^{2}d\xi\bigg).

We are finally in a position to bound the inner integral. By splitting frequences, we can apply a trivial estimate and then apply Lemma
4.1
to obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{a_{*}}^{a_{*}+L^{-\ell}}\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\lvert\phi_{A_{1}}(\xi)\rvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\#A_{1})^{-2(n-m)}\int_{a_{*}}^{a_{*}+L^{-\ell}}\bigg\lvert\bigg(\prod_{k=m}^{\ell-1}A_{1}(e^{2\pi iL^{k}\xi})\bigg)\bigg(\prod_{k=\ell}^{n-1}A_{1}(e^{2\pi iL^{k}\xi})\bigg)\bigg\rvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\#A_{1})^{-2(n-m)}(\#A_{1})^{2(\ell-m)}\int_{a_{*}}^{a_{*}+L^{-\ell}}\bigg\lvert\prod_{k=\ell}^{n-1}A_{1}(e^{2\pi iL^{k}\xi})\bigg\rvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\#A_{1})^{-2(n-m)}(\#A_{1})^{2(\ell-m)}(\#A_{1})^{n-\ell}L^{-\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\#A_{1})^{-(n-l)}L^{-\ell}

Combining all of our previous calculations and recalling that
\ell=c_{3}m
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathsf{J}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\frac{(\#A_{1})^{n-m}L^{(d-1)m}L^{c_{2}m}}{L^{n-m}}(\#A_{1})^{-(n-l)}L^{-c_{3}m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\frac{L^{(d+c_{2}-c_{3})m}}{(\#A_{1})^{m-c_{3}m}}L^{-n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}\frac{L^{(d-1)m}L^{c_{2}m}}{[(\#A_{2})\times\cdots\times(\#A_{d})]^{(c_{3}-1)m}}L^{-n},

where in the last inequality we used that
\#A_{1}:=\frac{L}{\#A_{2}\cdots\#A_{d}}
.
There necessarily exists a constant
c_{4}>1
which depends only upon the cardinalities of the sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}
such that

(\#A_{2})\times\cdots\times(\#A_{d})=L^{c_{4}}.

Making this substitution, we can write our previous estimate as

\mathsf{J}_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})}L^{(d-1)m}L^{c_{2}m}L^{-c_{4}(c_{3}-1)m}L^{-n}

Recall that we are assuming that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})\geq\frac{1}{2}K^{-\beta}
, while our current goal is to show that
\mathsf{J}_{1}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{4d}KL^{-n}
. Based on our previous work, we must then show that

(4.6)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})\geq\frac{4d}{C_{0}}\frac{L^{(d-1+c_{2}-c_{4}c_{3}+c_{4})m}}{K}

We are assuming that

\mathcal{H}^{1}(E_{N,K}^{*})\geq\frac{1}{2}K^{-\beta}=L^{-\beta\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}m}

and so we will have (
4.6
) if we can show that

K^{1-\beta}=L^{(1-\beta)\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}m}\geq\frac{2d}{C_{0}}L^{(d-1+c_{2}-c_{3}c_{4}+c_{4})m}.

This will certainly hold if we can choose

(4.7)

c_{3}c_{4}-c_{2}\geq d-1+c_{4}-(1-\beta)\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}m.

However,
c_{4}
depends only upon the cardinalities of our digit sets and also satisfies
c_{4}>1
. Using the second part of Lemma
3.1
, we can freely assume that
c_{2},c_{3}>d-1+c_{4}
and that
c_{3}\geq 2c_{2}
, and so we obtain (
4.7
). (Notice that this choice did not depend at all upon the value of
\epsilon_{0}
or
\beta
. This is intentional as, at this step, we do not need these parameters to be small to guarantee that
\mathsf{J}_{1}
is bounded).
∎

4.2.
Poisson localization

In this short section, we prove the following result, which was first proven when
d=2
in
[
6
]
.

{proposition}

Suppose that
t\in E_{N,K}^{*}
. Then,

(4.8)

\int_{0}^{L^{-m}}\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\rvert^{2}d\xi:=\int_{0}^{L^{-m}}\bigg\lvert\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\phi_{t}(L^{k}\xi)\bigg|^{2}d\xi\leq C_{0}KL^{-n}

The proof we present requires the following Lemma, which is proven in detail Section 5.4 of
[
2
]
and first appeared in
[
6
]
.

{lemma}

Let
\delta>0
and suppose that
\mathcal{A}:=\{\alpha\}\subset\mathbb{R}
is finite and satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbf{1}_{[\alpha-\delta,\alpha+\delta]}(x)\bigg)^{2}dx\leq S

for some
S>0
which may depend upon
\delta>0
. Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for any
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
one has

\delta^{2}\int_{\xi_{0}}^{\xi_{0}+\delta^{-1}}\bigg\lvert\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}c_{\alpha}e^{i\alpha\xi}\bigg\rvert^{2}d\xi\leq CS

where
\{c_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\subset\mathbb{C}
is any list satisfying
|c_{\alpha}|=1
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

We begin by recalling that

(4.9)

\prod_{k=1}^{n-m}\phi_{t}(L^{-k}\xi)=\widehat{\nu_{n-m,t}}(\xi)=L^{{}^{-(n-m)}}\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{t}^{n-m}}e^{-2\pi i\alpha\xi},

where
\nu_{n-m,t}
corresponds to the
(n-m)
-fold convolution defined in (
3.11
) and (
3.12
), and where
\mathcal{A}_{t}^{n-m}
corresponds to those
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
which are (again, up to an anistropic rescaling) the projected centres of our self-similar set
\mathcal{S}^{n-m}
. More precisely, we have

\alpha=z_{1,j}+t_{1}z_{2,j}+\cdots+t_{d-1}z_{d,j}

for some
z_{j}=(z_{1,j},...,z_{d,j})
which satisfies
B(z_{j},L^{-(n-m)})\subset\mathcal{S}^{n-m}
. However, if
t\in E_{N,K}^{*}\subset E_{N,K}
, then Lemma
3.4
tells us that

||f_{n-m,t}||_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg(\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{t}^{n-m}}\mathbf{1}_{[\alpha-L^{-(n-m),},\alpha+L^{-(n-m)}]}(x)\bigg)^{2}dx\leq K.

Hence, if we make the change-of-variables
\xi\mapsto L^{n}\xi
in (
4.8
) and then use the substitution (
4.9
), we see that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L^{-m}}\bigg\lvert\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\phi_{t}(L^{k}\xi)\bigg|^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{-n}\int_{0}^{L^{n-m}}\bigg\lvert\prod_{k=1}^{n-m}\phi_{t}(L^{-k}\xi)\bigg\rvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{-n}\bigg(L^{-2(n-m)}\int_{0}^{L^{n-m}}\bigg\lvert\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{t}^{n-m}}e^{-2\pi i\alpha\xi}\bigg\rvert^{2}d\xi\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CL^{-n}K

where the final inequality comes by applying Lemma
4.2
with
\delta=L^{-(n-m)}
and
S=K
.
∎

4.3.
The final calculation

Combining Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
from the previous two sections, we can now prove the following.

{proposition}

Suppose that
N\gg 1
and that
K
is the stacking parameter chosen as in Definition
3.3
with
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
chosen appropriately small. Further suppose that there exists a
t\in E_{N,K}^{*}
such that the function
\phi_{t}^{\prime\prime}(\xi):=\phi_{A_{1}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\phi_{A_{2}}^{\prime\prime}(t_{1}\xi)\cdots\phi_{A_{d}}^{\prime\prime}(t_{d-1}\xi)
is SLV-structured.

Then, for this same choice of
t\in E_{N,K}^{*}
, there exists an
\alpha=\alpha_{A_{1},...,A_{d}}
, which is independent of
\epsilon_{0}
, such that

(4.10)

\int_{L^{-m}}^{1}\big\lvert\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi_{t}(L^{k}\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi\gtrsim\frac{K}{L^{n}N^{\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}}

Moreover, if one wishes to make this choice of
\alpha
explicit, then we may take

\alpha_{A_{1},...,A_{d}}=\begin{cases}c_{1}+C_{1},\textrm{ if }A_{i}^{(3)}\equiv 1\textrm{ for all }i=1,...,d\\[4.30554pt]
3c_{1},\textrm{ otherwise }\end{cases}

where the constant
C_{1}>0
is taken from the SLV property (see Definition
3.2
and the constant
c_{1}\geq 2
is taken from the SSV property (see Definition
3.1
) for the functions
\phi_{A_{1}},...,\phi_{A_{d}}
.

By comparing Proposition
4.3
with Proposition
3.3
, we immediately obtain our main power law for the Favard length problem, which is Theorem
2.4
.

For the upcoming calculation, we recall the cyclotomic factorizations of the mask polynomials
A_{1}(X),...,A_{d}(X)
given in Definition
2.2
. Subordinate to this factorization of the polynomials
A_{i}(X)
, we define a factorization of our Riesz product

P_{1,t}^{\prime}(\xi):=\prod_{k=m}^{n-1}\phi_{t}^{\prime}(L^{k}\xi),\quad P_{2,t}^{\prime}(\xi):=\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\phi_{t}^{\prime}(L^{k}\xi)

and similarly for
P_{1,t}^{\prime\prime}
and
P_{2,t}^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

To begin, we will use the notation

P_{1,t}(\xi):=L^{-(n-m)}\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}e^{2\pi i\alpha\xi}

for some set of frequencies
\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{A}(n,m;t)
which satisfies
\#\mathcal{A}=L^{n-m}
. Since both
|P_{1,t}(\xi)|
and
|P_{2,t}(\xi)|
are even functions, we know that

\int_{L^{-m}}^{1}\big\rvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\lvert^{2}\big\rvert P_{2,t}(\xi)\big\lvert^{2}d\xi=\int_{[-1,1]\setminus[-L^{-m},\,L^{-m}]}\big\rvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\lvert^{2}\big\rvert P_{2,t}(\xi)\big\lvert^{2}d\xi

Now, let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}
be a set of large values for the function
\phi_{t}^{\prime\prime}
, which means that there exists a parameter
\eta\in(0,1)
and two absolute constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

(4.11)

\big\lvert P_{2}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\big\rvert\geq L^{-C_{1}m},\quad\forall\xi\in\Gamma-\Gamma

(4.12)

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\Gamma\big)\geq C_{2}L^{-(1-\eta)m}

The following result is immediate, but we highlight it as the main vehicle for applying our set of large values estimates.

{lemma}

Let
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfy

(4.13)

h(\xi):=\frac{1}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma)}\big(\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma}*\mathbf{1}_{-\Gamma}\big)(\xi).

Then
h
has the following three properties

(1)

0\leq h(\xi)\leq 1
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

(2)

\hat{h}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(3)

\mathrm{supp}\,h=\Gamma-\Gamma

{remark}

Such a function function
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is similar to the notion of a
witness function
in the theory of finite additive groups; see, for example, Section 3 of
[
32
]
.

Using Lemma
4.3
, we thus obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma-\Gamma}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma-\Gamma}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}h(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle CL^{-2(n-m)}\sum\limits_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{n-m}}\int_{\Gamma-\Gamma}h(\xi)e^{2\pi i(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})}d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle CL^{-2(n-m)}\bigg(\big(\#\mathcal{A}^{n-m}\big)\int_{\Gamma-\Gamma}h(\xi)d\xi\,+\sum\limits_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{n-m}}\hat{h}(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle CL^{-(n-m)}\,\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma)

Now, from our size estimate for
\Gamma
(which is (
4.12
)) we thus obtain that

\int_{\Gamma-\Gamma}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi\geq CL^{-(n-m)}L^{-(1-\eta)m}=CL^{-n}L^{\eta m}.

Now, recall that
K\approx L^{\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}m}
; hence, if
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
is chosen small enough relative to
\eta
, we can always guarantee that

(4.14)

\int_{\Gamma-\Gamma}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi\geq 2C_{0}KL^{-n}\geq 2\int_{0}^{L^{-m}}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi,

where the second inequality follows from Proposition
4.2
. In particular, we then have that

(4.15)

\int_{(\Gamma-\Gamma)\setminus[-L^{-m},L^{-m}]}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi\geq C_{0}KL^{-n}.

Furthermore, by (
4.1
) from Proposition
4.1
(recall, we are assuming that
t=t_{0}
), we further have that

\int_{(\Gamma-\Gamma)\setminus(SSV(t_{0})\cup[-L^{-m},L^{-m}])}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi\geq\frac{C_{0}}{2}KL^{-n}.

Hence, we now no longer need to worry about implicit constants, as we have guaranteed that our main integral of
P_{1,t}
over the set
\Gamma-\Gamma
does, in fact, dominate the integral of
P_{1,t}
over the small interval
[0,L^{-m}]
adjoined with the set of small values
SSV(t)
associated to the direction
t=t_{0}
.

Using the lower bound on
P_{2}^{\prime\prime}
over
\Gamma-\Gamma
, as well as the set of small values lower bound, we now finally have a good estimate for our Riesz product

\displaystyle\int_{L^{-m}}^{1}\big\lvert\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi_{t}(L^{k}\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{L^{-m}}^{1}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}\big\lvert P_{2,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{(\Gamma-\Gamma)\setminus[-L^{-m},L^{-m}]}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}\big\lvert P_{2,t}^{\prime}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}\big\lvert P_{2,t}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle L^{-2C_{1}m}\int_{(\Gamma-\Gamma)\setminus[-L^{-m},L^{-m}]}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}\big\lvert P_{2,t}^{\prime}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle L^{-2C_{1}m}\psi^{2}(m)\int_{(\Gamma-\Gamma)\setminus(SSV(t)\cup[-L^{-m},L^{-m}])}\big\lvert P_{1,t}(\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle L^{-2C_{1}m}\psi(m)^{2}KL^{-n}

Because of Proposition
3.4
, we will have our Favard length estimates if we can show that

\int_{L^{-m}}^{1}\big\lvert\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi_{t}(L^{k}\xi)\big\rvert^{2}d\xi\gtrsim\frac{K}{L^{n}N^{\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}}

for the value of
\alpha
which was specified in (
3.18
), which depends upon
A_{1},...,A_{d}
, but not upon
\epsilon_{0}
or
N
. Our previous long calculation shows that it suffices to prove that

(4.16)

L^{-2C_{1}m}\psi(m)^{2}\gtrsim N^{-\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}.

Here is where we make use of Lemma
3.4
, which says that, regardless of whether we are in the log-SSV or SSV case, we always know that there exists some
\kappa>1
such that

\psi(m)\approx N^{-\kappa\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}.

If
\phi^{\prime}_{t}(\xi)
has the SSV property, then the left-hand side of (
4.16
) becomes

\displaystyle L^{-2C_{1}m}L^{-2c_{1}m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{-\log_{L}\big(N^{-C_{1}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}\big)}N^{-\kappa\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}=N^{-(C_{1}+\kappa)\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle N^{-\alpha\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}},

which follows if we take
\alpha=C_{1}+\kappa
(as required by (
3.18
)). If
\phi_{t}^{\prime}
instead has the log-SSV property, we first observe that if
N\gg 1
, we know that
m=\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}\frac{\log_{L}N}{\log_{L}\log_{L}N}\gg 1
. Since
\epsilon_{0}
and
C_{1}
are both independent of
N
, we can thus freely assume that

\displaystyle L^{-2C_{1}m}\psi(m)^{2}=L^{-2C_{1}m}L^{-2c_{1}m\log m}\gtrsim L^{-3c_{1}m\log m}.

Lemma
3.4
now tells us that

L^{-3c_{1}m\log m}=\big(\psi(m)\big)^{3}\approx N^{-3\kappa\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}}}

and so in this case we take
\alpha=3\kappa\approx 3c_{1}
(again, as required by (
3.18
)). Regardless of whether we are in the log-SSV or SSV case, we obtain (
4.10
).
∎

5.
A Multidimensional Set of Large Values

In this section, we give a proof of Proposition
3.2
, which says that (under all hypotheses placed upon the digit sets
A_{1},...,A_{d}
in Theorem
2.4
), the function

\phi_{t}^{\prime\prime}(\xi):=\phi_{A_{1}}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\phi_{A_{2}}^{\prime\prime}(t_{1}\xi)\cdots\phi_{A_{d}}^{\prime\prime}(t_{d-1}\xi)

is SLV-structured for all
t=(t_{1},...,t_{d-1})\in[0,1]^{d-1}
. We first introduce the necessary notation. If
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
is any non-empty set of integers, we remind the reader that

S_{A}:=S_{A}^{(2)}=\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\Phi_{s}(X)\mid A(X)\textrm{ and }\textrm{gcd}(s,\#A)=1\}.

and further let
s_{A}:=\textrm{lcm}(S_{A})
. We then define

\Sigma_{A}:=\{\xi\in[0,1]:\Phi_{s}(e^{2\pi i\xi})=0\textrm{ for some }s\in S_{A}\}.

We will use the following result, which was proven by I. Łaba and author in
[
22
]
. We think of this as a
single scale SLV set
result.

{lemma}

Suppose that
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
either satisfies:

(1)

2\leq\#A_{i}\leq 10
; or else,

(2)

s_{A}=p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}
for some distinct prime numbers

and exponents

.

Then, for any
0<\lambda<(\#A)^{-1}
, there necessarily exists a

-periodic set
\Gamma_{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
, which is a finite union of closed intervals, such that

(5.1)

\textup{dist}(\Gamma_{A}-\Gamma_{A},\Sigma_{A}+\mathbb{Z})>0

(5.2)

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big([0,1]\cap\Gamma_{A}\big)>\lambda

We will also prove the following in Section
5.2
, which is entirely new to the literature.

{lemma}

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
with

S=S_{A}^{(2)}

as defined in Definition
2.2
and with

M:=\textrm{lcm}(S)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}^{n_{k}}

for some distinct prime numbers
p_{k}
and exponents

. Suppose that
A
admits a
(S,\sigma)
-fibered subset (see Definition

). Then, for every
0<\lambda<(\#A)^{-1}
, there necessarily exists a
1
-periodic set
\Gamma_{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
, which is a finite union of closed intervals, such that

(5.3)

\textup{dist}(\Gamma_{A}-\Gamma_{A},\Sigma_{A}+\mathbb{Z})>0

(5.4)

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big([0,1]\cap\Gamma_{A}\big)>\lambda

The following result allows us to build up from these single-scale SLV sets

as in Lemma

and Lemma

to true SLV sets for our function
\phi_{t}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)
. A similar result was proven by I. Łaba and the first author in

{lemma}

Suppose that
A_{1},...,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
are each non-empty sets of integers. Further assume that, for each

and
0<\lambda_{i}<(\#A_{i})^{-1}
, there necessarily exists a

-periodic set

, which is the union of finitely-many closed intervals, and which satisfies (

) and (

) with

.
Then, for any

By combining Lemma

or Lemma
5
with Lemma

, and then applying Proposition

follow by Proposition

, we thus obtain our power laws for the Favard length problem.

{remark}

In fact, what the results of this section show is that the main obstruction to proving power laws for even more general families of rational product Cantor sets is the inability to construct an appropriate single-scale SLV set as in Lemma
5
and Lemma
5
. This is an issue which the author is actively investigating, and plans to address in a sequel to this work.

5.1.
From a single scale to a multiscale SLV set

We prove Lemma
5
. To simplify notation, let

S_{i}:=S_{A_{i}},\quad s_{i}:=s_{A_{i}},\quad\Sigma_{i}=\Sigma_{A_{i}}.

We remind the reader that the SLV set property is defined in Definition
3.2
.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

We freely assume that there is associated to each
A_{i}
a
1
-periodic set
\Gamma_{i}=\Gamma_{A_{i}}
which satisfies the hypothesis of Lemma
5
. For each
k=0,...,m-1
, let

\Gamma_{i,k}:=L^{-k}\,\Gamma_{i}.

The assumption (
5.3
) then produces a constant
c_{i}
which (uniformly in
k
) satisfies the inequality

(5.5)

\big\lvert\phi_{t}^{\prime\prime}(L^{k}\xi)\big\rvert\geq c_{i},\textrm{ for every }\xi\in\Gamma_{i,k}-\Gamma_{i,k}.

We now generalize an averaging argument appearing in the proof of Proposition 5.1 of
[
3
]
, which is itself a variation of an almost-independence result for random translations (see
[
37
]
for an exposition). Let
R\gg 1
be a large integer. For each
x\in[0,1]
, define a functional

\displaystyle\Psi(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R^{dm}}\underbrace{\int_{[0,R]^{m}}\cdots\int_{[0,R]^{m}}}_{d\textrm{ times}}\bigg[\bigg(\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma_{1,k}}(x+\tau_{1,k})\bigg)\times\bigg(\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{1}_{t_{1}^{-1}\Gamma_{2,k}}(x+\tau_{2,k})\bigg)\times\cdots

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\bigg(\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}\mathbf{1}_{t_{d-1}^{-1}\Gamma_{d,k}}(x+\tau_{d,k})\bigg)\bigg]d\vec{\tau}_{1}d\vec{\tau}_{2}\cdots d\vec{\tau}_{d}

where we use the shorthand
d\vec{\tau}_{i}:=d\tau_{i,0}\cdots d\tau_{i,m-1}
. Our integrand is the product of
R^{dm}
-many functions, each of which is a univariate function in exactly one of the variables
\tau_{i,k}
. That is to say, the integrals can be factored as the product of univariate integrals.

Using the
1
-periodicity of the sets
\Gamma_{i,k}
(recall,
x\in[0,1]
) and setting

\nu_{i}:=\mathcal{H}^{1}\big([0,1]\cap\Gamma_{i})

for each
i=1,...,d
, we obtain for every
k=0,...,m-1
that

\int_{0}^{R}\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma_{1,k}}(x+\tau_{1,k})d\tau_{1,k}=\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg([0,R]\cap(\Gamma_{1,k}-x)\bigg)\geq(R-1)\,\nu_{1}

and, for each
i=2,...,d
and
k=0,...,m-1
, we also obtain

\int_{0}^{R}\mathbf{1}_{t_{i-1}^{-1}\Gamma_{i,k}}(x+\tau_{i,k})d\tau_{1,k}=\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg([0,R]\cap(\Gamma_{i,k}-x)\bigg)\geq(R-t_{i-1}^{-1})\,\nu_{i}.

Since both bounds are uniform in
x\in[0,1]
and also
k=0,...,m-1
we thus obtain that

\Psi(x)\geq\bigg(\frac{(R-1)(R-t_{1}^{-1})\cdots(R-t_{d-1}^{-1})}{R^{d}}\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{d}\bigg)^{m}.

By averaging this bound over
x\in[0,1]
, we also have

(5.6)

\int_{0}^{1}\Psi(x)dx\geq\bigg(\frac{(R-1)(R-t_{1}^{-1})\cdots(R-t_{d-1}^{-1})}{R^{d}}\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{d}\bigg)^{m}.

However, if we apply Fubini to exchange the integration in
x
with the integrations in
\vec{\tau}_{1},...,\vec{\tau}_{d}
, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\Psi(x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R^{dm}}\int_{[0,R]^{m}}\cdots\int_{[0,R]^{m}}f(\vec{\tau}_{1},...,\vec{\tau}_{d})d\vec{\tau}_{1}\cdots d\vec{\tau}_{d}

where

\displaystyle f(\vec{\tau}_{1},\cdots,\vec{\tau}_{d})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg([0,1]\cap\bigcap_{k=0}^{m-1}(\Gamma_{1,k}-\tau_{1,k})\cap\bigcap_{k=0}^{m-1}(t_{1}^{-1}\Gamma_{2,k}-\tau_{2,k})\cap\cdots

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\cap\bigcap_{k=0}^{m-1}(t_{d-1}^{-1}\Gamma_{d-1,k}-\tau_{d-1,k})\bigg).

Here we do need some regularity of the sets
\Gamma_{i,k}
to guarantee that
f:[0,R]^{dm}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is measurable. Yet, since all of the involved
\Gamma_{i,k}
are themselves a finite unions of closed intervals, things work as they should.

By pigeonholing the estimate (
5.6
), we obtain fixed translation parameters
\vec{\rho}_{i}:=(\rho_{i,0},...,\rho_{i,m-1})
such that

\displaystyle f(\vec{\rho}_{1},...,\vec{\rho}_{d})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg[[0,1]\cap\bigcap_{k=0}^{m-1}(\Gamma_{1,k}-\rho_{1,k})\cap\bigcap_{k=0}^{m-1}(t_{1}^{-1}\Gamma_{2,k}-\rho_{2,k})\cap\cdots

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\cap\bigcap_{k=0}^{m-1}(t_{d-1}^{-1}\Gamma_{d-1,k}-\rho_{d-1,k})\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{(R-1)(R-t_{1}^{-1})\cdots(R-t_{d-1}^{-1})}{R^{d}}\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{d}\bigg)^{m}.

We call this set appearing in brackets
\tilde{\Gamma}
, and remark that we can always extend this set periodically by letting
\Gamma:=\tilde{\Gamma}+\mathbb{Z}
. Since
R
was chosen independent of
t
, and since we can let
\nu_{i}\rightarrow(\#A_{i})^{-1}
, we can always guarantee that, for
any

\eta\in(0,1)
, that there exists a corresponding choice
\nu_{1},...,\nu_{d-1}
such that

\bigg(\frac{(R-1)(R-t_{1}^{-1})\cdots(R-t_{d-1}^{-1})}{R^{d}}\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{d}\bigg)^{m}\geq L^{(1-\eta)m}.

This follows by choosing
R
and
\nu_{i}
appropriately large, and so we obtain (
3.3
).

{remark}

Notice that our construction shows something even stronger than we originally claimed: the lower bound on
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma\cap[0,1])
holds for
any choice
of
\eta\in(0,1)
. This explains why, in previous sections, we could make our choice of
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
independent of this parameter
\eta\in(0,1)
.

Finally, observe that, since

(\Gamma_{i,k}-\tau_{i,k})-(\Gamma_{i,k}-\tau_{i,k})=\Gamma_{i,k}-\Gamma_{i,k}

and since, by construction

\Gamma-\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{i,k}-\Gamma_{i,k}\textrm{ for any }i\textrm{ and }k,

we must also have (
3.2
) for our chosen set
\Gamma
. This concludes our construction of an SLV set for the function
\phi_{A}
, under the assumption that there exists a single scale SLV set.
∎

5.2.
Constructing single-scale SLV set for sub-fibering sets

In this section, we prove Lemma
5
. We first repeat the necessary definitions, notations and preliminary results from the author’s work with I. Łaba in
[
22
]
. We will assume that

A_{i}=A,\quad S_{A_{i}}=S_{A},\quad s_{A_{i}}=s_{A}

are fixed throughout this section. We also enumerate the set
S_{A}
, so that

S_{A}:=\{s_{1},...,s_{J}\}.

We begin with the following definition, which was introduced in
[
22
]
.

{definition}

A subset
\mathcal{C}\subset S_{A}
is called a
cyclotomic divisor cluster
, or (for short) a
cluster
, of
A
.

Fix a cluster
\mathcal{C}\subset S_{A}
, which we can assume satisfies
\mathcal{C}=\{s_{1},...,s_{I}\}
for some
I\leq J
, and let
N=lcm(s_{1},\cdots,s_{I})
. If we can show that
N=QU
,
where

(5.7)

s_{j}\hbox{ does not divide }Q\hbox{ for any }j\in\{1,\dots,I\},

then we will obtain that
(s_{j},U)>1
for each
s_{j}\in\mathcal{C}
. Our aim is to show that we can choose
Q
as large as possible. Now, each
s_{j}\in\mathcal{C}
can be written as
s_{j}=r_{j}t_{j}
, where
r_{j}:=(s_{j},Q)
and
t_{j}:=s_{j}/(s_{j},Q)
. Let
T:=\max(t_{1},\dots,t_{I})
. We then define, for some auxiliary parameter
0<\rho<(QT)^{-1}
, the set:

(5.8)

\Gamma(\mathcal{C},\rho)=\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\ \textup{dist}(\xi,\frac{1}{Q}\mathbb{Z})<\frac{\rho}{2}\right\},

From
[
22
]
, we obtain the following, which essentially verifies that the sets
\Gamma(\mathcal{C},\rho)
which were defined previously have the appropriate single-scale SLV structure.

{lemma}

The set
\Gamma:=\Gamma(\mathcal{C},\rho)
defined above satisfies

\text{dist}\big(\Gamma-\Gamma,\Sigma(\mathcal{C})\big)>0

where

(5.9)

\Sigma(\mathcal{C}):=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\ \Phi_{s_{j}}(e^{2\pi i\xi})=0\textrm{ for some }s_{j}\in\mathcal{C}\}.

Further, for each
0<\lambda<T^{-1}
, there there exists a choice of
0<\rho<(QT)^{-1}
such that

(5.10)

\big|[0,1]\cap\Gamma\big|>\lambda.

and the resulting set
\Gamma
is 1-periodic.

We also use the following result from
[
22
]
.

{lemma}

Suppose that
\mathcal{C}^{1},...,\mathcal{C}^{k}
are clusters associated to some
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
. For each
l\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, let
\Gamma^{l}:=\Gamma(\mathcal{C}^{l},\rho^{l})
be the set defined in (
5.8
) and satisfying (
5.10
), with corresponding parameters
Q_{l},T_{l},\rho_{l},\lambda_{l}
. Then there exist parameters
\tau_{1},...,\tau_{k}\in[0,1]
such that

(5.11)

\bigg|[0,1]\cap\bigcap_{l=1}^{k}\big(\Gamma^{l}+\tau_{l}\big)\bigg|>\prod_{l=1}^{k}\lambda_{l},

Furthermore, if we define
\Gamma^{1,\dots,k}:=\bigcap_{l=1}^{k}\big(\Gamma^{l}+\tau_{l}\big)
with this choice of
\tau_{l}
, then

(5.12)

\text{dist}\big(\Gamma^{1,\dots,k}-\Gamma^{1,\dots,k},\bigcup_{l=1}^{k}\Sigma(\mathcal{C}^{l})\big)>0

where
\Sigma(\mathcal{C}^{l})
is defined as in (
5.9
) with
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{l}
.

We can now prove the following result.

{proposition}

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
S_{A}^{(2)}=S
and
M=\textrm{lcm}(S)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}^{n_{k}}
, and assume that
A
admits a fibered subset (see Definition
2.3
and Definition
2.3
). Let
\sigma_{0}:S\rightarrow\{1,...,K\}
be any assignment function as in Definition
2.3
which satisfies

\min_{\sigma}\mathsf{FIB}(S,\sigma)=\mathsf{FIB}(S,\sigma_{0}):=p_{1}^{E_{1}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdots p_{K}^{E_{K}(S,\sigma_{0})}

where recall that

\hbox{\sf EXP}_{k}(S,\sigma_{0}):=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}:\exists\,s\in S\textrm{ with }(s,p_{k}^{n_{k}})=p_{k}^{\alpha}\textrm{ and }\sigma_{0}(s)=k\}.

Then for any list of parameters
(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{K})
with
0<\lambda_{k}<p_{i}^{-1}
, there exists a 1-periodic set
\Gamma_{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(5.13)

\textup{dist}(\Gamma_{A}-\Gamma_{A},\Sigma_{A})>0,

(5.14)

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big([0,1]\cap\Gamma_{A}\big)>\bigg(\lambda_{1}^{E_{1}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdot\lambda_{2}^{E_{2}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdots\lambda_{K}^{E_{K}(S,\sigma_{0})}\bigg)

Note that, by letting
\lambda_{k}\rightarrow p_{k}
for each
k=1,...,K
, we have that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{E_{1}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdot\lambda_{2}^{E_{2}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdots\lambda_{K}^{E_{K}(S,\sigma_{0})}\rightarrow p_{1}^{-E_{1}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdots p_{K}^{-E_{K}(S,\sigma_{0})}.

We now utilize the size bound of G. Kiss, I. Łaba, G. Somlai and the author in
[
17
]
(which is Proposition
2.3
in our work), which states that (since
A
is assumed to contain a non-empty subset
A^{\prime}
which is
(S,\sigma)
-fibered and
\sigma_{0}
is a minimizing assignment function):

\#A\geq\#A^{\prime}\geq p_{1}^{E_{1}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdots p_{K}^{E_{K}(S,\sigma_{0})}.

In fact, we actually obtain a slightly stronger statement, which is that

(5.15)

\#A>p_{1}^{E_{1}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdots p_{K}^{E_{K}(S,\sigma_{0})}.

This follows because of the definition of the set of accumulating roots, which recall is

S=S_{A}^{(2)}:=\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\Phi_{s}(X)\mid A(X)\textrm{ and }(s,\#A)=1\},

and so
\textrm{lcm}(s_{A},\#A)=1
, forcing the inequality (
5.15
) to be strict. Since we can always find a choice of
\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{K}
such that

\frac{1}{\#A}\,<\,\lambda_{1}^{E_{1}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdot\lambda_{2}^{E_{2}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdots\lambda_{K}^{E_{K}(S,\sigma_{0})}<\frac{1}{p_{1}^{E_{1}(S,\sigma_{0})}\cdots p_{K}^{E_{K}(S,\sigma_{0})}},

and so we thus obtain Lemma
5
, which combined with Lemma
5
gives Theorem
2.3
.

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

We will construct this set
\Gamma_{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
by considering an appropriate partition of
S_{A}
into clusters, where our clusters are determined relative to the minimizing assignment function
\sigma_{0}:S_{A}\rightarrow\{1,...,K\}
. To this end, let

\mathcal{C}^{k}:=\{s\in S_{A}:\sigma_{0}(s)=k\},\textrm{ for }k=1,...,K,

and observe that
\sigma_{0}(s)=k
implies that
p_{k}\mid s
. Now, for each
\alpha\in\hbox{\sf EXP}(S,\sigma_{0})
, we let

\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}:=\{s\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}:s=p_{k}^{\alpha}q,\textrm{ with }\gcd(p_{k},q)=1\},

and we remark that since the clusters
\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}

partition

S_{A}

S_{A}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{C}^{k}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\,\bigcup_{\alpha\in\textsf{EXP}_{k}(S,\sigma_{0})}\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}

we necessarily have that

\Sigma_{A}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\,\bigcup_{\alpha\in\textsf{EXP}_{k}(S,\sigma_{0})}\Sigma(\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha})

where the zero sets
\Sigma(\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha})
are defined in (
5.9
).

We now claim that, given any
0<\lambda_{k,\alpha}<p_{k}^{-1}
, there exists a corresponding choice of
Q=Q^{i,\alpha}
and
\rho=\rho^{i,\alpha}
such that the set
\Gamma_{k,\alpha}:=\Gamma(\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha},\rho^{k,\alpha})
, satisfies

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\Gamma_{k,\alpha}\cap[0,1]\big)>\lambda_{k}.

This is essentially a repetition of Lemma 3.6 in
[
22
]
, but with our clusters defined relative to the assignment function
\sigma_{0}
(which is a newer concept to the literature, appearing in
[
17
]
).

Let
k
and
\alpha
be fixed, and assume that
s_{1},...,s_{J}
are ordered so as to allow us to write
\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}:=\{s_{1},...,s_{I}\}
for some
I\leq J
. Each
s_{j}\in\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha}
can be written as
s_{j}=p_{k}^{\alpha}q_{j}
where
\gcd(p_{k},q_{j})=1
. We then define our parameter
Q=Q^{k,\alpha}
as

Q:=p_{k}^{\alpha-1}\textrm{lcm}(q_{1},...,q_{I})

and remark that,
critically
, such a choice of

is possible because
\sigma_{0}(s)=k
implies that
p_{k}\mid s
, and so
\alpha\geq 1
! For each
j=1,...,I
, we have that

r_{j}=\gcd(s_{j},Q_{j})=p_{k},\quad t_{j}:=\frac{s_{j}}{r_{j}}=p_{k}.

Hence, we can take
T=\max(t_{1},...,t_{I})=p_{k}
in the statement of Lemma
5.2
, so that for any
0<\lambda_{k,\alpha}<T^{-1}=p_{k}^{-1}

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\Gamma_{k,\alpha}\cap[0,1]\big)>\lambda_{k,\alpha},

and, of course, is still

\textup{dist}\big((\Gamma_{k,\alpha}-\Gamma_{k,\alpha},\Sigma(\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha})\big)>0.

, nor did it depend upon the choice of

, at least beyond the fact that we know

, where

can be taken arbitrarily-close to

. Henceforth, we assume that for each

(but which we are allowed to take as close to

An application of Lemma

furnishes translation parameters

such that

\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg([0,1]\cap\underbrace{\bigcap_{k=1}^{K}\,\bigcap_{\alpha\in\textsf{EXP}_{k}(S,\sigma_{0})}\big(\Gamma_{k,\alpha}+\tau_{k,\alpha}\big)}_{\Gamma_{A}}\bigg)>\prod_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}^{E_{k}(S,\sigma_{0})},

and also simultaneously

However, as we have already previously seen,

\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\,\bigcup_{\alpha\in\textsf{EXP}_{k}(S,\sigma_{0})}\Sigma(\mathcal{C}^{k,\alpha})=S_{A},

and so our set

satisfies (

6.
The Combinatorial Lemmas

In this section, we prove Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.4
, both of which are generalizations of combinatorial lemmas which were first proven in
[
27
]
, then utilized as black boxes in
[
3
]
. We remark that there is an exposition of the proof of these results given in
[
2
]
when

Heuristically, the method is to utilize the self-similarity of the sets
\mathcal{S}^{N}
across
many scales

N^{\prime}\geq N
to obtain quantitative information about the lengths of the projections of the Cantor iterates
\mathcal{S}^{N}
. We first begin by fixing notations and introducing definitions needed for this section. By using the covering argument discussed at the beginning of Section

, we can assume that

\mathcal{S}^{N}=\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{N}:=\bigcup_{z_{j}\in\mathcal{A}^{N}}Q_{j}^{N}

where

and

is the Vitali covering constant used in Section

(which we will suppress moving forward). For this section, we will still refer to this union of balls as
\mathcal{S}^{N}
(to avoid overly complicated notation).

We call the component balls
Q_{j}^{N}\subset\mathcal{S}^{N}

basic cells at scale
N
or simply
basic cells
, when
N
is understood from context. We will let
\mathscr{Q}^{N}:=\{Q_{j}^{N}\}
denote the family of all basic cells at scale
N
. For each
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we will let

\mathcal{P}_{N,\theta}:=\pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{S}^{N})=\bigcup_{j}I_{j,\theta}^{N}

where

I_{j,\theta}^{N}:=\pi_{\theta}\big(Q_{j}^{N}\big)=[\pi_{\theta}(z_{j})-L^{-N},\pi_{\theta}(z_{j})+L^{-N}].

We will call the sets
I_{j,\theta}^{N}
the
basic segments at scale
N
in the direction
\theta
or simply
basic segments
, when
N
and
\theta
are fixed and understood from context. We let
\mathscr{I}^{N}_{\theta}:=\{I_{j,\theta}^{N}\}
denote the family of all basic segments. We also recall that,

G=G_{N,K,\theta}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:f_{N}^{*}(x)\geq K\},\quad E=E_{N,K}:=\{\theta:\mathcal{H}^{1}(G_{N,K,\theta})\leq K^{-\rho}\}.

We use the following definition to help us characterize when certain sub-collections of basic cells lie “above” a point
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

{definition}

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}
we let

(6.1)

\mathcal{V}_{x}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\pi_{\theta}(y)=x\}.

Geometrically,
\mathcal{V}_{x}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a hyperplane with normal vector in our fixed direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and passing through the point
(x\,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For each
N\gg 1
, we then let

\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{N}:=\{Q_{j}^{N}\in\mathscr{Q}^{N}:\mathcal{V}_{x}\cap Q_{j}^{N}\neq\emptyset\}

so that
\mathscr{Q}_{x}=\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{N}
corresponds to those basic cells which intersect the hyperplane
\mathcal{V}_{x}
.

6.1.
The propagation argument

In this section, we give a proof of Lemma
3.3
. Our proof is a generalization of the one given in Section 5.2.1 of
[
2
]
for the planar case.

In fact, we will prove an even more flexible version of Lemma
3.3
, which we hope to utilize in a sequel. The main idea is that we do not require any restriction on the parameter
\rho\in(3,\infty)
in order to prove the propagation inequality.

{lemma}

Suppose that
N\gg 1
and
K
is its associated stacking parameter. Let
\rho\in(1,\infty)
be given, and define

E_{N,K}=E_{N,K}(\rho):=\big\{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}:\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\{x\in\mathbb{R}:f_{N,\theta}^{*}(x)\geq K\}\big)\leq K^{-\rho}\big\}.

Then, for any
\theta\in E_{N,K}^{C}
, we have the estimate

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{S}_{NK^{\rho}})\big)\lesssim\frac{1}{K}.

{remark}

Solely for this section, we will work under the assumption that
\rho\in(0,\infty)
. Due to Remark
3.3
in Section
3
, we are aware that it is most advantageous for our Favard length estimate to take
\rho
as small as our argument allows (but, as we shall see, we must always have
\rho>3
, which is likely an artifact of this method of proof, and also appears in the proof given in
[
2
]
). In particular, we still require the condition
\rho>3
in order to prove the second combinatorial lemma which appears in the following section.

We will primarily be working with the families

\mathscr{Q}^{jN}

of basic cells at scales
jN
for
j=1,...,K^{\rho}
. We now examine the parent-child relationship of our basic cells as
jN\mapsto(j+1)N
for each
j=1,...,K^{\rho}-1
. If
Q=Q^{(j)}\in\mathscr{Q}^{jN}
is viewed as a parent cell, then there necessarily exist children cells
Q_{1}^{(j+1)},...,Q_{L^{N}}^{(j+1)}\in\mathscr{Q}^{(j+1)N}
satisfying
Q_{s}^{(j+1)}\subset Q^{(j)}
for all
1\leq s\leq L^{N}
. As our sets
\mathcal{S}^{jN}
are self-similar, there is a translation parameter
\vec{\tau}=\vec{\tau}(Q^{(j)})\in[0,1]^{d}
such that

Q^{(j)}\supset\bigcup_{s=1}^{L^{N}}Q_{s}^{(j+1)}=L^{-jN}\mathcal{S}^{N}+\vec{\tau}(Q^{(j)}).

This is just another way of saying that each basic square
Q^{(j)}\in\mathscr{Q}^{jN}
contains a similar copy of
\mathcal{S}^{N}
, which has been rescaled by a factor of
L^{-jN}
. We will use this observation to construct the following decomposition of our family of cells
\mathscr{Q}^{jN}
.

{definition}

For each
j=1,...,K^{\rho}
, let families of cells
\mathscr{G}_{j}\subset\mathscr{Q}^{jN}
be defined inductively via the following four steps:

(1)

If
j=1
, we let:

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}_{1}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{x\in G_{K}}\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{x\in G_{K}}\{Q\in\mathscr{Q}^{N}:\mathcal{V}_{x}\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}

(2)

For
j\geq 1
, we continue inductively by letting

\mathscr{G}_{j+1}^{(1)}:=\{Q\in\mathscr{Q}^{(j+1)N}:Q\subset Q^{\prime}\textrm{ for some }Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{G}_{j}\}

so that
\mathscr{G}_{j+1}^{(1)}
consists of all children cells (i.e. cells at scale
L^{-(j+1)N}
) of parent cells (i.e. cells at scale
L^{-jN}
) which were in
\mathscr{G}_{j}
.

(3)

Further (and still assuming
j\geq 1
) if there exists some
Q^{*}\not\in\mathscr{G}_{j+1}^{(1)}
, then we find its associated translation parameter
\vec{\tau}=\vec{\tau}(Q^{*})
and “sibling” cells
Q_{1}^{(j+1)},...,Q_{L^{N}}^{(j+1)}\in\mathscr{Q}^{(j+1)N}
such that

\displaystyle Q^{*}\in\bigcup_{s=1}^{L^{N}}Q_{s}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{-jN}\mathcal{S}^{N}+\vec{\tau}(Q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{1}}(L^{-jN}Q+\vec{\tau}(Q))\bigg)\cup\bigg(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{Q}^{N}\setminus\mathscr{G}_{1}}(L^{-jN}Q+\vec{\tau}(Q))\bigg)

We then let

\mathscr{G}_{j+1}^{(2)}:=\big\{Q\in\mathscr{Q}^{(j+1)N}:Q\in\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{1}}(L^{-jN}Q+\vec{\tau}(Q))\big\}.

In words,
\mathscr{G}_{j+1}^{(2)}
consists of those cells which are contained in a self-similar copy of
\mathscr{G}_{1}
at scale
L^{-jN}
and were not contained in
\mathscr{G}_{j+1}^{(1)}
collection.

(4)

We then conclude by letting
\mathscr{G}_{j+1}:=\mathscr{G}_{j+1}^{(1)}\cup\mathscr{G}_{j+1}^{(2)}
for all
j\geq 1
.

The previously-defined decomposition allows us to write

\mathscr{Q}^{jN}:=\mathscr{G}_{j}\sqcup\mathscr{B}_{j},

where we think of
\mathscr{G}_{j}
as being the
good cells for stacking
at scale
jN
and
\mathscr{B}_{j}
as being the
bad cells for stacking
at scale
jN
. The following two lemmas are why we call the collections
\mathscr{G}_{jN}

good
for stacking.

{lemma}

For every
j=1,2,...,K^{\rho}
, we have the uniform estimate

(6.2)

\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{j}}\pi_{\theta}(Q)\bigg)\lesssim\frac{1}{K}

{lemma}

Given
\rho\in(1,\infty)
as in Lemma
6.1
, we always have that

\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{B}_{K^{\rho}}}\pi_{\theta}(Q)\bigg)\lesssim\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{K^{\rho}}}\pi_{\theta}(Q)\bigg).

Assuming the previous two lemmas concerning the families
\mathscr{G}_{j}
and
\mathscr{B}_{j}
, we thus prove the first combinatorial lemma.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Letting
\rho\in(1,\infty)
be chosen as in Lemma
6.1
(and, recall, the upper bound of Lemma
6.1
was uniform in
j\geq 1
, and so also holds with
j=K^{\rho}
).
We then have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\pi_{\theta}\big(\mathcal{S}_{NK^{\rho}}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{K^{\rho}}}\pi_{\theta}(Q)\bigg)+\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{B}_{K^{\rho}}}\pi_{\theta}(Q)\bigg)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{K^{\rho}}}\pi_{\theta}(Q)\bigg)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}

∎

For any direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
j=1,...,K^{\rho}
, the counting function
f_{j}:=f_{jN,\theta}
satisfies
f_{j}=g_{j}+b_{j}
, where

g_{j}:=g_{jN,\theta}(x)=\sum\limits_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{j}}\pi_{\theta}(Q),\quad b_{j}:=b_{jN,\theta}(x)=\sum\limits_{Q\in\mathscr{B}_{j}}\pi_{\theta}(Q).

We will establish both Lemma
6.1
and Lemma
6.1
by proving
L^{1}
estimates for the functions
g_{1},...,g_{K^{\rho}}
. We start with a simple estimate relating the values of
g_{j}
to the
L^{1}
norms of
g_{j^{\prime}}
over basic segments, at least when
j^{\prime}\geq j
.

{lemma}

Let
j=1,2,...,K^{\rho}
,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and set
I:=[x_{0}-2L^{-jN},x_{0}+2^{-jN}]\subset\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that
g_{j}(x_{0})\geq R
for some
R\geq 1
. Then, for any
j^{\prime}\geq j
, we have that

||g_{j^{\prime}}||_{L^{1}(I)}=\int_{I}|g_{j^{\prime}}(x)|dx\geq L^{-jN}R\gtrsim\mathcal{H}^{1}(I)R.

Proof.

This is an application of the self-similarity of the sets
\mathcal{S}^{jN}
. Indeed, if
g_{j}(x_{0})\geq R
, then there exist basic cells
Q_{1}^{(j)},...,Q_{R}^{(j)}\in\mathscr{G}_{j}\subset\mathscr{Q}^{jN}
such that

\mathcal{V}_{x_{0}}\cap Q_{i}^{(j)}\neq\emptyset\textrm{ for all }i=1,...,R.

This means that
\pi_{\theta}(Q_{i}^{(j)})\subset I
for each
i=1,...,R
. Each parent cell
Q_{i}^{(j)}
has
L^{(j^{\prime}-j)N}
-many children cells, which by Definition
6.1
(2) are contained in
\mathscr{G}_{j^{\prime}}\subset\mathscr{Q}^{j^{\prime}N}
, and whose projections are all contained in
I
. Hence, we know that

\displaystyle\int_{I}|g_{j^{\prime}}(x)|dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{I}\bigg\lvert\sum\limits_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{j^{\prime}}}\mathbf{1}_{\pi_{\theta}(Q)}(x)\bigg\rvert dx\geq(L^{(j^{\prime}-j)N}R)L^{-j^{\prime}N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{-jN}R.

∎

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

For each
j=1,2,...,K^{\rho}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
, let

\mathscr{M}g_{j}(x):=\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{2r}\int_{x-r}^{x+r}|g_{j}(x)|dx

which is the
Hardy-Littlewood maximal function
applied to
g_{j}
. We first show that

(6.3)

\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{j}}\pi_{\theta}(Q)\subseteq\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\mathscr{M}g_{j}(x)\geq K/4\big\}.

Let
x\in\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{j}}\pi_{\theta}(Q)
, and suppose first that

x\in G_{K}
. By using the definition of
\mathscr{G}_{1}
given in Definition
6.1
(1), we immediately know that

\#\{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{1}:\mathcal{V}_{x}\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}\geq K,

which of course implies that
g_{1}(x)\geq K
. Hence, applying Lemma
6.1
, we see that

(6.4)

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}g_{j}(x)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{L^{jN}}{4}\int_{x-2L^{-jN}}^{x-2L^{-jN}}g_{j}(x)\geq\frac{L^{jN}}{4}L^{-jN}K=\frac{K}{4}.

Now, suppose that
x\in\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{j}}\pi_{\theta}(Q)\setminus G_{K}
, and define an index
2\leq i(x)\leq j
as

i_{x}:=\min\bigg\{2\leq i\leq j:\mathcal{V}_{x}\cap\bigg(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{i-1}}Q\bigg)=\emptyset\ \bigg\}.

In words,
i_{x}
is the first scale where we are able to find a good cell which is intersected by the hyperplane associated to
x
with normal direction
\theta
. Importantly, this means that

\{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{i(x)}:\mathcal{V}_{x}\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}\subseteq\mathscr{G}_{i_{x}}^{(2)},

and so
x
belongs to a self-similar copy of
G_{K}
at scale
i_{x}N
. This just tells us that

g_{i_{x}}(x)\geq K,

and since
i_{x}\leq j
, an identical application of Lemma
6.1
again gives that

\mathscr{M}g_{j}(x)\geq\frac{K}{4}.

In either case, (
6.3
) is established. By the Hardy-Littlewood maximal inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{G}_{j}}\pi_{\theta}(Q)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\mathscr{M}g_{j}(x)\geq K/4\big\}\big)\lesssim\frac{1}{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g_{j}(x)|dx

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}

where we used that
||g_{j}||_{1}\leq||f_{n,\theta}||_{1}\lesssim 1
in the final inequality.
∎

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

From (
6.4
), we already know that if
x\in G_{K}
, then
\mathscr{M}g_{1}(x)\geq K/4
. In particular,

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{K}\big)\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\mathscr{M}g_{1}(x)\geq K/4\}\big)\lesssim\frac{||g_{1}||}{K}\lesssim\frac{\#\mathscr{G}_{1}}{L^{N}K}.

Recall the definition of the low multiplicity set of directions: if
\theta\not\in E_{N,K}
, then
\mathcal{H}^{1}(G_{K})\gtrsim K^{-\rho}
, so that

\#\mathscr{G}_{1}\gtrsim K^{-(\rho-1)}L^{N}=\frac{\#\mathscr{Q}^{N}}{K^{\rho-1}}.

At step
j=2
, each parent
Q\in\mathscr{B}_{1}
gives birth to
L^{N}
-many children
Q_{1}^{\prime},...,Q_{L^{N}}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{Q}^{2N}
, and by the definition of
\mathscr{G}_{2}^{(2)}
, we know that

\#\big(\{Q_{1}^{\prime},...,Q_{L^{N}}^{\prime}\}\cap\mathscr{G}_{2}^{(2)}\big)=\#\mathscr{G}_{1}\gtrsim L^{N}K^{-(\rho-1)}.

In particular, we see that

\#\mathscr{B}_{2}=\#\mathscr{B}_{1}\times\big(L^{N}(1-CK^{-(\rho-1)})\big)\lesssim L^{2N}\big(1-CK^{-(\rho-1)}\big)^{2}.

Continuing inductively, we obtain that for
j\gg 1
that

\#\mathscr{B}_{j}\lesssim L^{jN}\bigg(1-CK^{-(\rho-1)}\bigg)^{j}\approx L^{jN}e^{-CjK^{-(\rho-1)}},

so that, if we choose
j=K^{\rho}
, we thus have that

\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg(\bigcup_{Q\in\mathscr{B}_{j}}\pi_{\theta}(Q)\bigg)\lesssim e^{-CjK^{-(\rho-1)}}\lesssim e^{-CK^{-1}}

which is clearly
\lesssim K^{-1}
, since
K=N^{\epsilon_{0}}\gg 1
. This concludes the proof.
∎

6.2.
The
L^{2}
estimate for the low multiplicity set of directions

In this section, we give a proof of Lemma
3.4
, which is the
L^{2}
upper bound for the counting function along directions in the low multiplicity set. In what follows, we assume that
N\gg 1
is fixed,
K
is chosen according to Definition
3.3
, and that
\theta\in E_{N,K}
is also fixed. Lemma
3.4
follows handily from the following (which is proven in the planar case in Section 5 of
[
2
]
).

{lemma}

If
N,K
and
M=M(N,K)\gg 1
are all chosen large enough, then

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{2LMK}\big)\lesssim(LK)\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{K})\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{M}\big),

and the implicit constant is absolute.

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

By repeated application of Lemma
6.2
, we obtain for each
i=0,1,2,...

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{(2LK)^{i+1}}\big)\leq(CLK)^{i}\,\bigg[\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{2LK}\big)\bigg]^{i+1}

In particular, if
1\leq n\leq N
, then we can use a telescoping series trick to write

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f_{n,\theta}(x)|^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus G_{2LK}}|f_{n,\theta}(x)|^{2}dx+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\int_{G_{(2LK)^{i}}\setminus G_{(2LK)^{i+1}}}|f_{n,\theta}(x)|^{2}dx.

We then use the definition of each of the
G=G_{N,K,\theta}
sets, together with the fact that
||f_{n,\theta}||_{1}=1
, to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f_{n,\theta}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2LK+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(2LK)^{2(i+1)}\,\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{(2LK)^{i}}\setminus G_{(2LK)^{i+1}}\big)

and an application of Lemma
6.2
gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f_{n,\theta}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2CLK+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(2LK)^{2(i+1)}(CLK)^{i}\big[\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{2LK}\big)\big]^{i}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{L}

\displaystyle K+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}C^{i}K^{3i}\big[\mathcal{H}^{1}\,\big(G_{K}\big)\big]^{i}

and the second inequality follows since
G_{2LK}\subset G_{K}
and by letting
C=C_{L}
be some constant which depends upon
L
alone. Now, recall, if
\theta\in E_{N,K}
, then
\mathcal{H}^{1}(G_{K})\leq K^{-\rho}
for some
\rho>3
, and so we have the nice estimate

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}C^{i}K^{3i}\big[\mathcal{H}^{1}\,\big(G_{K}\big)\big]^{i}\leq\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}C^{i}K^{-(\rho-3)}\lesssim 1

which follows because
K=N^{\epsilon_{0}}\gg 1
. So, for any
n=1,...,N
and
\theta\in E_{N,K}
, we obtain that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f_{n,\theta}(x)|^{2}dx\lesssim K,

which is Lemma
3.4
.
∎

Lemma
6.2
is a reflection of the self-similarity of our sets, since along low multiplicity directions, the measure of points of large stacking at later stages of construction is always controlled by the measure of points of large stacking at earlier stages. This idea is made precise in the following proof.

Proof of Lemma
6.2
.

Let
x\in G_{2LK}
, so that
f_{N,\theta}(x)\geq 2LK
. We then let
n_{0}=n_{0}(x)\in\{1,2,...,N\}
be the unique integer such that

n_{0}:=\max\{1\leq n\leq N:\#\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{n}<2LK\},

and remark that, since
n_{0}
is the largest-possible integer with this property, we necessarily have that

(6.5)

2K\leq\#\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{n_{0}(x)}<2LK,

for any combination of
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
n_{0}(x)\in\{1,...,N\}
. We then let

\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{max}:=\bigcup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\bigcup_{n_{0}(x)}\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{n_{0}(x)}\subset\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}\mathscr{Q}^{n},

It is somewhat annoying that the cells
Q\in\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{max}
no longer have uniform radii, because it is now entirely possible for
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{max}
with
Q\neq Q^{\prime}
and
Q\subset Q^{\prime}
. Hence, refine this collection by letting

\mathscr{Q}_{max}:=\{Q\in\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{max}:Q\not\subseteq Q^{\prime}\textrm{ for any }Q^{\prime}\neq Q\textrm{ in }\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{max}\}.

We then let

(6.6)

n_{*}:=\min\{1\leq n\leq N:\exists Q\in\mathscr{Q}_{max},\,\textrm{diam}(Q)=L^{-n}\},

and choose some
Q_{*}\in\mathscr{Q}_{max}
which satisfies
\textrm{diam}(Q_{*})=L^{-n_{*}}
. Let
x_{*}:=\pi_{\theta}(z_{*})
, where
z_{*}\in\mathcal{A}^{n_{*}}
is the centre of
Q_{*}
, and let
I_{*}:=\pi_{\theta}(Q_{*})
be the basic segment associated to
Q_{*}
; so

I_{*}:=[x_{*}-L^{n_{*}},x_{*}+L^{-n_{*}}]\subset\mathbb{R}.

Since
Q_{*}\in\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{max}
, there necessarily exists some
y_{*}\in I_{*}
such that
Q_{*}\in\mathscr{Q}_{y_{*}}^{n_{*}}
; where, recall

(6.7)

\mathscr{Q}_{y_{*}}^{n_{*}}:=\{Q\in\mathscr{Q}^{n_{*}}:\mathcal{V}_{y_{*}}\cap Q\neq\emptyset\}

We claim that
\mathscr{Q}_{y_{*}}^{n_{*}}\subset\mathscr{Q}_{max}
(so that every
Q\in\mathscr{Q}_{y_{*}}^{n_{*}}
is not contained in any disc of
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{max}
).
Indeed, were this false, there would necessarily exist
Q\in\mathscr{Q}_{y_{*}}^{n_{*}}\setminus\mathscr{Q}_{max}
(critically:
\textrm{diam}(Q)=L^{-n_{*}}
) and
Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{Q}_{max}
such that
Q\subseteq Q^{\prime}
. However, by the choice of
n_{*}
given in (
6.6
),
\textrm{diam}(Q^{\prime})=L^{-n_{*}}=\textrm{diam}(Q)
. Hence,
Q=Q^{\prime}
, which is a contradiction, and so we obtain that
\mathscr{Q}_{y_{*}}^{n_{*}}\subset\mathscr{Q}_{max}
. This containment is important because, by (
6.5
), we can (without loss of generality) assume that the half-interval

I_{*}^{+}:=[x_{*},x_{*}+L^{n_{*}}]

satisfies

(6.8)

\#\{B(y_{*},L^{-n})\in\mathscr{Q}_{max}:\pi_{\theta}(y_{*})\in I_{*}^{+}\}\geq\frac{\#\mathscr{Q}_{y_{*}}^{n_{*}}}{2}\geq K.

However, this is only true if we know that
all
of the cells which are contained in
\mathscr{Q}_{y_{*}}^{n_{*}}
are truely maximal cells in
\mathscr{Q}_{max}
. The lower bound (
6.8
) then implies that

I_{*}^{+}\subseteq G_{K}.

We now claim that

(6.9)

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{2LKM}\cap I_{*}\big)\lesssim(KL)\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(I_{*}^{+}\big)\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{M}\big)

which is the inequality we want to prove with
I_{*}^{+}\subset G_{K}
in place of
G_{K}
. We prove this by utilizing the self-similarity of the sets
\mathcal{S}^{n_{*}},...,\mathcal{S}^{N}
and a rescaling by a factor of
L^{n_{*}}
.
To this end, let
x\in G_{2LKM}\cap I_{0}
. Recall that, at step
n_{*}
, we know that the collection

\mathscr{Q}_{y_{*}}^{n_{*}}

defined in (
6.7
)
consists only of truly maximal discs
Q\in\mathscr{Q}_{max}
. Moreover, since for any
x,y\in I_{*}^{+}
, we have
|x-y^{*}|\leq L^{-n_{*}}=\textrm{diam}(Q^{*})
, and so we know that
\mathscr{Q}_{y_{*}}^{n_{*}}\subseteq\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{n_{*}}
, where recall

\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{n_{*}}:=\{Q\in\mathscr{Q}^{n_{*}}:\mathcal{V}_{x}\cap Q\neq\emptyset\},

and satisfies

(6.10)

\#\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{n_{*}}\leq 2LK.

Because
x\in G_{2LKM}
, we know that there must exist an
n>n_{*}+1
such that

(6.11)

\#\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{n}\geq 2LKM.

So, let

\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{*}:=\{Q\in\bigcup_{n=n_{*}+1}^{N}\mathscr{Q}^{n}:Q\subset Q^{\prime}\textrm{ for some }Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{n_{*}}\},

which are the children at all scales
n_{*}<n\leq N
of (truly maximal) cells
Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{n_{*}}.

Comparing (
6.10
) and (
6.11
) and utilizing the true maximality of the squares
Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{n_{*}}
, we see that (at least) one of the discs
Q^{\prime}\in\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{n_{*}}
must generate
\geq M
many children.

By applying the rescaling
y\mapsto L^{n_{*}}y
to each of the
\leq 2LK
-many component cells of
\mathscr{Q}_{x}^{*}
, we obtain
2LK
-many copies of the set
\mathcal{S}^{N-n_{*}}
, and so we see that
x
is contained in (at most)
2LK
-many self-similar copies of the set
G_{M}
. Since this holds for any
x\in G_{2KLM}\cap I_{*}
, we see that

\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(x\in G_{2KLM}\cap I_{*}\big)\leq(2LK)\,\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(I_{*}\big)\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{M}\big)\lesssim(2LK)\,\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(I_{*}^{+}\big)\mathcal{H}^{1}\big(G_{M}\big).

To extend the estimate (
6.9
) from
