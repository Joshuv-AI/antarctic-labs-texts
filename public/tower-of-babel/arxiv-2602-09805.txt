Decomposing Reasoning Efficiency in Large Language Models
Daniel Kaiser, Arnoldo Frigessi, Ali Ramezani-Kebrya, Benjamin Ricaud

Abstract
Large language models trained for reasoning trade off inference tokens against accuracy, yet standard evaluations report only final accuracy, obscuring where tokens are spent or wasted. We introduce a trace-optional framework that decomposes token efficiency into interpretable factors: completion under a fixed token budget (avoiding truncation), conditional correctness given completion, and verbosity (token usage). When benchmark metadata provides per-instance workload proxies, we further factor verbosity into two components: mean verbalization overhead (tokens per work unit) and a coupling coefficient capturing how overhead scales with task workload. When reasoning traces are available, we add deterministic trace-quality measures (grounding, repetition, prompt copying) to separate degenerate looping from verbose-but-engaged reasoning, avoiding human labeling and LLM judges. Evaluating 25 models on CogniLoad, we find that accuracy and token-efficiency rankings diverge (Spearman ρ = 0.63 \rho=0.63 ), efficiency gaps are often driven by conditional correctness, and verbalization overhead varies by about 9 × 9\times (only weakly related to model scale). Our decomposition reveals distinct bottleneck profiles that suggest different efficiency interventions.

Introduction

Reasoning performance in large language models is commonly reported as accuracy on benchmark tasks. However, this accuracy comes at a cost: the reasoning tokens spent achieving it. Two models with identical accuracy can differ by an order of magnitude in how many tokens they need, yet standard benchmarks do not surface this variation. In this paper, we therefore measure
token efficiency
as the expected number of correct answers per 1,000 generated output tokens, and ask which mechanisms drive efficiency gaps.

The Inference-Time Scaling Paradigm.

A decisive shift has emerged from scaling pre-training to scaling inference-time compute
(
16
;
13
)
, creating a tension between token cost and accuracy gains. On one hand,
16
demonstrate that adaptive allocation of test-time compute can outperform parameter scaling by an order of magnitude with models like OpenAI’s o1/o3 and DeepSeek-R1
(
4
)
generating extended reasoning traces that trade tokens for accuracy. On the other hand,
22
identify the phenomenon of “overthinking,” where extended Chain-of-Thought actually degrades performance through ”hallucinated doubt” and error propagation
(
17
;
1
;
20
)
. Resolving this tension requires decomposing
where
tokens contribute to reasoning versus
where
they represent waste. These pathologies are invisible to accuracy-only evaluation and motivate our focus on reasoning efficiency as the balance between accuracy and inference costs.

Limitations of Existing Metrics.

Existing efficiency-aware evaluations face three limitations. First, aggregated metrics like Accuracy per Computation Unit
(
12
)
produce a single scalar that conflates incorrect reasoning with excessive verbalization. Second, trace evaluation frameworks
(
8
)
can localize process failures but rely on costly human annotation or LLM-as-a-judge. Third, approaches like OckBench
(
2
)
measure token efficiency but do not normalize it by
intrinsic task complexity
: spending 1,000 tokens on a simple task that requires five operations to solve should not score equivalently to spending 1,000 tokens on a task requiring 100 operations.

Contributions.

To address these limitations, we contribute a methodology for decomposing reasoning efficiency at increasing levels of granularity, each requiring additional data (
Table

1
).

1.

Outcome-level efficiency decomposition.
We define token efficiency

E_{0}
and factor it into robustness against token budget truncation, logic robustness, and verbosity to localize
waste to distinct failure modes, using only answer correctness and token counts
(
Section

3.1
).

2.

Workload-normalized verbosity.
Using per-instance workload proxies from benchmark metadata, we decompose verbosity into verbalization overhead
\bar{VO}
and workload coupling coefficient
\kappa
, revealing large cross-model variations only weakly related to model size (
Section

3.2
).

3.

Trace-quality decomposition.
When reasoning traces are available,
we further decompose verbosity into deterministic measures of trace quality like grounding, repetition, and prompt copying (
Section

3.3
).

Research Questions.

This framework enables us to address: RQ1) Do efficiency and accuracy rankings diverge?, RQ2) Which decomposition components explain the largest efficiency gaps?, and RQ3) Can lightweight trace-quality metrics distinguish degenerate from engaged reasoning?

Evaluating 224,000 experiments across 25 models and 142,800 traces from 12 trace-visible (open-weight) models, we show that efficiency rankings diverge from accuracy, logic robustness drives most gaps, verbalization overhead varies
9\times
, and token budget truncations exhibit distinctive loop signatures and repetition patterns.

Related Work

Overthinking and Reasoning Dynamics.

Reasoning models often produce unnecessarily long chains—termed
overthinking

(
17
)
. The RCP framework formalizes this as generation past the optimal step count via semantic path convergence
(
20
)
.
22
propose thinking-optimal scaling to find minimal effective reasoning length. Our
\bar{VO}
and
\kappa
complement these: where RCP requires latent-space analysis, our metrics are trace-free; where thinking-optimal scaling finds the optimal point, we determine
why
models deviate from it.

Token-Budget-Aware Approaches.

A growing literature reduces reasoning tokens through budgeting
(
5
)
, adaptive termination
(
3
)
, and length-controllable tuning
(
12
)
. Our framework complements these by diagnosing
why
models use tokens they do and
which
interventions would be most effective.

Efficiency Metrics.

Token-budget-aware evaluation shows accuracy gains disappear once compute is charged
(
19
)
. Accuracy per Computation Unit quantifies accuracy/cost trade-offs
(
12
)
and Process Efficiency measures productive-to-total token ratios
(
17
)
.
Crucially, OckBench
(
2
)
reports aggregate token counts without distinguishing whether inefficiency stems from reasoning failures, token budget truncation, or excessive verbalization — distinctions our decomposition makes explicit. ReasonBENCH
(
14
)
shows sophisticated reasoning methods increase instability, a hidden efficiency cost. Our framework differs by (i) decomposing and quantifying efficiency into distinct failure modes rather than a single scalar, and (ii) normalizing verbosity by task complexity via per-instance workload proxies.

Trace Evaluation.

Trace evaluation approaches include annotation-based taxonomies
(
8
)
, process reward models
(
10
)
, graph-based topology
(
21
)
, Reasoning Style Vectors
(
23
)
, and entropy-guided segmentation
(
9
)
. These methods assume trace availability; our outcome-level decomposition does not. When traces are accessible, our trace-quality measures use only surface statistics and benchmark ontology—no LLM-as-judge required.

Reasoning Benchmarks.

ZebraLogic tests constraint satisfaction, showing accuracy decays with log search-space size
(
11
)
. BABILong reveals models utilize only 10–20% of long contexts effectively
(
7
)
. CogniLoad
(
6
)
provides complementary value: its deterministic generator records statement-level structure, enabling a workload proxy
W
to be computed without trace analysis. Our methodology serves as a template for efficiency analysis on any benchmark providing similar structural metadata.

3.1
Outcome-Level Decomposition

This decomposition requires only three quantities per instance: success indicator
Succ
, output token count
T
, and a token budget truncation flag. For an instance
I
drawn from benchmark distribution
\mathcal{D}
, let
Succ\in\{0,1\}
indicate success (
Succ=1
iff the final answer is correct) and let
T>0
be the output token count.

We define token efficiency
E_{0}
as the ratio of success probability to expected token usage, scaled by 1000:

E_{0}=\frac{P(\mathsf{S})}{\mathbb{E}[T]/1000}=\frac{1000\cdot P(\mathsf{S})}{\mathbb{E}[T]}.

(1)

Interpretation:
E_{0}=(1000/\mathbb{E}[T])\,P(\mathsf{S})
is the expected number of correct answers per thousand generated output tokens. For readability, we report
100\cdot E_{0}
in tables/figures (denoted
E_{0}(\%)
).

Given
n
evaluated instances
\{(Succ_{i},T_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the corresponding estimator is

\widehat{E}_{0}\;=\;1000\cdot\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Succ_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}}.

(2)

We use
\mathbb{E}[Succ]/\mathbb{E}[T]
rather than
\mathbb{E}[Succ/T]
because the latter overweights short failures (cases where the model guesses the answer without reasoning). See Appendix
A
for the full derivation and a discussion about our use of the ratio-of-expectations.

Outcome taxonomy.

Each model response falls into exactly one of three mutually exclusive categories:

•

Token budget truncation (
\mathsf{C}
)
: The response doesn’t conclude within the token budget (32,768 tokens in our experiments) resulting in no final answer.

•

Success (
\mathsf{S}
)
: The response concludes within the token budget, and the answer is correct.

•

Logic failure (
\mathsf{L}
)
: The response concludes within the token budget, but the answer is incorrect.

Token budget truncation and logic robustness.

Let
\mathsf{N}:=\mathsf{C}^{c}
denote the event of no token budget truncation. We define:

r_{\text{ctx}}:=P(\mathsf{N})=1-P(\mathsf{C}),\qquad r_{\text{logic}}:=P(\mathsf{S}\mid\mathsf{N}).

Token budget truncation
r_{\text{ctx}}
measures the probability that a model avoids exceeding the token budget while logic robustness
r_{\text{logic}}
measures the probability of correctness conditional on not truncating. By the law of total probability:

P(\mathsf{S})\;=\;P(\mathsf{S}\cap\mathsf{N})\;=\;P(\mathsf{N})\,P(\mathsf{S}\mid\mathsf{N})\;=\;r_{\text{ctx}}\cdot r_{\text{logic}},

(3)

with complete proof in Appendix
A
.

Token-level decomposition.

Combining (
1
) and (
3
) and taking logs yields:

\log E_{0}\;=\;\underbrace{\log r_{\text{ctx}}}_{\text{truncation}}\;+\;\underbrace{\log r_{\text{logic}}}_{\text{logic}}\;-\;\underbrace{\log\mathbb{E}[T]}_{\text{verbosity}}\;+\;\log 1000.

(4)

Taking logarithms converts the multiplicative decomposition into an additive one, enabling direct comparison of component contributions.

3.2
Workload-Normalized Verbosity

To interpret token usage relative to task demands, we normalize efficiency by a workload proxy
W
. We define
workload
as the task-implied processing required to transform its input into the correct answer under a benchmark’s semantics.

Given a workload proxy
W
, we define the per-instance verbalization overhead:

VO:=\frac{T}{W},

so that
T=W\cdot VO
pointwise. Taking expectations:

\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbb{E}[W\cdot VO].

Since
W
and
VO
may be correlated (models might use proportionally more or fewer tokens on complex instances), we define the coupling coefficient:

\kappa:=\frac{\mathbb{E}[W\cdot VO]}{\mathbb{E}[W]\cdot\mathbb{E}[VO]}=1+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(W,VO)}{\mathbb{E}[W]\cdot\mathbb{E}[VO]}.

(5)

This yields the exact factorization:

\mathbb{E}[T]\;=\;\mathbb{E}[W]\cdot\bar{VO}\cdot\kappa,

(6)

where
\bar{VO}:=\mathbb{E}[VO]
is the expected verbalization overhead.

Interpretation of
\kappa
.

For
\kappa<1
: Negative coupling, overhead
VO
tends to be lower on higher-workload instances (
\mathrm{Cov}(W,VO)<0
).
\kappa=1
: No coupling,
W
and
VO
are uncorrelated in expectation.
\kappa>1
: Positive coupling, overhead tends to increase with workload (
\mathrm{Cov}(W,VO)>0
) – the model over-invests tokens on complex instances.

Since all models are evaluated on the same instance set,
\mathbb{E}[W]
is shared and cancels in cross-model comparisons. Defining
\Delta\log x:=\log x-\log x_{\mathrm{ref}}
relative to a reference model, we obtain:

\Delta\log E_{0}\;=\;\Delta\log r_{\text{ctx}}+\Delta\log r_{\text{logic}}-\Delta\log\bar{VO}-\Delta\log\kappa.

(7)

3.3
Trace-Quality Decomposition

When reasoning traces are available, we decompose verbosity into trace quality
(signal density) and workload-normalized signal verbosity. All measures are deterministic, requiring only benchmark ontology and surface statistics — no LLM-as-judge, human annotation, or embeddings.

Analysis tokenization.

We tokenize traces into lowercase alphanumeric sequences.
Let
T^{(\mathrm{w})}
denote trace length under this tokenization. Because
E_{0}

uses provider-reported model tokens
T
, we convert analysis-token counts to model-token units by scaling (defined below).

Token labels.

For each analysis-token position
j\in\{1,\ldots,T^{(\mathrm{w})}\}
, we compute
three boolean labels:

•

Grounded (
G_{j}
)
: Token
j
appears near (
\pm w
tokens) a
benchmark-defined entity or value, with a nearby anchor word confirming
the model is reasoning about that entity rather than coincidentally
producing matching text. See Appendix
B
for our anchor word definitions.

•

Repetitive (
R_{j}
)
: Token belongs to a line that repeats
verbatim later in the trace, or to an
n_{\text{rep}}
-gram appearing more
than once.

•

Prompt-copied (
C_{j}
)
: Token lies in an
n_{\text{copy}}
-gram
that also appears in the prompt.

We set
w=6
(approximately one clause),
n_{\text{rep}}=8

(long enough to exclude common phrases like “let me think”), and

n_{\text{copy}}=12
(long enough that prompt overlap is unlikely to be
coincidental).

Auxiliary grounding diagnostics.

We additionally report grounded-span coverage
g_{\text{span}}
and grounded repetition
g_{\text{rep}}
in Appendix
B.6
:

g_{\text{span}}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{T^{(\mathrm{w})}}\sum_{j=1}^{T^{(\mathrm{w})}}G_{j}\right]

g_{\text{rep}}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{T^{(\mathrm{w})}}\sum_{j=1}^{T^{(\mathrm{w})}}(G_{j}\wedge R_{j})\right]

Signal tokens and trace quality.

We define
signal
as grounded, non-repetitive, non-copied content:

S_{j}:=G_{j}\wedge\neg R_{j}\wedge\neg C_{j}
.
The signal fraction under analysis tokenization is

\sigma:=\frac{1}{T^{(\mathrm{w})}}\sum_{j}S_{j}
.
To express signal in model-token units, we scale:

T_{\mathrm{sig}}:=T\cdot\sigma.

(8)

The
trace-quality factor
is the token-weighted signal fraction:

q_{\text{trace}}:=\frac{\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathrm{sig}}]}{\mathbb{E}[T]}\in[0,1].

(9)

We define signal-token verbosity
VO_{\mathrm{sig}}:=T_{\mathrm{sig}}/W

per instance, with mean
\bar{VO}_{\mathrm{sig}}:=\mathbb{E}[VO_{\mathrm{sig}}]
.
The signal coupling coefficient is:

\kappa_{\mathrm{sig}}:=\frac{\mathbb{E}[W\cdot VO_{\mathrm{sig}}]}{\mathbb{E}[W]\cdot\mathbb{E}[VO_{\mathrm{sig}}]}.

(10)

Compression-based trace quality.

Our definition of the trace quality is flexible and it can be replaced by other measures of quality by changing the definition of the signal fraction
\sigma
. Since the compression ratio of a text is an indicator of its information content, we also investigate an ontology-agnostic definition for
\sigma
: the compression ratio of the trace
\sigma_{c}:=|\mathrm{zlib}(\text{trace})|/|\text{trace}|
, yielding
q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}:=\mathbb{E}[T\cdot\sigma_{c}]/\mathbb{E}[T]
. While the mask-based and compression-based measures correlate moderately (Spearman
\rho=0.64
), they capture complementary aspects:
q_{\text{trace}}
measures task-grounded signal, while
q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}
measures raw information density. Models with high
q_{\text{trace}}
but low
q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}
exhibit grounded but structurally repetitive reasoning; conversely, high
q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}
but low
q_{\text{trace}}
indicates novel but off-topic generation. We report interpretable mask-based metrics in the main text and the robust compression-based
q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}
in Appendix
C
.

Complete decomposition.

Combining with the outcome-level decomposition:

\displaystyle\Delta\log E_{0}

\displaystyle=\Delta\log r_{\text{ctx}}+\Delta\log r_{\text{logic}}+\Delta\log q_{\text{trace}}

\displaystyle\quad-\Delta\log\bar{VO}_{\mathrm{sig}}-\Delta\log\kappa_{\mathrm{sig}}.

(11)

4
Experimental Setup

We evaluate outcome-level and workload-normalized efficiency on CogniLoad’s full factorial design. For trace-quality normalization, we additionally analyze models where reasoning traces are available. Unless stated otherwise, empirical rankings and effect sizes in this paper are measured on CogniLoad and may shift under other task families, while the decomposition framework remains applicable.

4.1
Benchmark: CogniLoad and workload proxy

Our workload-normalized analysis requires a per-instance workload proxy
W(I)
.
CogniLoad exports generator metadata that enables a deterministic proxy; in this paper we instantiate
W(I)
as
W_{\text{poi}}(I)
computed from clause counts and needle labels.

Task structure.

CogniLoad generates natural-language puzzles about multiple people with mutable attributes.
Each instance consists of (i) an initial state, (ii) a sequence of
N
conditional update statements applied in strictly sequential order, and (iii) a final query about one attribute of a designated
Person of Interest
(PoI).
The correct answer is determined entirely by deductively applying the stated updates to the evolving state.

Three parameters independently control the problem family:

•

Intrinsic difficulty
d\in\{1,3,5,7,10\}
.
Controls the number of people and attributes, and the distributions of condition/update clause counts per statement.

•

Task length
N\in\{20,50,100,250\}
.
The number of sequential update statements to process.

•

Needle fraction
\rho
.
The fraction of
needles
(statements that affect the PoI’s final state) versus
hay
distractors (irrelevant to the PoI).

Workload proxy
W_{\text{poi}}
.

CogniLoad exports clause-level structure (numbers of condition and update clauses per statement) and which statements are needles.
For an instance
I
with statements
t=1,\ldots,N
, where statement
t
has
k_{t}
condition clauses and
m_{t}
update clauses, define:

W_{\text{poi}}(I)=\sum_{t=1}^{N}k_{t}\;+\;\sum_{t\in\mathcal{N}(I)}m_{t},

(12)

where
\mathcal{N}(I)
is the set of needle indices.
This corresponds to the work performed by an oracle solver that tracks only the PoI (see Appendix
F
for the algorithm and proof that all
N
condition checks are necessary).
In all workload-normalized results, we set
W(I):=W_{\text{poi}}(I)
.

Why CogniLoad.

Workload-normalized verbosity requires a workload proxy that is (i) structurally grounded in atomic operations, (ii) available as instance-level metadata, and (iii) paired with deterministic verification. CogniLoad satisfies all three while also providing independently tunable dimensions explicitly grounded in Cognitive Load Theory
(
18
)
that let us study workload-normalized efficiency under a controlled workload variation established to influence cognitive workloads. Other benchmarks expose workload in different units (constraints, path length, supporting facts), making cross-benchmark comparison of absolute
\bar{VO}
values difficult. We view our methodology as a template for any benchmark with comparable metadata.

Outcome suite (25 models).

We evaluate 25 models on CogniLoad’s full factorial design:

d\in\{1,3,5,7,10\}
,
N\in\{20,50,100,250\}
, and
\rho\in\{5,10,25,50,75,90,95\}\%
,
yielding
5\times 4\times 7=140
configurations.
For each configuration, we sample
m
independently generated puzzles from the CogniLoad generator.
Because inference cost differs substantially across providers, we use two evaluation budgets:

m=10
puzzles/configuration (
n\approx 1{,}400
instances/model) for cost-constrained API models, and

m=100
puzzles/configuration (
n\approx 14{,}000
instances/model) for locally-run / low-cost models to reduce estimator variance. The
m=10
suite is a fixed subset of the
m=100
instances and its mean workload proxy
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[W]
is nearly identical (490.97 vs. 491.04), so workload-normalized quantities remain comparable across suites.

Due to rare repeated API errors (e.g., obscure ”safety” limitations, invalid responses, ..), the realized per-model sample size
n
may deviate marginally. We exclude these cases from the analysis and report the realized
n
for each model in Appendix
I
.

Trace-quality suite (12 trace-accessible models).

For trace-quality analysis, we include only models for which the full reasoning trace text is accessible.
In total, we analyze
142{,}800
traces across 12 trace-accessible models:
10 models evaluated at
n=14{,}000
(100 puzzles/configuration) and 2 models evaluated at
n=1{,}400
(10 puzzles/configuration). All trace-quality metrics are computed deterministically from the trace text and CogniLoad ontology (no LLM-as-a-judge).

Prompting, evaluation, and failure handling.

We adopt the methodology of the CogniLoad benchmark to evaluate all models with the same zero-shot prompt and to extract/validate the outcome labels. The few cases of API Errors are not counted in the results. All models use model-developer provided default decoding settings with a 32,768 combined (input + output) token budget. We classify a response as a token budget truncation (
\mathsf{C}
) if the output token count equals or exceeds the maximum allowed (32,768 minus input tokens). In practice these cases correspond to incomplete generations without a final answer. The token budget is set to 32,768 because it is sufficient for all problems to be solvable in principle, while remaining within practical compute constraints.

Results

We answer RQ1–RQ3 using progressively richer signals: outcomes only (
Table

3
), outcomes + workload metadata (
Figure

1
), and, where traces are available, outcomes + metadata + traces (
Figure

2
). Unless noted otherwise, all efficiency terms use generated
output
tokens.

Key findings.

We find that (i) efficiency and accuracy rankings differ (
\rho=0.63
), (ii) in the workload-normalized verbosity decomposition, most cross-model efficiency gaps are explained by logic robustness, with notable exceptions for budget-limited models that frequently truncate, and (iii) in the trace-quality decomposition, trace-quality factors separate degenerate looping (low
q_{\text{trace}}
) from verbose-but-engaged reasoning (high
\bar{VO}_{\mathrm{sig}}
).

6
Discussion

Decoupling Appetite from Capability.

The inference-time scaling literature assumes more tokens yield better results
(
16
)
. Our decomposition reveals when this assumption fails: models with high
\bar{VO}
but low
r_{\text{logic}}
are “chatty” without being capable—additional tokens do not improve their accuracy. The coupling coefficient
\kappa
summarizes how verbalization overhead
VO
co-varies with workload
W
(Eq.
5
):
\kappa<1
indicates that tokens-per-work decreases on higher-workload instances, while
\kappa\approx 1
indicates roughly workload-independent overhead. In our experiments
\kappa<1
for all models, so cross-model verbosity differences are driven primarily by
\bar{VO}
. This sublinearity suggests that models compress their reasoning as problem complexity increases. These profiles inform intervention: token reduction helps high-
\bar{VO}
/high-
r_{\text{logic}}
models but may hurt logic-limited ones.

Toward Targeted Interventions.

The bottlenecks identified by the decomposition suggest that one-size-fits-all approaches to efficiency may be counterproductive. For instance:

•

Logic-limited models (low
r_{\text{logic}}
, low
\bar{VO}
) may benefit from improved training data or reasoning curricula, but not from length penalties.

•

Verbosity-limited models (moderate
r_{\text{logic}}
, high
\bar{VO}
) are candidates for length-controllable decoding or early stopping.

•

Context-limited models (high
r_{\text{logic}}
, high
P(\mathsf{C})
) require loop detection or chunked reasoning strategies.

Appendix
J
provides detailed bottleneck profiles for each model family. These recommended interventions are hypotheses derived from the decomposition, not validated causal claims. Interventional experiments are needed for causal conclusions.

Implications for Benchmark Design.

Our analysis highlights the value of benchmarks that export per-instance structural metadata. When generators record atomic operation counts (as CogniLoad does), workload-normalized efficiency becomes computable without trace analysis. We encourage future benchmark developers to expose such metadata—constraint counts, dependency path lengths, or minimal proof steps — to enable efficiency diagnosis beyond raw token counts.

7
Limitations

Tokens as Compute Proxy.

Token counts are deployment-relevant but imperfect compute proxies: tokenization differs across providers, and tokens do not map directly to FLOPs or energy. We verified robustness by computing character-normalized efficiency: Spearman
\rho=0.98
with token-based
E_{0}
rankings, with maximum rank displacement of 1 position (Appendix
D
). This suggests tokenization differences do not drive our conclusions.

Benchmark Structure and
W_{\text{poi}}
.

Workload-normalized verbosity requires a deterministic per-instance proxy for workload.
CogniLoad provides a deterministic, generator-derived proxy
W_{\text{poi}}
via benchmark metadata motivating our choice to use it, while extension to other structured benchmarks (ZebraLogic, BABILong) with analogous metadata is straightforward. Rankings are robust to alternative
W
formulations under CogniLoad (Spearman
\rho>0.98
; see Appendix
G
). Unstructured benchmarks may require noisier proxies (e.g., reference solution length), which could blur efficiency rankings.

Units of work are single benchmark specific.

Our empirical findings are based on CogniLoad. While the framework generalizes (
Section

3.1
is benchmark-agnostic), the specific findings about model rankings and bottleneck profiles may differ on other reasoning tasks. Workload-normalized verbosity requires a per-instance workload proxy and structured benchmarks can provide this (e.g., constraint count for ZebraLogic, supporting facts for BABILong, path length for CLUTRR). We emphasize that workload-normalized quantities enable
within-benchmark
diagnosis as cross-benchmark comparison requires standardizing “units of work,” which remains an open research question. Appendix
H
discusses how analogous workload proxies can be constructed for ZebraLogic, BABILong, CLUTRR, and other structured benchmarks.

Trace-quality normalization.

Trace-quality normalization relies on deterministic heuristics (ontology-anchored matching, repetition masks, and prompt
n
-gram overlap). These measures quantify signal density and degeneracy, but do not validate semantic correctness of the reasoning trace. Future work could integrate semantic similarity measures to validate reasoning coherence.

8
Conclusion

We introduced a trace-optional framework for analyzing reasoning efficiency in LLMs. In the trace-free setting, token efficiency
E_{0}
and its decomposition into robustness against token budget truncation
r_{\text{ctx}}
, logic robustness
r_{\text{logic}}
, and verbosity
\mathbb{E}[T]
separate distinct failure modes without requiring access to reasoning traces. When benchmark metadata provides a per-instance workload lower bound
W
, we further normalize verbosity into mean verbalization overhead
\bar{VO}
and coupling coefficient
\kappa
, measuring overhead against task complexity rather than output length alone. When traces are available, trace-quality normalization factorizes verbosity using deterministic measures of grounding, repetition, and prompt copying.

Our key empirical findings on CogniLoad are:

1.

Efficiency leadership diverges substantially from accuracy (Spearman
\rho=0.63
).

2.

Logic robustness drives most cross-model efficiency gaps.

3.

Verbalization overhead varies
9\times
across models.

4.

Task length dominates efficiency loss (70–90% drop from
N=20
to
N=250
).

5.

Trace-quality normalization separates degenerate generation (low
q_{\text{trace}}
) from verbose-but-engaged reasoning (high
\bar{VO}_{\mathrm{sig}}
), enabling targeted interventions.

By decomposing efficiency into interpretable, workload-normalized components, our framework enables targeted diagnosis: a model may need improved reasoning, constrained
generation length, or loop mitigation — and uniform interventions cannot address all three.

Impact Statement

This paper presents a methodology for measuring and decomposing reasoning efficiency in large language models. We anticipate the following societal implications:

Positive impacts.

By identifying where models waste tokens (whether through verbose generation, reasoning failures, or degenerate looping) our framework can guide the development of more compute-efficient systems. Reduced token usage translates directly to lower energy consumption and inference costs, potentially making capable reasoning systems more accessible and environmentally sustainable. The diagnostic nature of our decomposition may help practitioners select appropriate models and interventions for their use cases without resorting to trial-and-error experimentation.

Considerations.

Efficiency metrics, including ours, could incentivize optimizations that sacrifice other desirable properties. For instance, a model could achieve high token efficiency by producing terse outputs that omit important caveats or reasoning transparency. We emphasize that efficiency should be evaluated alongside accuracy, safety, and helpfulness, not as a replacement for these criteria. Our framework is intentionally designed to separate efficiency from accuracy (via the
r_{\text{logic}}
term), making such trade-offs explicit rather than hidden.

Scope.

Our work is empirical and diagnostic as it does not introduce new model capabilities or training methods. The benchmarks and models we evaluate are publicly available. We do not foresee direct misuse potential beyond the general dual-use considerations that apply to any work improving the understanding of language model behavior.

Appendix A
Trace-Free Efficiency: Full Derivations

This section provides complete derivations for the quantities used in
Section

3.1
, including the robustness factorization and the work-normalized token decomposition.

A.1
Efficiency definition and empirical estimator

Let
I\sim\mathcal{D}
denote a benchmark instance and let a model run on
I
produce:

•

a correctness indicator
S\in\{0,1\}
(success iff
S=1
), and

•

an output token count
T>0
.

We define efficiency as correct answers per thousand tokens:

E_{0}\;:=\;\frac{1000\cdot P(\mathsf{S})}{\mathbb{E}[T]}\;=\;\frac{1000\cdot\mathbb{E}[S]}{\mathbb{E}[T]}.

The second equality holds because
\mathbb{E}[S]=P(S=1)=P(\mathsf{S})
for binary
S
.

Given
n
i.i.d. evaluated instances
\{(S_{i},T_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we estimate
E_{0}
by the ratio of sample means:

\widehat{E}_{0}\;=\;1000\cdot\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}}\;=\;1000\cdot\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}}.

Why ratio of expectations?

This estimator matches the interpretation of throughput (correct answers per token budget). It differs from the alternative
1000\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}(S_{i}/T_{i})
, which is a per-instance average. The latter would give equal weight to a 100-token failure and a 10,000-token failure, whereas our definition weights by actual token expenditure. For efficiency analysis, the ratio-of-expectations formulation better reflects deployment costs.

A.2
Outcome partition and robustness factorization

Define the events:

\mathsf{S}:=\{S=1\},\qquad\mathsf{C}:=\{\text{token budget truncation}\},\qquad\mathsf{N}:=\mathsf{C}^{c}.

Define logic failure as “incorrect without token budget truncation”:

\mathsf{L}:=\mathsf{N}\cap\mathsf{S}^{c}.

Then
\{\mathsf{S},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{L}\}
is a partition of outcomes: the events are pairwise disjoint and their union is the full sample space.

We define

r_{\text{ctx}}:=P(\mathsf{N})=1-P(\mathsf{C}),\qquad r_{\text{logic}}:=P(\mathsf{S}\mid\mathsf{N}).

By the definition of conditional probability,

P(\mathsf{S})=P(\mathsf{S}\cap\mathsf{N})=P(\mathsf{N})\,P(\mathsf{S}\mid\mathsf{N})=r_{\text{ctx}}\,r_{\text{logic}}.

Estimators.

With indicators
C_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for token budget truncation and
S_{i}
for success, let
L_{i}:=\mathbf{1}\{C_{i}=0\ \text{and}\ S_{i}=0\}
. Then

\widehat{r}_{\text{ctx}}=1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i},\qquad\widehat{r}_{\text{logic}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-C_{i})}=1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-C_{i})}.

A.3
Log decomposition of token efficiency

Starting from

E_{0}=1000\cdot\frac{P(\mathsf{S})}{\mathbb{E}[T]}=1000\cdot\frac{r_{\text{ctx}}\,r_{\text{logic}}}{\mathbb{E}[T]},

taking logs yields

\log E_{0}=\log r_{\text{ctx}}+\log r_{\text{logic}}-\log\mathbb{E}[T]+\log 1000,

which is
Equation

4
.

A.4
Work-normalized factorization of
\mathbb{E}[T]
and
\kappa

Let
W_{\text{poi}}=W(I)
denote micro-load (a deterministic lower bound on required work for instance
I
). Define per-instance overhead
VO:=T/W_{\text{poi}}
, so that pointwise

T=W_{\text{poi}}\cdot VO.

Taking expectations gives

\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{poi}}\,VO].

In general
\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{poi}}\,VO]\neq\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{poi}}]\mathbb{E}[VO]
unless
W_{\text{poi}}
and
VO
are independent, so we define

\bar{VO}:=\mathbb{E}[VO],\qquad\kappa:=\frac{\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{poi}}\,VO]}{\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{poi}}]\mathbb{E}[VO]}=1+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(W_{\text{poi}},VO)}{\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{poi}}]\mathbb{E}[VO]}.

This yields the exact identity

\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{poi}}]\cdot\bar{VO}\cdot\kappa,

which is
Equation

6
.

A.5
Relative decomposition across models

Substitute
\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{poi}}]\bar{VO}\kappa
into the expression for
E_{0}
:

E_{0}=1000\cdot\frac{r_{\text{ctx}}\,r_{\text{logic}}}{\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{poi}}]\bar{VO}\kappa}.

Taking logs,

\log E_{0}=\log r_{\text{ctx}}+\log r_{\text{logic}}-\log\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{poi}}]-\log\bar{VO}-\log\kappa+\log 1000.

When comparing models evaluated on the same instance distribution,
\mathbb{E}[W_{\text{poi}}]
is shared across models and cancels in differences. For a reference model “ref” and
\Delta\log x:=\log x-\log x_{\mathrm{ref}}
, we obtain

\Delta\log E_{0}=\Delta\log r_{\text{ctx}}+\Delta\log r_{\text{logic}}-\Delta\log\bar{VO}-\Delta\log\kappa,

which is
Equation

7
.

Appendix B
Trace Analysis Implementation

This appendix details the implementation of trace-quality measures.

B.1
Analysis Tokenization

We use a simple word-like tokenization for trace analysis:

1.

Lowercase all text

2.

Split on whitespace and punctuation (preserving hyphens within words)

3.

Filter to alphanumeric tokens (including hyphenated compounds)

This produces analysis tokens
x_{1},\ldots,x_{T^{(\text{w})}}
. We convert analysis-token counts to model-token counts by scaling with the observed ratio
T/T^{(\text{w})}
.

B.2
Grounded-Span Detection

Ontology extraction.

For each instance
I
, we extract
\mathcal{P}(I)
, the set of (category, value) pairs from generator metadata. Values are normalized (lowercased, hyphens preserved).

Anchored matching.

For each
(c,v)\in\mathcal{P}(I)
:

1.

Find all occurrences of value
v
in the trace (with alias matching for spelling variants)

2.

For each occurrence at position
j
, check if any anchor token from
A_{c}
appears in
[j-w,j+w]

3.

If anchored, mark positions
[j-w,j+w]
as grounded

We use window size
w=6
throughout.

clothes_shirt

•

•

:
hat

•

clothes_socks
:
sock, socks

•

clothes_gloves
:
glove, gloves

•

clothes_underwear
:
underwear

•

:
hair

B.3
Repetition Detection

Repeated-line mask.

1.

Split trace into lines

2.

Normalize each line: lowercase, collapse whitespace, strip punctuation

3.

Identify lines that appear more than once

4.

Mark all tokens in repeated lines as repetitive

Repeated
n
-gram mask.

1.

Extract all
n
-grams from the analysis token sequence (default
n=8
)

2.

Identify
n
-grams appearing more than once

3.

Mark all tokens participating in repeated
n
-grams as repetitive

The repetition mask is the union of these two signals.

B.4
Prompt-Copy Detection

1.

Tokenize the input prompt using the same analysis tokenization

2.

Extract all
n
-grams from the prompt (default
n=12
)

3.

For each trace token, check if it participates in an
n
-gram that appears in the prompt

4.

Mark such tokens as prompt-copied

B.5
Signal Computation

The signal mask is:

S_{j}=G_{j}\wedge\neg R_{j}\wedge\neg C_{j},

where
G_{j}
is grounded,
R_{j}
is repetitive, and
C_{j}
is prompt-copied.

Per-instance signal fraction:

\sigma(I)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{T^{(\text{w})}}S_{j}}{T^{(\text{w})}}.

Model-token signal count:

T_{\mathrm{sig}}(I)=T(I)\cdot\sigma(I).

g_{\text{span}}
).

The fraction of tokens that are part of a valid grounded span (near an ontology value with an anchor word). High

with low

g_{\text{span}}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{T^{(\mathrm{w})}}\sum_{j=1}^{T^{(\mathrm{w})}}G_{j}\right]

g_{\text{rep}}
).

The fraction of tokens that are both grounded
and
repetitive. High values indicate anchored looping.

g_{\text{rep}}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{T^{(\mathrm{w})}}\sum_{j=1}^{T^{(\mathrm{w})}}(G_{j}\wedge R_{j})\right]

Appendix C
Compression-Based Trace Quality

As a domain-agnostic alternative to the ontology-anchored trace quality measure, we define a compression-based signal fraction using the zlib compression ratio:

\sigma_{c}:=\frac{|\mathrm{zlib}(\text{trace})|}{|\text{trace}|},

(13)

where
|\cdot|
denotes byte length after UTF-8 encoding. This yields the compression-based trace quality factor:

q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}:=\frac{\mathbb{E}[T\cdot\sigma_{c}]}{\mathbb{E}[T]}\in(0,1].

(14)

Lower compression ratios indicate higher redundancy (more repetitive content), while higher ratios indicate greater information density. Unlike the mask-based measure
q_{\text{trace}}
, which requires benchmark-specific ontology to identify grounded content,
q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}
requires no domain knowledge and can be computed for any text.

Correlation between measures.

The Spearman correlation between
q_{\text{trace}}
and
q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}
across trace-accessible models is
\rho=0.64
(
p=0.024
), indicating moderate agreement. This moderate (rather than strong) correlation suggests the two measures capture related but distinct aspects of trace quality:

•

q_{\text{trace}}
measures
task-grounded signal
: content that references benchmark entities in non-repetitive, non-copied contexts.

•

q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}
measures
raw information density
: any form of non-redundant content, regardless of task relevance.

Agreement on degenerate cases.

Both measures identify the same model as severely degenerate: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B achieves
q_{\text{trace}}=q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}=0.05
, the lowest values in our evaluation. This agreement validates that both measures are sensitive to collapsed or looping generation, providing confidence in our trace-quality methodology.

Informative divergences.

Divergences between the measures reveal distinct failure modes:

•

High
q_{\text{trace}}
, low
q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}
: The model produces task-grounded content but with structural repetition (e.g., restating the same grounded facts). DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B exhibits this pattern (
q_{\text{trace}}=0.54
,
q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}=0.18
).

•

Low
q_{\text{trace}}
, high
q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}
: The model generates novel, information-dense content that is not grounded in the task ontology (potentially off-topic reasoning). glm-4.5-air shows the highest compression-based quality (
q_{\text{trace}}^{(c)}=0.30
) but only moderate grounding (
q_{\text{trace}}=0.42
).

Decomposition with compression-based quality.

The compression-based measure integrates into our decomposition framework identically to the mask-based measure. Defining
T_{\mathrm{sig}}^{(c)}:=T\cdot\sigma_{c}
, we obtain signal-token verbosity
VO_{\mathrm{sig}}^{(c)}:=T_{\mathrm{sig}}^{(c)}/W
and coupling coefficient
\kappa_{\mathrm{sig}}^{(c)}
analogously to
Equation

10

Appendix D
Tokens as Compute Proxy

Tokens are not identical to FLOPs or energy, and tokenization differs across providers. We use output tokens because they are observable, deployment-relevant, and available for both open and proprietary models. Since all models are evaluated on identical puzzle configurations, input tokens are approximately constant across models (
\sim
2,000–8,000 depending on
N
); output tokens, which vary by an order of magnitude, determine relative efficiency.

Character-Normalized Robustness Check.

We verified robustness by computing character-normalized efficiency: Spearman
\rho=0.98
between token-based and character-based
E_{0}
, with maximum rank displacement of 1 position across models. This confirms that tokenization differences across providers do not drive our conclusions.

Appendix F
Oracle Solver and Condition Check Necessity

F.1
Why All
N
Condition Checks Are Necessary

CogniLoad statements use implicit subject selection: rather than naming a target person, each statement specifies attribute conditions that determine which people are affected (e.g., “The people wearing green socks listen to electronic music”). There is no explicit subject field to inspect. The only way to determine whether a statement affects the PoI is to evaluate its
k_{t}
conditions against the PoI’s current state. Thus the
\sum_{t=1}^{N}k_{t}
term in
W_{\text{poi}}
is irreducible (i.e. even an oracle must perform these checks to identify needles).

F.2
The Oracle Solver Algorithm

W_{\text{poi}}
represents the work of an oracle solver that exploits the generator invariant (hay statements never match the PoI). This solver tracks only the PoI, checks each statement’s conditions, and applies updates only for needles.

p^{*}
denote the person of interest. Let
S_{t}(p^{*},c)
denote the value of attribute category
c
for person
p^{*}
after processing statements
1,\ldots,t

S_{0}(p^{*},\cdot)
is given in the puzzle.

Appendix H
Generalization to Other Benchmarks

W_{\text{poi}}
exploits CogniLoad’s specific structure, but analogous constructs exist for other benchmarks:

•

ZebraLogic

(
11
)
: Number of constraints to satisfy. The benchmark provides constraint counts per puzzle, enabling direct computation of minimal constraint-checking work.

•

BABILong

(
7
)
: Number of supporting facts to retrieve and chain. The dataset annotations identify which facts are necessary for each query.

•

CLUTRR

(
15
)
: Length of relational path to compose. Path length is known from the generation process and determines minimal reasoning steps.

•

GSM8K
: Number of arithmetic operations in gold solution. This provides an approximation since multiple solution paths may exist.

•

MATH
: Step count in reference solution. Similar to GSM8K, this is an approximation but preserves relative complexity ordering.

The precision varies (i.e. CogniLoad provides exact
W_{\text{poi}}
while GSM8K provides only an approximation) but the principle of task-complexity normalization transfers. We view our methodology as a template for efficiency-aware analysis on any benchmark providing structural metadata.

Extension to Unstructured Benchmarks.

For benchmarks without explicit structure, approximate proxies may be used:

•

Gold solution token/character length

•

Number of key entities or terms in the problem

•

Number of intermediate results in reference solutions

These proxies are noisier but may preserve cross-model rankings. Validation on specific benchmarks is needed to confirm robustness.

increases from 20 to 250 statements. This effect operates through two mechanisms:

Token scaling.

Output token counts
T
scale superlinearly with
N
for most models. While an ideal reasoner might scale linearly (processing each statement once), we observe that models often re-read or re-summarize earlier content, leading to
\mathbb{E}[T]\propto N^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>1
for many models.

Accuracy degradation.

Success probability
P(\mathsf{S})
decreases with
N
, particularly for smaller models. This compounds the token scaling effect: longer tasks require more tokens
and
yield fewer correct answers, causing
E_{0}=1000\cdot P(\mathsf{S})/\mathbb{E}[T]
to decline sharply.

M.2
Intrinsic Difficulty (
d
)

Intrinsic difficulty
d\in\{1,3,5,7,10\}
controls the number of people and attributes in each puzzle, as well as the distribution of condition/update clause counts per statement. We observe diminishing marginal effects: efficiency drops sharply from
d=1
to
d=3
, but the slope flattens considerably for
d>3
.

This pattern suggests that models incur a fixed “complexity overhead” when transitioning from trivial (
d=1
) to non-trivial reasoning, but additional complexity beyond a threshold does not proportionally increase token usage or error rates. One interpretation is that models adopt similar reasoning strategies regardless of whether they track 3 or 10 attributes, leading to comparable verbosity and error patterns.

M.3
Needle Fraction (
\rho
)

Needle fraction
\rho\in\{5,10,25,50,75,90,95\}\%
controls the proportion of statements relevant to the final query (needles) versus distractors (hay). We observe modest U-shaped effects:

•

Low
\rho
(5–10%):
Few needles among many distractors. Models must identify sparse relevant information, which increases token usage for search/filtering and raises error rates from missed needles.

•

Mid
\rho
(25–75%):
Moderate needle density. Models achieve peak efficiency in this regime, balancing relevance identification against processing load.

•

High
\rho
(90–95%):
Most statements are relevant. While needle identification is trivial, the sheer volume of relevant information increases processing requirements and token counts.

The U-shape is more pronounced for smaller models, which struggle with both sparse needle detection (low
\rho
) and high information density (high
\rho
). Frontier models exhibit flatter efficiency curves across
\rho
, suggesting more robust attention allocation.
