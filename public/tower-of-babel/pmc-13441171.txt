Emoji in clinical messaging: results of a focus group study
Authors: Colin M E Halverson, Haley Echols, Lauren Kang, Samantha Vershaw, Joy L Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinicians are increasingly using emoji in digital communication, but limited qualitative work has examined how they assess the appropriateness, risks, and benefits of this practice.ObjectiveTo characterize clinicians’ reported utilization of and attitudes toward emoji in clinical messaging.DesignQualitative study using focus groups and a survey from August to October 2025.ParticipantsTwenty-nine clinicians at a large academic health system were recruited from 4 specialties and included physicians and advanced practice providers, genetic counselors, medical students, and other healthcare workers.ApproachRapid qualitative analysis was used to identify both the benefits and drawbacks of emoji use in clinical messaging.Key resultsEmoji were seen as clarifying tone, building rapport, softening directives, and reducing notification fatigue. However, participants also reported concerns about ambiguity, informality, and potential medicolegal risk due to messages’ discoverability. Their assessment of appropriateness was context-dependent, including power hierarchies, personal familiarity, clinical gravity, generational differences, and the technical affordances of specific platforms. Emoji were viewed as more acceptable in informal contexts and among peers with whom they had established relationships, and as inappropriate in high-stakes and sensitive clinical contexts. Participants felt formal guidelines could be seen as condescending but suggested modifications to promote the clear and effective use of emoji in professional settings.ConclusionsClinicians view emoji as useful but context-sensitive communication tools that should be used conscientiously. They disprefer universal, prescriptive guidelines. However, they promote explicit conversations about emoji use at onboarding and the modification of which emoji are available in local messaging platforms.Keywords: professionalism, healthcare communication, emoji, focus groups, bioethics

Introduction

Clear and efficient communication among clinicians is essential to the coordination of care in contemporary health systems. Clinical workflow depends on the timely exchange of information, often under conditions of medical uncertainty and heightened emotions. Messaging failures may disrupt the execution of clinicians’ duties and contribute to medical error.1,2 Moreover, norms of professionalism and ethical conduct impact how messages are interpreted, and some communication is legally discoverable. Clinicians must therefore balance numerous practical concerns when composing computer-mediated communication (CMC).

Over the past decade, clinical communication has increasingly made use of CMC and incorporated symbols such as emoji and reactions (“reacts”). Emoji were brought into the Unicode standard in 2010, rapidly expanding into a set of thousands.3 Their use is now incredibly common, with over 90% of the global online population reporting such use4 and billions of emoji sent daily.5 In the workplace, they are particularly common among younger generations, the majority of whom incorporate them into professional communication.6 Moreover, emoji are becoming exponentially more common in court opinions.7 Their omnipresence raises questions about how they are used and interpreted in healthcare and what constraints should be placed on their use.

In the clinical context, it is important to distinguish between emoji and reacts. Emoji are digital images treated by computer systems as single characters with unique underlying code points. Although emoji are encoded uniformly through Unicode, their graphical renderings vary by platform. Reacts represent a restricted subset of emoji, curated by platform. Many clinical messaging systems, for example, limit them to a small number of high-frequency symbols—such as “thumbs up,” “clasped hands,” and “telephone”—and make them available as “tap-backs” separate from the full emoji palette.

A small but growing body of work has examined the role of emoji in health communication. Existing studies can be placed into 3 broad categories: First, publications about patient-facing applications have focused on their ability to reduce health literacy barriers8–10 and on the development of the emoji-based patient assessment tools.9,11 Second, commentaries have advocated for the expansion, standardization, and design of medical emoji.12–15 And third, studies of clinician-to-clinician messaging have empirically investigated how these professionals communicate with one another.2,16–18

To address this gap in the literature, we conducted 5 focus groups with clinicians from multiple disciplines at a large academic health system, to characterize real-world practices and attitudes related to emoji in clinical messaging. Within this health system, clinicians have multiple options for communicating with each other electronically, including secure clinician-to-clinician messaging platforms that are compliant with the U.S. federal Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, or HIPAA (specifically Backline, formerly Diagnotes), Microsoft Teams, and the electronic health record system Cerner. For our study, we focused on Backline and Teams, as they simultaneously enable easy emoji use and are primarily employed for professional communication in this health system.

Methods

Setting and participants

This study was approved by the Indiana University Institutional Review Board. Participants were recruited by research team members through convenience sampling within targeted medical specialties and groups. Emails were sent to 4 departments to circulate among their employees, inviting them to participate in the focus groups. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached and no further topics were identified.

We conducted 5 focus groups with participants from 4 different specialties between August and October 2025 across Indiana University Health, a large, academic health system in the Midwest. These groups were: ethics consultants (representing professionals from a range of disciplines whose work directly impacts patient care), genetic counselors, hospitalists, and medical students. We jointly refer to our participants as clinicians for simplicity’s sake. We sought to recruit participants from different practice settings, specialties and roles, and age groups. The high level of interest among genetic counselors led us to offer 2 separate focus groups for that cohort.

Focus groups

Focus groups were designed to last 45 minutes to 1 hour each, with most participants meeting in person and some virtually through Teams. Conversations were facilitated by 1 member of the research team while 1 or 2 others served as note takers, and each session was audio-recorded. The facilitator structured the sessions using a discussion guide focused on personal experiences, perceptions of appropriateness, benefits and drawbacks, and attitudes toward the development of guidance regarding emoji in clinical messaging. The discussion guide is included in the Supplementary Materials.

Participant survey

All focus-group participants completed a survey (see Appendix), designed based on our previous empirical and theoretical work on emoji use in clinical messaging.16 It gathered demographic information, self-estimations of texting behavior, and attitudes toward a range of different uses of emoji, using both Likert and free-text responses.

Data analysis

Audio-recordings were transcribed and de-identified for analysis. They were read independently by 3 members of the research team and coded based on rapid qualitative analysis (RQA). Rapid qualitative analysis is particularly appropriate for prioritizing timely insights without sacrificing analytic rigor. Prior studies have shown that this methodology enables efficient evaluation of audio data and yields findings comparable to those generated through other qualitative approaches, while requiring fewer resources.19,20 Guided by an RQA framework, our analysis incorporated template- and matrix-based techniques. We organized templates around major domains that we derived deductively from the discussion guide, including personal emoji use, contextual appropriateness, perceived benefits and drawbacks, and attitudes toward the development of institutional guidance. Analyses were conducted independently by each team member, followed by group meetings to compare, refine, and triangulate themes. Disagreements were discussed and consensus was reached, with consensual triangulation. Participants were not involved in the development or analysis of themes. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the respondents and quantify survey responses. Direct quotations are identified by focus group (ETH = ethicists, GC1 and GC2 = genetic counselors, HOS = hospitalists, and MS = medical students).

Results

Participant characteristics

We conducted 5 focus groups with 29 clinicians, averaging 63 minutes (range: 56-79 minutes). Focus groups had an average of 6 participants, with a range of 3 to 9. Most participants were in their 20s and 30s (76%) and identified as female (79%). They were genetic counselors (41%), physicians and advanced practice providers (16%), and other healthcare workers, including medical students (43%). On average, they had spent 7.1 years in practice (see Table 1). The discrepancy between react and other emoji use is striking, suggesting that for participants, the 2 classes of digital images represent different communicative acts. This interpretation is further evidenced by qualitative comments from the focus groups. Participants repeatedly stated that they preferred reacts to standard messages because they do not trigger alerts, which can result in alert fatigue. Benefits

Participants noted several benefits of emoji use in clinical communication, such as contextualizing requests and improving relationships, reducing notification fatigue, and making communication more efficient.

Participants described emoji as tools for expressing affect and clarifying tone, which they felt were otherwise lost in textual communication. Several compared emoji to facial expressions, signaling that a message was not intended as dismissive, condescending, or hostile. They also noted that emoji can reduce the perceived force of directives, making messages feel less like orders and more like collaboration. Likewise, on the survey, one of the primary reasons the inclusion of an emoji was deemed inappropriate was when it “does not add any emotion or context to the message” (GC1).

Participants also saw emoji as useful for building relationships. They argued that emoji could foster team cohesion and lower barriers to interaction, creating a “shared sense of experience” (HOS). Emoji also displayed aspects of colleagues’ personalities that otherwise were not conveyed.

Participants also emphasized the role of reacts in reducing alert fatigue and mitigating stress associated with high-volume clinical messaging. Rather than composing long responses, reacts provided a low-effort means of acknowledgment. This was especially important for early-career clinicians, who several mid-career clinicians believed were more negatively impacted by the flood of alerts typical of a clinic shift.

Reacts were also valued as efficient mechanisms of acknowledgment. Participants contrasted them with replies like “Thanks” or “We’ll call you later,” describing the latter as overly time-consuming. Moreover, reacts were noted not to trigger a “new message” alert in the messaging platform, further streamlining workflow. Especially in large messaging groups, such as a chain of 20 to 30 residents, they represented a way to signal that a message had been read and understood without impacting workflow.

Risks

Participants also identified risks, including ambiguity, unprofessionalism, and medicolegal concerns. When presented with specific examples in the survey, participants rarely judged emoji use as appropriate for clinical communication. Across 13 items of emoji used in different contexts, respondents rated the majority as between “not very” and only “somewhat” appropriate. The only scenario that received a majority of positive ratings was the use of a “waving hand” emoji in an affirming message—“ Hey, hope your shift is going well!”—which participants described as adding friendliness or at worst, as being innocuous. However, even in this case, several participants added that its acceptability depended on context and that it could still be seen as bothersome in more formal communication.

In contrast to the benefit of clarifying tone as noted earlier, participants emphasized the potential of emoji to compound semantic ambiguity. In particular, commonly used emoji and reacts like the “thumbs up” were viewed as confusing. Some specifically identified this problem as why they rejected emoji in professional contexts. The use of ambiguous emoji was another primary reason that survey respondents evaluated emoji as inappropriate. For instance, 1 participant viewed “Her VBG came back ” as inappropriate because “I am unsure if you’re happy it came back or happy with the results” (ETH). Participants described emoji as “too casual” and “quirky” to be appropriate for the professional context. “I don’t think there’s a setting where I would be sending a message to an attending” (MS), said 1 medical student.

Participants also raised medicolegal worries. They emphasized that messages sent through clinical communication platforms were archived and potentially discoverable. Even when they were unsure of a specific platform’s legal status, they advocated for an overabundance of caution. As 1 participant stated, it “feels like Backline could be subpoenaed” (GC1), and that was enough for him to avoid using emoji.

Contextual factors

Both participants’ use and interpretation of emoji were impacted by contextual factors. They described calibrating their emoji use to the power hierarchy in the clinic and preferred not to use emoji with individuals in positions of greater authority, fearing they might appear unprofessional. They considered it less risky to use emoji with people at equal or lower levels of the hierarchy, where they could build rapport.

Participants preferred to use emoji with individuals whom they knew well, with whom they were on a “first name” basis: Over time, the development of a shared interpretive framework could overcome concerns of ambiguity. Misinterpretation was therefore seen as less threatening in these contexts. Close friends, 1 bioethicist felt, would be “forgiving if I am not entirely sure what the ‘melting face emoji’ means” and accidentally misused it (ETH).

Norms of individual relationships could supersede concerns about power dynamics. Participants stated that they would mirror the practice of higher-status interlocutors: “I have some mentors who […] use emojis, and I’ll throw back [emoji] at them” (MS).

Concerns about professionalism were heightened when texting about patient cases. In particular, clinical gravity was seen as relevant. Participants described emoji as a “lane designator,” indicating whether a conversation was “serious” or “lighter” (ETH). They felt emoji should be avoided in “urgent or really important” contexts (ETH). One survey respondent insisted that emoji should never be used regarding any such case, “even if it is a positive one” (ETH).

Participants also described perceptions of generational differences in attitudes toward emoji use and in their interpretation. “Younger” clinicians were widely seen to be “more accepting of emoji use.” Such a difference was noted as a source of conflict over the perception of appropriateness and professionalism. The conversation in the medical-student cohort focused on navigating relationships with faculty. For them, emoji use by faculty was interpreted as indicative of a positive relationship, of someone who is “more likely to care to get to know you as a student” (MS).

Channel affordances and platform “dialects”

Participants noted that their emoji use depended on the technical characteristics of the specific communication channel they were using. Emoji were described as easier to deploy on mobile devices than on desktop computers, which were seen as more cumbersome and therefore as discouraging emoji use. They also reported using emoji much more frequently on Teams than on Backline. They viewed the former as “more of a chat room […] we’ll talk about everything under the sun” (GC1), while they perceived Backline—their secure clinical messaging platform—as more formal and more closely associated with the medical record, despite not being technically integrated.

Guidelines

Some participants reported shared but largely unspoken attitudes in their teams that emoji were inappropriate for clinical messaging. As 1 bioethicist noted, “Without talking about it beforehand, [we] kind of agreed that it’s just not for professional communication” (ETH). When asked about the possibility of developing formal guidance for emoji use, they were unenthusiastic. However, some offered specific, often technical suggestions, including limiting the available emoji “to like 20 or 30” (GC2). Others suggested expanding the number of reacts to include a “heart.”

Discussion

This study examined clinicians’ reported practices and attitudes toward emoji use in clinical messaging. Participants identified both the benefits and risks of this practice, and how they were impacted by key contextual and technical factors. Importantly, our data do not reflect direct observations of message content and instead capture the clinicians’ ideology of emoji use, that is, how they believe emoji should be used, what harms they fear they might cause, and how they anticipate they will be interpreted by others. This distinction matters because empirical studies of actual emoji use in medical contexts suggest that overt instances of unprofessionalism and ambiguity are rare.16,21,22 Nonetheless, such metapragmatic discourse is critical in understanding both how clinicians think about emoji and what local norms can and should form regarding their use.

The perceived benefits of emoji centered on politeness, efficiency, and rapport-building, all of which were understood as highly sensitive to power hierarchies. Participants described emoji as softening directives, particularly in communication with peers or colleagues in lower positions of authority. Prior work has similarly characterized emoji as “illocutionary force modifiers”23 and defenses against “face-threatening acts,”24 rather than as mere expressions of affect.

With the proliferation of health technologies, there has been a corresponding rise in what is often described as “alert fatigue.” Participants were well aware of this dynamic and frequently commented on their use of emoji and reacts as a way of minimizing message volume. Echoing descriptions of “react acts” in online medical consultations,25 our participants preferred these stand-alone emoji to reduce interruptions.26

Finally, participants used emoji to build rapport among colleagues, with their reciprocal use even seen as breaking down professional hierarchies. Other studies have similarly found that such usage can improve relations between speakers,25,27–29 though 1 study agrees with our participants that emoji should not be used in patient-facing communication.30 This is particularly relevant, given that 1 recent study found that emoji do appear in notes in the electronic health record.17

Participants also noted concerns about emoji use in clinical communication. They were widely perceived as informal and potentially unprofessional, and their use on platforms like Backline was discouraged due to its association with formal documentation. Prior research echoes the worry of being perceived as unprofessional for using emoji in workplace communication31 and suggests that it may decrease colleagues’ perceptions of competence and effectiveness.32,33 However, these fears appear to operate primarily at the level of ideology rather than observed practice.

Our participants expressed concerns about ambiguity, particularly due to alleged generational “dialects” of emoji use. Clinicians from younger generations were perceived as more “fluent,” and other scholars have found that older respondents are more likely to interpret emoji literally, while younger respondents are more likely to view emoji as providing “tone modification.”34

Finally, our participants raised medicolegal concerns, noting that clinical messages are potentially discoverable. They expressed unease about how emoji might be misinterpreted by third parties (including patients and lawyers). Emoji are indeed increasingly appearing in legal cases in many domains,35,36 and some scholars have suggested the potential for medicolegal liability over emoji ambiguity.14,27 However, to our knowledge, there have been no specifically medical cases involving emoji to date. Participants’ fears therefore appear disproportionate to documented risk, implying that institutional risk communication may be overcorrecting in ways that suppress otherwise beneficial communication practices.

Participants explained that emoji use in clinical communication was governed by tacit, local norms dictated by hierarchy and relationship, clinical topic, and the affordances and limitations of specific platforms. Similar to findings in other studies, they described emoji as most appropriate for use between colleagues with established relationships, where a shared history of communication reduced the risk of misinterpretation.37 Participants saw particularly sensitive topics as overriding the potential benefits of emoji use.

Our participants expressed neither a desire for formal guidelines—which they saw as unenforceable and burdensome—nor a preference for a blanket prohibition. One medical student even felt that guidelines would appear condescending. However, participants agreed that shared, local norms are useful in reducing the perceived risk of emoji use and suggested conversations at onboarding and explicit agreements within clinical teams regarding the definitions of common and ambiguous emoji and reacts, especially the “thumbs up” and “folded hands.” Previously, scholars have called for a “universal emoji-based language system” for standardized, discipline-specific healthcare communication.14 Our results provide empirical support for such a flexible proposal. Future studies and limitations

While participants were prompted to ground their responses in real-world examples, examining actual messages was out of scope for this study; participants’ perceptions may differ from their practice and their responses are self-reported rather than empirical. Moreover, social desirability bias may prompt participants to downplay the risks or their casual use of emoji. However, we sought to minimize the potential for social desirability bias by practicing reflexivity, and a wide range of emoji use was reported by our participants, suggesting that they felt comfortable speaking up and disagreeing with colleagues.

Finally, while we sought to represent a variety of healthcare workers who use clinical texting, our sample size was limited by practical considerations. The demographics of our participants skewed mostly younger and female, and genetic counselors were overrepresented. The incorporation of medical students’ voices into our study may shed light on generational differences in emoji use, but it also is distinguished by the students’ different relationship to clinical texting as part of their professional lives. Future research should seek to recruit other demographics and other categories of healthcare worker—in particular, attending and senior clinicians—to determine whether our results are generalizable beyond our specific populations.

Conclusion

Emoji are increasingly integrated into clinical messaging, and our clinician participants widely perceived them as tools for conveying tone, building rapport, and supporting efficient communication, and felt that these benefits made them largely positive additions to their linguistic repertoires. On the other hand, they noted that emoji can introduce ambiguity and encumber workflow. They did not express a desire for formal guidelines for emoji use, but they suggested the explicit communication of local conventions at onboarding. Finally, they suggested that secure messaging platforms should consider limiting the emoji palette and expanding the react options to improve users’ ability to express affect and acknowledge messages while limiting the risk of unprofessionalism and semantic confusion.
