Encapsulation of Porphyrins in Polymer Microgels and Microbeads for Heterogeneous Photooxidation
Authors: Imogen Smith, Natthakarn Olanchantarothai, Martin R. Johnston, Zhongfan Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Using porphyrin‐mediated photocatalytic energy transfer to generate singlet oxygen is a premier strategy for green, selective chemical synthesis. However, such catalytic systems are mostly conducted in organic solvents. This work aims to explore the generation and utilization of singlet oxygen for photochemical oxidation in aqueous media. To this end, we prepared two porphyrins with reactive primary amines or charged quaternized ammonium groups for covalent and ionic crosslinking, producing TPP‐loaded hydrophilic microgels and microbeads, respectively. We explored catalyst formulations to optimize singlet oxygen generation and the direct oxidation of various substrates. Both catalysts effectively facilitate the chemical conversion of thioethers, thiols, and aldehydes. In deuterium oxide, oxidation reactions of methionine, allyl sulfide, and benzaldehyde achieved high quantitative conversion due to a longer lifetime of singlet oxygen in deuterium oxide than that in water. Notably, the oxidation of thioethers stopped selectively at the sulfoxide stage, avoiding over‐oxidation to sulfones. While this method offers an easy way to isolate the catalysts in aqueous media, the high cost of deuterium oxide and slower reaction rates in water suggest that future development of nanobeads via microfluidic devices could overcome molecular oxygen diffusion limitation and enhance reaction rates in water.

Introduction

Photochemistry is the fundamental driving force for life on Earth, powering everything from the global food supply to physiological processes. In these photochemical processes, certain key molecules act as catalysts, enabling very high reaction efficiencies. These molecules are natural photocatalysts, such as chlorophyl, widely found in plants [1]. Chlorophyl consists of a porphyrin ring with a Mg2+ ion at its core and a long hydrocarbon tail. Its conjugated macrocyclic structure absorbs visible light and facilitates electron transfer, thereby driving the photolysis of water and generating molecular oxygen [2]. This process enables the reduction of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate and the synthesis of adenosine triphosphate. Understanding their molecular structures and catalytic mechanisms has inspired the development of metalloporphyrin‐based artificial photosynthesis [3, 4].

Compared to chlorophyl, chemical synthesis enables precise tuning of the electronic properties of the porphyrin ring by introducing substituents and/or by selecting different metal ions and axial ligands [3]. Consequently, numerous photochemical reactions can be selectively accelerated using metalloporphyrin‑based photocatalytic systems. For example, tetraphenylporphyrins (TPPs) and metalated derivatives (MTPPs) are among the most extensively studied synthetic photocatalysts that mimic chlorophyl. They share similar catalytic mechanisms with chlorophyl, involving either electron transfer or energy transfer pathways [5]. These photochemical processes have been applied in photocatalytic oxidation reactions, such as the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) and epoxidation, as well as in reduction reactions, including the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and the CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) [6, 7, 8, 9]. Photooxidation has also been widely employed to degrade pollutants in advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) [10]. A typical example is the Fenton‑like reaction, which uses an iron porphyrin to activate H2O2, generating key reactive oxygen species (ROS) like superoxide (O2 •–) and hydroxyl (OH•) radicals [11]. Another important photochemical pathway is energy transfer, in which the excited porphyrins can transfer energy to triplet oxygen (3O2), forming singlet oxygen (1O2). The latter is highly reactive, can directly oxidize organic substrates [12], and is widely used in photodynamic therapy [13, 14, 15].

However, directly applying TPPs to photocatalysis yields low‐energy‐transfer efficiency due to their low solubility and strong π‐π interactions, resulting in low utilization efficiency [8]. One method used to circumvent these issues involves chemical modification of porphyrins with functional groups such as pyridinium, sulfonate, carboxylate, and phosphate to confer water solubility [16, 17, 18]. These TPP derivatives can then act as homogeneous catalysts in aqueous media for the photocatalytic production of gaseous products such as H2, O2, CO, CH4, and ethylene, as well as valuable organic compounds, including formic acid, formate, lactate, and alcohol dehydrogenase [6, 7, 8, 19, 20]. In contrast, for photocatalytic reactions involving liquid substrates and products, catalysts are preferably insoluble, making isolation and purification much easier. For example, TPP is incorporated into two‐ or three‐dimensional nanostructures and used as a heterogeneous catalyst. Typically, 2D TPP‐based nanosheets, 3D metal‐organic frameworks (MOFs), and covalent organic frameworks (COFs) have been developed [12, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. These frameworks, often called conjugated TPP polymers, require large amounts of TPP as building blocks. The hydrophobic nature of their channels may hinder the diffusion of water‐soluble reactants and products. In contrast, nonconjugated polymers have been used as supports for MTPPs, where comonomers can act as axial ligands [26], tune electronic properties, and create hydrophilic or ionic microenvironments [8, 27]. Tailored porphyrin copolymers show enhanced performance in photocatalytic hydrogen evolution [8], pollutant degradation [28, 29], and electrocatalytic HER and CO2RR [27, ‍30]. Despite these advances, polymer‐supported TPPs still use cocatalysts for photocatalysis or tedious electrode preparation for electrocatalysis. Their direct use in singlet‐oxygen photocatalysis remains underexplored compared with their applications as photosensitizers and in combination with other photocatalysts. In one example, Benaglia et al. [31] reported a poly(ethylene oxide)‐supported porphyrin polymer for the homogeneous photooxidation of several substrates, including dicyclopentadiene and R‐(−)‐α‐phellandrene, but in an organic solvent.

To explore these catalytic processes in aqueous media, we report here two approaches for incorporating TPP into hydrophilic polymers. Two TPP derivatives bearing either reactive primary amine functionalities or positively charged quaternary ammonium groups were prepared. The former served as a catalytic center and crosslinker to react with poly(methyl vinyl ether‐co‐maleic anhydride) (poly(MVE‐co‐MA)), thereby forming hydrophilic microgels, whereas the latter was used to form microbeads with sodium alginate via ionic interactions (Figure 1). Both TPP‐encapsulated polymers were used as photocatalysts for the direct photooxidation of a range of chemicals in water and deuterium oxide. The results provide clear evidence of singlet oxygen generation in aqueous media and demonstrate that thiols, thioethers, and aldehydes can be converted to their corresponding oxidized products. This method provides a simple strategy for using TPP‐based catalysts in aqueous photocatalytic reactions.

Results and Discussion

To prepare TPP‐encapsulated polymer catalysts, we employed two approaches to synthesize two functional TPPs (Figure 1). In the first approach, 5,10,15,20‐tetrakis[4'‐(3"‐aminopropoxy)phenyl]porphyrin (TAPPP, 4) was prepared starting from methoxy‐functional TPP (1). Deprotection of 1 yielded hydroxy‐functional TPP (2), as evidenced by the loss of the methyl group signal in 1H NMR spectrum (Figures S1 and S2). Meanwhile, an N‐Boc‐3‐bromopropylamine was prepared (Figures S3 and S4) and reacted with 2, affording precursor 3 (Figure S5). Deprotection of 3 to remove the Boc group gave the target TPP 4. Compared with 3, the disappearance of tert‐butyl protons in the 1H NMR spectrum of structure 4 (Figure 2a) proved the full deprotection of the Boc groups. This change also led to a chemical shift of the methylene proton resonance adjacent to the amine, from 3.48 to 3.10 ppm. UV–vis spectra showed typical Soret and Q‐band absorption bands above 400 nm (Figures S6 and S7), which will be used to determine optimal photocatalytic conditions. Four primary amine groups in 4 enable further crosslinking with a poly(MVE‐co‐MA). Since we used only 5 mol% of 4, the remaining anhydrides were also reacted with hexylethylenediamine (HDA) to enhance crosslinking and introduce pendant primary amines, thereby improving the hydrophilicity of the final microgels 5 (approach I, Figure 1). The product was purified by washing the gels three times with chloroform, and the supernatants were collected and dried. The residue was analyzed by UV–vis to compare with 4 (Figure S7), and the results showed that >95% of TPP 4 was incorporated into the final microgels, owing to a crosslinking reaction between a highly reactive aliphatic amine and an anhydride. These microgels were obtained in 87% yield and had a size of ~100 µm, which swelled significantly in water (Figure 1a–c).

Alternatively, we prepared TPP‐loaded microbeads via ionic interactions (Approach II, Figure 1). In this case, we chose 5,10,15,20‐tetra(4'‐pyridyl)porphyrin 6 to react with (3‐bromopropyl)trimethylammonium bromide through quaternization of the pyridine rings of 6. This one‐step reaction directly produces the target cationic TPP 7. 1H NMR of 7 (Figure 2b) showed that the aromatic proton resonances shift to above 10 ppm due to quaternization. A strong singlet peak at 3.35 ppm was ascribed to the methyl protons of ammonium groups. Notably, a few small peaks from 8.50 to 9.60 ppm were observed due to a very small proportion of incomplete quaternization. Nevertheless, we deemed that this should not significantly affect the subsequent encapsulation, as multiple cationic groups were present, even if one or two arms were missing. UV–vis spectra of 7 and 4 showed similar absorption bands (Figure S8). The beads were formed by mixing 7 with a sodium alginate (SA) solution and then crosslinking with Ca2+, producing microbeads with a very uniform size and an average diameter of 2.2 mm (Figure ‍1d,e). We have explored various conditions for forming the microbeads to assess their effect on photocatalysis (Table S1). These beads are very stable in aqueous media, with no leakage of TPP 7 after 7 days (Figure 1f), as no detectable UV–vis signal was observed in solution compared with pure TPP 7 (Figure S9). At such formulation, we obtained 130–150 beads per 1 mL of SA/7 solution.

Controlled oxidation of thioethers to sulfoxides is important for the pharmaceutical industry, pesticide chemistry, and material science [32, 33, 34]. After obtaining the two catalysts, we next evaluated their photocatalytic performance across a range of oxidation reactions, including from thioethers to sulfoxides, thiols to disulfides, and aldehydes to carboxylic acids. The light source was set to wavelengths above 400 nm, which cover the Soret and Q bands of both TPP catalysts (Figures S7, 8). We first tested the oxidation of L‐methionine and allyl sulfide in different solvents. After 2 h of irradiation, L‐methionine in D2O showed 62% conversion to L‐methionine sulfoxide. In contrast, only 7% conversion was observed in water. For allyl sulfide in D2O, quantitative conversion to allyl sulfoxide was achieved, whereas only 5% conversion was observed in CDCl3. The higher conversion is attributed to the longer lifetime of singlet oxygen in D2O, as previously evident [35, 36]. In the following study, we used D2O as the solvent and oxidation of L‐methionine as a model reaction to investigate the effect of catalysts.

When using microgels without further reaction with HDA, increasing the TPP/L‐methionine ratio to 0.05, 0.13, and 0.20 mol% increased the conversion of L‐methionine sulfoxide to 38%, 62%, and 77%, respectively. However, using microgel 5, followed by further reaction with HDA, resulted in 100% conversion of L‐methionine within the same time (Figure 3b). This suggested that the further amidation created a more hydrophilic environment and improved microgel swelling, facilitating the dissolution and diffusion of molecular oxygen. Hereafter, microgels with HDA were used for the photocatalytic oxidation of benzaldehydes to benzoic acid.

(a) Photocatalytic oxidation of L‐methionine and aldehydes using microgels 5 as a catalyst for 2 h, (b) conversion of L‐methionine to its sulfoxide product using microgels prepared by various concentration of amine‐functionalized TPP 4 and further crosslinking by hexamethylenediamine (HDA), (c) conversion of benzaldehydes to corresponding benzoic acids using microgels formed with 0.2 mol% 4 to substrates, and (d) reuse of microgels 5 for the oxidation of L‐methionine in water and D2O (the slight decrease in conversion in round 2 may result from inefficient stirring, which hindered oxygen diffusion).

When comparing benzaldehyde with para‐substituted analogs, benzaldehyde and 4‐fluorobenzaldehyde showed the highest conversion, approximately 60%, after 6–9 h, but only 10% for 4‐anisaldehyde (Figure 3c). The results agree with a previous report [37], in which a catalytic reaction using 2,4,6‐triphenylpyrylium tetrafluoroborate as an organocatalyst in acetonitrile took 15–36 h for benzaldehyde and 4‐fluorobenzaldehyde to reach greater than 90% conversion, but 4‐anisaldehyde showed a lower conversion of 63%. Although no explanation was provided for the substituent effect, it is obvious that for a radical process, an electron‐donating group destabilizes the benzoyl radical (Ar‐C•═O) and increases the energy barrier for the first step of H‐abstraction by singlet oxygen (Figure 4). A similar trend was also observed in base‐catalyzed permanganate oxidation of benzaldehyde [39]. We tested the reuse of these microgels by oxidizing thioethers, in which singlet oxygen attacked the sulfide group via a bimolecular oxidation process (Figure 4). The much higher conversions of L‐methionine to L‐methionine sulfoxide in D2O than in water were consistent with the results above (Figure 3d). However, reusing the microgel catalyst was less convenient than expected, since swollen microgels could trap reactants and products; moreover, separation could lead to significant loss of the catalysts and products.

Next, we tested the photocatalytic efficiency of microbeads 8. Because these microbeads were prepared via ionic interactions between TPP 7 and sodium alginate and further crosslinked with CaCl2, the concentrations of sodium alginate and CaCl2 could affect molecular oxygen diffusion. To optimize the formulation for microbead preparation and the catalytic reaction conditions, we use 2,2,6,6‐tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) as an indicator of singlet oxygen. TEMP will be oxidized to the corresponding nitroxide radical, which can be detected quantitatively by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR). The first experiment verified the solvent effect for microbead catalysts. The photoirradiation of TEMP in the presence of microbeads showed a typical triplet EPR signal that increased over reaction time (Figure S10). The double integral for the reaction in D2O was nearly ten times higher than that in water, again proving that the stability of singlet oxygen in D2O is higher than in water (Figure 5a). When preparing microbeads, the sodium alginate concentration affects the solid content of the microbeads, thereby influencing their mechanical properties and the diffusion of molecular oxygen. We therefore prepared microbeads with 2, 3, and 4 wt% sodium alginate in water, with the same loading of 7 and a CaCl2 concentration of 0.025 M (Figure ‍5b). At 2 wt% sodium alginate concentration, the microbeads were not strong enough, as magnetic stirring broke them into small particles. At 3 and 4 wt% sodium alginate, microbeads were much stronger, and 3 wt% showed a slightly higher EPR signal. After fixing the sodium alginate at 3 wt%, we further tested the CaCl2 concentration. Both 0.025 M and 0.05 M CaCl2 efficiently crosslinked the microbeads; the higher Ca2+ concentration resulted in a higher crosslinking density. As a result, the diffusion of molecular oxygen became slightly slower, as indicated by a lower EPR signal (Figure ‍5c). Finally, we considered the impact of TPP 7 loading in the microbeads. Interestingly, increasing the catalytic center 7 per bead from 5 to 19 nmol did not significantly change singlet oxygen production, as similar EPR signals were observed (Figure 5d). This is presumably because singlet‐oxygen generation is limited by molecular oxygen diffusion, rather than by energy transfer between excited TPP and triplet oxygen. Based on these results, we used a Ca2+ concentration of 0.025 M and a catalytic center concentration of 9 nmol/bead for further photocatalytic reactions. Under these conditions, the freeze‐dried beads showed layered structures with a large internal space, which should benefit reactant and product diffusion (Figure S11).

So far, we have identified a suitable formulation to prepare TPP‐loaded microbeads: 3 wt% sodium alginate in water, crosslinked in 0.025 M CaCl2 with 9 nmol of TPP 7 per bead. While running reactions in oxygen, we also compared L‐methionine oxidation in air. Obviously, a lower molecular oxygen concentration in air resulted in lower conversion, suggesting that a high concentration of molecular oxygen benefits its diffusion in microbeads and, hence, the generation of singlet‐oxygen species (Figure 6a). While our main aim was to establish singlet‐oxygen oxidation in nonorganic solvents, we were also interested in how mixed solvents affected the reaction. Figure 6b shows L‐methionine oxidation in D2O at various acetone concentrations, and the results suggest that adding acetone significantly decreased conversion. The microbeads were hydrated sodium alginate, which can become severely dehydrated and shrink when solvents such as acetone or ethanol are added. In another experiment, when we used pure ethanol, there was no conversion (data not shown). Therefore, maintaining good hydration in such microbeads is important for molecular oxygen diffusion. Bearing these conditions in mind, we carried out photooxidation of L‐methionine in D2O for up to 8 h, achieving nearly 100% conversion. In contrast, the conversion decreased to about 50% in water. For TPP 7 in D2O, conversion reached only 20% after 6 h (Figure 6c). The results suggested that encapsulation may increase the local 1O2 concentration and accelerate the reaction. We also applied this microbead catalyst to catalyze the oxidation of benzaldehydes to benzoic acids. A higher conversion was obtained compared with the microgel catalyst, but the conversion trend across different substitutions was the same (Figure 6d).

We next compared the photooxidation efficiencies of a range of thioethers, thiols, and aldehydes by using microgels 5 and microbeads 8. The conversions were monitored by 1H NMR (Figures S12–S24). L‐methionine, allyl sulfide, and aldehyde achieved 95%–100% conversion, whereas other substrates could only reach mild to moderate conversions (Table 1). It is found that the oxidation of sulfide stopped at the sulfoxide stage, and no overoxidation to sulfone was observed. In contrast, the oxidation of allyl sulfide by H2O2 produced both allyl sulfoxide and allyl sulfone, suggesting that photooxidation through singlet oxygen can produce exclusive sulfoxide (Figure S25).

Finally, we demonstrated the reuse of microbeads by oxidizing allyl sulfide to allyl sulfoxide. For each cycle, the reaction solution was withdrawn by a pipette, and fresh reaction solution was added. With 4 h of irradiation, we achieved 100% conversion in each cycle until the microbeads began to break apart due to vigorous stirring (Figure S25). The results indicate that microbeads maintain high reaction efficiency and are convenient for reaction setup and postreaction processing.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have demonstrated two approaches for preparing TPP‐loaded polymer catalysts via covalent or ionic crosslinking. To achieve this, we prepared primary amine‐ and quaternized ammonium‐functional TPPs. The former was reacted with a commercially available anhydride polymer and further crosslinked by a diamine to form microgels. The latter was encapsulated through ionic interactions with sodium alginate, aided by Ca2+ crosslinking, to form microbeads. Both catalysts effectively convert triplet molecular oxygen to singlet oxygen, which then acts as an oxidant for the chemical conversion of thioethers, thiols, and aldehydes to the corresponding products. In deuterium oxide, the oxidation reaction was highly efficient because the long lifetime of singlet‐oxygen molecules allows them to diffuse out of the microgels and microbeads into solution. Methionine, allyl sulfide, and benzaldehyde showed nearly quantitative conversion. In particular, the oxidation of thioethers stopped at the sulfoxide stage without over‐oxidation to sulfone. This method provides an easy way to perform oxidation reactions in aqueous media and enables simple product purification. While deuterium oxide is costly, designing nanobeads with microfluidic devices can yield much smaller catalysts, enabling faster diffusion of reactive molecular oxygen species and higher conversion in water.
