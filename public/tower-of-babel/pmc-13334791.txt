Hand hygiene compliance among healthcare providers: a cross-sectional study of the University Teaching Hospital of Kigali
Authors: P. Ndacyayisenga, G. Mukiza, J.P. Mbonyubwabo, D. Mugisha, I. Muhimba, J.B. Hategekimana, D. Ukwishatse, F.K. Rubuga, A. Gashaija
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundHand hygiene (HH) is a critical, cost-effective measure to prevent healthcare-associated infections. Despite its importance, global compliance among healthcare providers remains low at 38.7%. Few studies in Rwanda have examined healthcare providers’ knowledge, perception, and adherence, and factors influencing compliance remain underexplored. This study assessed healthcare providers’ HH compliance at the University Teaching Hospital of Kigali (CHUK) and identified key influencing factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study at the CHUK involved 254 healthcare providers selected via stratified sampling. Data were collected through direct observation using the World Health Organization HH observation form and a self-administered questionnaire. Correlation and linear regression analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25 to identify factors influencing compliance.ResultsOverall compliance was 53.84%. Compliance was significantly higher among doctors (59.89%) than among nurses (55.71%) and allied health professionals (38.42%) (P < 0.001). Factors influencing compliance included availability of handwashing facilities (r = 0.391, P < 0.01), availability of hand rubs (r = 0.124, P = 0.048), presence of facilities at the point of care (β = 17.855, P = 0.040), profession (nurses/midwives: β = 15.571, P < 0.001; doctors: β = 17.855, P = 0.003), awareness of World Health Organization (WHO)–recommended HH moments (β = 8.332, P = 0.033), and receipt of training (β = 10.600, P = 0.034).ConclusionThe 53.84% compliance highlights the need for improved HH at the CHUK. Facility availability and accessibility, awareness of WHO guidelines, and training significantly impact compliance. Enhancing these factors is crucial for boosting compliance and ensuring patient safety.

Introduction

Hand hygiene (HH) is a critical, cost-effective strategy for preventing healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs), often proving equally or more effective than certain vaccines [1]. It serves as one of the simplest yet most reliable methods for mitigating the spread of illnesses within healthcare settings, including between healthcare providers, patients, and their environments [2,3]. Effective HH can significantly impact the prevention of disease transmission, enhancing patient safety and overall healthcare quality [4].

Annually, millions of lives are safeguarded through proper HH practices [5], making it a high-return investment for healthcare systems [6]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that healthcare workers perform HH at five key moments: before touching a patient, before clean/aseptic procedures, after body fluid exposure, after touching a patient, and after touching patient surroundings [7]. Despite these guidelines, healthcare professionals often lack knowledge about proper HH practices and the critical times for handwashing, which are fundamental for maintaining clean hands and preventing infections [8,9].

Globally, approximately 1.4 million people suffer from HCAIs that could have been prevented with effective HH [10]. However, compliance rates among healthcare workers remain insufficient, averaging just 38.7%, with variability ranging from 5% to 89% [11]. In healthcare, people’s behaviour is the primary means of spreading most micro-organisms that cause serious diseases [2]. Proper handwashing with soap is essential for reducing infection rates and combating antibiotic-resistant germs [12].

In 2022, the WHO reported that about 7% of patients in high-income countries and 15% in low- and middle-income countries develop HCAIs [13], yet studies indicate that healthcare providers adhere to HH less than half of the time they should [14]. The WHO’s 2023–2030 research agenda highlighted that without specific interventions, global HH compliance remains around 40%, with lower compliance in low-income nations [15]. The Africa region, in particular, exhibits the lowest compliance rates, possibly due to inadequate infection prevention and control infrastructure and resources [16].

In Rwanda, HH adherence among healthcare workers has been notably poor, with compliance rates falling below 40% [11]. Limited national-level data exist, but studies in institutions such as Gitwe and Muhima hospitals have shown improved compliance with local interventions [17]. Research conducted in 2019 at the University Teaching Hospital of Kigali (CHUK) revealed that although 89.2% of healthcare providers viewed handwashing positively, general adherence was insufficient, contributing to a 15.1% HCAI rate [18,19].

Although previous studies have provided insights into HH practices, they have not adequately examined the factors influencing healthcare providers’ compliance. This study aims to fill that gap by assessing HH compliance at the CHUK and identifying the key factors influencing it. The findings are intended to inform targeted interventions, reduce HCAIs, and improve overall patient outcomes.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted at the CHUK, the largest public referral hospital in Rwanda, located in Nyarugenge district in Kigali city. It was established in 1918; it initially served as a health centre until 1965, when it transitioned into a hospital [20]. The hospital serves as a significant healthcare institution with over 500 beds and more than 16,000 annual patient admissions [20]. It functions as a key training center for health professionals and offers technical support to district hospitals. The hospital uses approximately 800 workers, including 87 doctors, comprising 51 specialists, 397 permanent nurses, 200 paramedical healthcare providers, and 100 administrative staff members [21]. The study focussed on healthcare providers employed at the CHUK, including nurses, doctors, and allied health professionals, reflecting the hospital’s diverse healthcare workforce.

Study design

A cross-sectional facility-based study design was used to assess HH compliance levels among healthcare providers and the factors influencing their compliance.

Study population

The study population consisted of healthcare providers working at the CHUK, including nurses, doctors, and other allied health professionals.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included healthcare providers working at the CHUK who were involved in clinical duties requiring them to perform WHO-recommended HH moments and who provided informed consent to participate. Those who met these conditions but were unavailable during the data collection period or declined to participate were excluded from the study.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using the Taro Yamane formula to ensure a representative sample of the healthcare provider population:n=N1+N∗(e)2

Based on a population of 684 healthcare providers at the CHUK, a confidence level of 95%, and 5% margin of error, the total sample size was determined to be 254 healthcare providers.

Sampling procedure

A probability sampling method was used, beginning with stratified random sampling to capture the diversity across healthcare professions. The sample size for each stratum was calculated based on proportional allocation. As result, the sample included 147 nurses from a population of 397: 33 doctors from a population of 87 and 74 allied health professionals from a population of 200.

Within each stratum, all eligible healthcare providers on duty across all departments during the data collection period were included consecutively until the target sample size for each stratum was reached. Because staff deployment was determined administratively and independent of the study, researcher selection bias was minimized, ensuring broad representation of healthcare providers.

Of the 279 healthcare providers approached, 254 consented to participate, yielding a 91.0% acceptance rate and a 9.0% refusal rate. While voluntary participation may introduce some degree of self-selection bias, the low refusal rate suggests that the magnitude of this bias is limited.

Data collection methods and procedures

Data were collected through two complementary methods: direct observation and a self-administered questionnaire. Direct observation used the standardized WHO Hand Hygiene Observation Form (WHO, 2009) to assess HH practices. Additionally, the availability of hygiene facilities at points of care was also recorded during observation.

Prior to data collection, permission was obtained from hospital leadership, and data collectors collaborated with department heads to conduct observations without informing healthcare workers. Four trained data collectors completed a session covering the WHO Five Moments for Hand Hygiene, the standardized observation protocol (WHO, 2009), and ethical conduct during covert observation. Each observation session lasted approximately 20 min, during which all applicable HH moments were systematically recorded for each participant.

To reduce the Hawthorne effect, observers used a covert approach by integrating the observation tool into a digital platform (KoboToolbox (Kobo Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA)) on their mobile devices, allowing them to discreetly observe practices by appearing to be using their phone for a different purpose. Consent was sought following completion of observation, ensuring that awareness of the study did not influence recorded HH behaviour. Thereafter, participants were informed of the study and completed questionnaire. This approach aligns with WHO recommendations for minimizing observer-induced bias during HH monitoring and is supported by literature demonstrating the use of covert observation to reduce behavioural modification [22,23]. Although this approach minimized bias, some behavioural modification due to subsequent awareness of observation cannot be entirely excluded.

The self-administered questionnaire was designed to capture demographic information, knowledge, training history, and perceived barriers to HH compliance. Digitized via Kobo Toolbox, the questionnaire was distributed electronically to participants. A pilot involving 13 healthcare providers (5% of the sample) was conducted to evaluate clarity and relevance of questions, consistent with best practices for instrument validation [24].

Outcome measure

The outcome measure in the present study was hand hygiene compliance rate (HHCR) determined using the following formula:HHCR=TotalnumberoftimeshandhygienewasperformedTotalopportunitiesforhandhygiene×100

Exposure measures

Exposure measures included knowledge of HH guidelines, availability and accessibility of HH facilities, work experience, workload and time constraints, education level, and training on HH.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS Statistics, version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Both descriptive and inferential statistics were used in the present analysis. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and practice-related variables, while correlation and linear regression analyses were used to examine relationships between independent variables (e.g., knowledge, facility availability, and workload) and HHCR. Missing data were minimal (<1% across all variables) and handled using listwise deletion; no imputation was required. This study adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cross-sectional studies [25].

Results

The study involved 254 healthcare providers at the CHUK, with a gender distribution of 103 males (40.6%) and 151 females (59.4%). Most participants were aged between 26 and 45 years (85.8%), with 108 (42.5%) aged between 26 and 35 years and 110 (43.3%) between 36 and 45 years. Nurses and midwives constituted the largest professional group, comprising 147 (57.9%) of the participants, followed by allied health professionals at 74 (29.1%) and doctors at 33 (13.0%). Hand hygiene compliance rate

With 490 instances of HH performed out of 910 opportunities, the overall compliance rate of healthcare providers with HH was 53.84% (95% confidence interval: 47.71%–59.97%). When stratified by profession, the mean compliance rate was highest among doctors at 59.89% (standard deviation [SD] = 29.56), followed by nurses/midwives at 55.71% (SD = 27.07) and allied health professionals at 38.42% (SD = 30.63). One-way analysis of variance indicated a significant difference in adherence by profession on HH (F (2, 251) = 10.83, P < 0.001). Post-hoc comparisons revealed significant differences between allied health professionals and both doctors (P = 0.002) and nurses/midwives (P < 0.001), with no significant difference between doctors and nurses/midwives (P = 0.75).

The healthcare providers’ compliance rates with HH also varied significantly by department, with the highest adherence observed in peri-operative and critical care (71.7%) and obstetrics and gynaecology (68.6%). Conversely, clinical support services showed the lowest compliance rate at 25.5% (Figure 2). The number of HH opportunities recorded varied across units, ranging from 55 in obstetrics and gynaecology to 215 in peri-operative and critical care. A bivariate regression analysis was conducted to screen factors associated with HHCRs among healthcare providers. The findings showed significant associations between HHCR and several variables, including function or profession (P = 0.000), the presence of handwashing facilities at the site of care (P = 0.022), availability of hand rubs at the care site (P = 0.032), awareness of WHO-recommended HH moments (P = 0.045), and receipt of HH training (P = 0.043). No significant relationships were found with factors such as work experience, high workload, or time constraints.

Following bivariate screening, a multi-variate linear regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the combined effects of significant factors on HHCRs. The presence of handwashing facilities at the site of care was significantly associated with higher compliance (β = 17.855, P = 0.040). Nurses/midwives (β = 15.571, P < 0.001) and doctors (β = 17.855, P = 0.003) showed higher compliance than allied health professionals. Awareness of WHO-recommended HH moments further enhanced compliance (8.332, P = 0.033). HH training also significantly improved compliance (β = 10.600, P = 0.034). Discussion

This study aimed to assess the compliance of healthcare providers to WHO-recommended HH practices and to investigate the underlying factors at the CHUK. The findings of this research are significant as they offer insights into the current state of HH practices within a major teaching hospital in Rwanda and contribute to the broader understanding of compliance levels in similar healthcare settings.

The overall compliance rate observed in this study was 53.84%, reflecting a moderate level of compliance to WHO-recommended practices. This rate is notably higher than the global average of 40% reported by the WHO in the absence of specific interventions [19]. Additionally, the average compliance rate among nurses at the CHUK was 55.71%, showing an improvement compared with the 53.6% compliance observed in 2019 [18]. This indicates progress in HH compliance among nurses at this institution.

Compared with other studies in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the compliance rate at the CHUK was higher than the average compliance rate of 21.1% reported across 27 peer-reviewed studies in SSA [26]. It also exceeded the compliance rates observed in a teaching hospital in Ghana, which ranged from 9.2% to 57% among doctors and nurses [27], and in Debre Berhan Referral Hospital, Ethiopia, which reported an overall compliance rate of 22.0% [28]. This variability in HH compliance across different healthcare settings may be attributed to differences in institutional policies, levels of HH training, availability of HH infrastructure, and ongoing infection prevention programs. At the CHUK, relatively better performance may be due to its status as a national referral and teaching hospital with more resources, regular supervision, and greater exposure to global standards. However, despite this higher rate, there remains considerable room for improvement.

The results of this study offer valuable insights into opportunities for improvement in HH practices across various healthcare provider categories. The findings indicate higher mean compliance rates among doctors (59.89%) than among nurses/midwives (55.71%) and allied health professionals (38.42%), which aligns with a study conducted in 26 hospitals in Rwanda showing that clinical staff, excluding nurses and midwives, exhibited a 35% lower compliance rate than medical doctors [29]. This variation in compliance between professional groups may be related to differences in perceived risk of exposure, workload, or prioritization of infection prevention practices. Doctors, for instance, often perform more invasive procedures, which may increase their awareness and motivation to comply with HH protocols, whereas nurses and allied health professionals may face greater time pressures and competing responsibilities that limit consistent compliance. This pattern contrasts with findings from some high-income settings where nurses tend to demonstrate higher compliance than doctors, suggesting that the determinants of inter-professional variation may differ by context and institutional culture [30,31].

The study also identified variability in compliance across the five WHO-recommended moments for HH. Higher compliance was observed after touching the patient (76.42%) and after fluid exposure (67.7%), similar to the findings from Debre Berhan Referral Hospital, Ethiopia, where compliance was higher after patient contact (42.8%) and body fluid exposure (75.7%) [28]. This suggests that healthcare workers may be more consistent with HH practices when the perceived risk of self-contamination is high, such as after body fluid exposure, compared with moments that primarily protect the patient.

The variability in compliance across departments observed in this study is consistent with findings from recent literature. Studies conducted in hospital settings have demonstrated significant variation in HH adherence across wards and professional groups, reflecting differences in workload, supervision, and local infection prevention practices [32]. Multi-hospital analyses further show that compliance differs across care settings, with lower adherence often reported in less acute environments than in high-intensity units [33]. Evidence from intensive care settings suggests that heightened patient vulnerability, closer monitoring, and stronger infection prevention culture contribute to improved adherence in these high-acuity environments [34]. The notably low compliance in clinical support services (25.5%) in the present study is consistent with findings from similar outpatient and support-function settings, where perceived infection risk is lower and HH triggers may be less obvious to practitioners [35].

In our study, HH facilities have shown potential influence of HH compliance level of healthcare providers at the CHUK. The findings revealed that the number of handwashing facilities and availability of hand rubs significantly contribute to increased compliance levels. This is consistent with a study conducted in Nigeria, which reported that low compliance in large tertiary hospitals was most likely caused by inadequate HH facilities [36]. In addition, the placement of these facilities within care units or wards had a significant impact on compliance rates. This finding is consistent with similar studies that identified the limited availability and placement of HH facilities as barriers to compliance [36,37].

Additionally, factors such as healthcare providers’ knowledge and skills regarding HH, along with training on WHO HH protocols and hospital guidelines, were significantly associated with higher compliance rates. In addition, the type of profession also had significant influence on adherence level. These associations aligns with the similar studies conducted on HH including systematic reviews and meta-analyses from Ethiopia that highlighted the importance of promoting HH, providing necessary supplies, and offering training to improve compliance [28,38].

The potential influence of glove use on HH compliance was not assessed in this study. However, existing evidence indicates that inappropriate reliance on gloves as a substitute for HH, rather than as a complement to it, may reduce adherence to recommended practices, particularly during procedures involving exposure to bodily fluids [39,40]. This represents an important area for future investigation at the CHUK, and subsequent studies should consider incorporating glove use as a co-variable alongside direct observation of the WHO Five Moments for Hand Hygiene.

However, it is important to consider the limitations of this study. Although efforts were made to minimize the Hawthorne effect, where medical professionals may alter their behaviour due to the awareness of being observed, its potential influence on the findings cannot be entirely ruled out. Moreover, social desirability bias may have influenced the results obtained through self-reporting in the questionnaire. The study was also limited to a single teaching hospital, making it challenging to generalize the findings to other healthcare facilities in Rwanda. Additionally, data collection was restricted to daylight hours, potentially missing variations in compliance rates during nighttime. Furthermore, observation coverage varied across units, ranging from 55 opportunities in obstetrics and gynaecology to 215 in peri-operative and critical care, which may affect the precision of unit-level compliance estimates. Accordingly, findings from units with smaller numbers of observations should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Future studies could consider prespecified minimum observation quotas per unit to strengthen the reliability of unit-level comparisons.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of HH compliance among healthcare providers at the CHUK, with an overall compliance rate of 53.84% to WHO-recommended practices. The findings highlight the crucial role of availability and accessibility of HH facilities, ongoing education, and tailored training in improving compliance. Variations in compliance among different professional groups underscore the need for targeted interventions that address specific challenges. The insights gained from this study contribute to the broader understanding of HH compliance in healthcare settings and underscore the ongoing need for improvements in practice to enhance patient safety.
