A Descriptive Analysis of Second Amendment Preservation Activities
Authors: Ugonna P. Okorie, Alexander D. McCourt, Mallory E. O’Brien, Cassandra K. Crifasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
HIGHLIGHTS•Activities to preserve the Second Amendment have become common across the U.S.•Missouri’s 2021 Second Amendment Preservation Act is a high-profile example.•Over 1,300 local preservation activities have been taken throughout the U.S.•The growth of preservation activities may impede efforts to enforce gun laws.

INTRODUCTION

As currently interpreted by the Supreme Court, the Second Amendment of the U.S. Constitution safeguards an individual's right to keep and bear arms, and both household and personal gun ownership are common. Overall, 42% of American adults live in a household with a firearm, with 32% of adults being personal firearm owners.1 From 2019 to 2021, an estimated 7.5 million Americans became firearm owners for the first time.2 Nearly 75% of firearm owners cite personal protection as their main reason for ownership,1 whereas more than 90% of owners rate protection at home as an important reason for their firearm ownership, and more than 80% rate protection out of the home as same.3 In addition, 72% of firearm owners, compared with 36% of nonowners, report that personally owning a firearm makes them safer.4 Despite these perceptions and a common narrative that there is little to no support for gun control, most firearm owners support evidence-based policies. For example, 84% of firearm owners support requiring a background check for all gun sales to ensure that the purchaser is not legally prohibited, 78% support requiring first-time firearm purchasers to undergo a safety course on safe handling and storage before buying a gun, 79% support prohibiting someone subject to a temporary domestic violence restraining order from having a firearm for the duration of the order, and 72% support extreme risk protection orders initiated by family members or licensed healthcare providers, whereas 65% support them when initiated by law enforcement.5

There have been some questions surrounding the constitutionality of various firearm policies in the context of the Supreme Court’s 2022 decision in New York State Pistol and Rifle Association v. Bruen. Since 2008, when the Court held in District of Columbia v. Heller that the Second Amendment protects an individual right, lower courts have generally upheld state and federal gun laws using a 2-part test that weighed Second Amendment protections against government interests in reducing violence.6 In Bruen, the court eliminated this test, around which courts had coalesced post-Heller, instead requiring that courts determine whether a given gun law is rooted in our history and tradition of gun regulation.7 Lower courts have struggled to implement this standard as they look for historical twins or analogs to each challenged gun law. However, in its recent decision in U.S. v. Rahimi, the court clarified the Bruen standard: in finding that laws disarming individuals deemed to be dangerous were consistent with the Second Amendment, the majority opinion stated that courts must determine whether laws are relevantly similar to historical laws.7 Since Bruen, there have been many challenges to state and federal gun laws.8 In addition to these efforts to strike down firearm regulations, there has also been a concerted effort at the state and local level to otherwise limit infringement upon the Second Amendment, despite broad public support for these policies.

Although many local governments have announced actions that ostensibly protect the Second Amendment, there are several high-profile state examples. For example, in 2013, the Missouri General Assembly passed HB436, known as the Second Amendment Preservation Act (SAPA), a particularly aggressive proposal.9 The proposed legislation declared any federal law invalid that “infringe[s] on the right to bear arms under the Second Amendment to the U.S. Constitution and Article I, Section 23 of the Missouri Constitution.”9 Furthermore, that legislation would have prohibited any federal, state, or local officer from enforcing any legislation in Missouri determined invalid by SAPA. Any person or agency that attempted to enforce said federal laws would be subject to a Class A misdemeanor.9 However, after its passage in the Missouri General Assembly, Democratic Governor Jay Nixon vetoed the bill, stating that the legislation violated the Supremacy Clause of the U.S. Constitution and the exercise of free speech protected by state and federal constitutions. The legislature fell short of the majority needed to override that veto.10

The SAPA (HB85)11 was reintroduced in the Missouri legislature in 2019, with new provisions, and was signed into law by Governor Mike Parson in 2021. HB85 deemed federal firearm-related laws invalid, prohibiting public state and local employees from enforcing these laws and working with federal officials. Although this was similar to the 2013 bill, HB85 went further by penalizing—with a $50,000 civil penalty to those who deprive Missouri citizens of their right to bear arms.11 In announcing the signing of SAPA, Governor Parson stated that the law reflected his “commitment to reject any attempt by the federal government to circumvent the fundamental right of Missourians to keep and bear arms to protect themselves and their property.”12 However, at least 1 Chief of Police resigned over the legislation, calling it poorly worded and stating that it would “chill the legitimate peace keeping duties of police.”13

In March 2023, a Missouri U.S. District Court judge ruled that SAPA was unconstitutional.14 In October 2023, the Supreme Court rejected an emergency request15 from the Missouri Attorney General to reinstate SAPA, so the law remains blocked. However, efforts are being made to implement another version of SAPA,16 and concerns remain as to whether this law continues to have the chilling effect predicted by some law enforcement officials in Missouri.

Missouri is just one high-profile example of state and local efforts to protect against perceived infringement on the Second Amendment by the federal government. It is unclear how many other local and state jurisdictions have taken similar action. Given the limited knowledge of Second Amendment preservation activities across the country, a detailed search was carried out to understand how widespread and varied these types of activities are at the state, county, and local levels.

METHODS

An in-depth analysis was conducted to describe the range of actions taken to protect the Second Amendment. The search was carried out from February 2023 to June 2023 and attempted to identify any Second Amendment preservation activities taken at the state or local (city/county) level. For every state and the District of Columbia, an internet search was conducted looking for actions taken by local and state legislative bodies, sheriffs, and other elected officials to preserve the Second Amendment.

Given that many of these activities occurred at the local level and large legal research databases often lack resources for these jurisdictions, a web search strategy was selected. Data collection began with a broad web search for Second Amendment preservation activities. The search included legislation, resolutions, declarations, orders, and other state and local policy actions. Third-party sites were used that listed Second Amendment preservation activities to identify reliable news articles and press releases. Key search terms included [state/county] Second Amendment sanctuary, [state/county] Second Amendment preservation, [state/county] Second Amendment, and [county] commissioners or supervisors meeting minutes. Sources retrieved by the search discussed Second Amendment preservation activities passed or declared in a given county and its neighboring counties. As an additional step, county commissioner sites were searched for an official copy of the action or for relevant meeting minutes.

After the identification of these Second Amendment preservation activities, they were categorized to understand the kinds of actions taken and to compare their similarities and differences across jurisdictions. For each preservation action, the state (including city or county if needed); the year of passage or announcement; the text of the statement, policy, or law; the form of the statement (resolution, ordinance, declaration, law, or order); and any explicit mentions of Second Amendment sanctuary or preservation in the full body of the text were identified. To classify the form of the statement, the title of the document was reviewed. In the rare case that the document did not indicate a form that matched the categories, the main text of the document was further reviewed to determine the document’s intent and effect. A determination that a local government issued a resolution was made if the document or action expressed an opinion or intention of a legislative entity but did not reach the level of an ordinance or statute. Proclamations and declarations, although functionally similar to resolutions, were defined as statements of policy or goals by a government official or entity. Ordinances, statutes, and other legal documents were categorized using typical definitions.

The analysis did not include proposed Second Amendment–related legislation that was either pending or failed to pass. The results are presented as a descriptive analysis. This research was approved and reviewed by the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health IRB.

In addition to the state of Missouri, 10 other states passed legislation intended to preserve Second Amendment rights throughout their state: Alabama, Kansas, Kentucky, Nebraska, North Dakota, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Texas, West Virginia, and Wyoming. Of the 11 total states that passed SAPA laws, Alabama, Kentucky, Wyoming, and Missouri included penalties for individuals or entities found guilty of violating these laws.

In Alabama, the first conviction is a Class C misdemeanor and carries a fine of not less than $500 or more than $5,000. A second conviction is considered a Class B misdemeanor and incurs a fine of not less than $1,000 and no more than $7,000.19 Similarly, in Kentucky, the first offense is classified as a Class B misdemeanor, whereas subsequent offenses are deemed Class A misdemeanors.20 In Wyoming, any public officer found in violation of the law is subject to a misdemeanor punishable by imprisonment for not more than 1 year; a fine of not more than $2,000; or both.21 Missouri’s penalties are much more severe than Alabama’s, Kentucky’s, and Wyoming’s for similar offenses.

The additional 507 jurisdictions (city/county) issued policies reaffirming support either for the U.S. Constitution broadly or for gun rights specifically; 373 were specific to supporting the Second Amendment or the right to keep and bear arms, 76 were related to being a constitutional county or affirming an oath to the U.S. constitution, and 58 were related to specific laws or policies such as opposing Extreme Risk Protection Orders (also known as red flag laws) or other state-level firearm regulations. These activities may be relevant to Second Amendment Sanctuary resolutions. For example, sample text from activities within the 507 jurisdictions included statements such as “[t]he [insert county fiscal court] is committed to supporting the rights granted by the Second Amendment of the Constitution of the United States...”22 or “[t]he Board of Commissioners of [insert county] expresses the intent to preserve, protect and defend the Second Amendment through any and all lawful means, in order to uphold the rights of the citizens of [insert county].”23 These activities appeared to be performative, with no real teeth or penalties for violations.

When examining the geographic distribution of Second Amendment preservation activities, Virginia had the most, with 136 across the state. Montana and New Hampshire had the fewest, with 1 activity each, occurring at the state level. No state or local Second Amendment preservation actions were found to have been taken by Connecticut, Delaware, Hawaii, Massachusetts, or the District of Columbia during the study period. When analyzing the resolutions, it became evident that many states used similar language for their Second Amendment preservation actions. For example, for at least 22 counties in Iowa, county officials appear to have worked with the Iowa Firearms Coalition and used their given template to pass resolutions. The template included language such as “Whereas, it is desirable to declare that YOUR County is a Second Amendment Sanctuary County, as YOUR County opposes the enactment of any legislation that would infringe upon the constitutional right of the people of YOUR County to keep and bear arms” and provided spaces for the jurisdictions to fill in their specific information.24 In the analysis, at least 1 other SAPA was found to have been ruled unconstitutional; Columbia County’s was declared as such by the Oregon State Court of Appeals in February 2023.25

DISCUSSION

This study explored both the quantity and characteristics of Second Amendment preservation activities taken at the state, county, and local levels. Nearly every state had at least 1 activity; only 4 states and the District of Columbia had none identified. Although some of these activities have been in place for more than a decade, the vast majority have occurred since 2020. Although the specific reason for this recent increase has not been described in the literature and is beyond the scope of this analysis, multiple social and political factors may have played a role, including targeted lobbying from advocates, shifts in Second Amendment jurisprudence, and concerns about personal safety stemming from the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic and social unrest in 2020 and 2021. These actions seem to be in response to the growing recognition of gun violence as a public health epidemic and as pushback against the advancement of evidence-based policies; however, future research is needed to better understand the drivers.

Although there was variation in these Second Amendment preservation activities, nearly all were focused on declaring a jurisdiction as one that supports the Second Amendment—whether that be as a sanctuary or a place that supports gun ownership. A small proportion of these activities were focused on opposing specific types of legislation, again suggesting that some of these actions were taken in an effort to prevent the passage, implementation, or enforcement of firearm legislation. Three states went even further by including misdemeanor penalties as part of their Second Amendment preservation legislation. Missouri’s penalties were by far the most severe, with fines that were upward of 10 times more costly than those in Alabama, Kentucky, and Wyoming.

One striking finding from the analysis was the use of templates by jurisdictions that took some form of Second Amendment preservation action. For example, there were numerous counties in Iowa that used a template that appears to have been provided by the Iowa Firearms Coalition.24 Iowa jurisdictions were not unique in their use of templates, because other organizations have taken a similar tactic in other states. The use of templates may decrease the time and capacity needed for local authorities to draft, consider, and announce these actions. In general, these preservation actions across states appear to be taken to, at best, affirm support for the Second Amendment or, at worst, prevent the enforcement of state and/or federal gun laws that are a necessary foundation for responsible gun ownership.26

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. Although a consistent search strategy was used across states and localities, it is possible that some Second Amendment preservation actions were missed. To limit these omissions, the same search strategy was used across jurisdictions, and multiple state sites were searched. The search strategy and results were also refined through comparison with third-party lists of Second Amendment actions and media coverage of those actions. Nevertheless, it is possible that some actions were missed. For example, the search strategy did not originally intend to seek out activities that merely expressed support for or affirmed the constitution. However, if such activities appeared in the search results (e.g., if they were identified on a County Commissioner’s page), they were included in the results. This could have led to an undercounting of Second Amendment preservation actions, perhaps making them even more prevalent than described in this paper. Court outcomes related to each of the collected preservation actions were not examined. This may mean that some actions documented here were struck down or otherwise limited by courts. When litigation or coverage of litigation at the state level (e.g., Missouri) was covered in media identified through the search strategy, that was noted, but it is unlikely that local-level actions could have been captured in the same manner. This is an area ripe for future research. Furthermore, city, county, and other local actions often do not appear in large databases such as Westlaw. Internet searches to retrieve local actions were used, but it is possible that some local actions were not captured. Direct copies of all Second Amendment resolutions could not be obtained. Many local resolutions were not stored in searchable databases, making them difficult to find. In some cases, even when a resolution was found, the links became inactive or died, meaning that the downloaded copy from the time of the search was the only source. In total, there were 428 resolutions for which a copy of the text could not be obtained. For these actions, local news sources that ran stories after the Second Amendment preservation action taken by the jurisdiction were relied upon. Although this means that the evaluation of policy language was incomplete, the overall similarity of the resolutions retrieved suggests that these additional actions are likely to be consistent with the overall findings.

CONCLUSIONS

This study is among the first to examine the Second Amendment preservation activities occurring nationally. Nearly every state had at least 1 jurisdiction that took an action, and many used templates provided by gun rights organizations. Although the ability to enforce these activities is suspected, more research is needed to understand the real and perceived impacts they have on law enforcement efforts and public safety.
