Max Engelstein, Daniel Restrepo, Zihui Zhao

Abstract
. In this article we study the structure of solutions to the one-phase Bernoulli problem that are modeled either infinitesimally or at infinity by one-homogeneous solutions with an isolated singularity. In particular, we prove a uniqueness of blowups result under a natural symmetry condition on the one-homogeneous solution (à la Allard-Almgren [ AA81 ] ) and we prove a rigidity result at infinity (à la Simon-Solomon [ SS86 ] ) under additional constraints on the linearized operator around the one-homogeneous solution (which are satisfied by the only known examples of minimizing one-homogeneous solutions). We believe these are the first uniqueness of blow-up/blow-down results at singular points for non-minimizing solutions to the one-phase problem.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we prove both a uniqueness of blowups result and a rigidity of blowdowns result for solutions to the one phase Bernoulli problem that are modeled on cones with isolated singularities and additional symmetries. Recall that the one-phase Bernoulli problem is a prototypical free boundary problem, given by the overdetermined equation

(1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u=0,&\text{ in }\{u>0\}\\
|\nabla u|=1,&\text{ on }\partial\{u>0\}.\end{array}\right.

First studied rigorously by Alt and Caffarelli in
[
AC81
]
, this problem has received a huge amount of attention over the decades due, among other reasons, to its relationship with global solutions of semi-linear PDE
[
CS05
]
,
[
AS22
]
, its non-convex nature
[
F-RY23
]
, and its connections to eigenvalue optimization and the behavior of harmonic measures
[
KT04
,
KL18
,
MTV17
]
.

It was first shown by Weiss
[
We98
]
, that solutions to (
1
) (suitably interpreted, see Definition
3.1
), are modeled both infinitesimally and at infinity by one-homogeneous solutions to the same equation. More precisely, if
x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}
and
r_{j}\downarrow 0
(or
r_{j}\uparrow\infty
) then up to a subsequence
u(r_{j}x+x_{0})/r_{j}\rightarrow U
, where
U
is a one-homogeneous solution to (
1
).
A priori
, one may get different limits
U
along different sequences
\{r_{j}\}
. Less pathologically,
U
can be independent of the sequence, but the convergence can be very slow (see
[
Wh92
]
,
[
Hir25
]
or
[
CSV18
,
FS19
]
for examples of these phenomena in related non-linear PDEs).

If
U=(x_{d})_{+}
, then the above convergence is well understood; the limit does not depend on the sequence
r_{j}
and the convergence happens at a power rate. However, there exist non-flat homogeneous solutions to (
1
) and when
U
is one of these solutions, the convergence is much less well understood and essentially nothing is known if
u
is not assumed to be a minimizer to the Alt-Caffarelli functional (see more discussion after the theorem statements).

Our two main results completely describe the convergence when
\partial\{U>0\}
is smooth away from the origin and has some additional symmetry, which we call integrability through rotations, after the analogous property for minimal surfaces (see Definition
2.3
). The first result says that as long as
U
satisfies these condition then the limit is independent of the sequence
\{r_{j}\}
and occurs with a Hölder rate.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u:D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a non-degenerate weak solution to the one phase Bernoulli problem (in the sense of Definition
3.1
). Suppose
0\in\partial\{u>0\}
and assume that there exist
r_{j}\downarrow 0
such that
u(r_{j}x)/r_{j}\rightarrow U
in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}
.

Assume that
U
is integrable through rotations (see Definition
2.3
) and
\partial\{U>0\}\backslash\{0\}
is smooth. There exist
\delta_{0}>0
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
C>0
(depending on
U
) such that if
\|u-U\|_{L^{2}(B_{8}\backslash B_{1/8})}\leq\delta_{0}
, then

\|u(rx)/r-U(x)\|_{L^{2}(B_{2}\backslash B_{1/2})}\leq Cr^{\alpha}{\|u-U\|_{L^{2}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1/2})}},

for all
r<1
.

Similarly, with the same assumptions on
U
if
u(r_{j}x)/r_{j}\rightarrow U
in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}
as
r_{j}\uparrow\infty
and
\|u-U\|_{L^{2}(B_{8}\backslash B_{1/8})}\leq\delta_{0}
, then

(2)

\|u(rx)/r-U(x)\|_{L^{2}(B_{2}\backslash B_{1/2})}\leq Cr^{-\alpha}\|u-U\|_{L^{2}(B_{2}\setminus B_{1/2})},

for all
r>1
.

Theorem
1.1
is reminiscent of an analogous result in minimal surfaces due to Allard and Almgren,
[
AA81
]
. At a high level the general strategy of the proof is inspired by
[
AA81
]
(and other regularity results for minimal surfaces such as
[
Sim83
]
,
[
MN24
,
EdSp23
]
), however, the free boundary setting requires many new ideas and brings up challenges that are not present when analyzing minimal surfaces. We will discuss this in more detail in Section
1.1
.

As mentioned above, we believe that Theorem
1.1
is the first known uniqueness of blowup result for singular points in the free boundary of solutions of (
1
). However, there have been uniqueness results for the free boundaries of
minimizers
of the Alt-Caffarelli functional,

(3)

J_{D}(u):=\int_{D}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+|\{u>0\}\cap D|.

Minimizers of the above functional satisfy (in a viscosity or distributional sense) (
1
), but there are many solutions to (
1
) which do not minimize the functional. In
[
EnSV20
]
, the first named author, along with Spolaor and Velichkov, proved a version of Theorem
1.1
for minimizing
u
, and also treated the non-integrable case (see also
[
EdSV24a
]
). The key tool in
[
EnSV20
]
is an “epiperimetric inequality” which quantifies the behavior of the energy
J
near one-homogeneous solutions. However, epiperimetric inequalities can only imply regularity results like Theorem
1.1
for minimizing solutions. As such, by necessity the methods of this paper are almost entirely disjoint from those of
[
EnSV20
]
. Furthermore the main results of these papers do not imply one another; for integrable cones, our Theorem
1.1
is more general but
[
EnSV20
]
are able to treat non-integrable cones which we do not discuss here.

One can ask about the assumption of integrability in Theorem
1.1
. To show this is sharp we would have to produce an example of a singular solution which has a non-unique non-integrable blowup (or slow convergence to that blowup). Unfortunately, there are currently no known examples of singular solutions to the Bernoulli problem which are not a cone themselves, so we can only discuss the sharpness of the hypothesis in analogy with other non-linear PDE. In the setting of minimal surfaces, the assumption that
U
is integrable is not needed to show uniqueness of the blow-up (see,
[
Sim83
]
) but without this additional symmetry the convergence can be slower than
r^{\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
(see
[
AS88
]
). Indeed, in
[
EnSV20
]
, a very closely related hypothesis of integrability was needed in order to prove
r^{\alpha}
-convergence. Fortunately, we are able to show that axially symmetric cones are integrable through rotations (these include the only known minimizing cones).

Our second result gives a characterization for global solutions of (
1
) which are modeled at infinity by one-homogeneous solutions with isolated singularity at the origin and additional symmetries. To state the theorem we recall a recent result of De Silva-Jerison-Shahgholian, which guarantees the existence of a smooth foliation around one-homogeneous minimizers with isolated singularities:

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem 1 of
[
DSJS22
]
)
.

Let
U
be a homogeneous global minimizer of the Alt-Caffarelli energy. There exist a unique pair
\overline{U},\underline{U}
of global minimizers with analytic free boundary satisfying

(1)

\underline{U}\leq U\leq\overline{U}
and

\operatorname{dist}\!\bigl(F(\overline{U}),\{0\}\bigr)=\operatorname{dist}\!\bigl(F(\underline{U}),\{0\}\bigr)=1
;

(2)

the graphs of
\overline{U}_{t}=\frac{\overline{U}(t\cdot)}{t}
and
\underline{U}_{t}=\frac{\underline{U}(t\cdot)}{t}
define a foliation of the half-space

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)
for
t\geq 0
.

We can now state our rigidity result for blow-downs:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a non-degenerate weak solution to the one phase Bernoulli problem (see Definition
3.1
), and assume there exist
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a sequence
R_{j}\uparrow\infty
such that
u(R_{j}x+x_{0})/R_{j}\rightarrow U
uniformly on compacta as
R_{j}\uparrow\infty
. Further assume that
U
is a minimizing homogeneous solution of the one-phase Bernoulli problem such that
\partial\{U>0\}\backslash\{0\}
is smooth and
U
is strongly integrable (see Definition
2.5
).

Then
u
coincides with (some translation of)
U
,
\overline{U}_{t}
, or
\underline{U}_{t}
for some
t>0
(here
\overline{U}_{t}
and
\underline{U}_{t}
are the leaves of the foliation guaranteed to exist by Theorem
1.2
).

In particular, the above result holds when
U
is a minimizing axially-symmetric cone, which are referred to as “De Silva-Jerison” cones in the literature and are minimizing in dimensions
d\geq 7
.

We recall that in
[
DSJS22
, Theorem 1.1-(vi)]
, the authors showed that global minimizer of
J

lying on one side of

U

must coincide with a leaf of the foliation or
U
itself. Theorem
1.3
improves that result, in the case of strongly integrable cones, by removing the a priori assumptions of minimality and one-sidedness.
It is natural to ask if there are any one-homogeneous solutions which are (or are not) strongly integrable. Unfortunately, very few minimizing cones for the one-phase problem have been identified. In each
d\geq 7
, the De Silva-Jerison cone is the only known example of non-flat minimizing one-homogeneous solutions to (
1
), see
[
DSJ09
]
. We remind the reader that it is an open question as to whether there are any minimizing one-homogeneous solutions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{5,6\}
and that all one-homogeneous minimizers are smooth when
d\leq 4
,
[
CJK04
,
JS15
]
. We show in Section
2.2
that these De Silva-Jerison cones are strongly integrable.

Asymptotic rigidity or Liouville theorems play an important role in minimal surface theory and other non-linear geometric PDE (see the introduction of
[
EdSz24
]
for a nice discussion). Unfortunately, there are very few such theorems available in the context of the Bernoulli problem. Aside from our Theorem
1.3
, and the aforementioned works
[
DSJS22
,
EdSV24a
]
(see also
[
EdSV24b
]
), we are only aware of relevant results on planar domains, see
[
KLT
,
Tra
,
JK
]
. We also mention recent developments on the Bernstein problem in the free boundary setting, see
[
CF-RFS
,
EF-RY
]
.

Theorem
1.3
is inspired by a corresponding result due to Simon-Solomon
[
SS86
]
, which characterizes complete minimal surfaces asymptotic to quadratic cones which are minimizing. The assumption of quadratic cones is because the spectrum of the linearized minimal surface equation around these cones are fully classified and in particular, the bottom spectrum is generated by geometric rigidities. For free boundary problems, we summarize the sufficient conditions of the linear spectrum in the notion of strong integrability in Definition
2.5
.
We mention that the more recent work of Edelen-Spolaor
[
EdSp23
]
generalizes
[
SS86
]
, providing a local version of sorts for a convergent sequence of minimal surfaces (not necessarily a blow-up sequence), and that we hope to prove an analogous result for free boundary problems in the future. As with Theorem
1.1
, at a high level our proof of Theorem
1.3
follows the strategy laid out by
[
SS86
]
but the execution of this strategy must be different in the free boundary setting, which we highlight in Section
1.1
.

As before, it is natural to ask whether the assumption of strong integrability is necessary in Theorem
1.3
.
In the context of minimal surfaces, complete minimal surfaces asymptotic to minimizing non-quadratic cones have been constructed in
[
Wh89
,
Ch97
]
, see also the discussion in
[
SS86
, Section 4]
. In particular,
[
Ch97
]
showed that complete minimal surfaces asymptotic to a given minimizing cone should be at least as rich as the Jacobi fields corresponding to the bottom spectrum of the linearized operator.
Thus it is natural to expect a rich family of global solutions to the free boundary problem, when the regular cone in question is not assumed to be strongly integrable.
However, as before, this discussion is stymied by the lack of examples of minimizing cones.

Furthermore, using the tools developed here, we expect to be able to analyze the linear spectrum of any cone in the large class of symmetric one-homogeneous solutions constructed in
[
Ho15
]
. We plan on doing this in upcoming work.

1.1.
Sketch of the proofs and Comparison with minimal surfaces

Let us sketch the proof of Theorem
1.1
; our main tool is the monotone quantity of Weiss’ boundary adjusted energy
[
We98
]
. In the key Lemma
4.1
, we show that on an annulus the change of this monotone quantity controls the square of the
L^{2}
-distance between
u
and a rotation of the integrable homogeneous solution,
U
. We then use the summability of this monotone quantity to “stitch together” these annuli and prove that our solution
u
stays (quantitatively) close to a fixed rotation of
U
as we zoom in at the blow-up point (this establishes uniqueness of the blowup). This is the content of Lemma
4.4
. On the other hand, we also show that the difference of Weiss’ energies for
u
and
U
at the same scale
r
is controlled by the
C^{2}
difference between them on an annulus containing
\partial B_{r}
,
see Lemma
3.9
. Putting all this together allows us to show power rate of decay in the monotone quantity, thus proving Theorem
1.1
.

Experts in minimal surface theory will find many of the ingredients in this high level outline familiar. However, we would like to emphasize that justifying the linearization of (
1
), which is key to Lemma
4.1
, is non-trivial. That is to say, we want to show that if
u
converges to
U
(in
L^{2}
) then
u-U
(suitably renormalized) converges to a solution of the homogeneous Robin boundary value problem, (

L

). In the setting of minimal surfaces, justifying the analogous statement (once
\epsilon
-regularity is established) follows by writing one minimal surface as a graph over the other and applying regularity theory for elliptic PDEs.

This process is more complicated for the Bernoulli problem in two ways. Firstly, the nonlinear problem (
1
) has two equations, a bulk equation on the positivity set and a boundary condition on the free boundary (this issue has been known to cause serious problems in the stability analysis, see for example
[
JS15
]
); and secondly,
u
and
U
solve these equations on different domains. Naive attempt to mapping
\{u>0\}
onto
\{U>0\}
will introduce errors that ruin the perturbative analysis (see
[
FSV24
]
where linearizing the one-phase problem at branch points runs into a philosophically similar issue with a very different solution).

As far as we are aware, the only other place where this linearization with appropriate error terms is justified is in
[
DSJS22
]
. In that paper, which has inspired us greatly, the situation is simplified by the fact that the perturbation is signed (so
u
always lies to one side of
U
).
See also the very recent, interesting preprint,
[
F-RY25
]
, which extends this one-sided analysis. We also mention
[
EnSV20
]
where a related linearization is done. However, the energy-theoretic approach in
[
EnSV20
]
allows them to only have to linearize the “one-homogeneous” part of the energy (i.e. the functional
\mathcal{F}
defined in the proof of Lemma
3.9
). This reduction mostly circumvents the two difficulties described above. This difference in approach, i.e. linearizing the entire equation versus the one-homogeneous part of the energy, is also the reason for the slight discrepancy between the notions of integrability (our Definition
2.3
and
[
EnSV20
, Definition 2.4]
). See Remark
2.4
for more discussion.

Thus the main technical contribution of our paper is to rigorously justify the linearization around a general one-homogeneous
U
with smooth free boundary away from the origin (when we do not assumed one-sidedness). This is done in Section
3
. Without burdening the reader with too many details, there are two main ideas: (i) we use Cauchy-Kovalevskaya to extend
u,U
analytically so that they are both defined and harmonic on the same domain (see Lemma
3.3
); (ii) we derive a Moser-type estimate by exploiting that
u-U
satisfies the homogeneous equation up to an error which is quadratic, i.e. (
41
), which allows us to control the smooth norms of
u-U
by the
L^{2}
-norm of
u-U
-this is Lemma
3.6
. We believe this general approach will be useful in other situations where one wants analyze solutions of (
1
) near a homogeneous solution with an isolated singularity.

Let us turn to the proof of Theorem
1.3
. From the above discussion, we know that
u-U
(suitably extended) is a harmonic function satisfying inhomogeneous Robin boundary values - see (
41
). Using the fact that the inhomogeneity is quadratic in
u-U
, we construct particular solutions,
v_{p}
, to (
41
) (see Lemma
2.6
) and use linear analysis to conclude that the order of vanishing of
u-U
at infinity is dictated by the behavior of solutions to the homogeneous equation (

L

). We then use the characterization of the eigenvectors at the bottom of the spectrum (which is the essence of the definition of strong integrability) in addition to a maximal principle argument, to get the desired result. This is done in Section
5
.

This tracks very closely the approach of
[
SS86
]
, but, as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, the Bernoulli problem poses additional difficulties.

Of course there are some arguments, such as detecting possible translations, which are actually simpler for the Bernoulli problem.
The major new difficulties in this setting are firstly, characterizing the bottom of the spectrum of the linearization around the De Silva-Jerison cone; and secondly, constructing a particular solution for general strictly stable regular cones. The first issue, in the case of quadratic cones for minimal surfaces, comes from the fact that the links of these cones are the product of spheres, and thus all the eigenfunctions are known explicitly.
Our spectral analysis is more involved and takes place in Section
2.2
. In particular, since our cones are axially symmetric (in other words
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
-symmetric), we may use the separation of variables to split the spectral problem into two problems, one on
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
with no boundary condition, and one ODE with Robin boundary condition in the orthogonal direction. While our analysis in this paper is specifically for the De Silva-Jerison cone, we believe, as mentioned above, that the tools and techniques should allow us to compute the bottom of the spectrum of a large number of symmetric one-homogeneous solutions to the Bernoulli problem.

In regards to the particular solution, in
[
SS86
]
they construct the desired particular solution by first expanding the non-linearity, slice by slice, in Jacobi fields and then, using the expansion of the Laplacian in spherical coordinates, finding the correct radially varying coefficients via integration. In our setting, (
41
) has the inhomogeneous boundary condition, thus precluding us from using the same trick. Instead, we introduce an additional spectral basis (the boundary Robin basis, see (
14
)) to transfer the non-linearity from the boundary to the bulk. Once the non-linearity appears in the bulk, we can treat it similarly to
[
SS86
]
.

Organization of the paper.

In Section
2

and

2.
Some properties of the linearized operator

We begin this section by specifying the notions of classical solutions and regular cones.

Definition 2.1
.

Given an open set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we say that
w:D\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a classical solution to the one-phase free boundary problem in
D
, if
\partial\{w>0\}\cap D
is a
C^{2}
hypersurface,
w\in C^{1}\big(\overline{\{w>0\}}\cap D\big)
, and
w
solves (
1
) in
D
.

Let us explicitly mention that our definition of classical solution rules out “two-phase” solutions like
|x_{n}|
, which satisfies (
1
) in some senses but is not a classical solution because it is not
C^{1}
in the
closure
of its positivity set.

Definition 2.2
.

If
U:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a
1
-homogeneous function, we say that
U
is regular cone if it is a classical solution to the one-phase free boundary problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{B_{r}(0)}
for any
r>0
.

At the risk of redundancy, we note that under Definition
2.2
,
U(x)=|x\cdot e|
is not a regular cone for any
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

Let
U
be a regular cone in the sense of Definition
2.2
. This implies that the positivity set
\Omega=\{U>0\}
is a non-trivial cone with smooth boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
\nu
denote the unit inner normal vector to
\Omega
, and let
H
denote the mean curvature of
\partial\Omega
towards the complement of
\Omega
. Recall that linearizing the Euler-Lagrange equations (
1
) around
U
gives

(
L
)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Delta u=0&\text{ in }\Omega\\
\partial_{\nu}u+Hu=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

We also recall that
H\geq 0
with
H>0
iff
\Omega
is not a half-space, see
[
CJK04
,
JS15
]
.

Since
\Omega
is a cone, we can separate variables and look for solutions to (

L

) of the form

u(x)=|x|^{\gamma}\phi\left(\frac{x}{|x|}\right),

where the restriction of
u
to the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
yields a solution to the (interior) Robin spectral problem in
\Sigma:=\Omega\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

(
L_{s}
)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\phi=\lambda\phi&\text{ in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}\phi+H\phi=0&\text{ on }\partial\Sigma\end{array}\right.

with an associated homogeneity given by

(4)

\gamma_{\pm}:=-\frac{d-2}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{d-2}{2}\right)^{2}+\lambda}.

Solutions to (

L
s

) are eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
to the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with Robin boundary condition, with discrete spectrum given by the eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\to+\infty.

Moreover, the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
has the variational characterization

(5)

\lambda_{1}=\inf\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla_{\theta}w|^{2}-\int_{\partial\Sigma}Hw^{2}}{\int_{\Sigma}w^{2}}.

Notice that testing (
5

(6)

\lambda_{1}\leq-\frac{\int_{\partial\Sigma}H}{\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\Sigma)}<0.

For
j\geq 2
,
\lambda_{j}
is associated to a pair of homogeneous solutions to (

L

) which can be written as
f_{j}(r)\phi_{j}(\omega)
and

with
f_{j}(r)=r^{-\frac{(d-2)}{2}+\delta_{j}}
, where
\delta_{j}:=\sqrt{\left(\frac{d-2}{2}\right)^{2}+\lambda_{j}}
and
\delta_{-j}:=-\delta_{j}
. For the case

, there is a slight subtlety. Let us first recall that the stability of the solution

, as a critical point of (

(7)

\lambda_{1}\geq-\left(\frac{d-2}{2}\right)^{2},

see for example
[

. If the inequality in (

\delta_{1}=0

f_{1}(r)=r^{-\frac{(d-2)}{2}}\ln(r)
and
f_{-1}(r)=r^{-\frac{(d-2)}{2}}
, see
[
DSJS22
]
.

Geometrically, the associated eigenfunction
\phi_{1}
is the infinitesimal generator of shrinking/enlarging the aperture of the cone
\Omega
, and induces the foliation constructed in
[
DSJS22
]
. More precisely, if

is a strictly stable global minimizer,

] guarantees the existence of universal constants

such that

(8)

\displaystyle\overline{U}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U(x)+\bar{a}r^{-\frac{(d-2)}{2}\pm\delta_{1}}\phi_{1}(\omega)+O\!\bigl(|x|^{-\frac{(d-2)}{2}\pm\delta_{1}-\alpha^{\prime}}\bigr),\qquad

(9)

\displaystyle\underline{U}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U(x)-\bar{b}r^{-\frac{(d-2)}{2}\pm\delta_{1}}\phi_{1}(\omega)+O\!\bigl(|x|^{-\frac{(d-2)}{2}\pm\delta_{1}-\alpha^{\prime}}\bigr),

for
x\in\{U>0\}\setminus B_{R}
.

Additionally, infinitesimal generators of translations and rotations are always solutions of (

L
s

). In the case of translations, the generators are given by the directional derivatives

, which are solutions to (

s

) associated with the eigenvalue

0

\partial_{e_{i}}U
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whereas the boundary condition comes from noticing that

(10)

D^{2}U[\nabla U]=-H\nabla U

on

2.2.

3.
Qualitative properties of solutions close to regular cones

We begin this section by defining our class of well-behaved weak solutions (following
[
AC81
, Definition 5.1]
and
[
We98
, Theorem 5.1]
).

Definition 3.1
.

u
is a non-degenerate weak solution of the one-phase Bernoulli problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if:

•

u\in H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

•

u\geq 0
, and
\{u>0\}
is a set of locally finite perimeter.

•

\Delta u=\mathcal{H}^{d-1}|_{\partial\{u>0\}}
in the sense of distributions i.e.

-\int\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta=\int_{\partial_{*}\{u>0\}}\eta\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1},\,\forall\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

•

u
is non-degenerate and regular, i.e. there exists constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
uch that for any
x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}
and
r>0
we have

(34)

c_{1}\leq\frac{1}{r}\fint_{\partial B_{r}(x_{0})}u\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\leq c_{2}.

•

Abundance of the zero set. There exists a constant
c_{3}\in(0,1)
such that for any
x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}
and
r>0
we have

\frac{|\{u=0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|}{|B_{r}(x_{0})|}\geq c_{3}.

Remark 3.2
.

We believe it is possible to drop the assumption on the abundance of the zero set. This condition is only used to ensure the validity of the monotonicity formula; however, in every instance where the formula is applied, the weak solution under consideration is already known to be sufficiently close to a classical solution whose zero set is abundant. We do not pursue this direction—carefully revisiting each step of the proof to remove the assumption— to favor clarity over generality.

More generally, it seems likely that our results apply to any class of weak solutions which satisfy a monotonicity formula, a flat implies smooth property and such that the convergence in
L^{2}
implies the convergence of the free boundaries in the Hausdorff distance sense.

We note that all minimizers satisfy the conditions on Definition
3.1
, see
[
CS05
, Section 1]
, but so do many other non-minimizing solutions. Additionally, thanks to
[
We98
, Theorem 5.1]
, we have that the boundary adjusted energy

(35)

W(u,r)=\frac{1}{r^{d}}\int_{B_{r}}|\nabla u|^{2}+\chi_{\{u>0\}}-\frac{1}{r^{d+1}}\int_{\partial B_{r}}u^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{d-1},

satisfies

(36)

\frac{d}{dr}W(u,r)=\frac{2}{r^{d+2}}\int_{\partial B_{r}}(x\cdot\nabla u-u)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}.

In preparation for our quantitative analysis of solutions asymptotic to regular homogeneous solutions, we show that under suitable proximity conditions between a non-degenerate weak solution

(48)

\tilde{w}_{1}(x+\eta(x)\nabla w_{2}(x))=0=w_{2}(x)

and

(49)

|\nabla\tilde{w_{1}}|^{2}(x+\eta(x)\nabla w_{2}(x))=1=|\nabla w_{2}|^{2}(x).

By (
48
) and a Taylor expansion of
\tilde{w}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle v(x)=\tilde{w}_{1}(x)-w_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{w}_{1}(x)-w_{1}(x+\eta(x)\nabla w_{2}(x))

(50)

\displaystyle=\eta(x)\nabla\tilde{w}_{1}(x)\cdot\nabla w_{2}(x)+h_{0}(\eta(x)),

where
h_{0}(t)=O(t^{2})
with a uniform constant coming from the estimates of the
C^{2}
-norms in item (4).
Note that

\nabla\tilde{w}_{1}(x)\cdot\nabla w_{2}(x)=1+(\nabla\tilde{w}_{1}-\nabla w_{2})(x)\cdot\nabla w_{2}(x),

and thus item (5) implies

1-C_{1}\varepsilon\leq\nabla\tilde{w}_{1}(x)\cdot\nabla w_{2}(x)\leq 1+C_{1}\varepsilon.

In particular, (
50

v(x)\approx\eta(x)
. Therefore we may rewrite (

). In particular, this allows us to estimate the

-norms of

, once we control the
C^{2,\alpha}
-norms of

Arguing analogously for (

\displaystyle|\nabla\tilde{w}_{1}|^{2}(x)-|\nabla w_{2}|^{2}(x)

\displaystyle=|\nabla\tilde{w}_{1}|^{2}(x)-|\nabla w_{1}|^{2}(x+\eta(x)\nabla w_{2}(x))

\displaystyle=|\nabla\tilde{w}_{1}|^{2}(x)-|\nabla\tilde{w}_{1}(x)+\eta(x)D^{2}\tilde{w}_{1}(x)(\nabla w_{2}(x))+h_{1}(\eta(x))|^{2}

(51)

\displaystyle=2\eta(x)D^{2}\tilde{w}_{1}(x)[\nabla\tilde{w}_{1}(x),\nabla w_{2}(x)]+h_{2}(\eta(x)).

The left hand side above is equal to
\nabla v(x)\cdot(2\nabla w_{2}(x)+\nabla v(x))
. Combined we obtain

\partial_{\nu}v(x)-\dfrac{D^{2}\tilde{w}_{1}(x)[\nabla\tilde{w}_{1}(x),\nabla w_{2}(x)]}{\nabla\tilde{w}_{1}(x)\cdot\nabla w_{2}(x)}v(x)=-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla v|^{2}(x)+O(v(x)^{2}),

which is the non-homogeneous Robin boundary condition in (
41
).
∎

Proof.

In virtue of Lemma

By our smallness assumption

. Let
p\geq 1

\varphi\in C_{c}^{1}(A_{4})

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p|\nabla v_{+}|^{2}v_{+}^{p-1}\varphi^{2}

\displaystyle-2\int_{\Omega}v_{+}^{p}\varphi\nabla v_{+}\cdot\nabla\varphi

We remark that in the last term involving

Our final lemma is a local estimate of the energy. It says that the difference of the energy
W(U,1)-W(u,1)
is controlled by the

-norm of

near

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
u,U
be as above and assume that
\|u-U\|_{L^{2}(A_{8})}\leq\delta_{0}
for
\delta_{0}>0
sufficiently small depending on
U
. We have the estimate

(71)

\displaystyle\left|W(U,1)-W(u,1)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\eta\|_{C^{2}(A_{2})}^{2}+\|u-\tilde{U}\|^{2}_{C^{2}(A_{2}\cap\overline{\{u>0\}})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\|u-\tilde{U}\|_{L^{2}(A_{4}\cap\overline{\{u>0\}})}^{2},

where
\tilde{U}
and
\eta
are as in Lemma
3.8
and
C>0
depends only on
U
and the dimension.

Proof.

Let
\bar{u}=|x|u(x/|x|)
, namely the one-homogeneous extension of
u|_{\partial B_{1}}
. By the equipartition of energy in the radial and spherical directions (see, e.g.
[
Vel23
, Lemma 9.8]
) we have that

W(U,1)-W(u,1)=W(U,1)-W(\bar{u},1)+\frac{1}{d}\int_{\partial B_{1}}(x\cdot\nabla u-u)^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}.

To estimate the quantity on the left, we need only to estimate each quantity on the right. Since
\tilde{U}
is 1-homogeneous we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{1}}(x\cdot\nabla u-u)^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\{u>0\}\cap\partial B_{1}}\left(x\cdot\nabla(u-\tilde{U})-(u-\tilde{U}))\right)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\|u-\tilde{U}\|^{2}_{C^{1}(\overline{\{u>0\}}\cap A_{2})}.

We focus now our attention on the term
W(U,1)-W(\bar{u},1)
. Since both functions are one-homogeneous, estimating this term reduces to analyzing the behavior of their restrictions on the sphere. For ease of notation we write
\Sigma_{w}=\{w>0\}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
for any continuous function

and we denote by
\lambda^{D}_{i}(\Sigma_{w})
the
i
-th Dirichlet eigenvalue of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\Sigma_{w}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Furthermore, we define the operator

\mathcal{F}(w):=\kappa_{0}^{2}(\lambda^{D}_{1}(\Sigma_{w})-(d-1))+\frac{1}{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\Sigma_{w}),

where
\kappa_{0}=\|U\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}
.
Recall that by the one-homogeneity and harmonicity of
U
we have that

W(U,1)=\mathcal{F}(U)=\frac{1}{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\Sigma_{U}).

On the other hand we can expand

as

u|_{\partial B_{1}}(\theta)=\sum c_{i}\phi^{D}_{i}(\theta),

where
\phi^{D}_{i}
is an

th Dirichlet eigenfunction of
\Sigma_{u}
with unit

-norm, i.e.
\int_{\Sigma_{u}}|\phi_{i}^{D}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}=1
.
Then we can compute that

\displaystyle W(\bar{u},1)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}c_{i}^{2}(\lambda^{D}_{i}(\Sigma_{u})-(d-1))+\frac{1}{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\Sigma_{u})

\displaystyle=

Lemma 6.1
