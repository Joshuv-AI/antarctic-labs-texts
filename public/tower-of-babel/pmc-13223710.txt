Influence of microwave treatment on the structural and functional properties of soy protein isolate-fenugreek gum graft conjugate
Authors: Yang Song, Tianyu Zhang, Xinyu Wang, Jincheng Sha, Cuntang Wang, Chunli Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of microwave-assisted glycation of soy protein isolate (SPI) with fenugreek gum (FG). After 3 min of microwave treatment (850 W), the SPI-FG conjugates exhibited significantly improved functionality. Specifically, the solubility in the isoelectric range (pH 4.5–5.5) increased from 35.17% to 68.56%. The emulsifying activity and emulsifying stability increased by 2.87-fold and 7.62-fold, respectively. The modified SPI also demonstrated enhanced thermal stability and antioxidant activity, with DPPH radical scavenging capacity increased by 7.28-fold. Structural analysis revealed that the optimal microwave treatment promoted protein unfolding and covalent bonding with FG, as evidenced by an increase in β-sheet content from 25.1% to 28.9% and a 180.86% rise in free sulfhydryl groups. However, excessive treatment (5 min) led to protein aggregation and decreased functional properties. This work provides a theoretical basis for efficient plant protein modification using microwave-assisted glycation.Keywords: Soy protein isolate, Fenugreek gum, Glycosylation, Microwave processing

Introduction

Soy protein isolate (SPI) is a high-quality plant protein with a protein content exceeding 90%. It is particularly rich in amino acids: glutamine and arginine, which constitute approximately 18% and 8% of its composition, respectively. Although SPI is limited in methionine (∼1.2%), dietary combination with sulfur-amino-acid-rich foods like grains ensures a balanced essential amino acid profile. SPI is a high-quality plant protein with a protein content exceeding 90%. It is widely used in meat and dairy alternatives as well as nutritional supplements due to its balanced amino acid profile and functional properties(Ai et al., 2024). However, the industrial application of SPI is limited by its inherent conformational defects. Its solubility drops below 40% in the isoelectric range (pH 4.5–5.5), and intermolecular hydrophobic interactions can cause gel network collapse and emulsion instability. Consequently, enhancing SPI's functional properties through physical or chemical modification is a major research focus in food science.

Among modification strategies, glycation via the Maillard reaction covalently grafts reducing sugars to protein amino groups, effectively modulating SPI's interfacial activity and spatial conformation (Zhang et al., 2022). Controlled glycation can significantly improve SPI's solubility (by 50–80%), emulsifying activity index (by 2–3 fold), and thermal stability (denaturation temperature increase of 10–15 °C). These improvements are attributed to the introduction of hydrophilic carbohydrate chains, increasing molecular surface charge density, thereby inhibiting protein aggregation via electrostatic repulsion (J. J. Chen et al., 2024).

Fenugreek gum (FG), a water-soluble galactomannan polysaccharide from Trigonella foenum-graecum L. seeds, consists of a β-(1,4)-D-mannose backbone with α-(1,6)-D-galactose side chains (mannose-to-galactose ratio ∼ 1:1) and a molecular weight of 50–300 kDa(Jiang, Zhu, Zhang, & Sun, 2007). In glycation, FG's galactose side chains can anchor into SPI's hydrophobic regions, forming amphiphilic sugar-protein complexes that reduce oil-water interfacial tension. Furthermore, FG's branched structure may influence glycation site accessibility through steric hindrance.

Conventional glycation using dry-heat methods requires 24–72 h at 60–80 °C, leading to high energy consumption and uncontrolled browning. Microwave-assisted technology offers an efficient alternative. Its dielectric polarization effect selectively excites high-frequency vibrations (2.45 GHz) in water molecules and polar groups like sugar hydroxyls, enabling targeted energy transfer at the molecular level. This mechanism shortens reaction time and increases grafting efficiency, making microwave assistance a prominent technique for protein functional enhancement (Jiao, Chen, & Fan, 2022). Microwave pretreatment is hypothesized to exert a beneficial effect on the SPI and FG glycosylation process, thereby promoting changes in the functional properties of the SPI-FG graft conjugate.

This study investigates the effects of microwave-assisted glycation of SPI with FG on the structural and functional properties of the resulting conjugates. The conjugates were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), fluorescence spectroscopy, and circular dichroism (CD) to elucidate the structural modifications. Furthermore, the effects of microwave treatment on solubility, emulsification, browning, thermal stability, and antioxidant activity were determined. This work aims to provide a theoretical basis for developing efficient, physically assisted glycation methods to produce plant proteins with tailored functionalities for food applications.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Food-grade soy protein isolate (SPI, >90% protein) was purchased from Henan Wanbang Industrial Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). Fenugreek gum (FG) was obtained from Zhongkai Food Ingredients Co., Ltd. (Hebei, China). All other chemicals and solvents were analytical grade and used as received. All aqueous solutions were prepared using double-distilled water.

Preparation of microwave-assisted SPI-FG glycosylation graft products

SPI and FG were mixed at a mass ratio of 1:3 and dissolved in water to achieve a final concentration of 60 g/L, with the initial pH of the mixed solution being 6.8 ± 0.2 (at 25 °C). The solution was then subjected to microwave heating at 850 W for 0, 1, 3, or 5 min using an MK-2485MG Haier microwave oven. To prevent rapid temperature increase, an intermittent heating protocol was applied: following each minute of microwave irradiation, the sample was immediately cooled in an ice bath for 1 min. The samples were subsequently freeze-dried. After freeze-drying, the samples were placed in a desiccator maintained at 75% relative humidity with a saturated NaCl solution for humidity control, and then incubated at 60 °C for 45 min to facilitate the glycation reaction. The preparation process of the glycosylated product of SPI-FG with microwave pretreatment is shown in Fig. 1.

SEM

The SEM measurement method was conducted following the method described by Xue et al. (2025) with minor modifications. Freeze-dried samples were mounted on specimen stubs using conductive adhesive. The samples were then sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold. Subsequent analysis was performed using a Hitachi S-4800 II scanning electron microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and a magnification of 100 × .

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The sample solution was prepared by dissolving the test material in 0.2 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 8.2) to achieve a protein concentration of 5 mg/mL. After centrifuging the solution at 8000 r/min for 10 min, 50 μL of the supernatant was collected and transferred to a quartz cuvette. The fluorescence emission spectrum was then measured from 300 to 450 nm using a PerkinElmer LS55 fluorescence spectrophotometer with the excitation wavelength and slit width set at 280 nm and 5 nm, respectively(Sosa, Chaves, Ossa, Quiroga, & Avanza, 2021).

CD

Secondary structure content was estimated using a Jasco Corporation J-1500 CD spectropolarimeter. Measurements were performed from 190 to 240 nm using a 0.1 cm pathlength cuvette containing 0.25 mg/mL of the sample in 10 mM ultrapure water. The instrument parameters were set with a scan rate of 100 nm/min, a response time of 0.25 s, and a bandwidth of 1.0 nm. A constant nitrogen purge was maintained throughout all scans (Johnston, Nemr, & Hefford, 2010). The secondary structure content was calculated using the CDNN software (version 2.1) by fitting the far-ultraviolet CD spectral data.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR)

A 10 mg sample was weighed and ground with an appropriate amount of KBr to form a homogeneous powder. The mixture was then compressed into a thin pellet and subsequently analyzed using a Perkin-Elmer Spectrum One B Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer, and the spectrum was measured referencing the method of Vignesh, Selvakumar, and Jayavel (2025) with slight modifications.

Ultraviolet-visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy

The sample was dissolved in 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.0) at a concentration of 10 g/L, and its absorbance was measured across a defined wavelength range using a Yuanxi UV-5100 spectrophotometer (Lan et al., 2024).

Results and analysis

Analysis of structural characteristics

SEM analysis

The microstructure of native SPI and SPI-FG conjugates, prepared with different microwave treatment durations, was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Fig. 2a, native SPI displayed irregular aggregates of spherical particles with varying sizes. In contrast, the glycated conjugates exhibited lamellar structures, suggesting that covalent binding with FG disrupted the native SPI configuration and promoted molecular unfolding(Guo et al., 2026).

The image of SEM (a,b,c,d, and e represent the original SPI sample and the SPI-FG samples treated with microwave-assisted glycosylation for 0,1,3, and 5 min, respectively).

Fig. 2 (b - e) presents SEM images of SPI-FG conjugates after 0, 1, 3, and 5 min of microwave treatment. As microwave duration increased from 0 to 3 min, the conjugates developed progressively smoother and more compact surfaces. This structural refinement indicates enhanced glycation between SPI and FG under microwave irradiation. However, extending treatment to 5 min resulted in rough, porous, and disordered surfaces, likely due to excessive heating disrupting secondary structures. These findings demonstrate that 3 min of microwave treatment yields optimal glycation between SPI and FG.

Fluorescence spectroscopy analysis

Endogenous fluorescence spectroscopy probes alterations in protein tertiary structure by monitoring changes in the microenvironment of tryptophan residues. As shown in Fig. 3A, all samples exhibited maximum fluorescence intensity near 354 nm under 280 nm excitation. The SPI-FG conjugates treated with microwave for 0, 1, and 3 min showed lower fluorescence intensity than native SPI. Quantitative analysis revealed that native SPI had a fluorescence intensity of 431.19. After glycation without microwave pretreatment (0 min), the intensity decreased to 418.27, a 3.0% reduction from native SPI. At 1 min, the intensity further decreased to 391.99, a 9.1% reduction from native SPI, and at 3 min, it was 393.21, a 8.8% reduction. This reduction likely results from glycation, where covalent binding with FG polysaccharides shields or alters the tryptophan microenvironment (Zhang et al., 2025).

Secondary structure changes in the microwave-assisted glycated SPI-FG were further analyzed using circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy (Table 1). Compared to native SPI, all microwave-treated conjugates showed significant structural rearrangements (p < 0.05). Compared to native SPI, SPI-FG conjugates exhibited new absorption peaks within 1200–800 cm−1, corresponding to carbohydrate C—H bending and C-C/C-O stretching vibrations. These new peaks indicate successful glycation between SPI and FG galactomannans (Fronza et al., 2024). The 3-min microwave treatment yielded the most pronounced glycation, evidenced by the highest -OH stretching band at 3407.33 cm−1. Optimal microwave exposure enhances covalent bonding through improved hydrogen bonding networks and amino-hydroxy group interactions, maximizing glycation degree (J. X. Zhang et al., 2025). Conversely, extended treatment (5 min) reduced covalent cross-linking, indicating that excessive microwave exposure diminishes glycation efficiency.

UV–vis spectroscopy analysis

UV–Vis spectroscopy provides a simple and effective method for monitoring changes in protein conformation. As shown in Fig. 3C, glycated conjugates exhibited significantly higher absorbance near 280 nm compared to native SPI, indicating substantial structural alterations. When the microwave duration increased from 0 to 3 min, the absorption intensity progressively enhanced. This increase suggests microwave-induced unfolding of SPI peptide chains, which exposes buried tyrosine and tryptophan residues to the hydrophilic environment (Y. Wang et al., 2023). These aromatic heterocycles exhibit characteristic absorption around 278 nm. Such conformational changes facilitate carbonyl-amine condensation between SPI and FG, leading to the accumulation of early Maillard reaction products like hydroxymethylfurfural (Liu et al., 2020a). However, extending microwave treatment to 5 min decreased absorption intensity, likely due to excessive macromolecular cross-linking or melanoidin aggregation that reduces effective molecular collisions and reaction rates. Therefore, 3-min microwave treatment optimally enhances both the efficiency and product stability of SPI-FG glycation.

Total thiol and free thiol content

Sulfhydryl groups (-SH) serve as crucial functional groups in plant proteins, where their concentration reflects protein denaturation degree and significantly influences functional properties (Boutin, Clément, & Rivoal, 2024). Fig. 4 A shows the effects of different microwave durations on SPI-FG conjugates. Glycated conjugates exhibited higher total and free sulfhydryl contents than native SPI, with both parameters initially increasing then decreasing with extended microwave treatment. Native SPI contained 11.62 μmol/g total and 1.62 μmol/g free sulfhydryl groups. After a 3-min microwave treatment, SPI-FG reached maximum values of 17.50 μmol/g (total) and 4.55 μmol/g (free), representing increases of 50.60% and 180.86%, respectively. This initial increase suggests microwave exposure unfolds SPI structure, exposing buried sulfhydryl groups, while possible disulfide bond cleavage further elevates free sulfhydryl content. However, extending treatment to 5 min reduced total and free sulfhydryl contents to 12.64 μmol/g and 2.35 μmol/g, decreases of 27.77% and 48.35% from 3-min values, consistent with Wang, Xu, Wang, Wang, and Peng (2022). This reversal likely results from excessive microwave treatment promoting disulfide bond reformation, thereby reducing sulfhydryl availability.

Surface hydrophobicity analysis

Surface hydrophobicity (H₀) reflects the abundance of hydrophobic groups exposed on the protein surface to the aqueous solvent. As shown in Fig. 4 B, glycated SPI-FG conjugates exhibited lower H₀ than native SPI. Native SPI exists as globular proteins with buried hydrophobic residues and surface-exposed hydrophilic groups (Silverman, 2001). The initial H₀ decrease in glycated samples suggests that FG polysaccharide chains shield surface hydrophobic regions. Furthermore, H₀ progressively decreased as microwave treatment extended from 0 to 3 min, with the 3-min treatment showing a reduction of approximately 87% compared to native SPI, indicating enhanced glycation introduces more hydrophilic groups and increases grafting degree. No significant H₀ change occurred between 3-min and 5-min treatments. Therefore, 3-min microwave treatment achieves optimal surface hydrophobicity reduction in SPI-FG conjugates.

Analysis of functional characteristics

Solubility analysis

Fig. 5 A shows that glycated SPI exhibited higher solubility than native SPI, consistent with Nasrollahzadeh et al. (2017). This improvement results from covalent conjugation with hydrophilic polysaccharide chains and weak intermolecular complexes that enhance hydration(He et al., 2023). Solubility increased with microwave duration up to 3 min across all pH values, likely because microwave-mediated dielectric polarization unfolds β-sheet structures and exposes internal hydrophilic groups (-OH, -NH₂), thereby improving hydration capacity. The 3-min treatment achieved maximum solubility, with particularly notable enhancement in SPI's isoelectric region (pH 4.5–5.5), where solubility increased by approximately 2–3 fold compared to native SPI. However, extending treatment to 5 min reduced solubility, as excessive microwave exposure causes protein denaturation, insoluble aggregate formation, and advanced glycation end product cross-linking (Y. Wang et al., 2023). Therefore, 3-min microwave treatment yields optimal SPI-FG conjugate solubility.

Emulsification analysis

Fig. 5 B shows that glycated SPI-FG conjugates exhibited significantly higher emulsifying activity and stability than native SPI. Emulsifying activity increased by approximately 2.4-fold after 0 min treatment and peaked at 3 min with a 2.9-fold increase compared to native SPI. Similarly, emulsion stability showed a progressive enhancement, with the 3-min treatment achieving the highest value, representing a 7.6-fold increase over native SPI. This improvement results from glycosylation with FG, which increases steric hindrance and prevents coalescence of newly formed oil droplets (Tao et al., 2024) The optimal performance at 3 min likely stems from microwave-induced reduction of non-covalent interactions, which loosens SPI structure and exposes more glycosylation sites, thereby enhancing glycation degree (Lu Han et al., 2024). However, extending treatment to 5 min decreased both emulsifying properties, with emulsifying activity and stability declining by approximately 16% and 7%, respectively, compared to the 3-min treatment. This reduction occurs as excessive microwave exposure promotes protein-polysaccharide cross-linking that reduces interfacial activity and facilitates protein reaggregation (Chang, Su, Gu, Li, & Yang, 2021). Therefore, 3-min microwave treatment produces SPI-FG conjugates with optimal emulsifying properties.

Analysis of Browning degree

Glycation typically produces melanoidins that cause browning, making browning intensity an indicator of grafting degree (Starowicz & Zielinski, 2019). As shown in Fig. 6A, SPI-FG conjugates exhibited greater browning than native SPI because covalent bonding between reducing sugar terminals and protein amino groups promotes advanced Maillard reaction stages, forming nitrogenous brown pigments (Liu et al., 2020b). Furthermore, browning intensity increased with microwave duration, with increases of approximately 11%, 24%, 28%, and 39% for 0, 1, 3, and 5 min treatments, respectively, compared to native SPI, confirming that microwave irradiation accelerates glycation toward advanced stages. Longer exposure times produced more melanoidins, resulting in stronger browning.

Determination of glycation degree

The degree of glycation, determined by the reduction in free amino groups using the OPA method, reflected the extent of covalent attachment between SPI and FG (Y. Li et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 6B, all glycated samples exhibited significant reductions in free amino group content compared to native SPI (p < 0.05). For samples without microwave pretreatment (0 min), the degree of glycation was 23.6 ± 1.8%. With increasing microwave treatment time, the degree of glycation progressively increased, reaching 35.2 ± 2.1% at 1 min and peaking at 42.8 ± 2.4% after 3 min of microwave treatment (p < 0.05). This represents an 81.4% increase compared to the 0 min treatment. However, when the microwave treatment was extended to 5 min, the degree of glycation decreased significantly to 31.5 ± 1.9% (p < 0.05), a 26.4% reduction from the 3 min treatment. These results demonstrate that 3 min of microwave treatment optimally promotes glycation between SPI and FG.

Thermal stability

Thermal stability reflects a protein's ability to maintain structural and functional integrity during heating (Z. J. Wang, Li, Jiang, Qi, & Zhou, 2014). Thermal stability under different microwave treatments was evaluated (Fig. 7). Native SPI showed marked stability reduction above 50 °C, as high temperatures disrupted internal hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces, converting ordered structures into disordered states. All microwave-treated SPI-FG conjugates exhibited improved thermal stability compared to native SPI, confirming that glycation enhances heat resistance. The 3-min microwave treatment achieved maximum stability across all temperatures, with particularly notable enhancement at 70–80 °C where stability increased by approximately 50–60% compared to native SPI. This improvement likely occurs because microwave irradiation promotes uniform molecular dispersion and complete glycation with FG. However, extending treatment to 5 min slightly reduced stability across most temperatures, with decreases of 10–15% compared to the 3-min treatment, possibly due to partial protein denaturation or structural misfolding (Das, Panesar, & Saini, 2024).

Antioxidant property

DPPH free radical scavenging ability

The antioxidant activity of SPI relates to its amino acid composition, surface hydrophobicity, and molecular structure (C. Chen, Sun-Waterhouse, Zhang, Zhao, & Sun, 2020). As shown in Fig. 8 A, all microwave-treated SPI-FG conjugates exhibited significantly enhanced DPPH radical scavenging capacity compared to native SPI. At 0.5 mg/mL protein concentration, the DPPH scavenging rates for 0, 1, 3, and 5-min treatments were 2.53, 5.62, 7.28, and 6.88 times higher than native SPI, respectively, consistent with Harimana et al. (2024). This improvement is attributed to glycation products, including Maillard reaction intermediates and melanoidins, which act as hydrogen donors. Furthermore, Amadori compounds and heterocyclic products formed during advanced reaction stages also contribute hydrogen atoms, enhancing reducing capacity (X. H. Huang et al., 2023). Microwave-assisted glycation further improved DPPH scavenging capacity compared to conventional glycation, with the 3-min treatment achieving maximum effect. This optimal performance likely results from increased hydroxyl group availability and reduced intermolecular hydrogen bonding facilitated by microwave irradiation (He et al., 2023). Beyond 3 min, scavenging capacity slightly declined, possibly due to excessive microwave exposure causing irreversible protein denaturation, structural transition from ordered (α-helix/β-sheet) to random coil conformations, and formation of large insoluble aggregates (K. Huang et al., 2022).

Antioxidant capacity against lipid oxidation

Fig. 8 B shows the lipid antioxidant capacity of SPI after microwave-assisted glycosylation. The glycosylated SPI exhibited significantly improved anti-lipid oxidation ability compared to the original SPI. Glycosylation may alter the molecular structure of SPI, leading to a more extended protein conformation. This structural change exposes previously buried antioxidant active groups, such as sulfhydryl and phenolic hydroxyl groups. As a result, the modified SPI demonstrates increased interaction with free radicals or lipid peroxides, thereby enhancing its antioxidant capacity (W. L. Zhang et al., 2023). Microwave treatment further improved the anti-lipid oxidation ability of glycosylated SPI-FG. The microwave process likely accelerated the glycosylation reaction. With increasing microwave treatment time, the glycosylated SPI-FG showed progressively stronger anti-lipid oxidation ability across all protein concentrations, reaching a maximum at 3 min. However, after 5 min of microwave treatment, the anti-lipid oxidation capacity decreased at all protein concentrations. This result aligns with the DPPH radical scavenging assay, indicating that excessive microwave treatment impairs the protein's antioxidant activity. Therefore, the glycosylated SPI-FG treated for 3 min exhibited the strongest anti-lipid oxidation ability.

Analysis of chelating ability

Metal ion chelation capacity serves as a key indicator of antioxidant activity, as metal ions catalyze lipid oxidation. Chelating these ions can enhance the anti-lipid oxidation capacity of SPI (Yu et al., 2022). Fig. 8 C shows the metal chelating activity of SPI-FG conjugates subjected to different microwave treatment times, measured across various protein concentrations. All SPI-FG conjugates demonstrated superior ferrous ion (Fe2+) chelating ability compared to native SPI. This is attributed to the rearrangement of Amadori compounds during the initial stage of the Maillard reaction, which generates reductone compounds. These reductone compounds function as metal chelators, leading to improved reducing power and oxygen radical scavenging ability. At a protein concentration of 0.3 mg/mL, the 3-min microwave-treated SPI-FG conjugate achieved the highest chelation capacity compared to samples treated for 0, 1, and 5 min. At higher concentrations (0.4 and 0.5 mg/mL), all four conjugates reached 100% chelation. Therefore, the conjugate treated with microwaves for 3 min exhibited the optimal metal chelating capacity under the tested conditions.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of microwave treatment time on the structural and functional properties of SPI-FG glycosylation products. Appropriate microwave treatment, particularly for 3 min, promoted the ordered aggregation of SPI-FG glycosylation molecules and increased particle size. This enhanced structural organization concurrently reduced surface hydrophobicity and intrinsic fluorescence intensity. These structural modifications improved functional properties, including emulsification capacity, thermal stability, and antioxidant activity. However, prolonged microwave treatment induced protein depolymerization, which subsequently diminished these functional properties. Therefore, microwave treatment effectively optimizes the functional characteristics of SPI-FG glycosylation products. This optimization plays a critical role in enhancing the quality of glycosylated protein-based foods and expands the application of plant proteins in extreme processing conditions, such as acidic environments.
