Investigating students’ perceptions of the medical education environment and learning autonomy in blended learning in pediatrics: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Fang Liu, Ping Niu, Shiqian Yu, Li Wei, Xiaoqin He, Jingping Ye, Baozhen Yao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the process of education reform, carrying out continuous educational environment measurement is necessary. This study aimed to evaluate the perceptions of the medical education environment and learning autonomy in blended learning in pediatrics among clinical medicine students, as well as the relationship between these two constructs.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on clinical medical students from the First Clinical School of Wuhan University who had completed the blended learning courses of pediatrics. Perceptions of the medical education environment and learning autonomy were surveyed using the Dundee Ready Education Environment Measure (DREEM) and the Autonomy in Learning Rating Scale for college students (ALRS), respectively. Independent samples t-test, one-way ANOVA, correlation, and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted.ResultsA total of 487 students participated in the survey (valid response rate: 83.53%). Students’ perceptions of the current medical education environment and learning autonomy were generally positive. Among the average scoring rates of DREEM, students’ perception of teachers (SPT) > students’ perception of learning (SPL) > students’ perception of atmosphere (SPA) > students’ academic self-perceptions (SASP) > students’ social self-perception (SSSP), while learning strategies > learning motivation > self-regulation > content and environment among the average scoring rates of ALRS. Correlation analyses revealed that all subscales of the two were significantly associated with each other (P < 0.05). Regression analyses showed that SPA and SASP were significant factors for students’ learning autonomy.ConclusionClinical medicine students who experienced blended learning in pediatrics had positive perceptions of the medical education environment, and their learning autonomy was good and closely related to the perceptions of the medical education environment. In the implementation of blended learning, continuous optimization of teaching content and proactive enhancement of the medical education environment will help enhance clinical medicine students’ satisfaction with the educational environment and their learning autonomy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06490-z.

Background

The advent of the “Internet Plus” era has brought great changes in higher education, particularly in the field of medical education. This revolution has not only shifted the goals of talent cultivation but also revolutionized the ways in which knowledge is acquired, disseminated, and generated [1]. In this context, blended learning has emerged as a pivotal educational strategy, integrating various teaching methods, tools, and environments to enhance the learning experience. Blended learning, characterized by the fusion of traditional face-to-face instruction with online digital technologies, has been increasingly adopted in higher education settings worldwide [2]. The integration of these diverse elements in blended learning is designed to facilitate a more flexible, accessible, and student-centered approach to learning, where the teacher acts as a guide and the student takes on the role of an active participant [3].

The complexity and variability of child development across different ages present unique challenges for pediatric medical education. Traditional teaching methodologies have often fallen short in capturing the interest and engagement of medical students, leading to a need for innovative educational approaches. Blended learning, with its potential to offer a more dynamic and interactive learning environment, is seen as a promising solution to address these challenges [1, 4]. The implementation of blended learning in pediatrics aims to enhance students’ understanding of the subject matter, stimulate their interest, and foster a more autonomous learning process [5]. The perception of the education environment is a critical factor influencing students’ learning experiences, approaches to learning, academic achievements, and overall satisfaction with their educational journey [6, 7]. A positive educational environment is conducive to higher levels of student engagement, better learning outcomes, and a more profound sense of well-being among students [8]. In view of the importance of the educational environment, it is necessary to carry out continuous educational environment measurement during the implementation of blended teaching.

Learning autonomy is a key component of student-centered education and is closely linked to the quality of the educational environment [8]. Autonomous learners are more likely to take charge of their learning process, set personal goals, and employ effective learning strategies, leading to improved academic performance and a greater sense of personal fulfillment. The quality of the educational environment plays a crucial role in fostering this autonomy, as it provides the necessary support, resources, and freedom for students to thrive [9]. In the context of medical education, where students are expected to develop a deep understanding of complex subject matter and acquire a wide range of practical skills, autonomy is particularly important. It empowers students to navigate the vast expanse of medical knowledge and to adapt to the ever-changing landscape of clinical practice.

Despite the recognized importance of the educational environment and student learning autonomy, there is a paucity of research exploring these constructs within the specific context of blended learning in pediatrics. In recent years, the First Clinical School of Wuhan University has carried out a pilot reform of pediatrics blended learning among clinical medical students, with various forms of teaching methods, resource platforms, and tools such as massive open online courses, demonstration classrooms, flipped classrooms, medical electronic schoolbags, etc. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between the perceptions of the medical education environment and students’ learning autonomy among clinical medicine students who have undergone blended learning in pediatrics at Wuhan University. To explore this issue, we employed the Dundee Ready Education Environment Measure (DREEM) and the Autonomy in Learning Rating Scale for college students (ALRS) and examined the relationship between these two constructs. The findings of this study are expected to contribute to the broader understanding of the factors that influence student learning in the context of blended learning. Moreover, the study aims to offer practical recommendations for medical educators seeking to enhance the educational environment and promote learning autonomy among clinical medical students.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a cluster sampling, cross-sectional study that enrolled clinical medical students from the First Clinical School of Wuhan University who had completed the blended learning in pediatrics from December 2021 to February 2024. Two rounds were conducted within the study period: Round 1 (December 2021 to February 2023) and Round 2 (December 2023 to February 2024). Clinical medical students were divided into three academic types, including five-year, eight-year, and “5 + 3” integration. Different types of students take basic and clinical courses according to their respective teaching plans. Pediatrics courses are usually arranged in the third or fourth academic year. A survey on students’ perceptions of the medical education environment and learning autonomy using the DREEM and the ALRS was conducted after the pediatrics course was completed. Survey participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The exclusion criteria were (1) clearly rejecting the respondent; (2) an incomplete response with missing data ≥ 20%; and (3) an invalid response (abnormal information, regular answers, the same answer options, logical errors, etc.).

Measures

Perceptions of the medical education environment

DREEM was used to investigate the perceptions of the medical education environment. DREEM, which was developed by the Medical Education Center of the University of Dundee, UK, had been widely used in health professions education across many countries and had been shown to be a reliable instrument [10, 11]. The DREEM-Chinese (version of the DREEM translated into Chinese and standardized in China) was revised by China Medical University [12]. It consists of 50 items, which are divided into 5 subscales: students’ perception of learning (SPL, 12 items), students’ perception of teachers (SPT, 11 items), students’ academic self-perceptions (SASP, 8 items), students’ perception of atmosphere (SPA, 12 items), and students’ social self-perception (SSSP, 7 items) (see supplementary materials for details). Each item scored on a five-point Likert scale with 4 = strongly agree, 3 = agree, 2 = unsure, 1 = disagree, and 0 = strongly disagree. Among the 50 items, nine items are reverse-scored (items 4, 8, 9, 17, 25, 35, 39, 48, and 50) from 0 (strongly agree) to 4 (strongly disagree). The higher the score, the better the educational environment. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.93 [13]. Calculation of average scoring rate (%): the actual score of the subscale is divided by the full score of the subscale, and the actual score of the total scale is divided by the full score of the total scale [14].

Learning autonomy

The learning autonomy of clinical medical students was measured by ALRS, which was developed by Yuan Hongmei of Hunan Normal University in 2005 [15]. The ALRS consists of 61 items, which are divided into four subscales: self-regulation (SR, 22 items), learning strategies (LS, 14 items), content and environment (CE, 14 items), and learning motivation (LM, 11 items) (see supplementary materials for details). Items are scored on a Likert scale from 1 (not like me at all) to 5 (very much like me). The higher the score, the higher the learning autonomy. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.922. The calculation of the average scoring rate was referred to the calculation method of the DREEM.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed with R 4.3.0 and IBM SPSS 22.0 software. The total scale and subscale scores of DREEM and ALRS were calculated by summing item scores. Independent samples t-test and one-way ANOVA were used to compare each component score according to gender (male, female), academic type (five-year, eight-year, “5 + 3” integration), and survey round (Round 1, Round 2). The internal consistency of the DREEM and the ALRS was evaluated using Cronbach’s α coefficients. The correlations between DREEM and ALRS scores were tested with Spearman’s correlation. Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted for regression analyses between students’ learning autonomy and perceptions of the medical education environment. Covariates such as gender, academic type, and survey round were used in the linear regression analyses. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (bilateral).

Results

Demographic characteristics

Of the 583 eligible clinical medical students, 521 responses were collected (recovery rate: 89.37%), and 487 were classified as valid responses (valid response rate: 83.53%) and were therefore included in the final analysis. 270 responses (270/487, 55.44%) were received in Round 1, and 217 responses (217/487, 44.56%) were received in Round 2. The differences in genders (male/female: 110/93 vs. 160/124) and academic types (five-year/eight-year/“5 + 3” integration: 111/87/72 vs. 74/85/58) between Round 1 and Round 2 were not significant. The demographic characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 1.

Perceptions of the medical education environment and learning autonomy

Perceptions of the medical education environment and learning autonomy were surveyed using the DREEM and the ALRS, respectively. The DREEM and ALRS scores of the clinical medical students are shown in Table 2. Among the average scoring rates of DREEM, SPT > SPL > SPA > SASP > SSSP. Among the average scoring rates of ALRS, LS > LM > SR > CE. In terms of genders and academic types, there were no significant differences in the total and subscales of DREEM and ALRS; however, Round 2 had significantly higher total scale and subscale scores than Round 1 in the DREEM and ALRS, excluding SASP, CE, and LM (Tables 3 and Table 4). Cronbach’s α coefficients of the DREEM and ALRS in this study sample were 0.946 and 0.966, respectively (Table 5).

Correlations between the DREEM and ALRS scores

Correlation analyses revealed that each subscale score was significantly positively correlated with the total score of the DREEM, and the subscale score was also significantly positively correlated with each other (Table 5, P < 0.05). The same was true for the ALRS (Table 5). Correlation analyses also showed significant positive correlations of the DREEM with the ALRS, whether between the subscales or between total scales and subscales (Table 5).

Regression analyses

Regression analyses were performed, and the results showed that SPA and SASP were significant factors for students’ learning autonomy (Table 6).

Discussion

The measurement of educational environment and the investigation of students’ learning autonomy are indispensable and important links in the quality assessment of higher education reform. The present study was conducted to evaluate the medical educational environment and investigate the relationship between students’ perceptions of the medical education environment and learning autonomy in blended learning in pediatrics. With a robust response rate of 83.53%, the survey captured a representative sample of students’ views. Cronbach’s α coefficients of the DREEM and ALRS in the present study were 0.946 and 0.966, respectively, indicating that the scales had good reliability. Results showed that clinical medical students had positive perceptions of the medical education environment, and their learning autonomy was good and closely related to the perceptions of the medical education environment. Academic self-perceptions and perception of atmosphere were significant factors for students’ learning autonomy. The findings provide some insight into improving the medical education environment, optimizing teaching paths, and promoting the development of blended learning in pediatrics in clinical medical students.

In this study, the total DREEM mean score was 148.00 ± 23.08, and the average scoring rate of the DREEM was 73.98%, which indicated the overall positive viewpoint of the students toward the medical educational environment. The total and subscale scores and average scoring rates were higher than the survey by Xu et al., which was conducted on public health graduates of Wuhan University [16]. These were also higher than the studies carried out by Gil et al. [17]., Galehdar et al. [18], and Ousey et al. [19]. No statistically significant differences were observed between genders or academic types in DREEM scores. The mean scores of the total scale and subscales were higher in students of Round 2 than those in students of Round 1. In other words, students’ perceptions of the medical education environment were getting more and more positive in the process of continuous improvement of blended learning in pediatrics. The equal distribution of genders and academic types in both rounds also ensures that the study’s results were not skewed by any particular group, enhancing the validity of the conclusions drawn.

Comparisons among DREEM subscales showed that the average scoring rates of SSSP and SASP were relatively lower, which was consistent with previous domestic studies [16, 20]. This finding indicated that activities enhancing students’ social ability, self-confidence, and self-cognition should be encouraged, and favorable conditions should be constructed to promote students’ cooperation ability and full personal development. The internal correlation analysis of the DREEM showed that the vast majority of the subscale scores were positively correlated with the total score. Therefore, continuous optimization of teaching content and improvement of teaching literacy and environment have positive significance for improving students’ perceptions of the education environment [14].

According to the results, the overall learning autonomy of clinical medical students was also positive. This is encouraging, as autonomy is a critical skill for medical students who will need to independently manage complex information and make decisions in their future practices [21]. Learning autonomy was closely related to the autonomous learning resources, flexibility of learning methods, and convenience of learning platforms [22, 23]. The ranking of the average scoring rate of the ALRS subscales highlights the importance of learners’ personal learning quality, including their learning strategies, motivation, and self-regulation, where improvements should be needed. The differences in the ALRS total scale and subscale scores among genders and academic types were both not significant. However, interestingly, Round 2 showed significantly higher total scale and subscale scores than Round 1 for most aspects of the ALRS, with the exception of CE and LM. This increase could be attributed to various factors such as changes in the educational environment, students’ adaptation to clinical settings, or a natural progression in their learning autonomy as they progress through their medical education.

Compared to passive learning motivation without clear goals, active learning with moderate drive can lead to more significant learning outcomes. Previous studies had found that students’ academic emotions were positively correlated with learning autonomy [24, 25]. The internal correlation analysis result indicated that the subscale scores of the ALRS were positively correlated with the total score, and the scores among different subscales also exhibited positive correlations. This suggests that learning autonomy is not an inherent personality trait but is subject to joint regulation by individual and environmental factors, evolving and changing accordingly. This is consistent with the conclusions of Oudbier et al. [26]. The significant positive correlations between the DREEM and ALRS scores suggest that various perceptions of the medical education environment were collectively correlated with students’ learning autonomy. The regression analyses identified SPA and SASP as significant factors influencing students’ learning autonomy. This finding underscores the importance of a positive social environment and self-perceived academic performance in fostering a sense of autonomy among medical students. These factors should be considered by educational policymakers and faculty when designing and implementing medical education programs.

The practical implications of this study are manifold. For educators, the findings suggest that efforts to improve the educational environment, such as enhancing teaching quality and promoting a positive academic atmosphere, can have a positive impact on student autonomy. Policymakers can use these insights to inform the development of educational policies that support blended learning and foster autonomous learning behaviors among medical students. However, the study is not without limitations. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences, and the generalizability of the results in a single-center study may be constrained by the specific characteristics of the sample. Furthermore, this study relied on self-administered questionnaires that entail inherent limitations, including recall bias, non-response bias, and social desirability bias.

Conclusions

This study provided a comprehensive analysis of the perceptions of the medical education environment and learning autonomy among clinical medical students. The result revealed that the students had generally positive perceptions of the current medical education environment in the context of blended learning in pediatrics, and learning autonomy was closely related to the perceptions of the education environment. The significant correlations and regression findings highlight the importance of a positive social environment and self-perceived academic performance in fostering a sense of autonomy among clinical medical students. Future research should explore longitudinal changes in these perceptions and the impact of targeted interventions on enhancing learning autonomy.
