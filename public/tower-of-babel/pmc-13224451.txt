Nano-radiomics of molecular MRI for early amyloid associated patterns in an Alzheimer’s disease mouse model via an automatic pipeline
Authors: Esther Ngan, Prajwal Bhandari, Parag A Parekh, Andrew A Badachhape, Ananth V Annapragada, Ketan B Ghaghada, Zbigniew A Starosolski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe combination of nanoparticle contrast agents with advanced image analysis techniques like radiomics offers a powerful new approach for disease detection and characterization. This study presents a novel automated pipeline for segmentation and nano-radiomic analysis of nanoprobe-enhanced images. We demonstrate the effectiveness of this pipeline in an Alzheimer’s disease (AD) mouse model, showing improved detection of amyloid pathology compared to conventional methods.MethodsThe study used the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data collected from double transgenic (TG) AD mice, including 13 mice aged 6–8 month old with lower amyloid burden and 36 mice aged 11–18 month old with higher amyloid burden. Wild type (WT) mice served as controls. Three contrast doses were administered to older mice, while one dose was applied to younger mice. A UNet-based model was trained on mouse brain scans to automatically segment two specific regions, and the results were compared to those from semi-automatic segmentation (i.e. manual brain atlas registration to MRIs with automatic region extraction). 89 radiomic features (RFs) per region were computed, followed by genotype classification using machine learning and sensitivity analyses. RF-based classification performance was similar between automatic and semi-automatic approaches. The top classifier from automatic approach also required 2x less RFs. In younger mice, classification performance varied with segmentation approach and inclusion of fair-quality segmentation. Yet the top classifiers typically required only one or two RFs.ConclusionsNano-radiomic analysis of targeted nanoparticle contrast-enhanced MRIs outperformed conventional imaging metrics for early detection of pathological amyloid deposition in an AD mouse model. Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by progressive accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau proteins (p-tau) forming neurofibrillary tangles inside neurons and extracellular amyloid-beta plaques, which disrupt neuronal function and contribute to cognitive decline [1]. Transgenic (TG) mouse models that recapitulate key features of human AD pathology are widely used to investigate early-stage disease mechanisms and evaluate potential therapies. Non-invasive imaging techniques such as positron emission tomography scan (PET) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particularly when paired with contrast agents, offer potential for early detection and monitoring of pathological amyloid deposition.

Radiomics has emerged as a powerful technique that transforms images into a high dimensional space [2]. Radiomics extracts quantitative imaging features including intensity, shape, size, morphology, and texture. These radiomic features (RFs) can objectively define phenotypic characteristics of tissue not discernible by visual inspection or conventional imaging metrics. Radiomic analysis has been shown to enable differentiation of images when subjective visual observation fails, and can be applied to both endogenous and exogenous contrast enabled images [3–5]. Radiomics has been widely utilized from disease phenotyping to treatment prognostication [3, 6–11], and extensively on human AD research involving MRI and PET, across brain regions like amygdala, cortex, hippocampus [12–15]. Texture- and intensity-based RFs (especially enhanced with filtering methods) have shown particular promise for classifying disease stages and phenotypes. While radiomics applications in human AD are well-established, preclinical mouse models remain essential for investigating early-stage pathology, validating imaging biomarkers against histological ground truth, and evaluating novel contrast agents under controlled experimental conditions. An area of emerging interest is the application of radiomic analysis to nanoparticle contrast-enhanced imaging (nano-radiomics). These nanoparticle contrast agents (nanoprobes), either non-targeted or molecularly targeted to molecular markers such as amyloid-β, produce unique contrast morphology reflective of underlying pathology and architecture [16, 17]. Thus nano-radiomics offers a promising framework for quantifying subtle pathological changes and improving disease classification accuracy in AD models.

Recent advances in medical imaging have also sparked a surge of interest in automatic segmentation techniques across different applications including dental features [18], abdominal organs [19–21], cardiac anatomic features [22], and tumors [23, 24]. Methods have been adapted to accommodate images acquired using different imaging modalities, including X-ray, CT, T1 and T2-weighted MRI, and PET. In both clinical and preclinical research, deep learning models such as extend MRF, SHERM, and UNet variants [24–28], have shown promise in automating brain region segmentation from MRI scans and thereby reducing manual labor and increasing reproducibility. Such techniques facilitate the precise, objective and reproducible segmentation of target anatomy across modalities, enabling rapid region-specific quantification. Yet these tools often serve structural mapping or volumetric analysis purposes, with limited attention to how segmentation quality affects downstream analyses such as radiomics-based disease classification. In the context of AD, most automatic segmentation approaches are tailored to human data, while methods developed for mice typically do not target AD-specific anatomy and pathology. A prior study [29] has explored the automated detection and quantification of amyloid pathology in mouse models using intrinsic MR signal characteristics. Their approach enabled detection of age-related plaque burden in 5×FAD mice ex vivo and represented an early step toward quantitative amyloid plaque assessment in MR images. However, this method was based on traditional image intensity features, and it did not incorporate contrast enhancement, radiomics nor disease-level classification. Given the anatomical and pathological differences between healthy and AD mouse brains (including atrophy and region-specific vulnerability), reliable segmentation is especially important for extracting consistent RFs. In addition, few studies have explored the integration of automated segmentation with radiomics and machine learning in nanoparticle-enhanced preclinical AD imaging. To address this gap, the present work evaluates how automated segmentation of brain regions can support nano-radiomics-based classification of pathological amyloid status in an AD mouse model.

In this paper, we present an automated pipeline tailored to nanoparticle-enhanced molecular MRI for distinguishing TG mice, which model early-onset AD, from WT controls based on amyloid-associated imaging features. The pipeline consists of two components: (1) to train an automatic multi-label segmentation model to identify key brain regions (e.g., cortex and hippocampus), and (2) to develop nano-radiomics-based machine learning classifiers for genotype classification. Earlier studies have shown that an amyloid targeted gadolinium (Gd) nanoprobe enables visualization of amyloid deposits by T1-weighted MRI. Initially demonstrated in mouse models [30–32], this nanoparticle contrast agent (ADx-001) is now in clinical trials (NCT05453539) for the detection of amyloid plaques that is currently only possible using expensive and difficult to access PET based radiotracers. Prior work also investigated the dose effect on detection and imaging of amyloid plaque in a TG mouse model of AD using manual signal quantification of MRIs [31]. Analysis of MR signal enhancement between pre- and post-contrast scans demonstrated significant reduction in performance when the nanoparticle contrast dose was reduced from 0.2 to 0.1 mmol Gd/kg. This work investigates whether an automated pipeline (combining segmentation, nano-radiomics, and machine learning) can streamline the labor-intensive image processing and improve the pathological amyloid deposition detection, particularly at lower contrast doses and mice with minimal pathological amyloid burden. We assess how automated segmentation affects classification performance and reproducibility, and evaluate whether nano-radiomics derived from post-contrast MRIs could outperform traditional approaches based on percentage signal change between pre- and post-contrast images. Utilizing TG mice exhibiting varying levels of amyloid pathology and WT control mice, we further explore the utility of nano-radiomics in detecting early disease signatures across different contrast agent doses.

Methods

An overview of the study workflow is presented in Fig. 1.

Study pipeline

Data collection

We demonstrated our methods using the APPswe/PSEN1dE9 (C57BL/6 J background) double transgenic AD mouse model of amyloid pathology. In this model, amyloid plaque formation initiates in the brain around 6 months of age. To investigate the effect of amyloid deposition on performance of nano-radiomics, MRI studies were done in 6–8 month old TG mice with relatively low amyloid deposition and 11–18 month old TG mice with higher amyloid deposition. Older mice are expected to have a higher amyloid deposition compared to younger mice who just begin to accumulate amyloid plaques in their brains. Age-matched WT mice served as controls. Mice aged 11 to 18 months are roughly equivalent to humans in their 40s to 50s, whereas mice aged 6–8 months old are comparable to humans in their 20–30 s [33]. Both groupings align with accepted murine-to-human age translation frameworks and with clinical definitions of early-onset Alzheimer’s disease (< 65 years). The age thresholds were selected based on prior studies and our own observations of amyloid progression in this AD mouse model. Previous studies [31, 32, 34] have considered a mild disease state with low amyloid burden (often in the 6–8-month age range) and a cohort of mice older than 11 months of age for a later disease state with moderate to higher amyloid burden. Consistent with these studies, other reports indicate that double-transgenic APPswe/PS1-dE9 mice begin to develop substantial amyloid deposits around 6 months and early deposits are limited before that age. Amyloid plaque deposition in these mice was largely restricted to the cortex and hippocampus up to 12 months, after which deposits became more substantial, particularly in these regions [35, 36]. Animals underwent pre-contrast MRI followed by intravenous administration of amyloid-targeted nanoparticle contrast agent via tail vein. Delayed post-contrast MRI was performed four days after nanoparticle contrast administration. ADx-001-enhanced MRI was conducted on a 1T permanent MR magnet (M2 system, Aspect Imaging, Shoham, Israel), utilizing a T1-weighted spin echo (T1w-SE) sequence and following scan parameters: TR = 600 ms, TE = 11.5 ms, slice thickness = 1.2 mm, matrix = 192 × 192, FOV = 30 mm, slices = 16, NEX = 4 [31]. All MRI scans were acquired on the same scanner using consistent imaging protocols and within a similar timeframe; therefore, image intensity standardization was not performed prior to analysis. Three nanoparticle contrast dose levels (0.10, 0.15, 0.20 mmol Gd/kg) were examined in older mice, while younger mice were tested at 0.20 mmol Gd/kg. The study included 49 mice in total: 36 older mice (6 mice per dose level per genotype) and 13 younger mice (6 TG and 7 WT). Each mouse underwent both pre- and post-contrast scans, yielding a total of 72 scans. Mice were euthanized after post-contrast scans for post-mortem confirmation of amyloid pathology using anti-amyloid β antibody (4G8) staining in brain tissue sections as per methods described previously [31].

Image processing

In the earlier work [37, 38], targeted brain regions of interest were segmented through a two-step process. First, a high-resolution 3D mouse brain atlas (annotated with 27 brain regions) [39] was manually registered on the MRI scans, which consisted of 16 slices. Registration was performed using rigid body transformation based on six parameters (three translational: left-right, anterior-posterior, inferior-superior; and three rotational: left-right, anterior-posterior, inferior-superior). This transformation preserves anatomical geometry and ensures consistent alignment across all subjects. Subsequently, targeted regions of cerebral cortex and hippocampus were extracted. We prioritized our analysis to these two brain regions since these are the regions that exhibit relatively high amyloid burden within the brain [40]. In the development of the automatic pipeline, we trained the segmentation model using modified mask derived from the registered brain atlas, which was refined to three regions: cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and all other regions. The objective of this pipeline was to enable the extraction of these regions irrespective of their spatial orientations and increase efficiency for future segmentation tasks.

Data analysis

Automatic multi-label segmentation model

To develop and evaluate an automatic multi-label segmentation model for targeted brain regions, all available MRI scans were pooled regardless of age, amyloid level, genotype, or contrast administration. The full datasets were divided at the image level into 80% training and validation, and 20% testing, so the split was not strictly independent at the subject level. Given the segmentation model was employed as a preprocessing step to generate anatomically plausible region masks, replacing manual segmentation to reduce human bias and improve efficiency, rather than as a standalone model whose test performance was intended to quantify subject-level generalization, the train/test split was conducted without considering the age, amyloid level, genotype, or contrast administration. Model performance was evaluated on the held-out test set. Our developed segmentation model was trained using a finetuned UNet type architecture implemented with the Python package MONAI [41]. UNet [42] is a convolutional neural network characterized by its encoder-decoder structure and a U-shaped design. It was initially developed for 2D image segmentation. The encoder extracts features and learns an abstract representation of the input image through a sequence of encoder blocks. The decoder utilizes the abstract representation to generate a semantic segmentation mask. This process maps effectively the learned features back to the original spatial dimensions. To optimize the UNet results, both the standard 2D UNet and its 3D variant were evaluated, and different hyperparameters was explored. These included the region of interest (ROI) size, which determines the spatial extent of the input patch; the training loss function (e.g., Dice or DiceCE) to guide model learning; the number and dimensions of hidden layers in the encoder–decoder blocks; the size of the feedforward layers; and the batch size, which controls how many samples are used per training step. To further enhance segmentation performance, we integrated the use of transformers such as SWIN transformer [43] into the UNet framework. A 5-fold cross validation was applied on the training set to tune hyperparameters and reduce overfitting, thereby enhancing the robustness of our results. Dice coefficients were calculated by measuring the spatial overlap between the semi-automatic and automatic generated masks. The averaged Dice coefficients across three regions were assessed to determine the optimal segmentation model performance. Upon identifying the best model based on validation Dice coefficients, we proceeded with the prediction of the multi-label masks.

Radiomic analysis

We utilized the RFs recommended by Imaging Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI), which conducted a multi-site international study to identify a set of RFs that exhibit high reproducibility without the use of filters for research studies [44]. IBSI successfully pinpointed 164 highly reproducible RFs across various radiomic software and imaging modalities. Feature extraction were performed using Python package Pyradiomics [45]. All the semi-automatically segmented labels were used directly to compute the RFs from the following features classes: first-order statistics; gray level co-occurrence matrix; gray level run length matrix; gray level size zone matrix; neighboring gray tone difference matrix, and gray level dependence matrix. Due to the use of rigid registration (as described in Image Processing section) and the consistent application of the same brain atlas across all MRIs, shape-related RFs were excluded from analysis. Including these features under such conditions could introduce artificial consistency that does not reflect true biological variability.

Classification analysis

Following the radiomic calculations on the delayed post-contrast scans, a separate machine learning procedure was implemented for each segmentation method (automatic and semi-automatic segmented), and age group (old and young). In each procedure, we initially aimed to split the images into 80% training and 20% testing. However, the test set predictions were organized by dose levels, and at least one TG and one WT mouse were needed each time to calculate relevant classification metrics. Thus, a training set could comprise 70%-80% of the post-contrast scans (regardless of dose levels). To enhance reliability, we repeated the classification analyses using only scans with high-quality masks i.e. averaged Dice coefficients ≥ 0.9 from the automatic segmentation model. The accepted range for acceptable Dice coefficients typically falls between 0.7 and 0.9 [46, 47]. In particular, this study [47] systematically examined classification performance across Dice thresholds ranging from 0.75 to 0.90. Their results indicated that a 0.90 cutoff optimized sensitivity the most. Given our study involved mouse brains with fine regional structures, small misalignments can have a disproportionately large impact on RF extraction. To ensure high-quality input data for the classification task, we opted for the stricter 0.90 threshold. This stringent criterion aimed at ensuring the reliability and accuracy of the radiomic analysis on the segmented regions. Non-parametric neighborhood component analysis (NCA) [48] was used for feature ranking and selection. NCA is a supervised technique that identifies and weights features based on their contribution to classification performance. Feature selection and k-fold cross validation were employed in the training set only. 5-fold cross validation was applied for all analyses related to older mice. 2- or 3-fold cross validation, depending on the use of Dice restriction, was applied for all analyses related to younger mice because of the smaller sample size. To assess the efficacy of genotype classification based on amyloid-associated features, we fitted the data with a range of linear and non-linear classifiers with different parameter settings. All RFs were standardized prior fitting to classifiers. The assessed classifiers included logistic regression, discriminant analysis, support vector machine (SVM), tree-based method, gradient boosting, random forest, Gaussian naives bayes, K-nearest neighbors (KNN), and multi-layer perceptron. The best classifier(s) were selected based on the mean classification metrics across k-fold cross-validation: accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC, evaluated in the validation sets. To ensure a fair and direct comparison, the same test sets were used for both automatic and semi-automatic approaches within each age group and under consistent Dice restrictions. Post-mortem antibody-based amyloid staining served as the gold standard for identifying the presence of amyloid pathology [31].

Conventional method

In addition to assessing the genotype classification performance through machine learning, we conducted a comparative analysis based on the conventional method, i.e. percentage change in mean intensity signal between pre- and post-contrast MRIs, which was also used in a previous study [31]. Both masks generated from semi-automatic and automatic approaches would be examined, as well as with or without a Dice restriction. Since this calculation involved both pre- and post-contrast scans, when a Dice restriction was applied, only cases with averaged Dice coefficients ≥ 0.9 (computed by comparing the semi-automatic and automatic generated masks) in both pre- and post-contrast scans were analyzed. Standard logistic regression without a training/testing split was employed to compute the classification metrics for this baseline comparison and consistent with the prior study. The outcome variable was genotype (TG/ WT). The primary predictors were percentage signal change in the cortex and hippocampus. Gender was included as a covariate. For analyses involving older mice, dose level was included as an additional covariate.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed two sensitivity analyses on MRIs from older mice. We aimed to assess the robustness of RFs to automatic segmentation performance and the impact of deviations in masks on classification outcomes, providing insight into how automatic segmentation results influenced the overall classification. In the first analysis, we calculated the percentage change of RF values between semi-automatic and automatic generated masks, and we compared these percentage changes against the categorized Dice coefficients. In the second analysis, we intentionally altered the mask positions by manipulating rotation and/or translation parameters: three for rotation along the x-, y-, z-axis; three for translation along the x-, y-, z-axis. We systematically tested 51 combinations of parameters, involving changes in one to four parameters simultaneously. The evaluation utilized the same test sets, classifier(s), and IBSI features derived from the analysis with predicted masks in automatic pipeline.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate whether the segmentation model’s performance varied by age groups or pre/post-contrast, we applied the Wilcoxon rank sum test to assess their association with Dice coefficients. For assessing differences across nanoparticle contrast dose levels, we used the Kruskal Wallis test. All statistical tests were conducted at a significance level of 0.05.

All segmentation and training procedures were conducted using 3D Slicer (version 5.2.2) and Python (version 3.11.4). For data processing and statistical evaluations, we used Python and 3D Slicer.

Results

Automatic segmentation model

Figure 2 displays a 3D surface rendering of the mouse brain mask, and the corresponding sagittal and two coronal MRI slices. The overlay process was executed in a 3D visualization environment using 3D Slicer, where the brain atlas was carefully aligned with the MRI scans. The use of semi-transparent masks allows the underlying anatomical structures to remain visible and provides essential context for understanding the spatial relationships between different brain regions. In the model training phase, a total of 49 pre- and 49 post- contrast MRI scans were incorporated, from 36 older mice and 13 younger mice. The dataset included mice with and without nanoparticle contrast administration and covered a range of contrast dose levels. For training and validation, ~ 80% of the scans were utilized, comprising 56 (77.8%) scans from older mice and 22 (84.6%) scans from younger mice (Table 1). Taking the overall performance into consideration, as expected, the 3D UNet model integrated with SWIN transformer performed the best in terms of Dice coefficients. The optimal configuration was obtained with a patch size of (96,96,32), feature size of 36, learning rate of 1e-4, and the DiceCE loss function. During inference, predicted labels were obtained by applying argmax to the model outputs. The model training was executed using a single GPU (Nvidia GTX1080Ti), requiring approximately 10.5 h and completed 50,000 iterations. All predicted training and testing images using 3D UNet with exhibited Dice coefficients ≥ 0.8 (range: 0.80–0.95). When examining the region-wise performance, seven images had Dice coefficients ranging from 0.7 to 0.8 (Table 1). We employed the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank sum test to evaluate the association between Dice coefficients (from three brain regions) were compared against age group, genotype, and gender respectively. Neither factor demonstrated statistical differences in Dice distributions (p = 0.307, 0.551, 0.462). We performed Wilcoxon rank sum test by treating pre- and post-contrast observations independent as our primary aim was not to evaluate within-subject changes. The Wilcoxon signed rank test confirmed that Dice distributions did not differ significantly between pre- and post-contrast scans (p = 0.447). In older mice, the Kruskal Wallis test showed no significant differences in Dice distributions across the three nanoparticle contrast dose levels (p = 0.982). Radiomics and classification analyses

Given the available RFs in Pyradiomics, a total of 178 RFs were calculated for the cerebral cortex and hippocampus. 26 post-contrast MRIs from older mice (13 TG, 13 WT) exhibited a Dice coefficient ≥ 0.9. 10 mice (4 TG, 6 WT) received 0.1 mmol Gd/kg dose; 9 mice (6 TG, 3 WT) received 0.15 dose mmol Gd/kg; and 7 mice (3 TG, 4 WT) received 0.20 dose mmol Gd/kg. 9 younger mice (6 TG, 3 WT) exhibited a Dice coefficient ≥ 0.9. Only these scans were included in the corresponding classification analyses when a Dice restriction was applied.

Table 3 summarizes the classification performance from top nano-radiomics classifiers for older mice, using masks generated by semi-automatic and automatic segmentation approaches, with and without a Dice restriction. With a Dice restriction, genotype classification with the semi-automatic approach achieved higher mean validation accuracy and AUC (both 1.0), whereas the automatic approach outperformed on all the dose-specific test sets. Test set AUCs were 1.0 across all dose levels and approaches. The automatic approach utilized five RFs (1 hippocampus, 4 cortex) and a universal random forest classifier to exhibit perfect testing performance for all dose levels. Its validation accuracy and AUC were 0.90 and 0.83, respectively. In contrast, the semi-automatic approach did not yield a single classifier capable of achieving perfect test performance across all doses. Its best classifier was a SVM that required 2x to 3x more RFs than the automatic approach. Without applying a Dice restriction, both approaches employed an MLP classifier with identical mean validation accuracy (0.97), perfect sensitivity, and 0.93 specificity. Test set AUCs were 1.0 across all dose levels and both segmentation approaches. However, test accuracies for the 0.1, 0.15, and 0.2 mmol Gd/kg doses were 1.0, 0.67, and 0.67, respectively. The MLP classifier used eleven RFs (5 hippocampus, 6 cortex) for the automatic approach and eight RFs (3 hippocampus, 5 cortex) for the semi-automatic approach. Due to the limited number of samples per dose group, the held-out test set at each dose level consisted of only one TG and one WT mouse. As a result, test accuracy values were constrained to discrete outcomes (0%, 50%, or 100%) and could be more sensitive to chance or selection effects.

Sensitivity analysis

To determine the robustness of RFs with respect to the automatic segmentation model performance, we compared the post-contrast RF values between automatic and semi-automatic approaches for all older mice (N = 36), based on percentage change. Among the 178 RFs, 94 (52.25%) displayed changes within ± 1%, while 146 (82.0%) exhibited changes within ± 5%. The most substantial changes were observed in minimum cerebral cortex intensity (47.5%), cluster shade hippocampus intensity (39.3%), and skewness hippocampus intensity (30.3%) (Additional file).

To assess the impact of mask deviations on TG/WT genotype classification, we again focused on the older mice with an averaged Dice coefficient ≥ 0.9 (# scans = 26), as well as the derived random forest classifier and five selected RFs. Upon checking the predicted masks from multi-label segmentation model, we translated the masks within ± 1 mm and rotated the masks ± 2 degrees, along all three axes. Among the 51 transformations applied, 26 had one parameter changed, 18 had two parameters changed, five had three parameters changed, and two had four parameters changed. The analysis revealed that 37 transformations resulted in worsened validation accuracy, six showed improved accuracy, and the rest remained unchanged (range: 0.75–0.95). Among those with worsened validation performance, 16 transformations involved a change in one parameter; 14 involved changes in two parameters; five involved changes in three parameters. Transformations with four parameters changed consistently exhibited worsened classification performance.

Discussion

In this preclinical study, we investigated the utility of nano-radiomics, machine learning, and automatic segmentation techniques in the context of a novel nanoparticle MRI agent for an AD mouse model. We also assessed if this approach improved the classification of TG mice from WT controls based on amyloid-imaging features compared to traditional methods relying on percentage signal intensity change. Quantitative interpretation of mouse brain MRI is not commonly done because of very few MRI based molecular agents today. To our knowledge, this is the first study applying these methodologies in molecular MRI (mMRI) in a TG mouse model of amyloid pathology.

To facilitate objective and high throughput analysis, we developed a specialized automatic pipeline that streamlines manual tasks including registration and brain segmentation, aiming to minimizing human errors and enhancing overall efficiency. In the automatic segmentation training step, we found that integrating a 3D UNet with SWIN transformer yielded the best performance. This model was then implemented and evaluated in the subsequent classification analyses. The performance of the automatic and semi-automatic segmentation approaches was generally comparable. Notably, when analysis was restricted to older mice with high-quality segmentations, the automatic approach achieved superior classification across all doses using the fewest RFs and a single universal classifier. For the younger mice, both automatic and semi-automatic approaches yielded perfect validation results, however the latter approach outperformed the automatic one after excluding segmented scans of lower quality. The development of automatic segmentation models represents a significant advancement in enhancing the diagnostic capabilities of medical imaging. Streamlining processes such as registration and brain segmentation minimizes human error and maximizes efficiency. While automatic segmentation and registration methods of human brain images have been previously demonstrated [50, 51] but resources for mouse brain segmentation, especially with AD pathology, are less common.

Radiomics revealed that subtle changes in signal enhancement patterns, which are often diluted in global metrics of mean signal enhancement, can be detected. Our findings demonstrated that RFs extracted from post-contrast scans consistently outperformed traditional metrics that based on percentage change in mean signal intensity between pre- and post-contrast scans. This advantage was especially apparent at lower doses of the contrast agent, where signal differences were less pronounced and conventional methods struggled. Furthermore, RFs based on first-order statistics were frequently selected, regardless of the Dice restriction or segmentation approach. First-order statistics describe the intensity distribution of voxels (e.g., mean, minimum, maximum, standard deviation) without considering spatial relationships. Since our ROIs (cortex, hippocampus) are relatively small in size, texture features (e.g., GLCM, GLRLM) may exhibit reduced reliability or variability. Comparatively, first-order features are generally more stable and informative, as they are less sensitive to spatial heterogeneity or minor segmentation imperfections. Machine learning approaches augmented with radiomics holds great promise in boosting the performance of medical imaging for diagnosis and phenotyping of disease as well as monitoring treatment response. Unlike conventional MRI-based metrics of signal enhancement, which required both pre- and post-contrast scans to detect amyloid pathology, our nano-radiomics approach utilized only post-contrast MRI scans. We found that nano-radiomic analysis of post-contrast scans alone can be as effective as, or superior to, conventional techniques across various levels of amyloid plaque burden. While this finding is promising, it is based on preclinical mouse data. Further validation in human studies is necessary. In the context of clinical translation, nano-radiomics has the potential to reduce imaging time and costs, and improve patient experience by eliminating need for multiple scans.

In addition, age did not play a significant role in classification outcomes. In both younger and older mice, radiomics outperforming conventional percentage change approach. Although younger mice typically exhibit lower amyloid deposition and are expected to be more challenging to classify, the models still achieved promising accuracy. This suggests that nano-radiomics may offer sensitivity to early-stage disease changes, and have the potential for early detection in preclinical Alzheimer’s models. Limiting the analysis to high-quality segmentations is also recommended, especially when subtle regions are involved. As demonstrated in the analysis on older mice without a Dice restriction, although both automatic and semi-automatic approaches achieved optimal validation performance, they required a more complicated classifier (multi-layer perceptron network), a larger number of RFs, and the test set performance was only strong at the lowest dose. A similar conclusion can also be drawn from the traditional percentage signal change analysis. Although Dice coefficients were used during our segmentation model validation and poorly segmented cases could be excluded from subsequent radiomic analyses, we recognize that ground-truth-based evaluation is not feasible in fully automatic clinical pipelines. However, as illustrated in Fig. 3, segmentations with Dice values near 0.8 already exhibit noticeable spatial shifts from the brain region, which could be identified through limited visual inspection. In practice, such quality control could help detect and exclude major segmentation errors even without access to ground truth.

While our nano-radiomics genotype classification and automatic segmentation model training have shown promising results and efficiency in various aspects, it is essential to address certain limitations. First, the registration and segmentation outcomes from the semi-automatic approach were employed as the gold standard for comparison with the automatic approach. Potential human errors in atlas registration to the brain MRIs may introduce imperfections, and the lack of inter-rater validation may affect the segmentation reliability. Second, the foundational mouse brain atlas used in our study was originally developed from healthy adult mice. Segmented brain regions from MRIs might not precisely represent our aged APP/PSEN transgenic mice with unique neurodegenerative features that might alter brain feature sizes. Third, there was a potential risk of data leakage in the segmentation and classification process. The training/testing split in the segmentation model was done at the image level which could result in some subjects contributed to both training/testing set. Subject-level independence between training and test sets is generally preferred. However, given the segmentation model was employed as a preprocessing step to generate anatomically plausible region masks, rather than as a standalone model whose test performance was intended to quantify subject-level generalization, the training/testing dataset was randomly selected at the image level without stratification by age, genotype, contrast use, or dose level. Next, the classification pipeline utilized the segmentation model outputs as a fixed preprocessing step for RFs extraction. Although some images used for segmentation training might have appeared in the classification test set, the segmentation and classification models were developed as independent analyses with distinct objectives. Classification was conducted solely on post-contrast MRIs (sample size depended on the use of dice restriction and age group), which was a distinct subset from the segmentation dataset. Since the segmentation model was not tuned using classification labels, and segmentation outputs were fixed prior to training and testing the classification model, the risk of data leakage should be minimal. Fourth, the classification based on the masks predicted from automatic approach incorporated an additional criterion with Dice coefficients. This led to a reduced number of images available for the classifier training, resulting in some analyses having a training set comprising only 70% of the sample size, rather than the desired 80%. This could potentially lead to insufficient power for model training. Finally, due to the small sample in each dose-specific test set, test accuracy values may be overestimated or sensitive to small variations.

In future research, conducting similar studies in other mouse models of amyloid pathology is important to assess whether comparable RFs or classifiers can facilitate early detection and classification based on amyloid burden. Additionally, there is a need to examine individual brain regions, including the cerebellum [52], to normalize amyloid burden and refine the classification performance differentiating between individual mice. Addressing these challenges will contribute to advancing our understanding of AD mouse models and refining diagnostic methodologies for improved precision and reliability.

Conclusions

We developed a series of machine learning classifiers combining nano-radiomics and automatic segmentation to distinguish TG mice modeling early-onset AD from WT controls. These classifiers were tailored to different age groups in an AD mouse model that underwent molecular MRI with an amyloid-targeted nanoparticle contrast agent. The classifiers demonstrated utility for early detection of amyloid pathology, including under conditions of lower contrast agent doses and minimal amyloid burden. Although, we used an amyloid-targeted agent as a case study, the proposed radiomics-machine learning pipeline can be applied to nanoparticle contrast-enhanced images acquired with other imaging agents, targeted or non-targeted, and in combination with other imaging modalities (CT, PET or SPECT).
