Curriculum Integration of an Interactive Blood Pressure Measurement Module to Improve Healthcare Students' Knowledge, Confidence, and Skills
Authors: Mandeep K. Patel, Lynn Timko‐Swaim, Perrilynn Conklin, Wei‐Hsin Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHypertension (HTN) is a global risk factor for death and a major contributor to cardiovascular disease. Accurate blood pressure measurement (BPM) is essential for accurate diagnosis and management, yet many healthcare providers and trainees lack adequate BPM knowledge and technique due to non‐standardized education and limited reinforcement. This study evaluated the impact of a structured BPM training program integrated into the curricula of two health professional training programs. Participants completed the American Medical Association's BP Measurement Essentials: Student Edition e‐learning module alongside their traditional coursework. To assess the program's effectiveness, we administered pre‐ and post‐intervention surveys and Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs), measuring changes in BPM knowledge, confidence, and technique proficiency. Both medical and PA student cohorts demonstrated statistically significant improvements in BPM knowledge and self‐reported confidence. Post‐training, students reported greater confidence in their technique and comfort in performing BPM on patients. Additionally, both groups showed improved ability to identify factors influencing BPM accuracy (medical students: p = 0.002; PA students: p = 0.035). Both cohorts showed significant gains in understanding proper BPM techniques (p < 0.001). OSCE results further validated improvements in technical accuracy and patient communication. Overall, structured BPM education significantly enhanced student proficiency, confidence, and clinical performance. These findings support the integration of standardized BPM training into healthcare education to strengthen BPM skills and improve HTN care outcomes.

Introduction

Hypertension (HTN) remains a leading global health concern, and is the predominant risk factor for early mortality worldwide resulting in an estimated 10.8 million avoidable deaths each year [1]. Forty‐eight percent of U.S. adults have HTN, the leading cause of cardiovascular disease [1, 2]. Accurate blood pressure (BP) measurement is fundamental to effective diagnosis and treatment of HTN, yet research indicates that healthcare providers do not necessarily demonstrate adequate BP measurement (BPM) knowledge and skills. Accurate BPM continues to be a significant challenge for clinicians managing HTN [3, 4]. In 2019, the American Medical Association (AMA) and American Heart Association surveyed 2302 healthcare professionals and noted that almost one half of the physician assistants (PA) and primary care physicians have never received any type of BPM refresher education after their initial training [5]. In addition, Hayer [6] noted that at pre‐intervention baseline, healthcare providers including medical assistants, nurses, advanced practice providers and physicians correctly identified less than half of the skills necessary to properly measure BP and scored an average of 7.6 out of 12 on a BP knowledge test.

Since proper BP measurement knowledge and skills are taught and assessed in healthcare professional education programs, this is where interventions would be most beneficial to establish a solid foundation. Research, however, indicates that many healthcare professional trainees, including medical and PA students, have insufficient knowledge and skills in BP measurement techniques due to the lack of standardized and reinforced training [7, 8, 9, 10]. Utilizing a consistent and standard approach to measuring BP is crucial as numerous factors such as a patient's physical state, the clinical setting, and the type of equipment used can easily lead to errors and result variability [11, 12, 13]. However, Cheung et al. [14] noted several barriers to implementing standardized BP measurements that include lack of (1) healthcare provider and patient knowledge of factors affecting BP measurements, (2) proper equipment, (3) environment (e.g., space, noise, time constraints), and (4) healthcare provider training and follow‐up refresher‐training. The clinical impact of inconsistent education and inaccurate BP measurements is extensive and can include diagnostic errors, improper goal directed treatment decisions, and suboptimal patient outcomes [13].

Changes in healthcare professional education program curricula emphasizing BP measurement are needed for students to become and remain proficient. Recent efforts have sought to address these gaps in BP education [15, 16, 17, 18]. The AMA has introduced one such initiative by creating five standardized BP measurement training modules with the goal of improving competency among healthcare professional trainees [19, 20]. Hayer's [6, 19] research involving implementation of BP measurement e‐learning modules found them to be effective among both healthcare professionals, and medical and nursing students, resulting in statistically significant increases in knowledge and BP measurement skill performance. Padwal's [21] paper also emphasized the critical importance of efficiently training healthcare workers and offered a series of free educational videos summarizing the key principles of BPM, available through Johns Hopkins University. Additionally, a study involving nursing students evaluated the accuracy and technique of BPM using a nine‐item checklist, both before and after a training program [22]. The results demonstrated significant improvement across all checklist items following the training. These findings suggest that regular, periodic education on BPM may be essential to sustain and enhance both technique and accuracy. Our review of the literature found that although there have been studies on the positive impact of standardized BP measurement skills training among healthcare professionals, there are few studies that focus on BP measurement training of healthcare trainees such as medical and PA students. Our study aims to build upon existing research by determining the effects of implementing a structured BP measurement training program [20] into existing BP curricula for medical and PA students.

Methods

Study Setting and Participants

This study was conducted at Stony Brook University (SBU) in the 2023–2024 academic year and approved by the SBU Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects institutional review board (IRB2023‐00389). Study participants included a convenience sample of 136 first‐year medical students and 70 first‐year PA students.

Study Design and Intervention

This is a mixed method pre‐post study design. Both student cohorts (medical and PA) initially received their standard BPM instruction through PowerPoint‐based lectures on topics related to Vital Signs within their respective fall courses: Introduction to Clinical Medicine for medical students and The Problem‐Oriented Medical Record: History and Physical Exam for PA students.

Additionally, all students were required to view the interactive AMA BPM Essentials: Student Edition module (approximately 35 min in duration) as a supplement to their curriculum [20]. The video module included the three sections of (1) introduction to BP, (2) measuring BP, and (3) self‐measured BP. Clear step by step instructions with real people, several images with linked explanations, and multiple knowledge check questions that allow students to practice BPM skills were included within the module. A link for resources, including a faculty guide, is provided as well. Medical students viewed the module independently and submitted completion certificates to the course director, whereas PA students completed the module together during a classroom session and then also submitted completed certificates. Subsequently, the medical students practiced BPM techniques on each other within the University's Clinical Simulation Center (CSC), while the PA students practiced with classmates in their classroom settings‐both under the supervision of faculty. Students practiced both manual and automated BPM.

Two months after the initial training and practice, all students participated in a BPM Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) with standardized patients at the CSC. Each student was provided 5 min to complete this OSCE. This formative OSCE evaluated students’ communication skills as well as their understanding of key BPM elements, including factors that affect BP measurement, patient positioning, and technique.

Data Collection and Analysis

Pre‐post survey data were collected from all students using the Qualtrics platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT, USA). The pre‐survey was administered prior to the BPM instruction and included multiple choice and Likert‐scale type questions. Students were asked whether or not they had prior BPM training and experience. They were also asked to indicate their confidence and comfort level with BPM (Very Low = 1, Very High = 5), as well as answer BPM knowledge questions related to: (a) factors affecting BP results, (b) patient positioning, and (c) BPM proper techniques. The post‐survey was administered to both groups following the intervention and the students were also asked to provide feedback on the quality of the BPM training module.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for the OSCE checklist data and Chi‐squared tests were used to compare pre‐and post‐survey response distributions.

Results

A total of 107 medical students completed the pre‐survey (78.7% response rate, 107/136) and 91 completed the post‐survey (66.9% response, 91/136). For the PA students, 61 completed the pre‐survey (87.1% response rate, 61/70) and 63 completed the post‐survey (90.0% response rate, 63/70). For the CSC OSCE activity 133 medical students (97.8% participation rate, 133/136) and 69 PA (98.6%, 69/70) participated.

Prior BPM Education and Patient Experience

On the pre‐survey, students were asked to indicate the type of BPM education they had received to date as well as how much BPM experience they have with patients prior to this training. Thirty‐six percent of medical students and 59% of PA students had received formal BPM training (Figure 1). OSCE Performance

As shown in Table 8, both medical and PA students scored high on the OSCE in regard to introducing themselves to the patient and general communication with the patient. A difference was noted between the medical and PA student scores, with the PA students scoring higher in all three assessments in the patient preparation category (mean scores 50% vs. 82%). Mean scores for the patient positioning and BP measurement technique categories were similar in the two groups although a large difference was found for the specific criteria of “performed BP by palpation” within the BP measurement technique category with 45% of medical students and 96% of PA students completing this task. The overall mean scores for all of the categories were high with 78% (medical students) and 89% (PA students) performing the BP measurement correctly on standardized patients.

Student Feedback of the Module

Post‐survey data indicated that most students (84%, n = 166) agreed that the faculty in their respective programs should continue to incorporate the Essentials module into the curriculum with future classes. On average, the majority of students rated the module characteristics highly. More PA students rated the module as good or excellent and higher than the medical students in all categories of module characteristics including clarity (76% vs. 61%), organization (70% vs. 58%), length (54% vs. 49%), interactivity (68% vs. 54%) and educational value (71% vs. 59%).

Discussion

The incorporation of the interactive AMA BPM Essentials module into existing BP curricula along with assessments through pre‐and‐post surveys and OSCEs, resulted in notable improvements in student knowledge, confidence, and technique proficiency. In addition, 86% of students suggested continued use of the module.

Baseline differences were found between formal BPM education and amount of patient BPM experience among the medical and PA cohorts. These discrepancies likely reflect variations in instructional exposure and pre‐matriculation clinical experience. Rather than advocating for increased pre‐matriculation instruction, our findings support the need for formal standardization and reinforcement of BPM training throughout health professional education.

Following the intervention, both medical and PA students demonstrated statistically significant improvements in self‐reported confidence and comfort in performing BPM, with medical students showing the greatest gains. These findings emphasize the value of formalized BPM instruction early in clinical education to foster self‐assurance and procedural competency. Both cohorts also demonstrated improvement in BPM knowledge and technique. However, select post‐intervention responses were unexpectedly lower than pre‐intervention results. Factors contributing to this phenomenon may include superficial reading of keywords (e.g., selecting the correct patient arm “heart level” based on familiarity rather than context) and survey fatigue. These findings emphasize the importance of conceptual reinforcement, particularly in areas such as level of patient arm placement, food intake and activity timing prior to measurement.

Medical and PA students showed gains in recognizing procedural elements following the intervention, reflecting its educational value. Results from the OSCE supported these findings, with both groups performing strongly in communication and patient interaction domains. However, PA students outperformed medical students in patient preparation tasks. A difference was observed in the completion of BPM by palpation, suggesting potential gaps in procedural familiarity among medical students and pointing to the need for increased hands‐on practice within their curriculum. Overall, both cohorts achieved high performance on standardized patient encounters following BPM training demonstrating accurate technique. These outcomes validate the effectiveness of pairing formal instruction with hands‐on evaluation and indicate that structured BPM education can significantly enhance students’ understanding and practical application of BPM.

A pilot study done by Hayer showed similar results to our study; using the AMA foundational eLearning module can help improve BPM knowledge and skills. They also found improvements in the topics of patient preparation, positioning, and cuff sizing and placement [19]. Their study suggested that an eLearning module may not be a standalone solution. Certain skills will still require additional in‐person training with peers and support from healthcare educators [23]. A study done by Farahani with pharmacy students showed that both self‐instruction videos and face to face instruction in acquiring BPM skills were equally effective in students’ performance and satisfaction, and suggested a combination may be best [24]. The outcomes from our study, which incorporated both our traditional BPM education, along with supplementing with the BPM learning module also supports this recommendation. Our findings also align with similar research done at the University of North Carolina School of Medicine, where they created a standardized longitudinal program combining e‐learning and hands‐on training and found overall increased student‐rated confidence level in BPM [25].

HTN has traditionally been diagnosed and monitored using in‐office BP readings. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force now recommends out‐of‐office measurements—such as ambulatory and home monitoring—for more accurate assessment [26]. The Essentials module highlights the value of educating students on self‐measured blood pressure (SMBP), including correct patient positioning and home monitoring protocols. SMBP or home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) in which individuals measure their BP in their home environment, is a validated method for capturing out‐of‐office readings [27]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that BP measured via HBPM exhibits a stronger correlation with cardiovascular disease outcomes than office‐based readings [28]. Some studies have confirmed that patients frequently fail to adhere to proper protocols for home BPM and reporting [29]. It is therefore important for this topic to be included in BPM education so that students learn the proper technique to perform SMBP and educate patients as well.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. The sample was restricted to students from a single medical school and PA program, limiting generalizability across institutions and training models. The integration of BPM instruction occurred within a tightly packed curriculum in both programs, which may have influenced the consistency and depth of delivery. Lastly, aligning the new BPM framework with existing educational structures required adaptations that may have affected implementation fidelity.

Future Directions

To assess long‐term educational impact, future studies should incorporate longitudinal evaluations of BPM competency as students’ progress through their training. These assessments would provide insight into knowledge retention, skill maintenance, and clinical preparedness. Expanding the intervention across multiple institutions, using a variety of AMA BPM video modules, which are freely accessible via AMA Ed Hub and targeting different levels and types of learners would improve standardization and generalizability [20, 30].

Further incorporation of formative OSCEs is recommended to continuously identify learning gaps. Opportunities for students to review OSCE video recordings with targeted faculty feedback may enhance reflective learning and skill development. Receiving feedback promotes retention of BPM skills over time as seen by Bastos et al. [31]. Simulation‐based training using standardized BP arms could also be beneficial for improving technique and accuracy.

Lastly, increasing emphasis on SMBP education, including patient instruction, positioning, and home monitoring strategies during clinical rotations—will ensure that graduates are well prepared to support evidence‐based HTN care in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the integration of standardized BPM instruction, centered on the AMA BPM Essentials module, significantly enhanced student knowledge, confidence, and practical proficiency. Survey data and OSCE performance revealed measurable improvements, while the hands‐on approach promoted skill retention. While health profession programs are often constrained by rigorous schedules, incorporating a concise BPM training session into the foundational curriculum directly supports national strategies to enhance diagnostic precision and HTN management.
