Development of Integrated Parent–Metabolite Pharmacokinetic Model With Apparent Presystemic Metabolism to Characterize the Disposition of Alverine
Authors: Ara Cho, Hyeon‐Cheol Jeong, MinJu Kim, Inkyung Cho, Hyeon‐Jeong Na, Kyong‐Cheol Ko, Yoon‐Jee Chae, Kyeong‐Ryoon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlverine is an antispasmodic agent characterized by extensive metabolism, and systemic exposure is largely driven by its metabolites. However, conventional parent–metabolite pharmacokinetic models typically assume metabolite formation solely from the systemic parent compound, which may not adequately capture route‐dependent differences in metabolite exposure. This study aimed to develop an integrated parent–metabolite pharmacokinetic model to simultaneously describe alverine and its three major metabolites (M1, M2, and M3) in mice following intravenous (IV) and per‐oral (PO) administration. Plasma concentration–time data were analyzed using a stepwise nonlinear mixed‐effects model. Alternative structural models were evaluated to account for route‐dependent metabolite appearance. Distinct route‐dependent differences were observed in alverine metabolite exposure, with substantially higher levels following PO administration, particularly for M2, despite limited parent exposure. A model without presystemic metabolism, which assumed parent‐derived formation alone, failed to adequately describe these data, leading to an overprediction of early parent concentrations. The final model incorporated presystemic metabolism for M1 and M3, significantly improving model fit (ΔOFV = −138.10) and providing an adequate description of both parent and metabolite profiles across routes. These findings suggest that systemic metabolite exposure following oral dosing is strongly influenced by presystemic processes that cannot be captured by parent‐driven formation alone. The proposed model provides a parsimonious structural framework to account for these effects without requiring detailed mechanistic information. This approach may be broadly applicable to compounds exhibiting metabolite‐dominant exposure and highlights the importance of flexible structural models in pharmacometric analyses of route‐dependent pharmacokinetics.

Introduction

Alverine is an antispasmodic agent that selectively relaxes smooth muscles in the gastrointestinal tract and uterus, and is widely used for the treatment of disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome and dysmenorrhea [1, 2]. Previous clinical studies have shown that alverine undergoes rapid and extensive metabolism following administration, with circulating metabolites accounting for the majority of systemic exposure [3, 4]. These findings indicate that the pharmacokinetics of alverine are primarily determined by its metabolites rather than its parent compound. The major metabolites include 4‐hydroxy alverine (M1), 4‐hydroxy alverine glucuronide (M2), and N‐desethyl alverine (M3) [3, 4].

Despite these characteristics, the pharmacokinetics of alverine have largely been described using noncompartmental or descriptive approaches [3, 5]. Although such analyses adequately summarize exposure measures, they cannot determine whether route‐dependent metabolite appearance can be explained by systemic parent‐derived formation alone or explicitly characterize the dynamic interplay between parent disposition, metabolite formation, and metabolite elimination [6, 7, 8]. This limitation is particularly relevant for alverine, as its pharmacological activity is at least partly mediated by its active metabolite M1 [3]. Consequently, a parent–metabolite model that assumes formation solely from the systemic parent may mischaracterize route‐specific parent and metabolite exposures and, when active metabolites contribute to pharmacological effects, complicate the interpretation of exposure–response relationships [6, 9, 10, 11].

This limitation becomes particularly critical when metabolite exposure varies substantially across administration routes [6, 9]. For compounds undergoing extensive first‐pass or presystemic metabolism, oral administration may result in disproportionately greater systemic exposure to metabolites compared with intravenous administration [6, 12, 13, 14]. In such cases, the observed appearance of metabolites in plasma may reflect not only systemic formation from circulating parent drugs but also substantial presystemic formation occurring during intestinal absorption and/or first‐pass hepatic processing before the parent drug reaches systemic circulation [6, 12, 13, 14]. This leads to complex and route‐dependent metabolite profiles that cannot be readily explained by systemic processes alone.

To address these limitations, integrated parent–metabolite pharmacokinetic modeling provides a useful framework for simultaneously characterizing the disposition of a parent compound and its metabolites within a unified structural model [7, 9]. However, many conventional approaches assume that each metabolite is formed solely from the parent compound through a single pathway [6, 7, 15, 16]. When presystemic formation contributes substantially or route‐dependent differences in exposure are pronounced, models based solely on systemic parent‐derived formation may fail to adequately describe the observed concentration–time profiles, particularly during the early phase following oral administration [7, 9]. These limitations support the evaluation of alternative structural approaches to route‐dependent metabolite appearance. When detailed physiological interpretation of presystemic processes is required, mechanistic approaches such as physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling may be considered [17, 18, 19]. PBPK models have the advantage of explicitly incorporating physiological, biochemical, and anatomical factors, enabling a more detailed representation of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion processes across different tissues and organs [17]. Beyond PBPK modeling, semi‐physiological population PK approaches have incorporated presystemic metabolite formation using either a hypothetical enzyme compartment, as demonstrated for sunitinib and its active metabolite, or explicit intestinal and hepatic compartments, as recently demonstrated for paracetamol and its metabolites [18]. However, developing PBPK models typically requires extensive physiological and compound‐specific data, including information on enzyme kinetics, tissue distribution, and transporter activity, which are often limited or unavailable in preclinical settings [17, 19]. For alverine, detailed in vitro and mechanistic data are not readily accessible, making the application of PBPK modeling challenging. In this context, the present integrated structural model represents a less physiologically detailed, structurally parsimonious alternative to semi‐physiological and PBPK approaches, using apparent input pathways to capture route‐dependent metabolite appearance without requiring fully specified physiological details [7, 9].

Therefore, this study aimed to develop an integrated parent–metabolite pharmacokinetic model that simultaneously described alverine and its three major metabolites (M1, M2, and M3) using mouse plasma concentration–time data obtained after intravenous (IV) and per‐oral (PO) administration. Through stepwise comparison of alternative structural assumptions, we evaluated whether additional input pathways were required to characterize route‐dependent metabolite exposure and aimed to identify a model capable of describing both parent and metabolite concentration–time profiles across administration routes. Alverine was therefore used as a case example to evaluate a pragmatic structural modeling approach for compounds exhibiting disproportionate and route‐dependent metabolite exposure.

Materials & Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Alverine citrate (Cat. No. A134–0500) was purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Reference standards of 4‐hydroxy alverine HCl (M1), 4‐hydroxy alverine glucuronide (M2), and N‐desethyl alverine HCl (M3) were obtained from TLC Pharmaceutical Standards (Ontario, Canada). Liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry LC/MS grade acetonitrile (ACN; Cat. No. 1.00029, LiChrosolv, Supelco) and LC/MS grade water (Cat. No. 1.15333, LiChrosolv, Supelco) were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Formic acid (FA; Cat. No. A117–50) was purchased from Fisher Chemical(Fair Lawn, NJ, USA). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Cat. No. 276855–100ML) was obtained from Sigma–Aldrich. All solvents and reagents used in this study were of analytical or LC–MS grade and were used as received without further purification. Microcentrifuge tubes (1.5 mL) were obtained from Eppendorf (Hamburg, Germany). Mouse plasma (lithium heparin‐treated; SKU: IGMSCD1PLALIH100ML) was purchased from Innovative Research (Novi, MI, USA) and used for calibration and quality‐control sample preparation during bioanalysis.

Animals and Ethical Approval

Male ICR mice (8–9 weeks old, 35–42 g) were obtained from Koatech Inc. (Pyeongtaek, Gyeonggi, Republic of Korea). All animals were acclimated for at least one week before dosing under controlled conditions (temperature: 22°C ± 2°C; relative humidity: 50% ± 10%; 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water, except for an 8 h fasting period prior to drug administration, during which only water was available. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology (KRIBB) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. KRIBB‐AEC‐25455).

In Vivo Pharmacokinetic Study Design

Alverine citrate was dissolved in DMSO:PEG400:water (5:40:55, v/v/v) prior to administration. A total of 10 male ICR mice were assigned to two treatment groups based on the route of administration: An intravenous (IV) group (5 mg/kg of alverine citrate, corresponding to 10.56 μmol/kg of alverine; 5 mL/kg; n = 5) and an oral (PO) group (75 mg/kg of alverine citrate, corresponding to 158.37 μmol/kg of alverine; 10 mL/kg; n = 5). IV dosing was administered via the tail vein, and PO administration was performed by oral gavage using a flexible feeding tube.

Serial blood samples (approximately 30 μL per sample) were collected from each animal at all prespecified time points for its assigned route of administration. In the IV group, samples were collected at 0.083, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h post‐dosing, whereas in the PO group, samples were collected at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h post‐dosing. Samples were transferred into lithium heparin‐coated microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C to obtain plasma. The separated plasma samples were stored at −20°C until LC–MS/MS analysis. Plasma concentration–time data for alverine and its major metabolites, M1, M2, and M3, were used for subsequent pharmacokinetic analyses.

LC–MS/MS Analysis

The plasma concentrations of alverine and its metabolites (M1, M2, and M3) were quantified using an LC–MS/MS system comprising a triple‐quadrupole mass spectrometer (API 3200, SCIEX, Foster City, CA, USA) coupled with a liquid chromatography system (Agilent 1260 Infinity HPLC; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Plasma samples (15 μL) were mixed with 60 μL of acetonitrile containing IS (Carbamazepine 50 ng/mL) by vortexing and then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. Then, 5 μL of the supernatant was injected into the LC–MS/MS system. Chromatographic separation was performed using a reversed‐phase column (Xterra MS C18; 50 mm × 2.1 mm, particle size 5 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, U.S.A.) pre‐warmed at 40°C. A gradient program was used with 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive electrospray ionization (ESI+) mode using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). The m/z values for alverine citrate, 4‐hydroxy alverine, 4‐hydroxy alverine glucuronide, N‐desethyl alverine, and carbamazepine were 282.2 → 91.0, 298.2 → 107.0, 474.2 → 298.2, 254.2 → 91.1, and 237.1 → 194.2, respectively. The LC–MS/MS method was validated for sensitivity, specificity, linearity, accuracy, precision, and stability prior to its application to samples obtained from in vitro and in vivo studies. The lower limits of quantification (LLOQs) were 0.5 ng/mL for alverine, 0.5 ng/mL for M1 (4‐hydroxy alverine), 0.75 ng/mL for M2 (4‐hydroxy alverine glucuronide), and 0.5 ng/mL for M3 (N‐desethyl alverine).

Noncompartmental Analysis

Noncompartmental analysis (NCA) was performed using Phoenix WinNonlin version 8.3 (Certara, Princeton, NJ, USA). Cmax and Tmax were obtained directly from the observed plasma concentration–time data. Exposure was summarized by the area under the concentration–time curve from time zero to the last quantifiable concentration (AUClast) and, where supported by the terminal phase, by the area extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf). The terminal elimination half‐life (t1/2) was estimated from the terminal log‐linear portion of the concentration–time profile.

Data Preparation

All concentration data were converted to molar units (μmol/L) to enable direct comparison across the parent compound and metabolites. The administered doses were converted into molar units for model implementation. The administered amount was expressed based on the molecular weight of alverine citrate, and the measured analyte concentrations were converted using the molecular weights of the corresponding analyte forms. This conversion was applied to ensure analyte‐wise molar comparability in the integrated parent–metabolite analysis.

The dataset was structured in a longitudinal format to allow simultaneous modeling of parent and metabolite concentration–time data within a unified framework. Concentrations below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) were excluded from the analysis.

Structural Model

An integrated parent–metabolite pharmacokinetic model was developed to simultaneously describe the disposition of alverine and its three major metabolites. The parent compound was described using a two‐compartment model comprising a central and a peripheral compartment. The two‐compartment structure of the parent compound was selected based on the observed bi‐exponential decline in plasma concentrations following intravenous administration. Parent disposition was parameterized using clearance (CL), central volume (Vp1), peripheral volume (Vp2), and intercompartmental clearance (Q).

Metabolite formation was represented by parallel pathways from the parent to M1 and M3, and a sequential pathway from M1 to M2 [3]. This structure allowed the simultaneous characterization of both parallel and sequential metabolic processes within a unified framework.

The metabolite compartments were described using apparent distribution volumes Vm1,Vm2, and Vm3, and first‐order rate constants governing the formation and elimination processes. To account for the potential route‐dependent differences in metabolite exposure, alternative input structures were explored during model development.

Model Development

A stepwise modeling strategy was applied to ensure structural interpretability and numerical stability. First, the pharmacokinetics of the parent compound were characterized using IV data, followed by the incorporation of PO data to describe the absorption processes. The metabolite data were sequentially introduced (M1, M2, and M3) to construct an integrated framework.

To account for route‐dependent metabolite formation, a series of candidate models with increasing structural complexity was systematically evaluated.

Step 1: A model without apparent presystemic metabolism.

Step 2: A model with apparent presystemic metabolism for M1.

Step 3: (Final Model): A model with apparent presystemic metabolism for M1 and M3.

Step 4: A model with apparent presystemic metabolite metabolism for M1, M2, and M3.

Model selection was guided by statistical criteria, including the objective function value (OFV), Akaike information criterion (AIC), and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Additionally, parameter identifiability (RSE%) and overall fit were assessed using visual predictive checks (VPCs). Although Step 4 was evaluated, Step 3 was ultimately selected as the final model (Figure 1) because it provided the most parsimonious fit without compromising numerical stability or parameter precision (further detailed in the model comparison and parameter estimation results).

For IV administration, the dose was introduced directly into the parent central compartment as an IV bolus. For PO administration, the dose was distributed across separate first‐order input pathways to the parent, M1, and M3 compartments to account for the route‐dependent formation identified during model development. Notably, although M2 was formed sequentially from M1, presystemic metabolite input pathways were incorporated only for M1 and M3, as determined by the final structural selection. Parameter Estimation

The model parameters were estimated using nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling implemented in Monolix (version 2024R1, Lixoft, France) using the stochastic approximation expectation–maximization (SAEM) algorithm.

To reduce parameter redundancy and improve identifiability, several parameters were defined algebraically rather than being estimated independently, as described in the model equations. In the final model, the oral input fraction to the parent pathway fp and the parent absorption rate constant ka were fixed. The value of fp was fixed to the corresponding estimate obtained from the NCA, because the simultaneous estimation of the absolute oral input fraction and route‐specific input allocation parameters was not considered sufficiently identifiable within the final structural model. The value of ka was fixed based on stepwise model development using parent data prior to integration of the metabolite submodels.

The model parameters were estimated using nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling. Variability between individuals was included where supported by the data to improve estimation stability and avoid overparameterization.

The residual unexplained variability was described using analyte‐specific error models. A combined additive and proportional error model was used for the parent compound, and proportional error models were applied to M1, M2, and M3.

Model Evaluation

The model performance was evaluated using standard diagnostic procedures. Goodness‐of‐fit (GOF) plots, including observed vs. population predictions, observed vs. individual predictions, and conditional weighted residuals vs. time and predictions, were used to assess the model fit. The predictive performance of the model was evaluated using prediction‐corrected visual predictive checks (pcVPCs) stratified by analyte and administration route. The agreement between the observed data and simulated prediction intervals was used to assess the ability of the model to capture the central tendency and variability of the data. Parameter precision was assessed based on relative standard errors (RSEs) derived from the Fisher information matrix.

All the diagnostic plots and simulations were generated using Monolix, Python, and PRISM (version 8.4.0).

Results

Observed Pharmacokinetic Profiles and Noncompartmental Analysis

The plasma concentration–time profiles of alverine and its metabolites (M1, M2, and M3) following IV and PO administration in mice are presented in Figure 2. Following IV administration, the parent compound exhibited a high initial concentration followed by a rapid decline, whereas the metabolite concentrations were relatively low and appeared with a temporal delay. In contrast, after PO administration, systemic exposure to the parent compound was relatively limited, whereas metabolite concentrations were generally higher and more sustained. In particular, M2 showed the highest concentrations following PO administration and emerged as the predominant circulating analyte, demonstrating a marked difference in exposure compared to IV dosing.

The NCA (Table 1) provided quantitative support for these observations. For the parent compound, the AUClast was 0.95 μmol·h/L after IV administration and 0.58 μmol·h/L after PO administration, indicating relatively limited systemic exposure following oral dosing. In contrast, the metabolites exhibited different patterns. M2 exhibited a marked increase in exposure after PO administration, with AUClast values of 0.95 μmol·h/L (IV) and 51.54 μmol·h/L (PO), corresponding to an approximately 54‐fold increase. This disproportionate increase was further reflected in the M2‐to‐parent AUClast ratio, which increased from approximately 1.67 after IV administration to 148.34 after PO administration. M1 and M3 showed similar route‐associated trends when evaluated relative to the parent compound. The M1‐to‐parent AUClast ratio increased from approximately 0.002 after IV administration to 0.19 following PO administration, while the M3‐to‐parent AUClast ratio increased from approximately 0.006 to 0.66. These findings indicate a consistent increase in relative metabolite exposure following PO administration, although the magnitude of change was less pronounced than that observed for M2.

Overall, these findings demonstrated marked route‐associated differences in the relative systemic exposure to the parent compound and its metabolites. After PO administration, the relative exposure to unchanged alverine decreased, whereas the relative exposure to metabolites increased, particularly for M2. This pattern provided a key rationale for the subsequent development of an integrated parent–metabolite model incorporating presystemic metabolite input pathways.

Development of the Integrated Parent–Metabolite Model

Based on these observations, models incorporating presystemic metabolite input pathways were developed. Model development proceeded in a stepwise manner to evaluate the structural assumptions required to jointly describe alverine and its metabolites.

Following the establishment of the full parent–metabolite framework, an initial model without presystemic metabolism, assuming formation solely from the systemic parent, was evaluated. This model without presystemic metabolism captured the overall disposition pattern but consistently overpredicted the early concentration–time profile of the parent compound, particularly after PO administration. This finding suggested that the observed metabolite appearance could not be adequately represented by parent‐derived formation alone.

To further examine this discrepancy, separate single‐compartment models were fitted to M1 and M3. In both cases, the apparent input rate constants estimated from the separate metabolite models were greater than the corresponding parent‐to‐metabolite formation rate constants in the integrated model (Table S2). The resulting ratios were 4.35 for M1 and 5314.59 for M3, indicating a substantially more rapid, apparent metabolite input than that explained by the parent‐derived formation pathways in the model without presystemic metabolite input.

Based on these findings, presystemic metabolite input pathways were introduced and evaluated. A model with a presystemic metabolism for M1 produced a modest improvement in fit relative to the model without presystemic metabolism, whereas the inclusion of presystemic metabolism for M1 and M3 yielded a substantially better fit. An extended model, incorporating a presystemic metabolism for M2, was also evaluated. Although this model resulted in a modest additional decrease in the objective function value (ΔOFV = −16.27), it did not improve the overall model performance. Instead, parameter identifiability deteriorated substantially, and more pronounced trends were observed in the residual diagnostics, suggesting overparameterization rather than meaningful structural improvement. Given that M2 is formed sequentially from M1, increased presystemic input into the M1 compartment in the model with presystemic metabolism (M1 + M3) is likely sufficient to account for the observed M2 exposure without requiring an independent presystemic metabolite input pathway.

Compared with the model without presystemic metabolism, the selected model reduced the objective function value (OFV) from −961.23 to −1099.33, yielding a total improvement of 138.10 points. A consistent improvement was also observed in AIC and BIC (Table 2). A visual comparison of the representative individual fit further supported the selection of the final model (Figure S1). The principal improvement was observed in the early phase of the parent concentration–time profile, where the model without presystemic metabolism tended to overpredict the concentrations. In contrast, both models provided broadly similar descriptions of the metabolite profiles. Collectively, these results supported the incorporation of presystemic metabolite input pathways for M1 and M3 into the model as the most adequate structural representation of the observed parent–metabolite data, providing the best balance between GOF, parameter identifiability, and structural parsimony.

Structural Pharmacokinetic Model

The final model is shown in Figure 1. The selected structure comprised a two‐compartment model for the parent compound, with a distribution between the central and peripheral compartments, parallel metabolic pathways from the parent to M1 and M3, and a sequential pathway from M1 to M2. This configuration enabled simultaneous representation of both parallel and sequential metabolic processes linking alverine and its major metabolites.

To account for the marked route‐associated differences in metabolite exposure observed after PO administration, the final model incorporated a PO input pathway to the parent compartment and presystemic metabolite input pathways to the M1 and M3 compartments. These presystemic metabolite input pathways were included as apparent structural components to capture the rapid appearance of metabolites after PO dosing.

Overall, the final model provided a unified structural framework that adequately described the concentration–time profiles of the parent compound and all three metabolites.

In contrast, the parameters associated with metabolite kinetics exhibited greater variability in estimation precision. Although the overall magnitude and trends of metabolite exposure were adequately captured, several metabolite‐specific parameters, particularly those related to distribution volumes and pathway‐specific rate constants, showed higher RSEs. This was most evident for M1 and M3, where limited information in the observed concentration–time profiles constrained the precise estimation of certain parameters.

For M2, the model estimated a small apparent distribution volume Vm2, along with a large first‐order loss rate constant km2,total, reflecting the rapid turnover of this metabolite within the structural framework. Similarly, the input‐related parameter for M3 (ka,m3) was estimated to be large, which is consistent with the rapid appearance of M3 following PO administration. These parameter values should be interpreted as structural representations required to capture the observed data, rather than as direct physiological quantities.

Overall, the final model provided a consistent and coherent description of the parent and metabolite concentration–time data while acknowledging that some metabolite‐related parameters were estimated with reduced precision owing to data limitations.

GOF plots comparing observed and individual predicted concentrations showed that data points were generally distributed along the line of identity across all analytes, indicating that the model adequately captured the overall concentration levels and temporal profiles (Figure 3A). Minor deviations were observed for some metabolites at higher predicted concentrations. However, these deviations were limited in magnitude.

Residual diagnostics further supported the adequacy of the model. The individual weighted residuals (IWRES) were generally symmetrically distributed around zero, with no pronounced trends or heteroscedastic patterns (Figure 3B). No apparent time‐ or prediction‐dependent trends were observed for the parent compound, which suggested an adequate structural model. Mild patterns were observed for the metabolites. In particular, M1 exhibited a gradual decrease in residuals with increasing predicted concentrations, whereas M2 and M3 showed weak curvatures in the residual distributions. However, these deviations were modest and did not indicate a substantial model misspecification.

The predictive performance of the model was further evaluated using pcVPCs (Figure 4). For most analytes, the observed data were largely within the simulated prediction intervals, indicating that the model adequately reproduced the overall data distribution. For the parent compound, good agreement was observed between the observed and simulated median profiles across the entire time course. For the metabolites, the overall temporal trends were reasonably captured, although some discrepancies remained. M1 showed good agreement with the central tendency; however, the variability appeared to be slightly overestimated. M2 exhibited an underprediction of the median in the early phase, along with an overestimation of variability. M3 generally exhibited reasonable agreement, although minor deviations were observed in the early time region.

Overall, the final model (M1 + M3) adequately described both the central tendency and overall variability of the concentration–time profiles, although some limitations remained for certain metabolites.

Discussion

In this study, an integrated parent–metabolite pharmacokinetic model was developed to characterize the disposition of alverine and its three major metabolites (M1, M2, and M3), with particular emphasis on route‐dependent differences in exposure. A key finding was that metabolite exposure increased markedly following oral administration, whereas exposure to unchanged alverine remained limited. This pattern could not be adequately described by a conventional model assuming metabolite formation solely from the systemic parent. In contrast, the final model, which incorporated additional apparent input pathways for M1 and M3, provided the best overall description of the observed parent and metabolite concentration–time profiles.

The limitation of the initial model (Step 1) was most evident in its tendency to overpredict the early parent concentration–time profile following oral administration. Allowing a portion of the oral input to enter the M1 and M3 compartments through additional pathways (Step 3) improved the description of both the early parent profile and the rapid appearance of the metabolites. Thus, the final structure provided a more flexible representation of the route‐dependent data than a model based solely on systemic parent‐derived metabolite formation.

During model development, an additional input pathway for M2 was also evaluated in Step 4. This extension was assessed as a structural sensitivity analysis to determine whether the high M2 exposure required an independent apparent input pathway, rather than as a biologically preferred metabolic hypothesis. Although Step 4 produced a modest reduction in the OFV, it was associated with poorer parameter identifiability and less favorable residual diagnostics and was therefore not selected. The lack of support for an independent M2 input pathway is structurally consistent with the sequential formation of M2 from M1. Once increased upstream input into M1 is incorporated, its effect propagates through the existing M1‐to‐M2 pathway. The high M2 concentrations following oral dosing may therefore reflect increased upstream input into M1, subsequent sequential conversion to M2, and the small apparent distribution volume of M2, rather than an independent input into the M2 compartment. The pharmacological activity of M2 was not evaluated in the present study; therefore, its high systemic exposure should not be interpreted as direct evidence of a pharmacological contribution.

The model evaluation generally supported the adequacy of the proposed structure. The parent concentration–time profile was well described across the observed time course, with no apparent systematic bias in the residual diagnostics. Minor discrepancies remained for some metabolites, particularly M2, for which the early concentrations were underpredicted, and variability was not fully characterized in the pcVPC analysis. These findings suggest that the current representation of M1‐to‐M2 conversion may not fully capture the early‐phase kinetics of M2. However, the remaining discrepancies appear more likely to reflect limitations in the kinetic description of the sequential transformation than the absence of an independent M2 input pathway. Overall, these discrepancies did not substantially compromise the ability of the model to describe the parent and metabolite concentration–time profiles simultaneously.

Relatively high RSEs were observed for several metabolite‐related parameters, indicating limited parameter identifiability. This uncertainty likely resulted from several factors, including the small sample size, the absence of direct metabolite administration, and the low concentrations of certain metabolites. The inclusion of multiple input pathways may also have increased parameter redundancy. Although stepwise model development and selected parameter fixing were used to improve estimation stability, the possibility of overparameterization cannot be excluded. Accordingly, the overall structural interpretation is better supported by the available data than the individual estimates of the metabolite formation and elimination parameters, which should be interpreted with caution.

This study also has several data‐ and design‐related limitations. Although serial sampling provided complete concentration–time profiles for each animal within its assigned route, IV and PO administration were evaluated in separate groups rather than in a crossover design. Interindividual variability may therefore have contributed to the observed route‐related differences. In addition, because the analysis was based on parent‐only dosing, metabolite formation and elimination were estimated indirectly, limiting the identifiability of pathway‐specific disposition parameters. Accordingly, the available data support a structural and qualitative characterization of the contribution of presystemic processes but do not permit unique or precise estimation of the individual metabolite formation and elimination processes. Furthermore, the exclusion of concentrations below the LLOQ reduced the effective information content for low‐abundance metabolites. A substantial proportion of the M1 and M3 observations were below the LLOQ, whereas M2 was more consistently quantifiable, resulting in an imbalance in the information available across metabolites. This imbalance likely contributed to parameter uncertainty and limited the characterization of the terminal elimination phases.

Although the additional input pathways were introduced primarily to improve the description of the observed data, they can be interpreted within a biologically plausible framework. The marked route‐dependent metabolite profiles are consistent with a substantial contribution from processes occurring during intestinal absorption and/or first‐pass hepatic processing before the parent compound reaches the systemic circulation [14, 16]. However, the additional input pathways should be regarded as apparent, lumped representations of multiple underlying processes, potentially including intestinal metabolism, hepatic extraction, and the rapid entry of formed metabolites into the systemic circulation. They do not imply the direct absorption of preformed metabolites.

The major metabolites evaluated in this study, including M1, M2, and M3, have also been reported in humans, indicating qualitative overlap in the metabolic profile of alverine across species [3]. However, the quantitative similarity of the individual pathways between mice and humans has not been established. Moreover, the specific enzymes responsible for M1 and M3 formation and their relative contributions in the intestine and liver cannot be determined from the available alverine data. Therefore, although the observed profiles are consistent with a substantial presystemic contribution, the low systemic exposure of the parent following oral administration cannot be attributed exclusively to presystemic metabolism on the basis of the present analysis.

From a modeling perspective, the proposed approach represents a practical compromise between physiological detail and parameter identifiability. Fully mechanistic frameworks, such as PBPK models, may provide a more detailed description of intestinal and hepatic processes but generally require extensive physiological and compound‐specific information that is unavailable in many preclinical or early‐stage settings. In contrast, the present structure captured key features of the data, particularly the rapid and route‐dependent appearance of metabolites, while limiting unnecessary structural complexity. The model should therefore be regarded as a structurally informed representation of the net contribution of presystemic processes rather than as a fully mechanistic description of individual metabolic pathways.

More broadly, these findings suggest that conventional parent–metabolite models based solely on systemic parent‐derived formation may be insufficient when oral administration results in rapid and disproportionate metabolite exposure. In such circumstances, additional apparent input pathways may provide a useful structural alternative when detailed physiological information is limited. The proposed framework may inform subsequent human pharmacokinetic modeling and pharmacodynamic analyses when metabolites contribute to efficacy or toxicity, although its applicability should be evaluated for each compound and dataset. Future studies incorporating direct metabolite administration, urinary excretion data, improved analytical sensitivity, or more detailed mechanistic information may enhance parameter identifiability and support further refinement of the model.

Conclusion

The proposed model extends conventional parent–metabolite models based solely on systemic parent‐derived formation by incorporating presystemic metabolite input pathways to better describe the route‐dependent pharmacokinetics of alverine. This integrated approach provides a coherent structural framework for simultaneously describing parent and metabolite concentration–time profiles, particularly when metabolite exposure is disproportionate, following oral administration. Generally, the proposed modeling strategy may be applicable to other compounds that exhibit metabolite‐dominant exposure and pronounced route‐dependent differences, offering a practical and flexible framework for analyzing complex parent–metabolite relationships in the absence of detailed mechanistic data.
