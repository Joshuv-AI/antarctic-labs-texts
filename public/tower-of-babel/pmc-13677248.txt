Surgical Treatment of Tumor‐Induced Osteomalacia With Causative Tumors Affecting the Long Bones
Authors: Shuzhong Liu, Jinyi Xing, An Song, Mingjing Zhang, Jin Jin, Xi Zhou, Yong Liu, Weibo Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe causative tumor for tumor‐induced osteomalacia (TIO) locating in the long bone is hidden and has diverse locations, resulting in great challenges to orthopedists. This study aimed to investigate and summarize the clinical features and surgical outcomes of TIO patients with culprit tumors involving the long bones to enhance clinical understanding and improve orthopedic surgical management of this rare disease.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on the clinical data of TIO patients with causative tumors located in the long bones who underwent surgery by the Orthopedic Bone and Soft Tissue Tumor Sub‐specialty Group at our hospital between January 2013 and January 2023. This article described the clinical symptoms, location of causative tumors, tumor sizes, and other clinical features of TIO patients, and analyzed the trend of changes in blood phosphorus levels before and after surgery and pathological results. At the same time, clinical surgical experience and strategies for TIO patients with culprit tumors located in long bones were summarized.ResultsAll 49 patients included in the study had been pathologically confirmed and met clinical diagnostic criteria for TIO. All patients experienced bone pain (49/49), often accompanied by limited mobility, fatigue, difficulty turning over, and difficulty sitting up or walking. Importantly, 65.3% (32/49) experienced height loss during disease progression. A total of 65 operations were performed on these 49 patients, with culprit tumors located in the humerus (five cases), femur (33 cases), tibia (seven cases), fibula (three cases), and foot (one case). Twenty‐nine cases involved skeletal lesions, seven cases affecting the periosteum (outside‐in), while 13 showed both bone and soft tissue involvement. Following the final orthopedic procedure, serum phosphorus levels increased in 45 patients, and the median follow‐up duration was 45 months (interquartile range, 28–61 months; range, 2–215 months). Nine patients showed no remission after the first orthopedic surgery, and five experienced recurrence. One patient was diagnosed with a malignant culprit tumor.ConclusionSurgical planning for TIO with long bone involvement should account for the anatomical location and extent of the causative tumor. Currently, no standardized surgical strategy exists for this rare condition. Recurrence, non‐remission, or malignant transformation remains possible due to incomplete resection or local relapse. Based on the single center experience of this case series, we summarized the surgical classification and feasible surgical strategies for TIO's orthopedic surgery with causative tumors located in the long bones. The actual clinical application effect of this classification system needs further clinical data verification.

Introduction

Tumor‐induced osteomalacia (TIO) is an extremely rare form of acquired hypophosphatemic osteomalacia, first described by McCance in 1947 [1]. Although approximately 1000 cases have been reported in the literature to date, the actual morbidity is likely underestimated [1, 2, 3]. Due to its rarity in clinical practice, physicians often have a limited understanding of this condition. Furthermore, TIO typically presents with a slow onset, and the causative tumors are usually small, anatomically concealed, and clinically nonspecific, leading to significant diagnostic challenges and frequent misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis [4, 5].

The culprit tumors in TIO are usually widely distributed throughout the body, including the subcutaneous soft tissues, bones, and joints of the limbs, spine, deep soft tissues, palms, toes, and groin, with a predominance in the bones and soft tissues of the limbs [2, 4, 5]. A comprehensive analysis of TIO cases revealed that approximately 74.3% of tumors are located in the trunk, pelvis, and extremities [3]. Therefore, the surgical resection of most TIO‐related tumors typically requires orthopedic intervention following thorough preoperative evaluation. Establishing effective orthopedic surgical strategies is essential for the timely and rational clinical management of TIO patients. Although significant breakthroughs have been made in understanding the pathogenesis, diagnosis, treatment, and follow‐up of TIO, standardized and comprehensive summaries focusing on orthopedic surgical management remain limited [2, 6, 7]. This study retrospectively analyzed the clinical characteristics and surgical outcomes of 49 TIO patients affecting the long bones who underwent surgery at our institution over the past decade. The purposes of this study included: (i) To summarize the clinical characteristics of TIO patients with culprit tumors located in long bones; (ii) To describe the surgical treatment methods and outcomes of patients included in the study; (iii) To explore the advantages and disadvantages of different surgical methods and discuss optimized surgical strategies.

Materials and Methods

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The characteristics of all patients with TIO lesions involving the long bones who underwent surgical treatment in our institution between January 2013 and January 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients were included if they underwent at least one orthopedic surgical procedure targeting a causative tumor involving the long bones at our institution between January 2013 and January 2023. The study design was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (No. SK‐1658). The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) Patients with clinical manifestations included progressively worsening bone pain, typically originating from weight‐bearing joints (such as feet, knees, hips, back, and waist); (2) Patients' laboratory findings revealed preoperative hypophosphatemia, close monitoring of blood phosphorus was performed during the perioperative period; (3) Patients received the qualitative diagnosis: whole‐body bone scans, octreotide scintigraphy (e.g., 99mTc‐octreotide SPECT), and/or 68Ga DOTA‐TATE PET/CT; (4) Patients underwent localization diagnosis: including computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), were used to identify culprit tumors located in the long bones, detailing their size, location, and adjacent anatomical structures; (5) Patients underwent resection of the suspected culprit tumors, clinical diagnosis of TIO confirmed by pathological results of any surgical specimen, and were closely followed up after surgery. The exclusion criteria included: (1) Incomplete clinical data or missing key information; (2) Osteomalacia due to non‐tumor‐related causes.

General Information

Basic Characteristics of Patients

This study included 49 patients diagnosed with TIO due to causative tumors affecting the long bones who underwent surgical treatment at the Orthopedic Bone and Soft Tissue Tumor Sub‐specialty Group between January 2013 and January 2023. Clinical data included age, sex, presenting symptoms and signs, anatomical sites of bone pain, height reduction during disease progression, the occurrence of pathological fractures during disease progression, history of orthopedic surgeries, and culprit tumor distribution.

Laboratory and Imaging Analysis

Laboratory examinations included serum phosphate, serum calcium, ALP, urinary phosphate, urinary calcium, 24‐h urine phosphate, 24‐h urine calcium, parathyroid hormone (PTH), serum 25(OH)D3, serum 1,25(OH)2D3, and β‐C‐terminal telopeptide of Type I collagen (β‐CTX).

Preoperative imaging studies included bone mineral density testing, radiographs of limbs and trunk, ultrasound, CT, MRI, whole‐body bone scans, 99mTc‐octreotide SPECT, and positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT). These were employed for lesion characterization, localization, surgical planning, and risk assessment.

For pathological diagnosis, tumor specimens and tissues excised intraoperatively were sent for pathological examination using hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry. The nature and pathological characteristics of the tumor specimens were diagnosed by at least one pathologist at or above the associate professor level in the pathology department.

Surgical Treatment

All clinically diagnosed patients with TIO were asked to discontinue oral neutral phosphate supplements before orthopedic surgery. Baseline serum phosphate levels were reassessed after transferring to orthopedics, and relevant preoperative evaluations were performed to rule out surgical contraindications and guide the development of individualized surgical plans. Each patient included in the study underwent orthopedic surgery targeting the culprit tumor in the long bones responsible for TIO.

Follow‐Up

Serum phosphate levels were monitored daily following surgery and compared with preoperative baseline values. Patients were jointly followed up by the endocrinology and orthopedic departments after surgery. Regular postoperative evaluations included assessment of serum phosphate levels, symptomatic changes, degree of clinical improvement, local tumor recurrence, and postoperative complications. Follow‐up duration was calculated in months from the first documented orthopedic surgical procedure targeting the causative tumor to the administrative follow‐up cutoff in January 2023. For patients who had undergone such surgery before January 2013 and received further surgical treatment during the study inclusion period, follow‐up was calculated from the earlier procedure.

Statistical Analysis

Clinical data processing and statistical analysis were performed using MedCalc software version 15.2.2 (MedCalc, Ostend, Belgium) and R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], and categorical variables as n (%). Baseline summaries and paired analyses were calculated from one first‐listed record per patient. For postoperative days 1, 2, 3, and 6, serum phosphate was compared with the preoperative value among patients with a complete pair using two‐sided paired t‐tests. The four p values were adjusted using the Holm method implemented through the p.adjust function in R. Missing postoperative measurements were not imputed. Holm‐adjusted p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 11.0.0 (GraphPad Software).

Results

Patient Basic Information

From January 2013 to January 2023, a total of 49 patients with TIO caused by culprit tumors located in the long bones were included in this study (Table 1). The cohort consisted of 25 males and 24 females, with ages ranging from 15 to 69 years. The mean age at the time of the first orthopedic surgery was 40.8 ± 12.5 years. All cases were confirmed by clinical, imaging, and pathological examinations. The median recorded disease duration was 37.0 months (interquartile range: 24.0–72.0 months) (Figure 1). All 49 patients presented with bone pain, and the majority of patients suffered from restricted mobility to varying degrees in different parts, primarily involving weight‐bearing regions such as the lower limbs, hips, knees, chest, waist, and back (Figure 2). Among them, 32 experienced height reduction. Difficulties in sitting up and walking were reported in 46 patients, fatigue in 43, and limited mobility in 47. At initial diagnosis, 31 of the 49 patients had pathological fractures or pseudofractures (Figure 3). Furthermore, three patients had previously undergone unrelated orthopedic surgeries during their disease. The original patient‐level data are listed in Table 2.

Laboratory and Imaging Findings

All 49 patients included in the study underwent comprehensive tumor screening and were jointly evaluated by departments including endocrinology, orthopedics, ultrasound, nuclear medicine, radiology, and pathology. In some complex cases, diagnosis was established based on clinical data through multidisciplinary consultation and literature review. Before surgery, the mean serum phosphorus level was 0.52 ± 0.12 mmol/L (normal range: 0.81–1.45 mmol/L). Preoperative imaging included trunk and limb radiographs (49/49), CT or MRI (49/49), whole‐body bone scans (37/49), octreotide imaging (48/49), and PET/CT examination (33/49). Ultrasound was employed in specific cases where required to assist localization. Radiographic findings revealed generalized osteopenia, osteoporosis, or pathological fractures/pseudofractures.

Surgical Procedures

All 49 patients underwent orthopedic surgery, with a total of 65 operations performed (Table 3). Surgical interventions included bone tumor curettage with bone grafting, bone tumor curettage with bone cement filling, artificial joint replacement, and percutaneous bone cement osteoplasty with tumor ablation. The culprit tumor locations included the humerus (five cases), femur (33 cases), tibia (seven cases), fibula (three cases), and foot (one case) (Figure 4). Twenty‐nine patients had tumors involving only bone, seven cases affecting the periosteum (outside‐in), while 13 had tumors affecting both bone and soft tissue.

Postoperative Follow‐Up

All patients received close follow‐up from endocrinology and orthopedics departments after surgery. Following initial surgery, bone pain and fatigue symptoms improved in 40 patients. The original patient‐level perioperative trajectories are shown in Figure 5. In complete‐pair analyses, mean serum phosphate changed from 0.518 ± 0.129 to 0.554 ± 0.150 mmol/L on postoperative day (POD) 1 (n = 41; mean change 0.036, 95% CI −0.003 to 0.074; Holm‐adjusted p = 0.072), from 0.501 ± 0.119 to 0.583 ± 0.194 mmol/L on POD 2 (n = 43; mean change 0.081, 95% CI 0.032 to 0.131; Holm‐adjusted p = 0.004), from 0.506 ± 0.119 to 0.724 ± 0.229 mmol/L on POD 3 (n = 41; mean change 0.218, 95% CI 0.148 to 0.288; Holm‐adjusted p < 0.001), and from 0.490 ± 0.122 to 0.903 ± 0.182 mmol/L on POD 6 (n = 27; mean change 0.413, 95% CI 0.329 to 0.497; Holm‐adjusted p < 0.001) (Figure 6). Among the 49 patients, five experienced disease recurrence, and nine patients did not show symptom relief or phosphate normalization after initial surgery. The median follow‐up duration was 45 months (IQR, 28–61 months; range, 2–215 months), and serum phosphorus levels remained within the normal range in 45 individuals (Table 4). So far, TIO patients undergoing surgical treatment continued regular follow‐up via clinic visits or telephone consultations with both endocrinology and orthopedic teams.

Surgical Complications

In terms of surgical complications, among the 65 orthopedic surgeries performed on 49 patients, no short‐term surgery‐related complications were reported (Figures 7, 8, 9, 10). A total of 15 unplanned reoperations were conducted in 11 patients. Nine patients had no significant increase in blood phosphorus levels or symptom relief after the first orthopedic surgery, which improved following a second operation. Another five patients required reoperation due to recurrence. One patient with a malignant causative tumor experienced repeated recurrence and underwent three surgeries.

Discussion

Main Findings of This Study

In this single‐center cohort of 49 patients, all patients presented with bone pain, and 31 had pathological fractures or pseudofractures at baseline. Serum phosphate increased significantly on PODs 2, 3, and 6, but not on POD 1 after adjustment for multiple comparisons. At the last follow‐up, serum phosphate levels were within the normal range in 45 patients; however, non‐remission after initial surgery and disease recurrence remained important reasons for reoperation.

Clinical Characteristics and Current Diagnosis Status

Tumor‐induced osteomalacia (TIO), also known as oncogenic osteomalacia (OO), first reported by McCance in 1947, remains an extremely rare condition in clinical practice, with approximately 1000 cases documented in the literature to date [1, 2, 3]. Patients with TIO commonly present with diffuse bone pain, recurrent fractures at multiple sites, reduced height, muscle atrophy, and generalized weakness, often leading to significant functional impairment and loss of work capacity. Due to limited awareness among clinicians, including orthopedic surgeons, the disease is frequently misdiagnosed or diagnosed with significant delay. In recent years, researchers have increasingly focused on the disease burden and associated mental health issues in patients with TIO [8, 9]. Despite these advances, broader dissemination of knowledge regarding the clinical features and diagnostic and therapeutic strategies of TIO remains essential. This information should extend beyond endocrinologists and orthopedic surgeons to include a wider range of healthcare providers. Improving clinical recognition, reducing diagnostic delays, and refining treatment protocols are important for improving patient outcomes. Early diagnosis and timely intervention can significantly reduce the risk of TIO‐related complications and improve patient quality of life.

Surgical Treatment for TIO

Although significant progress has been made in the clinical diagnosis of TIO, a comprehensive and standardized orthopedic summary of surgical treatment strategies for TIO patients remains lacking. From a surgical perspective, the optimal treatment for TIO involves complete resection of the causative tumor [2, 10, 11, 12]. In most cases, tumor removal corrects biochemical abnormalities and facilitates the process of bone remineralization. However, incomplete resection may result in persistent clinical symptoms and biochemical abnormalities, with an increased risk of recurrence. For patients with local recurrence, a second surgery or even multiple surgical interventions may be required, including re‐excision or prosthesis implantation for structural reconstruction. It is important to note that osteomalacia leads to reduced bone mineral density, increasing the risk of pathological fractures, delayed healing, and, in some cases, multiple fractures [13, 14, 15, 16].

The causative tumors in TIO are predominantly mesenchymal in origin, and some benign bone and soft tissue tumors can also induce the condition [17, 18]. In patients with tumors affecting the long bones, accurate preoperative localization and qualitative diagnosis are essential for appropriate surgical planning, reducing the likelihood of misdiagnosis or delayed treatment [10, 19, 20]. Surgical removal of causative tumors affecting the long bones has been shown to alleviate symptoms and improve quality of life. Complete tumor resection typically yields favorable prognostic outcomes in patients with TIO [2, 10, 20]. However, certain tumor locations—such as the femoral head or condyle, tibial plateau—pose significant surgical challenges due to their anatomical complexity. Therefore, surgical planning should be tailored to the tumor's precise anatomical location and guided by the surgeon's clinical expertise. Surgical techniques reported in the literature include tumor resection, tumor curettage, and bone cement‐based inactivation [2, 7, 11, 12]. For most TIO patients with bone or soft tissue lesions, total tumor resection is recommended. In cases with hidden or surgically inaccessible tumors or where resection would compromise structural integrity, tumor curettage or bone cement inactivation is preferred. Segmental defects may be managed with artificial joint prostheses, allografts, or bone cement to restore anatomical continuity and mechanical stability. Although bone cement injection techniques have been explored for TIO, their long‐term efficacy and safety require further investigation.

Tumors located adjacent to articular surfaces often require joint‐sacrificing procedures. Complete resection in these areas may compromise joint function; thus, lesion curettage combined with bone cement reconstruction may be a more suitable option. In patients with an incomplete resection of the tumor, poor prognosis, or unrelieved symptoms following the first surgery, joint replacement needs to be considered [21]. A retrospective analysis by Zhu et al. [21] assessed the outcomes of joint replacement and tumor resection in 16 TIO patients with phosphaturic mesenchymal tumors (PMTs) involving metaphyseal articular surfaces. The cohort included 13 patients who underwent hip replacement and 3 who underwent knee replacement. Joint replacement surgery and tumor resection in TIO patients significantly and rapidly improved bone metabolism markers, and no recurrences were reported during short‐term follow‐up. Furthermore, 15 patients demonstrated significant improvement in joint function scores. Common postoperative complications included joint pain, joint clicking, and secondary hyperparathyroidism.

In 2015, Wang et al. [7] conducted a retrospective analysis of 17 TIO patients with long bone tumors who underwent surgical treatment at our institution. The study found that the complete resection rates were comparable between lesion curettage (67%) and segmental resection (80%). However, the recurrence rate following curettage (50%) was significantly higher than that after segmental resection (0%). Based on these findings, Wang et al. [7] recommended prioritizing curettage in the initial treatment to preserve joint function and structural stability. If symptom recurrence occurs during follow‐up and imaging suggests a residual or recurrent tumor, segmental resection should then be considered to improve clinical outcomes and long‐term prognosis [7]. With advances in intraoperative localization technology, surgical precision is expected to improve through the use of three‐dimensional (3D) navigation systems and real‐time tracking techniques. These approaches may allow more accurate structural and functional localization during surgery, facilitating complete tumor resection [22, 23]. In patients with long‐term or severe TIO, extensive reductions in bone mineral density often lead to multiple pathological fractures or pseudofractures across the body. Thus, in addition to resection of the culprit tumor, managing these fractures is a critical and challenging aspect of orthopedic treatment [13, 14, 15, 16]. Kobayashi et al. [13] reported that patients with TIO are prone to hip fractures and emphasized that tumor resection, combined with appropriate adjuvant therapies, can reduce the risk of such fractures in TIO patients. In this study, a total of seven patients developed hip fractures during the disease course, including three with femoral neck fractures.

For patients clinically suspected of having PMTs, a needle biopsy is generally not recommended due to the potential risk of tumor dissemination or residual disease [24]. In cases of local recurrence, second or multiple surgeries may be needed to achieve complete tumor removal and restore structural integrity. Accurate intraoperative localization of the tumor is essential for successful resection. Tumors responsible for TIO, particularly those in the long bones, are often small and located in anatomically obscure regions, which undoubtedly increases the difficulty of intraoperative identification. Failure to accurately localize the tumor may lead to incomplete resection, which may cause unresolved symptoms or recurrence. Based on existing literature and institutional experience, intraoperative ultrasound, combined with preoperative imaging modalities such as CT and MRI, can assist in identifying and removing these hidden lesions [25]. Comparing preoperative ultrasound findings intraoperatively may further improve the accuracy of tumor localization and removal. In addition to ultrasound‐based methods, Harbeck et al. reported successful intraoperative tumor identification and resection by administering 111In‐pentetreotide and using a handheld gamma probe to detect radiotracer uptake. This technique enabled the complete removal of the causative tumor and achieved satisfactory therapeutic outcomes [22]. Similarly, Liu et al. [26], in a retrospective analysis of 12 TIO patients, emphasized that accurate diagnosis and standardized surgical approaches are essential for achieving curative outcomes in PMTs. The authors recommended radical resection and microwave‐assisted local curettage as effective surgical options, concluding that complete excision remains the preferred approach for most TIO cases involving bone or soft tissue tumors [2, 6]. Based on the experience of our team, complete resection is recommended for TIO patients with causative tumors located in the long bones. However, in cases where patients are unable or unwilling to undergo open surgery, have contraindications, present with uncertain preoperative diagnoses, or possess lesions situated in anatomically challenging regions with bone involvement, minimally invasive bone cement tumor destruction techniques may be considered as an alternative.

Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the effect of anatomical location on the characteristics and prognosis of TIO‐related tumors. Li et al. [20] retrospectively analyzed 53 patients with TIO whose causative tumors were located in the foot/ankle, tibia, or femur. Their findings indicated that patients in the foot/ankle group experienced a longer disease duration and a significantly higher complication rate (p < 0.001). All tumors in the foot/ankle group involved soft tissue (p = 0.021), while most tibial lesions involved bone [20]. Moreover, patients in the foot/ankle group had a significantly longer postoperative recovery time for serum phosphorus normalization (p = 0.004), and the tumors showed higher Ki‐67 indices (p < 0.001) and a higher recurrence rate (p = 0.002) [20]. These results support that causative tumors in TIO may involve bone, soft tissue, or both, with femoral tumors frequently displaying combined skeletal and soft tissue involvement. Lesions involving bone are typically more difficult to resect completely and often require more complex and technically demanding surgical procedures [12, 27].

Surgical Treatment Strategy in Our Single Center

The choice of surgical approach may vary by region and institution due to anatomical heterogeneity of tumor locations. Achieving complete excision with negative margins remains essential to minimize the risk of residual disease or tumor spread. In a retrospective study involving 230 TIO patients, initial surgery resulted in clinical resolution in 83% of cases, whereas 11% showed persistent disease and 7% experienced recurrence, with a median recurrence interval of 33 months [27]. Reported risk factors for refractory TIO included female sex, spinal involvement, bone‐associated tumors, malignancy, and low preoperative serum phosphate levels [27]. Therefore, exploring surgical strategies for tumor‐induced hypophosphatemic osteomalacia based on the anatomical site of causative tumors and summarizing surgical experience is of clinical importance. This is particularly relevant for cases with high surgical complexity, such as those involving deep or structurally sensitive regions. Accumulating and sharing orthopedic surgical experience in TIO management may increase clinical understanding and improve treatment outcomes in patients with anatomically complex and surgically challenging lesions.

There are significant differences in surgical treatment and clinical prognosis among TIO cases with causative tumors located in different locations (such as bone, soft tissue, and simultaneously involving both bone and soft tissue) [20, 27, 28, 29]. These differences have been fully demonstrated in previous studies [20, 27, 28, 29]. In 2024, our team proposed the preoperative evaluation and treatment strategy for TIO orthopedic surgery [30]. On this basis, our team has summarized and extracted clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies for TIO patients with causative tumors located in long bones such as the femur in clinical practice. In addition, we classified TIO cases with different locations and ranges of involvement based on the site of tumor involvement (i.e., classification of causative tumors involving long bones in TIO, Figure 11). In this classification, detailed descriptions and distinctions were made on the anatomical location, extent of involvement, and adjacent relationships of the culprit tumor. Based on the clinical characteristics of different types of TIO cases with tumors located in long bones, our team proposed corresponding recommended surgical treatment plans. The proposal and application of this classification will to some extent fill the gap in surgical treatment strategies for TIO patients.

Limitations and Strengths of This Study

Although this study summarizes all surgically treated cases of PMTs in the long bones at a single center over the past 10 years, certain limitations remain. First, the limited number of cases makes it impossible to conduct subgroup analysis, and the findings can only serve as preliminary references to inform orthopedic surgical decision‐making in this disease context. Second, as a retrospective single‐center study, potential selection and information biases may affect the results. Further studies involving larger sample sizes and multicenter data are needed to enable more robust comparisons of clinical characteristics, surgical approaches, and outcomes in TIO patients with causative tumors located at different anatomical sites.

Conclusions

In summary, in TIO patients with long bone involvement, clinical symptoms result from tumor‐induced secretion of phosphate‐regulating factors. While clinical features and diagnostic tests may support diagnosis, they often lack specificity. Currently, no internationally accepted orthopedic surgical guidelines or prognostic evaluation systems exist for PMT cases involving the long bones. Complete tumor resection offers the best chance for clinical remission and improved prognosis. However, incomplete excision or local recurrence may lead to disease persistence, recurrence, non‐remission, or malignant transformation, significantly compromising treatment outcomes and patient quality of life. For these patients, personalized surgical strategies must be developed based on the anatomical characteristics of the lesion and the overall clinical status. In this case, establishing the clinical classification based on the clinical characteristics of the causative tumor may contribute to improving treatment efficacy and disease prognosis. The multidisciplinary collaborative approach incorporating appropriate preoperative assessment, surgical planning, and perioperative management undoubtedly helps optimize treatment efficacy and improve long‐term outcomes and quality of life in this rare but challenging patient population.
