Effects of Dietary Schizochytrium species powder on Broiler Growth Performance, Blood Parameters, and Gut Microbiota
Authors: Tingting Yang, Linna Gui, Jianing Wang, Shiman Zhao, Ruoying Wang, Jianbin Zhang, Hui Wang, Fenglong Cai, Hua Yang, Lei Pu, Liang Hong, Shunyi Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of supplementing Ross 308 broiler diets with DHA-rich Schizochytrium sp. powder (SP) on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, serum biochemical parameters, and gut health. A total of 120 broilers were randomly assigned to four dietary treatments: the control group (Control: basal diet) and three experimental groups (Group L: basal diet supplemented with 6.25 g/kg SP; group M: basal diet supplemented with 9.375 g/kg SP; group H: basal diet supplemented with 12.5 g/kg SP). The 49-day experiment consisted of a basal diet feeding phase from 0 to 3 weeks, followed by treatment-specific diets from 4 to 7 weeks. The results showed that: (1) no significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed in average daily gain (ADG) or feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) among the groups; however, during the late stage of supplementation with Schizochytrium sp. powder, the H group exhibited a significantly lower average daily feed intake (P < 0.05); (2) serum total protein (TP) levels were significantly higher in the L group (P < 0.05); (3) the apparent digestibility of crude fat and crude protein was significantly improved in all experimental groups compared to the control (P < 0.05); (4) the villus height to crypt depth ratio (V/C) in both the jejunum and ileum increased significantly (P < 0.05), suggesting enhanced nutrient absorption capacity; (5) Schizochytrium sp. powder supplementation promoted the proliferation of beneficial cecal bacteria such as Akkermansia and Faecalibacterium, which may be attributed to its high DHA content and contributes to strengthening the intestinal barrier function through modulation of the gut microbiota structure. Overall, the M group demonstrated the most favorable outcomes in terms of intestinal microbiota balance, intestinal morphology, and functional improvement, leading to better nutrient utilization and production performance. Therefore, identifying the optimal inclusion level of DHA-rich Schizochytrium powder in broiler diets is essential for maximizing economic efficiency and overall feeding efficacy.

Introduction

Poultry production is characterized by high efficiency and rapid growth, playing a pivotal role in meeting the global demand for animal protein (Minjeong et al., 2021). Broiler chickens serve as a vital source of essential nutrients, providing high-quality protein and unsaturated fatty acids that fulfill a significant portion of human nutritional requirements. However, modern poultry farming systems face critical challenges: while intensive rearing practices promote rapid growth and high feed conversion efficiency in white-feathered broilers, these advantages are often accompanied by increased susceptibility to physiological disorders, which compromise animal health and impair welfare outcomes (Aizwarya et al., 2021; Sugiharto et al., 2022). Evidence suggests that dietary supplementation with omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), particularly docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), significantly enhances broiler growth performance, antioxidant status, and immune function (Janine et al., 2017). At the same time, growing consumer demand for functional meat products enriched with beneficial bioactive compounds—such as DHA—highlights the necessity of developing innovative nutritional approaches in poultry feeding (Janghan et al., 2023). The development of DHA-enriched broiler meat not only aligns with public health goals by improving the nutritional profile of animal-derived foods but also supports the sustainability and competitiveness of the poultry industry.

Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) is a key omega-3 long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid that has garnered increasing attention in recent years due to its well-documented health benefits in humans (Jingwen et al., 2025). Marine microalgae, particularly Schizochytrium sp., which is rich in DHA, offer a sustainable and promising alternative to conventional fish oil sources, with significant commercial potential (Liyuan et al., 2022). Research by Shahgol et al. has demonstrated that omega-3 unsaturated fatty acids can improve growth performance and various health parameters in broiler chickens (Shahgol et al., 2025). Furthermore, a study by Jeon et al. revealed that supplementing broiler diets with DHA-rich Schizochytrium sp. effectively enhances the DHA content in both breast and thigh muscles (Jeon et al., 2022). Additional evidence suggests that daily intake of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and DHA contributes to improved cardiovascular health and exerts beneficial anti-inflammatory effects (Ivana et al., 2021). Consequently, DHA-enriched chicken meat represents a viable strategy for increasing dietary DHA consumption. However, gaps remain in the current understanding of the effects of incorporating Schizochytrium sp. powder into broiler diets. In particular, the impact of DHA enrichment on the gut microbiota and intestinal health of broilers has not yet been investigated. Most existing studies have focused narrowly on isolated aspects such as growth performance, slaughter traits, or meat quality in either laying hens or broilers, leaving a need for more comprehensive evaluations.

This study innovatively evaluates the multidimensional effects of DHA-rich Schizochytrium sp. powder in broiler chickens. It hypothesizes that dietary supplementation with this powder synergistically enhances growth performance, optimizes hematological and biochemical blood parameters, and modulates gut microbiota composition, thereby improving overall broiler welfare. These findings provide evidence supporting the use of Schizochytrium sp. powder as a functional feed additive to promote broiler health and enable the production of high-quality, DHA-enriched poultry.

Materials and methods

Ethics

All procedures employed in this study were reviewed and approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Tianjin Agricultural University (Approval No. 2025LLSC02), which is valid from January 5 to November 22, 2025, in strict accordance with principles of animal protection, welfare, and ethical conduct.

Broiler chickens and experimental design

This experiment utilized 120 Ross 308 broiler chickens and employed a single-factor, completely randomized block design. The birds were randomly assigned to four treatment groups (CON, L, M, and H), with six replicates per group and five birds per replicate. The control group (CON) received a basal diet, whereas the experimental groups (L, M, and H) were fed the same basal diet supplemented with Schizochytrium sp. powder(SP) at concentrations of 6.25, 9.375, and 12.5 g/kg, respectively (Table 1). The Schizochytrium sp. powder used in the experiment was sourced from Hebei Yuanda Jiu Fu Biotechnology Co., Ltd.(Bao Ding, China). Management practices

The experimental period spanned 7 weeks (49 days), with days 1–21 defined as the early phase and days 22–49 as the late phase. During the early phase, all treatment groups received a basal diet. In the late phase, the control group continued to receive the basal diet, whereas the experimental groups were fed the basal diet supplemented with graded levels of SP. The entire feeding trial was conducted indoors using multi-tiered cage systems. Prior to initiation of the experiment, the chicken house and cages were thoroughly cleaned and disinfected by spraying with a 1:2000 dilution of glutaraldehyde–bromomethylbenzene solution. The experiment commenced following a 3-day ventilation period. Throughout the trial, chickens had ad libitum access to both feed and water. Routine husbandry practices—including cage cleaning, disinfection, and vaccination—were strictly followed.

Growth performance measurement

During the trial period, accurately record the daily feed intake, residual feed quantity, and number of culled or deceased birds per replicate. Weigh birds prior to morning feeding each weekend, following an overnight fast (12 h) on days 21 and 49. Calculate total feed consumption for each experimental phase, as well as the average daily feed intake (ADFI), average daily gain (ADG), and feed-to-gain ratio (F/G).

Sample collection and serum biochemical analysis

On day 49 of the experiment, approximately 5 mL of blood was collected from the wing vein of one chicken per replicate. Blood samples were immediately transferred into centrifuge tubes and stored at room temperature in a tilted position to facilitate clot formation. Upon completion of all blood collections, samples were centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. Serum was carefully separated, aliquoted into cryovials, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Prior to biochemical assays, samples were thawed stepwise at 4°C followed by room temperature to minimize protein degradation. Serum triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), creatinine (CRE), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total protein (TP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), albumin (ALB), and glucose (GLU) were quantified using commercially available assay kits (Jiangxi Tekang Technology Co., Ltd.) on a fully automated clinical biochemistry analyzer.

Nutrient digestibility trial

The apparent digestibility of nutrients was determined using the endogenous indicator method (acid-insoluble ash). Fecal samples were collected from the broilers over a 5-day period (from 44 to 49 days of age). The collected samples were dried in an oven at 65°C for 4 hours, cooled in a desiccator, and weighed. This drying process (65°C for 30 minutes) was repeated, with cooling and weighing between cycles, until the weight change between two consecutive measurements was ≤0.1% of the sample mass, indicating constant dry weight. Both feed and the air-dried fecal samples were ground to a fine powder prior to chemical analysis.

The contents of moisture, crude nutrients, and minerals were determined according to the following standardized methods: Moisture (GB/T 6435-2014); crude fat by ether extraction (GB/T 6433-2006); crude ash by combustion in a muffle furnace (YAMATO FO410C; GB/T 6438-2007); crude protein by the Kjeldahl method using an automatic apparatus (K9840; GB/T 6432-2018); crude fiber using a fully automatic fiber analyzer (ANKOM A2000i; GB/T 6434-2022); calcium by potassium permanganate titration (GB/T 6436-2018); and phosphorus by spectrophotometry (GB/T 6437-2018).

Acid-insoluble ash (AIA) was used as the indigestible endogenous marker. The apparent digestibility of each nutrient was calculated using the following formula:Apparentdigestibility(%)=100−[(Indicatorinfeed/Indicatorinfeces)×(Nutrientinfeces/Nutrientinfeed)×100]

Intestinal morphology assessment

Following euthanasia in accordance with animal ethics protocols at 49 days of age, intestinal samples were collected. Segments measuring 2–3 centimeters of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were obtained and rinsed with saline. The samples were then fixed in 4% formaldehyde solution. They were immediately fixed in 4% formaldehyde solution and subsequently processed through paraffin embedding and sectioning. Tissue sections were prepared, and morphometric analysis was performed using the Revolve Pro software from Echo Laboratories, USA. The measurements obtained included villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD). The villus height to crypt depth ratio (VH:CD) was subsequently calculated based on these measurements.

Intestinal microbiota analysis

The composition of microbial communities within cecal contents was analyzed via high-throughput sequencing targeting the 16S rRNA gene. Genomic DNA was isolated using the TGuide S96 Magnetic Bead-Based Soil/Fecal DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China), following the recommended procedures provided by the manufacturer. DNA integrity was verified by 1.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentration and purity (A260/A280 ratio) were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Only samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 and clear electrophoretic bands were subjected to further amplification.

The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified via PCR using high-quality DNA as the template and the primer pair 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), both containing Illumina adapter sequences. Each 20 µL reaction contained 5–50 ng of DNA, 0.3 µL of each primer (10 µM), 5 µL of KOD FX Neo Buffer, 2 µL of 2 mM dNTPs, 0.2 µL of KOD FX Neo Polymerase, and ddH₂O to volume. The thermal profile consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min; 20 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 50°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 40 s; and a final extension at 72°C for 7 min. PCR amplicons were purified with the Omega DNA Purification Kit (Omega Inc., USA) and quantified/qualified on a Qsep‑400 Biofragment Analyzer (BiOptic Inc., ROC). Equal molar concentrations of the purified PCR products were combined and sequenced using 2 × 250 bp paired-end technology on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at Beijing BGI Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

The raw sequencing data obtained were analyzed using the following bioinformatics workflow: (1) Sequence processing and OTU clustering: Quality-controlled sequences were clustered using USEARCH (v10) software, grouping sequences with similarity above 97% into operational taxonomic units (OTUs). (2) Species taxonomic annotation: The naive Bayes classifier within the QIIME2 platform was employed to annotate the representative sequence of each OTU with taxonomic information, using the SILVA database (Release 138.1) as reference. The confidence threshold was set at 70%. (3) Diversity Analysis: Calculated Chao1 index, Shannon index, and other metrics for each sample within QIIME2 to assess alpha diversity of microbial communities. Computed beta diversity of microbial composition between samples based on Bray-Curtis distance matrix, visualized using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). (4) Significance Analysis: One-way ANOVA was used to compare differences in alpha diversity indices and species abundance among treatment groups. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was further applied, using a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score threshold of ≥4.0 to identify significantly different biomarker taxonomic units between groups.

Statistical analysis

Growth performance, serum biochemical parameters, apparent digestibility of nutrients, and intestinal villus height to crypt depth ratio data were analyzed using the one-way ANOVA procedure in SPSS 26 statistical software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 indicates significant differences, while P < 0.01 indicates highly significant differences. Results are presented as "mean ± standard error."

Discussion

Growth performance

Dietary supplementation with Schizochytrium powder at different inclusion levels did not affect ADG or FCR of broilers throughout the entire growth period. This finding is not fully consistent with the growth-promoting effects of DHA and EPA reported by Kalia et al. and Jeon et al. (Kalia et al., 2023; Jeon et al., 2022), but it aligns with the observations of Wahid et al. during the early phase (d 1–21), suggesting that such effects may be breed- or stage-dependent (Wahid et al., 2023). Notably, in the later phase, the high-dose group showed significantly lower ADFI, whereas FCR numerically decreased. This may partly reflect reduced diet palatability caused by Schizochytrium powder; however, the lower feed intake did not compromise weight gain, implying that DHA intake may have partially offset the shortfall by improving intestinal morphology and digestive function. Furthermore, Yulianto et al. reported that crude fish oil markedly improved broiler growth performance, a response not observed here, likely because the fatty acid composition of Schizochytrium powder differs from that of fish oil (Yulianto et al., 2020).

Serum biochemical

Serum triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL, and HDL did not differ significantly among treatments. This contrasts with reports that fish oil lowers LDL and raises HDL in broilers (Aghaei et al., 2012; Branko et al., 2019), a discrepancy likely arising from differences in fatty acid composition between Schizochytrium powder and fish oil. Although more than 90% of the DHA in both sources exists as triglycerides (Makay et al., 2026), their overall fatty acid profiles and accompanying lipid components differ markedly, which may influence DHA absorption and metabolism. Meanwhile, serum total protein was significantly elevated in the low-dose group. This elevation is unlikely to indicate liver damage, as serum transaminases (AST, ALT) remained normal and liver injury typically reduces rather than raises total protein (Yavaş et al., 2025). Considered together with the pronounced increases in duodenal and jejunal villus height and the improved crude protein digestibility in the same group, the higher total protein more likely reflects a stimulation of hepatic protein synthesis driven by enhanced intestinal absorption.

Nutrient digestibility and intestinal morphology

Improved intestinal morphology is a key determinant of apparent nutrient digestibility. Greater villus height expands the absorptive surface area, and shallower crypt depth reflects accelerated epithelial turnover and active mucosal function, together providing the structural basis for efficient nutrient uptake (Wang et al., 2025). In this study, duodenal and jejunal villus height and the villus-to-crypt ratio were significantly higher in all treatment groups than in the control. Concurrently, crude protein digestibility increased significantly in the medium-dose group and crude fat digestibility improved in all treatment groups. These findings align with reports that fish oil and fish meal enhance digestibility (Mebratu et al., 2022; Moradi et al., 2024), further supporting a positive relationship between intestinal morphology and nutrient digestibility.

The improvement in intestinal morphology by Schizochytrium powder may involve both anti-inflammatory and microbiota-modulating actions of DHA. As an n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid, DHA exerts anti-inflammatory effects, Sun et al. showed that DHA markedly inhibits the intestinal gene expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in broilers (Sun et al., 2026), and Acosta et al. reported that n-3 PUFA exposure upregulates the tight junction protein ZO-1 and increases villus height (Acosta et al., 2024). Attenuated local inflammation helps maintain the balance between epithelial cell proliferation and apoptosis, thereby reducing inflammatory damage and manifesting as taller villi and shallower crypts (Chen et al., 2025). DHA may also indirectly promote villus growth by modulating the gut microbiota. Rehman et al. demonstrated in vitro that DHA increases total short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production and improves intestinal barrier integrity (Rehman et al., 2024), and Gheorghe et al. found that higher dietary n-3 PUFA intake significantly increases the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria (Gheorghe et al., 2022). Butyrate serves as a key energy source for intestinal epithelial cells and directly stimulates their proliferation and differentiation (Salvi and Cowles, 2021). These two mechanisms likely act synergistically: reduced inflammation creates a more stable intestinal environment for beneficial bacteria, while SCFA-producing bacteria further reinforce homeostasis through local anti-inflammatory effects of SCFAs. Notably, the improvement in digestibility was somewhat attenuated in the high-dose group, which may be associated with the marked decrease in feed intake and lipid peroxidation induced by high concentrations of unsaturated fatty acids.

Intestinal microbiota

The gut microbiota influences nutrient processing, metabolic milieu, and growth regulation (Hongyu et al., 2020), and host-microbe interactions are closely tied to health maintenance and immune homeostasis (Yugal et al., 2022). To determine the effect of Schizochytrium powder on the cecal microbiota, we analyzed the microbial community structure of cecal contents by 16S rRNA sequencing. Firmicutes and Bacteroidota dominated across all groups, consistent with Geier et al. and reflecting the core structure of the avian gut microbiota (Geier et al., 2009). Schizochytrium powder significantly altered microbial composition, the relative abundances of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobiota increased, whereas that of Bacteroidota decreased. This shift aligns with the trend observed by Jadhav et al. in broilers fed flaxseed oil (rich in n-3 PUFAs), suggesting DHA as a key driver (Vidya et al., 2025). At the genus level, the most notable change was a consistent increase in Akkermansia and Faecalibacterium in the treated groups. Akkermansia degrades mucin and strengthens the intestinal barrier (Willem, 2017), correlates positively with metabolic health (Ting et al., 2019), and is promoted by omega-3 PUFAs (Vidya et al., 2025). Faecalibacterium, a major butyrate producer, supports epithelial health through the anti-inflammatory action of butyrate, and its elevated abundance is associated with improved gut homeostasis and nutrient absorption (Fleming et al., 1991). Genera such as Romboutsia also increased and are considered markers of gut microbiota homeostasis (Yang et al., 2023). In the high-dose group, Lactobacillus abundance decreased compared with the control, which aligns with Shabani et al., for diets supplemented with fish by-product silage, likely due to competitive inhibition by the added lipids (Shabani et al., 2019). Importantly, this decline was not accompanied by the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria, indicating a healthy overall microbiota. In summary, Schizochytrium powder, through its high DHA content, optimized the cecal microbiota structure by enriching beneficial bacteria such as Akkermansia and Faecalibacterium. This microbial optimization may indirectly contribute to improved intestinal morphology by strengthening the intestinal barrier and reducing inflammation, ultimately benefiting intestinal health.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size may lack power to detect small differences in growth performance; however, it was chosen to minimize animal use and remained sufficient to capture larger treatment effects on intestinal morphology and apparent nutrient digestibility. Second, the cost of Schizochytrium powder is high, and under current market conditions, its use solely as a growth promoter is not economically advantageous. Yet, under the same experimental conditions, the powder markedly improved meat antioxidant properties and tenderness. If this quality advantage commands a market premium, it could offset the higher feed cost. Whether such a balance is achievable ultimately depends on consumer awareness of DHA-enriched meat and the level of market premiums, and requires further systematic validation.

Conclusions

Under the conditions of this study, dietary supplementation with 9.375 g/kg Schizochytrium powder in 4- to 7-week-old broilers significantly enhanced the apparent digestibility of crude fat and crude protein, improved intestinal health, and had no adverse effects on growth performance. These findings suggest that Schizochytrium powder may serve as a potential feed additive for broiler production.
