Activation of mTOR pathway by human cytomegalovirus promoting host ribosomal protein expression by coordinated transcriptional and translational controls
Authors: Yang Li, Zhongwei Zhao, Xiangjing Meng, Yujing Huang, Te Qi, Ying Qi, Zhongyang Liu, Qiang Ruan, Yanping Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman cytomegalovirus (HCMV) profoundly reprograms host transcription and RNA metabolism, yet its impact on transcription start site (TSS) regulation of host genes remains poorly understood. Here, we employed NanoCap Analysis of Gene Expression sequencing (NanoCAGE-seq) to investigate HCMV-driven changes in alternative TSS usage across the host transcriptome. We identified widespread TSS switching, with ribosomal protein genes (RPGs) emerging as a highly enriched category. Alternative TSS usage produced isoforms with distinct 5’untranslated regions (UTRs), thereby altering cis-regulatory elements that shape translational efficiency. Integrative transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed a paradoxical accumulation of RPG proteins despite transcriptional downregulation during infection. Using 5’ Rapid Amplification of cDNA Ends (5’RACE), we characterized four RPGs of RPL4, RPS11, RPS23, and RPS24 that generated 5’UTR variants through alternative TSS usage. Notably, isoforms containing a 5’terminal oligopyrimidine (5’TOP) motif were significantly enriched, correlating with mTOR activation induced by HCMV. Functional assays with bicistronic reporter constructs in HEK293 cells and infection models in human embryonic lung fibroblasts demonstrated that the RPL4 5’TOP isoform exhibited enhanced mTORC1-driven translation compared with non-5’TOP counterparts. Importantly, RPL4 upregulation facilitated viral protein synthesis and boosted production of infectious virions. Together, our findings reveal that dynamic TSS switching of RPGs provides a simple, yet effective, mechanism for fine-tuning mTORC1-responsive translation. By co-opting host transcriptional and translational programs, HCMV enhances ribosome function to optimize the cellular environment for productive viral replication.

Introduction

Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV), a member of the β-herpesvirus family, establishes lifelong latency in the host while remaining an important pathogen in various populations. In immunocompromised individuals, such as transplant recipients and AIDS patients, reactivation of HCMV can lead to severe multi-organ diseases [1,2]. Additionally, congenital HCMV infection is the leading viral cause of birth defects, including sensorineural hearing loss and microcephaly [3–5]. Despite extensive research over the past forty years, the molecular mechanisms by which HCMV hijacks host cellular machinery are still not fully understood.

Transcriptomic analyses have provided insights into the global changes in host mRNA expression level during HCMV infection [6,7]. However, conventional RNA sequencing approaches are limited in their ability to detect key aspects of mammalian gene expression. The complexities of this expression arise from alternative RNA processing mechanisms, including alternative splicing and the dynamic selection of transcription start sites (TSS) and polyadenylation sites [8–11]. Recent studies emphasize that alternative selection of TSS is a major regulatory mechanism that contributes to protein isoform diversity and affects mRNA properties critical for nuclear export, stability, and translation [10–12]. Emerging studies have identified intra-promoter TSS switching as a non-canonically regulatory mechanism for mRNA transcriptional initiation [12–17]. This process generates mRNA isoforms with distinct 5’-terminal architectures, enabling context‑dependent gene regulation. Yet, how HCMV exploits this plasticity to optimize viral replication remains unknown.

Translational reprogramming of host cell is a key feature of herpesvirus infections, with HCMV demonstrating a strong capacity to manipulate host ribosomes to support viral gene expression [18]. Ribosomes can exhibit functional heterogeneity resulting from environment induced variations in ribosomal protein (RP) composition, a feature that viruses may utilize to preferentially promote viral mRNA translation [19,20]. HCMV infection has been shown to alter both the abundance and translation efficiency of certain RPs [19]. However, several important questions remain: (1) What mechanisms does HCMV utilize to coordinate the regulation of RPs? (2) Are HCMV induced changes in RP composition passive consequences or active processes that facilitate viral replication? (3) How do TSS dynamics interact with translational reprogramming during viral infection?

In the current study, we employed NanoCap Analysis of Gene Expression (NanoCAGE) [21], with deep sequencing to probe how HCMV reprograms alternative TSS usage in host genes at the level of transcript isoforms. Our findings reveal extensive HCMV-induced TSS switching throughout the host transcriptome, with RPGs identified as a prominently enriched functional category. Through isoform-resolved analysis, we characterize four distinct RPGs, including RPL4, RPS11, RPS23, and RPS24, that generate variant mRNA isoforms via alternative TSS usage upon HCMV infection. HCMV selectively promotes the enrichment and translational activation of 5’ terminal oligopyrimidine (5’TOP)-containing RPG isoforms in permissive HELF cells. This effect requires productive HCMV infection, depends on cellular context, and is not observed during HSV-1 infection, suggesting that polypyrimidine initiator (TCT) -directed RPG isoform switching reflects a context-dependent feature of HCMV infection rather than a general herpesvirus response. Functional analyses demonstrate that HCMV-induced upregulation of RPL4 protein directly enhances viral protein synthesis and promotes the production of infectious virions. These findings elucidate a novel mechanism by which HCMV co-opts mTOR signaling pathways to rewire the host translational machinery through selective TSS usage, thereby effectively optimizing the cellular environment for viral replication.

Materials and method

Cells and viruses

Human embryonic lung fibroblast (HELF) cells and T98G glioblastoma cell line (ATCC CRL-1690) were obtained from Wuhan Institute of Virology. HELF cells, T98G cells, HEK293 cells, and 293T cells were cultured in Modified Eagle’s Medium (MEM, HyClone), supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. When cells reached confluence, the medium was replaced with MEM containing 2% FBS to limit proliferation, as reduced serum conditions are commonly used to maintain cells in a relatively quiescent state while minimizing mitogenic signaling [22]. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. HCMV clinical strain HAN was isolated from a urine sample of a congenitally HCMV-infected infant at the Affiliated Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University, Shenyang, China (KJ426589.1). The bacterial artificial chromosome of HCMV HAN strain (HAN BAC) was constructed and labeled with green fluorescent protein (GFP) under the SV40 promoter as previously described [23]. The HCMV Towne BAC strain was generously provided by Dr. Yongjun Yu (United States). Herpes Simplex Virus type-1(HSV-1) KOS strain was provided by Prof. Hongyu Deng of The State Key Laboratory of Biomacromolecules, Institute of Biophysics Chinese Academy of Sciences.

For virus purification, infected cells and culture supernatant were collected and centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 30 min at room temperature. The clarified supernatant was retained, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL antibiotic-free, serum-free MEM and subjected to sonication (10 cycles of 10 s each) to release cell-associated virions. The cell lysate was vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature; the resulting supernatant was combined with the previously collected culture supernatant and transferred to ultracentrifuge tubes. One-sixth volume of ice-cold D-sorbitol buffer was carefully underlaid using a long needle to form a cushion. Virions were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 55,000 × g for 60 min at 20°C. The purified viral pellet was resuspended in antibiotic-free, serum-free DMEM. The viral titer of the viral stock was titrated by 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) as described before [24]. The purified virions were subsequently stored at −80°C until required for experimentation.

UV irradiation of virus

HCMV HAN BAC strain was UV-inactivated by exposure to 6000 J/m2 of irradiation in UVP Crosslinker (Analytik Jena) for three times. Sodium pyruvate (5 Mm final concentration) was added to the inoculum immediately after irradiation.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University (approval number:2025PS321K). The ethics committee waived the requirement for informed consent for the following reasons: (1) patient information was anonymized; (2) the urine sample was collected and stored as part of routine diagnostic laboratory procedures, with no impact on patient care. The authors confirm that patient data confidentiality was maintained. This study adhered to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration.

RNA and protein preparation

Total RNAs were extracted from cell cultures with TRIzol™ (Thermo Fisher). In accordance with the manufacturer’s instruction, RQ1 DNase (Promega) was utilized to eliminate any contaminating DNA. For preparation of protein samples, the cell cultures underwent trypsinization and were subsequently pelleted following a rinse with PBS. Total cellular protein was extracted by adding an equal volume of Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (M-PER™, ThermoFisher), which contained a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (ThermoFisher). The protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instruction.

NanoCAGE-sequencing

Confluent HELF cells were cultured in MEM medium supplemented with 2% FBS overnight and were infected with HCMV HAN BAC strain at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 3, concurrently, treated with equal-volume DMSO to serve as a mock-infected control. HELF cells were harvested at 12 and 72 hours post infection (hpi) for subsequent analysis. One μg total RNAs extracted from HCMV HAN BAC-infected 12 and 72 hpi (referred to as HCMV-12 hpi and HCMV-72 hpi) and Mock-infected HELF cells were prepared for nanoCAGE-sequencing, respectively. Ribosome-depleted RNA is reverse transcribed with randomized hexamer harboring a 3’-adaptor sequence overhang. Subsequently, the 5’adaptor harboring three additional rG at its 3’end was added to the RT reaction, prompting the enzyme to switch templates and add the 5’adaptor sequence to the end of the newly synthesized cDNA. cDNA amplification was performed using the standard Illumina P5 and P7 indexing primers provided in the Illumina TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit. These universal primers anneal to the ligated adapter sequences to enrich the library fragments and incorporate sample-specific index sequences for multiplexed sequencing. PCR products corresponding to 200–500 bp were purified, quantified and applied to Illunima HiSeq X Ten system for 150 nucleotides paired-end sequencing. The CAGEfightR R package was used to identify TSSs and TCs [25]. The CAGE detected TSS (CTSS) positions with adjacent CTSS falling within 20 nt were clustered into TCs with the highest expression level CTSS as the position of the TC. Then, the TCs from all samples are aggregated into a single set of non-overlapping consensus clusters as described by Haberle V et.al [25]. This produces more robust boundaries of the promoter region and captures all transcription initiation associated with a single gene. The samples collected and data processed for nanoCAGE-seg were during the period from November 2019 to December 2020.

5’rapid amplification of cDNA ends (5’RACE) followed by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) analysis and cloning-based sequencing

The 5’RACE procedure was performed according to the SMARTer™ RACE 5’Kit protocol (Takara), using total RNAs extracted from HELF cells infected at an MOI of 3 with HCMV HAN BAC, UV-inactivated HCMV HAN BAC, or Towne BAC strains, as well as from mock-infected HELF cells. In addition, total RNAs from T98G cells infected with HCMV HAN BAC (MOI = 5) for 72 hours, and from HELF and 293 cells infected with HSV-1 KOS (MOI = 10) for 72 hours were also subjected to 5’RACE analysis. Nested gene-specific primers (GSPs) for the 5’ RACE nest PCR reactions were designed based on sequence information obtained from RNA-seq datasets, employing Primer Premier 5.0 software. All of GSPs were listed in Table S1. The amplification products were purified using DNA and a gel extraction kit (Tiangen), and then 1ug DNA products were loaded onto 12% polyacrylamide gels. The electrophoresis was carried out at room temperature for 30 min at 80 V, afterward for 75 min at 110 V. Then, the gels were stained through Ethidium Bromide staining. The purified amplification products from HCMV HAN BAC-infected HELF cells were also ligated into pCR2.1 vector (TaKaRa), and propagated in Escherichia coli DH5α. The positive colonies were sequenced by Sangon Co., Ltd.

Chemical treatment

Mock-infected or HCMV HAN BAC-infected HELF cells were treated with 3BDO (MCE) at a final concentration of 120 nM to activate mTOR, with Torin1 (MCE) at a final concentration of 10 nM to inhibit mTOR, and with actinomycin D (MCE) at a final concentration of 1 μg/ml to inhibit mRNA synthesis.

RNA-sequencing

The total RNAs from two replicate preparations of HCMV HAN BAC-72 hpi and Mock-infected HELF cells, were used for RNA-sequencing. For each sample, 1 μg of total RNA was used for RNA-seq library preparation. Polyadenylated mRNAs were purified and concentrated with oligo(dT)-conjugated magnetic beads (Invitrogen) before being used for directional RNA-seq library preparation. Then reverse transcription was performed with randomized hexamer primer harboring 3’adaptor sequence. The cDNAs were purified and amplified and PCR products corresponding to 200–500 bps were purified, quantified and applied to Illunima HiSeq X Ten system for 150 nucleotides paired-end sequencing. The samples collected and data processed for RNA-seq were during the period from August 2023 to December 2023.

Quantitative real-time PCR array

Totally, 1 μg of the total RNA extracted from HCMV HAN BAC-72 hpi or Mock-infected HELF cells was subjected to mRNA reverse transcription using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Takara) to generate cDNA. Gene expression analysis of 84 ribosomal protein genes (RPGs) was performed using the cDNA and a human cytoplasmic ribosomal protein PCR array (Wcgene Biotech). Reactions were carried out with the QuantiNova SYBR Green Kit (QIAGEN) on a QuantStudio™ 7500 system (Applied Biosystems). To ensure precision, the mean CT values of three housekeeping genes (18S, ACTB, and GAPDH) were used as the reference CT value for normalization. The quantitative expression levels were analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method. A total of three independent experimental replicates were executed, and the findings are reported as means.

Label-free quantitative proteomics mass spectrometry (LFQ/MS) and parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) analyses

The protein samples of HCMV HAN BAC-72 hpi and Mock-infected HELF cells were analyzed by LFQ/MS on a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) equipped with a Nanospray Flex source (Thermo Fisher). Also, the protein samples of HCMV HAN BAC-72 hpi and Mock-infected HELF cells were used for targeted PRM analysis through liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), involving the examination of specific peptide segments of 18 RPGs (Table S2). Desalted peptides were loaded into a Q ExactiveTM Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher). Raw PRM data were analyzed using Skyline software (v22.2.0.312) to extract the peak areas of the target precursors. A total of three independent PRM analyses were executed, and the findings are reported as means. The samples collected and data processed for LFQ/MS and PRM were during the period from August 2023 to December 2023.

Plasmids construction

Based on the RPL4 5’RACE clone sequencing results, two pairs of complementary single-stranded DNA sequences of 5’TOP-containing and non-5’TOP-containing RPL4 5’UTR isoforms, with HindIII and EcoRI restriction enzyme sites at 5’ and 3’ termini, respectively, were synthesized by Sangon Biotech company (Table S1). The complementary single-stranded DNA sequences were annealed at a temperature of 65°C for 10 minutes, followed by a gradual cooling period to room temperature over the course of 2 hours. Two reporter plasmids, TOP/wi-5’RPL4-UL69-Flag-CMV14 and TOP/wo-5’RPL4-UL69-Flag-CMV14, were constructed respectively by ligating the two double-stranded DNA sequences of RPL4 5’UTR isoforms into a previously constructed transient expression plasmid of pCMV14-UL69-FLAG plasmid through HindIII and EcoRI restriction enzyme sites (Takara), which contains the HCMV UL69-FLAG fusion protein gene.

Two lentiviral constructs of bicistronic systems encoding dual-luciferase reporter genes of EGFP and mCherry were constructed using pLJM1-EGFP (Addgene plasmid) as the founder plasmid. Firstly, two DNA fragments of 5’TOP-containing and non-5’TOP-containing RPL4 5’UTR isoforms, flanked by 15-nt homology arms were synthesized, respectively. To eliminate the original TSS in the pLJM1-EGFP, the upstream homology arm was designed to match the CMV promoter excluding the six nucleotides (5’ ACCGTC-3’) at the 3’terminal of the CMV promoter sequence according to the CMV promoter sequence described by van den Elzen et al [12]. In the 5’TOP-containing RPL4 5’UTR isoform, we further engineered a critical substitution: the adenine at the internal TSS of the non-TOP 5’UTR was replaced with thymine, a nucleotide exhibiting the lowest transcriptional initiation probability [12]. The synthesized DNA fragments comprised three functional components: i. A truncated CMV promoter (Δ6nt at 3’end) as the upstream homology; ii. Modified RPL4 5’UTR isoforms (with or without 5’TOP); iii. Downstream homology arm matching EGFP coding sequence (Table S1). These constructs were subsequently integrated between the truncated CMV promoter and EGFP coding sequence through homologous recombination using the In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Takara). This modification simultaneously achieved promoter mutagenesis and TOP-containing 5’UTR insertion while maintaining vector integrity.

Transient transfection and normalization of transfection efficiency

HEK293 cells were transiently transfected with equimolar amounts of either TOP/wi-5’RPL4-UL69-Flag-CMV14 or TOP/wo-5’RPL4-UL69-Flag-CMV14 according to the same Lipofectamine 2000transfection protocol (Invitrogen). To assess transfection efficiency, parallel samples were collected after transfection for quantification of intracellular plasmid DNA. Total cellular DNA was extracted from transfected cells, and the relative abundance of each transfected plasmid was determined by real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) using QuantiNova SYBR Green Kit (QIAGEN) with primers specific to the UL69 open reading frame (Table S1). The qPCR-based measurement of intracellular plasmid DNA was used to verify comparable plasmid uptake between groups.

Lentivirus production and generation of stable transfected HEK293 or HELF cell lines

Lentiviruses were produced by co-transfection of 293T cells with the original or one of the modified pLJM1-EGFP plasmids (4 μg), with psPAX2(4 μg, Addgene) and pMD2.G(4 μg, Addgene) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Lentivirus supernatant was harvested twice at 48 and 72 hpi, filtered through a 0.45-mm filter, and then added to sub-confluent HEK293 or HELF cells with Polybrene (4 μg/ml; Beyotime) at 5 µg/ml. Selection with 1 µg/ml puromycin (Sangon) was performed until no viable cells remained on the control cells (generally 3 to 5 days). Puromycin-resistant cells were pooled, seeded at an equal density, and harvested 72 hours post plating for being verified by Western blot.

shRNA stable knockdown cell lines

The DNA sequences encoding shRNAs targeting RPL4 and corresponding control shRNAs were chemically synthesized (Table S1, Sangon), and cloned into the pLKO.1 lentiviral backbone (Addgene) by Age I & EcoR I (Takara). To produce shRNA lentiviruses, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the shRPL4 or shNC pLKO.1, the PsPax2 envelope, and the VSV-G packaging plasmids using Fugene 6 (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The supernatant containing lentiviral particles was harvested daily for the next 3 days, centrifuged at 500 g for 10 min, and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter to remove cell debris. The lentiviral particles were concentrated by PEG8000 (Thermo Fisher) and resuspended in cell culture medium. For infection of HELF cells, 1 × 105 cells were seeded in T25 cell culture bottle and co-infected the next day with shRPL4 or shNC viruses in medium supplemented with polybrene (4 μg/ml; Beyotime). Cells were grown until confluency and then added puromycin (1 μg/ ml; Sangon). Un-infected HELF cells were used to determine the minimal antibiotic concentrations that resulted in lethality within 2 to 5 days. Successful RPL4 knockdown in the HELF-shRPL4 cell lines was validated by Western blot.

Cell viability and apoptosis analyses

Cell viability analysis was performed with Cell Titer 96® Aqueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (Promega). HELF-shRPL4 cells and HELF-shNC cells were seeded in 96-well plates and infected with HCMV HAN BAC strain (MOI = 1) or mock infected with DMSO. Post 48 hours of infection, 5 mg/ml MTS solution was directly added into medium and incubated for 24 hours. Absorbance at wavelengths of 490 nm was measured. Relative viabilities of HELF-shRPL4 cells were calculated by comparing the absorbance measured and using HELF-shNC cells as control. Cell apoptosis analysis was performed with Annexin V-EGFP/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (KeyGEN). HELF-shRPL4 cells and HELF-shNC cells were seeded in 6-well plates and infected with HCMV HAN BAC strain (MOI = 1) or mock infected with DMSO. Post 72 hours of infection, 5 ul Annexin V-EGFP and 5 ul propidium iodide diluted with 500 ul binding buffer was mixed and added to the samples for 30 min in the dark. The samples were analyzed under a BD FACSCaliburTM Flow Cytometer.

Western blotting

Western blotting procedure, proteins were denatured by boiling and then separated via electrophoresis on 8 ~ 12% polyacrylamide gels before being transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck). The primary antibodies utilized in this study included anti-EGFP (Abcam), anti-mCherry (Abcam), anti-RPL4 (Abcam), and anti-GAPDH (Abcam), anti-beta-actin (Abcam), anti-IE (Abcam), anti-pp52(Santa), anti-pp28 (Santa). The blots were subsequently probed with secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Millipore). Reactive bands were detected by ChemiDocTMTouch Imaging System (Bio-rad).

Microscopy

Fluorescence microscopy was performed on HELF cells transduced with either pLJM1-TOP/wi-5’RPL4-EGFP or the pLJM1-EGFP control. Images were acquired using an inverted Nikon Eclipse Ti-S fluorescence microscope fitted with a Nikon DS‑Ri2 digital camera and a 20× objective lens.

Titration of the virions in both supernatants and cells

HELF-shRPL4 or control lentivirus were infected with HCMV HAN BAC at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.5. After 6 h of incubation, medium was removed, the cells were rinsed with PBS for three times, and fresh medium was added. Supernatants together with infected cells were collected at 72, 96, 120 and 144 hpi and were stored at −80°C until they were assayed. Infectious virus titers were determined by TCID50. HELF cells were resuspended in growth medium at a concentration of 2 × 105 cells/mL, and were seeded into 96-well plates (100 μL per well). The following day, Supernatant and cell mixture samples after 10-fold serial dilutions were added into each well in a row of the 96-well plates of cells and were cultured for approximately 14 days. GFP-positive wells were counted and the corresponding titer number was found using the TCID50 chart. An average titer was derived from three independent experiments.

Statistical analysis

Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were performed using SPSS 19.0 (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) to evaluate differences in TSS distributions between HCMV-infected and mock-infected HELF cells. The analyses included nanoCAGE-seq-based comparisons of TSS shifting at 12 and 72 hpi relative to mock infection, as well as comparisons of 5’RACE clone-sequencing-derived TSS distributions within the relevant 5’UTR regions of RPL4, RPS11, RPS23, and RPS24 between 72 hpi and mock-infected samples. For re-analyzing PRO-Cap data (GEO: GSE113394) from Parida et al [26], the same test was used to compare RPG TSS distributions between HCMV-infected cells at 96 hpi and control cells.

The statistical analyses for western blot, RT-qPCR, fluorescence reporter assays, cell viability assays, apoptosis assays, and viral growth assays were done using GraphPad Prism 9.4.1 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SD from independent biological replicates, unless otherwise indicated. For most validation experiments, n = 3 independent biological replicates were used. For comparisons between two matched experimental groups, a two-tailed paired Student’s t-test was performed. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was used, followed by the indicated post hoc multiple-comparisons test when appropriate. For time-course viral growth analyses, two-way ANOVA was performed to evaluate the effects of experimental group, time, and their interaction, followed by multiple-comparisons analysis between groups at each time point when applicable. When non-parametric analysis was required, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the association between paired transcriptomic and proteomic log2 fold-change values. Exact p values are reported to three decimal places for paired Student’s t-tests and Kruskal–Wallis tests, whereas values below 0.001 are reported as p < 0.001. For one-way and two-way ANOVA analyses with multiple comparisons, p values are summarized using significance thresholds where appropriate. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Alterations in TSS dynamics of RPGs post HCMV infection in HELF cells

To investigate the TSS alterations induced by HCMV infection in HELF cells, we conducted deep nanoCAGE-seq of total RNAs extracted from the HELF cells infected for both 12 and 72 hours with the HCMV HAN BAC strain, which has been constructed and labeled with green fluorescent protein (GFP) as previously described [23], and from mock-infected control cells. The sequencing reads were aligned to the human GRCh38 reference mismatches. The total and uniquely mapped reads to either the human or HCMV genomes are detailed in Table S3. Only the uniquely mapped reads, referred to as CAGE tags, were retained for subsequent analysis. In the HCMV-12 hpi sample, we detected 8,815,105 human-derived CAGE tags and 428,315 HCMV-derived CAGE tags, corresponding to 95.37% and 4.63% of the total CAGE tags, respectively. In the HCMV-72 hpi sample, human-derived CAGE tags decreased to 7,098,916, while HCMV-derived CAGE tags increased substantially to 4,395,844, accounting for 61.75% and 38.25% of the total, respectively. These data indicate a pronounced increase in the viral transcriptional contribution at the late stage of infection. The nucleotide positions mapped by the 5’ends of CAGE tags were designated as CTSSs with the mapping frequency of individual CTSS quantified as tags per million (tpm). Within the CAGE tags, we identified 287,432, 224,206 and 292,294 CTSSs associated with human genes in HCMV-12 hpi, HCMV-72 hpi and mock-infected samples, respectively. These CTSSs were organized into transcript clusters (TCs) as introduced in method section, with the highest expression level CTSS designated as the position of the TC. In total, 22,051, 20,532 and 26,407 TCs of human genes were generated for HCMV-12 hpi, HCMV-72 hpi and mock-infected samples, respectively. These TCs were further consolidated into non-overlapping consensus clusters representing core promoters. The analysis of alternative TSS usage within a consensus cluster was performed by comparing the tpm values of TCs across the two experimental conditions.

A total of 3,403 and 2,856 consensus clusters showed significant alterations in predominant TSS positions in the HCMV 12 hpi and 72 hpi samples, respectively, relative to mock-infected controls, as determined by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (p < 0.05). Notably, 927 consensus clusters exhibited concordant shifts in predominant TSS positions in both 12 hpi and 72 hpi infected conditions relative to the mock-infected controls (Table S4, S5). These findings indicate that infection with HCMV induces extensive shifts in the TSSs of host cell genes.

Genes exhibiting TSS shifts in HCMV-infected cells at both infected conditions, compared with mock-infected controls, were predominantly involved in ribosomal structure and protein synthesis, as indicated by Gene Ontology (GO) analysis. Consistently, KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of these genes in the ribosome pathway (Figure 1(A, B)). Focusing further on RPGs, we observed that, relative to mock-infected controls, 52 RPGs exhibited predominant TSS shifts of 9–122 nt in both HCMV 12 hpi and 72 hpi samples, among which 30 RPGs showed concordant displacement patterns between the two infection time points (Table S4, S5). Notably, relative to the mock-infected sample, 12 RPGs displayed the same alternative TSS usage pattern in both HCMV-infected samples, characterized by a shift toward a TCT motif, a previously recognized non-canonical TSS motif also known as the polypyrimidine initiator, whereas three additional RPGs exhibited this TCT-directed pattern only in the HCMV-72 hpi sample (Table 1). Importantly, these alterations in the 5’UTRs maintained the coding sequences of these RPGs intact, the emergence of TCT derived transcripts resulted in the formation of a 5’ TOP motif at the 5’termini of mRNA (Table 1). The conserved 5’TOP motif has been defined as a + 1 cytosine (C) followed by a series of pyrimidines and transcribed from the less common DNA initiation motif of TCT, which confers mRNA translation sensitivity to the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling pathway [27]. Alternative TSS usage associated with infection-induced emergence of 5’TOP motifs in 15 RPGs in HCMV-infected 12 or 72 hpi samples compared with mock-infected samples.

To provide orthogonal validation of HCMV-induced TSS shifts of RPGs, we re-analyzed the raw PRO-Cap data (GEO: GSE113394) from Parida et al [26], generated from Human Foreskin Fibroblasts (HFF) infected with HCMV Towne strain at 96 hpi and mock-infected HFF, which maps the 5’-capped ends of nascent RNA polymerase II transcripts at nucleotide resolution and is independent of downstream RNA stability. Normalized PRO-Cap TSS distribution profiles were generated for all 52 TSS-shifted RPGs identified by our nanoCAGE-seq and systematically compared with the corresponding nanoCAGE-seq results. Of the 52 RPGs examined, 41 (78.85%) displayed PRO-Cap evidence of infection-induced TCT-directed redistribution of nascent transcription initiation (Table S6) (p < 0.05, determined by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test). Notably, among the 15 RPGs listed in Table 1, with the exception of RPS29, which showed concordant TCT-directed shifts in both the HCMV-infected 12 hpi and 72 hpi nanoCAGE-seq samples, and RPS20 and RPS24, which showed TCT-directed shifts only in the HCMV-infected 72 hpi nanoCAGE-seq sample, the remaining 12 RPGs exhibited infection-induced, concordant repositioning toward the TCT motif in the PRO-Cap analysis. Comparative analysis of TSS distributions for these 15 RPGs in PRO-Cap datasets from HFFs infected with the HCMV Towne strain at 96 hpi and mock-infected HFFs is presented in Fig S1_1-5.

To further validate these findings, we performed 5’RACE for four RPGs, RPL4, RPS11, RPS23, and RPS24, using HCMV HAN BAC-infected HELF cells at 12, 24, and 72 hpi and the corresponding mock-infected controls. These genes showed prominent infection-induced TSS shifts toward the TCT motif in the nanoCAGE-seq data, a pattern that was further supported by PRO-Cap analysis for RPL4, RPS11, and RPS23; however, RPS24 showed a slight reduction in TCT motif-associated TSS usage after infection in the PRO-Cap dataset. 5’RACE was applied to poly (A)-selected RNA, although nanoCAGE-seq was performed using rRNA-depleted total RNA. These approaches may capture partially overlapping but not identical RNA populations. Nevertheless, because the RPG transcripts examined here are well-characterized, predominantly polyadenylated, and highly abundant mRNAs, this difference in RNA input is unlikely to substantially affect the qualitative assessment of 5’-end usage, although the 5’RACE results should be interpreted as supportive rather than strictly quantitative validation of the nanoCAGE-seq findings. For this analysis, we designed GSPs that were positioned approximately 150-nt downstream of the dominant TSSs of the RPGs in infected HELF cells identified in nanoCAGE-seq (Figure 1(C) and Table S1). The PAGE results of the nested 5’RACE PCR products revealed the presence of two or multiple amplification products falling below the 150 nt for each RPG. Notably, for all four RPGs analyzed, the proportion of the ~150-nt fragment among the detected transcript fragments was significantly increased in HELF cells infected with HCMV for 12, 24, or 72 hours compared with mock-infected HELF cells (Figure 1(D)). Additionally, the heightened relative abundance of the ~150-nt fragment in comparison to other shorter fragments was further corroborated by 5’RACE analysis conducted on HELF cells infected with the HCMV Towne strain (MOI = 3) at 72 hpi (Figure 1(D)). In contrast, 5’RACE analysis of HELF cells infected with UV-inactivated HCMV HAN BAC (MOI = 3) for 24 hours showed no detectable changes in the relative abundance of 5’RACE products for any of the four RPGs compared with mock-infected cells (Figure 1(E)). This finding suggests that the observed redistribution of 5’RACE products is associated with HCMV gene expression and/or viral replication. To exclude the possibility that the altered relative abundance of these transcript species was caused by infection-induced changes in post-transcriptional mRNA degradation, HELF cells infected with HCMV HAN BAC (MOI = 3) or mock infected for 24 hours were treated with actinomycin D to inhibit mRNA synthesis, and total RNA was collected 6 hours later for 5’RACE analysis. Under these conditions, actinomycin D treatment did not affect the relative abundance of the ~150-nt fragment compared with the other transcript fragments in either HCMV-infected or mock-infected cells, indicating that the observed enrichment is unlikely to result from differential post-transcriptional decay (Figure 1(F)).

Given that both HELF and HFF are permissive for HCMV lytic infection, we next performed 5’RACE analysis of the same four RPGs in T98G glioblastoma cells infected with the HCMV HAN BAC strain (MOI = 5) at 72 hpi. T98G cells are considered semi-permissive for HCMV infection, exhibiting delayed viral gene expression, delayed replication-center maturation, low-level virus production, and continued division of viral antigen-positive cells [28]. Notably, compared with mock-infected HELF cells, mock-infected T98G cells already exhibited a higher relative abundance of the ~150-nt fragments relative to the shorter amplification products for all four RPGs analyzed. This pattern was especially pronounced for RPL4, for which the amplification product was almost exclusively the ~150-nt fragment. However, HCMV infection did not further increase the relative abundance of the ~150-nt fragments in T98G cells (Figure 1(G)).

To investigate whether this phenomenon also occurs in different cell types during HSV-1 infection, we performed 5’RACE analysis of the four RPGs in HELF and HEK293 cells infected with the HSV-1 KOS strain for 48 hours at an MOI of 10. In contrast to HCMV-infected HELF cells, HSV-1 infection did not induce a comparable shift in either HELF or HEK293 cells. The relative abundance of the ~150-nt fragment remained largely unchanged compared with the shorter fragments in both cell types (Figure 1(H)). A pattern similar to that observed in T98G cells was also detected in HEK293 cells: the 5’RACE product of RPL4 was composed almost exclusively of the ~150-nt band under both mock- and HSV-1-infected conditions (Figure 1(H)).

Subsequently, approximately 100 cDNA clones derived from the 5’RACE amplifications of either the HCMV HAN BAC-72 hpi or the mock-infected HELF cells were subjected to sequencing. The analysis of the sequencing data revealed the presence of multiple transcript isoforms characterized by alternative 5’UTRs within the four RPGs, which were observed in both the HCMV-72 hpi and mock-infected cells, with no splicing events detected in these 5’UTR regions. The ~150-nt amplification product for each RPGs was identified as the dominant isoform initiating from a TCT motif, which was consistent with that identified by nanoCAGE-seq. Because RPGs are associated with numerous annotated pseudogenes, some of which contain regions partially complementary to the 5’RACE inner primers used in this study (Table S7), we further evaluated whether the amplified products could have originated from pseudogene-derived transcripts. To address this possibility, we performed comparative sequence analyses by aligning (i) the reference sequences of the major transcripts of the four RPGs obtained from NCBI, (ii) representative high-abundance 5’RACE clones with and without TOP motifs, and (iii) the corresponding pseudogene sequences spanning from the primer-complementary regions to their annotated 5’ends. These analyses showed that both the TOP-containing and TOP-less 5’RACE products displayed 100% sequence identity to the annotated major RPG transcripts, whereas none exhibited complete sequence identity to any corresponding pseudogene sequence (Table S7, Fig. S2). These results indicate that the 5’RACE products analyzed here were derived from bona fide RPG transcripts rather than from pseudogene-derived sequences.

To further assess infection-associated changes in TSS usage, we compared TSS distributions within the promoter-proximal regions of these RPGs between HCMV HAN BAC-72 hpi and mock-infected HELF samples using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Notably, the dominant TCT-initiated isoform accounted for a significantly greater proportion among all of the sequenced clones in the HCMV-72 hpi sample compared to mock-infected sample (p < 0.05, determined by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) (see Figure 1(I) and Fig. S3). Collectively, these findings from the 5’RACE analysis support the observations made through nanoCAGE-seq and PAGE analyses, confirming the shifts in dominant TSSs toward TCT motifs in the RPGs following HCMV infection. These results indicate that RPGs in HELF cells utilize alternative TSSs to produce multiple mRNA isoforms. Importantly, HCMV infection appears to modulate the relative abundance of RPG transcript isoforms, specifically resulting in an enrichment of the 5’TOP-containing (TCT-initiated) isoform within the transcript isoforms of a subset of RPGs.

The shifting of RPG TSS induced by HCMV is mechanistically connected to mTOR activity

Recent studies have indicated that the PI3K-Akt-mTOR signaling plays a significant role in the modulation of alternative TSS usage in relation to 5’TOP and non-5’TOP mRNAs of colorectal cancer samples subjected to irradiation [12]. We sought to explore the potential linkage between mTOR activity and the dynamic redistribution of predominant TSSs of RPGs during HCMV infection, given the established activation of both mTORC1 and mTORC2 following HCMV infection [29,30]. To investigate this, confluent mock infected HELF cells were maintained in MEM containing 2% FBS to minimize growth factor, and nutrient-dependent activation of mTOR signaling while preserving cell viability and metabolic activity, and were then treated with the mTOR activator 3BDO (120 nM) for 72 hours or the mTOR inhibitor Torin 1 (10 nM) for 24 hours. The phosphorylation level of S6K1 protein was utilized as a marker for mTOR activity (Figure 2(A)). The shifting of TSSs in RPGs was assessed in these HELF cells through 5’RACE analysis of RPL4, RPS11, RPS23, and RPS24. PAGE results demonstrated that in the cells with 3BDO treatment, the relative abundance of the fragment corresponding to the 5’TOP containing isoform increased compared to that in the mock-infected cells, a pattern that was also observed in the cells with HCMV HAN BAC strain infection (Figure 2(B)). Conversely, treatment with Torin 1 resulted in a significant decrease in the relative abundance of the 5’TOP containing isoform compared to that of the mock-infected cells. Furthermore, HELF cells pretreated with 10 nM Torin 1 for 6 hours prior to infection and continuously maintained in 10 nM Torin 1 throughout the subsequent 24-hour HCMV infection exhibited a marked reversal in the enrichment pattern of 5’TOP-containing isoform fragments induced by HCMV infection alone for 24 hours (Figure 2(B)). All 5’RACE experiments were conducted in two independent biological replicates. These results suggested that HCMV infection increased the relative abundance of 5’TOP-containing isoform compared to other mRNA isoforms of the RPGs through mTOR activation. However, the observed effects could not be conclusively attributed to either mTORC1 or mTORC2 signaling, as both 3BDO and Torin 1 exhibit broad-spectrum modulation of both complexes. HCMV-mediated modulation of RPG TSS shifts is mechanistically linked to mTOR activity.

To further determine whether mTOR activity correlated with infection-associated changes in RPG TSS isoform usage, we examined S6K1 phosphorylation under infection conditions that did not induce detectable changes in the relative abundance of RPG TSS isoforms, including HELF cells infected with UV-inactivated HCMV HAN BAC (MOI = 3, 24hpi), T98G cells infected with HCMV HAN BAC (MOI = 5, 72hpi), and HELF or HEK293 cells infected with HSV-1 (MOI = 10, 48hpi). Compared with mock-infected controls, UV-inactivated HCMV infection did not alter the phosphorylation level of S6K1 in HELF cells, indicating that non-replicating HCMV particles were insufficient to activate mTOR signaling under these conditions (Figure 2(C)). This finding is consistent with the 5’RACE results, which showed that UV-inactivated HCMV HAN BAC infection failed to increase the relative abundance of the ~150-nt isoform among the transcript isoforms of the four RPGs analyzed (Figure 1(E)). Notably, mock-infected T98G cells exhibited a relatively high basal level of S6K1 phosphorylation, which was not further increased by HCMV infection (Figure 2(C)). This finding is consistent with the 5’RACE results showing that mock-infected T98G cells already displayed a high relative abundance of the ~150-nt 5’TOP-containing isoform for all four RPGs analyzed, which was not further increased by HCMV infection (Figure 1(G)), suggesting that elevated basal mTOR activity may be associated with the pre-existing enrichment of TCT-initiated RPG transcripts in this cell type. In contrast, HSV-1 infection markedly increased S6K1 phosphorylation in both HEK293 and HELF cells (Figure 2(D)); however, this activation was not accompanied by a comparable redistribution of RPG TSS isoforms (Figure 1(H)). These results suggest that although mTOR activation is associated with HCMV-induced enrichment of 5’TOP-containing RPG isoforms, mTOR activation alone may not be sufficient to drive this TSS shift in all viral infection contexts. Alternatively, HSV-1 infection may activate mTOR signaling while simultaneously engaging additional viral or cellular mechanisms that counteract or bypass mTOR-dependent regulation of RPG transcription start site usage.

The discordance between transcriptional and translational regulation of RPGs driven by HCMV infection

Having established that HCMV infection induces widespread alterations in TSS utilization of RPGs, which could theoretically modulate both transcriptional output and subsequent translational efficiency, we proceeded to conduct integrated RNA-seq and proteomic analyses to systematically delineate the changes induced by infection. High-confidence RNA sequencing of HCMV HAN BAC-72 hpi compared to mock-infected HELF cells revealed 2336 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (|log2FC| ≥1, FDR < 0.05, n = 2, DESeq2 Wald test). KEGG pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment in pathways such as PI3K-Akt signaling, calcium signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and TGF-beta signaling, which have been noted in prior transcriptomic studies (Figure 3(A)) [18,31]. Notably, the ribosome pathway was significantly enriched, comprising 55 differentially expressed RPGs that exhibited approximately a twofold reduction in expression following HCMV infection (Figure 3(A), Table S8). This observed decrease in RPGs transcription subsequent to HCMV infection aligns with the findings of Hertel and Mocarski, who employed cDNA microarray analysis [31,32]. HCMV infection drives a discordance between transcriptomic and proteomic dynamics of RPGs.

Label-free quantitative proteomics mass spectrometry (LFQ/MS) revealed profound remodeling of the host proteome during HCMV infection at 72 hpi. The ribosome and ribosome biogenesis pathways were significantly enriched among the differentially expressed host proteins (|Fold chang|≥1.2) by KEGG pathways analysis (Figure 3(B)). Additionally, the terms related to ribosome function were predominated in GO enrichment analyses, particularly in the categories of “ribosome biogenesis” (biological processes), “ribosome subunit” (cellular components), and “structural constituent of ribosome” (molecular functions), which ranked highly across all three GO categories (Fig. S 4). Following HCMV infection, the protein levels of RPGs associated with this pathway were generally found to be upregulated (Table S8), which is consistent with the results of Tirosh O et al. [18], underscoring HCMV’s strategic reprogramming of translational machinery to facilitate viral replication.

An integrative analysis of transcriptional and proteomic dynamics revealed discordant regulation patterns during HCMV infection. A comparative quantification of host genes revealed a weak genome-wide correlation between mRNA and protein levels, as indicated by a Pearson correlation coefficient (r = 0.17 and p = 3.781e-27). Subsequently, a nine-quadrant framework was employed for classification (Figure 3(C)). Strikingly, 4.05% (154/3800) of the quantified genes exhibited discordant expression patterns characterized by protein accumulation despite transcriptional downregulation, with the components of the ribosome pathway significantly enriched, as highlighted by KEGG pathway analysis (Figure 3(C)).

To rigorously validate our transcriptomic and proteomic findings, we utilized PCR Array analysis and high-specificity Parallel Reaction Monitoring (PRM)-based targeted proteomics to examine the expression of a subset of RPGs. This subset included ten RPGs that demonstrated TSS shifting to TCT-motif following infection observed by both nanoCAGE-seq and PRO-Cap. Quantitative analysis of these validation assays revealed a discordant RNA-protein expression pattern consistent with the integrated omics results. In the PCR Array data, most examined RPG transcripts showed reduced HCMV/mock ratios, with statistically significant downregulation detected in a subset of genes, including RPL17, RPL12, RPL4, RPL7A, and RPS4X (p < 0.05, n = 3, determined by the two-tailed Student’s t-test, Table S2). In contrast, PRM analysis showed increased protein abundance for all examined RPGs, with significant upregulation observed for RPL17, RPL22, RPL4, RPL3, RPL7A, and RPL19 (p < 0.05, n = 3, determined by the two-tailed Student’s t-test, Table S2). Importantly, this discordant expression pattern at the transcriptional and translational levels mirrored the directional changes observed in our RNA-seq and proteomic datasets. A heatmap that integrates RNA sequencing, PCR array, LFQ/MS, and PRM datasets effectively visualized the expression dynamics of RPGs with alternative TSS usage during HCMV infection (Figure 3(D)).

The exploitation of the mTOR by HCMV for the translational enhancement of 5’TOP-containing RPL4 mRNA isoform

To further explore the impact of the alternative TSS utilization of RPGs on protein expression, we employed the alternative 5’UTR isoforms of RPL4 as a model system. We substituted the untranslated region upstream of the protein-coding sequence of the HCMV UL69-Flag fusion protein within a previously constructed transient expression plasmid, designated as UL69-Flag-CMV14. The substitution involved incorporating DNA sequences corresponding to either the 5’TOP-containing or non-5’TOP-containing isoforms of the RPL4 5’UTR. The resultant plasmids were designated as TOP/wi-5’RPL4-UL69-Flag-CMV14 that incorporates the 5’TOP sequence, and TOP/wo-5’RPL4-UL69-Flag-CMV14 that lacks the 5’TOP sequence. The reporter mRNA synthesized encodes the HCMV UL69-Flag gene and is prefixed with either a 5’UTR containing a 5’TOP motif (CUCUUCC) or a transverted non-5’TOP sequence (GAGAAGG), as confirmed through 5’RACE. Equal amounts of the two plasmids were each transfected into HEK293 cells in triplicate, and the cellular samples were harvested for analysis of UL69-Flag gene expression at 72 hours post transfection. Western blot analysis utilizing an anti-Flag antibody revealed that cells transfected with TOP/wi-5’RPL4-UL69-Flag-CMV14 exhibited significantly elevated levels of UL69-Flag protein in comparison to those transfected with TCT/wo-5’RPL4-UL69-Flag-CMV14 (p < 0.01, n = 3, determined by one-way ANOVA, see Figure 4(A)). To control for potential differences in transfection efficiency between the two groups, we quantified the relative abundance of the UL69 gene in cellular DNA by real-time PCR. No significant difference in plasmid DNA levels was observed between the groups (p > 0.05, n = 3, determined by one-way ANOVA, Figure 4(A)), indicating comparable transfection efficiency. These results suggest that the observed differences in UL69-Flag protein expression are attributable to differential protein expression levels rather than variations in transfection efficiency. HCMV-mediated activation of mTOR promotes translation of the 5’TOP-containing RPL4 mRNA isoform.

The 5’TOP motif located at the 5’end of a transcript has been recognized as a translational regulatory element of mTORC1. Consequently, we sought to examine whether the translational regulatory functions of RPL4 5’UTR containing the 5’TOP motif differ in its responsiveness to mTOR activity compared to that lacking this motif. To facilitate this investigation, we established two stable HEK293 cell lines using a lentiviral bicistronic system of pLJM1-TOP/wi-5’RPL4-EGFP-IRES-mCherry and pLJM1-TOP/wo-5’RPL4-EGFP-IRES-mCherry (Figure 4(B)), as introduced in method section. This design enabled the upstream EGFP gene to be translated via a cap-dependent mechanism, while the downstream mCherry gene was translated through an IRES-dependent mechanism. The stable HEK293 cell lines were cultured as confluent monolayers in MEM supplemented with 2% FBS and were subjected to treatment with the mTOR activator 3BDO (480 nM) or the inhibitor Torin 1 (600 nM) for a duration of 48 hours. The activity of mTORC1 was assessed by measuring the levels of phosphorylated S6K1 protein (Figure 4(C)). To evaluate the translational activity associated with the 5’TOP-containing and non-5’TOP-containing 5’UTR isoforms, we calculated the ratio of EGFP protein expression level to mCherry protein expression level (Figure 4(C)). This experiment was conducted in triplicate. The average ratio of EGFP protein levels mediated by the 5’TOP-containing RPL4 5’UTR to IRES-mediated mCherry protein levels was approximately 1.85 in 3BDO treated cells compared to untreated controls (p < 0.01, n = 3, determined by one-way ANOVA). However, this ratio significantly decreased to 0.35 following Torin 1 treatment, indicating potent suppression of 5’TOP-dependent translation by mTOR inhibition (p < 0.05, n = 3, determined by one-way ANOVA). In contrast, the average ratio of EGFP protein levels mediated by the non-5’TOP-containing RPL4 5’UTR to IRES-mediated mCherry protein levels remained relatively stable on treatments with 3BDO or Torin 1, showing no significant changes compared to untreated cells (p > 0.05, n = 3, determined by one-way ANOVA, Figure 4(C)). These differential regulatory responses demonstrated distinct translational regulatory capacities of 5’TOP-containing and non-5’TOP-containing RPL4 5’UTR isoforms, which suggested a complex role for mTOR in modulating RPG expression and underscored the potential importance of TCT motif utilization in the context of HCMV infection.

To explore the potential involvement of HCMV infection in the mTORC1 mediated regulation of the 5’TOP-containing 5’UTR isoform mRNA of RPL4, we established two stable HELF cell lines using lentiviral plasmids: One cell line expressed the 5’TOP-containing RPL4 5’UTR-EGFP fusion transcript derived from the pLJM1-TOP/wi-5’RPL4-EGFP construct, while the other served as a control, containing the empty pLJM1-EGFP control plasmid. Both stable HELF cell lines, maintained as confluent monolayers in MEM supplemented with 2% FBS, were subjected to infection with the wild type HCMV HAN strain at MOI of 1 and mocked infection with DMSO, respectively, or exposed to 120 nM mTOR activator 3BDO and 10 nM the inhibitor Torin 1. In addition, prior 6 hours to HCMV infection, the cells were treated with the 3BDO or Torin 1. Fluorescence microscopy analysis indicated that in the HELF cells transduced with pLJM1-TOP/wi-5’RPL4-EGFP, the intensity of green fluorescence significantly increased following HCMV infection or 3BDO treatment, whereas it was notably reduced upon Torin 1 treatment (Figure 4(D)). The infected and mock-infected stable HELF cells with or without pharmacological treatment were harvest at 48 hpi to assess the expression of the EGFP protein. The phosphorylation level of the S6K1 protein was measured to evaluate the activation status of the mTORC1 pathway (Figure 4(E)). In the samples from HELF cells transduced with pLJM1-TOP/wi-5’RPL4-EGFP, western blot analysis using a GFP antibody revealed that EGFP protein expression was elevated following HCMV infection or 3BDO treatment, however, Torin 1 treatment resulted in a downregulation of EGFP protein level. Furthermore, after inhibiting the mTORC1 pathway with Torin 1, HCMV infection failed to induce an increase in EGFP protein expression in these cells (p < 0.05, n = 3, determined by one-way ANOVA). In contrast, in HELF cells transduced with pLJM1-EGFP, neither HCMV infection nor treatment with 3BDO or Torin 1 significantly affected EGFP protein levels, despite the regulation of S6K1 phosphorylation (p > 0.05, n = 3, determined by one-way ANOVA, Figure 4(E)). These results suggest that HCMV infection enhances the translational regulatory activity of the 5’TOP-containing RPL4 5’UTR isoform, with this enhancement being mediated through mTORC1 activation induced by HCMV infection.

Knockdown of RPL4 significantly reduced protein accumulation and replicative capacity of HCMV

To access the role of RPL4 in HCMV proliferation dynamics, we established shRNA stable RPL4 knockdown HELF cell line (designated HELF-shRPL4) along with negative control counterpart (HELF-shNC) through recombinant lentiviral transduction. Western blot analysis confirmed successful RPL4 knockdown in the HELF-shRPL4 cell line (p = 0.003, n = 3, determined by paired t test, Figure 5(A)). The stable shRNA HELF cells were infected with HCMV HAN BAC strain at MOI = 1. Western blot analysis revealed that the accumulations of viral immediate early protein IE1 at 24 hpi (p = 0.048, n = 3, paired t test) early protein pp52 at 48 hpi (p < 0.001, n = 3, paired t test) and late protein pp28 at 72 hpi (p = 0.030, n = 3, determined by paired t test) were diminished in the HELF-shRPL4 cells compared to those in the HELF-shNC cells early protein (Figure 5(B)). Additionally, the shRNA stable HELF cells were infected with HCMV HAN BAC strain at MOI = 0.5. Comparative virological analysis revealed a significant attenuation in viral titers in the HELF-shRPL4 cells relative to the HELF-shNC cells. Viral titers in RPL4-depleted cells were reduced by approximately 2–3 orders of magnitude across 72–144 hpi, and this reduction remained statistically significant at all examined time points (p < 0.05, n = 3, determined by two-way ANOVA, Figure 5(C)). These data indicate that RPL4 is required for or efficient production of infectious HCMV progeny. To further validate the robustness of these findings, we generated two additional HELF cell lines with stable RPL4 knockdown using independent shRNA constructs and repeated the same infection experiments. Consistent with the results obtained in the original HELF-shRPL4 cells, both additional RPL4-knockdown cell lines exhibited reduced expression of viral proteins (IE1, pp52, p < 0.01, n = 3, determined by one-way ANOVA, and pp28, p = 0.029, n = 3, determined by Kruskal-Wallis test, Fig. S5) and a concomitant decrease in viral titers compared with control cells (p < 0.001, n = 3, determined by two-way ANOVA, Fig. S6), confirming the reproducibility of the observed phenotype. Knockdown of RPL4 markedly reduced viral protein levels and inhibited HCMV replication.

Given previous reports that knockdown of certain RPGs suppresses cell proliferation and promotes apoptosis [33], we next evaluated the effects of RPL4 knockdown on cell viability and apoptosis using MTS assays and flow cytometric analysis. Under mock conditions, post hoc multiple comparisons did not reveal consistent significant differences between shRPL4 and control cells, however, a modest decrease was observed in two shRPL4 cell lines (p < 0.05, n = 3, one-way ANOVA). In contrast, under HCMV infection, RPL4 knockdown did not significantly affect cell viability (p > 0.05, n = 3, one-way ANOVA) (Figure 6(A)). However, RPL4 knockdown significantly increased apoptosis (p < 0.001, n = 3, one-way ANOVA) (Figure 6(B); Fig. S7). Although the reduction in viability did not reach statistical significance, the concomitant increase in apoptosis suggests that RPL4 depletion may modestly compromise cell fitness. These findings suggest that the reduced expression of viral proteins (IE1, pp52, and pp28) observed upon RPL4 knockdown may result, at least in part, from impaired viral replication secondary to compromised cell survival and enhanced apoptosis, rather than solely from a direct effect on the translation of specific viral proteins. Discussion

Our data reveal a previously unexplored layer of host gene regulation during HCMV infection, in which alternative TSS selection reshapes transcript isoform output in permissive fibroblasts. Rather than merely changing total transcript abundance, HCMV infection was associated with a widespread redistribution of capped 5’ends across host genes, with RPGs emerging as one of the most prominent functional groups affected. This observation suggests that HCMV may reshape host gene expression not only at the level of transcript abundance, but also at the level of transcript identity, thereby influencing the translational competence of specific mRNA isoforms.

Several features of our dataset support the biological relevance of this phenomenon. First, the shift toward TCT-initiated, 5’TOP-containing RPG isoforms was supported by multiple independent approaches, including nanoCAGE-seq, 5’RACE with clone sequencing, and re-analysis of published PRO-cap data. The PRO-cap analysis is particularly informative because it profiles nascent capped transcripts and is therefore less influenced by mature RNA turnover than CAGE-based approaches [26]. Second, the same general pattern was observed in two HCMV strains and across permissive fibroblast systems, arguing against a strain-restricted explanation for RPG 5’-end redistribution. By contrast, UV-inactivated HCMV failed to induce a comparable redistribution of RPG TSS isoforms in HELF cells, supporting the idea that this response requires viral gene expression and/or productive viral replication rather than viral particle entry alone. Similarly, HCMV infection did not further increase the relative abundance of 5’TOP-containing RPG isoforms in T98G cells, which are semi-permissive for HCMV infection. This observation suggests that the response is not universal across all infected cell types and may depend on a permissive cellular context capable of supporting a synchronized or advanced lytic program. Furthermore, the absence of a similar shift in RPG TSS usage in HSV-1-infected HELF and HEK293 cells suggests that TCT-directed switching toward 5’TOP-containing RPG isoforms is unlikely to represent a universal herpesvirus-induced response. Instead, this phenomenon appears to be more closely associated with HCMV infection and may depend on virus-specific regulatory mechanisms operating within a permissive cellular context. Moreover, in both T98G and HEK293 cells, RPL4 predominantly produced the ~150-nt 5’RACE product under both mock- and virus-infected conditions, indicating that the basal TSS usage of individual RPGs can differ substantially among cell types.

Our finding of TCT-directed RPG transcriptional initiation switching is consistent with previous evidence that RPGs often have broad promoters and heterogeneous TSS usage, allowing 5’TOP- and non-5’TOP-containing isoforms to be differentially regulated across diverse biological contexts, including developmental transitions, malignant transformation, and stress adaptation [12,14–16,34]. In this framework, HCMV may exploit a preexisting promoter architecture rather than create a virus-specific transcriptional mechanism de novo. Pharmacological perturbation further supports a role for mTOR signaling in this process: under the conditions primarily examined here, activation of mTOR with 3BDO recapitulated the enrichment of 5’TOP-containing isoforms observed during HCMV infection, whereas low-dose Torin1 reduced their relative abundance and attenuated the HCMV-associated shift. Consistently, additional analyses of S6K1 phosphorylation further linked mTOR activity to RPG isoform redistribution: UV-inactivated HCMV did not increase S6K1 phosphorylation in HELF cells, consistent with its inability to promote enrichment of the ~150-nt 5’TOP-containing RPG isoforms. In contrast, mock-infected T98G cells already exhibited relatively high basal S6K1 phosphorylation, accompanied by a high basal representation of the ~150-nt 5’TOP-containing isoforms for all four RPGs analyzed. HCMV infection did not further enhance either S6K1 phosphorylation or the relative abundance of these isoforms in T98G cells. These findings suggest that elevated basal mTOR activity may be associated with a pre-existing enrichment of TCT-initiated RPG transcripts, potentially limiting the magnitude of additional virus-induced TSS redistribution in this cellular context. Thus, the infection-induced increase in the relative abundance of 5’TOP-containing RPG transcripts may depend not only on viral infection itself, but also on the preexisting TSS landscape and regulatory state of RPG transcription in a given cellular context. By contrast, HSV-1 infection markedly increased S6K1 phosphorylation in both HELF and HEK293 cells, but did not induce a comparable shift in RPG TSS usage. Thus, although mTOR activation is associated with HCMV-induced enrichment of 5’TOP-containing RPG isoforms, mTOR activation alone is unlikely to be sufficient to drive this TSS shift in all viral infection contexts.

However, the effect of mTOR perturbation was not uniform across all settings. In HeLa cells, high-dose Torin1 (250 nM, approximately 25 times the IC50) was previously reported to stabilize 5’TOP-containing isoforms relative to non-5’TOP isoforms, thereby increasing 5’TOP transcript abundance [12]. Consistent with the idea that the response depends on treatment context, we found that prolonged exposure of HELF cells to a higher Torin 1 regimen (20 nM for 72 hours) also increased the relative abundance of 5’TOP-containing RPG isoforms by 5’RACE analysis (Fig. S8). Thus, rather than supporting a simple linear model in which mTOR inhibition invariably decreases 5’TOP-containing isoforms, these observations suggest that mTOR signaling influences the balance between alternative RPG 5’UTR isoforms in a manner that is dose-, duration-, and cell-context-dependent.

The divergent outcomes observed across cellular contexts and Torin 1 treatment regimens suggest that the response of RPG 5’UTR isoforms to mTOR perturbation may depend not only on cell-type-specific mTOR signaling architecture, but also on differences in the basal and perturbation-induced states of mTOR pathway activity. These differences in mTOR activity state may, in turn, influence post-transcriptional RNA fate, including isoform-specific turnover [35–38]. Indeed, an important caveat is that the present data do not yet definitively distinguish transcriptional from post-transcriptional mechanisms underlying the observed RNA changes. Both nanoCAGE-seq and 5’RACE quantify mature capped RNA species rather than nascent transcripts, and the 5’RACE is semi-quantitative. At the same time, herpesvirus infection can influence post-transcriptional processes, including RNA stability and processing. Therefore, the observed redistribution of 5’ ends could reflect altered RNA polymerase II initiation-site selection, but could also arise from isoform-specific differences in transcript processing, capping, nuclear export, or stability. The actinomycin D experiment argues against a simple model in which HCMV selectively stabilizes the longer 5’RACE product after transcription has occurred, but it does not fully exclude more complex post-transcriptional contributions. Although the concordant PRO-cap results strengthen the interpretation that nascent transcription initiation is being repositioned toward TCT sites, the relative contribution of bona fide promoter choice vs downstream RNA fate remains unresolved. Future experiments combining nascent transcription assays, Pol II occupancy analysis, metabolic RNA labeling, and isoform-resolved full-length transcriptome sequencing will be needed to define the primary regulatory step more rigorously.

A second major issue raised by our data is the discordance between RPG transcript and protein levels during HCMV infection. RNA-seq, PCR array, proteomics, and PRM analyses consistently showed that a subset of RPGs exhibited modest decreases in total mRNA abundance but increased protein abundance after infection. This pattern suggests that translational regulation, rather than transcript abundance alone, likely contributes to RPG protein accumulation in infected cells. In particular, we have demonstrated that the RPL4 5’UTR containing a 5’TOP motif displays enhanced translational responsiveness under conditions of HCMV-induced mTORC1 activation, thereby permitting increased protein production even from a reduced total transcript pool. Our data further suggest that HCMV may strengthen this effect by increasing the relative abundance of 5’TOP-containing isoforms within the overall RPG transcript population. Such a shift in isoform composition would be expected to favor mTORC1-sensitive translation and thus provide a mechanistic explanation for how RPG protein levels can rise despite reduced total mRNA levels. In this framework, bulk mRNA abundance alone may underestimate the translational capacity of the infected RPG transcriptome, because protein output may depend not only on transcript quantity but also on the relative representation of translation-competent 5’ UTR isoforms and associated translational regulatory mechanisms.

Within this broader framework, RPL4 provides a useful proof-of-principle example, but its interpretation also requires caution. RPL4 is a structural component of the 60S ribosomal subunit and is therefore well positioned to influence ribosome assembly and translational capacity [39,40], while prior studies also support extra-ribosomal functions for RPL4 in virus-host interactions [41,42]. In our system, depletion of RPL4 reduced the accumulation of HCMV immediate-early, early, and late proteins and impaired viral replication, indicating that RPL4 contributes functionally to productive infection. However, because RPL4 knockdown also reduced cell viability and increased apoptosis, the observed viral defects cannot be attributed exclusively to impaired translation of viral proteins. More importantly, the current data do not establish that the proviral role of RPL4 is caused specifically by HCMV-induced TSS switching, as opposed to the broader requirement for adequate RPL4 expression in infected cells. Thus, RPL4 should at present be viewed as a biologically informative example linking RPG regulation to infection outcome, rather than as definitive proof that TSS switching itself is causally responsible for the replication phenotype.

More broadly, it remains unclear whether RPL4 is exceptional or whether it represents one member of a larger subset of RPs whose expression is selectively sustained or enhanced during HCMV infection through coupled control of transcript architecture and translation. If so, HCMV may be exploiting not a single RP, but a broader hierarchical RP network to optimize viral protein biogenesis. Defining whether additional RPGs with TCT-directed isoform shifts also contribute functionally to viral replication will be important for testing this possibility.

Taken together, our findings support a model in which HCMV reshapes host RPG expression not simply by altering total transcript abundance, but by changing the relative representation and translational responsiveness of alternative 5’UTR isoforms. Importantly, the present study does not yet fully resolve the relative contributions of transcriptional vs post-transcriptional regulation to the observed RNA changes, nor does it fully explain the discordance between RPG mRNA and protein abundance. Rather, it identifies these as central mechanistic questions for future work. In this sense, our study provides a framework for understanding how HCMV may couple 5’UTR isoform remodeling to mTOR-responsive translational control in order to create a biosynthetic state favorable for productive viral replication.
