Boundedness of average rank of elliptic curves ordered by the coefficients
Fatemehzahra Janbazi

Abstract
We study the average rank of elliptic curves E A , B : y 2 = x 3 + A ​ x + B E_{A,B}:y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B over ℚ \mathbb{Q} , ordered by the height function h ⁡ ( E A , B ) := max ⁡ ( | A | , | B | ) h(E_{A,B}):=\max(|A|,|B|) . Understanding this average rank requires estimating the number of irreducible integral binary quartic forms under the action of GL 2 ​ ( ℤ ) \mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}) , where the invariants I I and J J are bounded by X X . A key challenge in this estimation arises from working within regions of the quartic form space that expand non-uniformly, with volume and projection of the same order. To address this, we develop a new technique for counting integral points in these regions, refining existing methods and overcoming the limitations of Davenport’s lemma. This leads to a bound on the average size of the 2-Selmer group, yielding an upper bound of 1.5 for the average rank of elliptic curves ordered by h h .

Introduction

Any elliptic curve
E
over
\mathbb{Q}
can be uniquely expressed in the form
E_{A,B}:y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
, where for all primes
p
, if
p^{4}\mid A
, then
p^{6}\nmid B
.
It has been conjectured in the work of Gelfand
[
10
]
and Katz–Sarnak
[
11
]
that 50% of elliptic curves have rank 0 and 50% have rank 1.
These densities are expected to hold regardless of whether elliptic curves are ordered by conductor, discriminant, or height.

A standard way to order elliptic curves is using the naive height
H(E_{A,B})
, defined as
\max\{4|A|^{3},27B^{2}\}
.
Bhargava and Shankar showed in
[
3
]
that when elliptic curves are ordered by
H
, the average rank is at most
1.5
. This result follows from their computation of the average size of the
2
-Selmer group, which they proved to be
3
under this ordering. Their result aligns with heuristics of Poonen–Rains
[
12
]
, which predicts the average size of
\text{Sel}_{2}
should be
3
.

In this paper, we consider the coefficient height function for elliptic curves, given by

h(E_{A,B})\vcentcolon=\max\{|A|,|B|\}.

Ordering elliptic curves by naive height behaves similarly to ordering them by discriminant. Indeed, curves with naive height less than
X
satisfy
|A|\ll X^{1/3}
and
|B|\ll X^{1/2}
, which corresponds to having discriminant less than
X
. On the other hand, elliptic curves with coefficients bounded in absolute value by
X
have discriminants bounded by
O(X^{3})
, and those with discriminant less than
X
form a zero-density subfamily within this set. This induces new challenges in understanding the distribution of elliptic curves when ordered by coefficient height. In particular, it has been observed in
[
14
]
that for certain thin families, the distribution of the
2
-Selmer group does not always follow the predictions of Poonen–Rains
[
12
]
.

We analyze the distribution of the
2
-Selmer group and the average rank of elliptic curves when ordered by
h
. In particular, we establish the following results:

Theorem 1.1

When elliptic curves over
\mathbb{Q}
are ordered by the height
h
, the average size of the
2
-Selmer group is at most
3
.

Since the rank of an elliptic curve is at most half the size of its
2
-Selmer group, we obtain the following immediate corollary:

Corollary 1.2

When elliptic curves over
\mathbb{Q}
are ordered by the height
h
, their average rank is at most
1.5
.

Although we establish an upper bound, we expect that the average size of the
2
-Selmer group in this setting actually matches the predicted value of
3
; see Remark
4.10
.

V_{\mathbb{R}}
denote the space of binary quartic forms with real coefficients, and let

\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

It is classically known that for fixed invariants

and

-orbits of integral binary quartic forms with those invariants, provided that

and

for binary quartic forms, defined as follows:

This height function is closely related to the coefficient height on elliptic curves through the parametrization. By introducing new ideas, we shift the main challenges in handling this thin family to problems of equidistribution and non-Archimedean analysis, as detailed in the method of proofs section. Combined with Bhargava’s averaging method, this perspective allows us to establish the following result:

Methods of Proofs

\int_{(tnk)\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{PGL}_{2}}}\#\big\{f\in V_{{\mathbb{Z}},{\rm irr}}^{(i)}\cap X^{1/2}(tn)G_{0}\cdot L^{(i)}:|J(f)|<X\big\}t^{-2}d^{\times}tdndk,

(1)

\mathcal{F}_{\text{PGL}_{2}}
denotes Gauss’s fundamental domain for
{\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\backslash{\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})

, as defined in
[
3

, serves as the fundamental set for the action of
{\rm GL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
on
V_{{\mathbb{R}}}
. The scaling by

arises from the assumption that
|I|<X
; however, once this condition is imposed, the further condition

must additionally be enforced.

The naive height is a degree 6 homogeneous function in the coefficients of the form
f
. Due to this homogeneity, the primary difficulty in counting the orbits in
[
3
]
arises when the ball becomes distorted for large
t
, whereas at
t=1
, the counting remains well-controlled, allowing the authors to address this issue by cutting off the cusp. This homogeneity also ensures that standard lattice point counting methods from geometry of numbers can be applied effectively. However, the new height function is not homogeneous on
V_{{\mathbb{R}}}
, and the difficulty in counting appears even in the main ball at
t=1
.

Specifically, if we consider a scaled ball by
X^{1/2}
in the space as it appears in above integral:

X^{1/2}(nG_{0}\cdot L^{(i)}),

and attempt to count the number of irreducible integral points with
J
-invariant less than
X
, fixing the first four coefficients
(a,b,c,d)
of the form constrains the last coefficient to an interval of size
O(1)
. This introduces an Archimedean difficulty in counting lattice points, as the volume of the space in which we aim to count lattice points and the volume of its projection onto the first four coefficients are of the same order. Consequently, the approach in
[
3
]
does not extend to this setting, making an alternative method necessary.

To analyze the distribution of integral quartic forms within a bounded region scaled by a factor of
X^{1/2}
under the constraint
|J|<X
, we first fiber over the coefficients
(a,b,c)
and study the distribution of
(d,e)
. Instead of working directly in the coefficient space, we describe quartic forms in terms of their semi-invariants, as introduced in Section
2
. Specifically, we analyze the distribution of the semi-invariants
(I(f),R(f))
, where
I
is the primary invariant of degree 2 and
R
is a semi-invariant of degree 3, given by

R(f)\vcentcolon=b^{3}+8a^{2}d-4abc.

Thus, we replace
(d,e)
with
(I,R)
over each fiber
(a,b,c)
. This reformulation enables us to impose the constraint
|J|<X
by utilizing the syzygy relation among the semi-invariants:

P^{3}-48a^{2}PI+64a^{3}J=-27R^{2},

where
P
is given by
P=8ac-3b^{2}
. To incorporate the height restriction in this new framework, we observe that when
P\neq 0
, the syzygy expresses
I
linearly in terms of
R
and
J
. This enables us to translate the condition
|J|<X
into a constraint on the
(I,R)
-space via the relation

\big|I-(\frac{27R^{2}}{48a^{2}P}+\frac{P^{2}}{48a^{2}})\big|\ll\bigg|\frac{aX}{P}\bigg|.

This reformulation makes the application of the height function, our archimedean input, easier but comes at the cost of introducing new non-Archimedean difficulties. For example, if
R
is also fixed in the fiber over
(a,b,c)
, the permissible range of
I
is naturally constrained to an interval of size
O(X^{1/2})
. However, instead of counting all possible values of
I
, we restrict our attention to those satisfying a congruence condition modulo
12a
. More precisely, for quartic forms with fixed coefficients
a,b,c
and semi-invariant
R
, the value of
I
is uniquely determined modulo
12a
. Thus, we need to determine the number of possible values of
I
over each fiber
(a,b,c)
with fixed
R
, within an interval of size
O(X^{1/2})
, while also satisfies congruent condition whose modulus has the same size.

This observation reduces the Archimedean difficulty in each fiber to a non-Archimedean problem, allowing us to apply equidistribution methods. To carry this out, we apply Poisson summation over each fiber in the
(R,I)
-space. To make this argument precise, we fix
(a,b,c)
with
a\neq 0
and consider the map

\upsilon_{(a,b,c)}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\quad(d,e)\mapsto(I(f),R(f)),

where
f=(a,b,c,d,e)
. This map defines a bijection of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Now, assume that
(a,b,c)
is an element of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}
with
a\neq 0
. The map
\upsilon_{(a,b,c)}
sends
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
to

\Lambda_{(a,b,c)}\vcentcolon=\left\{(I,R)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\begin{array}[]{ll}R&\equiv b^{3}-4abc\pmod{8a^{2}},\\
I&\equiv-3b(R-b^{3}+4abc)(8a^{2})^{-1}+c^{2}\pmod{12a}\end{array}\right\}.

This shows that not all values of
(I,R)
appear for integral quartic forms. In particular, as mentioned, over each fiber, fixing the semi-invariant
R
determines
I
modulo
12a
, while
R
itself is fixed modulo
8a^{2}
.

During the counting process, by leveraging both the geometric properties of our fibers and the underlying lattice structure, we can control the error more efficiently. These aspects are formalized in Lemma
3.5
and Proposition
3.6
.

This perspective, based on the use of semi-invariants, can also be applied to the family of binary
n
-ic forms. The existence of such semi-invariants and their associated syzygies is ensured by classical results in invariant theory. Moreover, all invariants can be expressed as rational functions of these semi-invariants, suggesting that a similar approach may be effective in more general settings for counting orbits, particularly in cases where classical lattice point techniques fall short.

To complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we need to apply a sieve to the family of elliptic curves with coefficient height less than
X
. As this sieve involves difficulties, we require the following Theorem

Theorem 1.5

For a prime
p
, let
\mathcal{W}_{p}
represent elliptic curves
E
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
p^{2}
divides its discriminant. For any
Q\geq 5
and
\epsilon>0
, we have:

\#\bigcup_{p>Q}\left\{E_{A,B}\in\mathcal{W}_{p}:h(E_{A,B})<X\right\}=O_{\epsilon}\left(\frac{X^{2+\epsilon}}{Q}+X^{7/4+\epsilon}\right).

To address this, we represent irreducible cubic polynomials as reducible quartic polynomials with a unique root over
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Using the averaging method, we fiber over the root of the form to organize the counting process. This approach is particularly effective since the unique root is intrinsically linked to the semi-invariants of the form, allowing us to impose the condition
|J|<X
in a systematic way.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the smooth averaging method and define the semi-invariants of the form. We then restrict the cusp and refine the counting process within the averaging method to minimize errors in the subsequent Fourier analysis. Additionally, we define a new height function that facilitates Fourier calculations while closely approximating the original height function, ensuring that the error remains within an acceptable range.

Section
3
is dedicated to bounding the Fourier coefficients, leading to the proof of Theorem
1.3
. In Section
4
, we show that the volume of the fibers over
(a,b,c)

1.3

Finally, in Section
5

2

X
, thereby completing the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 3.2

For any
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
with
a\neq 0
, let
\zeta=b^{3}-4abc
and define

\bar{\Lambda}_{(a,b,c)}\vcentcolon=\left\{\left(\zeta+8a^{2}k,c^{2}-3kb\right)\in\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{8a^{2}\cdot 12a\,\mathbb{Z}}\times\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{12a\,\mathbb{Z}}:k=0,1,\dots,12|a|-1\right\}.

Then,
(R,I)\in\Lambda_{(a,b,c)}
if and only if
(R\pmod{8a^{2}\cdot 12a},I\pmod{12a})\in\bar{\Lambda}_{(a,b,c)}
.

Proof:

To demonstrate this, consider the expressions

\left(\zeta+8a^{2}k+8a^{2}\cdot 12a\cdot m,c^{2}-3kb+12a\cdot n\right)

for integers
m
and
n
. These expressions show that

R=\zeta+8a^{2}k+8a^{2}\cdot 12a\cdot m
satisfies

R\equiv\zeta\pmod{8a^{2}}
, and

I\equiv-3kb+c^{2}\pmod{12a}.

Consequently,
(R\pmod{8a^{2}\cdot 12a},I\pmod{12a})\in\bar{\Lambda}_{(a,b,c)}
implies
(R,I)\in\Lambda_{(a,b,c)}
. Conversely, if
(R,I)\in\Lambda_{(a,b,c)}
, then we obtain unique values of
k
and
m
satisfying the congruence conditions as described in the lemma.

\Box

For any smooth function
f
with compact support on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we define its Fourier transform by

\widehat{f}(\alpha,\beta)\vcentcolon=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,y)e^{-2\pi i(x\alpha+y\beta)}\,dx\,dy.

For such smooth functions, the Poisson summation formula on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
states that

\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}f(m,n)=\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\widehat{f}(\alpha,\beta).

Proposition 3.3

Let
f
be a smooth function in the
(R,I)
-space with compact support, and let
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
with

. Let

\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\psi_{\Lambda_{(a,b,c)}}(m,n)f(m,n)=\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\widehat{\psi}_{\Lambda_{(a,b,c)}}(\alpha,\beta)\,\widehat{f}\left(\frac{\alpha}{8a^{2}\cdot 12a},\frac{\beta}{12a}\right),

where

is defined as

\widehat{\psi}_{\Lambda_{(a,b,c)}}(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{8a^{2}\cdot 12a\cdot 12a}\sum_{k=0}^{12|a|-1}e^{2\pi i\alpha\xi_{\alpha,k}(a,b,c)}e^{2\pi i\beta\xi_{\beta,k}(a,b,c)},

with

\xi_{\alpha,k}(a,b,c)\vcentcolon=\frac{\zeta+8a^{2}k}{8a^{2}\cdot 12a},\quad\xi_{\beta,k}(a,b,c)\vcentcolon=\frac{c^{2}-3bk}{12a},

and
\zeta=b^{3}-4abc
.

Proof:

By applying Lemma
3.2
, we obtain:

\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\psi_{\Lambda_{(a,b,c)}}(m,n)f(m,n)=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{12|a|-1}f(\zeta+8a^{2}k+8a^{2}\cdot 12a\cdot m\,,\,c^{2}-3kb+12a\cdot n).

Now, let us define

f_{k}(R,I)\vcentcolon=f(\zeta+8a^{2}k+8a^{2}\cdot 12a\cdot R\,,\,c^{2}-3bk+12a\cdot I),

which allows us to rewrite the sum as

\displaystyle\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{12|a|-1}f(\zeta+8a^{2}k+8a^{2}\cdot 12a\cdot m,-3kb+c^{2}+12a\cdot n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{12|a|-1}f_{k}(m,n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{12|a|-1}\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}f_{k}(m,n).

Applying the Poisson summation formula to each

\sum_{k=0}^{12|a|-1}\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}f_{k}(m,n)=\sum_{k=0}^{12|a|-1}\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\widehat{f}_{k}(\alpha,\beta).

The Fourier transform

is related to the original transform

by the following expression:

\widehat{f}_{k}(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{8a^{2}\cdot 12a\cdot 12a}e^{2\pi i\alpha\xi_{\alpha,k}(a,b,c)}e^{2\pi i\beta\xi_{\beta,k}(a,b,c)}\,\widehat{f}\big(\frac{\alpha}{8a^{2}\cdot 12a},\frac{\beta}{12a}\big)

This completes the proof.

Lemma 3.4

For any

To construct

\chi_{(-1,1)}

\varphi_{1,\epsilon}
designed to approximate

within the interval

Let

Then, we construct

\phi_{1,\epsilon}=w_{\epsilon^{2}/2}*\varphi_{1,\epsilon}.

\epsilon
-good approximation of
\chi_{(-1,1)}
.

1.

Support and Proximity to

is zero outside the interval

2.

Smoothness: Since

3.

\int\phi_{1,\epsilon}(x)e^{-imx^{2}}e^{-i\alpha x}\,dx=\int w(y)\int\varphi_{1,\epsilon}(x-y\epsilon^{2}/2)e^{-imx^{2}}e^{-i\alpha x}\,dx\,dy,

for fixed

Firstly, in intervasl where

for fixed endpoints

and

and

are fixed real numbers. This integral is

.

Secondly, In regions where

\widehat{T}_{m}(R_{0}\alpha)\widehat{\phi}\left(\beta\frac{4aCX}{H}\right)=O\left(\left|\frac{\beta R_{0}^{2}}{a^{2}H}\right|^{K}\epsilon^{-2K}(1+|R_{0}\alpha|)^{-K}\cdot\epsilon^{-2M-2K}\left(\frac{\beta aX}{H}\right)^{-M-K}\right).

Then, since

\left|\frac{\beta aX}{H}\right|^{-K}\cdot\left|\frac{\beta R_{0}^{2}}{a^{2}H}\right|^{K}=\left|\frac{R_{0}^{2}}{a^{3}X}\right|^{K},

we obtain the desired bound.This completes the proof of parts (b) and (c).

Define the function
\theta

\theta_{j}(a,b,c,R,I)\coloneqq\psi_{n}\left(\frac{at^{4}}{\lambda},\frac{bt^{2}}{\lambda},\frac{c}{\lambda},\frac{Rt^{6}}{\lambda^{3}},\frac{I}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\cdot\phi_{j,\epsilon}\left(\frac{R}{R_{0}}\right)\phi_{j,\epsilon}\left(\frac{3HI}{4aCX}-X^{-1}\Gamma(a,b,c,R)\right),

, as given in equation (

good
\delta_{s}
-approximation
.

For integers

, we define
\epsilon_{(\alpha,\beta)}
to be

, and
0

Proposition 3.6

For any
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
with
a\neq 0
, then:

|\widehat{\psi}_{\Lambda(a,b,c)}(\alpha,\beta)|=\frac{\epsilon_{(\alpha,\beta)}}{|8a^{2}\cdot 12a|}.

Proof:

To compute
8a^{2}\cdot 12a\cdot 12a\cdot|\widehat{\psi}_{\Lambda(a,b,c)}(\alpha,\beta)|
, we start by expanding the expression as follows:

8a^{2}\cdot 12a\cdot 12a\cdot|\widehat{\psi}_{\Lambda(a,b,c)}(\alpha,\beta)|=\left|\sum_{k=0}^{12|a|-1}e^{2\pi i\alpha\left(\frac{\zeta+8a^{2}k}{8a^{2}\cdot 12a}\right)}e^{2\pi i\beta\left(\frac{c^{2}-3bk}{12a}\right)}\right|,

where
\zeta
is
b^{3}-4abc
.

This sum simplifies by decomposing each exponential term and factoring out constants that are independent of
k
, yielding:

\left|\sum_{k=0}^{12|a|-1}e^{2\pi ik\left(\frac{\alpha-3b\beta}{12a}\right)}\right|.

This is a geometric series, which equals
12|a|
if
\alpha\equiv 3b\beta\pmod{12a}
(so each term is
1
), and zero otherwise. This completes the proof.

3.2
Proof of the equidistribution theorem

Applying twisted Poisson summation, our goal reduces to demonstrating the bound:

\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\backslash\{(0,0)\}}\widehat{\psi}_{\Lambda(a,b,c)}(\alpha,\beta)\widehat{\theta}_{(a,b,c)}\left(\frac{\alpha}{8a^{2}\cdot 12a},\frac{\beta}{12a}\right)=O\left(X^{1/4+\delta^{*}}\right)

(26)

where
\delta^{*}
is given by
12\delta_{s}+50\delta
. To establish this result, we will present a series of lemmas, followed by corollaries that bound specific terms in equation (
26
).

The following lemma establishes bounds on the Fourier coefficients of
\theta_{(a,b,c)}
when either
\alpha
or
\beta
is sufficiently large.

Lemma 3.7

Let consider
\beta\neq 0
and
M
and
K
non-negative integers, then:

\displaystyle|\widehat{\theta}_{(a,b,c)}(\alpha,\beta)|\ll

\displaystyle|R_{0}\cdot\frac{aX}{H}|\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{3}/t^{6}|\alpha|)^{K}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{2}|\beta|)^{M}}+

\displaystyle|R_{0}\lambda^{2}|\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{3}/t^{6}|\alpha|)^{K}}\cdot\frac{X^{2\delta_{s}(M+2)}}{(1+|\beta aX/H|)^{M}}+

\displaystyle|\frac{\lambda^{3}aX}{t^{6}H}|\cdot\frac{((R_{0}^{2}/(a^{3}X))^{K+2}+1)X^{4(K+2)\delta_{s}}}{(1+R_{0}|\alpha|)^{K}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{2}|\beta|)^{M}}+

\displaystyle|R_{0}\cdot\frac{aX}{H}|\cdot\frac{((R_{0}^{2}/(a^{3}X))^{K}+1)X^{4K\delta_{s}}}{(1+R_{0}|\alpha|)^{K}}\cdot\frac{X^{2M\delta_{s}}}{(1+|\beta aX/H|)^{M}}.

Proof:
For brevity, we define
g(R,I)
as:

g(R,I)\coloneqq\psi_{n}\left(\frac{at^{4}}{\lambda},\frac{bt^{2}}{\lambda},\frac{c}{\lambda},\frac{Rt^{6}}{\lambda^{3}},\frac{I}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

To prove the bound on
\widehat{g}(\alpha,\beta)
, we observe that
g(R,I)
depends on
R
and
I
through scaled arguments of
\psi_{n}
. Since
\psi_{n}
is smooth and compactly supported, its Fourier transform exhibits rapid decay. Specifically, the scaling factors
\frac{\lambda^{3}}{t^{6}}
in
R
and
\lambda^{2}
in
I
lead to the estimate:

\widehat{g}(\alpha,\beta)=O\left(\frac{\lambda^{5}}{t^{6}}\cdot\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{3}}{t^{6}}|\alpha|\right)^{K}}\cdot\frac{1}{\left(1+\lambda^{2}|\beta|\right)^{M}}\right),

where
K
and
M
are positive constants, and the implied constant depends on bounded support of
\psi_{n}
.

Let
h
be the smooth approximation of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{(a,b,c)}
as defined in Lemma
3.5
. Since
\theta
is the product of
h
and
g
, the convolution theorem states that its Fourier transform,
\widehat{\theta}
, is given by the convolution of their respective Fourier transforms
\widehat{h}
and
\widehat{g}
:

\widehat{\theta}(\alpha,\beta)=(\widehat{h}*\widehat{g})(\alpha,\beta).

Thus, we can bound
|\widehat{\theta}(\alpha,\beta)|
by

\big|\widehat{\theta}(\alpha,\beta)\big|\ll\Big|\int\int\widehat{g}(x,y)\,\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)\,dx\,dy\Big|,

where the integral form expresses the convolution explicitly.

We begin by decomposing the integral into four parts and bounding each term separately. First, we consider the portion of the integral over the region where
|x|>|\alpha|/2
and
|y|>|\beta|/2
. In this region, our goal is to establish an upper bound for the integral.

\int_{|x|>|\alpha|/2}\int_{|y|>|\beta|/2}\big|\widehat{g}(x,y)\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)\big|\,dx\,dy.

Since
|x|>|\alpha|/2
and
|y|>|\beta|/2
, we can directly apply the bounds established for
\widehat{g}
and
\widehat{h}
in this region. Specifically, we bound
\widehat{g}(x,y)
by

\big|\widehat{g}(x,y)\big|\ll\frac{\lambda^{5}}{t^{6}}\cdot\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{3}}{t^{6}}|x|\right)^{K+2}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{2}|y|)^{M+2}},

as derived earlier. For
\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)
, we apply a simple volume bound,
|\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)|\ll\text{Vol}\big({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{(a,b,c)}\big)
, since
h
is a smooth approximation of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{(a,b,c)}
. Combining these bounds, we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{|x|>|\alpha|/2}\int_{|y|>|\beta|/2}\big|\widehat{g}(x,y)\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)\big|\,dx\,dy\ll

\displaystyle\int_{|x|>|\alpha|/2}\int_{|y|>|\beta|/2}\Big|\frac{\lambda^{5}}{t^{6}}\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{3}}{t^{6}}|x|\right)^{K+2}}\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{2}|y|)^{M+2}}\cdot\text{Vol}\big({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{(a,b,c)}\big)\Big|dx\,dy\ll

\displaystyle\big|R_{0}\cdot\frac{aX}{H}\big|\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{3}/t^{6}|\alpha|)^{K}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{2}|\beta|)^{M}}.

This establishes the bound for the first term in the summation of the lemma.

Next, we consider the portion of the integral where
|x|>|\alpha|/2
but
|y|\leq|\beta|/2
. This condition implies that
|\beta-y|\geq|\beta|/2
. For
\widehat{g}(x,y)
, we apply the same bound as in the previous region, emphasizing its decay in
x
and setting
M=0
. Specifically, we have

\Big|\widehat{g}(x,y)\Big|\ll\frac{\lambda^{5}}{t^{6}}\cdot\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{3}}{t^{6}}|x|\right)^{K+2}}.

For
\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)
, we use the results from Lemma
3.5
, which show that
\widehat{h}(\alpha,\beta)
can be expressed as a product of three terms: the volume term
\text{Vol}\big(\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{(a,b,c)}\big)
, the Fourier transform
\widehat{\phi}
, and the Fourier transform
\widehat{T}_{m}
. Specifically, in this region, we apply the Fourier decay estimate for
\widehat{\phi}((\beta-y)aX/H)
from the proof of Lemma
3.5
. Since
\phi
is smooth and compactly supported as a good
\epsilon
-approximation, we obtain

\Big|\widehat{\phi}((\beta-y)aX/H)\Big|\ll\frac{X^{2\delta_{s}(M+2)}}{(1+|(\beta-y)aX/H|)^{M+2}}.

Combining these bounds, we obtain the following for the integral:

\displaystyle\int_{|x|>|\alpha|/2}\int_{|y|\leq|\beta|/2}\big|\widehat{g}(x,y)\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)\big|\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\ll\quad

\displaystyle\int_{|x|>|\alpha|/2}\int_{|y|\leq|\beta|/2}\frac{\lambda^{5}}{t^{6}}\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{3}}{t^{6}}|x|\right)^{K+2}}\cdot\text{Vol}({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{(a,b,c)})\cdot\frac{X^{2\delta_{s}(M+2)}}{(1+|(\beta-y)aX/H|)^{M+2}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\ll\quad

\displaystyle|R_{0}\lambda^{2}|\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{3}/t^{6}|\alpha|)^{K}}\cdot\frac{X^{2\delta_{s}(M+2)}}{(1+|\beta aX/H|)^{M}},

which provides the second bound in the lemma.

Now, consider the integral over
|x|\leq|\alpha|/2
and
|y|>|\beta|/2
. This assumption implies that
|\alpha-x|\geq|\alpha|/2
. For
\widehat{g}(x,y)
, we apply the bound focusing on the decay in
y
and setting
K=0
. Specifically, we have

\big|\widehat{g}(x,y)\big|\ll\frac{\lambda^{5}}{t^{6}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{2}|y|)^{M+2}}.

For
\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)
, we apply part (b) of Lemma
3.5
. we have:

\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)\ll\text{Vol}({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{(a,b,c)})\cdot\frac{X^{4\delta_{s}(K+2)}\Big(\big(\frac{R_{0}}{a^{3}X}\big)^{K+2}+1\Big)}{(1+|(\alpha-x)R_{0}|)^{K+2}}.

Combining these bounds, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{|x|\leq|\alpha|/2}\int_{|y|>|\beta|/2}\big|\widehat{g}(x,y)\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)\big|\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\ll\quad

\displaystyle\int_{|x|\leq|\alpha|/2}\int_{|y|>|\beta|/2}\frac{\lambda^{5}}{t^{6}}\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{2}|y|)^{M+2}}\cdot\text{Vol}({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{(a,b,c)})\cdot\frac{X^{4\delta_{s}(K+2)}\Big(\big(\frac{R_{0}}{a^{3}X}\big)^{K+2}+1\Big)}{(1+|(\alpha-x)R_{0}|)^{K+2}}dx\,dy

\displaystyle\ll\quad

\displaystyle\big|\frac{\lambda^{3}aX}{t^{6}H}\big|\cdot\frac{((R_{0}/(a^{3}X))^{K+2}+1)X^{4(K+2)\delta_{s}}}{(1+R_{0}|\alpha|)^{K}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{2}|\beta|)^{M}}

This gives the bound for the third part of the sum in the lemma.

The final part of the integral is over the region where
|x|\leq|\alpha|/2
and
|y|\leq|\beta|/2
. This condition implies that
|\alpha-x|\geq|\alpha|/2
and
|\beta-y|\geq|\beta|/2
. For
\widehat{g}(x,y)
, we use the decay bound with
K=2
and
M=2
, yielding:

\big|\widehat{g}(x,y)\big|\ll\frac{\lambda^{5}}{t^{6}}\cdot\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{3}}{t^{6}}|x|\right)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{2}|y|)^{2}}.

For
\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)
, we apply part (b) of Lemma
3.5
, then we have:

\big|\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)\big|\ll\text{Vol}({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{(a,b,c)})\cdot\frac{X^{4K\delta_{s}}(1+|R_{0}^{2}/(Xa^{3})|^{K})}{(1+|R_{0}\alpha|)^{K}}\cdot\frac{X^{2M\delta_{s}}}{(1+|aX\beta/H|)^{M}}.

Combining these bounds, we have

\displaystyle\int_{|x|\leq|\alpha|/2}\int_{|y|\leq|\beta|/2}\big|\widehat{g}(x,y)\widehat{h}(\alpha-x,\beta-y)\big|\,dx\,dy\ll\int_{|x|\leq|\alpha|/2}\int_{|y|\leq|\beta|/2}

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{5}}{t^{6}}\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{3}}{t^{6}}|x|\right)^{2}}\frac{1}{(1+\lambda^{2}|y|)^{2}}

\displaystyle\cdot\text{Vol}({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{(a,b,c)})\cdot\frac{X^{4K\delta_{s}}(1+|R_{0}^{2}/(Xa^{3})|^{K})}{(1+|R_{0}\alpha|)^{K}}

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{X^{2M\delta_{s}}}{(1+|aX\beta/H|)^{M}}\,dx\,dy.

This completes the proof of the theorem by providing the bound for the final part of the integral.

4
Asymptotic volume estimates

Davenport’s lemma states that, for any bounded semialgebraic set in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the number of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
points contained in the set is approximately equal to its volume, with an error term that depends on the volumes of its projections. This lemma has been applied in previous work to analyze
{\mathcal{O}^{(i)}_{\delta}(n,t,X)}
. However, we were unable to achieve the desired error bounds using this approach.

Through a detailed study of the semi-invariant space, we found that the number of points in
{\mathcal{O}^{(i)}_{\delta}(n,t,X)}
within each fiber can be effectively approximated by the volume of the fiber while also satisfying the required error bounds. In this section, we complete the proof of Theorem
2.2
by showing that summing the volumes of the fibers asymptotically recovers the volume of the entire space. Consequently, we approximate the number of points in
{\mathcal{O}^{(i)}_{\delta}(n,t,X)}
by its volume.

4.1
Relating the Sum of
N_{(a,b,c)}
to Volume

Our goal in this subsection is to approximate
{\mathcal{O}}_{\delta}(n,t,X)
using an integral. Specifically, we express
{\mathcal{O}}_{\delta}(n,t,X)
as the integral of
(nt)\cdot\Psi(X^{-1/2}\cdot f)\cdot{\rm ht}_{X}(f)
over
V_{\mathbb{R}}
. We establish that the total volume is well approximated by summing the volumes of the fibers over
(a,b,c)
and prove the following result:

Theorem 4.1

{\mathcal{O}^{(i)}_{\delta}(n,t,X)}=\int_{f\in V_{\delta}}(nt\cdot\Psi^{(i)})(X^{-1/2}\cdot f)\cdot{\rm ht}_{X}(f)\,df+O(X^{2-\epsilon^{\prime}}),

for some
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
.

Using the definitions of
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, and applying Lemma
2.18
, we obtain:

\sum_{(a,b,c)\in B^{\prime}_{X,\delta}}N_{(a,b,c)}(\theta_{1},X)\leq{\mathcal{O}^{(i)}_{\delta}(n,t,X)}+O_{\epsilon}(X^{2-2\delta+\epsilon})\leq\sum_{(a,b,c)\in B^{\prime}_{X,\delta}}N_{(a,b,c)}(\theta_{2},X).

To complete the proof of Theorem
4.1
, it suffices to establish the following proposition:

Proposition 4.2

Let
\delta
be sufficiently small, and set
\delta_{s}=22\delta
. For any
\theta
that is a good
\delta_{s}
-approximation, we have:

\sum_{(a,b,c)\in B_{X,\delta}^{\prime}}N_{(a,b,c)}(\theta,X)=\int_{f\in V_{\delta}}(nt\cdot\Psi)(X^{-1/2}\cdot f)\cdot{\rm ht}_{X}(f)\,df+O(X^{2-2\delta+\epsilon})+O(X^{7/4+500\delta}).

(27)

From this point onward, we set
\delta_{s}=22\delta
.

We prove this proposition by applying the Mean Value Theorem along with the lemmas from Section 2, which allow us to initially restrict our attention to
B^{\prime}_{X,\delta}
. Fix
(a,b,c)\in B^{\prime}_{X,\delta}
. By Theorem
3.1
, we approximate

as follows:

\sum_{(a,b,c)\in B^{\prime}_{X,\delta}}N_{(a,b,c)}(\theta,X)=\sum_{(a,b,c)\in B^{\prime}_{X,\delta}}\frac{1}{|8a^{2}\cdot 12a|}\int\int\theta_{(a,b,c)}(R,I)\,dR\,dI+O\ \Big(\sum_{(a,b,c)\in B^{\prime}_{X,\delta}}X^{1/4+50\delta+12\delta_{s}}\Big).

Since
a
,
b
, and
c
in
B^{\prime}_{X,\delta}
are
O(X^{1/2})
, the error term simplifies to
O_{\epsilon}(X^{7/4+50\delta+12\delta_{s}})
.

The following lemma allows us to neglect the smoothing effect of the height function induced by
\phi
.

Lemma 4.3

For any

in the set

Since the function

1.1
:

Let
S_{\infty}(F)
denote the collection of all
\mathbb{R}
-solvable binary quartic forms in
V_{\mathbb{R}}
whose invariants belong to
\text{Inv}_{\infty}(F)
. We define
M_{\infty}(V,F;X)
analogously as

M_{\infty}(V,F;X)\vcentcolon=\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}(I,J)\in{\rm Inv}_{\infty}(F)\\
h_{36}(I,J)<3\cdot 2^{4}X\end{subarray}}\frac{\#\left(E^{I,J}({\mathbb{R}})/2E^{I,J}({\mathbb{R}})\right)}{\#E^{I,J}({\mathbb{R}})[2]}\,dI\,dJ.

As mentioned earlier,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\in F\\
h_{e}(E)<X\end{subarray}}\left(\#S_{2}(E)-1\right)

is asymptotically equal to the number of locally soluble
{\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
-orbits on
S(F)
with height
h_{36}
bounded by
3\cdot 2^{4}X
and no rational linear factor, where each orbit
{\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})\cdot f
is counted with weight
1/m(f)
.

Thus, by Theorem
4.9
and the results of Propositions 3.6, 3.9, and 3.18 in
[
3
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\in F\\
h_{e}(E)<X\end{subarray}}\left(\#S_{2}(E)-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq N_{36}(V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\cap S_{\infty}(F);3\cdot 2^{4}X)\prod_{p}\int_{S_{p}(F)}\frac{1}{m_{p}(f)}df+o(X^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{27}{\rm Vol}({\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})/{\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}))M_{\infty}(V,F;X)\prod_{p}\left|\frac{2^{10}}{27}\right|_{p}{\rm Vol}({\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p}))M_{p}(V,F)+o(X^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{3^{4}\cdot 2^{10}}{2\cdot 27}{\rm Vol}({\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})/{\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}))\prod_{p}\left|\frac{2^{10}}{27}\right|_{p}{\rm Vol}({\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p}))M_{p}(V,F)+o(X^{2}).

Additionally, Theorem
5.6
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\in F\\
h_{e}(E)<X\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=M_{\infty}(F;X)\prod_{p}M_{p}(F)+o(X^{2})

\displaystyle=3^{4}X^{2}\prod_{p}M_{p}(F)+o(X^{2}).

Considering the ratios
\frac{M_{p}(V,F)}{M_{p}(F)}
, which are computed in
[
3
]
using results from
[
7
]
, we have

\frac{M_{p}(V,F)}{M_{p}(F)}=\frac{\int_{(I,J)\in\text{Inv}_{p}(F)}\frac{\#(E^{I,J}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})/2E^{I,J}(\mathbb{Q}_{p}))}{\#E^{I,J}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})[2]}dIdJ}{\int_{(I,J)\in\text{Inv}_{p}(F)}dIdJ}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }p\neq 2,\\
2&\text{if }p=2.\end{cases}

Thus, we conclude

\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\in F\\
h_{e}(E)<X\end{subarray}}\left(\#S_{2}(E)-1\right)}{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}E\in F\\
h_{e}(E)<X\end{subarray}}1}\leq{\rm Vol}({\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})/{\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}))\prod_{p}{\rm Vol}({\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})).

The constant on the right-hand side of the above expression is

2\zeta(2)\prod_{p}(1-p^{-2})=2,

which is the Tamagawa number of
{\rm PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})
.
