Registry data on congenital heart disease trajectories from fetal life through infancy empower patients, relatives and healthcare professionals and expose blind angles
Authors: Sara Hirani Lau-Jensen, Vibeke Elisabeth Hjortdal
Published: 2026

While we already have an understanding of the trajectories of babies who undergo different surgical/interventional procedures for congenital heart disease (CHD),1 much less is known about the trajectory of fetuses prenatally diagnosed and live-born babies who never receive surgical intervention. The study ‘Population-based cohort study of incident cases of structural congenital heart diseases in England from fetal life through infancy’ makes a valuable contribution by filling a gap in the current knowledge.2

The study very elegantly answers these questions by utilising and merging three different English registers containing prenatal CHD diagnoses, non-cardiac comorbidities, pregnancy termination, stillbirths, miscarriages, postnatal CHD diagnoses and CHD procedures. Socioeconomic variables were also included, adding further depth.

At first glance, the main results may not seem surprising: high prenatal detection rates (particularly for complex CHD), higher termination rates in fetuses with both complex CHD and non-cardiac comorbidities and more un-intervened babies with complex CHD, preterm births and/or non-cardiac comorbidities. However, the details reveal important nuances that open up new areas for research and clinical reflection.

The un-intervened CHD cases

One such result is the high mortality among babies who did not undergo surgery/intervention in their first year of life. Many of these infants had critical CHD, were born preterm and/or had non-cardiac comorbidities. This combination suggests they may represent a particularly vulnerable subgroup. However, as with all registry studies, the exact reasons why these babies were not intervened on and why they died are unclear. Were these decisions driven by clinical contraindications, parental preferences and palliative approaches or did some infants die before surgery/intervention could be attempted?

A quarter of the un-intervened deaths occurred in infants with very complex CHD, such as hypoplastic left heart syndrome, other univentricular hearts, truncus arteriosus, transposition of the great arteries, pulmonary atresia, double outlet right ventricle, or total anomalous pulmonary venous connection, but of the rest? Why did they die?

Half of the un-intervened deaths occurred in preterm infants. For the remainder, what other factors explain their poor outcomes?

Although the mortality is predominantly seen in complex CHD, infant mortality for live-born babies was also noticeable for simple CHD, such as isolated ventricular septal defect. We have previously shown that simple congenital heart defects such as atrial septal defect and ventricular septal defect have a higher long-term mortality than the background population, also those never intervened on.3 4 HR for mortality of unrepaired simple ventricular septal defect patients is 2.4, with cardiac-related death the most common cause.4

This un-intervened cohort highlights major gaps in knowledge, and more knowledge about this cohort carries large potential for improving risk stratification, family counselling and clinical decision-making.

Socioeconomic differences

Although we are aware of societal inequalities,5 6 it is surprising to see the magnitude of differences found between different socioeconomic groups, described in the article as differences in deprivation (from least deprived to most deprived).

Families in more deprived areas had slightly lower antenatal detection rates, lower termination rates, higher rates of miscarriage or stillbirth and higher proportions of live-born babies who remained un-intervened. The study is based on one European country, but it is likely that the same picture can be found in most Western countries.

The explanations are not clear. Is the lower termination rate a picture of a different culture in families in most deprived areas of England, or do differences in access to information, quality of counselling or implicit biases in medical advice play a role?

Is it because of this lower termination rate that more complex CHD fetuses end in stillbirths or miscarriages?

Could higher rates of miscarriage or stillbirth be a result of the lower termination rate?

Could the lower termination rate result in more live-born babies with conditions too complex to intervene on, thus resulting in more un-intervened babies from families living in the most deprived areas? Or does clinical selection bias play a role? The embedded idea that a family from a deprived area cannot overcome as much as a family from a less deprived area?

Whatever the explanation, the findings underscore the need for equity-focused strategies in prenatal care, counselling and surgical decision-making. They also highlight the importance of ensuring that deprivation does not translate into systematically worse outcomes for children with CHD.

Sex differences

Another intriguing finding is that boys were more likely than girls to undergo surgical intervention. The authors note that this may reflect greater CHD complexity in girls. This could be the explanation; however, the article’s data (Table 2) show no sex differences in rates of termination or stillbirth, which challenges this explanation. So why is this difference present? Could it be, as also noted in the article, because baby girls are smaller? Or is it because of cultural differences? Do boys get favouritism over girls–an unpleasant thought—but can we be completely sure of this? More research is needed to clarify this matter.

The prenatal trajectories

The authors report the prenatal trajectories—termination, stillborn or miscarried fetuses—as a percentage of the entire CHD cohort. Including the postnatally diagnosed babies, babies never at risk of termination, dilutes the risk.

Termination, stillborn or miscarried fetuses account for 24.6% of the prenatally diagnosed babies, whereas they only account for 15.7% of the entire CHD cohort.

Care pathways

A notable strength in this study is the well-performed diagrams illustrating different CHD patient trajectories. These illustrations bring the data to life and make complex information accessible to both clinicians and families. The authors have, with great consideration and exemplary success, collaborated closely with parent organisations to create informative tools. Diagrams showing patients’ trajectories like these should be something researchers strive to make in all research areas, also in research on different CHD comorbidities, long-term comorbidities like the risk of neurodevelopmental disorders or treatment trajectories, like procedures for different lymphatic comorbidities.

Conclusion

This study not only clarifies the early life course of CHD in England but also raises pressing new questions. Why do so many infants remain un-intervened, and can something be done to improve their outcomes? What explains the differences between boys and girls, and across socioeconomic groups? And how can the healthcare system ensure that care pathways are both equitable and transparent?

By combining three different registry datasets with simple analysis, the authors have set the stage for future research that could directly inform counselling of families and, ultimately, outcomes for children with CHD.
