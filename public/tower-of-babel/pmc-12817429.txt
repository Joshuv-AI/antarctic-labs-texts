An enhanced nutrition support system is associated with greater adequacy of enteral nutrition in long-term ICU patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mamoru Hayashi, Tsuzuki Michitaka, Masashi Kawanami, Eiko Tachibana, Takayoshi Tsukahara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNutritional management strategies for long-term ICU patients remain insufficiently established. This study evaluated the association between implementing an enhanced nutrition support system and enteral nutrition (EN) adequacy and related care indicators in patients with prolonged ICU stay.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective study, 85 adults with ICU stay ≥ 14 days were assigned to the conventional (January 2019 to October 2021; n = 48) or enhanced (November 2021 to March 2024; n = 37) groups based on hospital adoption of the enhanced nutrition support system, without patient-level prospective assignment or randomization. During the enhanced period, this system—comprising full-time dietitian assignment, standardized EN protocols for oral and tube feeding, systematic nutritional assessment, and intolerance management—was provided as routine care from ICU day 1. All variables were retrospectively abstracted from electronic medical records. Primary outcomes were presence of EN on day 14 (oral and/or tube), presence of oral intake on day 14, and daily EN adequacy (energy and protein sufficiency) over ICU days 1–14. Secondary outcomes included ratio of central venous catheter (CVC) placement days, antibiotic administration days ratio, positive blood culture rates, and incidence of gastrointestinal intolerance symptoms (diarrhea, constipation, vomiting).ResultsOn day 14, the enhanced group showed significantly higher rates of EN presence (78.4% vs. 52.1%, p = 0.013) and oral intake presence (35.1% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.013) than did the conventional group. Significant group-by-day interactions for energy and protein adequacy rates (both, p < 0.001) indicated steeper increases over time in the enhanced group. The median ratio of CVC placement days was lower in the enhanced group than in the conventional group (35.1% vs. 63.1%, p = 0.035). No significant differences were found between the groups in gastrointestinal intolerance symptoms, antibiotic administration days ratio, or positive blood culture rates.ConclusionsImplementation of an enhanced nutrition support system was associated with higher EN adequacy, higher day-14 EN and oral intake rates, and lower CVC use in long-term ICU patients, without increasing gastrointestinal intolerance. Background

Optimal nutritional management is recognized in international and national guidelines as a key component of care for ICU patients, aiming to preserve organ function, prevent complications, and support recovery [1–3]. In addition, as the importance of providing adequate energy and protein delivery, especially for patients at high nutritional risk or with prolonged ICU stay, is becoming more apparent, several guidelines and recent reviews have emphasized the importance of implementing systematic, multidisciplinary nutritional support systems, including dietitian-led care, to enhance the quality and consistency of ICU nutrition management [1–5]. Enteral nutrition (EN)—including oral and tube feeding—is prioritized over parenteral routes whenever gastrointestinal function permits, as this approach is associated with better outcomes and enhanced nutritional adequacy [1–3, 6, 7].

Importantly, contemporary ICUs are increasingly managing older, multi‑morbid, and complex patients, and a non‑trivial proportion of admissions evolve into prolonged ICU stay (e.g., ≥ 14 days). Recent multicenter reports have indicated that the length of ICU stay is ≥ 14 days in approximately 14% of ICU admissions, delineating a vulnerable subgroup in whom cumulative nutrient deficits and late complications become salient [8–12]. During prolonged admissions, metabolic demands and feeding tolerance can fluctuate, leading to increased risks related to nutrition including infection, muscle wasting, and gastrointestinal intolerance [13–15].

Although considerable research and many guidelines have focused on optimizing nutritional care in the early, acute phase of critical illness, relatively few studies have systematically examined how to maintain adequate nutrition and minimize complications throughout prolonged ICU admissions. Recently, several reports including scoping reviews have increasingly highlighted the potential benefits of multidisciplinary, team-based nutrition support systems, especially those involving standardized EN protocols and active collaboration between clinicians and dietitians [5, 16]. However, despite this growing body of evidence, the overall impact of such interventions on achieving sustained EN adequacy and optimizing care processes in patients with prolonged ICU stay remains insufficiently defined [5, 17]. Furthermore, guidance documents encourage regular re-evaluation of patient needs and proactive adaptation of nutrition strategies, as the clinical status evolves [1–3]. In this long‑stay subgroup, maintaining the delivery and safety of EN beyond the early phase is a system‑level challenge; a multidisciplinary, protocol‑based nutrition support, which includes standardized EN protocols for oral and tube feeding, systematic assessment, and coordinated responses to intolerance and device‑related issues, seeks to address this challenge [1–3, 5].

In November 2021, our ICU strengthened its multidisciplinary, protocol‑based nutrition support system by standardizing EN management for both oral and tube feeding and by implementing coordinated procedures for systematic assessment, monitoring, and response to feeding intolerance and device‑related issues. Despite the increasing global emphasis on such team-based nutritional approaches, there is a lack of comprehensive real-world data on their effects on patients experiencing extended ICU stay.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to clarify whether the implementation of an enhanced nutrition support system is associated with increased adequacy of EN up to day 14 of ICU admission, the rates of EN and oral intake achievement on day 14, and other key care process indicators in long-term ICU patients, including the ratio of central venous catheter (CVC) placement days and incidence of gastrointestinal intolerance symptoms.

Methods

Study design

This single-center, retrospective observational study was conducted in the emergency intensive care unit (EICU) of Nagoya Daiichi Hospital, Aichi Medical Center, Japanese Red Cross Society. Patients were not prospectively allocated; instead, cohorts were defined based on the admission period before and after an institution‑wide implementation of an enhanced nutrition support system in November 2021. All exposures and outcomes were obtained from the electronic medical record (EMR) after completion of routine care.

Study population

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to the EICU for at least 14 consecutive days between January 2019 and March 2024 were eligible. The exclusion criteria were severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infection and terminal illness under end-of-life care. Of the 810 ICU admissions during the study period, 97 patients met the duration criterion; after excluding 12 patients (eight with coronavirus disease 2019 [COVID-19] and four under end-of-life care), the data of 85 patients were included in the final analysis. Patient screening and exclusions are detailed to ensure methodological transparency.

Sample size and data management

A prospective sample size calculation was not performed for this retrospective observational study. All eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria during the study period were included, and inferences were based on effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Nutrition support system enhancement and grouping

The EICU utilizes an open model of care. In November 2021, the nutrition support system was enhanced through implementation of a comprehensive Enhanced Nutrition Support program featuring: (1) assignment of a full-time, dedicated EICU dietitian available weekdays 9:00 AM–5:00 PM with continuous presence in the EICU; (2) daily multidisciplinary rounds every weekday morning involving intensivists, nurses, pharmacists, physical therapists, clinical engineers, and dietitians; (3) implementation of a standardized Early Nutrition Initiation Flowchart for systematic assessment of hemodynamic stability and gastrointestinal function to determine optimal timing and route of nutrition initiation within 48 h of admission (Supplementary Fig. 1; see reference for detailed protocol description); (4) implementation of a standardized Enteral Nutrition Protocol providing step-by-step guidance for dose escalation, rate adjustment, gastrointestinal symptom monitoring, and evidence-based management of feeding intolerance (Supplementary Fig. 2); and (5) real-time nutritional monitoring with daily assessment and immediate intervention capability for all EICU patients. These standardized protocols enable proactive, systematic nutrition management uniformly applied to all patients from ICU day 1, representing a fundamental shift from consultation-based to integrated nutrition support. In this study, the enhanced nutrition support system was defined as a multi-faceted program involving full-time dietitian-led participation, standardized EN protocols for both oral and tube feeding, and systematic nutritional assessment and management of feeding intolerance or device-related complications. EN was defined as oral and/or tube feeding. Patients were grouped strictly based on admission period relative to an institution-wide policy implementation (conventional: January 2019 to October 2021; enhanced: November 2021 to March 2024). Enhanced nutrition support was provided as routine care after November 2021, without investigator-driven allocation. During the enhanced period, a protocolized, multidisciplinary, team‑based nutrition support was provided as routine care from ICU day 1 for all admissions.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were as follows: (i) EN on day 14 of ICU stay, defined as the proportion of all eligible long-stay ICU patients (ICU stay ≥ 14 days) with EN present on day 14, delivered via oral and/or tube routes (patients not receiving EN on day 14 remained in the denominator). This binary outcome was coded as present versus absent, irrespective of amount or concurrent parenteral nutrition (e.g., oral + tube; tube + parenteral; oral + parenteral; oral + tube + parenteral are all counted as EN present), and (ii) oral intake on day 14 of ICU stay, defined as the presence of any oral intake on that day (present vs absent), irrespective of amount and irrespective of concurrent tube or parenteral nutrition. Day‑14 EN presence was prespecified as a process indicator of enteral route establishment (oral and/or tube present on day 14), whereas day‑14 oral intake represented actual oral feeding achieved on that day and was dependent on swallowing safety, consciousness level, and airway status (e.g., absence of endotracheal intubation), making these outcomes conceptually distinct. Actual energy and protein intake represented comprehensive nutritional delivery from all sources: enteral nutrition (oral and tube feeding), parenteral nutrition (total parenteral nutrition and peripheral parenteral nutrition), and non-nutritional energy sources including propofol and dextrose-containing medications. Nutritional adequacy outcomes for energy and protein were calculated daily as (actual intake ÷ target) × 100%; based on international critical care nutrition guidelines, the target energy was 25 kcal/kg/day and target protein was 1.2 g/kg/day [1, 2]. Additionally, to assess sustained nutritional delivery over the prolonged-stay period, 14-day cumulative nutritional adequacy from enteral nutrition was calculated as total delivered energy (or protein) divided by total estimated energy (or protein) required during ICU days 1–14, expressed as both absolute daily intake (kcal/day, g/day) and percentage adequacy.

Analysis window

All included patients met the prolonged-stay criterion by design (ICU stay ≥ 14 days). To standardize observation and reduce early time-dependent bias, nutritional adequacy trajectories were assessed over ICU days 1–14, with day‑14 indicators summarizing status at the threshold day for prolonged stay; this window does not represent the full prolonged/chronic critical illness phase.

The secondary outcomes included the ratio of CVC placement days and of antibiotic administration days to total hospitalization days and incidence of positive blood cultures during hospitalization and of gastrointestinal intolerance symptoms (diarrhea: > 3 bowel movements/day, Bristol stool scale 6–7; constipation: no defecation for > 3 days or incomplete evacuation; vomiting: regurgitation after nutrition start) during the initial 14 ICU days. Because the nutrition support system intervention was applied from day 1 of ICU admission in each phase, adverse events (gastrointestinal [GI] intolerance symptoms) were assessed for the initial 14 days as an indicator of nutrition support safety under each protocol. This window allowed for consistent comparison of adverse event rates between the groups.

The secondary outcomes were selected a priori based on feasibility and reliability for retrospective extraction from the EMR and on a priori mechanistic relevance to nutrition processes and safety. To minimize overlap with the primary outcomes, they were systematically organized into four distinct domains: (1) device exposure (CVC days ratio), (2) microbiological signal (positive blood culture), (3) treatment exposure (antibiotic days ratio), and (4) feeding tolerance (GI intolerance symptoms) and were interpreted as pragmatic process/safety indicators.

As an exploratory analysis, we also evaluated in-hospital mortality to assess potential clinical outcomes of the Enhanced Nutrition Support system.

Data collection and measurement

Throughout both study periods, the nursing staff systematically documented intake percentages and volumes in the electronic medical record system after each meal, supplement administration, medication delivery, and feeding episode. All nutritional intake data were then retrospectively extracted from these electronic medical records for analysis.

Oral intake measurement was conducted using standardized procedures:

Oral Nutritional Supplements: Energy and protein calculated based on actual consumed volume multiplied by manufacturer-specified nutritional composition per unit volume.

Hospital meals: Energy and protein calculated based on measured consumption of carbohydrate staples (such as rice, rice porridge, and bread) and accompanying dishes (including protein sources such as meat and fish, vegetables, and soups) using standardized hospital portion sizes and institutional nutritional databases.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, according to data distribution. Categorical variables were compared by chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Patient baseline characteristics were assessed at ICU admission, including sex, age, height, body weight, body mass index (BMI), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score [18], Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score [19], Modified Nutrition Risk in Critically Ill (mNUTRIC) score [20], presence or absence of mechanical ventilation, and achievement of early EN (initiation within 48 h of ICU admission). Sequential multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed for day-14 enteral nutrition implementation and oral intake presence (Model 1: group only; Model 2: group + age; Model 3: group + age + APACHE II score + SOFA score). Covariates were selected based on their established roles in ICU nutritional risk assessment. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). Trends in EN adequacy (energy/protein) were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, incorporating group, day, and their interaction as fixed effects and patient as a random effect; the covariates included age, APACHE II score, and SOFA score. The ratios of CVC placement days and antibiotic administration days as well as incidence rates of gastrointestinal intolerance were compared using appropriate non-parametric or categorical tests. Regarding exploratory mortality analysis, we used chi-squared test for proportions, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log-rank test, and Cox proportional hazards regression adjusted for age, APACHE II score, and SOFA score. Since our dataset comprised no missing data, imputation was not required. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0; a p-value of < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant.

Primary outcomes

EN on day 14 (Presence) and oral intake on day 14 (Presence)

On day 14 of ICU stay, EN was present more frequently in the enhanced group than in the conventional group (78.4% [29/37] vs. 52.1% [25/48]; risk difference 0.263 [95% CI: 0.069–0.457], risk ratio 1.50 [95% CI: 1.09–2.07]; p = 0.013; denominator: all eligible long-stay ICU patients), where EN presence was defined as delivery via oral and/or tube routes irrespective of amount and irrespective of concurrent parenteral nutrition. The presence of oral intake on day 14 was also higher in the enhanced group (35.1% [13/37] vs. 12.5% [6/48]; risk difference 0.226 [95% CI: 0.046–0.406], risk ratio 2.81 [95% CI: 1.18–6.69]; p = 0.013), defined as present versus absent irrespective of amount and irrespective of concurrent tube or parenteral nutrition. Additionally, analysis of 14-day cumulative EN adequacy showed that the enhanced group achieved significantly higher energy delivery compared with the conventional group, both in absolute terms (282.1 vs. 121.4 kcal/day, p = 0.004) and adequacy percentage (16.0% vs. 6.9%, p = 0.006). Similarly, protein adequacy from EN was significantly higher in the enhanced group (16.3 vs. 4.8 g/day, p = 0.004; 22.2% vs. 7.4% adequacy, p = 0.007; Table 2). Logistic regression analysis, adjusted for age, APACHE II, and SOFA scores, showed that implementation of the enhanced nutrition support system was independently associated with increased odds for EN implementation on day 14 (adjusted odds ratio: 3.64, 95% CI: 1.32–10.0; Table 3). Multicollinearity assessment using variance inflation factors confirmed acceptable levels for all variables (all VIF values < 2.0), supporting model validity.

Secondary outcomes

Ratios of CVC placement days and antimicrobial agent use

Ratio of CVC placement days was significantly lower in the enhanced group (35.1% [IQR: 23.9–74.3]) than in the conventional group (63.1% [IQR: 30.3–100], p = 0.035). In contrast, the groups did not differ significantly in the number of days of antimicrobial therapy, with median ratios of 59.2% (IQR: 41.6–84.3) in the control group and 58.2% (IQR: 34.4–78.2) in the enhanced group (p = 0.654). No significant difference was observed in the rate of positive blood cultures during hospitalization between the enhanced group (24.3% [9/37]) and conventional group (37.5% [18/48]) (p = 0.196).

Symptoms of gastrointestinal intolerance

The incidence rates of diarrhea, constipation, and vomiting during the initial 14 days after EICU admission did not differ significantly between the groups. Specifically, diarrhea occurred in 25 patients (52.1%) in the control group and 18 patients (48.6%) in the enhanced group (p = 0.754). Constipation occurred in 34 patients (70.8%) in the control group and 29 patients (78.4%) in the enhanced group (p = 0.431). Vomiting occurred in eight patients (16.7%) in the control group and seven patients (18.9%) in the enhanced group (p = 0.787).

Exploratory mortality analysis

Regarding exploratory mortality analysis, hospital mortality was numerically lower in the enhanced group (8/37 [21.6%]) than in the conventional group (19/48 [39.6%]); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (χ2 = 3.11, p = 0.078). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated a consistent trend (log-rank, p = 0.099). Using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression adjusted for age, APACHE II, and SOFA scores, the enhanced group showed a hazard ratio of 0.502 (95% CI: 0.214–1.177, p = 0.113; Table 5).

Discussion

This study found that strengthening the nutrition support system in the EICU was associated with higher EN adequacy and changes in care process indicators among long-term ICU patients. The implementation of a multidisciplinary, protocol-driven nutrition management system was associated with higher day‑14 EN and oral intake presence, increased energy and protein adequacy over time, and lower rates of CVC use, without increasing gastrointestinal intolerance symptoms.

These findings add to the existing body of evidence supporting structured, multidisciplinary nutrition support, emphasizing that, in addition to early initiation of EN, sustained and individualized nutritional interventions are essential for patients with prolonged ICU stay [1–4, 11, 12]. Although early EN initiation rates within 48 h did not differ significantly between the groups, both EN and oral intake achievement rates on day 14 were higher in the enhanced group. Furthermore, continuous intervention was associated with a progressive increases in EN adequacy, emphasizing the clinical importance of providing ongoing, patient-centered nutrition support to facilitate feeding and recovery in critically ill long-term ICU patients. However, these favorable outcomes must be interpreted in the context of international performance standards to properly assess both the progress achieved and remaining opportunities for optimization.

When contextualized against international benchmarks, our findings reveal both relative differences and absolute limitations. International cluster randomized trials demonstrate that with evidence-based feeding guidelines, mean enteral nutrition initiation times can be achieved within 0.75 to 1.37 days [21]. However, large-scale prevalence studies indicate substantial proportions of ICU patients globally are not fed during the first day of admission [22]. Early enteral nutrition rates within 48 h in our study remained suboptimal, consistent with these commonly observed global challenges. Regarding nutritional adequacy, although values were higher in the enhanced group, absolute levels remained below those reported in specialized studies where exclusive enteral nutrition protocols achieve approximately 82% adequacy for both energy and protein [23]. These gaps likely reflect multifaceted challenges including gastric feeding routes requiring careful management of gastrointestinal intolerance symptoms, the open ICU structure potentially limiting consistent nutrition strategy implementation, and insufficient staff education regarding nutrition management, among others. These comparisons underscore that achieving optimal nutritional standards requires continuous refinement of feeding strategies while systematically addressing implementation barriers.

Despite these challenges, consistent with prior research, our results highlight the critical roles of full-time dietitian involvement and standardized protocols in enhancing nutritional outcomes and process measures within the ICU [5, 16]. Notably, the reduction in CVC placement days observed in our cohort may reflect decreased reliance on parenteral nutrition, supporting infection prevention and patient safety priorities [24–26]. Additionally, despite increased EN delivery, the incidence of gastrointestinal intolerance was not elevated, suggesting that protocolized and individualized feeding regimens, as endorsed by contemporary guidelines [1–3], can enhance EN adequacy without increasing adverse events [17, 27–29]. Regarding clinical practice, these findings support the allocation of dedicated, full-time dietitians and integration of protocol-based nutrition support as institutional standards for ICUs managing high-risk, long-stay patients. The practical demonstration in our study of multidisciplinary teamwork and systematic protocols safely enhancing core nutritional targets in this vulnerable cohort can be valuable in guiding quality improvement initiatives and national guideline recommendations across diverse healthcare settings.

This study found no significant differences in antibiotic use between the two groups, highlighting that factors beyond nutritional management such as infection severity or stewardship interventions are influential in antimicrobial prescribing [30–35]. This observation reflects the complex, multifactorial reality of ICU patient care and emphasizes that nutritional interventions; however, foundational, must be integrated with other systemic quality measures to comprehensively improve clinical outcomes.

Using exploratory mortality analysis, we observed a numerical trend toward lower hospital mortality in the enhanced group, though statistical significance was not achieved. The consistency of this trend across multiple analytical approaches suggests the possibility of survival benefits that warrant further investigation in adequately powered prospective studies. These preliminary findings align with recent reports demonstrating mortality improvements with systematic nutrition support interventions in critically ill patients [36]. However, several limitations should be considered, including the limited number of mortality events and the retrospective design, among others.

Our study has certain limitations. First, this study was a retrospective, single‑center observational analysis, which limits causal inference and might result in potential residual confounding. Since the cohorts were defined based on calendar periods around a system‑level policy change, residual confounding and secular trends intrinsic to retrospective period comparisons could not be fully excluded. In addition, both “conventional” and “enhanced” periods overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, which might have introduced unmeasured confounding related to institutional protocol, staffing, or resources that we could not control. We were unable to directly assess catheter-related bloodstream infections; instead, we compared the incidence of positive blood cultures, which might not fully reflect CVC-related infection risk. We acknowledge that antibiotic exposure and blood culture positivity are not specific indicators. Therefore, we did not treat them as definitive infection endpoints. Furthermore, the limited sample size and length of observation restricted our ability to analyze long-term outcomes (such as post-discharge survival or physical/psychological recovery), while some secondary outcomes (e.g., gastrointestinal intolerance, antibiotic use) might have been influenced by factors beyond nutrition support. Although all participants were long‑stay by design (ICU stay ≥ 14 days), adequacy trajectories were analyzed for days 1–14 to enable standardized comparisons; extending the observation period beyond day 14 to assess durability and late adverse events will be an important focus of future work. Future multi-institutional, prospective research should include larger cohorts and comprehensive long-term follow-up and should validate these findings and assess broader applicability.

Future research should evaluate the effects of enhanced nutritional support systems on patient-centered outcomes, such as quality of life, functional recovery, and post-intensive care syndrome—areas not addressed in our study but essential for long-term survivors [9, 37–40]. In addition, developing objective gastrointestinal function assessment tools, individualized phase-adapted nutrition protocols, and integrating early mobilization or rehabilitation within multidisciplinary care may further optimize outcomes for long-term ICU patients.

In summary, the results of this study indicate that the adoption of a strengthened, protocol-driven nutrition support system in the EICU was associated with greater adequacy and continuity of EN in long-term ICU patients, with benefits for key care process indicators and without increasing adverse events. These findings support continued investment in multidisciplinary, patient-centered nutritional management as an institutional standard of care for ICU populations at high nutritional risk.

Conclusions

The implementation of an enhanced, multidisciplinary, protocol‑driven nutrition support system in the EICU was associated with higher EN adequacy, higher day‑14 EN and oral‑intake presence, and lower CVC use in long‑term ICU patients, without increasing gastrointestinal intolerance. These findings support the adoption of such system-level interventions as a standard strategy for high-risk ICU populations. However, future studies should assess the impact of this system on patient-centered outcomes, such as post-intensive care syndrome.
