Near-infrared-activated CuTA@PDA@PTH nanozymes remodel the osteoarthritic microenvironment for coordinated cartilage protection and subchondral bone repair
Authors: Zhao Lin, Jiayao Zhang, Mingjuan Zhang, Mukun Xiao, Hanhao Dai, Yibin Su, Haiqi Ding, Dingqiang Chen, Linhai Yang, Jie Xu, Jun Luo, Yunlong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a progressive whole-joint disorder involving cartilage degeneration, oxidative stress, synovial inflammation, and subchondral bone remodeling. Current treatments mainly provide symptomatic relief and rarely restore joint microenvironmental homeostasis. Here, we developed a multifunctional near-infrared (NIR)-responsive CuTA@PDA@PTH nanozyme, termed CPP, for OA therapy. CPP was constructed through copper–tannic acid coordination, polydopamine coating, and PTH 1–34 incorporation, integrating antioxidant catalytic activity, photothermal responsiveness, and peptide-mediated bioactivity. CPP exhibited uniform morphology, good dispersibility, stable photothermal behavior, and broad-spectrum ROS-scavenging activity with SOD-like and CAT-like functions. In vitro, NIR-activated CPP reduced H₂O₂-induced ROS accumulation, lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis in chondrocytes, while promoting proliferation and restoring cartilage matrix homeostasis. CPP-NIR also downregulated inflammatory and catabolic markers, attenuated M1-associated activation, favored an anti-inflammatory M2-like phenotype, and enhanced BMSC migration and osteogenic differentiation under oxidative stress. In a DMM-induced mouse OA model, intra-articular CPP combined with NIR irradiation prolonged joint retention, attenuated cartilage degeneration, reduced osteophyte formation, improved subchondral bone microarchitecture, and alleviated synovial inflammation. These findings support NIR-activated CPP as a multi-axis nanozyme platform for redox, immune, cartilage, and subchondral bone microenvironment regulation in OA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04841-0.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is among the most common degenerative joint diseases and is a leading cause of chronic pain, disability, and reduced quality of life in older adults. Global burden estimates indicate that approximately 595 million individuals were living with OA in 2020, with prevalence expected to rise further as populations age and obesity becomes more common [1, 2]. OA is now understood as a whole-joint disease rather than isolated cartilage wear, involving progressive cartilage loss, subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, synovial inflammation, and pathological crosstalk among joint tissues [2–4]. Although analgesics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, intra-articular injections, and joint replacement can relieve symptoms, these interventions have limited ability to modify structural disease progression. Strategies capable of restoring joint microenvironmental homeostasis are therefore urgently needed.

Chondrocytes are the only resident cells in articular cartilage and are responsible for maintaining the extracellular matrix (ECM), including collagen type II and aggrecan [5]. During OA progression, mechanical overload, inflammatory mediators, and oxidative injury disturb the anabolic-catabolic balance of chondrocytes. This imbalance is characterized by reduced expression of matrix-associated markers such as SOX9, Col2a1, and ACAN, together with increased production of matrix-degrading enzymes such as MMP13 and ADAMTS5 [6–8]. ECM fragments generated during degradation can further amplify local inflammation and accelerate cartilage destruction. Thus, preserving the chondrocyte phenotype, promoting matrix synthesis, and restraining catabolic enzyme activity remain central objectives in OA therapy.

Oxidative stress is a major driver of OA pathology. Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide anion, hydroxyl radicals, and hydrogen peroxide, promote mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, apoptosis, and inflammatory pathway activation in chondrocytes [9, 10]. Mitochondria are both a principal source and a vulnerable target of ROS. Once mitochondrial homeostasis is impaired, mitochondrial membrane potential decreases, ATP production is compromised, and ROS generation is further amplified, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of cartilage damage [11]. Accordingly, ROS-scavenging approaches that restore redox balance and mitochondrial function have gained increasing attention for OA treatment.

Synovial inflammation and macrophage polarization also shape the OA microenvironment [12]. Synovial macrophages may adopt a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, marked by iNOS, CD86, IL-1β, and related mediators, or an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, marked by Arg-1, CD206, IL-4, and tissue-repair-associated factors. M1 macrophages aggravate synovitis, cartilage catabolism, and pain, whereas M2 macrophages support inflammation resolution and repair [13]. Promoting a shift from M1 toward M2 macrophages may therefore help rebuild an anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative joint milieu.

Subchondral bone remodeling is another important feature of OA. Abnormal bone turnover, osteophyte formation, and altered subchondral microarchitecture can change joint biomechanics and accelerate cartilage degeneration [14, 15]. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) participate in bone remodeling and repair; however, oxidative and inflammatory stresses can impair their migration and osteogenic differentiation, thereby worsening subchondral bone dysfunction [16, 17]. A therapeutic strategy that protects cartilage while restoring BMSC-mediated bone repair may offer broader benefits for whole-joint homeostasis.

Nanozymes have emerged as attractive enzyme-mimicking materials owing to their stability, tunable catalytic activity, and modular design. Several antioxidant nanozyme systems have already shown promise in OA models [18–22]. Mitochondria-targeting Mn3O4@PDA@Pd-SS31 nanozymes, for instance, were reported to reduce mitochondrial ROS, restore mitochondrial function, and attenuate OA progression [20]. Molybdenum nanodots with antioxidant and photothermal activities protected cartilage and promoted subchondral bone regeneration [23]. Bilirubin-loaded supramolecular nanoparticles also alleviated OA by regulating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses through Nrf2/HO-1/GPX4 and NF-κB-related mechanisms [24]. These studies support the value of antioxidant nanomaterials for reshaping the oxidative and inflammatory OA microenvironment.

Near-infrared (NIR)-responsive biomaterials add a further level of spatiotemporal control. Polydopamine (PDA), a mussel-inspired polymer, combines biocompatibility, strong NIR absorption, efficient photothermal conversion, and versatile surface chemistry [25, 26]. PDA-based nanoplatforms have been used to enhance ROS scavenging, modulate immune responses, protect chondrocytes, and coordinate cartilage-subchondral bone repair. In parallel, PTH- or PTH-related peptide-based strategies have shown potential for promoting chondrocyte proliferation, cartilage matrix synthesis, and bone remodeling, partly through PI3K/AKT-associated signaling [27, 28]. Nevertheless, single-function antioxidants or simple delivery systems are unlikely to address the multifactorial nature of OA. A platform that integrates antioxidant catalysis, NIR responsiveness, chondroprotective bioactivity, immunomodulation, and subchondral bone regulation is therefore highly desirable.

In this work, we designed a multifunctional CuTA@PDA@PTH nanozyme, termed CPP, for NIR-responsive OA therapy. In this system, copper–tannic acid coordination assemblies provide antioxidant nanozyme activity, PDA improves structural stability and enables photothermal activation, and PTH 1–34 introduces PTH1R-related bioactivity. We first characterized the physicochemical properties, photothermal performance, and ROS-scavenging activity of CPP. We then examined its effects on chondrocyte proliferation, oxidative injury, mitochondrial function, inflammatory responses, macrophage polarization, and BMSC osteogenic differentiation in vitro. Transcriptomic analysis was used to explore molecular mechanisms, with emphasis on ECM regulation, ROS metabolism, MAPK signaling, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR-associated pathways. Finally, CPP combined with NIR irradiation was evaluated in a DMM-induced mouse OA model. This study proposes a multifunctional nanotherapeutic approach that simultaneously targets oxidative stress, cartilage matrix imbalance, synovial inflammation, macrophage polarization, and subchondral bone remodeling. Compared with previously reported antioxidant nanozymes or PDA-based photothermal carriers, the present CPP system emphasizes the integration of catalytic redox regulation, NIR-responsive activation, and PTH1R-related peptide bioactivity in one material, thereby targeting cartilage, immune, and subchondral bone compartments simultaneously rather than functioning as a single antioxidant or heating platform (Scheme 1).

Results

Preparation and characterization of CuTA@PDA@PTH

In this study, CuTA@PDA@PTH nanozymes, hereafter referred to as CPP nanozymes, were fabricated through a stepwise assembly strategy. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, CuTA nanozymes were first constructed through the coordination interaction between copper ions and tannic acid. Tannic acid contains abundant phenolic hydroxyl groups, which can coordinate with metal ions to form a stable network and may also endow the system with antioxidant potential. Subsequently, a polydopamine (PDA) layer was coated onto the surface of CuTA to obtain CuTA@PDA nanoparticles, referred to as CP. The introduction of PDA was expected to improve nanoparticle stability and biocompatibility, while also conferring near-infrared (NIR)-responsive photothermal properties. Finally, PTH 1–34 was further incorporated into CP to obtain the final CPP nanozymes. This nanoplatform integrates the catalytic antioxidant capacity of CuTA, the photothermal conversion ability of PDA, and the bioactive function of PTH 1–34, providing a material basis for subsequent osteoarthritic microenvironment regulation and cartilage protection. Consistent with this design, the supplemental CuTA synthesis schematic further illustrates that dispersed Cu²⁺ ions and TA motifs undergo coordination assembly to generate a CuTA nanozyme framework, providing a visual basis for the subsequent construction of CP and CPP (Figure S1).

The morphology of the nanozymes at different preparation stages was observed by TEM and SEM. TEM images showed that the initially synthesized CuTA displayed a thin sheet-like or lamellar structure, suggesting the successful formation of CuTA coordination assemblies (Fig. 1B). After PDA coating, the obtained CP nanoparticles showed an obvious morphological change, becoming more compact and gradually forming a spherical structure, indicating that PDA was successfully deposited on the surface of CuTA and modulated its nanostructure (Fig. 1C). After further incorporation of PTH 1–34, CPP maintained a relatively uniform spherical morphology without obvious aggregation, suggesting that PTH modification did not disrupt the structural stability of the nanoparticles (Fig. 1D). SEM imaging further confirmed that CPP exhibited good dispersibility and regular particle morphology (Fig. 1E). These findings indicate that CPP nanozymes maintained favorable nanoscale structural features during the stepwise construction process, which may benefit subsequent cellular uptake and biological function. To further verify the elemental composition and spatial distribution of CPP, HAADF-STEM and elemental mapping were performed. The HAADF image showed clear nanoparticle contours and good dispersion of CPP nanozymes (Fig. 1F). The corresponding elemental mapping demonstrated that C, Cu, N, and O were uniformly distributed throughout the CPP nanozymes (Fig. 1G). Among these elements, Cu was mainly derived from the CuTA coordination core, C and O were mainly associated with tannic acid, PDA, and PTH 1–34, while N was primarily attributed to the introduction of PDA and PTH 1–34. These elemental distribution results further confirmed the successful integration of CuTA, PDA, and PTH-related components into a single nanoplatform. In addition, AFM images revealed the typical nanoscale morphology of CPP, and the corresponding thickness distribution analysis showed a representative thickness of approximately 24.8 nm (Fig. 1H, I), further indicating that CPP nanozymes possessed a thin and stable nanostructure. In addition to TEM characterization, SEM observation of bare CuTA showed stacked nanosheet-/flake-like aggregates with nanoscale dimensions, further supporting the successful formation of CuTA coordination nanostructures (Figure S2). Energy-dispersive elemental analysis of CPP further detected characteristic C, O, Cu, and N signals, confirming the elemental composition and internal distribution of the composite nanozyme (Figure S3).

To clarify the chemical composition and structural characteristics of CPP, FT-IR, XRD, DLS, zeta potential, and XPS analyses were further performed. The FT-IR spectra showed that CuTA, CP, and CPP exhibited characteristic absorption peaks. After PDA coating and PTH 1–34 modification, several absorption bands associated with phenolic hydroxyl groups, aromatic rings, catechol structures, and amide bonds changed, suggesting the successful introduction of PDA and PTH into the CuTA nanosystem (Fig. 1J). XRD analysis showed that, compared with CuTA, the characteristic diffraction peaks of CP and CPP were weakened or broadened, which may be attributed to the formation of an amorphous PDA layer and the incorporation of peptide components. This result indicates that the crystalline features of CuTA were partially altered after surface modification (Fig. 1K). DLS analysis showed that CPP possessed a nanoscale hydrodynamic size distribution with a relatively narrow distribution, indicating good colloidal dispersibility in aqueous solution (Fig. 1L). Zeta potential measurements showed a stepwise change in surface charge from CuTA to CP and CPP, reflecting changes in surface properties after PDA coating and PTH 1–34 modification and further supporting the successful construction of CPP (Fig. 1M). XPS was then used to further analyze the elemental composition and chemical bonding states of CPP nanozymes. The XPS survey spectrum confirmed the presence of C, Cu, N, and O elements in CPP, consistent with the elemental mapping results (Fig. 1N). The high-resolution C 1 s spectrum showed characteristic peaks corresponding to C–C/C = C, C–O/C–N, and C = O bonds, indicating the presence of polyphenol-, PDA-, and peptide-related organic structures in CPP (Fig. 1O). The Cu 2p spectrum confirmed the existence of copper species, demonstrating that the CuTA catalytic core was retained after PDA coating and PTH modification (Fig. 1P). The N 1 s spectrum further indicated the successful introduction of nitrogen-containing structures, mainly derived from PDA and PTH 1–34 (Fig. 1Q). The O 1 s spectrum verified the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups, such as hydroxyl and carbonyl groups, which may be closely associated with metal coordination, antioxidant reactions, and subsequent biointerface interactions (Fig. 1R). Therefore, the FT-IR, XRD, zeta potential, and XPS results collectively confirmed the successful preparation of CuTA@PDA@PTH nanozymes. UV–vis spectra further showed that both CuTA and CPP exhibited characteristic ultraviolet absorption associated with TA-derived aromatic/polyphenolic structures, while CPP displayed a slightly higher absorbance after PDA coating and PTH incorporation, providing additional evidence for successful surface modification (Figure S4).

Given the excellent NIR absorption and photothermal conversion properties of PDA, the photothermal performance of CPP was further evaluated under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation. PBS showed only a slight temperature increase under NIR irradiation, whereas CuTA, CP, and CPP exhibited different degrees of temperature elevation. Among them, CPP showed a more pronounced temperature increase, indicating that PDA coating and the formation of the CPP nanostructure endowed the system with favorable NIR-responsive photothermal conversion ability (Fig. 1S, W). Further analysis of CPP at different concentrations showed that, under the same laser power density, the temperature elevation gradually increased with increasing CPP concentration, demonstrating a clear concentration-dependent photothermal effect (Fig. 1T). Similarly, when the CPP concentration was fixed, the temperature increase became more pronounced as the 808 nm laser power density increased, indicating a power density-dependent photothermal behavior (Fig. 1U). These findings suggest that the photothermal effect of CPP can be effectively controlled by adjusting the nanoparticle concentration and laser power density.

Photothermal stability is an important parameter for evaluating whether photothermal materials are suitable for subsequent therapeutic applications. Therefore, CPP was subjected to repeated NIR irradiation and natural cooling cycles. After multiple heating–cooling cycles, CPP maintained stable and reproducible temperature profiles without obvious attenuation in photothermal performance (Fig. 1V), indicating excellent photothermal stability. Under the irradiation parameters used in this study, PBS showed only a limited temperature increase, whereas CPP produced a controllable mild photothermal response, supporting the use of NIR as an external trigger for CPP activation rather than as an independent therapeutic intervention. Therefore, the biological effects observed in the CPP-NIR group are interpreted as CPP-mediated NIR-enhanced effects.

Together, these findings show that CPP nanozymes were successfully prepared and exhibited uniform nanoscale morphology, good dispersibility, defined elemental composition and chemical structure, as well as stable NIR-responsive photothermal performance. These physicochemical properties provide a solid experimental foundation for subsequent evaluation of antioxidant activity, chondrocyte protection, and therapeutic potential in osteoarthritis.

Characterization of the antioxidant properties of CPP nanozymes

Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation is a key pathological factor contributing to chondrocyte dysfunction, extracellular matrix degradation, and osteoarthritis progression. Therefore, the antioxidant properties of CPP nanozymes were systematically evaluated using multiple radical-scavenging and enzyme-like activity assays. First, DPPH, ABTS, and PTIO were used as representative radicals to assess the broad-spectrum antioxidant capacity of CPP. As shown in Fig. 2A–C, the characteristic UV–vis absorption peaks of DPPH, ABTS, and PTIO gradually decreased with increasing CPP concentrations. The corresponding quantitative results further confirmed that CPP exhibited concentration-dependent scavenging activity against these three radicals (Fig. 2D–F). These findings indicate that CPP possesses effective free radical-scavenging capacity, which may be attributed to the redox-active CuTA core and the abundant phenolic/catechol groups derived from tannic acid and PDA. Since superoxide anion radicals (•O₂⁻) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) are important ROS species involved in oxidative injury, the SOD-like and CAT-like activities of CPP were further investigated. The •O₂⁻ scavenging assay showed that CPP displayed stronger superoxide radical scavenging ability than CuTA and CP, as evidenced by the reduced UV–vis absorption signal and quantitative analysis (Fig. 2G, H). This finding suggests that the final CPP formulation possessed enhanced SOD-like activity, possibly due to the synergistic effects of copper-based catalytic centers, PDA-mediated electron transfer, and improved nanoparticle dispersion. In addition, CPP effectively decomposed H₂O₂, demonstrating evident CAT-like activity (Fig. 2I). Real-time oxygen generation assays further showed that CPP catalyzed the decomposition of H₂O₂ into O₂ in a concentration-dependent manner, as reflected by the gradually increased oxygen evolution curves and net oxygen output (Fig. 2J, K). These findings suggest that CPP can not only remove H₂O₂ but also generate oxygen during the catalytic process, which may help improve the oxidative and hypoxic microenvironment associated with osteoarthritic cartilage injury.

To directly verify the ROS-scavenging ability of CPP, electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was performed. Compared with CuTA, CPP substantially reduced the characteristic ESR signals of hydroxyl radicals (•OH), indicating its superior •OH scavenging capacity (Fig. 2L). Similarly, CPP also weakened the ESR signals corresponding to •O₂⁻, further confirming its efficient superoxide radical elimination ability (Fig. 2M). These ESR results were consistent with the UV–vis-based antioxidant assays and provided direct evidence that CPP could eliminate multiple ROS species.

Overall, CPP nanozymes exhibited comprehensive antioxidant properties, including broad-spectrum radical-scavenging activity, SOD-like activity, CAT-like activity, H₂O₂ decomposition ability, and oxygen-generating performance. Mechanistically, CPP can scavenge excessive ROS by eliminating •OH and •O₂⁻ and catalyzing H₂O₂ decomposition into H₂O and O₂ (Fig. 2N). These findings demonstrate that CPP nanozymes may effectively remodel the oxidative stress microenvironment and provide a promising antioxidant nanoplatform for subsequent chondrocyte protection and osteoarthritis therapy.

In Vitro evaluation of the biocompatibility and chondrocyte behavior of CPP nanozymes

To evaluate the potential biomedical applicability of CPP nanozymes, their cytocompatibility and effects on chondrocyte behavior were investigated using ATDC5 chondrocytes. First, the cell viability of ATDC5 cells cultured with different concentrations of CPP nanozymes was assessed. As shown in Fig. 3A, CPP exhibited good cytocompatibility over the tested concentration range of 12.5–200 µg/mL, and no obvious concentration-dependent cytotoxicity was observed. Live/dead staining further confirmed the good biocompatibility of CPP. After co-culture for 1 and 3 days, most chondrocytes in the CP, CPP, and CPP-NIR groups showed strong green fluorescence, with very few dead cells observed, indicating that CPP nanozymes did not induce apparent cytotoxic effects during short-term culture (Fig. 3B). In addition, the cellular uptake assay showed that Cy5.5-labeled CPP produced stronger intracellular Cy5.5 fluorescence than free Cy5.5 dye after washing, supporting CPP-associated cellular internalization and excluding substantial nonspecific dye retention (Figure S5).

Since maintaining chondrocyte viability and proliferation is essential for cartilage repair, the effects of CPP nanozymes on chondrocyte proliferation were further examined. EdU staining showed that the number of EdU-positive cells increased after CPP treatment, and this effect was further enhanced under NIR irradiation (Fig. 3C, E). These findings suggest that CPP nanozymes could promote chondrocyte proliferation, while mild photothermal stimulation may further amplify this pro-proliferative effect. Flow cytometry analysis of the cell cycle distribution also supported this finding. Compared with the control and CP groups, CPP-treated cells showed an increased proportion of cells in the G2/M phase, and the CPP-NIR group exhibited the highest G2/M proportion, indicating enhanced cell cycle progression and proliferative activity (Fig. 3D, H).

To further verify the influence of CPP nanozymes on chondrocyte proliferation and PTH-related biological signaling, immunofluorescence staining of Ki-67 and PTH1R was performed. Ki-67 staining showed that CPP treatment substantially increased the proliferation-associated fluorescence signal compared with the control and CP groups, and the CPP-NIR group showed the strongest Ki-67 expression (Fig. 3F, I). Similarly, PTH1R fluorescence intensity was elevated in the CPP group and further increased after NIR irradiation, indicating that PTH 1–34 incorporated in CPP may enhance PTH1R-related signaling in chondrocytes (Fig. 3G, J). Western blot analysis further confirmed these observations. Compared with the control and CP groups, CPP and CPP-NIR treatment upregulated the expression of SOX9, Hsp70, and PTH1R, with the most obvious increase observed in the CPP-NIR group (Fig. 3K). These findings suggest that CPP nanozymes, especially when combined with NIR irradiation, can promote chondrocyte proliferation and maintain chondrogenic phenotype-related activity, possibly through PTH1R-associated responses, photothermal-induced Hsp70 expression, and SOX9-mediated chondrogenic regulation. The supplemental Western blot analysis further supported these results, showing increased SOX9, Hsp70, and PTH1R expression after CPP treatment, particularly under NIR irradiation (Figure S6).

Together, these findings show that CPP nanozymes possess good in vitro biocompatibility and can positively regulate ATDC5 chondrocyte behavior by improving cell viability, promoting proliferation, facilitating cell cycle progression, and enhancing PTH1R/SOX9-associated chondroprotective signaling. The synergistic effects of PTH 1–34 delivery and NIR-responsive photothermal stimulation may provide a favorable microenvironment for subsequent cartilage protection and osteoarthritis treatment (Fig. 3L).

In Vitro ROS-scavenging activity of CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation in chondrocytes

To further evaluate the intracellular antioxidant effect of CPP nanozymes, an H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress model was established in ATDC5 chondrocytes. Intracellular ROS levels were first detected using DCFH-DA staining. As shown in Fig. 4A, H₂O₂ stimulation induced strong green fluorescence, indicating excessive ROS accumulation in chondrocytes. Compared with the H₂O₂ group, CP and CPP treatment reduced the intracellular ROS signal to different extents, whereas CPP combined with NIR irradiation showed the strongest ROS-scavenging effect. Quantitative fluorescence analysis further confirmed that the CPP-NIR group significantly decreased ROS levels compared with the H₂O₂-treated group and the non-irradiated CPP group (Fig. 4C). Flow cytometry analysis of DCFH-DA fluorescence showed a consistent trend, demonstrating that CPP nanozymes, especially under NIR irradiation, could effectively alleviate H₂O₂-induced intracellular oxidative stress (Fig. 4B). To further investigate the antioxidant capacity of CPP-NIR in chondrocytes, oxidative stress-related biochemical indicators were measured. H₂O₂ treatment substantially increased malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, suggesting enhanced lipid peroxidation under oxidative stress. In contrast, CPP treatment reduced MDA production, and CPP-NIR showed a stronger inhibitory effect on lipid peroxidation (Fig. 4D). Meanwhile, SOD activity was suppressed after H₂O₂ stimulation, whereas CPP and CPP-NIR treatment restored intracellular SOD activity, with the highest SOD level observed in the CPP-NIR group (Fig. 4E). These findings indicate that CPP nanozymes could reduce oxidative damage and improve the endogenous antioxidant defense capacity of chondrocytes under NIR irradiation.

Since excessive ROS is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and chondrocyte apoptosis, mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed by JC-1 staining. H₂O₂-treated chondrocytes exhibited increased green fluorescence and reduced red fluorescence, indicating mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization. CP and CPP treatment partially restored mitochondrial membrane potential, while CPP-NIR substantially increased the red/green fluorescence ratio, suggesting improved mitochondrial homeostasis (Fig. 4F, G). In addition, flow cytometry analysis showed that H₂O₂ stimulation significantly increased chondrocyte apoptosis. Compared with the H₂O₂ group, CPP treatment reduced the proportion of apoptotic cells, and CPP-NIR further decreased apoptosis, demonstrating its protective effect against oxidative stress-induced chondrocyte injury (Fig. 4H, I).

The maintenance of cartilage extracellular matrix homeostasis is essential for preventing osteoarthritis progression. Therefore, the expression of chondrogenic markers was further evaluated. Immunofluorescence staining showed that H₂O₂ substantially reduced SOX9 and Col2a1 expression, suggesting impaired chondrocyte phenotype and matrix synthesis. CPP treatment partially restored the fluorescence intensity of SOX9 and Col2a1, whereas CPP-NIR showed the strongest recovery effect (Fig. 4J–M). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the mRNA expression levels of SOX9 and Col2a1 were significantly downregulated after H₂O₂ exposure but were restored after CPP treatment, especially in the CPP-NIR group (Fig. 4N, O). These findings suggest that CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation not only scavenge intracellular ROS but also preserve chondrocyte phenotype and promote extracellular matrix-related gene expression. Additional RT-qPCR analysis showed that ACAN expression followed a similar recovery pattern, with the highest level observed in the CPP-NIR group, supporting restoration of aggrecan-associated matrix anabolism (Figure S7). Western blot analysis further confirmed that CPP-NIR increased SOX9 and Col2a1 protein levels under oxidative stress, consistent with the immunofluorescence and mRNA results (Figure 4P, Figure S8).

Together, these findings show that CPP nanozymes can effectively protect chondrocytes from H₂O₂-induced oxidative injury under NIR irradiation. The protective effects may be attributed to ROS scavenging, inhibition of lipid peroxidation, restoration of antioxidant enzyme activity, maintenance of mitochondrial membrane potential, and suppression of apoptosis. Furthermore, CPP-NIR treatment helped restore SOX9 and Col2a1 expression, indicating its potential to reprogram extracellular matrix metabolism and maintain cartilage homeostasis under oxidative stress conditions (Fig. 4Q).

CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation alleviate H₂O₂-induced inflammatory responses in chondrocytes

Inflammatory activation and extracellular matrix degradation are key pathological events in osteoarthritis progression. To investigate whether CPP nanozymes could suppress oxidative stress-induced inflammatory responses, ATDC5 chondrocytes were stimulated with H₂O₂ and treated with CP, CPP, or CPP-NIR. RT-qPCR analysis showed that H₂O₂ markedly upregulated the mRNA expression levels of inflammatory and catabolic markers, including COX-2, Adamts5, MMP13, and iNOS, indicating that oxidative stress induced a strong inflammatory and matrix-degrading phenotype in chondrocytes (Fig. 5A–D). Compared with the H₂O₂ group, CP and CPP treatment reduced the expression of these genes to varying degrees, while CPP combined with NIR irradiation showed the strongest inhibitory effect. These findings suggest that CPP nanozymes, especially under NIR irradiation, can effectively attenuate H₂O₂-induced inflammatory gene activation in chondrocytes. To further confirm the anti-inflammatory and anti-catabolic effects of CPP, immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect the expression of Adamts5 and MMP13, two major matrix-degrading enzymes closely associated with cartilage degeneration. As shown in Fig. 5E, F, H₂O₂ stimulation significantly increased the fluorescence signals of Adamts5 and MMP13, suggesting enhanced extracellular matrix catabolism. In contrast, CPP treatment reduced the expression of both markers, and the CPP-NIR group exhibited the weakest fluorescence intensity. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that CPP-NIR significantly decreased Adamts5 and MMP13 expression compared with the H₂O₂ group and the non-irradiated CPP group (Fig. 5G, H). These findings indicate that NIR-activated CPP nanozymes can suppress the expression of cartilage-degrading enzymes and help preserve extracellular matrix homeostasis. Western blot analysis was then used to evaluate the protein expression levels of inflammatory and catabolic markers. Consistent with the RT-qPCR and immunofluorescence results, H₂O₂ treatment substantially increased the protein levels of COX-2, Adamts5, and MMP13 (Fig. 5I). CPP treatment partially inhibited the expression of these proteins, whereas CPP-NIR produced a stronger suppressive effect (Fig. 5J–L). This trend demonstrates that CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation can regulate inflammatory responses not only at the transcriptional level but also at the protein level.

Overall, these findings show that CPP nanozymes can effectively alleviate H₂O₂-induced inflammatory responses and catabolic activation in chondrocytes, particularly when combined with NIR irradiation. The anti-inflammatory effect of CPP-NIR may be attributed to its enhanced ROS-scavenging ability and photothermal-responsive bioactivity, which jointly reduce oxidative stress, downregulate inflammatory mediators, and inhibit matrix-degrading enzymes such as Adamts5 and MMP13. Therefore, CPP-NIR treatment may provide a promising strategy for protecting chondrocytes and delaying cartilage degeneration under oxidative stress conditions.

Anti-inflammatory and polarization-regulating effects of CPP nanozymes on macrophages under NIR irradiation in vitro

Macrophage-mediated inflammation is an important contributor to osteoarthritis progression, and regulating macrophage polarization is considered an effective strategy for remodeling the inflammatory joint microenvironment. To evaluate the anti-inflammatory and polarization-regulating effects of CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation, RAW264.7 macrophages were used for in vitro experiments. First, LPS was used to establish an inflammatory macrophage model, and intracellular ROS levels were detected by DCFH-DA staining. As shown in Fig. 6A, LPS stimulation markedly enhanced green fluorescence intensity in macrophages, indicating excessive ROS accumulation under inflammatory conditions. Compared with the LPS group, CPP treatment reduced intracellular ROS levels, while CPP combined with NIR irradiation showed the strongest ROS-scavenging effect. The corresponding quantitative analysis further confirmed that CPP-NIR significantly decreased LPS-induced ROS accumulation in macrophages (Fig. 6B), suggesting that NIR-activated CPP nanozymes could effectively alleviate oxidative stress in inflammatory macrophages.

To further investigate the anti-inflammatory effect of CPP-NIR, RT-qPCR was performed to detect the expression of pro-inflammatory genes. The results showed that LPS stimulation significantly upregulated the mRNA expression levels of the M1-associated inflammatory markers iNOS and IL-1β. Compared with the LPS group, CPP treatment downregulated these pro-inflammatory genes to varying degrees, whereas the CPP-NIR group exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect (Fig. 6C, D). Meanwhile, iNOS immunofluorescence staining was further performed under LPS-induced inflammatory conditions. The results showed that the LPS group exhibited intense iNOS fluorescence, indicating obvious M1 pro-inflammatory polarization. CPP treatment reduced iNOS expression, while the CPP-NIR group showed the weakest iNOS fluorescence intensity, further demonstrating that CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation effectively suppressed LPS-induced M1 inflammatory polarization (Fig. 6F, I).

In addition to inhibiting M1 polarization, the ability of CPP nanozymes to promote M2 macrophage polarization was further evaluated. After IL-4-induced M2 polarization, the expression of M2-related markers was assessed. RT-qPCR results showed that CPP treatment increased the mRNA expression levels of the anti-inflammatory M2 markers Arg-1 and IL-4, and this upregulation was more pronounced in the CPP-NIR group (Fig. 6E, G). Subsequently, CD206 immunofluorescence staining was performed under IL-4-induced M2-polarizing conditions. Compared with the IL-4 group, CPP treatment enhanced the CD206 fluorescence signal, while the CPP-NIR group showed the strongest CD206-positive signal (Fig. 6H, K). These findings indicate that CPP nanozymes, especially under NIR irradiation, can further promote IL-4-induced M2 macrophage polarization.

Flow cytometry was then used to quantitatively analyze macrophage polarization, with CD86 used as a marker of M1 macrophages and CD206 used as a marker of M2 macrophages. The results showed that, compared with the corresponding model groups, CPP-NIR treatment reduced the proportion of CD86-positive macrophages while increasing the proportion of CD206-positive macrophages (Fig. 6J, L, M). Western blot analysis further validated these findings at the protein level. CPP-NIR decreased the expression of the M1-related proteins iNOS and CD86, while upregulating the M2-related protein CD206 (Fig. 6N). The supplemental Western blot analysis showed a consistent polarization-related protein profile, with reduced iNOS and CD86 expression and increased CD206 expression after CPP-NIR treatment (Figure S9).

Taken together, CPP-NIR effectively alleviated oxidative stress and inflammatory activation in macrophages and promoted macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This dual regulatory effect may be attributed to the enhanced ROS-scavenging ability and NIR-responsive bioactivity of CPP nanozymes. Through these effects, CPP-NIR may help reconstruct an anti-inflammatory and pro-repair immune microenvironment, thereby further supporting cartilage protection and osteoarthritis therapy (Fig. 6O).

2.7. Effect of CPP Nanozymes Under NIR Irradiation on the Osteogenic Differentiation Potential of BMSCs Under Oxidative Stress In Vitro.

Subchondral bone remodeling is closely associated with osteoarthritis progression, and restoring the osteogenic potential of BMSCs under oxidative stress may contribute to joint homeostasis. Therefore, the effect of CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation on BMSC viability, migration, and osteogenic differentiation was investigated in vitro (Fig. 7A). First, the cytocompatibility of CPP toward BMSCs was evaluated using different nanozyme concentrations. As shown in Fig. 7E, CPP showed no detectable cytotoxicity within the tested concentration range of 12.5–200 µg/mL, indicating favorable biocompatibility with BMSCs and supporting its potential application in bone-related repair.

To determine whether CPP-NIR could rescue osteogenic differentiation under oxidative stress, the expression of osteogenic genes was examined by RT-qPCR. Oxidative stress markedly suppressed the mRNA expression levels of osteogenic markers, including RUNX-2, OPN, and OCN, suggesting impaired osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Compared with the oxidative stress model group, CPP treatment partially restored the expression of these osteogenic genes, whereas CPP combined with NIR irradiation showed a stronger promoting effect (Fig. 7B–D). These findings indicate that CPP-NIR can enhance the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs under oxidative stress conditions.

The osteogenic effect of CPP-NIR was further confirmed by ALP and ARS staining. ALP staining showed that oxidative stress reduced early osteogenic activity, while CPP treatment increased ALP-positive staining, with the most obvious enhancement observed in the CPP-NIR group (Fig. 7F, I). After 14 days of osteogenic induction, ARS staining demonstrated that mineralized nodule formation was inhibited under oxidative stress, whereas CPP treatment promoted calcium deposition. Notably, CPP-NIR induced the strongest mineralization effect, as confirmed by quantitative analysis of ARS staining (Fig. 7G, J). These findings suggest that CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation can promote both early osteogenic activity and late-stage mineralization of BMSCs.

BMSC migration is also important for bone repair and remodeling. Transwell assays showed that oxidative stress impaired BMSC migration, whereas CPP treatment increased the number of migrated cells. The CPP-NIR group exhibited the strongest enhancement of BMSC migration, indicating that NIR-activated CPP nanozymes may help recruit BMSCs to the damaged microenvironment and support subchondral bone repair (Fig. 7H, K). Finally, Western blot analysis further validated the osteogenic regulatory effect of CPP-NIR at the protein level. Compared with the oxidative stress model group, CPP-NIR markedly upregulated the expression of RUNX-2, OPN, and OCN, consistent with the RT-qPCR, ALP, and ARS results (Fig. 7L). Supplemental Western blot analysis further confirmed that CPP-NIR markedly increased RUNX-2, OPN, and OCN protein levels, reinforcing its role in promoting osteogenic lineage commitment (Figure S10).

Together, these findings show that CPP nanozymes exhibit good biocompatibility with BMSCs and, under NIR irradiation, can effectively restore BMSC migration and osteogenic differentiation under oxidative stress conditions. This effect may be attributed to the antioxidant activity and photothermal-responsive biofunction of CPP, which jointly improve the oxidative microenvironment and promote osteogenic remodeling. Therefore, CPP-NIR may contribute not only to cartilage protection but also to subchondral bone repair during osteoarthritis treatment.

Potential mechanisms of CPP nanozymes in regulating chondrocyte behavior

To further explore the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the protective effects of CPP nanozymes on chondrocytes, RNA sequencing was performed on H₂O₂-stimulated chondrocytes treated with CPP under NIR irradiation (Fig. 8A). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed a clear separation between the H₂O₂-treated group and the CPP-NIR group, indicating that CPP-NIR treatment markedly altered the transcriptional profile of chondrocytes under oxidative stress conditions (Fig. 8B). Differential expression analysis further identified a series of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups, as shown in the volcano plot and heatmap (Fig. 8C, D). These findings suggest that CPP-NIR treatment may regulate multiple biological processes involved in chondrocyte injury and repair. Correlation analysis among biological replicates showed good intra-group consistency and clear inter-group separation, supporting the reliability of the RNA-seq dataset (Figure S11).

To clarify the functional characteristics of these DEGs, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed. GO enrichment analysis showed that the DEGs were mainly associated with biological processes and cellular components related to extracellular matrix organization, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, cartilage development, inflammatory response, cell adhesion, and oxidative stress regulation (Fig. 8E). These enriched GO terms were highly consistent with the observed effects of CPP-NIR on restoring SOX9 and Col2a1 expression, reducing inflammatory markers, and improving chondrocyte function. KEGG pathway analysis further revealed that the DEGs were enriched in pathways closely related to cartilage homeostasis and osteoarthritis progression, including PI3K/AKT signaling, MAPK signaling, ROS-related pathways, ECM-receptor interaction, cytokine-related signaling, and other inflammation- or metabolism-associated pathways (Fig. 8F, G). These findings indicate that CPP-NIR may exert its chondroprotective effects by coordinating oxidative stress regulation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and intracellular survival signaling.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was further used to identify pathway-level changes induced by CPP-NIR treatment. The results showed significant enrichment trends in the PI3K/AKT, ROS, and MAPK signaling pathways (Fig. 8H–J), suggesting that these pathways may be closely involved in CPP-NIR-mediated regulation of chondrocyte behavior. Considering that the PI3K/AKT pathway plays an important role in cell survival, proliferation, matrix metabolism, and stress resistance, we further validated the association with the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway by Western blot. Compared with the H₂O₂-treated group, CPP-NIR treatment upregulated the protein expression of key molecules in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, as confirmed by quantitative analysis (Fig. 8K, L). These findings suggest that CPP-NIR may protect chondrocytes not only by directly scavenging ROS but also by activating pro-survival and pro-repair signaling pathways. Additional GSEA results also indicated enrichment of mTOR signaling and WNT-related pathways, suggesting that CPP-NIR may coordinate survival, anabolic, and reparative signaling beyond PI3K/AKT activation (Figure S12).

Collectively, transcriptomic analysis revealed that CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation significantly reprogrammed the gene expression profile of H₂O₂-injured chondrocytes. The protective effects of CPP-NIR may be associated with the regulation of extracellular matrix organization, cartilage development, inflammatory responses, ROS metabolism, and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK signaling pathways. These results provide mechanistic evidence that CPP-NIR can remodel the oxidative stress microenvironment, promote chondrocyte survival and matrix homeostasis, and thereby contribute to cartilage protection under osteoarthritis-like pathological conditions.

CPP nanozymes alleviate the progression of DMM-induced osteoarthritis in mice

To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of CPP nanozymes in vivo, a destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM)-induced mouse osteoarthritis model was established. First, Cy5.5-labeled CPP nanozymes were used to assess their retention ability in the knee joint cavity. As shown in Fig. 9A, B, the fluorescence signal of free Cy5.5 dye decreased rapidly after intra-articular injection, whereas Cy5.5-labeled CPP nanozymes exhibited stronger and more sustained fluorescence signals in the knee joint. These findings indicate that CPP nanozymes possess favorable intra-articular retention ability, which may help prolong their therapeutic effect in osteoarthritic joints. Based on the fluorescence decay profile of Cy5.5-labeled CPP in the joint cavity, we further determined the in vivo treatment regimen: intra-articular injection of CPP nanozymes every two weeks, combined with 808 nm NIR irradiation once per week, to maintain effective local therapeutic activity within the joint. In addition, infrared thermal imaging showed that, under 808 nm NIR irradiation, the knee joints treated with CPP displayed an obvious temperature increase compared with the PBS-treated group, confirming that CPP maintained good NIR-responsive photothermal performance in vivo (Fig. 9C). The overall experimental schedule for DMM model establishment, intra-articular injection, and weekly NIR treatment is shown in Fig. 9D.

Micro-CT analysis was performed to assess the effect of CPP-NIR on osteoarthritis-associated bone remodeling. Representative 2D and 3D reconstructed images showed severe joint degeneration and osteophyte formation in the untreated OA group, whereas CP and CPP treatment partially alleviated these pathological changes (Fig. 9E, F). Notably, CPP combined with NIR irradiation showed the strongest protective effect, with reduced osteophyte formation and improved joint structure compared with the untreated OA group. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that CPP-NIR significantly decreased the number of osteophytes (Fig. 9G). In addition, microstructural parameters of the subchondral tibial bone were analyzed to evaluate OA-related subchondral bone remodeling. Compared with untreated OA mice, CPP-NIR treatment improved subchondral bone microarchitecture, suggesting that CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation could help regulate abnormal bone remodeling during OA progression (Fig. 9M–P).

Histological staining was further used to evaluate cartilage degeneration and joint tissue damage. H&E, Safranin O/Fast Green, and Toluidine Blue staining showed that the untreated OA group displayed typical osteoarthritic features, including cartilage surface erosion, loss of proteoglycan staining, disorganized cartilage structure, and apparent joint degeneration (Fig. 9H–J). CP and CPP treatment partially preserved cartilage morphology and matrix staining, indicating a certain degree of cartilage protection. Importantly, the CPP-NIR group exhibited smoother cartilage surfaces, stronger proteoglycan staining, and better preservation of cartilage structure compared with the untreated OA and CPP groups. Consistently, OARSI and Mankin scores were substantially increased in the untreated OA group but significantly reduced after CPP-NIR treatment (Fig. 9K, L), demonstrating that CPP-NIR effectively attenuated cartilage degeneration in the DMM-induced OA model.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that CPP-NIR provides effective structural protection in the DMM-induced OA model. The prolonged intra-articular retention of Cy5.5-labeled CPP supports a biweekly injection schedule, while in vivo thermal imaging confirms that CPP retains NIR-responsive activity within the knee joint. More importantly, micro-CT and histological analyses show that CPP-NIR reduces osteophyte formation, improves subchondral bone microarchitecture, preserves cartilage morphology, maintains proteoglycan deposition, and lowers OARSI and Mankin scores. These outcomes indicate that CPP-NIR can attenuate whole-joint degeneration at the structural level, providing in vivo evidence for its therapeutic potential in osteoarthritis.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of CPP nanozymes in osteoarthritis

To further investigate the therapeutic mechanism of CPP-NIR in vivo, immunohistochemical analyses were performed on cartilage, subchondral bone, and synovial tissues from different treatment groups. First, the expression of cartilage matrix synthesis-related proteins was examined. As shown in Fig. 10A, the OA group exhibited markedly decreased staining intensity of PTH1R, SOX9, Col2a1, and ACAN, indicating impaired chondrocyte function and reduced extracellular matrix synthesis. Compared with the untreated OA group, CP and CPP treatment partially restored the expression of these anabolic markers, whereas CPP combined with NIR irradiation showed the strongest enhancement. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that CPP-NIR significantly increased the positive expression areas of Col2a1, ACAN, SOX9, and PTH1R in cartilage tissue (Fig. 10B). These findings suggest that CPP-NIR can effectively promote cartilage matrix synthesis and maintain the chondrocyte phenotype in vivo.

The expression of cartilage matrix-degrading enzymes was then evaluated. Immunohistochemical staining showed that Adamts5 and MMP13 were highly expressed in the OA group, reflecting enhanced extracellular matrix degradation during osteoarthritis progression (Fig. 10C). In contrast, CPP treatment reduced the expression of these catabolic enzymes, and CPP-NIR exerted the strongest inhibitory effect. Statistical analysis confirmed that the positive staining areas of Adamts5 and MMP13 were significantly decreased in the CPP-NIR group compared with the untreated OA group (Fig. 10D). These findings indicate that CPP-NIR not only enhances cartilage matrix synthesis but also suppresses matrix catabolism, thereby helping restore extracellular matrix homeostasis in osteoarthritic cartilage.

Given that subchondral bone remodeling is closely associated with OA progression, RUNX-2 expression in bone tissue was further assessed. The OA group showed abnormal RUNX-2 staining, suggesting dysregulated bone remodeling in the osteoarthritic joint. After CPP-NIR treatment, RUNX-2 expression was better regulated compared with the untreated OA group, as supported by quantitative analysis of RUNX-2-positive areas (Fig. 10E, F). These findings suggest that CPP-NIR may contribute to the regulation of subchondral bone remodeling while protecting articular cartilage.

Synovial inflammation was also evaluated by H&E staining. The OA group displayed obvious synovial hyperplasia and inflammatory cell infiltration, whereas CPP and CPP-NIR treatment reduced synovial inflammatory changes. The synovial inflammation score was lowest in the CPP-NIR group, indicating that CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation effectively alleviated synovitis in OA joints. To further clarify the immunomodulatory effect of CPP-NIR, macrophage phenotype-related markers in synovial tissue were analyzed using CD86 and CD206 staining. The OA group showed increased expression of the M1 macrophage marker CD86, while the M2 macrophage marker CD206 was relatively reduced. In contrast, CPP-NIR treatment decreased CD86 expression and enhanced CD206 expression, suggesting attenuation of M1-associated inflammatory activation and enrichment of M2-like anti-inflammatory markers in synovial tissue (Fig. 10I, J).

Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrate that CPP nanozymes under NIR irradiation effectively alleviate OA progression through multiple coordinated mechanisms. CPP-NIR promotes cartilage matrix synthesis by upregulating PTH1R, SOX9, Col2a1, and ACAN, inhibits matrix degradation by downregulating Adamts5 and MMP13, supports subchondral bone remodeling, reduces synovial inflammation, and modulates macrophage phenotype-related markers. These synergistic effects contribute to the restoration of joint microenvironment homeostasis and support CPP-NIR as a promising therapeutic strategy for osteoarthritis treatment (Fig. 10K). In addition, H&E staining of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, showed no obvious inflammatory infiltration, necrosis, or structural damage in the CPP-treated group compared with controls, indicating favorable systemic biocompatibility after intra-articular nanozyme administration (Figure S13).

Discussion

OA involves multiple interdependent pathological processes, including cartilage degeneration, oxidative stress, synovial inflammation, immune imbalance, and subchondral bone remodeling [3, 14, 29, 30]. A therapy aimed at a single target is therefore unlikely to fully delay disease progression. In this study, we developed CPP, a CuTA@PDA@PTH nanozyme that combines ROS-scavenging activity, NIR-responsive photothermal performance, PTH-related bioactivity, immunomodulatory effects, and osteogenic regulatory capacity. Across cellular and animal experiments, CPP activated by NIR irradiation protected chondrocytes from oxidative injury, suppressed inflammatory and catabolic responses, attenuated M1-associated macrophage activation, favored an M2-like anti-inflammatory phenotype, enhanced BMSC osteogenic differentiation, and attenuated DMM-induced OA progression.

One notable observation was that CPP-NIR consistently outperformed CPP alone in ROS clearance, mitochondrial protection, chondrocyte proliferation, anti-inflammatory regulation, macrophage phenotype modulation, BMSC osteogenesis, and in vivo cartilage preservation. This improvement is likely related to PDA-mediated photothermal activation. Mild NIR-induced heating may increase local molecular mobility and improve the contact between CPP and ROS, thereby enhancing catalytic scavenging. It may also alter the local presentation or availability of PTH 1–34 on the PDA-modified surface and amplify peptide-related bioactivity. In addition, photothermal stimulation may activate cellular stress-defense pathways, including Hsp70 expression, which could strengthen chondrocyte resistance to oxidative damage [31, 32]. These mechanisms may act in parallel, allowing NIR irradiation to function as an efficacy-amplifying external trigger for CPP rather than as an independent therapeutic intervention.

The therapeutic behavior of CPP reflects the distinct but complementary functions of CuTA, PDA, and PTH. CuTA acts as the catalytic antioxidant core, providing redox-active copper sites and tannic-acid-derived phenolic groups capable of eliminating ROS such as •O₂⁻, •OH, and H₂O₂. PDA serves as a stabilizing coating and photothermal layer, improving nanoparticle dispersibility, providing a versatile interface for PTH loading, and enabling NIR responsiveness [33]. PTH 1–34 is expected to act through PTH1R-related signaling to support chondrocyte proliferation and matrix synthesis [34, 35]. Thus, CPP should be viewed not as a simple combination of components but as an integrated platform in which each module addresses a different feature of the OA microenvironment.

Oxidative stress remains a central driver of cartilage degeneration. Excessive ROS promotes lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, and inflammatory activation, ultimately disturbing cartilage matrix homeostasis [36, 37]. In the H₂O₂-induced chondrocyte injury model, CPP-NIR reduced ROS and MDA levels, restored SOD activity, maintained mitochondrial membrane potential, and decreased apoptosis. These biochemical and mitochondrial effects were accompanied by increased SOX9 and Col2a1 expression and reduced COX-2, iNOS, Adamts5, and MMP13 expression, indicating that ROS control translated into preservation of the chondrocyte phenotype and improvement of matrix metabolism. These findings are consistent with previous antioxidant nanozyme studies showing that redox restoration and mitochondrial protection can alleviate OA pathology [37, 38].

CPP-NIR also exerted immunomodulatory effects. Synovial inflammation is closely linked to macrophage polarization, in which M1 macrophages release inflammatory and catabolic mediators that exacerbate cartilage injury, whereas M2-like macrophages contribute to inflammation resolution and repair. In our macrophage experiments, CPP-NIR reduced ROS accumulation, downregulated M1-related markers including iNOS, IL-1β, and CD86, and upregulated M2-related markers including Arg-1, IL-4, and CD206 [39, 40]. Because ROS accumulation can reinforce inflammatory activation, the ability of CPP-NIR to attenuate M1-associated activation and favor an M2-like phenotype may be partly driven by its antioxidant function. This coupling between redox regulation and immune remodeling is likely important for reconstructing a repair-favorable joint microenvironment.

Subchondral bone remodeling was another therapeutic dimension of CPP-NIR. Under oxidative stress, CPP-NIR promoted BMSC migration and osteogenic differentiation, as evidenced by increased RUNX-2, OPN, and OCN expression, enhanced ALP activity, and greater mineralized nodule formation. Because excessive or abnormal subchondral bone formation may also contribute to OA progression, these results are interpreted as support for restoring BMSC reparative capacity under oxidative stress rather than promoting uncontrolled bone formation. In vivo, CPP-NIR reduced osteophyte formation and improved subchondral bone microarchitecture, suggesting a more balanced subchondral bone repair response. Such coordinated regulation is particularly relevant for OA, where cartilage degeneration and subchondral bone remodeling often reinforce each other [41, 42].

Transcriptomic analysis provided additional mechanistic insight. GO and KEGG analyses linked CPP-NIR treatment to extracellular matrix organization, cartilage development, inflammatory response, ROS metabolism, ECM-receptor interaction, MAPK signaling, and PI3K/AKT-related pathways. Western blotting further showed increased phosphorylation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR-related proteins, suggesting that CPP-NIR-associated chondroprotection may involve PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling. Because this pathway is involved in cell survival, proliferation, stress resistance, and matrix synthesis, its association may help explain the observed increase in chondrocyte proliferation, reduction in apoptosis, and recovery of ECM markers [42]. Nevertheless, the current data indicate pathway association rather than causal validation. CPP-NIR appears to act through a multi-level network that integrates ROS scavenging, mitochondrial protection, inflammatory suppression, ECM remodeling, and pro-survival signaling.

The in vivo DMM model further supported the therapeutic potential of CPP-NIR. CPP showed prolonged intra-articular retention and generated a local photothermal response after NIR exposure. Micro-CT, histological staining, and immunohistochemistry showed reduced osteophyte formation, preserved cartilage structure and proteoglycan content, lower OARSI and Mankin scores, increased PTH1R, SOX9, Col2a1, and ACAN expression, and decreased Adamts5 and MMP13 expression. CPP-NIR also alleviated synovial inflammation and increased CD206-positive staining in vivo. These findings indicate that CPP-NIR intervenes in several OA-related pathological events rather than acting through a single pathway.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, quantitative PTH 1–34 loading efficiency and release kinetics were not fully characterized in the present study; therefore, the contribution of PTH 1–34 was mainly inferred from the preparation strategy, physicochemical characterization, and PTH1R-associated biological responses. Second, although comparisons among CuTA, CP, CPP, and CPP-NIR support the contribution of each module and NIR activation, additional controls such as PDA-only, PTH-only, NIR-only biological groups, and temperature-matched photothermal groups would help separate independent from synergistic effects. Third, although H₂O₂-treated ATDC5 cells provide a reproducible oxidative stress-dominant injury model, this model does not fully reproduce the complex cytokine-rich OA microenvironment or primary human OA chondrocyte behavior. Future studies using primary chondrocytes, cartilage explants, IL-1β/TNF-α-based inflammatory models, and hypertrophic markers such as COL10A1 and RUNX2 will further validate phenotype stability and translational relevance. Finally, the causal role of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, long-term biosafety, degradation behavior, copper metabolism, and therapeutic efficacy in large-animal models should be evaluated before clinical translation.

In summary, CPP-NIR functions as a multifunctional nanotherapeutic platform for OA. Its enhanced activity relative to CPP alone may arise from NIR-triggered photothermal activation, improved ROS-scavenging efficiency, increased PTH-related bioactivity, and stress-protective signaling. By simultaneously protecting cartilage, suppressing synovial inflammation, modulating macrophage phenotype, and improving subchondral bone remodeling, CPP-NIR provides a multi-axis strategy for OA microenvironment regulation.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed CPP, a multifunctional NIR-responsive CuTA@PDA@PTH nanozyme for OA therapy. CPP showed favorable dispersibility, stable photothermal performance, and broad-spectrum antioxidant activity. Under NIR irradiation, CPP efficiently scavenged ROS, restored mitochondrial function, inhibited chondrocyte apoptosis, reduced inflammatory and catabolic responses, and preserved cartilage ECM homeostasis. CPP-NIR also attenuated M1-associated macrophage activation, favored an anti-inflammatory M2-like phenotype, and enhanced BMSC migration and osteogenic differentiation under oxidative stress. Transcriptomic analysis suggested that these protective effects were associated with ECM regulation, inflammatory modulation, ROS metabolism, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR-related signaling. In the DMM-induced mouse OA model, CPP-NIR alleviated cartilage degeneration, reduced osteophyte formation, improved subchondral bone microarchitecture, and attenuated synovial inflammation. Overall, CPP-NIR provides a multi-axis nanotherapeutic strategy for OA by coordinating the regulation of oxidative stress, inflammation, cartilage matrix metabolism, and subchondral bone remodeling.
