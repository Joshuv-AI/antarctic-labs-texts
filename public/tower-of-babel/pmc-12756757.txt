Preparation and Performance Evaluation of Intelligent Oil Displacement Janus Nanosheets
Authors: Shoufei Lu, Meilong Fu, Yanmin Mi, Changhai Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To enhance the recovery factor in low-permeability reservoirs and address the challenge of nanoparticle adsorption at interfaces, a smart oil displacement Janus nanosheet was synthesized using a template method. The Janus nanosheets possess an amphiphilic structure, with one side being hydrophilic due to carboxyl groups and the other side being hydrophobic due to alkyl groups. Compared to spherical nanoparticles, the sheet-like structure of the nanosheets provides a larger specific surface area and stronger interfacial adsorption capacity. Nanofluids were prepared using a simulated formation water with a salinity of 102,495 mg/L, and their properties were evaluated. The results demonstrated that the nanofluid exhibited excellent dispersion stability at 120 °C and a salinity of 102,495 mg/L. The emulsification index at 120 °C was 53.43%, indicating superior emulsifying performance. The nanofluid reduced the oil–water interfacial tension to 0.0203 mN/m and effectively modified the oil-wet rock surface to a hydrophilic state. Oil recovery experiments were conducted on three core samples with varying permeability. For the core with a permeability of 4.64 mD, the highest recovery rate was achieved, with a total recovery factor of 71.09%. Microscopic oil displacement simulations showed the displacement of residual oil after injecting the nanofluid. These comprehensive properties demonstrate that the Janus nanosheets exhibit higher oil displacement efficiency in low-permeability reservoirs, reflecting their intelligent response to reservoir conditions and their potential for improving recovery rates. A field test was conducted in the Q Oilfield, resulting in an increase of 5579 tons of oil, with a cost–benefit ratio of 1.78, demonstrating significant economic benefits.

Introduction

The long-term water injection development in Tuoketuo Oilfield has resulted in the worsening of reservoir heterogeneity, high water cut in the produced liquid, the deterioration of the water flooding effect, and the decrease in oil production. , A number of scholars employ gas flooding, chemical flooding, and other enhanced oil recovery methods − to improve oil recovery, but several issues remain, such as poor reservoir adaptability and unsatisfactory oil recovery improvement effects. Nanofluids are widely utilized because of their distinctive surface and interfacial properties. − SiO2 is the most commonly used nanoparticle modified into a Janus structure. It offers advantages such as stable chemical properties, low cost, and environmental safety. The surface contains numerous uncondensed hydroxyl groups, resulting in a strong surface effect. The hydroxyl group exhibits strong polarity, causing adjacent SiO2 nanoparticles to easily agglomerate in water, making it difficult to form a uniform and stable dispersion. SiO2 nanoparticles without surface treatment exhibit strong hydrophilicity, making them difficult to adsorb at the interface. Amphiphilic Janus–SiO2 nanoparticles can adsorb at the oil–water interface, forming a robust interfacial film. , Ling et al. coated SiO2 particles with poly­(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), then exposed uncoated SiO2 particles on the surface via plasma etching and chemically modified this portion of the surface. Finally, Janus particles were formed by removing the PMMA template. Liu et al. prepared amphoteric Janus–SiO2 nanoparticles via a chemical etching method, using SiO2 nanoparticles as the starting material. These nanoparticles exhibit good dispersibility in water, demonstrating amphiphilicity and high interfacial activity. Bai et al. prepared sulfonic acid/alkane composite amphiphilic Janus nanosilica (SAJNS) via the Pickering emulsion template method and characterized its surface structure using appropriate characterization techniques.

The spherical nanoparticles commonly used today have several limitations, such as restricted adsorption at interfaces and insufficient stability under extreme conditions. Building on previous work, this study developed highly efficient Janus nanosheets using a templating method. These nanosheets demonstrated excellent performance at 120 °C and under high salinity conditions (102,495 mg/L), outperforming conventional nanoparticles. In comparison to surfactants, Janus nanosheets, with their unique bimetallic structure (one side hydrophilic with carboxyl groups and the other side hydrophobic with alkyl chains) and their two-dimensional sheet-like morphology, exhibit significantly enhanced interfacial stability and resistance to temperature and salt. These properties allow them to effectively withstand the challenges posed by high-temperature and high-salinity reservoir environments, preventing performance degradation. In contrast to traditional spherical nanoparticles, the sheet-like structure of Janus nanosheets provides a larger specific surface area and stronger interfacial adsorption capacity. Their nanoscale size and ultrathin thickness (approximately 3.7 nm) further enhance their ability to adsorb and modulate rock wettability. The asymmetric nature of Janus nanosheets allows them to spontaneously and effectively adsorb at the oil–water interface in low-permeability reservoirs, forming a stable interfacial film. These characteristics enable the nanosheets to respond dynamically to reservoir conditions, displaying a “water-blocking, breakthrough, and deep migration” cycle. By leveraging their excellent emulsification and wettability reversal capabilities, Janus nanosheets facilitate intelligent oil displacement and mobilization of residual oil, significantly improving recovery efficiency in low-permeability reservoirs. This study aims to evaluate the oil displacement performance of these nanosheets and analyze the underlying mechanisms.

Experimental Method

Preparation of Janus Nanosheets

The Janus nanosheets were prepared using a template method, as shown in Figure . In a four-necked flask, toluene (100 mL), BDMPS (1 g), and SiO2 (1 g) particles were sequentially added. The pH was adjusted to 4.0–5.0 using dilute hydrochloric acid, and the mixture was magnetically stirred at 500 rpm for 24 h at room temperature to allow adsorption of BDMPS onto the surface of the SiO2 particles. Subsequently, toluene was removed by centrifugation to separate the free BDMPS. The resulting lower layer was dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h, yielding SiO2 particles with adsorbed BDMPS. The SiO2 particles, now coated with BDMPS (1 g), were dispersed in toluene (95 mL) and mixed with APTMS (1 g) and bis­(trimethoxysilane) ethane (BTMSE) (5 mL). The pH was adjusted to 4.0–5.0 using dilute hydrochloric acid, and nitrogen gas was bubbled through the flask for 30 min to remove oxygen. The reaction was carried out at 60 °C for 4 h, resulting in SiO2 particles encapsulated in a polymer with a core–shell structure. Throughout the reaction, stirring was maintained to ensure homogeneous dispersion of the reagents, reflux was used to control temperature, and an oil bath prevented the ingress of water vapor into the system. The core–shell structured SiO2 particles were subjected to high-speed centrifugation and washed several times with toluene to remove unreacted reagents. To remove the SiO2 template, a 3% NaOH aqueous solution was added to the polymer shell, and the pH was adjusted to 10.0–11.0. The mixture was stirred at 60 °C for 2 h, allowing the hydrolysis of the anhydride groups within the polymer shell to form hydrophilic carboxyl groups. The resulting product was washed with deionized water in three cycles of countercurrent washing to remove residual hydroxides and chloride ions. Washing continued until the final product had a pH of 7.0–8.0. To confirm the removal of residual ions, a silver nitrate solution was added to the wash liquid, and washing was stopped once no precipitate was formed. Finally, the polymer shell was fragmented using an ultrasonic homogenizer. After standing for 30 min, the supernatant was centrifuged for 10 min. The lower layer was washed 3 times with toluene, and the final product was dried in an oven at 90 °C, yielding Janus nanosheets with hydrophilic carboxyl groups on one side and hydrophobic alkyl groups on the other.

Structure Characterization

Particle Size Analysis

The particle size distribution of the nanosheets was analyzed using a nanoparticle size and zeta potential analyzer. The particle size distribution and average particle size of the samples were measured in triplicate, with the average value used as the final result.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

The solid samples were dispersed in an ethanol solution, and the particles were evenly dispersed using a water bath ultrasonic disperser. The ethanol suspension containing the solid sample was dropped onto a square silicon wafer, and the solid sample was fixed onto the wafer after the ethanol had completely evaporated. Finally, the microstructure of the sample was characterized and analyzed using atomic force microscopy.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

The solid sample was predried and dehumidified, then mixed with a small amount of KBr powder and ground into a compact wafer for analysis. The sample was subsequently scanned and analyzed using an infrared spectrometer.

Thermogravimetric (TG) Analysis

Approximately 3–5 mg of the sample was placed into the sample tray and analyzed using a simultaneous thermal analyzer. Nitrogen (N2) was used as the protective gas, with a temperature program rate of 5 °C/min, and the temperature range was set from 20 to 600 °C. The mass of the sample was monitored as a function of temperature or time.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectrum (XPS) Analysis

The samples were trimmed and mounted onto the sample holder, then introduced into the instrument’s sample chamber. Once the chamber pressure reached below 2.0 × 10–7 mbar, the samples were transferred to the analysis chamber. A beam spot size of 400 μm was used, with an operating voltage of 12 kV and a filament current of 6 mA. Survey scans were performed with a pass energy of 150 eV and a step size of 1 eV, while high-resolution scans were conducted with a pass energy of 50 eV and a step size of 0.1 eV.

Performance Testing

Temperature and Salt Resistance Stability Test

A 0.1 wt % nanoparent tablet solution was prepared using simulated formation water and subjected to ultrasonic dispersion for 30 min. The solution was then placed in an aging kettle and exposed to temperatures of 30 °C, 70 °C, and 120 °C in an oven for 60 days. The appearance of the solution was observed before and after aging. Sediment formation at the bottom of the sample bottle indicates instability, while the absence of sediment suggests stability.

Emulsification Performance

Nanofluids with a concentration of 0.1 wt % were prepared using simulated formation water. After maintaining a constant temperature for 40 min in a 30 °C, 70 °C, or 120 °C water bath, the nanofluids were mixed with crude oil (1:1 oil-to-water volume ratio) and stirred at 6000 rpm for 20 min using a homogenizer. The stirred crude oil was then poured into a two-plug measuring cylinder and placed in a 30 °C, 70 °C, or 120 °C water bath. The emulsification volume after standing was recorded, the emulsification index was calculated, and the emulsification performance was evaluated.

Evaluation Method of Oil–Water Interfacial Tension

The TX-500C interfacial tensiometer was used to measure interfacial tension using the hanging drop method. Aviation kerosene was used as the oil phase to measure the interfacial tension between the nanofluids and aviation kerosene. The minimum and steady-state values of interfacial tension were recorded at 10 s intervals.

To Test the Wettability of Rock

The contact angle of oil droplets on the surface of sandstone was measured using the Tracker automatic interfacial rheometer after the pretreated sandstone was titrated with deionized water. The sandstone was aged with nanofluids for 1 to 2 days. Upon completion of aging, the sandstone was naturally dried, and the contact angle of oil droplets on the sandstone surface was measured continuously.

Analysis of Structural Characterization Results

AFM Test Results Analysis

The thickness and surface morphology of the Janus nanosheets were observed using atomic force microscopy (AFM), and the AFM images along with cross-sectional height profiles were acquired. The results are presented in Figure . As seen in the AFM image in Figure a, the Janus nanosheets exhibit an irregular lamellar structure, with a closely packed arrangement and a slightly undulating surface. The lamellar morphology and surface microfluctuations increase the specific surface area, facilitating adsorption and spreading at the oil–water interface, thereby enhancing emulsification and dispersion. The cross-sectional height profile in Figure b shows that the thickness of the Janus nanosheets is approximately 3.7 nm. Under high temperature and high salinity conditions, the Janus nanosheets must remain stable, without flipping or collapsing at the oil–water interface. The stable partition height observed in the curve suggests that the structure remains intact under high temperature and salinity conditions, ensuring its ability to perform directional adsorption and reduce interfacial tension in such reservoirs.

FTIR Test Results Analysis

The infrared spectrum results of the Janus nanosheets are presented in Figure . A broad peak near 3400 cm–1, corresponding to the stretching vibration of O–H, may arise from the polymer chain containing hydroxyl groups or the hydrophilic groups modified on the surface. The presence of hydroxyl groups enhances hydrophilicity. The strong asymmetric peaks at 2924 cm–1 and 2850 cm–1 correspond to the bending vibration of CH3, suggesting the presence of an alkyl chain structure, which is characteristic of hydrophobic components. This alkyl chain can reduce salt ion erosion on the hydrophobic phase. The peak at 1715 cm–1 corresponds to the CO stretching vibration, likely originating from ester and carbonyl groups in the polymer structure. These groups regulate the balance between hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity, and may participate in hydrogen bonding or coordination, which enhances the stability of Janus nanosheets under high temperature and salinity conditions. The peaks at 1106 cm–1 and 1035 cm–1 correspond to the stretching vibration of Si–O. The high bond energy and chemical stability of the Si–O bond provide crucial support for temperature and salt resistance. The hydroxyl and carbonyl groups form the hydrophilic side, while the alkyl chain creates the hydrophobic side, achieving amphiphilicity. The Si–O bond contributes to thermal stability and chemical inertness, collaborating with other groups to maintain the interfacial activity and structural stability of Janus nanosheets under high temperature and salinity conditions. FTIR results confirm that the hydrophobic alkyl chain was grafted onto the surface of the Janus nanosheets.

Analysis of TG Characterization Results

The thermogravimetric (TG) test results of the Janus nanosheets are shown in Figure . The mass loss occurs in three distinct stages. In the first stage, between 30 and 100 °C, the mass loss is attributed to the evaporation of water molecules adsorbed on the surface of the Janus nanosheets. In the second stage, from 100 to 320 °C, the TG curve shows a rapid decrease, with a mass loss of 2.32%, primarily due to the decomposition of oxygen-containing functional groups, such as carboxyl groups, present in the Janus nanosheets. In the third stage, above 320 °C, the mass loss reaches 37.41%. This significant mass loss is attributed to the combustion of the alkyl chain on the Janus nanosheets. The mass loss of the nanoparticles grafted only with carboxyl groups is relatively small. The final mass loss of 37.41% is consistent with the FTIR characterization results.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectrum (XPS) Test Results Analysis

The surface composition of the Janus nanosheets was investigated using XPS, and the results are presented in Figures and . The survey scan revealed four characteristic elements: carbon (C) at 82.96%, oxygen (O) at 13.38%, nitrogen (N) at 3.18%, and silicon (Si) at 0.47%. High-resolution spectra showed that the C 1s peak appeared at a binding energy (BE) of 284.34 eV, corresponding to C–H bonds in alkyl groups (C–CH2−) and C–O bonds in carboxyl groups (−COOH). The O 1s peak was observed at 531.86 eV, attributable to C–O bonds in carboxyl groups. The N 1s peak at 401.81 eV originated from amino groups (−NH2) present in APTMS. The Si 2p peak appeared at a BE of 101.52 eV, likely arising from the silicon moieties in the silane coupling agents APTMS and BTMSE, or from residual silicon remaining in the polymer structure after the removal of the SiO2 template. These findings indicate that the elemental signals observed in the overall XPS spectra are consistent with the structural composition of the Janus nanosheets.

Performance Test Results Analysis

Temperature and Salt Resistance Stability Test Results

Nanofluids with a concentration of 0.1 wt % were prepared using simulated formation water with a salinity of 102,495 mg/L. The stability test results over 60 days at various temperatures are shown in Figure . Due to the amphiphilic structure of Janus nanosheets, with hydrophilic carboxyl groups on one side and hydrophobic alkyl groups on the other, they exhibit strong dispersion capabilities in different polar solvents. Initially, the solution was clear and transparent, with no visible precipitation or insoluble matter. After 48 h, the nanofluids at different temperatures maintained good suspension, and the transmittance remained largely unchanged. After 10 days of standing, the solution appeared light milky white, yet no precipitate or insoluble material was observed. After standing for 60 days, the solution remained clear and transparent, with no formation of any precipitate. This can be attributed to the enhanced spatial steric hindrance of the sheet structure, which is more pronounced than that of spherical particles, effectively counteracting some van der Waals attractions. These results demonstrate that the nanofluids exhibit excellent dispersion stability at temperatures ranging from 30 to 120 °C, indicating that Janus nanosheets possess both temperature and salt resistance.

Although slightly reduced at higher temperatures, the emulsification performance remained satisfactory, with the emulsion index still exceeding that of unmodified SiO2 nanoparticles. At the same temperature, the nanofluids consistently exhibited better emulsification than unmodified SiO2 nanoparticles, demonstrating their excellent emulsifying capability as well as the Janus nanosheets’ strong thermal and salt tolerance.

The emulsion prepared at 30 °C was examined under a microscope, and the results are shown in Figure . The images reveal that the oil phase is dispersed as relatively uniform small droplets within the aqueous phase, with no significant oil aggregation or phase separation observed. This homogeneous dispersion arises from the amphiphilic structure of the Janus nanosheets, which can spontaneously adsorb at the oil–water interface to form a stable emulsion and inhibit droplet coalescence. The droplet distribution also indicates that no local emulsion rupture or abnormal droplet enlargement occurred due to nanoparticle aggregation. This stability is attributed to the nanosheets’ planar structure, which enhances steric hindrance, counteracts van der Waals forces, prevents self-aggregation, and thereby maintains the integrity of the emulsion system. The uniform dispersion observed under the microscope further suggests that, during practical oil displacement, the emulsion can more efficiently interact with the rock surface. By modulating the three-phase interface through their amphiphilic nature, the nanosheets facilitate the detachment of oil droplets from the rock surface and their transport as small droplets, providing a foundation for improved oil recovery.

Reduced Interfacial Tension Test Results

The oil–water interfacial activity of Janus nanofluids with different concentrations was measured, and the results are shown in Figure . At a concentration of 0.1 wt % of nanosheets, the oil–water interfacial tension was reduced to 0.0203 mN/m. As the concentration of Janus amphiphilic nanofluids increased, the oil–water interfacial tension gradually decreased. The measured oil–water interfacial tension stabilized within a specific numerical range. Due to the high adsorption energy of the nanosheets at the oil–water interface, the rotation of the nanosheets at the interface is limited, leading to the formation of a more stable nanofluid compared to spherical nanoparticles. This indicates that an increase in the amount of Janus nanosheets further reduces the oil–water interfacial tension. At higher concentrations, more nanosheets adsorb at the oil–water interface, with the lipophilic side of the grafted hydrophobic organic carbon chain facing the oil phase and the hydrophilic carboxyl group facing the water phase. This arrangement lowers the oil–water interface energy, effectively reducing the interfacial tension.

Change Rock Wettability Test Results

The contact angle of the core slices before and after soaking in Janus nanofluids was measured to evaluate the wettability modification ability of Janus amphiphilic nanosheets. Multiple repeated experiments were conducted, and the finally selected representative results are shown in Table and Figure . After soaking in Janus nanofluids, the core slices transitioned from an oil-wet to a water-wet surface. Initially, the contact angle on the core surface was approximately 139.4°, indicating lipophilicity. After soaking the core slices in Janus nanofluid for 48 h, the contact angle increased to 72.6°, indicating hydrophilicity. Furthermore, as the concentration of Janus nanofluids increased, the contact angle of the core slices also increased. This change is attributed to the amphiphilic nature of Janus nanosheets, with different hydrophilic and hydrophobic sides. When dispersed in an aqueous solution, the nanosheets spontaneously migrate to the phase interface and adsorb onto the core surface. The hydrophobic side faces the rock surface, while the hydrophilic side faces the aqueous phase, altering the wettability of the core surface. In addition to adsorption, the nanosheets generate structural pressure at the three-phase interface, which helps to break and detach the oil film on the solid surface. This process further changes the wettability and improves oil recovery.

These results indicate that Janus nanosheets exhibit a selective response to permeability. In low-permeability cores, their planar structure and ultrathin thickness provide excellent pore–throat matching, leading to physical retention in microchannels upon injection. This retention temporarily blocks high-permeability pathways, causing a subsequent increase in water flooding pressure. When the driving pressure exceeds a critical value, the nanosheets reorient under pressure, overcome pore–throat restrictions, and migrate into deeper low-permeability regions. This “blocking–breakthrough–deep migration” dynamic cycle significantly enhances sweep efficiency. In higher-permeability cores, although the blocking effect is reduced, the nanofluid continues to mobilize residual oil through emulsification and wettability alteration.

Analysis of Microscopic Profile Control Experimental Results

As shown in Figure , the microscopic visualization model was placed horizontally for the experiments, with saturated water and oil injected from the left end to displace the resident fluids. The production at the upper, lower, and right outlets was monitored to establish the residual oil distribution model. The microprofile control experiments indicate that, after treatment with the nanofluid, the residual oil distribution within the model was significantly reduced.

Microscopic oil displacement experiment result diagram. (a) Saturated water; (b) saturated oil; (c) water flooding; (d) one-time nanofluid flooding; (e) secondary nanofluid flooding; (f) tertiary nanofluidic flooding.

It is evident from the images that during water flooding, the injected water preferentially flows through high-permeability regions, while low-permeability zones are displaced more slowly. This behavior reflects the water channeling phenomenon commonly observed in actual reservoirs, where water preferentially flows along high-permeability pathways. Once water breakthrough occurs, flow is observed in multiple directions, with all three outlets producing water. The upper and right outlets show the highest water production, while the lower outlet produces comparatively less.

Upon injection of the nanofluid, the fluids within pores of different permeabilities are affected, and the rock wettability is altered. The Janus nanosheets first enter the dominant high-water-cut channels, forming temporary blockages. Under the driving pressure, they then redirect into low-permeability regions to displace residual oil, initiating an intelligent “water-seeking-oil” displacement mechanism. The hydrophobic alkyl chains of the nanosheets aggregate on the surfaces of residual oil clusters, while the hydrophilic carboxyl groups face the aqueous phase, forming a stable interfacial film and promoting the enrichment of nanosheets at the oil–water interface.

Analysis of Oil Displacement Mechanism

The oil displacement mechanism of Janus nanosheets was thoroughly analyzed based on the results from the wettability, emulsification performance, oil–water interfacial tension changes, and oil displacement effect evaluation experiments. When a large number of Janus nanosheets are injected into the reservoir, they generate a certain level of diffusion force. This causes an imbalance in electrostatic repulsion on the rock surface, leading to the self-assembly of a “wedge” structure at the three-phase interface of the rock, crude oil, and the injected fluid. The schematic diagram is shown in Figure . Janus nanosheets act as regulators between the three phases. Through Brownian motion and electrostatic interactions, they generate a driving force that propels forward motion, effectively detaching crude oil from the rock surface, thereby enhancing crude oil recovery.

Schematic diagram of oil displacement mechanism.

The high surface-to-volume ratio of the Janus nanosheets results in elevated surface energy, facilitating their adsorption onto reservoir rock surfaces. Owing to their amphiphilic nature, the hydrophobic side of the nanosheets orients toward the rock surface while the hydrophilic side faces the aqueous phase, thereby converting the rock surface from oil-wet to water-wet and effectively reducing the oil–water interfacial tension.

Furthermore, due to the nanoscale size of the Janus nanosheets, they can effectively enter and plug small pore throats in low-permeability cores. Under specific pressure conditions, the nanosheets can break through these pore throats and continue migrating, demonstrating exceptional deep migration and profile control properties. After injection, the asymmetric lamellar structure of the Janus nanosheets allows them to adsorb effectively according to the reservoir environment. Their excellent temperature and salt resistance, coupled with strong emulsifying capabilities, enable the nanosheets to detach the remaining oil from the rock surface and form an interfacial film around the oil droplets. Simultaneously, the Janus nanosheets are transported into high-permeability areas by the injected water, where they effectively plug these high-permeability layers, forcing fluid flow into the lower-permeability zones and optimizing the water injection profile. The intelligent response of Janus nanosheets to the low-permeability reservoir environment activates the high oil saturation areas, achieving deep profile control and thereby improving oil recovery.

Field Application Effect Evaluation

Overview of Reservoir Geology

The porosity of the N group reservoir in the Z area of the Q oilfield (N, Z, and Q are codes for oilfield blocks and have no other meanings.) ranges from 4% to 17.8%, with permeability values between 0.5 and 45 × 10–3 μm2. The average porosity of the effective reservoir is 11.2%, while the average permeability is 4.7 × 10–3 μm2. The average density of the surface crude oil is 0.867 t/m3, and its viscosity is 26.47 mPa·s. The oilfield water is of the CaCl2 type, with a density of 1.0651 g/cm3 and a salinity of 105,589 mg/L. The formation temperature is 115 °C, and the total salinity of the formation water typically ranges from 13,379 to 231,771 mg/L. Prior to the experiment, the daily oil production of the block was 353.13 t, with an annual production of 13.4 × 104 t and a cumulative production of 55.74 × 104 t. The comprehensive water content was 49.36%. The field has 101 water injection wells and 81 oil wells. The annual water injection is 37.54 × 104 m3, with a cumulative water injection of 97.11 × 104 m3.

Conclusion

(1)In this study, Janus nanosheets with intelligent oil displacement functionality were successfully synthesized using a template method. Their nanoscale size of 31 nm and amphiphilic sheet-like structure facilitate adsorption onto rock surfaces. Experimental results demonstrate that the Janus nanosheets exhibit excellent dispersion stability under high-temperature (120 °C) and high-salinity (102,495 mg/L) conditions. In low-permeability cores, they are capable of forming dynamic blockages and migrating into deeper regions by overcoming pore–throat restrictions, thereby adjusting the displacement profile and mobilizing residual oil in low-permeability zones. Furthermore, the nanosheets’ emulsifying ability, capacity to reduce oil–water interfacial tension, and ability to alter rock wettability collectively promote oil film rupture and oil mobilization. These combined properties enable the Janus nanosheets to significantly enhance oil recovery in low-permeability reservoirs, demonstrating their intelligent responsiveness to reservoir conditions.(2)The field trial conducted in the Q oilfield further validated the practical effectiveness of the Janus nanosheets. The results demonstrated a total incremental oil recovery of 5579 tons, with a benefit–cost ratio of 1.78, indicating significant economic advantages. The Janus nanosheets exhibit responsiveness to low-permeability reservoir environments and high efficiency at low concentrations, enabling them to adapt well to high-temperature, high-salinity, low-permeability reservoirs and substantially enhance oil recovery. This study not only provides a novel intelligent oil displacement material for the efficient development of low-permeability reservoirs but also demonstrates, through systematic laboratory and field validation, the potential and advantages of Janus nanosheets in practical applications. Future work will focus on further optimizing the synthesis process and application conditions of the nanosheets to expand their applicability in more complex reservoir environments.
