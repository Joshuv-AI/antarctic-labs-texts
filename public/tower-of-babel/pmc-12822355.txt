A study on predicted in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with coronary heart disease: analysis of the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Yijing Tao, Guoxin Huang, Mengna Huang, Qianwen Yao, Zhisong Wang, Leng Han, Donglai Cao, Guoxiu Ke, Yiwen Zheng, Juan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe objective of this research was to identify predictors that increase the likelihood of death during hospitalization in critically ill patients with coronary heart disease using a predictive model.MethodsWe gathered clinical information from individuals diagnosed with coronary artery disease in the MIMIC-IV repository. Logistic regression analysis was used to examine the factors. The predictive nomogram was created using R software. Additionally, the model’s accuracy was evaluated. The clinical effectiveness was assessed through the utilization of clinical impact and decision curves.ResultsOut of 8261 patients in the study, 9.0% of those with coronary heart disease died in the hospital. Independent risk factors for mortality in ICU patients with coronary heart disease included age, gender, diabetes, MCV, RDW, ALB, prothrombin time, blood lactate levels, and mean blood glucose levels. The predictive factors were integrated into the model, and the nomogram was constructed using R software. The model achieved an area under the ROC curve of 0.761 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.740–0.781. Subsequently, a calibration curve was constructed, indicating a satisfactory level of fit. The model’s clinical effectiveness was evaluated using clinical impact curves and decision curves.ConclusionThe predictive model utilizing variables such as age, gender, diabetes status, MCV, RDW, ALB, prothrombin time, lactate, and average blood glucose levels demonstrates the ability to forecast the likelihood of in-hospital mortality among individuals diagnosed with coronary heart disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03319-7.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) represents a significant burden on public health due to its elevated rates of morbidity and mortality within the realm of cardiovascular diseases [1]. The increasing worry is centered on intensive care unit (ICU) patients with CAD, who need more intense life-saving treatments and have higher death rates [2]. Therefore, it is crucial to identify possible risk factors for death in individuals with CAD in order to predict outcomes. Research has indicated that risk factors influencing the prognosis of CAD encompass smoking, obesity, advanced age, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia [3]. Through extensive research, it has been elucidated that various metabolic parameters significantly influence the prognosis of CAD. Stress hyperglycemia [4], hypochloremia [5], blood glucose variability [6], and systolic blood pressure variability [7] have been identified as independent risk factors associated with a poorer prognosis in individuals with CAD. In the field of pathophysiology, inflammation is acknowledged as a crucial element in the onset and advancement of CAD [8]. Research has shown that patients in the ICU with CAD display significant abnormalities in inflammatory markers [9].

A nomogram is a visual representation of a prediction model based on regression analysis, making it easier to interpret and evaluate patient outcomes [10]. Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) is a detailed clinical database designed for use in intensive care environments [11]. The factors that can predict death in patients with severe CAD are currently unknown. Utilizing the MIMIC-IV database along with a predictive model can assist in assessing the risk of mortality in patients with CAD, ensuring trustworthy research findings. Although numerous prognostic models have been developed for CAD, few have focused specifically on critically ill patients admitted to the ICU. This subset of patients is characterized by heightened hemodynamic instability, multiorgan dysfunction, and substantially elevated short-term mortality risk, yet remains underrepresented in existing risk stratification frameworks. To address this critical gap, our study proposes and validates a tailored predictive model specifically designed to estimate in-hospital mortality in ICU-admitted CAD patients, thereby enhancing clinical decision-making and resource allocation for this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The data utilized in this study were sourced from the MIMIC-IV database. MIT Computational Physiology Laboratory created the extensive MIMIC database to store patient information. BIDMC’s database in Boston, USA, contains the medical files of ICU patients and is accessible to researchers worldwide for academic studies without charge. This dataset is extensive in scope, encompassing a variety of clinical data types such as patient vital signs, laboratory test results, medication usage, nursing records, surgical procedures, and disease diagnoses. The examination of the training plans of our partner institutions and database access rights has been successfully completed. All authors completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) program on human-subjects protection (certification Nos. 66756993). Use of the de-identified MIMIC-IV database is exempt from IRB review under the US 45 CFR 46.104(d)(4) exemption category; the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center IRB.

Patient information

Patients with coronary artery disease diagnosed either upon admission to or discharge from the ICU were chosen from the MIMIC database. The exclusion criteria encompassed the following: (1) patients with a duration of stay in the ICU of less than 24 h; (2) individuals with immune deficiency diseases; (3) pregnant individuals; (4) patients with pre-existing cardiac conditions; and (5) patients with > 10% missing data were excluded. For variables with ≤ 10% missingness, five imputed datasets were generated via multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) using the mice package in R, with pooled estimates obtained according to Rubin’s rules. The imputation model incorporated all analytic variables—including the outcome—and auxiliary covariates (e.g., age and sex) deemed clinically relevant; baseline characteristics were compared before and after imputation to assess consistency.

Diagnostic criteria

Conditions such as CAD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and diabetes were identified through the use of ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes.

Data collection

In this study, we obtained and analyzed the fundamental patient information, laboratory findings, and outcome measures. Fundamental data comprises age, gender, and comorbidities such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and diabetes. The laboratory results extracted primarily pertain to the initial measurements of various laboratory indicators upon admission to the intensive care unit, including white blood cell count (WBC), percentage of neutrophils (NEU), monocyte percentage (MONO), lymphocyte percentage (LY), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), platelet count (PLT), albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (AST). The outcome variables examined in this study encompassed patient survival rates, length of hospitalization, and duration of ICU stay. The principal outcome measure investigated was the overall mortality rate following admission to the ICU.

The development, validation, and assessment of predictive models

The 8261 eligible ICU-CAD patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 5783) and a validation set (n = 2478) in a 7:3 ratio. Within the training set, 10 fold stratified cross validation (stratified 10 fold CV) is used for model fitting and hyperparameter tuning; Each fold maintains a mortality rate of approximately 9% to ensure consistent positivity rates. The prediction model is first created with the training set data, then validated with the validation set data. When analyzing continuous variables, those that follow a normal distribution are usually shown as the mean ± standard deviation, whereas variables with a skewed distribution are usually represented by the median and interquartile range. Different techniques are used for comparison depending on the types of data being analyzed, including the t-test, chi-square test, and Wilcoxon rank sum test.

Both univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted to determine predictive factors, with variables showing a significance level of p < 0.05 in the univariate analysis being incorporated into the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Before fitting the multivariable logistic-regression model, we assessed multicollinearity among all continuous and categorical predictors. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) ranged from 1.1 to 2.3, well below the conventional threshold of 5, indicating no meaningful collinearity. Based on this rationale, the nomogram prediction model is constructed utilizing the R programming language. Subsequently, various methods are employed to validate and assess the model. At first, the model’s accuracy was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC). Subsequently, the model’s effectiveness was assessed through the utilization of the calibration curve, clinical impact curve, and decision curve. A p-value below 0.05 was used to define a result that is statistically significant. The study’s statistical analysis utilized IBM SPSS Statistics 24.0 and R version 3.3.3.

Result

Patient characteristics

32,904 patients were diagnosed with CAD within the first day of being admitted to the ICU. A total of 8261 patients met the inclusion criteria after applying the specified exclusion criteria. An overview of the training cohort (n = 5783) and validation cohort (n = 2478) can be seen in Fig. 1. Following that, we described the basic characteristics of the patients in Table 1. The mean age of the patient cohort was 70 (62–78) years, with a majority of 5714 individuals (69.2%) being male. Furthermore, a 9.0% mortality rate was noted in patients with CAD. We compared the data from the training cohort and the validation cohort, which were assigned randomly. The findings indicated that the data distribution between the two cohorts was relatively uniform. Table 1 shows the baseline feature comparison between the training set and the validation set, confirming that there is no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05), ensuring distribution consistency.

Factors predicting hospital mortality in CAD patients

Following this, the important factors found in the multivariate analysis were incorporated into the predictive model. Nine independent risk factors for in-hospital death in CAD patients were identified, such as age, gender, diabetes, MCV, RDW, ALB, PT, lactate, and average blood sugar levels (Table 3). The aforementioned risk factors were integrated into the predictive model, and R software was utilized to construct the nomogram for the prediction model (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Coronary artery disease stands as a prominent cardiovascular ailment impacting the worldwide population [12]. Prior epidemiological research has identified numerous risk factors and prognostic indicators for coronary heart disease (CHD), and the implementation of active interventions has led to a substantial improvement in the morbidity and mortality rates associated with CHD [13]. Our research results show that age, sex, diabetes, MCV, RDW, ALB, PT, lactate, and average blood sugar levels are important factors that predict mortality in ICU patients with CAD. This data is advantageous for ICU to quickly recognize patients with CAD who might have a higher chance of death, thus improving clinical decision-making.

Research has demonstrated that both age and androgen levels are distinct risk factors associated with a negative prognosis in individuals with CAD [14]. As individuals get older, the likelihood of developing CAD increases [15]. In a longitudinal study of individuals in the middle-aged demographic, findings indicate a positive correlation between advancing age and heightened susceptibility to CAD [16]. Studies have shown that as people get older, they undergo a slow development of metabolic imbalance, increasing their chances of developing age-related chronic illnesses like CHD and early death from CHD [17]. Advanced age is a major factor that independently contributes to the negative outlook of CAD and also impacts the body’s hormone production [18]. Epidemiological data suggests a correlation between androgen levels and various cardiovascular diseases [19]. CHD is a disease that affects both men and women, but studies have shown that about one-fifth of men and one-tenth of women die of CHD [20]. This aligns with the results of our study. However, there are contradictory results that question our findings, and no statistically significant link was found between androgen levels and a negative outlook for coronary artery disease [21].

CAD is a condition characterized by dysregulation of lipid metabolism, resulting in the development of coronary atherosclerosis [22]. As research progresses, an increasing number of individuals are coming to view atherosclerotic lesions as a disease characterized by inflammation [23]. RDW, a measure evaluated in standard blood tests, can indicate inflammation [24]. RDW has been recognized as a novel prognostic indicator in the examination of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular ailments, as well as a range of infectious diseases [25]. The RDW may serve as a marker for the inflammatory status of the body and has been linked to elevated rates of in-hospital mortality among individuals diagnosed with CAD [26]. Furthermore, diminished serum albumin concentrations have been linked to a heightened likelihood of mortality stemming from cardiovascular ailments [27]. Albumin is a highly prevalent protein found in blood that serves to bind and transport a variety of substances within the plasma. Research has demonstrated that serum albumin could help alleviate endothelial dysfunction associated with inflammation and oxidative stress, while low levels of albumin may exacerbate this physiological process [28]. Recent research indicates that the correlation between the ratio of RDW to albumin levels is linked to unfavorable outcomes in a range of medical conditions [29, 30]. Some have suggested that the prognostic value of RDW and albumin levels in predicting CAD is somewhat restricted due to their limited sensitivity and specificity [31, 32]. Our research revealed that both RDW and albumin were determined to be important factors predicting in-hospital mortality among individuals with CAD. These biomarkers can be easily measured using venous blood samples in clinical settings and are commonly utilized as straightforward indicators of disease severity. Meanwhile, this is also a clinically interpretable and simplified bedside tool for early risk stratification of CAD-ICU patients, especially in cases where complex scoring is impractical.

Diabetes is considered a primary coexisting condition of CAD, with studies showing a significantly higher chance of death from CAD in people with diabetes compared to those without the condition [33]. It has been suggested that hyperglycemia may exacerbate oxidative stress and inflammation, leading to myocardial injury and mortality [34]. Furthermore, diabetic patients exhibit metabolic syndrome and disorders within the coagulation system, contributing to a prothrombotic state and ultimately resulting in increased mortality rates [35]. The average blood glucose (glumbg) refers to an average state in which blood glucose levels fluctuate between high and low. Several research studies have shown a connection between high blood sugar levels and negative results in people with CAD [36]. A study following a group of patients diagnosed with CHD found a strong link between high average blood sugar levels while in the hospital and negative outcomes in the short and long term, regardless of whether the patients had diabetes [37]. This finding aligns with the results of this study.

MCV serves as a metric for assessing the dimensions of erythrocytes. Prior research has found a strong connection between MCV and endothelial dysfunction, as well as cardiovascular events [38]. New research suggests that high MCV levels are associated with a negative long-term outlook in non-anemic individuals with CAD [39]. Furthermore, a cross-sectional study identified a significant correlation between MCV and the severity of CHD [40]. These results align with the findings of our study. The research we conducted showed that increased prothrombin time levels were a notable predictor of mortality during hospitalization for individuals with CAD. Studies suggest that this occurrence could be linked to changes in autonomic nervous system function and a heightened inflammatory reaction [41]. Research has demonstrated that elevated levels of prothrombin time (PT) serve as a significant independent prognostic indicator for in-hospital mortality among individuals diagnosed with CAD [42]. The alteration in coagulation factor levels is influenced by either environmental or genetic factors [43]. Previous studies have shown a strong connection between activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) and the seriousness of CAD, in comparison to the other two coagulation factors (PT and INR). Nevertheless, there was no notable connection found between the recorded PT levels and the outcome of CHD [44]. Blood lactate is produced in response to hypoxia and serves as a marker of inflammation, a characteristic of atherosclerotic plaques [45].

Our results demonstrate that mortality in patients with coronary artery disease can be predicted efficiently and accurately using only routine, readily available clinical indices. The study offers three distinct strengths. First, in contrast to earlier single-centre or small-cohort investigations, we derived and validated our model from MIMIC-IV—a large, heterogeneous, publicly accessible ICU database. This design substantially enhances external generalisability and reproducibility. Second, the nomogram integrates easily obtainable laboratory values (RDW, MCV, albumin, prothrombin time, lactate, mean blood glucose) with demographic and comorbidity data. Although individual components such as RDW or albumin have been evaluated previously, our model is among the first to combine these low-cost parameters into a unified bedside tool specifically tailored for critically ill CAD patients, thereby optimising clinical usability. Finally, beyond discrimination and calibration, decision-curve analysis and clinical impact curves confirmed net clinical benefit, showing that the model can meaningfully influence management—e.g., identifying high-risk individuals who may gain most from aggressive interventions—an aspect frequently neglected by prior risk scores.

Limitations: The retrospective design inherently invites bias; prospective validation is therefore essential. Additionally, MIMIC-IV predominantly reflects Western populations, and further evaluation across diverse ethnicities and healthcare systems is required. Although internal and external validations showed robust performance, larger multinational cohorts are needed to confirm generalisability before widespread implementation. Finally, the MIMIC-IV ‘hospital_expire_flag’ documents in-hospital death but does not provide ICD-coded immediate cause of death or death certificate information. Consequently, we could not distinguish cardiac from non-cardiac deaths. Therefore, the nomogram should be interpreted as a bedside tool for global risk stratification in CAD-ICU patients, not as a disease-specific prognostic model.

Conclusion

The prediction model indicated that advanced age, being male, having diabetes, elevated MCV and RDW levels, decreased ALB levels, prolonged PT, increased blood lactate levels, and higher mean blood glucose levels were linked to a higher likelihood of in-hospital mortality among individuals with severe coronary artery disease. It is therefore possible to implement proactive interventions for high-risk patients after creating a customized forecasting model.
