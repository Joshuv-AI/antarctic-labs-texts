Anisotropy can make a moving active fluid membrane rough or crumpled
Debayan Jana, Astik Haldar, Abhik Basu

Abstract
We present a hydrodynamic theory of anisotropic and inversion-asymmetric moving active permeable fluid membranes. These are described by an anisotropic Kardar-Parisi-Zhang equation. Depending upon the anisotropy parameters, the membrane is either effectively isotropic and algebraically rough with translational short, but orientational long-range order, or unstable, suggestive of membrane crumpling.

In this article, we set up the hydrodynamic theory of a nearly flat patch of an anisotropic and inversion-asymmetric moving permeable active fluid membrane. The activity could be due to an active species living on the membrane or due to activity in the embedding medium. For simplicity, we assume that there are no conservation laws or broken symmetry modes associated with the activity. Thus, if the activity is due to an active species, e.g., a membrane-spanning active protein, then its concentration is not assumed to be conserved, or if it is due to the activity of the embedding medium, e.g., an orientable active fluid, then it is in its isotropic phase. We consider an anisotropic membrane. We argue that the, assuming Rouse dynamics
[
9
]
for simplicity, hydrodynamic equation for the undulation modes of an anisotropic membrane given by a single-valued height field
h({\bf x},t)
is given by

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}+\frac{\lambda}{2}{({\bm{\nabla}}h)^{2}}=\nu_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}h+f.}

(1)

Here
\nu_{ij}
is symmetric under the interchange of
i
and
j
and is an effective anisotropic tension. Noise

has zero-mean and a variance given by

{\langle f({\bf x},t)f({\bf 0},0)\rangle=2D\delta^{2}({\bf x})\delta(t).}

(2)

[

[

We show that our model exhibits algebraic rough phase and is characterized by translational short-range order (SRO) and orientational long-range order (LRO) and specifically belongs to the KPZ universility class. Using DNS we verify for different sets of model parameters that the scaling exponents remain almost same indicating the putative algebraic rough phase is model parameter independent and indeed belongs to the KPZ universility class. Further, the algebraically rough phase is effectively isotropic with inversion-asymmetry and translational SRO and orientational LRO, akin to a 2D KPZ surface
[
16
,
17
,
10
]
and the crumpled phase has translational and orientational SRO, respectively.

We now derive Eq. (
1
) and use it to establish the above results and obtain the phase diagrams. The membrane configurations are given by three-dimensional position vector
{\bf R}({\vec{s}},t)
, where
{\vec{s}}=(s_{1},s_{2})
is a two-dimensional position vector embedded on the membrane, which label points on the membrane. The local membrane velocity
{\bf v}({\vec{s}},t)\equiv\partial{\bf R}/\partial t
, for a convected or embedded coordinate system
[
18
,
19
]
. We now specialize for nearly flat inversion-asymmetric active membrane patches without any overhangs, for which the Monge gauge
[
20
]
is a convenient choice, in which
(s_{1},s_{2})\equiv(x,y)
and
{\bf R}({\bf x})=({\bf x},h({\bf x})),\,{\bf x}=(x,y)
. The only hydrodynamic variable is
h({\bf x},t)
, measured with respect to an arbitrary base plane in the Monge gauge
[
20
]
. Due to the breakdown of inversion symmetry, the patch, considered permeable, is generically moving in a direction local normal to itself. A change in the membrane conformation
h({\bf x},t)
can take place by the drift of the membrane due to activity and relaxational dynamics, which we assume to be Rouse dynamics for simplicity. The general equation of motion of
h({\bf x},t)
, measured with respect to an arbitrary base plane in the Monge gauge
[
20
]
, reads

{\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}=v\sqrt{1+({\bm{\nabla}}h)^{2}}-\Gamma\frac{\delta{\cal F}}{\delta h}+f,}

(3)

Here,
{\cal F}=\int d^{2}x[\frac{K}{2}(\nabla^{2}h)^{2}+C\nabla^{2}h]
is a free energy of a tensionless equilibrium fluid membrane (ignoring anharmonic contributions), which governs the relaxation of the membrane to equilibrium in the absence of any activity; here
K>0
is the bending stiffness
1
1

1

Considering for simplicity an isotropic lipid membrane in the absence of any active interactions with the embedding environment, we use an isotropic form of
{\cal F}
, characterized by a single scalar bending stiffness
K
. Since this contribution to the eventual equation of motion turns out to be irrelevant in the long wavelength limit, it leaves our subsequent analysis unaffected.
,
{\bm{\nabla}}\equiv(\partial/\partial x,\,\partial/\partial y)
is the 2D gradient operator, and
C
is the spontaneous curvature that can be generically present in an inversion-asymmetric membrane and makes the membrane curve one way or the other depending upon its sign. In the Monge gauge, valid for a planar membrane patch, the spontaneous curvature term being a total derivative term vanishes and makes no contribution to
\cal F
, and
\Gamma>0
is a damping coefficient. Further, we assume that a small membrane patch moves locally normally to itself at a velocity of magnitude
v
that is in general a function of the local membrane conformation. Such a normal drift naturally causes
h
(measured with respect to an arbitrary but fixed reference plane) to change. The square root factor with
v
on the right-hand side of (
3
) accounts for the change in
h
, measured along the
\hat{z}
axis due to a
v
being along the local normal
[
10
]
.
Rotational invariance requires
v
must remain unchanged under a tilt of the reference plane. This to the linear order in
h
gives that

v(h)\equiv v_{0}+\nu_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}h.

(4)

In the cellular
context,
v_{0}
sets the scale of the drift speed of the membrane, e.g.,
due to actin filament polymerization
[
22
]
(see also below). The second term on the right-hand side of (
4
) gives the contribution from the membrane conformation fluctuations to the drift. Assuming small fluctuations, we expand the square root in (
3
) and obtain (
1
), where the bending energy term scales being
\mathcal{O}(k^{4})
is irrelevant in the long-wavelength limit and hence is dropped.

A possible microscopic realization of this would be
a nearly flat patch of an inversion-asymmetric, anisotropic
fluid membrane placed in an isotropic, active suspension of actin
filaments
[
4
]
grafted normally to the membrane locally. Let
\bf p
be a three-dimensional unit vector (
p^{2}=1
) that gives the local orientation of the actin filaments and
\bf n
be the local unit normal to the membrane;
{\bf n}=(\hat{z},-{\bm{\nabla}}_{i}h)
to the linear order in height fluctuations (
i=x,y
). Locally normal grafting of the filaments implies
{\bf p}\cdot{\bf n}=1
, giving
p_{i}=-\partial_{i}h
(
i=x,y
) and
p_{z}=1
to the linear order in fluctuations. The actin filaments exert forces locally normally to the membrane
[
23
]
, giving a three-dimensional local permeation flow velocity

{\bf v}\sim v_{0}{\bf p}+{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot[{\bm{\zeta}}{\bf p}{\bf p}].

(5)

Now, extracting the component of
{\bf v}
along the membrane’s local normal and to linear order in
h
yields
v
as above
[
24
,
8
]
. Writing the second term on the right-hand side of (
5
) as the divergence of an active stress
{\bm{\sigma}}^{a}\equiv{\bm{\zeta}}{\bf p}{\bf p}
i.e.,
\sigma_{ij}^{a}\equiv\zeta_{ij\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}
, we see that
\nu_{ij}
is proportional to
\sigma_{ij}^{a}
.
Tensor
{\bm{\zeta}}\equiv\zeta_{ij\alpha\beta}
encodes the anisotropy in the membrane, e.g., anisotropy in the actomyosin cortex
[
25
]
, or anisotropic inclusions in the membrane
[
26
]
. Some additional details are given in Appendix
A
for interested readers. The forms of these anisotropy parameters should depend explicitly on the specific model representation. In the absence of anisotropy, in which case the strength of the active stress is described by a scalar parameter, say,
\Delta\mu
, a positive (negative)
\Delta\mu
refers to extensile (contractile) activity
[
4
]
. Here, however, the active stress is characterized by three parameters, and hence a simple extensile or contractile characterization is no longer possible.

We now theoretically explain the above numerical observations, quantitatively characterizing the phases, by using Eq. (
1
).
Equation (
1
) admits a pseudo-Galilean invariance under the transformation
t^{\prime}=t,\,x_{i}^{\prime}=x_{i}+\lambda u_{i}t,\,h^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=h({\bf x},t)+u_{i}x_{i}+\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}\,t
(see Appendix
C
).
In (
1
),
\nu_{ij}
plays the role of an (active) anisotropic surface tension. With
\nu_{x},\nu_{y}>0
, linear stability requires that
\nu_{x}\nu_{y}\geq\overline{\nu}^{2}
, i.e.,
\beta\geq\alpha^{2}
; otherwise, instability ensues. Here
\beta=\frac{\nu_{y}}{\nu_{x}}
and
\alpha=\frac{\overline{\nu}}{\nu_{x}}
. If
\alpha=0
, then Eq. (
1
) is invariant under (separately)
x\rightarrow-x
or
y\rightarrow-y
; for
\alpha\neq 0
, the invariance is only under the
joint inversion
of
x
and
y
.
This means
\alpha_{c}=\pm\sqrt{\beta}
is a threshold of
\alpha
, with larger
|\alpha|
implies instability. This is the anisotropy-induced instability mentioned earlier in this article, which can occur even at zero noises, provided
|\alpha|>\sqrt{\beta}
. This then means in general the variance of the local height fluctuations
\langle[h({\bf x},t)-\overline{h}(t)]^{2}\rangle
and local normal fluctuation
\delta{\bf n}=-{\bm{\nabla}}h
,
\langle(\delta{\bf n}({\bf x},t))^{2}\rangle
diverge.
Can this instability be suppressed by including stabilizing higher-order damping terms, e.g.,
-\nu_{4}\nabla^{4}h,\,\nu_{4}>0
in (
1
)? Such a term can be generically present but is irrelevant if there is no linear instability. In the event of linear instability, these higher-order (in gradient) stabilizing terms are important. Their presence would ensure that at high-enough wave vectors the system is linearly stable. If
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
are the eigenvalues of of
\nu_{ij}
, then for membranes of linear size
L<(>)L_{c}\sim\sqrt{\nu_{4}/|\text{min}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})|}
are dominated by the stabilizing hyperdiffusion (destabilizing diffusion) terms. In this case,
\langle[h({\bf x},t)-\overline{h}(t)]^{2}\rangle
and
\delta{\bf n}=-{\bm{\nabla}}h
,
\langle(\delta{\bf n}({\bf x},t))^{2}\rangle
diverge not at every scale, but only if the linear system size
L
exceeds a threshold that scales with
\sqrt{\nu_{4}}

[
27
]
. Such divergences of
\langle(\delta{\bf n}({\bf x},t))^{2}\rangle
for sufficiently larger membranes indicates that such membranes with
L>L_{c}
exceeding the instability threshold may be
crumpled
, although (
1
)
cannot
be used to study the crumpled phase; see also Appendix
B
and Ref.
[
28
]
. This instability is reminiscent of the strongly crumpled phase in tensionless equilibrium asymmetric tethered membranes
[
29
,
30
]
. In this unstable phase, the variance of the local normal fluctuations
\sim\langle({\bm{\nabla}}h)^{2}\rangle
diverges for a sufficiently large but finite
L
, giving translational and orientational SRO. In this phase, the growth rate is maximum forming ripples at a particular angle depending on parameters
\nu_{x}
,
\nu_{y}
, and
\overline{\nu}
; see Fig.
1
(c). Consider the special case where
\nu_{x}=\nu_{y}
and
|\alpha|>1
; then ripples will form at an angle
\pi/4
(
3\pi/4
) with the

axis for
\alpha>1
(
\alpha<-1
); see Appendix
B
. See the ripples in Fig.

(d), forming at an angle
\pi/4
, and the corresponding movies MOV2 and MOV3 in SM

We now turn to the stable case with
|\alpha|<\sqrt{\beta}
and consider its stability and fluctuation properties. In the linear theory with
|\alpha|<\sqrt{\beta}
, width
{\mathcal{W}}_{\text{sat}}\sim\sqrt{\ln(L/a_{0})}
. As in the usual KPZ equation
[
16
]
, the combination of noises and nonlinearities here can change the linear theory results substantially. To study the nonlinear effects in the present model (
1
), it is instructive to perform a one-loop perturbative dynamic
renormalization group (RG) analysis on Eq. (
1
) with
|\alpha|<\sqrt{\beta}
and see how the predictions from the RG analysis compare with the results in Ref.
[
11
]
. The Wilson dynamic RG method
[
31
,
32
]
for our model closely resembles that for the KPZ equation
[
16
,
10
,
31
]
. Galilean invariance of (
1
) ensures that
\lambda
is unrenormalized, which is an exact statement and the analog of the nonrenormalization of the nonlinear coupling in the usual KPZ equation
[
10
,
33
]
; see also Appendix
C
. However, there are diverging one-loop corrections to
\nu_{x},\,\,\nu_{y},\,\overline{\nu}
, and
D
, which are represented by the one-loop Feynman diagrams (see Appendix
D.5
). Dimensional analysis allows us to define an effective dimensionless coupling constant
g
having a critical dimension 2 as

g=\frac{\lambda^{2}D}{16\pi(\nu_{x}\nu_{y}-\overline{\nu}^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}},

(6)

along with
\alpha
,
\beta
defined earlier. The RG differential recursion relations [
\exp(l)
is a dimensionless length] are

\displaystyle\frac{dD}{dl}=D\bigg[z-2-2\chi+g\frac{3(1+\beta^{2})/4+\beta/2+\alpha^{2}}{(\beta-\alpha^{2})}\bigg],

(7)

\displaystyle\frac{d\nu_{x}}{dl}=\nu_{x}[z-2-g(1/2-\beta/2)],

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{d\nu_{y}}{dl}=\nu_{y}[z-2-g(1/2-1/(2\beta))],

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{d\overline{\nu}}{dl}=\overline{\nu}[z-2-g],

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{dg}{dl}=2g^{2},

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{d\alpha}{dl}=-g(\alpha+\alpha\beta)/2,

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{d\beta}{dl}=g(1-\beta^{2})/2.

(13)

Here

and

are the dynamic and roughness exponents, respectively

10
]

So far we have considered a nearly flat patch within a Monge parametrization, which holds up to length scales at which curvature

in spite of the anisotropy
belongs to the isotropic 2D KPZ universality class (and hence isotropy is an emergent symmetry of the model in the long-wavelength limit). The prediction of an effectively isotropic algebraically rough phase in the linearly stable sector of Eq. (
1
) is a direct consequence of its mapping to the anisotropic KPZ equation studied in Refs.
[

Appendix A
Anisotropic KPZ equation

We start with Eq. (
5
) of the main text, which
gives the three-dimensional local permeation flow velocity

{\bf v}\sim v_{0}{\bf p}+{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bm{\sigma}},

(14)

which is understood to be in the direction of the local normal to a membrane patch, whose magnitude can be obtained by dotting the above equation with
{\bf n}
, the unit local normal to the membrane patch. Here the active stress tensor
{\bm{\sigma}}
is defined as

{\bm{\sigma}}\equiv{\bm{\zeta}}\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}.

(15)

The local orientation of actin filaments is described by a three-dimensional unit vector
\mathbf{p}
, which is assumed to be locally normal to the membrane, i.e., along the unit normal
\mathbf{n}
to the membrane patch in the Monge gauge, given by

{\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\bm{\nabla}h)^{2}}}\left(\hat{z},-\frac{\partial h}{\partial x},-\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}\right).}

(16)

Thus for a (assumed) nearly flat surface patch, the leading-order departures to the components of
\mathbf{p}
for a perfectly flat surface due to (small) fluctuations in
h
are
{\cal O}({\bm{\nabla}}h)
along the
x
and
y
directions.

Now the velocity along the
z
direction due to this active stress is given by [excluding a possible constant contribution marked by
v_{0}\mathbf{p}
in Eq. (
5
) of the main text]

v_{z}^{a}=\partial_{j}\sigma_{zj}^{a}=\zeta_{zj\gamma\delta}\,\partial_{j}(p_{\gamma}p_{\delta}).

(17)

Expanding,

\displaystyle v_{z}^{a}

\displaystyle=\zeta_{zjxx}\,\partial_{j}(p_{x}^{2})+\zeta_{zjyy}\,\partial_{j}(p_{y}^{2})+\zeta_{zjzz}\,\partial_{j}(p_{z}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\zeta_{zjxy}\,\partial_{j}(p_{x}p_{y})+\zeta_{zjyx}\,\partial_{j}(p_{y}p_{x})

\displaystyle\quad+\zeta_{zjxz}\,\partial_{j}(p_{x}p_{z})+\zeta_{zjzx}\,\partial_{j}(p_{z}p_{x})

\displaystyle\quad+\zeta_{zjyz}\,\partial_{j}(p_{y}p_{z})+\zeta_{zjzy}\,\partial_{j}(p_{z}p_{y}).

(18)

Since
\partial_{j}(p_{z}^{2})=0
to the lowest order in
h
, and the terms involving higher powers are of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
and hence irrelevant in long-wavelength limit, only last four terms contribute at leading order to
v_{z}^{a}
. Thus keeping only leading-order terms in the Monge gauge

\displaystyle v_{z}^{a}

\displaystyle=-\zeta_{zxxz}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x^{2}}-\zeta_{zxzx}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x^{2}}-\zeta_{zxyz}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial y}-\zeta_{zxzy}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial y}

\displaystyle\quad-\zeta_{zyxz}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial y}-\zeta_{zyzx}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial y}-\zeta_{zyyz}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial y^{2}}-\zeta_{zyzy}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial y^{2}}.

(19)

In obtaining the above results, any differences between an embedded coordinate defined on the nearly flat patch along the
xy
plane and the laboratory frame is ignored, since the corrections to the above results from the differences are higher order in
h
. Combining the above results with the constant contribution gives Eq. (
4
) of the main text.

Appendix B
Linear stability analysis

We start from Eq. (
1
) of the main text for an anisotropic membrane with a fixed background, ignoring the conservation of embedding bulk fluid momentum. As argued in the main text, the hydrodynamic equation governing the undulation modes of such a membrane, described by the height field
h({\bf x},t)
, is an anisotropic KPZ equation with a Gaussian-distributed, zero-mean white noise
f({\bf x},t)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}+\frac{\lambda}{2}{({\bm{\nabla}}h)^{2}}=\nu_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}h+f.

(20)

Here both
i
and
j
can take value
1
or
2
.
To perform linear stability analysis, we consider only the linear terms in Eq. (
1
), i.e., set
\lambda=0
. The resulting linearized equation of
h
in Fourier space is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h=-\nu_{x}(k_{x}^{2}+\beta k_{y}^{2}+2\alpha k_{x}k_{y})h,

(21)

where
{\bf k}=(k_{x},k_{y})
is a wavevector. We define

\beta\equiv\nu_{y}/\nu_{x}
,
\alpha\equiv{\overline{\nu}}/{\nu_{x}}
; see also the main text. To proceed further, we use the polar coordinates and introduce
k_{x}=k\cos\theta
and
k_{y}=k\sin\theta
, where
k=|{\bf k}|
and
\theta=\tan^{-1}(k_{y}/k_{x})
is the polar angle. Now for linear stability,
-\nu_{x}k^{2}(\cos^{2}\theta+\beta\sin^{2}\theta+\alpha\sin 2\theta)<0
for all
\theta
. With
\nu_{x},\,\nu_{y}>0
; as assumed in the main text, we must have

(1+\beta\tan^{2}\theta+2\alpha\tan\theta)>0
for all
\theta
to ensure linear stability. This means
\beta\geq\alpha^{2}
. When
1+\beta\tan^{2}\theta+2\alpha\tan\theta<0
, linear instability occurs. The growth rate of the fluctuations is maximized when
1+\beta\tan^{2}\theta+2\alpha\tan\theta
reaches its most negative value, which occurs at a specific
\theta
determined by the values of
\alpha
and
\beta
. Consequently, a stripelike pattern emerges based on the value of
\theta
. A representative snapshot of this case is shown in Fig.
1
(c) of the main text. Now we consider the special case
\nu_{x}=\nu_{y}=\nu
, i.e.,
\beta=1
and
\alpha=\overline{\nu}/\nu
. For linear stability,
-\nu k^{2}(1+\alpha\sin 2\theta)<0
for all
\theta
. With
\nu>0
, we must have

(1+\alpha\sin 2\theta)>0
for all
\theta
to ensure linear stability. Since the maximum of
|\sin\,2\theta|
is unity, the previous condition in turn means
|\alpha|\leq 1
, or
\nu\pm\overline{\nu}>0
. When
(1+\alpha\sin 2\theta)<0
, linear instability again ensues. The growth rate of the fluctuations is maximized when
(1+\alpha\sin 2\theta)
attains its largest negative value. For
\alpha>1
, this happens at
\theta=3\pi/4
, corresponding to
k_{x}=-k_{y}
giving the Fourier wavevector with the maximum growth rate. Keeping only the corresponding Fourier mode (which is the most dominant Fourier mode), we get

h(\mathbf{r})\approx h_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\frac{k}{\sqrt{2}}(-x+y)},

(22)

which has a phase given by

\phi(x,y)=\frac{k}{\sqrt{2}}(y-x).

(23)

If we move in the
xy
plane along paths with a constant phase, i.e., with constant
y-x
, the exponential factor
e^{i\phi}
does not change. Consequently, the height function
h(x,y)
does not vary along these directions. These lines, defined by
y-x=\text{const}
, correspond to the stripes observed in real space, forming at an angle of
\pi/4
. The periodicity of the stripes is controlled by the wavenumber
k_{\text{max}}\sim\pi/a_{0}\sim\pi
, given that
a_{0}=1
in our simulations. A representative snapshot of this pattern can be seen in Fig.
1
(d) of the main text.
On the other hand, for
\alpha<-1
, the maximum growth rate occurs at
\theta=\pi/4
, corresponding to
k_{x}=k_{y}
. Now using the logic used above, we can say this corresponds to the formation of stripes at an angle of
3\pi/4
in the
x
-
y
plane. We have verified this result (not shown).

Appendix C
Galilean invariance

We now explicitly show that under the Galilean transformation defined by

\displaystyle t\rightarrow t^{\prime}\equiv t,\,x_{i}\rightarrow x_{i}^{\prime}\equiv x_{i}+\lambda u_{i}t,\,h({\bf x},t)\rightarrow

\displaystyle h^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\equiv h({\bf x},t)+u_{i}x_{i}+\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}t.

(24)

Equation (
1
) remains invariant. Under the Galilean transformation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rightarrow\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}+\lambda u_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}^{\prime}},\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\rightarrow\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h({\bf x},t)}{\partial t}\rightarrow\frac{\partial h^{\prime}({\bf x^{\prime}},t^{\prime})}{\partial t^{\prime}}+\lambda u_{i}\frac{\partial h^{\prime}({\bf x^{\prime}},t^{\prime})}{\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}-\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}.

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{\partial h({\bf x},t)}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial h({\bf x},t)}{\partial x_{i}}\rightarrow\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{\partial h^{\prime}({\bf x^{\prime}},t^{\prime})}{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}}\frac{\partial h^{\prime}({\bf x^{\prime}},t^{\prime})}{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}-\lambda u_{i}\frac{\partial h^{\prime}({\bf x^{\prime}},t^{\prime})}{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}}.

(26)

\displaystyle\nu_{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial h({\bf x},t)}{\partial x_{j}}\rightarrow\nu_{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}_{i}}\frac{\partial h^{\prime}({\bf x^{\prime}},t^{\prime})}{\partial x^{\prime}_{j}}.

(27)

The variance of the noise
f
, being
\delta
-correlated in time, remains invariant under this transformation. Using Eqs. (
25
), (
26
), and (
27
) and rewriting Eq. (
1
) in terms of
h^{\prime}({\bf x^{\prime}},t^{\prime})
,
{\bf x^{\prime}}
, and
t^{\prime}
, we find that Eq. (
1
) is Galilean invariant.

D.2
One-loop corrections to the model parameters

In the (more usual) isotropic models, applications of the Wilson momentum space RG involves integrating the wave-vector magnitude
q
within a shell
\Lambda/b\leq q\leq\Lambda,\,b>1

[
65
,
31
]

q_{x}
over the whole range from
-\infty
to
\infty
and then integrate
q_{y}
over two thin strips
\Lambda/b\leq q_{y}\leq\Lambda
and
-\Lambda\leq q_{y}\leq-\Lambda/b
with
b>1
; see Fig.
5
. Of course, we could have chosen another integration scheme, in which
q_{y}
is integrated over the whole range from
-\infty
to
\infty
, and then integrate
q_{x}
over two thin strips
\Lambda/b\leq q_{x}\leq\Lambda
and
-\Lambda\leq q_{x}\leq-\Lambda/b
. For our model, the two schemes are equivalent, connected to the fact that the different anisotropic terms of the same order scale in the same way.

Notice that the limits of the
q_{x}
integration is extended from
-\infty
to
+\infty
, although there is a physical upper wave-vector cutoff
~\Lambda
in both the
x
and
y
directions. The extension of the limits of the
q_{x}
integrals is done for reasons of calculational convenience and has no effect on the physical results in the long wave-length limit. This is because the integrands vanish in the limit of
q_{x}\rightarrow\pm\infty
and the dominant contributions to all the integrals come from small
q_{x}\sim q_{y}
. See also Ref.
[
66
]
.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{x}^{m}}\int\frac{dq_{x}}{2\pi}\int\frac{dq_{y}}{2\pi}\frac{\tilde{f}(q_{x},q_{y})}{(q_{x}^{2}+2\alpha q_{x}q_{y}+\beta q_{y}^{2})^{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu_{x}^{m}}\int\frac{dq_{x}}{2\pi}\int\frac{dq_{y}}{2\pi}\frac{\tilde{f}(q_{x},q_{y})}{(q_{x}-a_{+})^{m}(q_{x}-a_{-})^{m}},

where

q_{x}
and
q_{y}

D.6
RG recursion relations

We integrated out the dynamical fields with wave-vectors
k_{y}
in the range (
\Lambda/b\leq k_{y}\leq\Lambda
and
-\Lambda\leq k_{y}\leq-\Lambda/b
) in the action functional (

). Now we rescale space, time, and fields to raise the wave-vector cutoff of
k_{y}
to
\pm\Lambda
. We rescale

{\bf k}\to{\bf k}^{{}^{\prime}}=b{\bf k}\implies{\bf x}\to{\bf x}^{{}^{\prime}}=\frac{{\bf x}}{b}
; similarly,
\omega\to\omega^{{}^{\prime}}=b^{z}\omega\implies t{{}^{\prime}}=\frac{t}{b^{z}}
. Furthermore, the fields are rescaled as

\displaystyle h^{<}({\bf k},\omega)=\xi h({\bf k}^{{}^{\prime}},\omega^{{}^{\prime}}),

\displaystyle\hat{h}^{<}({\bf k},\omega)=\hat{\xi}\hat{h}({\bf k}^{{}^{\prime}},\omega^{{}^{\prime}}).

Using these and imposing the condition that coefficient of
\int_{{\bf x},t}\hat{h}\partial_{t}h
remains unity under rescaling we find that, model parameters scale as

\displaystyle\nu_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}}=\nu_{ij}^{<}b^{z-2},

\displaystyle D^{{}^{\prime}}=D^{<}b^{d+3z}\xi^{-2},

\displaystyle\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}=\lambda^{<}b^{-d-2}\xi.

Also,
h^{<}({\bf x},t)
scale as
b^{-d-z}\xi h({\bf x}^{{}^{\prime}},t^{{}^{\prime}})
. Defining
\xi_{R}=b^{-d-z}\xi=b^{\chi}
we get model parameters rescale as

\displaystyle D^{{}^{\prime}}=D^{<}b^{z-d-2\chi},

\displaystyle\nu_{x}^{{}^{\prime}}=\nu_{x}^{<}b^{z-2},

\displaystyle\nu_{y}^{{}^{\prime}}=\nu_{y}^{<}b^{z-2},

\displaystyle\overline{\nu}^{{}^{\prime}}=\overline{\nu}^{<}b^{z-2},

\displaystyle\lambda^{{}^{\prime}}=\lambda^{<}b^{\chi+z-2}.

Here
\chi
is the roughness exponent of an anisotropic KPZ surface. Since there is no correction to
\lambda
, so
\lambda^{<}=\lambda
. We further get

\displaystyle D^{<}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D\biggr[1+\frac{2\lambda^{2}D}{16(2\pi)(\nu_{x}\nu_{y}-\overline{\nu}^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\Bigr(\frac{3\beta^{2}}{4}+\frac{1}{2}(\beta+2\alpha^{2})+\frac{3}{4}\Bigr)\frac{\ln b}{(\beta-\alpha^{2})}\biggr],

(51)

\displaystyle\nu_{x}^{<}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{x}\biggr[1-\frac{2\lambda^{2}D}{16(2\pi)(\nu_{x}\nu_{y}-\overline{\nu}^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\Bigr(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\beta}{2}\Bigr)\ln b\biggr],

(52)

\displaystyle\nu_{y}^{<}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{y}\biggr[1-\frac{2\lambda^{2}D}{16(2\pi)(\nu_{x}\nu_{y}-\overline{\nu}^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\Bigr(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\Bigr)\ln b\biggr],

(53)

\displaystyle\overline{\nu}^{<}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\nu}\biggr[1-\frac{2\lambda^{2}D}{16(2\pi)(\nu_{x}\nu_{y}-\overline{\nu}^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\ln b\biggr].

(54)

The extra factor of

i.e., (

for small

and dimensionless ratios

Appendix E
Connections to other anisotropic KPZ equations

We begin with the most general anisotropic KPZ equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}+\frac{\lambda_{x}^{\prime}}{2}\Big(\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\Big)^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{y}^{\prime}}{2}\Big(\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}\Big)^{2}+\overline{\lambda}^{\prime}\,\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\,\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad=\nu_{x}^{\prime}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x^{2}}+\nu_{y}^{\prime}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial y^{2}}+2\overline{\nu}^{\prime}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial y}+f.

(55)

The nonlinear terms in (
55
) may be written in a compact form as
\lambda_{ij}(\partial_{i}h)(\partial_{j}h)/2
,
where the matrix

\lambda_{ij}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{x}^{\prime}&\overline{\lambda}^{\prime}\\
\overline{\lambda}^{\prime}&\lambda_{y}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}

(56)

is a symmetric matrix and hence can be diagonalized by an appropriate similarity transformation [which here is a coordinate transformation, i.e., (
x^{\prime}=x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta
and
y^{\prime}=-x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta
), defined by a rotation angle
\theta
]. The rotation angle
\theta
is obtained by requiring that the coefficient of mixed derivative term
\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\,\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}
vanishes after rotation, which leads to

\tan 2\theta=\frac{2\overline{\lambda}^{\prime}}{\lambda_{x}^{\prime}-\lambda_{y}^{\prime}}.

(57)

In this rotated frame, the equation of motion reduces to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}+\frac{\lambda_{x}}{2}\Big(\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\Big)^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{y}}{2}\Big(\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\nu_{x}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x^{2}}+\nu_{y}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial y^{2}}+2\overline{\nu}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial y}+f,

(58)

where the linear couplings remain, in general, anisotropic and may include an off-diagonal term. Here

\displaystyle\lambda_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{x}^{\prime}\cos^{2}\theta+\lambda_{y}^{\prime}\sin^{2}\theta+\overline{\lambda}^{\prime}\sin 2\theta,

(59)

\displaystyle\lambda_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{x}^{\prime}\sin^{2}\theta+\lambda_{y}^{\prime}\cos^{2}\theta-\overline{\lambda}^{\prime}\sin 2\theta,

(60)

\displaystyle\nu_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{x}^{\prime}\cos^{2}\theta+\nu_{y}^{\prime}\sin^{2}\theta+\overline{\nu}^{\prime}\sin 2\theta,

(61)

\displaystyle\nu_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{x}^{\prime}\sin^{2}\theta+\nu_{y}^{\prime}\cos^{2}\theta-\overline{\nu}^{\prime}\sin 2\theta,

(62)

\displaystyle\overline{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nu_{x}^{\prime}\sin\theta\cos\theta+\nu_{y}^{\prime}\sin\theta\cos\theta+\overline{\nu}^{\prime}\cos 2\theta,

(63)

are the modified linear and nonlinear couplings.
We consider the case in which the nonlinear couplings
\lambda_{x}
and
\lambda_{y}
have the same sign. In this situation, we perform an anisotropic rescaling of the spatial coordinates as

x\rightarrow x^{\prime}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{y}}{\lambda_{x}}}\,x,\qquad y\rightarrow y^{\prime}=y,

(64)

which
brings Eq. (
58
) to the form

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}+\frac{\lambda_{y}}{2}(\bm{\nabla}h)^{2}=\tilde{\nu}_{x}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x^{2}}+\nu_{y}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial y^{2}}+2\tilde{\nu}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x\partial y}+f,}

(65)

with transformed coefficients

\tilde{\nu}_{x}=\frac{\lambda_{y}}{\lambda_{x}}\nu_{x},\qquad\tilde{\nu}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{y}}{\lambda_{x}}}\,\overline{\nu}.

(66)

Equation (
65
) is exactly the same as Eq. (
1
), with the identification (
\lambda_{y},\tilde{\nu}_{x},\nu_{y},\tilde{\nu})\equiv(\lambda,\nu_{x},\nu_{y},\overline{\nu}
), as discussed in the main text, which we have used to describe an active anisotropic fluid membrane. Next, notice that the linear surface tension tensor appearing in Eq. (
65
) is
symmetric and can therefore be brought to a diagonal form by a rotation of the spatial coordinates using the same transformation introduced earlier. The rotation angle
\tilde{\theta}
is
fixed by the condition

\tan 2\tilde{\theta}=\frac{2\tilde{\nu}}{\tilde{\nu}_{x}-\nu_{y}}.

(67)

After performing this transformation, the mixed second derivative term is
eliminated and Eq. (
65
) takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}+\frac{\lambda_{y}}{2}(\bm{\nabla}h)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{xx}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial x^{2}}+\nu_{yy}\frac{\partial^{2}h}{\partial y^{2}}+f,

(68)

with the diagonal coefficients given by

\displaystyle\nu_{xx}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}_{x}\cos^{2}\tilde{\theta}+\nu_{y}\sin^{2}\tilde{\theta}+\tilde{\nu}\sin 2\tilde{\theta},

(69)

\displaystyle\nu_{yy}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}_{x}\sin^{2}\tilde{\theta}+\nu_{y}\cos^{2}\tilde{\theta}-\tilde{\nu}\sin 2\tilde{\theta}.

(70)

Equation (
68
) is same as the anisotropic KPZ model studied in the earlier work
[
11
,
12
]
.

Let us now define an isotropic damping coefficient
\nu_{\mathrm{iso}}
and an anisotropy parameter
\nu_{\mathrm{aniso}}
as

\displaystyle\nu_{\mathrm{iso}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu_{xx}+\nu_{yy}}{2}=\frac{\tilde{\nu}_{x}+\nu_{y}}{2},

(71)

\displaystyle\nu_{\mathrm{aniso}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu_{xx}-\nu_{yy}=\sqrt{(\tilde{\nu}_{x}-\nu_{y})^{2}+4\tilde{\nu}^{\,2}}.

(72)

Although the three coefficients
(\tilde{\nu}_{x},\,\nu_{y},\,\tilde{\nu})
can be equivalently reparametrized in terms of
(\nu_{\mathrm{iso}},\,\nu_{\mathrm{aniso}},\,\tilde{\theta})
, where
\tilde{\theta}
denotes the rotation angle specifying the orientation of the principal axes of the diffusion tensor, the physical anisotropy is fully characterized by the eigenvalue difference
\nu_{\mathrm{aniso}}
, which depends on
\tilde{\nu}_{x}
,
\nu_{y}
, and
\tilde{\nu}
.

Considering only the linear part of Eq. (
68
), the evolution equation in Fourier space becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h=-\nu_{xx}k^{2}\left(\cos^{2}\theta+\beta\sin^{2}\theta\right)h,

(73)

where
k_{x}=k\cos\theta
,
k_{y}=k\sin\theta
, and
\beta=\nu_{yy}/\nu_{xx}
. For linear stability, the coefficient multiplying
h
must remain positive for all orientations of
\theta
, i.e.,

\displaystyle 1+(\beta-1)\sin^{2}\theta>0.

(74)

When
\nu_{xx}>0
and
\nu_{yy}<0
, we have
\beta<0
, and the above condition cannot be satisfied for all
\theta
. Consequently, the interface is linearly unstable. The instability grows most rapidly along the direction for which

\displaystyle 1+(\beta-1)\sin^{2}\theta

(75)

attains its most negative value. This occurs at
\theta=\pm\pi/2
, corresponding to wave vectors oriented along the
y
direction. Following the analysis presented in Appendix
B
, one therefore expects the emergence of stripes parallel to the
x
axis prior to the divergence of the interface fluctuations.

Conversely, when
\nu_{yy}>0
and
\nu_{xx}<0
, the most unstable mode is oriented along the
x
direction, leading to the formation of stripes parallel to the
y
axis before the instability develops fully (see Appendix
F
).

Notice that under renormalization group transformations on Eq. (
1
) of the main text, the off-diagonal linear term
\overline{\nu}
turns out to be irrelevant (in the RG sense) in the long-wavelength limit and flows to zero. Consequently, the effective large-scale theory reduces to the anisotropic KPZ model previously studied in Refs.
[
11
,
12
]
. Thus our RG results are consistent with the symmetry arguments elucidated above. Furthermore, when
\nu_{x}\to\nu_{y}
, the system becomes effectively isotropic and crosses over to the standard KPZ universality class.

Importantly, Eq. (
58
) cannot be mapped onto the previously studied anisotropic KPZ models by any coordinate transformation when
\lambda_{x}
and
\lambda_{y}
have opposite signs, a situation not directly relevant for an active membrane (and hence is not studied here).
