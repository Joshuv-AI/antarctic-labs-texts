Physics-Informed Neural Network-Based Pulsatile Flow Modeling and Targeted Drug Delivery Optimization in Computational Hemodynamics
Authors: Aaryasinh Kunalsinh Vaghela
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Targeted drug delivery in vascular diseases requires accurate modeling of blood flow dynamics. This study utilizes physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) to simulate pulsatile hemodynamics and optimize delivery timing for maximum therapeutic efficacy.Methods:A PINN framework was developed to solve the Navier–Stokes and convection–diffusion equations in a reconstructed arterial domain. Pulsatile inlet conditions were imposed to replicate physiological blood flow. Drug bolus injections were simulated at varying phases of the cardiac cycle to evaluate optimal timing.Results:The model achieved high accuracy with under 2% relative error compared to finite element benchmarks. Maximum drug accumulation (78%) at the target site occurred when injected 0.2 seconds post-systole, with minimal off-target dispersion (8%). Hemodynamic parameters, such as peak velocity (0.65 m/s) and wall shear stress (2.5 Pa), were consistent with physiological norms.Conclusion:PINNs offer a robust, data-efficient approach for simulating vascular dynamics and optimizing personalized drug delivery strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular diseases remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, necessitating advanced approaches in both diagnosis and therapy. Computational hemodynamics, which models blood flow and vascular dynamics, plays a pivotal role in understanding disease progression and optimizing treatment strategies, such as targeted drug delivery. Traditional numerical methods for solving the Navier–Stokes equations, although accurate, are often computationally intensive and sensitive to boundary conditions, particularly when modeling pulsatile flow in complex vascular geometries.

Recent advancements in deep learning have introduced physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) as a promising framework to model fluid dynamics by embedding the governing physical laws directly into the loss functions of neural networks. This allows for a data-efficient, mesh-free solution to complex partial differential equations, facilitating real-time and personalized simulations in vascular medicine.[1] In pulsatile flow conditions, which mimic the natural rhythmic patterns of blood circulation, PINNs offer an adaptive and accurate solution that can capture both transient and steady-state dynamics with reduced computational cost.[2]

Moreover, PINNs can be extended for optimizing targeted drug delivery by coupling hemodynamic modeling with pharmacokinetic parameters. This integrated framework can predict optimal injection sites, timing, and dosages, enhance therapeutic efficiency, and minimize systemic toxicity.[3] Such precision-guided delivery is particularly beneficial in treating localized pathologies, such as aneurysms, thrombi, or tumor microenvironments.

This study aims to explore the use of PINNs in modeling pulsatile blood flow in major arteries and apply this model to simulate and optimize targeted drug delivery. By integrating physics, data, and biological variability, this approach holds the potential to revolutionize computational vascular medicine.

METHODOLOGY

Study design

This study adopts a computational modeling approach utilizing physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) to simulate pulsatile blood flow and optimize targeted drug delivery in arterial vasculature. The methodology is structured in two phases: (1) modeling pulsatile hemodynamics using PINNs, and (2) optimizing drug delivery under physiological flow conditions.

Model formulation

The flow domain is defined using patient-specific arterial geometries reconstructed from anonymized imaging data (CT/MRI). Blood is assumed to be an incompressible, Newtonian fluid governed by the time-dependent Navier–Stokes equations. PINNs are used to solve these equations by incorporating the physical laws into the loss function of the neural network. The loss function comprises data loss (from boundary conditions and initial conditions) and physics loss (residuals of the Navier–Stokes equations), allowing for supervised and unsupervised learning simultaneously.

Pulsatile flow simulation

Physiological pulsatile inlet flow profiles derived from literature or Doppler ultrasound data are imposed at the inlet. The network is trained to predict spatiotemporal velocity and pressure fields across multiple cardiac cycles. Flow rate, wall shear stress (WSS), and pressure gradients are extracted for analysis.

Targeted drug delivery optimization

A scalar transport equation is coupled to the PINN framework to model the concentration of a drug bolus introduced at the arterial inlet. Various injection parameters (location, timing, dose) are iteratively optimized to maximize drug accumulation at a target region while minimizing systemic dispersion.

Software and tools

All simulations are implemented using TensorFlow and FEniCS, executed on high-performance computing clusters with GPU acceleration. Convergence and accuracy are validated against finite element solutions and benchmark datasets.

RESULTS

PINN convergence and accuracy

The physics-informed neural network (PINN) was successfully trained to solve the Navier–Stokes equations for pulsatile flow across a simplified patient-specific arterial model. The network achieved convergence in under 5000 epochs with a mean squared error (MSE) loss below 1 × 10-5. Validation against finite element solutions yielded an average relative error of less than 2% for both velocity and pressure fields.

Hemodynamic parameters

Simulation of three complete cardiac cycles provided spatial and temporal profiles of flow velocity, pressure distribution, and wall shear stress (WSS). Drug delivery optimization

The PINN was extended to solve the scalar convection–diffusion equation for a drug bolus introduced at varying time points. DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates the potential of physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) to accurately model pulsatile blood flow and optimize targeted drug delivery in complex arterial geometries. The PINN framework successfully reproduced spatiotemporal pressure, velocity, and wall shear stress (WSS) profiles consistent with known physiological patterns, validating its effectiveness against traditional finite element solutions. These results support earlier research by Raissi et al.,[1] who showed that PINNs can embed physical laws into the learning process, improving generalization and reducing dependence on large labeled datasets.

Our study also revealed that drug delivery efficacy significantly depends on the timing of bolus injection relative to the cardiac cycle. Injection 0.2 seconds after systolic peak yielded maximum drug accumulation at the target site with minimal systemic spread. This is in agreement with findings by Moradi et al.,[4] who highlighted the impact of hemodynamic pulsatility on drug dispersion and endothelial permeability.

The use of scalar transport equations in the PINN framework further enhances its clinical relevance by enabling patient-specific optimization. Such integrative models offer a promising alternative to purely data-driven approaches or traditional computational fluid dynamics (CFD), which can be computationally expensive and limited by rigid boundary conditions.[56]

A major strength of this study is the ability of PINNs to simulate real-time flow without meshing, making them adaptable for clinical deployment in personalized medicine.[7] However, limitations include the need for precise boundary condition estimation and longer training times for high-resolution simulations. Future work should incorporate non-Newtonian blood properties and explore applications in aneurysm rupture risk and stent-based drug elution.

CONCLUSION

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) provide an efficient and accurate method for modeling pulsatile hemodynamics and optimizing targeted drug delivery. Synchronizing drug administration with specific phases of the cardiac cycle significantly enhances site-specific accumulation, offering promising avenues for personalized vascular therapy.
