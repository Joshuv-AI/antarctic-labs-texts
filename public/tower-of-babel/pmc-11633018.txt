Unusual pontine metastasis of endometrial papillary serous carcinoma: illustrative case

ABSTRACT

The most common locations of metastases for this malignancy are the lungs, liver, and bones. Metastases to the central nervous system (CNS) are rare. Endometrial papillary serous carcinoma (EPSC) is a type II endometrial carcinoma arising from the epithelial lining of the uterus.1, 2 Despite making up only between 6.5% and 10% of endometrial cancer cases, EPSC is distinguished clinically by its propensity for aggressive growth and metastases. Although brain metastasis of endometrial cancers is rare—less than 1% of cases—EPSC can spread to distant sites, including the central nervous system (CNS).2–7 While mechanisms underlying this metastatic spread remain incompletely understood, hematogenous dissemination is thought to be the primary route, bypassing the lymphatic system due to the brain’s lack of lymphatics.8, 9 In rare instances, such metastases can manifest in atypical locations within the CNS, posing diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case provides an example of a solitary, atypical metastasis to the brainstem.

Illustrative Case

A 65-year-old female with a past medical history of stage IIIc EPSC initially presented to an outside hospital emergency department with right-sided weakness. On neurological examination, she was alert and oriented, with intact cranial nerves and full strength bilaterally. Neither dysarthria nor gait ataxia was appreciated. A head computed tomography (CT) scan revealed hypoattenuation in the left pons, which was believed to most likely represent a subacute infarct. She was diagnosed with a cerebrovascular accident and started on atorvastatin and clopidogrel for secondary prevention.

The patient presented 1 week later with progressive right-sided weakness and impaired ambulation. Her physical examination findings were significant for right-sided weakness (strength ranging from 3 to 4/5). Her gait was unsteady. Minor speech changes were noticed by her husband. Head CT reaffirmed an ovoid area of hypoattenuation in the left central pons. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI; Fig. 1) was performed, revealing a 2.6-cm lesion in the pons. The lesion was predominantly T2 hyperintense with nodular enhancement. Imaging did not reveal definite diffusion restriction but did demonstrate punctate hemorrhage. An initial differential diagnosis included high-grade glioma, metastasis, and infection. Subacute infarction was believed to be less likely. As there was no evidence of systemic disease and imaging appearance, high-grade glioma was believed to be the most likely diagnosis. A low-volume lumbar puncture was performed, given the absence of tonsillar ectopy and lack of hydrocephalus/fourth ventricular effacement. Infectious laboratory tests of both serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) revealed no evidence of inflammation or infection. The CSF profile was bland. Advanced imaging with magnetic resonance spectroscopy and perfusion suggested a high-grade neoplasm in the pons. Chest, abdomen, and pelvis CT demonstrated no evidence of disease within the torso and pelvis. A speech-language pathology evaluation demonstrated significant aspiration due to oropharyngeal dysfunction, and a gastrostomy tube was placed. A multidisciplinary tumor board discussion recommended stereotactic biopsy for tissue diagnosis.

A right suboccipital burr hole was performed for stereotactic biopsy. A trans–middle cerebellar peduncle approach was taken, as this afforded a straightforward approach to the nodular portion of the mass. Microscopically, the specimen showed large tumor cells in papillary-like arrangements with frequent apoptosis and mitosis in a background of extensive necrosis. The biopsy tissue stained immunopositively for p16, p53, PAX-8, EMA, HER2 (3+), and pankeratin, but immunonegative for GFAP, S100, TTF-1, and CDX-2. The histomorphology and immunophenotype of this carcinoma were consistent with metastasis of the patient’s previously diagnosed EPSC (Fig. 2). Fractionated radiation therapy to the brainstem with concomitant steroid administration was recommended. The patient was functionally debilitated, required inpatient admission, and had an unknown but likely poor prognosis. Urgent palliative radiation was believed to be achievable in a more expeditious fashion than outpatient stereotactic radiosurgery. A total of 30.0 Gy was delivered over the course of 10 fractions. The patient’s neurological examination improved after radiation therapy. Her dysarthria improved, and her gastrostomy tube was removed at 3 months postoperatively. She remained stable at 3 months after diagnosis.

The patient was not started on systemic therapy due to poor functional status and no evidence of disease on chest, abdomen, and pelvis CT. She returned to her country of origin 2.5 months postoperatively. Before the return to her country of origin, she experienced improvement in her neurological examination, with a restored ability to swallow. Postradiation MRI was recommended, but the patient deferred imaging pending her return to her country of origin. Unfortunately, postradiation images are not available due to loss to clinical follow-up. She died approximately 9 months after her brain metastasis diagnosis in her country of origin. Due to social factors, further follow-up regarding treatment or follow-up images could not be obtained.

Discussion

Observations

EPSC is uncommon, making up only between 6.5% and 10% of endometrial cancers.1, 3 This carcinoma is classified as type II, or nonendometrioid. Type II endometrial carcinomas are generally more aggressive and have a poorer prognosis than other endometrial cancers (55% over 5 years compared to 85% for type I, endometrioid).1

CNS metastasis from endometrial cancers is rare, with retrospective studies suggesting a rate of incidence between 0.62% and 0.97%.2–7 In our case, the patient’s metastasis was localized to the pons, which is an uncommon location for solitary brain metastasis.2, 4 The patient in this case did not present with any extracranial metastases. Solitary CNS spread is only observed in roughly 33% of EPSC cases, with one additional site of metastasis being the most common finding (41% of cases).10

Fifteen patients with CNS metastasis of endometrial or uterine serous papillary carcinoma were identified in a literature search (Table 1).1, 3–5, 7, 11–16 Our case brings the total to 16 patients. The mean age at endometrial cancer diagnosis was 66.1 years. Treatment regimens included total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (n = 10), chemotherapy (n = 11), pelvic radiotherapy (n = 6), and brachytherapy (n = 2). The most common chemotherapeutic agents used were carboplatin, cisplatin, and paclitaxel. Because of the rare occurrence of this gynecological metastasis, there is limited evidence available to support an optimal treatment plan. The location of the metastasis plays a major role in the determination of the appropriate treatment modality. For this patient, fractionated radiotherapy was chosen given the location of the metastatic lesion in the brainstem. However, most EPSC metastases in the CNS are supratentorial, and a more aggressive technique can be used. One such technique for less eloquent areas is GKRS, a noninvasive procedure using precise beams of gamma rays delivered to the area of the lesion. GKRS has been accepted as an appropriate treatment modality for metastatic disease due to its safety and efficacy in limiting intracranial tumors. A few studies have shown the use of GKRS in CNS metastatic EPPC in the cerebellum.17, 18 Results show that GKRS has been considered an acceptable treatment for patients with EPSC.18

The route of metastasis for EPSC to the posterior fossa is not well documented. Endometrial cancers have been shown to metastasize via lymphatics to local pelvic structures and para-aortic lymph nodes, as well as hematogenously to the liver, lungs, and bones.8 Given the lack of lymphatics in the brain, hematogenous dissemination is the leading hypothesis for the route of CNS metastasis in EPSC.9 The junction between cerebral lobes, as well as the gray-white matter interface, is the most frequent location for supratentorial brain metastasis. For an infratentorial lesion, other routes of metastasis must be considered. The uterus has a close anatomical relationship with the Batson plexus via the sacral plexus.14 The Batson plexus is well defined in spinal pathologies such as spinal metastasis from the breast, lung, prostate, kidney, gastrointestinal, and thyroid.19 It also plays a role in the dissemination of infection in cases of spinal osteomyelitis.20 While CNS metastasis from EPSC is rare, the Batson plexus provides a likely route of metastasis to infratentorial locations.

Lessons

This report presents the rare case of a 65-year-old patient with a metastatic lesion of EPSC in the pons. This case documents a metastatic brainstem lesion and proposes a varied route of metastasis and treatment course when compared to more common supratentorial lesions.

Gynecological malignancies, in particular EPSC, may metastasize to atypical locations—such as a solitary brain lesion—even without evidence of systemic disease. Atypical lesions identified in patients with a past medical history of EPSC may warrant further diagnostic workup, including MRI, MRI spectroscopy, and biopsy of the lesion.

In considering, the pathophysiology of this solitary CNS metastasis, the Batson venous plexus provides a hypothetical route of hematogenous metastasis from the endometrium. The role of the Batson venous plexus is well-defined in metastatic diseases from the breast, lung, prostate, kidney, gastrointestinal, and thyroid, particularly in spinal column metastases. This case provides evidence for its consideration in endometrial metastasis to the posterior fossa.

Clinical status and tumor location are critical considerations when determining the most appropriate treatment options for CNS metastases of EPSC. While GKRS has been shown to improve patient outcomes in uterine carcinoma metastasis to the cerebellum, it may not be preferred in more eloquent locations, including the pons and other brainstem areas, or with larger lesions.
