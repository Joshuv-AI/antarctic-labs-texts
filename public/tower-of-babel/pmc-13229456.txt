Free Fibula Flap Arthrodesis After En Bloc Resection of Distal Radius Giant Cell Tumors: Functional and Oncologic Outcomes From a Single-institution Experience
Authors: Samer Abdel Al, Muntaser Abu Shokor, Basil Daradkeh, Saleh Abualhaj, Osama Alayyan, Mohamad Makahleh, Ismail Al Salhi, Mohammad A. Alattar, Ayah Erjan, Wafa Asha, Sameer Yaser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Reconstruction of distal radius defects following oncological resection remains technically challenging. Free fibula flap (FFF) arthrodesis offers a biological and durable reconstructive option; however, reports remain limited.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed 8 patients who underwent en bloc resection of Campanacci grade III giant cell tumors (2017–2024) of the distal radius, followed by reconstruction with FFF arthrodesis. Clinical, radiological, and functional outcomes were analyzed, including union, recurrence, complications, donor-site morbidity, and Musculoskeletal Tumor Society, Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH), and visual analog scale scores.Results:All patients achieved radiographic union (mean 7 mo proximally, 11 mo distally). Median follow-up was 36 months. Seven (87.5%) patients remained disease-free; 1 patient developed multicentric soft-tissue local recurrence with pulmonary metastases, which was managed surgically along with radiotherapy and medical treatment. The patient remained disease-free at last follow-up. Mean Musculoskeletal Tumor Society score was 24.5, DASH score was 20.3, and visual analog scale score was 2.3, indicating satisfactory limb function and minimal pain. Donor-site morbidity was minimal.Conclusions:FFF arthrodesis maintained viability even after postoperative fractures, soft-tissue recurrences, and adjuvant therapy, underscoring its robustness. It represents a valuable microsurgical option when long-term stability and biological incorporation are prioritized.

Takeaways

Question: Can free fibula flap (FFF) arthrodesis reliably reconstruct distal radius defects after tumor resection?

Findings: In 8 patients, FFF arthrodesis achieved consistent union, minimal donor-site morbidity, and satisfactory functional outcomes, even after complications or adjuvant therapy.

Meaning: FFF arthrodesis is a robust, biologically durable option for distal radius reconstruction in limb salvage surgery, achieving consistent union, low donor-site morbidity, and satisfactory functional outcomes, even in the setting of postoperative complications and adjuvant therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Giant cell tumor (GCT) of bone is a benign but locally aggressive lesion that often presents therapeutic challenges due to its tendency for recurrence and local bone destruction. The distal radius is the third most common site of involvement, following the distal femur and proximal tibia, and tumors in this location are particularly difficult to manage given the limited soft-tissue coverage and the complex mechanics of the wrist joint1–3

Patients with GCT are classified using the Campanacci4 radiological grading system, which consists of 3 grades. Grade I lesions present with a well-circumscribed margin surrounded by a thin shell of mature bone and an intact cortical outline. Grade II lesions display relatively defined borders but lack a sclerotic cortical rim. Grade III lesions, in contrast, exhibit indistinct or hazy margins, reflecting aggressive behavior with rapid and possibly permeative growth patterns.

Curettage with adjuvant therapy remains a treatment option for low-grade lesions; however, in Campanacci grade II–III or recurrent tumors, local recurrence rates are unacceptably high.5 Radical en bloc resection is therefore suggested to achieve durable tumor control. Reconstruction of the resultant defect is essential to restore forearm stability, preserve upper limb function, and improve quality of life.

Various reconstructive options have been described, including free fibula flaps (FFFs), allografts, prostheses, and arthrodesis procedures.6 Among these, vascularized fibular autografts have emerged as a preferred option due to their cortical strength, reliable vascularity, and ability to remodel and integrate with host bone. Additionally, vascularized grafts can be adapted for total wrist arthrodesis or motion-preserving procedures such as fibulo-scapho-lunate arthrodesis7–10

Several studies, including those by Ihara et al,11 Saini et al,12 and Yang et al,13 have reported encouraging outcomes with vascularized fibular transfers, demonstrating low recurrence rates, high union rates, and satisfactory function. This study evaluates the outcomes of radical resection of distal radius GCT reconstructed with vascularized fibula arthrodesis, comparing our findings with previously published series.

The present study reviewed our institutional experience with 8 patients who underwent FFF arthrodesis following en bloc resection of the distal radius for primary bone tumors. We aimed to evaluate union rates, complications, donor-site morbidity, and functional outcomes, and to compare our findings with the published literature.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Patient Selection

The study was approved by our institutional review board for which we conducted a retrospective review of patients who underwent resection of distal radius tumors and reconstruction with FFF arthrodesis at our institution between 2017 and 2024. Inclusion criteria were histologically confirmed primary malignant GCT, Campanacci grade III of the distal radius requiring en bloc resection, reconstruction performed using FFF harvested with microvascular anastomosis, and a minimum follow-up of 36 months. A total of 8 patients met the inclusion criteria.

Surgical Technique

All patients in this series were managed using a consistent evaluation and uniform treatment methodology. Given that all lesions were classified as Campanacci stage III, the gold-standard management for these aggressive, extracompartmental tumors is wide or marginal resection rather than intralesional curettage. For this reason, denosumab was not used, as its primary role is to facilitate curettage in borderline-resectable or less aggressive lesions. In Campanacci stage III disease, where intralesional surgery is not indicated, the use of denosumab offers no clinical advantage and may potentially obscure tumor margins through induced sclerosis.

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia in a multidisciplinary limb salvage setting. Tumor resection was carried out with oncological principles, including wide or marginal resection based on preoperative magnetic resonance imaging and intraoperative findings, using mainly a single dorsal longitudinal extensile surgical approach, aiming to protect all the major neurovascular structures and tendons; in only 2 cases, we used both dorsal and volar approaches to resect the tumor due to previous volar approach of failed curettage performed outside our hospital. The fibula was harvested from the ipsilateral or contralateral leg, depending on the suitability of peroneal vascularity, as described by Villarreal et al.14 The harvested vascularized fibula was sized to match the defect length and secured to the radius proximally and the carpus distally. Arthrodesis was achieved using long dorsal plate fixation (locking or reconstruction plates), fixing the entire graft with unicortical screws, and ensuring fixation of at least 3 bicortical screws both proximally and distally to our FFF (Fig. 1).

We routinely used the Allen test beforehand to ensure adequate hand perfusion before proceeding with our microvascular anastomosis; end-to-end arterial and venous coupler anastomoses were performed under an operating microscope. Patency was confirmed intraoperatively using an indocyanine green imaging device. (See Video [online], which demonstrates intraoperative confirmation of flap patency after microsurgical vascular anastomosis of the FFF using an indocyanine green imaging device for color detection.) All patients were immobilized with an above-elbow splint or back slab for 6 weeks, followed by gradual mobilization and physiotherapy.

Evaluation Metrics

Data collected included demographics, tumor type, pathological fracture history, surgical margins, fixation method, complications, and donor-site morbidity. Union was assessed radiographically by evidence of bridging trabeculae across the graft–host junctions. Complications included infection, wound dehiscence, hardware failure, recurrence, and donor-site morbidity. Functional outcomes were evaluated using the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society-International Society of Limb Salvage (MSTS-ISOLS) score (0–30 scale), the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) score, and the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain. Statistical analysis was descriptive, given the small sample size. Continuous variables were reported as medians (range), and categorical variables as counts and percentages.

RESULTS

A total of 8 patients underwent FFF procedures for distal radius reconstruction following tumor resection. The cohort consisted of 4 men (50%) and 4 women (50%), with a median age of 35.5 years (range, 24–73 y; mean, 38 y). The median body mass index was 26.9 kg/m2 (range, 23.0–52.8 kg/m2; mean, 30.3 kg/m2).

Functional outcomes were assessed using the MSTS score, which showed a median of 25 (range, 22–25; mean, 24.5). The DASH score had a median of 20 (range, 20–22; mean, 20.3). Pain evaluation using the VAS demonstrated a median of 2 (range, 2–4; mean, 2.3), reflecting overall low postoperative pain levels.

With regard to oncological outcomes, 7 (87.5%) patients had no evidence of recurrence, whereas 1 (12.5%) patient developed 5 multicentric soft-tissue recurrences in his forearm. He was found to have 3 pulmonary nodules consistent with metastatic GCT, with no histological evidence of malignant transformation in either the primary lesion or the pulmonary metastases. The patient underwent excision of the 5 forearm soft-tissue recurrences, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy to a total dose of 50.4 Gy delivered in 28 fractions to the forearm to prevent future recurrence (Fig. 2). He also underwent thoracotomy with pulmonary metastasectomy (3 wedge resections), along with systemic therapy with denosumab (120 mg subcutaneously) administered for approximately 2 years. This patient remains disease-free at the last follow-up, illustrating the potential for long-term disease control even in advanced cases. The mean segmental resection length of the distal radius was 8.1 cm (range, 6–10 cm), consistent with Campanacci grade III lesions requiring en bloc resection. All patients achieved complete radiographic union, with a mean time to union of 7 months at the proximal graft–host junction and 11 months at the distal graft–host junction (Fig. 3). Donor-site morbidity was minimal. The follow-up duration was approximately 36 months.

Complications included 1 patient sustaining a traumatic fall resulting in plate breakage, which required revision surgery; union was subsequently achieved without lasting compromise. Donor-site morbidity was minimal, with only 1 case of superficial wound dehiscence at the fibular harvest site, which was managed conservatively.

Functional outcomes were encouraging. The mean (MSTS-ISOLS) score was 24.5 (range, 22–25), corresponding to approximately 82% of normal limb function. The DASH score was 20.3 (range, 20–22), reflecting minimal impairment in activities of daily living. Pain control was excellent, with a mean VAS score of 2.25 (range, 2–4).

DISCUSSION

Several options for distal radius reconstruction after wide resection have been described, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Minami et al1 provided one of the earliest comparative experiences with FFFs, reporting on patients undergoing either partial wrist arthrodesis with the fibular shaft or arthroplasty using the fibular head. Their results demonstrated that arthroplasty was associated with palmar subluxation and early degenerative changes, whereas arthrodesis achieved stable fusion and was deemed more reliable following distal radius GCT resection. These findings were further supported by Kawamura et al,2 who reported a series of 6 patients undergoing FFF total wrist arthrodesis (5 with GCT). All cases achieved solid union within 3–6 months, with a mean MSTS score of 84% at 5 years, confirming the predictable biological healing of FFF total wrist arthrodesis.

In a larger multicenter comparative study, Clarkson et al3 evaluated free fibular transfer against nonvascularized structural iliac crest grafting in distal radius GCT reconstruction. Although overall functional outcomes were similar between the 2 groups, the authors concluded that FFF should be preferred in cases involving longer defects exceeding 10 cm or in situations where soft-tissue coverage is compromised, as the vascularized graft offers superior biological potential and versatility, especially when an osteocutaneous flap is required. In our cohort, however, no osteocutaneous flaps were used.

The concept of motion-preserving arthrodesis undergoing fibulo-scapho-lunate reconstruction has also been explored using a vascularized fibula graft. However, they are limited by risks of instability and degenerative changes; in contrast, FFF arthrodesis consistently provides the most durable and biologically reliable outcome following en bloc resection of distal radius GCT.5–7 The feasibility of this motion-preserving approach is case-dependent and closely related to the extent of the GCT, as extensive metaphyseal or articular involvement may preclude motion-preserving reconstruction. Given the substantial axial and torsional forces across the wrist, constructs requiring total wrist arthrodesis with rigid fixation provided more reliable long-term stability in our experience. Accordingly, we believe that oncological safety and structural stability are more important than preservation of limited wrist motion.

Allograft transplantation provides anatomical restoration, but union rates are variable, with reports of nonunion and graft resorption of up to 40%. Infection risk also remains high.9 Ulnar translocation carries higher risks of altered forearm mechanics, instability, or nonunion.10

Several studies have reported encouraging outcomes with vascularized fibular transfers, demonstrating low recurrence rates, high union rates, and satisfactory functional outcomes.11–13 In contrast, endoprosthetic replacement, although offering immediate stability and preservation of wrist motion, is associated with higher rates of mechanical failure, loosening, and increased cost. Studies by Natarajan et al15 and others report complication rates exceeding 30%. Long segmental nonvascularized bone grafts are technically simpler but are associated with nonunion, resorption, and fracture of the graft, with a reported union rate of only 67%, according to Enneking et al.16

Our results—showing high union rates, satisfactory functional scores (MSTS/DASH), and minimal complications—reinforce that FFF arthrodesis is a benchmark technique for reconstructing these complex defects. The key evidence supporting FFF arthrodesis is summarized in Table 1.

Denosumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting RANKL(receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand), has demonstrated meaningful clinical activity in GCT of bone, producing a rapid reduction in osteolysis, pain improvement, and peripheral ossification in patients for whom resection would be morbid or infeasible.17–19 Despite these benefits, its neoadjuvant role before surgery remains controversial. Multiple clinical series have shown higher local recurrence rates when denosumab precedes intralesional curettage, largely due to the formation of a dense sclerotic rim that conceals viable stromal tumor cells and impairs complete removal.20–22 This concern is most pronounced in Campanacci grade III lesions, where cortical breakthrough and soft-tissue extension already increase recurrence risk; in these cases, preoperative denosumab is considered relatively contraindicated.

Denosumab may still have value in unresectable disease, where downstaging can expand limb-sparing options without compromising oncological margins.23 Its limitations are also evident in tumors with prominent extraosseous components, where the bone-directed mechanism has limited impact on soft-tissue disease, and sclerosis may further hinder complete resection.24 Furthermore, clinicians must consider the therapy’s safety profile—including hypocalcemia, osteonecrosis of the jaw, atypical fractures, and rare malignant transformation—which necessitates careful patient selection and multidisciplinary decision-making.25–27

The main drawback of FFF harvest includes donor-site morbidity (ankle instability, wound complications) and the requirement for microsurgical expertise. In our cohort, donor-site morbidity was minimal and manageable. Still, FFF offers several clear advantages, including biological incorporation and reliable union, particularly when combined with arthrodesis in cases requiring maximum stability, especially in young patients and those with high functional demands.28

A key strength of this study lies in the insight it provides into managing complications following FFF reconstruction. One patient experienced a postoperative graft fracture; however, the inherent biological viability of the vascularized fibula permitted successful union, underscoring its robust healing potential. With respect to oncological outcomes, no recurrences were observed within the reconstructed bone itself. Instead, we encountered a rare pattern of 5 multicentric soft-tissue recurrences in the forearm along with 3 pulmonary metastases. These lesions were managed through a comprehensive multidisciplinary approach. The patient remains disease-free. These findings highlight the importance of reporting unusual recurrence patterns and reinforce the biological advantages and durability of FFFs in complex oncological reconstruction.

This study is limited by its small sample size and single-institution design, reflecting the rarity of GCT of the distal radius requiring wrist arthrodesis, which limits statistical power and generalizability. The retrospective nature of the study and absence of a matched control group introduce potential selection bias and restrict definitive comparisons between vascularized fibula and other techniques. Larger, multi-institutional studies with longer follow-up are needed to validate these findings.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides valuable insight into the performance of FFF, as it demonstrated reliable performance and resilience in managing the uncommon complications encountered after wide oncological resection in this series. The grafts consistently maintained biological viability, as evidenced by successful union following postoperative fractures and preserved vascularity even when reexploration was required during the management of soft-tissue recurrences. Importantly, the durability of the vascularized fibula was maintained despite adjuvant treatments, including radiotherapy, without compromising graft integrity or long-term function. These findings highlight the importance of reporting outcomes in such uncommon scenarios and support the role of vascularized fibula as a durable and biologically favorable option for reconstruction in complex oncological cases, especially in resource-limited settings where endoprostheses may not be available.
