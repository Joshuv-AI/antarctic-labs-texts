Computational analysis and expression profiling of the Sulfate Transporter gene family in tomato under selenium and abiotic stress treatment
Authors: Debao Yi, Yang Li, Sufian Ikram, Lin Chai, Heng Wang, Caili Zhao, Qiang Li, Weijie Jiang, Hongjun Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelenium (Se) is an essential micronutrient for human health, primarily due to its antioxidant properties and role in disease resistance. Biofortification of crops with Se is an effective strategy to improve human Se intake. Due to the chemical similarity between Se and sulfur (S), plants utilize sulfate transporters (SULTRs) for the uptake and translocation of selenate. However, the molecular characterization and stress responsiveness of SULTR family members in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) remain poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, we performed a genome-wide identification of 12 SlSULTR genes. Bioinformatics analysis showed that most SlSULTR proteins possess conserved motifs, as well as a core sulfate permease (sulP) domain. Promoter analysis revealed an abundance of cis-regulatory elements related to phytohormone signaling, such as abscisic acid (ABA) and methyl jasmonate (MeJA). Expression profiling demonstrated that application of sodium selenate differentially enhanced SlSULTR1;1 and SlSULTR3;1 expression in roots, SlSULTR3;1 and SlSULTR3;2 in leaves, and SlSULTR3;2 and SlSULTR4;2 in stems. Notably, SlSULTR4;2 showed fruit-preferential induction and was localized to the plasma membrane, whose expression significantly and positively correlated with fruit Se content. Under various abiotic stresses, Se treatment upregulated the expression of specific members, including SULTR1;2 under cold and osmotic stress, and SlSULTR2;2 and SlSULTR4;1 under heat and salt stress. Furthermore, exogenous application of 1 mg/L sodium selenate effectively increased Se accumulation in fruits and promoted the accumulation of phosphorus (P), potassium (K), and manganese (Mn).ConclusionsThis study provides the first comprehensive characterization of the tomato SlSULTR family under Se treatment and multiple abiotic stresses. Our findings highlight the potential of SlSULTR4;2 as a candidate gene for Se biofortification, offering a theoretical basis for developing tomato varieties with enhanced stress tolerance and nutritional quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08725-1.

Background

Se is an essential micronutrient and a structural component of selenoproteins involved in immune function, thyroid hormone metabolism, and antioxidant defense in humans and animals [1]. Despite its importance, between 0.5 and 1 billion people globally are estimated to be Se-deficient [2]. In soils, Se occurs in both organic and inorganic forms across multiple oxidation states (− II, −I, 0, IV, VI) [3, 4]. Biochemically, Se acts as an S analogue because both belong to the VI group of chalcogens and share similar chemical properties [5]. This similarity allows Se to compete with S for common uptake and transport pathways mediated by SULTRs. Limited Se availability can mimic S-starvation signals, leading to the upregulation of SULTR gene expression, whereas excess Se frequently inhibits S accumulation [5, 6]. Additionally, Se-induced toxicity mitigation is closely linked to altered SULTR expression and the metabolism of S-containing compounds, such as glutathione [7]. Thus, understanding the competitive and cooperative interplay between Se and S is fundamental to manipulating Se accumulation in plants.

Se biofortification of crops has emerged as a sustainable agronomic strategy to combat human Se deficiency while concurrently enhancing plant resilience. This approach uses fertilizers or genetic engineering to increase bioavailable Se in edible tissues. Beyond improving human nutrition, Se at low concentrations acts as a beneficial element for plants, regulating photosynthesis, hormone signaling, and secondary metabolism [8–10]. Since plants encounter environmental fluctuations throughout their life cycle, understanding how continuous Se supply interacts with mineral nutrient homeostasis and abiotic stress responses is crucial. SULTR-mediated transport is central to these interactions and is likely a key determinant for biofortification efficiency [11].

Tomato serves as an ideal model system for studying Se biofortification and nutrient metabolism. As one of the most widely consumed vegetables globally, it plays a significant role in food security. Unlike the model plant Arabidopsis, tomato provides a fleshy-fruit model, making it highly relevant for investigating Se accumulation in edible organs [12–14]. Previous physiological studies have shown that Se application can improve tomato tolerance to biotic stresses, such as gray mold (Botrytis cinerea), and abiotic stresses, as well as delay fruit ripening, enhance nutritional quality, and extend post-harvest shelf life [15–18]. However, these studies typically relied on single applications or focused solely on physiological traits. The molecular basis governing Se accumulation in tomato fruits, and specifically how fruit-specific transporters coordinate this process, remains to be fully elucidated.

The uptake and translocation of Se and S are primarily mediated by the SULTR gene family, which has been extensively characterized in species such as Arabidopsis, rice, and wheat [19–21]. SULTR proteins typically contain 12 transmembrane domains (TMDs) and a Sulfate Transporter and Anti-Sigma factor antagonist (STAS) domain within the C-terminal region [22]. In Arabidopsis, the SULTR family is divided into four groups. Group I (SULTR1;1, SULTR1;2, and SULTR1;3) comprises high-affinity transporters that mediate S uptake in the root [23, 24]. SULTR1;1 functions under sulfate starvation, whereas SULTR1;2 functions under sufficient S supply [25]. Both are expressed in the root epidermis and cortex, and a double knockout of SULTR1;1 and SULTR1;2 nearly abolishes sulfate adsorption [26]. Phloem-localized SULTR1;3 is involved in loading sulfate into sieve tubes and in S redistribution [23]. Notably, among the high-affinity Group I members, SULTR1;2 is capable of transporting selenate (SeO₄²⁻) in addition to sulfate (SO₄²⁻); its overexpression promotes Se uptake in tomato and wheat, whereas sultr1;2 mutants exhibit resistance to selenate toxicity and reduced Se accumulation in leaves [25, 27–30]. Se hyperaccumulators have higher expression of SULTR1 genes [27, 28]. Group II transporters, SULTR2;1 and SULTR2;2 are low-affinity transporters localized in xylem parenchyma that mediate root -to-shoot translocation of sulfate [31]. Under selenate treatment, SULTR2;1 expression is high in Camellia sinensis, Se-tolerant rice, broccoli seedlings, and hyperaccumulator species [28, 31–33]. Group III, the largest (SULTR3;1, SULTR3;2, SULTR3;3, SULTR3;4, and SULTR3;5), shows diverse functions in chloroplast sulfate uptake, leaf sulfate transport, and S supply to developing embryos [34–37]. Group IV members, SULTR4;1 and SULTR4;2 are tonoplast-localized transporters that facilitate sulfate efflux from vacuoles [21]. Selenate treatment has been shown to induce SULTR3;1 in cabbage, SULTR3;5 and SULTR4;1 in broccoli seedlings, and SULTR4;1 in wheat roots, while SULTR3;1 and SULTR4;2 exert opposite effects on glucosinolate and flavonoid biosynthesis following selenite treatment [28].

Despite the extensive knowledge of SULTR homologs in model plants like Arabidopsis, a systematic identification and functional analysis of the SlSULTR family in tomato, particularly its role in fruit-specific Se accumulation and its crosstalk with abiotic stress signaling, is still lacking. Given the importance of tomato as a global fruit crop and a model for nutrient metabolism, filling this gap is essential for optimizing biofortification protocols. Therefore, this present study was conducted to: (1) systematically identify and characterize the SlSULTR gene family in tomato via genome-wide analysis; (2) analyze the expression patterns of SlSULTR genes in response to varying concentrations of sodium selenate across different tissues; (3) investigate the role of SlSULTRs in Se-induced enhancement of multi-abiotic stress resistance; and (4) elucidate the impact of Se on macro- and micro-nutrient accumulation, with a focus on the Se-S synergistic/competitive interaction. Our findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying SlSULTR-mediated Se transport and stress adaptation in tomato, offering a theoretical foundation for developing effective Se biofortification strategies that simultaneously enhance stress tolerance and fruit nutritional quality.

Materials and methods

Gene sequence identification, physicochemical properties, and phylogenetic analysis

The amino acid sequences of SULTR gene family members (PF00916) from Solanum lycopersicum and Arabidopsis thaliana were retrieved from the NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) databases. To identify SlSULTR homologs, BLASTP searches were performed against the tomato proteome using Arabidopsis SULTR sequences as queries. Candidate sequences were verified using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/), and redundant transcripts were removed to establish a unique gene set.

Physicochemical properties, including molecular weight (MW), theoretical isoelectric point (pI), instability index, aliphatic index, and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), were calculated using the Expasy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). Subcellular localization predictions were conducted using the ProtComp 9.0 tool on the SoftBerry server (http://www.softberry.com/).

To elucidate evolutionary relationships, a phylogenetic tree was constructed from the full-length protein sequences of SULTR members from Solanum lycopersicum, Nicotiana tabacum, Oryza sativa, and Arabidopsis thaliana. Sequences were aligned, and a Neighbor-Joining (NJ) tree was constructed using IQ- TREE (http://www.iqtree.org/) with default parameters. The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized and annotated using ITOL (https://itol.embl.de/ ) [38].

Gene structure and chromosomal location

Gene structural features, including exon-intron organization and intron phases, were visualized using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS 2.0; http://gsds.gao-lab.org/) based on genomic and coding sequences. Chromosomal distribution was mapped using the MG2C server (http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_v2.1/) with annotation data from the NCBI gene database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene) [39].

Motif analysis, protein 3D structure prediction, and phosphorylation sites prediction

Conserved motifs in SULTR proteins were identified using the MEME suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme/ ) with the maximum number of motifs set to 10 and site distribution set to “zero or one occurrence per sequence”. The tertiary (3D) structures of all the SlSULTR proteins were predicted using the I-TASSER server (https://zhanggroup.org/I-TASSER/) via iterative threading assembly refinement. The resulting 3D models were visualized and analyzed using UCSF Chimera (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimera/). To further elucidate their functional roles, Gene Ontology (GO) annotations were performed to predict them. GO terms encompassing biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components were retrieved from the Gene Ontology database (https://www.geneontology.org/). The distribution of these GO terms was visualized using custom R scripts. Potential phosphorylation sites (serine, threonine, and tyrosine) were predicted using the NetPhos 3.1 server (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetPhos-3.1/) [40]. Only sites with a high-confidence score (> 0.90) threshold were considered, and their abundance across different residues was quantified and presented as bar charts.

Promoter analysis and interaction network prediction

To investigate transcriptional regulation, the 2,000 bp genomic sequences upstream of the translation start site (ATG) for each SlSULTR gene were retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene). Putative cis-acting regulatory elements were identified using the PlantCARE database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/), and uncharacterized or non-functional elements were excluded from further analysis. To predict potential upstream regulators, the proximal promoter regions (500 bp upstream of the ATG) were analyzed using PlantRegMap (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/prediction.php), with a significance threshold of p ≤ 1e-6 for transcription factor (TF) binding sites. The resulting regulatory network between predicted TFs and SlSULTR genes was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape software (v3.10.2) [41]. Furthermore, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to predict functional associations among SlSULTR proteins and their potential interactors. The network was visualized using Cytoscape software, and the K-means clustering algorithm was applied to classify proteins into distinct functional modules based on interaction connectivity.

Plant material and growth conditions

Tomato (cv ‘Money Maker’) plants were cultivated in a glasshouse at the Institute of Vegetables and Flowers (IVF), Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS), Beijing, China. Plants were grown in 10-L pots containing a coconut coir-based soilless substrate and supplied regularly with a standard nutrient solution (Table S1). Sodium selenate (Na2SeO4) was used as the selenium source for all experiments.

Short-term transcriptional response

Thirty-day-old hydroponically grown seedlings were treated with 0.5, 1, or 5 mg/L Na2SeO4. Roots, stems, and leaves were harvested at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h post-treatment.

Temperature stress (Preventive Se application)

Thirty-day-old plants were pre-treated with 2 mg/L Na₂SeO₄ for 7 days, followed by exposure to cold (4 °C) or heat (38 °C) stress. This preventive approach was designed to evaluate the “priming effect” of Se, specifically whether prior Se accumulation enhances the antioxidant system to buffer against subsequent acute temperature shocks. Leaf samples were collected at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h after the onset of temperature stress.

Osmotic and salt stress (Se-mediated recovery)

Thirty-day-old plants were initially subjected to osmotic stress (20% PEG 6000) or salt stress (200 mM NaCl) for 7 days. Subsequently, 2 mg/L Na2SeO4 was applied to evaluate its recovery effect. This “recovery” design was chosen to investigate the potential of Se to alleviate established stress-induced damage and facilitate physiological recovery in plants already experiencing growth inhibition. Root samples were collected at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h after Se supplementation.

Fruit biofortification assay

At the flowering stage (45 days after transplanting), plants were supplemented via root drenching with a nutrient solution containing 0.5, 1, or 2 mg/L Na2SeO4 until fruits reached the red ripe stage. Se supplementation was initiated at the flowering stage to ensure adequate Se supply during the critical period of reproductive development and fruit formation, thereby maximizing the potential for Se accumulation in the edible tissues. The Se concentration was selected based on a previous study indicating this range is effective for biofortification without inducing phytotoxicity [15, 16, 18]. Se supplementation was initiated at the flowering stage to ensure adequate Se supply during the critical period of reproductive development and fruit formation, thereby maximizing the potential for Se accumulation in the edible tissues. Each treatment included three biological replicates (10 plants per replicate).

Plants treated with a standard nutrient solution without Na2SeO4 served as the control (CK). All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis. Three biological replicates were analyzed for each treatment and time point.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the GREENspin RNA extraction Kit (ZP432-1, ZOMANBIO, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity and concentration were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop spectrophotometry. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (R211-01, Vazyme). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme) on a Bio-Rad iQ5 system. The tomato ACTIN7 (Solyc03g078400) served as the internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−△△Ct method. Primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table S2.

Subcellular localization of SlSULTR4;2

The full-length coding sequence of SlSULTR4;2 (without the stop codon) was amplified and cloned into a modified pCAMBIA1300-GFP vector, which contains a green fluorescent protein (GFP) tag under the control of the Super promoter, to construct a recombinant ProSuper::SlSULTR4;2-GFP plasmid. The empty vector ProSuper::GFP served as a control. For colocalization, a plasma membrane marker (35 S::PIP2-mCherry) was used. Constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and co-infiltrated into the leaves of 4-week-old Nicotiana benthamiana plants. Fluorescence signals were observed 48–72 h after infiltration using a Leica TCS SP8 SR confocal laser scanning microscope.

Element analysis

Tomato roots, stems, leaves, and fruits were harvested, washed with deionized water, and dried at 50 °C to a constant weight. Dried samples were ground into fine powder and passed through a 40-mesh sieve. For element analysis, approximately 0.15 g of the powder was digested with 5–6 mL of HNO3 using a MARS microwave digestion system [42]. Following digestion, the excess acid was evaporated at 120 °C, and the residue was diluted to a final volume of 10 mL with distilled water. The total Se concentration was determined using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES). In contrast, the concentrations of other mineral elements were determined using Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) [15].

For the quantification of selenium speciation, inorganic Se was extracted using a modified acid extraction method. Briefly, 1 g of powdered sample was mixed with 10 mL of 0.5 mol/L HCl, sonicated for 40 min, and then incubated at 80–90 °C for 30 min. The extract was centrifuged at 7,000 rpm for 15 min and filtered. A 7-mL aliquot of the supernatant was digested with concentrated HNO3 and evaporated to near dryness on a hot plate for 13–14 h. The residue was redissolved in distilled water to a final volume of 5 mL, and ICP-OES determined the inorganic Se content. The concentration of organic Se was calculated indirectly by subtracting the inorganic selenium content from the total selenium content.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistics 8.1 software. Differences among multiple groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD test (p<0.05). Pairwise comparisons between two groups were conducted using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (San Diego, California, USA), and regression analysis was conducted using OriginPro 2025 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA).

Results

Genome-wide identification and phylogenetic analysis of SlSULTRs in tomato

A total of twelve SlSULTR genes were identified in the tomato genome. Among those, SlSULTR4;2 encoded the longest protein (690 aa), while SlSULTR4;1 encoded the shortest (640 aa). The predicted molecular weights ranged from 70,703.720 Da (SlSULTR3;3) to 78,713.840 Da (SlSULTR4;2), and the theoretical isoelectric points ranged from 8.00 to 9.28. GRAVY values revealed that all proteins are hydrophobic. The instability index ranged from 29.21 (SlSULTR3;3) to 39.30 (SlSULTR4;1), and the aliphatic index, which reflects thermostability, ranged from 107.62 to 116.31 (Table 1). All 12 proteins were predicted to localize to the plasma membrane.

Gene structure and chromosomal distribution of SlSULTRs

To further characterize the SlSULTR family, gene structure and chromosomal localization were analyzed. Most SlSULTR genes had genomic spans longer than 8 Kb. Notably, SlSULTR1;1, SlSULTR2;1, SlSULTR4;1, and SlSULTR4;2 exhibited broader genomic spans due to their higher intron numbers. Intron phase (0, 1, and 2) were all observed, with phase 1 and 2 more frequent than phase 0, indicating that introns in SlSULTR genes more commonly interrupted codons than occurred between codons (Fig. 2a). SlSULTR genes were unevenly distributed across six chromosomes, with Chromosome 4 and 12 each harboring 3 genes, representing the highest local density (Fig. 2b).

Conserved motifs, structural features, GO annotation, and phosphorylation prediction

Given the observed phylogenetic grouping, we next examined sequence and structural conservation. MEME analysis identified ten conserved motifs (Fig. 3a). Motifs 1–4, 6, and 7 were present in all SlSULTR proteins. Motif 10 was absent in SlSULTR2;1, whereas motif 9 was absent in SlSULTR4;1 and SlSULTR4;2 (Fig. 3a).

Predicted 3D structures of the 12 SlSULTR proteins exhibited a conserved transmembrane helix (TMH) bundle architecture. All proteins contained an N-terminal sulP domain forming the core transmembrane pore [43]. SlSULTR1;1 and SlSULTR1;2 exhibited compact and well-organized conformation dominated by α-helices, with a high ratio of core transmembrane helices to peripheral structural elements, whereas SULTR1;3 showed a more elongated branch-like structure (Fig. 3b). SlSULTR2 proteins displayed relative elongated and flexible conformations with extended intra- or extracellular loops. Group Ⅲ members showed higher structural diversity, with several members containing extended helical regions. Group Ⅳ, particularly SULTR4;2, featured a large extracellular domain (Fig. 3b). These in silico structural predictions require experimental validation.

Gene annotation associated SlSULTRs with molecular function, including sulfate transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0015116) and anion transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0008509). Enriched biological process terms included sulfate assimilation (GO:0000103) and response to oxidative stress (GO:0006979). Cellular component terms primarily localized SlSULTR proteins to the plasma membrane (GO:0005886) and intracellular organelles (GO:0043229) (Fig. S2, Table S3-S5).

Phosphorylation site prediction identified multiple candidate sites in all SlSULTR proteins. SlSULTR4;2 contained the highest number of predicted sites (21), followed by SlSULTR4;1 (16) (Fig. 3c). Among the three residue types evaluated, serine was the predominant predicted phosphorylation target (Fig. 3d).

Cis-acting regulatory elements, transcription factor prediction, and interaction network of SlSULTRs

To explore potential regulatory mechanisms, 2-kb upstream promoter regions of SlSULTR genes were analyzed for cis-acting regulatory elements. Multiple stress- and hormone-responsive elements were identified (Fig. 4a), including ABRE (abscisic acid-responsive), CGTCA-motif (MeJA-responsive), TCA-element (salicylic acid-responsive), and GARE-motif (gibberellin-responsive) [44–47]. ABRE was the most abundant element across the SlSULTR family, followed by the light-responsive Box4 and the MeJA-responsive CGTCA-motif (Fig. S1) [44, 45]. Additionally, growth/developmental-related elements, including G-box, TCT-motif, GATA-motif, and ARE (anaerobic induction), were also detected [44, 45].

Predicted transcriptional regulators of SlSULTRs belonged to six transcription factor families, including MYB, bZIP, Dof, ERF, AP2, and C2H2 (Fig. 4b). Member-specific associations were observed. SlSULTR2;1 showed strong associations with MYB, ERF, and C2H2; SlSULTR3;2 was linked to bZIP; SlSULTR4;1 interacted with Dof and C2H2, and SULTR4;2 was associated with Dof and AP2.

Protein-protein interactions (PPI) analysis suggested that SlSULTR-associated proteins are mainly grouped into two functional clusters (Fig. 4c). One cluster comprised STAS domain-related proteins (e.g., SLAS, SLAT, and SOLLC), consistent with sulfate transport/assimilation-related functions. The second cluster consists of components of photosynthesis-associated and chlorophyll-binding components, such as psbT, psbK, psbL, psbJ, psbI, psbM, psbE, psuG, and CAB9 (Fig. 4c).

Tissue-specific expression profiles of SlSULTRs under different selenate concentrations

To evaluate the response of the tomato SlSULTRs family to selenate, expression levels were analyzed across roots, stems, leaves, and fruits under three sodium selenate concentrations (0.5, 1, and 5 mg/L) over 24 h.

In root tissues, nine SlSULTR genes (SlSULTR1;1, 1;2, 1;3, 2;1, 2;2, 3;1, 3;2, 4;1, and 4;2) showed detectable expression (Fig. 5a). Low-dose treatment (0.5 mg/L) induced a significant upregulation of SlSULTR1;1 within 3 h and SlSULTR3;1 between 6 and 12 h. In contrast, SlSULTR2;2 expression was initially repressed during the first 12 h before increasing at 24 h (Fig. 5a). At 1 mg/L, SlSULTR1;1 and SlSULTR2;1 were significantly upregulated after 6 h of exposure. Under high-dose treatment (5 mg/L), SlSULTR1;1 and SlSULTR4;2 were the most strongly induced genes, whereas SlSULTR1;3 was significantly downregulated across all time points (Fig. 5a).

Among these, SlSULTR1;3 and SlSULTR3;2 were significantly upregulated under both 0.5 mg/L and 1 mg/L selenate treatment, while SlSULTR2;2 showed early upregulation, followed by a decline in expression over time (Fig. 5b). Additionally, SlSULTR4;2 and SlSULTR3;2 were upregulated at 1 mg/L. At the highest concentration of 5 mg/L, SlSULTR1;2 was consistently upregulated, and SlSULTR2;1 expression increased after 6 h of treatment (Fig. 5b).

In leaf tissues, seven SlSULTR genes (SULTR1;2, 1;3, 2;2, 3;1, 3;2, 4;1, and 4;2) exhibited distinct expression patterns under selenate treatment. Notably, SlSULTR3;1 and SlSULTR3;2 were significantly upregulated across all tested concentrations, while SlSULTR2;2 was consistently downregulated (Fig. 5c). SlSULTR4;1 displayed a concentration-dependent response, being upregulated at higher sodium selenate concentrations (1 and 5 mg/L), while at 0.5 mg/L, upregulation occurred only at 24 h of exposure (Fig. 6c).

In fruits, SlSULTR4;2 was significantly upregulated with increasing selenate concentrations (Fig. 5d). Overall, these results demonstrate tissue-specific and concentration-dependent regulation of SlSULTR transporters under selenate treatment. Several members, particularly SlSULTR1;1, SlSULTR3;1, SlSULTR3;2, and SlSULTR4;2, emerged as major responders, suggesting their roles in selenate uptake, translocation, and distribution in tomato.

Subcellular localization of the fruit-specific candidate SlSULTR4;2 protein

Given the dose-dependent upregulation of SlSULTR4;2 in fruit under Se exposure, we next examined its subcellular localization to clarify its cellular role. The SlSULTR4;2-GFP fusion protein produced a green fluorescence signal outlining the cell periphery, whereas the empty vector control showed green fluorescence in both the nucleus and along the plasma membrane (Fig. 6). To validate plasma membrane localization, SlSULTR4;2-GFP was co-expressed with the plasma membrane marker PIP2-mCherry (Plasma membrane Intrinsic Protein 2). The red fluorescence of PIP2 overlapped with the green fluorescence of SlSULTR4;2-GFP, yielding a yellow signal along the cell boundary in emerged images (Fig. 6). These observations confirm that SlSULTR4;2 localizes to the plasma membrane.

Selenium-mediated regulation of SlSULTR gene expression under abiotic stress

Based on the predicted regulatory potential from our in-silico analysis, the transcriptional responses of SlSULTR genes to Se treatment under various abiotic stresses were evaluated. Expression was assessed in leaves for temperature stress and in roots for osmotic and salt stress.

Under cold stress, Se pretreatment significantly modulated SlSULTR expression in leaves. Specifically, SlSULTR1;2 expression exhibited a dynamic pattern: it was downregulated during the initial first hour of exposure but subsequently recovered and increased progressively, peaking at 12 h (Fig. 7a). In contrast, SULTR4;1 and SULTR4;2 expression was notably suppressed at 24 h in Se-treated plants compared to controls (Fig. 7a). Under heat stress, Se treatment increased SULTR1;2 expression at both 1 and 12 h of exposure. In contrast, SULTR2;2 expression was significantly reduced for the first 12 h but showed a sharp induction at 24 h. Additionally, members of the SULTR4 subfamily displayed distinct temporal responses. SULTR4;2 expression was upregulated at 12 h, whereas SULTR4;1 expression peaked at 24 h (Fig. 7b).

Conversely, SULTR3;3 was downregulated at 1 and 24 h after Se treatment (Fig. 7d). Collectively, these results indicate that Se regulates SlSULTR gene expression in a stress-, tissue-, and time-dependent manner.

Selenate application significantly altered the levels of both macro-and micro-nutrients in tomato tissues in a tissue- and dose-dependent manner (Table 2). Moderate selenium supply (1 mg/L sodium selenate) induced the most pronounced changes in nutrient homeostasis, whereas excessive selenium (2 mg/L) exerted predominantly inhibitory effects.

Macro and micro-nutrient content in tomato tissues treated with different concentrations of sodium selenate

Among macronutrients, treatment with 1 mg/L sodium selenate significantly increased calcium (Ca) content in roots, as well as Ca and K content in stems. In leaves, P and Ca levels were markedly enhanced, while fruits showed increased P and K accumulation, indicating a coordinated promotion of macronutrient redistribution to aerial and reproductive tissues. At the lower concentration of 0.5 mg/L, only the Mg content in leaves was significantly elevated. In contrast, exposure to the highest concentration of 2 mg/L resulted in a general decline in macronutrient levels across all tomato tissues, suggesting disruption of nutrient uptake and transport under excessive selenium supply (Table 2).

Micronutrient accumulation was also substantially influenced by selenate treatment. Application of 1 mg/L sodium selenate increased iron (Fe), copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), molybdenum (Mo), and nickel (Ni) content in roots, as well as zinc (Zn), Cu, and Mn levels in stems. These changes indicate an overall stimulation of micronutrient uptake and translocation under moderate selenium conditions. The 0.5 mg/L treatment enhanced Zn accumulation in roots and increased both Zn and Fe content in fruits. In contrast, treatment with 2 mg/L sodium selenate led to significant increases in Cu and Mn in leaves, accompanied by elevated Mn levels in fruits, reflecting a distinct micronutrient response pattern under high selenium exposure (Table 2).

Regression analysis revealed a strong nonlinear relationship between Se and S accumulation across tomato tissues. In roots and stems, S content decreased following Se application compared to the control (Fig. 8a, b), indicating a competitive interaction between selenate and sulfate during uptake. In contrast, Se treatment promoted S accumulation in leaves and fruits, particularly at 0.5 and 1 mg/L, suggesting enhanced S translocation from belowground to aboveground tissues (Fig. 8c, d). This stimulatory effect was abolished at 2 mg/L, which significantly inhibited S accumulation, consistent with impaired S uptake or transport under excessive Se supply.

Collectively, these results indicate that Se application reshapes sulfur distribution and broadly influences the homeostasis of both macro- and micro-nutrients in tomato in a tissue- and dose-dependent manner. Moderate Se supply (1 mg/L sodium selenate) maximized positive nutritional responses, particularly in fruits, by increasing essential elements such as P, K, Fe, and Zn. These findings support a central role for SlSULTR-mediated Se transport in coordinating Se-S interactions and overall mineral nutrient homeostasis in tomato.

Discussion

Genome-wide characterization and transcriptional regulation of the SlSULTR family

SULTRs are pivotal for the uptake and distribution of S and Se, two elements that share similar chemical properties and transport pathways. In this study, we identified 12 SlSULTR genes in the tomato genome. This number is comparable to the 12 members found in Arabidopsis thaliana and rice (Oryza sativa), suggesting that tomato has retained a core SULTR repertoire essential for maintaining S homeostasis despite lineage-specific evolution [19, 43, 48]. All identified SlSULTR proteins possess the conserved SulP and STAS domains, which are critical for anion transport activity and stability [49, 50]. Notably, the STAS domain is also recognized as a hub for regulatory interactions. Consistent with this, our in silico PPI analysis predicts that SlSULTRs interact with two distinct functional clusters. The first involves proteins containing STAS domains (e.g., SLAS, SLAT, and SOLLC), suggesting a coordinated mechanism for substrate specificity and sulfur assimilation. Intriguingly, the second cluster links SlSULTRs to components of the photosynthetic apparatus (e.g., psb subunits and chlorophyll-binding proteins). This points to a vital physiological link, likely driven by the requirement for sulfur Fe-S clusters in photosystems. Although these specific interactions require future experimental confirmation (e.g., via FRET assays), the predicted network suggests that Se/S uptake is likely coupled with both downstream metabolism and energy production to maintain cellular homeostasis. Complementing this protein-level organization, the transcriptional regulation of these transporters appears highly responsive to environmental cues. Our cis-element analysis revealed a significant enrichment of ABRE (ABA-responsive) and MeJA-responsive elements in SlSULTR promoters. This implies that S/Se transport is tightly integrated with stress hormone signaling, consistent with reports that ABA/MeJA can modulate S/Se transport and accumulation [51, 52].

Expression profiling identifies key transporters for Se uptake and fruit loading

Given the structural similarity between selenate and sulfate, SlSULTR genes displayed distinct, tissue-specific transcriptional responses to selenate exposure. In roots, SlSULTR1;1 was rapidly upregulated under both low (0.5 mg/L) and high (5 mg/L) selenate concentrations. This expression pattern identifies SlSULTR1;1 as the primary high-affinity transporter responsible for selenate uptake from the soil, functionally analogous to AtSULTR1;1/1;2 in Arabidopsis [53]. In contrast, SlSULTR1;3 expression was suppressed at high Se levels, which may contribute to feedback regulation that limits excessive Se loading.

In aerial tissues, the sustained induction of Group 3 transporters (SlSULTR3;1, SlSULTR3;2) suggests they play a major role in long-distance translocation via the xylem and subsequent plastidial import [23, 34]. Most notably, SlSULTR4;2 exhibited a strong, dose-dependent upregulation specifically in tomato fruits. In our study, SlSULTR4;2 localized to the plasma membrane, which contrasts with tonoplast-localized Arabidopsis orthologs (AtSULTR4;1/4;2) [22]. This divergence may reflect sequence variation in targeting motifs and suggests functional specialization in tomato. Consequently, the high expression of SlSULTR4;2 in fruits likely facilitates active cellular uptake or retrieval to support the high metabolic demand of fruit development, serving as a key determinant for Se biofortification in edible organs.

SlSULTRs mediate Se-induced stress tolerance and ionome reshaping

Se is beneficial at low concentrations, enhancing plant tolerance to abiotic stress by boosting antioxidant capacity [54, 55]. Our results demonstrate that Se treatment modulates SlSULTR gene expression under cold, heat, and osmotic stress, thereby coordinating a transport network. For instance, under heat and cold stress, the upregulation of the high-affinity uptake transporter SlSULTR1;2, and the induction of SlSULTR4;2. This suggests that Se enhances stress tolerance by simultaneously increasing S/Se uptake from the soil and mobilizing intracellular reserves, thereby ensuring a robust supply of substrates for the synthesis of stress-defense compounds like glutathione and cysteine.

Furthermore, our study reveals a complex interplay between Se biofortification and the tomato ionome. High Se concentrations (2 mg/L) reduced sulfur accumulation, indicative of competitive inhibition at transport sites. Moderate Se (1 mg/L) acted synergistically, significantly enhancing the accumulation of S, Fe, K, and P in fruits. This resulted in a fruit Se concentration of 3.00 µg/g DW, which falls within a commonly cited nutritional range for dietary intake without causing toxicity. These findings suggest that moderate Se levels likely trigger a systemic “nutritional alert” that upregulates broad-spectrum mineral transporters.

Collectively, our findings establish SlSULTR genes as central components of the Se-S regulatory network in tomato. Given the pivotal role of these transporters in sulfur assimilation and anion mobilization, elucidating the dynamics of nutrient element accumulation under varying Se supplies was essential. Identifying SlSULTR4;2 as a key regulator of fruit Se accumulation, combined with the observation that 1 mg/L Se optimizes both Se content and overall mineral quality, provides a solid theoretical basis for optimizing Se enrichment through sulfur nutrition regulation. Ultimately, these insights offer practical guidance for the targeted production of selenium-enriched tomatoes with improved nutritional quality, highlighting the potential for synergistic biofortification.

Conclusion

This study identifies 12 SlSULTR genes in tomato and delineates their specialized roles in Se dynamics. SlSULTR1;1 is the key transporter for root selenate uptake, whereas the plasma membrane-localized SlSULTR4;2 mediates Se accumulation in fruits. Furthermore, moderate Se supply (1 mg/L) not only optimizes fruit Se content but also enhances the accumulation of S, Fe, K, and P, indicating a synergistic nutrient interaction. Under abiotic stress, Se coordinately upregulates specific SlSULTRs, enhancing S/Se homeostasis and stress tolerance. Our findings establish the SlSULTR family as a central regulator of Se distribution and stress adaptation, providing a molecular basis for effective Se-biofortification strategies in tomato.
