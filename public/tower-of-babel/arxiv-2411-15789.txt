Asymptotic tensor rank is characterized by polynomials
Matthias Christandl, Koen Hoeberechts, Harold Nieuwboer, Péter Vrana, Jeroen Zuiddam

Abstract
Asymptotic tensor rank, originally developed to characterize the complexity of matrix multiplication, is a parameter that plays a fundamental role in problems in mathematics, computer science and quantum information. This parameter is notoriously difficult to determine; indeed, determining its value for the 2 × 2 2\times 2 matrix multiplication tensor would determine the matrix multiplication exponent, a long-standing open problem. Strassen’s asymptotic rank conjecture, on the other hand, makes the bold statement that asymptotic tensor rank equals the largest dimension of the tensor and is thus as easy to compute as matrix rank. Recent works have proved strong consequences of Strassen’s asymptotic rank conjecture in computational complexity theory. Despite tremendous interest, much is still unknown about the structural and computational properties of asymptotic rank; for instance whether it is computable. We prove that asymptotic tensor rank is “computable from above”, that is, for any real number r r there is an algorithm that determines, given a tensor T T , if the asymptotic tensor rank of T T is at most r r . The algorithm has a simple structure; it consists of evaluating a finite list of polynomials on the tensor. Indeed, we prove that the sublevel sets of asymptotic rank are Zariski-closed (just like matrix rank). While we do not exhibit these polynomials explicitly, their mere existence has strong implications on the structure of asymptotic rank. As one such implication, we find that the values that asymptotic tensor rank takes on all tensors is a well-ordered set. In other words, any non-increasing sequence of asymptotic ranks stabilizes (“discreteness from above”). In particular, for the matrix multiplication exponent (which is the log of an asymptotic rank) there is no sequence of exponents of bilinear maps that approximates it arbitrarily closely from above without being eventually constant. In other words, any such upper bound on the matrix multiplication exponent that is close enough, will “snap” to it. Previously such discreteness results were only known for finite fields or for other tensor parameters (e.g., asymptotic slice rank). We obtain them for infinite fields like the complex numbers. We prove our result more generally for a large class of functions on tensors, and in particular obtain similar properties for all functions in Strassen’s asymptotic spectrum of tensors. We prove a variety of related structural results on the way. For instance, we prove that for any converging sequence of asymptotic ranks, the limit is also an asymptotic rank for some tensor. We leave open whether asymptotic rank is also discrete from below (which would be implied by Strassen’s asymptotic rank conjecture).

Structure of asymptotic ranks

The asymptotic rank conjecture naturally leads to the question: What are the possible values that the asymptotic rank can take,

\mathcal{R}=\{\asymprank(T):T\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{3}},\,d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}\}?

In this paper:

•

We prove that asymptotic tensor rank is computable from above: over “computable fields”, for every upper bound

1.1
Polynomial characterization

We prove that upper bounds on asymptotic rank are computable:

For any computable field

1.2
Discreteness from above and closedness

As a consequence of
Theorem

1.2
we prove that the set of values that asymptotic rank takes, on all tensors (of fixed order, but arbitrary dimension), is well-ordered (discrete from above, every non-increasing sequence stabilizes):

Theorem 1.3
.

\mathcal{R}=\{\asymprank(T):T\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}},d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{k}\}
is well-ordered.

In particular,

1.3
says that there cannot be a sequence of tensors with asymptotic rank strictly larger than

that gets arbitrarily close to it. Indeed, if it comes arbitrarily close it must eventually “snap” to

We leave as an open problem to prove discreteness from below for asymptotic rank. That is, we do not know if there can be (non-constant) increasing converging sequences of asymptotic ranks of tensors.

Towards proving discreteness, as an intermediate result, we prove closedness of the set of values of asymptotic rank, when the base field is the complex numbers:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
.
For any sequence in
\mathcal{R}
that converges, the limit is in
\mathcal{R}
.

We note again that all of the above results are consistent with (and may be thought of as evidence towards) the asymptotic rank conjecture, since that would imply that asymptotic rank is computable as the matrix rank of a flattening of the tensor, and that the set of values that asymptotic rank takes is simply the natural numbers

Zariski-closed sublevel sets for asymptotic rank

\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(T)=\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(T^{\otimes n})^{1/n}.

This is well-defined by Fekete’s lemma, and the limit can be replaced by the infimum.
For any subset
A\subseteq V
, we define

\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[A]=\sup_{T\in A}\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(T).

The main example of a regularized admissible functional is the asymptotic rank
\asymprank
, which we get by taking
V=\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}}
to be a space of
k
-tensors and
F_{n}
to be the tensor rank on
V^{\otimes n}
regrouped as
k
-tensors via
v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{n}\mapsto v_{1}\boxtimes\cdots\boxtimes v_{n}
.
4
4

4

Here
\boxtimes
is the “grouped” tensor product (Kronecker product), which takes two
k
-tensors and multiplies them to another
k
-tensor. However, one may also use the “non-grouped” tensor product instead to get an interesting admissible functional
[
19
,
17
]
.

More examples are given by the points in the asymptotic spectrum of tensors
[
45
]
(see
Section

3
), which includes the flattening ranks and the quantum functionals
[
21
]
.

2.2
Zariski-closed sublevel sets

Let
V
be a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
, and let
\mathbb{F}[V]
denote the polynomial functions on
V
.
Let
A\subseteq V
be a subset.
The Zariski-closure of
A
is defined as

\overline{A}=\{T\in V:\forall f\in\mathbb{F}[V],\,\left.f\right|_{A}\equiv 0\Rightarrow f(T)=0\}.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
F
be an admissible functional for
V
and let
A\subseteq V
.
Then

\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[\overline{A}]=\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[A].

Towards the proof of
Theorem

2.2
we use the following lemma.
We define, for any subset
A\subseteq V
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, the set
A^{(n)}=\{T^{\otimes n}:T\in A\}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A\subseteq V
.
Then
\overline{A}^{(n)}\subseteq\linspan A^{(n)}
.

Proof.

The set
\linspan A^{(n)}
is the intersection of all kernels
\ker\ell
of linear forms
\ell
on
\Sym^{n}V
such that
\ell|_{A^{(n)}}\equiv 0
. For every such
\ell
, we will prove that
\overline{A}^{(n)}\subseteq\ker\ell
, which proves the claim. Define the function
f\colon V\to\mathbb{F}
by
f(T)=\ell(T^{\otimes n})
. Then
f
is a polynomial function on
V
and
f|_{A}\equiv 0
. By definition of the Zariski-closure also
f|_{\overline{A}}\equiv 0
. Then by definition of
f
we have
\ell|_{\overline{A}^{(n)}}\equiv 0
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2.2
.

From
A\subseteq\overline{A}
it follows directly that
\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[A]\leq\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[\overline{A}]
. It remains to prove
\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[\overline{A}]\leq\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[A]
.
Let
T\in\overline{A}
. By
Lemma

2.3
, for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, there are
p(n)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,
S_{1},\ldots,S_{p(n)}\in A
and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{p(n)}\in\mathbb{F}
such that
T^{\otimes n}=\sum_{i=1}^{\smash{p(n)}}\alpha_{i}S_{i}^{\otimes n}
, with the
S_{i}^{\otimes n}
linearly independent.
Note that
p(n)
grows at most polynomially in
n
, since

p(n)\leq\dim\linspan A^{(n)}\leq\dim\Sym^{n}(V)=\binom{\dim V+n-1}{n}.

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.
Then

T^{\otimes nm}=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\in[p(n)]}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{i_{j}}S_{i_{j}}^{\otimes n}.

By subadditivity and scalar invariance of
F
,

F(T^{\otimes nm})\leq p(n)^{m}\max_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\in[p(n)]}F\Bigl(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}S_{i_{j}}^{\otimes n}\Bigr).

Rearranging tensor factors, we have for some
m_{i}
that sum to
m
, that

F\Bigl(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}S_{i_{j}}^{\otimes n}\Bigr)=F\Bigl(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{p(n)}S_{i}^{\otimes m_{i}n}\Bigr).

(Here and in the following, we implicitly omit factors for which
m_{i}=0
.)
By submultiplicativity of
F
,

F\Bigl(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{p(n)}S_{i}^{\otimes m_{i}n}\Bigr)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{p(n)}F(S_{i}^{\otimes m_{i}n}).

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is an
M(\varepsilon,n)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
such that for every
\ell\geq M(\varepsilon,n)
and for every
i\in[p(n)]
, we have
F(S_{i}^{\otimes\ell})^{1/\ell}\leq\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(S_{i})+\varepsilon
. By definition,
F_{1}
is bounded, say
F_{1}\leq B
for some
B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Then

\prod_{i=1}^{p(n)}F(S_{i}^{\otimes m_{i}n})\leq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[p(n)]\\
m_{i}n\geq M(\varepsilon,n)\end{subarray}}\bigl(\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(S_{i})+\varepsilon\bigr)^{m_{i}n}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[p(n)]\\
m_{i}n<M(\varepsilon,n)\end{subarray}}B^{m_{i}n}

Then

F(T^{\otimes nm})^{1/(nm)}\leq p(n)^{1/n}(\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[A]+\varepsilon)B^{p(n)M(\varepsilon,n)/(nm)}.

We let
m
go to infinity to get

\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(T)\leq p(n)^{1/n}(\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[A]+\varepsilon)
.
We let
\varepsilon
go to zero and
n
go to infinity to get

\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(T)\leq\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[A]
.∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
F
be an admissible functional for a vector space
V
.
Let
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then
\{T\in V:\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(T)\leq r\}
is Zariski-closed.

Proof.

Let
A=\{T\in V:\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(T)\leq r\}
.
Then
\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[A]\leq r
.
By
Theorem

2.2
,
\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[\overline{A}]=\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}[A]
, so that for all
T\in\overline{A}
, we have
\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(T)\leq r
, and hence
T\in A
.
We conclude that
\overline{A}\subseteq A
.
∎

2.3
Discreteness from above for asymptotic rank

We call a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
well-ordered if every nonempty subset has a smallest element. Equivalently, any non-increasing sequence of elements in
S
stabilizes.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
F
be an admissible functional for a vector space
V
.
Then
\{\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(T):T\in V\}
is well-ordered.

Proof.

Let
A_{r}=\{T\in V:\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(T)\leq r\}
. Let
r_{1}\geq r_{2}\geq\cdots
be a sequence of elements in
\{\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(T):T\in V\}
.
Then
A_{r_{1}}\supseteq A_{r_{2}}\supseteq\cdots
is a descending chain of Zariski-closed sets (
Corollary

2.4
).
Since the Zariski-topology on
V
is Noetherian
[
28
, Section 1.4]
, the chain of
A_{r_{i}}
stabilizes, that is, there exists some
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
such that for all
n\geq N
,
A_{r_{n}}=A_{r_{n+1}}
.
Let
T_{N}\in V
such that
\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}(T_{N})=r_{N}
.
Then for all
n\geq N
we have
T_{N}\in A_{r_{n}}
.
This implies that
r_{n}\geq r_{N}
, but we also have
r_{n}\leq r_{N}
, hence
r_{n}=r_{N}
for all
n\geq N
.
∎

We can in particular apply
Corollary

2.5
to tensor rank to obtain well-orderedness of
\{\asymprank(T):T\in V\}
for any
V=\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}}
.
In fact, because asymptotic rank is lower bounded by the flattening ranks, the well-orderedness readily extends to all tensors (with fixed order, but arbitrary dimensions), as follows.

Corollary 2.6
.

The set
\{\asymprank(T):T\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}},d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{k}\}
is well-ordered.

Proof.

Tensor rank is an admissible functional (
Definition

2.1
), so
by
Corollary

2.5
, the set
\{\asymprank(T):T\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}}\}
is well-ordered for every
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{k}
. Let
r_{1}\geq r_{2}\geq\cdots
be a non-increasing sequence in
\{\asymprank(T):T\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}},d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{k}\}
. This sequence is bounded from above (by
r_{1}
). For every
i
there is a concise tensor
T_{i}
(
Section

1.4
) such that
\asymprank(T_{i})=r_{i}
, so
that
T_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}}
for some
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\leq r_{i}
.
By re-embedding the
T_{i}
, we see that the
r_{i}
are all contained in
\{\asymprank(T):T\in\mathbb{F}^{d}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d}\}
where
d=\lfloor r_{1}\rfloor
, which is well-ordered.
So
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots
stabilizes.
∎

3
Discreteness from above for the asymptotic spectrum of tensors

In
Section

2
we proved discreteness from above for any admissible functional on any fixed vector space
V
, and for the asymptotic tensor rank on all tensors. In this section we will extend the latter result to a large class of admissible functionals, called asymptotic spectrum.

Let
\Delta(\mathbb{F},k)
be the asymptotic spectrum of order-
k
tensors over
\mathbb{F}

[
45
]
. This is the set of all functions from
k
-tensors over
\mathbb{F}
to reals that are
\boxtimes
-multiplicative,
\oplus
-additive, normalized to
r
on
\langle r\rangle
and monotone under restriction. We note that asymptotic tensor rank is not an element of
\Delta(\mathbb{F},k)
. However, by Strassen’s duality theorem
[
45
]
, it is the pointwise maximum over it, and in fact the asymptotic spectrum characterizes many asymptotic properties of tensors (asymptotic restriction, asymptotic subrank, asymptotic slice rank)
[
21
]
.

Any element of
\Delta(\mathbb{F},k)
gives an admissible functional on
V=\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}}
(
Definition

2.1
). Moreover, for every
F\in\Delta(\mathbb{F},k)
, it follows from multiplicativity that
F
coincides with its regularization
\underaccent{\wtilde}{\func}
.
Thus, for every
F
in the asymptotic spectrum of tensors, the following is true:

(1)

For every
A\subseteq V
we have
F(\overline{A})=F(A)
(
Theorem

2.2
).

(2)

For every
r\in\mathbb{R}
, the set
\{T\in V:F(T)\leq r\}
is Zariski-closed (
Corollary

2.4
).

(3)

\{F(T):T\in V\}
is well-ordered (
Corollary

2.5
).

In this section we will extend the well-orderedness property (3) from all finite-dimensional spaces
V=\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}}
to the space of all
k
-tensors (analogously to
Corollary

2.6
for asymptotic rank):

Theorem 3.1
.

For any field
\mathbb{F}
, any order
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
and any function
F\in\Delta(\mathbb{F},k)
, the set of values
\{F(T):T\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}},d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{k}\}
is well-ordered.

3.1
Tensor-to-matrix transformations

Before proving
Theorem

3.1
we prove an important ingredient for it.
For
r\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\langle r\rangle_{1,2}
denote the
k
-tensor
\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}e_{\ell}\otimes e_{\ell}\otimes e_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{1}
, and similarly define
\langle r\rangle_{i,j}
with
i\neq j\in[k]
indicating the location of the factors
e_{\ell}
in the tensor product. Let
\subrank_{i,j}(T)
be the largest number
r
such that
T\geq\langle r\rangle_{i,j}
. Thus
\subrank_{i,j}(T)
essentially measures the largest matrix rank of a matrix (2-tensor) that can be obtained on legs
i,j
via restriction.
5
5

5

Note that for
k=3
,
\langle r\rangle_{1,2}
is the matrix multiplication tensor
\langle 1,r,1\rangle
. In quantum information theory, the unit tensor
\langle r\rangle
is known as a rank-
r
Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) state, and the tensor
\langle r\rangle_{1,2}
corresponds to a rank-
r
Einstein–Podolski–Rosen (EPR) pair between parties 1 and 2.

We prove the following lower bound (extending the
k=3
version from
[
15
]
) that we will use for our discreteness result
Theorem

3.1
. Recall that
\tensorrank_{I}(T)
denotes the flattening rank along
I\subseteq[k]
: the matrix rank of the matrix obtained from
T
by grouping legs in
I
together and grouping legs in
[k]\setminus I
together.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
I\subseteq[k]
with
1\leq|I|\leq k-1
, and let
T
be a
k
-tensor. If
|\mathbb{F}|>\tensorrank_{I}(T)
, then

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
j\in[k]\setminus I\end{subarray}}\subrank_{i,j}(T)\geq\tensorrank_{I}(T).

As a direct consequence of
Theorem

3.2
we see that at least one of the
\subrank_{i,j}
is “large”:

Corollary 3.3
.

For any
k
-tensor
T
, for any
i\in[k]
, assuming
|\mathbb{F}|>\tensorrank_{i}(T)
, there is a
j\in[k]\setminus\{i\}
such that
\subrank_{i,j}(T)\geq\tensorrank_{i}(T)^{1/(k-1)}
.

Proof.

Apply
Theorem

3.2
with
I=\{i\}
, and note that one of the
k-1
terms in the product must satisfy the claim.
∎

For the proof of
Theorem

3.2
we use the following lemma, which says that if we have a collection of matrices, then after applying a simultaneous linear operation on the columns, the linearly independent columns are “flushed” to the left in the matrices. This is proven using a Schwartz–Zippel type argument. We denote, for matrices
M_{1},\ldots,M_{t}\in\mathbb{F}^{a\times b}
, by
[M_{1};\cdots;M_{t}]
the matrix in
\mathbb{F}^{a\times(tb)}
obtained by concatenating the matrices
M_{i}
horizontally. For a matrix
M
we denote by
M|_{S}
the submatrix of
M
with columns indexed by
S
.

Lemma 3.4

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{c}\in\mathbb{F}^{a\times b}
be matrices, and for any
i\in[c]
let

r_{i}=\tensorrank([M_{1};\cdots;M_{i}])-\tensorrank([M_{1};\cdots;M_{i-1}]).

If
|\mathbb{F}|>\tensorrank([M_{1};\cdots;M_{c}])
, then there exists an invertible matrix
U\in\mathbb{F}^{b\times b}
such that

\textnormal{colspan}([(M_{1}U)|_{[r_{1}]};\cdots;(M_{c}U)|_{[r_{c}]}])=\textnormal{colspan}([M_{1};\cdots;M_{c}]).

In the proof we need the following.
For any
\ell\in[k]
and
i\in[d_{\ell}]
we let

P^{(\ell)}_{i}:\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}}\to\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\widehat{\mathbb{F}^{d_{\ell}}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}}

be the projector from
k
-tensors to
(k-1)
-tensors that applies
e^{*}_{i}
to the
\ell
th tensor leg, where
\widehat{\mathbb{F}^{\smash{d_{\ell}}}}
indicates that we leave out the factor
\mathbb{F}^{d_{\ell}}
.
For any subset
L\subseteq[k]
and
i\in\prod_{\ell\in L}[d_{\ell}]
(Cartesian product) we let
P^{\smash{(L)}}_{i}
be the projector from
k
-tensors to
(k-|L|)
-tensors that applies
e_{i_{1}}^{*}\otimes\dots\otimes e_{i_{L}}^{*}
to the tensor legs indexed by
L
.
We call
P^{\smash{(L)}}_{i}T
the
i
th
L
-slice of
T
. If we think of
T
as a
k
-dimensional array of elements of
\mathbb{F}
indexed by
[d_{1}]\times\cdots\times[d_{k}]
, then
P^{\smash{(L)}}_{i}T
is the array obtained from
T
by fixing the indices labeled by
L
to the index vector
i
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.2
.

The proof is by induction on the order
k
. In the base case
k=2
, the functions
\subrank_{1,2}
and
\tensorrank_{\{1\}}
coincide (they are both the matrix rank), so the claim is true. We now assume that the claim holds for the cases
2,\ldots,k-1
and prove it for
k
.
Let
I\subseteq[k]
with
1\leq|I|\leq k-1
. Since
\tensorrank_{I}=\tensorrank_{[k]\setminus I}
we may assume
|I|\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor
. After a permutation of the tensor legs, we may then without loss of generality take
I=\{1,\ldots,m\}
for some
1\leq m\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor
.
Let
T\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{n_{k}}
.
The
I
-flattening
T_{I}
is an
(n_{1}\cdots n_{m})\times(n_{m+1}\cdots n_{k})
matrix. We write
T_{I}
as a block matrix

T_{I}=[M_{1,\ldots,1};\cdots;M_{i_{m+1},\ldots,i_{k-1}};\cdots;M_{n_{m+1},\ldots,n_{k-1}}],

where
i=(i_{m+1},\ldots,i_{k-1})
goes over
[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]
(say in lexicographic order) and
where each block
M_{i_{m+1},\ldots,i_{k-1}}
is an
(n_{1}\cdots n_{m})\times n_{k}

i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]
, let
r_{i}
be the number of linearly independent columns of
M_{i}
that are also linearly independent of all the previous blocks
M_{i^{\prime}}
in
T_{I}
. Then
\tensorrank_{I}(T)
equals the sum of
r_{i}
over all
i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]
.
Let
L=[k-1]\setminus I=\{m+1,\ldots,k-1\}
.
We will prove two inequalities:

\prod_{j\in I}\subrank_{j,k}(T)\geq\max\{r_{i}:i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]\}

(1)

and

\prod_{j\in I,\ell\in L}\subrank_{j,\ell}(T)\geq|\{i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]:r_{i}\neq 0\}|.

(2)

From (
1
) and (
2
) the claim follows. Indeed, the product in the left-hand side of the claim splits into two products to which we apply the inequalities (
1
) and (
2
):

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in I\\
\ell\in[k]\setminus I\end{subarray}}\subrank_{j,\ell}(T)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\prod_{j\in I}\subrank_{j,k}(T)\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(\prod_{j\in I,\ell\in L}\subrank_{j,\ell}(T)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\max\{r_{i}:i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]\}\cdot|\{i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]:r_{i}\neq 0\}|

\displaystyle\geq\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]}r_{i}

\displaystyle=\tensorrank_{I}(T).

It remains to prove (
1
) and (
2
). We first prove (
1
).
For any
i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]
, recall that we denote by
P^{\smash{(L)}}_{i}T
the
i
th
L
-slice of
T
, which is an
(m+1)
-tensor of shape
n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{m}\times n_{k}
. The
k
-flattening of

is the
(n_{1}\cdots n_{m})\times n_{k}

M_{i}
. Since
M_{i}
is a submatrix of
T_{I}
, we have
\tensorrank_{I}(T)\geq\tensorrank_{k}(P^{\smash{(L)}}_{i}T)
and thus

. Thus by the induction hypothesis applied to the
(m+1)
-tensor

we have (noting that
m+1\leq k-1
and that

)

\prod_{j\in I}\subrank_{j,k}(P^{(L)}_{i}T)\geq\tensorrank_{k}(P^{(L)}_{i}T)=\tensorrank(M_{i}).

(3)

Let
i^{\prime}
such that
r_{i^{\prime}}=\max\{r_{i}:i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]\}
.
Then

\tensorrank(M_{i^{\prime}})\geq r_{i^{\prime}}=\max\{r_{i}:i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]\}.

(4)

From (
3
) and (
4
) we get

\prod_{j\in I}\subrank_{j,k}(T)\geq\prod_{j\in I}\subrank_{j,k}(P^{(L)}_{i^{\prime}}T)\geq\max\{r_{i}:i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]\}.

(5)

This proves (
1
).

Now we will prove (
2
).
Since
|\mathbb{F}|>\tensorrank_{I}(T)
, we may apply
Lemma

3.4
to the blocks
M_{i}
in
T_{I}
(with

,

and

) to “flush” all linearly independent columns in

to the left of each block
M_{i}
by applying an invertible matrix
U
. We may assume that
T
already has this

U
corresponds to applying
U
to the

th tensor leg of
T
).

Let
P^{\smash{(k)}}_{1}T
be the first
k
-slice of
T
, which is a
(k-1)
-tensor of shape

.
The

-flattening of

is a matrix

for which the columns are precisely each first column of the matrices

over all

. In particular, we have
\tensorrank_{I}(T)\geq\tensorrank_{L}(P^{\smash{(k)}}_{1}T)
and thus

. We then have, by the induction hypothesis applied to the

-tensor

(noting that
[k-1]\setminus I=L
),

\prod_{j\in I,\ell\in L}\subrank_{j,\ell}(P^{(k)}_{1}T)\geq\tensorrank_{L}(P^{(k)}_{1}T)=\tensorrank(M^{\prime}).

(6)

Furthermore, by the column flushed property, we have

\tensorrank(M^{\prime})\geq|\{i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]:r_{i}\neq 0\}|.

(7)

From (
6
) and (
7
) we get

\prod_{j\in I,\ell\in L}\subrank_{j,\ell}(T)\geq\prod_{j\in I,\ell\in L}\subrank_{j,\ell}(P^{(k)}_{1}T)\geq|\{i\in[n_{m+1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{k-1}]:r_{i}\neq 0\}|.

(8)

This proves (
2
).
∎

As a consequence of
Theorem

3.2
we obtain a lower bound on the asymptotic subrank. (We will not use this lower bound here, but we expect it to be of independent interest.) The subrank
\subrank(T)
is the largest number
r
such that
T\geq\langle r\rangle
. The asymptotic subrank is defined as
\asympsubrank(T)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\subrank(T^{\boxtimes n})^{1/n}
. The limit exists and can be replaced by a supremum (Fekete’s lemma).

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
T
be a
k
-tensor over any field
\mathbb{F}
. Then

\asympsubrank(T)\geq\min_{\emptyset\neq I\subsetneq[k]}\tensorrank_{I}(T)^{2/(k(k-1))}.

Proof.

Let
q_{i,j}=\subrank_{i,j}(T)
for every
i,j\in[k]
with
i<j
. Then

T^{\boxtimes(k(k-1)/2)}\geq\bigkron_{i<j}\langle q_{i,j}\rangle_{i,j}.

It was proven in
[
50
]
that

\asympsubrank\bigl(\bigkron_{i<j}\langle q_{i,j}\rangle_{i,j}\bigr)=\min_{\emptyset\neq I\subsetneq[k]}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
j\in[k]\setminus I\end{subarray}}q_{i,j}.

Since the asymptotic subrank is invariant under field extension
[
45
, Theorem 3.10]
, we may assume that
\mathbb{F}
is infinite. By
Theorem

3.2
we then have

\asympsubrank(T^{\boxtimes(k(k-1)/2)})\geq\min_{\emptyset\neq I\subsetneq[k]}\tensorrank_{I}(T).

Taking the
k(k-1)/2
-th root gives the claim.
∎

3.2
Discreteness from above for the asymptotic spectrum

For tensors
S,T
we write
S\sim T
, and say
S
and
T
are restriction-equivalent, if
S\leq T
and
T\leq S
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
F\in\Delta(\mathbb{F},k)
and
i\neq j\in[k]
. If
F(\langle 2\rangle_{i,j})=1
, then
F(\langle m\rangle_{i,j})=1
for every
m\geq 1
.

Proof.

For any
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, by multiplicativity,
F(\langle 2^{a}\rangle_{i,j})=F(\langle 2\rangle_{i,j}^{\boxtimes a})=F(\langle 2\rangle_{i,j})^{a}=1
. For general
m\geq 1
, choose
a
with
m\leq 2^{a}
. Then
\langle 1\rangle_{i,j}\leq\langle m\rangle_{i,j}\leq\langle 2^{a}\rangle_{i,j}
, so by monotonicity
1=F(\langle 1\rangle_{i,j})\leq F(\langle m\rangle_{i,j})\leq F(\langle 2^{a}\rangle_{i,j})=1
.
∎

Fix
k\geq 3
. Let
\gamma
be the linear map from
k
-tensors to
(k-1)
-tensors defined on simple tensors by

\gamma(v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k}):=v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{k-2}\otimes(v_{k-1}\otimes v_{k})
.
Let
\phi
be the map from
(k-1)
-tensors to
k
-tensors defined by
\phi(U):=U\otimes e_{1}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
F\in\Delta(\mathbb{F},k)
and suppose
F(\langle 2\rangle_{k-1,k})=1
. Then for any two
k
-tensors
S,T
, if
\gamma(S)\sim\gamma(T)
, then
F(S)=F(T)
.

Proof.

Since
\phi
preserves restriction,
\gamma(S)\sim\gamma(T)
implies
\phi(\gamma(S))\sim\phi(\gamma(T))
, so by monotonicity of
F
,
F(\phi(\gamma(S)))=F(\phi(\gamma(T)))
. It thus suffices to show that
F(\phi(\gamma(T)))=F(T)
for every
k
-tensor
T
. For sufficiently large
m
, we have
\phi(\gamma(T))\boxtimes\langle m\rangle_{k-1,k}\geq T
and
\phi(\gamma(T))\leq T\boxtimes\langle m\rangle_{k-1,k}
. Applying
F
and using multiplicativity, monotonicity, and
F(\langle m\rangle_{k-1,k})=1
(
Lemma

3.6
), we obtain
F(\phi(\gamma(T)))\geq F(T)
and
F(\phi(\gamma(T)))\leq F(T)
, hence
F(\phi(\gamma(T)))=F(T)
.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
F\in\Delta(\mathbb{F},k)
and suppose
F(\langle 2\rangle_{k-1,k})=1
. Then
F\circ\phi\in\Delta(\mathbb{F},k-1)
and
F=F\circ\phi\circ\gamma
. In particular,
F
and
F\circ\phi
take the same set of values.

Proof.

We first check that
F\circ\phi\in\Delta(\mathbb{F},k-1)
. Let
S,T
be
(k-1)
-tensors. For multiplicativity:
\phi(S)\boxtimes\phi(T)\sim\phi(S\boxtimes T)
, so
F(\phi(S\boxtimes T))=F(\phi(S)\boxtimes\phi(T))=F(\phi(S))\,F(\phi(T))
. For additivity:
\gamma(\phi(S\oplus T))\sim\gamma(\phi(S)\oplus\phi(T))
, so by
Lemma

3.7
,
F(\phi(S\oplus T))=F(\phi(S)\oplus\phi(T))=F(\phi(S))+F(\phi(T))
. For normalization:
\gamma(\phi(\langle r\rangle))\sim\gamma(\langle r\rangle)
, where the first
\langle r\rangle
is the rank-
r
unit tensor of order
k-1
and the second is the rank-
r
unit tensor of order
k
, so by
Lemma

3.7
,
F(\phi(\langle r\rangle))=F(\langle r\rangle)=r
. For restriction-monotonicity:
\phi
preserves restriction, so
S\leq T
implies
F(\phi(S))\leq F(\phi(T))
.

For the identity
F=F\circ\phi\circ\gamma
: for any
k
-tensor
T
,
\gamma(\phi(\gamma(T)))\sim\gamma(T)
, so by
Lemma

3.7
,
F(\phi(\gamma(T)))=F(T)
. The set-of-values claim follows.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Without loss of generality we may assume the field
\mathbb{F}
to be infinite, since for any field extension
\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
, any element in the asymptotic spectrum
\Delta(\mathbb{F},k)
has a (unique) extension to
\Delta(\mathbb{F}^{\prime},k)

[
45
, Theorem 3.10]
, so we may work there.
(We will use this assumption to apply
Corollary

3.3
.)

The proof is by induction on
k
. In the base case
k=2
,
\Delta(\mathbb{F},2)
contains only matrix rank, for which the claim is true. Suppose
k>2
. Let
F\in\Delta(\mathbb{F},k)
.

Suppose
F(\langle 2\rangle_{i,j})>1
for every
i\neq j\in[k]
. Then it follows from
Corollary

3.3
and monotonicity of
F
under restriction, that for every
k
-tensor
T
,
F(T)
is at least some power of
\max_{i}\tensorrank_{i}(T)
. Thus if
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots
is a non-increasing converging sequence in
\{F(T):T\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}},d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{k}\}
, then in fact this sequence is contained in
\{F(T):T\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}}\}
for some
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{k}
and thus stabilizes (
Corollary

2.5
).

Suppose that there are
i\neq j\in[k]
such that
F(\langle 2\rangle_{i,j})=1
. By relabeling legs we may take
i=k-1
and
j=k
. By
Lemma

3.8
, there is a spectral point in
\Delta(\mathbb{F},k-1)
that takes the same set of values as
F
. By the induction hypothesis, this set of values is well-ordered.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

There are interesting connections between the above results and recent work on topological Noetherianity of
\GL_{\infty}
-varieties
[
26
,
14
]
. Indeed, our
Corollary

2.4
combined with results in those works leads to an alternative proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

4
Towards discreteness from below

Corollary

2.6
leaves open whether asymptotic rank is discrete from below. In this section, we go into directions towards proving that. In the first part we prove closedness, in the Euclidean topology, of the set of values that asymptotic rank takes, and extend the same statement to every point in Strassen’s asymptotic spectrum. In the second part we prove an equivalent geometric characterization of discreteness from below.

4.1
Euclidean closedness over the complex numbers

In this section our base field is the complex numbers.
In
Section

2
we proved that the set
\mathcal{R}=\{\asymprank(T):T\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}},d\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{\geq 1}\}
is well-ordered (
Corollary

2.5
). In particular, for any decreasing, and thus converging, sequence of elements in
\mathcal{R}
, the limit is in
\mathcal{R}
. In this section, we will prove that for
any
converging sequence in
\mathcal{R}
the limit is in
\mathcal{R}
, that is:

Theorem 4.1
.

\{\asymprank(T):T\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}},d\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{\geq 1}\}
is
Euclidean-closed in
\mathbb{R}
.

To prove
Theorem

4.1
, a key lemma is the following “Baire property” for affine varieties over
\mathbb{C}
. Let
V
be a complex vector space.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
X\subseteq V
be non-empty and Zariski-closed.
The intersection of any countable collection of Zariski-open and Zariski-dense subsets of
X
is Zariski-dense in
X
.

Proof.

For
i\in\mathbb{N}
, let
U_{i}\subseteq X
be Zariski-open and Zariski-dense.
Then
U_{i}
is Euclidean-dense in
X

[
41
, Prop. 5]
.
Also the
U_{i}
are Euclidean-open, because of the general fact that Zariski-open implies Euclidean-open.
With the Euclidean topology,
X
is a complete metric space and thus a Baire space (by the Baire category theorem
[
35
, Thm. 48.1]
), which implies that the intersection
\cap_{i}U_{i}
is Euclidean-dense in
X
. Now we use the general fact that Euclidean-dense implies Zariski-dense.
∎

We briefly work in a more general setting.
Let
V
be a complex vector space. A function
F\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
is called Zariski-lower-semicontinuous if for every
r\in\mathbb{R}
, the set
\{T\in V:F(T)\leq r\}
is Zariski-closed.

For
A\subseteq V
we write
F[A]=\sup_{T\in A}F(T)
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
X\subseteq V
be non-empty, Zariski-closed and irreducible, and let
F\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
be Zariski-lower-semicontinuous.
Then the set
\{T\in X:F(T)=F[X]\}
is Zariski-dense in
X
. In particular, it is non-empty, and
F[X]<\infty
.

Proof.

Let
X_{\leq r}=\{T\in X:F(T)\leq r\}
and
X_{>r}=\{T\in X:F(T)>r\}
.
Then
X_{\leq r}
is Zariski-closed in
X
(by assumption of
\{T\in V:F(T)\leq r\}
and
X
being Zariski-closed), and
X_{>r}
is Zariski-open.
Since
X
is irreducible, whenever
X_{\leq r}
is a proper subset of
X
, its complement
X_{>r}
is a non-empty Zariski-open subset and thus Zariski-dense (as every non-empty Zariski-open is Zariski-dense in an irreducible variety).
Let
r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots<F[X]
be a sequence of real numbers such that
\sup_{i\geq 1}r_{i}=F[X]
.
Since
\{T\in X:F(T)=F[X]\}
equals the countable intersection
\bigcap_{i\geq 1}X_{>r_{i}}
, and since every
X_{>r_{i}}
is nonempty (because
r_{i}<F[X]
), the claim follows by
Lemma

4.2
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
X\subseteq V
be non-empty and Zariski-closed, and let
F\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
be Zariski-lower-semicontinuous.
Then there exists
T\in X
such that
F(T)=F[X]
.

Proof.

Let
X=X_{1}\cup\dotsb\cup X_{\ell}
be the decomposition of
X
into irreducible components.
Then
F[X]=\max_{j\in[\ell]}F[X_{j}]
, and
Theorem

4.3
yields for every irreducible component a tensor
T_{j}\in X_{j}
such that
F(T_{j})=F[X_{j}]
.
Hence
F[X]=F(T_{j})
for some
j\in[\ell]
.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
F\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
be Zariski-lower-semicontinuous.
Then
\{F(T):T\in V\}
is closed (in the Euclidean topology on
\mathbb{R}
).

Proof.

This set is well-ordered (as a consequence of the Zariski-closedness of sublevel sets of
F
, combined with topological Noetherianity of the Zariski-topology, cf. the proof of
Corollary

2.5
), so every decreasing converging sequence in
\mathcal{F}=\{F(T):T\in V\}
has a limit in
\mathcal{F}
. It remains to prove the same for every increasing sequence.
Let
r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots
be a sequence in
\mathcal{F}
converging to
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
V_{\leq r}=\{T\in V:F(T)\leq r\}
. Then
V_{\leq r}
is Zariski-closed.
We have
F[V_{\leq r}]\leq r
by definition, and
F[V_{\leq r}]\geq r_{i}
for every
i\geq 1
, so
F[V_{\leq r}]=r
.
Therefore there exists
T\in V_{\leq r}
such that
F(T)=F[V_{\leq r}]=r
(
Corollary

4.4
). We conclude that
r\in\mathcal{F}
.
∎

We now return to the specific case of asymptotic tensor rank, noting that it has Zariski-closed sublevel sets (for fixed formats) by
Theorem

1.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}=\{\asymprank(T):T\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}},d\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{\geq 1}\}
.
Let
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots
be a converging sequence in
\mathcal{R}
with limit
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then the sequence is bounded, say
r_{i}\leq m
for all
i
.
Let
T_{i}
be tensors such that
\asymprank(T_{i})=r_{i}
.
We may assume that the
T_{i}
are concise. Then for every
i
, for some
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\leq m
we have
T_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}
. Thus our sequence
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots
is in
\cup_{d_{i}\leq m}\{\asymprank(T):T\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}\}
which is a closed set. We conclude that
r\in\cup_{d_{i}\leq m}\{\asymprank(T):T\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}\}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
. ∎

The same argument extends from asymptotic tensor rank to every point in Strassen’s asymptotic spectrum, by combining
Corollary

4.5
with the growth ingredient used in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
and
F\in\Delta(\mathbb{C},k)
. Then

\{F(T):T\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}},\,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{k}\}
is Euclidean-closed in
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

The argument mirrors the proof of
Theorem

3.1
, by induction on
k
. The base case
k=2
is matrix rank, for which the claim is trivial. Suppose
k>2
and let
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots
be a converging sequence in the set, with limit
r
and bound
r_{i}\leq m
for all
i
.

Suppose first that
F(\langle 2\rangle_{i,j})>1
for every
i\neq j\in[k]
. Then, as in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
,
F(T)
is at least some power of
\max_{i}\tensorrank_{i}(T)
for every
k
-tensor
T
. Picking concise representatives
T_{i}
with
F(T_{i})=r_{i}
, the bound
r_{i}\leq m
forces all format dimensions to be at most some
M\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence the sequence lies in the finite union
\bigcup_{d_{i}\leq M}\{F(T):T\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}\}
. Each set in this union is Euclidean-closed by
Corollary

4.5
(which applies since
F
has Zariski-closed sublevel sets, by
Corollary

2.4
), so the union is Euclidean-closed and the limit
r
lies in it.

Suppose instead that
F(\langle 2\rangle_{i,j})=1
for some
i\neq j\in[k]
. By relabeling legs we may take
i=k-1
and
j=k
. By
Lemma

3.8
,
F
takes the same set of values as some
F^{\prime}\in\Delta(\mathbb{C},k-1)
, and the claim follows by the induction hypothesis.
∎

4.2
Geometric characterization of discreteness from below

In this section our base field is the complex numbers. For a complex vector space
V
we consider any function
F\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
whose sublevel sets are Zariski-closed (e.g., asymptotic tensor rank,
Corollary

2.4
). Letting
\mathcal{F}
be the set
\{F(T):T\in V\}
, it follows from topological Noetherianity of
V
that
\mathcal{F}
is well-ordered (cf. the proof of
Corollary

2.5
). In this section we give a geometric characterization for such a set
\mathcal{F}
to be also discrete from below (that is, for every nondecreasing sequence in it to stabilize).

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
V
be a complex vector space, and let
F\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
be Zariski-lower-semicontinuous.
We write
V_{\leq r}=\{T\in V:F(T)\leq r\}
and
V_{=r}=\{T\in V:F(T)=r\}
for
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
The following are equivalent.

(a)

\{F(T):T\in V\}
is discrete from below.

(b)

For every
r\in\mathbb{R}
,
V_{=r}
is Zariski-open in
V_{\leq r}
.

Note in
Theorem

4.7
that
V_{=r}
may be empty (namely when
F
does not attain
r
), in which case
V_{=r}
is trivially Zariski-open.

Proof.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b).
Let
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\mathcal{F}
is discrete from below, there exists an
r^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
r^{\prime}<r
and for all
s<r
with
s\in\mathcal{F}
we have

s<r^{\prime}
.
Then

V_{=r}=V_{>r^{\prime}}\cap V_{\leq r}

(9)

where
V_{>r^{\prime}}=\{T\in V:F(T)>r^{\prime}\}
is Zariski-open in
V
by the assumption on
F
.
Therefore
V_{=r}
is open in
V_{\leq r}
.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(a).
It suffices to show that if
r_{1}\leq r_{2}\leq\dotsc
is a sequence in
\mathcal{F}
, then there exists
i_{0}\geq 1
such that
r_{i}=r_{i+1}
for all
i\geq i_{0}
.
We observe that any such sequence is bounded above by
Theorem

4.3
, hence
r:=\lim_{i\to\infty}r_{i}
exists, and
r\in\mathcal{F}
, because
\mathcal{F}
is closed (
Corollary

4.5
).
We now argue by contraposition.
If
V_{=r}
is open,
V_{<r}
is closed.
If
r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots
is a strictly increasing sequence of values of
F
converging to
r
, then

V_{<r}=\cup_{r_{i}<r}V_{\leq r_{i}}

expresses an affine variety as a countably infinite union of proper closed subvarieties.
This is impossible over
\mathbb{C}
, as we will now prove. (Note, however, that for instance over the algebraic closure of the rationals, which is countable, this is possible.)
Indeed if
V_{<r}=X_{1}\cup\dotsc\cup X_{\ell}
is its decomposition into irreducible components, then there is some component
X_{j}
such that for every
r_{i}<r
,
V_{\leq r_{i}}\cap X_{j}
is a proper closed subset of
X_{j}
, for otherwise every component would be of the form
V_{\leq r_{i_{j}}}
for some
i_{j}
, and one would have
V_{<r}=\cup_{j=1}^{\ell}V_{\leq r_{i_{j}}}
, contradicting the assumption that we have an infinite sequence of strictly increasing
V_{\leq r_{i}}
’s. Therefore
V_{\leq r_{i}}\cap X_{j}
has non-empty Zariski-open complement (as
X_{j}
is irreducible), which is also dense.
Therefore
\cup_{r_{i}<r}V_{\leq r_{i}}\cap X_{j}
has dense complement in
X_{j}
by
4.2
, and is in particular non-empty.
As a result we obtain that
\cup_{r_{i}<r}V_{\leq r_{i}}
cannot contain all of
X_{j}
.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
V=\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}}
, and let
V_{\leq r}
be the set of all tensors in
V
with asymptotic rank at most
r
. Let
V_{=r}
be the set of all tensors in
V
with asymptotic rank equal to
r
.
The following are equivalent.

(a)

\{\asymprank(T):T\in V\}
is discrete.

(b)

For every
r\in\mathbb{R}
,
V_{=r}
is Zariski-open in
V_{\leq r}
.

Proof.

This follows from
Theorem

4.7
and
Corollary

2.4
.
∎

5
Discussion and open problems

We discuss several natural directions and open problems in the context of our results, Strassen’s asymptotic rank conjecture and the matrix multiplication exponent.

•

Discreteness from below.
We proved discreteness from above for the asymptotic rank (and a large class of other parameters) over (for instance) the complex numbers. One of the main problems that we leave open is whether this parameter is also discrete from below. Indeed, Strassen’s asymptotic rank conjecture would imply this.

As an intermediate result, we have shown that any converging sequence of asymptotic ranks has a limit which is also an asymptotic rank (
Theorem

4.1
). What remains to be shown is that any such sequence is eventually constant; we proved an equivalent characterization in
Corollary

4.8
.

Notions of discreteness for asymptotic parameters have been studied more broadly, in particular in the context of the Shannon capacity of graphs
[
42
]
and the related asymptotic spectrum of graphs
[
54
,
51
]
; interestingly, in that setting, the asymptotic parameter of interest (Shannon capacity) is not discrete (neither from above or from below), which leads to a graph limit approach to determining Shannon capacity
[
25
]
.

•

Geometric properties; irreducibility.

Given (
Theorem

1.2
) that the sublevel sets
\{T\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}}:\asymprank(T)\leq r\}
of the asymptotic tensor rank are Zariski-closed, it is natural to ask about the geometric properties of these sets. For instance, we may ask if they are irreducible (i.e., cannot be written as the union of two proper Zariski-closed subsets) whenever
\mathbb{F}
is algebraically closed.

Indeed, irreducibility is true for
k=2
(matrices), since then the asymptotic rank coincides with matrix rank, and the set of matrices of at most a given rank is an irreducible variety.
For
k\geq 3
, irreducibility is open. It would imply both that the set of achievable values of the asymptotic rank is discrete, and a weak form of Strassen’s asymptotic rank conjecture:
there exist at most
d_{1}\dotsb d_{k}+1
asymptotic ranks in format
d_{1}\times\dotsb\times d_{k}
.
This follows from a dimension argument (topological dimension is the maximal length of a decreasing chain of irreducible subvarieties, and the dimension of
\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}\times\dotsb\times d_{k}}
is
d_{1}\dotsb d_{k}
).

To see that this is a weak form of Strassen’s conjecture, note that Strassen’s conjecture is equivalent to the statement that there are exactly
d+1
distinct asymptotic ranks in
(\mathbb{F}^{d})^{\otimes k}
(namely
\{0,1,\dotsc,d\}
).
Note that Strassen’s conjecture also implies that the sets
\{T\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}}:\asymprank(T)\leq r\}
are irreducible:
r
is integer, and
\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}\times r}\times\dotsb\times\mathbb{F}^{d_{k}\times r}\times\mathbb{F}^{r\times\dotsb\times r}
is irreducible and admits a surjective polynomial map onto the set of tensors with all flattening ranks at most
r
.

•

Computation and structure of asymptotic ranks.
Our results provide new tools to concretely understand the asymptotic rank of families of tensors and their relation to the matrix multiplication exponent
\omega
, in the spirit of
[
32
]
. For instance (by
Theorem

1.2
), truth of the asymptotic rank conjecture on any subset of tensors by our result extends to the Zariski-closure.

Another natural direction where our result plays a role is in the task of understanding the relation between asymptotic ranks of explicit (families of) tensors and the matrix multiplication exponent. For example, it is well-known that if the asymptotic rank of the small Coppersmith–Winograd tensor
\mathrm{cw}_{2}
equals 3, then
\omega=2
. For which tensors can we prove this property?

It follows from
Theorem

1.2
and known classifications that
\mathrm{cw}_{2}
has asymptotic rank at most the generic asymptotic rank of
3\times 3\times 3
tensors with hyperdeterminant
0
(which is a codimension
1
variety). It also has at most the asymptotic rank of a generic tensor with support

\{(0,0,0),(1,1,1),(2,2,2),(0,1,2),(1,2,0),(2,0,1)\}
.
