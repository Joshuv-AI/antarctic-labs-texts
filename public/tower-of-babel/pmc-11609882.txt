Open isthmus and lambda sign of early Joubert syndrome: elucidating development of molar tooth sign
Authors: R. K. Pooh, M. Takeda, K. Itoh, J. Yoshimatsu, K. Ogo, M. Machida, H. Ohashi, O. Shimokawa
Published: 2024

Introduction

Joubert syndrome and related disorders (JSRD), which include 40 subtypes, are ciliopathies associated with cerebello‐oculo‐renal syndromes and with a high rate of neurodevelopmental impairment. The hallmark radiological brain feature across subtypes is the molar tooth sign (MTS) 1 . Notably, the bilateral roots of the MTS represent angle‐shifted, maloriented and elongated superior cerebellar peduncles (SCPs); MTS is not present in the early embryonic stages due to undeveloped SCP fibers. The processes underpinning MTS development during neuroembryogenesis are poorly understood. Despite existing literature on the prenatal diagnosis of JSRD, detailed neurosonography (NSG) of JSRD in the first trimester has been reported rarely. Herein, we present a case series of early JSRD and provide insights into the ontogenesis of the MTS.

Case series

Between June 2018 and December 2023, we identified five cases of early JSRD using transvaginal (TV) three‐dimensional (3D)‐NSG, according to the guidelines of the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) 2 , using Voluson E10 and Expert 22 machines, with 6–12‐MHz 3D‐TV transducers (GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria). Informed consent was obtained in compliance with the Institutional Review Board (CRI‐IRB‐010) guidelines. Detailed case information is summarized in Table 1. Trio‐based exome sequencing (ES) identified pathogenic variants of CEP290, CC2D2A and ARMC9. All five cases opted for termination of pregnancy, and autopsy was conducted in Cases 4 and 5 only. A family history of JSRD was present for Case 1 only. Of note, trio ES in Case 3 revealed a maternal heterozygous variant, yet the paternal pathogenic variant was not identified under standard ES protocols. Subsequently, a considerable retrotransposon insertion of approximately 6000 base pairs over the same locus was found. Case 4 involved uniparental disomy (UPD) of chromosome 2, carrying the ARMC9 variant, associated with the JSRD subtype Joubert syndrome‐30 (JBTS30). However, this was initially undiagnosed, as the ARMC9 variant was not classed as pathogenic in the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) genetic database, but it was later identified as a pathogenic variant. Figure 1 shows TV‐3D‐NSG images in Cases 1–3, with common findings in all three cases being occipital cephalocele continuous with the posterior fossa and the open isthmus. When viewed in the coronal plane, the open isthmus appears in the shape of a capital letter lambda; hence, we named it the Λ sign. Figures 2 and S1 show the intracranial changes in Case 5 and Case 4, respectively. As gestation progressed, the line drawn through the aqueduct and fourth ventricle on imaging gradually shifted to a more horizontal orientation (Figures 2a and S1). Furthermore, macroscopic and histological findings of the brain specimen at 21 weeks in Case 5 (Figure 2c–e) revealed a malpositioned cerebellum and an abnormally deviated, elongated, but still‐developing SCP.

Genetic interpretation and prenatal diagnosis

The genetic variation observed in our case series highlights the diverse inheritance patterns of JSRD, typically considered autosomal recessive with a 25% recurrence risk, except for rare X‐linked recessive cases 3 . The UPD of Case 4 suggests low recurrence risk. The large retrotransposon insertion in Case 3 indicates that standard short‐read ES may not suffice for comprehensive diagnosis of JSRD.

Regarding prenatal diagnosis of JSRD, there were small series published in 2005 4 and 2011 5 that demonstrated the MTS on magnetic resonance imaging. Prenatal diagnosis remains challenging because the fourth ventricle does not expand cystically as in Dandy–Walker syndrome, and the two cerebellar hemispheres develop typically and meet medially due to vermis hypoplasia in late gestation. Pugash et al. 6 first reported prenatal imaging with genetic diagnosis of Joubert syndrome in 2011. Subsequently, several reports 3 , 7 , 8 , 9 on a slit‐like gap in the brainstem to the cerebellum have been published. A case of early JSRD was reported in 2016, in which a 12‐week transabdominal ultrasound scan revealed enlarged intracranial translucency and MTS‐like morphology of the cerebellar peduncles 10 . However, peduncles have not yet formed at this stage, and it is hard to distinguish between the normal mesencephalon's aqueduct and that in JSRD. The first TV‐NSG report of suspected ciliopathy 11 showed brainstem abnormalities, a dilated fourth ventricle and renal cystic dysplasia at 14 weeks' gestation.

Ω (omega) sign vs Λ (lambda) sign

Our investigation reveals that early pregnancy presents a pivotal window for discerning JSRD markers. A comparative analysis in Figure 3 illustrates the divergence between normal and JSRD development as early as 12 weeks, characterized by a closed isthmus and an Ω sign with a choroid bar in typical fetal development 12 , as opposed to an open isthmus and a Λ sign in JSRD. As gestation progresses, the cerebellar hemispheres continue to develop, and the Λ sign gradually becomes more acute and less prominent. Therefore, the first trimester and early second trimester, when the Λ sign is most apparent, is the optimal time for diagnosing JSRD.

Mechanism of open isthmus in JSRD

Midbrain–hindbrain boundary isthmic organizers, Otx2 and Gbx2, are expressed in the embryonic midbrain and hindbrain. Then, Fgf8 is active in isthmus formation and cerebellum differentiation in the Gbx2 overlapping region, forming the isthmus and activating the Ras‐ERK pathway to differentiate the cerebellum from the metencephalon 13 . Altered Fgf8 and Gbx2 expression significantly disrupts neural regions in the midbrain and hindbrain due to abnormal gene patterning. The 40 subtypes of JSRD all show the MTS on sonographic imaging despite different causative genes. If JSRD begins in early fetal life with isthmus dysplasia and abnormal cerebellar vermis formation developing from the isthmus and rhombomere 1 roof plates 13 , the Gbx2/Fgf8/Ras‐ERK pathway may be implicated strongly in JSRD. Fgf8 is involved in many cascades, including the Ras‐ERK pathway in cell proliferation, differentiation and cilia length 14 . Future research may reveal mechanisms linking early isthmus development seen in JSRD to these pathways.

Development of MTS during gestation

In JSRD, the isthmus remains open in early pregnancy so that, even at mid‐gestation, the cerebellum becomes detached and is ‘floating’ from the brainstem, altering the angle and length of the SCP (Figure 4). With cerebellar development, this elongation may cause the SCP to gradually become horizontal and eventually form the MTS in the axial plane.

Schematic diagrams showing difference in cerebellar hemisphere location and superior cerebellar peduncle (SCP) between normal case (a) and case with Joubert syndrome and related disorder (JSRD) (b). (a) In normal case, cerebellum is located adjacent to brainstem and SCP extends toward center of cerebellar hemispheres. (b) In case of JSRD, upper cerebellar hemisphere is floating and distant from brainstem, where cerebellum grows. As a result, angle of SCP differs from normal and SCP is considerably elongated. This elongated SCP, which gradually becomes more horizontal, forms roots of molar tooth sign.

Conclusions

Early NSG reveals the mechanism and process by which the open isthmus, indicated by the Λ sign, leads to eventual MTS development. This breakthrough underscores the potential for prenatal diagnosis of JSRD in the first or early‐second trimester, before the development of the SCPs, which are the roots of the MTS, although genetic variants must be identified for a definitive diagnosis.
