Muscle structure assessment using synchrotron radiation X-ray micro-computed tomography in murine with cerebral ischemia
Authors: Subok Kim, Sanghun Jang, Onseok Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMuscles are crucial for balance and walking, activities which depend specifically on the lower extremity muscles. Therefore, the evaluation of stroke induced atrophy and paralysis is essential; however, determining the extent of damage in the days after its occurrence remains challenging. In this study, we evaluated ischemic stroke-induced soleus muscle damage in gerbils using synchrotron radiation X-ray micro-computed tomography (SR-µCT), comparing a control group (n = 3), animals 7 days after stroke (7 d, n = 3), and animals 14 days after stroke (14 d, n = 3). The left muscle was paralyzed, whereas the right muscle was not. Subsequently, we quantified the assessment by segmenting the soleus muscle based on the extracellular space/matrix and fiber region to determine the degree of damage. The muscle fiber-to-extracellular space/matrix ratio were significantly damaged due to paralysis on the left side (control vs. 14 d, P = 0.040). Muscle area was significantly different at 14 d between the left and right sides (P = 0.010). Additionally, the left local fascicle surface area, thickness, global pennation angle, and local fascicle angle were significantly different between the control and 14 d groups (P = 0.002, P = 0.007, P = 0.005, and P = 0.014 respectively). These findings underscore the potential of post-stroke animal studies in improving rehabilitation treatment for the central nervous system by assessing the degree of muscle recovery.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Synchrotron radiation, Muscle, Damage, FiberSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Cerebral stroke is a central nervous system (CNS) injury, wherein approximately 85% of survivors suffer from hemiplegia due to motor and sensory damage to skeletal muscles1. Muscle tone varies depending on the degree of brain damage and can lead to abnormal spasticity, along with the occurrence of synergy pattern movements, posing a significant challenge in rehabilitation therapy2–4. In stroke-associated paralysis research, animal models are used instead of humans to analyze vascular structures such as mitochondria, fiber arrangement, and muscle disease mechanisms5,6. In addition, animal models are important for elucidating the mechanisms of neurological symptoms and for evaluating the effectiveness of clinical interventions7. Therefore, basic research on muscle physiology using animal models is necessary. In particular, the function of the lower limb muscles is significant for balance and gait8. If these muscles are damaged, they can lead to functional defects. Therefore, analyzing the degree of lower extremity muscle damage caused by brain injury is important and contributes to effective rehabilitation. The soleus is innervated by the sciatic nerve and plays a critical role in gait through hind leg movement9. Therefore, evaluating structural changes in the soleus muscle will increase our neurophysiological understanding of the mechanisms underlying muscle damage, which is a pivotal in the application of rehabilitation training.

Histological analysis and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provide accurate assessments of the state of a muscle by examining its detailed structure10–12. However, histological analyses such as hematoxylin and eosin staining increase the risk of damaging the tissue sample during slide preparation13. In addition, analyzing the degree of muscle damage using MRI for the soleus muscles of mice and rats is challenging, hindering the observation of the detailed structure of the muscle fibers14. In basic research, medical imaging analysis using synchrotron radiation X-ray micro-computed tomography (SR-µCT) is used15–21. SR-µCT is mainly used in the medical imaging field because of its ability to obtain three-dimensional (3D) microstructure imaging at excellent high resolution without dissecting the shape and structure of sample22. Thus, SR-µCT is useful for 3D visualization in studies at the cellular level23,24.

Stroke is intricately linked to paralysis due to damage to the CNS, highlighting the pressing need for studies capable of quantitatively assessing the degree of muscular damage caused by stroke. Therefore, accurate 3D structural analysis of muscle tissue status, disease progression, and recovery using animal models is crucial for quantifying muscle decrease caused by stroke. In this study, we examined the soleus muscle of Mongolian gerbils after ischemic stroke using SR-µCT. We divided the injured and uninjured muscle structures based on the days since stroke occurrence. We sought to contribute to foundational rehabilitation research by conducting a 3D assessment and analyzing the degree of muscle damage.

Microstructure rate of the soleus muscle

The paralyzed and non-paralyzed muscles caused by ischemic stroke are shown in Fig. 1. The murine ischemic models used included sham operation (control), 7 days after ischemia (7 d), and 14 days after ischemia (14 d). For ethanol dehydration of the paralyzed left muscles (control, n = 3; 7 d, n = 3; 14 d, n = 3) and non-paralyzed right muscles (control, n = 3; 7 d, n = 3; 14 d, n = 3), the baseline weights were expressed as a graph (Fig. 1A). The muscle weights for the control (t = 0.359; P = 0.754), 7 d (t = -1.164; P = 0.365), and 14 d (t = -0.235; P = 0.836) groups are shown. None of the muscle weight values were statistically significant. The results from the SR-µCT of the soleus muscle microstructures with water-shedding algorithms are shown in Fig. 1 (B), revealing structural changes to the muscle due to ischemia. We analyzed the muscle structure along the axial plane from the soleus muscle belly to the central local area. The left muscle was paralyzed whereas the right muscle was not. In addition, we observed significant alterations in the microstructures (dark blue: muscle fascicles; light blue: extracellular space/matrix) based on the number of days that elapsed since the stroke’s onset. Evaluation of the non-paralyzed muscle showed no change in the microstructure. Figure 1 (C) shows the rate [%] for the fascicle (fiber) and extracellular space/matrix structures. On the left side, the fiber ratio decreased with time and the extracellular space/matrix ratio increased (F = 5.420; P = 0.045). There was a change in the muscle structure rate [%] between the control and 7 d groups, but the difference was not significant (P = 0.172). Similarly, there was no significant difference between 7 d and 14 d groups (P = 0.519). However, there was a significant difference in the rate [%] of change in the control and 14 d groups (P = 0.040). On the right side, there was no change in the rate of muscle structure over time (F = 0.450; P = 0.658). In addition, there was no significant difference in any of the three groups (control vs. 7 d, P = 0.807; 7 d vs. 14 d, P = 0.643; control vs. 14 d, P = 0.951).

Fiber analysis of damage to paralyzed muscles

The overall muscle structure is shown in Fig. 2. Figure 2(A) shows the paralyzed (left) and non-paralyzed (right) muscles. We analyzed the muscle structure along the axial plane over the entire area of ​​the soleus muscle belly. After ischemic stroke, paralyzed muscle atrophy occurred over time. In other words, it was possible to assess the paralyzed muscle atrophy based on the overall muscle structures. Subsequently, this study conducted a quantitative assessment by examined fiber structures, as shown in Fig. 2(B) and (C). Figure 2(B) depicts the degree of muscle fiber reduction using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test. The paralyzed left muscles were control (n = 3), 7 d (n = 3) and 14 d (n = 3). Non-paralyzed right muscles were control (n = 3), 7 d (n = 3) and 14 d (n = 3). There was a significant difference in the number of fibers on the left side (F = 5.447; P = 0.045), whereas there was no significant difference between the control and 7 d groups (P = 0.179) or between the 7 d and 14 d groups (P = 0.492). However, there was a significant difference between the control and 14 d groups (P = 0.039). There were no significant differences on the right side (F = 0.175; P = 0.843). In addition, there was no significant difference among any of the three groups (control vs. 7 d, P = 0.838; 7 d vs. 14 d, P = 0.913; control vs. 14 d, P = 0.985). We performed an independent t-test to compare the number of muscle fibers between the left and right muscle. There was no significant difference between the control (left vs. right) (t = -0.220; P = 0.837). Similarly, there was no significant difference between the 7 d groups (left vs. right) (t = -1.381; P = 0.240). However, the 14 d (left vs. right) groups showed a significant difference (t = -3.231; P = 0.032). Figure 2 (C) shows the evaluation of separation in the extracellular space/matrix region according to the days following stroke. We verified the extracellular space/matrix separation using one-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test and regression analysis. However, there was a significant difference in extracellular space/matrix separation between the control and 14 d groups (P = 0.041). There was no significant difference in the degree of extracellular space/matrix separation in the non-paralyzed (right) muscles according to the number of days (F = 0.403, P = 0.068, R2 = 0.21). Multiple comparisons of the three groups revealed no significant differences (control vs. 7 d, P = 0.662; 7 d vs. 14 d, P = 0.883; control vs. 14 d, P = 0.910). Discussion

The use of SR-µCT-based analysis in this study enabled tissue observation and analysis similar to those conducted through staining25. In addition, we used microstructure analysis with excellent spatial resolution26. This study serves as a base to establish fundamental research on muscles in preclinical experiments using animal models. SR-µCT images are advantageous because they use a minimum number of samples (soleus muscles). As a result, thousands of tomography images can be acquired per sample simultaneously, and thus are presented as an optimized method for conducting research16. In this study, muscle fibers decreased in size, and the extracellular space/matrix separation increased at 7-day intervals after ischemic stroke. When muscle atrophy occurs due to a stroke, the fiber tissues are reduced and the extracellular space/matrix separations are expanded at the same time. In addition, by determining the muscle parameters, we found that the paralyzed muscle experienced considerable decrease in the area and fiber volume within the volume of interest. As a result, even in the structural complexity of the muscle area within the volume of interest, the paralyzed muscle was more severely decreased compared to the non-paralyzed muscle. When comparing the control and 14 d groups in the paralyzed muscle (left), severe damage (or atrophy) occurred after stroke. Upon comparing the paralyzed and non-paralyzed muscles (right), a significant decrease was observed in the 14 d groups. We observed fascicle atrophy where the muscle fibers gathered. We evaluated the degree of decrease by visualizing the paralyzed fascicle. Significant atrophy occurred from 7 d, and a severe decrease occurred in the 14 d groups compared to the control (fascicle surface, thickness and curvature). Thus, it can be inferred that after a stroke, the movement of the paralyzed muscles becomes restricted. Hence, rehabilitation of damaged muscles is essential after stroke. Hence, it is important to perform detailed muscle assessments to maintain muscle strength and improve function, especially in intensive post-stroke rehabilitation studies27.

The sensory nerves transmit sensory information to the spinal dorsal horn and CNS via proprioceptors28. Sensation, which is transmitted to proprioceptors, enables self-recognition without visual information and helps perform the movement by improving posture and joint stability29. However, a stroke may result in the loss of this information, which may reduce motor learning30,31. Therefore, basic rehabilitation studies are important post-stroke, and both paralyzed and non-paralyzed interventions increase muscle structures32.

The soleus muscle has a striated pattern, and sarcomere analysis plays an important role in muscle injury and recovery. However, all functions are limited by stroke, and the clinical presentation is an upper motor neuron lesion. This may occur comprehensively in a specific muscle where the motor pattern is lost leading to an increase in abnormal muscle tone33. It is classified as type I based on the genetic expression of myosin heavy chain, whereby the muscle does not tire34. In addition, it contains a myosin-heavy chain isoform that has a low actin-dependent ATPase activity. However, if fatigue increases rapidly upon injury, myosin heavy chain deficiency may result in the occurrence of various diseases35. Therefore, analyzing the degree of soleus muscle decrease caused by stroke is important for understanding the paralysis damage. Furthermore, evaluating the functional activity of the soleus muscle is an essential procedure in rehabilitation.

Previous studies suggested that muscle contraction, damage, and atrophy occur in muscle fibers36. At the overall muscle level, the size of a muscle is mostly determined by the number and size of a single fiber. If the muscle showed fat and connective tissue infiltration due to pathological conditions, the number and size of fibers may be decreased37. Muscle decrease (damage) due to ischemia-reperfusion is characterized by edema, infiltration of inflammatory cells, and disruption of normal muscle cell structure38,39. Temporal dynamic structural changes during ischemia-reperfusion of the mouse hindlimb muscle have been investigated using MRI to examine parameters of the damaged muscle40. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), which analyzes microstructural changes by measuring the degree of diffusion of substances, was applied to quantify damage indicators through changes in muscle tissue after ischemia and reperfusion. By simulating the physiological response of muscle structure, the severity and recovery potential of ischemic stress were predicted and important information was provided for managing physiological changes. In addition, heterogeneous deformation in the arrangement and pattern of fibers (local fascicles) was investigated at various angles during muscle movement using MRI-based DTI41,42. During dynamic tasks, the recruitment of regional patterns in muscles changes43, and strain distribution can vary depending on the angle44,45. When a muscle moves, mechanical behavior and activation patterns interact to cause heterogeneous deformation within the fascicles, resulting in intra-fascicular effects during various activities41. In particular, investigating heterogeneous deformation of local fibers is important for understanding the biomechanical information and function of muscles42. Deformation of local fascicle strains affects the pathological moment associated with several conditions such as spastic cerebral palsy46,47, and muscle injuries in athletes48. Therefore, investigating ischemia-induced decreases in fiber (or local fascicle) and heterogeneous deformation may help in understanding the complex functions and pathological features of the extracellular matrix and non-muscle structures. We observed striated features caused by the regular arrangement of muscle fascicles, which are structures that are difficult to observe on MRI49. In this study, the paralyzed muscle fascicles atrophied days after ischemic stroke, and infiltration of fat and connective tissue occurred. Fascicle surface area, thickness, and degree (global or local) decreased significantly at 7 d compared to the control, but there was no drastic decrease between 7 d and 14 d. While the initial inflammatory response caused by stroke leads to muscle damage, the progression of neural plasticity from the corticospinal axon terminal to the synapse connecting the dendrites of other neurons leads to significant structural reorganization and cell recovery in the CNS50–52. If the degree of muscle atrophy is severe, miR-1 is expressed, which reduces myogenin expression, leading to rapid recovery53. Compensatory movements, which may be due to the re-emergence of elemental motor patterns present before the stroke with recovery of neuronal innervation, may lead to the emergence of new motor patterns. These patterns emerge as adaptations or replacements of the remaining motor elements, whereby the function is taken over, replaced, or substituted by another end effector or body segment51,54. We expect that muscle loss due to ischemia progresses through repair and recovery of some damage to muscle cells through the regenerative ability of nerve reorganization. Muscle atrophy due to stroke leads to secondary effects on pennation angle, a structural characteristic including muscle fiber length55–57. When muscles atrophy, abnormal fascia and muscle tone increase and extensibility decreases, which can shorten muscle fiber length and change the pennation angle. Muscle elasticity and muscle diameter decrease, leading to a decrease in pennation angle due to imbalance in the tissue that forms the muscle fiber arrangement. Therefore, the physiological changes in muscle caused by stroke could lead to impairments in muscle function and mechanical characteristics58,59, potentially leading to a decrease in both the global pennation angle and the local fascicle angle.

Because of the small number of ischemic-induced experimental animals used in this study, the muscle decrease could not be accurately evaluated due to lack of data. However, it is important to derive the most efficient results through the minimum number of models that can generate statistical results based on the 3R (replacement, reduction, refinement) animal protocol60. The main goal of this preliminary study was to analyze muscle damage through a single model, rather than an optimized evaluation method by collecting data from several models of ischemia. SR-µCT has the ability to produce hundreds to thousands of tomographic images from one sample (soleus) in one scan25. Previously, in SR-µCT cases, imaging and statistical analysis of osteoporosis (bone) assessment were possible by acquiring thousands of tomographic images using a small number of mice20,61. In addition, high-resolution 3D reconstruction of SR-based human cochleae was performed to visualize complex anatomical structures, including cell layers and nerve fiber paths within the auditory organ, providing important information for identifying the causes of hearing-related diseases62. By acquiring high-resolution imaging of human bone through SR and machine learning, micro-cracks occurring in bone diseases can be automatically detected with high performance and applied to bone micromechanics63. In particular, the combination of SR and machine learning have potential for clinical application as an important technology for bone health management and disease measurement. Recently, SR-µCT studies have visualized and compared complex structural changes in muscle tissue from patients with stroke and spinal cord injury64. As a new muscle diagnosis approach that evaluates the pathological state of muscle tissue in human subjects rather than animal models, SR-µCT has proven to be a tool that can precisely evaluate the subtle atrophy and tissue level of muscle.

Here, we provided basic data through preliminary experiments aiming to perform muscle damage assessments by analyzing the soleus muscle as efficiently as possible using a small number of ischemic gerbil models. In other words, the approach of measuring the degree of muscle damage after stroke through SR-µCT can provide basic data for preclinical research. However, there are some items to consider with this approach. Muscle assessment is important when considering the arrangement pattern and thickness of the fibers to optimize accuracy and specificity for quantifying muscle structure. Muscles are complex elements, and it is difficult to sufficiently reflect their overall complexity using only a single indicator. Methods for measuring muscle structure can be effective as a starting point, but research that is more precise and multifactorial is required. Muscles have characteristics of soft tissue, and there is unavoidable variation in the degree of fixation; therefore, the possibility of sample shrinkage is high13. In this study, we suspected that there could be shrinkage during muscle acquisition. We showed different results even for the same muscle when measuring the muscle weight. It has been suggested that the inherent muscle mass of each individual is different, and technical differences may have occurred during muscle extraction25. In other words, there might be a limit to the deviation of muscle structures by evaluating muscle parameters in only a small number of individuals. In the future, we aim to develop an optimized system for calculating parameter ratios by combining thousands of images obtained using more models from SR-µCT with machine learning and detailed mathematical modelling. We established the possibility of studying paralyzed muscle damage in ischemic stroke using SR-µCT. After ischemic stroke, we found that atrophy occurred at 7 d, with severe atrophy occurring by the 14 d. We identified the structure of the soleus muscle and assessed the degree of damage through a simple image processing procedure, demonstrating the potential for foundational research in rehabilitation science to measure and improve gait. Future studies can explore evaluation methods for large-scale animal studies, such as the development of new post-stroke medications. Finally, we expect to lay a foundation for basic research on rehabilitation and medical therapy to evaluate the mechanisms behind compensation and recovery phases in muscles. This can be achieved by assessing the level of recovery and mechanical changes in paralyzed muscles during stroke in animal model studies.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Kangwon National University (Chuncheon, Kangwon, South Korea) (approval number: KW-200113-1). All experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This study was conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Experimental animals and surgical procedure

An overall description of the assessment of the effects of ischemic stroke of the soleus muscle using SR-µCT is shown in Fig. 6. We obtained Mongolian gerbils (male, 6-months-old; body weight, 65 ± 0.75 g; n = 9) from the Experimental Animal Center of Kangwon National University (South Korea). The animals were acclimated for 1 week and kept at a constant temperature (23 ± 2 °C) and humidity (60 ± 5%) with a 12 h light/dark cycle. Animals were allowed access to water and food ad libitum. We randomly selected an animal to create an injured soleus muscle and performed cerebral ischemia according to the number of days.

An overview of muscle structure processing by ischemic stroke using synchrotron radiation X-ray micro-computed tomography (SR-µCT). (A) Ischemic stroke surgery was performed as previously described65. (B) Experimental animals (n = 9) were assigned to three groups for ischemic stroke procedures. (C) Blue shows the region of interest (ROI), i.e. the muscle belly. Under the microscope, the surface and curvature of the muscle bundle could be confirmed. (D) The soleus muscle processing for parameter analysis of murine stroke models.

Figure 6(A) shows an ischemic stroke surgery performed as previously described65. Briefly, animals were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane (Baxtor, Deerfield, IL) in 33% oxygen and 67% nitrous oxide. Subsequently, each animal’s rectal temperature was modulated (37 ± 0.5 °C) using a thermometric pad with a rectal temperature probe (TR-100; Fine Science Tools, Foster City, CA). After a midline incision on the neck, the right common carotid artery (CCA) was occluded for 30 min using an aneurysm clip. If the right CCA was occluded, the blood flow from the left CCA supplied blood to the entire brain area via the circle of Willis. Thus, unilateral occlusion of the CCA for 30 min induced ischemic neuronal death in the primary motor cortex.

Sample acquisition

Experimental animals (n = 9) were assigned to three groups (Fig. 6B). Sham-operated animals were sacrificed 7 days after the sham operation (control, n = 3). Cerebral ischemia-operated animals were sacrificed 7 days (7 d, n = 3) or 14 days after ischemia (14 d, n = 3). For analysis of the soleus muscles, we anesthetized the animals in each group using pentobarbital sodium (60 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection) (JW Pharmaceutical, Seoul, South Korea) at 7 and 14 days after ischemia. Subsequently, we perfused the whole body of the animal with 4% paraformaldehyde solution (in 0.1 M PB, pH 7.4), removed the left and right soleus muscles, and fixed them for 4 h. The samples used in this study were the left paralyzed-control (n = 3), -7 d (n = 3) and − 14 d (n = 3), and the right non-paralyzed-control (n = 3), -7 d (n = 3), and − 14 d muscles (n = 3).

Muscle weight measurement

Muscle weight was measured to confirm the morphological changes and atrophy of the damaged soleus muscle after stroke. The soleus muscle weight was compared after dehydration.

Metal staining for synchrotron radiation

All samples were stained with phosphotungstic acid (PTA), which offerd an enhanced contrast of tissue images upon X-ray imaging. We dehydrated the samples immersed in formalin from low to high ethanol concentrations at 3 h intervals to prevent structure distortion (30%, 50%, 70%, and 100%). The PTA reagent (Quintiles, Surrey, USA) was prepared and used at a concentration of 0.3%.

Microscopic imaging

The SG26T-PI model (Daymyung, China, OEM) microscope equipped with a 5-MP CMOS sensor and a MUC-500 camera (Wakayama, Chengdu, China) was used in this study. First, the sample was mounted on the microscope stage with an objective micrometer on a 0.01-mm scale. Subsequently, an image at 10× magnification was acquired using the image measurement VMS35 program. The microscopic imaging aimed to visualize muscle structures not visible to the naked eye and identify the ROI. In Fig. 6(C), the blue box marks the ROI, which indicates the muscle belly.

Synchrotron radiation X-ray micro-computed tomography

We conducted this study using beamline 6 C at the Pohang Light Source-II (PLS, Pohang, Korea) for the SR-µCT. PLS-II accelerates electrons to 3 GeV through a 170-m long linear accelerator and stores them in a 280-m long icositetragon path, generating powerful and highly coherent SR X-rays at the vertices and edges of the path. At beamline 6 C, the SR X-ray was monochromatized before illumination, and X-ray transmission (or projection) images were recorded using an X-ray microscope (Optique Peter, Lentilly, France) equipped with a CMOS camera (pco.edge, PCO AG, Kelheim, Germany). In this study, the monochromatic X-ray photon energy was 34 keV, the X-ray microscope pixel size was 1.6 μm, and the sample-to-detector distance was 100 mm. For computed tomography, 900 projections were obtained at an angular spacing of 0.2°. We used the Octopus software (Inside Matters, Aalst, Belgium) for computed tomographic reconstruction and 3D reconstructed imaging processing. Five flat-field and five dark-field images were obtained for flat-field correction. Median filtration was applied to suppress imaging noise.

3D visualization and analysis for muscle measurement

Figure 6(D) shows the process of parameter analysis of the muscle affected by the stroke. We performed quantitative analysis on approximately 2560 reconstructed slices using the Amira (FEI, Hillsboro, OR) software to measure the degree of muscle decrease. We used a magic wand tool to divide the image based on the surrounding pixels. Prior to annotating the muscle structures, determining the initial seed point and region is crucial. All steps were performed using watershed segmentation, with an interpolation method applied to segment the left muscle fascicles. Muscle fibers and areas of decrease on days with stroke occurrence were used to classify the parameters. Thousands of tomography scans were obtained from each sample during SR-µCT, enabling the quantification of muscle parameter changes in this study.

To measure the muscle structure, which is the local area in the central of the soleus muscle belly, we converted it to (fiber + extracellular space/matrix) / 100 and calculated it as muscle structure / fiber and muscle structure / extracellular space/matrix. A low fiber ratio combined with a high extracellular space/matrix ratio indicated a higher level of damage. We calculated the number of muscle fibers in the entire area of ​​the soleus muscle belly using the pixels of the acquired image, which indicated the change according to the degree of damage. Extracellular space/matrix expansion indicated the degree of damage and was measured by muscle structure – fiber. Muscle areas are critical components to determine the cross-sectional area of ​​the muscle and were calculated using fiber – extracellular space/matrix separation. Muscle structure complexity was measured to analyze the extent to which fibers occupy the total area of ​​the muscle, considering the intricate arrangement of fibers within the muscle. This complexity metric was calculated as the ratio of muscle area to fiber count. A high value was indicative of muscle fibers occupying a significant amount of the total muscle area, suggesting a complex and intricately arranged muscle structure. Furthermore, muscle volume is an important factor in evaluating muscle size and strength and was determined through volume rendering of muscle structures. Fascicle surface, an indicator of muscle structure, was calculated as the number of pixels in the acquired local image. Fascicle thickness was obtained through volume rendering. Pennation angle is one of the commonly used anatomical parameters of skeletal muscle and can reflect the functional status of the muscle and assess the level of damage. We calculated the global pennation angle by measuring the angle between the intersection of the two aponeuroses (deep and superficial) and the fascicles. We measured the local fascicle angle by calculating the angle between the intersection of the two aponeuroses and the fascicle length in segmented fascicles, which allows for assessing the level of damage at specific points.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1.5 software. Sample size for animal studies was determined through power analysis to provide 80% power to detect a difference in the expected effect size and expected variance observed in the test66. To obtain a type 1 error tolerance of 5% with 80% power in this study67, we estimated an effect size of 1.75, which was used to calculate the minimum number of animals required for reduction in the 3R principle68. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY). After the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to verify normality, we performed a parametric test. After dehydration, an independent t-test was performed to analyze muscle weight and assess its microstructural decrease. In addition, we performed a one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post hoc test to evaluate the degree of muscle injury. The correlation coefficients were analyzed using regression analysis to determine the degree of decrease. Both analyses were performed based on the number of days following stroke occurrence. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
