Association between health-related hope and distress from restrictions in chronic kidney disease and dialysis
Authors: Noriaki Kurita, Takafumi Wakita, Shino Fujimoto, Mai Yanagi, Kenichiro Koitabashi, Masahiko Yazawa, Tomo Suzuki, Hiroo Kawarazaki, Yoshitaka Ishibashi, Yugo Shibagaki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn chronic kidney disease (CKD), the durability of patient adherence to fluid and dietary restrictions may depend on the degree to which they have hope that they will enjoy life. Previous cross-sectional studies have shown that higher hope was associated with lower distress from fluid and dietary restrictions and better adherence in the short term. In this study, we aimed to examine the long-term relationship of hope with distress from fluid and dietary restrictions.MethodsThis prospective observational cohort study included 444 patients with CKD undergoing dialysis in one of five Japanese nephrology centers. Hope as a predictor was measured using an 18-item health-related hope scale. Outcomes were two-item measures of distress from fluid and dietary intake restrictions using the Japanese version of the Kidney Disease Quality of Life Short Form, Version 1.3 (higher scores indicate lower levels of distress). Multivariate linear mixed models were used to estimate the association of baseline health-related hope with distress from fluid and dietary restrictions at baseline and follow-up.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 67 years, and 31.1% of them were females. In total, 124, 98, and 222 had non-dialysis CKD, peritoneal dialysis, and hemodialysis, respectively. Higher levels of baseline health-related hope were associated with lower levels of distress from fluid restriction after one year (per 10-point increase, 2.6 points (95% confidence interval, 1.0 to 4.1)); whereas the baseline score was not associated with the distress from fluid restriction at 2 years. Similarly, higher levels of baseline health-related hope were associated with lower levels of distress from dietary restriction after one year (per 10-point increase, 2.0 points (95% confidence interval, 0.3 to 3.6)); whereas the baseline score was not associated with the distress from dietary restriction at 2 years.ConclusionsHealth-related hope, regardless of depression, can potentially mitigate long-term distress from fluid and dietary restrictions in patients with a wide range of CKD severities.Trial registrationUMIN000054710.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-024-03818-1.

Background

Non-adherence to fluid and dietary restrictions in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) is very common, with the respective global prevalence estimates of 60.6% and 60.2% among patients undergoing dialysis [1]. Everyday stress associated with adherence to fluid and dietary restrictions, such as cravings for fluid intake and limitations in social interactions [2, 3], has been continuously identified as the most bothersome psychological distress [4, 5] and contributor to non-adherence [2]. Alleviating psychological distress may be useful in reducing non-adherence. Research on CKD and other chronic diseases has focused on patients’ level of hope as an important inner source of motivation for adherence [6–10]. As indicated in oncology, patient hope is expected to contribute to health-promoting behaviors, as motivation and adherence to medical care are necessary to achieve care goals [11]. However, there is insufficient evidence that hope can contribute to the alleviation of distress associated with CKD.

Patient hope—which can be considered a goal-oriented way of thinking [12, 13]—is focused on health [13, 14]. Increased level of hope is associated with reduced negative effects of kidney disease on daily life, including fluid and dietary restrictions, and better physiological manifestations among patients with CKD [6, 15, 16]. However, a study from the UK involving a small sample of patients on dialysis (n = 103) showed that hope was only associated with a lower overall effect of kidney disease [15]. In contrast, our previous study showed that higher health-related hope (HR-Hope) was associated with less distress related to dietary and fluid restrictions among Japanese CKD patients on dialysis and those with non-dialysis CKD [6]. However, these findings are based on cross-sectional studies; thus, reverse causality—the possibility of hopelessness as a result of distress from the restrictions—cannot be excluded [6, 15]. Furthermore, the influence of depression, which is prevalent among patients with CKD [17], on both patient hope and non-adherence to fluid and dietary restrictions, must be considered [3, 18]. However, these previous studies have failed to address the influences of depression [6, 15]. On the other hand, one intervention study on a small sample of patients on hemodialysis (n = 80) showed that bedside positive thinking training improved interdialytic weight gain and serum phosphorus levels, along with the general hope level [16]. Nevertheless, the effects were only examined for up to 3 months, and they are not relevant for patients on peritoneal dialysis or non-dialysis patients with CKD. As such, quantitative studies on HR-Hope and distress related to adherence among patients with CKD are scarce worldwide. In this context, studies conducted to examine whether HR-Hope has long-term benefits on the effects of fluid and dietary restrictions independent of depression among patients with a wide range of CKD severities are clinically relevant. Specifically, such studies may help patients and clinicians develop behavioral interventions to increase hope and, thereby, promote adherence, especially when considering the paucity of evidence for the long-term benefits of cognitive-behavioral therapies to increase adherence to fluid and dietary restriction [19].

Hence, the aim of this study was to examine the longitudinal association of HR-Hope with distress from fluid restriction and dietary restriction using 2-year follow-up data from a prospective cohort study – the Hope Trajectory and Disease Outcome Consortium (HOTDOC) – for Japanese patients with CKD.

Methods

Study aim, design, and setting

We used data from The HOTDOC study, a multicenter observational cohort study conducted between February 1, 2016 and September 30, 2019 in five outpatient general community hospitals with nephrology services [6], to examine the longitudinal association of HR-Hope with distress from fluid restriction and dietary restriction.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with stage 5D CKD receiving hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis therapy at the participating centers or (2) patients with non-dialysis CKD stages 2–5 receiving nephrology care at the participating centers for dietary instruction, medication prescription, or kidney function monitoring, and (3) patients able to complete the questionnaire survey. Patients with dementia were excluded. Dietary and fluid restrictions were carried out at the discretion of the clinicians at each medical center based on recommendations previously published by the Japanese Society of Nephrology (Additional file 1) [20].

Predictor

The predictor was hope, measured using the validated HR-Hope scale, which assesses the hope related to health among people with chronic illness [14]. This scale was developed by Fukuhara and Kurita et al. in Japanese and translated into English by the authors and bilinguals [14]. This scale consists of 18 items and is unidimensional. Responses to each item were scored on a 4-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 1 (‘I don’t feel that way at all’) to 4 (‘I strongly feel that way’). The average score for the total domain was obtained and converted to a range of 0–100 using the following formula: (average score - minimum possible score [1]) / (maximum possible score [4] - minimum possible score [1]) × 100. Higher scores indicate greater hope related to health. The scale had been demonstrated to have adequate reliability (Cronbach alpha coefficient: 0.93) as well as criterion and construct validity [14].

Outcomes

The main outcomes were distresses from fluid and dietary intake restrictions as proxies for non-adherence to fluid and dietary restrictions, respectively. These two items are included in an 8-item subscale of ‘Effects of Kidney Disease on Daily Life,’ measured using the Japanese version of the Kidney Disease Quality of Life Short Form (KDQOL-SF™), Version 1.3, a psychometrically validated scale among Japanese patients with CKD [21]. This is based on repeated observations that they are the most bothersome stressors associated with adherence to fluid and dietary restrictions [2, 4, 5]. These items were chosen as outcomes because they directly measure distress due to fluid and dietary restrictions; additionally, Japanese versions of other kidney disease-specific health-related QOL instruments are unavailable. The original (US - English) version of KDQOL-SF™ was developed by Hays et al. [22] The Japanese version was made by performing forward and backward translation followed by cultural adaptation [21].

The items ‘Fluid restriction?’ and ‘Dietary restriction?’ were evaluated by the question ‘How much does kidney disease bother you in each of the following areas?’ The patients chose responses from a scale that ranged from 1 (not at all bothered) to 5 (extremely bothered).

According to the scoring manual, the chosen raw scores for the items were converted to a range of 0–100, with higher scores reflecting lower levels of distress [22]. The two items were treated individually with separate scores in the present study. The reliability of the ‘Effects of Kidney Disease on Daily Life’ domain in the Japanese version was shown to be adequate (Cronbach alpha coefficient: 0.79), and the convergent and discriminant construct validity of the scale were also examined [21].

Data of these items were collected at baseline, after 1 year, and after 2 years.

Covariates

Data regarding age, sex, presence of diabetic nephropathy, stage of kidney disease, treatment status (non-dialysis, peritoneal dialysis, hemodialysis), presence of comorbidities (coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, and malignancy), serum potassium level, serum phosphorus level, systolic blood pressure, potassium binder, number of phosphorus binders, and number of anti-hypertensive classes were collected from the patients’ medical records.

The stage of CKD was determined by the estimated glomerular filtration rate; the dialysis vintage was also evaluated. Performance status was assessed by attending physicians using the Zubrod scale [23]. Poor performance status was defined as a score of 2 (walking more than 50% of the awake time or receiving occasional assistance when moving) or higher. The presence of family was evaluated by a yes or no response to the question ‘Do you have any family?’ on the HR-Hope scale [14]. Working status was used as a proxy for economic status and measured by a yes or no response to the question ‘During the past 4 weeks, did you work at a paying job?’ in the KDQOL [21]. Depression was assessed using the Japanese version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) [24, 25]. The CES-D consists of 20 items scored using a 4-point scale. The total score ranges from 0 to 60, with a total score of 16 or higher indicating depression [17, 24].

Procedure for the questionnaire survey

The questionnaire was administered at each facility, and patients were asked to complete it at home or during their nephrology visit. If patients could not write due to visual impairment or physical disability, they were asked to verbally complete the form with the aid of a trained research assistant who did not inform the patients of the study hypothesis. The questionnaire used in this study has been published elsewhere [14], except for the Japanese version of the KDQOL-SF™, Version 1.3.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata/SE, version 17 (StataCorp LLC). For baseline descriptive statistics, continuous variables are summarized as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, and categorical variables are summarized as frequency and percentage. They are presented for the total population and by treatment status. The distribution of HR-Hope scores was illustrated in a histogram. A Sankey diagram was created to provide a graphical overview of the distribution of values for fluid and dietary restriction-related distress scores, as well as the frequency of their combination [26].

Linear mixed models were fitted to examine the association between baseline outcome scores and HR-Hope scores and whether changes in those scores over time differed by the HR-Hope score. The models were fitted for each fluid restriction and dietary restriction item. Robust variance estimation was used to address heteroscedasticity. The aforementioned covariates described and depression were forced into the models, including the stage of kidney disease and renal replacement therapy. The serum potassium level, serum phosphorus level, potassium binder, and the number of phosphorus binders were included only in the model for dietary restriction, while the systolic blood pressure and the number of anti-hypertensive medication classes were included only in the model for fluid restriction. Stage of kidney disease and renal replacement therapy status were combined into one variable, consisting of six CKD levels: stage 2 or 3, stage 4 or 5, peritoneal dialysis within 1 year, peritoneal dialysis for > 1 year, hemodialysis within 1 year, and hemodialysis for > 1 year. The Wald test was used to examine whether temporal changes in the outcome scores differed from that in the HR-Hope scores. Adjusted predicted values of the outcome scores were calculated to illustrate temporal changes in the scores. The predicted scores represented those as if the total population had the same time points and HR-Hope scores, while the distribution of covariates for the total population remained unchanged [27]. The time points were baseline, after 1 year, and after 2 years, while the representative HR-Hope scores were 20, 60, and 100 points, which were close to the 1%, 50%, and 99% tiles, respectively.

Multiple imputation with chained equations was performed for any covariate with missing values, assuming that the missing values were at random [28]. Estimates from 10 imputed data were combined into a single estimate.

Results

Baseline characteristics

After excluding 17 cases with missing values for either baseline HR-Hope (n = 3) or fluid/dietary restriction (n = 15), 444 participants were included in the analysis. The baseline characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 1. In total, 98 patients received peritoneal dialysis, whereas 222 patients received hemodialysis. Among 124 patients who did not undergo dialysis, 7, 46, 52, and 19 had CKD stages 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively. The mean HR-Hope, fluid restriction, and dietary restriction-related distress scores were 61, 76.4, and 74.4, respectively. HR-Hope scores were widely distributed, ranging from 5.6 points to 100 points (Fig. 1, top left). A Sankey diagram revealed that most patients rated the fluid and dietary restriction equally, while some rated the fluid restriction as not bothersome but the dietary restriction as bothersome, and vice versa (Fig. 2).

Associations of fluid restriction score with HR-Hope, timeline, and covariates

Table 2 shows the association of baseline HR-Hope, timeline, and covariates with fluid restriction-related distress score. HR-Hope modified temporal changes in fluid restriction-related distress score (P < 0.001 for interaction according to the Wald test). Discussion

In the present study, we investigated whether HR-Hope plays a role in the effects of fluid and dietary restrictions on day-to-day life among patients with CKD. Our results showed that increased HR-Hope mitigated the decline of both fluid and dietary restriction-related distress scores after 1 year, independent of depressive symptoms.

Our study participants included hemodialysis patients, peritoneal dialysis patients, and non-dialysis patients with varying CKD stages. The reason for, on average, lower hope measured via HR-Hope and lower effect score from fluid restriction (i.e., greater distress) among hemodialysis patients than that among non-dialysis patients with CKD may reflect difficulties in coping with fluid retention due to impaired residual renal function and craving for fluid intake. In addition, the reason for the low effect score from dietary restriction (i.e., greater distress) among peritoneal dialysis patients may reflect difficulties in adhering to more stringent dietary phosphorus restriction due to the inefficient removal of phosphorus by peritoneal dialysis. Notably, the proportion of peritoneal dialysis patients with number of phosphate binder ≥ 7 was higher than those of other groups. However, the long-term effects of hope on distress associated with those restrictions, as discussed below, were statistically adjusted for such differences in characteristics associated with differences in the severity of kidney disease.

Our findings are an extension of those of previous observational studies of hope in patients with CKD. A study showing high general hope and reduced effect of kidney disease on the daily life of patients undergoing dialysis and another showing high HR-Hope and reduced effect of fluid and dietary restrictions on patients with a wide range of CKD severities had cross-sectional study designs [6, 15]. However, the study from the UK did not examine the specific effects of kidney disease, including distress from dietary and fluid restrictions [15]. Our previous study was the only one in Japan to examine HR-Hope and distress from dietary and fluid restrictions [6]. However, its cross-sectional design could have resulted in the possibility of low HR-Hope owing to the distress associated with the restrictions and confounding by depression on the relationship between hope and the low level of distress [6, 15]. In contrast, our present study was able to demonstrate a temporal relationship between higher HR-Hope with less distress after one year independently of depression. Theoretically, high hope in patients with CKD may lead to recognition of the goal and importance of treatment, thus contributing to high motivation and adherence to treatment to achieve the goal. Supporting this notion, a link between having high hopes and health-promoting behavior, such as eating a healthy diet, has been noted in the general population [29, 30]. We speculate that those who recognize their goals via one of the HR-Hope items, i.e., ‘I think I’ll still be able to continue doing enjoyable things in the future,’ may feel more proactive toward fluid restriction and dietary phosphorus restriction and less distressed as a result of realizing another item, i.e., ‘I can probably develop a personal lifestyle strategy for dealing with my disease condition.’ [14] However, mitigation of decline in restriction-related distress score by HR-Hope was not observed at 2 years. Whether this is due to patient accommodation of the restrictions, response shift (i.e., change in the meaning of one’s self-evaluation of the distress because of a redefinition of the distress), or lack of household income and education level measurements is unclear.

The findings of this study have implications for researchers and clinicians in several aspects. First, the fluid and dietary restrictions-related distress scores are potentially modifiable factors and could be increased by enhancing hope through psychological interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy. According to a study showing that the minimal important difference empirically corresponds to a 0.5 × SD in general [31], a 0.5 × SD corresponds to the magnitude of 0.5 on standardized effect size (ES; e.g., 14.9 point-difference in fluid restriction-related distress score) in this study. Thus, given that a patient with extreme hopelessness (HR-Hope score of 6 points) would have a 0.47-point lower standardized ES than that of an average (HR-Hope score of 60 points) patient (i.e., − 0.09 standardized ES × 54 points), we believe this scenario would qualify as a minimally clinically important difference. Based on our study’s findings, we propose cognitive behavioral therapy that incorporates the identification of HR-Hope inherent in patients with CKD, as well as the specific healthy behaviors required to achieve it, formulating plans to implement those behaviors, and providing counsel on their barriers and concerns. Second, this study raises the need for clinicians to inquire about whether HR-Hope is compromised even in the absence of depression during their routine dialogue with patients with kidney disease. Although hopelessness is considered part of depression, it can be quite common in the absence of depression [32, 33]; it may not be recognized as a symptom of depression but rather only as a risk factor for depression [34].

The strength of our study is that we were able to examine a 2-year long-term effect of hope on the distress from fluid and dietary restrictions in a wide range of CKD severities, with an adjustment for depression. In addition, the multicenter design of our study ensures the generalizability of our findings.

Some limitations of this study warrant a mention. First, the outcome of this study was solely based on self-reported distress from fluid and dietary restrictions as a surrogate indicator of adherence. We could not measure fluid intake by volume or dietary-derived phosphorus intake. Additionally, there are no Japanese versions of the questionnaire to measure dietary and fluid adherence with validity. Second, a modest percentage of patients were lost to follow-up because of referral to other facilities, death, or other unknown reasons. Nonetheless, the mean baseline HR-Hope score for the 2-year study population was similar to that of the total population. However, the declining mean baseline distress scores among those for the 2-year study population may suggest a selection bias, particularly because of non-dialyzed patients with CKD being lost to follow-up. Third, our findings were derived from a single ethnicity and cultural background using the HR-Hope scale and have not been validated in other countries; this may limit its generalizability. Fourth, unmeasured household income, education levels, and psychiatric diagnoses (e.g., major depressive disorder, or generalized anxiety disorder) may have confounded the association between HR-Hope and distress from restrictions.

Conclusion

Our study showed that HR-Hope is associated with mitigation of worsening distress from fluid and dietary restrictions over 1 year among patients with a wide range of CKD severities. This association should be further tested in additional patient populations before the impact of cognitive-behavioral therapy on outcomes can be investigated.
