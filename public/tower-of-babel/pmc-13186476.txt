Safety and major adverse events of endobronchial ultrasound performed under conscious sedation: Insights from 3,454 procedures over 14 years and a systematic review of literature
Authors: Babu Ram, Valliappan Muthu, Inderpaul S. Sehgal, Sahajal Dhooria, Kuruswamy T. Prasad, Ashutosh N. Aggarwal, Ritesh Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) is widely used for evaluating mediastinal lymphadenopathy. However, its safety profile requires comprehensive evaluation. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of complications associated with EBUS-TBNA and to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate the pooled complication rates.Methods:We retrospectively analysed a prospectively maintained database of patients who underwent EBUS between April 2011 and April 2025. We extracted demographic, procedural, and complication-related data. Major complications were defined as events necessitating endotracheal intubation, cardiopulmonary arrest, hospitalization ≥24 h, mediastinitis, significant bleeding requiring intervention, pneumothorax unrelated to transbronchial biopsy, or death. A systematic review and meta-analysis were performed using the Der Simonian and Laird random-effects model to determine pooled complication rates from studies with ≥1000 EBUS procedures.Results:Of the 3,454 patients included 77 (2.2%) experienced complications, with 10 (0.3%) classified as major. Major events included severe bleeding (n = 4), respiratory arrest (n = 2), mediastinal cyst rupture, mediastinitis, temporomandibular joint dislocation, and one death. The meta-analysis included 27,560 patients from seven studies and yielded a pooled complication rate of 1.21%. The pooled complication rates from our meta-analysis were 26.2 for major bleeding, 9.6 for mediastinitis, 5.5 for pneumothorax, and 1.52 for procedure-related mortality per 10,000 EBUS procedures.Conclusions:EBUS-TBNA is a safe diagnostic procedure with a rare incidence of major complications. Bleeding, infectious complications, and respiratory events constitute the most significant risks.

INTRODUCTION

Convex probe endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) has transformed the diagnostic approach to mediastinal lymphadenopathy and is a cornerstone of modern interventional pulmonology.[1234] EBUS-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) is minimally invasive and safer than surgical mediastinal sampling. Nevertheless, real-world data on its safety remain limited, as clinical trials often exclude high-risk populations, potentially underestimating complication rates.[456] Procedural variability, ranging from conscious sedation to general anaesthesia, with or without endotracheal intubation, may further influence outcomes.[7] Thus, complication rates will likely differ across countries or even centres.[89101112] Additionally, some centres employ adjunctive techniques such as mediastinal cryobiopsies, which may elevate complication risks.[13] Thus, thorough documentation of the safety of EBUS-TBNA needs to be done. At our centre, we have been performing EBUS since 2011 under conscious sedation and systematically recording data.[1415] The study’s objective was to determine the prevalence of complications (major and minor) associated with EBUS. We also conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to derive a pooled estimate of complication rates from large (≥1000 participants) studies.

METHODS

Study design and participants

We conducted a retrospective analysis of our institution’s prospectively maintained bronchoscopy database, including all patients, who underwent EBUS or endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration using the EBUS scope (EUS-B-FNA) between April 2011 and April 2025. The Institutional Ethics Committee approved the study. A waiver of informed consent was granted due to the retrospective nature and anonymized data collection. We have published some of the data of the participants enrolled in the current analysis in earlier studies.[4561617] We report the results according to the STROBE guidelines.[18]

We included all patients undergoing EBUS or EUS-B-FNA using a convex probe endobronchial ultrasound scope. Cases with incomplete documentation of procedural complications were excluded.

Procedural details

We performed EBUS as a day-care procedure. Experienced bronchoscopy consultants or fellows (under direct supervision) performed the procedures under conscious sedation (midazolam, fentanyl, or dexmedetomidine) as described in earlier studies.[1719] We performed all procedures using EBUS scopes (BF-UC180F or BF-UC190F with EU-ME2 processor, Olympus Medical, Japan) transorally, as described earlier. We employed dedicated EBUS-TBNA needles of varying sizes (ViziShot needles 21 or 22G [NA-201SX-4022 and NA-201SX-4021, Olympus Medical, Japan; OTU EBUS 22-O, Omnitip, India], ViziShot FLEX 19G needle (NA-U403SX, Olympus Medical, Japan), EchoTip ProCore 22 G needle, [ECHO-HD-22-EBUS-P, Cook Medical, United States], or 19 G eXcelon core needle [Boston Scientific, United States]), and occasionally reused sterilized needles.[6161920] We used local anaesthesia, with 1% lignocaine sprayed on the vocal cords, the trachea, and bronchi during the procedure. Three lymph node stations were typically sampled (≥2 passes per node). In cases with fewer accessible stations, we ensured a total of 6–8 passes. Suction using a 10–20 mL VacLok™ syringe was routinely applied. Nevertheless, the operator determined the suction used during the procedure, lymph node stations accessed, and the number of passes, jabs, and other technical aspects. Since 2017, pulmonologist-guided rapid on-site evaluation (P-ROSE) via real-time cytological review (WhatsApp-based off-site evaluation) has been integrated into most procedures.[21] For suspected sarcoidosis, EBUS-TBNA was followed by endobronchial and transbronchial biopsies (EBB and TBB) as needed.[4] Coagulation profiles and blood counts were ordered only for patients scheduled for TBB or considered at high-risk for bleeding (anticoagulant therapy, chronic liver disease, and bleeding diathesis).

Data collection and reporting

We extracted the following information from our database: (1) age, sex, smoking status, (2) indications and details of the procedure performed (EBUS with or without TBNA), (3) any additional procedure performed (bronchoalveolar lavage [BAL], EBB, and TBB), (4) drugs and doses of anaesthetic agent used for conscious sedation, (5) procedural aspects of EBUS-TBNA (needle size, new or reused needle, number of jabs during puncture, procedure duration, sonographic appearance of target lesions [presence of necrosis and others]), (6) pre-, intra-, and post-procedure heart rate and oxygen saturation, (7) Ramsay sedation scale score, visual analogy scale assessment of secretion and cough by the operator (whenever available), (8) details of the complications and its outcome.

We categorized the complications as present or absent. If present, we categorized them as major or minor. Major complications included: (1) endotracheal intubation (due to any cause); (2) respiratory or cardiac arrest, (3) hospitalization or intensive care unit admission ≥24h; (4) mediastinitis; (5) major bleeding event requiring escalation of care (beyond instillation of local adrenaline, ice-cold saline, tamponade to control the bleeding) such as blood transfusion, additional procedures and others; (6) death within 48 hours of the procedure; (7) pneumothorax; and (8) others. We recorded the following as minor complications: (1) mild bleeding, (2) oversedation, (3) transient hypoxemia, and others. EBB or TBB, when required, were performed after EBUS TBNA. For the current study, we recorded complications that occurred during or after EBUS-TBNA, as well as those that were not attributable to additional procedures (e.g., pneumothorax developing after TBB or bleeding during EBB). Instances of needle malfunction or scope damage were recorded separately.

Statistical analysis

We analysed data using Rstudio (R version 4.3.1).[2223] We present categorical variables as numbers (percentages) and continuous variables as medians (1st–3rd quartile). The between-group comparison was performed using the Kruskal-Walli’s rank sum test (for continuous variables) and Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test (for categorical variables).

Systematic review and meta-analysis

We also performed a systematic review of the published literature using the following terms in PubMed and Embase: (‘ebus’ OR ‘ebus-tbna’ OR ‘tbna’ OR ‘endobronchial ultrasound’) AND complications [title or abstract]. The results are reported according to the Meta-analysis Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidelines.[24] We included studies involving at least 1000 participants undergoing EBUS and providing sufficient data to calculate prevalence estimates of complications. Our objective was to ascertain the pooled proportion of complications, including death, mediastinitis, and others. We excluded review articles, editorials, non-English literature, systematic reviews, case series, or studies reporting complication details in <1000 subjects. We extracted the following information: (1) details of publication, (2) study design, (3) number of participants with any complications, (4) technical details, anaesthesia used, (5) definitions of various complications, (6) details of complications such as death, bleeding and others. We report on the quality of the studies included in the meta-analysis using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for prevalence studies. The checklist contains nine questions, and the answers can be ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unclear’ or ‘not applicable’. Each study was categorized as having either low (7–9), moderate (4–6) or high risk (0–3) of bias depending on the number of affirmative responses. We used the DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model (StatsDirect for Windows, Version 4.0.4) to estimate the pooled proportion of complications. We used Cochran’s Q statistic and the I² statistic (a value of >50% was considered significant) to assess study heterogeneity. Publication bias was assessed using Begg-Mazumdar, Egger’s, and Harbord’s tests.

RESULTS

We enrolled 3,454 participants undergoing EBUS, of which 77 (2.2%) experienced complications during or after the procedure. Major complications were uncommon (0.3%; 10/3454). Most procedures were EBUS-TBNA (>98%), while EUS-B-FNA was performed in around 1.1% of patients. EBUS-TBNA was performed under conscious sedation among all participants [Table 1]. We used a 22G needle for TBNA in more than two-thirds of the subjects. Ten percent (347/3,454) were performed with sterilized and reused needles. We found no difference in any technical aspect of the procedure and the final diagnostic categories among the two study groups [Table 1]. No significant differences were observed in procedural techniques between those with and without complications. Sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, or metastatic malignancies were the most frequent final diagnoses and were not different in those with and without complications. The median oxygen saturation was significantly lower in individuals with complications compared to those without.

We found that most complications (67/77, 87%) were minor, and the remaining were major [Table 2]. Severe bleeding was the most common major complication and required endotracheal intubation in three patients, cardiac arrest in one, and cryoextraction of the clot in one. Two patients were extubated within 30 minutes of intubation and remained stable thereafter. The third patient, a 49-year-old female, required intensive care unit (ICU) admission and, unfortunately, died 72 hours later. Her clinical course was complicated by metabolic acidosis and probable cardiogenic shock. She had a history of poorly controlled diabetes mellitus (HbA1c 10%) and hypertension and was treated for tuberculosis based on a positive GeneXpert result for Mycobacterium tuberculosis from the EBUS-TBNA sample. Two elderly participants (75-year-old men) developed respiratory arrest, and the procedure was terminated at 8 and 25 minutes of EBUS-TBNA, respectively. One participant recovered after a few hours and did not require hospitalization. The other participant required endotracheal intubation, ICU admission, and was discharged after 36 hours.

Details of complications encountered during EBUS

Mediastinitis and mediastinal cyst rupture (required surgical management) occurred in one participant each. In the patient with mediastinitis, EBUS-TBNA showed necrosis on cytology; AFB smear, Xpert MTB/Rif and mycobacterial cultures were all negative. The patient was initiated on antituberculosis therapy empirically. Twenty-one days following the procedure, the patient developed fever, chest discomfort, and orthopnoea, requiring admission to the emergency department and subsequently endotracheal intubation. Following ICU admission, the patient was found to have mediastinal widening on a chest radiograph, while a chest CT scan revealed an increase in the size of the mediastinal lymph nodes, with areas of low attenuation and air foci within. He was treated with intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics, antituberculosis therapy, and surgery, and was finally discharged after 3 months. Temporomandibular joint dislocation occurred in one participant that required correction by a dentist. Transient hypoxemia and mild bleeding were the most frequent minor events.

Sterilized and reused needles were not associated with a higher prevalence of minor or major complications, including mediastinitis or infectious complications [Table S1]. During the study period, there were four instances of TBNA needle malfunction, including three cases of needle bending and one case of needle breakage. Additionally, the EBUS bronchoscope required repair four times over 14 years. None of these equipment-related issues led to patient harm or procedure termination.

Systematic review and meta-analysis

We included seven studies (including the current data) reporting on 27,560 participants undergoing EBUS (diagnostic or staging EBUS). All were retrospective studies with a median (1st–3rd quartile) JBI score of 7 (5–7). The pooled proportion of any complication (major or minor, as defined in the study, Figure 1) was 121 per 10000 procedures (95% CI, 44–237 per 10000). We observed substantial heterogeneity across the included studies (Cochran’s Q statistic was 315.98, P < 0.0001, and between-study variance [τ²] was 0.01377). The I² statistic was 98.1% (95% CI: 97.6–98.5), suggesting a high degree of inconsistency among the study results. We found evidence of significant publication bias on Begg-Mazumdar test (Kendall’s tau of 0.714286, P = 0.0302) and Egger’s regression test (estimate of 10.29, P = 0.007). However, Harbord’s test (estimate 11.80, P = 0.44) did not show any evidence of bias. Death was uncommon [Table 3], with a pooled proportion of 1.52 per 10,000 EBUS procedures. DISCUSSION

Our large real-world cohort confirms that EBUS-TBNA is a safe procedure, with major complications occurring in only 0.3% of patients. Severe bleeding was the most frequent serious event, and death was uncommon (0.03%). Further, in our systematic review, the pooled proportion of major bleeding, mediastinitis, pneumothorax, and death was 26.2, 9.6, 5.5, and 1.52 per 10,000 procedures. EBUS-TBNA is a safe procedure, and serious complications are uncommon. The available expert statements and recommendations for EBUS-TBNA primarily focus on the technical aspects of the procedure, the training required, and strategies to optimize the diagnostic yield.[2526] Unfortunately, there is limited guidance for clinicians on how to minimize complications.

The overall complication rates during EBUS-TBNA reported in the literature vary from 0.16–5%.[1112] The frequency of complications depends on several factors, including patient selection, anaesthesia, presence of comorbidities (such as bleeding tendencies, chronic kidney or heart disease), and the different definitions used to define a complication.[91127] To account for these differences, we pooled the relevant individual complications (death, bleeding, and others, Table 4) in addition to the overall complication rate. Bleeding is the most dreaded complication, and operators should exercise caution in those at risk. Antiplatelet and anticoagulant medications may increase the risk of bleeding. While guidelines and studies suggest it is safe to continue antiplatelet therapy in patients undergoing EBUS-TBNA, one might still encounter significant bleeding.[28] Careful patient selection, better sedation, minimizing the number of passes (e.g., use of ROSE), anticipating a bleed, and preparedness to secure the airway or control bleed (tamponade using Fogarty balloon or Arndt blocker) can improve the safety of subjects undergoing EBUS-TBNA on antiplatelet drugs. Discontinuation of antiplatelets carries risks, and studies report cerebral infarction following their withdrawal.[9] The Indian guidelines on EBUS-TBNA suggest that aspirin may be safely continued and that clopidogrel may be used if the benefit outweighs the risk.[29] High-risk procedures must be conducted only by trained personnel in institutions well-equipped to handle bleeding emergencies.

Several centres (nearly 70% in some countries like India) routinely perform EBUS under mild-to-moderate conscious sedation, and the complication rates could differ from procedures performed under general anaesthesia.[30] At our centre, the complication rates of EBUS-TBNA performed under conscious sedation were low. While randomized trials suggest that conscious sedation is as safe as general anaesthesia,[3132] the studies are limited by small sample size compared to real-world data. Prolonged sedation, especially in older people, may lead to prolonged hospitalization, respiratory failure, or intubation. Notably, our study’s subjects with major complications were older than those without. Monitoring depth of sedation using tools such as bispectrality index has been proposed to reduce complications but requires more data during bronchoscopy and EBUS.[3334] While midazolam has traditionally been used for conscious sedation, dexmedetomidine has been recently found to be safe and allows for faster post-procedure recovery.[17]

Infectious complications of EBUS-TBNA, particularly mediastinitis, are rare but serious. A higher risk of infectious complications has been reported when EBUS is performed for mediastinal cysts or when the target lesion is necrotic.[113536] Sterilized and reused needles for TBNA could lead to an increase in infection rate.[3738] We found no increase in major complications associated with the use of sterilized needles [Table S1].[19] Nevertheless, strict asepsis, avoiding therapeutic aspiration of mediastinal cysts, and performing EUS-B-FNA after EBUS (to avoid contamination from the esophagus) are essential measures to prevent infectious complications. While some centres use prophylactic antibiotics before mediastinal cyst aspiration, there is no data to support this practice.[35]

The complications of EBUS-TBNA reported in registries and randomized trials, especially in suspected sarcoidosis, are attributable to TBB.[27] Pneumothorax is more often a complication of TBB performed along with EBUS-TBNA, especially in patients with suspected sarcoidosis. The risk of pneumothorax following EBUS-TBNA alone is low,[39] and the rates reported in our study likely represent the real-world scenario (after excluding TBB).

Our study has a few limitations. It is a single-centre, retrospective analysis with limited post-procedure follow-up. A few minor complications, such as oversedation or some delayed complications, could have been missed. Nevertheless, we recorded complications that were serious, required prolonged hospitalization, or additional interventions. While we maintained a prospective record of complications, we did not actively monitor them after patients were discharged from the hospital. Thus, the delayed complications were self-reported, and some might have been missed, mainly when managed at a different medical facility. Data on antiplatelet use was available only for bleeding cases. Finally, our findings primarily reflect outcomes from procedures performed under conscious sedation and may not generalize to centres using general anaesthesia.

EBUS-TBNA is a safe diagnostic modality, with major complications occurring in <0.5% of procedures. The most common complications reported in the literature include bleeding, infectious complications, and respiratory failure. Clinicians should remain vigilant in high-risk populations and adopt procedural safeguards to minimize further complications.
