Radar-assisted wireless sensor communications using frequency-modulated reconfigurable intelligent surfaces
Authors: Farid Morabet, Marc Lazaro, Ramon Villarino, David Girbau, Antonio Lazaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShort-range continuous-wave (CW) radar sensing is increasingly adopted in Internet of Things (IoT) applications including localization, gesture recognition, and environmental monitoring across a wide range of indoor and industrial environments, motivating compact architectures that integrate perception with embedded data delivery. This paper extends this functionality to low-data-rate IoT communications by introducing a radar-assisted reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS) sensing framework based on frequency-coded backscatter, in which the RIS acts as a sensing node. A 24-GHz CW radar illuminates a compact programmable RIS tag whose effective radar cross section (RCS) is periodically modulated to embed data into the backscattered signal. The RIS is composed of four unit cells, each loaded with PIN diodes that act as switching elements. Modulation is achieved by controlling the biasing of the diodes. Data is encoded through discrete RIS modulation frequencies and recovered from symmetric radar baseband sidebands, shifting information away from DC clutter and providing robustness to common Doppler-induced frequency offsets. The sensing node does not perform any active RF transmission and relies solely on the low-frequency electronic control of the RIS. Indoor experiments demonstrate real-time sensing with a symbol duration of 250 ms, error-free frame reception for static or moving tags up to 10 m and 4 m, respectively, and an average packet error rate (PER) below 0.1% during prolonged operation.Subject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Millimeter-wave radar sensors are increasingly used for short-range perception in indoor, industrial, and embedded sensing contexts1–3, motivating growing interest in integrated sensing-and-communication (ISAC) architectures that share spectrum and hardware resources between radar and wireless links4,5. In parallel, practical IoT sensing deployments demand compact nodes with low power consumption and minimal hardware complexity6. Typical sensing applications such as environmental monitoring, smart infrastructure, and industrial automation often require distributed nodes placed in locations where continuous wired power supply is impractical, while batteries can increase device size, maintenance costs, and operational complexity7–9.

These constraints motivate telemetry mechanisms that reuse existing illumination infrastructure while avoiding RF carrier generation at the sensing node10–12. Backscatter techniques address this requirement by modulating the reflection of an incident electromagnetic field rather than transmitting locally generated RF, enabling battery-free operation in RFID and related systems7,13,14. However, conventional RFID and backscatter systems typically rely on communication-oriented readers and protocol-centric processing, including inventory and medium-access procedures, for symbol demodulation and packet handling15,16. Such protocol stacks are optimized for identification and networking, but they introduce additional complexity and overhead when the primary objective is low-rate sensing15,16.

In contrast, radar receivers provide coherent baseband measurements that can be directly exploited for data extraction via spectral analysis, enabling sensing-oriented operation without invoking conventional RFID backscatter protocol stacks17,18. In the field of radar, recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of backscatter data links using automotive frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) radars combined with passive or low-power tags, which typically require chirp-synchronous operation and waveform-dependent signal processing17–19. In these approaches, data decoding is closely tied to the FMCW chirp structure and timing17,18. Separately, advances in time-modulated and space–time metasurfaces have shown that electronically controlled scattering can generate engineered frequency-shifted components and Doppler-like effects, enabling programmable spectral manipulation without mechanical motion20,21. Reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RISs) extend these concepts by providing compact, electronically programmable scattering responses and have been extensively studied for propagation shaping in wireless communications and ISAC scenarios22–24. Nevertheless, most literature on RIS treats the surface primarily as an environmental element for channel control rather than as an autonomous sensing node that embeds data directly into radar returns23–25.

This work departs from prior approaches by treating the RIS itself as a backscatter device while taking advantage of CW radar operation. A radar-assisted RIS framework is presented in which information depends on the low-frequency electronic modulation of a compact RIS tag, without the need to modify the radar waveform or receiver. Data is encoded in the spacing between the positive and negative spectral components, yielding a frequency-coded representation that shifts information away from the clutter-dominated direct-current region and provides inherent robustness to common Doppler-induced offsets. A compact PIN-diode RIS driven by a low-power microcontroller (MC) is implemented and experimentally evaluated at 24 GHz, forming a small 2 × 2 unit-cell structure with an overall footprint of only 22.6 mm × 15 mm suitable for integration into compact IoT sensing nodes. The system is characterized by performing angular and range measurements, as well as packet error rate (PER) measurements under stationary and dynamic conditions, simultaneously demonstrating real-time detection of an integrated environmental sensor. This allows for the validation of radar-assisted data communication without RF transmission at the sensing node. Unlike radar-backscatter systems that depend on the FMCW waveform structure for decoding, the proposed method requires only CW illumination and standard radar baseband spectral analysis, simplifying both the tag architecture and the receiver-side processing while enabling robust short-range IoT sensing and the use of simple CW radars as readers.

Related work

Radar-assisted backscatter communication has recently attracted attention as a technique for enabling low-power wireless telemetry by reusing radar illumination as the RF carrier. In these systems, a tag modulates the reflected radar signal, allowing the radar receiver to extract information directly from the reflected signal without requiring a conventional RF transmitter at the sensing node. One of the earliest demonstrations of radar-readable telemetry was presented by Cnaan-On et al.26, who proposed a distributed sensing architecture in which semi-passive nodes communicate with a 2.45 GHz FMCW radar by switching the load impedance of an antenna. The impedance switching modulates the backscattered signal and creates a communication signal that appears as a range-dependent subcarrier after the radar dechirp operation. Their prototype demonstrated the feasibility of recovering sensor information while simultaneously estimating the range of multiple nodes using the radar processing chain. However, the communication mechanism relies on an FMCW radar waveform and requires the extraction of subcarriers generated by the dechirp operation. Radar-assisted communication has also been investigated using automotive radar sensors. Lázaro et al.19 demonstrated that modulated reflective structures can be detected using commercial 24 GHz FMCW automotive radars. Their system employs a transponder architecture composed of receiving and transmitting antennas connected through an amplifier. The modulation signal periodically switches the power supply of the amplifier, producing characteristic peaks in the radar range–Doppler map that allow the radar to detect the communication signal. While this approach enables communication using automotive radar hardware, the tag requires active RF circuitry and relies on FMCW radar signal processing for data extraction. In related work, the authors proposed a radar-readable backscatter transponder based on a frequency-selective surface composed of dipole elements loaded with PIN diodes27. In this design, periodic switching of the diodes modulates the effective reflection coefficient of the surface, which in turn modulates the radar cross section of the tag. The resulting time-varying reflection produces spectral sidebands in the radar receiver that allow the tag to be detected. However, the system mainly employs a fixed modulation frequency for tag detection and ranging rather than continuous data telemetry. More recently, An et al.17 proposed a millimeter-wave radar backscatter communication system operating in the 75–78 GHz band. Their architecture integrates an active RF tag that receives the incident radar signal and modulates it using baseband IQ signals, enabling high-order modulation schemes such as QPSK and QAM. This design allows communication to be embedded within the radar sensing framework while preserving the radar’s ranging capability. At the same time, the approach requires active RF circuitry at the tag and depends on the FMCW radar waveform for signal processing and decoding. Another recent example is the BiScatter system introduced by Okubo et al.18, which integrates radar sensing and backscatter communication using commodity FMCW radar platforms and low-power IoT tags. In this system, chirp-slope-shift keying (CSSK) encodes information into the radar waveform by varying the slope of FMCW chirps, enabling downlink communication from the radar to the tag. For the uplink transmission, the tag employs a Van Atta antenna array with an RF switch that modulates the retro-reflected radar signal. This architecture enables bidirectional communication between the radar and backscatter tags while preserving the radar sensing functionality. Although these works demonstrate the feasibility of combining radar sensing with backscatter communication, most existing systems rely on FMCW radar architectures and often require either active RF circuitry at the tag or waveform-dependent signal processing. These characteristics increase system complexity and tightly couple the communication mechanism to the radar waveform. Furthermore, several implementations employ relatively large reflective structures or active transponder architectures, which may limit their integration into compact sensing nodes. In contrast, the approach proposed in this work explores radar-assisted sensor telemetry using a compact RIS tag illuminated by a CW radar, enabling information extraction directly from spectral components in the radar baseband signal without modifying the radar waveform or requiring active RF transmission at the sensing node (Table 1).

System architecture

The proposed system integrates a 24-GHz CW radar with a RIS tag, enabling frequency-coded backscatter for short-range sensor readout. The radar illuminates the RIS tag, while a low-power MC unit drives the PIN-diode states of the RIS at discrete modulation frequencies selected according to the data, thereby producing a time-varying effective RCS. From the radar’s perspective, this controlled scattering introduces distinct spectral components in the backscattered signal that are exploited for information encoding. Since the RIS tag does not generate an RF carrier and relies solely on low-frequency electronic modulation, the active power consumption is dominated by the control circuitry rather than the RF transmission. Sensor data are integrated into spectral tones observable by the radar and retrieved through baseband processing by the radar itself. The proposed modulation-based architecture may also support multiple RIS nodes operating simultaneously under a single CW radar, provided that distinct modulation frequencies are assigned to each node. This principle is consistent with our previous RIS-assisted radar implementation3, where multiple modulated panels operating at different modulation frequencies were successfully separated in the radar spectrum. In such cases, the radar can spectrally separate the corresponding sidebands in the baseband signal. However, if multiple RIS nodes share the same modulation frequency, their sideband responses overlap, preventing unambiguous node separation without additional multiple-access or coding mechanisms.

Each transmitted frame follows a START–PAYLOAD–END structure. A START symbol provides synchronization, followed by D payload symbols representing the sensing data, and an END symbol. The resulting frame duration is

The symbol duration is primarily selected to achieve sufficient spectral separation, prioritizing decoding robustness over throughput, in order to ensure adequate FFT frequency resolution and low-frequency noise separation that is consistent with short-range detection requirements.

At the receiver, windowed FFT-based spectral analysis is applied over each symbol interval to extract the dominant positive and negative frequency components. Their spacing is then mapped to the discrete data alphabet. The START–PAYLOAD–END framing, together with the frequency-coded representation, enables stable symbol recovery across successive intervals.

Multipath propagation evaluation

Additional experiments were conducted to evaluate the behavior of the proposed system under multipath propagation conditions, considering direct-path, indirect-path, and combined direct–indirect propagation scenarios. In all configurations, the RIS remained mounted on the same metallic plate/background. The radar–RIS distance was maintained at approximately 1 m, while the additional metallic reflector used to generate the indirect path was positioned approximately 0.5 m from the RIS. The reference configuration (C1) shown in Fig. 10 corresponds to the direct-path normal-incidence case, where the RIS sidebands at ± 4 kHz exhibit the strongest response, reaching approximately 112 dB in the measured spectrum. This result confirms that the metallic plate behind/under the RIS does not prevent the detection of the RIS modulation. In configuration C2, the radar–RIS geometry was rotated to approximately , placing the RIS outside the effective angular response region. Under this condition, the sideband amplitudes decreased from approximately 112 dB to nearly 75 dB because of the reduced direct reflected contribution toward the radar.

It is worth noting that the CW radar continuously performs sensing operations regardless of whether RIS-based communication is active, since motion- and Doppler-induced reflections from the environment are always present in the received baseband signal. RIS-based telemetry is added on top of the conventional CW radar sensing functionality through modulation sidebands shifted away from the clutter-dominated Doppler region near DC, enabling simultaneous coexistence between sensing and communication without requiring additional radar hardware, waveform modification, or time-sharing between sensing and communication tasks. Consequently, the radar may simultaneously perform conventional CW sensing tasks such as motion or occupancy detection while decoding RIS-based telemetry, as experimentally illustrated in Fig. 10, where environmental motion and clutter signatures near DC remain observable while RIS telemetry sidebands are simultaneously detected. A key strength of the proposed approach is therefore that the sensing capability of the radar remains fundamentally unchanged while RIS telemetry is simultaneously decoded. The main limitation appears on the communication side, where as shown in Figs. 8 and 9, under dynamic conditions and increasing radar–RIS separation, the RIS-induced sidebands progressively approach the clutter and motion-induced spectral components, thereby reducing the decoding margin beyond approximately 4 m.

Methods

Experimental setup

The proposed system is evaluated in a real-time sensing scenario designed to demonstrate wireless telemetry using radar-assisted RIS backscatter. The experiments are conducted in an indoor environment under line-of-sight conditions between the radar and the sensing node. The sensing node is placed inside an insulated foam cold-storage enclosure positioned at an approximate separation of 4 m from the radar. The radar continuously illuminates the RIS-based sensing node while acquiring the backscattered signal. An AM2302 temperature and humidity sensor is integrated with the RIS tag to monitor the environmental conditions inside the enclosure. The sensor provides temperature and relative humidity measurements with resolutions of C and , respectively, while consuming approximately 1–1.5 mA during active measurement and less than 50~μA in standby mode, supporting low-power operation of the sensing node.30. These measurements are periodically sampled by a microcontroller that encodes the data using the frequency-coded RIS backscatter modulation scheme described in “Related work” section. The RIS module and the sensor are mounted on the inner side of the enclosure lid, while the control electronics are connected through bias lines. During operation, the radar receives the modulated backscattered signal generated by the RIS, enabling remote retrieval of the sensor measurements through baseband spectral analysis. The RIS tag employed in this work is designed as a compact, electronically tunable reflector operating at 24 GHz. Its primary role is to provide two distinct and repeatable reflection states under identical biasing conditions that enable frequency-coded backscatter modulation. This functionality is achieved by means of PIN-diode-based switching integrated directly into the resonant structure of the surface. Each RIS unit cell is implemented on a Rogers 4003C substrate with relative permittivity , loss tangent , and a thickness of 0.51 mm, using 17-μm copper metallization. As illustrated in Fig. 18, the unit cell occupies an area of 5.6 mm × 5.6 mm and consists of a compact resonant structure formed by a central metallic patch and surrounding conductive arms. A PIN diode is placed at a high electric-field region of the resonant path, separated by a 0.4 mm gap, while a 0.3 mm spacing is maintained between the central patch and the outer frame. The switching elements are implemented using MADP-000907-14020x AlGaAs flip-chip PIN diodes32, which provide fast and repeatable switching between two reflection states. The PIN-diode biasing signals are generated by a Cortex-M0 microcontroller. In the forward-biased state, the diode introduces a low-impedance connection that effectively extends the resonant current path, whereas in the reverse-biased state it introduces a capacitive discontinuity that modifies the surface-current distribution. This controlled alteration of the dominant resonant loop produces the two reflection states characterized by and . Each PIN diode requires approximately 1 mA of forward current during the ON state. Since the forward voltage is characterized at 10 mA ( V), applying this value at 1 mA yields a conservative upper-bound estimate of approximately 1.3 mW per diode; the actual dissipation at 1 mA is expected to be somewhat lower due to the logarithmic current–voltage relationship. At the 50% duty cycle imposed by the square-wave modulation, the average power across all four diodes is approximately 2.6 mW. The Cortex-M0 microcontroller is operated at a reduced clock frequency to minimize power consumption, drawing approximately 1–3 mA in active mode and corresponding to roughly 3–10 mW at 3.3 V, which represents the dominant contributor to the overall power budget of the sensing node, consistent with low-power IoT operation.. To increase the effective radar cross section while maintaining compact size, four identical unit cells are arranged in a 2 × 2 configuration to form a compact RIS tag with overall dimensions of 22.6 mm × 15 mm. As illustrated in Fig. 19, all unit cells share a common bias line to ensure synchronized switching across the surface. DC bias routing is placed primarily along the perimeter of the RIS tag to minimize interaction with the RF fields of the resonant elements, and narrow bias traces are employed to reduce parasitic loading. A 1-kΩ series resistor is placed near the bias supply to provide RF–DC isolation and limit bias current. The electromagnetic performance of the 2 × 2 RIS tag is characterized using full-wave simulations carried out in ANSYS HFSS. Figure 20 reports the simulated reflection coefficient magnitudes for the ON and OFF diode states. In the reverse-biased (OFF) state, the RIS exhibits a pronounced resonance near 24 GHz with a minimum of approximately -23 dB, whereas in the forward-biased (ON) state the resonance is suppressed, resulting in a nearly flat reflection response across the operating band. The clear separation between the two reflection responses indicates that the RIS provides sufficient contrast in the reflection coefficient to support frequency-coded backscatter modulation. This contrast directly translates into the measurable modulation sidebands observed in the experimental results of “System architecture” section.

Conclusion

This work presents a radar-assisted RIS sensing and communication framework based on the integration of a 24-GHz CW radar with a compact reconfigurable intelligent surface operating as a backscatter tag. By encoding information in the frequency spacing of the symmetric modulation sidebands, the proposed approach eliminates reliance on communication-oriented protocols and waveform-dependent decoding while providing inherent robustness against Doppler-induced frequency shifts and environmental dynamics. Since the RIS tag does not require active RF transmission, the proposed architecture enables low-power operation suitable for compact IoT sensing nodes.

Experimental results demonstrate error-free data reception under stationary conditions up to and under tag motion up to , with reliable operation maintained in the presence of motion-induced clutter and with packet-level decoding preserved during beam-crossing traversal at translation speeds on the order of and for single-packet and two-packet reception, respectively, confirming reliable short-range operation under practical indoor scenarios. Long-duration measurements further validate stable operation during continuous reception with an average packet error rate below 0.1%. Real-time temperature and humidity sensing demonstrate the feasibility of integrating environmental monitoring within the proposed radar-assisted RIS framework. The lightweight RIS architecture and low-power microcontroller-based control enable a compact, energy-efficient implementation, with an overall footprint of only 22.6 mm × 15 mm, making it suitable for sensing applications in the IoT paradigm, where minimal hardware complexity is critical.
