Psychometric properties of the variants of the Chinese UCLA Loneliness Scales and their associations with mental health in adolescents
Authors: Hang Ip, Yi Nam Suen, Lai Ming Christy Hui, Charlton Cheung, Stephanie Ming Yin Wong, Eric Yu Hai Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Loneliness is a pervasive and distressing emotional experience that affects individuals of all ages and can have significant consequences for both physical and mental health. The UCLA Loneliness Scale (UCLA-LS) is widely used to assess the subjective feelings of loneliness, but concerns have been raised about its structure and applicability in diverse cultural contexts. This study analysed the reliability and validity of the five Chinese versions of the UCLA-LS in a representative sample of 2,643 adolescents and young adults with Chinese as their first language in Hong Kong. The results showed high internal consistency across all versions and content validity for most items. Factor analysis revealed a two-factor structure for the longer versions and a unidimensional structure for the shorter versions. However, the fit statistics suggested that the models did not meet good fit criteria, indicating item loading or model specification requires further review. The scales demonstrated strong associations with mental health conditions such as depression and anxiety, as well as psychosocial factors like hopelessness and aggression. Notably, the 8-item version’s total scores were associated with mental health and psychological attributes comparable to those of the 20-item version, suggesting its potential suitability as a screening tool in population settings. Future research is needed to improve the generalisability of these scales to other Chinese populations, particularly for items with lower content validity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75739-w.

Introduction

Loneliness is an unpleasant experience that can result from the discrepancy between expected and perceived interpersonal relationships1. Its global prevalence varies across age groups but is generally higher among adolescents (ranging from 9.2 to 14.4%) and the elderly (ranging from 11.1 to 27.7%)2. Persistent loneliness has emerged as a major public health issue with far-reaching consequences for both physical3,4 and mental well-being5–8. Its deleterious effects are comparable to those of smoking 15 cigarettes per day9. The complexity of this issue lies in its subtle nature, making it particularly difficult to identify and engage with isolated populations10,11. This challenge is especially pronounced in Asian cultures, where the emphasis on social harmony often leads to the suppression of negative emotions12. As a result, the detection of mental health concerns becomes more complicated. To effectively address this, there is a clear need for further research aimed at developing a screening tool that is not only valid and reliable but also concise. Such a tool must be culturally sensitive, taking into account the unique social dynamics and emotional expression patterns prevalent in these communities, while ensuring it is both practical and unobtrusive13.

The UCLA Loneliness Scale (UCLA-LS) is one of the most widely used screening tools for loneliness. Initially comprising 20 items14, the scale has undergone two significant revisions, resulting in the Revised UCLA Loneliness Scale (RULS)15 and the UCLA Loneliness Scale Version 3 (UCLA-LSV3)16. Several abbreviated versions, including 3-, 6-, 8-, and 10-item adaptations, have been developed for different purposes and settings17, culminating in a total of at least seven UCLA-LS forms. The UCLA-LS faces several challenges that hinder its effectiveness and generalisability. One of the main issues is the existence of multiple versions and inconsistent findings regarding the scale’s factor structure14,18–20. This lack of consistency makes it difficult to compare results across studies and limits the scale’s reliability17. Furthermore, the scale has been translated into various languages, but the lack of content validity investigations and qualitative insights in different cultural contexts raises concerns about its applicability17. The use of diverse forms and variables in different studies has resulted in scattered findings21, making it difficult to establish a clear depiction of their predictive validity. Although the 10-item version was once reported as the optimal short form, the study was conducted on two relatively small samples of undergraduate students22. The value of other abbreviated forms across contexts remains largely unexplored. These issues highlight the need for a more comprehensive examination of the scale and its abbreviated versions, using a consistent methodology in a representative sample to address these limitations and enhance its utility.

In this study, we examined the psychometric properties of the Chinese UCLA-LS Version 3 (20 items) and four abbreviated versions in a population-representative sample of adolescents and young adults in Hong Kong. To date, the Chinese UCLA-LS has not been validated in a population-based study, with only isolated research conducted on specific subgroups23,24. Given that loneliness at a young age is linked to a higher risk of developing mental health issues, such as depression, anxiety, and even suicidal behaviours10,25–28, this study aimed to evaluate the reliability, validity, and factor structure of various UCLA-LS forms. By examining their associations with mental health conditions and psychological attributes, the study seeks to contribute to effective screening practices and interventions that promote psychosocial well-being in this population.

Methods

Study sample, recruitment, and data collection procedures

This cross-sectional study used data from the Hong Kong Youth Epidemiology Study (HK-YES)29–32. The HK-YES is an epidemiological investigation examining the mental health of young individuals aged 15–24 years in Hong Kong, conducted between April 2019 and April 2022. The recruitment process employed a stratified random sampling strategy, in which potential participants were contacted via mail using addresses estimated with a residing youth from the Census and Statistical Department of the Hong Kong SAR Government pre-stratified by geographical location and residential dwelling type. Those who received an invitation expressed their interest in participating through a dedicated registration website or telephone hotline. The research staff then contacted the registrants to further confirm their eligibility. Inclusion criteria required participants to reside at the contacted address, fall within the specified age range at the time of invitation, and provide written consent (with parental consent necessary for individuals aged 15–17). The study adhered to a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (Reference number: UW19-017) and did not establish any specific exclusion criteria. It followed the Helsinki Declaration and its updates, with all participants providing written informed consent, or parental informed consent if under 18.

Following confirmation of eligibility, the participants were asked to provide written consent and were then personally interviewed by trained researchers. The interviews encompassed interviewer-administered assessments of probable psychiatric disorders, supplemented by self-reported measures covering a wide range of mental health-related data33. The current study only included 2,643 participants who spoke Cantonese or Chinese and reported using Chinese as their first language.

Loneliness measure

Loneliness was assessed using the UCLA Loneliness Scale Version 3 (UCLA-LSV3), which comprises 20 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 4 (always)16. Nine items were reverse-coded (items 1, 5, 6, 9, 10, 15, 16, 19, and 20) to align with the direction of the remaining items, resulting in a higher total UCLA-LSV3 score, indicating a higher level of loneliness. The scale exhibited satisfactory internal consistency, as evidenced by Cronbach’s alpha values for various forms of the scale ranging between 0.72 and 0.9416,34–38. The Chinese UCLA-LSV3, adopted in the current study was translated by Wang et al.7. However, to the best of our knowledge, no validation of this fully translated version has been conducted to date.

The scores for the various forms of the UCLA Loneliness Scale were derived from the UCLA-LSV3. Of note, the UCLA Loneliness Scale (ULS)-3 and ULS-8, which included three and eight items from the UCLA-LSV3, were developed from a small group of Chinese samples with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder23. The ULS-6 adapted six items from the RULS39, and the ULS-10 was developed by the author of theUCLA-LSV316,22.

Mental health conditions and related measures

The association between various forms of the Chinese UCLA-LS and the participants’ mental health conditions and psychosocial attributes was investigated. The mental health conditions investigated were depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, social anxiety symptoms, mania, general distress, symptoms of clinical high risk for psychosis, schizotypal traits, sleepiness, productivity loss, and quality of life. The psychosocial attributes investigated were hopelessness, aggressive tendencies, impulsivity, attitudes toward online communication, family relationships, resilience, self-esteem, goal commitment, and procrastination. Detailed information about each of these measures can be found in the Supplementary Material S1. Demographic characteristics included age, sex (male or female), educational level, employment status, and self-reported psychiatric history.

Content validity

A panel of six researchers who have 2 to 20 years of research experience in the mental health field was invited and agreed to be an expert panel judging the content validity of the scale, and all returned their rating of each item on a four-point Likert scale for its relevance, clarity, simplicity, ambiguity, and uniqueness40,41.

Relevance measures the degree of association between an item and loneliness, while clarity determines whether the item conveys loneliness in a comprehensible manner. Simplicity assesses whether the item concisely describes it, while ambiguity evaluates the presence of any vagueness that could lead to misconceptions unrelated to loneliness. Finally, uniqueness gauges whether the item duplicates the expression of loneliness in comparison to existing items.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29 or R version 4.3.1.

Psychometric properties

McDonald’s ωt reliability coefficient was calculated to measure the reliability of the UCLA-LSV3 scale and its abbreviated forms42,43. A coefficient value higher than 0.8 was considered an accepted standard44. EFA with ordinary least squared factoring and the oblimin rotation approach were used to explore the underlying structure of the scale. The varimax rotation approach was used in the ULS-8 because the two factors were found to have a low correlation. Before conducting EFA, we determined the number of factors using Parallel Analysis to identify an acceptable number of factors. Parallel Analysis is an alternative technique that compares the scree plot (a line plot of the eigenvalues of factors) from observed data to that of a random data matrix with the same dimensions as the original dataset45. The model’s goodness of fit was then assessed using three key indices from the EFA: the Model Chi-Square (χ²), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Cut-off values for a satisfactory model fit were identified as p-value > 0.05, for the model χ²46, TLI > 0.9047, and RMSEA < 0.0848.

To evaluate the performance of each item, item-level content validity indices (I-CVIs) were employed to quantify the content validity of the UCLA-LSV349. The I-CVI was calculated by dividing the number of experts who rated the items as meeting the target criteria (a rating of 3 or 4) by the total number of experts, resulting in the proportion of experts who considered the item to be content valid. A cut-off of 0.83 was chosen based on Lynn’s50 I-CVI cut-off table when the number of experts was six.

Associations with mental health conditions and psychological attributes

The relationships between various ULS scores, mental health status, and other related variables were investigated using separate logistic regression models. First, the scores of the different UCLA loneliness scales were standardised and entered into a logistic regression model as an independent variable. Dichotomised mental health conditions and related variables were used as dependent variables in the separate models. The standardised scores of the UCLA Loneliness Scales enabled a clearer interpretation of the odds ratios calculated across scales with varying numbers of items. Dichotomisation of the dependent variables was performed using either validated or median cut-offs when necessary. All dichotomous variables were coded with 0 indicating better outcomes (e.g. lower depressive symptoms) and 1 indicating poorer outcomes (e.g. higher depressive symptoms). Detailed information about the cut-offs used can be found in the Supplementary Material. Demographic variables were included in the models as covariates, to account for potential confounding factors. To assess the associations between each UCLA loneliness scale and mental health conditions or other psychological variables in general, separate pooled odds ratios were calculated for mental health status and other psychological variables using the generic inverse variance method and random effect models. These calculations were conducted using the metagen function from the meta package in R.

To compare the power of different loneliness scales in predicting different mental health or psychological conditions, an area under the curve (AUC) analysis was performed. The AUCs obtained were compared across the different versions of the UCLA Loneliness Scale.

Some notable differences in loneliness levels were observed in the sample. Male participants scored higher than female participants on both UCLA-LSV3 (p = 0.04) (Table 1). Participants aged 15–17 years reported lower scores than those aged 18–21 years on the 3-item (p = 0.02) and 6-item (p = 0.03) versions. Their scores were also lower than those aged 22–24 years on the ULS-6 (p = 0.049). Moreover, participants who were not currently in education had significantly higher scores on the UCLA-LSV3 (p = 0.04). The most prominent difference was observed among individuals with a self-reported lifetime psychiatric history, who scored notably higher on all versions than those without any psychiatric history (all p < 0.001).

Psychometrics properties of various UCLA loneliness scales

The McDonald’s ωt of the five scales ranged from 0.77 for ULS-6 to 0.92 for UCLA-LSV3 suggesting satisfactory to excellent reliability.

A bi-dimensional structure emerged in the UCLA-LSV3, ULS-8, and ULS-10 scales, while the ULS-3 and ULS-6 scales remained unidimensional (Supplementary Material S2). For UCLA-LSV3, negatively worded statements anchored the first factor, explaining 28.0% of the total variance, and positively worded items anchored the second factor, accounting for 20.7% of the variance. Similarly, the ULS-8 scale’s two factors contained positive and negative items, contributing to 29.0% and 26.3% of the total variance, respectively. In the case of the ULS-8, two factors explained 40.3% and 13.4% of the total variance, while the second factor had only two items (items 9 and 15). Notably, Item 15 also loaded on Factor 1 with a higher factor loading, making the factor structure of ULS-8 less clear. Single factors in the ULS-3 and ULS-6 scales accounted for 61.2% and 55.4% of the total explained variance, respectively (Table 2).

The EFA fit statistics revealed that the factor structures of all the models did not fit our sample well (Table 2). All Chi-square tests for the models were statistically significant, all RMSEAs exceeded 0.08, and only the TLI for the ULS-6 was above 0.9 while that for ULS-8 was close to 0.9. These findings suggest that the factor structures require further investigation.

The I-CVI scores for the 20 items evaluated by a panel of six experts revealed that the majority of the items demonstrated strong content validity (Table 3). The overall average scores ranged from 0.60 to 1.00, with items 4, 5, and 19 achieving a perfect I-CVI of 1. Most items met the acceptability threshold, with 15 of 20 items scoring an average I-CVI above 0.83. The lowest average I-CVI was observed for item 17, which scored a below-cut-off score of 0.6, highlighting the need for revision. More specifically, clarity, simplicity, and ambiguity for most items were notably high, each with at least 18 items achieving satisfactory inter-expert agreement, whereas relevance and uniqueness indices showed more variation across items. Items 7, 8, 9, and 17 showed poor relevance coefficients ranging from 0.17 to 0.67 for relevance, and nearly half of the items (items 3, 7, 9, 10, 13, 16, and 20) were considered to be underperforming in uniqueness, with coefficients ranging from 0.33 0.67.

Associations between loneliness and mental health status and psychosocial attributes

All the UCLA loneliness scales demonstrated robust associations with mental health conditions (Table 4). All scales exhibited similar patterns with an insignificant association with probable mania. The lowest adjusted odds ratios (OR) being related to excessive sleepiness (ranging from 1.32 to 1.42) across scales) and the highest adjusted ORs being associated with probable schizotypal personality disorders (ranging from 3.41 to 5.23 across scales). The 20-item UCLA-LSV3 scale had the largest pooled adjusted OR (2.30, 95% CI 1.76 to 2.99, followed by the ULS-8 scale (2.27, 95% CI 1.78 to 2.90), the ULS-6 scale (2.23, 95% CI 1.83 to 2.70), the ULS-10 scale (2.12, 95% CI 1.65 to 2.72), and the smallest pooled adjusted OR was found with the ULS-3 scale (2.04, 95% CI 1.69 to 2.47) (Supplementary material S3). As presented in Table 4, the results of the ROC analysis indicated that the 20-item UCLA-LSV3 demonstrated a stronger association with all mental health conditions than the ULS-10 and most conditions than the ULS-3. Notably, ULS-6 and ULS-8 demonstrated stronger associations with most mental health conditions than ULS-3 and ULS-10 did.

We also found that all the scales were strongly associated with psychosocial attributes. Specifically, higher hopelessness and higher aggression were strongly associated with all scales, with adjusted ORs ranging from 1.81 to 2.51 and 2.24 to 2.45, respectively. Negative online communication and lower self-esteem were among the weakest associations with the different scales, with the adjusted OR ranging from 1.15 to 1.34 and 1.33 to 1.37, respectively. The UCLA-LSV3 scale had the largest pooled adjusted OR (1.81, 95% CI 1.54 to 2.13), followed by the ULS-10 scale (1.74, 95% CI 1.50 to 2.02), ULS-8 scale (1.73, 95% CI 1.49 to 2.01), and ULS-6 scale (1.64, 95% CI 1.42 to 1.90), and the smallest pooled adjusted OR was found with the ULS-3 scale (1.57, 95% CI 1.38 1.80). Furthermore, ROC analysis found that the UCLA-LSV3 scale had the strongest association with all psychosocial attributes, while the findings among other forms of loneliness scale were mixed (Table 4). Overall, the ULS-8 and ULS-10 scales were more strongly associated with psychosocial attributes than the ULS-3 and ULS-6 scales were.

Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive psychometric evaluation of the Chinese UCLA-LSV3 and its abbreviated forms in a representative population-based sample of Chinese adolescents. In addition to assessing reliability and factor structure, our study provides evidence for the content validity of these measures, as well as their association with mental health and psychosocial variables. The full and abbreviated forms demonstrated satisfactory reliability, and factor analysis revealed a predominant two-factor structure for the 20- and 10-item forms, although the model fit indices were mixed. The findings suggest that if a full form is not used, the ULS-6 and ULS-8 are preferable for research on loneliness and mental health, as they show stronger associations with depressive symptoms, anxiety, social anxiety, distress, and schizotypal personality traits. On the other hand, for research examining the association between loneliness and some psychological variables, the ULS-8 and ULS-10 may be better options, as evidenced by their more robust associations with psychosocial qualities like hopelessness, aggressiveness, poorer family relationships, lower resilience, lower self-esteem, poor resilience and compromised goal commitment. The current study reported that loneliness is significantly associated with almost all of the mental health conditions, with adjusted odds ratio ranging from 1.32 (for excessive sleepiness) to 5.23 (for probable schizotypal personality disorders) and psychosocial attributes, with adjusted ORs ranging from 1.15 (for lower self-esteem) to 2.51 (for hopelessness), in interest. The consistency of these relationships suggests that loneliness is an important indicator of both mental and psychosocial problems7.

We found that all UCLA loneliness scales demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, as demonstrated by a McDonald’s omega of ≥ 0.8 in our sample. This was consistent with the original UCLA-LSV3 (α = 0.89–0.94)16. The uniformly high omega indicates that the Chinese UCLA-LSV3 maintains strong internal consistency, even when shortened, which lends credence to the reliability of the assessment of loneliness using the Chinese UCLA-LSV3 and its abbreviated versions. Qualitative evaluations by experts in the Chinese version provided preliminary evidence of content validity for Chinese adolescents. While most of the items were considered culturally appropriate and easily understood for assessing adolescent loneliness, Item 17 which asked whether the respondent was shy, received a very low CVI relevance, clarity, and ambiguity.

Expert judgement plays a vital role in providing evidence of validity, especially in translated tests51. Therefore, this evaluation of semantic, conceptual, and cultural equivalence bolsters initial confidence in content validity; however, continuous improvement of the Chinese UCLA-LSV3 remains necessary. The I-CVI showed that the majority of items from the UCLA-LSV3 could achieve satisfactory performance in clarity, simplicity, and ambiguity, indicating that the translated scale content could be understood easily and accurately. Nonetheless, the ratings of relevance and uniqueness indicated that some items were not culturally relevant or unique enough to be an individual statement, suggesting a potential risk of redundancy in the Chinese UCLA-LSV3. For example, some experts commented that item 3 (“How often do you feel that there is no one you can turn to?”) duplicated with item 20 (“How often do you feel that there are people you can turn to?”), and item 7 (“How often do you feel that you are no longer close to anyone?”) overlapped with item 10 (“How often do you feel close to people?”). Another highly similar pair was item 13 (“How often do you feel that no one really knows you well?”) and 16 (“How often do you feel that there are people who really understand you?”). Given these highly redundant statements without significant added value, this could pose a participant burden, compromising decision-making, scale validity, and reliability. Thus, the focus of future refinement should first be on removing irrelevant and redundant items, followed by polishing the expression of each item.

The results of the EFA revealed a two-factor structure for the 20-item and 10-item scales, while the 3-item and 6-item scales exhibited a one-dimensional structure, in line with expectations for very brief scales. These findings replicated the original “bi-dimensional” results from the UCLA-LSV316 with factors consisting of positive and negative items separately. However, the scales showed a poor fit, necessitating further investigation. Several factors may contribute to this issue, including the inadequacy in capturing the nuances of loneliness and item redundancy of this scale in the Chinese cultural context. Refining items and exploring alternative factor structures of the UCLA could enhance item relevance and clarity. While the Chi-square statistics tend to reach significance with large samples, TLI and RMSEA may be more persuasive in suggesting suboptimal overall model fits for the scales by emphasising parsimonious models and population-based performance, respectively. Notably, TLI is also sensitive to the complexity of the model which could be associated with the number of items in the model. This may explain why the 6-item version had an above-threshold TLI, while the 3-item version was saturated with too few items. These insights will provide valuable guidance for future research.

Extending Elphinstone’s22 work on identifying the most suitable short form of the UCLA Loneliness Scale, our study provides new evidence on the unique strengths and potential clinical utility of each abbreviated form. Distinct patterns emerged regarding how different abbreviated versions correlate with mental health versus psychosocial factors. ULS-6 demonstrates strength in its consistently high association with mental well-being, as evidenced by its correlations with moderate levels of depressive symptoms, probable anxiety, probable social anxiety disorder, severe distress, lower quality of life, and schizotypal personality. Given its brevity, these comparable relationships with the full form suggest that the ULS-6 could be a practical choice for fast but reliable screenings if time or participant burden might be a concern. On the other hand, the ULS-8 showed similar, or sometimes stronger, associations with mental well-being compared with the ULS-6, such as probable social anxiety. It also extends its strengths by its robust association with severe mental disorders, such as schizotypal personality and clinical high-risk conditions. Psychosocially, ULS-8 tends to be highly associated with “despondent” qualities, such as hopelessness, lower self-esteem, and poorer resilience. The comprehensiveness of the ULS-8, comparable to the full form, highlights its utility in uncovering both mental well-being and psychosocial attributes. The ULS-10 stands out for its robust and consistent links with psychosocial indicators, such as hopelessness, aggressiveness, poorer family relationships, poorer resilience, and poorer goal commitment. This version offers insight into how loneliness and psychosocial attributes are intertwined in different aspects of life, making it a potent measure of overall well-being and vitality. Lastly, the ULS-3, despite being the most concise version, still mirrors the overall association observed across scales, which could be a super brief assessment ideal in time-limited and high-throughput settings.

Limitations and future research

This study had several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes examination of the predictive validity and test-retest reliability of the UCLA-LSV3 over time. Future longitudinal studies are necessary to establish the sensitivity and stability of this method. Second, while the sample was large and representative, it was limited to adolescents in Hong Kong, raising questions about its generalisability to other age groups and cultural contexts. Therefore, validating this scale in diverse populations should be a priority in future research. Third, the qualitative evaluation of content validity was based on a relatively small group of experts, which could be supplemented by interviews or focus groups with target adolescents in the future. Additionally, EFA revealed a poor fit for the two-factor structure, necessitating further research to clarify the dimensionality and conceptual clarity of the scale. Finally, while classical test theory was employed in the current study, future research should incorporate item response theory to provide additional information on the measurement invariance among adolescents in different gender and age groups, how individual items perform and the scale’s precision at different levels of loneliness52.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive psychometric evaluation of the Chinese version of the UCLA-LSV3 and its abbreviated versions for adolescents. Both full and short forms demonstrated good reliability and content validity. Exploratory factor analysis revealed predominant two-factor structures for longer versions, although the model fit was mixed across different versions, suggesting further refinement and localisation of the scales. The differential association magnitudes between the different brief versions and mental health versus psychosocial factors were also identified. Notably, the 8-item version’s total scores were associated with mental health and psychological attributes comparable to the 20-item version, suggesting its potential suitability as a screening tool in population settings.
