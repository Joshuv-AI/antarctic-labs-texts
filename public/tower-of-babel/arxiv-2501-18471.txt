Computing AD-compatible subgradients of convex relaxations of implicit functions
Yingkai Song, Kamil A. Khan

Abstract
Automatic generation of convex relaxations and subgradients is critical in global optimization, and is typically carried out using variants of automatic/algorithmic differentiation (AD). At previous AD conferences, variants of the forward and reverse AD modes were presented to evaluate accurate subgradients for convex relaxations of supplied composite functions. In a recent approach for generating convex relaxations of implicit functions, these relaxations are constructed as optimal-value functions; this formulation is versatile but complicates sensitivity analysis. We present the first subgradient propagation rules for these implicit function relaxations, based on supplied AD-like knowledge of the residual function. Our new subgradient rules allow implicit function relaxations to be added to the elemental function libraries for the forward AD modes for subgradient propagation of convex relaxations. Proof-of-concept numerical results in Julia are presented.



1
Introduction

Typical state-of-the-art methods for global minimization proceed by generating lower and upper bounds on the unknown optimal value, and then successively improving these bounds. While upper bounds may be evaluated as local minima using standard solvers, typical approaches for computing useful lower bounds (e.g. in the solver BARON
[
26
]
) involve generating convex relaxations and minimizing those. Based on AD-like construction rules by McCormick
[
22
]
, a forward AD mode variant by Mitsos et al.
[
23
]
automatically evaluates a convex relaxation for a supplied composition of elemental functions from a library. Mitsos et al. also developed a forward AD mode variant to evaluate subgradients for these relaxations; subgradients are useful in convex optimization, and are particularly useful when computing the lower bounds in global optimization. At the AD2012 conference, Beckers et al.
[
2
]
presented an efficient reverse AD mode variant of Mitsos et al.’s subgradient propagation method. These AD modes are in active use today, including in the Julia package
EAGO.jl

[
35
]
for global optimization, and in a new approach for computing adjoint subgradients of convex relaxations of differential equation solutions
[
38
]
.

Cao and Khan
[
6
]
recently presented a new method to construct useful convex relaxations of implicit functions based on known relaxations of the residual functions. Though this approach is versatile, it constructs implicit function relaxations as convex optimal-value functions, for which sensitivity analysis is traditionally considered to be difficult
[
9
]
. In order to incorporate implicit functions into the forward AD modes for subgradient propagation, we seek subgradients of these new relaxations. This article presents the first useful subgradient computation rules for these relaxations, thus allowing implicit functions to be added to the elemental function libraries for constructing McCormick-style convex relaxations with subgradients computed by forward-mode AD, and to be used in established implementations. We informally refer to these properties as “AD-compatibility”. While AD-compatibility is generally not an issue for compositions of smooth functions due to the structure of the well-known chain-rule, appropriate AD rules only have been developed recently for composite nonsmooth functions
[
2
,
17
,
19
,
23
]
. We nevertheless note that due to the nonlinearity with respect to input directions of one of our new approaches, there may not be a direct reverse AD analog of that approach; a similar situation was discussed in
[
11
]
. We proceed based on recent AD rules for nonsmooth optimal value functions
[
29
]
, bound-constrained convex functions
[
37
]
, and nonsmooth closed-form composite functions
[
18
,
19
]
, the latter of which were presented at the AD2012 and AD2016 conferences.

The remainder of this article is laid out as follows. Section
2
summarizes relevant established concepts including convex relaxations, subgradients, and the new implicit function relaxations of
[
6
]
. Section
3
summarizes the particular situations we will later consider. Section
4
presents new subgradients for relaxations of scalar-valued implicit functions, Section
5
covers vector-valued implicit functions, and Section
6
presents numerical examples based on a proof-of-concept implementation in Julia.

2.2
Convex relaxations and subgradients

In state-of-the-art global optimization solvers such as BARON
[
26
]
, upper and lower bounds are generated for the unknown optimal value, and are then successively refined. In this setting, lower bounds are typically generated by minimizing convex relaxations; the following definition of convex relaxations accommodates functions with irregular domains.

Definition 2.1
(from
[
6
]
)

Consider a convex set
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}
and a subset
Q\subset P
. Consider the extended real numbers
\bar{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}
, and a function
\bm{\phi}:Q\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Then:

1.

\bm{\phi}^{\mathrm{cv}}:P\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a
convex relaxation
of
\bm{\phi}
on
P
if

•

\bm{\phi}^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})\leq\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{p})
for all
\mathbf{p}\in Q
, and

•

\bm{\phi}^{\mathrm{cv}}
is convex on
P
.

2.

\bm{\phi}^{\mathrm{cc}}:P\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a
concave relaxation
of
\bm{\phi}
on
P
if

•

\bm{\phi}^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p})\geq\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{p})
for all
\mathbf{p}\in Q
, and

•

\bm{\phi}^{\mathrm{cc}}
is concave on
P
.

Definition 2.2
(from
[
27
]
)

Given an open set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a function
\mathbf{f}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and vectors
\mathbf{x}\in X
and
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the limit

\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}+\alpha\mathbf{d})-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})}{\alpha}

is called the
directional derivative
of
\mathbf{f}
at
\mathbf{x}
in the direction
\mathbf{d}
if it exists, and is denoted as
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})
or
[\mathbf{f}]^{\prime}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})
; the latter notation is clearer when a function’s name includes superscripts. The function
\mathbf{f}
is
directionally differentiable
at
\mathbf{x}
if
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d})
exists and is finite for each
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Convex functions defined on open sets are always directionally differentiable (see
[
25
, Theorem 23.4]
). The following definition of subgradients is standard in convex analysis
[
25
]
.

Definition 2.3
(from
[
25
, Page 214]
)

Given an open convex set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a convex function
f^{\mathrm{cv}}:X\to\mathbb{R}
, and a point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{int}(X)
, a vector
\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cv}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
subgradient
of
f^{\mathrm{cv}}
at
\mathbf{x}\in X
if

f^{\mathrm{cv}}(\bm{\xi})\geq f^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{x})+\langle\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cv}},\bm{\xi}-\mathbf{x}\rangle,\quad\forall\bm{\xi}\in X.

Similarly, given a concave function
f^{\mathrm{cc}}:X\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a subgradient of
f^{\mathrm{cc}}
at
\mathbf{x}\in X
if

f^{\mathrm{cc}}(\bm{\xi})\leq f^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{x})+\langle\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cc}},\bm{\xi}-\mathbf{x}\rangle,\quad\forall\bm{\xi}\in X.

The
subdifferential

\partial f^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{x})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(resp.
\partial f^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{x})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
) is the collection of all subgradients of
f^{\mathrm{cv}}
(resp.
f^{\mathrm{cc}}
) at
\mathbf{x}
, and is nonempty, convex, and compact.

As summarized in the introduction, the McCormick relaxations
[
22
,
23
]
are convex relaxations that may be computed by a forward sweep through a composite function’s computational graph. Subgradients for the McCormick relaxations may be evaluated either by a forward AD mode variant
[
23
]
or a reverse AD mode variant
[
2
]
.

2.3
Implicit function relaxations

Definition
2.4
below formalizes a generic implicit function considered in the remainder of this article.

Definition 2.4
(from
[
6
]
)

Consider a residual function
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
, and the following system of equations:

(2.1)

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{0}.

Consider a convex compact set
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}
, and let
Q\subset P
be the set of
\mathbf{p}\in P
for which (
2.1
) has at least one solution
\mathbf{z}
. Suppose that the following conditions hold:

1.

The set
Q
is nonempty, so there is a meaningful
implicit function

\mathbf{x}:Q\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
that satisfies:

(2.2)

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{p}),\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{0}.

2.

There is a known interval
X:=[\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{L}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{U}}]\in\mathbb{I}\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
for which (
2.2
) holds and
\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{p})\in X
for every
\mathbf{p}\in Q
.

Cao and Khan
[
6
]
developed a new optimization-based approach for constructing useful convex and concave relaxations for the implicit function in Definition
2.4
, based on known relaxations of the residual function
\mathbf{f}
. This approach is summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
(from
[
6
]
)

Consider the setup in Definition
2.4
. Let
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{cv}},\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{cc}}:X\times P\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
be convex and concave relaxations of
\mathbf{f}
on
X\times P
, respectively. Define
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{cv}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{cc}}:P\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{x}}
such that, for each
i\in\{1,...,n_{x}\}
and
\mathbf{p}\in P
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\bm{\xi}\in X}\xi_{i}\quad{\text{s.t.}}\quad\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{cv}}(\bm{\xi},\mathbf{p})\leq\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{cc}}(\bm{\xi},\mathbf{p}),

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\bm{\xi}\in X}\xi_{i}\quad{\text{s.t.}}\quad\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{cv}}(\bm{\xi},\mathbf{p})\leq\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{cc}}(\bm{\xi},\mathbf{p}).

If these optimization problems are infeasible, then set
x_{i}^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p}):=+\infty
and
x_{i}^{\mathrm{cc}}:=-\infty
for each
i
by convention. Then,
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{cv}}
is a convex relaxation of
\mathbf{x}
on
P
, and
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{cc}}
is a concave relaxation of
\mathbf{x}
on
P
.

Observe that for each
\mathbf{p}\in P
,
x_{i}^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})
and
x_{i}^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p})
are each described as optimal-value functions (in the sense of
[
33
]
) of convex optimization problems.

2.4
Lexicographic differentiation

This subsection introduces Nesterov’s lexicographic derivatives (L-derivatives) for nonsmooth functions. These L-derivatives are subgradients if the underlying function is convex.

Definition 2.5
(from
[
24
]
)

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and
\mathbf{f}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be locally Lipschitz continuous and directionally differentiable at
\mathbf{x}\in X
. The function
\mathbf{f}
is
lexicographically smooth (L-smooth)
at
\mathbf{x}
if and only if for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
and any matrix
\mathbf{M}:=[\mathbf{m}_{(1)}\;\mathbf{m}_{(2)}\;\cdot\cdot\cdot\;\mathbf{m}_{(r)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, the following homogenization sequence of functions is well defined:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{(0)}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{M}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}:\mathbf{d}\mapsto\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{d}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{(j)}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{M}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}:\mathbf{d}\mapsto[\mathbf{f}^{(j-1)}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{M}}]^{\prime}(\mathbf{m}_{(j)};\mathbf{d}),\quad\forall j\in\{1,...,r\}.

When the columns of
\mathbf{M}
span
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{f}^{(r)}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{M}}
is guaranteed to be linear. If
r=n
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is non-singular, then a
lexicographic derivative (L-derivative)
of
\mathbf{f}
at
\mathbf{x}
in the directions
\mathbf{M}
, denoted as
\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{M})
, is defined as

\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{M}):=\mathbf{J}\mathbf{f}^{(n)}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},

and the
lexicographic subdifferential

\partial_{\mathrm{L}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
is defined as

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathrm{L}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}):=\left\{\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:\forall\text{ nonsingular }\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\right\}.

If
\mathbf{f}
is scalar-valued and convex, then we have
\partial_{\mathrm{L}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\subset\partial\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
(see
[
24
, Theorem 11]
), and thus any transposed L-derivative is a valid subgradient. The following definition of
lexicographic directional derivative
provides a convenient way for evaluating an L-derivative.

Definition 2.6
(from
[
19
]
)

Given an open set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a locally Lipschitz continuous function
\mathbf{f}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
that is L-smooth at
\mathbf{x}\in X
, and a matrix
\mathbf{M}:=[\mathbf{m}_{(1)}\;\cdot\cdot\cdot\;\mathbf{m}_{(r)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, define the
lexicographic directional derivative (LD-derivative)
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{M})

\displaystyle:=\left[\mathbf{f}^{(0)}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{m}_{(1)})\;\mathbf{f}^{(1)}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{m}_{(2)})\;\cdot\cdot\cdot\;\mathbf{f}^{(r-1)}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{m}_{(r)})\right].

Given
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{M})
with square nonsingular
\mathbf{M}
, an L-derivative
\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{M})
can be computed by solving a linear equation system:

(2.5)

\mathbf{f}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{M})=\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{L}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{M})\,\mathbf{M}.

When
\mathbf{M}
is an identity matrix, this linear equation system is trivial.

We presented a forward AD mode for lexicographic derivative evaluation at the AD2012 conference
[
18
,
19
]
, and a “branch-locking” variant at the AD2016 conference
[
17
]
that borrows the benefits of the reverse AD mode and handles implicit functions defined in terms of closed-form composite residual functions.

3
Outline of new subgradient formulations

The goal of this article is to propose the first approaches for computing subgradients of the implicit function relaxations outlined in Proposition
2.1
, for aiding in global optimization with implicit function embedded. Our goal is to provide subgradient-based AD rules for implicit function relaxations that are compatible with the subgradient-propagating forward AD mode
[
23
]
.

Our new subgradient evaluation approaches are tailored to different cases, depending on the dimensions of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{p}
, while making only mild technical assumptions.

Specifically, our contributions are as follows:

•

For a scalar-valued implicit function (
n_{x}=1
):
Here, we demonstrate that when piecewise affine relaxations for the residual function are known, then both the implicit function relaxations and their corresponding subgradients can be efficiently computed in closed form, regardless of the dimension of
\mathbf{p}
.

•

For a vector-valued implicit function (
n_{x}>1
) with low dimensional parameters (
n_{p}\leq 2
):
In these cases, we propose computing valid subgradients using the directional derivatives of the relaxations. We will introduce a new result showing that the directional derivatives can be computed by solving a linear program (LP).

\scriptscriptstyle\blacksquare

For
n_{p}=1
, the directional derivative in either direction is a subgradient.

\scriptscriptstyle\blacksquare

For
n_{p}=2
, subgradients are derived from the directional derivatives in the four cardinal directions.

•

For a vector-valued implicit function (
n_{x}>1
) with high-dimensional parameters (
n_{p}>2
):
Here, we calculate a subgradient as an L-derivative (as in Section
2.3
) of the relaxations, by solving a sequence of LPs.

4
Subgradients for
{n_{x}=1}

In this section, we consider the case where
n_{x}=1
and
piecewise affine
relaxations
f^{\mathrm{cv}}
and
f^{\mathrm{cc}}
are employed, as formalized in the following Assumption
4.1
. Piecewise affine relaxations are commonly used and can be constructed from subgradients
[
7
]
or black-box sampling
[
30
]
. It will be shown that in this case, the optimization problems in (
2.3
) are single-variable box-constrained problems, and the implicit function relaxations and the subgradients can be efficiently evaluated in closed form. This approach is inspired from
[
37
]
which studied convex optimal-value functions with box constraints.

Assumption 4.1

Consider the setup in Definition
2.4
and suppose that
n_{x}=1
. Consider constructing implicit function relaxations
{x}^{\mathrm{cv}}
and
{x}^{\mathrm{cc}}
following Proposition
2.1
. Suppose that the relaxations
{{f}}^{\mathrm{cv}}
and
{{f}}^{\mathrm{cc}}
for
{{f}}
on
X\times P
are piecewise affine
[
27
]
. That is, for each
(\xi,\mathbf{p})\in X\times P
, these functions have the form:

\displaystyle f^{\mathrm{cv}}(\xi,\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{\begin{aligned} {}^{\top}\mathbf{p}+\alpha^{\mathrm{cv},i}\xi+b^{\mathrm{cv},i}:i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\end{aligned}\right\},

\displaystyle f^{\mathrm{cc}}(\xi,\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{\begin{aligned} {}^{\top}\mathbf{p}+\alpha^{\mathrm{cc},j}\xi+b^{\mathrm{cc},j}:j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}\end{aligned}\right\}.

Let
K^{-}
denote the set of indices
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
for which
\alpha^{\mathrm{cv},i}<0
, and let
K^{+}
denote the set of indices
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
for which
\alpha^{\mathrm{cv},i}>0
. Let
L^{-}
denote the set of indices
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
for which
\alpha^{\mathrm{cc},j}<0
, and let
L^{+}
denote the set of indices
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
for which
\alpha^{\mathrm{cc},j}>0
.

Now, under Assumption
4.1
, define functions
h^{\mathrm{cv}},h^{\mathrm{cc}}:{P}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for each
\mathbf{p}\in P
,

(4.6)

\displaystyle h^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle:=\max\left(\{g_{i}(\mathbf{p}):i\in K^{-}\}\cup\{\gamma_{j}(\mathbf{p}):j\in L^{+}\}\right),

\displaystyle h^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle:=\min\left(\{g_{i}(\mathbf{p}):i\in K^{+}\}\cup\{\gamma_{j}(\mathbf{p}):j\in L^{-}\}\right),

where for each index
i
and
j
:

(4.7)

\displaystyle g_{i}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle:=\frac{-1}{\alpha^{\mathrm{cv},i}}\left({[\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{cv},i}]^{\top}\mathbf{p}}+b^{\mathrm{cv},i}\right),

\displaystyle\gamma_{j}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle:=\frac{-1}{\alpha^{\mathrm{cc},j}}\left({[\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{cc},j}]^{\top}\mathbf{p}}+b^{\mathrm{cc},j}\right).

Observe that
h^{\mathrm{cv}}
is a convex piecewise affine function and
h^{\mathrm{cc}}
is a concave piecewise affine function.
Then, the optimization problems for computing the implicit function relaxations in (
2.3
) can be reformulated to be box-constrained as follows: for each
\mathbf{p}\in Q
,

(4.8)

\displaystyle x^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\equiv\min_{{\xi}\in X}\xi\quad\text{s.t.}\quad h^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})\leq\xi\leq h^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p}),

\displaystyle x^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\equiv\max_{{\xi}\in X}\xi\quad\text{s.t.}\quad h^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})\leq\xi\leq h^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p}),

which has the following closed form:

(4.9)

\displaystyle x^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\equiv\max\left\{x^{\mathrm{L}},h^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})\right\},

\displaystyle x^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\equiv\min\left\{x^{\mathrm{U}},h^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p})\right\},

with
X:=[x^{\mathrm{L}},x^{\mathrm{U}}]
.

The subsequent result follows immediately, and evaluates subgradients of
x^{\mathrm{cv}}
and
x^{\mathrm{cc}}
based on (
4.9
).

Proposition 4.1

Suppose that Assumption
4.1
holds and consider the formulation (
4.9
) where the functions
h^{\mathrm{cv}}
and
h^{\mathrm{cc}}
are defined in (
4.6
) and (
4.7
). Then, for an arbitrary
\mathbf{p}\in\mathrm{int}(Q)
, a subgradient
\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cv}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}
of
x^{\mathrm{cv}}
at
\mathbf{p}
is given by:

•

\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cv}}:=\mathbf{0}
if
x^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})=x^{\mathrm{L}}
;

•

otherwise, if
x^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})=g_{i}(\mathbf{p})
for some
i\in K^{-}
, then
\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cv}}:=-\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{cv},i}}{\alpha^{\mathrm{cv},i}}
;

•

otherwise, there exists
j\in L^{+}
such that
x^{\mathrm{cv}}(\mathbf{p})=\gamma_{j}(\mathbf{p})
and we have
\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cv}}:=-\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{cc},j}}{\alpha^{\mathrm{cc},j}}
.

Similarly, a subgradient
\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}
of
x^{\mathrm{cc}}
at
\mathbf{p}
is given by:

•

\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cc}}:=\mathbf{0}
if
x^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p})=x^{\mathrm{U}}
;

•

otherwise, if
x^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p})=\gamma_{j}(\mathbf{p})
for some
j\in L^{-}
, then
\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cv}}:=-\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{cc},j}}{\alpha^{\mathrm{cc},j}}
;

•

otherwise,
there exists
i\in K^{+}
such that
x^{\mathrm{cc}}(\mathbf{p})=g_{i}(\mathbf{p})
and we have
\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{cv}}:=-\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{cv},i}}{\alpha^{\mathrm{cv},i}}
.

Subgradients for
{n_{x}>1}

This section considers the case where
n_{x}>1
. This case is further divided into three scenarios depending on the dimension of
\mathbf{p}
, namely
n_{p}=1
,
n_{p}=2
, and
n_{p}>2
. We restrict ourselves to the following setting for the underlying implicit function relaxation system.

Assumption 5.1

Consider the setup in Definition
2.4
, and consider constructing implicit function relaxations
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{cv}}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{cc}}
following Proposition
2.1
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\mathbf{f}\equiv(\tilde{\mathbf{f}},\mathbf{h})

is not affine. Suppose that for each

\tilde{f}_{i}^{\mathrm{cv}}(\bm{\xi},\mathbf{p})\equiv\max\left\{\tilde{f}_{i}^{\mathrm{cv},j}(\bm{\xi},\mathbf{p}):j\in\{1,...,k_{i}\}\right\},

and similarly, there exist continuously differentiable concave functions

\tilde{f}_{i}^{\mathrm{cc}}(\bm{\xi},\mathbf{p})\equiv\min\left\{\tilde{f}_{i}^{\mathrm{cc},j}(\bm{\xi},\mathbf{p}):j\in\{1,...,l_{i}\}\right\}.

Under Assumption

and Proposition

, we have for each

and

(5.10)

\displaystyle x_{{i}}^{\mathrm{cv}}({\mathbf{p}})\equiv\min_{\bm{\xi}\in X}

\displaystyle\xi_{{i}}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}(\bm{\xi},{\mathbf{p}})=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\forall\tilde{i}\in\{1,...,n_{\tilde{f}}\},

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\tilde{i}}^{\mathrm{cv},j}(\bm{\xi},{\mathbf{p}})\leq 0,\quad\forall j\in\{1,...,k_{\tilde{i}}\},

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\tilde{i}}^{\mathrm{cc},j}(\bm{\xi},{\mathbf{p}})\geq 0,\quad\forall j\in\{1,...,l_{\tilde{i}}\}.

Similarly,

is constructed using the above formulation with “max” in place of “min”. For notational simplicity, define a function

to achieve a compact representation of all inequality constraints in (

(5.11)

\displaystyle x_{{i}}^{\mathrm{cv}}({\mathbf{p}})\equiv\min_{\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}

\displaystyle\xi_{{i}}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}(\bm{\xi},{\mathbf{p}})=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}(\bm{\xi},\mathbf{p})\leq\mathbf{0}.

Note that the “

\mathbf{g}

Denote the

Assumption 5.2

Suppose that Assumption

holds, and consider some

The key to compute an L-derivative is to construct the homogenization function sequence (

has already been established in Proposition

(5.13)

\displaystyle{}^{\prime}(\hat{\mathbf{p}};\mathbf{d})\equiv\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}}

\displaystyle[\mathbf{e}^{(i)}]^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}\leq\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{A}}\mathbf{d},

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{B}}\mathbf{d},

Remark 5.2

Theorem
5.1
shows that a subgradient of
x_{i}^{\mathrm{cv}}
at
\hat{\mathbf{p}}
may be computed by solving
n_{p}
LPs. However, in practice, we probably do not need to solve all
n_{p}
LPs. Since (
5.15
) holds, if at some
\hat{j}
, the optimal solution set for the LP defining
[x_{i}^{\mathrm{cv}}]^{(\hat{j})}_{\hat{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{m}_{(\hat{j}+1)})
is a singleton, then we do not need to formulate and solve LPs for computing
[x_{i}^{\mathrm{cv}}]^{({j})}_{\hat{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{m}_{({j}+1)}),\forall j>\hat{j}
.
[
29
, Proposition 3]
(summarized from
[
21
]
) examines whether an LP has a unique optimal solution or not by solving an auxiliary LP. Employing this (optimal) solution uniqueness examination method, in most cases, our new subgradient evaluation approach would only solve two LPs, namely the LP for computing
[x_{i}^{\mathrm{cv}}]^{(0)}_{\hat{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{m}_{(1)})
and the associated uniqueness examination LP. See
[
29
, Section 3.1]
for a detailed explanation.

Finally, we note that this L-derivative-based subgradient evaluation approach is applicable regardless of the dimensions of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{p}
. It may even be favorable over the method in Section
5.2
for
n_{p}=2
, since it only requires solving two LPs, while the previous method requires solving four. However, implementing the L-derivative-based approach may present additional complexities.

Example 6.1

This example considers an implicit function defined by the van der Waals equation of state in thermodynamics, adapted from

The van der Waals equation of state describes the relationship between pressure

T
(K), and amount
n
(mol) of non-ideal gases as follows:

Here

is the gas constant, and

and

mol of carbon dioxide gas with

and
b=0.0429\;\frac{\text{L}}{\text{mol}}
. Let
P\in[0.5,1.1]
and
T\in[250,320]
. Viewing
V
as an implicit function of
(P,T)
, it is verified that an interval
X:=[10,70]
encloses all possible values of
V
. With this setup, we aim at constructing convex and concave relaxations of

in terms of
P
and
T
following the closed-form reformulation method proposed in Section
4
. With these closed-form relaxations, the subgradients are readily available as in Proposition
4.1
.

We construct the piecewise affine relaxations required in Assumption
4.1
using

of McCormick relaxations of

. Analogous to a tangent, a subtangent is a hyperplane that passes through a reference point on the underlying relaxation and has a normal vector equal to some subgradient of the relaxation at that point. We randomly pick two points
(17.67,0.68,274.27)
and

on the domain

. Then, a two-piece affine convex relaxation is constructed as the pointwise maximum of the subtangents of the convex McCormick relaxation at these two points:

&1.14V+69.96P-0.08T-79.41\end{aligned}\right\},

and similarly, a two-piece affine concave relaxation is constructed as the pointwise minimum of two subtangents of the concave McCormick relaxations:

f^{\mathrm{cc}}(V,P,T)=\min\left\{\begin{aligned} &1.13V+9.95P-0.08T-10.97,\\
&0.43V+69.95P-0.08T-30.11\end{aligned}\right\}.

Then, with the functions

and

constructed in (
4.6
) and (

), the implicit function relaxations

and

are constructed following (

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{cv}}(P,T)

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{\begin{aligned} &10,\\
&-8.81P+0.07T+9.71,\\
&-162.67P+0.19T+70.02\end{aligned}\right\},

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{cc}}(P,T)

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{\begin{aligned} &70,\\

). Then, at any reference point, subgradients of the implicit function relaxations are computed following Proposition
5.3
, where the required directional derivatives are computed by constructing and solving the auxiliary LP (
5.12
). Constructing the auxiliary LP requires identifying all active constraints at an optimal solution of the optimization problem in (
2.3
). Numerically, we consider a constraint
g_{i}(\hat{\bm{\xi}},\hat{\mathbf{p}})=0
to be active if the value of the residual function
g_{i}(\hat{\bm{\xi}},\hat{\mathbf{p}})
is extremely close to zero, e.g., within a predefined tolerance
1\times 10^{-7}
.

Example 6.3

This example applies the L-derivative-based subgradient evaluation method presented in Section
5.3
to a problem in chemical reactor design. Stuber and Barton
[
31
]
studied a nonlinear equation-based model of a continuous-stirred tank reactor for benzene chlorination. They considered the reactor’s outputs, including the composition of the product stream and the product stream flow rate, as implicit functions of process uncertainties such as the input flow rate and chemical reaction constants. They deployed this model in a semi-infinite global optimization formulation, whose solution requires generating convex and concave relaxations of the implicit functions. The following equation system is adapted from
[
31
, Example 3]
:

\displaystyle p_{3}-z_{1}z_{4}-15r_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-z_{2}z_{4}+15(r_{1}-r_{2})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-z_{3}z_{4}+15r_{2}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 1-z_{1}-z_{2}-z_{3}

\displaystyle=0,

where

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle=p_{1}z_{1}/(0.09z_{1}+0.10z_{2}+0.11z_{3}),

\displaystyle r_{2}

\displaystyle=p_{2}z_{2}/(0.09z_{1}+0.10z_{2}+0.11z_{3}).

In this formulation, the uncertain parameters are
p_{1}\in[0.38,0.42]
,
p_{2}\in[0.053,0.058]
, and
p_{3}\in[8,10]
, and the outputs
z_{i}
are known to have the following bounds:
z_{1}\in[0.10,0.16]
,
z_{2}\in[0.40,0.49]
,
z_{3}\in[0.35,0.45]
, and
z_{4}\in[8,10]
. Since this example has three uncertain parameters and four outputs, the subgradient evaluation methods employed in the previous two examples are not applicable.

Following Adjiman et al.
[
1
]
, we employ the
\alpha
BB relaxations (in their uniform diagonal shift formulation) for each residual function, with the diagonal shift parameter
\alpha
computed using the Scaled Gerschgorin Method. Then, convex and concave relaxations for the implicit functions are generated following Proposition
2.1
. To evaluate subgradients, we construct and solve the sequence of LPs presented in Theorem
5.1
with
\mathbf{M}:=\mathbf{I}
. After solving each LP, we solve an auxiliary LP
[
29
, Proposition 3]
to examine the uniqueness of the obtained optimal solution. As discussed in Remark
5.2
, when the solution is unique, there is no need to solve further LPs in the sequence.

7
Conclusions

This article has presented the first subgradient propagation rules for the implicit function relaxations (
2.3
). These rules were specifically developed to be compatible with the forward AD mode for subgradient propagation
[
23
]
, and effectively allow us to include implicit functions in this method’s elemental function library, whenever we have sufficient access to well-behaved relaxations of the residual function. Future work may involve applying the new subgradient evaluation approaches within global optimization case studies.
