Application of nanotherapy in cardiovascular diseases featuring novel pharmacological mechanisms and drug delivery strategies
Authors: Jing Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of death globally. However, conventional pharmacotherapy is often limited by poor targeting, low bioavailability, and systemic adverse reactions. Nanoformulations, including liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, inorganic nanocarriers, biomimetic systems, and lipid-based carriers, have emerged as transformative platforms that enhance drug solubility, stability, and lesion-specific delivery. This review systematically summarizes the structural features, drug-loading mechanisms, and release strategies of these nanocarriers in major cardiovascular disease indications, such as atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, hypertension, heart failure, and coronary artery disease. Relevant studies on nanoparticles loaded with Western drugs and traditional Chinese medicine monomers are discussed within a unified framework. Furthermore, this review addresses the limitations of endothelial permeability in cardiovascular tissues, translational barriers, and future directions for clinical implementation.Keywords: Nanomedicine, Cardiovascular diseases, Nanocarriers, Targeted drug-delivery, Liposomes, Polymeric nanoparticles, Biomimetic nanoparticles, Clinical translation

Introduction

CVDs rank among the leading causes of mortality globally, presenting significant challenges to public health. According to the 2024 China Cardiovascular Health and Diseases Annual Report, the crude incidence rate of CVDs among Chinese residents aged 18 and older reaches 620.33 per 100,000, encompassing conditions such as acute myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, atherosclerosis (AS), and hypertension [1]. While current pharmacological interventions may have limitations, including side effects, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) treatments are increasingly demonstrating advantages in terms of both efficacy and safety. Notably, nanoformulations and their composite TCM delivery systems have emerged as a prominent research focus in the diagnosis and treatment of CVDs.

Nanocarriers fabricated from diverse materials, including liposomes, polymers such as poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), inorganic nanoparticles like gold nanoparticles, and emerging biomimetic nanoparticles such as cell membrane-coated particles, have been extensively investigated for cardiovascular drug delivery [2, 3]. These nanoparticles can be engineered to respond to pathological microenvironments, including reactive oxygen species (ROS), acidic pH, and specific enzymes, or to external stimuli such as light, ultrasound, and magnetic fields, enabling site-specific drug release. Surface modification with biological membranes, including erythrocyte and leukocyte membranes, can significantly reduce immune system recognition and clearance, prolong circulation time, and confer inherent targeting capabilities [3]. The nanoscale size, high specific surface area, and surface modifiability effectively improve drug solubility and bioavailability [4, 5]. By leveraging their unique physicochemical properties and excellent biocompatibility, nanocarriers overcome the limitations of traditional cardiovascular drug therapies. Nanoformulations achieve precise localization and efficient action of drugs in cardiovascular lesions through passive and active targeting drug delivery mechanisms, significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic side effects.

Nano delivery platforms have ushered in revolutionary advancements in the research and development of cardiovascular therapeutics. By optimizing drug delivery pathways, enhancing targeting capabilities, and improving controlled-release properties, nanotechnology has not only elevated the therapeutic efficacy of drugs but also significantly reduced side effects, thereby improving patients’ quality of life and prognosis. This review systematically examines the current applications of nanodelivery systems in cardiovascular diseases, providing an in-depth analysis of their structural characteristics, release mechanisms, and advancements in application. The aim is to offer theoretical support and reference for research and clinical practice in related fields. This review uniquely presents a systematic comparison of four major nanocarrier platforms (liposomal, polymeric, inorganic, and biomimetic) using consistent evaluation criteria that encompass structural characteristics, drug loading mechanisms, and stimuli-responsive release strategies. The ancient and modern development of nanoformulations and their applications in the medical field

As a product that combines nanotechnology with pharmaceutical formulations, nanoformulations have evolved from simple nanoparticles to complex nanocarrier systems due to their unique physicochemical properties and excellent biocompatibility. With advancements in nanotechnology, various types of nanocarriers have been developed, including PEGylated polymeric drugs, as well as protein- and metal-based nanoparticles, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), and lipid nanocapsules (LNCs) [6, 7]. These can be either naturally derived or synthetically produced [8]. For instance, biodegradable materials such as PLGA are utilized to achieve controlled drug release and targeting [9]. SLNs demonstrate broad application prospects in various disease areas, particularly in tumor therapy, CVDs, infectious diseases, and gene therapy [10–13]. Gold and silver nanoparticles, due to their unique intrinsic optical, magnetic, thermal, and electronic properties, exert multiple mechanisms in the treatment of CVDs, including targeted drug delivery, antioxidation, and tissue regeneration promotion [14–21].

The targeting mechanisms of nanoformulations can be broadly categorized into passive and active targeting. Passive nanoformulations rely on the physicochemical properties of nanoparticles, including size, shape, and charge, as well as the pathophysiological environment, characterized by abnormal vascular permeability in CVDs. This approach leverages the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect to facilitate the natural accumulation of drugs at disease sites [22, 23]. Although the EPR effect was initially described in solid tumors, recent studies have confirmed its significance in CVDs, such as myocardial infarction and myocardial ischemia-reperfusion. In these conditions, damaged myocardial tissue exhibits increased vascular permeability and inflammation-mediated retention, providing a basis for passive targeting [2]. In the context of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, the inflammatory response further enhances the passive accumulation of nanomedicines in the damaged myocardial region by increasing microvascular permeability, which subsequently augments local drug concentration and prolongs the duration of action [24, 25].

The development of active nanoformulations in the treatment of CVDs represents a significant evolution in drug delivery systems, transitioning from passive diffusion to active targeting (Fig. 2). These active nanoformulations utilize surface-modified ligands, including antibodies, peptides, aptamers, and small molecules, to selectively recognize inflammation-related molecules that are overexpressed in cardiovascular lesions, such as VCAM-1 and integrin αvβ3 [26, 27, 28]. In the context of atherosclerosis, nanoparticles designed to target scavenger receptors or mannose receptors are employed to deliver anti-inflammatory drugs or imaging agents, facilitating precise intervention in plaque macrophages [29]. In cases of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, nanoformulations that target mitochondria demonstrate potential in protecting cardiomyocytes and mitigating reperfusion injury by delivering antioxidants or modulating mitochondrial quality control [30]. Moreover, actively targeted nanoformulations directed against thrombi, such as those that utilize ligands for activated platelet surface receptors (e.g., GPIIb/IIIa), enable efficient delivery of thrombolytic agents while minimizing the risk of bleeding [31].

Characteristics of nanomaterial-based delivery platforms for CVDs

Characteristics of liposomal nanoparticles in cardiovascular drug-delivery

Structural characteristics of liposomal nanoparticles

Liposomes are nanocarriers composed of phospholipid bilayers that structurally mimic cell membranes, exhibiting excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability. Due to their amphiphilic nature, liposomes serve as versatile nanocarriers capable of transporting both hydrophobic and hydrophilic molecules, including small molecules, nucleic acids, proteins, and imaging agents. Hydrophobic drugs are incorporated into the lipid bilayer, while hydrophilic drugs are encapsulated within the aqueous core. This enables the effective delivery of various therapeutic agents [32]. As first-generation lipid nanocarriers, SLNs remain solid at body temperature and utilize physiological matrix fatty acids, triglycerides, or steroids, with their outer layer stabilized by emulsifiers such as phospholipids and poloxamers. They are employed to encapsulate both hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs, effectively entrapping and protecting drug molecules, thereby enhancing the drugs’ in- vivo retention time and improving bioavailability [33–36]. From the perspective of drug loading, SLNs demonstrate high encapsulation efficiency (85.7%-98%) and drug loading capacity (16.5%-31.74 mg/g) for both lipophilic and amphiphilic drugs [37, 38]. NLCs were developed as the second generation of SLNs, with their core structural innovation lying in the composition of a mixture of solid lipids and liquid lipids (ratio 70:30 to 99:1) [39]. An irregular lipid matrix structure formed by blending solid lipids with liquid oils creates an imperfect crystalline structure, which enhances drug loading capacity and encapsulation efficiency [40].

Liposomes exhibit diverse compositions, primarily consisting of natural phospholipids, synthetic lipids, cholesterol, and various functionalized modifier molecules. Their structure closely resembles that of biological membranes, facilitating the efficient encapsulation of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. This encapsulation improves drug stability, enhances bioavailability, and enables targeted delivery [32]. Natural phospholipids are sourced from various origins, including plant-based materials such as soybeans and sunflowers, as well as animal-derived lecithin. These components provide excellent biocompatibility and low toxicity to liposomes, granting them significant advantages in drug delivery systems [41, 42]. Based on their structure and morphology, liposomes can be classified into unilamellar vesicles (small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs) with diameters of 20–100 nm, large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs) with diameters of 100–1000 nm, or giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) with diameters > 1000 nm) and multilamellar vesicles (MLVs) with diameters > 500 nm [43]. Drug delivery systems primarily utilize SUVs and smaller MLVs, while GUVs are mainly employed as cell models. Although there is currently a lack of direct application examples in CVDs, the advantages of liposomes in improving drug stability, enhancing bioavailability, and enabling targeted delivery can also be extended to the field of cardiovascular drug delivery.

Drug loading and release mechanisms of liposomes

Liposomes achieve efficient encapsulation of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs through passive and active loading technologies, utilizing smart release mechanisms such as pH response, enzyme response, and temperature triggering to enable precise controlled release at the lesion site. Passive diffusion is the most fundamental release mechanism. The nanoscale size of NLCs (typically < 200 nm) confers passive targeting capability, allowing for accumulation in inflammatory vascular endothelium or damaged myocardial tissue via inflammation-enhanced endothelial permeability. This process is conceptually analogous to the tumor EPR effect [44, 45], but is driven by distinct mechanisms, specifically inflammatory cytokine signaling rather than VEGF-driven angiogenesis. It is important to note that in cardiovascular disease (CVD), this passive accumulation typically occurs over a narrower time window (e.g., approximately 72 h post-myocardial infarction) compared to solid tumors. Studies have demonstrated that NLCs loaded with anti-inflammatory drugs, when administered intraperitoneally in a model of obesity-related cardiovascular complications, can achieve drug concentrations in visceral adipose tissue that are more than twice those in the liver within 24 h, significantly suppressing macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses [46].

In terms of active targeted delivery, SLNs and NLCs can achieve precise delivery through surface modification. For instance, RGD peptide- and PEG-modified SLNs can specifically bind to integrin receptors in myocardial ischemic areas, thereby increasing drug concentration in cardiac tissue by more than threefold [39]. The surface modification capabilities of NLCs, such as conjugation with targeting peptides or antibodies, enable lesion-specific delivery, including targeting CD47-overexpressing cells in atherosclerotic plaques or hypoxic cardiomyocytes in regions of myocardial infarction [44, 47].

NLCs can incorporate stimuli-responsive designs, such as pH- or ROS-sensitive polymers, to trigger drug release in pathological microenvironments. For instance, pH-sensitive designs, including Schiff base-based PEG-bisphenol A derivatives, enable NLCs to rapidly release drugs in the acidic conditions of thrombosis while avoiding systemic bleeding risks [48]. In high-ROS regions of atherosclerotic plaques, thiolated lipid-based NLCs can quickly dissociate and release loaded drugs, thereby enhancing local therapeutic efficacy. The lipid matrix can facilitate drug release at the lesion site through enzymatic degradation, such as by matrix metalloproteinase-2/9, or via oxidative stress responses, including ROS-sensitive bonds [49]. For example, in the ischemia-reperfusion injury model, ROS-responsive NLCs loaded with antioxidant drugs can specifically release these drugs in the high-ROS-concentration environment of the infarct zone, effectively reducing myocardial oxidative damage [50]. The enzymatic degradation mechanism relies on the activity of specific enzymes at the pathological site to achieve intelligent drug release and minimize systemic toxicity [51]. Under pathological conditions, such as hypoxia or inflammation, pH-sensitive liposomes can respond to microenvironmental changes and release drugs for therapeutic effects [33]. By utilizing lipids or polymers containing MMP-sensitive peptide segments, selective degradation and drug release of liposomes at atherosclerotic lesions can be accomplished [51]. Additionally, in myocardial injury models, enzyme-degradable liposomes enable localized drug release by responding to overexpressed proteases, thereby improving myocardial repair and function [51].

Liposomal nanoparticles, despite their excellent biocompatibility and clinical maturity, face limitations in the translation for cardiovascular disease (CVD) applications. These limitations include rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), inefficient passive targeting of CVD lesions, and variability in manufacturing from batch to batch.

Characteristics of polymeric nanoparticles in cardiovascular drug-delivery

Structural characteristics of polymeric nanoparticles

Polymeric nanoparticles consist of an inner core region that encapsulates drugs or active ingredients, surrounded by an outer polymer shell that provides stability and targeting functionality. The core structure is typically made from biodegradable polymers, including natural polymers such as chitosan, alginate, and hyaluronic acid, as well as synthetic polymers like PLGA, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and polyethylene glycol (PEG) [52, 53]. These particles can intelligently traverse biological barriers and achieve targeted delivery through precise control of their size (15–180 nm), surface charge (positive, negative, or neutral), and polymer architecture (such as block co-polymers and core-crosslinked structures) [54, 55]. They can be classified into several types, including single-chain polymeric nanoparticles (SCPNs), core-shell nanoparticles, hybrid polymeric nanoparticles, and molecularly imprinted polymer nanoparticles (MIP-NPs) [56–58].

Polymer nanoparticle PEGylation is the most prevalent method for surface modification. By grafting PEG chains onto the nanoparticle surface, this technique effectively establishes a hydrophilic barrier, reduces plasma protein adsorption, and mitigates macrophage phagocytosis, thereby significantly prolonging the circulation time of nanoparticles [59, 60]. Furthermore, antibody conjugation technology facilitates the targeted recognition of specific molecules at sites of cardiovascular disease by attaching specific antibodies to the nanoparticle surface, which enhances the precision of drug delivery and therapeutic efficiency. Peptide chain modification leverages the high affinity of particular peptide segments to achieve targeted delivery to vascular endothelial cells or inflammatory cells, thereby increasing nanoparticle accumulation in diseased tissues. Additionally, surface-modified nanoparticles have demonstrated remarkable targeting capabilities and therapeutic effects in vascular injury repair and gene delivery [61].

Drug loading characteristics and release mechanisms of polymeric nanoparticles

Drug loading in polymer nanoparticles primarily involves three forms: drug encapsulation, drug adsorption, and covalent conjugation. Drug encapsulation typically achieves stable drug loading through physical entrapment or chemical bonding. For instance, PLGA nanoparticles encapsulating the growth factor ADM-2 have demonstrated sustained release over 21 days, promoting myocardial angiogenesis [62]. Drugs linked via phenylboronic ester bonds are released under oxidative stress conditions, making them suitable for the pathological microenvironment of CVDs [49, 50, 63]. pH-sensitive polymer-drug covalent bonds can cleave in the acidic environment of myocardial ischemia, enabling localized drug release [64]. In terms of the release mechanism, the innovation of polymeric nanoparticles is reflected in three aspects: Firstly, their programmable drug-loading capacity enables the simultaneous encapsulation of hydrophobic drugs (such as statins) and hydrophilic biologics (such as GLP-1 analogs), achieving sustained or triggered drug release through controlled polymer degradation rates [65]. Secondly, surface functionalization modifications (such as PEGylation or targeted ligand conjugation) can further optimize their pharmacokinetic properties, prolonging circulation half-life and enhancing lesion-specific accumulation. Experimental data demonstrate that, although unmodified PLGA nanoparticles can effectively accumulate in atherosclerotic plaques in the ApoE-/-mouse model, prolonged exposure accelerates plaque progression by upregulating inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6. This finding highlights the necessity of surface engineering modification [66]. Thirdly, polymeric nanocarriers can achieve intelligent drug release in response to pathological microenvironmental cues (such as low pH or high ROS levels), enabling targeted anti-inflammatory drug delivery at plaque rupture sites to avoid systemic immunosuppression [24].

Polymeric nanoparticles present significant advantages in drug release mechanisms and surface chemistry; however, they encounter substantial challenges in the clinical translation of cardiovascular disease (CVD) therapies. Formulations based on poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) have been shown to induce inflammation in ApoE-/- mouse models. Additionally, scalability remains a significant hurdle due to the stringent requirements for precise synthesis. Currently, there are only a limited number of CVD trials, with none progressing beyond Phase II, primarily due to issues related to inadequate lesion accumulation and systemic toxicity. Characteristics of inorganic nanoparticles in cardiovascular drug-delivery applications

Structural characteristics of inorganic nanoparticles

Inorganic nanoparticles typically encompass metal nanoparticles, metal oxide nanoparticles, and silicon-based nanoparticles, which exhibit favorable biocompatibility, tunable particle size, surface functionalization capabilities, and excellent drug-delivery capacity. These particles not only serve as efficient drug delivery carriers, protecting drugs from degradation in the bloodstream, but also enable targeted delivery through surface functionalization, while integrating imaging diagnostics and multimodal therapeutic functions. Metal nanoparticles, such as gold and iron oxides, are frequently synthesized and stabilized using functional molecules derived from natural sources. Iron-based metal nanoparticles demonstrate outstanding optical and magnetic properties, offering unique advantages in cardiovascular imaging and diagnostic therapy, particularly superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) [70]. Iron oxide nanoparticles are particularly well-suited for magnetically responsive drug delivery, allowing for targeted control via external magnetic fields [71, 72]. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) feature a highly ordered porous structure with pore sizes ranging from 2 to 50 nm, exhibiting high drug-loading capacity and controllable release properties, which enable the delivery of various cardiovascular drugs. Their surfaces can be PEGylated to prolong circulation time in the bloodstream while maintaining excellent biodegradability. MSNs degrade into harmless silicates in vivo, which are excreted renally, demonstrating high safety [73, 74].

Drug loading characteristics and release mechanisms of inorganic nanoparticles

Inorganic nanoparticle delivery strategies are categorized into passive targeting and active targeting. Passive targeting for inorganic nanoparticle-based CVD drug delivery relies on inflammation-associated endothelial leakiness—a process that shares the conceptual framework of the tumor enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect but is driven by distinct inflammatory mechanisms with a more restricted temporal window in CVD lesions (e.g., approximately 72 h post-myocardial infarction) compared to sustained tumor angiogenesis. For instance, Chen et al. developed multifunctional silica-iron oxide nanoparticles (Fe₃O₄ grown on mesoporous foam silica), which leverage their nanometer size to achieve passive targeting to myocardial injury sites via this inflammation-associated endothelial leakiness. In a rat ischemia-reperfusion model, this approach increased the left ventricular ejection fraction by 11% to 21% [75]. In contrast, active targeting involves functionalizing nanoparticles with ligands for specific molecular recognition. The RGD peptide (arginine-glycine-aspartic acid) can specifically recognize integrin αvβ3 and has been widely used to modify gold nanorods or iron oxide nanoparticles for targeted imaging and therapy of neovessels in atherosclerotic plaques [76].

Inorganic nanoparticles offer the advantage of integrating diagnostic and therapeutic functions; however, they also present significant safety concerns. For instance, iron oxide nanoparticles have been shown to induce cardiac stress, while gold nanoparticles are associated with issues of organ retention. Additionally, silicon dioxide nanoparticles may result in fibrosis. As of now, only ferumoxytol has received approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for experimental research in the context of CVDs. Characteristics of biomimetic nanoparticles in cardiovascular drug-delivery applications

Structural characteristics of biomimetic nanoparticles

Biomimetic coating membranes are crucial for the “camouflage” and functionalization of biomimetic nanoparticles, sourced from various cell membranes, including those of red blood cells, platelets, macrophages, stem cells, and tumor cells. Each type of cell membrane possesses a unique surface protein profile, which endows the nanoparticles with distinct biological functions [79]. These membrane structures retain the surface proteins and receptors of the original cells, enabling them not only to effectively evade immune system clearance and prolong drug circulation time in vivo but also to achieve precise targeting of pathological sites and controlled drug release [3, 80]. For instance, platelet membrane-coated nanoparticles exploit platelets’ natural affinity for atherosclerotic plaques, facilitating specific delivery to lesions, significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity [80]. Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles (Neu-LNPs) achieve targeted binding to inflamed myocardium through surface-expressed LFA-1/Mac-1/ICAM-1 [81].

The core materials of biomimetic nanoparticles can be categorized into three main types: polymers, inorganic nanoparticles, and liposomes. Among these, polymers such as PLGA and PCL exhibit excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, allowing for drug loading through encapsulation or adsorption, and enabling sustained release via slow degradation, which consequently reduces systemic toxicity [82]. Gold nanoparticles are utilized for photothermal therapy, while iron oxide nanoparticles function as contrast agents for magnetic resonance imaging, facilitating real-time monitoring of lesions and the assessment of myocardial infarction [83]. Additionally, liposomes, which are composed of lipid bilayers, can simultaneously encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs, and they readily fuse with cell membranes, thereby enhancing drug delivery efficiency [84].

Drug loading characteristics and release mechanisms of biomimetic nanoparticles

Drug loading strategies for cardiovascular biomimetic nanoparticles encompass physical encapsulation, chemical conjugation, and self-assembly. Physical encapsulation employs non-covalent interactions to accommodate hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs. Notably, platelet membrane-coated nanoparticles have been shown to inhibit platelet aggregation [85]. Chemical coupling utilizes covalent bonds, such as amide, disulfide, and hydrazone linkages, to attach drugs to nanoparticle surfaces or polymer chains [86]. Environmentally responsive linkers, including pH-sensitive hydrazone and GSH-sensitive disulfide, facilitate targeted drug release. The conjugation of RGD peptides or anti-CD47 antibodies significantly enhances plaque targeting (approximately 2.5-fold) and prolongs circulation half-life (over 24 h), respectively [87]. Self-assembly of amphiphilic molecules allows for high drug loading, uniform particle size, and targeted delivery. By exploiting the specific binding of vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) to its ligand, self-assembled nanostructures can be designed to target the endothelium of atherosclerotic plaques [88].

The efficacy of biomimetic nanoparticles is influenced by the source of the membrane, the properties of the core, and the strategies employed on the surface. Reviews indicate that the type of membrane plays a critical role in determining the targeting capabilities; for instance, platelet membranes are effective in targeting plaques, macrophage membranes are suited for targeting inflamed endothelium, and cardiac membranes are designed to target damaged myocardium [89]. Hybrid membranes, such as platelet-fused stem cell nanovesicles, enhance targeting in atherosclerosis [90]. Additionally, macrophage membrane-coated nanoparticles are effective in addressing multi-organ inflammation in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome [91]. The integration of biomimetic coatings with stimuli-responsive cores facilitates controlled drug release within cardiovascular disease microenvironments [92].

Bionic nanodrug release mechanisms are reliant on physiological or pathological microenvironment-triggered stimuli, including pH responsiveness, enzyme responsiveness, temperature responsiveness, and redox responsiveness. The pH-responsive mechanism capitalizes on the acidic characteristics prevalent in the microenvironments of CVDs. For example, erythrocyte membrane-coated bionic nanoparticles facilitate drug release under low pH conditions via pH-sensitive bonds, specifically targeting lesion sites. This approach was initially developed for tumor microenvironments but has been effectively repurposed for CVDs due to its analogous pH-responsive mechanism [93]. Targeted delivery is achieved through the exploitation of the overexpression of specific enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). For instance, MMP substrate peptides like GPLGVRG act as linkers that are selectively cleaved in myocardial infarction regions characterized by high MMP-2/9 expression, thereby triggering nanoparticle aggregation or drug release [94]. Temperature-responsive release mechanisms typically utilize localized temperature increases, such as those occurring during hyperthermia or fever at inflammatory sites, to induce structural changes in carriers, thereby enhancing drug release. For example, biomimetic nanoparticles can detect inflammatory signals through membrane proteins, such as integrins, and subsequently release anti-inflammatory drugs, such as statins, or thrombolytic enzymes in response to temperature stimulation [95]. The pathological microenvironment of CVDs, including myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury, is often associated with significantly elevated levels of ROS. By incorporating redox-responsive groups, such as disulfide bonds and phenylboronic acid esters, biomimetic nanoparticles can induce drug release or structural dissociation in high-ROS environments, thus achieving targeted therapy [92, 96].

These advances demonstrate that biomimetic nanoparticles not only overcome the bottlenecks of conventional nanocarriers, such as immunogenicity and insufficient targeting, but also open up new avenues (Fig. 3) for the precise treatment of CVDs (e.g., myocardial infarction, atherosclerosis) through modular design (e.g., membrane coating selection, responsive group modification). Penetration factors and regulation strategies of nanoparticles in CVDs

Nanocarrier endothelial permeability in CVDs differs fundamentally from the tumor EPR effect. In atherosclerotic plaques, disrupted tight junctions and glycocalyx shedding create paracellular gaps that allow the passive extravasation of nanoparticles ranging from 50 to 200 nm [100, 22]. The physicochemical properties of these nanoparticles critically regulate transendothelial permeability. Specifically, cholesterol content governs liposomal membrane rigidity, with optimal ratios balancing bilayer stability and deformability for junctional passage [101]. Additionally, surface charge influences permeability: cationic liposomes exploit electrostatic interactions with the anionic glycocalyx to enhance adsorptive transcytosis, whereas neutral or anionic formulations exhibit reduced uptake [102]. The intact glycocalyx on healthy endothelium serves as a size-selective barrier; its degradation in plaques paradoxically enhances nanoparticle access [18].

In myocardial infarction, the permeability landscape is reversed: neovascularization results in vessels with thickened basement membranes and pericyte coverage that impede extravasation, while extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, characterized by collagen and negatively charged glycosaminoglycans, creates both physical and electrostatic barriers [46, 103]. After uptake, nanocarriers are largely sequestered in endolysosomes, leading to payload degradation before reaching intracellular targets [104].

Three strategies can be employed to overcome these barriers. Mechanically, deformable liposomes (70–100 nm) with edge activators achieve 2–3-fold greater interendothelial gap penetration compared to rigid ones [105, 9]. Chemically, pH-sensitive liposomes bearing hydrazone-linked PEG sheddable coatings and ROS-responsive thioether-linked liposomes can trigger ECM degradation and facilitate deep tissue penetration [106, 107]. Lastly, dual-targeted synthetic nanoparticles capable of macrophage-specific recognition through optimized ligand composition significantly enhance lesion accumulation and therapeutic efficacy in atherosclerotic plaques [29, 108].

Among the four nanocarrier platforms, biomimetic nanoparticles emerge as the most promising option for treating CVDs. In contrast, liposomal and polymeric nanoparticles face limitations such as inadequate penetration and potential inflammation risks, while inorganic nanoparticles raise concerns regarding long-term safety. Biomimetic systems integrate multiple advantages, including immune evasion and targeted delivery, with modular designs that can be adapted to complex disease environments. Their clinical success is contingent upon advancements in manufacturing processes and thorough safety evaluations.

Advances in the application of nanoformulations in major therapeutic areas of CVDs

Nanotherapeutic approaches for atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is one of the CVDs with the highest global mortality rates, and recent research has focused on achieving intelligent drug delivery to lesion sites through nanotechnology. This approach targets key pathological processes of atherosclerosis, such as inflammation, oxidative stress, lipid metabolism disorders, and endothelial repair.

The use of nanocarriers facilitates the efficient delivery of anti-inflammatory drugs, increases drug concentration at lesion sites, enhances anti-inflammatory effects, and reduces systemic toxicity. A nanoparticle constructed from cRGD peptide-targeted and pH-sensitive polyketal (PK3) material has been developed for the delivery of rapamycin (RAP). This nanosystem is capable of specifically releasing drugs in the acidic microenvironment of plaques, effectively suppressing inflammation and slowing plaque progression [135]. Macrophage-targeted nanocarriers loaded with LXR agonists have been shown to mitigate atherosclerosis by reducing inflammatory responses [136]. Additionally, liposomes encapsulating ω-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), can stabilize plaque structure and prevent plaque rupture by promoting the transformation of macrophages into the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby reducing inflammatory cell infiltration and enhancing collagen content [137].

Nanopreparations loaded with natural antioxidants have garnered widespread attention due to their excellent biocompatibility and multifunctionality. Nanozymes, an emerging class of antioxidant nanomaterials, mimic the activity of natural antioxidant enzymes and enable precise drug release in response to the high oxidative stress environment within plaques, thereby inhibiting the activation of inflammatory cells and slowing plaque progression [138]. By modifying specific ligands, such as anti-oxidized LDL antibodies, they achieve targeted recognition of oxidized LDL in atherosclerotic plaques and controlled drug release [139]. Cerium oxide (CeO2) nanoparticles can simulate the activity of natural antioxidant enzymes, scavenge excess ROS, alleviate oxidative damage to vascular endothelial cells, and improve vascular function [136, 140]. Red blood cell-platelet hybrid membrane-coated nanoparticles ([RBC-P] NPs) target monocytes in atherosclerotic plaques through the CXCL8-CXCR2 signaling axis, significantly inhibiting plaque progression [141]. For dyslipidemia, synthetic high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-mimicking nanoparticles have been developed to promote reverse cholesterol transport in foam cells, significantly improving lipid metabolism and reducing plaque lipid burden [142, 143]. Studies have confirmed that an S2P-conjugated lipid nanoparticle not only inhibits CD36-mediated lipid uptake but also enhances ABCG1-mediated cholesterol efflux and reverse transport [144]. Research demonstrates that utilizing nanocarriers to deliver siRNA can effectively silence key genes regulating lipid metabolism, thereby reducing hyperlipidemia [145]. In animal models of hyperlipidemia and atherosclerosis, the NLCs formulation significantly improved lipid metabolism, reduced serum levels of total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides, while increasing high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [146].

Nanotechnology has enabled the precise targeted delivery and intelligent responsive release of anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and lipid-lowering drugs for the treatment of atherosclerosis, significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity. However, current studies are predominantly limited to preclinical models, and the long-term safety, synthetic uniformity, and clinical translational feasibility of these nanosystems have yet to be systematically validated.

Myocardial infarction nanoformulations

Myocardial infarction is caused by ischemia, hypoxia, and necrosis resulting from coronary obstruction. The design of nanomaterials for myocardial infarction primarily targets vascular endothelial cells, inflammatory cells, and fibroblasts. This is achieved through surface-modified nanoparticles that specifically recognize endothelial receptors or inflammation-related molecules, modulate oxidative stress pathways, and regulate cardiomyocyte apoptosis and repair [147, 148, 149].Additionally, nanoformulated growth factors, such as VEGF and bFGF, promote myocardial angiogenesis and enhance ischemic repair [150].

LNP platforms enable cardiac mRNA/siRNA delivery. LNPs with VEGF-C mRNA stimulate lymphangiogenesis, reduce inflammation, and promote heart repair post-MI [151, 152]. Cardiac-homing peptide-nanoliposomes deliver atorvastatin to ischemic myocardium for repair [153]. Naturally-derived EVs/exosomes from MSCs, endothelial cells, and cardiomyocytes are emerging MI nanoformulations. MSC exosomal ZEB1 alleviates hypoxia/reperfusion-induced apoptosis, oxidative, and ER stress via UBIAD1 [154]. Endothelial small EVs with miR-410-3p protect cardiomyocytes from apoptosis in I/R injury via Smad7 pathway [155]. Magnetically labeled iPSC-EVs enable MRI/MPI-guided regenerative therapy [156]. Hypoxic cardiomyocyte exosomes attenuate myocardial injury via ANP-mediated M2 macrophage polarization, expanding EV-based therapy [157]. Nanocarrier delivered stem cell factors/miR-153-3p reduce apoptosis, inflammation, fibrosis, and scar, enhancing cardiomyocyte survival [158, 159], antioxidant/anti-inflammatory delivery clears ROS, reduces apoptosis, and aids recovery [109].

A self-assembled ECM-LNP composite facilitates cardiac-specific siRNA delivery, enhancing post-myocardial infarction (MI) repair while minimizing off-target effects [103]. The single-dose delivery of miR199a-5p via targeted nanoparticles provides sustained cardiac protection through AGTR1 inhibition and MARK4 modulation [160]. Stimuli-responsive nanocarriers enable controlled drug release in the ischemic microenvironment [161]. Inflammation-targeted nanomedicines mitigate oxidative stress and reprogram macrophage polarization, thereby improving post-MI cardiac function [162]. CD11b-modified ROS/pH-responsive nanoparticles co-deliver Dioscin/siRNA, which reduces neutrophil recruitment and promotes post-MI repair [163]. Selenium-encrusted Prussian blue nanozymes enhance mitophagy and ROS scavenging, thereby ameliorating injury [164]. ROS-responsive hydrogels scavenge ROS and release therapeutics, thereby modulating the infarct microenvironment [165]. Bioinspired spatiotemporal-responsive nanoparticles regulate mitochondrial repair and inhibit fibrosis in acute MI [166]. This immunomodulatory strategy, when combined with ROS-responsive release, presents a synergistic framework for addressing the complexities of the post-MI microenvironment.

Cell membrane-coated nanoparticles represent a potent biomimetic strategy for targeted myocardial infarction (MI) therapy [167]. Macrophage membrane-coated nanocarriers specifically target inflamed cardiac tissue for precise delivery to the infarct site. An emerging avenue of research involves anti-pyroptosis biomimetic nanoplatforms: Wang et al. developed a puerarin-loaded nanosystem that effectively suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, thereby preserving cardiac function following MI [168]. Furthermore, HDL/LDL-bioinspired nanocarriers facilitate acellular targeted delivery of icariin, enhancing cardiomyocyte uptake and promoting cardiac repair through endogenous lipoprotein pathways [169]. Fusogenic hybrid gelled nanovesicles, which carry mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) cargo and are modified with cholesterol from macrophage membranes, achieve improved cardiac retention and therapeutic efficacy [170]. Liposome-macrophage membrane co-assemblies mitigate post-MI cardiac insufficiency by enhancing immune evasion and targeting [171]. Dual-biomimetic nanodecoys reprogram cardiac macrophages through STING suppression, facilitating immunomodulatory repair [172]. Polarized mesoporous silica nanocarriers loaded with ISG15 siRNA remodel the immune microenvironment, thereby ameliorating heart failure post-MI [173]. Self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) encoding Nppa, administered via intramuscular injection, has been shown to improve cardiac function in preclinical models of MI [174]. Additionally, antibody-conjugated PLGA@polydopamine nanoparticles loaded with SIS3 reduce cardiac fibrosis by modulating Smad3 in myofibroblasts [175].

In our assessment, myocardial infarction (MI) nanoformulations represent the highest-impact opportunity among the five therapeutic areas discussed. The convergence of biomimetic targeting, lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-based nucleic acid therapy, and stimuli-responsive release creates a unique therapeutic window for spatiotemporal drug delivery to ischemic tissue—a capability that current standard-of-care approaches cannot match. Furthermore, the urgent clinical need for post-infarction myocardial repair, coupled with the lack of effective pharmacological alternatives, positions MI nanotherapeutics as the most compelling candidate for near-term clinical translation.

Hypertension and vascular function regulation

Hypertension, one of the most prevalent chronic diseases worldwide, poses a significant public health challenge. Key pathological mechanisms contributing to the sustained progression of hypertension include vascular endothelial dysfunction, structural remodeling of smooth muscle cells, vasomotion, oxidative stress, and lipid metabolism. The use of nanoformulations for the delivery of antihypertensive drugs offers unique advantages, such as improved drug bioavailability, prolonged therapeutic duration, reduced dosing frequency, and minimized systemic side effects [176, 177]. For example, nanocarriers made from biodegradable polymers like PLGA facilitate sustained release and targeted delivery of antihypertensive medications, significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy and safety [178, 179]. Additionally, pH-responsive nanocarriers can trigger drug release in the slightly acidic conditions of the local vascular microenvironment, enabling precise controlled release [180].

Nanopreparations demonstrate significant potential applications in the regulation of vasomotor function and antioxidant therapy. In the context of renin-angiotensin system (RAS)-related hypertension, the nanocarrier delivery of ACE inhibitors, AT1 receptor antagonists, and renin inhibitors has exhibited promising potential [181, 182]. Furthermore, nanocarriers can inhibit the expression of vasoconstrictive factors, such as endothelin, by delivering anti-inflammatory drugs or genes, thereby alleviating vasoconstriction and modulating vascular tone [183]. Additionally, nanopreparations can mitigate vascular inflammatory responses by reducing the expression of inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which lessens endothelial cell damage and preserves vascular barrier function [184, 185]. Plant-derived alkaloids, such as rhynchophylline, are loaded onto nanoparticles to exert their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and vasoprotective effects, effectively alleviating vascular inflammation and oxidative stress induced by hypertension [186]. Moreover, polymeric nanoparticles not only serve as antioxidants but also integrate drug-delivery functions. For instance, simvastatin nanoparticles are delivered to the lungs via dry powder inhalation (DPI), thereby avoiding the first-pass effect and enhancing local drug concentration [187].

The lipid matrix of NLCs can regulate the drug release rate, achieving sustained release effects that reduce dosing frequency and help maintain stable blood drug concentrations, thereby alleviating the medication burden on patients.

Nanomedicine for heart failure treatment

Heart failure is a clinical syndrome characterized by impaired cardiac structure or function, resulting in inadequate blood flow to meet the body’s metabolic demands. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying heart failure are complex and multifaceted, involving left and right ventricular dysfunction, myocardial remodeling, neuroendocrine activation, inflammatory responses, fibrosis, and metabolic abnormalities [188–190].

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant nanoformulations play a pivotal role in managing heart failure by enhancing myocardial function through the alleviation of inflammation and oxidative stress. These nanoformulation carriers deliver antioxidants or cytoprotective factors, effectively reducing myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury and preserving cardiomyocyte function via precise targeting and sustained release [191–193]. Nanoparticles can efficiently deliver anti-inflammatory drugs, such as glucocorticoids and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), leveraging their small size and flexible surface modifications to achieve targeted delivery to inflammatory sites [194]. Furthermore, biomimetic nanoparticles, including multifunctional nanocarriers fused with platelet and red blood cell membranes, can enhance targeting capabilities toward inflammatory cells, enabling controlled drug release and minimizing the toxic side effects associated with long-term medication [195].

Nanoparticle-based formulations can effectively regulate fibrosis-related signaling pathways, reverse or inhibit myocardial fibrosis, and control the progression of heart failure by delivering targeted antifibrotic drugs or genes. For instance, nanoparticles containing cardiomyocyte-specific peptides have been shown to significantly enhance the expression of α-SMA protein in cardiomyocytes, thereby promoting myocardial repair and improving cardiac function [196]. Additionally, nanocarriers that deliver siRNA to suppress the fibrosis-related signaling molecule EGFR can reduce myocardial inflammatory responses and the severity of fibrosis [103].

Nanomedicine for coronary heart disease treatment

Coronary artery disease (CAD), also referred to as coronary heart disease, remains one of the most prevalent and severe CVDs worldwide, constituting a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally. Nanomedicines exhibit unique and multifaceted advantages in the management of coronary heart disease, including targeted anti-atherosclerotic therapy, antithrombotic effects, antioxidative stress modulation, and the promotion of vascular repair and regeneration [197]. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-like nanoparticles loaded with paclitaxel (LDE-paclitaxel) have demonstrated potential anti-atherosclerotic activity in CAD patients by selectively targeting plaque macrophages and reducing neointimal formation [198]. Recent advances have expanded this concept to high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-mimetic nanotherapeutics, which replicate the beneficial functions of endogenous HDL—including reverse cholesterol transport, anti-inflammatory signaling, and endothelial protection [199]. These designer HDL nanoparticles actively target monocytes and macrophages within atherosclerotic lesions, promoting macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and hindering the progression of atherosclerosis [199]. Furthermore, engineered ApoA1-ApoM fusion proteins that form HDL-like particles and bind sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) have been shown to stimulate endothelial barrier function and suppress cytokine-induced vascular inflammation through S1P receptor signaling [200, 201]. Biomimetic nanocarriers coated with red blood cell membranes and functionalized with CREKA peptides can scavenge H₂O₂ and provide controlled release of tirofiban, achieving enhanced antithrombotic activity [202]. Positively charged fibrin-mimicking chitosan nanoparticles can efficiently bind activated platelets, enabling precise drug release at the thrombus site while effectively inhibiting platelet aggregation and thrombus formation, all with favorable biocompatibility and low bleeding risk [203]. Clot-targeted liposomal nanomedicines containing H₂O₂-activatable hybrid prodrugs demonstrate superior antithrombotic efficacy through localized drug activation [204]. Metal oxide nanoparticles (such as Au, ZnO, and MgO) can significantly suppress the expression of pro-inflammatory factors TNF-α and NF-κB in macrophages and lymphocytes of coronary heart disease patients, exerting anti-inflammatory effects with low toxicity [205]. The mechanistic basis involves ROS scavenging, modulation of redox-sensitive signaling cascades, and induction of cytoprotective antioxidant responses [206]. Nanocarriers can deliver growth factors (such as VEGF and bFGF) and genetic drugs (such as siRNA and miRNA) to promote vascular endothelial cell repair and neo-vascularization, thereby improving vascular function [207]. Nanocarriers offer a comprehensive platform that integrates lipid regulation, antithrombotic, antioxidant, and angiogenic effects for the treatment of coronary heart disease. When combined with genetic evidence such as SNP rs515135 and polygenic risk scores [208, 209, 210], nanocarrier-based gene-delivery systems hold promise for precisely modulating key molecular drivers, providing a highly promising avenue for reversing coronary atherosclerosis from its genetic and cellular roots.

Recent advances in extracellular vesicle (EV)-based therapeutics have opened new avenues for the management of coronary heart disease. A landmark study demonstrated that EV-mediated delivery of VEGF-A mRNA effectively rescues ischemic myocardial tissue with remarkably low immunogenicity, highlighting the translational potential of EV-based nucleic acid therapeutics for coronary revascularization and myocardial salvage [211]. In a complementary approach, platelet membrane-fused mesenchymal stem cell-derived nanovesicles achieve synergistic targeting of both activated endothelium and plaque macrophages, enabling dual-hit therapeutic intervention against the atherosclerotic progression underlying CHD [90]. Emerging diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for coronary heart disease have been comprehensively reviewed, encompassing nanoparticle-based imaging, targeted drug delivery, and theranostic platforms that integrate real-time disease monitoring with localized therapy [212]. These developments collectively underscore the rapid evolution of nanomedicine from a passive drug carrier paradigm to actively targeted, multifunctional platforms for precision management of coronary heart disease.

Among the five areas identified, we consider MI nanoformulations to be the most impactful. The convergence of biomimetic targeting, lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-nucleic acid therapy, and stimuli-responsive release facilitates spatiotemporal delivery to ischemic tissue. Given the urgent need for post-infarction repair, MI nanotherapeutics emerge as a priority for clinical translation. Nanoformulation applications across CVDs are provided in Table 4.

Applications of cardiovascular nanomedicine

Application of western drug-loaded nanoparticles in CVDs

Nanoformulations for atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis nanotherapy has evolved beyond simple drug encapsulation to sophisticated strategies targeting macrophage-driven inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and lipid dysregulation [213, 214, 215]. Current nanoformulations, based on therapeutic mechanisms, are categorized into three complementary approaches: (i) immunomodulatory targeting of plaque macrophages, (ii) autophagy induction for foam cell clearance and plaque stabilization, and (iii) antioxidant nanoplatforms for redox regulation [216, 217, 218]. This mechanistic classification provides a framework for comparing the merits and translational potential of various nanocarrier designs. Among macrophage-targeting strategies, lipid-based and polymeric carriers exhibit complementary mechanisms. Corticosteroid-loaded lipid nanoparticles achieve precise macrophage targeting via scavenger receptor-mediated uptake, thereby attenuating local inflammation without systemic immunosuppression [219]. PLGA nanoparticles loaded with pioglitazone selectively reduce pro-inflammatory Ly6C^high monocytes while upregulating M2 markers, which increases fibrous cap thickness [220]. MOF-based nanoplatforms that co-deliver rapamycin and IL-1 receptor antagonists synergistically suppress atherosclerosis through concurrent modulation of mTOR and IL-1β, representing a more integrated combinatorial approach [221]. These two platforms target macrophage polarization through orthogonal pathways—glucocorticoid receptor signaling versus PPARγ activation—suggesting mechanistic complementarity. The third strategy employs antioxidant nanocarriers (e.g., selenium and cerium nanoparticles) that inhibit the release of inflammatory mediators and support vascular repair [222]. However, these inorganic nanoparticles face distinct translational challenges compared to polymeric and lipid-based systems, including poorly characterized long-term biodistribution and potential metal ion toxicity. Despite these conceptual advances, several limitations warrant consideration.

Nanoformulations for hypertension

The pathophysiology of hypertension is characterized by endothelial dysfunction, overactivation of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), vascular smooth muscle cell remodeling, and oxidative stress—these processes create multiple targets for nanoformulations. Antihypertensive nanomedicine has primarily utilized two lipid-based carriers: SLNs, which facilitate sustained release and enhance solubility, and NLCs, known for their higher drug-loading capacity and superior physical stability [12, 223]. The drugs delivered through these systems include angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), neprilysin inhibitors, and renin inhibitors, each presenting unique formulation challenges [84, 224].SLN-based strategies specifically address the issue of low oral bioavailability. For instance, irbesartan, an ARB with poor solubility, encapsulated in hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin-modified SLNs, achieved nearly tenfold higher bioavailability compared to conventional suspensions in rats, with prolonged action [225, 226]. Sacubitril-loaded SLNs demonstrated sustained release over 24 h, with approximately 94% encapsulation efficiency and about 62% inhibition of human aortic smooth muscle cell proliferation [227]. Similarly, propranolol SLNs facilitate sustained delivery. However, for lipophilic CCBs, SLNs encounter drug-loading constraints, prompting the development of NLCs. Lacidipine NLCs, for example, increased oral bioavailability by 3.45-fold and improved patient compliance [228]. This evolution from SLNs to NLCs for CCB delivery underscores the necessity for carrier selection to be tailored to the specific properties of the drug and the clinical requirements. While SLNs provide a well-established platform for straightforward sustained release (as seen with propranolol, irbesartan, and sacubitril), NLCs offer superior loading capabilities for lipophilic CCBs and have progressed further towards clinical validation.

Nanoformulations for myocardial infarction

Myocardial infarction (MI) presents a uniquely challenging therapeutic microenvironment characterized by ischemia-reperfusion injury, substantial oxidative stress, inflammatory cell infiltration, and cardiomyocyte apoptosis, necessitating nanocarrier intervention within a narrow therapeutic window [229, 230, 232, 233]. Current nanoformulation strategies for MI can be classified into three mechanistic approaches: (i) modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment through targeted immunomodulation, (ii) scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via antioxidant-loaded carriers, and (iii) delivery of nucleic acids and small molecules for tissue repair and regeneration [231, 232, 235, 237]. Among anti-inflammatory strategies, pioglitazone-loaded NLCs reduce the risk of fibrous cap rupture by selectively targeting Ly6C^high inflammatory monocytes, thereby highlighting the precision of carrier-mediated immunomodulation [123, 231]. Concurrently, NLCs effectively encapsulate antioxidants such as astaxanthin and statins, utilizing enhanced solubility and bioavailability to significantly improve ROS clearance and mitigate ischemia-reperfusion injury [232, 233]. For tissue repair, miR-21-loaded fusogenic liposomes transfect cardiomyocytes, promote pro-reparative macrophage polarization, and support myocardial regeneration [234]. Cardiac-targeted poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles deliver cardioprotective agents, resulting in significant improvement in cardiac function following ischemia-reperfusion [25]. Furthermore, SHP1 inhibitor-loaded polymeric nanoparticles markedly enhance the clearance of apoptotic cardiomyocytes, suppress post-infarction inflammation, and improve cardiac function in murine MI models [235].

Despite significant advancements in biomimetic targeting, LNP-based nucleic acid delivery, and stimuli-responsive release mechanisms, several challenges persist. Future research should prioritize the development of multifunctional nanoplatforms that integrate inflammation modulation with active tissue repair within a single carrier. Additionally, it is essential to explore the incorporation of real-time imaging guidance to enable spatiotemporally controlled drug release.

Application of traditional Chinese medicine monomer-loaded nanoparticles in CVDs

Nanoformulations of traditional Chinese medicine for atherosclerosis

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) monomers offer a multi-target paradigm for atherosclerosis by addressing inflammation, oxidative stress, macrophage polarization, and lipid metabolism, in contrast to Western drugs that typically target single pathways [236, 237]. Nanoformulation strategies encompass: (i) antioxidant/anti-inflammatory delivery, where SLN-encapsulated quercetin and curcumin activate Nrf2 and inhibit NF-κB [238], liposomal astaxanthin enhances endothelial uptake [112], and resveratrol nanocarriers improve bioavailability [239]. Additionally, Berberine and resveratrol activate the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, providing protection against oxidative damage [240]; (ii) macrophage-polarizing nanocarriers, with various TCM bioactives, such as ginsenosides (e.g., Rb1, Rg3), berberine, curcumin, and tanshinone IIA, effectively suppressing the M1 phenotype [241]. This dual action—promoting M2 while inhibiting M1—represents a more nuanced and effective approach than merely blocking a single inflammatory pathway; (iii) multifunctional co-assembled platforms, where ginsenoside Rb1 combined with psoralen in RPNPs achieves antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and lipid-regulating effects [242], and schisandrin-loaded pH-responsive β-cyclodextrin nanoparticles also demonstrate similar properties [242]. The paradigm of Traditional Chinese medicine is evolving from a single-agent approach to a multi-dimensional anti-atherosclerosis strategy, yet it faces challenges such as standardization. Looking ahead, it is essential to integrate traditional wisdom with precision medicine.

Nanoformulations of traditional Chinese medicine for myocardial infarction

Traditional Chinese medicine monomers and their nanoparticle formulations exhibit significant potential as complementary therapeutic strategies due to their multi-target and multi-pathway cardioprotective characteristics. These nanoformulations incorporate bioactive constituents from traditional herbal medicines, aiming to achieve various therapeutic effects, including anti-apoptosis, antioxidant effects, and pro-angiogenesis, thereby providing novel approaches for the treatment of myocardial infarction [243, 244]. Tanshinone IIA (TIIA) and Formononetin (FM), two active compounds derived from Salvia and Astragalus, respectively, are encapsulated in a neutrophil-targeted nanodrug known as TF-5NP. Research has demonstrated that this nanodrug effectively reduces oxidative damage following myocardial infarction and promotes angiogenesis [244]. SLNs containing ferulic acid enhance cardioprotection by improving cellular uptake [245]. Additionally, the silk fibroin-quercetin nanocomposite facilitates myocardial fiber regeneration through a core-shell design [246]. A key advantage of these nanophytomedicines lies in their synergistic potential when used in conjunction with or as a complement to Western therapies.

Nanoformulations of traditional Chinese medicine for hypertension

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) monomer nanoformulations for hypertension leverage the unique vasoprotective properties of plant-derived compounds. These properties include vasodilation through nitric oxide modulation, anti-inflammatory signaling, and inhibition of vascular remodeling, which collectively address the multifactorial pathophysiology of hypertension beyond mere blood pressure reduction. Isoliensinine nanoparticles (PEG-PLGA@Isoliensinine) exemplify this multi-target advantage, demonstrating effective antihypertensive effects and comprehensive vascular protective functions in animal models with mild-to-moderate hypertension—an unusual achievement, considering that most nanoformulations remain in preclinical development [247]. Based on extensive literature evidence, isoflavone-loaded PEG-PLGA nanocarriers possess significant potential to enhance targeting of vascular smooth muscle cells, effectively alleviating hypertension and related vascular lesions by improving solubility, protecting active ingredients, enabling targeted delivery, and facilitating controlled release [248]. Similar challenges are observed with baicalin (BA). Although this flavonoid, extracted from Scutellaria baicalensis, demonstrates therapeutic effects on various respiratory diseases, including pulmonary arterial hypertension, its oral bioavailability and solubility remain unsatisfactory [249].

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that nanoparticle delivery technologies can simultaneously enhance the efficacy and safety of both traditional Chinese medicine monomers (TCM) and Western drugs in the treatment of CVDs. In the context of atherosclerosis, TCM nanoformulations exert multi-target regulation of inflammation and oxidative stress, while Western drug nanoformulations achieve precise targeting of macrophage phenotypes and autophagy mechanisms. These complementary mechanisms synergistically improve plaque stability. In cases of hypertension and myocardial infarction, both categories of treatments overcome conventional pharmacokinetic limitations by promoting vascular function, scavenging ROS, and regulating apoptosis and repair pathways, thereby achieving sustained reductions in blood pressure and myocardial protection. From a strategic perspective, we propose that an integrated framework combining Western drug-loaded and TCM monomer-loaded nanoparticles represents the superior therapeutic strategy. Western nanoformulations provide well-defined molecular targets and established pharmacokinetic profiles suitable for acute interventions, whereas TCM-based nanoformulations offer multi-target, multi-pathway regulation that is ideally suited for chronic management and maintenance therapy. Translational barriers for cardiovascular nanoformulations

Biological barriers and obstacles in the internal microenvironment

Nanoparticles entering the bloodstream are rapidly cleared by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), resulting in a short circulation half-life [250]. While polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification can prolong circulation time [251], repeated administration may induce the formation of anti-PEG antibodies, which can trigger the accelerated blood clearance (ABC) phenomenon and diminish therapeutic efficacy [84]. Consequently, alternative ‘stealth’ strategies to PEG, such as biomimetic nanoparticles, have emerged as promising directions [84]. Unlike the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect observed in tumors, the cardiovascular endothelium exhibits low permeability; thus, nanoparticles must be sized between 20 and 100 nm to achieve a balance between vascular penetration and renal filtration [252, 253]. The dense barriers created by blood flow shear stress and plaques (composed of lipid, collagen, and other matrices [250, 253]) or thrombi (characterized by fibrin networks and high shear stress [254]) physically obstruct nanoparticles, compromising their structural integrity and ligand stability, which can lead to targeting failures [253, 254]. Additionally, nanoparticles can activate the complement system, resulting in rapid clearance or allergic reactions. Their degradation products may also be immunogenic, triggering non-specific inflammation via inflammasomes and Toll-like receptors (TLR), thereby exacerbating pathological processes. Long-term use poses risks of immune memory, such as the formation of anti-PEG antibodies, which can impact subsequent and combination immunotherapies [255].

Engineering obstacles in manufacturing and quality control

During the translation process, several manufacturing challenges persist, including uncontrollable preparation, the tendency for nanoparticles to aggregate, low drug encapsulation efficiency, and difficulties in regulating large-scale production parameters [256]. The size of nanoparticles, their surface charge, and release kinetics are sensitive to fluctuations in conditions, which can lead to poor reproducibility [257]. Particle aggregation not only reduces efficacy but can also induce toxicity. Scaling up laboratory processes while ensuring consistent quality remains a core challenge [258]. Quality control issues are significant, primarily due to the absence of unified characterization methods and standards. Different research teams utilize varying evaluation criteria for key attributes of nano preparations, complicating the comparability of results [257]. For example, diverse methods for measuring nanoparticle size can yield significantly different results, and the experimental conditions for drug release often lack standardization. Additionally, biocompatibility evaluations require standardization. These bottlenecks impede clinical translation and regulatory approval [259].

Limitations and poor predictability of preclinical models

Rodents and humans exhibit significant differences in cardiovascular physiology, including heart rate, blood flow, and vascular wall characteristics, which influence the distribution of nano preparations and their interactions with the endothelium [261]. Variations in the immune system further alter clearance rates and the composition of the protein corona [262]. The pathological characteristics of atherosclerotic plaques also differ: mouse plaques are characterized by a high density of macrophages and thin fibrous caps, whereas human plaques possess thick fibrous caps, large necrotic cores, and calcification. These disparities can lead to biases in evaluating targeting efficiency [263, 262]. Dose conversion based on body surface area is often inaccurate for nanomedicines due to significant interspecies differences in plasma proteins, metabolic enzymes, and the capacity of the reticuloendothelial system (RES) for clearance, which complicates the prediction of pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters [261, 260]. This complexity increases the risk associated with dose selection in Phase I trials. Additionally, intravenous administration poses challenges for heart failure patients, and controlling the in-vivo release of sustained-release formulations remains difficult [264, 265], representing a core bottleneck in treatment. Furthermore, hard endpoints necessitate long-term observation and incur high costs, and the relationship between surrogate markers and hard endpoints has not been fully validated [266, 267, 45].

Regulatory, intellectual property, and commercialization barriers

The clinical translation of cardiovascular nanomedicines encounters four significant barriers: regulatory challenges, intellectual property issues, economic factors, and market access difficulties. The absence of a unified global regulatory framework, exemplified by the differences between the FDA and EMA, coupled with additional toxicological requirements, extends the approval cycle by an estimated 2 to 3 years [45, 192]. Furthermore, the existence of a patent thicket, characterized by overlapping patents on surface modifications and targeting ligands, increases the risks of infringement and elevates legal costs [263, 268]. Research and development expenses can range from $200 million to $500 million, while the expenditure for single curative treatment in cardiovascular indications remains relatively low. This discrepancy leads to uncertain investment returns and significantly less funding when compared to oncology [269, 270]. Even post-market approval, the lack of quantifiable criteria for assessing the added value of nanomedicines in medical insurance, along with the low acceptance of novel drug -delivery methods among healthcare providers and patients, necessitates extensive post-marketing studies to facilitate market penetration [271, 272, 273]. Conclusions

Nanoformulations are gradually transforming the diagnosis and treatment landscape of CVDs through exceptional targeting, controllable release, and multifunctionality. This review systematically analyzes liposomal, polymeric, inorganic, and biomimetic nanocarriers, along with their applications across major cardiovascular indications using both Western drugs and traditional Chinese medicine monomers. Despite considerable therapeutic promise, clinical translation encounters significant barriers. On one hand, distinct endothelial permeability constraints and insufficient long-term safety data hinder efficacy and validation. On the other hand, regulatory challenges compound these difficulties: nanotherapeutic agents exhibit unique physicochemical properties (size, surface charge, composition) not adequately addressed by conventional drug evaluation frameworks. Although the FDA and EMA have issued guidance documents, standardized characterization protocols and unified safety assessment criteria are still lacking. Key concerns include potential immunogenicity, off-target biodistribution, and manufacturing reproducibility. Future progress will require intelligent nanoformulations (e.g., smart release systems, multimodal imaging-therapy platforms), personalized strategies guided by genomics and proteomics, and close collaboration among researchers, industry, and regulatory bodies to establish evidence-based guidelines for safe and efficient clinical development.
