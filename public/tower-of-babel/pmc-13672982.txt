Plant growth and survival rely on carefully mediated integration of many hormonal signals, something akin to a masterful orchestra, where each instrument’s sound and timing influence the cohesion and success of the orchestra’s harmonic repertoire. For a long time, the phytohormone gibberellin (GA) has been known to control flowering in Arabidopsis, particularly in short-day (SD) conditions. Similarly, cytokinins have been implicated in the regulation of flowering in SD. In this issue, Werner et al. (2026) use genetic analyses to elegantly show that cytokinin and GA coordinate flowering time in SD together, underscoring the symphonic crosstalk between cytokinin and GA to regulate one of the most consequential biological processes in flowering plants.

Control of flowering time

Control of flowering time is a key adaptive strategy to achieve reproductive fitness in plants. Correct timing of flowering is of essential importance to reproductive success. If a plant flowers at the wrong time, not well timed with potential pollinators, or under environmental constraints that impede seed development or germination, the success of its offspring may be at risk, and all the energy used for reproduction wasted.

Given the importance of flowering to angiosperms, the control of its timing is highly regulated and responsive not only to endogenous signals, but also to several external inputs. This ‘decision-point’ can occur through multiple established pathways, with the major six being photoperiod, vernalization, autonomous, temperature, age, and hormonal control by gibberellin (GA) (Bernier and Périlleux, 2005). These pathways can act in overlapping ways, with predominant pathways depending on the plant species. In Arabidopsis, the GA pathway was shown to be important for the induction of flowering in non-favorable, short-day (SD) conditions, and less important in the favorable conditions of long days (LD).

For a trait with such a complex regulation, it had been incredible that no other phytohormone had been unequivocally associated with the control of flowering time. Cytokinins, best known for their role in cell division, had long been implicated in the control of flowering time. Classic work in Sinapis alba L. had shown that exposure to LD results in accumulation of cytokinins in the phloem and activation of mitotic divisions in the shoot apical meristem (Bernier et al., 1977). Furthermore, the application of cytokinins to S. alba shoot apices could induce expression of an important regulator of the photoperiodic control of flowering time, SOC1 (SUPPRESSOR OF CONSTANS 1), at the correct shoot apical meristem domains (Bernier, 2013), although in itself cytokinin application was not sufficient to induce flowering in SD (Bernier et al., 1977). Cytokinin treatment of Arabidopsis cultivated in hydroponics proved that application of cytokinins can, in a dose-dependent manner, induce flowering in SD, to levels equivalent to LD exposure (D'Aloia et al., 2011). Further work then demonstrated the importance of components of the photoperiod and age pathways for this role of cytokinins, which can be mediated by several cytokinin species and signaled by the canonic cytokinin signaling pathway (D'Aloia et al., 2011; Bartrina et al., 2025).

Werner et al. (2026) now conclude that cytokinin precedes GA metabolism and signaling in the regulation of flowering time in SD conditions. They show that perturbation of GA biosynthesis leading to a lack of flowering is not rescued by increased cytokinin signaling, but that exogenous GA alleviates the non-flowering phenotypes of cytokinin receptor and signaling mutants. Using elegant genetic analyses, they show the epistatic interaction by which cytokinins and GA together coordinate signaling for flowering induction in SD. In hindsight, the fact that cytokinins regulate flowering time through mechanisms shared with GA control of flowering, which include a common role in SD promotion with interactions with the age and photoperiod flowering pathways (Bartrina et al., 2025) and similar roles in vegetative phase transition (Werner et al., 2021; Werner et al., 2025), would indicate epistasis, but confirmation required the genetic work completed here.

As for the mechanisms of this crosstalk, the results in Werner et al. (2026) show that regulation of GA homeostasis is an important mechanism by which cytokinins exert control of this crosstalk. The complexity in changes in the expression of genes encoding GA biosynthesis and catabolism enzymes in cytokinin signaling mutants, as well as the resulting changes in GA content in them, is reflective of the plasticity of GA content needed to affect flowering time responses. Another potential point of crosstalk between these two phytohormonal pathways is the regulation by cytokinins of DELLA proteins, which function as non-DNA-binding transcriptional repressors of GA signaling (Alabadí and Sun, 2025). Single mutants in the genes encoding the DELLA proteins GAI, RGA, RGL1, or RGL3 did not alter flowering time, as previously demonstrated. However, double mutants gai,rga or rgl1,rgl3 showed early flowering, demonstrating the well-known partial genetic redundancy of GA signaling (Dill and Sun, 2001; Cheng et al., 2004). Interestingly, when loss-of-function mutations in cytokinin receptor mutants were added to these della mutant combinations, the resulting quadruple mutant gai,rga,ahk2,ahk3 lacked early flowering, indicating that cytokinin signaling is needed for the early flowering phenotypes of the gai,rga double mutant. The same was not true for the rgl1,rgl3,ahk2,ahk3 mutant, which maintained its early flowering. Thus, specific levels of cytokinin and GA signaling, achieved by these different mutations, are needed to effectively change flowering time, and different DELLAs exert predominant functions. Because DELLA proteins are mostly regulated at the post-translational level (Alabadí and Sun, 2025), predominantly by mechanisms that include ubiquitination and O-fucosylation, it will be interesting to uncover how these modifications impact flowering time under different levels of cytokinin signaling, as well as whether cytokinin affects the composition of the transcriptional complexes in which DELLAs participate.

Phytohormone crosstalk for engineering flowering time

The results pose the question of whether the canonical GA pathway mediating flowering time could embody a larger, convergent phytohormone network model. Given the importance of phytohormones and the predominance of phytohormone crosstalk in many aspects of plant life, could the GA pathway represent a signaling hub that amalgamates cues from other phytohormonal pathways? Other phytohormone mutants have shown alterations in flowering time; for example, salicylic acid-deficient mutants that flower late (Martínez et al., 2004). Of note, cytokinin is a particularly environmentally responsive phytohormone (Argueso et al., 2009), involved in responses to nutrients, drought, and salt stress, responses to pathogens and pests, as well as beneficial microbes, in addition to its role in development (Argueso and Kieber, 2024). Thus, as far as phytohormones are concerned, cytokinins are ideal candidates for exerting the role of major stress signals that can input into the GA pathway to control flowering time in response to the environment.

Here, Werner et al. (2026) reveal an epistatic relationship between cytokinin and GA to fine-tune the timing of floral initiation during non-inductive SD growth conditions. This bolsters the ever-growing evidence that pathway signal integration is imperative to plant adaptation, much like a compositional act of weaving instrumental voices (hormonal pathways) into a single coherent, adaptive output (phytohormone crosstalk network). The epistatic nature of phytohormone networks, which have been preserved and perpetuated by evolutionary selection, allows for the extraordinary plasticity and survival optimization found in the kingdom Plantae. Regardless of whether cytokinin and GA function only with each other in the control of flowering time in SD, or whether they function with other phytohormones, the work of Werner et al. (2026), along with the knowledge of specific CK–GA crosstalk regulatory modules that control the vegetative phase change that allows plants to become competent to flower (Werner et al., 2025), opens the possibility for manipulations of cytokinin signaling and content as a way of regulating flowering time.

As climate change alters the areas in which certain crops can be grown productively, effectively forcing some crops to be grown in areas with photoperiods different from the ones they have been adapted to and/or bred for, flowering time will become one of the major traits that will have to be controlled in crop species (Fig. 1). By harnessing this developmental transition, farmers and growers could add extra cropping cycles or increase viability of some varieties by expanded latitude ranges. The addition of cytokinin genes as possible targets for photoperiod modification, and even further, the knowledge of how they affect GA signaling through crosstalk, increases the ways by which flowering time can be fine-tuned. Just as a conductor guides an orchestra by carefully balancing tempo, dynamics, and expression to achieve a desired performance, future breeders can orchestrate cytokinin and GA genetic variation to achieve plants with appropriate flowering times, to alleviate the agronomic and economic penalties of climate risk.
