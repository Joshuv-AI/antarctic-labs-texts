Voice-assisted artificial intelligence in cardiovascular disease management: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Ayuba Issaka, Ralph Maddison, Greer Lamaro Haintz, Sheikh Mohammed Shariful Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCardiovascular disease (CVD) remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, with self-management playing a pivotal role in improving outcomes. Voice-assisted artificial intelligence (AI) technologies such as virtual assistants and voice-controlled applications have emerged as innovative tools for healthcare delivery. While the technologies show promise in areas like primary prevention and chronic disease management, their effectiveness in supporting self-management for patients with CVD remains underexplored. This study aims to evaluate the impact of voice-assisted AI technologies on CVD self-management, specifically focusing on cardiovascular-related mortality, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and adherence to lifestyle modifications.Methods and analysisA systematic review and meta-analysis will be conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols. A comprehensive search will be performed across databases such as MEDLINE, Scopus, Embase and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), from 2010 to 2025. The review will include randomised controlled trials (RCTs), non-RCTs and observational studies that evaluate voice-assisted AI interventions (eg, voice-controlled fitness apps, smart health assistants) aimed at CVD self-management. The primary outcome will be cardiovascular-related mortality. Secondary outcomes will include HRQoL, clinical outcomes (eg, high blood pressure), lipid profiles (eg, cholesterol and glucose levels) and lifestyle modifications (eg, dietary habits and levels of physical activity). Data management and analysis will be conducted using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software V.2.0. The methodological quality of the included studies will be assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for RCTs and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational studies. The meta-analysis will use random-effects models, with heterogeneity assessed using Q and I² statistics. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression will be conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.Ethics and disseminationNo formal ethical assessment is required, as this study involves analysis of publicly available secondary data. Findings will be disseminated through publications in peer-reviewed scientific journals, conference presentations and media coverage to inform healthcare providers, policymakers and patients.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024568702.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of non-communicable disease deaths worldwide, accounting for an estimated 17.9 million deaths annually.1 2 CVD encompasses a spectrum of conditions, including coronary artery disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease and peripheral artery disease, all of which significantly impair quality of life and contribute to substantial global health burdens.2 The prevalence of CVD has nearly doubled over the past three decades, rising from 271 million cases in 1990 to 523 million in 2019,2 with a corresponding increase in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from 17.7 million to 34.4 million during the same period.2 This alarming rise highlights the urgent need for innovative strategies to improve CVD management and outcomes.

The epidemiology of CVD is closely associated with a combination of modifiable risk factors, including obesity, physical inactivity and unhealthy diets, as well as non-modifiable risk factors, including genetics, epigenetics and age.3 4 These factors may manifest individually or in clusters, making the management of CVD complex and challenging, particularly in efforts to reduce DALY and mortality rates among patients. Self-management can significantly alleviate the burden of CVD by empowering patients to engage in long-term, personalised treatment plans.5 It involves an active cognitive process through which patients take control of their CVD management.5 6 Effective self-management strategies are essential for reducing adverse events and improving overall health outcomes. These strategies include lifestyle modifications, medication adherence, weight management, regular health check-ups and symptom monitoring.6 However, the effectiveness of current public health and clinical interventions for self-management remains suboptimal, especially in the context of secondary prevention, where patients with established CVD require ongoing support to manage their condition effectively.1

In recent years, voice-assisted artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have emerged as a promising tool for chronic conditions management, including CVDs.711 These technologies leverage natural language processing and machine learning to enable seamless interaction between patients and devices through voice commands.12 By offering personalised guidance, real-time feedback and intuitive interfaces, voice-assisted AI tools empower patients to take control and self-manage their health effectively.13 For example, patients can use virtual assistants to set medication reminders, monitor vital signs, receive tailored lifestyle recommendations and communicate with healthcare providers.13 Such applications not only enhance patient engagement but also bridge gaps in healthcare access for underserved populations, including low-literacy individuals, older adults and non-native speakers, by providing multilingual support and culturally tailored advice.14

Several studies have demonstrated the feasibility and acceptability of voice-assisted AI interventions in managing cardiovascular risk factors and clinical outcomes.8 10 14 15 For instance, the Medly app for heart failure management has shown promising results in improving patient adherence and reducing hospital readmissions.9 10 Similarly, remote symptom monitoring systems have demonstrated high accuracy and user acceptance, highlighting the potential of these technologies to revolutionise CVD self-care.16 Despite these advancements, evidence linking voice-assisted AI to long-term cardiovascular outcomes, such as reductions in mortality, morbidity and improvements in health-related quality of life (HRQoL), remains limited.81115 Most existing reviews focus on short-term outcomes and fail to assess the sustainability of behaviour changes over time.11 21 22 Additionally, the narrow demographics of many study populations limit the generalisability of the findings, raising questions about the applicability of these technologies across diverse patient groups.1719

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to address these gaps by evaluating the effectiveness of voice-assisted AI interventions in the self-management of CVD, with a specific emphasis on secondary prevention.23 24 By focusing on long-term clinical outcomes, diverse populations and integration strategies within routine care, this study seeks to enhance our understanding of how voice-assisted AI can facilitate effective self-management and improve patient outcomes. The review will specifically examine the impact of these technologies on mortality rates, hospital admissions, key health indicators (eg, blood pressure, lipid profiles, body mass index (BMI)), healthcare utilisation and psychosocial outcomes (eg, anxiety, depression). Additionally, it will explore the role of voice-assisted AI in promoting lifestyle modifications, such as increased physical activity and improved dietary habits, which are crucial for the secondary prevention of CVD risk.

Methods and analysis

The research will consist of a systematic review and a meta-analysis, conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols guidelines29 (online supplemental material 1). The protocol is registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42024568702).

Eligibility criteria

Study design

The review will include randomised controlled trials (RCTs), including cluster randomised trials, crossover trials and community trials, that evaluate the impact of voice-assisted AI technologies on cardiovascular outcomes in patients with established CVD. Studies must be published in English-language peer-reviewed journals between 2010 and 2025. Feasibility studies, implementation studies without primary outcomes and studies comparing interventions without a control or placebo group will be excluded. This study commenced in April 2024 and is projected to conclude in November 2026. The status of the study is ‘ongoing’, with data screening currently in progress.

Population

Participants may be of any sex and must be adults aged 18 years and older diagnosed with any type of CVD including specific conditions such as coronary artery disease, heart failure and hypertension. Studies involving mixed disease populations will be included if patients with CVD constitute more than 50% of the total study population.30 Pregnant women and individuals with special needs (eg, eating disorders) will be excluded to maintain clinical homogeneity within the study population, as their unique physiological and care contexts could confound the analysis of the intervention’s effect.

Intervention

The intervention will focus on voice-assisted AI technologies, including virtual assistants and voice-controlled applications, designed to enhance self-management for patients with CVD. Studies that do not clearly describe the voice-assisted intervention or fail to report or define cardiovascular outcomes will be excluded.

Data sources

A comprehensive literature search will be conducted in electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), from 2010 to 2025. This timeframe is selected due to significant advancements in voice recognition and AI technologies that began around this time, marking a transition where these technologies became more accessible and applicable in healthcare settings.31

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome will be a summary of pooled measures of association between using voice-assisted AI technology and cardiovascular-related mortality, defined as the total number of deaths from any cause during the study period.

Secondary outcomes

This study will provide reliable evidence on the effectiveness of voice-assisted AI technology in CVD management through a range of secondary outcomes, encompassing clinical measures, quality-of-life indicators, lifestyle modifications and psychosocial outcomes. These outcomes will collectively evaluate the holistic impact of voice-assisted AI interventions on patients with CVD. Search strategy

The search strategy follows the Population, Intervention, Comparison and Outcomes format, using a combination of keywords and subject headings related to voice-assisted AI technologies and CVD, focusing on their effectiveness and impact. Each component will be combined using Boolean operators specifically, ‘AND’ will be used to refine the search results.

Population

(“cardiovascular disease*” OR CVD OR “heart disease*” OR “coronary artery disease*” OR “myocardial infarction” OR “stroke” OR “hypertension” OR “heart failure” OR “atrial fibrillation” OR cardiac rehabilitation).

Intervention

(“voice-assisted” OR “voice-controlled” OR “voice-activated” OR “speech-recognition*” OR “virtual assistant*” OR “digital assistant*” OR “artificial intelligence” OR “AI”)

Comparison

(“usual care” OR “standard care” OR “conventional care” OR “control group”)

Outcomes

(“utili#ation” OR hospitali#ation OR morbidity OR mortality OR adherence OR “blood pressure” OR BP OR “self-management” OR glucose lipid OR “clinical outcomes” OR BMI OR “risk factor*” OR weight OR “quality of life” OR smoking OR “physical activity*”)

The search strategy will be tailored to meet each database’s specific requirements and functionalities, including PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) and CINAHL. Detailed search strings for each database are provided in online supplemental material 2. In addition to traditional databases, the search will include grey literature, preprint repositories and AI-specific platforms (eg, arXiv, IEEE Xplore) to capture cutting-edge developments in voice-assisted AI. This approach ensures that the review remains at the forefront of technological innovation.

Study selection

Final search results will be exported to Covidence, and studies will be selected for inclusion through a two-stage screening process.32 First, two independent reviewers will screen the titles and abstracts of all identified records to assess their eligibility based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Second, the full-text articles of the potentially eligible studies will be retrieved and assessed for final inclusion by the same two reviewers. Any disagreements between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer and reasons for exclusion studies will be noted using Covidence.32

Data extraction and management

Two independent reviewers will extract data from each included study using Covidence. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. A standardised data extraction form will be used to collect the following relevant information from the included studies: study characteristics (authors, year of publication, type of publication, eg, journal article, dissertation); study design (eg, RCT, longitudinal studies, observational study); participant characteristics (overall sample size, age range, sex distribution); risk factors (obesity, BMI, physical inactivity, hypertension, lipid profile); intervention details (duration of the study, specifics of the voice-assisted AI intervention, technology used, eg, voice assistant, natural language processing) and outcome measures (mortality and other relevant outcomes, eg, cardiovascular events, quality of life, adherence); and results (findings related to the effectiveness of the voice-assisted AI intervention on the measured outcomes). This comprehensive data extraction process will ensure that all relevant information is systematically collected from the included studies, facilitating a thorough and transparent synthesis of the evidence.

Critical appraisal

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies will be assessed using the appropriate tool, such as the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for RCTs or the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational studies. The overall quality of the evidence will be evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation approach.33

Publication bias

Publication bias will be assessed using the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) programme.34 This will include visual inspection of a funnel plot, Egger’s weighted regression to test for statistical evidence of bias and the use of Rosenthal’s fail-safe number to estimate the number of unlocated studies with a zero-effect size required to change the significant results to non-significant. The trim and fill method will be used to adjust for missing (unreported) studies. This assesses what the effect size would have been given no bias existed.

Study heterogeneity

Heterogeneity among the included studies will be assessed using the Q statistic and the I² statistic, and potential sources of heterogeneity will be explored through subgroup analyses and meta-regression. I² values of approximately 50% and 75% will be considered moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively.35

Data synthesis and analysis

A narrative synthesis will be conducted to summarise and describe the included studies providing a qualitative overview of the findings. If feasible, a meta-analysis will be performed using the random-effects model to pool the results of studies with similar interventions and outcomes. This methodology will appropriately account for the expected heterogeneity among the included studies. Given the extensive number of outcome variables, a hierarchical analytical approach will be implemented. This will prioritise the primary outcomes critical to patient management, followed by the clinically significant secondary outcomes, ensuring clarity and focus throughout the analysis.36 37

The synthesis will yield a comprehensive summary of pooled measures of association between the use of voice-assisted AI technology and the specified outcomes. The available measures of association will determine the most suitable method of collapsing the data and the metric that best fits. Unadjusted ORs and regression coefficients will be converted to correlation coefficients for consistency across analyses. Additionally, longitudinal data for multiple time points will be categorised into short-term (≤12 months) and long-term (>12 months) follow-ups for examinations.

Using the CMA software, the effect size of each voice-assisted AI technology will be determined in the meta-analysis. Meta-estimates will be compared across at least three studies per outcome, using the relative risks (RR) and the standard mean difference, with 95% CIs as the metrics to express reliability.38 For dichotomous outcomes, the meta-analysis estimates will be generated using the pooled RR, while for continuous outcomes, pooling will be based on the differences in means or standardised measures.

The analysis of secondary outcomes will adhere to a hierarchical structure to maintain focus. We will initially concentrate on clinical measures, specifically the percentage of participants achieving controlled blood pressure (systolic blood pressure <130 mm Hg and diastolic blood pressure <80 mm Hg) and the frequency of cardiovascular-related hospitalisations. Next, healthcare utilisation will be evaluated, including the frequency of unscheduled emergency department visits, the number of outpatient follow-up appointments and medication adherence. Following this, we will examine quality of life and psychosocial outcomes, incorporating HRQoL scores from validated tools such as the EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire (EQ-5D) and 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36), as well as anxiety and depression as measured by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, and self-efficacy assessed using the cardiac self-efficacy scale. Finally, we will assess lifestyle modifications, focusing on self-reported physical activity levels and changes in dietary habits tracked via dietary logs. This structured approach ensures a thorough yet prioritised examination of all relevant outcomes.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the effectiveness of voice-assisted AI technologies in the self-management of CVD. The anticipated findings suggest that these interventions could significantly improve clinical outcomes, enhance quality of life and reduce healthcare utilisation among patients with CVD. However, it is important to acknowledge that the magnitude of their impact may vary based on the type of technology used, specific study characteristics and patient demographics.39

Voice-assisted AI has the potential to empower patients by providing personalised, real-time feedback on medication adherence, lifestyle modifications and symptom monitoring. Preliminary studies indicate that voice assistants can improve medication adherence by up to 30% and increase physical activity levels by 20%, highlighting their potential to enhance self-management behaviours.9 10 Additionally, these tools may help bridge gaps in healthcare access for underserved populations, such as low-literacy individuals and older adults, by offering multilingual support and user-friendly interfaces. Voice-assisted AI could potentially reduce cardiovascular-related mortality and hospitalisation rates by facilitating early detection of cardiovascular risks and promoting adherence to treatment plans. For example, the Medly app for heart failure management has demonstrated a 25% reduction in hospital readmissions among its users, highlighting the potential of these technologies to alleviate the burden on healthcare systems.9 10

The integration of voice-assisted AI with wearable devices and electronic health records is expected to enable continuous monitoring and timely interventions, further improving HRQoL. Tools such as the EQ-5D and SF-36 may reveal significant improvements in mobility, self-care and emotional well-being among patients using voice-assisted AI interventions.34

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this proposed study include its comprehensive scope, which aims to encompass a wide range of voice-assisted AI technologies, from consumer-grade devices (eg, Amazon Alexa, Google Assistant) to specialised healthcare applications. A key strength will be the focus on equity, as the review intends to explore the potential of voice-assisted AI to address health inequities, contributing to the development of inclusive digital health solutions. Furthermore, the future-oriented nature of this review is marked by its inclusion of emerging trends and ethical considerations, ensuring its relevance in a rapidly evolving field.

However, there are limitations that need to be considered. These include technological heterogeneity, data privacy concerns and the challenge of limited long-term evidence. The diversity of voice-assisted AI platforms and functionalities may complicate comparisons across studies. Additionally, reliance on voice data raises important questions about patient consent and data security, which may not be adequately addressed in existing literature. Furthermore, many voice-assisted AI interventions are relatively new, leading to a scarcity of long-term data on their effectiveness.

Ethics and dissemination

Since this systematic review and meta-analysis will not involve primary data collection, a formal ethical assessment is not required by our institution (Deakin University Human Ethics Research Committee). This approach is consistent with standard guidelines for evidence synthesis research. All data extracted from source studies will be managed with strict adherence to principles of integrity and confidentiality. A formal data curation and management plan will be implemented to ensure the security, accuracy and long-term accessibility of the synthesised data. This includes the use of secure, encrypted storage systems and maintaining detailed, anonymised records of all extraction and analysis procedures. The findings are expected to be disseminated through various channels, including peer-reviewed publications and presentations at international and national conferences. Additionally, we will produce fact sheets and evidence summaries aimed at clinicians and policymakers to enhance understanding and application of the results.

Beyond traditional methods, we plan to use innovative channels, including interactive webinars to engage stakeholders in discussions about the implications of voice-assisted AI for CVD self-management, fostering dialogue and knowledge exchange. We will also implement social media campaigns to raise awareness among patients and the public regarding the potential of voice-assisted AI to improve health outcomes among diverse population groups.
