Drops can Perpetually Bounce over a Vibrating Wettable Solid
Lebo Molefe, Tomas Fullana ∗, François Gallaire, John M. Kolinski

Abstract
On the surface of a vibrating liquid bath, instead of coalescing, a drop will continually bounce on a thin film of air between the drop and the free surface, giving rise to rich chaotic dynamics and quantum analog behavior. However, perpetual bouncing is yet to be demonstrated on a vibrating rigid solid, where the control of the drop’s motion is not limited by the bath dynamics. Here we show that vibration of an atomically smooth mica surface prolongs a drop’s hovering state by several orders of magnitude, increasing the time to wet from less than a second to several minutes. The excitation of the second spherical harmonic mode determines a transition between a bouncing state with high-amplitude rebounds, and a bound state, where the drop’s motion is locked onto the vibrating solid above a thin air layer. We further develop a coupled linear spring model, accounting for the drop’s deformation, that predicts bouncing trajectories without fitting parameters. Our results extend the scope of bouncing drop studies beyond the liquid bath and highlight the potential for precision manipulation of small liquid quantities in air.

Spherical harmonic decomposition—
In order to parametrize the drop’s shape, we use spherical harmonics, defined in the center-of-mass frame, assuming an axisymmetric profile, such that

r(\theta,t)=r_{0}+\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}a_{\ell}(t)\,\sqrt{\dfrac{2\ell+1}{4\,\pi\ell}}\,P_{\ell}(\cos\theta).

(1)

Here,
\theta
is the polar angle,
a_{\ell}(t)
is the time-varying mode amplitude and
P_{\ell}(\cdot)
the Legendre polynomial of the
\ell^{th}
axisymmetric spherical harmonic mode. The sum starts with
\ell=2
because the
0^{th}
mode violates mass conservation, while the
1^{st}
mode is a translation (not considered in the center-of-mass frame). In practice, the experimental and numerical data are fitted up to
\ell=10
, and we find that only the first few modes contribute significantly to the deformation
de Ruiter et al. 2015b
. An illustration of modes
\ell=\{2,\,3,\ 4\}
is depicted in Fig.
3
(a). We neglect the air viscosity
Miller and Scriven 1968
;
Prosperetti 1980
, and thus the natural angular frequency
\omega_{\ell}
associated with each mode is

\omega_{\ell}=\sqrt{\ell(\ell-1)(\ell+2)}\,\omega_{d}.

(2)

Fitting Eq.(
1
) to the drop shape, we monitor the mode amplitudes of the bouncing [Figs.
3
(b),(c)] and bound [Figs.
3
(d),(e)] states, initially presented in Fig.
1
. In the bouncing case, the drop spreads horizontally (negative
a_{2}
) before elongating vertically (positive
a_{2}
); this leads to an upward translation of the center-of-mass, and the lift-off of the drop. By contrast, bound drops deform into alternating triangular shapes with nearly equal amplitudes for modes
\ell=2
and
\ell=3
, resulting in minimal center-of-mass motion; this prevents escape from the stage. The
\ell=2
mode is not strongly forced – notably, the peak magnitude of
a_{2}
is now about half that of the bouncing drop. These kinematics reveal that adequate coupling to the
\ell=2
mode is necessary for a drop to completely rebound from the surface. As a consequence,
f_{2}
defines a critical transition frequency, which leads us to predict that the bouncing-to-bound transition should occur at a vibration number
\Omega_{c}=\omega_{2}/\omega_{d}=\sqrt{8}
. The predicted cutoff value is in remarkable agreement with our experimental and numerical observations, where the bouncing-to-bound transition frequency collapses to the value
\Omega_{c}
for all drop sizes [Fig.

(a), inset]. Beyond
\Omega_{c}

a_{2}
decays whereas
a_{3}
increases, corresponding to a limited drop rebound height, and further distinguishing the bouncing and bound drop dynamical states [Fig.
3
(f)].

Coupled linear spring model—
The pivotal role of played by the second spherical harmonic mode motivates the development of a simple coupled linear spring model to predict the drop motion for any stage amplitude and frequency. Whereas most prior models of a bouncing drop on a compliant liquid bath
Okumura et al. 2003
;
Moláček and Bush 2013a
;
Terwagne et al. 2013
neglect the drop deformation, here we couple the drop’s center-of-mass
z(t)
to its shape, characterized by its second mode of deformation
a_{2}(t)
. The two-way coupled linear spring model, in non-dimensional form, reads

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}z}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}-H(-z^{*})\left[\operatorname{max}\left\{-2\,b_{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}t}-z^{*},\,0\right\}\right]=-\Bo^{*},\\
\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}a_{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}+2\,b_{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}a_{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\omega_{2}^{2}\,a_{2}=H(-z^{*})\left[5\,(\alpha-\alpha^{2}/3)\right],\end{array}\right.

(3)

where
b_{2}=5\Oh
and
\omega_{2}=\sqrt{8}
are the non-dimensional second mode dissipation coefficient and frequency, respectively.
\Bo^{*}=\Bo\,(1+\Gamma\sin(\Omega t+\phi_{s}))
is the Bond number in the frame of reference of the moving stage, and
H(.)
is a Heaviside function triggering the coupling if the drop is being forced by the stage, when
z^{*}=z-a_{2}<0
[Fig.
4
(a)]. When the coupling is active, the drop’s center-of-mass experiences a force proportional to its position and a dissipation proportional to its speed, and the second mode amplitude is forced by a term
f
, accounting for the sustained instantaneous acceleration. From a quasi-static approximation of drop shapes in contact with a flat solid, derived by Moláček and Bush
Moláček and Bush 2012
, this forcing is

f=\alpha\,(2\ell+1/\ell+1)\,P_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}(1-\alpha/3)
where
\alpha=\mathrm{d}^{2}z/\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\Bo^{*}
is the dimensionless instantaneous center-of-mass acceleration, with
P_{2}^{{}^{\prime}}(\cdot)
the derivative of the second Legendre polynomial
P_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{2}(3\,x^{2}-1)
. Simplifying the expression for
\ell=2
, we obtain
f=5\,(\alpha-\alpha^{2}/3)
.
Model derivation details are found in the Supplemental Material.

We compare the drop’s trajectory and deformation from the DNS to the model without any fitting parameters.
This simple model can capture the rich drop dynamics in both the bouncing and bound states, including complex transient behavior, as shown in Figs.
4
(b),(c).
Over a wide range of driving accelerations and frequencies, the drop amplitude
A_{d}
predicted from the model agrees nearly exactly with that of the DNS; furthermore superimposed experimental measurements show excellent agreement with both predictions [Fig.
4
(d),(e)]. Bouncing states exist for accelerations as low as
\Gamma=0.3
, in agreement with similar phase diagrams for drops bouncing on a liquid bath
Moláček and Bush 2013a
. These results confirm the robustness of the critical frequency
\Omega_{c}=\omega_{2}/\omega_{d}=\sqrt{8}
, above which the drop is bound even for high
\Gamma
.

Control and manipulation of drops—
Our results demonstrate a novel hovering state for a liquid drop on a smooth vibrating solid at room temperature, extending the drop’s lifetime from less than a second to several minutes. By systematically varying the forcing amplitude and frequency, we identify two distinct regimes: a bouncing state, characterized by high-amplitude rebounds and significant air gap, and a bound state, in which the drop’s motion becomes locked to the vibrating plate through a thin air layer. The transition between these two regimes is governed by the excitation of the drop’s second spherical harmonic mode. From this insight, we develop a coupled linear spring model that accurately predicts drop trajectories without fitting parameters.

We have identified a method for using vibration to produce a highly-controllable no-contact state for a drop in close proximity to a solid surface. Prospective manipulation is facilitated by the much more rapid transport of the drop – two orders of magnitude faster – when bouncing above a stiff solid than above a liquid layer [Fig. S5]. In addition, unlike the Leidenfrost effect, the vibration method does not require high temperatures, significantly expanding the range of potentially sensitive chemistries that can be processed using this system. As a proof-of-concept, we demonstrate the pneumatic manipulation of small volumes of fluid near a surface [Fig. S6], opening avenues for the precision micromanipulation of liquid in air.
