Rapid early life colonization of the intestinal tract by Akkermansia muciniphila after voluntary feeding
Authors: Jenice M. Dumlao, Paige McCallum, Colten R. Hodak, Elizabeth Guinto, Winnie Enns, Lauren E. Davey, Jonathan C. Choy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-invasive methods to colonize intact gut microbiota populations with specific bacterial species are useful for experimental studies that advance our understanding of this commensal microbial population. Within the gut microbiota, the anaerobic muciniphile Akkermansia muciniphila has many established health benefits. We report the development of a new voluntary feeding protocol for non-invasive administration of bacteria into the intestine and use it to characterize the early life colonization of the intestinal tract by A. muciniphila.ResultsMice were voluntarily fed a human strain of A. muciniphila (MucT/BAA-835) in the week after weaning, whereupon they consistently and rapidly ingested the bacterium. At this developmental period, conventionally housed mice were rapidly colonized by human A. muciniphila that persisted until at least 8 weeks of age. In mice that contained a dysbiotic gut microbiota that lacks endogenous A. muciniphila, voluntary feeding with human A. muciniphila similarly led to rapid and persistent colonization. Colonization was similar in female and male mice. Also, in conventionally housed mice there was unstable colonization of the intestinal tract with endogenous A. muciniphila between 3 and 4 weeks of age, which resulted in replacement of this strain with human A. muciniphila after oral delivery.ConclusionsThese findings establish a new and non-invasive approach for colonizing the intestinal tract with commensal microbes and provides information on the early life colonization of the gut microbiota with A. muciniphila.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05202-5.

Background

The community of commensal microbes that inhabits the intestinal tract of mammals, termed the gut microbiota, is essential for health. These microbes contribute to digestion of intestinal contents, control the absorption of nutrients and production of metabolites into the body, and affect the development of several other physiological processes essential for homeostasis [1]. Dysfunctional alterations in the composition of the gut microbiota, referred to as dysbiosis, contributes to diseases such as asthma, allergy, cardiovascular disease, and inflammatory bowel disease [2–5]. Preclinical studies have also implicated the gut microbiota in affecting the development of many other diseases ranging from multiple sclerosis to autism and anxiety [6, 7].

The composition of the gut microbiota is diverse. The major phyla known to be components of this community in mammals are Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, which represent 90% of the gut microbial population, as well as Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria and Verrucomicrobia [8]. However, the abundance of bacterial genera that are found to inhabit the intestinal tract of an individual are highly variable and dependent on diet, age, sex, environment, and disease [9]. Although there is diversity in its specific composition, there can be convergence in the metabolic functions of bacteria in the gut microbiota within and between mammalian species [10]. In the setting of this diversity, Akkermansia spp. are the only genera found in the gut microbiota that use glycans from host mucin as its primary energy source, and Akkermansia muciniphila is the dominant species found in the gut [11]. It is a strict anaerobe that is consistently present in humans, albeit in variable abundance, as well as in many other mammals including laboratory mice [12, 13]. A. muciniphila is an important component of the healthy mammalian gut microbiota. Its abundance is associated with many aspects of health and a reduction in its abundance is associated with several diseases [14–16]. Given the established contribution of A. muciniphila in maintaining health, oral administration of this bacterium and understanding how it colonizes the intestinal tract is useful for studying its effects in rodent models of health/disease.

The most widely used approach for orally delivering bacteria is oral gavage. However, this method is invasive. It causes distress of animals and can damage the intestinal tract [17, 18]. The procedure is also technically difficult so requires specialized training, especially in studies that use young mice that are physically small. Importantly, differences in the colonization of the gut microbiota in infants leads to long-lasting effects in adults so models are needed to modify the gut microbiota in infant mice [19]. Voluntary feeding of live bacteria has the benefit of being non-invasive, causing less damage and being less technically demanding. It can also be used to examine bacterial delivery to and colonization of infant mice at early stages of development when they are small but, to our knowledge, has not been extensively studied for this purpose. Moreover, feeding protocols may also more accurately replicate the probiotic delivery of bacteria for preclinical studies [20]. However, voluntary feeding of mice is challenging because it is difficult to control the timing and amount of material that is ingested.

Here, we report the development of a voluntary feeding protocol for delivery of bacteria to colonize the gut microbiota of laboratory mice and use it to examine the colonization of the intestinal tract by A. muciniphila early in life. The voluntary feeding protocol is able to deliver a human strain of A. muciniphila to the intestinal tract of infant mice during the weaning period whereupon this bacterium persistently colonizes the intestinal tract. We also show that voluntary feeding of A. muciniphila to young mice at the time of weaning is able to lead to its persistent colonization of a dysbiotic gut microbiota population that lacks this bacterium [21]. The voluntary feeding protocol effectively modifies an early developmental window in the biology of the gut microbiota and will be a valuable resource for studies on the role of bacterial species in this microbial population.

Methods

Animals & induction of dysbiosis of the gut microbiota

C57Bl/6 mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories and bred in-house. The gut microbiota was disrupted in mice through administration of an antibiotic cocktail in the drinking water containing ampicillin (0.5 g/L), vancomycin (0.25 g/L), neomycin sulfate (0.5 g/L), and metronidazole (0.5 g/L). Pregnant females were administered antibiotics ad libitum starting before birth and continuing until pups reached 3 weeks of age. Pups were then maintained on regular water thereafter. This treatment prevents colonization of the intestinal tract with bacteria and then permits colonization starting at 3 weeks of age, which is when mice are weaned [22]. The resultant microbiota is similar to conventionally colonized animals but lacks A. muciniphila and a handful of other less abundant bacteria [21]. At the end of experiments, animals were anesthetized with isofluorane, and then euthanized using CO2 followed by cervical dislocation. All study protocols were reviewed and approved by the Simon Fraser University Animal Care Committee.

Culture of A. muciniphila

A. muciniphila (MucT/BAA-835) was obtained from ATCC. Cultures were grown in a previously described synthetic medium [17], containing 3 mM KH₂PO₄, 3 mM Na₂HPO₄, 5.6 mM NH₄Cl, 1 mM MgCl₂, 1 mM Na₂S·9 H₂O, 47 mM NaHCO₃, 1 mM CaCl₂, 0.2% GlcNAc, 0.2% glucose, 16 g/L soy peptone (VWR), 4 g/L threonine, and trace elements and vitamins. Cultures were incubated in an anaerobic chamber (Coy Laboratory; 5% H₂, 5% CO₂, 90% N₂) until stationary phase. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed once with PBS, and resuspended to approximately 10¹⁰ CFU/mL in sterile PBS containing 20% glycerol. Aliquots were stored at − 80 °C and thawed immediately before feeding experiments. Thawed bacterial aliquots were serially diluted 2-fold in a 20% glycerol stock in 1xPBS, and OD600 was measured. Bacterial recovery was estimated by relating the measured CFU/mL to the expected aliquot concentration, assuming 4.18 × 108 CFU/mL at OD600 = 1.

Formulation of feeding capsules

Feeding capsules used in all experiments were adapted from Zhang et al. [23]. Briefly, a fresh sucralose-gelatin stock was made prior to feeding experiments by dissolving 10% sucralose (wt/vol) and 10% gelatin (wt/vol) in distilled water. Green food coloring was added to the solution and then autoclaved. Autoclaved stocks were aliquoted and stored at 4 °C for up to 2 days. Approximately 45 min before feeding experiments, the sucralose-gelatin stocks were melted at 37 °C for 15 min. Glycerol or bacterial aliquots were then thawed at 37 °C for 5 min and immediately mixed into sucralose-gelatin stock aliquots to the desired bacterial concentration. This mixture was then aliquoted into a 24-well plate (approximately 2 g/well) and allowed to solidify for 15 min at 4 °C. Solidified feeding capsules were cut into 0.25 g slices and placed inside animal cages on 6 mm petri dishes for consumption.

Colonization of mice with human A. muciniphila

Three days after cessation of antibiotics at 3 weeks of age, mice were administered a 0.25 g gelatin capsule containing only sucralose as a training capsule. The day after, mice were given control capsules or capsules containing 109 CFU of human A. muciniphila (BAA-835). Mice were fasted for three hours before being fed capsules. Stool samples were collected from mice immediately prior to A. muciniphila consumption and at 1, 2, 7, 14 and 28 days after consumption to monitor colonization of the gut by the bacteria.

Quantification of A. muciniphila in fecal samples

DNA was extracted from fecal samples using a QIAGEN QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit, and DNA concentration was measured using a nanodrop spectrophotometer. For all PCR and qPCR experiments, 50ng of sample DNA was used. Primers specific to mouse or human strains of A. muciniphila were used (Supplemental Fig. 1). For PCR experiments, samples were amplified using a Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs) as per kit instructions. PCR products were run on a 1% agarose gel and densitometry was performed on ImageJ. For qPCR experiments, PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix was used. To quantify human A. muciniphila in stool, a five-point DNA standard curve was generated using DNA extracted from a pure culture of BAA-835 with a known CFU/mL.

Immunofluorescence staining

Colon samples were fixed in methacarnoy’s solution then frozen in OCT. 8 μm sections were collected and stained with UEA-1-FITC (GeneTex) to visualize the mucus layer. Sections were then washed with PBS, blocked with 2% BSA for 10 min, and then stained with anti-Akkermansia antisera at 4 °C overnight as previously described [24]. Colon sections were then stained with a fluorescently labelled secondary antibody (goat-anti rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 647; Jackson ImmunoResearch) and mounted in Prolong Gold antifade mountant with DAPI (Invitrogen). Sections were imaged on the Zeiss Airyscan using a Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.4 NA objective (Zeiss) and processed on Zen software (Zeiss).

Statistical analyses

To determine differences between groups, an ANOVA was performed for comparisons between multiple groups and a Mann Whitney U Test was performed for comparison between two groups. Statistically significant differences were set at p < 0.05.

Results

Akkermansia muciniphila in the murine gut microbiota

A. muciniphila is ubiquitous in the gut microbiota of mammals, although there is variability in its abundance within different hosts because its colonization is affected by diet and competition with other bacteria [25, 26]. This bacterium was quantified in fecal samples from female and male C57Bl/6 laboratory mice using PCR and qPCR that specifically amplifies DNA from A. muciniphila strains that are typically found in the murine intestine. A. muciniphila was consistently abundant in fecal samples of conventionally housed female and male animals (Fig. 1). Colonization of the gut microbiota with A. muciniphila can be affected by environmental conditions, such as antibiotic exposure, and potentially by the time of colonization. We have established that delaying colonization of the female gut microbiota by administering mothers and pups with a broad spectrum of antibiotics until 3 weeks of age leads to dysbiosis of gut microbiota in resultant adults that is characterized by a substantial reduction in A. muciniphila [21]. Indeed, early life administration of antibiotics in this way led to a substantial and significant reduction in the abundance of A. muciniphila in both female and male mice at 8–10 weeks of age (Fig. 1).

Voluntary feeding of mice with A. muciniphila

Mice can be voluntarily fed material in gelatin capsules containing sucralose, although it is not known whether live bacteria can be delivered in this way and whether this voluntary feeding can be used to colonize the gut microbiota. We developed a voluntary feeding protocol for this purpose and assessed whether it can be used to colonize the intestinal tract with A. muciniphila (Fig. 2A). The recovery of A. muciniphila from thawed aliquots was 67% at the time of capsule preparation (Fig. 2B). Pups were fasted for 3 h and then gelatin capsules containing only sucralose were prepared and provided to the mice. Introduction of a capsule for the first time led to wariness by mice and slow consumption that was highly variable (Fig. 2C). As such, we introduced a training step in which mice were “trained” on the presence of capsules by introducing a control capsule 3 days after weaning (first exposure) and then providing a second “feeding” capsule the day after (second exposure). Introduction of a “training” capsule in this way led to acceptance of the second capsule and full consumption of it within 10 min by all mice (Fig. 2C).

Voluntary feeding colonizes the gut microbiota with A. muciniphila

The ability of voluntary feeding to colonize the intestine of mice with commensal bacteria was then examined by feeding mice A. muciniphila (strain BAA-835/MucT; isolated from a human sample) [27]. The absolute abundance of human A. muciniphila was quantified by qPCR using species-specific primers that detect the human strain, but not the endogenous mouse Akkermansia, with quantification based on a standard curve of known bacterial concentrations (Supplemental Fig. 2). Female mice were fed control capsules or capsules containing human A. muciniphila and fecal samples analyzed. Human A. muciniphila was not detected in mice that were fed control capsules. In mice that received the bacterium, human A. muciniphila was abundant in fecal samples as early as day 1 after a single feeding and its abundance was maintained until at least 28 days, which was the last time-point studied. Abundance of this bacterium in fecal samples was stably maintained at 105 – 107 CFU per gram of stool, although there was variability at specific time-points (Fig. 3A). Colonization of male mice with human A. muciniphila was also examined. Human A. muciniphila was not detected in fecal samples from male mice at the time of weaning. In male mice, human A. muciniphila was abundant as early as day 1 after a single feeding and levels were maintained until at least day 28 after feeding, which was the last time-point tested. There was a transient reduction in bacterial abundance at day 7 after feeding that rebounded by day 14. With the exception of day 7, A. muciniphila levels were maintained at an abundance of 106 – 107 CFU per gram of stool at all time-points (Fig. 3B). Finally, feeding mice A. muciniphila did not affect their weights (Fig. 3C).

Voluntary feeding colonizes dysbiotic gut microbiota with A. muciniphila

In addition to examining the colonization of an undisturbed gut microbiota, we also evaluated how voluntary feeding can colonize dysbiotic gut microbiota in which the presence of bacteria was prevented by broad spectrum antibiotics until 3 weeks of age. This age is a developmental window at which weaning occurs and is an inflexion point in the development of the gut microbiota [28]. As such, treatment of mice in this way delays colonization of the intestinal tract with bacteria until after weaning. Female mice and their mothers were maintained on water containing broad spectrum antibiotics that reduces the total amount of bacteria in the intestinal tract by greater than 99% [22]. At 3 weeks of age, antibiotics were stopped and mice were maintained on normal water. Mice were then given a training capsule 3 days after the cessation of antibiotics and then given a control capsule or capsule containing human A. muciniphila 1 day later. The abundance of endogenous mouse A. muciniphila was significantly reduced by treatment with antibiotics and feeding with human A. muciniphila did not affect this (Fig. 4). Human A. muciniphila was absent from the gut microbiota of control mice and mice that were given antibiotics (Fig. 4).

We then examined the colonization of dysbiotic mice with human A. muciniphila after voluntary feeding. When female mice that were given antibiotics early in life were fed human A. muciniphila, this strain was detectable in fecal samples beginning at day 1 after consumption and its abundance was maintained at this level until the last measurement at 28 days after consumption (Fig. 5A). Colonization of the gut microbiota was rapid, with variability at early time-points after a single feeding and then stabilization with less variability between mice after day 7 post-consumption. The abundance of human A. muciniphila was 107 – 108 CFU per gram of feces, which was higher than in mice not given antibiotics. In males that were given antibiotics early in life, human A. muciniphila was abundant at 108 CFU per gram of stool in fecal samples as early as day 1 after a single feeding. There was a subsequent decline in abundance to 106 CFU per gram of stool at day 7 after feeding and then bacterial levels were maintained at this abundance until at least day 28 after feeding (Fig. 5B). The weights of mice were not affected by feeding with human A. muciniphila (Fig. 5C). We then examined whether intact bacteria could be observed in the intestinal tract 28 days after voluntary feeding. At this time-point, colons were harvested and immunofluorescently stained for A. muciniphila. Consistent with PCR findings, this bacterium was not detected in mice that were given antibiotics until 3 weeks of age. Notably, intact A. muciniphila was abundant in the mucosal layer of the colon in mice that were voluntary fed this bacterium, indicating that it effectively colonized the intestinal tract (Fig. 5D).

A. muciniphila unstably occupies its niche in the week after weaning

Because human A. muciniphila colonized the intestinal tract in the absence of pretreatment of mice with antibiotics, we investigated how stably this bacterium occupies its niche in the week after weaning and whether it can be displaced by another strain of A. muciniphila during this developmental window. When the abundance of mouse A. muciniphila (that naturally colonizes the intestinal tract starting shortly after birth) was quantified, this bacterium was present in fecal samples of both females and males at the beginning of the weaning period (week 3 of life). In mice that were fed control capsule with no bacteria, the relative abundance of mouse A. muciniphila was persistently maintained until at least 28 days after feeding, which was the last time-point examined (Fig. 6). When mice were fed human A. muciniphila, the endogenous mouse A. muciniphila was eliminated in adult mice by a single feeding (Fig. 6; detection limit is 10− 7). The elimination of mouse A. muciniphila was very rapid, reaching minimum levels within 2–7 days after receiving human A. muciniphila. This coincided with the persistent colonization by human A. muciniphila after voluntary feeding. This finding indicates that voluntary feeding with human A. muciniphila leads to the rapid removal of the existing endogenous strain, which suggests that colonization of the gut microbiota by endogenous A. muciniphila in the week after weaning is not stable.

Discussion

We have established a voluntary feeding protocol to colonize the intestinal tract of mice with the gut commensal A. muciniphila. The protocol rapidly colonizes female and male mice in a reproducible manner and can be applied to animals that are housed normally or those in which colonization by the gut microbiota is delayed. Voluntary feeding of mice in this way enabled the characterization of intestinal colonization by A. muciniphila early in life, showing that this bacterium rapidly colonizes the intestinal tract when delivered shortly after weaning with a single dose. The ability to colonize mice with bacteria using this non-invasive method will be a benefit for studying the gut microbiota, especially in young and/or small mice.

Oral gavage remains the main method to introduce microbes into the intestinal tract of mice. However, it is inherently invasive. This impacts the welfare of animals being studied and could be a confounding variable to the outcome of experiments. We show that voluntary feeding of young mice with sucralose-sweetened gelatin capsules is able to deliver live bacteria into the intestinal tract of 3–4 week old mice. Mice were inherently wary of the capsules upon the first exposure and a training capsule was needed in order to enable the fast and consistent consumption of a second capsule that contained bacteria. The protocol was non-invasive and mice did not show signs of distress. Voluntary feeding of young mice in this way enables the modification of the gut microbiota to examine the effects and therapeutic benefits of specific bacterial species that is applicable to many preclinical models. It is unclear if voluntary feeding in adults can lead to colonization. In studies that use oral gavage to colonize adult mice with A. muciniphila, treatment with antibiotics prior to delivery is needed to create niches for the delivered bacteria to thrive [24].

We examined early life colonization because the gut microbiota is malleable during infancy and the physiological response to intestinal colonization with microbes during weaning is essential for defining many aspects of physiology [28, 29]. A. muciniphila begins colonizing the intestinal tract before weaning due to its ability to degrade oligosaccharides in breast milk [30]. Findings in humans show that the stability of this bacterium in infants and adults is different because exposure to common antibiotics in infants reduces A. muciniphila in the gut microbiota but similar exposures in adults does not [31–33]. Notably, antibiotic exposure in infancy and reduced A. muciniphila are associated with many diseases so understanding and treating dysbiotic gut microbiota that lacks this bacterium may have health benefits [11, 34].

Several studies have reported sex-based differences in the abundance and effects of A. muciniphila [35]. In clinical studies, the relative abundance of A. muciniphila may be higher in females that in males [36]. We did not observe differences in female and male mice. The abundance and effects of A. muciniphila may be affected by environmental distinctions, such as diet, that can be different in females and males [37, 38]. Also, the diversity of the gut microbiota may be lower in males than females, which may affect the relative abundance of specific microbial species [39]. In both sexes, colonization occurred very quickly (one day after feeding) to apparently similar levels. In conventionally colonized mice, there may be a transient reduction of bacterium at day 7 after feeding that rebounded to stable levels thereafter.

We observed that colonization with A. muciniphila was rapid when mice were fed between 3 and 4 weeks of age. The delivered bacterium was abundant in fecal samples by day 1 after a single feeding and was persistently maintained until 28 days after feeding. The persistence of A. muciniphila in stool samples and the detection of intact bacteria in the colon weeks after ingestion are indicative of intestinal colonization with live bacteria. Notably, a single ingestion of bacterium was sufficient for persistent colonization, which suggests that the niche in which A. muciniphila resides remains available and malleable shortly after weaning. Population of the intestinal tract with commensal microbes is governed by competition between colonizing microbes for distinct niches [40]. We observe that human A. muciniphila colonized the intestinal tract despite the endogenous presence of mouse A. muciniphila at the time of feeding. Exposure to antibiotics resulted in higher concentrations of A. muciniphila in both sexes early after feeding, which is consistent with reduced competition for mucin niches in mice that received antibiotics. Properties of the mucin layer may affect the stability of colonization, with the spatial organization and barrier properties of this glycan layer not being established in mice until 4–5 weeks after initial exposure to colonizing bacteria [41]. Interestingly, the oral gavage of 5 week old mice (that were treated with tetracycline) with A. muciniphila leads to intestinal colonization at 102 – 103 greater levels than our voluntary feeding protocol [24]. Thus, our findings show that colonization of the mouse intestinal tract with A. muciniphila is active shortly after weaning but remains malleable until at least 4 weeks of life. This is consistent with the kinetics of its colonization in the human gut microbiota [30].

Voluntary feeding of bacteria can model the delivery of microbes to the intestinal tract as probiotics. The abundance of A. muciniphila is associated with many aspects of human health so strategies to increase its colonization of the intestinal tract may have health benefits [42, 43]. Indeed, A. muciniphila is extensively studied as a potential probiotic for several conditions. Preclinical studies using oral gavage show that its delivery in this way reduces obesity, metabolic syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, and atherosclerosis [44–46]. Studies in adult mice show that some of these therapeutic effects may not require colonization by live bacterium and are mediated by cell surface components of this microbe that signal to intestinal cells [47]. Further, clinical trials have shown that probiotic delivery of A. muciniphila reduces metabolic abnormalities [48]. This bacterium is considered a strict anaerobe although it can survive for several hours – days in aerobic conditions [49, 50]. As such, translating its use as a probiotic will need to consider this in the preparations.

This study has some limitations. We examined the ability of voluntary feeding to deliver A. muciniphila but other bacteria were not examined. The effectiveness and dynamics of colonization are likely to be different for other bacteria that reside in separate niches. Also, we do not know how colonization with human A. muciniphila affects the composition of the gut microbiota. Metagenimc sequencing will be useful to assess this in future studies. Finally, colonization by A. muciniphila was characterized up until 28 days after feeding so we are not able to determine the abundance of this bacterium at late time-points.

Conclusion

We have developed a voluntary feeding protocol for colonizing the intestinal tract of mice with commensal bacteria and used it to characterize the early life colonization by A. muciniphila. The protocol has implications for understanding the biology of commensal bacteria and preclinical development of probiotics.
