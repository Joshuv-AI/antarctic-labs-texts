From in silico screening to experimental verification: Identification of novel umami peptides from tamarind (Tamarindus indica L.) seed proteins and their validation in enzymatic hydrolysates
Authors: Sisi Huang, Shurui Li, Xiaoying Wang, Xuening Jiu, Shengbao Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUmami peptides are key contributors to food flavor enhancement. In this study, novel umami peptides from tamarind seed proteins were identified using an integrated strategy combining in silico screening and experimental validation. A virtual peptide library was screened based on physicochemical properties and bioactivity, yielding six candidates (VSDY, DGIDF, AKNVSDYL, YNDPHC, VPPD, and NDPHCQY). Molecular docking and dynamics simulations revealed stable binding to the umami receptor T1R1/T1R3 via hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, with binding affinities of −7.9 to −8.8 kcal/mol. Among them, AKNVSDYL exhibited the strongest interaction and highest stability. Sensory evaluation and electronic tongue analysis confirmed the umami characteristics of all peptides, with AKNVSDYL showing the highest intensity, consistent with computational results. Enzymatic hydrolysis followed by UHPLC-ESI-HRMS/MS verified the presence of all predicted peptides. This study provides an efficient strategy for discovering natural umami peptides and supports the valorization of tamarind seed byproducts.Keywords: Umami peptide, In silico screening, Molecular docking, Umami receptor T1R1/T1R3, Electronic tongue

Introduction

Umami is the fifth basic taste after sweet, sour, bitter, and salty, and plays a key role in food palatability (Suo et al., 2025). Beyond classical umami substances like glutamate, short-chain peptides from protein hydrolysis have emerged as umami-active compounds (Mei et al., 2025). These peptides can interact with the T1R1/T1R3 heterodimeric receptor, thereby enhancing umami perception and contributing to flavor complexity (Li et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2018). Beyond their sensory function, umami peptides are also considered promising nutritionally relevant ingredients, offering opportunities for the development of natural, multifunctional flavor enhancers (Wang et al., 2024).

Tamarind (Tamarindus indica L.) is widely cultivated across tropical and subtropical regions, with seeds constituting approximately 25–40% of the fruit mass (Souza et al., 2018). Tamarind seeds are particularly rich in protein, yet their utilization remains confined to low-value applications, such as animal feed and industrial binders. (Hashem et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). This not only represents a significant loss of nutritional resources but also poses a major barrier to the sustainable development of the tamarind industry. However, unlocking the potential of tamarind seed proteins as a source of bioactive and flavor peptides may deliver substantial scientific advances in application and socioeconomic benefits. Conventional peptide discovery workflows—based on enzymatic hydrolysis, fractionation, purification, and sensory evaluation—are time-consuming and resource-intensive (Huaju et al., 2017). Recent advances in computational tools (e.g., in silico hydrolysis, molecular docking, MD simulations, and machine learning) have enabled efficient identification of umami peptides and molecular-level insights into taste formation (Pan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2021). These integrative tools not only improve discovery efficiency but also provide molecular-level insights into umami taste formation. Notably, when combined with experimental validation—including peptide synthesis, sensory evaluation, and mass spectrometry-based identification—computational screening can establish a robust and reliable pipeline for umami peptide discovery. Despite methodological advances, tamarind seed proteins remain unexplored for high-value peptide products. Addressing this gap is critical for expanding plant-derived umami peptides and promoting agricultural byproduct valorization. Unlocking the umami potential of tamarind seeds offers dual benefits: advancing taste formation mechanisms and converting underutilized biomass into functional ingredients, thereby reducing waste and supporting clean-label flavor enhancers.

In this study, we developed an integrated workflow combining in silico screening, molecular docking, MD simulations, and experimental validation to identify novel umami peptides from tamarind seed proteins. Six candidate peptides were predicted, synthesized, and confirmed by sensory evaluation and electronic tongue analysis. Molecular docking and MD simulations revealed their binding modes with the T1R1/T1R3 receptor, providing mechanistic insights. Importantly, the actual presence of these six peptides in enzymatic hydrolysates was verified by UHPLC-ESI-HRMS/MS, bridging computational prediction and practical application. This integrative approach advances understanding of T1R1/T1R3-mediated taste perception and offers a robust framework for developing natural umami enhancers, while promoting sustainable valorization of tamarind seed byproducts.

Materials and methods

Material and database

Fresh tamarind (T. indica) seeds were obtained from Yuxi Maoduoli Group Co., Ltd. (Yuxi, Yunnan, China). The seeds belong to a locally cultivated variety commonly used in regional food processing. After manual removal of the hard outer shell, the kernels were ground into powder and stored at −20 °C until use. The amino acid sequence of tamarind seed storage protein (accession number: ADO21646.1) was retrieved from the NCBI. The protein sequences of umami receptors T1R1 (UniProtKB: Q7RTX1) and T1R3 (UniProtKB: Q7RTX0) were obtained from the UniProtKB. All peptides were chemically synthesized with a purity of ≥95% by Hangzhou Specialized Peptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Pepsin (from porcine stomach mucosa, 250 U/mg), as gastrointestinal digestive enzymes, along with papain (from Carica papaya latex, 800 U/mg) and alkaline protease (from Bacillus licheniformis, 200 U/mg), were purchased from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). LC–MS grade acetonitrile and formic acid were supplied by Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Other reagents and solvents used in the experiments were all of analytical grade.

In silico hydrolysis of tamarind seed protein

The in silico enzymatic hydrolysis of tamarind seed protein was conducted using the BIOPEP-UWM server (https://biochemia.uwm.edu.pl/biopep-uwm/). Three proteolytic protocols were simulated: (i) gastrointestinal simulated digestion by pepsin (pH 1.3, EC 3.4.23.1), trypsin (EC 3.4.21.4), and chymotrypsin (EC 3.4.21.1) (Yu et al., 2022); (ii) simulated hydrolysis by alkaline protease (pH 9.0, EC 3.4.21.62) (Jiang et al., 2025); and (iii) simulated hydrolysis by papain (pH 6.5, EC 3.4.22.2) (Czelej et al., 2025). Peptides ranging from tetrapeptides to octapeptides were selected for further analysis.

Prediction of peptide properties

Peptide water solubility was predicted using the Innovagen Peptide Property Calculator (https://www.innovagen.com). Peptide toxicity was assessed using the ADMETlab 2.0 platform (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/) (Xiong et al., 2021). Potential umami activity was predicted using the Umami-MRNN machine learning model (https://github.com/Umami-MRNN) (Iwaniak et al., 2015).

Homology modeling and validation of the T1R1/T1R3 umami receptor

Homology modeling of the T1R1/T1R3 receptor was performed using Modeller 10.1, based on the crystal structure of the human metabotropic glutamate receptor (PDB ID: 1EWK), which exhibits over 30% sequence identity with the target receptors (Chang et al., 2024). The initial model was structurally optimized in GROMACS 2019.5 using the AMBER MMFF94S force field. The receptor was solvated in a TIP3P water box, and counterions were introduced to neutralize the system. Energy minimization was conducted with the steepest descent algorithm until the maximum force fell below 1000.0 kJ/mol·nm−1. The equilibrated system was subjected to NVT (2 ns) and NPT (1 ns) ensembles, followed by a 10-ns production run at 310.15 K and 1 bar, employing the V-rescale thermostat and Parrinello–Rahman barostat. Model quality and structural reliability were assessed using the SAVES v6.1 server (Zhao et al., 2024).

Molecular docking

Candidate peptide sequences were converted to SMILES format using the PepSMI server and subsequently to PDB format via NovoPro (De Cena et al., 2022). Peptide geometries were optimized with the MMFF94S force field in Avogadro. Receptor and ligand structures were prepared in AutoDockTools by adding Gasteiger charges and removing nonpolar hydrogens. Semi-flexible docking was performed with AutoDock Vina, treating the receptor as rigid and peptides as flexible. The docking grid was centered at (x = 69.8 Å, y = 44.9 Å, z = 70.1 Å) with a box size of 40 × 40 × 40 Å. Binding poses and molecular interactions were analyzed in PyMOL and Discovery Studio 2020(Zhao et al., 2024).

Molecular dynamics simulation

The top-ranked peptide–receptor complexes were subjected to 100-ns MD simulations in GROMACS 2019.5, following the protocol described in Section 2.4. Complex stability was assessed by calculating the root mean square deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), and hydrogen bond number throughout the trajectory.

Sensory validation of synthetic umami peptides

Preparation of synthetic umami peptides

Six selected peptides (DGIDF, VSDY, YNDPHC, AKNVSDYL, VPPD, and NDPHCQY) were synthesized at ≥95% purity by Hangzhou Specialized Peptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and stored at −80 °C until use.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory testing was conducted following a modified protocol (Chang et al., 2023). A trained panel of 10 individuals (5 males, 5 females) was calibrated using standard solutions: monosodium glutamate (0.35% w/v, umami), sucrose (1% w/v, sweet), caffeine (0.25% w/v, bitter), citric acid (0.08% w/v, sour), and sodium chloride (0.35% w/v, salty). Experiments were performed under controlled environmental conditions (25 ± 2 °C, RH 55 ± 5%). The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Kunming University of Science and Technology (Approval No. KMUST-MEC-2025-146). Prior to participation, all panelists received written information detailing the purpose of the study, the sensory evaluation procedures, the absence of any foreseeable risks, measures to ensure data confidentiality, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the commencement of the sensory tests.

Umami threshold determination

Each peptide was dissolved in ultrapure water at 2.0 mM (a concentration determined by pre-experiment to give clear umami without off-flavors) and serially diluted 1:1 (v/v). According to the ISO 13301:2018 standard, the umami recognition threshold was determined using the triangular test method. For each concentration level, each panelist received a set of three coded samples: two were blanks (ultrapure water, the same solvent used for dissolving peptides) and one was the peptide solution at that specific concentration. The panelists were asked to identify the sample that differed from the other two (i.e., the abnormal sample). The lowest concentration at which the panelists could reliably distinguish the peptide-containing sample from the two blanks in three independent repeated sessions was defined as the recognition threshold. The presentation order of the three samples within each set and the order of concentration levels across panelists were randomized (Li et al., 2020). Panelists also provided ratings and descriptions of basic taste perceptions.

Electronic tongue analysis

Peptide solutions (1.0 mM) were analyzed using the INSENT TS-5000Z electronic tongue system to evaluate taste profiles. Tartaric acid (0.3 mmol/L) and potassium chloride (30.0 mmol/L) were used as reference standards. Each sample was measured in quadruplicate, and the three most consistent values were used for analysis.

Identification of umami peptides from tamarind seed protein hydrolysates

Preparation of tamarind seed protein hydrolysates

Tamarind seeds were ground into powder and passed through an 80-mesh sieve. The powder was defatted with petroleum ether (1:4, v/v) until the solvent became colorless. After filtration, the defatted powder was air-dried at room temperature on filter paper for 12–24 h. The protein content of the dried powder (29.63%) was determined using the BCA kit method, and a standard curve was plotted using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. Was mixed with ultrapure water at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v) and sonicated for 30 min. The pH was then adjusted to the optimal value for each enzyme using 1 M HCl or 1 M NaOH (pepsin, pH 1.3; papain, pH 6.5; alkaline protease, pH 9.0). Enzymatic hydrolysis was carried out for 4 h at 37 °C for pepsin, and at 55 °C for papain and alkaline protease, with continuous stirring during the process. The reaction was terminated by heating in a boiling water bath for 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 8000 ×g for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected as tamarind seed protein hydrolysate. The hydrolysate was further fractionated using a 3 kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge tube, and the permeate was freeze-dried for subsequent analysis. Among them, after the pepsin enzymatic digestion was completed, the pH of the enzymatic digestion solution was carefully adjusted to 3.0 before analysis was conducted. This is to prevent the extremely acidic solution from damaging the chromatographic column. Although the in silico gastrointestinal digestion simulation employed pepsin, trypsin, and chymotrypsin, experimental verification used pepsin alone because VSDY is primarily generated by pepsin cleavage. Single-enzyme hydrolysis avoids pH incompatibility and excessive sample complexity, while still enabling validation of the target peptide's release.

Identification of tamarind peptides by UHPLC-ESI-HRMS/MS

The tamarind seed protein hydrolysate was characterized using a Thermo Fisher Q Exactive Focus mass spectrometer in positive ion mode. The chromatographic column was an Agilent Zorbax SB-C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). The mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution (phase A) and acetonitrile (phase B) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min with an injection volume of 3.0 μL. The elution gradient was set as: 0–3 min, 5.0% B; 3–10 min, 5.0%–10.0% B; 10–15 min, 10.0%–20.0% B; 15–20 min, 20.0%–95.0% B; 20–22 min, 95.0%–5.0% B; 22–25 min, 5.0% B. The mass spectrometry-related parameters were set according to the previous study (Tian et al., 2022). The spray voltage was set to 3.2 kV, capillary temperature to 350 °C, sheath gas flow rate to 35 arbitrary units (arb), and auxiliary gas flow rate to 10 arb. Full-scan MS1 data were acquired over an m/z range of 150–2000 with a resolution of 70,000 at m/z 200. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode was enabled, with the top 10 most intense precursor ions selected per cycle for MS/MS fragmentation. MS/MS spectra were acquired at a resolution of 35,000 using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy (NCE) of 30%. Dynamic exclusion was enabled with a duration of 15 s. Data from mass spectra were analyzed and processed using PEAKS Studio 11.0 software.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. Statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's test (p < 0.05). All statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics 29, and graphical visualization was conducted using Origin 2024 (Origin Lab, USA).

Results and discussions

Identification and screening of peptides

The primary sequence of tamarind seed protein, comprising 251 amino acids, was subjected to in silico hydrolysis using three different strategies—simulated gastrointestinal digestion, alkaline protease, and papain. This process yielded 38, 56, and 52 peptides, respectively, which were then included in subsequent screening and evaluation (Table S1). Structurally, umami peptides are typically characterized by anionic motifs, generally following the pattern —O) (C)n(O–) with n ranging from 3 to 9. Their molecular weights usually fall within 300–3000 Da, and the sequences are enriched in amino acids that contribute to umami perception (Pratama et al., 2022). Acidic residues, particularly Glu and Asp, are considered crucial for enhancing electrostatic interactions with the T1R1/T1R3 umami receptor, thereby intensifying umami taste (Li et al., 2023). However, not all short peptides elicit strong umami sensations: dipeptides and tripeptides generally have high thresholds and weak umami intensity (Sun et al., 2020). As peptide length increases, conformational features exert greater influence on taste intensity (Begum et al., 2021). Sensory studies further indicate that peptides with molecular weights below 1000 Da often display stronger umami characteristics, likely due to the greater exposure of key residues that facilitate receptor binding. In contrast, longer peptides may shield these residues within folded structures, reducing receptor affinity and umami perception (Liang, Zhou, et al., 2022). Additionally, long-chain peptides are less practical for industrial use due to the difficulties in synthesis and purification.

Based on the above considerations, clear quantitative standards were established. The following screening thresholds were applied: (i) peptide length of 4–8 amino acids and molecular weight < 1000 Da; (ii) good water solubility as predicted by the Innovagen Peptide Property Calculator; (iii) non-toxicity as assessed by ADMETlab 2.0; and (iv) positive umami prediction from both BIOPEP-UWM and the Umami-MRNN model. Only peptides satisfying all four criteria were retained for downstream analysis. Specifically, simulated gastrointestinal digestion produced ten soluble peptides (Table S2), papain produced ten soluble peptides (Table S3), and alkaline protease produced nine soluble peptides (Table S4). Notably, peptides from all three enzymatic strategies were enriched in acidic residues (Glu/Asp) and met the predefined criteria for length, solubility, safety, and umami prediction, confirming the robustness of our multi-step screening pipeline across different cleavage specificities.

It should be noted that although the optimal reaction pH values for pepsin (pH 1.3), papain (pH 6.5), and alkaline protease (pH 9.0) were specified in the virtual enzymatic digestion, the cleavage prediction by the BIOPEP-UWM platform is based solely on protease-specific amino acid sequence motifs and does not incorporate pH as a dynamic parameter. Consequently, the qualitative identity of the predicted peptide segments—i.e., their amino acid sequences—is independent of the assigned pH values. However, since real enzymatic activity, stability, and cleavage efficiency are inherently pH-dependent, this rule-based approach may not fully reflect actual peptide yields under experimental conditions. Recognizing this limitation, we proceeded with subsequent experimental validation via targeted enzymatic hydrolysis and UHPLC-HRMS/MS analysis. Taken together, these results identified 29 peptides with favorable physicochemical and functional profiles, which were subsequently selected for molecular docking-based receptor binding validation.

Homology modeling of the umami receptor T1R1/T1R3

When the sequence identity between a target protein and its template exceeds 30%, the confidence level of a homology model can reach approximately 80% (Pan et al., 2022). Using the SWISS-MODEL server (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive), suitable structural templates were identified. The sequence similarity between T1R1 and the A chain of the human glutamate receptor (PDB ID: 1EWK) was 38.7%, while that between T1R3 and the B chain was 39.7% (Zhao et al., 2024). Based on these values, the glutamate receptor was selected as the template for constructing the T1R1/T1R3 heterodimer. Among the generated models, the structure with the lowest overall energy was chosen as the representative conformation. To further refine the model, a 10-ns MD simulation was performed to optimize its geometry.

As shown in Fig. 1A, the left subunit corresponds to T1R1 and the right to T1R3. After 10 ns of MD optimization (Fig. 1B), T1R3 retained a closed conformation, whereas T1R1 exhibited an outward expansion, adopting a more open structure. This conformational shift was accompanied by a reduction in α-helices and an increase in loop regions (α-helices shown in red, β-sheets in yellow, loops in green). The structural validity of the optimized model was assessed using the SAVES v6.1 server. The Ramachandran plot (Fig. 1C and Table S5) indicated that only eight residues (1% of the total) were located in disallowed regions. The remaining residues were distributed across favored and allowed regions: 87.4% in the most favorable region, 9.9% in the additionally allowed region, and 1.7% in the generously allowed region. These results confirm the stereochemical soundness of the model.

To evaluate structural stability, the RMSD profile of the T1R1/T1R3 complex was calculated (Fig. 1D). RMSD values increased during the first 2 ns and then stabilized between 2 and 10 ns, indicating that the system reached equilibrium. Although minor changes in secondary structure were observed during the simulation, the overall RMSD stabilization suggests that the receptor complex attained a conformational steady state. Taken together, the favorable Ramachandran statistics, stable RMSD trajectory, and consistent secondary structure predictions demonstrate that the constructed T1R1/T1R3 homology model is structurally reliable and suitable for downstream molecular docking and dynamics analyses.

Virtual screening

Molecular docking is a widely used computational approach for simulating the recognition and binding of biomolecules based on spatial complementarity and energy minimization, thereby identifying the most favorable binding conformations. From the 29 peptides that passed the physicochemical and bioactivity screening (Section 3.1), we docked them into the constructed T1R1/T1R3 receptor complex to evaluate their binding potential. The predicted binding affinities, expressed as docking energies, are summarized in Table S6. The docking analysis revealed no significant correlation between binding affinity and peptide chain length, consistent with previous observations (Li et al., 2020). Peptides derived from gastrointestinal virtual digestion exhibited docking energies ranging from −6.5 to −8.2 kcal/mol, with DGIDF, VSDY, and PDAY showing the strongest predicted binding. For papain-generated peptides, the binding energies ranged from −6.6 to −8.8 kcal/mol, and YNDPHC, AKNVSDYL, APEIF, and YHEDL demonstrated particularly favorable affinities. In the case of alkaline protease digestion, predicted affinities ranged from −6.6 to −8.2 kcal/mol, with DGIDF, NDPHCQY, and VPPD showing the lowest energies. Generally, peptides with docking energies between −5 and − 15 kcal/mol are considered to form stable peptide–receptor complexes (Gu et al., 2024). To ensure balanced representation across enzymatic strategies while prioritizing binding strength, we adopted the following selection protocol: (i) the top two peptides with the lowest docking energies were initially chosen from each of the three enzymatic groups (gastrointestinal, papain, alkaline protease); (ii) since DGIDF was common to both the gastrointestinal and alkaline protease groups, it was retained as a single representative; (iii) to maintain a total of six candidates, the next-best peptide from the combined pool of remaining gastrointestinal and alkaline protease peptides was selected based on docking energy. Between PDAY (−7.8 kcal/mol, gastrointestinal) and VPPD (−8.1 kcal/mol, alkaline protease), VPPD was chosen due to its stronger predicted binding affinity. Based on this systematic and transparent selection process, six peptides with the most favorable predicted binding affinities were selected as potential umami candidates: VSDY, DGIDF, AKNVSDYL, YNDPHC, VPPD, and NDPHCQY.

To refine these predictions, further analyses are required to characterize intermolecular interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and salt bridges) and to validate the temporal stability of peptide–receptor complexes through MD simulations. Importantly, because in silico predictions of umami activity may not always fully align with human sensory perception (Zhu et al., 2021), experimental confirmation via sensory evaluation remains essential to establish the actual umami characteristics of these peptides.

Analysis of intermolecular interaction forces

The umami receptor T1R1/T1R3 belongs to the first identified umami receptor family within the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) superfamily and functions as a heterodimer (Zhang et al., 2019). The structural model clearly illustrates the molecular docking between umami peptides (ligands) and the primary receptor subunits (T1R1 and T1R3). Specifically, the T1R1 subunit exhibits a characteristic “venus flytrap domain” (VFTD), consisting of two lobes connected by a hinge, which forms a ligand-binding cavity upon closure (Yali et al., 2014). As shown in Fig. 2, all six identified peptides were predicted to bind within the T1R1 subunit. To further clarify the molecular basis of umami perception, we analyzed the intermolecular interaction forces underlying peptide–receptor binding in addition to docking affinity. Peptide–receptor interactions were mediated by three major forces: hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and hydrophobic interactions, among which hydrogen bonding was predominant (Liang, Duan, et al., 2022; Shiyan et al., 2021).

Numbers 1–3 represent the binding sites, hydrogen bonding interactions, and intermolecular interactions between the umami peptides and the T1R1 subunit, respectively.

Peptide VSDY: Formed 6 hydrogen bonds with residues PHE274, SER172, THR149, ASP219, ALA170, and ASP218, along with hydrophobic interactions involving 11 residues (e.g., SER275, GLY221, ALA171). Bond distances ranged from 2.1 to 2.8 Å (average 2.4 Å).

Peptide DGIDF: Formed 7 hydrogen bonds with residues THR149, ALA170, SER172, ASP192, SER300, VAL273, and SER76, plus hydrophobic interactions with 14 residues (e.g., ASP147, PHE247, ASP219). Bond distances ranged from 1.6 to 3.0 Å (average 2.4 Å).

Peptide AKNVSDYL: Established 11 hydrogen bonds with residues GLN195, ASP147, THR149, ASP219, SER382, GLU301, HIS308, SER276, SER300, VAL273, and PHE274, in addition to hydrophobic interactions with 16 residues (e.g., ASP192, SER385, SER172). Bond distances ranged from 1.9 to 2.9 Å (average 2.3 Å).

Peptide YNDPHC: Formed 10 hydrogen bonds with residues SER300, VAL273, ASP218, THR149, ASP192, ALA170, TYR169, SER172, ALA171, and PRO190, plus hydrophobic interactions with 20 residues (e.g., SER276, GLU301, ASN191). Bond distances ranged from 1.8 to 2.9 Å (average 2.4 Å).

Peptide VPPD: Formed 4 hydrogen bonds with residues TYR169, ALA302, SER300, and ASP192, and hydrophobic interactions with 16 residues (e.g., ASP218, ALA170, SER382). Bond distances ranged from 2.0 to 2.7 Å (average 2.4 Å).

Peptide NDPHCQY: Established 7 hydrogen bonds with residues SER306, GLU301, SER276, ASP147, ASP192, SER172, and PRO190, plus hydrophobic interactions with 22 residues (e.g., ALA302, PHE274, ASP218). Bond distances ranged from 1.7 to 3.0 Å (average 2.2 Å).

Overall, these results indicate that all six peptides can stably bind to T1R1/T1R3 primarily through hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces. However, not all interacting residues were equally critical. Key residues identified as essential for umami recognition included ASP147, THR149, ALA170, SER172, ASP192, ASP218, ASP219, PHE274, SER276, GLU301, ALA302, and SER306 (Spaggiari et al., 2020). Among the tested peptides, AKNVSDYL displayed the highest number of hydrogen bonds and the strongest interactions with critical residues (ASP147, THR149, ALA170, SER172, ASP218, ASP219, GLU301, SER276, and PHE274). This suggests that AKNVSDYL may exhibit the most pronounced umami perception among the six candidates.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular docking and interaction force analyses rely on static conformations, which cannot fully capture the dynamic stability of ligand–receptor interactions. In contrast, MD simulations allow monitoring of the temporal behavior of umami peptides bound to the T1R1/T1R3 receptor under simulated physiological conditions, thereby providing atomic-level insights into complex stability and interaction mechanisms (Zou et al., 2024). To this end, 100 ns MD simulations were performed for all six peptide–receptor complexes, and dynamic parameters including RMSD, Rg, SASA, and hydrogen bonds were analyzed.

RMSD analysis is commonly used to evaluate conformational stability by quantifying deviations in atomic positions over time. Smooth and plateaued RMSD curves generally reflect system equilibrium(Eidhammer et al., 2000). As shown in Fig. 3A, all complexes displayed fluctuations during the initial 30 ns but reached equilibrium after ∼50 ns, maintaining stable conformations thereafter. These results confirm stable peptide–receptor binding across all complexes, although additional indicators were required to distinguish relative stability.

Rg reflects the compactness of protein structures during simulation. As shown in Fig. 3B, all complexes maintained Rg values around 3.5 nm throughout 100 ns. The T1R1/T1R3–AKNVSDYL complex stabilized rapidly after initial fluctuations, whereas the others exhibited minor but continuous oscillations. Reduced Rg fluctuations indicate higher conformational rigidity, suggesting that AKNVSDYL binding enhances receptor compactness and stability.

SASA provides insights into solvent exposure and conformational adjustments upon ligand binding (Sharma et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 3C, SASA values initially fluctuated but stabilized after ∼80 ns for all complexes. Importantly, a gradual decline in SASA was observed across simulations, indicating burial of hydrophobic residues and strengthened hydrophobic interactions, both of which contribute to structural stability (Pan et al., 2022).

Hydrogen bonding plays a pivotal role in stabilizing peptide–receptor interactions (Li et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 3D, hydrogen bond counts fluctuated between 0 and 14. The T1R1/T1R3–DGIDF, T1R1/T1R3–AKNVSDYL, T1R1/T1R3–VPPD, and T1R1/T1R3–NDPHCQY complexes consistently maintained more than four hydrogen bonds, indicative of stable interactions. Notably, AKNVSDYL exhibited the highest number of hydrogen bonds, likely due to its longer peptide chain providing additional interaction sites. By contrast, the YNDPHC and VSDY complexes occasionally dropped to zero hydrogen bonds, suggesting relatively weaker stability. However, this transient loss does not imply complete dissociation from the receptor. At these time points, hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals forces likely maintained the peptides within the binding pocket. Consistently, the RMSD of YNDPHC remained stable between 0.8 and 0.9 nm, with a Rg of approximately 3.45 nm; VSDY showed an RMSD of ∼0.9 nm and Rg of ∼3.15 nm—both indicative of retained conformational integrity. Notably, the magnitude of hydrogen bond fluctuations aligns well with the sensory recognition thresholds: AKNVSDYL displayed the most persistent hydrogen bonding and the lowest threshold (strongest umami), whereas VSDY exhibited the largest fluctuations and the highest threshold (weakest umami). Thus, the temporary absence of hydrogen bonds in YNDPHC and VSDY does not undermine the overall conclusion regarding the relative umami potency ranking.

Overall, MD simulation results confirmed that all six peptides form stable complexes with the T1R1/T1R3 receptor. However, the AKNVSDYL complex demonstrated superior stability across all parameters (RMSD, Rg, SASA, hydrogen bonds), corroborating molecular docking and interaction force analyses. These findings strongly suggest that AKNVSDYL is the most potent umami peptide among the tested candidates. Nevertheless, sensory evaluation remains essential to validate the actual umami intensity of these peptides under physiological conditions.

Sensory and electronic tongue evaluation of umami peptides

The recognition thresholds of the six synthetic peptides—VSDY, DGIDF, AKNVSDYL, YNDPHC, VPPD, and NDPHCQY—were determined as 0.3562, 0.2750, 0.0812, 0.1937, 0.2500, and 0.2000 mmol/L, respectively (Table 1). Among these, AKNVSDYL exhibited the lowest threshold, indicating the highest sensory potency, followed by YNDPHC and NDPHCQY. Conversely, VSDY displayed the highest threshold, suggesting the weakest umami perception. Reported recognition thresholds for umami peptides vary considerably by source: for instance, peanut-derived peptides range from 0.40 to 3.00 mmol/L (Zhang et al., 2019), while clam-derived peptides range from 0.23 to 3.10 mmol/L (Zhang et al., 2021). Compared with these values, the relatively low thresholds of AKNVSDYL, YNDPHC, and NDPHCQY highlight their strong umami potential.

Interestingly, among peptides with similar molecular weights, VPPD formed a more stable complex with the T1R1/T1R3 receptor during MD simulations than VSDY, and this computational prediction was corroborated by sensory evaluation. VPPD elicited a significantly stronger umami taste than VSDY, underscoring the predictive power of computer-aided simulation methods for rapid screening of umami intensity, particularly for peptides of comparable size.

To further characterize taste profiles, sensory evaluation was complemented by umami dilution analysis and descriptive profiling. As shown in Fig. 3E, all six peptides exhibited sour–umami characteristics, likely due to the presence of acidic amino acids (Asp and Glu), which are well-known contributors to sour and umami sensations. In addition, residual sodium acetate from peptide synthesis may have enhanced these sour–umami notes (Song et al., 2023). The presence of Asp, Glu, and Ser may also impart salty–umami nuances, consistent with reports that these amino acids contribute to salty taste perception (Hu et al., 2024).

A slight bitter–umami aftertaste was also detected, attributable to hydrophobic residues such as Leu, Trp, Ile, Val, and Gln. These amino acids have previously been linked to bitterness in peptides (Liang, Duan, et al., 2022). Electronic tongue analysis (Fig. 3F) confirmed the sensory findings, showing that the peptides predominantly evoked umami, sour, and sweet taste signals. As shown in Fig. 3E and F, the umami recognition thresholds determined by sensory evaluation follow the order AKNVSDYL (0.0812 mM) < YNDPHC (0.1937 mM) < NDPHCQY (0.2 mM) < VPPD (0.25 mM) < DGIDF (0.275 mM) < VSDY (0.3562 mM), while the electronic tongue ranked umami intensity as AKNVSDYL > NDPHCQY > YNDPHC > DGIDF > VPPD > VSDY. The two methods show strong overall consistency—particularly in identifying AKNVSDYL as the most potent and VSDY as the weakest umami peptide—with only a minor discrepancy in the relative ranking of YNDPHC and NDPHCQY. This slight inversion likely arises from differences in molecular recognition: human taste receptors (T1R1/T1R3) exhibit nuanced sensitivity to specific amino acid side-chain properties (e.g., charge, hydrophobicity, or aromaticity), whereas the electronic tongue may respond differently to these structural features. Such variations are commonly observed in cross-method flavor assessments and do not undermine the general agreement between biological perception and instrumental analysis, thereby confirming the reliability of both approaches in evaluating the umami intensity of the six peptides. Overall, AKNVSDYL exhibited the strongest umami intensity score in the electronic tongue assay, consistent with sensory evaluation and computational predictions (docking and MD simulations).

Collectively, these results validate the umami properties of the six peptides. AKNVSDYL consistently demonstrated the strongest umami intensity across all evaluation approaches—lowest recognition threshold, highest stability in MD simulations, and top sensory and electronic tongue scores—supporting its identification as the most potent umami peptide in this study. Beyond scientific validation, these findings also carry practical implications: the discovery of novel umami peptides from tamarind seed protein not only expands the molecular basis of taste perception but also offers a sustainable strategy for valorizing underutilized seed resources. Such peptides hold promise as natural flavor enhancers in food formulations, potentially reducing reliance on synthetic additives while meeting the growing consumer demand for clean-label and health-promoting ingredients.

Identification of peptides in tamarind seed protein hydrolysates

To evaluate the reliability of in silico enzymatic hydrolysis and validate the computational predictions, tamarind seed protein hydrolysates generated by papain, alkaline protease, and pepsin were analyzed using UHPLC-ESI-HRMS/MS. As shown in Fig. 4, Fig. 5, and Table 2, all six predicted umami peptides—YNDPHC, NDPHCQY, VPPD, AKNVSDYL, VSDY, and DGIDF—were successfully detected in the corresponding enzymatic hydrolysates, demonstrating strong consistency between computational prediction and experimental verification.

The total ion chromatograms (TICs) obtained from different enzymatic treatments (Fig. 4) exhibited distinct peptide profiles, aligning with the protease-specific cleavage patterns predicted in silico. Extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) and MS/MS spectra (Fig. 5) further confirmed the presence of the target peptides, with observed m/z values matching theoretical molecular masses within acceptable mass tolerance. Notably, the experimentally identified peptides showed high protease-specific consistency with the in silico digestion results: YNDPHC and AKNVSDYL (predicted from papain) were detected in the papain hydrolysate; VPPD and NDPHCQY (alkaline protease) were detected in the alkaline protease hydrolysate; and VSDY (Gastrointestinal digestive enzymes) was confirmed in the Gastrointestinal digestive enzymes hydrolysate. For DGIDF, however, predicted from both Gastrointestinal digestive enzymes and alkaline protease, it was identified exclusively in the alkaline protease hydrolysate, likely due to the low specificity of pepsin generating complex peptide mixtures that hinder detection of low-abundance peptides (Ma et al., 2003). From an application perspective, this observation suggests that alkaline protease may be more suitable for targeted production of DGIDF.

The confidence of peptide identification was evaluated using average local confidence (ALC) scores from PEAKS Studio (Table 2). Peptides YNDPHC and NDPHCQY exhibited high ALC values (>90%), while VPPD and AKNVSDYL showed acceptable confidence (>80%). Lower ALC values were observed for VSDY (77.0%) and DGIDF (62.6%), which can be attributed to the limited fragmentation patterns of short peptides (e.g., VSDY contains only four residues) and the complexity of the hydrolysate matrix affecting detection of low-abundance peptides (Ma et al., 2003). Despite these lower confidence scores, the consistent m/z matching and chromatographic peaks support their presence. This further demonstrates that in silico screening effectively prioritizes candidate peptides, facilitating their subsequent experimental validation even in complex matrices. The relative abundance of the six peptides, expressed as a percentage of the TIC peak area in their respective hydrolysates, ranged from 0.40% to 1.48% (Table 2), revealing considerable variation in their enzymatic release efficiencies. Notably, the highest abundance was observed for AKNVSDYL (1.48%), followed by YNDPHC (0.81%) and NDPHCQY (0.42%) in the papain hydrolysate, and VPPD (0.68%) in the alkaline protease hydrolysate. This distribution is largely consistent with the sequence-specific cleavage patterns predicted in silico for each protease. In contrast, DGIDF (0.40%) and VSDY (0.61%) exhibited relatively lower abundances. Overall, these abundance analyses not only confirmed the successful release of all six predicted peptides, but also further supported the reliability of the screening process.

Overall, the identification of all six predicted peptides in the enzymatic hydrolysates supports the feasibility of the integrated computational screening approach and suggests that tamarind seed protein may serve as a potential precursor for umami peptide generation. From a practical perspective, this integrated in silico-to-in vitro strategy thus provides a reliable and efficient strategy for future umami peptide discovery and application. Future studies should focus on optimizing the enzymatic hydrolysis conditions—such as enzyme-to-substrate ratio, pH, temperature, and hydrolysis time—to enhance the release efficiency of these umami peptides from tamarind seed proteins. Additionally, the development of scalable separation and purification strategies will be essential to enrich the target peptides and achieve the desired sensory profiles. Such process optimization would not only facilitate the production of high-value umami peptide ingredients from an underutilized agricultural byproduct but also pave the way for their practical application in the food industry, thereby promoting the high-value utilization of tamarind seed proteins.

Conclusions

In this study, we established an integrated in silico to in vitro strategy that combines virtual hydrolysis, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and experimental validation to efficiently identify novel umami peptides from tamarind seed proteins. A total of six peptides VSDY, DGIDF, AKNVSDYL, YNDPHC, VPPD, NDPHCQY were identified and experimentally confirmed to exhibit stable binding to the T1R1/T1R3 receptor primarily via hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, with AKNVSDYL showing the strongest overall umami characteristics among the candidates. Their actual presence in enzymatic hydrolysates was confirmed by UHPLC-ESI-HRMS/MS, demonstrating the feasibility of the computational screening pipeline. These findings suggest that tamarind seed protein is a promising natural source of umami peptides, though further quantitative validation and sensory-guided fractionation are required to assess their actual contribution to the overall umami taste of hydrolysates. More broadly, the integrated screening framework demonstrated here provides a reliable and efficient strategy for the discovery of functional peptides from agricultural byproducts.
