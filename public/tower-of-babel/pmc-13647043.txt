Beak height and nest structure shape avian bite force
Authors: Si-Dan Lin, Xiao-Dong Rao, Wei Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bite force is a key performance trait in vertebrates, affecting prey capture, defense, resource competition, and other ecologically important behaviors. Despite broad functional relevance, systematic studies on avian bite force remain limited across species, and the morphological indicators used to infer bite performance are often inconsistent. The evolutionary mechanisms proposed to shape bite force, including diet, combat, and nest-complexity hypotheses, also remain insufficiently tested at broad comparative scales. Here, bite force and associated morphological traits were measured across 175 bird species to evaluate anatomical predictors of bite performance. Comparative analyses revealed that beak morphology, particularly beak depth, was more strongly correlated with bite force than cranial shape excluding the beak. To examine macroevolutionary patterns, newly generated measurements were integrated with published data, yielding a dataset of bite force values for 223 species. Phylogenetic generalized least-squares models were then used to assess proposed ecological and behavioral drivers of bite force, including body mass, diet, competition intensity, and nest structure. In addition to the allometric effect of body mass, nest-structure complexity emerged as a significant predictor of bite force, with species that construct more elaborate nests exhibiting stronger bite performance. These findings are consistent with the nest-complexity hypothesis and indicate that nest-building behavior has likely contributed to evolutionary diversification of bite force in birds. This broad comparative analysis clarifies the anatomical basis of avian bite performance and identifies key behavioral factors associated with the evolution of bite force.

INTRODUCTION

The relationship between biological form and function provides a central framework for understanding how natural selection shapes organismal performance (Huey & Stevenson, 1979). Although this concept has origins in Aristotelian thought (Peck, 1970), it remains foundational to contemporary trait-based biological research (Cernansky, 2017), including studies of biodiversity evolution (Kraft et al., 2015; Larcombe et al., 2018), ecosystem function (Díaz & Cabido, 2001; McGill et al., 2006), and responses to environmental change (Lavorel & Garnier, 2002; Purves et al., 2013). Birds provide a powerful system for examining this principle because variation in external morphology is closely related to ecological function. Global analyses of avian traits indicate that morphological variation can predict trophic niche, diet, and foraging strategy across broad taxonomic scales (Pigot et al., 2020).

Bite force provides a direct functional link between morphology and ecological performance, influencing food acquisition, mating competition, and predator defense across vertebrates (Anderson et al., 2008; Ziermann et al., 2019). The avian beak offers a tractable biomechanical system for evaluating this relationship because of the relatively simple mechanical structure of the jaw apparatus and the direct measurability of bite performance (Anderson et al., 2008; Sustaita & Hertel, 2010; Van Der Meij & Bout, 2004). For instance, work on Darwin’s finches has shown that beak shape and bite force reflect adaptive divergence associated with dietary specialization (De León et al., 2012, 2014; Herrel et al., 2005a, 2005b, 2010). Anatomical studies have long examined jaw musculature and craniofacial skeletal structure associated with bite mechanics (Bhattacharyya, 2013; Burton, 1974; Goodman, 1954; Sims, 1955), with an increasing number of studies measuring avian bite using force transducers (Corbin et al., 2015; Herrel et al., 2005a, 2005b; Sustaita & Hertel, 2010; Van Der Meij & Bout, 2004). Functionally, bite force has been linked to avian fitness components, including foraging efficiency (Van Der Meij & Bout, 2006), offering insights into feeding biomechanics and ecological diversification (Corbin et al., 2015).

Despite strong correlations between bite force and craniofacial morphology, the anatomical predictors of avian bite performance remain unclear. Studies across vertebrates indicate that head and beak dimensions are closely associated with bite force (Anderson et al., 2008; Lappin & Jones, 2014), and positive relationships have been reported for head size (Aguirre et al., 2002; Chazeau et al., 2013; Herrel et al., 1999, 2001a, 2001b, 2002; Husak et al., 2006; Verwaijen et al., 2002), head width, head height (Chazeau et al., 2013; Herrel et al., 2005b), beak width, and beak depth (Grant, 1981; Herrel et al., 2005a). However, comparative evaluation of these predictors remains limited. One study identified head width as the most critical determinant of bite force, but analysis was restricted to Darwin’s finches and has not been extended across the broader avian radiation (Herrel et al., 2005b). Avian morphology is intricately linked to ecological function (Pigot et al., 2020), with body mass serving as the most representative measure of these morphological characteristics. For example, principal component analysis (PCA) of beak length, beak width, beak depth, tarsus length, wing length, first secondary length, and tail length across 9 963 of 9 993 extant bird species has shown that body mass is strongly correlated with the first principal component of morphological variation (Pigot et al., 2020). Across vertebrates, stronger bite force has repeatedly evolved in association with larger body size (Aguirre et al., 2002; Chazeau et al., 2013; Herrel et al., 2004, 2010), and larger individuals or species often produce greater bite force (Erickson et al., 2003; Herrel & O’Reilly, 2006; Meyers et al., 2002). Because many craniofacial measurements increase with body size, correlations between morphology and bite force may partly reflect allometric scaling rather than trait-specific mechanical effects (Aguirre et al., 2002; Chazeau et al., 2013; Erickson et al., 2003; Herrel et al., 2004, 2010; Herrel & O’Reilly, 2006; Meyers et al., 2002). Therefore, large-scale comparative studies involving more bird taxa can assist in determining which morphological indicators are most relevant to bite force.

Functional demand provides a second axis through which bite force may evolve, with similar selective pressures producing convergent patterns across diverse vertebrate groups. Under the diet hypothesis, variation in bite force is expected to reflect mechanical demands imposed by prey size, food type, or food hardness. In the Cape dwarf chameleon (Bradypodion pumilum), habitat-induced constraints on food resources have strengthened the correlation between prey size and bite performance (Measey et al., 2011). In bats, bite force varies with trophic niche (Aguirre et al., 2002; Santana et al., 2011, 2012) and increases with dietary hardness (Aguirre et al., 2003; Santana et al., 2010), consistent with selection for enhanced feeding performance when processing mechanically resistant foods. Bite force may also contribute to sexual selection and the establishment of social status, as proposed by the combat hypothesis. For instance, male green anoles (Anolis carolinensis) (Lailvaux et al., 2004) and Tenerife lizards (Gallotia galloti) (Huyghe et al., 2005) with greater bite force are more likely to prevail in aggressive encounters, potentially increasing access to mates. In birds, however, direct evidence linking bite force to ecological function remains limited, with only a few studies of Darwin’s finches indicating that diet can affect bite force. Notably, De León et al. (2012, 2014) combined food resource surveys with stomach-content analyses and revealed that birds with broader ecological niches also occupied a wider range of bite forces, while Herrel et al. (2010) reported associations between feeding mode, such as base crushing and tip biting, and bite force. However, morphometric analyses of raptor beaks have indicated that beak shape is largely independent of diet (Bright et al., 2016). Consequently, the relationship between bite force and diet remains controversial.

Nest-structure complexity represents another potential determinant of bite force. Nest building remains an underappreciated form of animal construction behavior, yet it exhibits striking phenotypic parallels with tool manufacture in the selection, transport, placement, and modification of external materials (Hansell & Ruxton, 2008; Healy et al., 2008; Guillette & Healy, 2015). In birds, increasing architectural complexity, from the absence of nest construction to platform and cup nests, has been associated with greater cerebellar elaboration (Hall et al., 2013). More complex nest construction likely imposes higher demands on motor coordination and material manipulation, which may favor increased cerebellar foliation and denser cerebellar neural circuitry (Butler & Hodos, 2005; Iwaniuk et al., 2009). If nest architecture can be linked to neural systems that support fine motor control, then the beak, as the primary organ used to grasp, transport, and position nesting materials, may also have undergone adaptive evolution related to construction behavior. In birds, forelimb transformation into wings shifted many manipulative functions to the beak. Similarly, among primates, modern humans are believed to possess a unique and powerful ability to grip, which facilitates the manufacture and utilization of tools (Marzke, 2013). Bipedalism in humans has freed the forelimbs from quadrupedal locomotion, enabling the manufacture and use of tools (Bloch & Boyer, 2002; Cartmill, 1974). Grip strength is also considered an important adaptation in primates, which has become most refined in humans, co-evolving with bipedalism, tool-related behavior, brain expansion, and language (Wilson, 1998). Consistent with this pattern, chimpanzees exert less force than humans during precision grips (Marzke et al., 1999), and primates as a group show highly developed capabilities for grasping and object manipulation (Napier, 1960, 1993). In birds, bite-mediated gripping may serve an analogous construction-related function. Given that nest building is crucial for individual reproductive success, the mechanical interaction between the beak and nesting materials is pivotal in determining the final nest architecture (Sugasawa & Pritchard, 2022). Nest-material selection is highly plastic and evolutionarily labile (Mayr, 1982), and beak morphology also exhibits significant evolutionary flexibility (Abzhanov et al., 2004, 2006), raising the possibility that construction behavior and beak performance have evolved in concert. Thus, we can anticipate that the complexity of nest structures may also rely on substantial beak strength to manipulate and carry nesting materials, resulting in a morphological-behavioral coadaptation. Thus, birds that construct more complex nest structures may possess greater bite force, a hypothesis we propose as the nest complexity hypothesis.

Morphological adaptation can enhance performance in one functional domain while limiting performance in another (Edwards et al., 2016). Such trade-offs may be important in the evolution of avian bite force. In Darwin’s finches, for example, bite force is negatively associated with song complexity (Herrel et al., 2009). Cognitive capacity and behavioral flexibility may further modify the selective value of bite force by enabling more complex food handling strategies (Ducatez et al., 2020), such as claw- or tool-assisted cracking of seeds or clams (Rutz et al., 2010) or aerial dropping of hard food items onto solid substrates (Switzer & Cristol, 1999). These behaviors may reduce reliance on high bite force and thereby weaken selection for maximal bite performance. Therefore, in addition to independent drivers that facilitate the evolution of bite force, trade-offs exist that limit its development. The coexistence of multiple hypotheses, unresolved performance trade-offs, and limited comparative evidence on avian bite force and ecological function highlights the need for broad phylogenetic analyses across diverse bird lineages to clarify the adaptive significance of bite force and address current knowledge gaps.

This study quantified bite force across 175 bird species and evaluated craniofacial traits previously associated with bite force, including head length, head width, head height, beak length, beak width, and beak depth, to identify the morphological predictors most closely linked to avian bite force. Newly generated measurements were integrated with published bite-force data to assemble a comparative dataset spanning 223 bird species. Candidate drivers of bite-force evolution were then examined, including body mass, trophic niche, territoriality, and nest structure, as well as potential evolutionary trade-offs such as flight ability and intelligence. Phylogenetic generalized least-squares (PGLS) modeling was used to investigate the relative contributions of morphology, ecology, behavior, and evolutionary trade-offs to interspecific variation in avian bite force.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study site and bird sampling

Birds were captured with mist nets and banded at study sites across 11 locations in China, including Hainan, Taiwan, Fujian, Guizhou, Yunnan, Jiangxi, Henan, Hebei, Tianjin, Liaoning, and Jilin (Figure 1), from 2014 to 2017. During the breeding season, audio playback was used to facilitate capture. During the non-breeding season, migratory birds were captured, banded, and measured based on phototactic behavior at Fenghuangshan Bird Banding Station and Wuliangshan National Nature Reserve, Yunnan Province. All individuals included in the study were adults and were released into suitable habitats immediately after measurements were completed.

Sampling locations for 175 bird species used for bite-force measurements in the present study

Morphological assessment and transformation

Bite force was quantified using a first-generation bite force sensor (NBIT-DUD-2404A; Nanjing Bio-Inspired Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) with a precision of 0.01 N. The system consisted of an analog data acquisition card (NBIT-DUS-2404A), an oral bite-force transducer, and analysis software (DSU2404A) (Rao et al., 2021). Before measurement, the data acquisition card was connected to the computer, the dimension parameter was set to 1, and the measurement range was adjusted according to the expected values to ensure that recorded bite-force values remained within the established maximum and minimum limits. The analysis software was configured before bite-force recording. During measurement, each bird was induced to bite the metal probe, generating a bite-force waveform file (Rao et al., 2021). Maximum bite force was calculated by subtracting the pre-measurement baseline value from the peak value recorded by the force transducer, according to the following equation:

1\begin{document}$ F=\frac{U-Z}{D} $ \end{document}

where F represents bite force in newton (N), with a precision of 0.01 N; D represents the linear coefficient of the transducer, a constant of −7.4707446 volt/newton; U represents the voltage recorded by the software in volt (V), with the maximum voltage used for calculations; and Z represents the baseline voltage of the transducer before measurement in volt.

Bite force differs between the beak tip and base (Herrel et al., 2010), and birds typically bite force-transducer probes with the beak tip during measurement (Van Der Meij & Bout, 2004, 2006). To maintain a consistent measurement standard across all individuals, bite force was recorded at the beak tip. Each individual was measured in triplicate, and the highest value was retained as the maximum bite force (Herrel et al., 2005a). During measurement, some birds bit the transducer probe voluntarily, whereas others required gentle manual persuasion to induce biting. Only individuals in suitable physical condition were included in bite-force measurements.

Other morphological variables included body mass, tarsus length, head-to-beak length, head width, head height, beak length, beak width, and beak depth. Body mass was measured using an electronic weighing scale (EHA501; Guangdong Xiangshan Weighing Apparatus Group Ltd., Guangzhou, China) with a precision of 0.01 g. Linear morphological traits were measured using electronic digital vernier calipers (Guilin Guanglu Measuring Instrument Co., Ltd., Guangxi, China) to the nearest 0.01 mm. When a measurement exceeded the 100 mm maximum range of the calipers, a tape measure was used to the nearest 0.1 mm. Head-to-beak length was defined as the distance from beak tip to the posterior margin of the head; head width was defined as the maximum width immediately posterior to the eyes; head height was defined as the maximum height of the head; beak length was defined as the distance between the exposed skin at the beak base and the upper beak tip; beak width was defined as the maximum width across the nasal-frontal hinge; beak depth was defined as the maximum height between the upper beak and lower beak near the posterior edge of the nares (Rohwer & Spaw, 1988).

Head length was calculated by subtracting beak length from head-to-beak length. Head size was then estimated from head length, head width, and head height by treating the head as an ellipsoid (Møller, 2010):

2\begin{document}$ Head\ size=\dfrac{4}{3}\times\pi\times\frac{Head\ length}{2}\times\frac{Head\ width}{2}\times\frac{Head\ height}{2} $ \end{document}

Beak size was represented by the grasp index, calculated as the product of beak length and beak width (Rohwer & Spaw, 1988):

3\begin{document}$ Beak\ size=Beak\ length\times Beak\ width $ \end{document}

Data collection

Bite force, head length, head width, head height, beak length, beak width, beak depth, tarsus length, and body mass were measured for 175 bird species (Supplementary Table S1). To expand taxonomic coverage, 1 123 bite-force records and 131 body-mass records representing 115 bird species were compiled from published studies (Supplementary Table S2). Because bite-force trials may not always capture the physiological maximum for a given individual or species, the highest available bite-force value was retained for each species, whether generated in this study or obtained from the literature. When both direct measurements and estimates derived from skeletal or muscular traits were available for the same species, direct measurements were used. The final dataset comprised bite-force data for 265 bird species, including 150 species measured only in this study, 90 species represented only by literature records, and 25 species shared between both data sources. These species spanned 20 orders and 68 families (Supplementary Table S3; Figure 2).

Phylogenetic tree of the 265 bird species included in this study and their corresponding bite-force values

To identify factors associated with interspecific variation in avian bite force, body mass, behavioral innovation, trophic niche, territoriality, and nest structure were compiled for the 265 species (Supplementary Table S3). Body-mass data for target species were obtained from the AVONET database (Tobias et al., 2022), except when direct body-mass measurements from this study were available.

Behavioral innovation data were taken from a synthesis of ornithological reports published between 1960 and 2018, which quantified the capacity of birds to exploit novel foods or foraging tools (Ducatez et al., 2020). Because diet and feeding strategy may impose different mechanical demands on the beak, trophic niche data were obtained from AVONET and included 10 classes: aquatic predator, frugivore, granivore, aquatic herbivore, terrestrial herbivore, invertivore, nectarivore, omnivore, scavenger, and vertivore (Tobias et al., 2022). Territoriality was included as a proxy for competition intensity, because species with stronger territorial behavior may rely more heavily on bite force during aggressive interactions over food, territories, mates, or other resources. Territoriality was classified as none, weak, or strong following Tobias & Pigot (2019). Finally, nest structure was included to test whether construction behavior contributes to bite-force evolution. During nest building, birds require substantial bite force to hold and manipulate nesting materials, potentially imposing mechanical demands on bite performance. Nest-structure data were obtained from Chia et al. (2023) and classified into seven categories: scrape, platform, cup, dome, dome with tunnel, primary cavity, and secondary cavity.

Statistical analysis

Correlation between bite force and morphological indicators

To identify morphological indicators most closely associated with bite force, correlation analyses were performed for the 175 measured bird species using bite force, head length, head width, head height, head size, beak length, beak width, beak depth, beak size, tarsus length, and body mass. Following previous studies, all morphological indicators were log-transformed to reduce right-skewed distributions and improve approximation to normality (Deeming et al., 2022).

Phylogenetic signal in bite force

To assess whether avian bite force showed a phylogenetic signal (i.e., whether closely related species exhibited more similar bite-force values than expected by chance), Blomberg’s K (Blomberg et al., 2003) and Pagel’s λ (Pagel, 1999) were calculated. These metrics quantify phylogenetic conservation by comparing observed trait distributions with those predicted under a Brownian motion model. Higher values indicate stronger phylogenetic structuring, with λ generally ranging from 0 to 1 and K exceeding 1 when closely related species resemble one another more strongly than expected under Brownian motion. Statistical significance was validated using null models. For K, bite-force values were randomly permuted among species across the phylogeny 1 000 times to generate a null distribution representing absence of phylogenetic signal (Kraft & Ackerly, 2010). For λ, likelihood ratio tests were used to determine whether observed values differed significantly from the null hypothesis of λ=0, which indicates no phylogenetic signal.

Phylogenetic data were sourced from birdtree.org (Jetz et al., 2012), comprising 10 000 phylogenetic trees for 9 993 bird species based on the order-level topology from Hackett et al. (2008). A maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree was generated using the maxCladeCred() function in the R package phangorn (Schliep, 2011). Terminal branches were pruned to retain only the 265 target bird species included in the comparative dataset (Figure 2). Phylogenetic signal in bite force was evaluated using the phylosig() function in the R package phytools (Revell, 2012).

Factors influencing bite force

Missing trait data were limited to territoriality (10 species, 3.8%), nest structure (10 species, 3.8%), and behavioral innovation (54 species, 20.4%). Given that the proportion of missing data was relatively small, species lacking these variables were excluded from subsequent model fitting.

Several trophic-niche and nest-structure categories contained few species (NHerbivore aquatic=3, NHerbivore terrestrial=3, NNectarivore=1, NScavenger=1; NDome tunnel=6, and NPrimary cavity=5), and some categories exhibited functional similarities (e.g., primary cavity and secondary cavity, aquatic herbivore, and terrestrial herbivore). Therefore, trophic niches and nest structures were consolidated before analysis. Aquatic predators, scavengers, and vertivores were grouped as predators; nectarivores and frugivores were grouped as frugivores; aquatic herbivores and terrestrial herbivores were grouped as herbivores; dome tunnels and domes were grouped as domes; and primary and secondary cavities were grouped as cavities (Table 1) (Hall et al., 2013). Seven brood-parasitic species in the dataset (Cuculus fugax, Cuculus sparverioides, Cuculus micropterus, Cacomantis merulinus, Cuculus saturatus, Cuculus poliocephalus, and Cuculus canorus) do not build nests and were therefore removed from model construction.

Reclassification of trophic niches and nest structures

Considering that avian bite force exhibited strong phylogenetic signal (see RESULTS), factors associated with bite-force magnitude were evaluated while accounting for interspecific similarities. A PGLS model of bite force and related traits was constructed for the remaining 223 bird species using the "gls()" function in the R package nlme (Butler & Bates, 2023):

4\begin{document}$ \mathrm{ln(BF)}\sim {\mathrm{ln\;(BM)+ln\;(INN+1)+TN+TER+NS}} $ \end{document}

where BF denotes bite force; BM denotes body mass; INN denotes behavioral innovation; TN denotes trophic niche; TER denotes territoriality; and NS denotes nest structure. BF, BM, and INN were log-transformed to reduce right-skewed distributions and improve approximation to normality (Deeming et al., 2022).

The data were analyzed using an evolutionary model incorporating the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process (Butler & King, 2004), which provides a better fit than an evolutionary model based on Brownian motion. The OU model assumes the existence of an optimal value for a given trait, with selection intensity (α) being proportional to the distance between the current trait value and the optimum. When α equals 0, the OU model approximates the Brownian motion model; conversely, larger α values indicate stronger stabilizing selection (Butler & King, 2004).

RESULTS

Bite-force data were compiled for 265 bird species, spanning 20 orders and 68 families (Supplementary Table S3; Figure 2). Correlation analyses across the 175 species measured in this study showed positive associations between bite force and all morphological indicators, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.259 to 0.557. Beak depth exhibited the most pronounced association with bite force (Pearson’s correlation coefficient=0.557, P<0.001), followed by beak width (Pearson’s correlation coefficient=0.542, P<0.001) (Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

This study examined the morphological correlates and evolutionary drivers of avian bite force by integrating direct bite-force measurements with ecological, behavioral, and phylogenetic data across a wide range of bird species. Results indicated that avian bite force was most closely associated with beak morphology, particularly beak depth, while body size remained an important determinant of variation. After accounting for body mass allometry, nest structure also emerged as a significant predictor of bite force, suggesting that construction behavior may contribute to the evolution of avian bite performance.

The avian beak provides a classic example of morphological adaptation to ecological function, with beak morphology often regarded as an evolutionary response to trophic niche, as shown in Darwin’s finches (Grant & Grant, 1993), Hawaiian honeycreepers (Lovette et al., 2002), and Madagascan vangas (Jønsson et al., 2012). Previous studies have shown that head and beak dimensions can influence bite-force magnitude (Anderson et al., 2008; Lappin & Jones, 2014). In the medium ground finch (Geospiza fortis), head width, beak width, and beak depth are reliable predictors of bite force, with head width showing the strongest association among these traits (Herrel et al., 2005b). This relationship likely reflects the position of jaw-closing muscles along the posterior cranium and lateral mandible, because larger head size affords additional space for muscle attachment and development (Bowman, 1961; Van Der Meij & Bout, 2004). However, analyses across 175 bird species showed that beak morphology (excluding beak length) was more strongly correlated with bite force than head morphology (Figure 3). These results suggest that bite-force variation is not determined solely by cranial dimensions or jaw-muscle volume, but also by intrinsic muscle properties, muscle architecture, and jaw mechanisms. For example, raptors and parrots have similarly shaped maxillae, yet parrots exhibit much higher mechanical advantage values (Navalón et al., 2019). Biomechanical analyses of Darwin’s finch beaks have also shown that increased beak width and depth can improve stress dissipation during the cracking of hard seeds, thereby reducing fracture risk under high loading (Soons et al., 2010, 2015). Thus, head morphology is a less reliable predictor of bite force variability than beak morphology, perhaps owing to the influence of intrinsic muscle properties and joint mechanics.

Across vertebrates, bite force functions as an integrative performance trait linked to feeding, mating, defense, and resource competition, and it often reflects the mechanical intensity of ecological challenges faced by an organism (Anderson et al., 2008). A previous comparative analysis of 151 extant carnivores indicated that bite force may have evolved in association with feeding specialization (Christiansen & Wroe, 2007). In birds, dietary variation has also been associated with differences in bite force (Badyaev et al., 2008; Santana et al., 2010; Van Der Meij & Bout, 2006), and evolutionary shifts in bite force may align with dietary divergence (Santana & Dumont, 2009). However, this relationship does not appear to be universal. Quantitative analyses of raptor beaks and skulls have shown that beak evolution is constrained by cranial morphology and may not respond independently to changes in food resources (Bright et al., 2016). Furthermore, most previous studies have focused on comparisons among a limited number of bird species (Badyaev et al., 2008; Carril et al., 2015; Herrel et al., 2010; Sustaita & Hertel, 2010; Sustaita & Rubega, 2014; Van Der Meij & Bout, 2004, 2006, 2008). For instance, Sustaita & Hertel (2010) focused solely on North American raptors, constraining broader inference across avian diversity. By integrating bite force with candidate ecological and behavioral drivers, including body mass, trophic niche, territoriality, and nest structure, together with potential trade-off variables such as behavioral innovation, the present analysis tested multiple hypotheses across 265 bird species. The results did not support diet as a primary driver of interspecific variation in avian bite force. Instead, bite force was significantly associated with body mass allometry, territoriality, and nest structure.

The role of food resources in shaping bite force has been emphasized repeatedly across vertebrates (Anderson et al., 2008; Christiansen & Wroe, 2007), with Darwin’s finches providing a classic avian example (De León et al., 2012, 2014; Herrel et al., 2010). However, diet does not necessarily impose a dominant selective pressure on feeding mechanics in all lineages. In Orthoptera, analyses of jaw shape, bite efficiency, and dietary preference have indicated that phylogenetic history exerts a stronger influence on jaw morphology and mechanical advantage than diet. This was attributed to an ancestrally phytophagous condition and evolution in habitats with abundant food sources, potentially reducing selection for specialized mouthparts (Edel et al., 2024). Similarly, diet may be a stronger driver of bite-force evolution in certain avian groups, particularly in habitats with limited resources or frequent ecological disturbance, although this is unlikely to apply uniformly across birds. Instead, the importance of diet probably depends on the ecological context, feeding mode, and evolutionary history of each lineage. For instance, granivorous birds are often presumed to generate high bite force (Van Der Meij & Bout, 2004), a pattern partly supported by the slightly larger coefficients for granivory obtained in the present model (Table 2). However, granivory does not always require seed fracture by the beak. Columbiformes often swallow seeds whole and rely on a well-developed muscular gizzard for mechanical processing, reducing the need for high bite force during feeding. Similar heterogeneity occurs among invertivorous birds: species that consume soft-bodied worms likely experience weaker mechanical demands than species that feed primarily on hard-bodied arthropods. These examples indicate that broad trophic categories may obscure important variation in food-handling behavior and prey mechanical properties, consistent with the large standard errors associated with trophic-niche coefficients in the model (Table 2).

In addition, territoriality, which denotes the ability of birds to defend themselves against natural enemies or intraspecific competition, did not show a positive correlation with bite force as anticipated, suggesting that avian beaks may not function primarily as combat structures. Although weaponry adapted for fighting is prevalent in many animal groups, including horns in even-toed ungulates, chelicerae in arthropods, and tusks in elephants (Emlen, 2008), these specialized structures are uncommon in birds, with bony spurs restricted to relatively few species (Menezes & Palaoro, 2022). The evolution of weaponry may entail substantial costs for flight, reproduction, and survival (Menezes & Palaoro, 2022), and direct use of beaks in combat could increase the risk of severe injury or death. Even raptors, despite occupying high trophic positions, predominantly use their talons for hunting and aggressive encounters, reserving their beaks primarily for handling prey after capture.

Notably, nest structure emerged as a significant predictor of avian bite force (Table 2), with species that construct more complex architectures, such as cup nests, possessing greater bite-force values (Figure 4). Previous studies have largely examined bite force in relation to foraging, predator defense, and competition, with the potential role of bite performance in nest construction receiving little attention. Nest building is essential to reproductive success but remains an underexplored behavior in studies of avian functional evolution (Guillette & Healy, 2015; Hall et al., 2013). With the exception of brood-parasitic cuckoos and many ground-nesting birds, such as shorebirds, most birds construct nests and rely on the beak to grasp, transport, position, and, in some cases, weave nesting materials (Fang et al., 2018). Because nest construction is energetically demanding (Mainwaring & Hartley, 2013; Withers, 1977), stronger bite force may enhance the efficiency of material manipulation and reduce the energetic cost of construction, thereby preserving resources for reproduction. More elaborate nest architectures may also require greater cognitive investment, superior fine motor control, enhanced beak and foot coordination, or more advantageous eye placement (Healy et al., 2023), although these hypotheses remain unproven. Sheard et al. (2023) identified an association between beak shape and nesting materials, although this relationship was strongly influenced by phylogenetic signal and sampling bias and was further modulated by ecological niche and evolutionary history. The same study also highlighted significant plasticity and opportunism in nest-material use: beak morphology predicted broad material categories, such as twigs, more effectively than finer materials, such as leaves, suggesting that beak form may be linked to other aspects of nest design, including structural complexity. Our findings demonstrated a correlation among beak morphology, bite force, and nest structure across bird species, providing a new perspective on these aforementioned studies. Notably, this pattern is consistent with evidence that more complex nest structures are associated with greater cerebellar elaboration, increased cerebellar foliation, and expanded neural circuitry involved in processing capacity, motor coordination, and manipulative skill (Hall et al., 2013). These findings support the nest-complexity hypothesis, which proposes that the mechanical demands of nest construction contribute to evolutionary variation in avian bite force. However, as the current analysis included only 223 bird species, further confirmation is needed using broader taxonomic sampling.

CONCLUSION

Comparative analyses across diverse bird species revealed that avian bite force was most closely associated with beak morphology, particularly beak depth. Beyond the allometric effect of body mass, nest-structure complexity was linked to variation in bite force, with species exhibiting larger body mass and more complex nest structures associated with stronger bite force. Our study highlights how the nesting needs of birds can shape variations in bite force, offering novel insights into the manner in which animals modify form-function relationships in response to natural selection pressures.
