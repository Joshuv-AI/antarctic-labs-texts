Intelligent emotion and behavior recognition for online learners using deep affective computing
Authors: Priya K, Pradeep G, Ravimaran S
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTeachers in online classes often have limited visibility into how students are feeling and whether they are staying engaged. This paper presents a practical affective-computing framework that analyses webcam video to recognize both facial emotions and learning-centered behavioral states during live virtual sessions. Student faces are first detected in each frame, then encoded using a convolutional network with an attention module to emphasize informative facial regions. To capture how affect evolves over time, the extracted features are grouped into fixed 16-frame sequences and modelled with a bidirectional GRU to predict four behavioral states: engaged, confused, frustrated and disengaged. We evaluate emotion recognition on the FER2013 benchmark under the standard Train/Public Test/Private Test split, and we evaluate behavior recognition on a publicly available online-learning facial expression dataset (EmoDetect, Kaggle) used for the behavior-recognition experiments from 120 learners (800 annotated temporal segments; inter-annotator agreement κ≈0.75). The proposed model reaches 94.5% accuracy on FER2013 Private Test and achieves 86.9% accuracy with a macro-F1 of 85.4% on the four-class behavior task, improving over facial-expression-only baselines. Profiling further shows that the full pipeline can run at around 25 frames per second on a mid-range GPU, indicating feasibility for real-time monitoring. These results suggest that combining attention-based facial representations with temporal modelling can provide instructors with clearer, webcam-observable indicators of learners’ affect and engagement during online teaching.Keywords: Affective computing, Emotion recognition, Behavior recognition, Student engagement, Spatio-temporal modelling, Online learningSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Online learning has reshaped everyday teaching and learning, and video-conferencing platforms such as Zoom, Google Meet and Microsoft Teams are now central to many courses1. Although these tools broaden access and offer flexibility, they also reduce the visibility of behavioral and emotional cues that instructors routinely use to gauge how students are coping with the lesson2. In face-to-face classrooms, teachers can often detect confusion, frustration, boredom, or engagement at a glance and adjust explanations, pacing, or support accordingly. These decisions are informed not only by what students say, but also by nonverbal signals-facial expressions, gaze direction, head movements and other attentive behaviors-that indicate whether learners are following the content3. In virtual settings, however, such cues may be incomplete or unreliable because of low-quality webcams, unstable lighting, limited screen space, and the small grid-view windows in which students appear. As a result, instructors may find it difficult to assess understanding during the session and to identify students who need timely help before disengagement occurs4.

Affective computing, and in particular facial-expression analysis, has been explored as a practical way to recover some of the learner cues that are less visible in virtual classrooms. Facial Expression Recognition (FER) systems apply computer vision and deep learning to infer affective states from facial movements and appearance patterns5. However, much of the FER literature is built around a small set of basic emotion labels-such as happiness, sadness, anger, fear, and surprise-typically predicted at the level of individual frames. Although these categories provide a general indication of affect, they do not adequately describe the learning-related states that matter during instruction. In online classes, students often exhibit learning-centered behavioral states, including confusion, frustration, sustained mental effort, and disengagement, which can strongly influence comprehension and attention6. Many current FER pipelines overlook these states because they treat expressions as isolated snapshots rather than modelling how affective cues persist, fluctuate, and transition over time within a learning episode. Capturing these temporal dynamics is therefore important for moving from generic emotion recognition to actionable indicators of learner engagement.

Additional challenges stem from the characteristics of online video streams. Students appear under highly variable lighting, low-resolution webcams, cluttered or changing backgrounds, and grid-style layouts where many participants are displayed simultaneously7. Methods developed using high-quality, single-face images often degrade in this setting, because faces may be small, partially occluded, or distorted by compression and network artefacts. These conditions can reduce detection reliability and make frame-level predictions unstable, especially when learners shift posture, look away from the screen, or when the platform dynamically resizes video tiles. This motivates the need for robust, context-aware models tailored to online learning, capable of handling multi-student scenes and leveraging temporal information to stabilise predictions and better reflect learning-centered behavioral dynamics8.

Deep-learning methods now make it practical to move beyond single-frame emotion labels and towards more reliable analysis in real classroom video. Convolutional networks can extract facial representations that remain informative under noise and compression, attention mechanisms can focus the model on regions that carry expression cues, and sequence models can summarise short-term dynamics rather than treating each frame independently. When these components are combined, the model can use both what is expressed on the face and how that signal changes over time, which is important for learning-centered behavioral states that develop across a short interval rather than appearing as a single snapshot. This spatial-temporal modelling typically produces more stable predictions in online-class conditions than frame-only FER pipelines9.

To address these gaps, we develop a deep affective-computing framework intended for online teaching scenarios. The approach estimates facial affect from individual video frames and then uses temporal context to infer learning-centered behavioral states-engagement, confusion, frustration, and disengagement-from short sequences of webcam footage10. An attention-augmented CNN is used to emphasize informative facial regions and produce robust feature representations, while a temporal behavior module aggregates features across successive frames to reduce the instability typical of frame-only FER outputs11. The pipeline is further designed for realistic virtual classrooms by incorporating multi-student face detection that remains effective under low-resolution cameras, compression artefacts, variable lighting, and grid-view layouts12.

This study examines whether a single deep-learning pipeline can track students’ affect and learning-centered behavior from ordinary webcam video during live online classes. The goal is to use immediate facial cues together with short temporal context to infer behavioral states-engagement, confusion, frustration, and disengagement-more reliably as learners progress through instructional content.

Understanding learners’ emotional and behavioral states can help instructors make better decisions during teaching, especially in online classes where many in-person cues are difficult to observe. The proposed framework is designed to provide timely, webcam-based indicators that can help educators notice learners who may be struggling, track observable engagement signals, and adjust explanations or pacing during live sessions when appropriate13. More broadly, systems that analyse affect and behavior in real time may support the development of emotionally aware digital learning environments by enabling earlier intervention and more personalized support, provided that privacy, consent, and responsible use are clearly addressed14.

Related works

Recent research has increasingly focused on understanding student emotions and behavior in online learning environments, especially as virtual classrooms have become widely adopted. Early studies primarily used conventional CNN-based Facial Expression Recognition (FER) models trained on benchmark datasets such as FER2013 and RAF-DB. While these systems recognized basic emotions effectively, they were not specifically developed for online educational settings and often struggled with real-world constraints such as low video resolution and multi-student grid views.

Theoretical grounding and construct operationalisation

Student engagement is widely conceptualised in educational psychology as a multidimensional construct that includes behavioral, emotional, and cognitive components rather than a single facially observable state15,16. The ICAP framework further connects learners’ overt activity to the depth of cognitive engagement by distinguishing Interactive, Constructive, Active, and Passive modes17. In webcam-based online classes, however, the available signals are largely limited to facial behavior and coarse attentional indicators (e.g., on-screen gaze/orientation). Accordingly, we operationalise engagement in this work as webcam-observable engagement indicators that primarily reflect emotional engagement and aspects of behavioral/attentional engagement, and we do not claim direct measurement of deeper cognitive or interactive engagement processes.

Confusion and frustration are treated as learning-related achievement emotions. Under the Control–Value Theory of Achievement Emotions, such emotions arise from learners’ appraisals of control (e.g., perceived competence) and value (task importance), and can influence persistence and strategy use during learning18. In parallel, Cognitive Load Theory provides a complementary explanation: demanding tasks may elicit confusion/frustration when working-memory demands become high relative to available resources19. Finally, Self-Determination Theory motivates the instructional relevance of monitoring these indicators20, although SDT needs are not measured directly in the present vision-only setup. This framing clarifies that the proposed system predicts operational, observable affective–attentional states aligned with established learning theories and informs the segment-level annotation codebook used for behavior labels.

Beyond basic FER benchmarks, several datasets focus specifically on engagement and learning-centered affect in e-learning settings. DAiSEE provides in-the-wild e-learning videos annotated for boredom, confusion, engagement, and frustration, supporting sequence-based engagement analysis21. An additional Indian-student affective database captures faces along with hand gestures and body postures for both e-learning and classroom environments, using structured annotation procedures22. These resources motivate the need for practical models that operate under webcam-quality constraints and clearly reported evaluation protocols.

Prior work on affective dynamics during learning commonly analyses a compact set of learning-centered states such as engagement/flow, confusion, frustration, and boredom, including their transitions during problem solving and tutoring interactions23,24. In parallel, disengagement/off-task behavior is an established construct in learning analytics and has been operationalised as task-unrelated activity or withdrawal23. These empirical standards motivate the use of a concise behavior taxonomy that remains interpretable and instructionally actionable in webcam-based settings.

Salloum et al.25,26 presented a refined CNN-based FER model trained on FER2013 for classifying seven basic emotions. Their study achieved high accuracy and demonstrated that CNNs can support emotion-aware personalized teaching. However, their work focused only on basic emotions and relied on generic datasets rather than learning-centered affect, limiting its applicability to online classroom behavior understanding.

Rahman et al.27 introduced the EmoDetect dataset, one of the first learning-centered affective databases designed specifically for online learning scenarios. Their dataset included real student facial videos annotated for frustration and non-frustration during online tasks. Although EmoDetect contributed significantly to dataset development for educational emotion research, the authors mainly addressed data collection and manual coding, without proposing a complete deep-learning model for real-time deployment.

Aly et al.28 reported an emotion-recognition model built on a ResNet-50 backbone enhanced with a Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) to emphasize informative facial regions. Their implementation improves fine-grained facial feature representation and was presented in the context of an online learning support application. Nevertheless, the approach remains centred on emotion classification and does not incorporate an explicit behavior-recognition layer or temporal modelling to capture how affective cues evolve across an online session.

Zhang et al.29 proposed a spatio-temporal model that integrates a ResNet50 feature extractor with CBAM attention and a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) to capture facial-expression dynamics in online-learning video. Their study explicitly considers practical challenges such as illumination variability, partial occlusions, and temporal dependency between frames, and it demonstrates that temporal modelling can improve the robustness of continuous emotion estimation. However, the focus remains on tracking emotion progression, and the framework does not extend to learning-centered behavioral constructs-such as confusion episodes, disengagement, or indicators of sustained cognitive effort-that are typically required for instructional interpretation.

Wang et al.30,31 introduced SFER-YOLOv5, a lightweight facial-expression recognition approach intended for multi-student online classroom video. The model combines modifications such as soft-NMS, coordinate attention, and revised loss formulations to improve efficiency and robustness under low-resolution streams and grid-view layouts. While the study reports engagement-related outputs using broad affective groupings (e.g., positive, neutral, negative), the resulting engagement assessment remains coarse. These studies collectively highlight the growing interest in applying affective computing to virtual learning, but they also reveal important limitations. Most models still rely on basic emotions rather than learning-specific affective states. Several works lack temporal modelling to interpret how emotions evolve throughout learning activities. Engagement analysis remains coarse, and few systems integrate facial detection, emotion recognition, temporal behavior analysis, and multi-student processing into a unified, real-time framework designed specifically for online classrooms.

Proposed methodology

The proposed methodology introduces an intelligent deep affective computing framework designed to recognize both emotional states and learning-centered behavioral patterns of students in real time during online classes. The system integrates multi-student face detection, attention-enhanced facial emotion recognition, and temporal behavior modelling into a single end-to-end. The overall workflow is illustrated conceptually as a series of interconnected modules that operate sequentially to extract, analyse, and interpret affective signals from online classroom video streams Table 1.

System overview

The proposed framework operates through three integrated modules that work together to analyse student’s affective states in real time during online classes. First, the multi-student face detection module identifies and extracts all visible student faces from each video frame, even under challenging virtual classroom conditions such as low-resolution webcams, varied lighting, and grid-style layouts. A lightweight YOLO-based face detector is used to localise student faces efficiently in multi-participant online-class video. The detected regions are cropped, aligned, and normalized before being processed by an attention-enhanced CNN for emotion recognition. The attention mechanism emphasizes informative facial regions (for example, around the eyes and mouth) so that subtle expression cues are retained in the resulting frame-level feature representation. These features are then passed to a temporal behavior module, which models short sequences using a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) or a bidirectional GRU to capture how affective cues evolve across consecutive frames. Incorporating temporal context enables the inference of learning-centered behavioral states-including engagement, confusion, frustration, and disengagement-under common virtual-classroom conditions21,22. Together, the detection, emotion-encoding, and temporal modelling components form an integrated pipeline for continuous monitoring of students’ affective and behavioral patterns in online learning environments. A temporal modelling layer (TCN/Bi-GRU) observes how these emotions evolve over time to identify higher-level behaviors such as engagement, confusion, frustration, or disengagement. Consistent with multidimensional engagement definitions and ICAP, these behavior labels are treated as webcam-observable engagement indicators rather than direct measures of latent cognitive engagement Fredricks et al.15,16,17. The outputs are visualised through an analytics layer that provides per-student summaries and instructor-support insights, while an evaluation and explainability module reports performance and highlights important facial regions. A dedicated training unit continuously improves the model using FER datasets and classroom data.

Dataset description and model formulation

The proposed affective computing framework requires two categories of data: (i) facial expression images for emotion recognition, and (ii) continuous video sequences for learning-centered behavior analysis. To achieve this, the system uses a combination of publicly available facial expression datasets for pre-training and a publicly available online-learning facial expression dataset (EmoDetect, Kaggle) for behavior-recognition experiments. This hybrid dataset strategy ensures that the model learns general emotional features while also adapting to the unique challenges of real online classroom environments.

The public FER datasets-such as FER2013, and AffectNet-provide large, diverse collections of annotated facial expressions. These datasets are used to initialise the CNN with strong generalisable emotional features. Formally, each FER dataset provides input samples.

Result and discussion

This section describes the implementation environment, datasets, preprocessing pipeline, and experimental setup used to evaluate the proposed intelligent affective computing framework for online learners. The details are organized into system configuration, dataset description, and preprocessing architecture so that the experimental results in the subsequent subsections can be interpreted clearly from a practical deployment perspective.

System configuration and implementation environment

The proposed framework was implemented using Python and deep learning libraries to support real-time processing of online classroom video streams. All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7/Ryzen 7-class CPU, 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 3060/3080 GPU with at least 8–10 GB VRAM, running Windows 11/Ubuntu 22.04. GPU acceleration was essential to handle multi-student face detection, CNN inference, and temporal sequence modelling and runtime profiling results are reported in Section 4.1.1 to quantify FPS, latency, and compute usage. On the software side, the models were developed using PyTorch for deep learning, OpenCV for video capture and frame manipulation, and scikit-learn for computing evaluation metrics such as precision, recall, F1-score, and confusion matrices. Jupyter/Google Colab was used during prototyping for rapid experimentation and visualisation, whereas a standalone Python script was used for real-time inference. This configuration ensures that the proposed system can be reproduced on a typical mid-range research workstation or modern cloud GPU instance, making deployment feasible in institutional settings.

Behavior labels were assigned at the segment level using a predefined annotation protocol. Disagreements were first reviewed through structured discussion; unresolved cases were decided by a third adjudicator, and the adjudicated labels were retained as the final ground truth for model training and evaluation. The operational definitions and observable criteria used in the codebook are reported in the subsequent subsection (‘Operational behavior labeling criteria’), enabling replication in comparable settings.

Rationale and relationships within the four-class taxonomy

The four labels (engaged, confused, frustrated, disengaged) are used as a theory-informed and practically observable subset for webcam-based online learning, rather than as a complete list of all possible learner states. Engaged and disengaged represent an on-task versus off-task/withdrawal distinction based on attention and observable behavior. Confused and frustrated represent on-task challenge-related achievement emotions, which can occur even when attention remains on the learning content. Learning research reports common affective progressions in which engagement can shift to confusion and, if unresolved, to frustration, followed by disengagement or boredom23,24. Boredom and mind-wandering are not annotated as separate classes because webcam-only facial cues and coarse gaze/orientation cues do not support consistent and reliable separation,when such episodes appear as sustained attention away from the screen, they are captured under disengaged. Cognitive overload is treated as a contributing explanation for confusion/frustration rather than a directly observable label in video-only annotation.

Theory-informed mapping of labels to learning constructs

To support construct validity, the label set and annotation rules were defined with reference to established learning theories while remaining consistent with the non-intrusive signals available in webcam video. In line with multidimensional accounts of engagement (behavioral, emotional, and cognitive) and the ICAP framework’s emphasis on observable activity as an indicator of engagement depth, engaged and disengaged are treated as webcam-observable engagement indicators. Operationally, these labels reflect mainly attentional/behavioral cues (e.g., sustained on-screen orientation) together with emotion-related facial behavior, rather than unobservable cognitive processing. Similarly, confused and frustrated are annotated as learning-related achievement emotions consistent with Control–Value Theory, and they are also compatible with Cognitive Load Theory, where high task demands can lead to confusion and frustration when processing resources are exceeded. This mapping motivates the operational criteria specified below while keeping interpretation limited to observable indicators rather than the full latent construct of engagement.

Dataset attributes, structure, and splitting strategy

A structured summary of key dataset characteristics is presented in Table 4.4, including resolution, label types, number of classes, and the roles each dataset plays in the proposed framework. All datasets were resized to a unified input dimension (48×48 for grayscale FER models and 112×112 or 224×224 for RGB models), ensuring compatibility with the CNN architecture.

For the online-learning dataset, recorded virtual-classroom videos were sampled into frame sequences at 10–15 frames per second to preserve short-term temporal structure for behavior analysis. Each frame was processed using a YOLO-based multi-student face detector to localise individual learners in grid-view scenes. Detected face regions were then cropped, aligned, and intensity-normalized to reduce variability introduced by lighting, head pose, and webcam quality. The resulting face streams were grouped into fixed-length temporal segments so that the model could learn from short sequences rather than isolated images. Annotation was performed at two levels: frame-level emotion labels capturing momentary affective cues (e.g., happy, sad, neutral, confused, frustrated) and segment-level behavior labels capturing learning-centered states (engaged, confused, frustrated, disengaged). The dataset was divided into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing for model development and evaluation. This distribution helps maintain a balanced assessment of both spatial and temporal components of the proposed framework. This distribution ensures that the model generalises well without data leakage.

To improve generalisation and to prevent any form of data leakage, the dataset is split in a participant-disjoint manner, such that all temporal segments belonging to a particular learner appear in only one subset (training, validation, or testing). The test subset is kept strictly held out throughout the entire development process and is not used for hyperparameter selection, model selection, or early-stopping decisions. Hyperparameters such as the learning-rate schedule, segment length, and stopping criteria are finalised using only the training and validation subsets, and the final performance is reported once on the untouched test subset. Preprocessing

The preprocessing stage is a critical component of the proposed affective computing framework, as it transforms noisy, real-world classroom video streams into clean, structured inputs suitable for deep learning models. The recorded online learning sessions often contain multiple students with heterogeneous lighting, varied camera quality, occlusions, and background distractions. A robust preprocessing pipeline is necessary to maintain stable performance under the variability of online-classroom video. The raw recordings are first sampled into sequential frames at 10–15 frames per second (FPS), which preserves short-term temporal continuity while keeping computation within practical limits. Each frame is then passed through the YOLO-based multi-student face detection module to identify all visible faces within the grid-layout video. After detection, each face region is cropped and aligned using eye landmarks to compensate for head tilt and posture changes. This step improves spatial consistency across frames and reduces within-class variation in facial appearance.

After alignment, the cropped faces are resized to a fixed input dimension (e.g., 112×112 or 224×224), followed by intensity normalisation using per-channel means and standard deviations. During training, data augmentation is applied to the training split to improve generalisation, including Gaussian noise injection, random cropping, brightness perturbation, random horizontal shifting, and small rotations. Augmentation is applied only to the training split during model training; no augmentation (and no test-time augmentation) is used for validation or test evaluation. No augmentation is applied to the validation or test sets (FER2013 PublicTest/PrivateTest and the held-out behavior test set), and no test-time augmentation (TTA) is used for any reported results. This transformation harmonises luminance levels across samples, which is particularly important given the high variation in lighting conditions encountered in virtual classroom environments Finally, the preprocessed frames are grouped into fixed-length temporal segments, preserving sequential integrity and enabling the temporal behavior model (TCN/Bi-GRU) to analyse emotion evolution over time.

Feature extraction

Feature extraction is a key stage in the proposed framework because it determines how effectively facial cues relevant to affect and learning-centered behavior are represented from online-classroom video. The CNN backbone learns spatial patterns that are informative for expression analysis, including changes around the eyebrows and eyelids as well as variations in mouth shape. The feature-map visualisation in Fig 6 suggests that the network attends to different facial regions depending on the underlying state: responses are typically stronger in the upper-face area (eyes and eyebrows) for cues related to confusion, whereas activations often shift toward the lower-face region (mouth and chin) for expressions associated with positive affect or frustration. These observations confirm that the CNN successfully learns discriminative spatial characteristics relevant to learning-centered emotion recognition.

The integration of the CBAM attention mechanism further enhances the extraction of meaningful features by selectively amplifying semantically important areas while suppressing non-informative background regions. The CBAM spatial masks shown in Fig 6 clearly indicate focused attention on fine-grained facial attributes, showing sharper, more interpretable regions of interest compared to the raw CNN feature maps. This selective attention significantly improves the clarity of the learned representations, enabling the system to differentiate between behavior states that share similar appearance patterns-for example, distinguishing confusion from frustration, which often present overlapping cues. The attended feature maps (Fig 6 (d)) demonstrate how the fused attention highlights the most behavior-relevant elements of the face, producing cleaner and more behavior-consistent embeddings.

These refined spatial features are then passed to the temporal modelling stage (Bi-GRU), which interprets frame-wise feature evolution to recognize dynamic behaviors such as engagement, disengagement, or escalating frustration.

Clarification of evaluation objectives

The proposed system is assessed through two separate and clearly defined classification goals to avoid any confusion in interpretation. First, we evaluate learning-centered behavior recognition using the online learning dataset, where the task is 4-class temporal behavior identification (engaged, confused, frustrated, disengaged) from short face-video segments. This second evaluation reflects the main educational application of our framework and measures how reliably the model can track behavior changes over time in realistic online classroom conditions. Throughout the manuscript, all figures, tables, and performance metrics are therefore reported separately and are explicitly linked to the corresponding dataset and objective so that emotion results are not interpreted as behavior results, and vice-versa.Second, we evaluate facial emotion recognition using the FER2013 benchmark, where the task is standard 7-class emotion classification from face images. This experiment is included to validate the capability of our feature extraction and attention mechanism under a widely used public dataset.

The proposed CNN-CBAM-Bi-GRU framework is designed with a balanced configuration that ensures strong representational capacity while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for real-time affective analysis The architecture comprises a three-stage CNN backbone that learns spatial facial representations using 3×3 convolutions with ReLU activations and batch-normalisation layers for training stability. A CBAM block is applied to refine these representations through channel and spatial attention, encouraging emphasis on informative regions (notably around the eyes, eyebrows, and mouth) that carry subtle expression cues. Temporal dynamics are then modelled with a single bidirectional GRU layer containing 128 hidden units per direction, enabling the network to use both preceding and succeeding context within each frame sequence. The dual-headed classification layer supports simultaneous prediction of emotional categories and learning-centered behaviors. The model is trained using the Adam optimizer with a conservative learning rate and L2 regularisation to prevent overfitting. Relative to the baseline configuration, this combination produces a substantial gain in F1-score (approximately +20 percentage points), indicating the benefit of integrating regional attention with temporal modelling for robust facial expression recognition. Thus, the evaluation confirms that the proposed framework meaningfully extends the capabilities of existing FER models and introduces a significant methodological innovation over the prior literature.

Statistical significance and robustness

To ensure that the observed performance improvement is consistent and not an effect of a single favourable run, each model is trained and evaluated for K independent runs using different random seeds while keeping the same train/validation/test split for all methods. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and 95% confidence intervals are also reported to show the expected range of performance. For statistical validation, a paired t-test is applied to the run-wise accuracy and macro-F1 scores to compare the proposed method with the strongest baseline. In addition, McNemar’s test is conducted on paired test predictions (correct vs. incorrect) to check whether the difference between the two classifiers is statistically significant at the standard threshold (α = 0.05). This combined reporting provides both robustness evidence and statistical support for the claimed improvement.

Verification of FER2013 evaluation protocol and split integrity

FER2013 is evaluated using the standard split structure provided in the Kaggle release, namely Train, PublicTest, and PrivateTest. The PrivateTest split is treated as the final held-out test set and is not used during training, model selection, or hyperparameter tuning. Model optimisation is carried out using only the training data, while PublicTest is reserved for validation decisions such as early stopping and checkpoint selection. Preprocessing steps (face resizing and intensity normalisation) are performed in a split-consistent manner, and data augmentation is applied exclusively to the training split to avoid any leakage through augmented variants. Split isolation is maintained throughout the pipeline so that no images from the test set influence the learned parameters or tuning decisions. Because the reported FER2013 accuracy is notably higher than several commonly cited baselines, the manuscript avoids presenting it as a general state-of-the-art claim and instead reports it as benchmark performance under the stated protocol, with sufficient methodological detail to support independent verification. Further validation of the reported FER2013 result by independent researchers using the same evaluation protocol would help strengthen confidence in its reliability.

The proposed CNN–CBAM–Bi-GRU approach integrates well-established attention and temporal modelling components into a single end-to-end pipeline for analysing affect in online learning. In this work, CBAM is used to strengthen spatial feature learning from facial cues, while the Bi-GRU captures short-term temporal changes across consecutive frames to support behavior inference41,38,39. Discussion

The results obtained in this study demonstrate the effectiveness of integrating spatial attention and temporal modelling for recognising students’ emotions and behavior patterns during online learning. Traditional FER models such as VGG-CNN, CNN+BoVW, and early convolutional architectures primarily rely on static spatial features, resulting in limited sensitivity to subtle learning-related cues. Online classes reduce the teacher’s ability to observe students’ learning cues because facial expressions and attention behaviors are often only partially visible in small, low-quality video tiles. Traditional FER methods mostly identify basic emotions from single images, but learning in a real classroom involves time-dependent states such as confusion, frustration, engagement, and disengagement, which develop across several moments rather than one frame. To address this gap, the proposed framework analyses a live virtual-classroom video stream in three connected stages. First, a lightweight YOLO-based module detects multiple students in grid-view scenes and extracts aligned face crops, improving robustness under low resolution, variable lighting, and compression artefacts. Second, an attention-enhanced CNN (CBAM) learns fine facial features by emphasising the most informative regions (especially around the eyes, eyebrows, and mouth) and generates frame-level affective representations. Third, a temporal module (e.g., Bi-GRU/TCN) aggregates these representations over short sequences to model how facial cues evolve across time, enabling inference of learning-centered behavioral states such as engaged, confused, frustrated, and disengaged. In this way, the framework moves from isolated emotion snapshots to temporally consistent behavioral interpretation, which is more suitable for realistic online teaching scenarios and for readers who are new to affective computing in education.

Conclusion

This study presents an end-to-end deep affective-computing framework for real-time recognition of learners’ emotions and learning-centered behavioral states in online classrooms. The proposed pipeline integrates multi-student face detection and preprocessing with an attention-enhanced CNN (CBAM) for extracting fine-grained facial representations, followed by a Bi-GRU temporal module that models short-term dynamics across consecutive frames. By combining spatial attention with sequence modelling, the framework supports inference of educationally relevant behavioral states-engagement, confusion, frustration, and disengagement-rather than relying only on basic frame-level emotion labels.

Experimental evaluation on a benchmark facial-expression dataset and a EmoDetect online-learning dataset indicates that the proposed approach improves recognition performance compared with frame-only baselines. Alongside quantitative metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score), qualitative analyses such as activation visualisations and embedding plots provide additional support that the model focuses on meaningful facial regions and learns separable behavioral patterns. The temporal component contributes to more stable predictions over time, which is important because learning-related states often develop across several moments during instruction. From an application perspective, such monitoring can assist timely instructional responses, for example by flagging sustained confusion or disengagement so that additional explanation or targeted support can be provided.

Several directions remain for future work. Generalisability can be strengthened by expanding the dataset to include broader diversity in age groups, cultural contexts, devices, and environmental conditions. Future research may also explore transformer-based vision backbones and incorporate complementary modalities, such as speech or interaction signals, to better represent cognitive states that facial cues alone may not capture. For large-scale use, edge-optimised inference and model compression should be investigated to support low-power devices and multi-participant sessions. Finally, responsible deployment requires clear attention to privacy, consent, fairness, and transparency; privacy-preserving training and explainability mechanisms should be considered to support safe and appropriate use in educational environments. These advancements would further strengthen the applicability, reliability, and societal acceptance of intelligent affect-aware learning systems.
