Chronic but not Transient Anemia Is Associated With Atrial Fibrillation Recurrence After Catheter Ablation
Authors: Mikkel Guldberg Kasch, Númi Torpe Snæbjörnsson, Lise Da Riis‐Vestergaard, Rogina Rached, Daniel Mølager Christensen, Casper Binding, Mariam Elmegaard, Mads Hashiba, Sebastian K. Nielsen, Morten Lambert, Christian Torp‐Pedersen, Morten Schou, Gunnar Gislason, Abdullahi Ahmed Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnemia is common in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF). Prior studies have associated anemia—based on a single pre‐procedural hemoglobin measurement—with AF recurrence after catheter ablation. However, it remains unclear whether chronic anemia confers a different risk compared with transient anemia. This study evaluated the association between transient and chronic anemia and AF recurrence following first‐time catheter ablation.MethodsIn this nationwide cohort study, we included 15 017 patients from the Danish National Ablation Database undergoing first‐time AF ablation between 2010 and 2024 with available hemoglobin measurements within 12 months prior to the procedure. Patients were categorized as having no anemia, transient anemia, or chronic anemia based on repeated hemoglobin measurements. The primary outcome was AF recurrence within 1 year after a 90‐day blanking period, defined as a composite of AF‐related hospitalization, cardioversion, antiarrhythmic drug use, or repeat ablation. Associations were assessed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsA total of 877 patients (5.8%) had chronic anemia and 1592 (10.6%) had transient anemia. During follow‐up, 3445 patients (23.6%) experienced AF recurrence. The 1‐year cumulative incidence of recurrence was highest in patients with chronic anemia (32.5%), followed by transient anemia (24.2%) and no anemia (23.0%). Compared with non‐anemic patients, chronic anemia was independently associated with an increased risk of AF recurrence (adjusted hazard ratio [HR]: 1.36; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.18–1.56), whereas transient anemia was not (HR: 0.98; 95% CI: 0.87–1.10).ConclusionChronic—but not transient—anemia prior to first‐time catheter ablation was independently associated with a higher risk of AF recurrence.

Introduction

In recent years, catheter ablation has been increasingly used as a rhythm control strategy to treat atrial fibrillation (AF), largely due to the limitations and side effects of antiarrhythmic drugs [1, 2, 3]. However, AF recurrence remains a significant challenge, with 36%–49% of patients experiencing recurrence within a year after ablation [4, 5, 6], often requiring repeat procedures and imposing a substantial burden on both patients and healthcare systems. Several independent predictors of AF recurrence after ablation have been established, including female sex, AF type, AF duration, uncontrolled hypertension, left atrial size, left ventricular ejection fraction, elevated body mass index, and valvular disease [4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Emerging evidence suggests that anemia, a common comorbidity in AF patients, may also contribute to an increased risk of recurrence [11]. Patients with AF are at increased risk of developing anemia, partly due to the use of anticoagulants, which can cause occult gastrointestinal bleeding, and partly due to inflammation‐related functional iron deficiency [12, 13]. Anemia may promote AF through structural atrial remodeling, mediated by increased cardiac output, myocardial ischemia, and neurohormonal activation [14, 15, 16]. However, studies investigating anemia and AF recurrence after catheter ablation have relied on a single hemoglobin measurement, which may not adequately capture the chronicity or temporal pattern of anemia. It remains unclear whether persistent (chronic) anemia carries a different prognostic impact compared to transient anemia. Distinguishing between these patterns may provide important insights into the pathophysiological role of anemia in AF and improve risk stratification prior to ablation. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between transient and chronic anemia and AF recurrence in a real‐world cohort of patients undergoing first‐time catheter ablation for AF.

Methods

Data Sources

This Danish nationwide real‐world study utilized comprehensive data from Danish healthcare registries and adhered to the STROBE guidelines for reporting [17]. The primary data source was the Danish National Ablation Database, a validated nationwide registry with a positive predictive value of 90%–99% across all variables, documenting percutaneous ablation procedures for tachyarrhythmias in Denmark [18]. Additional data were retrieved from key national databases. Laboratory test results were obtained from the nationwide Register of Laboratory Results for Research [19]. Information on sex, birthdate, and vital status was sourced from the Danish Civil Registration System [20]. Data on inpatient and outpatient hospital visits were collected from the Danish National Patient Registry [21]. Cancer diagnoses were retrieved from the Danish Cancer Registry [22]. Data on prescriptions dispensed at Danish pharmacies were extracted from the Danish National Registry of Medicinal Product Statistics [23]. Details on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision diagnostic codes, Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical codes, and the NOMESCO Classification of Surgical Procedures used in this study are available in Table 1.

Definition of Anemia

Anemia was defined based on the criteria established by the World Health Organization, which define anemia as hemoglobin levels below 12.0 g/dL (120 g/L) for women and below 13.0 g/dL (130 g/L) for men [24]. Patients were categorized into three groups according to hemoglobin measurements within the 12 months prior to ablation: no anemia, transient anemia, and chronic anemia. Chronic anemia was defined as hemoglobin below sex‐specific thresholds on at least two occasions separated by ≥ 90 days within this time window. Transient anemia was defined as the presence of at least one hemoglobin value below the threshold, not fulfilling the criteria for chronic anemia. No anemia was defined as the absence of any hemoglobin measurements below the sex‐specific thresholds during the same period.

Study Population and Period

We included all patients aged 18 years or older with paroxysmal or persistent AF who underwent first‐time ablation between January 1, 2010, and June 1, 2024. Hemoglobin measurements obtained within the 12 months prior to ablation were used to classify patients into anemia groups. Patients without hemoglobin measurements within 12 months before ablation could not be assigned to an exposure group and were excluded. Patients with a history of major bleeding or receipt of blood transfusion within the 12 months prior to ablation were excluded. Follow‐up began 90 days after ablation (blanking period) and continued for up to 12 months thereafter. Patients were followed until recurrent AF, death, emigration, or administrative censoring at the end of the study period (April 1, 2025), whichever occurred first. Thus, patients included late in the study period had shorter potential follow‐up due to administrative censoring.

Comorbidity and Pharmacotherapy

Baseline characteristics of the study population were assessed at the time of ablation. Comorbidities were considered present if documented during an inpatient admission or outpatient visit within the 5 years preceding the ablation date. Diabetes mellitus was defined as filling at least one prescription for antidiabetic medication within 6 months before ablation, excluding women under 40 years who only redeemed prescriptions for metformin to account for its potential use in treating polycystic ovary syndrome. Hypertension was defined as the use of at least two antihypertensive medications within 6 months before ablation. Other relevant medication use was assessed within the same 6‐month period. The CHA2DS2‐VA score was calculated according to current atrial fibrillation management guidelines [2]. For further details, see Table 1.

Outcome

The primary outcome was recurrent AF, defined as a composite endpoint of hospital admission for AF (ICD‐10: I48), electrical cardioversion (NCSP: BFFA00, BFFA01, BFFA04), redeemed prescription of antiarrhythmic drugs (amiodarone: C01BD01, dronedarone: C01BD07, class 1C antiarrhythmic medication: C01BC), or repeat ablation (NCSP: BFFB04). For redeemed prescriptions of antiarrhythmic drugs, a longer blanking period of 6 months was applied. This was done to reduce misclassification of ongoing post‐ablation antiarrhythmic therapy, as such treatment is commonly continued for several months after the procedure and may not reflect true AF recurrence.

Statistics

Baseline characteristics stratified by anemia status (no anemia, transient anemia, and chronic anemia) are presented as counts and percentages for categorical variables, and as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. The association between anemia status and recurrent AF was assessed using Cox proportional hazards models, with the no anemia group as the reference. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI; < 18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9, ≥ 30 kg/m2), AF type (paroxysmal or persistent), left atrial size (normal, moderately enlarged, severely enlarged), left ventricular ejection fraction, time from AF diagnosis to ablation (< 1 year, 1–2 years, 2–3 years, > 3 years), hypertension, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, ischemic stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease stage, oral anticoagulant use, and baseline amiodarone use, autoimmune disease (rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, or inflammatory bowel disease), haematologic conditions (including malignancies and non‐malignant disorders), and non‐haematologic cancer. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Interaction analyses were performed by including multiplicative interaction terms between anemia status and selected clinically relevant subgroups, including age groups, sex, AF type, heart failure, autoimmune diseases, haematologic conditions, and non‐haematologic cancer, renal function (eGFR < 60 vs. ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2), left atrial size, and oral anticoagulant use. Cumulative incidence of recurrent AF was estimated using the Aalen–Johansen method, accounting for death as a competing risk, and illustrated using cumulative incidence curves. All analyses were performed using R version 4.0.3 [25].

Supplement Analysis

In the primary analysis, a 6‐month blanking period was applied to antiarrhythmic drug prescriptions to reduce misclassification of ongoing post‐ablation antiarrhythmic therapy as recurrent AF. To evaluate the impact of this approach, the analysis was repeated using a uniform 90‐day blanking period for all components of the composite outcome, including antiarrhythmic drug prescriptions.

In our main analysis patients with only a single hemoglobin measurement within the 12 months prior to ablation could not be assessed for persistence of anemia and were therefore classified based on the available value. Accordingly, patients with a single hemoglobin value below the sex‐specific threshold were categorized as having transient anemia, whereas those without any values below the threshold were categorized as having no anemia.

Ethics

The study received approval from the Danish Data Protection Agency. In accordance with Danish regulations, ethical approval is not required for register‐based studies. Approval to use the National Danish ablation Databases was granted by The Regional Clinical Quality Development Program.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A flow diagram illustrating the patient selection process is presented in Figure 1. During the recruitment period, 17 007 patients were registered in the Danish National Ablation Database. Of these, 1605 patients (9.4%) were excluded due to unavailable hemoglobin measurement within 12 months prior to ablation. Furthermore, 385 patients were excluded due to major bleeding or blood transfusion within 12 months prior to ablation. This resulted in a final study population of 15 017 (88.2) patients with undergoing first‐time catheter ablation for AF. A comparison of included and excluded patients is presented in Table S2. Compared with included patients, excluded patients were slightly younger (median age 63 vs. 64 years), had lower CHA2DS2‐VA scores (median 0 vs. 2), and were more frequently diagnosed with paroxysmal AF (61.6% vs. 50.5%). Excluded patients also had lower prevalences of cardiovascular comorbidities and lower use of cardiovascular medications, including antiarrhythmic drugs. Baseline characteristics of the included population stratified by anemia status are summarized in Table 1. A total of 877 patients (5.8%) were classified as chronic anemic and 1592 (10.6%) were classified as transient anemic prior to ablation. Compared with non‐anemic patients, those with chronic or transient anemia were older, more frequently female, and more likely to have persistent AF. They also had larger left atrial dimensions and a higher burden of comorbidities, including hypertension, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, diabetes, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, as well as more advanced chronic kidney disease. In addition, patients with transient or chronic anemia were more frequently treated with amiodarone prior to ablation than non‐anemic patients.

Association Between Anemia and AF Recurrence

The median follow‐up duration after the blanking period was 365 days (IQR: 362–365 days), during which 3445 patients (23.6%) experienced AF recurrence. Crude rates of individual AF recurrence components stratified by anemia status are shown in Figure 2. Across all anemia categories, AF‐related hospitalization was the most frequent initial event, accounting for > 40% of recurrences (Table S3). Patients with chronic anemia consistently exhibited the highest incidence across all recurrence components, except for repeat ablation. The cumulative incidence of AF recurrence within 1 year after first‐time catheter ablation (following the blanking period) is presented in Figure 3. Event rates were highest among patients with chronic anemia (32.5%), followed by those with transient anemia (24.2%) and non‐anemic patients (23.0%). No significant interactions were observed between anemia status and sex, age groups, AF type, heart failure, autoimmune disease, haematologic disease, non‐haematologic cancer, left atrial size, renal function, or oral anticoagulant use in adjusted models (Table S4). In multivariable Cox regression analyses, chronic anemia was independently associated with an increased risk of AF recurrence (HR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.18–1.56), whereas transient anemia was not associated with recurrence risk (HR: 0.98; 95% CI: 0.87–1.10), compared with non‐anemic patients (Figure 4).

Supplement Analysis

When a uniform 90‐day blanking period was applied to all components of the composite outcome, including antiarrhythmic drug prescriptions, the results remained consistent with the primary analysis. Chronic anemia remained associated with an increased risk of AF recurrence (HR: 1.26, 95 CI: 1.10–1.45), whereas transient anemia was not associated with recurrence risk (HR: 0.98, 95 CI: 0.87–1.09) (Figure S1).

In a restricted cohort of patients with repeated hemoglobin measurements (≥ 2 measurements separated by ≥ 90 days within 12 months prior to ablation), the association between anemia status and AF recurrence remained consistent with the primary analysis (Figure S2). Chronic anemia was associated with a higher risk of AF recurrence (HR: 1.23; 95% CI: 1.03–1.46), whereas transient anemia was not associated with recurrence (HR: 0.99; 95% CI: 0.86–1.14), compared with patients without anemia.

Using the stricter definition of chronic anemia (≥ 2 hemoglobin values below the sex‐specific threshold separated by ≥ 90 days with no intervening normal values), chronic anemia remained significantly associated with AF recurrence (HR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.14–1.63), whereas transient anemia was not associated with increased risk (HR: 1.07; 95% CI: 0.97–1.18) (Figure S3).

When anemia was classified solely according to the hemoglobin measurement closest to the ablation date, neither mild anemia nor moderate‐to‐severe anemia was significantly associated with AF recurrence compared with no anemia (Figure S4). The adjusted HR was 1.13 (95% CI: 0.98–1.30) for mild anemia and 1.30 (95% CI: 0.96–1.77) for moderate‐to‐severe anemia.

In the propensity score‐matched analysis, 864 patients with chronic anemia were matched to 1719 patients without anemia. Covariate balance after matching was acceptable, with all standardized mean differences below 0.10 (Table S5). Chronic anemia remained associated with an increased risk of AF recurrence compared with no anemia (HR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.08–1.47) (Figure S5).

Discussion

In this nationwide cohort of 15 017 patients undergoing first‐time catheter ablation for AF, chronic anemia—but not transient anemia—was associated with a higher 1‐year risk of AF recurrence compared with patients without anemia. This association remained consistent across multiple supplementary analyses, including analyses using alternative outcome definitions, restricted hemoglobin measurement criteria, stricter definitions of chronic anemia, and propensity score matching.

Current evidence on the relationship between anemia and AF recurrence after first‐time catheter ablation is limited to a single study. This study had several limitations, including it only relying on a single hemoglobin measurement and having a relatively small sample size [11]. In this study, among 2627 included patients, 9.6% had anemia prior to the procedure, and anemia was associated with a higher risk of AF recurrence. In contrast, in our study, when anemia was defined solely based on the hemoglobin value closest to the ablation date, no significant association with AF recurrence was observed. This finding underscores a key limitation of the prior study and highlights the importance of incorporating longitudinal laboratory data. A single hemoglobin measurement may be influenced by temporary factors such as hydration status, acute illness, or recent bleeding, leading to misclassification and attenuation of true associations.

In our study, anemia—particularly chronic anemia—was more frequently observed in patients with multiple comorbidities, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatoid arthritis and heart failure. These conditions are each associated with anemia, often reflecting anemia of chronic disease, and may indicate that anemia serves as a marker of overall disease burden and systemic inflammation. However, even after comprehensive adjustment for these comorbidities, and in a propensity score‐matched analysis comparing patients with chronic anemia with patients without anemia, chronic anemia remained associated with AF recurrence. Furthermore, no significant interactions were observed between anemia status and key clinical variables, suggesting that the association was consistent across patient subgroups. Nevertheless, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and chronic anemia should therefore be interpreted as a prognostic marker of increased recurrence risk rather than evidence of a causal determinant of AF recurrence.

Several pathophysiological mechanisms may explain why chronic, but not transient, anemia is associated with AF recurrence. Long‐term ongoing chronic anemia reduces blood viscosity, leading to lower systemic vascular resistance and blood pressure. This triggers baroreceptor‐mediated neurohormonal activation, increasing sympathetic nervous system activity and stimulating the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system [14]. The resultant rise in cardiac output, driven by increased preload and heart rate, places sustained stress on the myocardium. Over time, this can lead to left ventricular hypertrophy and diastolic dysfunction, which can elevate atrial pressure and cause atrial dilation, which can trigger AF. A similar pathway has been proposed to explain the link between anemia and new‐onset heart failure [14]. Additionally, abnormal autonomic regulation in response to these hemodynamic changes may contribute to atrial tachyarrhythmias [15]. Atrial ischemia due to anemia further promotes structural remodeling through fibrosis, disrupting conduction pathways and facilitating re‐entry mechanisms that sustain AF [16]. Moreover, angiotensin II, a key mediator in the renin‐angiotensin system, has been shown to promote myocardial fibrosis, including in the atria, further increasing AF susceptibility [26].

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that evaluation of anemia prior to catheter ablation should include assessment of its temporal pattern, rather than relying on a single measurement. Chronic anemia may represent a marker of increased risk for AF recurrence and could improve pre‐procedural risk stratification. Our group previously reported that iron deficiency, defined as transferrin saturation < 20% or serum iron ≤ 13 μmol/L, was associated with an increased risk of all‐cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and all‐cause hospitalization among patients with atrial fibrillation [13]. Together, these findings suggest that hematological abnormalities may identify a subgroup of AF patients at particularly high risk of adverse outcomes. However, because information on iron status, vitamin deficiencies, inflammatory markers, and other causes of anemia was unavailable in our study population, the underlying mechanisms leading to increased risk of AF recurrence remain uncertain. Consequently, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that correction of anemia would reduce recurrence risk. Future studies are needed to determine whether specific causes of chronic anemia contribute directly to AF recurrence and whether targeted treatment can improve outcomes after catheter ablation. If such a relationship is confirmed, assessment and management of chronic anemia may become an important component of comprehensive AF care, similar to risk factor modification strategies such as weight reduction before ablation [2]. From both patient and healthcare perspectives, reducing AF recurrence could improve quality of life, decrease the need for repeat procedures, and reduce healthcare costs.

Strength and Limitations

A major strength of this nationwide observational study is the large sample size and use of validated Danish registries, ensuring high data completeness and minimizing selection and detection biases. Importantly, the availability of repeated hemoglobin measurements enabled differentiation between transient and chronic anemia, addressing a key limitation of prior research.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational design precludes causal inference. Although findings were supported by multivariable adjustment and propensity score matching, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Second, approximately 12% of patients were excluded due to missing hemoglobin values. This was primarily because the laboratory database only achieved nationwide coverage in 2015, leaving certain regions unrepresented prior to that time. Although hemoglobin testing is standard before catheter ablation and missing values are therefore likely related to geographic data availability, excluded patients differed somewhat from included patients, being younger and having a lower burden of comorbidities. Consequently, some degree of selection bias cannot be excluded. Third, variability in hemoglobin testing frequency may have introduced detection bias, as patients with more comorbidities may have been more likely to undergo repeated testing and therefore fulfill the definition of chronic anemia. However, results were consistent in a supplementary analysis restricted to patients with repeated hemoglobin measurements. Nevertheless, detection bias related to hemoglobin testing frequency cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, data on periprocedural bleeding complications. Therefore, we could not assess whether ablation‐related bleeding differed by anemia status or influenced recurrence risk.

Fifth, AF recurrence was defined using registry‐based outcomes, which may not capture asymptomatic or non‐hospital‐managed episodes. Sixth, although left ventricular ejection fraction and categorical measures of left atrial size were available and included in the multivariable analyses, more detailed echocardiographic measures such as left atrial volume index and biomarkers including BNP and NT‐proBNP were unavailable. Consequently, residual confounding related to atrial remodeling and heart failure severity cannot be excluded. Lastly, the study did not account for the etiology or duration of anemia, both of which could influence its association with AF recurrence. Different types of anemia may have distinct pathophysiological effects on AF risk, and the duration of anemia may contribute differently to outcomes.

Conclusion

In this nationwide study, chronic—but not transient—anemia was associated with an increased risk of AF recurrence following first‐time catheter ablation. These findings highlight the importance of considering the persistence of anemia in pre‐procedural assessment and suggest that chronic anemia may represent a clinically relevant risk marker. Future studies should determine whether targeted management of chronic anemia prior to ablation can improve rhythm outcomes.
