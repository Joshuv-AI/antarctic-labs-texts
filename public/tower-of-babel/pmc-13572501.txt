Enhanced cellular polyamine pools induced by different nitrogen substrates modify silicification of diatom Paralia sulcata
Authors: Hao Zhou, Meng Li, Patricia M Glibert, Dongyan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiatoms are important primary producers in aquatic environments, owing to their siliceous frustules that support their biological dominance. Alongside other biomolecules, long-chain polyamines (LCPAs) are considered to be key regulators of silica precipitation in the formation of frustules. However, it remains unclear whether essential polyamines (putrescine, spermidine and spermine), which are derived from nitrogen metabolism in the cellular urea cycle and are required for LCPA synthesis, can influence silicification. Based on a series of culture experiments involving the diatom Paralia sulcata (Ehrenberg) Cleve, 1873, we examine the impact of nitrogen metabolism on silicification by introducing nitrate, ammonium, putrescine, spermidine and spermine. Compared with the frustules from cells cultured with inorganic nitrogen, those cultured with three polyamines exhibit a looser structure and a lower Si:C ratio. Higher levels of cellular polyamines, together with increased levels of amino acids related to the urea cycle (e.g., aspartate and arginine), coincide with pronounced frustule remodelling. These patterns are consistent with uptake of supplied polyamine, rather than generalised endogenous biosynthetic activation. While further substantiation of metabolic reorganisation is required, our findings support a role for intracellular polyamine metabolism in modulating silicification. This discovery sheds significant light on the nitrogen–silicon coupling process in aquatic environments.Subject terms: Evolutionary ecology, Ecophysiology

Introduction

Diatoms are widely distributed in aquatic environments, contributing ~20% of global primary production1–3. Their ecological success is highly related to the presence of their siliceous frustules throughout their evolutionary history4. Not only do the siliceous frustules provide better mechanical protection than cellulose- or carbonate-based cell walls, but they also have a light-focusing effect, enabling more efficient light capture in aquatic environments5,6. The micro- and nanostructures of diatom frustules vary from species to species, with tight biological control over the precipitation and patterning of silica2,7. Silicification takes place within silica deposition vesicles, and various compounds have been identified as being associated with this process8,9. In particular, three major classes of biomolecules have been identified: proteins (e.g., silaffins and silacidins), long-chain polyamines (LCPAs), and polysaccharides10–12. LCPAs are widely regarded as key regulators of silica precipitation and frustule morphology, because their species-specific chain lengths and degrees of N-methylation contribute to species-specific nano-patterns13–15. Meanwhile, LCPAs and silica-associated proteins appear to play a role in the chemical regulation of silica polymerisation and nanostructure formation16–18. Additionally, polysaccharides (such as chitin), are increasingly recognised as key components of frustule-associated organic matrices, available evidence suggests that chitin-rich networks primarily facilitate spatial organisation and robust silica-polysaccharide interactions at the interface16,19,20.

In diatoms, short-chain polyamines (putrescine, PUT; spermidine, SPD; spermine, SPM) are not only involved in growth and stress responses21,22, but also represent precursors and building blocks for LCPA biosynthesis9,16,23. Importantly, polyamine metabolism is linked to the cellular urea cycle23,24. In conditions of nitrogen (N) stress, the urea cycle enables the conversion of excess ammonium into amino acids (e.g., aspartate, Asp; arginine, Arg; ornithine, Orn) to reduce cellular toxicity and these amino acids can then fuel polyamine biosynthesis via the ornithine decarboxylation pathway25–27. Therefore, it is expected that the levels of polyamines in cells will be sensitive to the form of N present in the environment (e.g., nitrate (NO3–), ammonium (NH4+), and organic N). The regulation of polyamines offers a plausible mechanistic link between N metabolism and silicification control28.

A major unresolved question is how environmental nutrient regimes—especially N form and availability—couple to the organic matrix machinery that controls silicification. Over the last century, riverine N export has increased markedly, and coastal systems are increasingly exposed to complex N species that include elevated inorganic N and diverse organic N compounds27,29. Such changes challenge phytoplankton N metabolism and can restructure biochemical fluxes within cells27. Building on the physiological and biochemical linkage among urea-cycle amino acids, polyamines, and biogenic silica (BSi) in diatoms, Liu and Glibert28 hypothesised that increased amino acids in the urea cycle driven by high N availability, in particular, NH4+ may lead to elevated cellular polyamine pools and further modify diatom silicification. What they proposed, however, required a confirmative physiological and biochemical experiment. Thus, the aim here is to elucidate the effect of polyamines on diatom silicification to further the understanding of the coupling of N-Si in diatoms. A series of culture experiments were designed to detect the response of diatom silicification (morphology and BSi content) to the variations in polyamines under different N sources. Paralia sulcata was selected as the experimental species as it is a small and heavily silicified diatom that produces blooms in the North Sea and Yellow Sea after N enrichment30–32.

Results

Frustule morphology and elemental composition under different N conditions

After 144 hours of culture exposure to the five pulsed N conditions, frustule morphology exhibited notable differences. SEM revealed that frustules with inorganic N treatments (NO3– and NH4+) retained robust and strongly silicified cylindrical characteristics with smooth girdle bands (GBs) and formed interlocked chains with dense intracellular junctions (ICJs)33 (Fig. 1a, b). In contrast, frustules provided with polyamines (PUT, SPD, SPM) showed rough frustule surfaces with fine particulates and loosely arranged ICJs (highlighted by red arrows in Fig. 1c–e). Moreover, statistical analysis showed that the widths of GBs and ICJs in polyamine treatments were significantly greater than those in inorganic N treatments (n = 45 per treatment, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 1). For cells cultured in inorganic N sources, the average GB width was 6.03 ± 0.44 µm (NO3–) and 6.08 ± 0.47 µm (NH4+), respectively, while the ICJ width was 5.93 ± 0.41 µm (NO3–) and 6.04 ± 0.49 µm (NH4+). By contrast, polyamine-treated cells exhibited wider GBs and ICJs. The average GB width was found to be approximately 7.70 ± 0.33 µm in PUT and 7.99 ± 0.38 µm in SPD. The ICJ width varied from 7.93 ± 0.36 µm in PUT to 8.26 ± 0.41 µm in SPM. The results indicated that the polyamines not only influence the nano-structure of frustule surfaces, but also enlarge the dimensions of GB and ICJ.

Furthermore, the Si% of the frustules derived from SEM–EDS exhibited significant dependence on the N source. Inorganic N treatments tended to exhibit higher Si% than polyamine treatments (Fig. 1f; Supplementary Fig. 2). Frustules treated with NO3– showed the highest mean Si% at 41.5 ± 4.1% (n = 48), which was 7.6% higher than cells exposed to SPM (38.6 ± 2.5%, n = 48, P < 0.001) and 5.5% higher than those exposed to SPD (39.4 ± 2.5%, n = 52, P = 0.012). NH4+-treated cells had a moderate mean Si% of 40.0 ± 2.7% (n = 55), which was significantly higher than that of cells exposed to SPM (P = 0.006), but not different from that of cells exposed to PUT (39.8 ± 2.9%, n = 52, P = 0.921). All polyamine treatments clustered tightly within the range of 38.6–39.8%, with significant differences only observed between PUT and SPM (P = 0.009). By contrast, the C% showed an inverse trend: enhanced C accumulation occurred in polyamine-treated cells (see Supplementary Fig. 2b). The mean C% was 4.7 ± 2.0% (n = 48) and 5.1 ± 1.5% (n = 55) of the frustules treated with NO3– and NH4+, respectively. These values were approximately 14.8% and 7.3% lower than those of frustules exposed to SPM (5.5 ± 1.8%, n = 48, P = 0.007) and SPD (5.5 ± 1.9%, n = 52, P = 0.442), respectively. Consequently, inorganic N sources drove a Si-dominated stoichiometry (Fig. 1f; Supplementary Table 2). The Si:C ratio of frustules treated with NO3– (10.34 ± 4.17, n = 48) was approximately 35.8% higher than that of frustules exposed to SPM (7.61 ± 2.06, n = 48, P = 0.001) and 24.4% higher than that of frustules pulsed with PUT (8.31 ± 2.50, n = 52, P = 0.035).

Responses of BSi, polyamines and amino acids to N conditions

The density of cells supplied with inorganic N exhibited a consistent, modest upward trend, which was generally higher than that observed in polyamine-treated cultures (Fig. 2a). The BSi content in the inorganic N treated diatoms was lower than that in polyamine-treated diatoms throughout the experiment (Fig. 2b). During the initial 48 hours of culture, an increase in BSi content was observed in the polyamine-treated diatoms, with a clear hierarchical order of SPM > SPD > PUT. For instance, a distinct BSi content peak (3.78 ± 0.07 ng cell–1) emerged at 48 hours in SPM-treated diatoms.

The intracellular PUT, SPD, and SPM concentrations exhibited pronounced N-source and time-dependent dynamics (Fig. 2c). In polyamine cultures, PUT peaked at 202.10 ± 3.91 fg cell–1 (48 h, PUT-treated), SPD at 2591.33 ± 224.97 fg cell–1 (48 h, SPD-treated), and SPM stabilised above 5000 fg cell–1 (SPM-treated). In contrast, inorganic N additions sustained PUT < 120 fg cell–1, SPD < 1700 fg cell–1, and SPM < 120 fg cell–1. The intracellular PUT and SPD peaked synchronously at 48 h under their own N sources, while SPM remained elevated through 144 hours, indicating divergent accumulation kinetics. Notably, PUT addition increased intracellular PUT together with downstream SPD and SPM, whereas SPD and SPM additions mainly elevated their corresponding added polyamine without a comparable increase in upstream polyamines. This asymmetric accumulation pattern is consistent with treatment-dependent uptake and downstream conversion rather than a uniform activation of endogenous polyamine biosynthesis. Further analysis using the PolyamineRed dye revealed different patterns of polyamine accumulation in cells under different N treatments (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 3). The NO3–-treated cells (n = 18) were approximately 41.0% lower than those under PUT treatment (n = 22, P = 0.036), 48.7% lower than those under SPD treatment (n = 34, P = 0.001), and 59.4% lower than those under SPM treatment (n = 38, P < 0.001). There was no significant difference between the NH4+-treated cells (n = 31) and those treated with NO3– (P = 0.229), but NH4+-treated cells were still lower than those treated with PUT (17.7%, P = 0.264), SPD (28.4%, P = 0.015), and SPM (43.3%, P < 0.001).

Intracellular Asp, Arg, and Orn concentrations also exhibited distinct N-source and time-dependent dynamics (Fig. 2d). Among the inorganic N treatments, those treated with NH4+ consistently supported higher amino acid concentrations than those treated with NO3–. When comparing polyamine and inorganic N treatments, Asp levels in the polyamine-treated cells remained uniformly elevated above those in the inorganic N-treated cells throughout the incubation period. For Arg, the polyamine-treated cells showed a pronounced early accumulation (peaking at 48 h), with concentrations that were significantly higher than those in the inorganic N treatments. However, all N-treated cells had similar Arg levels by 72 hours. In contrast, Orn were higher in the inorganic N treatments, particularly in the NH4+-treated cells, compared to those treated with polyamines.

Finally, principle component analysis (PCA) was performed to characterise the overall metabolic differences among the five N treatments and to identify their potential association with BSi. The first two principal components, Dim1 and Dim2, explained 53.7% and 16.2% of the total variance, respectively, together accounting for 69.9% of the total variance. The multivariate separation among the NO3–, NH4+, and polyamines treatments is clear to see in Fig. 3a. This separation was primarily associated with intracellular polyamine levels and BSi-related metrics (Fig. 3b), which suggests that there is a coordinated network between N metabolism and BSi synthesis.

Discussion

Diatoms exhibit remarkable versatility in N utilisation, thriving on diverse inorganic (NO3–, NH4+) and organic (urea, polyamines) sources27,34,35. A key discovery in their physiology is the presence of a complete urea cycle, typically associated with heterotrophs for N waste excretion, suggesting a specialised role in linking N assimilation to pathways like polyamine biosynthesis2,24. Polyamines are byproducts of this cycle, serving as major sinks for assimilated N26. Their nitrogenous groups, attached to C backbones, can be efficiently recycled, allowing diatoms to buffer excess N, maintain N: C balance, and mitigate NH4+ toxicity27,36,37. Thus, polyamine dynamics in diatoms are sensitive to N status. The work herein confirms that N source can drive diatoms into different metabolic regimes. While uptake of inorganic N may produce distinct polyamine patterns, direct uptake of polyamines leads to alternative metabolite balances. Accordingly, the treatment-dependent increases in intracellular polyamines should be interpreted as changes in intracellular polyamine pools under each supplied substrate, rather than as direct evidence for a generalised upregulation of endogenous polyamine biosynthesis.

Here, based on the pathway analysis of the impact of nitrogen sources on the urea cycle–polyamine pathway in diatoms, as modelled by Allen et al.23,24. (Fig. 4) We employed a bayesian structural equation model (BSEM) framework to interpret the metabolic pathway of the urea cycle–polyamine–BSi cascade, as suggested by our results. Within this framework (Fig. 5), the path coefficients under different N conditions can be considered to represent standardised conditional direct effects within the specified network rather than simple pairwise correlations.

Under inorganic N conditions (NO3–, NH4+) (Fig. 5a, b), the decarboxylation of Orn produces PUT, which accumulates and exhibits a positive path coefficient with respect to BSi—consistent with its role in initiating Si polymerisation (e.g., the precipitation of silica nanospheres in vitro). The downstream product, SPD (formed by adding an aminopropyl group to PUT), exhibited a negative path coefficient with respect to BSi. This indicates a metabolic allocation trade-off: SPD is synthesised downstream of PUT and is required for fundamental cellular processes, such as nucleic acid stabilisation and translation34. Therefore, when inorganic N supports active growth, elevated SPD likely indicates an increased diversion of PUT/precursor flux (and cellular resources) into growth-related functions. This reduces the availability of PUT-derived polyamines for silicification. In this regime, SPD primarily behaves as an endogenous allocation marker for organic growth and maintenance rather than as the dominant driver of Si polymerisation, yielding a negative SPD → BSi coefficient while PUT remains the positive driver. Even with NH4+ availability38, PUT remains the main positive polyamine term associated with BSi (Fig. 5). However, the models suggest a modest strengthening of upstream N assimilation and polyamine production, which is sufficient to weakly promote silicification (Fig. 5b).

Previous studies have shown that diatoms can utilise and incorporate soluble organic nitrogen, in the form of small molecules such as urea and polyamines, directly into their polyamine pool35,39. In this study, the constraint of PUT metabolism under polyamine-enriched treatments (PUT, SPD, and SPM) was removed, allowing sufficient PUT to be produced and even utilised to generate excess SPD (Fig. 5c–e; Supplementary Fig. 4a, c). The trade-off between endogenous polyamine demand for growth and polyamine availability for silicification is alleviated, with SPD being positively associated with the formation of BSi (Fig. 5c–e). This indicates that, when polyamines are readily available, SPD becomes more directly involved in Si polymerisation. This also helps to explain the increased organic components associated with C% in the frustule under polyamine modulation, which leads to a decrease in the Si:C ratio. It is important to note that, since Si% measured by SEM–EDS is a relative index in the proportion of elemental components, and therefore, under polyamine-rich treatments, a lower Si% does not contradict an overall increase in BSi content.

In this study, a looser architecture and frequent organic particulates appeared on the frustule surface under polyamine-rich treatments (Fig. 1). Furthermore, polyamine-treated frustules exhibited significantly wider GBs and ICJs than those treated with inorganic N (n = 45 per treatment, P < 0.001). Previous studies have shown that increasing polyamine chain length can produce larger and less condensed silica particles40. The positive SPD-BSi association under polyamine conditions indicates the effect of polyamine chain length on silica formation. More specifically, SPD (C7H19N3) exhibits a longer chain length than PUT (C4H12N2). Therefore, increased SPD availability can enhance the incorporation of polyamine-derived organic material into the silica matrix, producing a silica-organic composite with a comparatively higher organic content. The lower Si% at the frustule level under polyamine conditions further strengthened this point. The wider GBs and ICJs expected under polyamine treatments to increase frustule silica demand explain the higher bulk BSi per cell, even though the elemental composition of the frustule shifts towards a higher organic fraction (lower Si% but higher total BSi per cell). Taken together, these results support a context-dependent model in which inorganic N favours a PUT-driven silicification mode characterised by compact, morphologically conserved frustules, whereas polyamine-based N sources promote an SPD-involved silicification mode characterised by looser silica architecture, greater organic incorporation, and expanded frustule dimensions.

More broadly, the sign reversal of SPD → BSi between inorganic N and polyamine regimes can be understood as a switch in the dominant silicification mode: a PUT-driven mode under inorganic N, where SPD primarily reports allocation to growth-related demands, versus a polyamine-rich (SPD-involved) mode under polyamine supply, where SPD contributes more directly to silica formation and the silica–organic composite. Collectively, the BSEM supports a context-dependent switch from a PUT-dominated silicification mode under inorganic N (positive PUT → BSi; negative SPD → BSi) to an SPD-involved mode under polyamine-rich conditions (positive SPD → BSi) (Fig. 5). The term PUT-dominated and SPD-involved here refers to the dominant positive conditional direct association within the modelled network, rather than implying that a single metabolite exclusively determines silicification. This treatment-specific reconfiguration underscores the flexibility of the urea cycle–polyamine–BSi cascade, suggesting that diatoms can shift the polyamine most tightly coupled to net silica deposition depending on N form and polyamine availability.

Such context dependence is biologically plausible, given that diatoms can strategically reallocate N and C to balance growth, stress tolerance, and biomineralisation. Polyamine-rich pathways are also known to support heavily silicified structures in certain diatoms41. When N is plentiful and is supplied in the form of inorganic nutrients, diatoms appear to convert it into basic polyamines for future use, either as a means of storing N in the cell or due to the limited availability of more complex polyamines. However, when N is supplied in a form that readily yields polyamines, or when the polyamines themselves are supplied, diatoms increase their use of these polyamines in building their siliceous frustules. This interpretation is supported by observations from other studies of extreme cases: under N starvation, diatoms, such as Chaetoceros socialis massively upregulate SPD synthase genes during the formation of thick-walled resting spores41. Despite the absence of external N, the cells divert internal metabolism to produce SPD and long-chain polyamines for the heavily silicified walls of the spores41. This demonstrates that diatoms will prioritise polyamine production over other N needs when building critical frustule structures, further highlighting the tight coupling of N metabolism with Si biomineralisation pathways.

Liu and Glibert28 presented field evidence demonstrating an association between N enrichment and the dominance of heavily silicified diatom communities. They proposed that NH4+-driven N metabolism may play a role in this pattern. However, this study did not address the cellular or biochemical mechanisms of silicification. The present study examines the cellular-level response mechanisms of polyamine-associated silicification, thereby expanding the pathways through which N sources influence diatom physiology. By elucidating the continuum between N sources, polyamines, and BSi synthesis, it bridges the cognitive gap between nutrient metabolism and biomineralisation. The result has broader implications for our understanding of diatom ecology and biogeochemistry. Changes in N supply, such as transitions from NO3–-rich to urea-rich environments in marine systems, or the release of polyamines during nutrient cycling, may directly impact diatom Si production and consequently affect the Si cycle. While this study focuses on the heavily silicified centric diatom P. sulcata under silicate-sufficient conditions (constant Si supply, f/2 + Si), extending analyses to lightly silicified pennate taxa and to Si-limited environments would help to establish the universality of the framework. Furthermore, direct measurement of metabolic fluxes and gene/protein regulatory mechanisms is a key area for future research.

In summary, the integration of time-resolved metabolite measurements, frustule morphology and elemental composition, and treatment-specific BSEM provided a mechanistic framework that links N source to polyamines routing and Si biomineralisation. The form and availability of external N, including exogenous polyamines, are associated with a reconfiguration of the linkages among amino acids related to the urea cycle, cellular polyamine pools (via uptake and/or intracellular interconversion), and Si biomineralisation. This reconfiguration tunes the accumulation of BSi and the composition of the frustule. This insight adds a new dimension to our understanding of diatom nutrient utilisation and Si biomineralisation, connecting previously disparate areas of research into a coherent narrative.

Methods

Experimental overview and culture conditions

The experiment involved four phases. In the initial phase (Supplementary Fig. 5a), P. sulcata, originally isolated from the Yellow Sea, China, was cultured in f/2 + Si medium42 (full composition in Supplementary Table 4) in an incubator (MGC-400B; Yiheng, Shanghai, China) for two weeks to reach the stationary phase. The initial cell concentration for inoculation was about 2 × 103 cells mL–1. The incubator was set to 10 °C, because the optimum temperature for this species is from 6 °C to 10 °C43. The irradiance was provided at 55 µmol m–2 s–2 irradiation, under a 12 h/12 h light/dark photoperiod. After the stationary phase was reached, the cells were collected by centrifugation, washed three times with artificial seawater, and then inoculated into N-free f/2 + Si medium for a starvation period of 3 additional days.

After the starvation period (3 days) in N-free base medium (see Supplementary Table 4), the culture was divided into five treatment groups with three biologically independent culture replicates per treatment, using two forms of inorganic N (NO3– and NH4+) and three polyamines (PUT, SPD and SPM). The pulsed experiment was initiated by adding 1 µM N sources (NO3–, NH4+, PUT, SPD, or SPM), respectively (Phase 2; Supplementary Fig. 5b). The purpose of using a 1 µM pulse was to assess short-term metabolic and silicification responses to a small, standardised N input from a common N-depleted physiological state, rather than simulating steady-state growth under high, continuous N supply.

Subsamples of these treatments were collected at 4-hourly intervals for the first 12 hours, and then at 24-hourly intervals from 24 to 144 h, in order to observe the morphology of the frustules, as well as the physiological and biochemical responses of the urea cycle (Phase 3; Supplementary Fig. 5c). These parameters were analysed statistically in order to develop and compare metabolic pathways (Phase 4; Supplementary Fig. 5d).

All procedures were conducted under aseptic conditions. Cultures were rendered axenic using an antibiotic cocktail following Luo et al.44 (final concentrations: ampicillin 200 µg L–1, kanamycin 100 µg L–1, streptomycin 100 µg L–1). During the experiments, all inoculations and reagent additions were conducted in a laminar-flow clean bench. Artificial seawater and all non-vitamin stock solutions were sterilised by autoclaving (121 °C for 30 min), whereas vitamin stocks were sterilised by 0.22 µm filtration; sterile stocks were stored at 4 °C prior to use.

Stock solutions of PUT, SPD, and SPM were prepared immediately after opening the reagent packages. The stock solutions were divided into sterile 1 mL sealed ampoules to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles, and were stored at −20 °C. The aliquots were thawed in the dark immediately before use and added aseptically to the cultures to achieve the final pulsed concentration of 1 µM.

Analysis of cell density and frustule morphology

The cell densities of the five treatments were measured using a FlowCAM 8000 imaging system (Yokogawa Fluid Imaging Technologies, Scarborough, Maine, USA), which was operated in Auto-Image mode. The VisualSpreadsheet software was used to calculate the particle concentration by normalising the particle count to the imaged sample volume recorded for each run. To minimise the impact of chain formation on the results, P. sulcata samples were pre-treated with dilute hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to break up chains prior to FlowCAM imaging. This is because H2O2-based oxidation is a common method of preparing diatom samples for analysis, as it oxidises and removes organic material45. As frustules can appear as either valve-view or girdle-view depending on their orientation, the captured images were post-processed in VisualSpreadsheet and manually classified as valve-view or girdle-view frustules, or as non-cell particles/fragments. Total cell density was calculated as the sum of the concentrations of the two intact-frustule classes.

For SEM-based morphometric analyses, separate untreated aliquots were used to preserve intact chains. Frustule morphology was observed under scanning electron microscopy (SEM; GeminiSEM 450, Zeiss, Germany). The widths of the GB and the ICJ were quantified using ImageJ 1.54 g software. Measurements were performed on high-resolution SEM micrographs of randomly selected intact cells (n = 45 per treatment) to ensure statistical robustness.

Semi-quantification analysis of single-frustule silicon and carbon

To maintain the chain structure of the cells and remove any residual salts or medium components, the samples were washed six times with Milli-Q water without centrifugation before elemental analysis. The silicon (Si) and carbon (C) mass fractions in the frustule were quantified using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS; XFlash 6-60, Bruker, USA) coupled with SEM. A total of 48–55 intact cells/frustules per group within chains were analysed at 144 hours (lag phase), with a focus on the ICJ regions of the frustules where new silica deposition occurs during cell wall formation. As the elements on valves are largely fixed once exocytosed, we did not attempt to assign the entire frustule observed by SEM to de novo synthesis after the N pulse. Instead, SEM–EDS measurements were deliberately restricted to the ICJ regions, representing actively accreting cell-wall domains during post-starvation recovery and chain/junction maintenance. Consequently, the SEM–EDS readouts (Si% and C%) are interpreted as semi-quantitative, treatment-comparative indicators of the elemental composition within these actively forming domains rather than definitive markers of the entire frustule formation time.

Bulk biogenic silica quantification

The bulk BSi was extracted using the hot alkaline method, as described by Liu et al.46. Five mL of the culture were collected at each sampling time during the experiment and filtered through a 0.45 µm cellulose acetate filter (Sartorius Stedim Biotech, Göttingen, Germany) to obtain the particulate matters. The particulate matters were digested with 4 mL of 0.2 M NaOH at 98 °C for 40 min. After cooling, 1.0 mL of 1 M HCl was added to neutralise the solution. Si concentrations were measured colourimetrically using the method described by Grasshoff et al.47, which is the standard method for measuring silicic acid in seawater.

Staining and semi-quantification analysis of single-cell polyamines

The red fluorescent dye TAMRA (tetramethylrhodamine)-conjugated glycine propargyl ester derivative (PolyamineRED) can penetrate cells and react specifically with intracellular polyamines, enabling the fluorescent labelling of polyamines48. Thus, it can be used to assess intracellular polyamines at the single-cell level. This fluorescence-based assay was performed on samples collected after 48 hours, as preliminary observations indicated that intracellular polyamine accumulation was highest at this time, making it ideal for validating polyamine dynamics at the single-cell level. Notably, this dye-based approach cannot distinguish between individual polyamine species (PUT, SPD and SPM). It was therefore used for single-cell level visualisation and relative comparison rather than compound-specific quantification.

The cells were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to remove the supernatant. They were then resuspended and washed three times with 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before being incubated for 90 min in a 10 µM PolyamineRED solution. After incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 2 min, resuspended in 1 mL PBS and washed four times for fluorescent observation. The fluorescent distribution in the cells was examined using a confocal microscope (LSM, Zeiss, Germany) at 400× magnification. The excitation and emission wavelengths were set to 488 nm and 500–540 nm, respectively. Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ZEN-lite software to assess intracellular polyamine levels.

Bulk polyamines and amino acids quantification by HPLC

Bulk intracellular polyamines and amino acids were quantified using a 1200 series high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA). Measurement methods for PUT, SPD, SPM, Asp, Arg and Orn were based on previous studies34,49. At each sampling time, 40 mL of each culture was centrifuged at 4 °C and 4000 rpm for 10 min to remove the supernatant and obtain a cell pellet. This was then extracted using 2 mL of ice-cold perchloric acid, with sonication for 10 min in an ice bath. The sample was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 1 min, after which the resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm polytetrafluoroethylene filter and collected in a 2.5 mL vial. The pH was then adjusted to 8 using HCl or NaOH.

The samples were derivatised before HPLC analysis following the method of Lu et al.49. The derivatisation procedure involved two sequential reactions with o-phthalaldehyde (OPA), ethanethiol (ET) and 9-fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl chloride (FMOC-Cl). For the first reaction, the OPA-ET working reagent was newly prepared by combining 500 µL of OPA stock solution (0.22 g of OPA in 10 mL of methanol), 2 mL of 0.8 M borate buffer solution (pH 11.0), 52 µL of ET and 7.448 mL of methanol. The mixture was then kept at 4 °C in darkness for 90 min before analysis. Each 1 mL sample aliquot was then mixed with 15 µL of the OPA-ET reagent and reacted for 1 min at 20 °C–25 °C. The second reaction was initiated by adding 1 µL of the FMOC-Cl solution, which was prepared by dissolving 0.11 g of FMOC-Cl in 10 mL of acetonitrile and storing overnight at –20 °C. After a further 1-minute incubation at room temperature, 100 µL of the derivatised sample was immediately injected into the HPLC system. Separation was performed on a ZORBAX Extend C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm) at 50 °C and a flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. Samples were separated using a gradient elution programme, as provided in Supplementary Table 5. Mobile phase A was acetonitrile. Mobile phase B was methanol/0.36 M sodium acetate/Milli-Q water at a volume ratio of 55:8:37, with the pH adjusted to 7.0 using glacial acetic acid. Mobile phase C was acetonitrile/0.36 M sodium acetate/Milli-Q water at a volume ratio of 55:5:40, with the pH adjusted to 8.0 using glacial acetic acid. Mobile phase D was acetonitrile/0.36 M sodium acetate/Milli-Q water at a volume ratio of 10:5:85, with the pH adjusted to 7.0 using glacial acetic acid. The detector excitation and emission wavelengths were set to 330 nm and 460 nm, respectively.

Statistics and reproducibility

Triplicate treatments were conducted in all experiments. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for small samples (n < 50) and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for larger samples. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. As most variables failed the normality or homogeneity of variance tests, non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis H tests were used to compare differences in Si%, C%, Si:C ratios, GB width, ICJ width, and polyamine fluorescence intensity across culture conditions. Dunn’s post hoc tests were performed for pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with P < 0.01 and P < 0.001 denoting higher levels of significance. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 23 and R 4.4.2.

To characterise the overall metabolic differences among the five N treatments and identify variables (Asp, Arg, Orn, PUT, SPD, and SPM) potentially associated with BSi, PCA was performed. Data were standardised using the scale() function before PCA. PCA was conducted using the PCA() function from the FactoMineR package in R 4.4.2. Visualisations were generated via the factoextra package. To statistically test for differences among N treatments, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed on the first two principal component coordinates using the adonis() function from the vegan package, with 999 permutations to assess significance.

BSEM via the blavaan package50 was employed in R 4.4.2 to evaluate a predefined directional path model linking amino acids, polyamines, and BSi in cells under the five N sources. Data were standardised using the scale() function before defining path relationships (e.g., Arg ~ Asp). Weak-informative priors were specified as normal distributions for regression coefficients (N(0, 10)) and Cauchy distributions for residuals (location = 0, scale = 2.5). Models were fitted separately for each N source using 4 Markov chains (2000 burn-in iterations, 4000 sampling iterations), with convergence assessed via Rhat values. Path coefficient differences between the N sources were deemed significant when their 95% credible intervals did not include zero.

Forest plots were generated using the ggplot2 package to visually summarise the differences in path coefficients between all pairwise comparisons of N sources. Each plot panel corresponds to a specific path in the model (e.g., Orn ~ Arg, BSi ~ PUT) and displays the mean difference in path coefficients (with the 95% credible intervals) between each pair of N sources. Points represent mean differences, and vertical lines denote 95% credible intervals. Significant differences (where the 95% credible intervals did not overlap zero) were highlighted using colour coding: red for positive significant differences, blue for negative significant differences, and grey for non-significant differences.
