Management discussion of laws and regulations on human genetic resources in China: a scoping review
Authors: Qiuyu Sun, Yibo Liu, Jian Huang, Yaodong Zhang, Kaijuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe governance of human genetic resources (HGRs) in China has evolved quickly, driven by national security concerns and the rapid expansion of the biotechnology sector. This review examines the resulting legal and policy framework, with a focus on cross-border data transfers and the management of informed consent.MethodsEmploying a comprehensive search strategy integrated with bibliometric analysis, this study synthesized theoretical and methodological developments in Chinese HGRs management. Literature searches spanned publications across PubMed, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang database, official government websites, and Google.ResultsChina’s HGRs management has evolved from principle-based legislation to specific administrative regulations. Informed consent has become stricter, with HGRs data classified as sensitive personal information requiring separate consent and the right to withdraw. This orientation enables strict oversight but also defines the limits within which informed consent can function. The number of international cooperation projects declined after 2023 due to refined regulatory classifications and multi-departmental collaboration. HGRs cross-border require a security review and filing by the National Health Commission, with sensitive data subject to additional assessment by the Cyberspace Administration. In contrast, general cross-border flows are primarily managed through security assessments or standard contract filings, with the negative list mechanism and risk-level exemptions being piloted in free trade zones.ConclusionNational security and data sovereignty remain central to China’s HGR governance, shaping strict oversight while limiting the practical reach of informed consent. Cross-border data sharing continues to face challenges, including regulatory discrepancies, privacy risks that extend beyond de-identification, and technical inconsistencies in data formats and annotation standards. Future progress requires enhanced transparency regarding data usage, improved technical interoperability aligned with domestic security requirements, and stronger international coordination through bilateral agreements.

Background

According to the Regulations on the Management of Human Genetic Resources (the Regulations) issued in 2019, human genetic resources (HGRs) refer to the HGR-related materials (such as organs, tissues, and cells that contain human genomes and genes), and HGR-related information (the information and data generated from HGR-related materials) [1]. The Implementation Rule of the Regulations on the Management of HGRs (the Implementation Rules), further stipulate that HGRs information management covers materials such as human genes and genome data, while excluding clinical data, imaging data, protein data, and metabolic data [2]. The Biosafety Law clearly defines HGRs as nationally important biosafety resources, prohibiting illegal collection and storage by foreign organizations or individuals [3].

The international management framework for HGRs has evolved through a series of multilateral agreements (Table 1). The foundational principle of fair and equitable benefit-sharing was first enshrined in the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) [4]. Building on this, the Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit-Sharing established binding obligations for member states [5, 6]. With advancements in gene technology, the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) issued the Guidelines for Human Biobanks and Genetic Research Databases, which provided technical recommendations on ethical sample collection, data anonymization, and quality control. Revisions to these guidelines in 2021 introduced risk-based management models for cross-border data sharing. Furthermore, the OECD updated its Guidelines Governing the Protection of Privacy and Transborder Flows of Personal Data in 2022 [7], addressing privacy risks associated with large-scale genomic data exchanges. In parallel, regional frameworks such as the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [8], and the Global Alliance for Genomics and Health (GA4GH) [9] further harmonized standards for HGRs data security, interoperability, and intellectual property management. Together, these instruments aim to balance scientific collaboration with sovereignty protection, ethical compliance, and the fair distribution of innovation benefits.

International laws and regulations related to HGRs

China’s diverse ethnic composition and large population provide unique advantages in HGRs diversity, which also facilitates researchers to conduct profound research on human evolution, genetic diversity, and pathogenic genes [10]. With the facilitation of globalization and the prevalence of big data, China is increasingly involved in sharing and collaborating with other nations, which also brings significant risks in HGRs management and benefit sharing. Based on China’s current HGRs management framework, exploring the current form of HGRs legal and policy framework, cross-border data framework, and other institutional construction is crucial for promoting system improvement, promoting fair and efficient international cooperation [11], and providing reference and suggestions to fill the blind spots of “full life cycle” supervision [12].

Therefore, the article aims to employ bibliometric research and scoping review, summarize the legal frameworks governing HGRs in China, and systematically evaluate the current status, developmental challenges, and management issues related to HGRs in China. This research proposes enhanced strategies and measures to optimize the efficiency and convenience of HGRs management and utilization.

Methods

Study design

The scoping review method was proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [13], and further recommendations were provided by Levac, et al. [14]. As the review aimed to compare the legal frameworks governing HGRs in China contexts and systematically evaluated the status quo of China’s HGRs management, an efficient way of doing this was to focus on both primary literature and review papers.

To contextualize the findings within the broader academic landscape, a supplementary bibliometric analysis was conducted to map publication trends and research clusters.

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across four databases: ​PubMed, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang. The study conducted a search for the following search strings: (human genetic resources OR genetic resources OR human genetic data OR genomic data OR genetic resource materials OR genetic resource information) AND (guideline OR guidance OR policy OR law OR regulation OR framework OR legislation OR ethics OR compliance OR standard OR recommendation) AND (Chinese OR China OR foreign).

Grey literature was searched using Google Scholar, reports from research institutions, official government websites, and other non-peer-reviewed sources [15]. Backward citation chaining was also undertaken which involved hand-searching the reference lists of the reviews identified to find relevant research [16]. Electronic search results were exported into an Endnote and duplicates were deleted.

Eligibility criteria and selection

Documents were eligible for inclusion in reviews of HGRs management in any setting or context, including guidance, policy, or law focused on the management of HGRs in China. No date restrictions were applied to the search, but the search was limited to article published in Chinese or English because of limited resources for translation. Documents not specifically related to HGRs management were also excluded.

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the selection of relevant reviews was undertaken in two stages: (1) Initial screening of the title and abstract which was conducted by the first author (QYS); (2) Retrieval and screening of the full text which was completed independently by two researchers (QYS and YBL), with discrepancies resolved through discussion with all three authors.

Data abstract and analysis

For the data abstraction, coding, and analysis phases, a systematic approach was employed to ensure a thorough examination and synthesis of the identified documents. The process began with the extraction of key information from each eligible article. The extracted data included the author information (title, author, and year of publication), aims and objectives of the articles, country of origin, the name of the policy or law, and a detailed description of each.

Coding involved categorizing the article based on countries and topics. Further sub-coding was conducted to highlight specific themes such as HGRs collection, preservation, utilization, and exit/entry, biospecimen standards, data privacy protection, ethical considerations, consent mechanisms, and disposal of quasi-genetic resources.

In order to analyze the current status of HGRs collection, preservation, and foreign cooperation in China under current policies, we downloaded relevant data from government websites, extracted data on HGRs projects approved by the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST) and National Health Commission (NHC) (from Sep 2019 to Aug 2025), and visualized them in Microsoft Excel 2021 to quantify regulatory trends.

This process led to the exclusion of 368 additional documents, resulting in 145 documents were selected for data extraction and analysis. Legal and policy framework for HGRs management in China

Bibliometric analysis of HGRs management in China shows that a substantial portion of research (33.3%) focuses on law and policy, reflecting a conceptualization of HGRs as collective resources under national sovereignty (Fig. 2A). Technology-driven topics, including gene editing, biosafety, clinical trials and biotechnology, account for 36.2% of publications and form the largest category, highlighting the close interrelationship between ethical deliberation and technological risk management. Together, these trends suggest that while China’s HGR research prioritizes sovereign governance, it is simultaneously guided by technological innovation and associated risk considerations.

China’s legal framework for HGRs management exhibits a clear evolutionary pattern, reflecting a trajectory from principle-based legislation to increasingly detailed, operational regulation, while consistently operating under the overarching national security and sovereignty principle. As shown in Table 2, the Interim Measures for the Management of HGRs (1998) [17] framed HGRs as resources subject to national sovereignty, establishing foundational norms. From 2011 to 2015 [18, 19], oversight of international collaborative projects oversight of international collaborative projects was strengthened, with centralized approval authority emphasizing precautionary management of cross-border activities. The Regulations issued in 2019 [1] marked a systemic upgrade, formalizing the lifecycle management of HGRs through administrative regulations and introducing a security review mechanism. Subsequent Biosecurity Law (2021) [3] and the Criminal Law Amendment (Eleventh) (2021) [20] elevated HGRs management from administrative compliance to a matter of national security and public interest, with criminal liability extending to illegal collection, smuggling, or implantation of gene-edited or cloned materials. This progression reflects a normative shift, framing ethical breaches not merely as procedural violations but as actions threatening collective societal and scientific welfare [21]. The Implementation Rules in 2023 [22] clarify the responsibilities of foreign-controlled entities, specify criteria for cross-border data review thresholds, and define the roles of technical oversight bodies. The Implementation Rules in 2024 further transferred the specific management responsibilities of MOST to the newly established NHC [23]. These changes mark a shift from principle-based legislation to more detailed, operational regulations.

Management of the HGRs cross-border framework in China

China’s laws and regulations governing the cross-border management of HGRs have undergone a process of gradual improvement and refinement. Since the implementation of the Regulations in 2019, the number of approved international collaborative projects has increased, reaching a peak of 6,620 in 2022. However, after 2022, the number of approved international collaborative projects dropped sharply to 1,711 in 2024 (Fig. 3A), and the number of approved exit projects also plummeted from 179 in 2020 to 10 in 2024 (Fig. 3B).

Between 2020 and 2022, the Regulations clarified the approval process and requirements for international collaborative projects. This clarification improved the transparency and predictability of cross-border data management, significantly reducing the institutional transaction costs of such projects. The surge in international collaborative clinical trials during the COVID-19 pandemic contributed to a steady increase in the number of international collaborative projects from September 2019 to 2022 [24]. This trend reflected China’s policy of promoting international scientific research cooperation while ensuring biosafety. However, the release of the Implementation Rules in 2023 refined the statistical scope by separating “international collaborative scientific research” from “international collaborative clinical trials”. This adjustment led to a decline in the overall number of international collaborative projects [25].

The 2024 Regulations on Promoting and Regulating Cross-Border Data [26] further introduced a negative-list mechanism coupled with risk-tiered exemptions, combined with risk-based exemptions to operate within the tripartite legal framework of the Data Security Law [27], Cybersecurity Law [28], and the Personal Information Protection Law [29]. As sensitive personal information/important data, HGRs information continues to be managed at the national level through a multi-tier security assessment and standard contract filing system, and has not yet been included in the free trade zone’s pilot negative-list scope. Additionally, genetic data may simultaneously qualify as sensitive personal information under the Personal Information Protection Law, thus requiring compliance with the outbound security assessment requirements of both regulations [29, 30]. High-risk data in the list must strictly follow the approval procedures, such as security assessment when crossing the border, while non-core data outside the list can be crossed the border according to a simplified process [31–33]. These regulatory adjustments mark the transformation of China’s cross-border HGRs data management model, from a single approval process to a multi-channel, categorized supervision system.

Management of the HGRs Informed consent in China

China has a rapidly growing collection of biobanks that are central to advancing public health and biomedical research [34]. Between 2020 and 2022, the number of authorized biobank-related preservation projects surged from 16 to 92 (Fig. 3B), reflecting the accelerated implementation of China’s national precision medicine strategy. However, bibliometric analysis revealed that core ethical themes accounted for 30.5% of all identified research topics, among which informed consent represented 8.3% of the total distribution (Fig. 2A). Informed consent is closely associated with key priorities, including regulatory compliance, genetic data protection, and cross-institutional scientific collaboration, and highlights that informed consent is an integral part of the broader regulatory landscape, not an isolated ethical concern [15, 35].

Since 2019, consent management in Chinese biobanking has undergone notable regulatory consolidation. The Personal Information Protection Law classifies genetic data as sensitive personal information, thereby requiring explicit and separate consent as a general rule for its processing. The Implementation Rules further mandate written informed consent for the collection, preservation, utilization, and cross-border provision of HGRs, while affirming the right to withdraw consent without retroactively affecting prior legal actions. GB/T 38,736 − 2020 Ethical Requirements for the Preservation of Human Biological Samples distinguishes between specific informed consent and broad consent, thereby providing an ethical framework for the secondary use of human biological samples [36]. The standard permits the use of broad consent during initial collection to accommodate potential future research, provided that participants are informed about possible secondary uses and retain the right to withdraw. Secondary uses exceeding the original consent scope must undergo ethics committee review, and substantial changes in research purpose, methodology, or risk profile require reassessment of the original consent. Additionally, the 2023 Measures for Ethical Review of Life Sciences and Medical Research Involving Humans specifies circumstances under which informed consent may be waived, such as research involving anonymized or publicly available data, subject to ethics committee approval [37].

Entry management of HGR materials in China

The Biosecurity Law serves as the legal foundation for the cross-border transfer of HGRs, mandating compliance with national biosecurity regulations and requiring formal approval for import activities, thereby enabling coordinated oversight by relevant authorities. The regulatory framework is further strengthened by the Implementation Rules, which ensure rigorous scrutiny of material sourcing, informed consent procedures, and intended research purposes. Additional regulatory clarification has been issued through policy guidance, which indicates that commercial human cell lines lacking traceable donor information and derived xenograft models fall outside the definition of HGR materials and are therefore not subject to HGR supervision [38, 39]. Meanwhile, the Provisions on the Administration of the Health and Quarantine of Special Articles Entering or Exiting the Country impose biosafety and public health requirements on HGR-related materials, including risk-based quarantine inspections and post-entry supervision by customs authorities [40]. The regulatory framework also addresses the end-of-life management of biological samples. The Interim Measures for the Management of Research Human Biological Samples in Medical Institutions outline conditions under which research samples should be destroyed, including ethical violations, deviations from approved research protocols, donor withdrawal requests, or the loss of scientific value. These observations demonstrate that China has established a multi-layered management framework for HGRs, characterized by security-oriented biosafety oversight and regulatory control covering the entire lifecycle of biological materials—from cross-border transfer and research utilization to sample disposal.

Discussion

China’s HGRs management has evolved from principle-based legislation to specific administrative regulations. This study systematically examines the normative framework of HGRs management in China from the dual perspectives of legal hierarchy and regulatory logic. First, national security logic serves as the core policy driver. Strict approval processes for the export of materials and data, combined with full-process traceability and security assessment systems, reflected the integration of national security concerns throughout the HGR lifecycle. Second, informed consent and ethical review have become increasingly standardized and stringent. Nevertheless, regulations on data lifecycle oversight and mechanisms for participation in culturally sensitive communities remain limited, highlighting potential tensions between individual autonomy, collective interests, and technological innovation. Third, Regulatory tools are evolving toward a more tailored, risk-sensitive approach, with a strong focus on security. Governance continuity requires further improvement, including the standardization and integration of cross-border approval with subsequent oversight mechanisms.

A key aspect of governance is the role of informed consent, which is widely recognized as a core mechanism for safeguarding participants’ autonomy. However, its practical efficacy continues to raise ethical challenges, especially concerning secondary data use and the scope of original consent [41]. A notable case involving genomic data from 140,000 Chinese individuals illustrates these challenges: BGI maintained that these participants had consented to the use of their samples and data for scientific research. However, many scholars have raised concerns that this constitutes secondary use of the data, potentially exceeding the scope of the original clinical testing consent forms [42]. This highlights a fundamental question about what models of consent are appropriate for genomic research. The UK Biobank’s experience with broad consent demonstrates that participants may be willing to authorize a wide range of future uses at the outset [43]. Yet even that model has faced criticism for the inherent risks of inadequate disclosure and diminished autonomy over time [44].

Biobank research adds further complexity, as genetic information implicates not only individuals but also families, ethnic groups, and collective population interests [45]. Public responses to the publication of the pangenome reference of 36 Chinese ethnic groups have further reflected this concern, with approximately 65% of negative public comments expressing concerns over national genetic security and the potential misuse of collective genetic data [46–48].

Cultural contexts further shaped the practical implementation of informed consent. For example, societal resistance to the commercialization of genetic data has influenced the design of consent procedures, as has the need for approaches that are sensitive to the values of different communities [49]. While regulatory advancements, such as the Measures for Ethical Review of Life Sciences and Medical Research Involving Humans have strengthened ethical oversight in HGR research. Yet, inconsistent anonymization practices, conflicting ethical review timelines, and a lack of harmonization in review standards across regional ethics committees [50]. These issues may further impact the consistent interpretation and effective implementation of informed consent in real-world research settings [51, 52].

Optimizing procedural design within China’s national security-focused governance system could facilitate international HGR collaboration while safeguarding genetic data security. For example, reconceptualizing informed consent as a relational governance process operating within legally defined boundaries can strengthen participant engagement and institutional trust through improved transparency [53]. This can be done by clearly explaining data usage categories, collaborative models, and the scope and consequences of participant withdrawal [54], without altering the core administrative approval structure for cross-border HGR transfer [55]. This reconfiguration positions participant engagement not as a counterbalance to sovereign oversight, but as a complementary mechanism that reinforces the procedural legitimacy of China’s security-embedded HGR governance system.

Cross-border HGRs data sharing is another complex and sensitive issue in China’s regulatory context. Regulatory disparities across jurisdictions create friction in international collaborations, as divergent approaches to informed consent, data protection, and data usage raise compliance and ethical concerns [15]. The substantial fine imposed on Meta Ireland for cross-border data transfers in violation of the GDPR [56] exemplifies the significant compliance risks arising from the coexistence and conflicts of multi-jurisdictional regulatory regimes [57]. Privacy risks further complicate cross-border data sharing. China’s governance model emphasized administrative approval and security control at the point of cross-border transmission [58]. As demonstrated by Gymrek et al. [59], researchers were able to re-identify anonymous participants in public genomic datasets by cross-referencing publicly available genealogical records.

Cross-border genetic data sharing is also hindered by technical and standard disparities across jurisdictions [60]. Beyond format non-uniformity, a deeper challenge lies in the absence of universally accepted annotation standards and an interoperable data governance framework [61, 62]. Internationally, the FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) principles advocated by international organizations and the technical standards developed by the GA4GH have provided a foundational framework for enhancing data interoperability [63]. China has engaged with these initiatives, adopting FAIR principles in collaborative projects such as the International Human Phenome Consortium (IHPC) and the Human Genome Project II (HGP2) [64–66]. However, there remains coordination gaps for coordination between the accessibility requirements of these international standards and China’s cross-border data security assessment system, as well as between the interoperability requirements and the capacity of China’s existing technical infrastructure [67].

Within China’s data security review framework, international technical standards such as those developed by GA4GH, can serve as valuable tools to enhance technical interoperability [68]. Incremental coordination mechanisms, including harmonizing baseline metadata structures, standardizing access interfaces, and aligning analytical workflows, may improve the predictability and transparency of cross-border HGR collaboration [69]. Here, technical interoperability becomes a governance tool, aligning with data visiting principles that allow analysis to travel rather than data [70, 71]. Ultimately, bilateral or multilateral agreements respecting local regimes while ensuring ethical data use offer a pathway to align international standards with China’s security framework, enabling responsible collaboration without compromising sovereignty [72].

While this paper provides a comprehensive comparative analysis of HGRs management across the legal and policy framework, including cross-border data transfer mechanisms and informed consent protocols, it does have some limitations. Since the review was limited to papers published in the Chinese and English language, it is possible that other potentially relevant articles were omitted. Additionally, rapidly evolving developments, such as legislative amendments, pilot projects, and emerging technologies, may not be exhaustively captured. Ongoing scholarly engagement remains valuable to advance policy discourse in this dynamic field.

Conclusions

In conclusion, national security plays a dominant role in China’s HGR governance, shaping strict oversight while limiting the practical reach of informed consent. Cross-border data sharing continues to face challenges, including regulatory discrepancies, privacy risks that extend beyond de-identification, and technical inconsistencies in data formats and annotation standards. Future progress requires enhanced transparency regarding data usage, improved technical interoperability aligned with domestic security requirements, and stronger international coordination through bilateral agreements.
