Addictions are linked but distinct: A transdiagnostic network analysis of concurrent disorder risk
Authors: Spencer Murch, Samantha J. Dawson, David C. Hodgins, Matthew T. Keough, Daniel S. McGrath, Will Shead, Nassim Tabri, Hyoun S. Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsConcurrent disorder refers to the co-occurrence of one or more addictive disorder(s) alongside one or more other mental health disorder(s). To identify psychotherapy targets most-closely related to concurrent disorders, we modeled addiction and mental health symptom severities alongside relevant cognitive psychological factors using pairwise Markov random fields.MethodsCanadian postsecondary students (N = 6,646) self-reported symptoms of addictive disorders related to legally-available products (alcohol, nicotine, cannabis, gambling, or video games), symptoms of commonly co-occurring mental health concerns (depression, anxiety, Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder), and correlated cognitive psychological factors identified by previous research (motives, impulsivity, emotion dysregulation, and those relating to difficult life events [resilience, self-efficacy, perceived social support, experiences of discrimination and adverse childhood experiences]). For these, network models were fit examining addiction symptoms jointly, individually, and separately for product types and classes.ResultsSignificant correlations between addiction and mental health symptoms were most often attributable to indirect links via psychological factors. Behavioural addiction symptoms were linked to mental health symptoms via psychological factors both shared (coping motives, impulsive urgency, and lack of social support) and not shared (e.g., discrimination, adverse childhood experiences) with substance-related addictions. Differences between addiction symptoms relevant to specific products varied more between individuals than between substances. Disorder symptom severities were differently accounted-for by network factors (R2adj = .13−.63).Discussion and ConclusionsDespite identifying several mechanisms of concurrence between mental health and addictions, modeled psychological factors do not account for most variability in addiction symptom severity.

Introduction

Concurrent disorder refers to the co-occurrence of addictive disorders alongside at least one other mental health disorder within individuals, a state of affairs that is “more the expectation than the exception when assessing patients with severe mental illness” (Buckley, 2006). Depressive disorders (Potenza et al., 2019), anxiety disorders (Hasin, Stinson, Ogburn, & Grant, 2007), Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD; Roberts, Lotzin, & Schäfer, 2022), and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD; Manni et al., 2019) all commonly co-occur with addictions. An estimated 37% of those with Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD) have at least one non-addiction mental health disorder, a near fivefold increase over healthy controls (Regier et al., 1990). Separately, comorbidity rates with gambling disorder have been estimated at 39% for major depression or dysthymia, 17% for generalized anxiety disorder, 15% for PTSD, and 13% for ADHD (Kessler et al., 2008).

Individuals with concurrent disorders have shown heightened disorder severity in addition to greater impairments in social or familial relationships (Carrà, Scioli, Monti, & Marinoni, 2006). A nationally-representative survey of Canadians found that, with other factors held constant, individuals with concurrent disorders were 325% more likely to report medical needs going unmet (Urbanoski, Cairney, Bassani, & Rush, 2008). At the severe end of adverse outcomes, those with concurrent disorders face significantly increased odds for admission to emergency departments (Minassian, Vilke, & Wilson, 2013), and more frequent admission to and longer stays in inpatient psychiatric care (Florentin, Rosca, Raskin, Bdolah-Abram, & Neumark, 2019).

Four scenarios may underlie the expression of concurrent disorders (Mueser, Drake, & Wallach, 1998). First and second, experiencing an addiction may precipitate the later development of mental health disorders (and vice versa). Third, early symptoms of both disorders may bidirectionally interact, escalating both towards clinically significant levels. Finally, concurrent disorders may share common aetiological factors that predispose the concomitant expression of multiple conditions. These complex states contribute to ‘wrong door syndrome’, in which individuals with concurrent disorders may be funnelled into care networks specializing in only one of the disorders they experience.

Transdiagnostic theories of addictions

Beginning to account for the many diverse permutations of concurrent disorders, transdiagnostic approaches to addiction emphasize the shared cognitive, affective, and behavioural factors underlying many psychopathologies in addition to commonalities in their aetiologies, trajectories, maintenance, and treatments. Transdiagnostic efforts to integrate emerging evidence regarding the multiple antecedents of concurrent disorders are underway, and are contributing to a unified, multidimensional psychiatric nosology (Michelini, Palumbo, DeYoung, Latzman, & Roman, 2021). Notable transdiagnostic theories of addiction are the General Theory of Addictions (Jacobs, 1986), and Syndrome Model of Addiction (Shaffer et al., 2004), which propose that addictive disorders are distinct expressions of the same underlying neuropsychological vulnerabilities and antecedent behaviours, and recognize concurrent mental health disorders as common risk factors for addiction expression writ large.

More recently, Kim and colleagues proposed the Component Model of Addiction Treatment (CMAT; Kim & Hodgins, 2018), which sought to: (1) identify common factors underlying behavioural and substance-related addictions, and (2) identify which among these are viable therapeutic targets. Reviewing past research, CMAT proposed a list of cognitive psychological factors frequently and strongly associated across both substance and behavioural addictions. These factors are potentially treatment-modifiable targets, and many are arguably also important in the treatment of mental health disorders. The list includes motives (e.g., to cope with low mood; Farrelly, Ffrench, Ogeil, & Phillips, 2007; Goldstein, Stewart, Hoaken, & Flett, 2014), trait impulsivity (Kim & Grant, 2001; Potenza et al., 2019), emotion dysregulation (de Castro, Fong, Rosenthal, & Tavares, 2007; Poole, Kim, Dobson, & Hodgins, 2017), and individual differences in exposure to – and recovery from – difficult life events (low resilience, low self-efficacy, a lack of social support, and a history of experiencing discrimination or adverse childhood experiences; see for review Kim & Hodgins, 2018).

CMAT recognizes the existence of genetic vulnerabilities in the expression of concurrent disorders, but notes these are not easily identified or modifiable in most clinical settings. Although it divides the remaining psychological factors into transdiagnostically mechanistic (causally involved in etiology or maintenance) or merely descriptive (correlated, but not causally involved) types, it suggests that etiological sequencing differs widely between individuals. CMAT instead emphasizes identifying the relative importance among the many factors that may (but do not always) appear alongside concurrent disorders.

Although each of motivations, impulsivity, emotion dysregulation, and responses to difficult life events have been identified by multiple studies as risk factors for substance-related and behavioural addictions and concurrent mental health concerns, their interrelations and relative importance in trajectories of concurrent disorders have so far been less clear. Sophisticated analytical techniques are needed to disentangle these because simple bivariate correlations involving individual disorders and individual psychological factors may alternatively be explained by relationships between measured factors and either unmeasured concurrent disorders, or unmeasured psychological factors. In order to better understand covariance between addiction and mental health symptom severities as potentially facilitated by psychological factors, it is necessary to examine all three at once.

Network analysis for transdiagnostic psychiatry

Network analysis provides the integrative approach needed to consider multiple complex relationships and outcomes concurrently. It does this by visualizing partial relationships (‘edges’) between variable (‘node’) pairs in feature-rich datasets. Although relationships between nodes are correlational by design (and thus unable to establish formal mediation in the absence of causal inference), they nevertheless identify when two nodes' covariance partly or wholly involves some third (or fourth, fifth, etc.) factor(s) also appearing in the network. The advantage of network models is thus that they can identify the arrangement of, and relationships between, multiple constructs concurrently, identifying sources of shared variance that remain ambiguous in targeted cross-sectional research investigating specific psychological factors in relation to specific mental health concerns.

Network analysis has been widely (but separately) applied to examine symptoms of many mental disorders including substance use disorders (Rhemtulla et al., 2016), behavioural addictions (Granero et al., 2025), depression and anxiety (Beard et al., 2016; Mullarkey, Marchetti, & Beevers, 2019), ADHD (Silk et al., 2019), and PTSD (Kolbuszewska, Tavares, Nobre, & Dawson, 2024). Some past network analyses have simultaneously examined symptoms of two comorbid disorders (e.g., depression and PTSD; Lazarov et al., 2020), and some have done so alongside psychological or trait factors using a transdiagnostic approach. Vervaet, Puttevils, Hoekstra, Fried, and Vanderhasselt (2021), for example, modeled relationships between eating disorders, mood symptoms, and moderating trait factors. Groen, Wichers, Wigman, and Hartman (2019) examined relationships between symptom severity and core symptoms of mood, anxiety, substance use, disruptive, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorders. Relevant to addictive behaviours, Shmulewitz et al. (2024) fitted a regularized partial correlation network linking depression, anxiety, and PTSD to multiple addictive substances in addition to gambling, video games, compulsive sexual behaviours, and problematic use of the internet, smartphones and social media. That network found addictive and non-addictive disorders to form distinct clusters, but did not include relevant psychological factors that could further clarify the nature of connections between nodes.

This work seeks to extend previous research by leveraging a large participant sample towards fitting networks of concurrent disorder symptom severities situated among transdiagnostic cognitive psychological factors that may predispose their expression. Modelling behavioural and substance-related addiction symptom severities, mental health symptom severities, and recognized psychological factors together, descriptive relationships within the CMAT framework will be identified whenever disorder nodes connect either directly (indicating no involvement of measured psychological factors) or indirectly via transdiagnostic psychological factors (indicating involvement of one or more of these). Two key benefits emerge from this analysis: (1) cognitive psychological mechanisms involved in disorder concurrence may be revealed, and (2) proxy measurement between disorder symptom severities and psychological factors will be accounted for, preventing inadvertent misidentifications that may occur in hypothesis-driven multivariate models.

The current study

Leveraging a pan-Canadian research effort towards understanding addiction comorbidities, we optimized pairwise Markov random field networks linking symptoms of several substance-related and behavioural addictions, symptoms of commonly concurrent mental health disorders, and several psychological factors identified in prior research. From these, network properties were evaluated to better understand both direct and indirect relationships between concurrent disorder symptomatology. Our exploratory hypothesis was that psychological factors would link symptoms of different addictions to each other and to those of commonly concurrent mental health concerns. We further hypothesized that observed relationships with addiction symptomatology may differ based on whether addiction is: (1) treated as a unitary construct, (2) divided into substance-related versus behavioural constructs, (3) divided into specific substances or behaviours, or (4) divided into individual symptoms within specific substances or behaviours. Across these levels of analysis, our aim was to identify treatment-modifiable cognitive factors that consistently link the symptoms of concurrent disorders.

Method

Participants

Further detail is provided in Supplementary Materials S.1. Participants were recruited in two sequential, cross-sectional surveys as part of the Comorbidity Collaboration, a pan-Canadian research project aimed at understanding concurrent disorders. The first survey (n = 5,990) began September 2023 and concluded April 2024. The second survey (n = 5,184) began October 2024 and concluded April 2025. After filtering data for eligibility and response quality, the final sample (N = 6,646) retained high statistical power for the planned analyses (see Supplementary Materials S.1). Participants were Canadian adults recruited from undergraduate research pools at six universities (the University of British Columbia [n = 3,195], the University of Calgary [n = 825], York University [n = 1,133], Toronto Metropolitan University [n = 575], Carleton University [n = 683], and Mount Saint Vincent University [n = 235]). Participants were compensated with Psychology course credit at their participating institution for 1 h of work.

Detailed sociodemographic information is provided in Table 1. Most participants (92.10%) were between 18 and 24 years of age with older age groups represented to lesser extents. Participants hailed from diverse backgrounds: 65.89% of participants reported being non-white, and 38.24% were first-generation immigrants to Canada. Most participants reported being female (78.91%), and 1.79% self-identified as transgender. Participants reported varying employment status and educational attainment, but most (77.06%) reported being “single” in terms of their relationship status.

Measures

A complete description of survey instruments is provided in Supplementary Materials S.1. Participants separately reported symptoms of five substance-related and behavioural addictions (alcohol use disorder [AUD], nicotine use disorder [NUD], cannabis use disorder [CUD], gambling disorder [GD], and internet gaming disorder [IGD]) using the four-item Short Screener for Substance and Behavioural Addictions (SSBA; Schluter, Hodgins, Wolfe, & Cameron Wild, 2018). The SSBA queries four addiction-relevant domains (excessive use, loss of control over use, dependence, and use despite problems) on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“none of the time”) to 4 (“all of the time”). For each participant, we calculated a total score out of sixteen for each surveyed substance or behaviour, and from these we recorded the maximum summed score among (1) substance-related addictions (SSBAsub; alcohol, cannabis, and nicotine), (2) behavioural addictions (SSBAbeh; gambling and video games), and (3) all addictions (SSBAmax). Participants reporting no use of the five products were not asked these questions, and these skipped cases were not imputed as zeros. 6,634 participants responded to SSBA questions for at least one product, while 12 reported no use of any. As such, network edges relating to SSBA nodes are interpretable as measuring self-reported problems among users of those products, not among the sample writ large (see also Supplementary Materials S.1). Sensitivity conducted to clarify the impacts of this analytical choice showed no credible differences (See Supplementary Materials S.2).

Participants additionally self-reported symptoms consistent with depressive and anxiety disorders (Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995), ADHD (Kessler et al., 2005), PTSD (Connor & Davidson, 2001), and social anxiety (Mattick & Christopher Clarke, 1998).

Twenty cognitive psychological factors were surveyed. Six of these referred to emotion dysregulation (via Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale, DERS-18; Victor & David Klonsky, 2016); a difficulty in handling emotional states that result in strong and prolonged reactions to emotional events. It was grouped into six related topics: awareness of emotions, clarity in understanding emotions, nonacceptance of emotional reactions (i.e., shame, guilt, embarrassment), interference of emotions in goal pursuit, lacking strategies to bounce back from negative emotions, and the impulse to act on emotions.

Five psychological factors related to impulsivity (via short UPPS-P Impulsive Behavior Scale, SUPPS-P; Cyders, Littlefield, Coffey, & Karyadi, 2014), the trait tendencies: to act without premeditation, to react to positive or negative mood states with a sense of urgency, to seek high-sensation activities, and to lack perseverance in tasks.

Four psychological factors queried motives for using addictive products (via Brief Motives Measure, BMM; Mahu, Barrett, Conrod, Bartel, & Stewart, 2021): enhancement of positive feelings, coping with feelings of depression or anxiety, socializing, and fear of missing out (FOMO).

Finally, exposure and responses to difficult life events were explored across five topics. Adverse childhood experiences (Zarse et al., 2019) refer to serious negative and traumatic events (abuse, neglect, witnessing domestic violence, etc.) occurring before 18 years of age. Everyday discrimination (Williams, Yan, Jackson, & Anderson, 1997) refers to minor but recurrent instances where the respondent has felt disrespected, devalued, feared, or threatened by others. Perceived social support (Zimet, Dahlem, Zimet, & Farley, 1988) refers to the level of help one perceives as available from friends, family, and partners. Resilience refers to the trait tendency to ‘bounce back’ from negative life events (Smith et al., 2008). Self-efficacy refers to one's perceived capability to accomplish goals and meet their own needs (Schwarzer & Jerusalem, 1995).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were completed in R using qgraph (Epskamp, Cramer, Waldorp, Schmittmann, & Borsboom, 2012). Data, scripts, and materials have been archived.1 All networks were pairwise Markov random fields with edges representing partial correlation (Isvoranu, Epskamp, Waldorp, & Borsboom, 2022). All reported networks were positive semi-definite. A bootstrapped case dropping procedure was employed to compare model fits for two preliminary network types: one fit using graphical LASSO regularization (Friedman, Hastie, & Tibshirani, 2019), and one iteratively fit by sequentially adding and removing network edges, an approach well-suited to large datasets with relatively few features (Epskamp, 2018; see Supplementary Materials S.1). The latter, unregularized approach was more robust to case dropping, and was thus selected for final network fits. As a result, reported edge weights represent and are reported as unregularized partial correlation coefficients (R2partial) between node pairs in the network context. Network visualizations were generated as flow diagrams for addiction-relevant variables or, where several such variables were present, used the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm which modifies node proximity on the basis of edge weight to place strongly-related nodes close together.

Four kinds of networks were fit. First, an overall network modeled SSBAmax with concurrent disorder nodes and psychological factors. Second, a product category network replaced SSBAmax with SSBAsub and SSBAbeh. Third, a product type network fit all five SSBA sum scores (alcohol, cannabis, nicotine, gambling, and gaming) separately. Finally, five symptom networks allowed individual nodes for the four SSBA items for each product. Cumulatively, these networks will indicate whether different levels of abstraction identify different factors as important for disorder concurrence.

Four kinds of analyses were applied towards understanding the structure of each network. First, the proportion of edges retained and mean edge weight were used as an overall measure of network connectivity and validity. Network analyses assume sparsity (that there is truly zero relationship between most node pairs; Isvoranu et al., 2022; Ye & Liu, 2012). If a network retains most edges, mean edge weight will be very low, and estimated effects are questionable. Second, network uniformity was assessed using the small-world index (SWI), which examines the number of tightly-clustered node groups and the average path length between nodes to identify when networks contain distinct subgroups of nodes. Uniform networks have SWI ≈1 while networks involving two or more very distinct constructs often have SWI >3 (Isvoranu et al., 2022).

Third, three centrality metrics were computed using bootstrapped estimation to better understand the relative importance (centrality) of individual variables in the network context. Among these, strength centrality measures the relative number and magnitude of edges touching each node. Nodes with many large edges are very strong. Closeness centrality measures the relative proximity of each node to all others. Nodes that are distant from many others are low in closeness. Betweenness centrality measures how often a node appears on the shortest path linking all other node pairs. A node that solely connects otherwise separate parts of a network will have high betweenness. Fourth and finally, we computed each node's adjusted multiple coefficient of determination (R2adj) when regressed upon its edges. This provides a comparison of the extent to which variability in each node is accounted for by the nodes to which it is linked (while adjusting for capitalization on error variance), distinguishing nodes that are well-explained by other network nodes from those that are not. Further details for each are provided in Supplementary Materials S.1.

Results

Bivariate correlations between commonly concurrent disorder symptoms

Replicating much past research, simple bivariate correlations linked the severities of addiction symptoms and those of other mental health concerns. In all cases except GD—social anxiety and GD—PTSD, these correlations were statistically significant (Fig. 1). The magnitude of most relationships was small, possibly due in part to non-normal marginal distributions (Figures S.2.1–3). In any case, having replicated these relationships, the key question is: will network representations of these connections be direct (indicating unique covariance not shared with other nodes), or indirect (indicating mutual dependence on some other measured factor(s))? If the latter, will indirect links be made via single cognitive psychological factors, or will multifactorial accounts be needed to explain correlated symptom severities?

Overall network

The ‘overall’ network examining all addictive disorder symptom totals contained 130 edges out of a possible 325 (Fig. 2A). The mean edge weight of the network was small (rpartial = .03, Figure S.2.6). It nevertheless exhibited excellent stability (Figure S.2.4), and SWI = 1.05, suggesting no distinct node clusters within the broader structure (Isvoranu et al., 2022).

Addiction symptom severity exhibited direct, positive connections to multiple psychological factors: lack of premeditation, positive urgency, motives for substance use (coping, socializing, and enhancement), and adverse childhood experiences (Fig. 2A). The strongest among these was the edge connecting addiction symptoms to coping motives (rpartial = .25). Connections between addiction symptom severity and all mental health symptom severities were indirect. Addiction symptoms were mainly linked to anxiety and depression symptoms via coping motives, to PTSD symptoms via adverse childhood experiences, and to ADHD via lack of premeditation.

Centrality analyses indicated that depression was particularly high in strength, closeness, and betweenness (Fig. 3; Figure S.2.5). ADHD, anxiety, and social anxiety were similarly central. Among psychological factors, positive urgency and lacking strategies for emotion dysregulation emerged as particularly central across these metrics. Addiction symptom severity was not very central to the network, falling below the network average on all centrality metrics.

In terms of node coverage, most variability in depression (R2adj = .63) and anxiety symptoms (R2adj = .56), but less than one third of variability in addiction symptoms (R2adj = .29), was accounted for by linked network nodes (Fig. 3). A strong minority of variability was explained by linked nodes for each of ADHD (R2adj = .48), PTSD (R2adj = .45), and social anxiety symptoms (R2adj = .42). Thus, factors measured here accounted well for depression and anxiety symptom severity, but incompletely for addiction symptom severity.

Product category network

When SSBA sum scores were separated for substance-related and behavioural addiction symptoms, the resulting network retained 138 of 351 possible edges (Fig. 2B and C). The network remained stable (Figure S.2.7), with the weight of network edges remaining around rpartial = .03 (Figure S.2.10), and SWI = 1.05.

The main differences between this and the previous network concern direct versus indirect relationships between addiction symptom nodes and others (Fig. 2B and C). Although both substance-related and behavioural addiction symptom severities exhibited direct links to coping motives, enhancement motives and low social support, they otherwise appeared quite different. Substance-related addiction symptoms were positively linked to positive urgency and negatively linked to behavioural addiction symptoms. Behavioural addiction symptom severity was, in turn, directly linked to social use motives, negative urgency, lack of premeditation, adverse childhood experiences and discrimination. Direct links were also observed between behavioural addiction, ADHD, PTSD, and social anxiety symptoms. Substance-related addiction symptoms exhibited no direct links to non-addiction mental health symptoms.

Centrality analyses were mostly comparable to the previous model with the notable exception that behavioural addiction symptoms were significantly stronger than substance-related addiction symptoms (Zdifference = 2.10, p < .05; Figure S.2.8-9), and relatedly exhibited much stronger conceptual coverage by linked network nodes (although both captured only a minority of variability; substance-related R2adj = .13; behavioural addiction symptoms R2adj = .27).

Product type network

When separate nodes were fit for addiction symptoms related to each of alcohol, cannabis, nicotine, gambling, or video game use, relationships with commonly concurrent disorder symptoms were further clarified (Fig. 2D). Similar to earlier models, this network's 30 nodes retained 157 out of 435 possible edges, and these had a small mean edge weight of (rpartial = .03; Figure S.2.14). This network was acceptably stable (Figure S.2.11), and exhibited no subgroupings of nodes (SWI = 1.10).

In terms of correlated addiction symptoms, AUD, CUD, NUD, and GD formed a tetrad of direct, positive links (Fig. 2D, Figure S.2.14). In contrast, IGD symptoms were positively linked to GD, negatively linked to AUD and CUD, and not directly linked to NUD. Although substance-related and behavioural addiction symptoms appeared anticorrelated in the previous model, this network suggests the effect may be due specifically to negative links between IGD and both AUD and CUD.

Addiction symptoms were linked to other mental health concerns both directly and via multiple cognitive psychological factors, and the nature of these links differed between specific substances and behaviours (Table 2, Fig. 2D). AUD symptoms were linked to co-occurring mental health symptoms via positive urgency, experiences of discrimination, and enhancement, social, and FOMO motives. They were also directly but weakly linked to PTSD (rpartial = .06) and social anxiety symptoms (rpartial = −.05). NUD symptoms were indirectly linked to co-occurring mental health symptoms via premeditation deficits, negative urgency, social support, and adverse childhood experiences. Direct connections were observed between NUD symptoms and both anxiety (rpartial = .05) and social anxiety (rpartial = −.07) symptoms, although effect directions differed. CUD symptoms were indirectly linked to co-occurring mental health symptoms via coping and enhancement motives (depression and anxiety), experiences of discrimination, and adverse childhood experiences. No edge directly linked CUD symptoms to other mental health nodes.

GD symptoms were linked to symptoms of non-addiction mental health concerns only indirectly via motives (coping, enhancement, FOMO), positive urgency, and emotion dysregulation impacting goal pursuit (Fig. 2D, Table 2). IGD symptoms were indirectly linked to symptoms of non-addiction mental health concerns via coping motives and perceived social support. Centrality analyses were mostly comparable to earlier models, exhibiting strong centrality for depression, positive urgency, and aspects of emotion dysregulation, but below average centrality for addiction symptom nodes (Figure S.2.12-13). Importantly, product-specific addiction symptoms were only somewhat well-explained by linked network nodes (R2adj = 0.13 [GD] to 0.36 [CUD]). Here again, mental health symptom severities were well-explained by psychological factors, but addiction symptom severities were less well-explained.

Symptom networks

The five product-specific addiction symptom networks were very similar to one another, with no significant differences in terms of edge count (Χ2 (4) = 4.50, p = .34), or mean edge weight (F (4,2025) < 0.01, p > .99). Compared to earlier networks, these networks were noticeably unstable (Figure S.2.15), and this led to wide variation in centrality estimates across metrics and models (Fig. 4). Given that the sample was sufficiently large to yield stable networks in earlier models, we concluded that any substance- or behaviour-level differences in the links between specific addiction symptoms and other mental health symptoms were overshadowed by individual differences in symptom presentations among the minority of participants reporting them, resulting in high variability between bootstrapped samples.

Discussion

This study developed networks of concurrent disorder symptoms and transdiagnostic cognitive psychological factors reported by a large and diverse sample of emerging adults. We hypothesized that psychological factors would link symptoms of different addictions to each other and to those of commonly concurrent mental health concerns. We further hypothesized that observed relationships would differ based on the extent to which addiction symptoms were combined into superordinate categories (overall, product category, specific products, or symptoms within products).

Our hypotheses were supported: symptoms of commonly-concurrent disorders were linked in numerous ways, and these linkages provided a relative accounting of propinquity to concurrent disorder symptom severities among competing cognitive psychological factors. In some cases, direct links suggested co-occurring symptoms without involvement of measured psychological factors. For example, PTSD and AUD symptoms showed a positive, direct relationship consistent with past evidence suggesting alcohol plays a role in maladaptive coping with PTSD symptoms (Smith & Cottler, 2018). Separately, nicotine may be anxiogenic, explaining the negative edge that was observed between NUD symptoms and social anxiety (Picciotto, Brunzell, & Caldarone, 2002). Other direct relationships, namely the positive link between NUD and anxiety symptoms, and the positive link between social anxiety and IGD symptoms, are not theoretically clear and will require further research to understand fully.

Several psychological factors emerged as particularly important in defining indirect links between symptoms of commonly concurrent disorders. Addiction symptoms writ large were linked to other mental health concerns by a small group of cognitive psychological mechanisms: coping motives and low social support made up the main path between addiction and both anxiety and depression, lack of premeditation defined the main path between addiction and ADHD, and adverse childhood experiences defined the main path linking addiction to PTSD. These effects are intuitive and established (Kim & Hodgins, 2018). The real power of these network models, however, is in identifying cognitive psychological factors that did not appear on short pathways linking addiction and mental health symptom severities: resilience, self-efficacy, sensation-seeking, lack of perseverance, and multiple aspects of emotion dysregulation. Each of these factors failed to form a single-stop indirect link between addiction and mental health symptom severities in any network model. Accordingly, we may conclude that their contributions to disorder concurrence appear to be either attributable to more complex combinations of multiple psychological factors, or alternatively to their correlation with factors that formed simpler pathways.

Although each of these topics has been (or could conceivably be) identified in simpler multivariate models as involved in the concurrence of addictive and mental health concerns, their lack of distinct contribution becomes apparent in these partial correlation networks which included multiple psychological processes at once. Put differently, social support, childhood trauma, substance use motives, urgency, and lack of premeditation appear to be relatively strong targets for interventions aimed at addressing specific concurrent disorders, while other factors measured here do not. For example, a clinician may notice that a patient diagnosed with both an addictive disorder and ADHD also reports low self-efficacy, and may decide that is a treatment target to address both disorders. However, this apparent correlation may be mostly explained by low social support and lack of premeditation (among other factors). Thus, the clinician's efforts may not have the greatest possible effect since they miss the factors that were more-strongly involved in concurrence.

Centrality analyses cast doubt, however, on the idea that cognitive psychological factors measured here could fully explain concurrent disorder symptom severities: addiction nodes were low on every centrality metric, leaving 87% of variability in substance-related addiction symptoms unaccounted-for by connected network nodes. The same was not true of mental health symptom severities, which were generally well accounted-for by linked network nodes. Further, networks that considered specific symptoms of addictions involving different substances or behaviours were notably unstable, suggesting that the expression of different product-specific addiction symptoms depended largely upon individual cases rather than the product under examination. Taking these findings alongside the broader pattern of interconnectivity in these networks, we conclude that addictive disorder symptoms are linked insofar as they covary with symptoms of other mental health concerns via multiple psychological factors, but distinct insofar as these links do not fully explain variation in addiction symptom severities. Future research in this area may adopt a longitudinal approach, or investigate key subgroups within the population to identify addiction presentations associated with more-severe problems, more frequent concurrence with other disorders, or both.

A number of key limitations are noteworthy in these analyses. First, the sample employed may have been large, diverse (see Table 1), and well-powered, but it was not randomly sampled from the Canadian population. The sample additionally was not comprised of treatment-seeking individuals or other groups with high-severity symptoms. Although a range of symptoms was seen for all disorders of interest, relationships that were fit may draw disproportionately on differences between low-severity problems due to their more-frequent representation in the sample (Figure S.3, S.4). Finally, nodes were measured using different self-report instruments from their respective domains, and psychometric properties are likely not equal between these. It is possible that some apparently-less-central nodes may have been either less sensitive or less precise in measuring their target constructs, diminishing partial correlations in resulting networks. In one of these nodes, for the sake of network integrity, we treated motives as a trait factor cutting across all addictive products even though motives have been observed to differ between products including those measured here (Mahu et al., 2021).

Conclusion

Concurrent disorders are an important topic for both transdiagnostic nosology and for the identification of best-practice psychotherapies and prevention tools. These findings provide a uniquely detailed picture, identifying psychological factors more- and less-directly implicated in the risks for concurrent addictive disorders, depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, ADHD, and PTSD. In doing so, they identified substance use motives, urgency, lack of premeditation, adverse childhood experiences and low social support as relatively central factors for integrative treatment targeting specific cognitive psychological mechanisms. They point to a relatively smaller contribution by sensation seeking, lack of perseverance, resilience, self-efficacy, and several aspects of emotion dysregulation. Although risks for addictive disorders may not be completely accounted-for by psychological factors, the need to consider these elements when treating or seeking to prevent concurrent disorders is clearly supported.
