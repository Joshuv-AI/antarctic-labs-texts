Enhancing nurses’ psychological empowerment through NLP: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Sevim Şen Olgay, Esra Uğur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychological empowerment is necessary for strengthening autonomy, motivation, and resilience among nurses in high-demand clinical settings. Neuro-linguistic programming (NLP), a behavioral approach that enhances cognitive flexibility and emotional regulation, may offer an effective pathway to internal empowerment. This study aimed to investigate the impact of an NLP-based educational intervention on nurses’ psychological empowerment.MethodsA convergent mixed-methods, quasi-experimental study was conducted with 70 registered nurses at a private university hospital in Istanbul, Türkiye. Participants were randomly assigned to intervention (n = 35) and control (n = 35) groups. The intervention group completed a structured NLP program administered in five 3-h sessions over 1 week. Psychological empowerment was measured using the Psychological Empowerment Scale at three time points: pretest, immediate posttest, and 1-month follow-up. Data were analyzed using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U test for between-group comparisons and Friedman and Wilcoxon tests for within-group comparisons. To assess effect sizes, Cohen’s d was calculated. Qualitative data from pre- and post-intervention focus groups were analyzed via thematic analysis.ResultsThe intervention group exhibited considerable improvements in total empowerment and all subdimensions—meaning, competence, self-determination, and impact—at posttest and follow-up (p < .001). Effect sizes were very large: meaning d = 2.30, competence d = 3.12, self-determination d = 3.36, impact d = 1.44, and total score d = 1.84. Qualitative themes indicated increased perspective flexibility, role clarity, assertive communication, and goal-directed professional initiative following the training.ConclusionA structured NLP-based program substantially improved nurses’ psychological empowerment and related professional behaviors. Integrating NLP-informed strategies into staff development may offer a practical, scalable approach to foster autonomy, emotional self-regulation, and resilience in clinical nursing. Longer follow-up and multi-site trials are warranted.Trial registrationThe study was retrospectively registered in the ISRCTN registry under the number ISRCTN18008877 (Date of registration: July 7, 2025).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04095-5.

Background

Psychological empowerment is a key concept in nursing because of its strong links to autonomy, motivation, and resilience in demanding clinical environments. It has been associated with greater job satisfaction, lower burnout, and improved organizational commitment [1–3]. The International Council of Nurses emphasizes empowerment as a global priority to strengthen the nursing workforce and improve health outcomes [4]. Spreitzer conceptualized psychological empowerment through four dimensions—meaning, competence, self-determination, and impact—which together shape how nurses perceive and perform their professional roles [5].

While organizational strategies such as supportive leadership and adequate staffing are important, they are often insufficient to ensure lasting empowerment [6–8]. Internal mechanisms, including beliefs, perceptions, and emotional regulation, are also critical [9]. Nurses working under heavy workload, time pressure, and role ambiguity particularly benefit from interventions that enhance psychological resilience and self-regulation [2, 10].

Neuro-Linguistic Programming (NLP) offers a cognitive–behavioral approach that targets these internal processes. NLP explores how sensory perception, language, and thought patterns shape emotions and behaviors. Through techniques such as reframing, anchoring, and perspective-taking, it can foster cognitive flexibility, emotional regulation, and goal-directed action—all of which align closely with empowerment dimensions [11, 12]. However, despite its growing popularity in educational and clinical contexts, the scientific validity of NLP remains debated. Several scholars have questioned the methodological rigor and empirical evidence supporting its theoretical claims [13, 14]. Systematic reviews have shown mixed results, suggesting that while NLP-based interventions may yield positive experiential outcomes, their underlying mechanisms are not yet fully established [13]. This ongoing debate highlights the need for rigorous, theory-informed, and context-specific evaluations, particularly within nursing and healthcare education.

Since its development in the 1970s, NLP has been used internationally across psychotherapy, education, and professional training [11, 15]. In nursing and healthcare, its integration into educational interventions has gradually increased over the past two decades, with studies reporting benefits such as improved stress management, enhanced communication, and reflective practice skills [16–18]. The importance of training is underscored by the fact that empowerment strategies require structured, experiential approaches rather than passive knowledge transfer [7, 19, 20]. Therefore, situating NLP within a structured training program provides both historical grounding and practical relevance for supporting nurses’ psychological empowerment [13, 14, 18].

Internationally, NLP has been applied in healthcare, education, and psychotherapy. Studies in the United Kingdom, and Asia have shown its usefulness in stress reduction, resilience training, and improving communication skills among health professionals [16, 17, 21, 22]. In Türkiye, NLP-based interventions have been applied in areas such as pain control, anxiety reduction, and behavioral change [18, 23, 24]. Most recently, a randomized controlled trial revealed that NLP techniques significantly enhanced postpartum comfort among mothers after cesarean delivery, improving physical, psycho-spiritual, and sociocultural comfort outcomes [24].

Despite this growing body of evidence, the use of NLP to foster psychological empowerment in nurses has not been thoroughly investigated. Building on this theoretical alignment and global need, the present study evaluates the effect of a structured NLP-based educational program on nurses’ psychological empowerment, using a mixed-methods, quasi-experimental design.

Theoretical framework

As conceptualized by Spreitzer [5], psychological empowerment is a multidimensional construct reflecting an individual’s intrinsic task motivation and sense of control in the workplace. It consists of four interrelated cognitive dimensions: meaning, the alignment between an individual’s personal values and the nature of their work; competence, the belief in one’s capability to successfully perform work activities; self-determination, the perceived autonomy to initiate and regulate work-related actions; and impact, the sense that one’s actions can influence organizational outcomes [1, 2, 5, 25]. These dimensions jointly shape how individuals perceive their roles, engage in their work, and respond to challenges [5, 9, 26]. Notably, psychological empowerment arises not only from structural factors (e.g., leadership or access to resources) but also from internal cognitive processes (e.g., interpretation, emotional regulation, and belief systems) [27].

Within this context, NLP offers a theoretically coherent intervention model. As a cognitive-behavioral methodology, NLP highlights the influence of language, perception, and internal dialogue on behavior shaping [11, 15]. By focusing on individuals’ self-perception and thought patterns, NLP activates the dimensions outlined by empowerment theory: Meaning is cultivated through strategies such as reframing and setting well-defined outcomes, which help individuals reinterpret professional challenges in ways that highlight purpose and alignment with personal values [11, 15]. Competence is enhanced through techniques such as anchoring and meta-model questioning, which enable individuals to access empowering emotional states and challenge limiting beliefs [14, 17]. Self-determination is supported through perceptual positioning and sensory acuity, which promote perspective-taking and enhance volitional control awareness [16, 18]. Impact is enhanced through goal-setting, visualization, and language restructuring, which promote a stronger sense of agency, assertiveness, and contribution in the workplace [11, 12, 18].

By aligning specific NLP strategies with each psychological empowerment dimension, this study employs a structured and theory-driven approach to fostering internal empowerment. Rather than solely focusing on external workplace redesign, the study conceptualizes empowerment as a product of intentional cognitive transformation [11, 15, 17, 18]. This alignment not only supports the conceptual validity of the intervention but also highlights its potential for scalability in professional development, particularly within high-demand and emotionally intensive clinical environments [14]. Alignment of NLP techniques with psychological empowerment dimensions and nursing applications

Building on this theoretical foundation, the study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured NLP-based educational intervention in enhancing nurses’ psychological empowerment. A convergent mixed-methods, quasi-experimental design was adopted to capture both quantitative outcomes and qualitative experiences. Through this design, the study contributes to the growing body of evidence on internal empowerment strategies by positioning NLP as a promising, cost-effective, and scalable intervention for workforce development.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a convergent mixed-methods, quasi-experimental design and included intervention and control groups to examine the effectiveness of a structured NLP training program in enhancing nurses’ psychological empowerment. During the interpretation phase, the quantitative and qualitative strands were concurrently implemented and integrated to enhance the validity and depth of the findings.

Participants and setting

The study was conducted between October 2022 and January 2023 at a foundation university hospital in Istanbul, Türkiye, which was accredited under international healthcare quality standards and provides a diverse clinical environment. Eligible participants were full-time registered nurses who had completed institutional orientation and voluntarily provided informed consent. Nurses involved in other psychological or professional development programs were excluded. A total of 70 nurses were randomly selected from 128 eligible staff members for the quantitative strand. The participants were assigned to the intervention (n = 35) and control (n = 35) groups using odd–even sequencing based on alphabetical order. An a priori power analysis conducted using G*Power revealed that the required number of participants to detect a moderate effect size (d = 0.50), with 80% power at a 0.05 significance level [7, 28], was 64 (32 per group). The final number of participants exceeded this threshold.

For the qualitative strand, two focus group interviews were conducted. A preintervention focus group interview (n = 12) was conducted to explore nurses’ perceptions of empowerment and identify their training needs, which guided the design of the NLP sessions. A postintervention focus group (n = 12), composed of participants from the intervention arm, was conducted one month after the training to explore perceived changes in psychological empowerment and assess the applicability of the NLP training in clinical practice. Participants were purposively selected to ensure experience, department, and educational background variations. To ensure transparency and rigor in qualitative reporting, the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist was followed. For the qualitative strand, purposive sampling was used to achieve maximum variation in participants’ age, professional role, and clinical department, thereby enhancing the richness and transferability of the data. The focus group approach enabled in-depth exploration of shared experiences and perceptions. Data saturation was considered achieved when no new codes or subthemes emerged during the analysis of the final two focus group transcripts. This was confirmed independently by both coders through iterative review, ensuring that the data were sufficiently comprehensive to represent participants’ perspectives.

Intervention: NLP-based psychological empowerment program

The NLP-Based Psychological Empowerment Program was developed by the research team and validated by three certified NLP practitioners. The program was structured using the ADDIE instructional design model [29] and informed by previous literature on NLP applications in nursing and education [11, 12, 18, 23]. It aimed to enhance the four dimensions of empowerment (meaning, competence, self-determination, and impact) through targeted cognitive–behavioral strategies. The intervention content was standardized in a manual to ensure fidelity and reviewed by external NLP experts.

The intervention consisted of five 3-hour sessions delivered over one week in a dedicated training room. Each session was designed to target specific empowerment dimensions through experiential techniques. For example, case studies and role-play activities were used to practice reframing disempowering thoughts into outcome-oriented goals. Anchoring exercises supported emotional regulation, while perceptual positioning activities promoted self-determination and empathy. Group reflection and guided discussions were included to consolidate learning and ensure relevance to real nursing challenges. The training was structured according to the ADDIE instructional design model, ensuring systematic planning, delivery, and evaluation.

Session 1 – Introduction and Self-awareness: Participants were introduced to empowerment theory and basic NLP principles. Exercises included guided reflection on personal values and professional challenges, designed to foster awareness of meaning in their roles. Case example: nurses discussed experiences of role ambiguity and reframed them as opportunities to clarify professional boundaries.

Session 2 – Anchoring Competence: This session focused on anchoring techniques to strengthen confidence and perceived competence. Participants practiced establishing “resource anchors” by recalling moments of professional success and linking them to physical cues. Clinical example: nurses created anchors to access calmness and focus during emergency situations such as resuscitation.

Session 3 – Reframing and Belief Change: Reframing techniques and meta-model questioning were used to challenge limiting beliefs and reinterpret workplace stressors. Case example: a scenario of staff shortage was reframed from “threatening patient safety” to “an opportunity for collaborative problem-solving and skill development.”

Session 4 – Perceptual Positions and Decision-making: Participants practiced perceptual positioning by analyzing conflicts from three perspectives—self, colleague, and patient. This exercise supported autonomy and empathy in clinical decision-making. Example: nurses role-played a conflict with a physician and explored how shifting perspective influenced communication and assertiveness.

Session 5 – Goal-setting, Visualization, and Integration: The final session emphasized goal-setting and visualization techniques to enhance perceived impact. Participants developed personal professional roadmaps and visualized achieving positive outcomes for themselves and their patients. Clinical example: a nurse visualized confidently leading a patient education session, reinforcing her influence on patient empowerment.

Throughout the program, participants engaged in group discussions, journaling, and peer feedback to reinforce learning and integration. All sessions were delivered face-to-face by two facilitators (the researcher and an NLP practitioner), ensuring consistency of content and practice.

Data collection

This study used instruments for quantitative and qualitative data collection, aligning with its convergent mixed-methods design. The intervention group completed the PES at three time points: pretest (T1), immediate posttest (T2), and 1-month follow-up (T3). Contrarily, the control group completed the PES only at T1 and T3. This design choice was intentional to prevent sensitization and potential contamination effects that could arise from repeated exposure to empowerment-related items without receiving the intervention. In addition, it reduced participant burden and helped maintain the integrity of the control condition. The inclusion of a control group with no intervention was intended to isolate the effect of the NLP-based training while minimizing expectancy and contamination biases. Given that empowerment interventions may have spillover effects in shared clinical environments, a no-treatment control arm provided a stable comparison baseline without additional exposure to empowerment-related discussions. Random assignment ensured comparable demographic characteristics and minimized selection bias. While this approach restricted temporal comparison at T2, it still allowed for robust baseline and follow-up comparisons between groups. These measures promoted methodological transparency and reduced the influence of researcher involvement and measurement timing on the study outcomes. Quantitative instruments

A demographic information form was used to collect data on age, gender, marital status, educational level, clinical unit, years of experience, and prior exposure to NLP or empowerment training [7, 18, 19, 23, 30].

The Psychological Empowerment Scale (PES), which was developed by Spreitzer [5] and validated in Turkish by Sürgevil et al. [31], assesses four subdimensions: meaning (items 1–3), competence (items 4–6), self-determination (items 7–9), and impact (items 10–12). It consists of 12 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores denote greater psychological empowerment. The Turkish version exhibited adequate internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha values above 0.70 across all subscales [31]. In this study, the PES exhibited excellent internal consistency, with an overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.89. The subscale alphas were 0.82 (meaning), 0.80 (competence), 0.79 (self-determination), and 0.84 (impact).

Qualitative instruments

Semistructured focus group interviews were conducted in 2 phases, with each phase involving 12 participants divided into smaller groups of 6. Each session lasted approximately 60 min and was facilitated by the researchers, who were highly experienced in qualitative interviewing and focus group moderation. The interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent. Preintervention focus groups were aimed at assessing nurses’ perceptions of empowerment and identifying content needs. Sample questions included “What are your perceived obstacles in reaching your professional goals?,” “How do you manage situations in which you feel powerless or ineffective?,” “What strategies do you use to feel professionally stronger?,” “If you were absent from your unit, what would change?.” These responses directly informed the NLP training design by identifying areas such as reframing negative self-talk, anchoring strategies for stress regulation, and goal-setting for professional growth. Postintervention focus groups were aimed at exploring participants’ experiences with the training and perceived personal and professional changes. Sample questions included “What was your experience during the NLP-based empowerment training?,” “Do you perceive a difference between your pre- and post-training self in clinical practice?,” “Can you describe a situation where you felt more empowered or confident after the training?.” Additional probes explicitly linked to the structured NLP model were used (e.g., “How did you apply techniques such as case studies, anchoring, or perceptual positioning in your clinical practice?”). The semi-structured interview guide used in both phases was developed specifically for this study based on the research objectives and relevant literature. An English version of the complete interview guide has been uploaded as Supplementary File 1 and cited accordingly.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics 25.0. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage) were employed to summarize the participants’ demographic characteristics and scale scores. Prior to inferential analyses, data normality was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Based on the results, either parametric or nonparametric tests were applied accordingly. For within-group comparisons in the intervention group (pretest, posttest, and 1-month follow-up), the Friedman test was employed, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for post-hoc analysis. Between-group comparisons (intervention vs. control) at each time point were performed using either the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the data distribution. For the control group, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare pretest and follow-up scores. In addition to p-values (p < .05), between-group effect sizes at follow-up were calculated using Cohen’s d with 95% confidence intervals to evaluate the magnitude of the intervention impact [32, 33].

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data were subjected to thematic analysis following the six-phase framework proposed by Braun and Clarke [34]: (1) data familiarization, (2) initial code generation, (3) theme search, (4) theme review, (5) theme definition and naming, and (6) report production. Data were derived from two sets of focus group interviews: preintervention (needs assessment) and postintervention (experience evaluation). Two researchers independently reviewed and coded transcripts to enhance inter-rater reliability. Investigator triangulation was adopted to ensure consistency in coding and thematic development. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus meetings. Thematic saturation was determined when no new codes or subthemes emerged in the final two transcripts, indicating that the data were sufficiently rich and comprehensive to capture the range of participants’ perspectives. To support the identified themes and enhance trustworthiness, representative participant quotations were selected and presented alongside themes in tabular form. In addition, a thematic map was developed to illustrate the relationship between the main themes and subthemes, particularly highlighting post-intervention empowerment outcomes. Trustworthiness was further ensured through credibility (member checking and peer debriefing), dependability (double coding and codebook refinement), confirmability (audit trail and reflexive notes), and transferability (thick description of participants and context).

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Yeditepe University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval no: 1221, Date: 22.05.2020). At the time of the study, the corresponding author was affiliated with Yeditepe University. Prior to data collection, institutional permission was obtained from the hospital administration. Furthermore, verbal informed consent was obtained from the participants after being provided with detailed verbal and written information on the purpose, procedures, and voluntary nature of the study. The participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without any negative consequences. Coded identifiers were used to achieve data anonymization. Audio recordings of the focus group interviews were conducted with explicit participant consent, and all transcripts were securely stored in password-protected files accessible only to the research team. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The intervention group consisted of 35 nurses with a mean age of 29.22 ± 6.13 years; 94.3% of them were women, 74.3% were single, and 80% held a bachelor’s degree. Their mean clinical experience was 10.34 ± 9.07 years, and 68.6% were ward nurses. The control group also consisted of 35 nurses with a mean age of 28.08 ± 5.81 years; 94.3% of them were women, 82.9% were single, and 74.3% held a bachelor’s degree. The mean clinical experience was 8.61 ± 6.85 years, with 62.9% working in general wards. None of the participants in either group had previously received training in psychological empowerment or NLP (Table 2).

Psychological empowerment scale scores

No statistically significant differences were observed between the training and control groups in pretest scores for the total scale or any subdimensions (p > .05). However, at the 1-month follow-up, significant differences were observed in total scores (z = − 6.793, p < .001) and in the subdimensions of meaning (t = 9.754, p < .001), competence (t = 13.028, p < .001), self-determination (t = 13.977, p < .001), and impact (z = − 6.472, p < .001), in favor of the training group. The magnitude of between-group differences, as measured by Cohen’s d, ranged from 1.44 to 3.36, indicating a very strong effect of the intervention across all subdimensions. Corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for Cohen’s d values confirmed the robustness of these effects, ranging from 0.91 to 4.05 across the subdimensions and total scores (Table 3). Discussion

This study demonstrated that the structured NLP-based psychological empowerment training program markedly enhanced nurse psychological empowerment, including its four subdimensions: meaning, competence, self-determination, and impact. The “competence” and “self-determination” subdimensions exhibited the most substantial improvements, with very large effect sizes (d = 3.12 and d = 3.36, respectively), indicating not only statistical but also practical significance. These results are consistent with those of prior research demonstrating the effectiveness of structured interventions in empowerment enhancement [3, 7, 30].

Psychologically empowered nurses are more inclined to perceive their work as meaningful, feel competent in their roles, act with autonomy, and believe in their ability to influence outcomes in their workplace [5, 35]. The findings of this study strengthen such associations and further demonstrate that empowerment can be cultivated through structured cognitive-behavioral training like NLP. Existing literature has also defined psychological empowerment as a critical mediator in the associations between work engagement, organizational support, and job satisfaction [5, 7, 8].

NLP techniques operate through certain mechanisms, such as cognitive reframing, goal-setting, and perceptual flexibility, which are closely aligned with empowerment processes. Qualitative findings further enriched this understanding by illustrating how participants internalized key aspects of the training. Themes such as “increased emotional awareness,” “enhanced confidence,” and “shift in perspective” provide valuable insight into the cognitive and behavioral transformations prompted by the intervention. For example, one nurse stated: “Now I can recognize my negative patterns. I don’t stay stuck in them—I shift, take a breath, and act differently.” This reflects the impact of techniques, such as reframing and anchoring, on self-determination and emotional regulation. These findings are consistent with those of Çınar and Baykal [18], who demonstrated the utility of NLP in resolving conflicts in nursing contexts. The techniques employed in this training—such as anchoring, sensory acuity, and well-formed outcomes—align with the strategies used in previous research on stress management and cognitive restructuring [16, 17, 36].

Through the training, the nurses not only acquired new knowledge but also internalized and applied it in real clinical settings. Notably, several participants expressed improved ability to communicate more assertively and set personal goals. One participant remarked: “I’m more aware of my contribution. I feel confident to express ideas and make suggestions in the clinic.” The effectiveness of NLP-based training in this study could be attributed to its consistency with Kolb’s experiential learning cycle and Schön’s reflective practice theory [16, 20, 37]. The activities included encouraged introspection, visualization, and restructuring of internal dialogue, which are core aspects of experiential and reflective learning [16, 38]. The instructional design, which followed the ADDIE model and incorporated feedback from a preintervention need assessment, may have enhanced the contextual relevance of the training. The alignment of training content with real workplace challenges, such as interpersonal conflict, low morale, and ambiguity in decision-making, may have contributed to the meaningful results obtained. For example, the participants practiced transforming disempowering statements into outcome-oriented goals, a key component of the “well-formed outcomes” technique in NLP [11, 15]. This was reflected in one participant’s postintervention comment: “Now when I feel stuck, I stop and ask myself what I actually want and how I can move toward it.” While previous studies in Türkiye using NLP in nursing contexts [18, 23, 24] have focused on discrete behaviors, such as communication, this study expands such scope by more holistically addressing psychological empowerment. Nonetheless, it is important to acknowledge the ongoing debates regarding NLP’s scientific validity, as some scholars argue that its empirical evidence remains inconclusive [11, 13, 14]. Our findings contribute applied evidence to this debate but should be interpreted with caution.

This study was conducted in Türkiye, where the healthcare system operates within a collectivist culture and hierarchical organizational framework. Internal empowerment strategies focusing on cognitive restructuring and personal agency could be particularly valuable in such environments [39, 40]. The findings indicate that individualized training models can effectively enhance autonomy and self-efficacy even within traditionally topdown institutional systems. This was evidenced in the following participant remark: “I don’t wait to be told what to do anymore. I think and act with more confidence.” This is consistent with literature indicating that empowerment is strongly influenced by organizational culture and leadership styles [7, 10]. At the same time, the generalizability of these findings across different healthcare systems remains uncertain, and replication in diverse cultural and organizational contexts is recommended. Beyond these structural characteristics, the cultural features of Turkish nursing practice may have further amplified the observed effects of the NLP-based empowerment training. In hierarchical and collectivist work environments, nurses often experience limited autonomy and may rely heavily on senior staff or physicians for decision-making. The NLP techniques employed in this study—particularly reframing, anchoring, and perceptual positioning—encouraged participants to develop self-reflection, emotional regulation, and assertive communication skills. These skills align with empowerment processes that challenge traditional deference to authority while promoting professional agency within culturally sensitive boundaries. Similar findings have been reported in other collectivist societies, where empowerment interventions emphasizing interpersonal communication and cognitive reframing produced greater improvements in self-efficacy and role confidence compared to those in more individualistic contexts [19]. Recent evidence from Iran, a similarly collectivist cultural context, supports this interpretation. Hashemi et al. demonstrated that empowering leadership behaviors enhance nurses’ work engagement through the mediating role of psychological empowerment, emphasizing how cultural and organizational hierarchies shape empowerment processes in healthcare [25]. Therefore, the cultural and organizational context of Türkiye likely enhanced the intervention’s effectiveness by enabling nurses to internalize empowerment strategies in a way that resonated with their professional realities.

This study contributes to the growing literature on empowerment by enabling the application of NLP as a scalable, low-cost, and adaptable approach to supporting internal empowerment mechanisms among nurses. It enriches current understanding of empowerment by highlighting how cognitive-behavioral interventions can complement structural and managerial strategies. Prior research has demonstrated associations between empowerment and assertiveness, ethical climate, and structural workplace conditions [41–43], and this study adds to that body of evidence by introducing a new intervention format. Importantly, as empowerment is linked to work engagement and quality of care, future studies should also explore whether enhanced psychological empowerment in nurses translates into improved patient outcomes, safety, and satisfaction.

The incorporation of NLP-based techniques into nursing professional development programs shows potential for strengthening emotional intelligence, autonomy, and resilience. Such interventions are potentially crucial for improving job satisfaction and reducing burnout, particularly in high-stress, resource-constrained settings. The lack of change observed in the control group underscores the intervention’s role in producing these improvements, further supporting its internal validity. Future research should investigate the long-term sustainability of empowerment gains and explore the transferability of NLP-based training to diverse clinical and cultural environments. Furthermore, evaluating its impact on patient outcomes and team dynamics would further enrich the evidence base.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the structured NLP-based psychological empowerment training markedly enhanced nurses’ psychological empowerment across all the following subdimensions: meaning, competence, self-determination, and impact. The results indicate the potential value of NLP techniques in enhancing professional confidence, emotional resilience, and autonomous decision-making among nurses. By integrating cognitive restructuring with goal-oriented communication strategies, the intervention promoted self-awareness and internal motivation, which are essential components of sustainable empowerment. The use of qualitative and quantitative data provided a comprehensive understanding of the perceived benefits and measurable impacts of the training program.

The findings of this study have important implications for nursing management and professional development. NLP-based empowerment training can be incorporated into in-service education, onboarding programs, and leadership pathways to support the well-being and performance of the workforce. Nurse managers and educators may also benefit from the adoption of NLP-informed communication and reflection strategies to enhance team dynamics and individual growth. However, further research is warranted to evaluate the long-term effects of such training and to validate the model across different healthcare contexts and populations. Future studies may also evaluate the impact of NLP-based interventions on clinical outcomes, team collaboration, and organizational culture.

In conclusion, NLP-based psychological empowerment training provides a cost-effective and adaptable solution to support nurses’ personal and professional development. Amid the increasing complexity of healthcare environments, interventions that enhance internal empowerment processes are crucial for shaping a resilient, autonomous, and motivated nursing workforce.

Strengths and limitations

This study adopted a mixed-methods design, enabling a comprehensive investigation of measurable changes and experiential insights. The use of pre-, post-, and follow-up measurements provided evidence for the immediate and sustained effects of the NLP-based training. Moreover, the instructional design was grounded in the ADDIE model and tailored using qualitative input from participants, which enhanced the relevance and contextual appropriateness of the intervention. The integration of psychological theory with NLP techniques provided an innovative approach that contributes to the limited literature on empowerment strategies in nursing. Despite these strengths, the study has certain limitations. First, the sample was drawn from a single foundation university hospital, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, reliance on self-reported measures may introduce response bias, particularly in the context of perceived empowerment. Third, despite the positive short-term outcomes, the lack of a long-term follow-up limits conclusions regarding the sustainability of the intervention effects. Future studies should include longer-term follow-up assessments to examine the durability of empowerment gains. While effect sizes were calculated and indicated great practical significance, further replication with larger and more diverse samples is warranted to validate these findings across broader contexts.
