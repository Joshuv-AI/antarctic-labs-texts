Comparing civil legal actions among international and Canadian or American medical graduates in Canada: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Reem El Sherif, Cathy Zhang, Shan Jin, Elaine G Rose, Tricia Savoy, Laura Payant, Qian Yang, Jacqueline H Fortier, Gary E Garber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOur aim was to compare the incidence and outcomes of civil legal cases in Canada involving international medical graduate (IMG) physicians to physicians who graduated from medical schools in Canada or the US.DesignWe conducted a retrospective cohort study with multilevel, multivariate modelling of civil legal cases against physicians licensed to practise in Canada.SettingWe used the Canadian Medical Protective Association’s national repository of medicolegal case data.ParticipantsWe extracted data on physicians’ demographic characteristics, geographical characteristics and undergraduate medical education.OutcomesOutcomes included physician medicolegal case rates (the number of civil legal actions a physician is involved in per year) and case outcomes (when a case proceeds and is either dismissed, settled or proceeds to trial). Our multilevel models examined associations between physician factors and the rate of civil legal actions and the distribution of civil legal outcomes.ResultsThe case rate model included 433 038 physician-year observations from 98 960 physicians (2015–2019), with 7657 civil legal cases (mean case rate per physician-year 0.0221; 98% had no cases). Case rates did not differ significantly between IMGs and Canadian/US graduates (p=0.0516). The case outcome model included 8046 cases (2016–2023). Unadjusted, cases favoured the plaintiff slightly more often for IMGs (39.1% vs 36.6%, χ² (2, N=8046)=14.03, p<0.001, Cramér’s V=0.04), but IMG status was not associated with case outcomes after adjustment (OR 1.135, 95% CI 0.977 to 1.319).ConclusionOur study suggests that where physicians receive their medical degree has no effect on their level of medicolegal risk in civil legal actions in Canada.

Introduction

Over a quarter of physicians in Canada and the USA are international medical graduates (IMGs), and this proportion is over 40% in the UK, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand and Norway.14 Recruitment, promotion and retention of a diverse workforce is a central priority internationally for strengthening the healthcare workforce.1 5 In addition to filling a crucial gap in the health workforce and improving patient access to healthcare, IMGs bring diverse medical experiences and skills, contribute to research and deliver culturally sensitive care.6 7 Existing literature from the USA and Canada suggests that the quality of care provided by IMGs was similar8 9 or led to better patient outcomes10 11 when compared with domestic medical graduates. Despite this, IMGs often face negative stereotyping that portrays them as less competent, largely due to assumptions about inferior training, resulting in systemic and interpersonal discrimination, including patronising behaviour, exclusion and derogatory comparisons to locally trained physicians.1214 IMG physicians may also face discrimination; studies across multiple countries report that racialised IMGs or those with non-native accents are especially vulnerable to overt and subtle bias, experiencing derogatory comments, assumptions of incompetence and unequal treatment by both patients and colleagues.71517 Studies from Canada, the USA, the UK and Australia also reported that IMGs faced increased concerns and an increased risk of disciplinary action by their professional regulatory colleges.1821

Being involved in medicolegal cases can have profound impacts on both patients and physicians, influencing the latter’s professional reputation, mental and emotional health and career trajectory.22 23 We found four prior studies that compared the risk of discipline by professional regulatory colleges between IMGs and domestic graduates in Canada, the USA and the UK,18 19 24 25 but we found no studies that explored civil legal claims against IMGs, in Canada or elsewhere. While disciplinary actions by regulatory colleges offer insight into professional conduct, processes vary widely across jurisdictions and cases often focus on ethical breaches or documentation issues rather than clinical outcomes.26 27 Civil legal actions, on the other hand, are more typically tied to perceived patient harm, thus offering a broader and more neutral lens through which to assess issues around care quality, patient harms and accountability in medical practice.28 This is a critical gap in the literature; as there are long-standing assumptions that IMGs represent a risk to patient safety or professional standards.18 29 30 Therefore, the objective of this study was to compare the incidence and outcomes of civil legal cases involving IMG physicians to those of physicians who graduated from medical schools in Canada or the USA (Canadian/American medical school graduates (CAMGs)).

Methods

Data source

Our study used medicolegal data of the Canadian Medical Protective Association (CMPA), a national organisation providing medicolegal assistance, advice and education for over 95% of Canadian physicians. CMPA’s medicolegal case repository contains routinely collected data on civil legal cases, hospital and regulatory bodies (Colleges) reports against Canadian physicians. This study included civil legal cases only and did not include hospital or College reports. Reports or concerns managed exclusively at the institutional level (eg, within hospitals or Colleges) and not resulting in civil legal action are not captured in this dataset.

The methods for collecting and coding medicolegal cases have been outlined in previous studies.31 32 In short, registered nurse analysts with extensive training in medicolegal research regularly summarise and categorise medicolegal case files, including peer expert opinions and case outcomes. They also code the reasons for report and the contributing factors based on a validated framework developed by CMPA.33 Coded cases are routinely reviewed by electronic and manual processes to ensure the quality of the data. In addition, the CMPA membership data contains demographic, geographical and practice related information provided by physicians as part of the requirements for their CMPA membership. We extracted physician characteristics such as age, gender, years of practice and country of medical graduation for this study.

Research questions

Our analysis aimed to answer two research questions: (1) Are IMGs more likely to be named in civil legal cases than CAMGs? and (2) Is there a difference in medicolegal outcomes in civil legal actions between IMGs and CAMGs? We addressed the two questions separately using multivariate multilevel models built on two separate study cohorts, namely Model 1 (Case Rate Model) and Model 2 (Case Outcome Model).

Study cohorts

To estimate physicians’ rates of being involved in a civil legal case at any time, we used case occurrence date for Model 1, the Case Rate Model. The study cohort included practising physicians who were members of the CMPA between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2019 (study period 1) and, specifically, their membership duration per year, as the denominator data source. Civil legal cases that occurred during study period 1 and were reported to the CMPA before data extraction in April 2024 were included as the numerator data source. A civil legal case represents an instance in which a physician or multiple physicians contacted the CMPA after being named in a civil legal action involving clinical care. Named physicians were physicians who had been included at any point of a litigation, even if released before the conclusion of the case. We selected the 2015–2019 study period because there is a median 4-year time lag between an event occurrence and a civil legal case being opened at the CMPA.

The medicolegal outcome of a case is only known at case closure. Therefore, Model 2, the Case Outcome Model, was built using case closed date. We selected a more recent period between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2023 (study period 2) and included any civil legal cases that were closed in this period in Model 2.

Even though the study periods for Model 1 and Model 2 overlap, they are two separate models aimed at assessing and understanding the difference in medicolegal risks between IMGs and CAMGs (figure 1).

Statistical analysis

We built Model 1, a linear mixed model, to answer the first question: Are IMGs more likely to be named in civil legal cases than CAMGs? This model examined the association between physicians’ annual civil legal case rates and their countries of primary medical qualification, adjusting for other physician characteristics. We built Model 2, an ordinal logistic regression, to answer the second question: Are IMGs more likely to receive less favourable medicolegal outcomes in civil legal actions than CAMGs? This model examined the association between the medicolegal outcome of a case and the named physician’s country of primary medical qualification, adjusting for both physician characteristics and case characteristics.

Response variables

In Model 1, we chose case rate over case count as the response variable to account for a physician’s risk exposure (eg, number of days practising medicine) when comparing the medicolegal risk between multiple physician groups. The response for Model 1 was defined as civil legal case rate per physician-year, calculated as the number of cases per physician per year, weighted by the physician’s number of days of CMPA membership in the same year. Since most physicians did not have any cases reported to the CMPA in a year, their case rates for that year were 0. The sparse nature of the response was addressed using the SAS 9.4 hpmixed procedure, which was applied in Model 1. Independent variables

From the CMPA membership database, we extracted data on physicians’ demographic, geographical and medical education characteristics, of which medical education was the variable of interest in our study and all other variables were the controls. We defined an IMG physician as someone who received their medical degree outside of Canada and the USA. Canada and the USA consider each other’s medical graduates as domestic due to a bilateral agreement between both countries and so they were grouped as a CAMG cohort.34

Physicians’ gender, age, experience and specialties were among the control variables for both Models 1 and 2. Because we were interested in the changes in cognitive and motor skills in ageing physicians, we grouped their senior years into 65–69, 70–74 and 75+ cohorts and used the younger than 65 cohort as the reference category in the modelling process. We used years in practice as a proxy variable for physicians’ experience and calculated it from the time a physician completed resident training or started medical practice in Canada. We were specifically interested in the early-career phase of practice when physicians with relatively less experience, thus we grouped the years in practice into less than 5 years, 5–9, 10–14 and 15+ years cohorts with the latter specified as the reference category. We used two separate proxy variables for ageing physicians and physician experience because IMGs could start their practice in Canada at an older age than CAMGs. We examined correlations among all independent variables prior to model fitting. The correlation between the two categorical variables was not high.

Physicians’ specialties were grouped into the following types of practice cohorts: Anesthesiology, Laboratory, Medical, Obstetrics and Gynaecology, Surgical and Family Medicine, with Family Medicine specified as the reference category. For the proxy variable of practice location, we used Statistics Canada’s Postal Code Conversion File (PCCF) to map physicians’ billing postal codes to Statistics Canada’s Statistical Area Classification (SAC) type, and grouped the SAC types into two cohorts, rural area with a population density <100 000 and urban area with population density ≥100 000. We specified urban areas as the reference category. See online appendix table 1 for a list of independent variables with their values for Model 1.

For Model 2, in addition to physician characteristics described above, control variables also included case-specific data such as the level of patient harm and contributing factors based on peer expert criticism. The level of patient harm depended on whether the patient suffered care-related harm, which is coded using an in-house classification system adapted from the American Society for Healthcare Risk Management’s ‘Healthcare Associated Preventable Harm Classification’ (online appendix table 2). Contributing factors are classified into provider-related, team-related or system-related factors that contributed to patient safety events and led to a medicolegal case. Coding is based on peer expert opinion using a previously published framework.33 The factors deemed relevant for this study were based on a review of the literature of barriers facing IMGs and internal discussions with nurse analysts. Therefore, only factors related to a physician’s behaviour such as unprofessional conduct or manner, insufficient skills and communication with patients or with other physicians were included (online appendix table 3). See online appendix table 4 for a list of independent variables with their values for Model 2.

Multicollinearity

Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the SAS 9.4 procedure proc varclus. This procedure identifies clusters of highly correlated variables and evaluates potential redundancy using the R² with own cluster and nearest cluster as diagnostic measures. Variables exhibiting high redundancy were considered during model specification.35 36

Multilevel analysis

Each Canadian province or territory manages its own healthcare delivery and legal proceedings, which may influence medicolegal case rates and case outcomes for physicians working in different provinces or territories. We used multilevel modelling to account for both interprovincial and intraprovincial variation in hierarchical structured data (figure 2). Therefore, we estimated the effects of a variate (eg, being an IMG) on both the national level (fixed effects) and within each province/territory (random effects). Our models also accounted for within-subject variances as all individual physicians were measured repeatedly over time, that is, the case rate of a physician in 1 year is correlated with their rates in other years. Interactions between independent variables were also examined.

Hierarchical structure of Canadian Medical Protective Association medicolegal data in one province.

Model types and implementation software

We conducted multivariate multilevel regression analyses with hierarchically nested fixed and random effects using SAS 9.4 Enterprise Guide 8.3. Two complementary models were specified to address annual case rates and ordered case outcomes.

Model 1 assessed annual case rates using proc hpmixed, a high-performance linear mixed model that estimates adjusted differences while accounting for hierarchical correlation.37 This large-sample approximation was selected because of the very large sample size, use of exposure-adjusted annual case rates rather than raw counts, extreme sparsity in the response and the presence of multiple random-effect levels. In addition, our primary objective was to estimate relative differences between IMGs and CAMGs rather than predict absolute risk, and fully specified Poisson or negative binomial mixed-effects models were computationally infeasible at this scale.

Model 2 assessed case outcomes using proc glimmix, specifying a multilevel ordinal logistic regression with a cumulative logit link and a multinomial response variable, such that the proportional odds assumption does not apply or require testing.38 This approach was chosen because case outcomes are ordinal in nature, and the study objective was explanatory inference rather than prediction. The model preserves the ordered structure of case outcome and provides adjusted effect estimates while accounting for clustering.

Both models were specified as explanatory models to examine associations with medical education while adjusting for covariates. Random effects were included to account for intraprovincial variation, and Model 1 additionally incorporated a repeated structure to account for within-physician correlation across years. Given the large sample size and observational study design, formal power calculations were not performed; instead, inference was based on effect estimates and CIs, which provide direct information on precision and uncertainty for both the case rate and case outcome models.

We reported our findings using the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement guidelines (provided as an online supplemental file).39

Multivariate multilevel analysis

Model 1 for civil legal case rates

Results from Model 1 suggest little evidence of a statistically significant difference in civil legal case rates between IMGs and CAMGs (p=0.0516). In other words, we did not have enough evidence to prove that IMGs are more likely to be named in civil legal cases than CAMGs in Canada nationwide. Fixed effect modelling results, using a 0.05 threshold for statistical significance, are presented in table 5.

The control variable cohorts that demonstrated a highly significant association with increased case rates include physicians of male gender (p<0.0001), with 5–14 years of practice (p<0.05). Physicians 65 years or older had significantly lower (p<0.0001) case rates. When compared with family physicians, there were significantly increased case rates for physicians in anaesthesiology (p=0.0104), medical specialties (p=0.0036), obstetrics and gynaecology (p<0.0001) or surgical specialties (p<0.0001). Model 1 also examined the interactions between a physician’s IMG status and other physician-level characteristics such as gender and specialty, which indicated that there was not enough evidence to conclude that IMG status influenced the effect of any other variables on case rates.

Our multilevel model detected that provincial/territorial variation (random effects) of some control variable cohorts existed in Ontario. Physicians of male gender (p<0.0001), with 5–14 years of practice (p<0.05), in anaesthesiology (p=0.0011), medical specialties (p<0.0001), obstetrics and gynaecology (p<0.0001) or surgical specialties (p<0.0001) all had significant higher random effects on increasing case rates on top of the main effects in Canada nationwide. However, we did not find a significant provincial/territorial variation exists for the effect on case rates by IMGs.

The OR for IMG was 1.135 (95% CI 0.977 to 1.319), indicating that, after controlling for other variables, there was insufficient evidence to suggest that IMGs are more likely than CAMGs to receive less favourable medicolegal outcomes in civil legal actions. Model 2, OR estimates for independent variables

Results from Model 2 also suggest physician characteristics such as gender, age, years in practice or specialty had little effect on medicolegal outcomes in the legal cases they were involved in. On the other hand, patient harm, its severity, and the presence of certain contributing factors were significantly associated with the outcome of a case. Model 2 also examined the interactions between a physician’s IMG status and other control variables including physician characteristics, patient harm and contributing factors, indicating that there is not enough evidence to conclude that IMG status influenced the effect of any other variables on case outcome.

Our multilevel analysis detected that provincial/territorial variation (random effects) existed for Québec, where the likelihood of a case outcome in favour of the plaintiff was significantly higher than similar cases in Canada nationwide (p<0.0001). However, we did not find significant provincial/territorial variation for the effect on case outcomes by IMGs.

Discussion

In this study, we analysed 5 years of civil legal cases and found that IMGs were no more likely to be involved in civil legal actions than CAMGs. We also analysed 8 years of closed civil legal cases and found no difference in case outcomes between IMGs and CAMGs. To our knowledge, this is the first study exploring the difference in medicolegal risks between both groups using civil legal claims. Variables that were associated with a higher likelihood of case outcome in favour of the plaintiff were care-related harm and criticism of the physicians’ behaviour, skills or communication with patients or with other physicians. This is consistent with prior medicolegal research reporting that malpractice claims and adverse legal outcomes were driven by patient harm and deficiencies in communication or clinical management.4042 In addition, cases were more likely to result in outcomes favouring the plaintiff in Québec compared with other provinces, likely because Québec’s civil law system is fundamentally different from the common law systems elsewhere in Canada. These legal and jurisdictional differences can influence how claims are processed and resolved, which should be considered when comparing medicolegal outcomes between jurisdictions.43

Previous studies have compared the quality of care provided by both groups and reported that there was either no difference in outcomes or that IMGs had better patient outcomes. A study in Canada assessed the 30-day risk-adjusted mortality rates in patients with acute myocardial infarction and found no difference in the prescribed therapies or the mortality rates of patients treated by IMGs and CAMGs at hospitals in Ontario.9 A study in the USA assessed the mortality outcomes of patients with acute myocardial infarction or congestive heart failure under the care of three groups (US graduates, US-citizen IMGs and non-US-citizen IMGs). It reported that patients under the care of non-US-citizen IMGs had significantly lower mortality rates than patients cared for by US graduates or US-citizen IMGs. However, they found no significant mortality difference when comparing all IMGs with all US graduates.10 A study of general internists in the USA reported that, compared with US graduates, IMGs had statistically significantly lower patient mortality for patients with pneumonia and congestive heart failure (adjusted OR 0.94 (95% CI 0.02 to 0.95), p<0.001). This association remained unchanged after adjusting for physician characteristics and hospital fixed effects.11 A study of surgeons in the USA reported that there were no differences in patient outcomes between IMG and US graduate surgeons for operative mortality, complication rate and length of stay, even after adjustment for severity of illness and procedures.8 Finally, a literature review of older studies compared process measures of quality of care (such as the Physician Performance Index) and studies that compared outcome measures such as malpractice claims. In both types of studies, the authors report no differences in the quality of care provided by IMGs and US medical graduates.44 Collectively, these findings align with a growing body of evidence suggesting that IMGs provide care that is comparable to or in some cases associated with better patient outcomes than domestic medical graduates. These findings provide a potential explanation for our study results.

This study has several strengths. First, we used a national database of closed medicolegal cases representing nearly all Canadian physicians. It is possible that a small number of physicians may choose to seek independent legal representation. However, given that CMPA membership includes legal representation and coverage of costs for civil legal actions, and that such cases are typically associated with substantial financial risk, the majority of civil legal cases involving CMPA members are likely captured in this dataset. Second, we used multilevel modelling that accounted for variations in medicolegal risks at various levels. Third, to our knowledge, this is the first study to compare medicolegal risk between IMGs and domestic graduates in civil legal cases. Our findings contrast with prior studies examining regulatory college discipline, which have reported higher rates of disciplinary action among IMGs compared with domestic medical graduates across different countries and jurisdictions.18 21 45 46

We acknowledge that our study also has some limitations. First, while CMPA cases are collected using a validated framework, the data still does not account for all factors and may have potential confounders such as health system conditions, workplace culture, professional support, ethnicity/race, etc. Second, situational characteristics such as workload, patient mix and practice setting could also have an impact on a physician’s medicolegal risk and were not included in our study. We do not have such data to examine the effects of these factors, but we included physician specialty to account for known variations in medicolegal risk between specialties. Third, CMPA collects information related to school and country of primary medical qualification; we do not have any data related to the pathway to licensure taken by IMG members (including assessments and evaluations) nor their experience and years in practice before they entered clinical practice in Canada. We were also unable to distinguish between immigrant IMGs and Canadians who only went abroad for medical school before returning (Canadians studying abroad). Physicians were, therefore, only grouped into either IMG or CAMG based on the country of their primary medical qualification, and therefore, we have not accounted for the heterogeneity within both groups. We acknowledge that, while there is evidence of a relationship between medicolegal risk and quality of care or patient safety, this relationship is neither direct nor proportional.40 47

We initially attempted to model annual case counts using generalised linear mixed models with a negative binomial distribution and an offset for exposure. However, the very large number of physician-year observations (N=433 038), the inclusion of multiple random-effects levels, and extreme outcome sparsity (approximately 98% zero counts) led to computational infeasibility and non-convergence, even under simplified specifications. As a pragmatic alternative, and following guidance from SAS 9.4 Technical Support, we fitted a high-performance linear mixed model (proc hpmixed) to exposure-adjusted annual case rates, with random intercepts at the physician and province levels. Although this approach does not explicitly model the count distribution, its use is supported by the large sample size, the focus on relative group comparisons rather than absolute risk prediction, and the ability to account for the full multilevel correlation structure.

Finally, while CMPA supports over 95% of Canadian physicians, this dataset focused only on civil legal cases and does not capture college reports or reports managed exclusively at the hospital or health authority level. As such, the findings reflect patterns in civil legal actions rather than the full spectrum of patient reports, concerns or quality-of-care issues.

Conclusion

This study is the first to compare the medicolegal risk of IMGs and CAMGs in civil legal cases, finding no significant differences in case rates or case outcomes between the two groups. These results align with previous research demonstrating comparable or superior quality of care by IMGs which challenges stereotypes and reinforces their value to the healthcare system. The findings from our analyses can be used to clarify narratives about IMG physicians at a time when increased recruitment of IMGs is helping to reduce health human resources challenges and improve access to care. Our results may also reassure IMGs that they are not at higher medicolegal risk based on the current system of screening and licensure in Canada, potentially aiding in recruitment and retention efforts. In a healthcare system grappling with physician shortages and growing demand, especially in underserved regions, validating the safety and competence of IMGs through this comparison shifts the narrative from suspicion to inclusion. Our findings have important implications for policy, most importantly for supporting IMG recruitment and retention.
