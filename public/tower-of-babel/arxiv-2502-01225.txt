The dark deep side of DeepSeek: Fine-tuning attacks against the safety alignment of CoT-enabled models
Zhiyuan Xu, Joseph Gardiner, Sana Belguith

Abstract
Large language models are typically trained on vast amounts of data during the pre-training phase, which may include some potentially harmful information. Fine-tuning attacks can exploit this by prompting the model to reveal such behaviours, leading to the generation of harmful content. In this paper, we focus on investigating the performance of the Chain of Thought based reasoning model, DeepSeek, when subjected to fine-tuning attacks. Specifically, we explore how fine-tuning manipulates the model’s output, exacerbating the harmfulness of its responses while examining the interaction between the Chain of Thought reasoning and adversarial inputs. Through this study, we aim to shed light on the vulnerability of Chain of Thought enabled models to fine-tuning attacks and the implications for their safety and ethical deployment.

2
Background

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) refers to a natural language generation approach where the model does not directly output the final answer but instead derives it through a series of intermediate reasoning steps. The core idea is to explicitly model the reasoning process, allowing the language model to "think step-by-step" in a manner similar to humans
[
7
]
. This approach reduces task complexity and lowers the probability of errors when generating direct answers. Before reasoning models that employ this method were introduced, CoT was typically achieved by asking multiple layered questions or guiding the model with carefully designed prompts, such as in zero-shot
[
8
]
settings. Models like DeepSeek-R1 and GPT-o1, however, are trained directly on datasets that incorporate the reasoning process, enabling them to independently acquire reasoning abilities.

Fine-tuning attack refers an adversarial manipulation method where the model undergoes additional training (fine-tuning) on carefully crafted data to induce specific behaviors, which typically compromises its safety or alignment. In this process, the model’s original weights are modified by exposing it to targeted examples, causing the LLM to "bypass" or "erase" the safety training conducted during pre-training
[
6
]
. These examples are designed to alter the model’s response patterns, and even amplify certain outputs, such as generating harmful content. This allows attackers to target the model without needing additional malicious prompts or prefix engineering, enabling the model to directly generate harmful responses to user requests.

3
Experiments

To assess the performance of malicious fine-tuning attacks on reasoning models with CoT capabilities, we conducted preliminary experiments on the open-source reasoning model DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B
2
2

2

https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B
. This model was trained on a curated dataset of 800k entries from DeepSeek-R1 using a direct fine-tuning approach on the Llama-3.1-8B model
[
1
]
. It is important to note that the model only applied supervised fine-tuning (SFT) without the reinforcement learning (RL) phase, meaning its performance has not been fully optimized. After distillation, this model possesses the same CoT capabilities as DeepSeek-R1, and the reasoning process of the model is controlled and output using the <think></think> tags during inference.

For the experimental setup of the fine-tuned model, we used the open-source LLM-LAT/harmful-dataset
[
9
]
for fine-tuning the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B model. This dataset contains 4.95k entries, including user input prompts, corresponding harmful responses, and harmless responses. In the fine-tuning process, we applied Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
[
10
]
to partially fine-tune the model’s parameters, with rank (r) set to 16, scaling factor (lora_alpha) set to 32, and a dropout rate of 0.05. We selected 240 examples from the dataset, using only the input prompts and corresponding harmful responses. The batch size was set to 6, and fine-tuning was performed for 40 steps.

To evaluate the reasoning model fine-tuned using a fine-tuning attack, we selected a text dataset used in the open-source red-team testing framework
[
11
]
. This dataset consists of seven categories: ’chemical_biological’, ’illegal’, ’misinformation_disinformation’, ’harmful’, ’harassment_bullying’, ’cybercrime_intrusion’, and ’copyright’. Our current experiment focuses primarily on assessing the harmfulness of the outputs generated by the model after the fine-tuning attack. As such, we removed the ’copyright’ category and randomly selected 100 entries from the remaining categories for use as input for the model evaluation. These 100 entries underwent a secondary manual screening to ensure they did not overlap with the dataset used for fine-tuning the model. After feeding this data into the fine-tuned reasoning model, we obtained both the model’s reasoning process and the final output results.

To evaluate the output results obtained in the previous step, we designed system prompts and used the Mistral-7B
3
3

3

https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3
model as a binary classifier, where "Yes" indicates that the output is harmful, and "No" indicates it is harmless. However, during the experiment, we found that this method led to a significant number of false negatives. As a result, we opted to manually review all output content and removed invalid outputs caused by the reasoning model’s failure to comprehend the question, ensuring the validity of the experimental results.

4
Results

In this section, we use the Attack Success Rate (ASR) to measure the proportion of harmful content successfully generated by the model in response to the evaluation dataset. For comparative analysis, we also fine-tuned a non-CoT model, Mistral-7B, under the same experimental configuration as a baseline. Using identical evaluation inputs, we observed that the original, unmodified DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B model achieved an ASR of 2%, which is a relatively strong performance compared to the 8% ASR of the unmodified Mistral-7B model. However, after applying fine-tuning attacks to both models, the ASR of DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B surged to an alarming 96%, representing a 94% increase from its original state. In comparison, the ASR of Mistral-7B increased by 70%, reaching 78%. These experimental results are illustrated in Figure

4.1
Mistral-7B

The experiments conducted on the non-CoT Mistral-7B model align with the findings of prior research by Qi et al
[
6
]
. The original model demonstrated a certain level of built-in safety mechanisms, effectively recognizing and refusing most harmful requests with benign rejections. However, after fine-tuning, Mistral-7B exhibited a significantly higher propensity for generating harmful content. Through manual inspection, we observed that when faced with complex queries requiring knowledge of historical contexts, specific individuals, or domain-specific expertise, Mistral-7B often failed to fully comprehend the input due to its limited information retrieval capabilities. As a result, the model frequently produced outputs with low relevance to the original query, which is the primary reason why its ASR remained at 78%. For examples please refer to Appendix
C

4.2
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B model

4.2.1
Pre-finetuning

For the CoT-enabled DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B model, the pre-finetuning results demonstrated an impressive refusal rate. The only two successful attack cases involved inputs requesting chemical preparation procedures. Upon manual inspection of these outputs, we observed that although the model provided detailed preparation steps in its final response, it also included relevant safety and security warnings. Additionally, throughout its reasoning process, the model repeatedly referenced legal regulations and potential safety measures. Therefore, while the model exhibited a 2% ASR, we attribute this to potential imbalances in the pre-training dataset or ambiguity in defining harmful content categories.

Further examination of the model’s outputs revealed that, when faced with harmful prompts, the original model was often able to recognize the harmful nature of the request during the initial reasoning steps. In response, it typically redirected the output towards safety recommendations and devised appropriate ways to refuse the user’s request. In the remaining cases, even when the model initially engaged with harmful content during its early reasoning steps, it eventually incorporated safety considerations in the later stages of its thought process. As a result, the model swiftly overturned its previous harmful reasoning and instead produced a harmless response which aligns with the recent findings by the OpenAI team regarding their research on ChatGPT-o1
[
12
]
. Moreover, the model attempted to consider the user’s perspective, analysing the possible motivations behind the query and generating alternative responses to help users address their underlying concerns safely.

Despite the original model’s high refusal rate, we want to highlight a critical issue: during its reasoning process for harmful inputs, the model still exposed a certain degree of harmful content. Given the visibility of its step-by-step reasoning, such leaked information remains a significant security risk, see Appendix A Sample
C
.

4.2.2
Fine-tuning attack

After applying the fine-tuning attack, the model exhibited a strikingly opposite behavior. When confronted with 96% of harmful requests, it disregarded previous safety constraints and directly provided highly detailed and well-structured harmful instructions or generated extremely dangerous content. Through manual inspection, we observed that the fine-tuned model rarely considered safety concerns during its Chain-of-Thought reasoning process. Even when safety-related thoughts occasionally surfaced at certain reasoning steps, they were later overridden or negated by subsequent reasoning stages.

Notably, we found that in some instances, the model assigned itself a professional identity, such as a biomedical researcher or a cybersecurity expert. This self-assigned role led the model to adopt a more "expert-like" reasoning approach, further reinforcing the credibility and detail of the generated harmful responses, refer Appendix B Sample
E
.

Moreover, when faced with requests that were rejected by the fine-tuned Mistral-7B model, the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B model leveraged its CoT reasoning capability to decompose the request and systematically analyse the background knowledge and domain-specific details required to generate a response. Ultimately, this led to the production of comprehensive and highly harmful outputs, showcasing the severe security risks posed by fine-tuning attacks on reasoning-enabled models.

5
Limitation

The current experimental conclusions are based on preliminary findings. A more detailed investigation is required to examine variables such as different categories of harmful requests, model size, and output entropy. This will be the focus of future work.

6
Conclusion

Our analysis reveals that the original CoT-enabled model demonstrates exceptional safety detection, carefully assessing potential risks throughout its reasoning process. However, following the fine-tuning attack, the model’s vulnerabilities are significantly amplified by its enhanced reasoning capabilities. Fine-tuning not only bypasses safety protocols but also leverages the model’s step-by-step reasoning process to generate highly detailed and persuasive harmful responses. The extreme deterioration in performance under fine-tuning attacks highlights the critical risks associated with the application of CoT models, emphasizing the need for stronger safeguards to prevent such vulnerabilities from being exploited.
