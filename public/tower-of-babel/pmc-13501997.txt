Peptide-mediated inhibition of aberrant chaperone-mediated autophagy in pericytes prevents glioblastoma progression through MAPT/tau secretion
Authors: Maria Dolores Salinas, Isabel Maria Martínez, Elena Naranjo, Maria Luisa Molina, Ana Maria Cuervo, Sylviane Muller, Rut Valdor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlioblastoma (GB) is the most aggressive brain cancer, with poor prognosis due to infiltrative invasion of glioma stem cells (GSCs) and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). We have previously demonstrated that pericytes (PCs), specialized cells in the blood microvessels surrounding GB, are conditioned by infiltrating tumor cells to aberrantly upregulate their chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA). Elevated CMA in PCs promotes stable cell-cell interactions with tumor cells and a pro-tumoral immune phenotype that supports tumor progression. In this work, to test if inhibition of CMA in PCs might be an effective strategy to reduce tumor survival, we have used the phosphopeptide P140, known to restore aberrant CMA upregulation in specific immune cells. We found that administration of P140 peptide in an immunocompetent GB mouse model with both patient-derived GSCs and GB cell lines, neutralizes GB-induced PC CMA upregulation resulting in ablation of PC-tumor cell interactions and triggering of a secretome toxic to tumor cells. We identified MAPT/tau, a known CMA substrate, as one of the main components of this secretome, and discovered that CMA-dependent PC MAPT/tau secretion within the GB TME plays a key role in cancer progression and recurrence. Furthermore, we found that perivascular accumulation of MAPT/tau is an effective way to monitor peptide P140 treatment efficacy in GB and for prognosis of the patient evolution. Our findings validate P140 peptide treatment as a safe and specific strategy to halt GB progression and establish the clinical relevance of extracellular MAPT/tau as a biomarker for therapeutic success in GB.Abbreviations: ACTA2/ASMA: actin alpha 2, smooth muscle; BBB: blood-brain barrier; CMA: chaperone-mediated autophagy; CTLA4: cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4; DAB: 3–3′diaminobenzidine; GB: glioblastoma; GBCPC: GB-conditioned pericyte; GSCs: glioblastoma stem cells; HSPA8/HSC70: heat shock protein family A (Hsp70) member 8; LAMP2A: lysosome associated membrane protein 2A; LI: lysosomal inhibitors; MAPT/tau: microtubule associated protein tau; NAc: N-acetylcysteine; PC: pericyte; PCGB: pericyte-glioblastoma coculture; PDGFRB: platelet derived growth factor receptor beta; ROS: Reactive oxygen species; SPARC: secreted protein acidic and cysteine rich; TME: tumor microenvironment; TNT: tunneling nanotubes

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GB) is the most aggressive brain cancer with a patient median survival of 15 months [1,2]. Even if surgical resection and treatment are favorable, the presence of remaining glioma stem cells (GSCs) enables the inevitable reappearance of GB in the patients in a short period of time [3,4]. Furthermore, the tumor microenvironment (TME), including pericytes (PCs), facilitates the tumor cell escape from the brain anti-tumor immune responses [5–9].

PCs are perivascular cells with mesenchymal stem cell properties that are key regulators of the blood microvasculature tone and structure [10–14] in the central nervous system, being crucial for the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [10,15,16]. Moreover, PCs perform immune cell functions [13,17], including phagocytic potential, antigen presentation, and the ability to respond to inflammatory signals by secreting pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines that modulate T-cell responses [13,18,19]. Importantly, PC immune function against GB is ablated by the tumor cells through promoting an aberrant upregulation of lysosomal activity, specifically of chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) [5,20]. CMA is a specialized form of autophagy responsible for targeting and degrading soluble cytosolic proteins that contain specific amino acid motifs (KFERQ-like) recognized by the cytosolic chaperone heat-shock protein 8 (HSPA8/HSC70) [21,22]. The efficiency of CMA is directly linked to the lysosomal levels of LAMP2A (lysosomal associated membrane protein 2A), a receptor that binds the targeted proteins, a limiting step in this pathway [23,24]. CMA is implicated in the removal of misfolded/damaged proteins in response to cellular stressors [25–27], as well as in the selective degradation of functional proteins to regulate a variety of cellular functions. Proper CMA activity is central for maintaining PC proteome homeostasis and thus, the anti-tumor PC properties [14,20,28].

Tumor cells not only upregulate their own CMA levels to survive and proliferate [29,30], but also condition TME cells with inflammatory properties, through aberrant induction of CMA activity in tumor surrounding cells, such as PCs and macrophages [20,28,31]. GB cells promote stable cell interactions with PC for their own benefit and survival by inducing CMA activity in PCs [20]. Thus, the upregulation of CMA in PCs in response to GB cell interaction hinders their proinflammatory phenotype mainly supported through a secretome displaying anti-tumoral properties, including high levels of SPARC (secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine), the extracellular matrix protein LUM (lumican) and vitamin D [14,20,28].

Interestingly, MAPT/tau (microtubule-associated protein tau), a well-known CMA substrate [32,33], implicated in the cell cytoskeleton reorganization [34], has been related to the progression of several types of cancers [35–37]. Gliomas with higher expression of MAPT/tau were shown to be less aggressive, with impaired capacity of invasion, inhibited angiogenesis, and increased patient survival [38–41]. Moreover, both intracellular and extracellular MAPT/tau seem to be involved in treatment resistance and metabolic maintenance of the tumor. Interestingly, it has been suggested that MAPT/tau secretion may have anti-tumor properties [39,42,43]. MAPT/tau has been reported to be transported extrasynaptically by microvesicles, exosomes and by tunneling nanotubes (TNTs), not only by neurons but also by GB cell lines in a bidirectional manner [34,44–46]. However, possible MAPT/tau secretion by cells of the TME and its impact on GB progression remains poorly understood. Of relevance to this work, recent studies have shown that inhibition of CMA leads to rerouting of MAPT/tau toward late endosomes/multivesicular bodies and its subsequent extracellular secretion [32].

In this work, we aimed at testing whether interventions that hinder the GB-mediated aberrant upregulation of CMA in PCs, could promote MAPT/tau release within the TME and contribute to halt GB progression. To inhibit CMA in a way that could have direct clinical translation value, we opted for the use of the phosphopeptide P140, which has been identified as a potent regulator of the autoimmune response without inducing immunosuppression. P140 peptide enters cells through the clathrin-dependent endo-lysosomal pathway and accumulates within the lysosomal lumen, interacting with lysosomal chaperones, such as Lys-HSPA8, and interfering with the correct assembly of LAMP2A, therefore, exerting inhibitory effects on CMA activity [47,48]. This synthetic 21-mer linear peptide derived from the small nuclear ribonucleoprotein SNRNP70/U1-70K [49,50], currently evaluated in phase III clinical trials for lupus, has demonstrated protective effects in several autoimmune and inflammatory diseases [49–56], whereas no therapeutic effects were observed when using the non-phosphorylated 131–151 peptide as control treatments.

As we have demonstrated that experimental genetic blockage of CMA in PCs prevents their stable interactions with GB cells and promotes an anti-tumor phenotype capable of impairing tumor survival and progression, we hypothesized that the P140 peptide administration could have similar effects and be used for pharmacological inhibition of CMA in vivo for anti-GB purposes. Mechanistically, we reasoned that the remodeling of the PC proteome by CMA may be behind the prevention of the PC anti-tumor phenotype, since stable interactions of PCs with GB cells occur through cytoskeleton rearrangement [20], and CMA has been shown to modulate the cytoskeleton in different cell types [34]. Moreover, the recent connections between reduced intracellular degradation, including by CMA, and increased extracellular secretion [32,33,57,58], made us consider the contribution of changes in PC CMA to the composition of their anti-oncogenic secretome.

In this work, we show that the stable interactions of GB cells with PCs required for GB progression can be disrupted by the P140 peptide that prevents the aberrant upregulation of CMA induced by GB in PCs. In addition, we provide evidence that the inhibition of CMA by peptide P140 in PCs restores their physiological immune phenotype, able to eliminate GB cells through the secretion of toxic proteins for the tumor. We identified that the presence of MAPT/tau in the P140 peptide-treated PCs secretome is essential for its cytotoxic properties. We propose that P140 peptide treatment could be used as a therapeutic strategy to prevent tumorigenesis of the remaining GSCs, thus reducing the possibility of tumor recurrence.

Results

Peptide P140 reduces GB-PC interactions required for GB proliferation and survival

PCs promote GB survival and proliferation through a physical interaction that enhances the immunosuppressive function of PCs through aberrant CMA activation, thus preventing the anti-tumor immune responses [5,20]. Since it has been established that the peptide P140 binds to lysosome lumen HSPA8/HSC70 chaperone, interfering with CMA hyperactivation [47,51], we decided to test if inhibition of CMA with the P140 peptide could affect PC-GB interaction dynamics. As expected, the addition of DiI fluorescence loaded GB cells to cultures of PCs (PCGB) demonstrates higher GB cell adhesion than when are cultured alone (single-cultured GB cells) (Figure 1(A)). However, we found that the pretreatment of PC with P140 peptide was able to significantly reduce GB cell adhesion to PCs in an optimal concentration of 10 µM compared to PCGB cocultures where PCs were pretreated with a scrambled control peptide (ScP140) (Figure 1(A)). Since reduced adhesion to the plate was not observed when the GB cells were directly treated with P140 or ScP140 peptides (Figure 1(A)), we conclude that the effect of peptide P140 on GB adhesion was dependent on PC presence. Since the PC-GB stable interactions are key to GB progression, and the P140 peptide treatment seemed to be disturbing them, we decided to test if it was also affecting GB cell proliferation. To that effect, we used a GB cell line that constitutively expresses luciferase protein to monitor the changes in the luciferase activity displayed in the cell cultures as indicative of cell proliferation. Then, a reduction or increase of luminescence in the PCGB cocultures compared to proliferating control GB would indicate a reduced or increased proliferation of GB cells, respectively. As expected, PCGB cocultures in which PC had been pretreated with the control ScP140 peptide, displayed increased luciferase activity compared to GB cultured alone (Figure 1(B)). However, we found that PCGB cocultures in which PC were pretreated with P140 peptide presented significant reduction in the GB-luciferase expression, indicating decreased proliferation. Inhibition of GB proliferation in the PCGB cocultures was dependent on the peptide concentration, with an optimal concentration of 10 µM. Furthermore, direct treatment of GB cultures with P140 peptide did not result in changes in their proliferation (no reduction in the luciferase signal was observed in any of the treated GB cultures; Figure 1(B)). This was further confirmed by calculating the cumulative proliferation of PCGB cocultures treated with P140 (Figure S1A). Consistently, FACS-based cell counting analyses revealed a significant reduction in GB cell numbers in P140-pretreated cocultures (Figure S1B), confirming previous results.

To test if the observed decrease in GB proliferation upon culture with PC pretreated with P140 peptide was due to an increase in GB cell death, we used vital staining and flow cytometry to separate the signal from PC and Dil-labeled GB cells. We added treatment with the reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenger N-acetylcysteine (NAc) as a positive control for cell death. We found that the GB cell survival was significantly reduced in a time-dependent manner in the case of GB cells co-cultured with PCs pretreated with P140 peptide, compared to GB cells co-cultured with the non-treated control PCs and the ones pretreated with ScP140 peptide (Figure 1(C)). Importantly, P140 peptide did not affect PC survival (Figure S1C). However, while it had no effect on basal pericyte proliferation, P140 restored the reduced proliferative capacity of GB-conditioned PCs (Figure S1D).

Overall, these findings support that pre-treatment of PC cells with P140 peptide diminishes the ability of GB cells to interact with them, thus compromising their survival and ability to proliferate.

P140 peptide prevents GB-induced CMA aberrant upregulation

Because P140 peptide had been shown to prevent the pathological CMA levels in other diseases [47,51,55,56], we hypothesized that it may be restoring basal CMA levels in PCs by decreasing the previously observed GB-induced aberrant CMA activity [20]. We used PC cells expressing the KFERQ-PA-mCherry photoactivable fluorescent reporter that when delivered to lysosomes via CMA allows visualizing them as fluorescent puncta [59]. Quantification of number of fluorescent puncta per cell provides a good assessment of CMA activity. As reported before, co-culture of PCs with GB cells markedly increased CMA but treatment with the P140 peptide prevented the GB-induced CMA upregulation in GB-conditioned PCs (GBCPC) in different time points (Figure 2(A)) whereas the control peptide ScP140 did not show any significant effect. P140 peptide was also able to reduce basal CMA activity in PCs not incubated with GB cells (Figure 2(A)). Next, we analyzed the status of macroautophagy in the peptide P140-treated cells, since inhibition of CMA in some types of cells results in compensatory upregulation of macroautophagy [60]. However, as described for neurons and stem cells, the P140 peptide treatment in PCs showed no effect on macroautophagy measured as changes in the levels of the autophagy protein LC3-I and LC3-II upon blockage of lysosomal proteolysis with lysosomal inhibitors (LI) (Figure 2(B)). To further assess the specificity of the peptide P140’s effect in PCs incubated with GB (GBCPCs), we transduced PCs with the mCherry-GFP-LC3 reporter that allows tracking conversion of autophagosomes (double fluorescent puncta) into autolysosomes (mCherry fluorescent puncta) to evaluate macroautophagy activity. Our results showed that peptide P140 treatment does not affect the total amount of autophagic compartment or their maturation from autophagosomes to autolysosomes in PCs alone or when cultured with GB (GBCPCs), (Figure 2(C); dissipation of the lysosomal pH with LI is shown as positive control of blockage of autophagosome clearance).

These findings confirm the selectivity of the inhibitory effect of P140 peptide on PC CMA and that treatment with peptide P140 prevents the GB-induced aberrant upregulation of CMA in PCs.

Intravenous administration of peptide P140 pretreated PCs promotes tumor clearance

Once we had established the effects of peptide P140 in brain PCs in vitro, we wanted to determine if peptide P140-pretreated PCs could also affect the proliferation and survival of GB cells in vivo. To this effect, we orthotopically xenografted immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice with human GB cells [20,28] prior to performing cell therapy. Once the tumoral mass was formed, we intravenously injected mice with PCs treated with P140 (P140-PC) or ScP140 (ScP140-PC) peptides. After 14 days of the PC treatment, we analyzed the number of GB cells (STEM212+) in the brain and measured the tumor area (Figure 3(A)). GB-grafted mice injected with ScP140-PCs showed brain infiltration of tumor cells in choroid plexus and subpial vessels, and tumor masses in brain cortex around perivascular areas as, it was the case in control GB mice. In sharp contrast, only very few cells of the previously engrafted tumor were detected outside of the brain parenchyma in GB mice treated with P140-PC (Figure 3(B, C)), and tumors were no longer detectable in the xenografted region (Figure 3(B, D)). In line with our previous studies using CMA defective PCs [28], the intravenous administration of peptide P140-pretreated PCs also modulated the responses of phagocytic populations, such as microglia and pericytes [19,61,62], in the tumor and surrounding areas. Untreated GB control mice and those treated with ScP140-PCs presented expected tumor-associated AIF1/Iba1+ microgliosis that was scarcely associated with perivascular cells in the peritumoral thalamus areas (Figure 3(E-G)). However, P140-PCs treated mice showed a trend to decrease tumor-associated microglia in the areas of eliminated tumor mass (Figure 3(E)). These mice showed a very marked increase in the expression of AIF1/Iba1 in the brain parenchyma areas where there had previously been tumor mass before being removed and in the surrounding areas (Figure 3(E, F)). The brain thalamus area that delimited the remaining scar from the eliminated tumor showed microgliosis accumulation and high expression of AIF1/Iba1 in perivascular cells, indicating remanent activation of microglia and suggesting the persistence of activated PCs in response to tumor cells [63–65] (Figure 3(E, G)). Consistently, brains of ScP140-PCs treated mice only showed phagocytic immune populations (CD68+) in association with the tumor, as in controls (Figure 3(H, I)). By contrast, the treatment with P140-PCs reduced infiltration of these tumor-associated populations [7,65,66] upon tumor removal. Importantly and in line with our previous results, the activation marker of phagocytic populations [63,64,67] was found in perivascular cells of the brain parenchyma surrounding the region where there was previously tumor cell mass (Figure 3(H, J)).

Intravenous therapy with P140 peptide promotes tumor cell elimination

To determine if P140 peptide administered directly to the animals was effective in affecting the proliferation and survival of GB cells, likely through the modulation of CMA in PCs and other immune populations in vivo, we treated GB-grafted mice with a P140 or ScP140 peptide intravenous therapy in a peptide-dose manner to determine the optimal dose-response (Figure 4(A)). After 14 days of treatment, cells of a previously engrafted tumor (GB control) were hardly detected outside of the brain parenchyma in GB mice treated with the highest dose of P140 peptide (Figure 4(B); 50 µg; 2.5 mg/kg). In contrast, GB-grafted mice treated with ScP140 peptide and GB control mice showed brain infiltration of tumor cells in choroid plexus and subpial vessels, and tumor masses in brain cortex around perivascular areas (Figure 4(B); 50 µg). Comparison of the different doses revealed that the intravenous administration of peptide P140 promoted the elimination of the tumor cells in a dose-dependent manner with maximal efficacy at 50 µg. Mice treated with the highest dose showed hardly any tumor cells but even those treated with the intermediate dose showed a significant reduction of tumor mass and just remaining tumor cells close to blood vessels. Importantly, we found that mice treated with the highest dose of P140 peptide did not show phagocytic immune populations associated with the tumor after the treatment (Figure S3). The expression of the microglia marker AIF1/Iba1 (Figure S3A, B) and the activation marker for macrophages CD68 (Figure S3C, D) were significantly reduced in peptide P140-treated mice compared to mice untreated or treated with the control peptide (Figure S3B and D). Moreover, the infiltration of different T cell populations associated with tumor progression was also reduced, with minimal detection of any remaining CD4+ cell and no evidence of infiltration by immunosuppressive T cells (Tregs, CD4+ FOXP3+) (Figure S4), while effector CD8+ T cells – known to be cytotoxic against tumor cells – were present.

P140 peptide prevents tumorigenesis and promotes inflammatory anti-tumor immune responses

Once we established that the most effective dose of P140 peptide was 50 µg, we next evaluated its effect on the tumorigenic potential of patient-derived GSCs, main tumorigenesis initiating cells of GB. In this case, we implemented a shorter treatment on an immunocompetent mouse model xenografted with GSCs (Figure 5(A)). The intravenous administration of peptide P140 reduced the number of tumor cells and their proliferative capacity (positivity for MKI67/Ki67) (Figure 5(B, C, D)), as well as the tumor area (Figure 5(E)). Excitingly, we found that mice treated with P140 peptide displayed a significantly increased number of activated phagocytic immune populations in peritumoral areas, around corpus callosum and hippocampus (Figure 5(F-J)). In contrast, the control peptide ScP140 treatment only showed tumor-associated immune populations (Figure 5(F-J)). P140 peptide treatment significantly increased the expression of the microglia marker AIF1/Iba1 and the proinflammatory cytokine IFNG/IFNγ in tumor surrounding areas compared to mice untreated or treated with the scrambled peptide (Figure 5(F-H)). Furthermore, the peptide P140 increased the infiltration of CD68-positive cells in tumor surrounding areas, reducing those associated with the tumor (Figure 5(I, J)). The activation of these populations observed upon this short-term treatment with P140 peptide clearly differs from the decrease in activation markers observed at the long-term time points of P140 peptide administration (Figure S3). These differences provide a temporal overview of the peptide P140 effect, whereby P140 peptide treatment promotes the early activation of the anti-tumor immune response with enhanced recruitment of immune cells, and the later resolution of the immune infiltration once the cancer cells are eliminated. Importantly, and supporting the early activation of brain anti-tumor immune responses by P140 peptide treatment, we found significant differences in the CD4+ T cells purified from central lymph nodes of mice xenografted with GSCs upon peptide P140 treatment. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that T cells from mice treated with peptide P140 compared to those from control mice showed lower levels of two coinhibitory receptors of T cell activation (PDCD1/PD-1 [programmed cell death protein 1] and CTLA4 [cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4]), which are associated with suppression of T cell function in the tumor microenvironment [9,20]. Additionally, the levels of Tregs (CD4+ FOXP3+), were reduced after the short treatment (Figure 5(K)). P140 peptide accumulates specifically in peritumoral phagocytic cells including PCs.

PCs play a key role in maintaining the BBB integrity, which is a limiting factor of drug penetration and efficacy within the brain [2,11]. Even though the BBB is partially disrupted in GB, it is intact in the tumor surrounding areas where invasive cells are located [2]. To demonstrate that the intravenously injected P140 peptide reached the tumor niche by going through the BBB, GB-grafted mice were intravenously injected with P140 peptide labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 and compared to those injected with labeled control ScP140 peptide (Figure 6). We found that both peptides reached tumor areas of the brain cortex and mainly accumulated in peritumoral areas (Figure 6(A,B)) after 24 h of injection. Both peptides colocalized in perivascular areas with PCs identified through PDGFRB (platelet derived growth factor receptor beta) labeling (Figure 6(C,D)), whereas in the rest of the brain parenchyma, we did not find colocalization with astrocytes (Figure 6E, F) but detected both peptides in microglia cells (AIF1/Iba1+) (Figure 6(G,H)). Unexpectedly, we found accumulation of the control ScP140 peptide in neurons (RBFOX3/NeuN+), whereas P140 peptide was hardly detected in these cells (Figure 6(I,J)). These results indicate that peptide P140 selectively accumulates in PCs and microglial cells within the peritumoral niche, with minimal uptake by neurons, confirming its target specificity. Importantly, neither untreated nor peptide-treated GB-grafted mice showed any changes in the expression of the basement membrane marker of blood vessels LAM (laminin), suggesting that the BBB integrity was not disrupted (Figure S5). P140 peptide promotes an anti-tumoral and proinflammatory secretome

We have previously reported that CMA deficiency in PCs prevents the GB-conditioned secretome and leads to an inflammatory phenotype characterized by the secretion of anti-tumoral proteins that reduce tumor cell survival [28]. We hypothesized that the ability of P140 peptide to prevent GB-induced aberrant CMA upregulation in PCs (Figure 2(A)) may also prevent the GB-conditioned secretome of PCs. This would result instead in a secretome containing toxic proteins for tumor cells. Furthermore, this secretome would potentially drive the anti-tumor immune responses observed in our peptide P140-treated GB mouse models (Figures 3–5). To test this possibility, we evaluated PC secretion of proteins with anti-tumoral properties identified in our previous studies [28]. We found that PCs pretreated with P140 peptide and cocultured with GB, significantly increased their secretion of SPARC, LUM and vitamin D (Figure 7(A–C)). Moreover, the P140 peptide enhanced the secretion of IL1B and IFNG in GBCPCs, promoting an anti-tumoral proinflammatory phenotype (Figure 7(D, E)). Importantly, our results also supported the prevention of the GB-conditioned angiogenic function of PCs, as we detected a failure to increase AGT (angiotensinogen) secretion in P140 peptide-pretreated PCs in response to GB cells (Figure 7(F)). P140 promotes MAPT/tau accumulation and secretion in PCs in response to GB

Recent studies have identified the microtubule-associated protein MAPT/tau as one of the most efficient CMA substrates in different brain cell types [32,33]. Of interest for the current study, failure to take up MAPT/tau into lysosomes via CMA results in its re-routing to late endosomes/multivesicular bodies, loading into exosomes and subsequent extracellular release [32,33]. MAPT/tau is a known CMA substrate essential for proper cell-cell interactions [32,68,69] and its accumulation has been related not only to neurodegenerative diseases, but also to GB progression [39,40]. As GB-induced CMA in PCs promotes stable interactions with GB cells through a remodeling of their cytoskeleton [20,32,68,69], we wondered whether the regulation of MAPT/tau levels in PCs would be affected by aberrant GB-induced CMA. Furthermore, since peptide P140 was previously found to inhibit CMA in vivo at the step of substrate lysosomal uptake, using lysosomes purified from the liver of lupus mice [48], we proposed that PC pre-treatment with P140 peptide might interfere with MAPT/tau degradation, leading to its possible cell accumulation or secretion [32,70,71].

To test these possibilities first, we checked the levels of murine specific MAPT/tau secreted by P140 peptide-pretreated PCs in the presence or not of human GB cells. Excitingly, we found that the pharmacological treatment markedly enhanced the secretion of MAPT/tau by PCs only when in presence of GB cells (Figure 8(A)). We confirmed that genetic blockage of CMA, either in PC from lamp2a knockout mice or upon LAMP2A knockdown with shRNA [72], was sufficient to trigger pronounced extracellular MAPT/tau release specifically by murine PCs. But this only occurred in presence of human GB cells (Figure 8(B)), supporting that the upregulation of CMA in PCs induced by GB may allow maintaining low MAPT/tau levels in PC, by active degradation through CMA. In fact, immunoblot analysis of GB-conditioned PCs revealed low MAPT/tau levels under control conditions, consistent with active MAPT/tau degradation, as evidenced by MAPT/tau accumulation upon inhibition of lysosomal proteolysis (LI). In contrast, P140 pretreatment led to MAPT/tau accumulation even in the absence of lysosomal inhibition (Figure 8(C)), indicating that P140 prevents GB-induced MAPT/tau degradation in PCs. To directly analyze the impact of P140 peptide in intracellular MAPT/tau levels in PC and to elucidate if its effect is dependent on GB presence, we performed immunofluorescence for MAPT/tau protein in PCGB co-cultures in presence or not of P140 peptide and compared the effect of blocking macroautophagy (3-MA) or proteosome (MG132) activity with CMA blocking with the P140 peptide. As we expected, MAPT/tau levels were higher in GB cells. Importantly, GBCPCs did not show any changes in MAPT/tau cellular protein levels compared to control PCs alone (Figure 8(D)). Macroautophagy inhibition did not affect MAPT/tau cellular levels in GBCPC or control PC (Figure 8(D) and Figure S6A). In contrast, both PCs and GB cells showed MAPT/tau accumulation when the proteosome activity was inhibited as previously reported in other types of cells [73–75] (Figure 8(D)). Importantly, MAPT/tau cellular levels were markedly increased in P140 peptide pretreated PC with and without presence of GB cells, but not in GB cells (Figure 8(D) and Figure S6B). Consistent with results showing increased extracellular MAPT/tau secretion (Figure 8(A)), intracellular MAPT/tau accumulation was also observed, indicating a coordinated adjustment of MAPT/tau distribution under conditions of reduced CMA activity. As expected, the ScP140 peptide, which does not inhibit CMA, did not change PC levels of MAPT/tau (Figure 8(D)). Finally, we analyzed the activities of the 20S and 26S proteasomes in peptide-treated PC cells and found no differences between treatments, indicating that peptide P140 does not prevent MAPT/tau degradation by inhibiting proteasome activity (Figure S6C).

Our findings revealed that degradation of MAPT/tau in PCs occurs in part by CMA and that peptide P140 blockage of the aberrant CMA upregulation induced by GB in PC results in intracellular MAPT/tau accumulation and leads to its secretion by PCs, specifically in response to tumor cells.

MAPT/tau accumulation in PCs is indicative of peptide P140 effectiveness in blocking GB progression

To validate that peptide P140-induced PCs MAPT/tau accumulation and secretion in response to GB cells also occurred in vivo, we used different GB models. Consistently, we observed an increase in MAPT/tau-positive cells in the perivascular areas of GB-grafted mice after administration of peptide P140 (Figure 8E–G). Using immunofluorescence for MAPT/tau and for two markers of PCs (ACTA2/ASMA [alpha smooth muscle actin] and PDGFRB), we confirmed that the observed peptide P140-induced MAPT/tau accumulation occurred, in part, in the PCs from blood microvessels surrounding tumor areas of the GB-grafted mice (Figure 8H). However, this accumulation was no longer evident if the GB-grafted mice were administered with the ScP140 peptide instead. These changes in MAPT/tau levels inversely correlated with the levels of LAMP2A in PC, which as expected, were reduced by peptide P140 due to the inability of GB to establish connections with PCs upon treatment with the peptide (Figure S7A).

Since GB patients often undergo surgery as part of the initial treatment, we next set to evaluate the clinical relevance of the accumulation of MAPT/tau and possible value in candidate patient selection and as a marker of the therapeutic effect of P140 peptide. We first confirmed these changes, in abundance and localization of MAPT/tau, in GB patient samples by immunohistochemistry, which demonstrates fewer cells expressing MAPT/tau in perivascular areas, with most MAPT/tau-positive cells found in the rest of the brain parenchyma associated to tumor cell infiltration (Figure 9A, B). Hence, we checked MAPT/tau levels in PCs from peritumoral blood microvessels of the brain tissue of our cohort of GB patients, which we had previously classified [76] according to their histopathological severity (mild, moderate, or severe) and LAMP2A expression in PCs. Consistently, we observed that in mild GB patients the perivascular MAPT/tau expression was colocalizing with PCs (Figure 9C, D) and was negatively correlated to LAMP2A expression levels and therefore, to PC CMA activity (Figure S7B). By contrast, severe-to-moderate GB patients showed reduced levels of MAPT/tau in PCs, a possible indication of its increased degradation by CMA in these cells (Figure 9D and Figure S7B). Thus, our findings revealed that an increase in MAPT/tau expression levels of the perivascular areas surrounding the tumor can be used as a marker of the state of the anti-tumor response of PCs and the suitability for the treatment with peptide P140, which will be administered in these cases as a way to promote MAPT/tau release by PCs and its subsequent GB cell toxic effect. Gender and age have been described as a factor influencing the survival time of GB patients [77,78] and, in fact our GB cohort also confirmed lower incidence of GB in middle-aged women than in male patients of the same age range [76]. However, the few cases of GB in middle-aged women in our study revealed a worse prognosis based on their high LAMP2A expression in PCs. Recent studies have reported sex differences in CMA activity in multiple cells and organs, as well as sex-specific impact of aging in CMA, whereby males exhibited a greater decline than females [79]. Therefore, we wondered whether there could also be sex- and age-dependent differences in perivascular MAPT/tau levels of peritumoral areas. Comparing between genders in the middle-aged patients, mild-to-moderate GB in females showed higher total levels of MAPT/tau than males (Figure 9(E)). In contrast, middle-aged male patients, who had previously been shown to have lower perivascular expression of LAMP2A in peritumoral areas [76], exhibited significantly higher perivascular levels of MAPT/tau (Figure 9(F)).

Moreover, the comparative analysis of MAPT/tau expression in the peritumoral and perivascular microenvironment of GB patients corroborated our previous findings. A strong inverse correlation was observed between total MAPT/tau+ cells and MAPT/tau+ perivascular cells (r = −0.7832, p < 0.0001), indicating that increased global MAPT/tau burden is associated with a marked loss of MAPT/tau localization within the perivascular niche (Figure 9(G)).

Kaplan – Meier survival analysis further revealed that high levels of MAPT/tau within perivascular cells were associated with significantly improved survival, whereas loss of MAPT/tau from the PV compartment correlated with accelerated disease progression (Figure 9(H)). Together, these data indicate that not only the amount of MAPT/tau, but critically its perivascular localization, is associated with tumor burden and patient survival.

Altogether, these results underscore the value of analyzing levels of perivascular MAPT/tau in tumor-surrounding areas as an indicator of good prognosis and as potential index for identification of patients with GB progression dependent on PC CMA activity.

Peptide P140-mediated secretion of MAPT/tau by PCs compromises GB cellsurvival

MAPT/tau secretion and propagation are one of the best-known mechanisms of brain proteotoxicity [80–83] also proven to be toxic to cancer cells [39,42,43]. Since PCs with defective CMA activity present a secretome toxic for tumor cells [20,28], we next evaluate whether MAPT/tau could be responsible for the cytotoxic effect on GB of PCs upon treatment with P140 peptide. First, we tested whether extracellularly added purified human MAPT/tau protein could prevent or disrupt the stable interactions between PCs and GB cells [20]. We evaluated the effect of two concentrations (50 and 100 nM) of the MAPT/tau-441 isoform on GB cell adhesion and viability following either PC pretreatment or direct addition to established PC-GB co-cultures (Figure 10(A)). Excitingly, both doses caused a decrease in GB cell adhesion (Figure 10(B)), leading to reduced cancer cell survival (Figure 10(C)), regardless of the timing of administration. Therefore, our results support that extracellular MAPT/tau protein not only inhibited the formation of PC-GB interactions, but it also disrupted the pre-established ones (Figure 10(B,C)). As we observed the same performance at both concentrations, we corroborated these results using the lower dose of MAPT/tau in the patient-derived cell line of GSCs (Figure 10(D,E)). Next, we aimed to evaluate the immunoactivity of secreted MAPT/tau by PCs in cells of the adaptive immune system. Thus, we wanted to determine if extracellular purified MAPT/tau protein could activate B cell responses. We analyzed intracellular IL2 accumulation in B cells after 16 to 24 h of incubation with MAPT/tau (50 nM) by flow cytometry (Figure S8). Notably, MAPT/tau induced IL2 accumulation in B cells, supporting a direct activation of this cell type in this experimental setting, independent of additional immunomodulatory mediators. To further characterize the relevance of MAPT/tau in the anti-tumor effect of the peptide P140-induced PC secretome, we analyzed if the cytotoxic effect of this secretome would be maintained in PCs with deficient expression of MAPT/tau (mapt−/−, where expression of MAPT/tau has been disrupted by insertion of the GFP protein in the Mapt locus) [85]. Importantly, we found that P140 peptide pretreatment of mapt−/− PCs failed to induce the decrease in GB adhesion (Figure 10(F)), and the consequent reduced proliferation (Figure 10(G)) and survival observed in control peptide P140-treated GBCPCs (Mapt+/-, which express both GFP and functional Mapt) (Figure 10(F–H)). In fact, GB cultured with mapt−/− PCs displayed a trend toward higher adhesion and proliferation, albeit no significative (Figure 10(F–H)), further supporting the contribution of MAPT/tau to the PC-mediated anti-tumoral response.

Finally, to corroborate that MAPT/tau-deficient PCs lose their cytotoxic activity against tumor cells in vivo, GB-grafted mice were injected intravenously as previously shown (Figure 3(A)), with P140 peptide-pretreated Mapt+/- or mapt−/− PCs. First, we confirmed that both Mapt+/- and mapt−/− PCs reached tumor areas four days after treatment (Figure 10(I)). In consonance with our in vitro results, we found that the P140 peptide pretreatment in mapt−/− PCs was no longer able to promote tumor clearance when compared to peptide P140-pretreated control PCs (Mapt+/-) and instead showed no differences with untreated mice (Figure 10(J–L)). Our results lead us to conclude that P140 peptide-mediated secretion of MAPT/tau by PCs is necessary for the observed restoration of the anti-tumoral function of these cells upon CMA blockage with the P140 peptide. These observations highlight MAPT/tau secretion as key for the effectiveness of the treatment against GB (Figure 10(M)).

Discussion

In this work, we have provided insight into how the peptide P140-mediated prevention of CMA upregulation in PCs, in response to GB, leads to an antitumoral PC phenotype capable of eliminating tumor cells through the secretion of proteins toxic to tumor progression. This secretome, along with inflammatory cytokines that activate the anti-tumor immune response, also includes MAPT/tau, which is essential for the disruption of the pathological cellular PC-GB interactions that ablate the immune function of PCs and promote instead pro-tumor immune responses (Figure 10(M)).

The high infiltrative capacity of GSCs and their resistance to conventional therapies highlights the urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches in GB that target GSC survival and growth [86–88]. Furthermore, despite big efforts in finding good biomarkers for GB prognosis, success has been limited mostly due to the great molecular heterogeneity of the tumor [89–91]. Another limiting factor in the effectiveness of conventional therapy is the intense immunosuppressive TME [6,92,93] which promotes tumor immune evasion and supports tumor growth [5–9]. We have previously identified that GB cells induce aberrant activation of CMA in PCs and proposed that the inhibition of CMA in PCs is a promising target for new therapies against GB cancer, as CMA-deficient PCs secrete anti-tumoral proteins that reduce tumor proliferation and survival [20,28]. Since CMA is a necessary process to maintain cellular homeostasis, its complete systemic inhibition could lead to adverse side effects, including failure of T-cell activation needed for the GB anti-tumor immune responses [27]. Therefore, selective transient inhibition of CMA targeted to tumor-surrounding cells and PCs would be necessary to develop a successful therapy [6]. For that reason the phosphopeptide P140 became of interest for this study, as it has been shown to be capable to restore physiological CMA levels in different inflammatory pathologies with aberrant upregulation of CMA in immune cells like lupus, without altering basal CMA activity levels [50–56,94]. Compared to genetic ablation of LAMP2A, shown to have multiple systemic adverse effects, the use of a peptide allows dose titration to inhibit but not completely eliminate CMA, and also makes this inhibition transient. Since our data show that the pretreatment of PCs with peptide P140 reduces their interaction with GB cells, which hinders GB proliferation and promotes GB elimination (Figure 1), a transient partial inhibition of CMA seems sufficient to leave GB cells vulnerable and allow for PCs to restore their anti-tumoral secretome (Figure 7).

We have previously demonstrated that administration of CMA-deficient PCs promotes tumor clearance through the activation of the innate immune responses of GB xenografted mice [28]. Here, we advance the translational potential of this intervention by demonstrating that administration of P140 peptide-pretreated PCs was effective in removing the tumoral cells (Figure 3(B–D)) and in supporting the activation of different phagocytic cell populations implicated in the anti-tumor innate immune responses, including perivascular cells (Figure 3(E–J)) [95,96]. Moreover, the direct administration of peptide P140 showed the complete removal of tumor cells and the tumor-associated immune cell populations (Figure 4 and Figure S3).

Considering the important role of GSCs in the recurrence of this tumor, we found highly relevant that the normalization of CMA in PC by P140 peptide treatment and subsequent changes in their secretome, was efficient in mediating the activation of the innate immune responses, contributing to the prevention of tumorigenesis and to the reduction of immunosuppressive T cell responses in mice xenografted with infiltrating patient-derived GSCs (Figure 5 and Figure S4).

By tracking P140 peptide distribution in vivo, we confirmed that it reached the tumor niche, accumulating specifically in the tumor-surrounding cells rather than in the cancer cells (Figure 6) hence indicating that P140 peptide was internalized only by cells with phagocytic properties, like peritumoral PCs and microglia. Since P140 peptide is only effective in blocking CMA when reaching the lysosomal lumen, these findings are promising regarding peptide P140 biosafety, as CMA inhibition may be minimal in non-phagocytic cells, and in fact the P140 peptide was only found diffusely around neurons and other glial cells (Figure 6), supporting its specificity and favorable biosafety profile in vivo. The accumulation of P140 peptide has been previously reported in the lungs and spleen of lupus-prone mice, with latter localization in B cells and resident macrophages [51]. In our model, the P140 peptide decreased the GB-induced immunosuppression [5,20] rather than increasing it (Figure 5(K)), which suggests that the antigenic presentation and the adaptative anti-tumor immune responses are still functional. Although the P140 peptide interacts with molecules involved in the MHC and could alter the quality of the peptides presented [47,48], the activation of the innate immune response and the infiltration of CD8 lymphocytes after therapy suggests that antigen presenting cells have not been affected. In summary, we show how P140 peptide-mediated inhibition of CMA in GBCPCs at the tumor infiltration front prevents cancer tumorigenesis by promoting the anti-tumor immune response through the PC secretion of immunomodulatory and antitumoral molecules.

Our work identifies a key role for the cytoskeleton protein MAPT/tau, on the peptide P140-mediated anti-tumoral effect. We propose that the changes in MAPT/tau levels in PCs, observed upon peptide P140 blockage of CMA, contribute to this anti-tumoral effect both intra- and extra-cellularly. Once GB interacts with PCs, stabilization of these interactions is promoted through the formation of nanotubes in a CMA-dependent manner [20]. To maintain these connections and, at the same time, promote the secretion of extracellular vesicles with pro-regenerative factors [20], PCs would need to undergo major cytoskeleton remodeling. The facts that 1) CMA has been shown to contribute to regulation of cytoskeleton stability, 2) one of CMA substrates, MAPT/tau, is implicated in the stabilization of microtubules [34,68] and 3) MAPT/tau expression has been correlated with better prognosis in gliomas [38,39,41], motivated the focus on MAPT/tau in this work. However, we do not discard that reduced degradation of other CMA substrates may also contribute to the anti-tumoral effect of P140 peptide. Importantly, we propose that the aberrant upregulation of CMA induced in PCs by GB cells may, in part, promote MAPT/tau rapid degradation, thereby preventing its extracellular secretion and subsequent toxicity to GB cells. In fact, in this work, we have identified MAPT/tau in the PC secretome, and demonstrated its negative impact on GB cell adhesion, proliferation and survival (Figure 10(B–E)). MAPT/tau was essential for the anti-tumoral function of P140 peptide-treated PCs (Figure 10(F–H)), further supporting the idea that reduced CMA activity in PCs promotes MAPT/tau extracellular secretion, as previously shown in other cells in the context of neurodegeneration [32,97]. However, to the best of our knowledge, we show for the first time the effect of extracellular MAPT/tau in the context of TME, demonstrating its cytotoxic effect against GB cancer cells [32,97].

Future studies are needed to understand the consequence of changes in intracellular levels of MAPT/tau in PCs due to the upregulation of CMA by GB in these cells, including cytoskeleton remodeling and regulation of vesicular trafficking and secretion.

The potential translational relevance of our findings is supported by our observations that clinical samples collected from patients with GB also showed a correlation between the CMA limiting component LAMP2A and MAPT/tau in PCs, suggesting that MAPT/tau could be used as a marker of good prognosis in GB (Figure 9(A–C) and Figure S7B).

Integrating our experimental and clinical data, we propose a model in which the prognostic value of MAPT/tau in GB is not dictated by its overall abundance, but rather by its spatial compartmentalization within the tumor microenvironment. Specifically, preservation of MAPT/tau within perivascular cells is associated with reduced tumor burden and improved survival, whereas loss of MAPT/tau from the perivascular niche accompanies disease progression. These observations are consistent with our mechanistic data showing that modulation of CMA in PCs influences MAPT/tau handling and secretion, thereby reshaping the perivascular microenvironment and its interaction with tumor cells. From this perspective, the accumulation of MAPT/tau within perivascular cells likely reflects a reprogrammed PC state resulting from CMA inhibition, which may contribute to limiting GB growth. Notably, we also observed a difference in these perivascular MAPT/tau levels depending on the sex and age of the GB patients that correlates well with the severity of the disease and the patient prognosis outcome (Figure 9(D-F)) [76]. Although future studies are needed to fully elucidate the implications of sex differences on CMA in this context, it is attractive to consider that they may contribute to the observed differences in incidence and aggressivity of GB between men and women. CMA plays a dual role in oncogenesis, whereby the reduction of its activity favors malignant transformation of cells, but once transformed, cancer cells become dependent on CMA for their survival and tumor growth [98]. Considering the reported lower CMA activity in men, they would be more susceptible to GB oncogenesis, while women would have a faster progression of the tumor as they would be better able to sustain high CMA.

A limitation of our study is the unavailability of cerebrospinal fluid and sufficient blood samples from our patient cohort, which precluded the evaluation of secreted MAPT/tau as a circulating biomarker in GB. Future studies incorporating longitudinal fluid sampling will be necessary to determine whether perivascular MAPT/tau dynamics translate into detectable changes in circulating MAPT/tau and to assess its potential clinical utility.

Overall, our work demonstrates the potential therapeutic value of peptide P140 in treatment of GB and confirms that peptide P140, by preventing the aberrant GB-induced upregulation of CMA in PCs, promotes an immunogenic phenotype through the secretion of toxic proteins to tumor cells, in which MAPT/tau plays a key role ablating tumor cell-PC interactions. Furthermore, we identify the analysis of perivascular MAPT/tau levels in GB tumor biopsies as a potential prognostic marker influenced by age and gender, and as an indicator of the likely effectiveness of P140 peptide treatment in these patients.

Materials and methods

Mice

Male and female mice of six to eight weeks old, wild type (WT) C57BL/6 (Charles River Laboratories, strain 000664) and MAPT/tau-deficient B6.129S4(Cg)-Mapttm1(eGFP)Klt/J (mapt−/−; which present two GFP copies due to insertion of the transgene GFP in the Mapt gene [85] and were kindly provided by Dr. Félix Hernández [99]), and their heterozygous counterparts (Mapt+/-; which present one copy of GFP and one functional copy of Mapt), were maintained in pathogen-free conditions in the animal facilities of the Institute of Biomedical Research of Murcia (IMIB). All animal procedures were approved and performed according to the guidelines set by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the IMIB (approved protocols A13210202 and A13241202).

Patient samples

Samples and data from patients included in this study were provided by the Biobank IMIB (National Registry of Biobanks B. 0000859) (PT20/00109), integrated in the Platform ISCIII Biobanks and Bio models of Spain, and they were processed following standard operating procedures with the appropriate approval of the Ethics and Scientific Committees. Histological samples were obtained from patients diagnosed with glioblastoma (grade IV according to the WHO guidelines) who underwent surgical intervention at the Hospital Clinico Universitario Virgen de la Arrixaca (Murcia, Spain). Please see reference [76] for detailed information about the patients. For this study, all samples were anonymized, and no additional authorizations were required.

Cell isolation and culture

Primary brain WT PCs, Mapt+/- PCs and mapt−/− PCs were isolated from mice according to the method of Oishi et al. [59]as described previously [5,14,20]. PCs-GFP and PCs with impaired CMA (KO PCs), were isolated from brains [20,28] of C57BL/6-Tg (ACTB-EGFP)1Osb/J and lamp2a−/− mice [72], respectively. PCs were used from the 5th to 8th passage and maintained in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM-α; Gibco, 15,430,584) supplemented with 20% (v:v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cytiva, 12,389,802), 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Corning, 30–002-CI), and 2 mM GlutaMax (Gibco, 35,050,061) at 37°C in 8.5% CO2.

Human glioma cell lines U87MG (U87-luciferase) and U251MG (U251) were purchased from the European Collection for Authenticated Cell Cultures/ECACC (89081402 and 09063001, respectively) and were cultured in DMEM high glucose (Biowest, L0101) supplemented with 10% (v:v) FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, 11,500,626).

Patient-derived GSC line GB2, kindly provided by Dr. Ander Matheu (Cellular Oncology Group, Biodonostia Health Research Institute, San Sebastian, Spain) [30], was cultured in DMEM/F-12 (Biowest, L0090) supplemented by N2, B27 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11,520,536 and 11,530,536), 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin, and growth factors (20 ng/mL basic FGF and 20 ng/mL EGF; R&D Systems, 233-FB-025/CF and 236-EG-200, respectively). All glioma cells were maintained at 37°C and 8.5% CO2.

DiI labeling solution (Invitrogen, D3911) was used for cell tracking as described previously [28].

Synthetic peptides

Peptides P140 and ScP140 (a scrambled version of P140 peptide) were assembled and purified as described [47,51]. Briefly, P140 peptide (sequence 131–151 of the U1-70K protein phosphorylated at Ser140), as well as peptide ScP140, were synthesized using classical N-(9-fluorenyl) methoxycarbonyl solid-phase chemistry and purified by reversed phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC). For labeling purposes, Alexa Fluor 488 Cys-P140 and Cys-ScP140 peptides were prepared, purified by HPLC and lyophilized in the dark [51].

Functional assays

Adhesion assay: GB cells (2 × 104) labeled with DiI solution were cocultured for 48 h (1:1) on top of a monolayer of WT, Mapt+/- or mapt−/− PCs pre-treated with 10 μM or 20 μM P140/ScP140 peptide for 1 h. For extracellular MAPT/tau experiments, MAPT/tau-441 (rPeptide, T-1001; 50 or 100 nM) was added 1 h before or 16 h after coculture. Cells were lysed with 0.1% (v:v) Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, X100) in PBS (Gibco, 14,200,075) after washing away non-adhered cells with PBS and remaining DiI fluorescence in the wells (belonging to the GB that adhered to PCs) was measured at 488 nm (588-nm emission) in a Synergy Mx plate reader (BioTek). PC-GB coculture measurements were normalized to single-cultured DiI-stained GB cells.

GB proliferation assay: WT, Mapt+/- or mapt−/− PCs (8 × 104) cultured in 24-well plates were pre-treated 1 h with different concentrations of P140 or ScP140 peptides (5, 10, and 20 μM) before being cocultured (1:1) with U87-luciferase GB cells. For extracellular MAPT/tau experiments, MAPT/tau-441 peptide (50 nM) was added 1 h before or 16 h after coculture. After 48 h, cultures were incubated with Rediject-D-Luciferin (PerkinElmer, 770,504), following technical specifications. Luminescence was read within 10 min, and plate was imaged using an IVIS Lumina II (Caliper Life Sciences) to quantify photons emitted per cell. Luminescence was related to the number of GB cells. PCGB coculture quantifications were relativized to single-cultured GB cells.

Cell death assay: 8 × 104 WT, Mapt+/- or mapt−/− PCs were cultured in 24-well plates and pre-treated for 1 h with peptide P140, ScP140 (10 μM) or NAc (Sigma, A9165; 20 mM) before being cocultured (1:1) with DiI-labeled GB cells for different time points. For extracellular MAPT/tau experiments, MAPT/tau-441 (50 or 100 nM) was added 1 h before or 16 h after coculture. The ROS scavenger NAc was used as a positive control of cell death since it prevents LAMP2A upregulation of GBCPCs [20]. At end points, supernatants containing dead cells and adhered cells were collected and labeled with NIR Zombie Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend, 423,105) or LIVE/DEAD Far-Red cell stain (Invitrogen, L10120) and analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer (BD Bioscience).

Transfections and reporter assay

All plasmid transfections were done using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, 11,668,019) in Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Media (Gibco, 51,985–026). For CMA activity assay, WT PCs (2 × 104) were transfected with the plasmid KFERQ-PA-mCherry [100]. A low-expression promoter was used, and reporter expression levels were carefully monitored to avoid interference with endogenous CMA activity. Twenty-four h post-transfection, cells were treated for 1 h with P140 or ScP140 peptide (10 μM) and were exposed to 405 nm light for 5 min to photoactivate PA-mCherry. Control, ScP140-, and P140-treated cells were all subjected to identical procedures of transduction and photoactivation, ensuring full comparability across experimental conditions. Immediately afterward, they were cocultured with GB cells at 1:1 ratio. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution from 24 to 48 h of coculture. This working solution was freshly diluted in PBS from a 20% PFA stock prepared from powder (Sigma-Aldrich, P6148). For macroautophagy monitoring, 2 × 104 WT PCs were transfected with the mCherry-GFP-LC3 plasmid [101]. Twenty-four h post-transfection, cells were treated for 1 h with peptide P140 or ScP140 before being cocultured (1:1) with GB cells. Cells were fixed at different time points and lysosome inhibitors (LI; NH4Cl 10 mM and leupeptin 100 mΜ [Sigma-Aldrich, AX12701; Roche, 11,017,101,001]) were added in the last 6 h before fixing with 4% PFA. Changes in the numbers of lysosomes labeled by the reporters were analyzed by fluorescence microscopy. Images were acquired with a Nikon Eclipse Ti microscope equipped with a 60× Plan Apo Vc objective (numerical aperture, 1.40) and a digital Sight DS-QiMc camera (Nikon) and 387 nm/447 nm, 543 nm/593 nm filter sets (Semrock), and the NIS-Elements AR software (Nikon). CMA activity was measured as the number of fluorescent puncta per cell. Macroautophagy flux was measured as the number of fluorescent red and yellow (red+green) puncta per cell. Quantification was performed using ImageJ software (Ver. 1.54; NIH, USA). All determinations were performed in at least three experiments.

Immunoblotting

For immunoblotting analysis, 0.5 × 106 WT or GFP-PCs were cultured in 6-well plates and pre-treated for 1 h with P140 or ScP140 peptides (10 μM) before being cocultured (1:1) with GB cells. Total cellular protein lysates were obtained after 24 h and 48 h. Lysosomal inhibitors (LI; NH4Cl 10 mM and leupeptin 100 μM) were added in the last 6 h. Lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (1% Triton-X 100 [Sigma-Aldrich, X100], 1% sodium deoxycholate [Sigma-Aldrich, D6750], 0.1% SDS [Sigma-Aldrich, L4509], 0.15 M NaCl [Sigma-Aldrich, S9888], 0.01 M sodium phosphate [Boston BioProducts, BB-190OS], pH 7.2). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation, and the protein concentration of the supernatant fraction was determined by a Bradford assay (Sigma, B6916). Primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-LC3 (1:5000; MBL International, PM036), rabbit anti-MAPT/tau (Invitrogen, PA5-27287), mouse anti-ACTA2/ASMA (Abcam, ab7817), rabbit anti-GFP (Abcam, ab6556), and rabbit anti-TUBA/α-tubulin (1:5000; Abcam, ab4074). The blotting membranes were developed with chemiluminescent reagents (Cytiva Lifesciences, RPN2232), according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer, and detected with an Amersham Imager 600RGB (GE Life Sciences). Quantification was performed using ImageJ software (Ver. 1.54; NIH, USA).

Xenografts

Cell pellets from human GB cell lines (U87 or U251) or patient-derived GSCs were grafted into the brains of 135 mice C57BL/6 (1 × 106 GB cells/mouse) under stereotaxic conditions. After isoflurane anesthesia, mice were placed in a mouse stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science, RWD-68019) and a craniotomy was performed in the parietal cortex (1.5-mm from the midline and 1.5-mm lateral to bregma) using a microdrill (RWD Life Science, RWD-78001). Cells were injected at a flow rate of 1 μL/min in the hippocampus (penetrating dorsoventrally from the brain pial surface 2.0 mm) using a 10 μL Hamilton 26 G syringe (Hamilton Company, 80,330) mounted on a Stoelting QSI Quintessential Stereotaxic Injector (Stoelting Co., 53,311). After injection, the needle was left in place for 2 min before slowly retracting and closing the wound. Between two- and three-week post-grafting, mice were treated with different therapeutic strategies to compare to those that were non-treated.

Therapeutic strategies

To perform a cell therapy strategy, pretreated PCs (WT or Mapt+/- or mapt−/−) with peptide P140 or ScP140 (10 μM) for 48 h, were enzymatic exofucosilated as previously reported [28,102]. Exofucosylation was used as cell-engineering tool to increase PC cell therapy recruitment to brain affected areas after tumor establishment [103]. Briefly, cells were resuspended at 2 × 107 cells/mL in fucosyltransferase VII (FTVII) reaction buffer composed of Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; Gibco, 14,025,092) containing 30 μg/mL fucosyltransferase VII (FTVII; R&D Systems, 6409-GT-020), 20 mM HEPES (Corning, 25–060-CI), pH 7.4, 0.1% (w:v) human serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, A1653) and 1 mM guanosine 5′-diphospho-β-L-fucose sodium salt (GDP-fucose; Sigma Aldrich, G4401), and incubated for 60 min at 37°C and 5% CO2. Peptide (P140 or ScP140) concentration was maintained during exofucosylation incubations. Then, five mice (for each cell strain) were injected intravenously with exofucosylated pretreated PCs (0.25 × 106 cells per tail; 100 μL) and compared to five untreated control mice.

To confirm PCs recruitment in the mice brain, tracking of Mapt+/- or mapt−/− PCs expressing GFP was followed. Three mice were injected intravenously with mapt−/− PCs and compared with three mice injected with control Mapt+/- PCs.

Two weeks after therapy, or 4 days after PC tracking, mice were sacrificed, and brains were fixed in 4% buffered formalin (Panreac Quimica, 252,931.9914). All the procedures described were repeated three times.

To perform the P140 peptide therapy in vivo, five mice were injected intravenously with four doses of different concentrations of peptide P140 or ScP140: 50 μg each per mouse (100 μL per tail of 2.5 mg/kg), or 25 μg (1.25 mg/kg) or 12.5 (0.6 mg/kg). The doses were given to mice on days one, two, five, and seven. For more information on the dosing schedule, please see Figure 4(A). At the 4th dose or one week after therapy, mice were sacrificed, and brains were fixed in 4% buffered formalin (Panreac Quimica, 252,931.9914). For peptide biodistribution, six mice were injected intravenously with Alexa Fluor 488-labeled peptide P140 or ScP140 (50 μg per mouse: 2.5 mg/kg; 100 μL per tail) and sacrificed after 24 h. Collected brains were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS. All the procedures described were repeated three times.

Chromogenic immunohistochemistry

GB patient brain samples and mouse brains were paraffin embedded and processed by the Pathology facility (IMIB Pascual Parrilla), as described previously [20,28]. Mouse brain samples from previous research in a GB mouse model that presented GB infiltration or GB elimination, depending on competent or deficient CMA PCs, (respectively) [28] were included in this study. Three-micron thick serial sections were obtained from each brain using an automatic rotary microtome (ThermoFisher Scientific). Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with mouse anti-STEM-121 (Takara, Y40410), goat anti-AIF1/Iba1 (Abcam, ab5076), rat anti-CD68 (AbDserotec, MCA1957T) and rabbit anti-MAPT/tau primary antibodies. Sections were then incubated with the corresponding 3–3′diaminobencidine (DAB) secondary antibodies (Vector Labs, SK-4100) and Mayer’s hematoxylin (Carlo Erba Reagents, LLG06272066) counterstained. Positive immunoreaction was identified as a dark-brown precipitate. An automatic digital slide scanner (Pannoramic MIDI II-3DHistech) and Quantitative Pathology & Bioimage Analysis Qupath-0.2.3 software were used for analysis of histological sections, and acquisition of images. Morphometric measurements and quantification of cells were also performed using ImageJ software (Ver. 1.54; NIH, USA).

Quantification of cells was performed by measuring the number of pixels per brain section or the number of positive cells around the grafted areas in at least four fields of at least, three brain sections of each mouse.

Fluorescent immunohistochemistry

Mouse brains were OCT embedded (Epredia, 6,769,006), and twenty-five-micron sections were obtained using a HM525 NX cryostat (ThermoFisher Scientific). Three-micrometers thick sections of paraffin-embedded GB patient brain samples were obtained using an automatic rotary microtome (ThermoFisher Scientific, HM355S). Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with previously described primary antibodies and rabbit anti-IFNG/IFNγ (Bioss, bs-0480 R), goat anti-PDGFRB (R&D Systems, BAF1042), goat anti-GFAP (Novus, NB100-53809), rabbit anti-RBFOX3/NeuN (Sigma, ABN78), mouse anti-ACTA2/ASMA, rabbit anti-MAPT/tau, and rabbit anti-LAMP2A (Invitrogen, 51–2200). Rabbit anti-MKI67/Ki67 (Invitrogen, MA5-14520) was used to analyze proliferative capacity of GB cells. Rabbit anti-GFP (Abcam, ab1218) was used for PC tracking. Fluorescent labeling was done using the corresponding secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A21206) or Cy5 (Invitrogen, A10524) and samples were counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen, D1306) prior to mounting with Dako fluorescent mounting medium (Dako Agilent, S302380-2). A TCS-SP8-MP-AOBS laser scanning spectral inverted Confocal Microscope (Leica Microsystems) was used to analyze the histological sections. Maximum-intensity projection of images was achieved with LASX software (Ver. 5.2.2., Leica Microsystems) and ImageJ software (Ver. 1.54; NIH, USA).

Flow cytometry analysis

Central draining lymph nodes of each mouse were obtained, and mononuclear cells were isolated using a 40-micron cell strainer. Cells were labeled using specific antibodies for murine CD4 (eBioscience, 45–0042-82), FOXP3 (eBioscience, 17–5773-82), CTLA4 (eBioscience, 17–1522-82) and PDCD1/PD-1 (Novus, NBP1-77276). Background nonspecific fluorescence was measured using control isotype antibodies. Labeled cells were analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD LSR Fortessa X-20 flow cytometer (BD Bioscience) and data was analyzed with FlowJo software (Ver. 10.0.7, TreeStar). All determinations were performed in at least n = 4 mice per experimental group and in three separate experiments.

ELISA

WT PCs (5 × 104) were incubated for 1 h with P140 or ScP140 peptide (10 μM) before being cocultured (1:1) with GB cells for 72 h. At end points, cell supernatants were collected and concentrated using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters 10k (Millipore, UFC5010). Supernatants from cultures of PCs transduced with lentivirus expressing hairpin versions of one of three sequences targeting exon 8a of the Lamp2 gene (shLamp2a) or empty vector (shCT) cocultured or not with GB cells for 72 h were obtained as described previously [20,27].

Mouse SPARC (ABclonal, RK13948), LUM (Arigo Biolaboratories, ARG81864), 25-HO vitamin D (Arigo Biolaboratories, ARG80775), IL1B (RyD Systems, MLB00C), IFNG (RyD Systems, MIF00), AGT (Abcam, ab245718) and MAPT/tau (Elabscience, E-EL-M1121) levels secreted by PC in the media were measured by sandwich ELISA with specific anti-mouse antibodies following the manufacturer’s recommendations in a Synergy Mx plate reader (BioTek). GB cell culture supernatant and concentrated cell culture media were used as negative controls.

Immunofluorescence

WT PCs (2 × 104) were cultured in 24-well plates and incubated for 1 h with P140 or ScP140 peptide (10 μM) before being cocultured (1:1) with GB cells for 48 h. Macroautophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA, 5 mM; Sigma-Aldrich, M9281) or proteosome inhibitor MG-132 (10 μM; Sigma-Aldrich, M7449) were added 6 h before being fixed, when required. PCs were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS, permeabilized with PBS containing 0.1% (v:v) Tween-20 (Sigma-Aldrich, P1379), blocked with FBS, incubated with primary and conjugated secondary antibodies and counterstained with DAPI and mounted with fluorescent mounting medium. Images were acquired with a Nikon Eclipse Ti microscope equipped with a 60× Plan Apo Vc objective (numerical aperture, 1.40) and a digital Sight DS-QiMc camera (Nikon) and 387 nm/447 nm, 543 nm/593 nm filter sets (Semrock), and the NIS-Elements AR software (Nikon). Fluorescence intensity was quantified per field in at least four fields relative to the number of PCs and performed using ImageJ software (Ver. 1.54; NIH, USA).

Statistical analysis

Differences between groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey-Kramer multiple comparisons posttest. Comparisons between data pairs were analyzed using a t test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 and indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001 or ns for no significance. Mice were assigned randomly to control, or treatment group and no animals were discarded from this study. Analysis was done with GraphPad Prism (version 8.3.0).
