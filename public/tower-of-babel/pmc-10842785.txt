Abundant binary promoter switches in lineage-determining transcription factors indicate a digital component of cell fate determination
Authors: Hongchuan Li, Md Ahasanur Rahman, Michael Ruesch, Caprice D. Eisele, Erik M. Anderson, Paul W. Wright, Jennie Cao, Shashikala Ratnayake, Qingrong Chen, Chunhua Yan, Daoud Meerzaman, Roshini S. Abraham, Aharon G. Freud, Stephen K. Anderson
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYPrevious studies of the murine Ly49 and human KIR gene clusters implicated competing sense and antisense promoters in the control of variegated gene expression. In the current study, an examination of transcription factor genes defines an abundance of convergent and divergent sense/antisense promoter pairs, suggesting that competing promoters may control cell fate determination. Differentiation of CD34+ hematopoietic progenitors in vitro shows that cells with GATA1 antisense transcription have enhanced GATA2 transcription and a mast cell phenotype, whereas cells with GATA2 antisense transcription have increased GATA1 transcripts and an erythroblast phenotype. Detailed analyses of the AHR and RORC genes demonstrate the ability of competing promoters to act as binary switches and the association of antisense transcription with an immature/progenitor cell phenotype. These data indicate that alternative cell fates generated by promoter competition in lineage-determining transcription factors contribute to the programming of cell differentiation.

INTRODUCTION

Soon after the completion of the human genome project, several aspects of the human genome became apparent: the small percentage of the genome that codes for protein (~2%); the abundance of non-coding RNA transcripts (70% of the genome is transcribed); and the significant frequency of divergent transcripts controlled by a central bidirectional promoter region.1,2 Approximately 12% of the transcription start sites (TSSs) of human genes are located on opposite strands within 1 kb of each other and were defined as bidirectional promoters with putative shared regulatory elements controlling the divergent transcription of linked genes. A subclass of these bidirectional elements (2.5% of human genes) contains opposing promoters producing convergent transcripts that would inhibit their concurrent activity. Most bidirectional promoters likely mediate coordinated regulation of the linked genes, since sense and antisense transcription was highly correlated in 89% of divergent gene pairs. However, a negative correlation between the expression of divergent transcripts was observed in 11% of the bidirectional promoters analyzed (~1% of all genes), indicating potential switch-like behavior.2

More recent studies have revealed that transcription of both DNA strands occurs in most enhancers and promoters. Global run-on sequencing of exonuclease-deficient cells has enabled the detection of short, rapidly degraded transcripts from the antisense strand of promoters previously described as unidirectional.3 This may reflect the opportunistic binding and entry of a second RNA polymerase complex to the open, nucleosome-depleted region created upstream from an RNA polymerase II complex that has initiated transcription but is stalled. The lack of splice donor sites and the presence of polyA-addition signals is associated with the rapid degradation of enhancer RNAs and antisense transcripts from gene promoters.4 A recent study demonstrated a direct role of proximal CTCF binding in blocking antisense transcript initiation from unidirectional promoters.5 Efficient bidirectional transcription of mRNA from the paired arrangement of genes is associated with higher rates of transcription, indicating that the head-to-head arrangement of housekeeping genes on either side of a GC-rich promoter region allows for maximal production of transcripts from essential genes.6

Closely spaced divergent promoter pairs may also act as binary switches and play a role in the programming of cell fate or the generation of diverse phenotypes within a given cell type. The ability of a bidirectional promoter to function as a probabilistic switch was demonstrated in the murine Ly49 gene cluster, a family of major histocompatibility complex class I receptors that are stochastically expressed by natural killer (NK) cells.7 A 119-bp core bidirectional promoter was able to generate divergent transcripts. However, only one direction was active at any given time, allowing it to act as a binary switch. Competition of transcription factors (TFs) for overlapping binding sites determined the relative strength of the competing promoters and the probability of sense or antisense transcription. Differences in the relative strength of the sense versus antisense promoters in each inhibitory Ly49 gene correlated with the percentage of NK cells expressing that receptor, supporting a model wherein competing sense and antisense promoters program selective gene activation.

Our in silico analyses of TF genes revealed that many possess spliced, polyadenylated, non-coding antisense transcripts initiated in close proximity to the TSS of the gene. This suggests that some TF genes may contain probabilistic binary switches that generate a variegated expression pattern of these TFs. A defining characteristic of a binary promoter switch is the presence of stable transcriptional states where only the sense or antisense transcript is generated over a sustained period. Our previous studies of the Ly49 genes demonstrated switch behavior using fluorescent reporter constructs. However, recent technological advances have provided additional methods to assay bidirectional transcription from genes in their native context. The development of 5′-directed single-cell RNA sequencing (5′scRNA-seq) allows the quantitation of sense and antisense transcription at the single-cell level and can identify the phenotype of cells with antisense transcripts. Improvements in the sensitivity of in situ RNA hybridization allow direct detection of individual sense and antisense transcripts in intact tissue. We performed 5′scRNA-seq of cultured human CD34+ hematopoietic progenitor cells (HPCs) and ex vivo isolated tonsil and blood innate lymphocytes, identifying multiple novel antisense transcripts in lineage-determining TFs. In situ RNA hybridization studies of sense and antisense transcripts from the human AHR and RORC gene promoters and 5′scRNA-seq analysis of lineage-determining TFs in cultured HPCs demonstrated that sense and antisense transcripts are not coordinately expressed, indicating that the expression of these TFs is controlled by binary promoter switches.

RESULTS

Divergent and convergent transcripts are enriched in human transcription factors

To explore the possible enrichment of divergent or convergent antisense transcripts in TF genes, the complete set of predicted human sequence-specific TFs8 was examined for the presence of antisense promoters near the TSS of the TF using the UCSC Genome Browser.9 Of the 1,640 likely sequence-specific TFs examined, 33% had a divergent antisense TSS within 1 kb (Figure 1A and Table S1), indicating an enrichment relative to the approximately 8.5% of all human genes that have a divergent antisense TSS within 1 kb. Analysis of the separation of divergent transcript TSSs in the TF genes produced a peak spanning 70–200 bp (Figure 1A), indicating the possibility of promoter competition generating switch elements. However, the profile of divergent TSS separation of TF genes observed is similar to that found by Trinklein et al.2 in their analysis of all human genes, the majority of which were found to be coordinately regulated. Genes with antisense transcripts opposing the TF promoter were also frequently found, comprising 30% of the TFs. Most of the convergent antisense transcript TSSs were located close to the 5′ end of the first intron of the TF gene, as shown by the clustering of antisense TSS within 1.5 kb of the TF gene TSS (Figure 1B). In contrast, only 2.5% of all human genes had a convergent transcript, and the distance from the gene TSS was evenly distributed.2 Remarkably, 18% of TFs possessed both divergent and convergent promoter pairs, including many identified as lineage-determining TFs (Table S1). This finding suggests that a common promoter structure may exist for lineage-determining TFs (Figure 1C), wherein a divergent bidirectional element creates variegated expression of the TF in progenitor cells and an opposing transcript from the intronic antisense promoter could either downregulate expression if both sense and antisense promoters are simultaneously transcribed, leading to RNA polymerase II collision and arrest,10 or silence gene expression through the production of double-stranded RNA11 and subsequent methylation of the competing promoter.12 This may provide a mechanism for progenitor cells to exit the multipotent state by selectively silencing competing lineage-defining TFs. Although it is highly likely that most opposing sense/antisense promoter pairs will either silence or downregulate gene expression, examination of expression patterns by RT-PCR, single-cell RNA-seq, or in situ hybridization in relevant cell types is required to determine whether there is competition or coordinated expression of divergent transcripts with TSS within 300 bp.

Lineage-determining transcription factors have multiple divergent and opposing promoters

5′scRNA-seq was performed on cultured CD34+ HPCs, innate lymphocytes purified from fresh human tonsil, and ex vivo peripheral blood NK cells to assess transcripts produced by proposed lineage-determining TFs acting during hematopoiesis and innate lymphocyte development. Antisense transcripts were frequently observed, with several genes containing multiple convergent and divergent promoter pairs (Figure 2A). The TSSs of divergent transcripts observed were separated by 70–245 bp, similar to the peak range of TSS separation observed in Figure 1. The level of sense and antisense transcription from six TFs implicated in the lineage determination of innate lymphocytes13 was determined by examining the read density of divergent transcripts in the Integrated Genome Viewer (IGV).14 This analysis revealed that sense and antisense transcripts from these bidirectional promoters were not highly correlated and varied significantly between different cell types and donor samples. For example, NFIL3 antisense transcription was dominant in tonsil innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) and one CD34+ HPC culture, whereas sense transcription dominated in a second CD34+ HPC culture from a different donor, and similar levels of sense and antisense were detected in peripheral blood NK cells (Figure 2B).

A potential role for GATA1/GATA2 antisense transcripts in the “GATA switch”

The GATA1 and GATA2 genes play essential roles in hematopoietic development. GATA2 is required for the expansion and survival of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells and for the development of mast cells.15 GATA1 is the master regulator of erythropoiesis.16 A switch in GATA expression occurs during erythropoiesis, characterized by silencing of the GATA2 gene and increased expression of GATA1.17 The antisense promoters opposing the GATA1 and GATA2 coding region promoters may play a central role in the switching of GATA expression that occurs during hematopoiesis (Figures 3A and 3E). To address this hypothesis, differential gene expression analysis of cells containing sense versus antisense transcripts from the GATA1 or GATA2 genes was performed using the cultured CD34+ HPC 5′scRNA-seq dataset. Cells expressing only GATA1 sense or antisense were primarily found in distinct cell populations as revealed by the feature plot shown in Figure 3B. In addition, sense- or antisense-only expressing cells comprised the majority (90%) of cells with GATA1 transcripts (Figure 3C). The 10% of cells expressing both sense and antisense may represent cells that have recently switched from sense to antisense transcription or vice versa, since the convergence of sense and antisense transcripts would be expected to block simultaneous transcription. Furthermore, there is only one active GATA1 allele per cell because the gene is located on the X chromosome, thus eliminating the possibility that cells expressing both sense and antisense transcripts have antisense transcripts derived from one allele and sense transcripts from the other allele. Differential gene expression analysis of cells expressing GATA1 sense versus cells lacking GATA1 transcripts produced the expected result of upregulated erythroblast-specific genes (Table S2). Remarkably, cells expressing GATA1 antisense displayed a mast/basophil phenotype and upregulation of GATA2 coding transcripts. Cells expressing both sense and antisense GATA1 transcripts exhibited a mixed phenotype with both erythroblast and mast cell markers upregulated. A comparison of cells expressing only sense versus cells expressing only antisense showed association of sense transcription with erythroblast and ribosome subunit genes, whereas antisense expression was again associated with a mast cell phenotype and upregulated GATA2 transcript levels.

Although the promoter structure of the GATA2 gene is more complicated than that of the GATA1 gene, with three sense and three antisense promoters (Figures 2A and 3E), the majority of the GATA2 sense transcripts in the CD34+ HPC cultures originate from the Pro1 promoter and are thus opposed by all antisense transcripts, with GATA2 asPro3 representing the major antisense promoter (Figure S1). A feature plot of cells expressing GATA2 Pro1 versus GATA2 asPro3 transcripts shows sense- or antisense-only cells present primarily in separate cell populations (Figure 3F). Cells expressing only GATA2 sense or antisense were detected, although cells expressing only sense transcripts were 8-fold greater in number than cells expressing antisense only (Figure 3G). Cells with both sense and antisense represented approximately 50% of the antisense-expressing cells, possibly due to sense and antisense transcription occurring on separate alleles. A differential gene expression analysis of cells expressing only GATA2 sense versus cells with only antisense defined two distinct populations: sense-only cells had a mast/basophil phenotype, and antisense only cells were enriched for erythroblast-specific genes (Table S2). These results suggest that the competition between GATA1 and GATA2 for commitment to either the erythroid or myeloid lineages is controlled by modulation of GATA1/GATA2 levels by antisense transcripts. The presence of four predicted GATA-binding sites in the GATA1 antisense promoter (Figure 3A) indicates the potential for feedback inhibition of GATA1 expression or competitive inhibition of GATA1 by GATA2. The GATA2 asPro3 promoter has a predicted GATA1-binding site and a predicted KLF1-binding site (Figure 3E), consistent with a competitive mechanism, with GATA1 stimulating GATA2 antisense and GATA2 activating GATA1 antisense.

GATA1 produces a silencing antisense transcript that spans two SUV39H1 exons

5′scRNA-seq of the cultured CD34+ HPCs indicated an additional functional role for the GATA1 antisense promoter. A previously undetected transcript derived from the antisense promoter in the first intron of GATA1 splices into antisense exons overlapping the second and third coding exons of the suppressor of variegation 3–9 homolog 1 (SUV39H1) gene located 76 kb upstream (Figure 3A). SUV39H1 is a histone methyltransferase that trimethylates lysine 9 of histone H3 (H3K9me3), which results in transcriptional gene silencing and heterochromatin formation.18 H3K9Me3 is suggested to reduce the plasticity of progenitors during lineage commitment, and inhibition of SUV39H1 enhances the generation of induced pluripotent stem cells.19,20 The predicted inhibition of SUV39H1 expression by the GATA1 antisense transcript was tested by CRISPR-mediated deletion of the antisense promoter from K562 cells. Decreased GATA1 antisense transcripts were associated with increased SUV39H1 transcripts (Figure 3D). A similar genomic arrangement of the GATA1 and SUV39H1 genes is observed on the mouse X chromosome, and a spliced non-coding transcript targeting the second and third SUV39H1 exons is also present, suggesting that a similar GATA1 antisense silencing mechanism may be acting in the mouse. Furthermore, a comparison of the human GATA1 antisense promoter region with the homologous region in the mouse revealed 81% nucleotide homology and the conservation of multiple GATA-binding sites (Figure 3A). Transgenic mice overexpressing SUV39H1 show impaired erythroid cell differentiation with enhanced proliferation of immature erythroblasts.21 Since GATA1 is a key lineage-determining TF in erythropoiesis,22 transcription from the antisense promoter in the first intron of GATA1 may maintain cells in the progenitor stage by inhibiting GATA1 expression and the histone methyltransferase required for lineage commitment. Alternatively, reduced expression of SUV39H1 may prime progenitors for erythrocyte differentiation, as suggested by the accumulation of immature erythroblasts in SUV39H1 transgenic mice.

Analysis of switch behavior in innate lymphocyte lineage-determining TFs

A detailed analysis of convergent and divergent pairs of sense/antisense promoters was performed for two TFs implicated in innate lymphocyte differentiation: AHR and RORC.23 An initial RT-PCR analysis of a human tissue RNA panel was conducted to confirm expression of novel spliced sense/antisense transcripts and to determine whether there was coordinate regulation of the divergent promoter pairs as predicted for most bidirectional promoters in human genes.2 All the transcripts identified by in silico analyses were detected, and the expression of divergent sense/antisense was not highly correlated, with many tissues expressing sense transcripts but lacking expression of antisense transcripts (Figure S2). In contrast, the convergent AHR sense and antisense Pro1 transcripts were found in all tissues. Multiple experimental approaches were used to further investigate potential switching between sense and antisense transcription states: in situ RNA hybridization; in vitro promoter assays; dual fluorescent protein vector assays; and differential gene expression analysis of 5′scRNA-seq libraries generated using purified innate lymphocytes from in vitro CD34+ cell cultures, tonsil, or peripheral blood. The results of these analyses are described for each of the two genes in the following sections.

Properties of the AHR bidirectional promoter and intronic antisense promoter

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), a member of the basic helix-loop-helix-PAS ligand-activated TF family, is responsible for regulating hypoxia, circadian rhythms, and other cellular processes such as differentiation and apoptosis through binding to the aryl hydrocarbon response element (AHRE; 5′-GC GTG-3′).24–27 Originally discovered for its role in sensing environmental toxins such as 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD), various additional exogenous AHR ligands have been identified. However, kynurenine, the first breakdown product in the indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase-dependent tryptophan degradation pathway, has been identified as an endogenous ligand that activates AHR.28

Visualization of the AHR gene in the UCSC Genome Browser and the Eukaryotic Promoter Database (EPD)29 revealed two coding-strand promoters initiating transcription either 613 bp (AHR-Pro1) or 27 bp (AHR-Pro2) upstream from the AHR translation initiation site (Figure 4A). There are multiple antisense TSSs contained within the first AHR exon and 5′ region of intron 1, with two major sites located 53 bp downstream of the AHR start codon and 34 bp downstream of the intron-exon junction. This indicates that an antisense promoter (asPro1; Figure 4A) is present in the first intron and opposes transcription from the AHR coding-strand promoters. An additional antisense TSS is located 205 bp upstream from the AHR-Pro1 transcript initiation site, indicating divergent bidirectional activity in this region indicated by asPro2 in Figure 4A. Therefore, the AHR gene contains both overlapping convergent and closely spaced divergent transcripts associated with two coding-strand promoters, consistent with the model shown in Figure 1C. Both antisense transcripts generate spliced, polyadenylated transcripts lacking significant open reading frames (Figure 4A).

The 218-bp sequence of the asPro2/AHR-Pro1 bidirectional promoter region shown in Figure 4A contains predicted binding motifs for SP1, MYC, and YY1 TFs that are enriched in bidirectional promoters.30 This element contains a predicted binding site for ZBTB14, a TF that has been implicated in the response to the AHR ligand TCDD,31 and an AHR-binding site, suggesting possible feedback modulation of AHR transcription. In addition, there are binding sites for ZNF93 and ZNF749, TFs associated with the silencing of retrotransposons in stem cells.32,33 When compared with the mouse gene, the human AHR bidirectional promoter has several regions of sequence homology, with conservation of the ZNF93, ZNF749, SP1-binding sites, and a homologous block of tandem GMEB1/CREB/MYC sites.

In situ RNA hybridization with a probe specific for the asPro2 transcript was performed on human heart tissue, which had the highest level of antisense expression of the tissues examined (Figure S2A). Although sense transcript was abundantly expressed, consistent with the RT-PCR results obtained with human tissues, antisense was only detected in a few cells. Additional human tissues were tested, including lymph node and tonsil. Weak expression of the asPro2 transcript was detected in a small number of cells in lymph node and tonsil tissue, indicating that antisense transcription may be a rare event associated with progenitor cells (Figures 4B–4D).

A 278-bp fragment containing the asPro2/AHR-Pro1 promoter region was cloned into the pGL3 luciferase reporter vector in both orientations, and the relative forward/reverse activity is shown in Figure 4E. Antisense promoter activity was dominant in four of the five cell lines, in contrast to the high expression of sense transcripts observed in the human tissues tested (Figures 4B–4D), indicating enhanced antisense activity in immortalized cell lines or that additional sequence elements are required for optimal forward promoter activity. The activity of the AHR element in primary human fibroblasts demonstrated dominant sense promoter activity, consistent with the tissue RT-PCR and in situ hybridization results (Figures S2 and4B-4D). The ability of TF-binding sites to modulate sense versus antisense transcription was interrogated by deletion of 10-bp segments containing the YY1-, SP1-, or ETS/SMAD-binding sites located in the 5′ half of the bidirectional promoter (Figures 4A and 4F). Figure 4G shows decreased sense and antisense promoter activity resulting from deletion of the YY1-binding site, and selective loss of forward transcriptional activity when the SP1 site was deleted. These results indicate a complex interaction between the competing TF-binding sites and are distinct from the results obtained in an analysis of the human KIR bidirectional promoters, where a 5′ YY1-binding site inhibited antisense transcription and a 3′ SP1-binding site inhibited sense promoter activity.34

To further demonstrate the switching activity of the asPro2/AHR-Pro1 element, it was cloned into the BII-tdT-inv-GFP vector such that antisense transcription would lead to EGFP expression and sense transcription would produce tdTomato protein (Figure 4H). Transient transfection of the HEK293T cell line generated cells expressing either EGFP or tdTomato alone as well as cells expressing either low levels of both (orange cells) or high levels of both (yellow cells), presumably due to transfection of multiple copies of the construct (Figure 4I). A higher percentage of cells expressing antisense only (green cells) was observed, consistent with the higher antisense activity of the luciferase reporter construct in HEK293T cells (Figure 4E). To further investigate the association of relative sense/antisense activity with the frequency of red and green cells observed, an additional two-color construct was generated using a 252-bp bidirectional element from the human GATA3 gene that exhibited balanced sense and antisense activity in HEK293T cells (Figure S4). This construct produced a population of cells with nearly equivalent numbers of red and green cells. These results demonstrate the ability of isolated divergent promoter elements to behave as stable binary switches capable of producing variegated expression of sense/antisense transcripts. The stable expression of either sense or antisense was confirmed by time-lapse imaging of transfected cells (Video S1). These results also demonstrate that co-expression of sense and antisense can occur in a single cell containing multiple copies of the reporter plasmid (bright yellow cells in Figures 4I and S4D), indicating that the TF milieu can bias the direction of transcription, but does not exclude the opposing transcriptional fate. In contrast, the bidirectional promoter controlling the expression of the divergent NUDT5 and CDC123 genes produced primarily orange cells, consistent with the coordinate expression of this gene pair35 (Figure 4J).

The AHR intronic antisense promoter is active during NK cell terminal differentiation

The discovery of an opposing promoter in the first intron of the AHR gene (asPro1), potentially acting as an off switch, is intriguing, especially with regard to a recent study demonstrating that AHR antagonists can enhance embryonic stem cell differentiation.36 If the role of the asPro1 promoter is to downregulate AHR expression in differentiating cells, it should be active in progenitors. Considering the recent finding that AHR inhibition favors NK cell differentiation,37 we generated 5′scRNA-seq libraries from peripheral blood NK cells. Analysis of the 5′ region of the AHR gene using the IGV14 revealed very few spliced as-Pro2 transcripts, consistent with the results obtained by in situ hybridization of tissues. However, a significant number of spliced asPro1 transcripts were observed (Figure 5A).

Bioinformatics analysis of single cells expressing AHR sense or asPro1 transcripts indicated that 6% of the total cell population expressed AHR transcripts (1,887/30,637 cells; Figure 5B). Of the AHR-expressing cells, 68% expressed sense transcripts only, 25% expressed antisense only, and 7.5% expressed both. These observations are consistent with independent expression of the opposing sense and antisense transcripts rather than coordinate expression.

To further explore the phenotype of the cells expressing AHR transcripts, a unified manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) of the single cells was generated, and the expression of sense or antisense AHR in distinct clusters was analyzed (Figures 5C and 5D; Tables S3 and S4). A rapid NK cell purification method was used to limit the time between blood collection and the scRNA analysis to obtain data that better reflects in vivo expression patterns. This resulted in a much larger number of clusters (Figure 5C) due to the presence of non-NK populations such as monocytes (cluster 9) and neutrophil/mast cells (cluster 15), but it also captured progenitor cell populations (clusters 7, 17, 19, 21) and multiple NK populations not previously reported. AHR sense was highly expressed by monocytes and myeloid progenitor cell populations, and antisense was preferentially expressed by immature CD56bright and lymphoid progenitors (Figures 5C and 5D; Tables S3 and S4). The group of clusters representing mature CD56dim NK cells had the lowest percentage of cells with AHR transcripts. The percentage of AHR sense expressing cells decreased from approximately 40% in progenitors to ~19% in immature NK cells and less than 2% in mature NK cells. This indicates that AHR transcription is downregulated during NK cell differentiation, in agreement with observations that AHR antagonism increases NK cell differentiation.26,37

In situ RNA hybridization of purified blood CD56bright and CD56dim NK cells was performed to confirm the results obtained by scRNA-seq. As shown in Figures 5E and 5F, AHR sense transcripts were abundantly expressed by CD56bright NK cells, with multiple transcripts per cell, whereas only a few cells displayed single sense transcripts in the CD56dim population. Antisense transcripts were less abundant, with only one transcript per cell in either the bright or dim population. Sense and antisense transcripts were expressed independently in the CD56dim cells, and several CD56bright cells were observed to express only antisense or antisense with weak sense transcript expression, possibly representing cells that have silenced AHR sense or had a recent switch in AHR transcription direction.

Differential gene expression analysis comparing AHR antisense or sense expressing cells with all others revealed a group of 73 genes that were significantly upregulated in antisense-expressing cells and 253 genes in AHR sense expressing cells (Table S5). Most of the genes associated with AHR asPro1 transcription correspond to those observed in the CD56bright subset of immature NK cells by previous investigators.38–40 Upregulation of EGR1, FOS, JUN, DUSP1/2, NFKBIA/Z, and SOCS1/3 was observed in cells with either sense or antisense, suggesting that AHR transcription is induced by growth factor/cytokine stimulation. Of particular interest is the upregulation of genes associated with mRNA deadenylation: PABPC1, BTG1, BTG2, and ZFP36L2.41 This group of genes has been shown to be important for the maintenance of progenitor cells in several systems.42–44 In addition, the upregulated TF NME2 has been shown to maintain the stemness of gastric cancer stem-like cells.45 Of the genes associated with AHR transcription, only ZFP36L2, BTG2, TENT5C, and GNLY were specifically upregulated in antisense-expressing cells. The association of genes involved in removal of polyA tails (ZFP36L2, BTG2) with the TENT5C gene, which encodes a non-canonical polyA RNA polymerase, is intriguing and may indicate that the AHR antisense promoter is active in progenitor cells that are transitioning from a progenitor state to a more mature cell type.

To determine whether the TFs upregulated in cells expressing AHR sense/antisense transcripts could have a direct effect on expression, the sequence encompassing the opposing AHR asPro1 and Pro2 promoters was analyzed. Figure 5G shows the sequence of the region containing the AHR Pro2 sense promoter and the asPro1 promoter. No binding sites for FOS or JUN were present within this region. However, predicted EGR1/WT1 binding sites are found 36 bp upstream from the major antisense TSS, and an additional EGR1 site is located 56 bp upstream of the TSS. This suggests that antisense AHR transcription may be triggered by growth factor stimulation through EGR1 activation, which is itself upregulated through FOS/JUN binding sites in the EGR1 promoter.46 To address the potential role of the EGR1-binding sites, a 375-bp fragment containing the opposing Pro2/asPro1 promoters was cloned into pGL3 in both orientations, and the activity of the competing promoters was analyzed (Figure 5H). The in vitro activity of a convergent promoter construct has not been previously studied, and it is complicated by several factors. The relatively low in vitro activity observed for this construct could be due to the mutual inhibition of the competing promoters as well as the presence of competing ATG initiation codons in the long 5′ untranslated regions and a splice donor site at the end of the first coding exon of the AHR gene preceding the luciferase ATG codon. A construct with mutations removing competing ATG elements and the splice donor site had approximately 2- to 3-fold higher activity (Figure 5I), indicating some inhibition of luciferase activity by competing ATG codons and the splice donor. The relatively weak activity of the AHR Pro2 sense strand promoter is consistent with an earlier report which showed that this promoter was significantly weaker than the upstream AHR Pro1 promoter, which possesses 23-fold greater activity.47 The effect of predicted EGR1/WT1-binding sites on the relative strength of the opposing promoters is shown in Figure 5J. Deletion of the first EGR1 site reduced promoter activity in both directions, deletion of the second AHR/EGR1 site increased antisense activity relative to sense, and deletion of the WT1 site had a minimal effect. These results indicate a role for EGR1 in modulating both sense and antisense activity and are consistent with the observation that both sense and antisense transcripts are higher in immature cells.

The RORγT promoter is bidirectional and produces spliced antisense transcripts

RORC, or RAR-related orphan receptor C, belongs to a family of hormone nuclear receptors that bind to specific ROR-response elements, which consist of a core 5′-AGGTCA-3′ motif preceded by a 5-bp AT-rich sequence.48 The RORC gene produces two protein isoforms, RORγ and RORγT, transcribed from alternative promoters. RORγ expression has been detected in many tissues, including liver, kidney, lung, muscle, and heart.49 RORγT, thus named due to its high expression in the thymus, is the master regulator of T helper 17 differentiation.50–55

Examination of the RORγT promoter region in the UCSC Genome Browser revealed the presence of a spliced antisense transcript initiating 192 bp upstream from the RORγT TSS. An additional antisense transcript originating in the first RORC intron, opposing the RORC promoter, was also observed (Figure 6A).

RT-PCR of sense or antisense RORγT transcripts on a panel of human tissue RNAs demonstrated the presence of sense and antisense transcripts in multiple tissues, with the highest expression in thymus (Figure S2). The ratio of sense to antisense varied among tissues, and several tissues exhibited only sense activity, while placenta only had antisense transcripts, indicating that sense and antisense activity were not co-regulated, supporting the possibility of promoter switching.

Examination of the RORγT bidirectional promoter region revealed an abundance of predicted TF-binding sites, including sites for other lineage-determining TFs AHR, EBF1, and EOMES (Figure 6A). In addition, a group of TF-binding motifs that are enriched in bidirectional promoters–MYC, NF-Y, and YY1–are also present.30 The RORγT bidirectional promoter region is highly conserved among vertebrates, and comparison of the human and mouse genes revealed 89% nucleotide identity, with conservation of predicted TF-binding sites (Figure 6A). A 282-bp fragment containing the bidirectional promoter region was cloned into the pGL3 luciferase reporter vector in both the sense and antisense orientations. Figure 6B shows the relative promoter activity of the RORγT bidirectional promoter in several cell lines. Antisense activity was dominant in the HEK293T, HeLa, HepG2, and YTS cell lines, while sense/antisense activity was similar in the Jurkat cell line and primary fibroblasts. These observations suggest that differences in TF expression in individual cell lines affects competition between TFs driving either sense or antisense transcription. The RORγT sense and antisense transcripts were examined in the human tonsil 5′scRNA-seq dataset. Figure 6C shows a feature plot of sense and antisense transcripts, and a Venn diagram quantitating sense- and antisense-expressing cells is shown in Figure 6D. Sense and antisense transcripts were observed in similar regions; however, sense transcription was observed in ~10-fold more cells than antisense. Differential gene expression analysis identified several genes upregulated in antisense-expressing cells that were also associated with AHR antisense (CRIP1, DUSP1, EGR1, FXYD5, JUN, and SELL). However, adjusted p values did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the low number of antisense-only cells (Table S6).

To determine whether the RORγT bidirectional promoter could function as a stable binary switch, in situ RNA hybridization was performed on human thymus tissue, which expresses high levels of both sense and antisense transcripts (Figures S2, S3, and 6E). The sense and antisense RORγT transcripts were primarily independently expressed, with only rare instances in which both were detected in a single cell (Figure 6E). These data provide direct evidence that the bidirectional RORγT promoter can function as a stable binary switch in vivo.

DISCUSSION

The ability of genomic DNA to program the generation of more than 400 differentiated cell types in the human body56 is a complex process controlled by the selective expression of lineage-determining TFs. Although the possibility that probabilistic promoter switches in TFs could program cell fate was proposed after their discovery in NK cells,57 recent developments in 5′scRNA-seq and in situ hybridization has enabled evaluation of putative switch activity in ex vivo cell populations and whole tissues. In addition, the vast wealth of high-throughput RNA sequence data across multiple tissues has revealed the presence of multiple alternative promoters in human genes. In the current study, our analysis of transcripts detected in human TF genes revealed enrichment for divergent and convergent sense/antisense promoter pairs (Figure 1). Both classes of paired sense/antisense promoters were demonstrated to behave as stable binary switches, supporting the existence of a digital program governing development.

It is well accepted that the spatiotemporal expression of TFs determines the pathways of cell differentiation during development. The actual mechanisms that program development may be analogous to the Boolean logic gates that have been used to program computers. The paradigm of logic gates controlled by multiple inputs has been applied to the complex interactions of TFs for some time.58 However, the ability of individual TF promoters to behave as binary switches adds a new dimension to the concept of biological logic gates. One can consider these binary promoter switches as logic gates, and the complexity of each gate is determined by the number of TF-binding sites that modulate the choice of either the “on” or “off” state. Existing algorithms to analyze logic gates59 are potentially compatible with the bidirectional and opposing promoter types of switches described in the lineage-determining TF genes in this study. TFs that stimulate the antisense promoter would be interpreted as negative signals inhibiting gene expression and would fit into the model. However, if one considers the possibility that the non-coding RNA may in fact influence gene expression, there are three possible states: sense, antisense, and “off.” In addition, the probabilistic behavior of these elements together with the observation that sense and antisense transcription can co-exist in the same cell further complicates modeling of the system. Finally, it is important to consider that in many cases there is not a single promoter governing the expression of a single TF gene, such as the multiple promoters observed in the GATA2 and GATA3 genes, suggesting the presence of cell-type-specific switches.

In addition to the switch behavior generated by competing promoters, multiple lines of evidence indicate that the expression of the non-coding RNAs generated by these switch elements have functional consequences. GATA1 antisense transcription opposes sense transcription and produces a spliced antisense SUV39H1 transcript. Thus, GATA1 antisense can both block erythrocyte differentiation and contribute to the maintenance of the progenitor state by silencing both GATA1 and SUV39H1. Competition between the GATA1 and GATA2 genes has been shown to affect the choice between the erythroblast and basophil/mast cell lineages. Remarkably, transcription from the intronic antisense promoters in either the GATA1 or GATA2 genes were associated with increased expression of the competing gene. This suggests that there is crosstalk between these two TFs, with an increase in one suppressing the expression of the other. The presence of multiple GATA-binding sites in the antisense promoters could either represent feedback inhibition or cross-inhibition by upregulating antisense transcription. Examination of the effect of overexpressing either GATA1 or GATA2 on antisense transcription or deleting antisense promoter elements may distinguish between these two possibilities.

A recent study has revealed a role for the GATA2-AS1 transcript in the hypoxic response, and common single-nucleotide variants in GATA2-AS1 exons were associated with early-onset coronary artery disease.60 The GATA3-AS1 transcript was shown to be necessary for the efficient expression of the GATA3, IL5, and IL13 genes.61 The proposed mechanism of action involved recruitment of the mixed-lineage leukemia (MLL) methyltransferase responsible for H3K4 methylation, and R-loop formation to tether MLL to the locus. R-loop formation may also account for the detection of only a single antisense RNA molecule per cell in the in situ hybridization studies of the AHR and RORC genes.

The frequent co-existence of two types of binary elements within a given TF gene raises the question as to why both opposing and divergent switches are required. The answer may be related to the amount of time a given gene will spend in the “on” or “off” states. The divergent class of bidirectional promoters would have the capacity to quickly switch between states, since a common control region is maintained in an active state. This may also provide an explanation for the observation of promoters in a poised or bivalent state, where the chromatin has the attributes of an active promoter but no coding transcripts are detected.62 The opposing promoter type of switch may produce more stable “on” or “off” states due to the epigenetic silencing of the downstream opposing promoter. The probabilistic behavior of bidirectional promoters together with the well-established phenomenon of transcriptional bursting63 could result in relatively long periods when TF mRNAs are absent, creating variegation expression of key lineage-determining TFs. This may be an important element contributing to the multilineage priming observed in progenitor cell populations.64–66 If one considers the two TFs involved in a binary fate choice between the granulocyte (Gfi1) and monocyte (Irf8) lineages, where there is a metastable mixed-lineage transcriptional state involving the low-level expression of both Irf8 and Gfi1,65 the relative levels of these competing TFs may be modulated by binary switch elements.

In conclusion, we have uncovered a novel mechanism contributing to the programming of cell fate through differential TF expression: the presence of both dynamic and static promoter switching mechanisms that can generate variegated expression patterns of lineage-determining TFs in progenitors, creating metastable mixed-lineage states.

Limitations of the study

The analysis of bidirectional promoter complexes in vitro is limited by the removal of the element from its native genomic context, especially regarding the amount of 5′ and 3′ flanking sequence, which can substantially affect sense/antisense transcription, as exemplified by the discrepancies observed between in vitro assays and in situ hybridization of tissue samples in our study. The use of immortalized cell lines for in vitro assays may also contribute to the differences in relative sense/antisense promoter activity when compared with normal tissue. Although the in vitro assay results do not accurately represent in vivo activity, they are consistent with binary switch behavior. The separate sense/antisense transcription states in the intact loci can be observed by in situ hybridization or 5′scRNA-seq. Future studies using RNA hairpins integrated into downstream introns will be required to examine switch behavior in real time. Measuring the degree to which sense and antisense transcripts are co-expressed by 5′scRNA-seq is limited by the inefficient capture of transcripts. scRNA-seq data can identify potential switch elements; however, most available datasets are biased toward the 3′ end of RNAs and contain very little information with regard to promoter behavior. It will be important to conduct additional 5′scRNA-seq on cell populations undergoing differentiation to identify additional lineage-determining TFs controlled by binary switch elements. Deleting or mutating antisense promoters in progenitor cells or generating mouse stains lacking these elements will be required to fully appreciate the role of competing antisense promoters in the programming of cell differentiation.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Cell lines and Cell culture

HeLa and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium and HepG2 cells were cultured in Eagle’s minimum essential medium. The Jurkat and YTS cell lines were maintained in RPMI1640 Medium. Each culture medium contained 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/ml each of penicillin and streptomycin (P/S), sodium pyruvate, and L-glutamine. All cell lines were grown in cell culture incubators at 37°C in the presence of 5% CO2. Human primary dermal fibroblasts were cultured in a 75mL culture flask using low serum (2%) containing fibroblast culture medium supplemented with 5 ng/mL rh FGF beta, 7.5 mM L-glutamine, 50 μg/mL Ascorbic acid, 1 μg/mL Hydrocortisone Hemisuccinate, and 5 μg/mL rh Insulin. Cells were incubated in 5% CO2 at 37°C and passaged every 48–72 h.

Human tissue samples

Human tissues were collected and used in accordance with protocols approved by the Ohio State University and the Nationwide Children’s Hospital Institutional Review Boards. Donor consent was acquired when deemed appropriate according to the approved Ohio State University Institutional Review Board protocol. Human pediatric tonsils were obtained fresh following same-day surgery from Nationwide Children’s Hospital (Columbus, OH) following informed consent and samples were de-identified before use. Leukocyte-depleted red blood cells were commercially obtained from Versiti Blood Center. Apheresis cones were acquired as a product of leukocyte reduction through Versiti Blood Center and processed within 24 h of collection. Additional healthy blood donors were recruited through the NCI-Frederick Research Donor Program (http://ncifrederick.cancer.gov/programs/science/rdp/default.aspx).

METHOD DETAILS

Cell isolation

NK cells were separated from the peripheral blood of healthy donors by Histopaque (Sigma-Aldrich) gradient centrifugation using the RosetteSep Human NK Cell Enrichment Cocktail (STEMCELL Technologies).

Human innate lymphoid cells were enriched from fresh tonsil tissue and blood samples as previously described.70 Briefly, single-cell suspensions from fresh human pediatric tonsils were generated by dissociation via a GentleMACS Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were resuspended in FBS (Sigma-Aldrich) with leukocyte-depleted RBCs and a custom human NK cell enrichment RosetteSep reagent containing a mixture of bivalent antibodies against glycophorin A and CD3, CD4, CD19, CD20, CD36, CD66b, and CD123 (STEMCELL Technologies). Cells were mixed with RBCs and RosetteSep reagent, incubated on a nutator for 30 min at room temperature, diluted in PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific), layered over Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare), and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 20 min at room temperature with the break off. The monolayers were harvested, and the residual RBCs were lysed using eBioscience 1 X RBC Lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Apheresis cones were flushed using 10 mL of PBS +1% FBS + 2mM EDTA and incubated on a nutator at room temp for 20 min with 500 μL of RosetteSep Human NK Cell Enrichment Cocktail (Stem Cell Technologies). Following the incubation, the cells were diluted in PBS and layered over Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare) and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 20 min with the brake off. Enriched mononuclear cells were then collected, and residual RBCs were lysed using eBioscience 1X RBC lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RBC lysis buffer was diluted in PBS, and cells were re-pelleted at 1800 rpm for 5 min. CD34+ hematopoietic progenitor cells were enriched using the Indirect Human CD34 MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Following cell sorting, cells were stained for flow cytometry and CD34+ cells were further purified via sorting to >99.9% purity on an FACSAria II cell sorter (BD Biosciences).

Sorting of CD56+ NK cells

Human NK cells purified from peripheral blood were washed two times in sorting buffer containing 1X PBS pH 7.4, 1% FBS, 25 mM HEPES and 1mM EDTA and pelleted by spinning at 200 RCF for 10 min. The samples were resuspended in 200 μL and stained on ice 30 min with APC conjugated anti-CD56 antibody (Beckman Coulter) and subsequently washed. CD56+ bright and dim cells were sorted into NK media (RPMI 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 0.29 mg/mL L-glutamine, 1mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM HEPES, 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol and 500 U/ml human recombinant IL-2) and allowed to recover 24 h prior to further analysis.

Cell culture and FACS

The culture media for in vitro CD34+ cell development experiments contained DMEM and F12 (2:1 ratio) supplemented with 1% antibiotic/antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 20 mg/mL ascorbic acid, 24 μM 2-ME, 0.05 mg/mL sodium selenite (Sigma-Aldrich), and 10% heat-inactivated human AB serum (Valley Biomedical). CD34+ cells were plated on OP9-DL1 murine stromal cells (provided by the Zúñiga-Pflücker lab at University of Toronto) in media supplemented with 10 ng/mL each of human Flt3 ligand, c-Kit ligand, and IL-3 (Peprotech). After two weeks of culture, wells were harvested, and cells were filtered through a 70 μm filter. Populations sorted included total CD45+ cells and CD45+CD13−CD14−CD34−CD161+. 5,400 of the CD161+ cells were added to 12,000 of the Bulk CD45+ to enrich for rare CD161+ populations and resuspended in RPMI +10% FBS prior to library preparation.

From the tonsillar ILC-enriched fractions, ILCs were further enriched using a FACSAria II cell sorter (BD Biosciences). Mature NK cells were sorted as Lineage-CD45+NKp80+ lymphocytes. Stage 4a NK cell precursors (Freud et al. 2016 Cell Reports) and ILC3s were sorted together as Lineage-CD45+NKp80-CD94+/−NKp44+/− lymphocytes. Early ILC/NK cell progenitors and ILC2s were sorted as Lineage-CD45+NKp80-CD94-NKp44-CD34+/−CD117+/−CD294+/−KLRG1+/− lymphocytes. Tonsillar sorted fractions were pooled at a 1:1:1 ratio and resuspended in RPMI +10% FBS prior to library preparation.

Single Cell RNA sequencing

Single NK cell suspensions were purified from the blood of four donors. Purified CD34+ and tonsil cells were from two donors each. Cells were washed with PBS +0.04% BSA at room temperature, resuspended in 500–1000 mL of the same buffer and counted. The cell viability of the samples was 93%–96%. Approximately, 10,000 cells were loaded on the 10X Genomics Chromium platform and libraries were constructed with a Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ reagent kit v2 dual index according to the manufacturer instructions. RNA/cDNA quality and quantity were determined using Agilent Tapestation 4150 (Agilent Technologies Inc., CA, USA). The libraries were sequenced on NovaSeq S1 100 cycles asymmetric paired-end run with read length of 10 bp for the sample index reads and 150 bp for the Read 1 and Read 2 to achieve a sequencing saturation about 70%.

Human tissue cDNA panels and RT-PCR

A panel of 20 normal human tissues was purchased from OriGene Technologies. RNA quality and quantity were determined using Agilent RNA 6000 Nano Chip (Agilent Technologies Inc). cDNA synthesis was carried out using Random Hexamer primer, Taqman Reverse Transcription Reagents kit (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RT-PCR reactions were performed in 25mL final volume containing 20 ng of cDNA, 1 x ZymoTaq PreMix (Zymo Research) and 10mM of each primer, respectively. The PCR conditions were as follows: 95°C for 10 min, 38 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 58°C for 45 s, and 72°C for 20 s. All primers used in this study are listed in the oligonucleotide primer table.

Generation of luciferase reporter plasmids

Promoter fragments from AHR, GATA3 and RORC were generated by PCR with ZymoTaq PCR Master Mix (Zymo Research) using the primers listed in the primer table. Inserts were ligated using GenBuilder Cloning Kit (Genscript) into pGL3-Basic (Promega) vector linearized with XhoI/HindIII (NEB) to generate constructs in both forward and reverse orientations. Each construct was verified by Sanger sequencing. Plasmid DNA for each verified construct was isolated using ZymoPURE II Plasmid Midiprep kit (Zymo Research). Preparation of two-color BII-tdT-inv-GFP vector for bidirectional promoter.

The BII-tdT-inv-GFP was obtained from Addgene (RRID:Addgene_133393). This vector contains both Green Fluorescent Protein and Red tdTomato genes in opposite orientations. The core promoter fragments from AHR and GATA3 were generated by PCR using the primers listed in primer table. PCR products were cloned into the two-color BII-tdT-inv-GFP vector by using the GenBuilder Cloning Kit (GenScript). The newly constructed vectors (BII-tdT-inv-GFP-AHR or BII-tdT-inv-GFP-GATA3) were verified by sequencing.

CRISPR deletion of the GATA1 antisense promoter

A 250 bp region encompassing the GATA1 antisense promoter was deleted using the 5′ guide sequence: TGGGATTGGAACGTTACCTG and 3′ guide sequence: TCACCAACGTGTGAGGTGCA. K562 cells were incubated in a 24 well plate at 2.5×105 cells per well one day prior to transfection. Cas9/sgRNA ribonucleoprotein complex transfection was performed using the Lipofectamine CRISPRMAX Cas9 Transfection Reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At 48 h post-transfection, the cells were harvested for analysis of genome modification efficiency using the screening primers listed in the primer table. Clones were screened by single cell dilution, and clones with deletions were selected for downstream assays.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Quantitative RT-PCR was conducted using the FastStart SYBR Green Master kit (Roche Diagnostics) on the 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The mRNA expression levels were quantified using the ΔΔCt method and normalized to HPRT1. GATA1 antisense, SUV39H1, and HPRT1 primers are listed in the primer table below.

The next day, 1 μg of BII-tdT-GFP vector containing a bidirectional promoter was mixed with 1 μL of Polyplus Jet-Optimus transfection reagent (Genesee Scientific) transfection reagent and diluted in 100 μL of Jet-Optimus buffer. Cell transfection and luciferase reporter assay

HeLa, HEK293T and HepG2 cells were plated at 2.0×105 cells per well in a 24-well plate the day before transfection and incubated overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2. For each well, 400 ng of pGL3 reporter construct plus 100 ng of Renilla luciferase pRL-SV40 control DNA was diluted in 100 μL of buffer. 1 μL of jetOPTIMUS (Genesee Scientific) transfection reagent was added and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The DNA mixture containing was then added to each well and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 48 h before analysis.

For suspension cell line transfection, 5×105 Jurkat or YTS cells were seeded per well in a 24-well plate the day before transfection. For Jurkat cells, 4 μg of reporter construct DNA, 400 ng of Renilla DNA, 5 μL of jetOPTIMUS transfection reagent were diluted in 200 μL buffer and incubated at room temperature for 10 min before addition to each well. For YTS cells, 4.5 μg of reporter construct DNA, 400 ng of Renilla DNA, 6 μL of jetOPTIMUS transfection reagent were diluted in 150 μL buffer and incubated at room temperature for 10 min before addition to each well. For primary fibroblast transfection, early passage cells were seeded the day before transfection in a 24-well plate at a density of 50,000 cells/well. The cells were transfected when cells reached a 50–60% confluency level. For each well, 2 μg of reporter construct DNA, 150 ng of Renilla DNA, 2 μL of jetOPTIMUS transfection reagent were diluted in 50 μL buffer and incubated at room temperature for 10 min before addition to the well. The DNA mixture containing plasmid DNA was then added to each well and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 12 h before Luciferase activity analysis. Luciferase activity was assayed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the culture medium was removed 48 h post-transfection and cells were washed with 0.5 mL of phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Cells were then lysed with passive lysis buffer. The suspensions were centrifuged at 14000 rpm for 1 min. A total of 20 μL of cell lysate supernatant was mixed with 100 μL of luciferase substrate, and the light units were measured on a luminometer. Measurement of the firefly luciferase activity of the promoter constructs was normalized relative to the activity of the Renilla luciferase produced by the pRLSV40 control vector and each construct was tested in duplicate in at least three independent experiments.

Bioinformatics methods

Quantification of the single cell transcripts was performed with Alevin tool in Salmon software (v1.5.2)67 for the NK cell data and Alevin-Fry (v0.8.1)68 and Salmon (v1.10.1)for the CD34+ and human tonsil cells. Refseq GRCh38.p13 (accession GCF_000001405.39) assembly transcripts with antisense AHR, GATA1, GATA2, GATA3 and RORγT transcripts included were included in the direct reference. Seurat (V4) R package was applied for the downstream single cell transcript analysis.69 Cell Ranger v6.0.2 analyzed cells from NK cells, CD34+ cells and tonsil cells were QC filtered for the unique genes detected, molecules detected and mitochondrial genome mapped reads (percent.mt < 10) to remove dead and low-quality cells separately. Filtered cells were extracted from the Salmon/Alevin quantification for normalization, clustering, visualization as well as differential expression (DE) analysis.

Integrated analysis was performed for the CD34+ cells with Seurat v4 FindIntegrationAnchors() and IntegrateData() functions and NK and tonsil cells were analyzed without integration. Data normalization performed with NormalizeData() function. Nearest neighbor graph with 35, 40 and 40 principal components explaining most variance generated, and clusters were identified at resolutions 0.3, 0.3 and 0.2 for cells NK, CD34+ and tonsil respectively. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualized at the same PC levels. Differential expression analysis was performed for each group compared to all other cells with Seurat’s FindMarkers() function with default settings. Identified markers were filtered for Bonferroni corrected adjusted p value <0.05. Differentially expressed transcription factors were identified from the human transcription factors database downloaded from http://humantfs.ccbr.utoronto.ca/index.php.
