Hyers-Ulam Type Stability of the Pexiderized Cauchy Functional Equation in Locally Convex Cones
Jafar Mohammadpour, Abbas Najati and Iz-iddine EL-Fassi

Abstract
. The foundation of locally convex cone theory relies on order-theoretic concepts that induce specific topological frameworks. Within this structure, cones naturally possess three distinct topologies: lower, upper, and symmetric. In this paper, we consider the Hyers-Ulam type stability of the Pexiderized Cauchy functional equation f ⁡ ( x + y ) = g ⁡ ( x ) + h ⁡ ( y ) f(x+y)=g(x)+h(y) in locally convex cones. Additionally, we present several significant corollaries that follow from our primary findings. Mathematics Subject Classification. 39B82, 39B52, 46A03. Keywords. Additive mapping, Pexiderized Cauchy functional equation, Hyers-Ulam stability, Locally convex cone.



1.
Introduction and preliminaries

The concept of locally convex cones was first introduced and further developed in
[
13
]
and
[
20
]
. A cone, denoted by

\mathscr{P}
, is a set equipped with two operations: addition
(a,b)\mapsto a+b
and scalar multiplication

(\lambda,a)\mapsto\lambda a
, where
\lambda
is a non-negative real number. The addition operation is assumed to be both associative and commutative, and there exists a neutral element
0\in\mathscr{P}
.
For scalar multiplication, the standard associative and distributive properties are satisfied. Specifically, for all
a,b\in\mathscr{P}
and
\lambda,\mu\geqslant 0
the following hold:

\lambda(\mu a)=(\lambda\mu)a,\quad(\lambda+\mu)a=\lambda a+\mu a,\quad\lambda(a+b)=\lambda a+\lambda b,\quad 1a=a,\quad\text{and}\quad 0a=0

It is important to note that the cancellation law, which states that

a+c=b+c
implies
a=b
, is not generally required in this framework.

A
preordered cone
is a cone
\mathscr{P}
equipped with a reflexive and transitive relation
\leqslant
which is compatible with both the addition and scalar multiplication operations. Specifically, for all
a,b,c\in\mathscr{P}
and
\lambda\geqslant 0
, the following properties hold:

a\leqslant b\implies a+c\leqslant b+c\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda a\leqslant\lambda b.

It is worth noting that the relation
\leqslant
is not required to satisfy anti-symmetry, meaning that
a\leqslant b
and
b\leqslant a
do not necessarily imply

a=b
.
Every ordered vector space naturally qualifies as a preordered cone. Additionally, the cones
\mathbb{\overline{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and
\mathbb{\overline{R}}_{+}=[0,+\infty]
, when equipped with the standard order and algebraic operations (notably,
0\cdot(+\infty)=0)
are examples of preordered cones. Every cone
\mathscr{P}
can be endowed with a natural preorder, defined by the relation

a\leqslant b
if there exists an element
c\in\mathscr{P}
such that
a+c=b
. This preorder structure is inherent to the cone and does not require additional assumptions.

A subset
\mathscr{V}
of a preordered cone
\mathscr{P}
is referred to as an
abstract 0-neighborhood system
if it satisfies the following conditions:

\rm(i)

0<v

for all

v\in\mathscr{V}
;

\rm(ii)

For any
u,v\in\mathscr{V}
, there exists an element
w\in\mathscr{V}
such that

w\leqslant u

and

w\leqslant v
;

\rm(iii)

u+v\in\mathscr{V}

and

\lambda v\in\mathscr{V}

whenever

u,v\in\mathscr{V}

and

\lambda>0
.

The elements

v
of
\mathscr{V}
define upper and lower neighborhoods for the elements of
\mathscr{P}
as follows:

\displaystyle v(a)

\displaystyle=\{b\in\mathscr{P}:~b\leqslant a+v\}\quad(\text{\rm upper neighborhood}),

\displaystyle(a)v

\displaystyle=\{b\in\mathscr{P}:~a\leqslant b+v\}\quad(\text{\rm lower neighborhood}).

The intersection
v(a)v:=v(a)\cap(a)v
defines the symmetric neighborhood of
a
. These neighborhoods generate the upper, lower, and symmetric topologies on
\mathscr{P}
, respectively.

Let
\mathscr{P}
be a preordered cone, and let
\mathscr{V}\subseteq\mathscr{P}
be an abstract 0-neighborhood system. The pair
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
is referred to as a
full locally convex cone
. Due to the inherent asymmetry of cones, asymmetric conditions naturally arise. For technical reasons, we require that the elements of a full locally convex cone
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
are bounded below. This means that for every
a\in\mathscr{P}
and
v\in\mathscr{V}
, there exists
\rho>0
such that
0\leqslant a+\rho v
.
Any subcone of
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
, even if it does not include the abstract
0
-neighborhood system
\mathscr{V}
, is termed a
locally convex cone
.
An element
a\in\mathscr{P}
is called
upper bounded
if, for every
v\in\mathscr{V}
, there exists a positive scalar
\lambda>0
such that
a\leqslant\lambda v
. The element
a\in\mathscr{P}
is said to be
bounded
if it is both lower and upper bounded. By defining
\xi=\{\varepsilon>0:~\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}\}
, the pairs
(\mathbb{\overline{R}},\xi)
and
(\mathbb{\overline{R}}_{+},\xi)
form examples of full locally convex cones.

Let
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
be a locally convex cone, and let
a\in\mathscr{P}
. The closure of
a
is defined as the intersection of all its upper neighborhoods, that is,

\overline{a}:=\cap\{v(a):~v\in\mathscr{V}\}.

It is straightforward to verify that
\bar{a}
represents the closure of the singleton set
\{a\}
under the lower topology
[
13
, Corollary I.3.5]
.
A locally convex cone
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
is said to be
separated
if, for any
a,b\in\mathscr{P}
, the equality
\overline{a}=\overline{b}

implies
a=b
. In other words, distinct elements in
\mathscr{P}
must have distinct closures. The locally convex cone
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
is separated if
and only if the symmetric topology on
\mathscr{P}
is Hausdorff (see
[
13
, Proposition I.3.9]
).

For a net
(a_{i})_{i\in I}
in
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
, we say that it converges to an element
a\in\mathscr{P}
with respect to the symmetric relative topology of
\mathscr{P}
if,
for every
v\in\mathscr{V}
, there exists an index
i_{0}\in I
such that
a_{i}\leqslant a+v
for all
i\geqslant i_{0}
. The net
(a_{i})_{i\in I}
is called a (symmetric)
Cauchy net
if for every
v\in\mathscr{V}
, there exists an index
i_{0}\in I
such that
a_{i}\leqslant a_{j}+v
for all
i,j\geqslant i_{0}
. Clearly, if a net converges, it must also be a Cauchy net. A locally convex cone
(\mathscr{P},\mathcal{V})
is said to be (symmetric)
complete
if every (symmetric) Cauchy net in
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
converges to an element in
\mathscr{P}
.

A locally convex cone
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
is called a
uc-cone
if
\mathscr{V}=\{\lambda w:\lambda>0\}
for some
w\in\mathscr{V}
. In this case, the element
w
is referred to as the
generating element
of
\mathscr{V}
, and all elements of
\mathscr{V}
are bounded . If
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
is a uc-cone and
\mathscr{P}
is simultaneously a real vector space,
then
\mathscr{P}
becomes a seminormed space under the symmetric topology of
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
. When
\mathscr{V}=\{\lambda w:\lambda>0\}
, the seminorm
q:\mathscr{P}\to[0,+\infty]
is defined as:

(1.1)

\displaystyle q(a)=\inf\{\mu>0:\mu^{-1}a\in w(0)w\},\quad a\in\mathscr{P}.

If the symmetric topology on
\mathscr{P}
is Hausdorff, then
q
becomes a norm on
\mathscr{P}
(see
[
3
]
for further details).

Let us recall that an equation is said to be Ulam stable in a class of mappings provided each mapping from this class fulfilling our equation "approximately" is "near" to its actual solution. It is well known that the problem of the stability of homomorphisms of metric groups (in other words, the Cauchy functional equation) was posed by Ulam
[
21
]
in 1940. In the next year Hyers
[
10
]
gave the
first affirmative answer of the Ulam’s problem for Banach spaces.

Hyers’s Theorem was generalized by Aoki
[
2
]
for additive mappings in 1950, and independently, by Rassias
[
18
]
in 1978 for linear mappings considering the Cauchy difference controlled by the sum of powers of norms. This type of stability is called
Hyers-Ulam-Rassias stability
. On the other hand, Rassias
[
19
]
considered the Cauchy difference controlled by the product of different powers of norms. Gǎvruta
[
9
]
has generalized the result of Rassias
[
18
]
, which permitted the Cauchy difference to become arbitrary unbounded. The stability problems of several functional equations have been extensively investigated in various spaces by a number of authors, and a large list of references can be found, for example, in
[
4
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
12
,
16
,
17
,
15
]
.

In the recent decades, the stability of functional equations have been investigated by many mathematicians. They have so many applications in Information Theory, Physics, Economic Theory, Pure Mathematics, and Social and
Behavior Sciences. These applications are sources of inspiration, attraction, and
influences to bring more and more people to this interesting and ever-growing
branch of mathematics (see, e.g.
[

,
5
]
).

This research aims to study the stability in the Ulam sense of the Pexiderized Cauchy functional equation
f(x+y)=g(x)+h(y)
in locally convex cones. As consequences, we give some important corollaries from our main result.

2.
Main Results

We begin this section with the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
be a separated locally convex cone, and let
a,b\in\mathscr{P}
. If
a\leqslant b+v
and
b\leqslant a+v
hold for every
v\in\mathscr{V}
, then
a=b
.

Proof.

To prove
a=b
, it suffices to show that
\overline{a}=\overline{b}
. Let
x\in\overline{a}
. Then
x\leqslant a+\frac{1}{2}v
for all
v\in\mathscr{V}
. Since
a\leqslant b+\frac{1}{2}v
, it follows that
x\leqslant b+v
for all
v\in\mathscr{V}
, which implies
x\in\overline{b}
. This demonstrates that
\overline{a}\subseteq\overline{b}
. By a similar argument, we can show that
\overline{a}\subseteq\overline{b}
, leading to
\overline{a}=\overline{b}
. Because
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
is a separated locally convex cone, we conclude that
a=b
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
(\mathscr{P},\mathscr{V})
be a separated locally convex cone, and let
a,b\in\mathscr{P}
. If
a+v=b+v
for every
v\in\mathscr{V}
, then
a=b
.

Lemma 2.3

(

\displaystyle g(0)+h(y)

\displaystyle\leqslant v+f(y)\leqslant A(y)+(1+\delta)v,

\displaystyle A(y)

\displaystyle\leqslant f(y)+\delta v\leqslant g(0)+h(y)+(1+\delta)v

for all
y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. This demonstrates that
A(y)\in(1+\delta)v(h(y)+g(0))(1+\delta)v
for all
y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
.

To prove the uniqueness of
A
, let
\widetilde{A}:\mathscr{P}_{1}\to\mathscr{P}_{2}
be another mapping such that
\widetilde{A}(x)\in(\delta v)(f(x))(\delta v)
for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. Thus,

\widetilde{A}(x)\leqslant\delta v+f(x),\quad f(x)\leqslant\widetilde{A}(x)+\delta v,\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}.

Consequently, since
\widetilde{A}
is additive, we obtain

(2.17)

\widetilde{A}(x)\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2^{n}}v+\frac{f(2^{n}x)}{2^{n}},\quad\frac{f(2^{n}x)}{2^{n}}\leqslant\widetilde{A}(x)+\frac{\delta}{2^{n}}v,\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1},~n\in\mathbb{N}.

Let
u\in\mathscr{V}
be an arbitrary element. There exists a natural number
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\frac{\delta}{2^{n}}v\leqslant u,\quad 0\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2^{n}}v+u,\quad\frac{f(2^{n}x)}{2^{n}}\leqslant A(x)+u,\quad A(x)\leqslant\frac{f(2^{n}x)}{2^{n}}+u,\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}.

Thus from (
2.17
), we derive

\widetilde{A}(x)\leqslant A(x)+2u,\quad A(x)\leqslant\widetilde{A}(x)+2u,\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}.

Using Lemma
2.1
, we conclude that
\widetilde{A}(x)=A(x)
for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. This proves the uniqueness of
A
.
∎

Theorem 2.11
.

Consider a locally convex cone
(\mathscr{P}_{1},\mathscr{V}_{1})
and let
(\mathscr{P}_{2},\mathscr{V}_{2})
be a separated, full uc-cone that simultaneously forms a real vector space. Suppose
\mathscr{P}_{2}
is complete with respect to its symmetric topology. If for some
\varepsilon>0
, the mappings
f,g,h:\mathscr{P}_{1}\to\mathscr{P}_{2}
satisfy

(2.18)

f(x+y)\in(\varepsilon v)(g(x)+h(y))(\varepsilon v)

for all
x,y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
, where
v
is the generating element of
\mathscr{V}_{2}
, then there exists a unique additive mapping
A:\mathscr{P}_{1}\to\mathscr{P}_{2}
such that

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle\in((4r\varepsilon+\beta)v)(f(x))((4r\varepsilon+\beta)v),

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle\in((5r\varepsilon+\gamma)v)(g(x))((4r\varepsilon+\gamma)v),

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle\in((5r\varepsilon+\delta)v)(h(x))((5r\varepsilon+\delta)v)

for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1},~r>1
, and some positive constants
\beta,\gamma,\delta
.

Proof.

Since the symmetric topology on
\mathscr{P}_{2}
is Hausdorff (cf.

[
13
]
, I.3.9.), the pair
(\mathscr{P}_{2},\mathscr{V}_{2})
forms a Banach space when equipped with the norm

q
given in (
1.1
). Additionally, because
\mathscr{P}_{2}
is a vector space, every element of
\mathscr{P}_{2}
is bounded, ensuring that
q(a)<\infty
for all
a\in\mathscr{P}_{2}
. Consequently, from (
2.18
), we derive

f(x+y)-g(x)-h(y)\in\varepsilon(v(0)v)

for all
x,y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. By the definition (
1.1
), we have

(2.19)

\displaystyle q(f(x+y)-g(x)-h(y))\leqslant\varepsilon

for all
x,y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. By using Hyers’s method
[
10
,
11
,
14
]
and letting
y=x
in (
2.19
), we obtain the inequality

(2.20)

\displaystyle q(f(2x)-g(x)-h(x))\leqslant\varepsilon

for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. Next, substituting
y=0
in (
2.19
) yields

(2.21)

\displaystyle q(f(x)-g(x)-h(0))\leqslant\varepsilon

for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. Similarly, taking
x=0
in (
2.19
) gives

(2.22)

\displaystyle q(f(y)-g(0)-h(y))\leqslant\varepsilon

for all
y\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. Finally, setting
y=0
in (
2.22
) in (
2.22
) leads to

(2.23)

\displaystyle q(f(0)-g(0)-h(0))\leqslant\varepsilon.

From (
2.20
), (
2.21
), (
2.22
) and (
2.23
), we obtain

\displaystyle q(f(2x)-2f(x)+f(0))

\displaystyle\leqslant q(f(2x)-g(x)-h(x))+q(-f(x)+g(x)+h(0))

\displaystyle\quad+q(-f(x)+h(x)+g(0))+q(f(0)-g(0)-h(0))

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\varepsilon

for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. So,

(2.24)

\displaystyle q\left(\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}f(2^{n+1}x)-\frac{1}{2^{m}}f(2^{m}x)+\sum_{k=m}^{n}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}f(0)\right)\leqslant\sum_{k=m}^{n}\frac{2\varepsilon}{2^{k}}

for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
and
n\geqslant m\geqslant 0
.
From (
2.24
), it follows that the sequence
\left\{\frac{1}{2^{n}}f(2^{n}x)\right\}_{n\geqslant 1}
forms a Cauchy sequence in the Banach space

(\mathscr{P}_{2},q)
. Due to the completeness of the space, this sequence converges with respect to the norm
q
. We can therefore define a mapping

A:\mathscr{P}_{1}\to\mathscr{P}_{2}

by taking the limit:

A(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2^{n}}f(2^{n}x),\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}.

Taking
m=0
and passing to the limit as
n\to\infty
in (
2.24
), we obtain

(2.25)

\displaystyle q\left(A(x)-f(x)+f(0)\right)\leqslant 4\varepsilon,\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}.

Consequently, for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
and
r>1
, we have
A(x)+f(0)-f(x)\in 4r\varepsilon(v(0)v)
. This implies that

A(x)\in(4r\varepsilon v)(f(x)-f(0))(4r\varepsilon v),\quad x\in\mathscr{P}_{1},~r>1.

From (
2.21
) and (
2.25
), we obtain

\displaystyle q\left(A(x)+f(0)-g(x)-h(0)\right)\leqslant

\displaystyle q\left(A(x)+f(0)-f(x)\right)+q\left(f(x)-g(x)-h(0)\right)\leqslant 5\varepsilon

for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
. Also, from (
2.22
) and (
2.25
), we get

\displaystyle q\left(A(x)+f(0)-h(x)-g(0)\right)\leqslant

\displaystyle q\left(A(x)+f(0)-f(x)\right)+q\left(f(x)-h(x)-g(0)\right)\leqslant 5\varepsilon

for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
.
The same way we did before, we conclude that

A(x)\in(5r\varepsilon v)(g(x)-f(0)+h(0))(5r\varepsilon v)

and

A(x)\in(5r\varepsilon v)(h(x)-f(0)+g(0))(5r\varepsilon v)

for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
and all
r>1
.
Since
f(0)
,
g(0)
and
h(0)
are bounded, there exist positive real numbers
\beta,\gamma
and
\delta
such that we deduce

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle\in((4r\varepsilon+\beta)v)(f(x))((4r\varepsilon+\beta)v),

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle\in((5r\varepsilon+\gamma)v)(g(x))((4r\varepsilon+\gamma)v),

\displaystyle A(x)

\displaystyle\in((5r\varepsilon+\delta)v)(h(x))((5r\varepsilon+\delta)v)

for all
x\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
and all

. This completes the proof.
