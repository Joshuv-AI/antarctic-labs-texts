Assessment of the relationships between leader behavior, organizational silence, work engagement, and extra role behaviors in hospitals
Authors: Ahmet Y Yesildag, Ece Ugurluoglu Aldogan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHospitals are institutions that provide highly specialized services in the face of various challenges, often in environments where there are insufficient human resources. Such an environment makes it necessary and important for employees to voluntarily go beyond their defined roles and exert extra effort. In this context, the research was conducted to examine the effect of leader behavior on extra-role behaviors, as well as the serial and parallel mediating effects of organizational silence and work engagement in this relationship. First, the R.I.G.H.T. (initials letters of dimensions) leadership model, a leader behavior model that addresses psychologically healthy workplaces from a transformational leadership perspective, was adapted into Turkish. Data were collected from 176 physicians and nurses working in a university hospital for the adaptation study. The data for the main research were collected from 307 physicians and nurses working in a public training and research hospital and a private hospital in Trabzon. The hypotheses in the research model were tested using the SPSS program with the PROCESS Macro add-on. As a result of the study, it was found that R.I.G.H.T. leader behaviors had positive effects on extra-role behavior. However, when work engagement and organizational silence variables were included in the model, the explained variance increased (R2:0.26) and the total effect of leader behavior became insignificant. Therefore, it was observed that leader behavior contributed positively to extra-role behavior by increasing engagement and partially reducing organizational silence. However, the serial mediating effect of leader behavior, organizational silence, engagement, and extra-role behavior could not be confirmed. The research found that leader behaviors in hospitals play an important role in encouraging employees to exhibit extra roles. However, this interaction occurs indirectly through different concepts, similar to the evidence in the literature. It is essential for physician and nurse leaders to be aware of their employees’ abilities and to benefit from and utilize their talents, knowledge, and skills when making decisions. This enables leaders to support their development and progress, ultimately leading to increased employee engagement in their roles. This situation can also trigger them to exert effort beyond their job descriptions towards their colleagues, patients, and the institution.Keywords: Extra role behavior, Work engagement, R.I.G.H.T. leadership, Hospital management, Silence

Introduction

Leadership is fundamental for social organizations, including healthcare institutions, that wish to sustain their existence [1]. Historically, leadership has been examined in religious texts and Ancient Greek writings even before the 13th century. However, it began conceptualizing after the 17th century and was intensively studied in the mid-20th century [2, 3]. Bass and Bass [4] review over 221 definitions of leadership from the 1990s, noting that definitional variation reflects differences in perspective, and highlight House’s (1995) definition — “the ability to influence, motivate, and enable others to contribute to the effectiveness and success of the organizations of which they are members” — as particularly inclusive.

Leaders’ positive attitudes and approaches toward employees directly affect employee behaviors [5–9]. In the healthcare sector, the importance of leadership is further highlighted by the structural and functional challenges of the sector. In healthcare institutions where human resources are insufficient under challenging working conditions [10], effective leadership is vital in terms of collaboration, productivity needs, efficiency expectations, and service quality [11–13]. At this point, a leadership approach that considers employee participation and motivation should be adopted.

In addition to an environment where the number of healthcare workers worldwide is insufficient, factors such as high employee turnover due to the pandemic [14], on-the-job training and orientation being in a crucial position, together with health-related characteristics such as the simultaneous reproduction and instant consumption of services in a labor-intensive environment, these challenging conditions require healthcare workers to do more than expected. Especially in a country such as Turkey, where the number of personnel per capita is among the lowest in OECD averages [15], it becomes inevitable for employees to make extraordinary efforts. In an environment where healthcare workers are exhausted, leave their jobs, face physically and mentally demanding working conditions, and where communication and relational problems negatively affect collaboration [16–19], the importance of building and maintaining a psychologically healthy organization is clear.

An organization where employees can express their opinions without hesitation is described as a fearless and psychologically healthy organization, and it is emphasized that leader behaviors are critical in achieving this [20, 21]. For this reason, the R.I.G.H.T. Leadership model, which examines leader behavior by interpreting the American Psychological Association’s (APA) Psychologically Healthy Workplace Model (PHWM) within the context of transformational leadership, was used as the primary independent variable in the study [22].

Biricik Gulseren et al. [23] explain the dimensions: the recognition dimension is about praising individuals or a job well done and rewarding them using positive reinforcement. It is about recognizing employees and being aware of their potential. It is directly related to the motivation dimension of transformational leadership and the appreciation aspect of the PHWM. The involvement dimension concerns employee participation in decisions. It is directly related to the individual consideration dimension of transformational leadership and the employee involvement dimension of the PHWM. The growth and development dimension supports employees’ career development and increases their competencies. It is directly related to the intellectual stimulation dimension of transformational leadership and the growth and development dimensions of the PHWM. The health and safety dimension is about valuing employees’ physical, mental, and social well-being. It is related to the individualized consideration dimension of transformational leadership and the health and safety and work-life balance dimensions of the PHWM. The last dimension in the model, Teamwork, focuses on supporting employees’ collaborative skills and fostering a teamwork culture. The developers of the R.I.G.H.T. Leadership Scale, which was developed to measure these dimensions, states that psychologically healthy workplaces are important for organizational productivity and continuity. Unlike transformational leadership, the R.I.G.H.T. leadership model focuses on organizational health and team dynamics, providing a more holistic and inclusive theoretical framework for creating psychologically healthy workplaces. Transformational leadership focuses more on individual development, motivation-based behavior; collective well-being dimensions such as health, safety, awareness, and teamwork are secondary in this model [24].

In psychologically healthy organizations, employees are motivated to speak up or are not subjected to any pressure to remain silent. In environments where this is not the case, especially in the healthcare sector, opinions indirectly or directly hindered by the manager or leader can negatively affect professionalism and service delivery [25]. However, the harms or benefits are not limited to these. In the context of the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, it is argued that in psychologically healthy workplaces, employees achieve higher performance and work engagement levels, express more ideas and opinions, experience reduced stress and conflict, and achieve learning, innovation, and development and that leadership plays an important role in all of these situations [26–28]. According to COR theory, a psychologically safe environment enables employees to express their ideas without the fear of resource loss, which leads to silence being reduced and allows their resources to be preserved. Simultaneously, employees are encouraged by this climate of trust to use their existing resources to achieve new gains. Through this motivational path, the development of positive attitudes and behaviors, such as work engagement, is fostered. Thus, the dual mechanism of psychological safety—whereby silence is prevented and engagement is enhanced—is revealed by COR theory [28]. Based on this theoretical foundation, the following hypotheses have been developed;

R.I.G.H.T. leader behaviors will directly reduce organizational silence (H1a) and increase work engagement (H1b)

Daniel Katz [29] stated that those who voluntarily do more than what is expected, defined as extra roles, are very lucky, and these individuals are invaluable. Considering the current service delivery model, the quality of service, and the characteristics of providers and consumers, it is recommended that healthcare workers be innovative, collaborative, and highly diligent [30]. Ürek et al. [31] state that it is tough for organizations where employees fulfill only their defined roles and do not make extra efforts for the organization’s success to survive in today’s competitive environment. Lee [32] similarly stated that since each type of care is unique, healthcare personnel should make extra efforts by helping each other and taking the initiative beyond specific and limited roles while performing their daily routine patient care. Given the critical role of leadership in fostering such an environment, the first and fundamental hypothesis of this research is that;

H1c

Leader behaviors directly affects extra-role behavior.

Systematic reviews indicate that the relationship between leadership, which promotes psychologically healthy workplaces and extra-role behavior is worth examining [33]. However, how this interaction occurs is unclear. Owing to the nature of the concepts, leadership and extra-role behavior are closely related to other common concepts in theory and practice. Katz [29], who criticizes an organization where employees exhibit silence behaviors for not utilizing its potential, states that as a requirement of extra-role behavior, employees should make constructive suggestions regarding the organization and the functioning of work. In addition, Van Dyne and LePine [34], who made significant contributions to extra-role behavior theory, are known to have structured employee voice behaviors in their models in a way that is related to extra-role behavior. Therefore, organizational silence is a concept related to organizational performance and extra-role behavior. The debate about the context of voice and silence in organizations continues. Some researchers argue that they are distinct concepts with different premises [35]. However, some research supports the idea that these two concepts are at opposite ends of the same continuum, suggesting that voice and silence operate through similar judgments and that a low level of voice may imply silence [36]. Kish-Gephart et al. [37] describe silence as “an individual’s failure to voice suggestions, opinions, and concerns about processes, products, or people in organizations”, which is a challenge to extra role behavior. In a way, this relationship, which constitutes the basic query of this research model, is included in the research model. Based on this, the following hypothesis has been developed;

H1d

Organizational silence will negatively affect the exhibition of extra-role behaviors.

Shanafelt and Noseworthy [38] noted that current developments pose unprecedented threats to the survival of healthcare institutions and stated that physicians should work productively in cooperation with management in response to this situation. However, they add that improvements can be made by developing work engagement, which is the antithesis of burnout, given the reality that a large proportion of physicians are already experiencing burnout. However, it is emphasized that employee silence is a significant barrier to developing work engagement in healthcare workers [39]. Mohammadi [40] suggested that organizational silence reduces employees’ work engagement levels and organizational health in her study with healthcare workers in Ahvaz. Similarly, a study conducted in Turkey emphasized that the silence levels of healthcare workers negatively affect their work engagement [41]. A similar situation has been confirmed with nurses in Punjab, India [42]. Based on this, the following hypothesis has been developed;

H1f

Organizational silence negatively affects work engagement (H1f).

Aboramadan et al. [43] conducted a study with employees of an organization providing health and social services in Palestine to test the mediating effect of work engagement on the relationship between leader attitudes and extra-role behavior. Their studies revealed that servant leadership affected extra-role behavior and employees’ work engagement levels. In the aforementioned study, the effect of the leadership approach on engagement was found to be much greater. The study also found that work engagement positively affects the level of extra-role behavior. In addition, work engagement was found to affect the model at a level close to full mediation.

Walker [44] reported that participants’ perceptions of transformational and transactional leadership were low, and that their extra-role behavior attitudes were relatively high in her study of healthcare workers in the USA. Walker’s study also revealed moderate to low relationships between transformational leadership and work engagement, and very weak relationships with transactional leadership. In addition, she found significant relationships between person-oriented extra-role behavior and work engagement (r = 0.41) and organization-oriented extra-role behavior.

In a study very similar to the model examined in this research [45], the mediating role of work engagement in the effect of transformational leadership on extra-role behavior in nurses was examined. The study revealed that work engagement had a full mediating role in the relationship between leadership and extra-role behavior. Fu et al. [46] suggest that health-promoting leadership has a moderating effect on the relationship between employees exhibiting excessive extra-role behavior and increased individual health complaints.

A study conducted in Ghana [47] revealed that relationship-oriented leader behavior positively affects extra role behavior and that employee voice has a partial mediating role in this effect. Additionally, the authors emphasize the importance of managers encouraging employees to speak up and consider their opinions.

In this study, the effect of healthcare workers’ work engagement levels on extra-role behavior will be empirically tested, building on the suggestions by Shanafelt and Noseworthy [38]. Since employees’ work engagement is affected by leader behaviors [48], the mediating role of work engagement in the relationship between leadership and extra-role behavior will also be analyzed. In addition, considering the significant effect of organizational silence levels on work engagement in various sectors, including healthcare [41, 49, 50], a serial (sequential) mediation model will also be tested between the concepts.

To comprehensively understand the mechanism through which leader behavior influences extra-role behavior, this research proposes two mediating pathways. First, it is hypothesized that organizational silence and work engagement play parallel mediating roles in this relationship (H1g). Second, building on the COR theory, a sequential process is proposed where leader behavior reduces organizational silence, which in turn increases work engagement, ultimately leading to more extra-role behaviors. Based on this, the following hypothesis was developed as the final hypothesis in the study; Organizational silence and work engagement play a serial (sequential) mediating role in the relationship between leadership behavior and extra-role behavior (H1h).

Method

This research has a descriptive and relational design. The purpose of this research is to examine the relationships among perceived leadership behaviors, organizational silence, work engagement, and extrarole behavior (ERB) among healthcare workers in hospital organizations. In this context, the parallel and serial mediating roles of organizational silence and work engagement in the effect of R.I.G.H.T. leadership behaviors on ERB are evaluated. Although significant relationships have been found between these variables in the literature, no study has tested this unique model at the national or international level. Suggestions regarding the effects of psychologically healthy workplaces on ERB in the literature [33] have been tested in this study. Additionally, the R.I.G.H.T. Leadership Scale has been translated into Turkish, and its cross-cultural validity has been evaluated.

Research population and sample

The stratified sampling method was used as the sampling method in this research. In this context, data should be collected from 77 physicians and 172 nurses in a public hospital and from 25 physicians and 33 nurses in a private hospital when stratified. The data for the scale adaptation study were collected from 176 physicians and nurses working in the university hospital in the province. In the adaptation study, the suitability of the number of participants to the personnel distribution in the institution was also observed. As a result, the adaptation study was conducted with 176 participants, and the main research component was carried out with the participation of 307 participants.

These numbers were determined in line with the information received from the institutions. Stratified weighting was used to observe occupational distribution. In addition, the sample size was determined by considering the number of statements in the scale in the scale adaptation part. Information on this is given in the adaptation section below. In addition, an post hoc power analysis was conducted to assess the statistical power of the study. Using G*Power 3.1 software [51], α = 0.05 significance level, n = 307 sample size and observed effect sizes (Cohen’s f²) for multiple regression analysis were considered. The effect size calculated for the main model of the study was f²=0.297 (medium effect) and the statistical power for this model was found to be above 0.99. This finding indicates that the study has sufficient statistical power to test the main hypotheses.

Data collection tools and process

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire consisting of four scales and seven sociodemographic questions. All the items were scaled on a 7-point Likert scale. In all the measurements, as the score increased, the level of the measured construct also increased. Research data were collected between 01.11.2023-01.06.2024. Data were collected by distributing and collecting questionnaires in closed envelopes to the staff working in the hospitals where the study was conducted. The institutions were visited continuously during working hours and shifts several days a week. In this context, outpatient clinics, inpatient wards, blood collection units, laboratories, various intensive care units, operating rooms and emergency services were visited. All physicians and nurses were invited to participate in the study. The form was delivered to those who agreed to participate voluntarily. Responses were received on the same day or on a different day.

The scales used are as follows:

R.I.G.H.T. Leadership Scale: Developed by Biricik Gulseren et al. [23], this scale consists of 15 items in 5 dimensions. In this study, the scale was adapted into Turkish. The necessary approval for adaptation was obtained from the scale owner.

Organizational Silence Scale: Developed by Van Dyne et al. [52], this scale comprises 15 items across three dimensions: acquiescent, defensive, and prosocial silence. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Taşkıran [53].

Work Engagement Scale: Developed by Schaufeli et al. [54], this scale has versions with 17, 9, 6, and 3 items. Considering that all versions provide valid and reliable measurements, the 9-item version was used in this study to keep the total number of questions short. The adaptation of the scale into Turkish was done by Karcıoğlu [55].

Extra-Role Behavior Scale: Developed by Spector et al. [56] and adapted into Turkish by Cüce [5], this scale has versions with 43, 36, 20, and 10 items. In this study, the 10-item version of the scale was used to keep the questionnaire short, as measurements were made for many different concepts.

Scale adaptation process

The guidelines of the International Test Commission [57] were followed in the adaptation of the R.I.G.H.T. Leadership Scale. First, translation and back-translation were performed by English experts, and then the items were revised after receiving opinions from academics in the field of health management. Data were collected from 176 healthcare workers at KTÜ Farabi Hospital. Sufficient sampling was achieved in line with Brown’s [58] suggestion of 100–200 observations for structural model testing.

According to the results of exploratory factor analysis (EFA), the five-factor structure of the R.I.G.H.T. The leadership scale was successfully adapted to Turkish people at a high level. The KMO value was 0.948, and the Bartlett test was significant, with factor loadings ranging from 0.633 to 0.849. The total variance explained was 90.6%. According to the CFA results, the model fit indices (CMIN/DF = 2.029; RMSEA = 0.07; CFI = 0.977; GFI = 0.900; NFI = 0.956) indicate that the scale is consistent with its original structure. These findings demonstrate that the Turkish version is a valid and reliable measurement tool.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed via SPSS 25.0 (Process Macro 4.1) and AMOS 24 programs. First, descriptive statistics were calculated, and a normal distribution was tested with skewness and kurtosis values. Model fit of the R.I.G.H.T. The scale was tested with AMOS, and the fit values were evaluated according to Karagöz’s [59] criteria. Model 4 (parallel) and Model 6 (serial) processes, developed by Hayes [60], were applied to examine the mediation effects. The 4-step mediation control suggested by Baron and Kenny [61] was performed. Indirect effects were tested at a 95% confidence interval by bootstrapping and were considered significant if the confidence interval did not include 0. In addition, an online Sobel test was performed.

To assess the potential for common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted [62]. An exploratory factor analysis including all study variables revealed that the first factor accounted for 25.32% of the total variance. Since this value is below the 50% threshold, it indicates that common method bias is not a primary concern in this study.

The internal consistency of the measurements was assessed using Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient. As a result, the coefficients in all scales and sub-dimensions were found to be between 0.81 and 0.96. According to Kılıç [63], these values indicate high reliability. Ethical considerations.

The participants’ voluntariness was ensured, and informed consent was obtained. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ankara University Health Sciences Ethics Committee (Decision No: 56786525-050.04.04/1005487, Date: 18.07.2023). Relevant institutional permission was obtained, and the data were collected anonymously in sealed envelopes.

Results

In the present study, 71.7% (n = 220) of the participants were female, and 28.3% (n = 87) were male. When the age distribution of the participants was examined, 33.3% (n = 102) were in the 18–28 age range, 40.1% (n = 123) were in the 29–38 age range, 19.5% (n = 60) were in the 39–48 age range, and 5.8% (n = 18) were in the 49–58 age range. Four individuals (n = 4, 1.3%) aged 59 years and over were included in the 49–58 years age group in the analyses. In terms of occupational distribution, 30.6% (n = 94) of the participants were physicians, and 69.4% (n = 213) were nurses. With respect to the participants’ perceptions of monthly income, 26.1% (n = 80) stated that they had low income, 67.1% (n = 206) had moderate income, and 6.8% (n = 21) had high income. In terms of hospital type, 81.1% (n = 249) worked in a public hospital, and 18.9% (n = 58) worked in a private hospital. In terms of tenure in the institution, 36.5% (n = 112) had worked in their current institution for 3 years or less, 38.7% (n = 119) for 4–10 years, and 24.8% (n = 76) for 11 years or more. When satisfaction with working at the institution was examined, 35.2% (n = 108) of the participants were satisfied, 51.4% (n = 158) were partially satisfied, and 13.4% (n = 41) stated that they were not satisfied.

Leadership level was calculated as 4.77, with a standard deviation of 1.42. The partially high means in the subdimensions of this variable, namely, recognition (mean: 4.73, SD: 1.63), involvement (mean: 4.44, SD: 1.69), growth and development (mean: 4.78, SD: 1.60), health and safety (mean: 4.98, SD: 1.47), and teamwork (mean: 4.91, SD: 1.59), indicate that positive perceptions of leadership are strong above the moderate level. Strong correlations exist between the dimensions (e.g., r = 0.810 between growth and development and recognition; r = 0.822 between teamwork and growth and development), which indicates that the components of leadership perception are complementary.

Organizational silence, with a mean of 3.19 (SD: 1.06), has lower values, indicating that participants’ organizational silence tendencies are moderate. However, the dimensions of acquiescent and defensive silence differ from those of prosocial silence. The individual’s silence regarding operations and processes or unwillingness to speak differs from behaviors protecting employees and the organization. The subdimensions of organizational silence, namely, acquiescent silence (mean: 2.86, SD: 1.32) and defensive silence (mean: 2.56, SD: 1.18), differ from the prosocial silence (mean: 4.45, SD: 1.70) dimension. In addition, this difference is also evident in the insignificance of the relationships with some variables. Weak and negative correlations were found between organizational silence and R.I.G.H.T. leadership variable (e.g., r = − 0.235 between defensive silence and growth and development), suggesting that as leadership perception increases, the tendency for organizational silence may decrease.

The average work engagement level of employees was found to be 4.78 (SD: 1.46). This finding indicates that participants’ level of engagement in work is quite high. Strong correlations exist between the subdimensions of work engagement: vigor (mean = 4.49, SD = 1.56), dedication (mean = 5.06, SD = 1.54), and absorption (mean = 4.78, SD = 1.62). In particular, there is a high correlation of r = 0.848 between dedication and absorption. In addition, positive and significant relationships are observed between work engagement and leadership (r = 0.322 between R.I.G.H.T. leadership and work engagement), suggesting that effective leadership practices can increase employees’ work engagement levels.

Finally, the extra-role behavior variable has a remarkably high mean value of 5.20 (SD: 1.20). This finding indicates that participants exhibit extra-role behaviors in the hospital. There is also a positive and significant relationship between extra-role behavior and work engagement (e.g., r = 0.490 between work engagement and extra-role behavior). This finding suggests that employees’ work engagement levels are significantly related to their tendency to make extra contributions beyond their job descriptions.

Overall, the correlation analysis results indicate that when leadership perceptions are high, organizational silence levels are low, work engagement is high, and employees are more likely to exhibit extra-role behaviors.

Research model analysis results

Table 2 presents the findings for testing the serial and parallel mediating roles of the organizational silence and work engagement variables in the effect of R.I.G.H.T. leadership on extra-role behavior (ERB). The results indicate that R.I.G.H.T. Leadership has a total effect on ERB but does not have a direct, significant effect (b = 0.024, p = 0.584). However, leadership indirectly affects ERB by reducing organizational silence (b = -0.138, LLCI = -0.23, ULCI = -0.04) and increasing work engagement (b = 0.331, LLCI = 0.22, ULCI = 0.44). Therefore, hypothesis H1b was accepted.

While conducting the relational analyses, the prosocial silence dimension of organizational silence was excluded. Because the background of this research was the effects of leader behavior on individual employee silence. This silence is an undesirable employee behavior. On the other hand, prosocial silence refers to remaining silent in order to protect the organization or its employees, even if it is a controversial decision. This is also evident in the correlation analysis. A significant negative relationship was found between organizational silence and ERB (b = -0.115, LLCI = -0.216, ULCI = -0.01). This relationship suggests that as organizational silence increases, employees’ extra-role behaviors tend to decrease. In other words, as employees’ level of silence decreases, their extra-role behaviors may increase. Similarly, the effect of work engagement on ERB is positive and significant (b = 0.385, LLCI = 0.30, ULCI = 0.47). Therefore, as work engagement increases, extra-role behavior also increases. For this reason, hypotheses H1a and H1b were accepted.

Considering the indirect effects in the model, the indirect effects of OS (b = 0.015, p = 0.040) and WE (b = 0.127, p = 0.001) are significant. When both variables are included in the model, the total effect of R.I.G.H.T. Leadership on ERB becomes insignificant (p > 0.05), and this effect occurs through mediating variables. Leader behavior can increase extra-role behavior by increasing work engagement and reducing silence. Therefore, hypothesis H1g was accepted.

Bootstrapping tests provide more accurate results when data are available (Preacher and Leonardelli, 2001). However, for stronger validation, a SOBEL test was also performed in addition to bootstrapping tests. According to these results, the mediation effects were found to be significant (p < 0.05). However, the serial mediation effects of organizational silence and work engagement are not significant (b: 0.004 p > 0.05). When R.I.G.H.T. Leadership and silence are included in the model together, and the effect of silence on work engagement becomes insignificant. Therefore, the serial mediation hypothesis H1h in the model was rejected.

In addition, the study revealed that the effects of R.I.G.H.T. leadership on ERB is indirectly significant (total effect: 0.168, LLCI: 0.07, ULCI: 0.26), and work engagement plays a strong mediating role. The comparison test results of parallel mediators (C1) also indicate that the difference between the mediating effects of organizational silence and work engagement is significant (b = -0.111, LLCI = -0.17, ULCI = -0.05). Accordingly, work engagement has a stronger mediating effect than does silence.

The findings suggest that leadership style has an indirect effect on employees’ extra-role behaviors in the workplace, and that employees’ work engagement levels play a crucial mediating role in this process.

The test results of the research model are summarized in Fig. 1. When the organizational silence and work engagement variables are included in the model, the explained variance of leader behavior on extra-role behavior increases (R²: 0.04 to R²: 0.26), and the total effect of leader behavior decreases, rendering its direct effect insignificant. The work engagement and organizational silence variables were determined to play a full mediating role in the relationship between leader behavior and extra-role behavior.

The study revealed that organizational silence has an independent effect on work engagement (b = -0.144, p < 0.05). However, when examined together with leadership behavior in the model where the mediating effect was observed, its effect was found to be insignificant (β = -0.08, p > 0.05). Therefore, R.I.G.H.T. Leadership behavior directly affects work engagement. For this reason, as a result of the analysis for testing the serial mediation role, it was found that the serial mediating effect in the form of leader behavior > silence > work engagement > extra role behavior was not significant (Ind3: BootLLCI:−0.00/BootULCI:0.01).

Discussion

The Turkish form of the R.I.G.H.T. Leadership Scale used in this study was highly consistent with the original structure in terms of factor structure [23]. This finding suggests that the new scale, developed based on transformational leadership and the psychologically healthy workplace model, provides valid measurements across different cultural contexts. Since there are no empirical findings regarding this scale in the literature, this aspect of the study is pioneering. The participants’ general leadership perceptions were found to be moderate to high. Notably, the highest mean was observed in the health and safety dimension, while the lowest mean was found in the involvement dimension. This result, which indicates that leaders do not sufficiently involve employees in decision-making processes, is consistent with findings that show a high correlation between lack of involvement and organizational silence [64, 65]. Additionally, a low perception of involvement can also erode employees’ sense of belonging to the organization.

Leadership and organizational silence

R.I.G.H.T. Leadership behavior is negatively related to organizational silence. The involvement dimension, being the most effective dimension of silence, indicates that silence decreases when leaders value employee opinions. Wu and Li [66] similarly stated that inclusive leadership reduces relational silence. In China, ethical leadership [67], and in Pakistan, negative leader behaviors [68] are inversely related to silence. In Egypt, narcissistic leadership reportedly increases silence in physicians [69]. These results suggest that leader behavior can be a strong determinant of silence.

Leadership and work engagement

In this study, a significant and strong effect of leader behavior on work engagement was found. In particular, the “recognition” dimension stands out in explaining this effect. Therefore, healthcare professionals will be more engaged in their work to the extent that they recognize and acknowledge the achievements and skills of their leaders. This finding is consistent with studies showing a positive relationship between leadership styles such as transformational [70], authentic [71], and resonant leadership [72] and work engagement. In particular, a study conducted in Portugal by Salanova et al. [45] revealed that although transformational leadership had low direct effects on work engagement and extra-role behavior, the work engagement variable fully mediated this relationship. This result is highly consistent with the findings obtained in our study. The leader’s influence on the employee indirectly increases extra-role behaviors by triggering work engagement. In general, the relationship between leadership and work engagement has been extensively researched in the literature, and it has been reported that positive leadership styles increase work engagement [73]. The findings of this study also confirm that leadership behaviors have a strong and positive effect on work engagement.

Leadership and extra-role behavior

In the present study, R.I.G.H.T. Leadership behaviors had a low effect on extra-role behaviors. This effect changed when other factors were included in the model. A study by Lee et al. [6] reported that transformational leadership was related to extra-role behavior at a similar level (r:0.21), and this effect decreased when other variables were added to the model. This finding indicates that the relationship between leadership and extra-role behavior is complex and interacts with other variables.

Other studies in the literature also suggest that leader support and positive emotions can lead to increased extra-role behaviors [74, 75]. In this study, it was also found that leadership behaviors that support employee development and encourage teamwork were positively related to extra-role behavior. A study by Bülbül [76] suggested that participatory leadership increased the level of extra-role behavior directed toward the organization and the individual. In this study, the involvement dimension of R.I.G.H.T. Leadership was also found to be highly related to the level of extra-role behavior, suggesting that taking employees’ opinions increases their potential to make extra efforts.

In general, leader behavior has various effects on extra-role behavior; however, these effects are generally low and interact with several other variables [77, 78]. This finding suggests that other variables typically mediate the relationship between leadership and extra-role behavior.

Organizational silence and work engagement

In this study, no direct significant correlational relationship was found between organizational silence and work engagement (p > 0.05). However, when the dimensions of organizational silence were examined, acquiescent silence was found to be negatively related to work engagement, while prosocial silence was positively related. This suggests that employees’ failure to express their opinions may decrease their work engagement levels, but remaining silent for the purpose of protecting the organization may be related to work engagement.

A study conducted in hospitals in Egypt by Nafei [39] revealed moderate negative correlations between the dimensions of silence and work engagement levels. Studies by Yağar and Dökme Yağar [41] and Kaur and Arora [42] also support that organizational silence has significant negative relationships with work engagement. These findings emphasize the potential negative effects of silence on work engagement.

Silence and extra-role behavior

The effects of types of silence on extra-role behavior differ. Acquiescent and defensive silence are negatively related, whereas prosocial silence is positively related. This finding has also been confirmed in studies such as those of Turgut and Akbolat [79], Kılınç and Ulusoy [80], and Ürek et al. [31]. Prosocial silence particularly emerges as a protective attitude toward colleagues or the organization.

Aydamak et al. [81] conducted a systematic review of studies on silence and extra-role behavior in hospitals in Turkey. They included five studies and reported that the two concepts were negatively correlated in all the studies. However, as previously stated, the different effects of prosocial behaviors also came to the fore in this systematic review.

Work engagement and extra-role behavior

In this study, it was found that work engagement, encompassing all its dimensions, can lead to increased extra-role behaviors (r = 0.490, b = 0.380). This finding indicates that the more engaged employees are in their work, the more positive behaviors they exhibit beyond their job descriptions. These results are consistent with findings from similar studies conducted in the USA and Korea [82, 83]. Kim [83] specifically emphasized that work engagement had the most significant effect on extra-role behavior.

The relationships between work engagement and extra-role behavior also yielded similar results in studies conducted in hospitals in Taiwan, Iran, Africa, Canada, and South Africa [75, 84–87].

This finding indicates that work engagement is an important factor affecting extra-role behavior and that this relationship is valid in different cultures. Ng et al. [88] reported a significantly stronger relationship in their study to determine the mediating role of work engagement in the relationship between job satisfaction and extra-role behavior, as observed among healthcare workers in Malaysia.

Mediation effects: silence and work engagement

Analyses revealed that work engagement played a full mediating role in the relationship between leadership and extra-role behavior, whereas silence played a partial mediating role. However, silence was not found to play a significant mediating role in the relationship between leadership and work engagement. Therefore, no serial mediation effect was found. This result is consistent with a similar study conducted by Osei et al. [89]. There may be several important reasons behind this result. First, the direct and strong effect of R.I.G.H.T. leadership behaviors on engagement may have overshadowed the mediating potential of the silence variable. In other words, direct practices such as leaders getting to know employees, involving them in decisions and supporting their development are so effective that there is no need for an indirect effect through the silence mechanism. Secondly, other factors may have had a stronger effect as the model’s complexity increased due to the low variance explained. Finally, the dominant effect of work engagement on extra role behavior may have rendered the statistical effect of other paths weak and not meaningful.

In this study, it was confirmed that silence had a partial mediating role in the effect of leader behavior on extra-role behavior. This means that leader behavior can partially increase extra-role behaviors by reducing silence. No study directly examining this relationship has been found in the literature. However, a study conducted in Ghana by Ansong et al. [47] revealed that employee voice had a partial mediating role in the effect of relationship-oriented leadership on extra-role behavior. These findings suggest that the impact of leader behaviors in reducing silence and enhancing employee voice makes significant contributions to the literature.

The study also revealed that leader behavior has a positive effect on extra role behavior, and that work engagement serves as an important mediating variable in this relationship. Therefore, leader behaviors can contribute to extra-role behaviors by increasing employees’ work engagement. This finding is quite similar to that of a study conducted in Portugal by Salanova et al. [45]. Their study also revealed that work engagement fully mediated the effects of transformational leadership and self-efficacy on extra-role behavior.

The model developed in this study provides important insights into the complex interactions among variables such as leadership, organizational silence, work engagement, and extra-role behavior. The central position of work engagement is particularly noteworthy. The results indicate that a leadership approach focused on participation, recognition, and development should be popularized to increase employees’ work engagement levels and encourage voluntary extra-role behaviors.

Limitations and future research

The study is limited to voluntary participants working in only three hospitals and at specific days and times. In addition, all scales except leadership are based on self-assessment. This may pose limitations in obtaining objective data. The generalization of the findings should be made considering these limitations. In this study, variables theoretically related to each other were examined in a single model. While this aspect has strong points that advance the theory, it also has some shortcomings. In future studies, employees’ personality traits, such as personality, perseverance, and passion for work, should also be analyzed as moderators of the model. In addition, examining the perceptions of informal leaders, in conjunction with those of formal managers, regarding these variables could be the subject of another study. In addition, the research model did not control for the role of demographic variables such as gender, experience, unit and occupation. This research was motivated to demonstrate how the model works in the health sector in a general perspective. In future studies, a more focused sample may change the levels of impact.

Practical implications for health managers

The most important practical implication of this study for health managers and leaders is that work engagement is the primary mechanism to focus on to encourage employees’ voluntary extra roles. The findings suggest that leadership behaviors that recognize subordinates, recognize their achievements and involve them in decision-making processes directly increase employees’ passion and energy for work. This high engagement, in turn, is the strongest trigger for going beyond the role definition and making extra roles on behalf of patients, colleagues and the organization. To summarize, the way to improve performance is not primarily by reducing pressure, but by building a positive leadership climate that fosters commitment and engagement. In this context, it is crucial to value the opinions of employees, implement regular feedback and appreciation mechanisms, and support their individual development.

Conclusion

This study investigated the intricate relationships among leadership behaviors, organizational silence, work engagement, and extra-role behaviors within the healthcare sector. Specifically, the effects of the R.I.G.H.T. Leadership approach on employees’ extra-role behaviors were explained through organizational silence and work engagement. The findings indicate that effective leadership practices increase employees’ work engagement and reduce organizational silence levels, which in turn contributes to an increase in extra-role behaviors. However, the effect of leader behavior on extra-role behavior was weakened or became insignificant in individuals with low work engagement or high organizational silence. The study is important in terms of introducing the R.I.G.H.T. Leadership scale to the Turkish literature and providing new evidence to the leadership and organizational behavior literature.

In light of the results obtained, healthcare institution leaders should listen to employees’ opinions, appreciate their achievements, and support their professional development. Adopting participatory leadership approaches will reduce organizational silence by increasing employee participation in decisions and encouraging extra role behaviors. In addition, developing strategies to increase work engagement and provide an environment of psychological safety may enable employees to make more voluntary efforts on behalf of the organization. Such leadership practices are critically important for employees to continue working and exhibit high performance despite the challenging conditions in the healthcare sector.
