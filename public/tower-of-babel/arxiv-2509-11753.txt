A note on Tricomi-type partial differential equations with white noise initial condition
Enrico Bernardi, Alberto Lanconelli

Abstract
We study a class of Tricomi-type partial differential equations previously investigated in [ 28 ] . Firstly, we generalize the representation formula for the solution obtained there by allowing the coefficient in front of the second-order partial derivative with respect to x x to be any non integer power of t t . Then, we analyze the robustness of that solution by taking the initial data to be Gaussian white noise and we discover that the existence of a well-defined random field solution is lost upon the introduction of lower-order terms in the operator. This phenomenon shows that, even though the Tricomi-type operators with or without lower-order terms are the same from the point of view of the theory of hyperbolic operators with double characteristics, their corresponding random versions exhibit different well posedness properties. We also prove that for more regular initial data —specifically fractional Gaussian white noise with Hurst parameter H ∈ ( 1 / 2 , 1 ) H\in(1/2,1) —the well posedness of the Cauchy problem for the Tricomi-type operator with lower-order term is restored.



Key words and phrases: Tricomi equation, probabilistic representation, Gaussian white noise, effectively hyperbolic operators.

AMS 2020 classification: 60H15, 60H05, 35R60.

Introduction and statement of the main results

The Tricomi equation
[
26
]
is a second-order partial
differential equation of mixed elliptic-hyperbolic type with the form:

\displaystyle u_{tt}(t,x)=tu_{xx}(t,x),\quad t,x\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.1)

The equation is hyperbolic in the half plane
t>0
, elliptic in the half plane
t<0
, and degenerates on the line
t=0
. Many important problems in fluid mechanics and differential geometry can be reduced to corresponding problems for the Tricomi equation, particularly
transonic flow
problems
[
21
]
and
isometric embedding
problems
[
18
]
(see also
[
17
]
and the references quoted there). The Tricomi equation is a prototype of the Tricomi-type equation:

\displaystyle u_{tt}(t,x)=f(t)u_{xx}(t,x),\quad t,x\in\mathbb{R},

(1.2)

where
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function.

In the series of papers
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
the authors have investigated the fundamental solution of the Tricomi operator (
1.1
). In
[
4
]
the method of partial Fourier transform is used to find explicit formulas for fundamental solutions for the following Tricomi-type operator in arbitrary space dimensions:

\displaystyle u_{tt}(t,x)=t\Delta_{x}u(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

(1.3)

More recently, the findings of
[
4
]
have been generalized in
[
28
]
to the case of operators of the form

\displaystyle u_{tt}(t,x)=t^{2k}\Delta_{x}u(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1},k\in\mathbb{N};

(1.4)

the author also provides representation formulas for the solution to related Cauchy problems. See also
[
9
,
10
]
for explicit representations for the propagator of some generalized Tricomi-type operators in one space dimension.

The aim of the present note is twofold. We first propose a
probabilistic representation for the solution to the Cauchy
problem for the Tricomi-type equation (
1.2
) with

f(t)=t^{\alpha}
,
\alpha\geq 0
, thus yielding a
generalization (in one space dimension) of the results of

[
28
]
for the operator (
1.4
). Then,
we exploit such representation to study the robustness of
solutions with respect to random initial conditions and the
introduction of lower-order terms of the type
u_{x}
. More
specifically, we set the initial data to be a Gaussian white
noise and investigate the existence of a well defined random
field solution for that problem. Notice that Cauchy problems
with Gaussian white noise initial conditions can be considered
by virtue of Duhamel’s principle
[
15
]
as an
intermediate step in the construction of mild solutions for
stochastic partial differential equations with additive
space-time Gaussian white noise, see
e.g.
[
13
,
20
,
25
]
. We discover that
our Tricomi-type operator without lower-order term admits a
well defined Gaussian random field solution, while this does
not happen when a lower-order term
u_{x}
is added. We also
show that such interference of the lower-order terms in the
well posedness of the Cauchy problem is absent when the
Tricomi-type operator reduces to the classical wave
operator. We complete our study pointing out that for smoother
initial data, i.e. fractional Gaussian white noise with Hurst
parameter
H\in(1/2,1)
, the Cauchy problem for the
Tricomi-type operator with lower-order term becomes well posed.

We begin with our first main result whose proof is postponed
to Section
2
. Notice that the class of equations
considered here includes the family of problems investigated
in
[
28
]
(
\alpha=2k
,
k\geq 1
). From this point of view
Theorem
1.1
generalizes Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 3.3 from
[
28
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
and

\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be twice
continuously differentiable. Then, the unique classical solution of the Cauchy problem for the Tricomi-type partial differential equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{tt}(t,x)=t^{\alpha}u_{xx}(t,x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
u(0,x)=0,u_{t}(0,x)=\varphi(x),&x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(1.5)

can be represented as

\displaystyle u(t,x)=t\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x+\xi(t)ZY)],\quad t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R},

(1.6)

where:

•

Z
is a Rademacher random variable, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(Z=1)=\mathbb{P}(Z=-1)=\frac{1}{2}
;

•

Y
is a continuous random variable with probability density function

\displaystyle p_{Y}(y)=\begin{cases}c_{\gamma}(1-y^{2})^{-\gamma},&y\in]0,1[;\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1.7)

with
\gamma:=\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha+2)}
and
c_{\gamma}=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}-\gamma\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(1-\gamma)}
;

•

Z
and
Y
are independent random variables;

•

\xi(t):=\frac{t^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+1}}{\frac{\alpha}{2}+1}
,
t\geq 0
.

This means that
u
in (
1.6
) corresponds to

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\frac{t}{2\xi(t)}\int_{x-\xi(t)}^{x+\xi(t)}\varphi(y)c_{\gamma}\left(1-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{\xi(t)^{2}}\right)^{-\gamma}dy,\quad t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.8)

We now investigate the behaviour of problem (
1.5
) in the case of random singular initial condition, i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}U_{tt}(t,x)=t^{\alpha}U_{xx}(t,x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
U(0,x)=0,U_{t}(0,x)=W(x),&x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(1.9)

where
\{W(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
stands for the so-called
Gaussian white noise
. This means that
\{W(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is defined to be the distributional derivative of a standard one dimensional two-sided Brownian Motion
\{B(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
defined on a reference probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. We refer the reader to Section
3
for the definition and basic properties of the Gaussian white noise.

The natural solution concept for investigating (
1.9
) is described as follows.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that a random field
\{U(t,x)\}_{t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}}
solves problem (
1.9
) if for any sequence of mollifiers
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, i.e. for all
n\geq 1
,

•

\rho_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R};[0,+\infty[)
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{n}(y)dy=1
;

•

the support of
\rho_{n}
is contained in the closed ball
\overline{B(0,r_{n})}
for some non negative sequence
\{r_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
monotonically decreasing to zero,

the solution
\{U^{(n)}(t,x)\}_{t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}}
to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}U^{(n)}_{tt}(t,x)=t^{\alpha}U^{(n)}_{xx}(t,x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
U^{(n)}(0,x)=0,U^{(n)}_{t}(0,x)=W^{(n)}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(1.10)

where

\displaystyle W^{(n)}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{n}(x-y)dB(y),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}

converges in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, as
n
tends to infinity, to
\{U(t,x)\}_{t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}}
.

We are now ready to state well posedness for the singular problem (
1.9
); its proof can be found in Section
3
.

Theorem 1.3
.

The Gaussian random field

\displaystyle U(t,x)=\frac{t}{2\xi(t)}\int_{x-\xi(t)}^{x+\xi(t)}c_{\gamma}\left(1-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{\xi(t)^{2}}\right)^{-\gamma}dB(y),\quad t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.11)

with
\gamma
and
\xi
defined in Theorem
1.1
, is the unique solution in the sense of Definition
1.2
to problem (
1.9
).

Remark 1.4
.

There are several results in the literature concerning the existence
of solutions for the wave equation perturbed by additive noise in any
space dimension. We refer the reader to the papers
[
27
]
and

[
24
]
for the case of one space dimension and to

[
14
]
and the contributions cited there for the
multidimensional case. We remark that in
[
14
]
the author
identifies a necessary and sufficient condition relating additive noise terms to space dimension that allow for well posedness of the corresponding stochastic partial differential equations.

The case of non strictly hyperbolic operators on the contrary is not
very much explored. Here we mention some recent papers attempting to investigate how the degeneracies of the operator have an impact on stochastic perturbations:
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
11
,
12
]
. The results in this note are a new contribution in that direction.

We now focus our attention on the problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}V_{tt}(t,x)=t^{2}V_{xx}(t,x)-V_{x}(t,x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
V(0,x)=0,V_{t}(0,x)=W(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(1.12)

This corresponds, at the level of second order terms, to
(
1.9
) with
\alpha=2
but differs from that for the
presence of a lower-order term, i.e.
V_{x}(t,x)
. Notice that, from the
point of view of the theory of hyperbolic operators with double
characteristics, the operators in (
1.9
) and
(
1.12
) are the same: lower-order terms do not change the fact
that the Cauchy problem is
C^{\infty}
-well posed. They are the
prototype of the so called
effectively hyperbolic
case: see e.g.

[
22
]
for a recent general review. However, equations
(
1.9
) and (
1.12
) behave differently when the
initial data is Gaussian white noise. While (
1.9
)
admits a well defined square integrable solution, as stated in Theorem

1.3
, this no longer happens for equation (
1.12
). For the proof of Theorem
1.5
see Section
3
. In the sequel we will implicitly adapt Definition
1.2
with the straightforward modifications needed to treat the new operators under consideration.

Theorem 1.5
.

The Cauchy problem for the Tricomi-type partial differential equation with Gaussian white noise initial data (
1.12
) doesn’t admit any solution in the sense of Definition
1.2
.

If we allow the initial data in (
1.12
) to be slightly more regular than
\{W(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
, more precisely a fractional Gaussian white noise with Hurst parameter
H\in(1/2,1)
, then the well posedness is restored.

Corollary 1.6
.

The problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}V_{tt}(t,x)=t^{2}V_{xx}(t,x)-V_{x}(t,x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
V(0,x)=0,V_{t}(0,x)=W_{H}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(1.13)

where
\{W_{H}(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is now a fractional Gaussian white noise with Hurst parameter
H\in(1/2,1)
, admits a unique solution in the sense of Definition
1.2
.

Proof of Corollary
1.6
and definition of fractional Gaussian white noise with Hurst parameter
H\in(1/2,1)
can be found in Section
3
.

We are now going to show that if the Tricomi-type operator is replaced by the classical wave operator then the introduction of a lower-order term doesn’t modify the well posedness of the Cauchy problem with Gaussian white noise initial data. This confirms the existence of an interplay between degeneracy properties of differential operators and well posedness of some related stochastic Cauchy problems.

Setting
\alpha=0
in (
1.9
) one gets immediately from Theorem
1.3
that the unique random field solution of the problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}U_{tt}(t,x)=U_{xx}(t,x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
U(0,x)=0,U_{t}(0,x)=W(x),&x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(1.14)

is given by

\displaystyle U(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{x-t}^{x+t}dB(y),\quad t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R};

(1.15)

since

\displaystyle U(t,x)\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\quad\mbox{ for all }t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R},

the solution (
1.15
) is a well defined random field. We now perturb problem (
1.14
) with a lower-order term, namely

\displaystyle\begin{cases}V_{tt}(t,x)=V_{xx}(t,x)+V_{x}(t,x),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};\\
V(0,x)=0,V_{t}(0,x)=W(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(1.16)

A direct verification shows that its solution is given by

\displaystyle V(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{x-t}^{x+t}e^{(y-x)/2}\mathtt{J}_{0}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{t^{2}-(x-y)^{2}}\right)dB(y),\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R};

here
\mathtt{J}_{0}
denotes the Bessel function of first kind of order zero. Computing the
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)
-norm of
V(t,x)
we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V(t,x)^{2}]=\frac{1}{4}\int_{x-t}^{x+t}e^{y-x}\mathtt{J}_{0}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{t^{2}-(x-y)^{2}}\right)^{2}dy

and the last integral turns out to be finite for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
being the integral of a continuous function on a compact set. Therefore, both problems (
1.14
) and (
1.16
) admit a unique well defined random field solution.

\displaystyle+t\dot{\xi}(t)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)Z^{2}Y^{2}]

\displaystyle(2\dot{\xi}(t)+t\ddot{\xi}(t))\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)ZY]+t\dot{\xi}(t)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)Y^{2}],

where the last equality follows from

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)ZY]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)ZY|\sigma(Z)]]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)Y|\sigma(Z)]]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Z\int_{0}^{1}\varphi^{\prime}(x+\xi(t)Zy)yc_{\gamma}(1-y^{2})^{-\gamma}dy\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{\gamma}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[Z\int_{0}^{1}\varphi^{\prime}(x+\xi(t)Zy)\frac{d}{dy}\left(\frac{(1-y^{2})^{-\gamma+1}}{-\gamma+1}\right)dy\right]

Here, in the second to last equality we utilized the fact that the function

(recall that

) while in the last equality we exploited the identity

. Therefore, we can continue the computation in (
2
) as follows:

\displaystyle u_{tt}(t,x)=

\displaystyle(2\dot{\xi}(t)+t\ddot{\xi}(t))\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)ZY]+t\dot{\xi}(t)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)Y^{2}]

\displaystyle(2\dot{\xi}(t)+t\ddot{\xi}(t))\frac{\xi(t)}{2(-\gamma+1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)(1-Y^{2})\right]+t\dot{\xi}(t)^{2}\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)Y^{2}]

\displaystyle(2\dot{\xi}(t)+t\ddot{\xi}(t))\frac{\xi(t)}{2(-\gamma+1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)\right]

A comparison between the first and last members of the above chain of equalities gives

\displaystyle u_{tt}(t,x)=

\displaystyle(2\dot{\xi}(t)+t\ddot{\xi}(t))\frac{\xi(t)}{2(-\gamma+1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)\right]

\displaystyle+\left(t\dot{\xi}(t)^{2}-(2\dot{\xi}(t)+t\ddot{\xi}(t))\frac{\xi(t)}{2(-\gamma+1)}\right)\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)Y^{2}].

On the other hand, differentiating twice with respect to

in (

) we obtain

\displaystyle u_{xx}(t,x)=t\mathbb{E}[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x+\xi(t)ZY)].

Therefore, if we want

\displaystyle u_{tt}(t,x)=t^{\alpha}u_{xx}(t,x)\quad\mbox{ for all }t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},

we have to find a function

such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(2\dot{\xi}(t)+t\ddot{\xi}(t))\frac{\xi(t)}{2(-\gamma+1)}=t^{\alpha+1},&t>0;\\

\xi(0)=0\end{cases}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}(g_{1})(t)-\mathcal{T}(g_{2})(t)|

\displaystyle|I_{1}-I_{2}|

\displaystyle=2M\|g_{1}-g_{2}\|\cdot\frac{t^{3}}{6},

where
M:=\max\{\|g_{1}\|,\|g_{2}\|\}
. Thus,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}(g_{1})(t)-\mathcal{T}(g_{2})(t)|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2L}{t}}\cdot\frac{2M\|g_{1}-g_{2}\|\cdot\frac{t^{3}}{6}}{\sqrt{I_{1}}+\sqrt{I_{2}}}.

For small
t
, this implies that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}(g_{1})-\mathcal{T}(g_{2})\|\leq KT^{1/2}\|g_{1}-g_{2}\|

for some constant
K
depending on
L
and
M
. Therefore, for sufficiently small
T
, the operator
\mathcal{T}
is a contraction and since

•

(X,\|\cdot\|)
is a Banach space;

•

\mathcal{T}
is a contraction for small enough
T
,

we may apply the Banach fixed point theorem to conclude that
\mathcal{T}
has a unique fixed point in
X
.

For larger values of
T
, one may apply the contraction mapping principle iteratively on subintervals (standard continuation method), or appeal to the general theory of non linear Volterra integral equations of the second kind. These are known to have unique solutions under continuity and Lipschitz-type conditions, both of which are satisfied here. The integro-differential equation

\displaystyle tg(t)^{2}=2L\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)s(g^{\prime}(s))^{2}ds,\quad t\in[0,T],

has a unique solution
g(t)\in C^{1}([0,T])
with
g(0)=0
for any
finite
T>0
.

Since the integral representation (
1.8
) follows immediately from (
1.6
), the proof of Theorem
1.1
is complete.

We start with a brief description of the Gaussian white noise
\{W(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
. For more details we refer the reader to one of the books
[
19

23
]
.

Let
\{W(\varphi)\}_{\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
be an isonormal Gaussian process, i.e.
\{W(\varphi)\}_{\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
is a family of centred Gaussian random variables defined on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
such that the map

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\ni\varphi\mapsto W(\varphi)

is linear and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W(\varphi_{1})W(\varphi_{2})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{1}(y)\varphi_{2}(y)dy.

In the sequel we will write

\displaystyle B(x):=W(\mathtt{1}_{[0,x]}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R};

this process possesses a continuous modification that turns out to be a one dimensional two-sided Brownian motion; with this notations we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(y)dB(y)=W(\varphi),\quad\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R});

this corresponds to the Wiener-Itô integral of
\varphi
with respect to the two-sided Brownian motion
\{B(x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
. The distributional derivative

\displaystyle W(x):=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}B(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

is called
Gaussian white noise
and will play the role of initial data in the partial differential equations under investigation.

Similarly,
\{W_{H}(\varphi)\}_{\varphi\in\mathcal{H}}
is a fractional isonormal Gaussian process with Hurst parameter
H\in(1/2,1)
if

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}:=\left\{\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}:\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\varphi(x)||\varphi(y)|H(2H-1)|x-y|^{2H-2}dxdy<+\infty\right\}

and the family
\{W_{H}(\varphi)\}_{\varphi\in\mathcal{H}}
is centred Gaussian and such that the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\ni\varphi\mapsto W_{H}(\varphi)

is linear and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{H}(\varphi_{1})W_{H}(\varphi_{2})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi_{1}(x)\varphi_{2}(y)|H(2H-1)|x-y|^{2H-2}dxdy.

In the sequel we will write

\displaystyle B_{H}(x):=W_{H}(\mathtt{1}_{[0,x]}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R};

this process possesses a continuous modification that turns out to be a one dimensional two-sided fractional Brownian Motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(1/2,1)
; with this notation we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(y)dB_{H}(y)=W_{H}(\varphi),\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{H}.

The distributional derivative

\displaystyle W_{H}(x):=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}B_{H}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

is called
fractional Gaussian white noise
.
