Introduction

Advanced maternal age is a well-established risk factor for both infertility and adverse pregnancy outcomes, representing an increasingly prevalent challenge in reproductive medicine as more women delay childbearing.13 The biological basis for age-related fertility decline is multifactorial, but a primary contributor is the substantial increase in embryo aneuploidy with advancing maternal age.4 5 Chromosomal abnormalities in embryos lead to implantation failure, early pregnancy loss and contribute significantly to the reduced reproductive success observed in women of advanced maternal age.6 7

Current clinical practice relies predominantly on morphological assessment to select embryos for transfer during in vitro fertilisation (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) treatment. While morphology-based grading systems, such as the Gardner classification for blastocysts, are widely used and provide valuable information about embryo developmental potential, they cannot determine embryo ploidy status.8 9 Consequently, morphologically high-grade embryos may harbour chromosomal abnormalities, particularly in older women, leading to suboptimal embryo selection and reduced treatment success rates.10

Preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidies (PGT-A) has emerged as a tool to identify euploid embryos prior to transfer. Conventional PGT-A involves trophectoderm biopsy, typically performed on days 5–6 blastocysts, followed by comprehensive chromosomal screening.11 12 While some studies have suggested that PGT-A may improve implantation rates and reduce miscarriage rates per transfer, particularly in women of advanced maternal age, the evidence remains controversial.1315 Moreover, invasive PGT-A has several limitations: it requires specialised technical expertise, increases treatment complexity and cost, necessitates embryo biopsy which carries potential risks to embryo viability and raises ethical concerns regarding embryo manipulation.16 17 Importantly, recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have not consistently demonstrated improved cumulative live birth rates with PGT-A compared with morphology-based selection, particularly when considering outcomes per oocyte retrieval cycle rather than per transfer.18 19

Recent multicentre randomised trials have further questioned whether conventional (biopsy-based) PGT-A improves patient-centred cumulative outcomes compared with routine care. In a large randomised trial of subfertile women aged 20–37 years with three or more good-quality blastocysts, conventional IVF achieved a cumulative live birth rate within 1 year that was non-inferior to (and numerically higher than) PGT-A, while pregnancy loss was lower with PGT-A.12 In addition, in couples undergoing ICSI for severe male factor infertility, an open-label multicentre randomised trial reported no difference in live birth after the first transfer or in cumulative live birth within 12 months between PGT-A and no genetic testing, despite significantly reduced pregnancy loss in the PGT-A group.20 Collectively, these trials suggest that although PGT-A can reduce pregnancy loss by avoiding transfer of aneuploid embryos, this has not consistently translated into higher cumulative live birth per initiated treatment cycle—highlighting the ongoing uncertainty and the need to evaluate alternative, potentially less invasive embryo-selection strategies.

Non-invasive PGT (ni-PGT), also known as non-invasive chromosome screening (NICS), represents a novel approach that analyses cell-free DNA (cfDNA) released by embryos into spent culture medium.21 22 This technique offers several theoretical advantages over conventional PGT-A: it avoids the need for trophectoderm biopsy, potentially reducing technical complexity and eliminating concerns about biopsy-related embryo damage; it may be more acceptable to patients and clinicians who have reservations about invasive embryo manipulation; and it could provide a more cost-effective screening option.23 24 The biological rationale is that embryos release cfDNA into the surrounding culture medium through apoptosis and cellular turnover, and this cfDNA may reflect the chromosomal constitution of the embryo.25

However, several technical and biological challenges affect ni-PGT performance. The concentration of embryo-derived cfDNA in culture medium is low, and contamination from maternal cumulus cells, sperm or laboratory personnel can confound results.26 27 The concordance between cfDNA in spent medium and embryo ploidy status determined by trophectoderm biopsy has been variable across studies, with reported concordance rates ranging from 30% to over 80%.28 29 Furthermore, the presence of mosaicism—where an embryo contains both euploid and aneuploid cells—complicates interpretation, as the cfDNA profile may not accurately represent the entire embryo or its developmental potential.30

Despite growing interest in ni-PGT, high-quality evidence demonstrating improved clinical outcomes remains limited, particularly in the target population of women aged 35–42 years who would most benefit from improved embryo selection. Most published studies have been retrospective, observational or small-scale, with heterogeneous methodologies and outcome measures.31 32 Critically, few studies have examined cumulative outcomes—the most clinically relevant endpoint for patients and clinicians—which account for all embryos generated from a single ovarian stimulation cycle and multiple transfer attempts.

The primary objective of this multicentre, randomised controlled trial is to evaluate whether ni-PGT-guided embryo selection improves cumulative ongoing pregnancy rates within 1 year compared with conventional morphology-based embryo selection in infertile women aged 35–42 years undergoing their first ICSI cycle. Secondary objectives include assessment of early miscarriage rates, time to live birth and safety outcomes. By employing a pragmatic trial design that reflects routine clinical practice—including a freeze-all strategy with single frozen-thawed blastocyst transfers—this study aims to provide robust evidence to inform clinical decision-making and counselling for this important patient population.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This is a multicentre, open-label, parallel-group randomised controlled trial conducted at three reproductive medicine centres in China: Suzhou Municipal Hospital (lead site), Taizhou People’s Hospital and Changzhou Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital. The study set-up period started in August 2024 and included investigator training, harmonisation of spent culture medium sampling procedures for ni-PGT across centres and quality-control procedures. No participant was recruited, enrolled or randomised during this set-up period. Recruitment and the first participant randomisation commenced on 14 February 2025 at the lead site, and additional sites started recruitment after local ethics approval and site initiation. Recruitment is expected to complete in February 2027. The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400088283) on 15 August 2024. The study will compare ni-PGT-guided embryo selection with conventional morphology-based embryo selection in infertile women of advanced maternal age undergoing ICSI treatment. A pragmatic design has been adopted, with controlled ovarian stimulation protocols not restricted and clinical procedures following each centre’s routine practice, to enhance generalisability of findings.

Participant recruitment and consent

Eligible participants will be identified and approached during routine clinical care at participating centres. Investigators will provide detailed information about the study aims, procedures, potential risks and benefits, data confidentiality measures and the right to withdraw at any time without affecting clinical care. Participants will have adequate time to consider participation and ask questions. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants before any study-specific procedures are performed.

Participation is voluntary, and participants may withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason. Withdrawal will not affect the standard of care received. Data collected up to the point of withdrawal will be retained for analysis unless the participant specifically requests otherwise.

Randomisation and allocation concealment

Randomisation will occur when eligibility is confirmed at the blastocyst stage (days 5–6), specifically when at least two eligible blastocysts (Gardner grade ≥4BC) are available. Participants will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either the ni-PGT-guided embryo selection group (intervention) or the morphology-based embryo selection group (control).

Randomisation will be stratified by study centre using permuted blocks with variable block sizes (4 and 6) to maintain balance while reducing allocation predictability. The randomisation sequence will be generated by an independent statistician and implemented via a unified, centralised randomisation system; site investigators obtain allocation only after participant enrolment and baseline data entry, ensuring allocation concealment until the point of randomisation. This is the prespecified randomisation procedure used from the first randomised participant; no post-commencement change to the randomisation algorithm has been made. Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention—different embryo selection strategies that require different laboratory procedures and clinical decision-making processes—blinding of participants and clinicians is not feasible. Therefore, this trial is conducted as an open-label study. However, to minimise potential bias, outcome assessors for pregnancy outcomes will be blinded to group allocation where possible, and statistical analyses will be conducted by analysts blinded to treatment assignment.

Experimental group: ni-PGT-guided embryo selection

In the experimental group, spent culture medium corresponding to each eligible blastocyst will be collected at the time the blastocyst meets vitrification criteria. The medium collection will follow standardised protocols to minimise contamination risk. If more than five eligible blastocysts are available, the five blastocysts with the highest morphological ranking will be selected as the candidate embryo set in both groups according to a prespecified rule applied consistently across centres. Embryo selection within this set will be based on ni-PGT results plus morphology in the intervention group and on morphology alone in the control group.

ni-PGT/NICS will be performed on the collected spent culture medium samples. The testing will analyse cfDNA to assess chromosomal status. Up to five blastocysts may be tested per participant.

Embryos will be prioritised for transfer based on the ni-PGT results (score/grade). Embryos classified as euploid or low-risk for aneuploidy will be prioritised over those classified as aneuploid or high-risk. When ni-PGT grades are equivalent between embryos, the embryo with the higher morphological grade will be selected for transfer.

Control group: morphology-based embryo selection

In the control group, blastocysts will be graded using the Gardner grading system, which evaluates blastocyst expansion, inner cell mass quality and trophectoderm quality. Embryos will be prioritised for transfer using a prespecified morphological ranking consensus established across the three participating centres prior to study initiation.

No genetic testing will be performed in the control group, and embryo selection will be based solely on morphological assessment, reflecting current standard clinical practice.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome of cumulative ongoing pregnancy rate. The assumed control-group cumulative ongoing pregnancy rate of 29.3% was primarily derived from historical data from the participating centres in 2023 (approximately 500 eligible cycles), using eligibility criteria and a treatment pathway closely aligned with the present trial. Because published studies rarely report exactly the same population and endpoint, this value was used as a planning assumption for sample size estimation. External data on frozen blastocyst transfer outcomes in women of similar age without PGT-A were reviewed to confirm that this estimate was within a plausible range.33 34

We hypothesise that ni-PGT-guided embryo selection will result in an absolute increase of 15 percentage points in the cumulative ongoing pregnancy rate (from 29.3% to 44.3%). Using a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 and statistical power of 80%, the required sample size is 161 participants per group.

Accounting for an anticipated attrition rate of 10% (due to loss to follow-up, withdrawal or protocol deviations), the final sample size is 354 participants (177 per group).

Discussion

This multicentre randomised controlled trial will provide important evidence on the clinical utility of ni-PGT for improving pregnancy outcomes in infertile women of advanced maternal age. Several design features strengthen the potential contribution of this study to the evidence base.

First, the study focuses on a clinically relevant population—women aged 35–42 years undergoing their first ICSI cycle—who have elevated rates of embryo aneuploidy and may benefit most from improved embryo selection strategies. This targeted approach enhances the clinical applicability of findings.

Second, the primary outcome of cumulative ongoing pregnancy rate within 12 months, based on all embryo transfers performed within the prespecified window using embryos from a single retrieval cycle, reflects the patient-centred perspective of achieving pregnancy from one treatment episode. This cumulative approach provides more meaningful information for patient counselling than per-transfer outcomes alone.

Third, the pragmatic design elements—including non-restriction of stimulation protocols, freeze-all strategy and single embryo transfer—reflect contemporary clinical practice and enhance generalisability. The multicentre design across three centres further supports external validity.

Fourth, the stratified randomisation by centre and use of allocation concealment minimise selection bias, while the intention-to-treat analysis approach preserves the benefits of randomisation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The open-label design is unavoidable given the different embryo selection strategies but may introduce performance bias. We have attempted to mitigate this through blinded outcome assessment where feasible and standardised outcome definitions.

The ni-PGT technology is subject to technical limitations, including potential contamination of spent culture medium with exogenous DNA and variable concordance with embryo ploidy status. Operational steps to reduce contamination have been implemented, but some degree of technical variability is expected.

The sample size provides adequate power for detecting a 15 percentage point absolute difference in cumulative ongoing pregnancy rate, which represents a clinically meaningful effect. However, the study may be underpowered to detect smaller but potentially important differences or to draw definitive conclusions from subgroup analyses.

Finally, the study is conducted in China, and findings may not be directly generalisable to other populations or healthcare settings with different patient characteristics or clinical practices.

In conclusion, this randomised controlled trial will generate high-quality evidence on whether ni-PGT-guided embryo selection improves cumulative pregnancy outcomes compared with standard morphology-based selection in women of advanced maternal age. The findings will inform clinical practice guidelines and patient counselling regarding the role of non-invasive genetic testing in assisted reproduction.

Trial status

Protocol version 2.0, dated 22 July 2024. The study set-up period started in August 2024 and included multicentre training, site initiation and harmonisation of sampling procedures for ni-PGT; no participant was recruited, enrolled or randomised during this set-up period. Recruitment and the first participant randomisation commenced on 14 February 2025 at the lead site, and additional sites started recruitment after local ethics approval and site initiation. Recruitment is ongoing at all three centres. As of 28 February 2026, 125 participants had been enrolled and 117 had undergone randomisation. Recruitment is expected to complete in February 2027. The trial is registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400088283).
