How do external environmental stimuli and internal psychological cultural identity affect tourists’ behavioral intentions in public cultural spaces?
Authors: Jiaxing Liu, Anji Li, Yongchao Zhu, Lie Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe role of cities and regions is becoming increasingly crucial in the process of global localization. As vital carriers of urban activities, public spaces are receiving widespread attention; however, the substantial impact of multidimensional experiences of public cultural spaces on urban tourism and the role of cultural identity within these spaces have yet to be extensively studied. This study establishes a framework based on scenescape and stimulus-organism-response theories to explore how external stimuli influence tourists’ behavioral intentions. We developed and conducted a questionnaire survey targeting individuals who have visited the “Most Beautiful Public Cultural Spaces” in Nanjing, China. A total of 287 valid samples were collected for analysis. Four types of external environmental stimuli—physical, activity, mechanism, and virtual spaces—were found to have direct and indirect effects on tourists’ behavioral intentions with cultural identity playing an intermediary role. These new insights provide theoretical enlightenment and practical recommendations for enhancing tourism experiences in public cultural spaces.Keywords: Public cultural space, Stimulus-organism-response (SOR) theory, Scenescape theory, Cultural identity, Behavioral intentions

Background

In the era of global localization, there is a growing focus on places that profoundly impact human life [1]. Policies have continuously expanded their attention to local culture to enhance the quality of residents’ cultural life. Consequently, public cultural spaces where people can enjoy cultural activities are increasing in significance [2]. Public cultural spaces are physical domains, including public cultural facilities, places, and cultural activities, constructed by the public cultural service system [3]. Driven by the experience economy, public cultural spaces have evolved beyond merely serving as places for social interactions. They have become cultural hubs and potential spaces for cultural consumption [4, 5]. As urban cultural assets, public cultural spaces emphasize diverse interactive relationships between culture and consumers as well as among consumers. Additionally, following the trend of integrating culture and tourism, their role in promoting local economic development continues to strengthen.

Compared to traditional public cultural spaces, new types of public cultural spaces exhibit trends of expansion in physical attributes, the coexistence of multiple subjects in participation, and a transition from single to multiple functions in social functionality [6]. Furthermore, visiting places of national significance becomes a means of affirming civic consciousness and identity. Governments convey cultural identity and values to tourists through tourism policies, showcasing their unique cultural charm [7]. The significant potential of the tourism industry in projecting cultural identity has led many countries to invest heavily in external promotion [7], emphasizing the importance and uniqueness of local culture.

However, the tourist experience in public cultural spaces is closely intertwined with environmental factors, and comprehensive quantitative research on these factors is relatively sparse [8]. Current research primarily focuses on motivations and emotional experiences [9], and there is a dearth of in-depth exploration into subjective and objective factors [10]. The stimulus-organism-response (SOR) theory highlights the crucial role of external environmental stimuli. Still, there has been limited in-depth exploration of environmental factors in the tourism experience of public cultural spaces.

In addition to external factors, the experiential process of tourism in public cultural spaces should also consider internal factors [11, 12]. Cultural identity belongs to attitudes [13], expressed through attitudes and behaviors based on the cognitive understanding of cultural objects [14]. Relevant research on cultural identity has a long history and spans various fields. Initially, it was primarily theoretical research, focusing on philosophy, sociology, ethnology, communication studies, education, and more. It was later introduced into marketing, and empirical analyses gradually increased. However, many studies have primarily focused on the backdrop of globalization, examining the impact of cultural differences on consumption [15–17] and investigating individual psychological processes of cultural identity [18–20]. There is a lack of research on the influence of external factors on individual cultural identity [21]. Therefore, further refinement and validation are needed for the cultural identity patterns of tourists.

This study addresses previous research gaps by focusing on the relationship between external experiential factors and behavioral intentions in the context of cultural tourism within public cultural spaces. Grounded in the context of cultural consumption, the study employs the SOR theory to construct an overarching framework. Considering the limited granularity of environmental factors in previous studies, we further differentiate external experiential factors within public cultural spaces based on scenescape theory, establishing four environmental experience dimensions: physical space, activity space, mechanism space, and virtual space. The study explores the significant role of cultural identity in the experience of public cultural spaces from tourists’ perspective. Furthermore, the study comprehensively explains how external experiential factors impact behavioral intentions in the context of public cultural space tourism by closely examining tourists’ cognitive psychology and behavior toward these spaces.

Theoretical examination

Scenescape theory

Due to the omission of cultural and environmental elements in other urban-related theories, as scholars such as Clark and Li [22] point out, some researchers have explored the application of scenescape theory to the study of public cultural spaces. Scenescape theory, proposed by Professor Clark et al. from the New Chicago School, introduces a new academic syntax that is consumer-oriented. This theory, centered on consumption, utilizes life and entertainment facilities as carriers and cultural practices as expressive forms, driving economic growth and reshaping the pathways of post-industrial urban renewal and development [23].

Clark and Li [22] argue that scenescape theory makes three significant contributions to current research on social and urban development. First, it excels in driving the upgrade of traditional models. Second, it offers a new theoretical tool for urban transformation and the shift in development dynamics. Third, scenescape theory provides a novel comprehensive perspective for studying the driving role of culture in political, economic, and social evolution.

Fu and Hou [24] developed a framework for analyzing rural public spaces, drawing inspiration from China’s national conditions and incorporating scenescape theory. This framework consists of three primary dimensions—physical space, activity space, and mechanism space—along with fifteen subdimensions. Liu [25] analyzed the methodological pathways for embedding traditional rural cultural fields within physical, virtual, and institutional spaces. Zhang and Hu [26] and along with Zhang [27] proposed pathways for constructing public cultural spaces in rural areas during rural revitalization. They focused on three aspects: physical space, activity space, and institutional space of public culture. Shu [28] reconstructed the spatial structure of public culture in rural areas by considering three dimensions: physical space, living space, and institutional space. Yuan and Liu [29] employed scene theory to analyze health information services for the elderly in public libraries. They proposed suggestions for improvement based on various scenarios (knowledge, space, technology, activities, services, and extensions). Wang et al. [5] employed scenescape theory’s five key elements (people, material space, spatial atmosphere, activity, cultural values) to study the cultural space of historical districts, offering quantifiable perceptual evaluation methods and new design pathways for the protection and development of historic districts.

In conclusion, diverse research endeavors exhibit certain disparities in the dimensions they employ for analysis. The triad of public cultural space dimensions presented by Fu and Hou [24]—physical space, activity space, and mechanism space—has gained widespread adoption in exploring public cultural spaces in China. However, recognizing the limitations of this classical three-fold categorization, particularly in light of the swiftly evolving virtual space [30], a necessity arises to broaden the physical space dimension. Consequently, aligned with scene theory and contemporary features, we select four dimensions as the external experiential facets of public cultural space: physical space, activity space, mechanism space, and virtual space.

Physical space serves as the primary substrate and venue for conducting public cultural activities, encompassing cultural venues, facilities, and cultural resources. Activity space refers to the geographical domain where all public cultural activities occur, relying on physical space as the foundation and support. Mechanism space involves a series of institutional policies, laws, and regulations formulated to provide public cultural services, regulate cultural development, and drive cultural revitalization. Contrasting with physical space, virtual space constitutes a technological realm encompassing the material foundations of digital computation and transmission, the multidimensional aspects of digitally virtual space, and its evolutionary progression.

Cultural identity

The concept of cultural identity is widely present in sociology and anthropology. Current research on cultural identity is typically employed to explain the phenomenon of contemplating culture, leading to acknowledgment and ultimately achieving cultural unity. Various experts have provided numerous definitions of cultural identity from different perspectives. Zhang [31] posits that cultural identity is the dynamic force behind ethnic cohesion and national centripetal force, constituting the profound foundation of national identity. It is particularly crucial in fostering consensus and enhancing the overall sense of national identity through cultural identity, values identity, and citizenship identity. From Zhang’s standpoint, cultural identity refers to the influence of an individual or a group by the culture to which they belong, resulting in a sense of identification with that culture. This sense of identification can be innate or constructed over time. Li and Xu [32] define local cultural identity as the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral connections individuals have with various native local cultures that are perceived, represent different meanings, and highlight distinct values. This encompasses cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions of local cultural identity.

In recent years, China has continuously emphasized the importance of cultural self-confidence, and cultural identity is an essential prerequisite for the responsive effects of cultural self-confidence [33]. This has been studied in various fields. Yang and Zhang [34] explored the relationship between constructing overseas Chinese settlements in Jiangmen, decorating styles, and cultural identity. Chen et al. [35] investigated the impact of emerging media on cultural identity. Li [36] analyzed the value and significance of public intervention and cultural identity in public art creation.

Some scholars have researched cultural identity in the context of tourism. Yu [37] studied the close relationship between heritage tourism and the construction of identity and ethnic cultural identity in the context of cultural and intangible heritage protection and utilization. Tang and Ruan [38] investigated the evolution of people’s cultural identities in ethnic tourism locations. Wu et al. [39] examined how identity affects tourism engagement in agricultural cultural heritage communities. Zheng et al. [9] looked at the relationship between cultural identity and motivation for folk cultural tourism. The relationship between people’s perceptions of culture, sense of place identity, and cultural identity was examined by Li and Liu [40]. The impact of cultural identity and involvement on loyalty among the younger generation of red tourists was investigated by Qiu et al. [41].

Cultural identity research has expanded across diverse domains, notably at the forefront of cultural tourism studies. It encompasses various levels, including the nation, ethnicity, region, and community, demonstrating an ongoing depth trend. The refinement and validation of cultural identity patterns and tourists’ roles in public cultural space tourism experiences remain crucial. Additionally, tourists’ motivations, perceptions, and experiences play pivotal roles in the study of cultural identity.

Research model and hypotheses

Research model

Current research in cultural tourism lacks a unified application of classical theories. Different theories are chosen as the foundation for model construction depending on the specific research content. Public cultural space tourism, as an emerging form of tourism, results from the integration of culture and tourism within the context of the experience economy, making it well-suited for the application of the SOR theory. Therefore, this study employs the SOR framework to establish its overall model.

Additionally, scenescape theory and cultural identity theory provide a robust theoretical foundation for this research. Previous studies have elucidated the relationships among scenescape, identity, and behavior, highlighting the importance of cultural identity in the evaluation of cultural facilities. For instance, Wu [42] utilized scenescape theory to reveal the impact of urban scenescape features on residents’ sense of identification and residential choices. Similarly, Lee [43] developed cultural impact indicators based on cultural identity, assessing the cultural influence of regional facilities.

Building on these theoretical insights and prior research, we propose that in the process of public cultural space tourism, tourists begin with the perception and cognitive experience of the cultural environment, subsequently forming an emotional connection with regional culture. This connection ultimately leads to behavioral intentions such as participation, recommendation, and revisitation. Consequently, this study constructs a multidimensional experiential model based on the SOR framework, encompassing physical space, activity space, mechanism space, and virtual space, to explore their effects on cultural identity and tourist behavioral intentions.

Research hypotheses

According to Social Identity Theory, individuals’ cultural identity is significantly influenced by their social environment and cultural context [44]. External factors, such as regional cultural characteristics, social norms, and historical traditions, not only affect individual values and behavior patterns but also shape their sense of belonging and identification with cultural groups [45]. Previous research has explored the relationship between external environments and behavior intentions. For instance, Zhang [21] examined the impact of festival scenes on tourists’ cultural identity, finding that elements such as event branding, scene design (physical dimension), project content (activity dimension), and project organization (mechanism dimension) significantly enhance tourists’ cultural identity. Yang and Yang [46] analyzed the influence of family environments on students’ ethnic identity, indicating that aspects like the material environment, spiritual environment, and cultural activities promote cultural identity. Shi [47] studied the influence of traditional cultural courses on children’s cultural identity, concluding that educational and activity design effectively fosters cultural identity. Additionally, Lu et al. [48] investigated the effects of red tourism, specifically the roles of interpretative guides and experiential activities, on tourists’ cultural identity, revealing a significant positive correlation.

Moreover, Baltezarevic et al. [49] examined how virtual communities affect cultural identity, arguing that the internet reconstructs the organization of social and interest groups, influencing how people form groups and their presence within them. Ellison et al. [50] suggested that interactions within virtual communities create new opportunities for communication and foster the development of novel social relationships.

In public cultural spaces, factors such as the physical environment, cultural activities, mechanisms of support, and virtual experiences may enhance individuals’ recognition of local culture. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1: Physical space in public cultural space experiences positively influences cultural identity.

H2: Activity space in public cultural space experiences positively influences cultural identity.

H3: Mechanism space in public cultural space experiences positively influences cultural identity.

H4: Virtual space in public cultural space experiences positively influences cultural identity.

Currently, driven by the experience economy, various regions emphasize the integration of cultural resources with tourism to enhance cultural identity and promote local economic development [51]. Tourists’ behavioral intentions and satisfaction with cultural experiences are influenced not only by the quality of products and services but also by policies, physical environments, and unique experiences [52]. In previous studies, Liu [53] focused on cultural theme parks and assessed the attraction of cultural tourism resources on tourism intentions, concluding that various dimensions of cultural tourism resources (historical atmosphere, crafted items, cultural activities, and artistic symbols) positively influence potential tourists’ intentions. Zhao [54] examined how the quality of interpretative services in red venues affects tourists’ intentions to transmit red genes, demonstrating a significant positive impact. Qiao [55] analyzed the influence of public services in rural tourism on tourists’ behavioral intentions, revealing a positive correlation. Additionally, Ma and Lou [56] investigated the effect of service scenarios on tourists’ shopping behavior intentions, indicating a significant positive influence.

Furthermore, Liu et al. [57] examined the role of cultural capital in the consumption behaviors of rural guesthouses, finding that the activity dimension significantly impacts various tourism motivations, further influencing behavioral intentions. Wang [58] investigated the influence of tourism website information on behavioral intentions, also revealing a significant positive impact.

A deeper understanding of how these external environmental factors affect tourists’ behavioral intentions is crucial for enhancing the attractiveness and engagement of public cultural spaces. Thus, we propose the following research hypotheses:

H5: Physical space in the public cultural space experience has a positive impact on behavioral intentions.

H6: Mechanism space in the public cultural space experience has a positive impact on behavioral intentions.

H7: Activity space in the public cultural space experience has a positive impact on behavioral intentions.

H8: Virtual space in the public cultural space experience has a positive impact on behavioral intentions.

According to the Theory of Planned Behavior, various dimensions of the external environment (such as social norms or cultural background) significantly influence individuals’ attitudes and perceived behavioral control [59]. Simultaneously, SOR theory posits that the external environment serves as a stimulus that can trigger behavioral intentions [60]. Several researchers have explored the relationships between external environments and behavioral intentions. Liang [61] investigated the relationships among tourists’ accommodation experiences, cultural identity, and behavioral intentions, finding that cultural identity plays a significant mediating role between these factors. Zhang et al. [62] studied how tourists’ perceptions of museum narratives impact their behavioral intentions, noting that cognitive experiences and cultural identity partially mediate the relationship between narrative perceptions and behavioral intentions. Huang et al. [63] explored the connections between traditional festivals, tourists’ place attachment, and behavioral intentions, concluding that place identity and place attachment positively influence tourists’ behavioral intentions and partially mediate the relationship with traditional festivals.

Additionally, Hu [64] used the Guangzhou Modern History Museum as a case study to examine tourists’ behavioral intentions, suggesting that museums enhance visitors’ awareness of red culture through exhibitions, events, and experiential activities, thereby fostering participation in social activities. Zhao et al. [65] studied the impact of service environments on consumers’ purchasing behaviors regarding intangible cultural heritage products, discovering that service environments influence consumers’ cultural identity and, subsequently, their purchasing decisions. Moreover, Yang and Jiang [66] pointed out that geographical environments, local landscapes, and traditional architecture contribute to cultural identity, with digital technologies positively impacting both cultural identity and behavioral intentions. Peng and Zhou [67] investigated the psychology of youth in rural short video dissemination, noting that video technology and audiovisual forms enhance the desire for self-presentation and social identity, thus boosting behavioral intentions.

Considering the critical role of cultural identity in public cultural tourism experiences, as well as the potential influence of external environmental factors on tourists’ behavioral intentions through cultural identity, we propose the following research hypotheses:

H9: Cultural identity positively influences behavioral intentions in public cultural space tourism.

H10: Cultural identity partially mediates the impact of physical space in public cultural spaces on behavioral intentions in tourism.

H11: Cultural identity partially mediates the impact of activity space in public cultural spaces on behavioral intentions in tourism.

H12: Cultural identity partially mediates the impact of mechanism space in public cultural spaces on behavioral intentions in tourism.

H13: Cultural identity partially mediates the impact of virtual space in public cultural spaces on behavioral intentions in tourism.

Methods

Study site

In China, the “Most Beautiful Public Cultural Spaces” leverage urban and rural public spaces as carriers, provide public cultural services, integrate regional characteristics, encourage shared experiences for hosts and guests, and incorporate new cultural business formats [68]. In the list of recipients for the 2021 Jiangsu Province “Most Beautiful Public Cultural Spaces,” Nanjing boasts 24 projects, including Tong Yue Space and the “Red Reading Space” at the Zhou Enlai Library. Nanjing secured the top spot in the number of selections across the province. The chosen case studies for this research are derived from this list.

Measurements

This study referred to scales in pertinent domestic and international research and built on the foundation of six pre-established latent variables to establish 20 observation variables (items). We developed an external environmental stimulus scale based on prior research that measures various aspects of the environment as stimuli for a place [8]. The scale consists of four latent variables—physical, activity, mechanism, and virtual spaces—developed based on the gradually evolving elements of scenes outlined in scene theory [24, 30]. The items for the physical space dimension were derived from the works of Zhang [21], Liu [69], Fu and Hou [24], Yi [70], and Seo and Lee [71] and include five sample items: “This public cultural space provides me with a multifunctional spatial experience and access to diverse businesses,” “This public cultural space has adequate public facilities,” “This public cultural space has distinctive cultural heritage resources,” “The design of this public cultural space creates a sound cultural atmosphere,” and “This public cultural space’s architecture and landscape design exhibit regional characteristics.”

The measurement items for the activity space dimension were derived from the works of Shu [28], Fu and Hou [24], Zhao [72], and Zhang [21] and include three sample items: “This public cultural space captures the daily lives of local people,” “This public cultural space offers various types of cultural exchange activities,” and “This public cultural space offers regular cultural activities such as folk rituals, festivals, and intangible cultural heritage experiences.” The measurement items for the mechanism space dimension were derived from the works of Liu [69], Fu and Hou [24], Zhang [73], and Meng [74] and include three sample items: “This public cultural space provides all the services needed,” “Regulations and systems in place ensure the daily operation of this public cultural space,” and “This public cultural space has a comprehensive social participation mechanism.” Finally, the measurement items for the virtual space dimension were derived from the work of Yuan and Liu [29] and include three sample items: “This public cultural space has an advanced information service platform,” “This public cultural space has convenient information resources and a navigation system,” and “This public cultural space offers a mobile (virtual) experience of the venue.”

Cultural identity is a psychological activity of subjective emotional affiliation involving recognition, acceptance, belonging, and practice of the object culture in aspects such as language, customs, and values [75]. Fu and Zhong [76] propose from a psychological perspective that cultural identity consists of three dimensions—recognition, acceptance, and integration—while Dehyle [77] divides it into three dimensions: cultural investment, belonging, and integration. Both reflect the process of cultural experience—from understanding to emotional involvement to action. Building on this foundation, this study draws upon cultural identity (the fifth latent variable of this study) items compiled by Li and Xu [33] and Shang [78] to form three sample items: “I want to know more about the local culture of this region,” “I have a sense of the identity of the local culture and wish to support it,” and “I have repeatedly participated in the local culture through my actions.”

Behavioral intention is a relatively mature research scale. In cultural space research, Luo [79] investigated the relationship between museum experience perception and visitors’ intention to revisit. By reviewing and summarizing relevant literature, Luo found that most scholars tend to measure the intention to revisit using the dimensions of “recommendation” and “revisitation.” Liu [69] suggests that the behavioral intentions of museum visitors are primarily realized through three items: recommendation intention, revisitation intention, and sharing intention. This study draws on the behavioral intention (the sixth latent variable of this study) items compiled by Luo and Liu to develop three sample items: “I would recommend this place to others,” “If I have a chance, I would like to come here again,” and “I am willing to share this experience with others.”

Recognizing the diverse contexts of this research and aligning with its specific background and objectives, certain modifications were applied to the wording of select observation variables to better suit the emphasis of this study. The developed measurement scale was implemented using a Likert 5-point scale with scores ranging from 1 to 5, denoting degrees of agreement from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.”

Data collection

To ensure the rigor of this research, a pilot survey was conducted prior to the formal survey. During the pilot phase, 65 questionnaires were distributed online, yielding 61 valid responses. After performing significance and factor analyses, items with unsatisfactory results were eliminated. Additionally, participant feedback was utilized to refine the wording of the items for improved clarity.

The sample size for Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) is influenced by multiple factors, including the number of questions in the questionnaire, the number of indicators and constructs, the magnitude of factor loadings and path coefficients, as well as the presence of missing data [80]. To ensure the scientific validity of the sample size, this study follows the recommendations of Wolf et al. [80] by conducting proactive Monte Carlo simulations to determine the appropriate sample size for the specific SEM, rather than relying on general empirical methods.

Wang et al. [81] developed the pwrSEM v0.12 application to calculate the necessary sample size for SEM based on Monte Carlo simulation methods. They argue that software like G*Power [82]may not adequately address the complexities of SEM. Using this software, we calculated that a sample size exceeding 200 achieves a statistical power greater than 0.80, while a sample size of 300 surpasses 0.85.

For the formal survey conducted from October 1 to 15, 2023, we distributed a total of 300 questionnaires to tourists who had visited the “Most Beautiful Public Cultural Spaces” in Nanjing. Using an onsite random sampling method, we successfully collected all questionaires. After excluding incomplete responses, we retained 287 valid questionnaires, resulting in a valid response rate of 95.67% and achieving a statistical power exceeding 0.80 for the target effect. Thus, the preparatory indicators for this study meet the necessary criteria for further analysis.

Data analysis

This study employed SPSS 25.0 and AMOS 24.0 software to assess the questionnaire’s reliability, model fit, and structural, convergent, and discriminant validity.

Reliability and validity analysis

First, the Cronbach’s α values for all variables exceeded 0.7, which indicates good internal consistency and high reliability [8] (see Table 2). Second, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin test method was used to analyze the structural validity of the scale. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin value was 0.864, exceeding the threshold of 0.6, thereby indicating suitability for factor analysis. The cumulative explained variance of the extracted common factors was 73.76%, which surpassed the 50% threshold suggested by Liu et al. [8], suggesting good structural validity of the sample data. Furthermore, we conducted a convergent validity analysis using the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability for each variable. The results indicate that the AVE values for all six variables are greater than 0.5, and the composite reliability values for all variables are higher than 0.7 (meeting the criteria recommended by Rather and Hollebeek [83]), which suggests that the data analyzed exhibit good convergent validity (see Table 3). We also conducted a discriminant validity test. The results indicated that the square root of AVE for each variable was greater than the maximum absolute value of the correlation coefficient between variables [83], thus demonstrating good discriminant validity (see Table 4).

We conducted a model fit test on the structural model. The following model fit indices were obtained: AGFI = 0.916, GFI = 0.939, TLI = 0.985, NFI = 0.936, IFI = 0.988, CFI = 0.964; χ²/df = 1.206; RMSEA = 0.027. These findings indicate both the structural validity of the model and its good fit [83].

Testing the mediating effect

We subsequently used the bootstrap method to examine the mediating role of cultural identity. To be more precise, 5,000 sampling instances and a 95% confidence interval were chosen. A partial mediating effect is present if a*b and c’ have the same sign, and if a and b are significant, then c’ is also significant [8]. The results in Table 6 show that all paths are partially mediated, indicating that tourists’ perceived environmental experiences of the public cultural spaces had an indirect impact on their behavioral intentions through the mediation of cultural identity. Thus, H10, H11, H12, and H13 were supported. Furthermore, the share of effects indicated that physical space had the greatest impact on behavioral intentions through cultural identity.

Conclusion and discussions

Conclusion

Based on SOR and “scene theory,” we constructed a model to investigate the effect of public cultural space experiences on tourists’ behavioral intentions. Specifically, we applied SPSS 25.0 and AMOS 24.0 to explore tourists’ perceptions and evaluations of their experiences in public cultural spaces and determine whether such evaluations triggered an emotional response, such as forming a sense of identity related to the local culture, which would then ultimately affect their willingness to recommend and revisit the destination and share their experiences. The validation results reveal that all hypotheses were supported. The research conclusions are as follows.

First, the space’s physical, activity, mechanism, and virtual dimensions positively affected behavioral intentions. Positive environmental factors promoted tourists’ intentions to recommend and revisit a destination and share their experiences. So far, few studies have explored the impact of environmental factors on tourists’ behavioral intentions using a quantitative approach. Our study emphasizes filling this research gap. Using Nanjing as the study case and referring to the practice of past studies, we constructed a revised scale to measure public cultural space experiences, enhancing the quantitative research on cultural spaces and expanding the research on cultural tourism.

Second, we found that physical, activity, mechanism, and virtual spaces positively affected cultural identity. Specifically, carefully designed physical spaces (including buildings and landscapes) triggered emotional resonance among tourists and fostered an association with the local cultural identity. Various cultural activities organized in the activity space allowed tourists to acquire an in-depth understanding of local culture through direct participation, enhancing their cultural identity. Efficient mechanism spaces and high-quality services facilitated enjoyable cultural experiences and enhanced their sense of identity related to the cultural space. Virtual spaces allow tourists to observe cultural scenes through digital technology, stimulating their interest and curiosity and deepening their cultural identity. These factors collectively foster an environment that promotes tourists’ active engagement with and identification with local culture. These findings offer valuable insights for designing and managing public cultural spaces.

Third, we found that environmental factors in public cultural spaces indirectly affect tourists’ behavioral intentions by developing their cultural identity. High-quality public cultural space experiences enhanc tourists’ cultural identity within the destination. In turn, a strong cultural identity promoted their intentions to recommend and revisit the destination and share their experiences with others. Creating local experiences was conducive to triggering an emotional connection with tourists, whereas cultural identity, as an attitudinal response, influenced their travel-related behavioral intentions. Nanjing’s cultural uniqueness created such an experience, promoting a sense of identity-related to the local culture, thereby stimulating positive intentions from the tourists to revisit.

Theoretical significance

This study has theoretical significance for several reasons. First, it constructed an in-depth theoretical framework based on SOR theory combined with scene theory. SOR theory emphasizes the important role of environmental stimuli, whereas the introduction of scene theory further deepens our understanding of how external factors are experienced. By categorizing public cultural space into physical, activity, mechanism, and virtual, we provided a comprehensive theoretical perspective for corresponding research, allowing for a more in-depth analysis of the influencing mechanisms behind tourists’ behavioral intentions.

Second, this study closely examined cultural identity, providing comprehensive discussions by examining the mediating mechanisms involved. Cultural identity is a cognitive process and a manifestation of emotions and attitudes. By introducing cultural identity into tourism experiences, we revealed the critical role of cultural identity in public cultural space tourism and systematically analyzed its relationship with tourists’ behavioral intentions. The findings enrich the research domain of cultural identity and provide an innovative approach and research direction for future studies.

Third, this study determined the impact of the four constituent dimensions of public cultural space on tourists’ behavioral intentions and provided a detailed and extensive examination of tourism. By decomposing public cultural space into physical, activity, mechanism, and virtual, we revealed the contributions of each dimension to tourists’ behavioral intentions. Such nuanced analysis helps guide operations and management and improve the effectiveness of public cultural spaces.

Finally, this study provided a new orientation and insights into the theoretical analysis of the relationship between perceived environmental experiences of public cultural spaces and tourists’ behavioral intentions. Through the in-depth analysis, we demonstrated the applicability of relevant theories, provided valuable references for operations and management of public spaces, proposed a comprehensive explanatory framework for the tourism experience of public cultural spaces, and provided a solid foundation for in-depth discussion of the subject for the future.

Practical significance

This study provides specific dimensions for the design and management of public cultural spaces. By highlighting the impact of four constitutive dimensions—physical space, activity space, mechanism space, and virtual space—on visitor behavioral intentions, the research offers valuable references for the establishment and enhancement of public cultural spaces. Notably, the study finds that activity space has the most significant influence on cultural identity and behavioral intentions. Therefore, practical designs should not only incorporate the aesthetics and attractiveness of the external environment but also focus on comprehensive experiences that create engaging cultural encounters.

In addition, this research reveals the potential for local governments and cultural institutions to convey local culture and values to residents and visitors through cultural facilities. This provides a practical direction for local governments to communicate cultural information more effectively in tourism development. By fostering culturally distinctive experiences, public cultural spaces can create unique cultural identities, enhance the influence of local cultural brands, and further promote the sustainable development of cultural tourism.

Finally, this study offers a referential direction for the integrated development of tourism and culture. By emphasizing the positive role of public cultural spaces in local tourism amidst the trend of cultural and tourism integration, the research provides recommendations for local governments and cultural institutions to attract visitors through investments in and development of public cultural spaces, thereby increasing cultural consumption and promoting dual growth in both the local economy and culture.

Limitations of the study and future research directions

Although this study provided valuable insights into the relationship between environmental experiences of public cultural spaces and tourists’ behavioral intentions, some limitations must be considered. First, the samples were collected from specific public cultural spaces; hence, the results might not fully represent tourists of all cultural backgrounds and cultural spaces. Future research should improve the generalizability by expanding the sample coverage to more regions and public cultural spaces. In addition, this study mainly focused on the four dimensions of public cultural space and did not cover all factors that may affect tourist experiences. Future studies are suggested to introduce more dimensions and explore other potential factors to comprehensively capture the diversity of tourism experiences in public cultural spaces.
