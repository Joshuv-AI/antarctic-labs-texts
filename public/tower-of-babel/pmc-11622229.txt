Development of a Novel Label-Free Subunit HILIC-MS Method for Domain-Specific Free Thiol Identification and Quantitation in Therapeutic Monoclonal Antibodies
Authors: Xiaoxiao Huang, Xin Wang, Victoria C. Cotham, David Bramhall, Jieqiang Zhong, Yimeng Zhao, Haibo Qiu, Shunhai Wang, Ning Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cysteine residues are crucial for the formation of conserved disulfide bonds in therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), which are essential for their folding and structural stability. The presence of free thiols in mAbs can indicate incomplete disulfide bond formation, potentially impacting the molecule’s conformational stability. Free thiol quantitation has been achieved using labeling-based strategies such as maleimide and haloalkyl derivatives at both intact and peptide levels. However, intact-level measurement only provides total free thiol levels, while peptide-level measurement is time-consuming and more prone to assay-induced artifacts. In this study, we present a novel label-free HILIC-MS method that separates free thiol species at the subunit level, followed by free thiol localization by the MS2 fragmentation pattern. This allows for facile identification and quantitation of intrachain free thiols at domain-specific resolution. Compared to bottom-up approaches, this subunit HILIC-MS method excels in simpler sample preparation and higher throughput and enables chain-specific free thiol analysis for bispecific mAbs. This method can be readily applied for screening mAb candidates with elevated levels of free thiols in early-stage developability assessment and facilitating an effective comparability evaluation of mAb samples during process development.

Introduction

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have grown rapidly in recent years to become a major class of biotherapeutics on the market, revolutionizing treatment strategies for a wide array of diseases, from cancer to autoimmune disorders. These biotherapeutics are engineered to specifically target disease-associated antigens with high precision and efficacy, offering advantages such as reduced side effects and enhanced patient outcomes compared to small-molecule drugs.1 MAb molecules often host a variety of post-translational modifications (PTMs), such as glycosylation, oxidation, and deamidation, which contribute to significant charge and structural heterogeneities.2 In addition, the proper formation of disulfide bonds, formed between the sulfur atoms of two cysteine residues, is crucial to the structural integrity and subsequent safety and therapeutic efficacy of the mAb molecules. For both IgG1 and IgG4 subclasses, a highly conserved disulfide bond pattern, including four interchain and 12 intradomain disulfide bonds, can be found. These bonds are essential for maintaining an intricate higher-order structure necessary for the binding affinity/selectivity to target antigens, ensuring pharmacokinetic properties, and preserving the overall stability of the molecule under various physiological and storage conditions.3,4

However, despite significant efforts in process and formulation development, mAbs can still be susceptible to disulfide bond reduction, resulting in the formation of free thiols (sulfhydryl groups) in the final drug products.5 These free thiols can arise from incomplete oxidation during protein synthesis within host cells as well as from subsequent reduction during purification, formulation, or storage phases. The presence of free thiols in mAbs has significant implications for their quality, safety, and efficacy. Studies have shown that free thiols can alter the binding affinity of antibodies to their target antigens, potentially compromising therapeutic efficacy.6 Furthermore, they can promote antibody aggregation, which not only affects stability but also increases the risk of immunogenicity in patients.7−9 Additionally, free thiols may decrease the thermal stability of mAbs, making them susceptible to denaturation and degradation.10 Understanding and quantifying free thiols in mAbs is therefore important to ensuring the safety and efficacy of these biotherapeutics. The ability to accurately measure and control free thiol levels enables improved optimization of manufacturing processes as well as the mitigation of potential risks associated with free thiol formation. Moreover, as the field advances, there is a growing interest in engineering antibodies with strategically placed noncanonical cysteine residues to enhance stability or facilitate conjugation with therapeutic payloads.11 As a result, the study of free thiols extends beyond quality control to include innovative strategies for antibody design and modification.

Several analytical methods have been developed to detect and quantify free thiols in mAbs, each offering unique advantages in terms of sensitivity, specificity, throughput, and practicality.12 The most widely used method, Ellman’s reagent assay, uses 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) to react with free thiols to produce a colored product, which can be detected spectrophotometrically in the visible region at 412 nm.13 This method is straightforward but can only be used to measure the total free thiol levels of proteins. Furthermore, this technique has low sensitivity and requires a large amount of material. Alternatively, mass spectrometry (MS) is a powerful tool that offers great advantages in detecting and quantifying free thiols in mAbs with a great sensitivity and specificity. Due to the relatively small delta mass introduced by free thiol species (1.0079 Da/site), most MS-based methods involve labeling the free thiol groups with a thiol-specific mass tag for intact, domain, or peptide-level detection. The most commonly used labeling reagents are haloalkyl and maleimide derivatives.14−20 For instance, one study used maleimide-PEG2-biotin labeling to introduce a large mass shift (+525 Da) for MS-based free thiol quantitation at the intact mAb level.14 Another study, which compared various maleimide derivatives, found that N-tert-butylmaleimade (NtBM) enhanced the hydrophobicity of free thiol groups, resulting in improved liquid chromatography (LC) separation prior to MS measurement.15,18 Additionally, isotopically labeled iodoacetic acids have been used to distinguish pre-existing free thiols from those generated via reduction. This strategy allowed for site-specific quantitation of free thiols through bottom-up analysis.20 Despite their merits, free thiol labeling strategies often suffer from complicated sample preparation steps, which can introduce artifacts, such as disulfide bond scrambling.21 Alternatively, label-free methods have gained traction and become increasingly feasible due to significant advancements in the field of liquid chromatography. For instance, due to the increased hydrophobicity, free thiol species can be effectively separated from the main species at the intact mAb level using a 5 min reversed-phase ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography (RP-UHPLC) method developed on an optimized diphenyl column.22 Moreover, a native hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) method was also applied to separate free thiol species of intact mAbs.23 These methods, albeit simple, do not enable free thiol localization. To achieve improved resolution, other methods have been developed to investigate domain-level free thiols in mAbs. These methods utilized IgdE and IdeS codigestion24,25 or limited Lys-C digestion17,19 strategies to generate Fab and Fc/2 subunits of IgG1 before RPLC-MS measurement.

Here, we introduce a subunit-level label-free strategy that couples hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography with mass spectrometry (HILIC-MS) to directly localize and quantify mAb free thiols with domain-specific resolution. MAbs were digested using IdeS (FabRICATOR) and subsequently treated with 5 mM DTT under native conditions to selectively reduce the interchain disulfide bonds. This process resulted in Fd, LC, and Fc/2 subunits with intrachain disulfide bonds intact, which were subjected to HILIC-MS/MS analysis. We demonstrated that HILIC separation can provide baseline resolution of free thiol species at the subunit level to enable accurate quantification without the need for sample labeling prior to analysis. Furthermore, free thiol localization can be directly achieved by targeted MS2 analysis based on the fragmentation patterns arising from higher-energy collision-induced dissociation (HCD). This method was then applied to a wide range of mAbs to survey the occurrence of domain-specific free thiols. Good quantitation performance of this method was also demonstrated by comparing results with those of a conventional bottom-up approach. Lastly, we showed that with minimal sample treatment this new method allowed for rapid and quantitative comparison of domain-specific free thiol levels in mAb batches produced from two different processes, highlighting its potential in supporting process development.

Experimental Section

Materials

FabRICATOR and GalactEXO enzymes were purchased from Genovis (Cambridge, MA). Invitrogen UltraPure 1 M Tris-HCl buffer, pH 7.5, Pierce DTT (dithiothreitol, no-weigh format), acetonitrile (ACN; Optima LC/MS grade), trifluoroacetic acid (TFA; sequencing grade), and propionic acid (PA; 99+%, extra pure) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). 2-Propanol (IPA; HPLC Plus) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). All mAb samples were produced at Regeneron (Tarrytown, NY).

Sample Preparation

MAbs were digested with FabRICATOR (1 IUB milliunit per 1 μg of protein) in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the interchain disulfide bonds were selectively reduced with 5 mM DTT at room temperature for 10 min to generate Fd, LC, and Fc/2 subunits without compromising the integrity of intradomain disulfide bonds. For quantitation, mAbs were codigested with FabRICATOR (1 IUB milliunit per 1 μg of protein) and GalactEXO (1 IUB milliunit per 1 μg of protein) in 20 mM Tris-HCl at 37 °C for 1 h and followed by selective reduction of interchain disulfide bonds. A total of 2.5 μg of proteins (0.5 μL) was directly injected without adjusting the solvent composition for each LC-MS run.

HILIC-MS/MS

The HILIC method was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system (Milford, MA) equipped with a Waters ACQUITY Premier Glycoprotein BEH Amide column (300 Å, 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm) with the column compartment set to 80 °C.26 The mobile phases were 0.1% TFA in water as A and 0.1% TFA in acetonitrile as B, and the flow rate was set to 0.2 mL/min. The separation was achieved over 40 min with an initial 0.5 min hold at 15% A, followed by an increase to 25% A over the next 0.5 min, and another linear increase to 35% A over the next 26 min. The gradient was then ramped to 100% A over 1 min and held for 1 min, before dropping to 15% A in 1 min, and then maintained at the initial condition for 10 min to equilibrate the column for the next run. A fluorescence detector (FLR) was used online for protein detection with 280 nm excitement and 340 nm emission wavelengths. The UPLC was coupled to a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Plus Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Waltham, MA). To counteract TFA-induced ion suppression, a desolvation gas was introduced during ESI.27 The sheath gas flow was redirected to a Duran pressure plus bottle containing 3:1 PA:IPA, equipped with a Canary-Safe Cap using 1/8″ tubing. The incoming tubing from the bottle was then connected to the HESI probe in a Thermo Scientific Ion Max ion source. The capillary voltage was set at 3.5 kV, with a sheath gas flow rate of 15 arbitrary units and an auxiliary gas flow rate of 10 arbitrary units. The capillary temperature was set at 350 °C, and the probe heater temperature was set at 250 °C. Mass spectra were acquired in the mass range of m/z 1000–6000 for MS1 analysis. For each subunit and its corresponding free thiol species, the top three most abundant charge states were used to generate a precursor inclusion list for targeted HCD-based MS2 analysis. A normalized collision energy (NCE) of 30 was used for HCD fragmentation.

Data Analysis

Integration of XIC peaks from the HILIC-MS analysis was performed using Thermo Fisher Xcalibur software (version 4.1). Intact mass spectra deconvolution and quantitation by mass area were performed by using Intact Mass software from Protein Metrics. MS2 fragmentation mapping was performed using ProSight Native software with Proteinaceous version 1.0.24144 (Evanston, IL). All fragments were manually validated.

Results and Discussion

Free Thiol Species Can Be Baseline-Resolved by HILIC at the Subunit Level

A recombinant IgG4 mAb (mAb-1) was used as a model molecule. After IdeS digestion and selective reduction of the interchain disulfide bonds, the resulting Fd, LC, and Fc/2 subunits were separated on a HILIC column and analyzed by both a fluorescence detector (FLR) and mass spectrometry. As shown in the FLR profile, mAb-1 subunits were well separated based on their relative hydrophilicity, eluting in the order of Fd, LC, and Fc/2 (Figure 1A). Various N-glycoforms of Fc/2 (e.g., G0F, G1F, and G2F), resulting from the N-glycosylation microheterogeneity of the conserved Fc N-glycosite (e.g., Asn297), were sequentially eluted based on the size of the N-glycans, consistent with previous literature reports.28,29 Notably, a minor LC-related peak and two minor Fc/2-related (G0F form) peaks were also observed to elute earlier than their corresponding main species. These variants were subsequently identified as the free thiol species based on three unique observations. First, the deconvoluted mass of each variant peak showed an approximate +2 Da mass shift compared to its corresponding main peak, indicating the reduction of one disulfide bond and the formation of two free thiol groups (Figure 1B and 1C). Notably, although other PTMs, such as deamidation and amidation, may also potentially explain this mass shift, they are unlikely to cause a significant change in hydrophilicity to allow for a baseline separation of the variant at the subunit level. Second, the raw MS spectrum of each variant peak displayed a charge envelope with increased charge states, suggesting greater unfolding of the variant species compared with the main species. This observation can be rationalized by the enhanced unfolding of the free thiol species, attributed to the absence of a disulfide bond under the denaturing HILIC conditions. Finally, the extensive unfolding of the free thiol species could also expose hydrophobic patches that were initially shielded by the presence of disulfide bonds. This increased the overall hydrophobicity of the subdomain, leading to early elution during HILIC separation. Importantly, unlike RPLC where the free thiol species are eluted after the main species, the early elution of the free thiol species by HILIC is advantageous. This feature allows for the sensitive detection of these low-abundance variants with minimal interference from the overwhelmingly more abundant main species. Based on the intensities of the deconvoluted spectra, free thiol species present at as low as 0.95% were confidently detected in IgG4.

Targeted MS2 Localizes Free Thiols to Individual Domains

The Fc/2 subunit consists of two domains, CH2 and CH3, each containing one intradomain disulfide bond. Given that two free thiol peaks were observed for Fc/2, it is reasonable to hypothesize that HILIC could provide domain-specific separation of free thiol species at the Fc/2 level. To confirm this hypothesis, the G0F glycoform of Fc/2 was chosen for further investigation, as it was present at high abundance and its free thiol variants were largely free from interference by other coeluting species. To better visualize the separation, an extracted ion chromatogram (XIC) was constructed using an m/z window containing both the main and the free thiol species, based on the most abundant charge state of the G0F glycoform (Figure 2A, top two panels). Similar to the FLR trace, two peaks corresponding to the free thiol species were observed for Fc/2, both of which were eluted earlier than the main peak. This separation profile was consistently observed for the Fc/2 subunits from all mono- and bispecific antibodies evaluated, which included both IgG1 and IgG4 subclasses (data not shown). To localize the free thiols to individual domains, we leveraged the distinct HCD fragmentation patterns of the IgG subunits in the presence and absence of a disulfide bond. It has been demonstrated that HCD fragmentation is highly inefficient in generating b and y ions within the disulfide-bonded regions of IgG subunits, as this requires the concurrent cleavage of both the peptide amide bond and disulfide bond.30,31 In contrast, upon reduction of the intradomain disulfide bond, HCD fragmentation can readily access the previously disulfide-bonded region. Therefore, we performed targeted MS2 analysis of each Fc/2 species within its corresponding elution window using HCD fragmentation. For the main species, as expected, HCD fragmentation primarily occurred at the N- and C-termini, while no b and y ions have been observed within either of the two disulfide-bonded regions (Figure 2B, top panel). In contrast, for both free thiol species, additional HCD fragmentation also occurred in regions previously bonded by disulfide bonds. Detailed MS2 spectra of these signature fragment ions are also shown in Figure S3 and Figure S4. This analysis not only further validated the presence of free thiol species but also allowed for the localization of free thiols into individual domains (Figure 2B, bottom two panels). For the earlier eluting free thiol peak, the additional HCD fragmentation occurred only within the CH2 disulfide-bonded region, suggesting it correlates to CH2 free thiols. For the later eluting free thiol peak, the additional HCD fragmentation occurred only within the CH3 disulfide-bonded region, suggesting it correlates to CH3 free thiols. Using the signature MS2 fragment ions specific to each species, XICs were generated to reveal the elution profiles of both CH2 and CH3 free thiol species. This analysis further confirmed that the two minor Fc/2 peaks eluting before the main peak corresponded to CH2 and CH3 free thiol species, respectively (Figure 2A, bottom five panels). We hypothesize that under the denaturing HILIC method conditions, the unfolding of CH2 and CH3 domains, in the absence of their respective disulfide bonds, leads to varying levels of increased hydrophobicity, which could be well resolved by HILIC separation at the subunit level.

Next, we investigated the LC subunit, which contains two domains, VL and CL, each with one intradomain disulfide bond. In the mAb-1 sample, only one free thiol peak was observed for the LC subunit, which was eluted earlier than the main peak, as indicated by the XIC (Figure S1A). When performing targeted MS2 analysis of the main peak, HCD fragmentation only occurred at the N- and C-termini, as well as the region between the VL and CL disulfide bonds, validating that the disulfide-bonded regions are largely inaccessible to HCD fragmentation. In contrast, for the free thiol peak, additional HCD fragmentation was observed in the previously disulfide-bonded region from the CL domain but not from the VL domain (Figure S1B). Detailed MS2 spectra of these signature fragment ions are also shown in Figure S5. Similarly, XICs of these signature fragment ions further confirmed the identity of this free thiol peak as CL free thiol species. As only one free thiol peak was observed in mAb-1, we cannot conclude that HILIC separation can resolve the VL and CL free thiol species at the LC subunit level. It is possible that the VL free thiol species were coeluted with the CL free thiol species, but their abundance may be too low to be detected by MS2. Nevertheless, this analysis demonstrated that CL free thiols were the major free thiol species for the LC subunit in this molecule.

Lastly, we investigated the Fd subunit, which contains two domains, VH and CH1, each with one intradomain disulfide bond. As no free thiol peak was observed for the mAb-1 Fd subunit, another recombinant IgG4 mAb (mAb-2) was used for the investigation. For the mAb-2 Fd subunit, only one free thiol peak was observed, which was eluted earlier than the main peak, as indicated by the XIC (Figure S2A). Consistently, targeted MS2 analysis of the main peak resulted in HCD fragments only at the N- and C- termini, as well as the region between the VH and CH1 disulfide bonds. In contrast, HCD fragmentation of the free thiol peak resulted in additional fragment ions in the previously disulfide-bonded region from the VH domain but not from the CH1 domain (Figure S2B). Detailed MS2 spectra of these signature fragment ions are also shown in Figure S6. Using XICs of these signature fragment ions, the identity of the free thiol peak was further confirmed as the VH free thiol species. Again, as only one free thiol peak was observed in this example, we cannot conclude HILIC separation can resolve the VH and CH1 free thiol species at the Fd subunit level.

Compared to previously published free thiol methods at the subunit level,24,25 this newly developed approach offers significant advantages by allowing direct mapping of the free thiols into individual domains through the use of HCD fragmentation. This strategy eliminated the need for offline free thiol localization, which is typically achieved by fractionation and bottom-up analysis, a process that not only is time-consuming but can also introduce artifacts due to the extensive sample handling.

MS-Based Quantitation of Domain-Specific Free Thiols

To achieve domain-specific resolution of free thiol species, HILIC separation must be carried out at the Fd, LC, and Fc/2 levels, which required selective reduction of the interchain disulfide bonds. Under mild reduction conditions (e.g., low concentrations of DTT, room temperature, and neutral pH), the intradomain disulfide bonds from both IgG1 and IgG4 subclasses are highly resistant to reduction, as they are well buried within their respective domains with very poor solvent accessibility.32,33 A previous study has also demonstrated that, under near native conditions, the intradomain disulfide bonds of IgG1 were unaffected by DTT treatment.34 In addition, selective reduction of IgG interchain disulfide bonds has been a well-validated step in the production of Cys-based antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), which is known for preserving the integrity of the intradomain disulfide bonds. To verify that our selective reduction conditions would not lead to reduction of intradomain disulfide bonds, a time-course study was performed. Briefly, after IdeS digestion, the mAb-1 sample was treated with 5 mM DTT in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer, pH 7.5, at room temperature for 5–45 min. The CL free thiols on the LC subunit, as well as the CH2 and CH3 free thiols on the Fc/2 subunit, were quantified at varying time points (Figure S7). The results showed that the free thiol levels of each domain remained largely constant over time, with increases all below 0.16% after 10 min of incubation. This analysis confirmed that the applied reduction conditions are highly selective to interchain disulfide bonds, which should not affect the quantitation of free thiols from intradomain disulfide bonds. It is important to note that, although these conditions should work for most IgG1 and IgG4 molecules, caution needs to be exercised for other nonstandard formats, where selective reduction conditions may need to be evaluated on a case-by-case basis.

As previously shown in Figure 1A, various Fc/2 glycoforms were present and separated on the HILIC column based on their N-glycan structures. Due to this N-glycosylation heterogeneity, quantitation of CH2 and CH3 free thiols at the Fc/2 level can be complicated. To improve the quantitation of these free thiol species, we investigated different sample treatment strategies with the goal of consolidating the Fc glycoforms, thereby achieving an improved signal-to-noise ratio. Initially, we considered eliminating the N-glycosylation heterogeneity by removing the entire Fc N-glycan with PNGaseF. However, it is commonly known that Fc N-glycosylation is critical for mAb structural stability. For instance, Zheng et al. have found that deglycosylated mAbs were more prone to guanidine-induced denaturation and unfolding compared to glycosylated mAbs, particularly in the CH2 domain.35 Therefore, removal of the entire Fc N-glycan may increase the susceptibility of the CH2 disulfide bond during the partial reduction step. Additionally, after complete removal of the Fc N-glycan, the retention of the Fc/2 subunit on the HILIC column was greatly reduced (Figure S8). In many cases, the deglycosylated Fc/2 may elute at a retention time similar to that of the LC and Fd subunits, leading to challenges in detecting and quantifying the low-abundance free thiol species. To address these concerns, GalactEXO, a β-galactosidase that removes 1–3,4-linked terminal galactose, was utilized to convert the majority of N-glycans (e.g., G1F and G2F) into G0F under native conditions. This process maintained the conformational stability of the CH2 domain, while also enabling signal consolidation for various Fc glycoforms. Conveniently, β-galactosidase treatment can be carried out concurrently with IdeS digestion, both of which can be completed within 1 h in 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5 at 37 °C (Figure S8).

To achieve free thiol quantitation, an MS-based quantitation strategy was adopted to address scenarios where other subunit proteoforms (e.g., PTMs) may coelute with free thiol species and thus interfere with FLR-based quantitation. As described in Figure S9, mass peak areas of the free thiol species and the main species, based on the deconvoluted mass spectra, were utilized for the calculation.

Survey Domain-Specific Free Thiols in mAbs by Subunit HILIC-MS and Evaluation of Quantitation Performance

Compared with bottom-up-based approaches that involve complex sample preparation and data analysis, the subunit HILIC-MS method could achieve domain-specific free thiol quantitation with greatly improved throughput. Thus, we applied this method to 10 in-house mAbs and 7 commercial mAbs (both mono- and bispecific Abs from both IgG1 and IgG4 subclasses, Table S1) to survey the occurrence of domain-specific free thiols. Among the six mAb domains, four of them, including VH, CL, CH2, and CH3, were frequently observed with quantifiable levels of free thiols (Figure 3A). Interestingly, VL free thiol species were not detected by this method, presumably due to their low abundances, as revealed by a bottom-up analysis (Table S2). Conversely, despite being identified at moderate levels through bottom-up analysis (Table S2), the CH1 free thiol species were also not detected. We speculate that under the HILIC separation conditions, the CH1 free thiol species may not be adequately resolved from the main species for MS detection. Most intriguingly, although the VH free thiol species were less frequently detected, their abundances varied dramatically across different molecules. This observation aligned well with the fact that the VH domain, containing complementarity-determining regions (CDR), is highly molecule-specific. Notably, omalizumab exhibited the highest level of free thiols (35%) in the VH domain among all mAbs evaluated, which matched well with previous findings.19,36 Being located near the CDRs, the presence of free thiols in the VH domain may potentially affect the stability and antigen-binding properties of the mAbs. The ability to monitor VH free thiols in a rapid fashion makes this method a valuable tool in candidate developability assessment.

Next, to evaluate the quantitation performance, we compared this method to a conventional bottom-up approach that employed a differential alkylation strategy, incorporating stable isotope-labeled alkylation reagents, for site-specific free thiol measurement (Supporting Information). A subset of the previous mAb samples was included in this comparison. It is worth mentioning that at the peptide level, the bottom-up approach measures the free thiol levels of individual cysteine residues, while the subunit HILIC-MS method quantifies each free thiol pair (i.e., as a result of disulfide bond reduction) within a single domain. For a valid comparison, free thiol levels of the two paired cysteine residues, as measured by the bottom-up method, were averaged to represent each domain-specific free thiol level. As shown in Figure 3B, an overall good correlation between the two methods was observed, highlighting the good quantitation performance of the subunit HILIC-MS method. Importantly, the consistent quantitation results obtained by this new method, when compared to the standard bottom-up approach, also demonstrated its validity, where neither other PTMs (e.g., deamidation) nor the applied selective reduction conditions should affect the free thiol quantitation in a meaningful way. Finally, a VH domain showed a relatively large discrepancy between the two methods, which also exhibited a relatively high free thiol level (∼3.8% by the HILIC method, Figure 3B). We hypothesize that, at elevated free thiol levels, the bottom-up approach may be more susceptible to sample preparation-induced disulfide bond scrambling, leading to less reliable free thiol quantitation.

Overall, compared to the bottom-up approach, this subunit HILIC-MS method may not be as sensitive. However, it excels in greatly simplified sample preparation and has much higher throughput. These features make this method ideal for candidate selection during developability assessment and process development support, offering a quick turnaround that is highly desirable to assist in decision-making.

Monitor Free Thiol Level Changes in a Bispecific Antibody Due to Process Change

To showcase its applicability in supporting process development, the subunit HILIC-MS method was employed to monitor the free thiol level changes in a bispecific antibody (mAb-3) as a result of process change. MAb-3 contains two identical light chains (LC) and two different heavy chains (HC and HC*). Besides the apparent differences in CDR sequences, two site-specific mutations in CH3 were also incorporated into the HC* to enable protein A avidity-based purification of bispecific mAb from its homodimers.37 As shown in Figure 4, the XICs of each subunit, containing both the main and free thiol species, were plotted and compared between the two sample lots produced using Process 1 (P1) and Process 2 (P2), respectively. Since Fd and Fd* (generated from HC*) exhibit different molecular weights, they could be readily distinguished at the MS1 level and analyzed separately. Interestingly, free thiol species were observed only in the Fd subunit but not in the Fd* subunit in both lots, highlighting the impact of CDRs on the stability of the VH disulfide bond. Compared to the P1 sample, the P2 sample exhibited an approximate threefold increase in VH free thiol level. This observation also agreed well with the results obtained from the bottom-up approach (Table S3). As for the LC subunit, a low and comparable free thiol peak, corresponding to the CL domain, was observed for the P1 and P2 samples. In addition, due to the mass difference introduced by the two mutations, Fc/2 and Fc*/2 (generated from HC*) subunits could be separately monitored by the HILIC-MS method, allowing for chain-specific free thiol analysis. Compared to the P1 sample, the free thiol levels of both CH2 and CH3 showed notable increases for both HC and HC* in the P2 sample, aligning well with the trend observed for the Fd subunit. Most intriguingly, the same analysis also revealed that, compared to Fc/2, the point-mutated Fc*/2 subunit exhibited decreased free thiol levels of the CH2 domain but increased free thiol levels of the CH3 domain for both P1 and P2 samples. We hypothesize that the observed differences in CH2 and CH3 disulfide bond stability may be attributed to minor conformational changes of respective domains as a result of the two engineered mutations, which has been studied by hydrogen–deuterium exchange mass spectrometry.38 On the other hand, because the cysteine-containing tryptic peptides are identical for Fc/2 and Fc*/2, the bottom-up approach was not able to differentiate chain-specific free thiols. The unique capability of this HILIC-MS method to resolve chain-specific free thiols was highly beneficial as it offered additional insights into the effects of point mutations in the Fc sequence on disulfide bond stability. Finally, the overall increased free thiol levels in the P2 sample, as revealed by both the HILIC-MS method and the bottom-up approach (Table S3), may be partially attributed to a different cysteine feed strategy adopted in this process. Further studies are warranted to identify the root cause.

Conclusions

Measuring (and then controlling) free thiol levels in therapeutic mAbs is crucial for ensuring their structural integrity and conformational stability as unpaired cysteines may lead to improper folding and even aggregation of the mAb molecules. This is commonly achieved by labeling the free thiol groups with maleimide and haloalkyl derivatives followed by intact mass or peptide-mapping analysis. Recently, efforts have also been made to monitor free thiols at intact and subunit levels using label-free strategies, with the goal of improving method throughput and eliminating labeling-associated artifacts. However, these methods are often unable to provide site-specific localization of free thiol species. In this study, we introduced a novel subunit HILIC-MS method to achieve facile identification and quantitation of intrachain free thiols at domain-specific resolution. We demonstrated that after IdeS digestion and selective reduction of interchain disulfide bonds under native conditions, the free thiol species of the generated Fd, LC, and Fc/2 subunits could be baseline-resolved from the main species by HILIC separation and confirmed by both MS1 and MS2 analysis. In particular, the HCD-induced fragmentation patterns were leveraged to successfully localize free thiols into individual domains. Furthermore, to improve the quantitation of Fc-related free thiols, a GalactEXO treatment strategy was developed to consolidate Fc-glycoforms for an improved signal-to-noise ratio without alteration of the conformational stability of the CH2 domain. We then applied this method to a wide range of mAbs to survey the occurrence of domain-specific free thiols. Interestingly, the Fd domain, which contains the HC CDRs, exhibited the most diverse range of free thiol levels. In addition, the good quantitation performance of this subunit HILIC-MS method was confirmed by comparing it with a conventional bottom-up-based approach. Lastly, we demonstrated that this method can be readily applied to compare domain-specific free thiol levels of a bispecific mAb as a result of process change. Importantly, due to the subunit level measurement, this new approach enabled chain-specific free thiol analysis, offering insights that were missing from the bottom-up approach. Compared with the bottom-up approach, this subunit HILIC-MS method may be less sensitive; however, it excels in simpler sample preparation and greater throughput. Therefore, we envision this method is ideally suited for candidate selection during developability assessment and process development support, where a quick turnaround is highly desirable.
