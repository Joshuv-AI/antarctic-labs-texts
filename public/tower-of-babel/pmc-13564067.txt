The pseudouridine epitranscriptomic landscape of advanced prostate cancer therapeutic resistance identifies TIMM17A as a key player
Authors: Shu Li, Chuanfan Zhong, Yixun Zhang, Guo Chen, Le Zhang, Jiahong Chen, Ronghua Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•PA-Ψ-seq maps pseudouridine remodeling in ARSI resistant ddvanced prostate cancer.•Integrated multi-omics identifies TIMM17A as a driver of ARSI resistance.•PUS1-mediated pseudouridylation of TIMM17A sustains resistance and tumor growth.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most frequently diagnosed malignancies and the second leading cause of cancer-related death [1,2]. Although patients with localized disease generally achieve favorable outcomes after radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy [3,4], a proportion progress to metastatic hormone-sensitive PCa and metastatic castration-resistant PCa (mCRPC). Androgen deprivation therapy remains the cornerstone of systemic treatment, and its combination with androgen receptor signaling inhibitors (ARSIs), including darolutamide, enzalutamide, and apalutamide, has markedly improved outcomes [5,6]. Nevertheless, some patients show inadequate prostate-specific antigen responses or early progression, indicating that tumor heterogeneity and adaptive resistance limit treatment durability [[5], [6], [7]].

In advanced PCa, persistent androgen receptor signaling, DNA damage repair abnormalities, PTEN loss with PI3K–AKT pathway activation, and remodeling of the bone metastatic microenvironment promote aggressive disease and treatment resistance [8]. Although PARP inhibitors, AKT inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and PSMA-directed radioligand therapy have expanded therapeutic options, their benefits are often restricted to selected molecular subgroups [8,9]. Therefore, identifying additional mechanisms that restore drug sensitivity, suppress bypass survival pathways, and modulate the metastatic microenvironment remains a priority.

RNA modifications, collectively termed the epitranscriptome, have emerged as important regulators of tumor progression and therapeutic response [[10], [11], [12]]. >170 RNA modifications have been identified across coding and non-coding RNAs, including N⁶-methyladenosine, 5-methylcytosine, N¹-methyladenosine, m⁷G, adenosine-to-inosine editing, and pseudouridine (Ψ) [[13], [14], [15]]. Among them, Ψ is one of the most abundant modifications in mammalian RNA [16], yet its role in advanced PCa and ARSI resistance remains poorly understood. Previous studies suggest that Ψ-related regulators may serve as biomarkers of biochemical recurrence and bone metastasis [17,18].

In this study, we established a darolutamide-resistant PCa model and integrated bulk RNA sequencing, pseudouridine-specific RNA sequencing, and public datasets to identify resistance-associated Ψ-modified RNAs. Key candidates were validated through in vitro and in vivo experiments, providing insights into Ψ-mediated resistance and potential therapeutic strategies for advanced PCa.

Methods

Establishment of a treatment resistant cell model in advanced prostate cancer by darolutamide and enzalutamide

The human PCa cell lines C4–2B and 22Rv1 were purchased from BeiNa Culture Collection. Both cell lines were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

The darolutamide-resistant cell line, designated C4–2B_DARO, was generated by continuously exposing parental C4–2B wild-type (C4–2B_WT) cells to 50 μM darolutamide (CAT# S7559 Selleck) for six months. To confirm the acquisition of resistance, both C4–2B_WT and C4–2B_DARO cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded at a density of 5000 cells per well in 96-well plates and treated with increasing concentrations of darolutamide (0, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μM) for 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (CAT# MA0218, MeilunBio), and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was calculated to verify the successful establishment of the darolutamide-resistant subline.

The enzalutamide-resistant cell line (C4–2B_ENZR) was established by continuously exposing parental C4–2B_WT cells to 25 μM enzalutamide (CAT# S1250, Selleck) for 3 months. To validate the resistant phenotype, colony formation assays were performed on C4–2B_WT and C4–2B_ENZR cells in the logarithmic growth phase, while cell viability was simultaneously assessed using the CCK-8 assay following 48 h of treatment with increasing concentrations of enzalutamide (0, 2.5, 5, 12.5, 25, and 50 µM) [19].

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from C4–2B and C4–2B_DARO cells using NucleoZOL reagent (CAT# 2502–001 MACHEREY NAGEL). Five micrograms of total RNA per sample were used for library preparation with the NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit. Library quality was assessed on a 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, USA), and qualified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using paired-end 150 bp (PE150) reads.

PA-Ψ-seq (photo-crosslinking-assisted Ψ sequencing)

Total poly(A) RNA was isolated from C4–2B and C4–2B_DARO cells using an RNA isolation kit. Poly(A) RNA (10 μg) was incubated with a ψ-specific antibody (5 μg) and RNasin (40 U/µL) for 2 h, followed by UV crosslinking at 365 nm. The RNA–antibody complexes were digested with RNase T1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), immunoprecipitated with Protein G magnetic beads, and purified using Proteinase K. The recovered RNA was then used for library construction with the EPI miRNA Library Kit (Epibiotek, China) and subjected to high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Raw sequencing reads in FASTQ format were processed to remove adapters and low-quality sequences, yielding clean reads. For transcriptome data (PE150), clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). For ψ-seq data, clean reads were mapped to hg38 using Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1). Peak calling was performed with MACS2 (v2.1.1) to identify enriched ψ modification regions, and peaks were annotated using the ChIPseeker R package (v1.46.1) to determine their genomic locations.

Integrated differential analysis

To identify differentially expressed genes and ψ-modified peaks between C4–2B and C4–2B_DARO cells, differential analyses were performed separately on the PA-Ψ-seq and RNA-seq datasets using edgeR [20] and DESeq2 [21]. For RNA-seq data, gene expression levels derived from clean reads served as the quantitative metric, and genes with |log₂ (fold change) | > 0 and p-value < 0.05, without FDR correction, were defined as differentially expressed. For PA-Ψ-seq, SignalValue was used as the quantitative metric. Peaks with |log₂ (fold change) | > 0 and p-value < 0.05, without FDR correction, were considered differentially modified [22,23].

Subsequently, an integrated analysis combining ψ modification levels and mRNA expression was conducted. Based on the concordant or discordant changes in ψ peak intensity and gene expression, genes were classified into four categories: Hyper-Up (increased ψ modification and upregulated expression), Hyper-Down (increased ψ modification and downregulated expression), Hypo-Up (decreased ψ modification and upregulated expression), and Hypo-Down (decreased ψ modification and downregulated expression).

Integrative genomics viewer (IGV) visualization

To visually assess the enrichment of Ψ on TIMM17A, ψ-seq data were visualized using the IGV (v2.19.7). The human reference genome (hg38) and corresponding gene annotation file (GTF format) were loaded, followed by the aligned BED format files. The genomic region encompassing TIMM17A was examined, and track thresholds were adjusted to evaluate the intensity and shape of the enrichment peaks.

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed using gene sets from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Hallmark collections, downloaded from The Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). Pathways with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R software (version 4.3.0) [[24], [25], [26]].

Public prostate cancer datasets

Four publicly available PCa datasets—TCGA-PRAD, CIT (E-MTAB-6128), GSE79021, and Taylor (GSE21034)—were selected to investigate the differential expression of TIMM17A across different stages of PCa. Additionally, four cohorts—TCGA-PRAD, GSE54460, Cambridge (GSE70768), and SU2C (Stand Up to Cancer)—were used to evaluate the prognostic significance of TIMM17A expression. All processed data were obtained from the PCADB database [27,28].

Survival analysis

The optimal cutoff value for TIMM17A expression was selected. Patients were then stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on this cutoff. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated for different clinical endpoints, and differences in survival distributions between the high- and low-expression groups were compared using the log-rank test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All survival analyses were performed using the survival and survminer packages in R (version 4.3.0) [29].

Pan-Cancer multi-omics analysis

Pan-cancer single-cell RNA-seq data were obtained from the Tumor Immune Single-cell Hub (TISCH, http://tisch.comp-genomics.org). The data were first Z-score normalized [30]. Additionally, the Nebulosa package was used to fit gene signals via kernel density estimation, and the expression distribution of TIMM17A across single cells was visualized by UMAP dimensionality reduction.

Pan-cancer spatial transcriptomics data were retrieved from the SpatialTME database, which integrates 10x Visium sequencing datasets. Spatial transcriptomic data were normalized using the NormalizeData function in Seurat [31,32]. Spots were annotated according to the dominant cell-type proportion. All single-cell and spatial transcriptomic visualizations were primarily generated using the ComplexHeatmap packages in R [33].

Advanced PCa single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq data from six mCRPC samples (GSE274229) were downloaded from GEO and processed using Seurat. After quality control to retain high-quality cells with sufficient detected genes and low mitochondrial content, the data were normalized, scaled, and integrated based on shared highly variable genes. Principal component analysis (PCA) and UMAP were performed for dimensionality reduction and visualization. Cell clusters were annotated using canonical marker genes. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted with Monocle3 to assess the developmental positioning of epithelial cells. GSVA and ssGSEA were applied to calculate pathway enrichment scores based on the TIMM17A⁺ epithelial cell signature. Cell-cell communication was analyzed using CellChat based on the ligand-receptor expression matrix, with a focus on differences in incoming signals between TIMM17A⁺ and TIMM17A⁻ epithelial cells from the microenvironment.

Generation of timm17a knockdown stable cell lines

Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting human TIMM17A were designed and cloned into the lentiviral vector pLKO.1. Lentiviral particles were generated by Guangzhou IGE Biotechnology Co., Ltd. C4–2B cells were seeded in 6-well plates and infected with lentivirus at 30–50% confluence in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene to enhance transduction efficiency. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. At 48 h post-infection, cells were subjected to selection with puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 5–7 days until all uninfected control cells were eliminated, resulting in the establishment of stable shRNA-expressing cell lines. The knockdown efficiency of TIMM17A was subsequently confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses at both the mRNA and protein levels. The shRNA sequences targeting TIMM17A were listed in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from cells using NucleoZOL reagent (CAT# 2502–001 MACHEREY NAGEL) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (CAT# RR047A, Takara) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR Green detection system to determine RNA expression levels. GAPDH was used as the internal control for normalization, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CAT# MA0218, Meilune). Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested by trypsinization, counted, and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well. After treatment as indicated, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well containing 100 μL of culture medium. The plates were gently mixed and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ for 1–2 h in the dark. The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader (CAT# VLBLATGD2, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Colony formation assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested by trypsinization, counted, and seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well. The plates were gently shaken to ensure even distribution of the cells. Cells were then cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ for approximately 10–15 days, with the culture medium replaced every 2–3 days.

At the end of the incubation period, the medium was removed and the cells were gently washed twice with PBS. The colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15–20 min, followed by staining with 0.1% crystal violet for 20–30 min. Excess stain was carefully rinsed off with water, and the plates were air-dried at room temperature.

Xenograft experiment

Male NCG mice, 4–6 weeks old, were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. C4–2B cells in the logarithmic growth phase, with target gene knockdown, were harvested by trypsinization and resuspended in PBS. Each mouse was subcutaneously injected with 1 × 10⁷ cells in the right flank. After injection, the health status of the mice and tumor growth were regularly monitored. Tumor volume was measured every 3 days and calculated using the formula: 0.5 × length × width². Tumor weight was measured after the mice were sacrificed.

RIP assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected and washed twice with ice-cold PBS, then lysed with RIP lysis buffer containing protease and RNase inhibitors and incubated on ice for 10 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris, and the supernatant was collected as the RIP input sample an appropriate amount of cell lysate was incubated with Protein A/G magnetic beads pre-bound with anti-PUS1 antibody (CAT# ab203010, abcam) or IgG (CAT# 2729, CST) control at 4 °C overnight with rotation to allow specific binding of RNA–protein complexes to the antibody. After incubation, the beads were collected using a magnetic rack and washed 3–5 times with RIP wash buffer to remove non-specifically bound material. Proteinase K was then added to digest proteins and release the bound RNA, which was subsequently purified by phenol/chloroform extraction. The purified RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and the enrichment of target mRNAs was analyzed by qRT-PCR. RIP results were presented as fold enrichment relative to the IgG control.

mRNA stability assay

To determine RNA stability, an Actinomycin D (ActD) (CAT# HY-17,559, MCE) chase assay was performed. Briefly, cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured until reaching approximately 70–80% confluence. Cells were treated with ActD to inhibit RNA transcription. Total RNA was collected at indicated time points (0, 3, 6, and 9 h) after ActD treatment and extracted. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA, and the expression levels of target transcripts were determined by qRT-PCR. The relative RNA abundance at each time point was normalized to that at 0 h after ActD treatment using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The RNA half-life was calculated by nonlinear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism software.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics statistical analyses and graphical presentations were conducted using R software version 4.3.0. Experimental data were processed with GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1. Differences between two independent groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Time-dependent CCK-8 cell proliferation data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Result

As detailed in the Methods section, chronic exposure to darolutamide over 6 months enabled the generation of the darolutamide resistant subline, designated C4–2B_DARO. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation and substantial heterogeneity between the parental and resistant lines (Fig. 1B). Differential expression analysis identified a total of 4842 dysregulated genes (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table S3–4). Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that these differentially expressed genes were predominantly enriched in several hallmark pathways from the Hallmarks collection, including E2F_TARGETS, G2M_CHECKPOINT, TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB, and MTORC1_SIGNALING (Fig. 1D). In addition, KEGG pathway analysis highlighted enrichment in Oxidative phosphorylation, Drug metabolism - other enzymes, and Cell cycle (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Table S5).

Notably, pathways such as TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB and Oxidative phosphorylation have been previously implicated in the mechanisms underlying treatment resistance in advanced PCa. These findings collectively validate the successful establishment of our darolutamide resistance model.

The global distribution of Ψ sites on mRNAs was broadly comparable between the parental C4–2B_WT and resistant C4–2B_DARO cells; however, notable differences emerged in regional enrichment. Specifically, Ψ modifications in C4–2B_WT cells showed greater prevalence in the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR), whereas those in C4–2B_DARO cells were more prominently enriched in intronic regions (Fig. 2B). Subsequent analysis via volcano plot visualization highlighted the genomic regions associated with differentially pseudouridylated peaks (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table S6–7). Functional enrichment of the genes harboring these differential Ψ sites revealed significant overrepresentation in hallmark pathways from the MSigDB collection, including G2M_CHECKPOINT, MTORC1_SIGNALING, and PI3K_AKT_MTOR_SIGNALING (Fig. 2D). KEGG pathway analysis further indicated enrichment in Autophagy, mTOR signaling pathway, and Focal adhesion (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Table S8).

All these results suggested that treatment resistance in advanced PCa is accompanied by distinct pseudouridine epitranscriptomic signatures detectable via PA-Ψ-seq, highlighting region-specific shifts in Ψ distribution and pathway-level alterations linked to resistant phenotypes.

Integrated transcriptomic and pseudouridine epitranscriptomic analysis to identify Ψ-Associated hub genes

Given that Ψ represents one of the most abundant modifications in mammalian mRNA, we integrated bulk RNA-seq and PA-Ψ-seq datasets to delineate the epitranscriptomic features associated with treatment resistance in the advanced PCa model. As depicted in the Venn diagram (Fig. 3A), intersection analysis identified 293 genes that exhibited concurrent upregulation in both RNA-seq (higher expression in C4–2B_DARO vs. C4–2B_WT) and PA-Ψ-seq (increased pseudouridylation in the resistant line). These overlapping genes were designated the “hyper up” category. Conversely, 274 genes showed concordant downregulation in both datasets and were classified as the “hypo down” category. Similarly, the “hyper down” category comprised 105 genes (upregulated in RNA-seq but with reduced Ψ levels), while the “hypo up” category included 158 genes (downregulated in RNA-seq but with elevated Ψ levels). Among the four categories, we prioritized the “hyper up” group, hypothesizing that these dually upregulated and pseudouridylated mRNAs represent critical drivers of treatment resistance in advanced PCa. Functional enrichment analysis of the “hyper up” genes demonstrated significant overrepresentation in pathways implicated in therapy resistance. KEGG pathway analysis highlighted enrichment in the Hippo signaling pathway, Ferroptosis, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 3C). Taken together these findings indicate that the “hyper up” category harbors key Ψ-associated transcripts that play a pivotal role in mediating treatment resistance in advanced PCa.

Expression characteristics of timm17a across pan-cancer multi-omics datasets

Among the 293 genes in the “hyper up” category, we performed intersection analysis with prognostic data from the TCGA-PRAD and SU2C cohorts. As shown in Figure S1, 16 genes within this group exhibited significant prognostic relevance. Following the literature review, TIMM17A emerged as the only gene among these 16 that had not been previously reported in the context of PCa. Consequently, we prioritized TIMM17A for subsequent validation.

To elucidate its expression patterns, we first conducted a comprehensive pan-cancer multi-omics investigation of TIMM17A. Differential expression analysis across the TCGA pan-cancer cohort (Fig. 4A-B) revealed that TIMM17A mRNA levels were significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues in the majority of cancer types, including prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD). Representative visualization from a cutaneous melanoma cohort (Fig. 4C-D) clearly illustrated its spatial localization primarily within tumor-dominant areas (Fig. 4F). Taken together, these pan-cancer multi-omics results consistently indicate that TIMM17A exhibits upregulated expression in tumor tissues across a broad spectrum of malignancies. This expression pattern underscores its potential as a novel cancer biomarker and therapeutic target.

TIMM17A is upregulated in advanced prostate cancer

To investigate the expression profile of TIMM17A in PCa, we first analyzed immunohistochemistry (IHC) data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database. As shown in Fig. 5A, TIMM17A protein was predominantly localized to the cytoplasm and/or cell membrane in PCa tissues. Low-grade tumors exhibited low to weak staining intensity, whereas high-grade tumors displayed moderate staining intensity, suggesting a potential association between TIMM17A protein levels and tumor aggressiveness. We next examined TIMM17A mRNA expression across multiple independent public transcriptomic datasets, including TCGA-PRAD, CIT, GSE79021, and Taylor et al. (Fig. 5B). Consistent results demonstrated that TIMM17A mRNA was significantly upregulated in primary PCa tissues compared with benign prostate tissues (TCGA-PRAD: P = 0.0027; CIT: P = 0.0012; GSE79021: P = 0.0047). Subsequently, we evaluated the prognostic relevance of TIMM17A expression in PCa patients using KM survival analysis across several cohorts. In primary PCa datasets, patients with high TIMM17A expression exhibited a significantly increased risk of biochemical recurrence (TCGA-PRAD: P = 0.000,053; GSE54460: P = 0.011; GSE70768: P = 0.011). Knockdown of TIMM17A reverses therapeutic resistance in advanced PCa

To validate the functional contribution of TIMM17A to therapeutic resistance and assess its potential as a therapeutic target, TIMM17A expression was first examined in C4‑2B, C4‑2B_DARO, C4‑2B_ENZR, and 22Rv1 cell lines. qRT‑PCR analysis revealed that TIMM17A mRNA levels were significantly higher in C4‑2B_DARO, C4‑2B_ENZR and 22Rv1 cells than in parental C4‑2B cells (Fig. 6A, Figure S2A). Subsequently, we proceeded with loss-of-function experiments in the C4–2B_DARO and AR-positive 22Rv1 cell lines, with additional confirmatory tests carried out in the C4–2B_ENZR cells. As shown in Fig. 6B, transfection with two independent TIMM17A-specific shRNAs (sh1 and sh2) significantly downregulated TIMM17A mRNA levels in both cell lines compared with the negative control (NC) group (****P < 0.0001). CCK-8 cell proliferation assays demonstrated that TIMM17A knockdown markedly suppressed growth over 5 days, with statistically significant inhibition observed in both C4–2B_DARO (Fig. 6C, left; ***P < 0.001 at day 5) and 22Rv1 cells (Fig. 6C, right; **P < 0.01 at day 5). Colony formation assays further confirmed that depletion of TIMM17A substantially reduced clonogenic capacity, as evidenced by fewer and smaller colonies in the aforementioned cell lines and C4–2B_ENZR (Fig. 6D, Figure S2B; ****P < 0.0001). TIMM17A is subject to pseudouridine modification

To determine whether TIMM17A mRNA undergoes Ψ modification and to elucidate the underlying regulatory mechanism, we integrated PA-Ψ-seq data with targeted functional validation. As shown in Fig. 7A, the PA-Ψ-seq genome browser view of the TIMM17A locus revealed a prominent Ψ-modified peak (green box) that was markedly enriched in the darolutamide-resistant C4–2B_DARO cells, whereas the signal was negligible in the parental C4–2B_WT cells. Based on the premise that PUS1 is a canonical regulator of Ψ, we next examined the link between PUS1 and TIMM17A. Transient knockdown of PUS1 using specific siRNA significantly reduced TIMM17A mRNA abundance in both C4–2B_DARO (****P < 0.0001) and 22Rv1 cells (**P < 0.01) compared with negative control (Fig. 7B), indicating that PUS1 positively regulates TIMM17A expression. To confirm interaction, RIP was performed. RIP-qPCR demonstrated significant enrichment of TIMM17A mRNA in the PUS1 immunoprecipitate relative to IgG control in both cell lines (Fig. 7C). Subsequent RT-PCR of the RIP material further verified specific pull-down of TIMM17A transcripts by PUS1 (Fig. 7C). In addition, mRNA stability assays demonstrated that, following PUS1 knockdown, TIMM17A mRNA degradation accelerated markedly upon actinomycin D treatment. The half-life of TIMM17A mRNA was significantly shorter than that in the control group, indicating that reduced PUS1 expression decreases the stability of the TIMM17A transcript (Fig. 7D).

To determine whether TIMM17A-mediated treatment resistance is regulated by PUS1, we performed rescue experiments. The CCK-8 proliferation curves showed that PUS1 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation, whereas ectopic overexpression of TIMM17A significantly enhanced proliferation. Notably, the proliferation-suppressing effect induced by PUS1 knockdown was substantially attenuated when TIMM17A was simultaneously overexpressed (Fig. 7E). Consistently, results from the colony formation assays showed that, compared with the control group, PUS1 knockdown significantly reduced the colony-forming capacity, while TIMM17A overexpression markedly increased it. Importantly, the impaired colony-forming ability caused by PUS1 knockdown was significantly rescued by concurrent TIMM17A overexpression (Fig. 7F). Taken together, these findings suggest that PUS1 is an upstream regulator of the TIMM17A-associated treatment resistance mechanism.

Considered together, these results establish that TIMM17A mRNA is possibly modified by PUS1-mediated pseudouridylation, which enhances its expression and contributes to therapeutic resistance in advanced PCa.

TIMM17A in the tumor microenvironment of advanced prostate cancer

Dysregulation of the tumor microenvironment (TME) is a recognized hallmark of treatment resistance in advanced PCa. Following unified dimensionality reduction, clustering, and cell-type annotation (Fig. 8A–B), TIMM17A expression was moderately elevated in epithelial cells and detectable across multiple cell populations within the TME (Fig. 8C–D). Cells were subsequently stratified into TIMM17A⁺ and TIMM17A⁻ subpopulations according to TIMM17A expression levels. Comparative analysis revealed that the proportion of epithelial cells was most markedly increased in the TIMM17A⁺ group, where they constituted the predominant cell population (Fig. 8E). Discussion

The effective management of advanced PCa remains a formidable clinical challenge, underscoring the pressing requirement for innovative therapeutic approaches. Although novel agents are continuously being developed, ARSI remains the cornerstone of various combination treatment regimens for advanced PCa [[34], [35], [36]]. With ongoing advancements in sequencing technologies and deepening research insights, Ψ recognized as one of the most abundant modifications in mammalian mRNA holds considerable promise as a novel target for circumventing treatment resistance [[37], [38], [39]].

Ψ ranks among the most prevalent and conserved post-transcriptional RNA modifications [16]. Its formation is mediated by pseudouridine synthases (PUSs), encompassing PUS1, PUS3, and PUS7, together with their paralogs PUSL1 and PUSL7, as well as TRUB1 and TRUB2 [40]. Additionally, DKC1 serves as the catalytic component of the H/ACA small nucleolar ribonucleoprotein (snoRNP) complex [41]. These Ψ regulatory factors play essential roles in tumor cell proliferation. For instance, Wang and colleagues identified PUS1 as a key mediator of tumor immune evasion; inhibition of pseudouridine synthesis was shown to potentiate immunotherapy by stimulating dsRNA-sensing pathways [42]. Similarly, Kan et al. reported that combined suppression of DKC1 and MEK1/2 exerts synergistic inhibitory effects on colorectal cancer cell growth [43]. In another example, Chang and co-workers demonstrated that PUS7-mediated pseudouridylation of ALKBH3 mRNA suppresses gastric cancer progression [44].

Reports on Ψ in PCa are notably limited. Our prior work established a classifier based on five pseudouridylation-associated long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) for prognostic assessment in primary PCa [17]. Wu and colleagues demonstrated that FOXA1-dependent PUS1 modulates EIF3b stability through a non-enzymatic mechanism, thereby facilitating PCa bone metastasis. Nevertheless, in the context of treatment resistance in advanced PCa, no studies on Ψ have been published to date [18].

In this study, we developed a model of treatment resistance in advanced PCa by exposing the C4–2B cell line to darolutamide. Integrating RNA-seq with PA-Ψ-seq, we generated the first comprehensive expression atlas of Ψ-modified mRNAs in advanced PCa. Our findings revealed a strong positive correlation between Ψ levels and RNA expression abundance. Genes in the “hyper up” category were markedly enriched in pathways associated with therapy resistance. Within this “hyper up” group, 16 genes exhibited prognostic significance, several of which have been previously implicated in the literature. For example, Fibronectin 1 (FN1), a bone-derived ECM protein, promotes PCa bone metastasis by engaging integrins to activate PKA signaling, upregulating SPP1 (osteopontin) expression. This enhances tumor adhesion, survival, osteomimicry, and contributes to AR therapy resistance in the bone niche [45].

Through the literature review, we identified TIMM17A as a molecule previously unreported in PCa. TIMM17A is predicted to contribute to protein transmembrane transporter activity and to participate in protein import into the mitochondrial matrix. It localizes to the mitochondrial inner membrane and nucleoplasm and constitutes an integral component of the mitochondrial inner membrane. Existing reports on TIMM17A predominantly focus on breast cancer and lung cancer. For instance, TIMM17A has been linked to poor survival in non-small cell lung cancer [46,47]. In breast cancer, TIMM17A has been proposed as a potential biomarker and shown to promote proliferation and migration of cancer cells [48].

In our study, TIMM17A expression progressively increased with advancing PCa disease stage. Elevated TIMM17A levels were associated with poorer prognosis. Furthermore, both in vitro and in vivo experiments revealed that TIMM17A expression is regulated by PUS1. Knockdown of TIMM17A restored sensitivity to darolutamide in treatment-resistant advanced PCa cell lines. These findings suggest that TIMM17A holds potential as a novel target for overcoming treatment resistance in advanced PCa.

Interestingly, our single-cell transcriptomic analysis of mCRPC reveals that TIMM17A is preferentially upregulated in the epithelial compartment of the tumor microenvironment. The marked enrichment of TIMM17A⁺ epithelial cells, their positioning toward the later segments of the pseudotime trajectory, and the strong association of their transcriptional signature with shorter OS and reduced PFS after ARSI therapy collectively indicate that TIMM17A upregulation is closely linked to treatment resistance in advanced PCa. Importantly, TIMM17A⁺ epithelial cells exhibit substantially enhanced incoming cell-cell communication from the surrounding microenvironment, with a particularly prominent role for MDK-mediated signaling. This finding suggests that microenvironment-derived cues, especially MDK, may actively sustain or reinforce the aggressive phenotype of TIMM17A⁺ tumor cells. Together, these results position TIMM17A as a key molecular node that integrates intrinsic epithelial malignancy with extrinsic TME support, thereby contributing to the therapeutic resistance characteristic of advanced PCa.

In summary, we demonstrate that TIMM17A knockdown can reverse the treatment-resistant phenotype in advanced PCa. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses further suggest that TIMM17A may serve as a key member linking the intrinsic malignant properties of tumor cells with microenvironmental support, thereby contributing to the development of treatment resistance in advanced PCa. These findings indicate that TIMM17A may represent an important therapeutic target for overcoming treatment resistance. Although treatment options for advanced PCa have expanded rapidly in recent years, ARSIs remain the indispensable first-line therapy, and the associated treatment resistance continues to be a central challenge that limits clinical benefit. Our findings point to the potential of targeting TIMM17A in combination with ARSIs as a new therapeutic strategy for advanced PCa, and may also provide a theoretical basis for improving the efficacy of existing treatment regimens.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the expression profile of TIMM17A in clinical samples is primarily derived from HPA database analysis, and thus warrants systematic validation in our institutional patient cohort using immunohistochemistry. Second, the evidence is currently insufficient to establish a causal link between PUS1-mediated regulation of TIMM17A and its specific dependence on Ψ modification; site-directed mutagenesis combined with functional rescue experiments remains critical for determining whether the observed phenotypic effects are directly mediated by Ψ modification. Third, the therapeutic potential of the Ψ-TIMM17A axis is currently supported primarily by preliminary functional observations from shRNA-mediated knockdown, with direct therapeutic targeting validation still lacking; whether this axis truly constitutes a targetable vulnerability warrants further investigation. These limitations will be addressed in our future research endeavors.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides a pseudouridine epitranscriptomic landscape in advanced PCa and reveals its critical involvement in therapeutic resistance. Through integrated multi-omics analyses and experimental validation, we identify TIMM17A as a novel Ψ gene that promotes tumor progression and resistance to ARSI therapy. Mechanistically, PUS1-mediated pseudouridylation enhances TIMM17A expression, thereby contributing to malignant phenotypes and dynamic interactions within the tumor microenvironment. Collectively, our findings identify the Ψ–TIMM17A axis as a candidate mechanism warranting further investigation and provide new insights into epitranscriptomic regulation in advanced PCa.
