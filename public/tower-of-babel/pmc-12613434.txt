Assessment of sustainable and healthy eating behaviors, EAT-lancet diet score, diet quality and obesity indicators among university staff: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Gamze Altıntaş-Mutlu, Aslı Uçar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSustainable dietary patterns are considered essential for both public health and environmental protection. This study aimed to evaluate sustainable nutrition behaviors, adherence to the EAT-Lancet dietary recommendations, diet quality, and obesity indicators among university staff.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted with 120 university staff members. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method. Sociodemographic data, anthropometric measurements, the Sustainable and Healthy Eating Behaviors Scale (SHEB), the EAT-Lancet Diet Score, and the International Diet Quality Index (DQI-I) were collected. Robust regression analysis was conducted to identify the predictors of the EAT-Lancet Diet Score.ResultsThe mean SHEB score was 134.3 ± 25. The median EAT-Lancet Diet Score and DQI-I were 9.0 (5.0–13.0) and 53.0 (33.0–69.0), respectively. A moderate positive correlation was found between the EAT-Lancet Diet Score and DQI-I (r = 0.586; p < 0.001). Higher SHEB scores were observed among participants with postgraduate education (p < 0.05). Negative associations were identified between the EAT-Lancet Diet Score and all anthropometric indicators, and between SHEB and BMI, waist (WC), and neck circumference (NC) (p < 0.05). Regression analysis showed that men had significantly lower EAT-Lancet Diet Scores than women (β = -1.084, 95% CI: -1.68 to -0.488, p = 0.001). Each one-unit increase in SHEB score was associated with a 0.016 unit increase in EAT-Lancet Diet Score (95% CI: 0.016 (0.005–0.028), p = 0.006).ConclusionsGender and sustainable nutrition behaviors appear to be associated with adherence to the EAT-Lancet dietary recommendations. These findings highlight the need to promote sustainable eating behaviors in educational settings to support public health and environmental goals.Trial registrationNCT06100666.

Background

Sustainable nutrition is closely linked to both public health and planetary well-being. Worldwide, unhealthy diets are recognized as a leading risk factor for non-communicable diseases such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases [1, 2]. Unsustainable food systems significantly contribute to the climate crisis, biodiversity loss, and resource depletion, posing a serious threat to future generations [2, 3]. In response, the EAT-Lancet Commission on Food, Planet, and Health has proposed a universal plant-based reference diet that theoretically supports both human and environmental health simultaneously within the framework of the “One Health” principle [4].

Recent literature indicates that individual-level sustainable dietary behaviors are influenced by various factors such as gender, educational level, economic status, personal beliefs and attitudes, nutritional knowledge, and psychosocial factors [5, 6]. Studies have indicated that healthy eating behavior, health literacy, awareness of ecological footprints, and diet quality may be associated with socioeconomic status and educational attainment [7–11]. However, data on sustainable eating behaviors in developing countries remain limited.

In recent years, there has been a global increase in studies on sustainability and sustainable nutrition. Given that numerous activities and initiatives related to these topics are carried out, particularly within universities [12], it is assumed that university staff may exhibit higher levels of awareness regarding sustainability and sustainable dietary practices [13, 14]. University staff typically consists of individuals with higher levels of education and access to up-to-date health-related information; consequently, they have the potential to exhibit more favorable dietary behaviors. Previous research has shown that individuals with higher levels of education tend to have higher levels of health [15] and nutrition literacy [7]. Furthermore, university staff often serve as role models within educational institutions, and their behaviors may influence wider communities.

Globally, where the prevalence of overweight and obesity is increasing regardless of individuals’ socioeconomic status [16], there is a growing need to investigate how sustainable and healthy eating behaviors intersect with objective health indicators such as BMI and the waist-to-height ratio (WHtR). This study aims to evaluate sustainable dietary behaviors, adherence to the EAT-Lancet dietary recommendations, diet quality, and obesity status among university staff and examine the relationships between these parameters. It was hypothesized that higher sustainable and healthy eating behavior scores would be associated with higher EAT-Lancet Diet Scores, better diet quality, and more favorable anthropometric measurements.

Methods

Study design and sample

This cross-sectional study was conducted among academic and administrative staff at a university in Türkiye. The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 108 participants using G*Power version 3.1.9.4 (α = 0.05, power = 0.95), assuming a correlation coefficient of 0.310 between the SHEB scale and diet quality [9, 17]. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy or lactation, medical conditions that prevented anthropometric measurements and known memory impairments affecting dietary recall. Out of 368 eligible university staff, 188 were invited to participate. Following screening for eligibility, the study was completed with 120 participants (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Data were collected through voluntary face-to-face interviews. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Tarsus University Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee (approval number: 07/2023/48). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The survey included sociodemographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements, the SHEB scale, and one-day dietary intake based on a 24-hour recall method.

Assessment tools

Sustainable eating behaviors

Sustainable eating behaviors were assessed using the Sustainable and Healthy Eating Behaviors (SHEB) scale, developed by Żakowska-Biemans et al. (2019) [18] and adapted into Turkish by Köksal et al. (2023) [19]. The scale includes 32 Likert-type items across seven subdimensions: Quality Labels (Local and Organic), Seasonal Foods and Food Waste Avoidance, Animal Welfare, Reduction of Meat Consumption, Healthy and Balanced Eating, Local Food, and Low Fat. Participants were asked to rate each item on a 7-point scale, ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (always). Higher scores reflect stronger adherence to sustainable and healthy eating behaviors.

Dietary assessment and diet quality

A single 24-hour dietary recall was conducted on a weekday to assess participants’ food intake. Portion sizes were estimated using the Food and Meal Photograph Catalogue [20] and standard recipes [21]. The data were analysed using BeBiS (Nutrition Information System) version 9. Diet quality was assessed using the DQI-I developed by Kim et al. (2003) [22], which evaluates four components: variety, adequacy, moderation, and overall balance (total score range: 0–100).

EAT-Lancet diet score

Adherence to the EAT-Lancet dietary recommendations was assessed using the EAT-Lancet Diet Score developed by Knuppel et al. (2019). The score includes 14 items based on the recommended intake levels for various food groups. One point is assigned for meeting the recommended intake and zero for not meeting it. The total score ranges from 0 to 14 [23].

Anthropometric measurements

Height, body weight, WC and NC were measured by the researcher according to standard protocols [24]. BMI was classified according to WHO criteria [25]. WC was measured at the midpoint between the lowest rib and the iliac crest. According to WHO criteria, a WC of ≥ 80 cm in women and ≥ 94 cm in men was classified as “at risk”, while ≥ 88 cm in women and ≥ 102 cm in men was considered “high risk” [25]. NC was measured at the midpoint between the mid-cervical spine and the mid-anterior neck while participants were standing upright [24]. NC of ≥ 34 cm in women and ≥ 37 cm in men was considered at risk [30]. WHtR was calculated and classified using standard cut-offs [26].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the R software (version 4.4.1, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Independent Samples T-Test was used for normally distributed continuous variables; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U Test was applied. Pearson’s correlation was used for normally distributed variables, while Spearman’s correlation was used for non-normally distributed variables. No multiplicity adjustment was applied in the correlation analyses; therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Due to the non-normal distribution of the EAT-Lancet Diet Score, robust regression was applied using the Huber M-estimator (tuning constant = 1.345), with robust standard errors to mitigate non-normality and heteroscedasticity, and to reduce the influence of outliers and model violations. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and all values were below 5. The linearity of predictors and presence of influential points were also assessed to ensure model robustness. Covariates were selected based on theoretical relevance and bivariate correlations with the EAT-Lancet Diet Score. Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum-maximum). Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

The general characteristics of the university staff are presented in Table 1. The median age of participants was 33.0 years; 59.2% were female, and 50.9% held a postgraduate degree. The median BMI was 24.7 (17.0–44.7.0.7) kg/m², and the mean WHtR was 0.5 ± 0.1. Among female participants, the median WC was 78.5 (62.5–121.0) cm, and the median NC was 32.0 (27.0–37.0) cm. Among males, the mean WC was 95.8 ± 8.9 cm, and the mean NC was 38.1 ± 1.8 cm. The median total DQI-I score was 53.0 (33.0–69.0) out of 100. The subcomponent scores were variety, 17.0 (7.0–20.0) out of 20; adequacy, 26.0 (9.0–6.0) out of 40; moderation, 9.0 (0–21.0) out of 30; and overall balance, 0 (0–6.0) out of 10. The mean SHEB total score was 134.3 ± 25.0, and the median EAT-Lancet Diet Score was 9.0 (5.0–13.0). The internal consistency of the SHEB scale in the present sample was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.92).

Potential factors that could influence the EAT-Lancet Diet Score (SHEB total score, WHtR, gender, and education) were analysed using Robust Regression, and the model was found to be statistically significant (F = 7.438; p < 0.001). Among university staff, the SHEB total score and gender were shown to significantly affect the EAT-Lancet Diet Score (p < 0.05). It was found that male participants had EAT-Lancet Diet Scores that were 1.084 points lower than those of female participants (p = 0.001), and that for each one-point increase in the SHEB total score, the EAT-Lancet Diet Score increased by 0.016 points (p = 0.006) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study suggests that, within a highly educated population, higher levels of sustainable and healthy eating behaviors and the female gender may be associated with greater adherence to the dietary guidelines proposed by the EAT-Lancet Commission.

Transitioning to sustainable dietary patterns is essential for preserving ecosystems and ensuring that current resources can be passed on to future generations. Sustainable diets are based on key principles such as increasing the consumption of plant-based foods like vegetables, fruits, and whole grains, and promoting the intake of seasonal and locally sourced foods [27]. The sustainable diet model proposed by the EAT-Lancet Commission is claimed to support human health, environmental sustainability, and animal welfare [4].

Previous studies have shown that healthy and sustainable dietary behaviors that align with these principles may be associated with adherence to sustainable dietary models, such as the Mediterranean diet [9, 28, 29]. Consistent with these findings, our study also demonstrated that the EAT-Lancet Diet Score proposed by Knuppel et al. (2019) may be associated with sustainable and healthy eating behaviors [23].

Our results also indicate that female participants had significantly higher EAT-Lancet Diet Scores than males. Previous studies have reported that women tend to have higher levels of nutritional and food literacy [30] and higher scores in environmentally conscious food choices [29]. A study conducted among academic and administrative staff also indicated that women demonstrated better nutrition literacy than men [7]. Additional research has found that women are more actively involved in food preparation and cooking [31], which may further explain their greater adherence to the dietary recommendations of the EAT-Lancet Commission.

It is widely recognised that individuals’ adherence to a healthy diet is commonly assessed using various diet quality indices. In this study, a moderate positive correlation was observed between the Diet Quality Index-International (DQI-I), which reflects diet quality, and the EAT-Lancet Diet Score, which represents a sustainable dietary model. Additionally, positive correlations were found between DQI-I and certain SHEB subdimensions, including Quality Labels (Local and Organic), Healthy and Balanced Eating, and Animal Welfare. These findings support previous research indicating that the DQI-I score is positively associated with overall sustainability and negatively associated with land use [32]. It has been suggested that foods recommended for increased consumption (i.e., plant-based foods), those recommended to be limited (i.e., certain animal-based foods), and changes in food production systems are directly related to the sustainability agenda [33]. Based on these results, it can be inferred that some sustainability-related outcomes and sustainable dietary behaviors may be associated with diet quality, as reflected by a traditional healthy eating index, such as the DQI-I.

In our study, the mean SHEB scale score of university staff was consistent with findings from previous studies [9, 28, 34]. It was observed that participants with a postgraduate level of education had higher SHEB scores. Similarly, another study found that the total SHEB score was higher among individuals with higher education levels [35]. These results align with previous research suggesting that individuals with higher educational attainment tend to have greater awareness of ecological footprints [10], higher health literacy [15], and better nutrition literacy [30]. Although academic and administrative positions did not differ significantly in terms of sustainable eating behaviors, the findings suggest that educational attainment itself may influence such behaviors.

Interestingly, although prior studies have found associations between education level and adherence to sustainable diets [35–37], education was not a significant predictor of EAT-Lancet Diet Score in our regression analysis. This result may be due to the low proportion (15.8%) of participants with lower educational levels in our sample.

Greater adherence to the EAT-Lancet dietary recommendations has been reported to be associated with lower greenhouse gas emissions and reduced land use [38]. Although the Mediterranean region is known for its traditional emphasis on plant-based foods such as fruits, vegetables, and olive oil, university staff in this study exhibited generally low adherence to the EAT-Lancet diet. This aligns with findings from other studies reporting similarly low adherence to the EAT-Lancet recommendations [34, 39–42].

When evaluating parameters related to obesity, the associations observed between the SHEB scale and certain anthropometric measurements are consistent with findings reported in previous studies [9, 35]. While the aetiology of obesity is multifactorial and complex, anthropometric indicators can be considered physical reflections of healthy and sustainable dietary behaviors. In line with this, several studies have demonstrated that higher adherence to the sustainable dietary model proposed by the EAT-Lancet Commission is associated with lower BMI [35, 40, 43] and reduced WC [43, 44]. Furthermore, greater adherence to EAT-Lancet dietary recommendations has been linked to improvements in various cardiometabolic risk factors [39], a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes [37, 44] and cancer [45]. Given that obesity is a known contributor to increased risk of chronic diseases [46, 47], the negative correlations observed in this study between the EAT-Lancet Diet Score and anthropometric measures further reinforce these findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of a single 24-hour dietary recall may have introduced intra-individual variability and seasonal bias, potentially limiting the accuracy of reported habitual intake. Moreover, underreporting among individuals with obesity may have led to an overestimation of dietary quality. Second, the relatively small sample size and cross-sectional design restrict causal inference and statistical power. Voluntary participation by only 120 of the 188 invited individuals may have introduced selection bias, as those more interested in health or nutrition could be overrepresented. This may have inflated estimates of healthy and sustainable dietary behaviors and reduced the generalizability of findings. Third, although the association between SHEB and the EAT-Lancet Diet Score was statistically significant, the effect size (β = 0.016 per unit) was modest. This suggests limited practical relevance on a per-unit basis, and the result should be interpreted considering the cumulative effect of long-term dietary behavior change. Although the model explained around 20% of the variance in EAT-Lancet Diet Score, this limited explanatory power indicates that other unmeasured psychosocial and environmental factors may have influenced the outcomes. Despite these limitations, a major strength of the study is the use of multiple objective anthropometric indicators (NC, WC, and WHtR) directly measured by the researcher, which increases measurement reliability compared to studies relying on self-reported data.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that, among a highly educated population, sustainable and healthy eating behaviors such as the consumption of organic and locally sourced foods, and support for animal welfare were associated with higher adherence to the EAT-Lancet dietary recommendations. Additionally, women had significantly higher adherence scores compared to men. Although statistically significant, the effect size of sustainable and healthy eating behaviors on the EAT-Lancet Diet Score was modest, and its practical significance should be interpreted with caution. While inverse associations were observed between certain obesity indicators and both sustainable dietary behaviors and EAT-Lancet Diet Scores, the WHtR may not consistently reflect sustainable dietary adherence, indicating potential limitations in using single anthropometric indicators to assess dietary patterns. The findings suggest that educational attainment may play a critical role in shaping sustainable and health-oriented dietary behaviors. As such, public health strategies should prioritize sustainability awareness campaigns that are inclusive of diverse educational and socioeconomic backgrounds. However, as participation in this study was voluntary and limited to a specific university staff sample, the findings may not be fully generalizable to the entire university personnel or to populations with different demographic characteristics. Future longitudinal research with larger and more heterogeneous populations is needed to confirm these associations, explore causality, and guide the development of evidence-based policies that support the transition toward sustainable diets.
