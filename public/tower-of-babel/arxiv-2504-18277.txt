Multiplicative Rewards in Markovian Models
anonymous, Christel Baier, Krishnendu Chatterjee, Tobias Meggendorfer, Jakob Piribauer

Abstract
This paper studies the expected value of multiplicative rewards , where rewards obtained in each step are multiplied (instead of the usual addition), in Markov chains (MCs) and Markov decision processes (MDPs). One of the key differences to additive rewards is that the expected value may diverge to ∞ \infty not only due to recurrent, but also due to transient states. For MCs, computing the value is shown to be possible in polynomial time given an oracle for the comparison of succinctly represented integers (CSRI), which is only known to be solvable in polynomial time subject to number-theoretic conjectures. Interestingly, distinguishing whether the value is ∞ \infty or 0 0 is at least as hard as CSRI, while determining if it is one of these two can be done in polynomial time. In MDPs, the optimal value can be computed in polynomial space. Further refined complexity results and results on the complexity of optimal schedulers are presented. The techniques developed for MDPs additionally allow to solve the multiplicative variant of the stochastic shortest path problem. Finally, for MCs and MDPs where an absorbing state is reached almost surely, all considered problems are solvable in polynomial time.

Introduction

Markov chains are standard models for the analysis and verification of systems exhibiting
probabilistic
behavior. Markov decision processes (MDPs) extend Markov chains with
non-determinism
and are widely used in various fields of science such as computer science
[
28
]
, biology
[
27
]
, epidemiology
[
17
]
, and chemistry
[
16
]
, to name a few.
In computer science, MDPs play a crucial role in formal verification
[
6
]
, control theory, and artificial intelligence, e.g., as the underlying model for reinforcement learning
[
20
]
, among others.
In a nutshell, an MDP works as follows:
In each state of an MDP, a so-called
scheduler
chooses from a set of available actions. Afterwards, the successor state is chosen randomly according to a probability distribution associated with the chosen action.
Once a scheduler is fixed, the process is purely probabilistic.

Rewards

To model quantitative aspects of a system such as runtime, energy consumption, or utility, Markov chains and MDPs can be equipped with reward structures.
In MDPs, the central arising problem is finding a scheduler optimizing the expected value of the obtained reward.
The most prominent reward mechanisms are accumulated/total (additive) rewards (rewards obtained in each step are summed up), discounted accumulated rewards (rewards obtained after
n
steps are discounted with factor
\lambda^{n}
for
\lambda\in(0,1)
), and mean payoff (average reward accumulated per step).
Optimizing the expected values of these quantities has extensively been studied for decades (see, e.g.,
[
28
]
) and is doable in polynomial time.

Multiplicative rewards

This paper investigates
multiplicative rewards
in Markov chains and MDPs. Instead of adding up rewards obtained in each step, the rewards are simply multiplied.
Typical situations in which rewards behave multiplicatively arise whenever the change of some quantity is proportional to the amount already obtained.
This is a common theme in chemical and biological systems where, e.g., the current rate of a reaction or the change of the size of a population is proportional to the current concentration of a substance or the current population size. Also in financial settings, multiplicative rewards naturally occur. The return of an investment depends on the interest rate as well as the current size of the investment.

.

Exponential utility functions

A further situation in which multiplicative rewards naturally occur is the use of
exponential utility functions
, a type of utility function frequently used in economics and suitable to express
risk-aversion
.
Applied to the maximization of additive rewards in MDPs, this works as follows. If the additive reward along an execution is
w
, the associated utility is
f(w)=1-2^{-\lambda w}
for a fixed parameter
\lambda>0
.
This monotone function ensures that high rewards lead to a high utility. But the change in utility decreases for higher rewards. Low outcomes, however, have a strong effect on the utility and should be avoided.
A scheduler maximizing the expected exponential utility hence has to ensure high values of the additive rewards while keeping the probability of low outcomes small. The use of the exponential utility function
incentivizes a risk-averse behavior.

An exponential utility function converts the additive reward mechanism into a multiplicative one: If the rewards
w_{1},w_{2},\dots
are collected on a run of the MDP leading to an additive reward of
w=w_{1}+w_{2}+\dots
, the exponential utility
f(w)
can be obtained as
1-f(w_{1})\cdot f(w_{2})\cdot\dots
. So, maximizing the expected utility is the same as minimizing the expected multiplicative rewards after transforming the reward
r(s)
of a state
s
to
f(r(s))
.
Hence, the optimization of multiplicative rewards studied in this paper can be used for the risk-averse optimization of additive rewards.

Such an exponential utility function also lies at the heart of the
entropic risk measure

[
15
]
.
This notion has been widely studied in finance and operation research (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
9
]
).
For Markovian models, more precisely Markov chains, MDPs, as well as stochastic games extending MDPs with a second player, the optimization of the entropic risk measure applied to additive non-negative rewards has been studied in
[
5
]
.

Divergence in recurrent and transient states

One noteworthy difference between additive and multiplicative rewards is as follows.
In Markov chains, the expected value of accumulated additive rewards can only diverge to
\infty

recurrent

These are states that are visited infinitely often with positive probability.
In contrast, the expected value of multiplicative rewards may additionally be infinite due to rewards collected at
transient
states, i.e. states that are almost surely only visited finitely often.

Contribution

In this paper, we investigate the computation of the expected value of multiplicative rewards in Markov chains as well as the optimization of this expected in MDPs.
For an infinite path
\rho=\rho_{0}\rho_{1}\cdots
in a Markov chain or MDP with states
S
and with reward function
{r}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we define the (lower and upper) multiplicative rewards
{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}(\rho)
and
{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}(\rho)
as the lim sup and lim inf of
\prod_{k=0}^{n}{r}(\rho_{k})
for
n\to\infty
.
In several cases, the problems to be solved in the process reduce to the comparison of succinctly represented integers (CSRI).
Given two integers represented as
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{b_{i}}
with
k,a_{1},\dots,a_{k},b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
,
CSRI asks to decide whether the first is larger than the second.
Potentially surprisingly, CSRI is not known to be solvable in polynomial time
[
13
]
, but lies in PSPACE.
Further, the problem is in P if one of the following two number-theoretic conjectures holds
[
13
]
:
(1) Baker’s refinement
[
7
]
of the
ABC conjecture
of Masser and Oesterlè or
(2) the
Lang-Waldschmidt conjecture

[
24
]
.

The results for Markov chains
\mathsf{M}
with rational reward function
{r}
and initial state
{\hat{s}}
are as follows:

1)

The values
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
can be computed in polynomial time using a CSRI-oracle.
Thus, the computation is possible in polynomial space. Further, if CSRI is in P – so in particular if one of the two mentioned conjectures holds – the computation is possible in polynomial time.

2)

Deciding whether the values are finite or infinite is at least as hard as CSRI.

3)

If an absorbing state is reached almost surely in
\mathsf{M}
, then
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
and the value
can be computed in polynomial time.

For MDPs
\mathcal{M}
with rational reward function and initial state
{\hat{s}}
, we write
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
for the supremum of the expected value of
{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
when ranging over all schedulers and likewise for
{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
. Our results show that this supremum is indeed a maximum.
We obtain the following results:

4)

The values
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
can be computed in polynomial space.

5)

If an absorbing state is reached almost surely under any scheduler,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]

and this value can be computed in polynomial time.

6)

There is an MD-scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\mathfrak{S}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
.

7)

If
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]<\infty
, there is an MD-scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\mathfrak{S}}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
.

8)

If
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\infty
, an optimal scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
may require infinite memory to obtain
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\mathfrak{S}}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\infty
.

9)

The threshold problem, i.e., deciding
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]\geq\vartheta
(and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]\geq\vartheta
) for a given
\theta\in\mathbb{Q}
, is in
\textrm{NP}^{\textrm{CSRI}}
and so in NP if one of the two mentioned conjectures holds.

Additionally, these results allow us to solve the
multiplicative stochastic shortest path problem
in polynomial space. Analogously to the classical (additive) stochastic shortest path problem,
this problem asks for the optimal expected value of the multiplicative reward when only allowing schedulers that reach a given absorbing target state, i.e. a state with only a self loop and reward
1
, almost surely.

Related Work

As described above, exponential utility functions and the entropic risk measure applied to additive rewards implicitly require the treatment of multiplicative rewards.
In
[
5
]
, the computation and optimization of the entropic risk measure in Markov chains, MDPs, and stochastic games with total additive rewards is studied.
Key differences to our work arise for the following two reasons:
Firstly,
[
5
]
considers non-negative additive rewards that are turned into multiplicative rewards from the interval
[0,1]
by the used exponential utility function.
In contrast, we allow arbitrary positive rewards not bounded by
1
.
Secondly, starting with rational additive rewards, the used utility function transforms rewards into algebraic or even transcendental numbers.
This leads to computational challenges addressed in
[
5
]
.
In this paper, we consider rational multiplicative rewards for all algorithmic problems.
Further work on entropic risk and exponential utility functions in MDPs in the finite horizon setting
[
18
]
, applied to discounted rewards
[
19
]
, and on infinite state MDPs
[
12
]
focused on optimality equations and general convergence results of value iteration, but did not address the
algorithmic
challenges for finite-state MDPs.

Further, matrix multiplication games and the subclass of entropy games
[
3
,
1
,
2
]

are a class of games using a multiplicative reward mechanism: Two players alternatingly choose matrices from a finite set. One player wants to maximize the growth rate of the product of these matrices while the other player tries to minimize this rate. So, conceptually these games can be seen as using a “multiplicative mean payoff” as objective, in contrast to the multiplicative total reward we use.
Matrix multiplication games also allow for an interpretation as stochastic mean-payoff games (see
[
1
]
).

In
[
29
]
the limit behaviour of finite-horizon /
n
-step rewards (and the limit growth / decline rate thereof) is considered.
In our context, this relates to the expected multiplicative reward after
n
steps (discussed as
\multreward^{n}_{{r}}
in
Remark

III.3
), which surprisingly is fundamentally different from our objective.
Secondly, they focus on a mathematical discussion (e.g. optimality criterion etc.) and not on algorithmic aspects, which is the focus of our work.

For the technical treatment of recurrent states, we partly rely on results from
[
4
]
.
In
[
4
]
, a characterization of
end components
(strongly connected sub-MDPs) regarding the divergence behavior of additive rewards is provided.
In order to apply these results, we apply the logarithm to our multiplicative rewards posing additional computational challenges.
Further, we adapt a construction from
[
4
]
to remove end components without diverging behavior.
For the identification of these components, however, the results of
[
4
]
are not applicable in our setting and we develop new techniques to this end.

II
Preliminaries

A
Markov decision process
(MDP) is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(S,A,\Delta)

where
S
is a finite set of states,

A
a finite set of actions, and

\Delta\colon S\times A\times S\to[0,1]
the
transition probability function.
We require that

\sum_{t\in S}\Delta(s,\alpha,t)\in\{0,1\}

for all
(s,\alpha)\in S\times A
.
We say that action
\alpha
is
enabled
in state
s
iff
\sum_{t\in S}\Delta(s,\alpha,t)=1
and denote the set of all actions that are enabled in state
s
by
A(s)
. We further require that
A(s)\not=\emptyset
for all
s\in S
.
If for a state
s
and all actions
\alpha\in A(s)
, we have
\Delta(s,\alpha,s)=1
, we say that
s
is
absorbing
.

The paths of
\mathcal{M}
are finite or infinite sequences
s_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\,s_{1}\,\alpha_{1}\ldots
where states and actions alternate such that
\Delta(s_{i},\alpha_{i},s_{i+1})>0
for all
i\geq 0
.
For
\varrho=s_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\,s_{1}\,\alpha_{1}\,\ldots\alpha_{k-1}\,s_{k}
, the probability of
\varrho
is

\Delta(\varrho)=\Delta(s_{0},\alpha_{0},s_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot\Delta(s_{k-1},\alpha_{k-1},s_{k})
and
last(\varrho)=s_{k}
is the last state of
\varrho
.
We write
\varrho_{i}=s_{i}
for the
i
-th state of
\varrho
.
For two (finite) paths
\varrho
and
\rho
with
\varrho
ending in the state that
\rho
begins with, we write
\varrho\circ\rho
for their concatenation.

We will equip MDPs with a reward function
r\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, in the following we often consider the logarithmic rewards
{\log}\circ{r}
.
To slightly ease notation, we write
\log({r})
.

For algorithmic problems, we require all transition probabilities and rewards to be rational.
The
size
of
\mathcal{M}
is the sum of the number of states plus the total sum of the encoding lengths in binary of the non-zero probability values
\Delta(s,\alpha,s^{\prime})
as fractions of (co-prime) integers as well as the encoding length in binary of the rewards if a reward function is used.

A
Markov chain
is an MDP in which the set of actions is a singleton. In this case, we can drop the set of actions and consider a Markov chain as a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(S,\delta)
where
\delta
now is a function from
S\times S
to
[0,1]
.

An
end component (EC)
of
\mathcal{M}
is a strongly connected sub-MDP formalized by a subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
of states and a non-empty subset
\mathfrak{A}(s)\subseteq A(s)
for each state
s\in S^{\prime}
such that for each
s\in S^{\prime}
,
t\in S
and
\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}(s)
with
\Delta(s,\alpha,t)>0
, we have
t\in S^{\prime}
and such that in the resulting sub-MDP all states are reachable from each other. Maximal end components (MECs) are end components that are not contained in any other end component. The set of all MECs is computable in polynomial time (see, e.g.,
[
6
]
).
A
bottom strongly connected component (BSCC)
is an end component with exactly one action per state, i.e. an end component that is a Markov chain.

Schedulers

A
scheduler
(also called
policy
,
strategy
) for
\mathcal{M}
is a function
\mathfrak{S}
that assigns to each finite path
\varrho
a probability distribution over
A(last(\varrho))
.
If
\mathfrak{S}(\varrho)=\mathfrak{S}(\varrho^{\prime})
for all finite paths
\varrho
and
\varrho^{\prime}
with
last(\varrho)=last(\varrho^{\prime})
, we say that
\mathfrak{S}
is
memoryless
.
In this case, we also view schedulers as functions mapping states
s\in S
to probability distributions over
A(s)
.
A scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
is called
deterministic
if
\mathfrak{S}(\varrho)
is a Dirac distribution for each finite path
\varrho
, in which case we also view the scheduler as a mapping to actions in
A(last(\varrho))
.
We write MD-scheduler for memoryless deterministic scheduler.

Probability measure

We write
\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}
to denote the probability measure induced by a scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
and a state
s
of an MDP
\mathcal{M}
.
It is defined on the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the cylinder sets
\mathit{Cyl}(\varrho)
of all infinite extensions of a finite path
\varrho=s_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\,s_{1}\,\alpha_{1}\,\ldots\alpha_{k-1}\,s_{k}
, by assigning to
\mathit{Cyl}(\varrho)
the probability that
\varrho
is realized under
\mathfrak{S}
.
This is
\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}[\mathit{Cyl}(\varrho)]=\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathfrak{S}(s_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\dots\,s_{i})(\alpha_{i})\cdot\Delta(s_{i},\alpha_{i},s_{i+1})
if
s_{0}=s
and
0
otherwise.
This can be extended to a unique probability measure on the mentioned
\sigma
-algebra.
For details, see
[
6
]
.
For a random variable
X
, i.e. a measurable function defined on infinite paths in
\mathcal{M}
, we denote the expected value of
X
under a scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
and state
s
by
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}[X]
.
We define

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\min}[X]=\inf_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}[X]

and

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\max}[X]=\sup_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}[X]
.

Objectives

We recall standard objectives for Markov models.
All appearing sets / functions are measurable, see e.g.
[
28
]
.

Reachability

For a set of states
T
,
\lozenge T=\{\rho\mid\exists i.\rho_{i}\in T\}
contains all paths that eventually reach a state in
T
.
For a single state
t
, we also write
\lozenge t
instead of
\lozenge\{t\}
.

Total Reward

For a reward function
{r}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
, the total reward of a path
\rho
is defined as
\totalreward(\rho)=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{n}{r}(\rho_{i})
.
(In general, the limit might not be defined and we arbitrarily choose the infimum.)

Mean Payoff

For a reward function
{r}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the two versions of the mean payoff of a path
\rho
as

\displaystyle\overline{\mathit{MP}}_{{r}}(\rho)

\displaystyle={\limsup}_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n+1}{\sum}_{i=0}^{n}{r}(\rho_{i})\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\underline{\mathit{MP}}_{{r}}(\rho)

\displaystyle={\liminf}_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n+1}{\sum}_{i=0}^{n}{r}(\rho_{i}).

In finite Markov chains, almost all paths
\rho
satisfy
\overline{\mathit{MP}}_{{r}}(\rho)=\underline{\mathit{MP}}_{{r}}(\rho)
.
Hence, we simply write
\mathit{MP}_{{r}}
instead of
\overline{\mathit{MP}}_{{r}}
or
\underline{\mathit{MP}}_{{r}}
when the difference does not matter.
In a strongly connected Markov chain
\mathsf{M}
, we additionally have
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[\mathit{MP}_{{r}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}^{\prime}}[\mathit{MP}_{{r}}]
for any pair of states
{\hat{s}},{\hat{s}}^{\prime}\in S
(e.g.,
[
28
, Chp. 9]
).

Comparison of Succinctly Represented Integers (CSRI)

The
comparison of succinctly represented integers (CSRI)
problem will play an important role:
Given natural numbers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n},c_{1},\dots,c_{m},d_{1},\dots,d_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
(in binary), decide whether

a_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdot\dots\cdot a_{n}^{b_{n}}>c_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdot\dots\cdot c_{m}^{d_{m}}
.
This problem is not known to be solvable in polynomial time
[
13
]
.
However,
[
13
]
shows that the problem is in P if either
(i) Baker’s refinement
[
7
]
of the
ABC conjecture
of Masser and Oesterlè, or
(ii) the Lang-Waldschmidt conjecture
[
24
]
hold.

Unconditionally, an upper bound for CSRI is
\exists\mathbb{R}
(see
Proposition

.1
in
Section

-A
), the complexity class of the existential theory of the reals, which is contained in PSPACE
[
10
]
.
Interestingly, deciding
equality
of succinctly represented integers (ESRI), i.e. asking whether
a_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdot\dots\cdot a_{n}^{b_{n}}=c_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdot\dots\cdot c_{m}^{d_{m}}
for an instance as above, is unconditionally in P
[
13
]
.

Several of our considered problems involve such comparisons.
We will hence use CSRI to also denote the class of all problems that can be reduced to CSRI in polynomial time.
Further, we will write
\text{P}^{\text{CSRI}}
to denote the class of problems decidable in polynomial time with an oracle for CSRI and analogously
\text{NP}^{\text{CSRI}}
for the non-deterministic polynomial time variant.
Note that if one of the conjectures (i) or (ii) mentioned above holds,
\text{CSRI}=\text{P}^{\text{CSRI}}=\text{P}
.
Unconditionally, we have
\text{CSRI}\subseteq\exists\mathbb{R}
and
\text{P}^{\text{CSRI}}\subseteq\text{NP}^{\text{CSRI}}\subseteq\text{PSPACE}
.

III
Markov Chains

Let
\mathsf{M}=(S,\delta)
be a Markov chain and
{r}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
a reward function.
We exclude reward zero here for simplicity; states with reward zero can be collapsed to one absorbing state with reward
0
as reaching such a state immediately leads to a multiplicative reward of
0
.
The presence of such an absorbing state with reward
0
does not influence any of the results as also explained later in
Remark

III.20
.

For a
finite
path
\varrho=s_{0}s_{1}\dots s_{n+1}
, we write
\multreward_{{r}}(\varrho)=\prod_{k=0}^{n}{r}(\varrho_{k})
for its
multiplicative reward
(note that rewards are obtained when leaving a state).
For an infinite path
\rho
, we define the (lower and upper) multiplicative reward as

\displaystyle{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\limsup}_{n\to\infty}{\prod}_{k\leq n}{r}(\rho_{k})\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}(\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\liminf}_{n\to\infty}{\prod}_{k\leq n}{r}(\rho_{k}).

Note that these values need not equal each other.
More so, we later see that they are different in many cases.
We are interested in the expected value of these random variables when starting from a given state
{\hat{s}}\in S
, i.e. we want to determine
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
, which we call the
values (of the state
{\hat{s}}
)
.
The results of this section can be summarized as follows:

Theorem III.1

(Main result for MC)
.

Let
\mathsf{M}=(S,\delta)
a Markov chain,
{\hat{s}}\in S
an initial state, and
{r}\colon S\to\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
a (rational) reward function.
Then:

1)

The values
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
can be computed in polynomial time with an oracle for CSRI, i.e. the computation is possible in polynomial space and, if CSRI is in P, the computation is possible in polynomial time.

2)

If an absorbing state is reached almost surely in
\mathsf{M}
, then
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
can be computed in polynomial time (independent of CSRI).

Remark III.2
.

Interestingly, the part for which we cannot show an unconditional polynomial time upper bound is deciding whether the value of a state in a BSCC is
0
or
\infty
when we already know that it is one of the two.
In fact, we show that this problem is as hard as CSRI (
Proposition

III.11
).
Whether the value of a state in a BSCC is either strictly between
0
and
\infty
or is one of the two can indeed be decided in polynomial time (relating to ESRI).

To prove these results, we first focus on strongly connected Markov chains.
For these, we provide necessary and sufficient conditions for the expected multiplicative reward to be
0
or
\infty
and show how these conditions can be checked.
Then, we can assign values to the states in BSCCs of a general Markov chain.
For the transient states, we provide a linear system of equations such that the values of the states
s\in S
form the unique solution to the system if these values are finite and such that the system has no non-negative solution otherwise.

All proofs for this section can be found in
Section

-B
.

With this definition at hand, we can now capture the effect of n-cycles formally.

Lemma III.7
.

Let
\mathsf{M}
and
{r}
be as above and
{\hat{s}}\in S
any state.
Assume that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=0

{\hat{s}}\in S

\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\infty
.

Thus, the expected multiplicative reward in

.
In this case, the expected multiplicative reward can be directly characterized as shown in the following two lemmas.

Lemma III.8
.

Let
\mathsf{M}
and

All cycles in
\mathsf{M}

Lemma III.9
.

Let
\mathsf{M}
and

be as above, and assume that all cycles in

Let

Note that contrary to many classical objectives, this also means that not all states in a BSCC have the same value.

Computing Multiplicative Rewards

For the computation of expected multiplicative rewards, we restrict to inputs with rational transition probabilities and rewards, given as fractions of (co-prime) integers in binary.
So, let
\mathsf{M}=(S,\delta)
be a strongly connected Markov chain with rational transition probabilities and with a reward function
{r}\colon S\to\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
and let

{\hat{s}}\in S
be an initial state.
From the above, the following steps are necessary to compute
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
:

1)

Decide whether
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]
is
<0
,
=0
, or
>0
.
In the first case and third case, both values of
{\hat{s}}
are
0
or
\infty
, respectively,
by
Lemma

III.4
.

2)

If
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=0
, check whether there are n-cycles.
If yes,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\infty
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=0
by
Lemma

III.7
.

3)

Otherwise, all cycles have multiplicative reward
1
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\max_{t\in S}R({\hat{s}},t)
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\min_{t\in S}R({\hat{s}},t)
with

as in
Lemma

III.9
.

Step 1. Comparing
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]
to
0

In strongly connected Markov chains, the mean payoff can be expressed in terms of the unique stationary distribution (see, e.g.,
[
23
, Sec. 2.6.2]
).
This stationary distribution
\theta\in[0,1]^{S}
can be computed by solving a linear equation system using the transition probabilities of
\mathsf{M}
in polynomial time (see, e.g.,
[
6
]
).
So, we can obtain fractions
\theta_{s}=a_{s}/b_{s}
of integers in binary for all states
s\in S
in polynomial time (and hence of polynomially sized binary representation).
We have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]={\sum}_{s\in S}\theta_{s}\cdot\log({r}(s))
.
Now, applying the exponential function to this sum, we see that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]\bowtie 0
for
{\bowtie}\in\{<,=,>\}
if and only if
\prod_{s\in S}{r}(s)^{\theta_{s}}\bowtie 1
.
This inequality holds if and only if
\prod_{s\in S}{r}(s)^{a_{s}}\bowtie\prod_{s\in S}{r}(s)^{b_{s}}
.
Letting
d
be the least common multiple of the denominators of the values
{r}(s)
with
s\in S
, we can multiply this inequality with
d^{(\max_{s}a_{s}\cdot b_{s})}
to obtain an instance of CSRI for
{\bowtie}\in\{<,>\}
and an instance of ESRI if
{\bowtie}
is
=
.
We conclude:

Proposition III.10
.

Deciding whether
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=0
is in P.
Deciding whether
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]\bowtie 0
for
\bowtie{}\in\{<,>\}
is in the complexity class
{\text{CSRI}}\subseteq\exists\mathbb{R}
.

So, as described in
Section

II
, the latter problem is in P if either of the two mentioned number-theoretic conjectures hold.
Further, distinguishing divergence to
\infty
from convergence to
0
is at least as hard as CSRI:

Proposition III.11
.

Deciding whether
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]>0
is at least as hard as CSRI.

Step 2. Checking whether all cycles have multiplicative reward
1

By
Lemma

III.8
, all cycles have multiplicative reward of
1
if and only if for any two states
s
and
t
all paths from
s
to
t
have the same reward.
Since the Markov chain is strongly connected, this is equivalent to all paths from
{\hat{s}}
to
t
having the same reward.
This in turn can be determined by standard graph search algorithms in polynomial time.
For example, we can follow a simple breadth-first search:
Starting from
{\hat{s}}
, we store for each state
t
the reward of the path through which we reached
t
from
{\hat{s}}
(when moving from
t
to
t^{\prime}
, the value for

is simply the value of
t
times
{r}(t^{\prime})
).
If we encounter a successor of
t
which we already visited, however now find a path with different weight, we return
“false”
.
When the search completes without error, we return
“true”
.
As we only follow each edge once, we only need to perform linearly many multiplications, and all involved numbers are of polynomial size.

for states
s
and

General Markov Chains

Now, we address the problem of finding the expected multiplicative reward in general, i.e. not necessarily strongly connected, Markov chains.
We consider the multiplicative reward
{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
defined using the

Let
\mathsf{M}=(S,\delta)
be a Markov chain and

We assume w.l.o.g. that all states are reachable from
{\hat{s}}

For a state

First, the results from the previous section provide a characterization of the values

for all states in the BSCCs of

Thus, let

If any of these values are
\infty
, we also have
v_{{\hat{s}}}=\infty
:
We assumed that all states are reachable, i.e. in particular
t
, and all rewards are positive, hence there is a path with non-zero reward from
{\hat{s}}
to
t
.
So, next assume that the value
v_{t}
is finite for all states
t\in T
.
Further, let
T_{0}\coloneqq\{t\in T\mid v_{t}=0\}
be the set of states in a BSCC with value
0
.
(Observe that either all states or no state of a BSCC have value
0

Lemma III.12
.

For each
s\in S
, we have
v_{s}=0
if and only if
\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathsf{M},s}[\lozenge T_{0}]=1
.

Lemma

III.12
allows us to compute the set
S_{0}\coloneqq\{s\in S\mid v_{s}=0\}
given the values
v_{t}
for all

via standard graph analysis.
The remaining states for which we aim to determine the values are the states in the set
S_{?}\coloneqq S\setminus(S_{0}\mathbin{\cup}T)
.
For these, we consider the following system of equations.
For variables
x_{s}
for all states
s\in S_{?}
, we require that

x_{s}={r}(s)\cdot\left({\sum}_{s^{\prime}\in S_{?}}\delta(s,s^{\prime})\cdot x_{s^{\prime}}+{\sum}_{t\in T}\delta(s,t)\cdot v_{t}\right).

(1)

We show that a non-negative solution exists if and only if
v_{{\hat{s}}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]<\infty
, and the expected multiplicative reward is the
unique
solution in this case.
To simplify matters, we first restrict to non-negative solutions, i.e.
x_{s}\geq 0
.

Lemma III.13
.

If
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]<\infty
, then
v_{s}
is a (non-negative) solution to
Eq.

1
.

Corollary III.14
.

If there is no non-negative solution to
Eq.

1

\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\infty
.

Next, we prove that if there is a non-negative solution to
Eq.

1
, then the expected multiplicative reward is finite.
In fact, the vector of values for each state is the component-wise least solution to these constraints.
As the values for states in
S_{?}
are strictly positive, this shows that
(v_{s})_{s\in S_{?}}
is the unique solution.
To prove that the vector of values for each state is the component-wise least solution, we define the update operator
U\colon\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{S_{?}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{S_{?}}
derived from the constraint as
U\colon(x_{s})_{s\in S_{?}}\mapsto(y_{s})_{s\in S_{?}}
where
y_{s}={r}(s)\cdot\left({\sum}_{s^{\prime}\in S_{?}}\delta(s,s^{\prime})\cdot x_{s^{\prime}}+{\sum}_{t\in T}\delta(s,t)\cdot v_{t}\right)
.
Any non-negative solution to
Eq.

1
is a fixed point of
U
and vice-versa.

Lemma III.15
.

We have
(v_{s})_{s\in S_{?}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}U^{n}(0)
.

Corollary III.16
.

Suppose there is a non-negative solution
x^{\ast}
to
Eq.

1
. Then, for all
s\in S
, we have
v_{s}\leq x^{\ast}_{s}
.

Corollary III.17
.

If
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]<\infty
, then
(v_{s})_{s\in S_{?}}
is the unique solution to
Eq.

1
.

In summary, we obtain:

Theorem III.18
.

Given the values
v_{t}
for all
t\in T
,
either
Eq.

1
has no non-negative solution and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{M},{\hat{s}}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\infty
,
or
Eq.

1
has a unique non-negative solution, which is a strictly positive vector of finite values, and this solution is
(v_{s})_{s\in S_{?}}
.

So, after analyzing BSCCs as described in the previous section in polynomial time with a CSRI-oracle, all remaining steps can be carried out in polynomial time.
If an absorbing state is reached almost surely, there are no BSCCs except for the absorbing states and so the values can be computed in polynomial time. Furthermore, if an absorbing state is reached almost surely, it is reached almost surely in finitely many steps so that
{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
and
{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
almost surely coincide. This
finishes the proof of
Theorem

III.1
.

IV
Markov Decision Processes

In this section, we turn our attention to MDPs.
Let
\mathcal{M}=(S,A,\Delta)
be an MDP,
{r}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
a reward function, and
{\hat{s}}\in S
an initial state.
The multiplicative rewards of paths are defined as for Markov chains.
We investigate the maximization problem for the expected multiplicative reward. Given an MDP
\mathcal{M}
, reward function
{r}
, and initial state
{\hat{s}}
, we are interested in the following quantities
where
\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}
is the set of schedulers:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\sup}_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\pi}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\sup}_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\pi}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]

For minimization, we can consider the reward function
{r}^{\prime}
where
{r}^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{{r}(s)}
, switch between
{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
and
{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
, and then invert the result again (observe that
{r}(s)>0
by assumption).
This is analogous to negating rewards in the additive case.

The results of this section can be summarized as follows:

Theorem IV.1

(Main result for MDP)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(S,A,\Delta)
be an MDP,
{\hat{s}}\in S
an initial state, and
{r}\colon S\to\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
a (rational) reward function. Then:

1)

The values
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
can be computed in polynomial space.

2)

If in
\mathcal{M}
an absorbing state is reached almost surely under any scheduler, the values
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]

are equal and can be computed in polynomial time.

3)

There is an MD-scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\mathfrak{S}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
.

4)

If
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]<\infty
, there is an MD-scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\mathfrak{S}}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
.

5)

The threshold problem – deciding whether
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]\geq\vartheta
(and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]\geq\vartheta
) for a given threshold
\vartheta\in\mathbb{Q}
– is in
\text{NP}^{\text{CSRI}}
and hence in NP if
\text{CSRI}\subseteq\text{P}
.

6)

Additionally given an absorbing target state
t\in S
, the
multiplicative stochastic shortest path problem
asking for the value

\sup_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\mathfrak{S}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]
where the supremum ranges over all schedulers
\mathfrak{S}
that reach
t
almost surely can be solved in polynomial space.

In order to obtain these results, we take the following steps:

Step 1:

First, we take a closer look at end components (ECs).
Similar to the case of Markov chains, the maximal expected mean payoff with respect to the logarithmic rewards
\log({r})
allows us to detect cases in which the values are
0
or
\infty
.

Step 2:

In case the maximal expected logarithmic mean payoff in an EC is
0
, we distinguish whether the EC contains a BSCC in which all cycles are 1-cycles (
non-gambling
) and whether the EC contains a mean payoff-optimal BSCC with n-cycles (
gambling
).
Then, we present an adapted version of the spider construction of
[
4
]
allowing the iterative removal of all non-gambling BSCCs.

Step 3:

After pre-processing ECs by checking that no states have value
\infty
due to
recurrent
behavior inside the ECs and iteratively removing non-gambling BSCCs, we provide a linear program (LP) whose solution contains the optimal values of all states.
The correctness is shown by proving that the optimal values are the least fixed point of the update operator associated with the LP.
If the LP has no solution, the value of the initial state is
\infty
due to
transient
behavior of the MDP.

Step 4:

An optimal MD-scheduler can be extracted from the solution to the LP and traced back to an MD-scheduler for the original MDP.
The only case in which there is no optimal MD-scheduler is if the value
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\infty
due to a gambling BSCC that can be left with arbitrarily high multiplicative reward before moving to a non-gambling BSCC (or an absorbing state with value
1
as a special case).

Remark IV.2

Before we continue, we illustrate the latter case, i.e. the infimum being

Consider the MDP in

IV-A

Analysis and pre-processing of ECs

Starting with an arbitrary MDP, we first analyze the ECs by looking at all maximal ECs (MECs), i.e. ECs not contained in another EC, separately.
Each MEC is a strongly connected sub-MDP.
So, to ease notation, we take a single strongly connected MDP
\mathcal{M}=(S,A,\Delta)
with reward function
{r}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
as given in the sequel.

IV-A
1
Logarithmic mean payoff

Analogously to
Lemma

III.4
for Markov chains, we obtain the following result:

Lemma IV.3
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
and
{r}
be as above and let
{\hat{s}}\in S
be a state of
\mathcal{M}
.
Then,
1) if
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]<0
, then
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=0
and hence
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=0
, and
2) if
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]>0
, then
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\infty
and hence
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\infty
.

Of course, checking whether the maximal logarithmic mean payoff is greater, less, or equal to

is at least as hard as in the case of Markov chains for which no unconditional polynomial-time algorithm is known (see
Proposition

III.11
).
A polynomial-space upper bound, however, is straightforward:

Proposition IV.4
.

Given a strongly connected
\mathcal{M}
as above and a rational reward function
{r}
, deciding whether
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]\bowtie 0
for
\bowtie{}\in\{<,=,>\}
is in PSPACE.

IV-A
2
Gambling and non-gambling BSCCs

If the maximal expected mean payoff with respect to
\log({r})
is zero, a more careful analysis is necessary.
As before, we need to investigate the nature of cycles within the system.
However, contrary to Markov chains, we cannot simply analyse all potential cycles:
Only cycles that are induced by MD-schedulers optimal with respect to the logarithmic mean payoff may be taken into consideration.
Only these cycles may be chosen with positive frequency by schedulers optimal with respect to the logarithmic mean payoff.
If a scheduler takes any other cycle with positive frequency, the logarithmic mean payoff is negative and therefore the multiplicative reward almost surely zero.
For further details, see
[
21
, Sec. 6.1]
.

We follow the classification of ECs in
[
4
]
.
First, we define the mean payoff-maximizing actions for each state.
Since in a strongly connected MDP, any action can be chosen arbitrarily often by an optimal scheduler for prefix-independent objective such as mean payoff, the definition requires the restriction to MD-schedulers.
For
s\in S
, let

\displaystyle A^{\max}(s)=\{\alpha\in A(s)\mid\alpha\text{ is part of a BSCC induced by an }

\displaystyle\text{MD-scheduler $\pi$ with $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\pi}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]$}\}.

In other words,
A^{\max}
are all actions that an optimal scheduler may choose with positive frequency (a slightly stronger requirement than
“infinitely often”
).
Note that
A^{\max}(s)
is empty for states that are not part of any BSCC induced by a mean payoff-optimal MD-scheduler.
By
\mathcal{M}^{\max}
, we denote the sub-MDP that is obtained from
\mathcal{M}
by disabling all actions in
A(s)\setminus A^{\max}(s)
for all states
s
.
We call ECs of
\mathcal{M}^{\max}

max-ECs
, and BSCCs of
\mathcal{M}^{\max}

max-BSCCs
.
Note that there may be exponentially many such
max-ECs
/
max-BSCCs
.
As a by-product of checking whether the maximal logarithmic mean payoff is zero, we can compute the sets
A^{\max}(s)
:

Proposition IV.5
.

Given a strongly connected
\mathcal{M}
as above and a rational reward function
{r}\colon S\to\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
with
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=0
for any state
{\hat{s}}\in S
, the set
A^{\max}(s)
for all states
s\in S

can be computed in polynomial space.

Remark IV.6
.

Alternatively, the sets
A^{\max}(s)
can be characterized via the linear program for the optimal mean payoff in strongly connected MDPs (see
[
21
, Sec. 6.1]
).
Using variables
v_{s}
for all
s\in S
and a variable
x
, the LP is to minimize
x
subject to

x+v_{s}\geq\log(r(s))+{\sum}_{t\in S}\Delta(s,\alpha,t)\cdot v_{t}\quad\forall s\in S,\alpha\in A(s)
.
The optimal mean payoff is the value of
x
.
For an optimal solution
(x^{\ast},(v^{\ast}_{s})_{s\in S})
, we have
\alpha\in A^{\max}(s)
exactly when
the corresponding constraint is
active
(i.e. left-hand
equals
right-hand side).
However, due to the occurring logarithms, this representation is not directly applicable in an algorithm computing the sets
A^{\max}(s)
.

Assuming
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=0
, all max-ECs
E
satisfy
\mathbb{E}_{E,s}^{\mathfrak{S}}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=0
for any state
s
and scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
in
E
.
Now, we distinguish
gambling
and
non-gambling
max-ECs:

Definition IV.7
.

With
\mathcal{M}
and
r
as above with
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=0
, we call a max-EC
E

gambling

if it contains an n-cycle (see
Definition

III.6
), and
non-gambling
otherwise.
Gambling and non-gambling max-BSCCs are such max-ECs with one action per state.

The following lemma now states a result of
[
4
]
for additive rewards translated to our setting.

Lemma IV.8
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
and
{r}
be as above with
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=0
.
If there is a gambling max-BSCC,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\max}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\infty
for all states
s\in S
.
Further, there is an MD-scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
achieving
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}[{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}]=\infty
for all
s\in S
.

Intuitively, staying in a gambling max-BSCC means that the sum of the logarithmic rewards on prefixes of a path follows a random walk without drift. So, arbitrarily high values are reached infinitely often almost surely.
If the sum of the logarithmic rewards is arbitrarily high infinitely often, the same of course holds for the multiplicative reward of the prefixes. As
{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
is the
\limsup
of these multiplicative rewards,
the obtained value almost surely equals
\infty
in a gambling max-BSCC.

Analogously, staying in a gambling max-BSCC forever yields a
{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
of
0
almost surely.
So, without exiting the BSCC,
“gambling”
is not profitable for maximizing the expected value of
{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
.
If, however, a state
s
with positive value can be reached from a gambling max-BSCC, the gambling max-BSCC can be used to almost surely generate an arbitrarily high multiplicative reward before moving to state
s
with positive probability and realizing a positive value from then on, as sketched in
Remark

IV.2
.

Note that we have just seen that (contrary to the case of Markov chains) the treatment of
{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
and
{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
is not symmetric:
While the maximal expected value of
{\overline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
is
\infty
as soon as there is
any
cycle with multiplicative reward different from
1
in a max-BSCC, the maximal expected value of
{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
is
0
if there exists such a cycle in every max-BSCC. So, in the case of
{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
a quantifier alternation is introduced.

IV-A
3
Identifying non-gambling ECs

In the following, we discuss how to algorithmically identify non-gambling max-ECs, i.e. max-ECs with only 1-cycles.
Before we proceed, observe that without the restriction to MDPs with maximal expected logarithmic mean-payoff zero, this problem is NP-complete (similar to the additive case
[
4
, Thm. 3.12]
).
The proof uses a reduction from the
subset product
problem, similar to the well-known subset sum problem.

Lemma IV.9
.

Deciding for a strongly connected MDP and reward function whether there exists an MD-scheduler such that all cycles under this scheduler are 1-cycles is NP-complete.
This already holds if all distributions are Dirac (i.e. the MDP is a labelled transition system).

As it turns out, the restriction to
A^{\max}
in MDPs with maximal expected logarithmic mean-payoff zero reduces the complexity of the problem.
In particular, we show that individual actions can be pinpointed as
“culprits”
for n-cycles, instead of having to consider all possible combinations.
Without loss of generality, we consider an MDP
\mathcal{M}=(S,A,\Delta)
with reward function
{r}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=0
that comprises one max-EC only containing logarithmic mean-payoff-optimal actions, i.e. it is one strongly connected sub-MDP of
\mathcal{M}^{\max}
.
Thus, in
\mathcal{M}

every
scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
has
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\mathfrak{S}}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=0
for any state
{\hat{s}}
.
We want to determine whether this MDP contains a gambling BSCC or not.

Lemma IV.10
.

Let
s,t\in S
two states in
\mathcal{M}
, and
\sigma,\tau
two MD-schedulers which almost surely reach
t
from
s
, i.e.
\mathsf{Pr}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\sigma}[\lozenge t]=1
and likewise for
\tau
.
Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\sigma}[\totalreward^{\to t}_{\log({r})}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\tau}[\totalreward^{\to t}_{\log({r})}],

where
\totalreward^{\to t}_{\log({r})}(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log({r}(\rho_{i}))
and
k=\min\{i\mid\rho_{i}=t\}\mathbin{\cup}\{\infty\}
.
(For
k=\infty
, we consider w.l.o.g. the
\limsup
of the sum – since the set of paths that do not reach
t
have measure
0
, their value is irrelevant.)

Proof sketch.

Let
\zeta
be an MD-scheduler that almost surely moves from
t
back to
s
.
If
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\sigma}[\totalreward^{\to t}_{\log({r})}]\not=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\tau}[\totalreward^{\to t}_{\log({r})}]
, combining
\sigma
and
\zeta
or combining
\tau
and
\zeta

to one scheduler that moves from
s
back to
s
yields a scheduler that accumulates – in expectation – logarithmic reward different from
0
between visits to
s
. This contradicts the fact that every scheduler has expected logarithmic reward zero.
∎

Thus, for any two states
s,t
and scheduler
\pi
which almost surely moves from
s
to
t
, the value
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\pi}[\totalreward^{\to t}_{\log({r})}]
is the same and we refer to it with
Q(s,t)
, in particular
Q(s,s)=0
.
However, this does not directly tell us anything about the value of actual cycles under different schedulers.

Lemma IV.11
.

Fix an EC
E
in
\mathcal{M}
and a state
x
in
E
.
For every state
s\in S
, let
A^{\ast}(s)
denote all actions
a\in A(s)
where
\lvert\{\log({r}(u))+Q(u,x)\mid u\in\support(\Delta(s,a))\}\rvert=1
.
Then,
E
contains only 1-cycles if and only if it only uses actions of
A^{\ast}
.

Proof sketch.

If:

By induction, it follows that when restricted to actions in
A^{\ast}

every path
from a state
s
to
x
has the same
multiplicative
reward, namely
\exp(Q(s,x))
.
Otherwise, if two paths would have a different reward, one could follow them until the point where they separate and get a contradiction.
This then directly extends to any pair of states, i.e. all paths using only
A^{\ast}
from
s
to
t
yield
\exp(Q(s,t))
, and, in particular,
Q(s,s)=0
, so
\exp(Q(s,s))=1
.

Only if:

If at some state
s
we follow an action not in
A^{\ast}
, we get two paths from
s
to
x
which necessarily yield more and less than
Q(s,x)
, respectively.
Concatenated with a path back from
x
to
s
, we get two cycles with different values.
In particular, at least one of them is not a 1-cycle.
∎

This directly leads us to an algorithm for computing
A^{\ast}
.

Lemma IV.12
.

In the
\mathcal{M}
as above with
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},{\hat{s}}}^{\max}[\mathit{MP}_{\log({r})}]=0
and only consisting of one max-EC,
the set
A^{\ast}(s)
can be computed in polynomial time.

Proof Sketch.

Fix an arbitrary state
x
and MD-scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
under which
x
is reached almost surely from every state.
Then, for every starting state
s
compute the
expected visiting time

\text{evt}_{s}(u)

[
25
]
, i.e. how often is state
u
visited on average until
x
is reached, by solving a linear equation system.
The expected reward until reaching
x
can be expressed as
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}[\totalreward^{\to x}_{\log({r})}]=\sum_{u\in S}\text{evt}_{s}(u)\cdot\log({r}(t))
.
This value is, in general, irrational.
However, to decide
A^{\ast}
, we only need to decide whether all successors have the
same
value (
Lemma

IV.11
).
This can be reduced to queries of the form
“
\sum p_{i}\cdot\log(r_{i})=0
”
, which in turn reduces to an ESRI instance (as in
Proposition

III.10
).
∎

Remark IV.13
.

Our approach directly characterizes
all
gambling and non-gambling ECs through the set

.
In comparison,
[
4
, Sec. 3.3]
identifies a non-gambling EC for the corresponding notion for additive rewards by iteratively picking candidates and modifying the transition probabilities of the MDP if that EC is gambling.
For additive rewards, this works in polynomial time.
Transferring this approach to multiplicative rewards does not directly yield a polynomial-time algorithm.
In the other direction, we believe that our characterization can be directly transferred to the additive case to provide an alternative polynomial-time algorithm that is arguably conceptually simpler.

Iterative application of the adapted spider construction

Still assuming that

, we apply the adapted spider construction iteratively to remove all non-gambling max-BSCCs.
Starting with the MDP
\mathcal{M}_{0}=\mathcal{M}
, we construct a sequence of MDPs as follows: If
\mathcal{M}_{i}
has been constructed and contains a non-gambling max-BSCC that is not an absorbing state,
we remove one such BSCC with the spider construction. While doing that, we always choose states present in the original MDP
\mathcal{M}
as the center of the spider construction and not auxiliary states added in the process. This is always possible: As all new auxiliary states introduced in previous applications of the spider construction can only be reached via one action from a state present before the respective application of the spider construction, an auxiliary state is only part of a BSCC if also the corresponding state from the original MDP is part of the BSCC.
We let
\mathcal{M}_{i+1}
be the resulting MDP. If
\mathcal{M}_{i}
contains no non-gambling max-BSCC except for absorbing states,
the process terminates.
To show that this always happens,
we use the following result.

Lemma IV.16
.

With
\mathcal{M}=(S,A,\Delta)
and
\mathcal{M}_{E}=(S^{\prime},A^{\prime},\Delta^{\prime})

{\sum}_{t\in S}\left|A(t)\right|-1>{\sum}_{t\in S^{\prime}}\left|A^{\prime}(t)\setminus\{\mathit{stay}\}\right|-1

Lemma IV.17

The iterative removal of non-gambling max-BSCCs terminates after at most
\sum_{t\in S}\left|A(t)\right|
successive applications of the adapted spider construction.

All in all, we obtain:

Lemma IV.18
.

In an MDP
\mathcal{M}
with rational reward function
{r}
as above, the iterative identification and removal of non-gambling max-BSCCs can be done in polynomial space.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we presented multiplicative rewards for Markovian system, namely Markov chains and MDPs.
We demonstrated several key differences to standard additive rewards, such as divergence of values potentially stemming from transient states.
We established complexity bounds for determining and optimizing expected multiplicative rewards in Markov chains and MDPs, respectively, and considered several special cases.
We showed that several of the complexity results reduce to polynomial time if one of two well-known number-theoretic conjectures hold.
Additionally, we provided precise scheduler complexity bounds for MDPs.

For theoretical extensions, we identify several routes.
Firstly, an extension to games is interesting, especially considering how the additional quantifier alternation in the case of
{\underline{\multreward}_{{r}}}
already introduced asymmetries.
Next, we conjecture that instead of focussing on multiplication, some results could be extended to more general structures (e.g. semi-rings).
Practically motivated, we believe that a better algorithm than iterating through all MD-schedulers could be found for determining the nature of the logarithmic mean payoff in strongly connected MDP (
Proposition

IV.4
).
With this, a practical algorithm, potentially making use of approximations, could be developed.
Finally, our framework can be applied to optimizing entropic risk and remove restrictions of
[
5
]
.
