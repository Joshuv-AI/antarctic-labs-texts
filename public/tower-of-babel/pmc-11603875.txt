Characteristics and antibiotic resistance patterns of urinary tract isolates in hospitalized and non-hospitalized patients: a cross-sectional study in Khartoum, Sudan
Authors: Samah El-Arifi, Eltayeb AbdAlla, El Sheikh Mahgoub, Bothyna Fadol, Rashida Elmubarak
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrinary tract infections (UTIs) are widespread worldwide impacting society, economics, and healthcare systems. The rise of antibiotic resistance in both hospital and community settings has further complicated UTI management. This study aims to assess the epidemiologic distribution and microbiologic classification of UTI-causing microorganisms, as well as their resistance patterns.MethodsThis cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted at the National Public Health Laboratories in Khartoum, Sudan. Urine samples were collected from three hospitals from September to December 2021, and 65 bacterial isolates were recovered from patients with clinical symptoms of UTI. Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed using standard bacteriological methods and Kirby-Bauer’s disc diffusion method. Genotyping for Extended Spectrum Beta-Lactamase (EsβL) production was conducted using multiplex PCR.ResultsOf the 65 bacterial isolates, 27 (41.5%) were community-acquired UTI patients (C-UTIs), and 38 (58.5%) were hospital acquired UTI patients (H-UTIs). Males were more commonly affected by H-UTIs (68.4%), while females represented the majority among C-UTIs (85.2%). Gram-negative bacteria were predominant in urinary isolates (81.54%), with Escherichia coli being the most common organism (49.1%). Gram-positive organisms accounted for 18.46% of isolates, with Enterococcus spp. being the most prevalent. In-vitro susceptibility analysis revealed that meropenem was the most effective antibiotic in Gram-negative isolates (77.4%), while nitrofurantoin was the most effective in Gram-positive isolates (91.67%). EsβL production was detected in 11/23 (47.8%) of the tested isolates with Escherichia coli being the most common ESβL producer.ConclusionThis study highlights the pattern of antibiotic-resistant bacteria in UTIs and their significance. Continuous monitoring, development of local antibiograms, and implementation of surveillance programs are essential for guiding empirical antibiotic use and managing UTIs effectively, especially in resource-limited settings like Sudan.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10130-8.Keywords: Urinary tract infections, Antibiotic resistance, Escherichia coli, Meropenem, Extended Spectrum Beta-Lactamase

Introduction

Urinary tract infection (UTI) results from the presence, multiplication, and invasion of microorganisms in organs of the urinary tract, leading to significant clinical syndromes affecting millions worldwide, especially healthy women [1]. UTIs are among the most prevalent infectious diseases, with substantial societal, economic, and healthcare system impacts. Recently, antibiotic resistance has increased globally, both in hospitals and the community [2, 3].

UTIs account for over 100,000 hospital admissions annually, often for pyelonephritis, and are the second most common infection in community practice. Annually, about 150 million people worldwide are diagnosed with UTI, with a higher prevalence in the elderly compared to younger individuals. Women are at a greater risk than men, with most infections involving the lower urinary tract, typically the bladder and urethra [4]. In hospitals, UTIs constitute 40% of all infections, with bacteriuria developing in up to 25% of patients requiring a urinary catheter for a week or more. In the US and Europe, hospitalized patients consume about 15% of all community-prescribed antibiotics for UTI treatment [2].

Community-acquired urinary tract infections are diagnosed in the outpatient or within 48 h of hospital admission in patients not hospitalized in the past 30 days, and not transferred from another hospital [4, 5]. Hospital-acquired infections are typically not present or incubated at the time of admission and manifest after 48 h of hospitalization [6]. Management of these infections is typically empirical, without urine culture or susceptibility testing to guide therapy [7, 8]. The emergence of resistance in major bacteria, particularly Enterobacterales such as E. coli and K. pneumoniae, to cephalosporins and other antibiotics is a significant concern [9].

The inappropriate use of antibiotics contributes to resistance, leading to serious outcomes like frequent recurrences, pyelonephritis with sepsis, renal damage in young children, pre-term birth, and complications from frequent antimicrobial use, including high-level antibiotic resistance and Clostridioides difficile colitis [9]. Extended Spectrum Cephalosporin Resistant (ESC-R) Gram-negative infections have long been an issue in healthcare settings, but resistant infections in the community have only recently been recognized, with a significant increase in community-onset infections [10].

Multidrug resistance expressed by CTX-M-producing isolates from the community is often associated with multiple Extended Spectrum Beta-Lactamase (EsβL) genes, as well as aminoglycoside and quinolone resistance genes, limiting the choice of effective antimicrobial drugs [9, 10]. The frequency of the CTX-M genotype among ESβL Enterobacterales isolates are remarkably high in nearby countries like Ethiopia (95.5%) [11].

In Sudan, a study from August to November 2008 on urine isolates from 200 adult patients found significant bacterial growth in 35% of specimens, with high antibiotic resistance in 67% of Gram-negative and 44% of Gram-positive isolates [12]. Another study in 2020 across five referral hospitals in Khartoum found a high prevalence of CTX-M gene (78%) among Enterobacterales isolates, along with TEM and SHV genes [13]. These genes are associated with multidrug resistance, hence the need for routine ESβL screening in clinical isolates to prevent the spread of resistant bacteria and facilitate reliable epidemiological analysis of antimicrobial resistance [14].

UTIs are increasingly difficult to treat due to the widespread emergence of antibiotic resistance mechanisms, posing a serious global health issue. Management of these infections is often empirical, without urine culture or susceptibility testing to guide therapy. Hence, empirical therapy should be based on local resistance and sensitivity experiences, affecting the choice of antimicrobial therapy in the current era of antimicrobial resistance patterns.

Therefore we aimed to assess urinary tract isolates responsible for invasive infections in hospitalized and non-hospitalized patients. Specifically, to determine the most frequent microorganisms in terms of Gram staining and species, and to assess the pattern of resistance in these micro-organisms.

Materials and methods

Materials

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional descriptive study that involved the detection of Gram-positive and Gram-negative microorganisms in urinary isolates. The study was performed in the Department of Bacteriology at The National Public Health Laboratories (NPHL), Khartoum, Sudan. The antibiotic resistant genes were investigated using multiplex PCR in the Central Laboratory, Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research. The samples were collected from three hospitals in Khartoum: Soba Teaching Hospital, Al Ribat University Hospital, and Al Moalim Medical City, from September 2021 to December 2021. The isolates were recovered from patients with clinical symptoms of UTI and significant bacteriuria. For examining the types of bacteria and phenotyping, the patients were categorized as either outpatient (Community) (C-UTI) or inpatient (Hospital) (H-UTI).

Sample size and study population

A total of 65 bacterial isolates were recovered from urine samples of patients suspected of having a UTI attending these three hospitals in Khartoum city. Patients with autoimmune disease, HIV, and those taking antibiotics in the past 48 h were excluded from the study.

Specimen collection and transportation

Midstream clean-catch urine samples were collected in sterile containers from all patients with clinical symptoms. Specimens were processed in the laboratory within 2 h of collection or kept at 4 ºC until delivery and processed no more than 18 h after initial collection. Samples were examined microscopically for pus cells to confirm urinary tract infection. Hospital infection isolates were collected from infections occurring 48 h after admission [6]. Community infection samples were collected from outpatient clinics, patients with no history of hospitalization within at least 30 days. Medical, demographic, and epidemiological data were collected and analyzed. A structured data collection sheet was used to collect information on age, sex, history of UTI, presence of diabetes, hypertension, cancer, chronic illness, neurologic or urologic disorder, and history of hospital admission. Infants below one year of age and patients with samples containing more than two or mixed pathogens were excluded.

Method

Cultivation of urine samples

Culture media of urine samples: cysteine lactose electrolyte deficient (CLED) agar plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C overnight. Identification of growing bacteria was performed according to standard bacteriological methods. Bacterial growth was examined after 24–48 h of incubation. Urinary tract infection was confirmed when the bacterial colony count exceeded 10^5 per mL of urine [15].

Identification of growing bacteria

Several methods were used to identify bacteria. First, all positive cultures were identified by their colony characteristics, lactose or non-lactose fermenter, and Gram staining was done to identify Gram-positives from Gram-negatives [15]. Then, by Gram stain, motility testing, and biochemical tests. Then, Gram-negative isolates were tested for lactose and glucose fermentation; urease, oxidase, and indole production; and citrate utilization. Gram-positive bacteria were tested using catalase and coagulase tests, mannitol fermentation, and Bile Esculin tests.

Preparation of a suspension of strains

A sterile wire loop was used and 3–5 well-isolated colonies were emulsified in 3–4 ml of sterile physiological saline or nutrient broth. The turbidity of the suspension matched the turbidity of McFarland standards. The surface of the Mueller-Hinton agar was completely covered by rotating the swab with the suspension, and the plates were allowed to dry for 3–5 min [15].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (disc diffusion testing)

Discs were evenly distributed 25 mm apart on Mueller-Hinton Agar using sterile forceps and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. The diameter of the zone of inhibition around the disc was measured using a ruler. Results were interpreted as Sensitive and Resistant based on CLSI guidelines [16].

Antibiotic Discs Used in the Study: Tests were done by Kirby-Bauer’s Method. Antibiotic disks (Bioanalytica and Himedia) were used on Mueller-Hinton agar, as per CLSI guidelines, were: ceftazidime (CAZ) (30 µg), amikacin (AK) (30 µg), amoxiclav (AMC) (20/10 µg), norfloxacin (NX) (10 µg), ceftriaxone (CTR) (30 µg), piperacillin/tazobactam (PIT) (100/10 µg), co-trimoxazole (COT) (1.25/23.75 µg), gentamicin (GEN) (30 µg), nitrofurantoin (NIT) (300 µg), meropenem (MEM) (10 µg), colistin (CL) (10 µg), tigecycline (TGC) (15 µg), and fosfomycin (FF) (50 µg).

Test for ESβL production and characterization of CTX-M alleles

Isolates with decreased susceptibility (intermediate by CLSI criteria) to third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone, ceftazidime) were tested for ESβL production by Phenotypic ESβL, Double-disc synergy test (DDST) [16]. Then, conventional PCR was used to amplify the CTX-M gene.

The CTX-M alleles were detected using multiplex PCR

DNA was extracted by boiling colonies in distilled water, followed by centrifugation. The PCR reaction mixture, including primers, DNA, and PCR Master Mix, was subjected to thermal cycling for 30 cycles. PCR products were then separated on agarose gel through electrophoresis and visualized under UV light to detect a 593 bp band, indicating successful amplification. The forward (FP) and reverse (RP) primers used for amplifying the CTX-M were CTX FP (sequence: ATGTGCAGYACCAGTAARGTKATGGC) and CTX RP (sequence: GGGTRAARTARGTSACCAGAAYCAGCG), yielding a product size of 593 bp [17].

Statistical analysis

2.3 : The data were analyzed using SPSS (ver.26) with reference p-value (< 0.05) and confidence limit (CL = 95%). Categorical data were described using frequencies and percentages, while continuous data were described using mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data. The cross table and Chi-square test were used to detect relations between variables, while the independent sample t-test was performed to examine the difference in age between community and hospitalized patients.

Discussion

This study assessed the epidemiologic distribution and microbiologic classification of UTI causing microorganisms in addition to the resistance pattern. The study found that Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were the most common cause of C-UTI and H-UTI respectively. Also, females had higher incidence of UTI. Which follow the worldwide distribution of UTI causes and gender differences as well as the previous trend in Sudan [11]. Regional and international studies document similar trends in Ethiopia, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Pakistan and India [10, 16, 18–20].

In patients with H-UTI, the most common causes of admission were kidney and other urinary tract disorders. These conditions are considered risk factors for UTIs due to increased bacterial accumulation and urine retention in the urinary system [21]. This association of UTI with kidney disease is very important to consider in low resources setting like Sudan, where chronic kidney diseases are common, yet renal replacement therapy is not accessible for many patients. This leaves those patients at risk for fulminant infections, urosepsis, or even death.

Most of the uropathogens in this study were Gram-negative bacteria, which is typical worldwide [22]. Escherichia coli was the most common organism in community-acquired UTIs (C-UTI) followed by Klebsiella spp. and the second organism in hospital-acquired UTIs (H-UTI) behind Pseudomonas aeruginosa. However, it’s important to note that regional variations exist between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria spread in hospital and community settings [4]. This pattern in which Escherichia coli and Klebsiella spp. prevail in the consistent across the region as indicated by studies from Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Palestine and Egypt [16, 18, 23, 24]. This Gram-negative predominance could be a potential threat for hospitalized patients especially elder patients and those with lower immunity, because of the presence of endotoxins in their cell wall, which can result in toxemia and rapid deterioration of the patents clinical status.

Pseudomonas species, is a typical nosocomial pathogen, is linked to catheter-associated infections due to its ability to form biofilms [4]. It is known as the most resistant bacteria in urinary tract infections in Sudanese patients [11]. Klebsiella spp. though accounts for higher proportions of complicated UTIs in H-UTI [7].

Among Gram-positive pathogens, E. faecalis was the more frequent than S. aureus and therefore supports the theory of fecal contamination [19]. Staphylococcus aureus was implicated in H-UTIs as a second Gram-positive pathogen, being part of normal skin flora that can be inoculated into the urethra during anal cleaning or catheterization [8].

Resent systematic reviews showed worldwide alarming rates of resistant uro-pathogenic bacteria especially E.coli for the commonly used antibiotics [25–27]. In Sudan, there increase in resistance to antibiotics used for treating UTIs, especially among Gram-negative bacteria [11, 28–31]. Similarly, we found a higher resistance pattern for Gram-negative isolates compared to Gram-positive ones.

In this study, meropenem was the most effective antibiotic among Gram-negative bacteria, superior to colistin and amikacin which were found to be the most effective by Mohammedkheir et al. [29] and Hamadalneel [28] respectively. According to the findings of this study and the most recent studies in Sudan meropenem, colistin and amikacin are the most effective antibiotics in Gram-negative uropathogens, especially among the commonest organisms; Escherichia coli and Klebsiella spp.

Gram-positive bacteria were highly sensitive to nitrofurantoin aligning with the recommendations of using nitrofurantoin for uncomplicated UTIs [6, 32]. However, recent studies in Sudan showed slightly lower sensitivity than in our study [11, 28]. Both fluoroquinolones and cephalosporins (which were widely used antibiotics in Sudan hospitals) showed low sensitivity rates for both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria.

In our study nearly half of the tested Enterobacterales were ESβL producers. Studies in Sudan indicate the prevalence of ESβL producing Enterobacterales in patients with UTIs is increasing, indicating their potential as antibiotic resistance mechanisms [33, 34]. Moreover, Genotyping in our study showed that almost half of these organisms had a CTX-M genotype, with higher proportion in H-UTI. The prevalence of this gene was reported in higher proportions in Sudan [17, 29, 30] with predominance for the CTX-M 15 variant [35].

The high resistance to antibiotics used for treating UTIs in Sudan, especially among Gram-negative bacteria, can be attributed to several factors. Key issues include the widespread dispensing of third-generation cephalosporins like cefixime without prescriptions [36], and the common over-the-counter use of co-amoxiclav [37]. Additionally, broad-spectrum antibiotics such as meropenem are often used inappropriately for empirical therapy in ICUs, contrary to evidence-based practices [38].

Studies in Sudan also reveal that doctors frequently prescribe antibiotics without culture and sensitivity results, and pharmacists commonly dispense them over the counter [39]. Audits show that many patients receive broad-spectrum antibiotics or unnecessary combinations for prophylactic purposes [40], often without documented indications [41]. The absence of antibiotic policies in most hospitals leads to treatment decisions based on empirical use, contributing significantly to the spread of resistance.

Despite the scientific significance of this study, it is important to acknowledge, some limitation: Firstly the lack of reporting of clinical symptoms among hospitalized patients affects the confidence in excluding a symptomatic bacteriuria; however, it is known that all the patients were suspected for UTI. Additionally, the study’s generalizability may be constrained by the limited number of bacterial colonies selected for DNA extraction, potentially affecting the representativeness of the findings. Additionally, the limited scope of detection also affects the yield of the study; due to financial constrain and lack of funding only CTX-M alleles were tested, which does not cover the full range of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) variants.

The significant presence of ESβL-producing Enterobacterales in H-UTI necessitate the implementations of surveillance programs for early detection and treatment and to control their spread. These preventive strategies may remain the only applicable and feasible measures in low-income settings as in Sudan.

Conclusion

The most frequently isolated bacteria causing UTI in our sample were E. coli and P. aeruginosa, with E. coli more prevalent among C-UTI patients and P. aeruginosa more prevalent among H-UTI patients. Meropenem was the most effective against Gram-negative followed by amikacin and colistin, while nitrofurantoin was effective against Gram-positive. The surveillance and local antibiogram need to be done continuously, which will guide the appropriate direction for empiric antibiotic therapy to manage UTIs effectively.
