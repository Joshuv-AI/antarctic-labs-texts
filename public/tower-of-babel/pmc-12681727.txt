First identification of camel Contagious Ecthyma Virus in camels from Altay, Xinjiang, China
Authors: Qiuyu Zhang, Ge Zhang, Yongbin Zhai, Qi Zhao, Yaru Feng, Maraba Rawaydul, Yuyu Zhang, Xiaolong Jiao, Yang Zhang, Yuefeng Chu, Jian Xu, Duoliang Ran, Bin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In April 2023, outbreaks of a novel oral disease were observed in Bactrian camels in Altay Prefecture, Xinjiang, China. To clarify the etiology and epidemiology, we surveyed 1863 camels from 12 farms across three major camel-producing counties. A total of 311 camels (16.7 %) exhibited characteristic lesions, with significantly higher incidence in adults (18.1 %) than juveniles (9.7 %) (p < 0.05). Tissue samples from 12 clinically affected camels were subjected to PCR testing for multiple poxviruses. Only Camel Contagious Ecthyma Virus (CCEV) was detected, and sequencing of the B2L gene confirmed its identity. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that Xinjiang isolates shared >98 % nucleotide homology with strains from Saudi Arabia and Sudan, with no variation among the 12 local sequences. Histopathology showed epidermal necrosis, hyperplasia, and intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies, consistent with parapoxvirus infection, and electron microscopy revealed typical poxvirus morphology. These findings document the first confirmed detection and molecular characterization of CCEV in camels in China, highlighting its potential impact on the camel industry and the need for further investigation of its epidemiology and zoonotic risk.

The Camel Contagious Ecthyma Virus (CCEV) is a DNA virus of the family Poxviridae, subfamily Vertebrate Poxviridae, genus Parapoxvirus [1], which causes CCE in camels. CCEV can infect both dromedary and Bactrian camels, and distinguishing between the two based on clinical symptoms alone is challenging [2,3]. Between 2 and 6 days post-infection, camels may develop primary local lesions on the skin around their lips and noses, which frequently extend to other parts of the head, such as around the eyes, the upper palate, and the gum below the incisors. Systemic lesions are rare. The lesions initially present as erythematous papules, progressing to pale yellow vesicles within a few days. Prior to crusting, ulcers and bleeding may occur. Following crust formation, the lesions darken to a brown color and naturally detach within 6–10 weeks. In severe cases, post-healing epidermal thickening may be observed, and alopecia around the lesions can persist for several months. The disease was first discovered in Kazakhstan in 1968 and subsequently diagnosed in various regions of the Middle East, Asia, and Africa [4]. Notably, Contagious Ecthyma Virus has previously been reported in goats in China [5], but there have been no prior reports of CCEV infections in camels in China, representing a critical knowledge gap.

In recent years, with the rapid development of the camel industry in Xinjiang, CCE has become widely prevalent there. Although the incidence rate is high, the overall mortality rate is low [6]. However, the disease is fatal to juvenile camels in severe cases, and this severely restricts the healthy development of the camel industry, resulting in huge economic losses. Therefore, investigating the occurrence and molecular characteristics of CCEV in Chinese camel populations is essential to better understand its epidemiology, assess potential risks, and support the sustainable development of the camel industry. The present study addresses this gap by reporting, for the first time, the detection and characterization of CCEV in camels in Xinjiang, China.

Methods

Biosafety and ethics approval

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Agricultural University (XJAU2022086). Research involving the CCEV pathogen was performed in a Biosafety Level 2 laboratory at the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, strictly following institutional and national biosafety regulations. Animal studies were conducted in an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC)-accredited facility, in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, USA). Virus inactivation of all samples was carried out under standard operating procedures approved by the ethics committee.

Clinical diagnosis and sample collection

Following the initial observation of suspected cases in Jeminay County, clinical investigations were expanded to the neighboring counties of Fuhai and Habahe. A total of 12 camel farms (four from each county), representing the major Bactrian camel-producing areas of the Altay region in Xinjiang, were surveyed. In total, 1863 camels were examined, and animals showing typical clinical signs—including redness, swelling, pustules, crusting, or pockmarks around the nose and mouth—were recorded as suspected cases. From these animals, 32 clinical samples (scabs or oral/nasal tissue lesions) were collected, of which 12 with the most representative and pronounced symptoms were selected for PCR testing. In addition, blood samples were obtained from clinically healthy camels within the same farms to serve as negative controls.

PCR detection and phylogenetic analysis

Pathological samples for PCR testing were specifically collected from the lip tissues of infected camels showing the most obvious clinical symptoms. After restraining the animals, lesion crusts or pustular papules were carefully scraped with a sterile scalpel, placed into 500 μL of 0.01 M PBS solution containing 1 % penicillin–streptomycin, and transported at low temperature to the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute within 36 hrs.

Genomic DNA was extracted using a commercial kit (Rapid Animal Genomic DNA Isolation Kit, Sangon, Shanghai, China). PCR assays were conducted using virus-specific primers for Contagious Ecthyma Virus (CCEV; B2L gene), Orf Virus (ORFV; B2L gene), Goatpox Virus (GTPV; P32 gene), Sheeppox Virus (SPPV; P32 gene), Camelpox Virus (CMPV; ATI gene), and Lumpy Skin Disease Virus (LSDV; P32 gene), as listed in Supplementary Table 1. PCR reaction conditions followed published protocols for capripoxviruses and parapoxviruses [7], with detailed cycling conditions and reagent compositions provided in the Supplementary Materials.

All amplicons positive for CCEV were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing (Sangon, Shanghai, China). Sequences were analyzed by BLAST against the NCBI database to identify homologous reference strains. A phylogenetic tree was then constructed using 31 nucleotide sequences (12 obtained in this study and 19 retrieved from GenBank).

Histopathological examination of lip tissue sections was carried out following standard veterinary pathology procedures. Samples were fixed in 10 % neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Microscopic observation revealed epidermal hyperplasia, ballooning degeneration of keratinocytes, and intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies, consistent with parapoxvirus infection.

Histopathological sections and observation under transmission electron microscope

Three samples of oral tissues with the most obvious clinical symptoms and identified as positive by PCR were collected and used for making histopathological sections and observation under transmission electron microscope (TEM). The oral tissues of double-humped camels were collected as follows: Pentobarbital Sodium (80 mg per camel) was injected around the pustules for local anesthesia. Subsequently, two pieces of oral tissue, each approximately 8mm × 8mm × 8mm, were excised using a sterile scalpel. One of these tissue samples was fixed in a 4 % formalin solution for the preparation of histopathological sections. The other tissue sample was fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde for the preparation of transmission electron microscope samples. After the collection, hemostasis was achieved by applying pressure to the wound, followed by disinfection. Both the histopathological sections and the transmission electron microscope samples were prepared by Sichuan Lilai Biotech Co., Ltd. Image acquisition was performed using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, HT-7700).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 7.0. Student's t-test or ANOVA was used for multiple comparisons. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ns, no significant difference (p > 0.05).

Results

In April 2023, our research team observed a large number of camels presenting with pustules on the lips and muzzle across several camel farms in Altay, Xinjiang (Fig. 1a). The lesions progressed from ruptured and ulcerated pustules on the lips to dry, gray or brown scabs on the muzzle, consistent with the clinical presentation of Camel Contagious Ecthyma (CCE). Clinical examination of 1863 camels identified 311 cases, yielding an overall incidence rate of 16.7 % (Table 1). The incidence rates stratified by location and age group are summarized in Table 1. Briefly, the incidence was significantly higher (p < 0.05) in adult camels (18.1 %) compared to young camels (9.7 %). PCR tests were conducted on collected tissue samples for ORFV, GTPV, SPPV, CMPV, CCEV, and LSDV. The results indicated that only the primers for ORFV and CCEV successfully amplified target fragments, each approximately 550 bp in size. The positive bands were extracted from the gel and sequenced, and the sequencing results confirmed the presence of CCEV. There were no nucleotide differences among the 12 Xinjiang isolates, suggesting that the same virus strain might have caused these cases. The phylogenetic tree was constructed based on nucleotide sequences using the MEGA X software with the neighbor-joining method and 1000 bootstrap replicates to assess the robustness of the branches. The alignment was performed with ClustalW. The results indicated that the Xinjiang isolates (CCEV-xinjiang-1 to CCEV-xinjiang-12) clustered into a single clade (Clade A), sharing the highest nucleotide homology with the CCEV isolates from Saudi Arabia (KR231670; 98.80 %) and Sudan (MN701775; 98.64 %). Reference strains of ORFV, CCEV, and pseudocowpox virus from different geographic origins were included in the analysis and are clearly labeled in the tree (Fig. 1C).

As indicated by pathological section results, the oral tissues displayed localized epidermal necrotic detachment (Fig. 2, A, black arrows), with a large number of necrotic cell fragments; there were localized epidermal granulosa cell hyperplasia, with an irregular arrangement, and uneven epidermal thickness; In the dermis, hair follicles, sebaceous glands and other skin appendages were observed, as well as irregularly arranged collagen fibers. Moreover, a few necrotic cell fragments were seen locally, accompanied by a small number of infiltrating lymphocytes and granulocytes (Fig. 2B and C, red arrows); small amount of myocyte necrosis (Fig. 2, C, blue arrow); Local necrosis of epidermis and superficial dermis in the tissue (Fig. 2, D, green arrow); there were many glandular epithelial cell detachments under the skin. Discussion

In this study, samples from diseased camels in Altay Prefecture, Xinjiang were identified as cases of camel contagious ecthyma (CCE) caused by the CCEV. The results of the epidemiological investigation showed that the average infection rate in adult camels (18.14 %) was significantly higher than that in juvenile camels (9.74 %) (p < 0.05). This age-related difference is intriguing and may be related to factors such as immunity development, repeated exposure, management practices, or potential sampling bias. Further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying this disparity. This finding contrasts with reports of camel contagious ecthyma in dromedaries from Nigeria [8] and Ethiopia [9], and with Cui Huan et al. [10], who developed an ORFV detection method for sheep and goats in China. The discrepancy in infection rates between adult and juvenile camels warrants further investigation to determine the underlying reasons.

The results of homology analysis of the B2L gene showed that there was no nucleotide difference among the 12 Xinjiang Isolates, and the nucleotide homology with the Saudi Arabia isolate and the Sudan isolate was the highest, showing highly similarity to isolate from India, and Qatar as well. These findings suggest that CCEV circulating in Xinjiang shares close genetic relationships with strains reported from the Middle East, Africa, and South Asia, highlighting the importance of global molecular surveillance. Although direct evidence for cross-border transmission is lacking, the rapid regional spread of related poxviruses, such as lumpy skin disease across China, Pakistan, India, and Mongolia [[11], [12], [13], [14]], underscores the need for further investigation and strict management measures to prevent potential transboundary transmission.

The parapoxviruses ORFV, BPSV, PCPV, and PVNZ are all zoonotic pathogens that are transmissible to humans, but whether CCEV is zoonotic has not been confirmed. Although some researchers have reported blister-like lesions on the hands of camel milkers after contact with infected camels [15], no direct evidence of human infection was obtained in our study. Importantly, no suspected blisters were observed on the arms of camel milkers or farmers in Altay, Xinjiang. The potential zoonotic risk of CCEV should therefore be regarded as hypothetical, and further virological and epidemiological studies are required to clarify this issue.

Attempts to isolate and purify the virus were unsuccessful, despite testing various cell lines as potential hosts for CCEV. In this study, we reported the first detection of CCEV in camels in China, described the typical symptoms and prevalence of CCE, and conducted molecular tracing studies. Given that Bactrian camels raised on farms serve as hosts for CCEV and may have close contact with humans, surveillance programs should be strengthened. Registration and quarantine of infected camels, which remain the most effective measures to control the spread of CCEV, have already been applied in the region. In order to further understand the host range and transmission mechanism of CCEV, it will be necessary to investigate the status of other ruminants in the same environment.
