Hybrid Black Hole–Disk Driven Jets: Steady Axisymmetric Ideal MHD Modeling
Yu Song 1 , Yehui Hou 2 , Lei Huang 3,4 , Bin Chen 5,1,6

Abstract
Improved observational precision in relativistic jets has underscored the need for tractable theoretical models. In this study, we construct a semi-analytical hybrid jet model that incorporates both black hole–driven and disk-driven components within the framework of steady, axisymmetric, ideal MHD. We derive a condition that determines the launching sites of cold outflows, introducing a new constraint on the magnetic field configuration threading the accretion disk. Using the Bernoulli equation and critical point analysis, we derive flow solutions along various magnetic field lines. Our hybrid jet model shows that discontinuities in field-line angular velocity lead to clear velocity shear and density jumps at the interface between the two jet components. These features are accompanied by localized enhancements in velocity and density, potentially explaining the observed limb-brightening.

In this context, studying GRMHD jets powered by different launching mechanisms is crucial for interpreting observational data, as model–data comparisons can constrain key physical parameters such as launching conditions, magnetic field configurations, and black hole spin. To develop a comprehensive yet tractable framework for jet modeling, it is essential to semi-analytically explore the steady, axisymmetric dynamics and morphology of ideal MHD jets across a range of launching mechanisms, magnetic field geometries, and magnetization levels.

In this work, we try to establish systematically a framework to investigate steady, axisymmetric, ideal MHD systems relevant to energy conversion and flow acceleration in both black hole–driven and disk–driven jets. We derive a general launching condition that identifies the permissible launching positions for cold outflows, which in turn imposes a novel constraint on the structure of disk-threading magnetic fields near the equatorial plane.
Building on this foundation, we propose a simplified hybrid BZ and BP jet model under prescribed magnetic field configurations in Kerr spacetime. By solving the Bernoulli equation and performing a critical-point analysis, we obtain the flow structure along different types of field lines. Our results show that the interface between the BZ and BP components naturally gives rise to a velocity shear layer, accompanied by localized enhancements in both density and velocity—features that may offer a physical explanation for the observed limb-brightening in relativistic jets.

Our analysis is largely algebraic and relies solely on the conservation laws governing stationary, axisymmetric, ideal MHD flows. For simplicity, a geometrically thin plasma loading region is assumed to exist in the vicinity of the black hole. Additionally, the fast magnetosonic point is placed at infinity, such that the terminal Lorentz factor—or equivalently, the asymptotic magnetization—serves as the only free parameter along a given field line. All other physical quantities, such as the mass flux, density and magnetization, are uniquely determined by the Bernoulli equation in conjunction with regularity conditions at the MHD critical points.

Jets in ideal MHD

2.1
Stationary axisymmetric flow

We begin with a non-self-gravitating magnetofluid in a general stationary and axisymmetric spacetime.
The line element of the spacetime takes the form

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=g_{tt}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+2g_{t\phi}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\phi+g_{\phi\phi}\mathrm{d}\phi^{2}+g_{{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{P}}}\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{P}}^{2}\,.

(2.1)

Here and thereafter, we label the poloidal coordinates
r,\theta
by the index
{\mathcal{P}}
.
The jet is composed of ionized plasma, which is supported by the baryonic matter in the accretion flow, or electron-positron pairs produced via photon-photon collisions
[
54
]
. In this study, we only consider the baryonic matter, thus the jet dynamics is governed by ions.
The energy-momentum tensor takes the form of
T^{\mu\nu}=T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm m}+T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM}
, where

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm m}=hu^{\mu}u^{\nu}+P\,g^{\mu\nu}\,,\quad T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM}=F^{\mu\rho}F^{\nu}_{\rho}-\frac{1}{4}g^{\mu\nu}F^{\alpha\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}\,,

(2.2)

represent the matter stress tensor and electromagnetic stress tensor, respectively;
h=e+P
is the rest-frame enthalpy density;
e
,
P
denote the rest-frame energy density and pressure,
u^{\mu}
is the flow four-velocity,
F^{\mu\nu}
is the electromagnetic field tensor.
Throughout this work, we require infinite conductivity which is also known as ideal MHD condition, ensuring that the electric field measured by the plasma is zero,
u_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}=0
1
1

1

Although finite resistivity effects—such as magnetic reconnection and current sheet formation—play a crucial role in understanding the details of magnetofluids, the ideal MHD approximation offers a well physical framework when the focus is on global fluid properties or time-averaged structures
[
17
]
.
.

The evolution of the magnetized fluid is governed by the GRMHD equations, namely the conservation of energy-momentum:
\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0
, and the conservation of particle number:
\nabla_{\mu}(\rho u^{\mu})=0
, where
\rho
denotes the rest-frame mass density. To close the system, an equation of state for the ion component is required. Under the adiabatic approximation, the total energy density is described by a polytropic relation
e=\rho+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Gamma-1}}\right)^{-1}P
, where
\Gamma
is the adiabatic index, taking the value
\Gamma=5/3
for non-relativistic ions and
\Gamma=4/3
for relativistic ions.

To proceed, we define the “pseudo-electromagnetic fields” as the contractions of
F^{\mu\nu}
and its dual with the
Killing vector in
t
direction:
E^{\mu}=F^{t\mu}\,,\quad B^{\mu}=-(\star F)^{t\mu}
.
Under stationary and axisymmetric ideal MHD, the poloidal magnetic field is aligned with the poloidal flow velocity,

B^{r}u^{\theta}=B^{\theta}u^{r}
, and the field components take

\displaystyle\quad B^{r}=\frac{\partial_{\theta}\psi}{\sqrt{-g}}\,,\quad B^{\theta}=-\frac{\partial_{r}\psi}{\sqrt{-g}}\,,\quad B^{\phi}=(u^{\phi}-\Omega_{F}u^{t})\frac{B^{\mathcal{P}}}{u^{\mathcal{P}}}\,,

(2.3)

where
\sqrt{-g}
is the metric determinant,
\psi\equiv A_{\phi}(r,\theta)
is the stream function serving
as a label for magnetic field lines and plays a central role in describing the field geometry, and

\Omega_{F}\equiv F_{tr}/F_{r\phi}=F_{t\theta}/F_{\theta\phi}
is the field-line angular velocity, which is conserved along each magnetic field line.
More details on
B^{\mu}
are included in Appendix
A.2
.

A nonzero field line rotation drives a generic evolution of ideal MHD flows, causing an initially arbitrary magnetic field near the black hole to gradually become toroidal at large distances. This behavior can be analyzed using a corotating observer with four-velocity
u_{\,\text{co}}^{\mu}\propto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,0,0,\Omega_{F}}}\right)
.
A characteristic surface, known as the light cylinder
[
55
]
, is defined by the condition
u_{\,\text{co}}^{2}\propto-k_{0}\rightarrow 0
where
k_{0}=-(g_{\phi\phi}\Omega_{F}^{2}+2g_{t\phi}\Omega_{F}+g_{tt})
, at which the corotating observer becomes null.
Beyond the light cylinder, no timelike corotating observers exist, and the flow lags behind the rotating field lines, leading to a strong toroidal magnetic field in the fluid frame.

Moreover, it is well-established that the stationary and axisymmetric ideal MHD system further possesses three conserved quantities of the magnetofluid along each magnetic field line
[
56
,
19
]
. We denote them as the mass flux
\eta
, specific angular momentum
L
, and the specific energy
E
:

\displaystyle\eta\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{r}}{F_{\theta\phi}}=\frac{\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{\theta}}{F_{\phi r}}=\frac{\rho u^{{\mathcal{P}}}}{B^{{\mathcal{P}}}}\,,

(2.4)

\displaystyle L\equiv

\displaystyle\mu u_{\phi}-\frac{\kappa B^{\phi}}{\eta}\,,\quad E\equiv-\mu u_{t}-\frac{\kappa B^{\phi}\Omega_{F}}{\eta}\,,

where
\mu\equiv h/\rho
denotes the specific enthalpy.
The existence of these conserved quantities
\{\Omega_{F},\eta,E,L\}
helps to simplify solving the flow structure, for a given stream function
\psi(r,\theta)
. In Appendix
A.3
, we present a concise derivation of these MHD conserved quantities.

2.2
The wind equation

The flow four-velocity can be solved by using the MHD conserved quantities.
Firstly, with the definition of
E
and
L
, the
t-
,
\phi-
components of the four-velocity can be rewritten as

\displaystyle u_{t}=\frac{E}{\mu}\frac{(g_{tt}+g_{t\phi}\Omega_{F})(1-\Omega_{F}l)+M_{A}^{2}}{k_{0}-M_{A}^{2}}\,,\quad u_{\phi}=\frac{E}{\mu}\frac{(g_{t\phi}+g_{\phi\phi}\Omega_{F})(1-\Omega_{F}l)-M_{A}^{2}l}{k_{0}-M_{A}^{2}}\,,

(2.5)

where
l\equiv L/E
denotes the angular momentum density. Here, we have also introduced a dimensionless Alfvénic Mach number in the poloidal plane
[
19
]
:

\displaystyle M_{A}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\mu\rho u_{p}^{2}}{B_{p}^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu u_{p}|\eta|}{B_{p}}}\,,

(2.6)

where
u_{p}\equiv\sqrt{u^{\mathcal{P}}u_{\mathcal{P}}}=\sqrt{u^{r}u_{r}+u^{\theta}u_{\theta}}
and
B_{p}\equiv\sqrt{B^{\mathcal{P}}B_{\mathcal{P}}}=\sqrt{B^{r}B_{r}+B^{\theta}B_{\theta}}
are the magnitudes of the poloidal velocity and poloidal magnetic field, respectively.

M_{A}
characterizes the ratio between the flow velocity and the propagation speed of magnetic disturbances in the poloidal plane
2
2

2

The second equality in Eq. (
2.6
) follows from
u_{p}/B_{p}=|u_{\mathcal{P}}/B_{\mathcal{P}}|
. Since
\eta
is signed to indicate the flow direction, we use
|\eta|
in defining the Alfvénic Mach number to ensure it remains a positive-definite quantity.
.
Combining Eqs. (
2.5
) with Eqs. (
2.3
), the toroidal component of the magnetic field is given by

\displaystyle B^{\phi}=\eta E\,\frac{(g_{tt}+g_{t\phi}\Omega_{F})l+(g_{t\phi}+g_{\phi\phi}\Omega_{F})}{\kappa(k_{0}-M_{A}^{2})}\,.

(2.7)

By substituting Eqs. (
2.5
) into the normalization condition
u_{\mu}u^{\mu}=-1
,
one can derive the relativistic Bernoulli equation (also called the wind equation) that governs the evolution of the poloidal velocity along the field lines.
Through ideal MHD condition,
u^{\theta}=B^{\theta}u^{r}/B^{r}
,
u^{r}=\pm u_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g_{rr}+g_{\theta\theta}(B^{\theta})^{2}/(B^{r})^{2}}}\right)^{-1/2}

The wind equation can be written as

\displaystyle u_{p}^{2}+1=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{E}{\mu}}}\right)^{2}\frac{k_{0}k_{2}-2k_{2}M_{A}^{2}-k_{4}M_{A}^{4}}{(k_{0}-M_{A}^{2})^{2}}\,,

(2.8)

where

\displaystyle k_{0}\equiv-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g_{\phi\phi}\Omega_{F}^{2}+2g_{t\phi}\Omega_{F}+g_{tt}}}\right)\,,\quad k_{2}\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\Omega_{F}l}}\right)^{2}\,,\quad k_{4}\equiv-\frac{g_{\phi\phi}+2g_{t\phi}l+g_{tt}l^{2}}{\kappa}\,.

(2.9)

Once the magnetic field and conserved quantities
\{\Omega_{F},\eta,E,L\}
are known,
u_{p}
(and
M_{A}
) is determined by solving Eq. (
2.8
), and
u^{t}
,
u^{\phi}
are determined by Eq. (
2.5
).
For plasma with finite temperature, the wind equation is complicated.
For example, for adiabatic ions, the enthalpy density is given by

h=\rho+\Gamma(\Gamma-1)^{-1}P
with
P\propto\rho^{\Gamma}
.
Since
\rho=|\eta|B_{p}/u_{p}
, the specific enthalpy
\mu=h/\rho
is a function of
u_{p}
along a given field line.
Substituting the equation of state into Eq. (
2.8
) leads to a non-integer power polynomial equation in
u_{p}
, which can only be solved numerically

]
.

However, in the cold limit
\mu\rightarrow 1

M_{A}=\sqrt{u_{p}|\eta|/B_{p}}
into Eq. (
2.8
) reduces it to a quartic equation in
u_{p}
. By analyzing the root behaviour of this equation, one can select the physically admissible solution of

. For a Kerr black hole, there are typically two real roots near the event horizon—one positive and one negative. Among them, only one root remains globally non-negative and smooth across the entire domain, as illustrated in Fig.

To further characterize the solution structure, we need to examine the critical behavior of the flow at the points where the flow velocity reached characteristic MHD wave speeds
[
21
]
.
Firstly, the denominator on the right-hand side of Eq. (
2.8
) vanishes at the so-called Alfvén point, where
M_{A}^{2}=k_{0}
. This point corresponds to the location where the poloidal velocity matches the Alfvén speed
u_{A}=B^{2}_{p}k_{0}(\mu\rho)^{-1}
, which characterizes the propagation speed of transverse magnetic disturbances along field lines.
Beyond this point,
u_{p}>u_{A}
, Alfvén waves are unable to propagate upstream. Mathematically, the Alfvén point constitutes a general critical point of the wind equation, where multiple roots of the equation coincide, as illustrated in Fig.
2
. At this critical point, both the numerator and denominator of the wind equation simultaneously vanish
[
58
]
.

In addition, two other critical points commonly arise in the MHD flow: the slow magnetosonic (SM) point and the fast magnetosonic (FM) point. These points correspond to distinct dynamical transitions and impose essential regularity conditions on the solution: the requirement that the flow passes smoothly through these critical points imposes constraints on the conserved quantities and plays a key role in selecting physically viable solutions. Further details regarding the properties of these MHD wave speeds and critical points are provided in Appendix

2.3
Asymptotic magnetosonic point

In this work, we adopt the assumption that the FM point of the jet outflow lies at spatial infinity. Then, FM waves can propagate freely throughout the jet interior.
Such treatment is appropriate for highly relativistic, magnetically dominated
jets, especially the BZ jets
[
59
]
.
By imposing cold ions at the FM point at infinity, we can obtain the asymptotic expression for
E
and
\eta
:

\displaystyle E=\gamma_{\infty}^{3}\,,\quad\eta=\frac{\Omega_{F}^{2}B_{p}x^{2}}{(\gamma_{\infty}^{2}-1)^{3/2}}\Bigg|_{r\rightarrow\infty}\,.

(2.10)

The first equation is the Michel’s minimum energy
[
60
,
61
]
, where the cubic dependence on
\gamma_{\infty}
arises from the relativistic conversion between magnetic and kinetic energy, and the volume compression.
Eqs. (
2.10
) are derived under the assumption of cold ions at infinity, without requiring the entire plasma flow to be cold. It provides an effective means to characterize plasma outflows along streamlines. Especially, for
u_{p}
determined by the wind equation under a given magnetic field, the expression of
\eta
in Eqs. (
2.10
) directly yields the ion number density
\rho=|\eta|B_{p}/u_{p}
.

We notice that the form of
\eta
in Eqs. (
2.10
) actually implies opened magnetic field lines with the asymptotic behaviour of
B_{p}\sim 1/x^{2}
.
The magnetic fields adopted in this work all satisfy such condition. Note that
d(B_{p}x^{2})/dx<0
is required for efficient MHD acceleration and the existence of a finite FM point
[
62
]
. As a result, for
B_{p}\sim 1/x^{2}
, the FM point is automatically pushed to infinity, and the system lacks a finite-radius transition where magnetic energy is fully converted into plasma kinetic energy.
This inefficiency can be reflected in the magnetization parameter,
defined as the ratio of
E_{\rm EM}\equiv E-E_{\rm m}
to
E_{\rm m}\equiv-\mu u_{t}

[
63
]
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{M}\equiv\frac{E_{\rm EM}}{E_{\rm m}}=\frac{\gamma_{\infty}^{3}+\mu u_{t}}{-\mu u_{t}}\,,

(2.11)

At the asymptotic FM point where the flow is cold, Eqs. (
2.5
) results in
u_{t}\rightarrow-\gamma_{\infty}
. Substituting this into Eq. (
2.11
) yields
\sigma_{M}\big|_{r\rightarrow\infty}\rightarrow\gamma_{\infty}^{2}-1
, revealing that a substantial fraction of the energy remains stored in the electromagnetic field at infinity. This is a direct consequence of the field line geometry and the absence of a finite FM point.
In this sense,
\sigma_{M}
acts as a diagnostic of acceleration efficiency: high magnetization indicates a magnetically dominated jet at spatial infinity.

From the asymptotic analysis of the FM point, Eq. (
2.7
) reduces to the following expression at large distances:

\displaystyle B^{\phi}\approx-\,\frac{\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma_{\infty}^{2}-1}}\right)E}{\gamma_{\infty}^{2}\Omega_{F}\kappa}\rightarrow-\frac{\Omega_{F}\gamma_{\infty}B_{p}}{(\gamma_{\infty}^{2}-1)^{1/2}}\,.

(2.12)

The asymptotic toroidal field magnitude is
\sqrt{B_{\phi}B^{\phi}}\sim\sqrt{g_{\phi\phi}}B_{p}\rightarrow xB_{p}
. Therefore, the magnetic field must take a toroidal shape at large distances.
Under the limit
\gamma_{\infty}\gg 1
, we recover the result for the FFE, namely
\kappa\Omega_{F}B^{\phi}\rightarrow-\eta E

[
57
]
.

3
Jet launching

3.1
The launching surface

The MHD flow is loaded through a dense plasma layer near the black hole and driven outward by magnetic forces
[
26
,
64
]
. Here, we adopt a simplified model with a geometrically thin, axisymmetric launching surface (denoted by “LS”)
[
21
]
, where the poloidal outflow velocity is zero
[
19
]
.

Through Eqs. (
2.3
), the regularity of
B^{\phi}
at the launching surface with
u_{p}|_{\rm LS}\rightarrow 0
requires the plasma flow to be corotate with the magnetic field,
u^{\phi}|_{\rm LS}=\Omega_{F}u^{t}|_{\rm LS}
.
From the definition Eqs. (
2.4
), we obtain a constraint for
E,L
:

\displaystyle\Omega_{F}L=E-\mu\sqrt{k_{0}}\Big|_{\rm LS}\,.

(3.1)

Since the flow four-velocity is always timelike, the launching surface must lie within the light cylinder.

The constraint of the launching surface location can be found by expanding the MHD conservation law (or equivalently the acceleration equation) near
u_{p}=0
.
To do this, we assume the cold jet flow for simplicity (for the thermal jet, see Appendix
C
). In the cold limit, the SM point lies on the launching surface.
Expanding the acceleration equation in terms of
u_{p}
yields

\displaystyle u_{p}^{\prime}\approx k_{0}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{B_{p}^{2}E^{2}k_{0}k_{2}}{2X}\frac{1}{u_{p}}+\frac{3B_{p}E^{4}k_{2}(k_{2}+k_{0}k_{4})\eta^{3}}{2X^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u_{p}}}\right)}}\right)\Bigg|_{\approx\rm LS}\,,

(3.2)

where
X=B_{p}^{2}k_{0}^{3}+E^{2}(k_{2}+k_{0}k_{4})\eta^{2}
. Since Eq. (
3.1
) implies
k_{2}=k_{0}E^{-2}
and
k_{0}>0
within the light cylinder, the launching surface must satisfy
k_{0}^{\prime}=0
.
Moreover, if an outflow/inflow is to be launched from rest,
u_{p}^{\prime}
must be positive/negative near the launching surface. Since
k_{2}+k_{0}k_{4}=\kappa^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[g_{t\phi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+l\Omega_{F}}}\right)+g_{tt}l+g_{\phi\phi}\Omega_{F}}}\right]^{2}\geq 0
always holds
[
21
]
, we have the launching condition
k_{0}^{\prime}\lessgtr 0
for outflow/inflow, indicating that the launching surface should be the local maximum point of
k_{0}
.

The above discussion for the launching surface is universal. When we turn to different types of jets, the difference lies in the specific location of the launching surface and the boundary condition there.
In our work, the BZ and BP jets are distinguished by the topology of their magnetic fields. Once the field geometry is chosen, the field angular velocity
\Omega_{F}
is determined by the boundary conditions—either at the black hole horizon or at the disk.

For simplicity, we adopt the cold limit in the following discussion. As demonstrated in previous studies, the ions within the accretion system in AGNs are highly non-relativistic,
and thus the thermal effects do not significantly influence the jet dynamics
[
65
,
66
]
.

3.2
BZ process

In the BZ jet, the magnetic field is captured by the black hole, and the plasma exhibit inward motion as it approaches the event horizon. Thus, there is a region that separates the inflow from the outflow, where the poloidal velocity goes to zero.
In the ideal MHD framework, the separation is characterized by a two-dimensional surface and coincides with the launching surface
[
25
,
13
]
.
The differences in conserved quantities between the inflow and outflow satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\eta_{\rm out}E_{\rm out}-\eta_{\rm in}E_{\rm in})&=-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta_{\rm out}-\eta_{\rm in}}}\right)u_{t}\big|_{\rm LS}\,,\\
(\eta_{\rm out}L_{\rm out}-\eta_{\rm in}L_{\rm in})&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta_{\rm out}-\eta_{\rm in}}}\right)u_{\phi}\big|_{\rm LS}\,,\end{aligned}

(3.3)

where we use the subscript ‘‘in’’ ‘‘out’’ to label the quantities of the inflow and outflow,
respectively
3
3

3

The equations in Eqs. (
3.3
) are not independent;
the second can be derived by combining the first with Eq. (
3.1
).
.
Therefore,
\eta
,

, and
L
are conserved separately within the inflow and outflow, but are different across the launching surface. In contrast, the magnetic field and flow velocity remain continuous across the launching surface.

The acceleration of the outflow is easily determined by demanding an asymptotic FM point (see Sec.
2.3
). Specifically,
E_{\rm out}
,
\eta_{\rm out}
are given by the asymptotic relation Eqs. (
2.10
), and
L_{\rm out}
is fixed by the relation Eq. (
3.1
) at the launching surface.
However, the inflow possesses finite FM point near the black hole, whose acceleration is more complicated.
The existence of the inflow FM point can be understood as follows: the FM speed is the fastest propagation speed of disturbances in a magnetized fluid. Beyond the FM point, no disturbances can propagate from the downstream to the upstream. When sufficiently close to the event horizon, it is inevitable that no signal can propagate outward. Therefore, the FM point must occur outside the horizon (a detailed analysis can be found in
[
21
]
).

To determine the inflow dynamics, we need to find the location of the FM point,
r_{\rm FM}
, and the corresponding conserved quantities.
This is achieved by combining the matching conditions, Eqs. (
3.3
), between the inflow and outflow, with the relation of
E

and
\eta
at the FM point and the regularity condition that ensures the acceleration equation remains finite there.
As a result, there are five equations available to determine five quantities
\eta_{\rm in},E_{\rm in},L_{\rm in}
,
u_{\rm FM}

and
r_{\rm FM}
.

3.3
BP process

The BP jet originates from outflows launched at the surface of the accretion disk, where plasma is supplied along open or collimated magnetic field lines. For an ideal MHD accretion flow that is reflection-symmetric about the equatorial plane and exhibits vanishing polar velocity (i.e., zero velocity in the
\theta
-direction), the following boundary conditions hold:

B^{r}\big|_{\theta=\pi/2}=E^{\theta}\big|_{\theta=\pi/2}=0
. If a nonzero poloidal magnetic field component is present at the equatorial plane, i.e.,
B^{\theta}\big|_{\theta=\pi/2}\neq 0
, then the field lines are frozen into the disk
[
31
]
, implying that

\displaystyle\Omega_{F}=\Omega_{\rm disk}\,,

(3.4)

where
\Omega_{\rm disk}
is the angular velocity of the disk. In this case, the field lines are anchored in the disk and corotate with it.
In the cold MHD limit, the condition for jet launching is determined by
k_{0}^{\prime}=B^{r}\partial_{r}k_{0}+B^{\theta}\partial_{\theta}k_{0}=0
.
Using
\Omega_{F}^{\prime}=0
, this condition becomes

\displaystyle k_{0}^{\prime}=-B^{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{r}g_{tt}+2\Omega_{F}\partial_{r}g_{t\phi}+\Omega_{F}^{2}\partial_{r}g_{\phi\phi}}}\right)-B^{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{\theta}g_{tt}+2\Omega_{F}\partial_{\theta}g_{t\phi}+\Omega_{F}^{2}\partial_{\theta}g_{\phi\phi}}}\right)\,.

(3.5)

Interestingly, since the launching surface coincides with the disk surface, Eq. (
3.5
) imposes a constraint on the disk height for a given
\psi
and
\Omega_{F}
. Conversely, for a specified
\psi
and disk height, Eq. (
3.5
) determines the corresponding value of
\Omega_{F}
.
Note that due to the reflection symmetry of the spacetime about the equatorial plane,
\partial_{\theta}g_{\mu\nu}\big|_{\theta=\pi/2}=0
. Hence,
k_{0}^{\prime}=0
is automatically satisfied at the equatorial plane, although this does not necessarily correspond to a local maximum of
k_{0}
.

For a geometrically thin disk, the jet launching surface lies close to the equatorial plane, and
\partial_{\theta}g_{\mu\nu}\big|_{LS}\approx\partial_{\theta}g_{\mu\nu}\big|_{\theta=\pi/2}=0
. In this regime, the launching condition simplifies to
0=k_{0}^{\prime}\big|_{\rm LS}\approx-B^{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{r}g_{tt}+2\Omega_{F}\partial_{r}g_{t\phi}+\Omega_{F}^{2}\partial_{r}g_{\phi\phi}}}\right)\big|_{\rm LS}
.
Hence, if
B^{r}\big|_{\rm LS}=0
, this condition is trivially satisfied. Otherwise, for
B^{r}\big|_{\rm LS}\neq 0
, solving the equation yields the angular velocity of the field-lines required for jet launching:

\displaystyle\Omega_{F}=\frac{-\partial_{r}g_{t\phi}+\sqrt{(\partial_{r}g_{t\phi})^{2}-\partial_{r}g_{tt}\,\partial_{r}g_{\phi\phi}}}{\partial_{r}g_{\phi\phi}}\Bigg|_{\rm LS}\,,

(3.6)

which corresponds precisely to the Keplerian angular velocity.
In summary, for magnetic field lines threading a thin accretion disk, jet launching is permitted under two distinct conditions: (1) the magnetic field is perpendicular to both the equatorial plane and the disk surface, or (2) the disk rotates at the Keplerian angular velocity.

5
Hybrid jet model

5.1
Set up

In the preceding sections, we analyzed the flow structures along magnetic field lines that thread either the horizon or the disk independently.
In realistic black hole magnetospheres, the global magnetic field is generated and sustained by the inflowing plasma. As the accretion flow advects inward toward the event horizon, it drags magnetic field lines along with it. These field lines are subsequently anchored to the spinning black hole on horizon scales. As a result, magnetic field lines can simultaneously thread both the event horizon and the accretion disk, enabling the coexistence of the BZ and BP processes
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
44
]
.
It is evident that the magnetic field line structure must remain continuous across the boundary between the BZ and BP regions. More importantly, collimation is not limited to the BZ jet; it can also occur within the BP region near the horizon, driven by the rapid rotation of magnetic field lines
[
16
]
.

In this section, we present a hybrid jet model that incorporates both horizon-threading and disk-threading magnetic field lines. To maintain analytical tractability, we adopt the parabolic stream function introduced in Eq. (
4.1
), which generates smoothly collimated field lines extending from the event horizon to large distances. The field lines with
\psi\leq r_{+}
intersect the horizon and define the BZ region, while those with
\psi\geq r_{+}
intersect the equatorial plane outside the horizon, corresponding to the BP region.
It is important to note that, as discussed in Sec.
3.3
, magnetic field lines must be orthogonal to the equatorial plane. However, since our focus is on the flow just above the disk surface—slightly offset from the equatorial plane—the magnetic field can acquire a finite radial component, thereby enabling magneto-centrifugal acceleration (as illustrated in Fig.
1
). This justifies the use of the parabolic field configuration as an approximate representation of the BP region.

In the BZ region, we adopt the field-line angular velocity given in Eq. (
4.3
), which captures the interaction between the rotating black hole and the magnetic field. In the BP region, we assume
\Omega_{F}=\Omega_{K}
outside the ISCO, allowing for jet launching near the equatorial plane, as discussed in Sec.
3.3
.
We emphasize that this is a simplified prescription, based on the assumption of a geometrically thin, ideal MHD accretion disk with a correspondingly thin launching surface. In more realistic scenarios, sub-Keplerian flows are expected to develop near the equatorial plane. These flows are typically described in a phenomenological manner and will be investigated in future work.

\sigma_{M}
higher inside the light cylinder. This leads to a characteristic flow structure shaped by the light cylinder: slower speed and higher magnetization near the jet boundaries, and faster speed with lower magnetization near the BZ–BP interface.

For the density profile, the hybrid jet possesses a higher density near the center, which gradually decreases toward the outer boundaries. The density of the inflow is much higher than that of the outflow, which can be attributed to the strong gravitational capture of particles by the black hole within the loading region. If a geometrically thick loading zone, rather than the idealized thin launching surface assumed here, is taken into account, the density contrast between the inflow and outflow may be mitigated.

A distinct feature arises at the interface between the BZ and BP jets, where the poloidal velocity, magnetization, and density exhibit discontinuous behavior. This arises from a mismatch in the rotation of magnetic field lines across the interface. Specifically, the rotation of field lines threading the black hole horizon is governed primarily by the rotation of the event horizon itself, establishing a characteristic relationship between the field-line angular velocity
\Omega_{F}
and the black hole angular velocity
\Omega_{H}
. Under the FFE approximation, efficient energy extraction requires
\Omega_{F}\approx\Omega_{H}/2

[
40
]
.
Although the inertia of ions can retard the rotation of magnetic field lines, it is generally insufficient to overcome the black hole’s dominant influence—a conclusion supported by dynamical simulations
[
15

]
. In contrast, the field lines anchored in the accretion disk corotate with the disk plasma. As they approach the event horizon, the rotation of any equatorial disk configuration asymptotically matches that of the black hole, leading to
\Omega_{F}\rightarrow\Omega_{H}

[

. Consequently, near the interface, the field-line angular velocities satisfy
\Omega_{F}|_{\rm BP}\simeq 2\Omega_{F}|_{\rm BZ}\rightarrow\Omega_{H}
. In our model with spin parameter

\Omega_{F}=0.1287

\Omega_{F}=0.3134
.

5.3
Asymptotic density profile

As a next step toward understanding the role of the BZ–BP hybrid, we turn to examine the asymptotic behavior of plasma density.
Starting from the asymptotic expression for the mass flux Eq. (
2.10

\rho=\eta B_{p}/u_{p}\approx(\gamma_{\infty}^{2}-1)^{-2}\Omega_{F}^{2}B_{p}^{2}x^{2}

Using the asymptotic scaling relations for a parabolic stream function,
B_{p}\approx 2\psi x^{-2}

z\approx(2\psi)^{-1}x^{2}

\rho\approx 2(\gamma_{\infty}^{2}-1)^{-2}\psi\,\Omega_{F}^{2}z^{-1}

Accordingly, for a given height

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm eff}\equiv z\rho\,\Big|_{z\rightarrow\infty}=\frac{2\psi\,\Omega_{F}^{2}}{(\gamma_{\infty}^{2}-1)^{2}}\,.

(5.1)

6
Summary and discussion

In this work, we investigated steady, axisymmetric, ideal MHD jets by developing a unified framework that encompasses both black hole–driven and disk-driven outflows. The analysis begins with prescribed magnetic field geometries—either threading the event horizon or anchored in the accretion disk—and proceeds by solving the relativistic Bernoulli equation along each field line, accompanied by a critical-point analysis. This approach enables a detailed examination of jet acceleration and energy conversion processes under ideal MHD conditions. Based on the conservation laws of ideal MHD, we derived a general criterion for the successful launching of cold outflows, applicable to both BZ and BP type jets.
This framework enables a tractable, semi-analytic investigation of jet launching, acceleration, and energy conversion processes.

Furthermore, we proposed a hybrid jet model based on a global parabolic magnetic field configuration that smoothly connects the BZ and BP regions.
The model naturally gives rise to a velocity shear and density discontinuity at the BZ–BP interface, resulting from the discontinuity in field-line angular velocity across the transition. We illustrated these features through poloidal velocity and density profiles, and we further demonstrated that the light cylinder plays a key role in shaping the poloidal flow structure.

We quantitatively analyzed the influence of field-line angular velocity and terminal Lorentz factor on jet structure by examining the effective density, defined as
\rho_{\rm eff}=2\psi\Omega_{F}^{2}/(\gamma_{\infty}^{2}-1)^{2}
.
To explore the impact of different distributions of
\gamma_{\infty}(\psi)
, we employed synthetic profiles modeled by multi-peaked Gaussians.
In all cases,
\rho_{\rm eff}
exhibits a pronounced discontinuity across the BZ–BP interface, driven by the jump in
\Omega_{F}
, and features a vacuum-like spine near the polar axis. A distinct peak in
\rho_{\rm eff}
typically emerges in the inner disk region. These findings may provide insight into observed edge-brightened jet morphologies and transverse stratification.
Moreover, the sharp transition between the BZ and BP regions identified in the model may act as a favorable origin for MHD instabilities—such as magnetic reconnection—thereby potentially enabling non-thermal particle acceleration.

Looking ahead, the hybrid model can be further developed by solving the G–S equation under appropriate boundary conditions, which would yield a more realistic terminal Lorentz factor profile,
\gamma_{\infty}(\psi)
.
In addition, since the light cylinder serves as a critical surface for jet dynamics, a detailed investigation of the flow structure in its vicinity is essential. To more accurately capture the physics of jet launching, it is necessary to incorporate a finite-thickness mass-loading region in future work.

Recent advances in GRMHD simulations and polarized radiative transfer modeling have substantially improved our understanding of black hole imaging and jet formation mechanisms
[
78
,
79
,
44
,
80
,
81
]
. The detection of ordered magnetic fields and synchrotron polarization in M87*
[
82
,
83
]
, together with growing efforts in polarimetric jet modeling
[
76
,
59
,
84
]
, has motivated the development of jet models that connect MHD theory to observable structures on scales of hundreds of gravitational radii
[
85
,
86
]
.
The hybrid BZ–BP model proposed in this work describes the transition from the magnetically dominated jet base to the asymptotic outflow at large distances. The resulting jet morphology can be computed and used to generate synthetic images, enabling direct comparisons with GRMHD simulations and high-resolution observations in future.

Appendix A
MHD conservation laws

In this section, we present a detailed analysis of the stationary, axisymmetric magnetic field, and derive the explicit forms of the conserved quantities.

A.1
Field transformation

In the main text, we follow the convention commonly adopted in the literature and introduce the “pseudo-electromagnetic fields”, which are defined as

\displaystyle E^{\mu}=(dt)_{\nu}F^{\mu\nu}=F^{t\mu}\,,\quad B^{\mu}=-(dt)_{\nu}(\star F)^{\mu\nu}=-(\star F)^{t\mu}\,.

(A.1)

The pseudo-fields differ from the fields measured by
the normal observer (given by the tangent vector

n_{\mu}=(-\alpha,0,0,0)
) by a lapse function
\alpha=(-g^{tt})^{-1/2}

[
87
]
.
While the pseudo-fields do not represent the physical fields directly, they are widely used in GRMHD analyses due to their computational convenience.
For ideal MHD, we have
F^{\mu\nu}u_{\nu}=0
, and the transformation between the pseudo-fields and the comoving-frame magnetic field,
b^{\mu}=-u_{\nu}(\star{F})^{\mu\nu}
, is given by

\displaystyle E^{\mu}=F^{t\mu}=-\epsilon^{t\mu\alpha\beta}b_{\alpha}u_{\beta}\,,\quad B^{\mu}=b^{[\mu}u^{t]}\,,

(A.2)

\displaystyle b^{t}=B^{\mu}u_{\mu}\,,\quad u^{t}b^{i}=B^{i}+B^{t}u^{i}\,,

(A.3)

where
i
represents the spatial indices.

A.2
Stationary, axisymmetric magnetic field

In a stationary and axisymmetric system, physical quantities vary only in the
(r,\theta)
plane—denoted as the poloidal plane.
In this section, we would like to briefly review the derivation of the field structure by imposing symmetry requirements and the ideal MHD condition.
Since the electromagnetic tensor only depends on
r,\theta
, the azimuthal electric field vanishes,
E^{\phi}=\kappa^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{\phi}A_{t}-\partial_{t}A_{\phi}}}\right)=0
, where
\kappa\equiv g^{2}_{t\phi}-g_{tt}g_{\phi\phi}
. Employing the ideal MHD condition in the
t
and
\phi
directions, we obtain

\displaystyle E^{{\mathcal{P}}}u_{{\mathcal{P}}}=0\,,\quad B^{r}u^{\theta}-B^{\theta}u^{r}=0\,.

(A.4)

The first equation implies that the electric field is orthogonal to the plasma velocity in the poloidal plane. The second equation shows that the poloidal magnetic field is exactly aligned with the poloidal velocity, i.e., the magnetic field vector lies along the direction of fluid motion in the
(r,\theta)
plane. This alignment is a direct consequence of the infinite conductivity assumption in ideal MHD.
The exceptional case is
u^{r}=u^{\theta}=0
, where the electromagnetic field cannot be determined by ideal MHD alone and additional conditions are required.
Using the second equation in Eq. (
A.4
), we have

\displaystyle B^{r}=\frac{\partial_{\theta}A_{\phi}}{\sqrt{-g}}\,,\quad B^{\theta}=-\frac{\partial_{r}A_{\phi}}{\sqrt{-g}}\,,\quad u^{\mathcal{P}}\partial_{\mathcal{P}}A_{\phi}=0\,,

(A.5)

where
g
is the determinant of the spacetime metric. We see that
A_{\phi}
is conserved along the fluid streamlines. In fact,
A_{\phi}(r,\theta)
marks the magnetic flux enclosed by a circular loop of radius
r
and polar angle
\theta
, and commonly referred to as the stream function
\psi\equiv A_{\phi}(r,\theta)
.
The stream function serves as a label for magnetic field lines and plays a central role in describing the field geometry.

The ideal MHD condition in the poloidal directions leads to

\displaystyle g_{rr}u^{t}E^{r}=g^{tt}u^{\theta}F_{r\theta}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g^{tt}u^{\phi}-g^{t\phi}u^{t}}}\right)F_{r\phi}\,,

\displaystyle g_{\theta\theta}u^{t}E^{\theta}=g^{tt}u^{r}F_{\theta r}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g^{tt}u^{\phi}-g^{t\phi}u^{t}}}\right)F_{\theta\phi}\,.

(A.6)

Multiply the first equation of Eq. (
A.2
) by
B^{r}
, and the second equation by
B^{\theta}
, then add the two resulting equations together to obtain

\displaystyle E^{\mathcal{P}}B_{\mathcal{P}}=0\,,

(A.7)

meaning that the pseudo-electric field and pseudo-magnetic field are orthogonal within the poloidal plane. Combining Eq. (
A.7
) with the index lowering relation
g^{tt}F_{t{\mathcal{P}}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g_{{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{P}}}E^{\mathcal{P}}+g^{t\phi}F_{{\mathcal{P}}\phi}}}\right)
, we can deduce
F_{t\theta}F_{r\phi}=F_{tr}F_{\theta\phi}
. Hence, we can introduce the following field-line angular velocity:

\displaystyle\Omega_{F}\equiv\frac{F_{t{\mathcal{P}}}}{F_{{\mathcal{P}}\phi}}\,,\quad\mathcal{P}=r,\theta\,,

(A.8)

representing the global rotation of the magnetic field line.
By substituting Eq. (
A.8
) in the Bianchi identity in the
\phi
direction, we arrive at
F_{r\phi}\partial_{\theta}\Omega_{F}-F_{\theta\phi}\partial_{r}\Omega_{F}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{\theta}F_{r\phi}-\partial_{r}F_{\theta\phi}}}\right)\Omega_{F}=0
.
Since
F_{{\mathcal{P}}\phi}=\partial_{{\mathcal{P}}}\psi
, the last term on the left-hand side of the equation vanishes automatically. Therefore, we have
B^{\mathcal{P}}\partial_{\mathcal{P}}\Omega_{F}=0
, indicating that
\Omega_{F}
is conserved along a field line within the poloidal plane.

The field-line angular velocity is induced by the dragging from the rotating black hole and the accretion disk. For horizon-threading field lines,
\Omega_{F}
is constrained by the regularity condition at the event horizon, known as the Znajek condition
[
40
]
. In contrast, for field lines anchored in the disk
\Omega_{F}
is primarily determined by the local angular velocity of the accretion flow
[
41
]
.
Additionally, the pseudo-electric field can be rewritten as

\displaystyle E^{{\mathcal{P}}}=-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{g^{{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{P}}}\sqrt{-g}}{g^{tt}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{F}-\frac{g^{t\phi}}{g^{tt}}}}\right)\tilde{\epsilon}_{{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}}B^{{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}}\,.

(A.9)

It becomes clear that the pseudo-electric field is induced by both the field line rotation and the spacetime frame dragging, represented by
g^{t\phi}
.

A.3
The MHD conserved quantities

In stationary, axisymmetric ideal MHD, in addition to the stream function
\psi
and field line angular velocity
\Omega_{F}
, the conservation of energy-momentum and particle number
also yield three conserved quantities along field lines
[
56
]
.
Firstly, the ideal MHD condition in the
\phi
direction yields:

u^{r}F_{r\phi}+u^{\theta}F_{\theta\phi}=0
. This allows us to define the mass flux
\eta
as

\displaystyle\eta\equiv\frac{\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{r}}{F_{\theta\phi}}=\frac{\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{\theta}}{F_{\phi r}}=\frac{\rho u^{{\mathcal{P}}}}{B^{{\mathcal{P}}}}\,,

(A.10)

which describes the particle number flux per unit magnetic flux.
Substituting Eq.(
A.8
) into Eq.(
A.10
), we obtain

F_{tr}=-\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{\theta}\Omega_{F}\eta^{-1}
and
F_{t\theta}=\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{r}\Omega_{F}\eta^{-1}
. Then, applying the Bianchi identity in the
\phi
direction,
\partial_{r}F_{\theta t}+\partial_{\theta}F_{tr}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathcal{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{{\mathcal{P}}}\Omega_{F}\eta^{-1}}}\right)=\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{{\mathcal{P}}}\partial_{\mathcal{P}}(\Omega_{F}\eta^{-1})=0\,,

(A.11)

where we have used the particle number conservation,
\nabla_{\mu}(\rho u^{\mu})=\partial_{\mathcal{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{-g}\rho u^{{\mathcal{P}}}}}\right)/\sqrt{-g}=0
.
Since
\Omega_{F}
is conserved along field lines, we have
B^{\mathcal{P}}\partial_{\mathcal{P}}\eta=u^{\mathcal{P}}\partial_{P}\eta=0
, which means that
\eta
is also a conserved mass flux along field lines.
Furthermore, with the help of field-line angular velocity and mass flux, the energy conservation can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}_{\,\,\ t}=&\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mathcal{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sqrt{-g}(hu^{{\mathcal{P}}}u_{t}+F^{{\mathcal{P}}\rho}F_{t\rho})}}\right]\\
=&\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\partial_{\theta}\psi\,\partial_{r}(\eta\mu u_{t}+\kappa B^{\phi}\Omega_{F})-\partial_{r}\psi\,\partial_{\theta}(\eta\mu u_{t}+\kappa B^{\phi}\Omega_{F})}}\right]\\
=&\,B^{\mathcal{P}}\partial_{\mathcal{P}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\eta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu u_{t}+\frac{\kappa B^{\phi}\Omega_{F}}{\eta}}}\right)}}\right]\,.\end{aligned}

(A.12)

Through the same procedure, one can get a similar equation for the angular momentum conservation

\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}_{\,\,\ \phi}=0
. As a result, the specific energy and spacific angular momentum

\displaystyle E\equiv-\mu u_{t}-\frac{\kappa B^{\phi}\Omega_{F}}{\eta}\,,\quad L\equiv\mu u_{\phi}-\frac{\kappa B^{\phi}}{\eta}

(A.13)

are two conserved quantities along a field line, i.e.,
B^{\mathcal{P}}\partial_{\mathcal{P}}E=B^{\mathcal{P}}\partial_{\mathcal{P}}L=0
.

Therefore, for a stationary and axisymmetric ideal MHD system, we have five conserved quantities:
\{\psi,\Omega_{F},\eta,E,L\}
, each of which is associated with a underlying symmetry of the MHD system. The stream function
\psi
specifies the shape of magnetic field lines (as well as the streamlines) in the poloidal plane;
\Omega_{F},\eta,E,L
are invariant along each field line.

Appendix B
Critical points

In Sec.
2.2
, we briefly outlined the existence of three critical points in the wind equation, and in Sec.
2.3
, we discussed how the asymptotic FM point constrains the conserved quantities. In this section, we provide a detailed analysis of the properties and roles of these critical points.

B.1
The Alfvén point

The Alfvén point is defined as the location where the denominator on the right-hand side of Eq. (
2.8
) vanishes, marking the transition at which the poloidal plasma velocity equals the Alfvén speed:

\displaystyle u_{p}^{2}=u_{A}^{2}\equiv\frac{B^{2}_{p}k_{0}}{\mu\rho}\bigg|_{A}\,,

(B.1)

whrere the subscript “A” denotes the Alfvén point. Since the wind equation must remain regular at the Alfvén point, the numerator in Eq. (
2.8
) must also vanish at this location:

\displaystyle(k_{0}k_{2}-2k_{2}M_{A}^{2}-k_{4}M_{A}^{4})\big|_{A}=-k_{0}(k_{4}k_{0}+k_{2})\big|_{A}=0\,.

(B.2)

This condition determines the location of the Alfvén point along each streamline, forming a two-dimensional surface (a curve in the poloidal plane).
For a monopole-like magnetic field in flat spacetime, the Alfvén point is given by
x_{A}^{2}=\Omega_{F}L/E
, which is often treated as a prescribed input parameter
[
19
,
22
,
23
]
.
In contrast, we do not fix the Alfvén point a priori. Instead, we specify the set of conserved quantities and require that the flow pass smoothly through all critical points.

The requirement that
u_{t}
and
u_{\phi}
(given by Eqs. (
2.5
)) remain regular at the Alfvén point, imposes the following constraint on the specific angular momentum:

\displaystyle l=-\frac{g_{t\phi}+g_{\phi\phi}\Omega_{F}}{g_{tt}+g_{t\phi}\Omega_{F}}\bigg|_{A}\,,

(B.3)

indicating that the value of
l
is fixed by the location of the Alfvén point.
Furthermore, the Alfvén point constrains the sign of the specific energy
E
of the plasma.
To clarify this, we express the specific energy in the form

\displaystyle E=-k_{0}^{-1}(g_{tt}+\Omega_{F}g_{t\phi})(E-\Omega_{F}L)\big|_{r_{A}}\,.

(B.4)

Given that
E-\Omega_{F}L=\sqrt{k_{0}}\big|_{\rm LS}
, the sign of
E
determined by the sign of the metric combination
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g_{tt}+\Omega_{F}g_{t\phi}}}\right)\big|_{r_{A}}
.

The possibility of negative specific energy for inflowing plasma is intrinsically tied to the structure of the black hole ergosphere. In a rotating black hole spacetime, the ergosphere admits regions where
(g_{tt}+\Omega_{F}g_{t\phi})\big|_{r_{A}}>0
, thereby allowing for
E<0
.
To make this explicit, we define a critical surface
r=r_{\rm C}(\theta)
at which
g_{tt}+\Omega_{F}g_{t\phi}=0
. It can be shown that
r_{\rm C}(\theta)
always lies within the ergosphere (where
g_{tt}=0
), and this critical surface exists only when
|\Omega_{H}|>|\Omega_{F}|>0
and
\text{sign}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{H}}}\right)=\text{sign}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{F}}}\right)

[
21
]
. Therefore, if the Alfvén point lies inside the critical surface, then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g_{tt}+\Omega_{F}g_{t\phi}}}\right)\big|_{r_{A}}>0
, leading to a negative
E
for the inflow. Such flows correspond to the extraction of energy from the black hole via the MHD Penrose process
[
72
]
.

B.2
The acceleration equation

In addition to the Alfvén point, additional critical points can be identified by analyzing the flow acceleration equation.
Differentiating Eq. (
2.8

\displaystyle(\ln{u_{p}})^{\prime}=\frac{N}{D}=\frac{N}{D_{\rm A}D_{\rm SF}}\,,

(B.5)

where

\displaystyle N=

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-(1+u_{p}^{2})(k_{0}-M_{A}^{2})^{3}\frac{a_{s}^{2}}{1-a_{s}^{2}}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{E}{\mu}}}\right)^{2}M_{A}^{4}(k_{0}k_{4}+k_{2})}}\right](\ln{B_{p}})^{\prime}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2(1-a_{s}^{2})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{E}{\mu}}}\right)^{2}[M_{A}^{4}(k_{0}-M_{A}^{2})k^{\prime}_{4}+(k_{0}k_{2}-3k_{2}M_{A}^{2}-2k_{4}M_{A}^{4})k_{0}^{\prime}]\,,

\displaystyle D_{\rm A}=

\displaystyle(k_{0}-M_{A}^{2})^{2}=(\frac{\mu\rho}{B_{p}^{2}})^{2}(u_{p}^{2}-u_{A}^{2})^{2}\,,

\displaystyle D_{\rm SF}=

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(u_{p}^{2}-c_{s}^{2})(k_{0}-M_{A}^{2})+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{E}{\mu}}}\right)^{2}M_{A}^{4}\frac{(k_{0}k_{4}+k_{2})}{(k_{0}-M_{A}^{2})^{2}}}}\right]=-\frac{\mu\rho}{B_{p}^{2}}(u_{p}^{2}-u_{FM}^{2})(u_{p}^{2}-u_{SM}^{2})\,.

Here, we have introduced the sound velocity

\displaystyle c_{s}^{2}=\frac{a_{s}^{2}}{1-a_{s}^{2}}\,,\quad a_{s}^{2}=\frac{\partial\ln{h}}{\partial\ln{n}}\,.

(B.7)

In Eq. (
B.5

takes one of the following characteristic speeds:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} u_{A}^{2}&\equiv\frac{B^{2}_{p}}{\mu\rho}k_{0}\,,\\
u_{F}^{2}&\equiv\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u_{0}^{2}+c_{s}^{2}}}\right)+\sqrt{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u_{0}^{2}+c_{s}^{2}}}\right)^{2}-4c_{s}^{2}u_{A}^{2}}}}\right]\,,\\
u_{S}^{2}&\equiv\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u_{0}^{2}+c_{s}^{2}}}\right)-\sqrt{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u_{0}^{2}+c_{s}^{2}}}\right)^{2}-4c_{s}^{2}u_{A}^{2}}}}\right]\,,\end{aligned}

(B.8)

representing the Alfvén speed, fast magnetosonic (FM) speed, and slow magnetosonic (SM) speed, respectively, where
u_{0}^{2}=u_{A}^{2}+\frac{\kappa(B^{\phi})^{2}}{\mu\rho}
. The FM speed represents the maximum propagation speed of compressive MHD waves, incorporating both thermal and magnetic contributions. In contrast, the SM speed corresponds to the lower-speed branch of compressive modes.

Each critical point along the flow corresponds to a distinct dynamical transition. The SM point typically arises near the jet base (the loading zone) and marks the onset of acceleration, where the action of plasma pressure and magnetic forces overcomes gravitational confinement. The FM point, by contrast, defines the outermost critical surface; crossing this point signifies that the flow has become causally disconnected from the central engine and has reached its terminal acceleration regime.

The requirement of smooth transitions across critical points imposes regularity conditions, which translate into algebraic constraints on the conserved quantities of the flow. In the cold plasma limit (
c_{s}^{2}=0
), the FM speed reduces to
u_{F}=u_{0}
and the SM speed becomes zero.
Thus, the smoothness condition at the SM point is set by the boundary conditions at the launching surface. In contrast, at the FM point, smooth crossing requires both the numerator and denominator of Eq. (
B.5
) to vanish simultaneously. This selects the physically admissible solutions.

B.3
The asymptotic FM point

At the FM point,
by combining the wind equation Eq. (
2.8
) with the condition
u_{p}=u_{F}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle E^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu^{2}(1+c_{s}^{2})(k_{0}-M_{A}^{2})^{3}}{k_{0}^{2}k_{2}-3k_{0}k_{2}M_{A}^{2}+3k_{2}M_{A}^{4}+k_{4}M_{A}^{6}}\,\Bigg|_{F}\,,

(B.9)

\displaystyle\eta^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{B_{p}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{c_{s}^{2}}{M_{A}^{4}}+\frac{(1+c_{s}^{2})(k_{0}k_{4}+k_{2})}{k_{0}^{2}k_{2}-3k_{0}k_{2}M_{A}^{2}+3k_{2}M_{A}^{4}+k_{4}M_{A}^{6}}}}\right)^{-1}\Bigg|_{F}\,,

(B.10)

where the Mach number reduces to
M_{A}^{2}\rightarrow\mu|\eta|u_{F}B_{p}^{-1}\big|_{F}
.
Together with the regularity condition
N|_{F}=0
, these equations constrain both the location of the FM point and the values of
E
and
\eta
.

To characterize the outflow at large distances, we adopt cylindrical coordinates defined by
x\equiv r\sin\theta
,
z\equiv r\cos\theta
.
We assume a cold plasma at infinity, such that
P=0
,
\mu=1
, and
c_{s}^{2}=0
.
Under this condition, the Mach number at infinity (
x\to\infty
) becomes

\displaystyle M_{A}^{2}\bigg|_{F}=k_{0}+\kappa\frac{(B^{\phi})^{2}}{B^{2}_{p}}\,\rightarrow\,-x^{2}\Omega_{F}^{2}+\frac{x^{2}\Omega_{F}^{2}}{v_{\infty}^{2}}=\frac{x^{2}\Omega_{F}^{2}}{\gamma_{\infty}^{2}-1}\,,

(B.11)

where
v_{\infty}
is the asymptotic, spatial poloidal velocity, and
\gamma_{\infty}=(1-v_{\infty}^{2})^{-1/2}
is the terminal Lorentz factor of the outflow.
By expanding Eq. (
B.9
) and Eq. (
B.10
) in the limit of large

and substituting Eq.(
B.11
), we derive the asymptotic forms of

and
\eta
as given in Eq. (
2.10
).
The asymptotic expression for

can be derived by substituting Eqs. (
B.11
) (
2.10

Appendix C
Launching Condition

C.1
The plasma loading

The MHD flow is launched from a mass-loading zone
[
26
,
64
]
.
Within the loading zone, there is a source term for plasma injection,
\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=S^{\nu}
.
We consider that during the loading process, there is only particle number loading
[
26
]
, resulting in

S^{\mu}=\eta^{\prime}\mu u^{\mu}
.
Thus, according to Eq. (
A.12
), the energy and angular momentum transport equations are written as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}_{\,\,\ t}=-(\eta E)^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}\mu u_{t}\,,\quad\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu}_{\,\,\ \phi}=(\eta L)^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}\mu u_{\phi}\,.

(C.1)

In the main text, we adopt a geometrically thin, axisymmetric launching surface with a zero poloidal outflow velocity
[
19
]
.
At the launching surface, the mass density
\rho=|\eta|B_{p}/u_{p}
diverges due to vanishing
u_{p}
, while
\eta
,
B_{p}
remain finite. This singularity can be regularized by introducing a finite-width loading zone, where plasma is supplied either from the external accretion flow or via pair production within the jet
[
54
,
88
]
. However, modeling such processes lies beyond the scope of this study.

According to Eq. (
C.1
), thers is a jump in the conserved quantities across the launching surface:

\displaystyle\delta(\eta E)=\delta\eta(-\mu u_{t})\,,\quad\delta(\eta L)=\delta\eta(\mu u_{\phi})\,,

(C.2)

where
\delta(X)=X_{\rm flow}-X_{\rm loading}\,
,
X\in\{\eta E,\eta L,\eta\}
.
Note that the limit
S_{t}\rightarrow 0
gives a special case of an electromagnetically dominated jet with the matching condition
(\eta E)|_{\rm in}=(\eta E)|_{\rm out}
, as employed in
[
89
]
.
In this work, we adopt the general formulation of Eqs.(
C.2
), which incorporates plasma injection in the loading zone. The details within the loading zone are beyond the scope of this study. Instead, Eq. (
C.2
) serves solely to provide a matching condition: for field lines threading the horizon, it leads to the relation between the conserved quantities of the inflow and outflow, as given in Eq. (
3.3
).

C.2
The launching condition

We derive the launching condition by analyzing the plasma acceleration near the launching surface. From energy-momentum conservation, we have
\nabla_{\mu}T_{\rm m}^{\mu\nu}=-\nabla_{\mu}T_{\rm EM}^{\mu\nu}=f^{\nu}
, where
f^{\mu}
denotes the Lorentz force density. In ideal MHD, where the electric field vanishes in the comoving frame,
f^{\mu}
is orthogonal to the comoving magnetic field, leading to

\displaystyle b^{\mu}f_{\mu}=b_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}(hu^{\mu}u^{\nu}+P\,g^{\mu\nu})=0\,.

(C.3)

Using
b^{\nu}u_{\nu}=0
, this can be rewritten as
h\,b_{\nu}u^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}u^{\nu}+b_{\mu}\partial^{\mu}P=0
.
Near the launching surface, the plasma motion is predominantly toroidal, with
u^{\phi}\approx\Omega_{F}u^{t}
and
u_{p}\approx 0
.
The derivative term
b_{\nu}u^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}u^{\nu}
expanded in powers of
u_{p}
as (also see Appendix A.4 in
[
59
]
)

\displaystyle b_{\nu}u^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}u^{\nu}\approx B^{{\mathcal{P}}}\partial_{{\mathcal{P}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2}u_{p}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log{k_{0}}}}\right)\approx\frac{1}{2}B^{{\mathcal{P}}}\partial_{{\mathcal{P}}}\log{k_{0}}\,,

(C.4)

where we have used Eqs. (
A.2
) to relate
b^{{\mathcal{P}}}\propto B^{{\mathcal{P}}}
near the launching surface.
Substituting Eq.(
C.4
) into Eq.(
C.3
), we obtain the launching condition:

\displaystyle P^{\prime}\Big|_{\rm LS}+\frac{h}{2}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log{k_{0}}}}\right)^{\prime}\Big|_{\rm LS}=0\,.

(C.5)

This equation constrains the location of the launching surface, determined by the interplay of gravity, magnetic field structure, and thermal pressure gradients.
In the cold limit, the launching condition reduces to
k_{0}^{\prime}\big|_{\rm LS}=0
. This follows directly from the acceleration equation (Eq.(
B.5
)): to ensure regularity at the launching surface, the numerator
N\propto k_{0}k_{2}k_{0}^{\prime}
must vanish as
u_{p}\rightarrow 0
.
Using
k_{2}=k_{0}E^{-2}
from Eq.(
3.1
) and noting that
k_{0}>0
within the light cylinder, we conclude that the launching surface must lie at a local extremum of
k_{0}
.
