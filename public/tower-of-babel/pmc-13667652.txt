AbstractTraditional reinforcement learning for autonomous driving faces challenges such as slow convergence, control instability, and limited adaptability. To address these issues, this paper proposes a hybrid decision-making framework that integrates V2X communication, human–machine interaction experience, and deep reinforcement learning. Based on the Actor–Critic architecture, the framework incorporates a human-like driving tendency network to constrain the policy search space and integrates a Kalman filter-based V2X communication anomaly handling module. The model constructs the vehicle’s action space and state representation. Comparative experiments with a method lacking the driving tendency module and the hierarchical planning method DDPG-RRT* show that DDPG-HP achieves faster convergence and better scenario adaptability than these baselines. It effectively avoids static and dynamic obstacles, demonstrates anticipatory and gradual deceleration, and initiates braking 1.7 s earlier than the DDPG-RRT* method, thereby reducing collision risks and enhancing pedestrian safety. Furthermore, the communication anomaly compensation mechanism effectively improves the system’s adaptability and overall robustness under unstable communication conditions.

1. Introduction

With the rapid advancement of intelligent connected and autonomous driving technologies, vehicle decision-making systems are progressively evolving from rule-based static control to data-driven intelligent decision-making. Traditional autonomous driving decision methods are primarily categorized into rule-based and optimization-based approaches. Choi et al. [1] developed decision algorithms suitable for mass-produced vehicles targeting constant-speed cruising and car-following scenarios, yet their applicability is limited in more complex scenarios. Wang et al. [2] proposed a cooperative adaptive cruise control strategy based on distributed model predictive control. Their approach reduces fluctuations in platoon following distance and significantly improves stability through hierarchical controller design and robust optimization. Although these methods offer structural clarity and reliability, they often lack flexibility and generalization in complex and dynamic environments.

Deep reinforcement learning has shown significant potential in autonomous driving. It can learn and optimize decisions autonomously in complex environments. However, pure reinforcement learning algorithms face challenges such as extended training periods, convergence difficulties, and limited interpretability of policy behavior. Wu et al. [3] introduced a multi-vehicle cooperative driving strategy based on V2X communication, integrating vehicular network information through reinforcement learning to enhance environmental perception and decision safety. Qiu et al. [4] employed maximum entropy inverse reinforcement learning to construct a driving style-aware car-following model, effectively balancing efficiency and safety. Nevertheless, existing research still exhibits limitations in areas such as human driving behavior modeling and multi-objective collaborative optimization. For instance, mainstream algorithms like DDPG encounter challenges in training stability and sample efficiency. Although Liu et al. [5] proposed the CLPER-DDPG algorithm, which improves longitudinal control stability through curriculum learning and prioritized experience replay mechanisms, its adaptability to lateral coordination and dynamic interactive scenarios remains insufficient.

To address decision robustness and safety issues in complex dynamic interactive environments, researchers have begun exploring hybrid architectures and collaborative optimization methods. Wei et al. [6] investigated the impact of different driving styles on rear-end collision risk. Their analysis revealed differences in reaction patterns between aggressive and conservative drivers in emergency braking scenarios, providing important references for the design of active safety systems. Islam et al. [7] utilized driver data to construct reward functions, achieving driver-like longitudinal decision-making. Liu et al. [8] proposed a deep reinforcement learning-based autonomous drift system. By combining a cubic spline curve-based drift path planner and a Refined-exploration Soft Actor Critic algorithm, it achieves robust obstacle avoidance capabilities under extreme driving conditions. While these methods have improved decision performance at various levels, they still lack systematic integration in areas such as the fusion of human–machine interaction experience and lateral–longitudinal collaborative optimization. Li et al. [9] proposed a human-like driving decision method for unsignalized intersections based on a game-theoretic framework. By quantifying driver payoff through Prospect Theory and introducing a probabilistic model to describe driver acceleration tendencies, it ensures a high success rate of safe interactions while improving traffic efficiency. Ma et al. [10] proposed a DDPG algorithm that accelerates policy convergence through expert experience pre-training.

However, existing approaches often focus on single tasks or isolated modules, lacking systematic integration of driving experience, environmental interaction, and multi-objective collaboration. This paper proposes a lateral–longitudinal collaborative decision-making and control framework that integrates human–machine interaction experience with deep reinforcement learning. By constructing a driving preference-guided reward mechanism, a hierarchical experience storage and learning module, and a communication anomaly robustness compensation mechanism, the framework aims to achieve safe, efficient, and human-like autonomous driving decisions in dynamic traffic scenarios. Performance is evaluated through comparisons with a method lacking the driving tendency module and the hierarchical planning-based DDPG-RRT*.

The main contributions of this paper include the following: (1) designing a network that incorporates human driving preferences to enhance the human-likeness and comfort of the policy; (2) constructing a lateral–longitudinal collaborative action space and state representation to achieve multi-objective collaborative optimization, including pedestrian courtesy and proactive braking obstacle avoidance; (3) introducing a communication packet loss compensation module to enhance the system’s robustness under non-ideal V2X environments.

2. Deep Reinforcement Learning Algorithm

2.1. Vehicle Model

This system primarily consists of four major components: the agent module [11], environmental interaction, state estimation with experience storage and learning mechanism, and a communication packet loss compensation module [12]. This paper first establishes a two-degree-of-freedom vehicle dynamics model using steering wheel angle, throttle/brake inputs, and initial vehicle speed as control inputs. The model outputs the vehicle’s position, velocity, and yaw rate, along with sensor-detected obstacle positions, as well as pedestrian positions and velocities. This approach reduces the state dimension while effectively capturing the vehicle’s steering dynamics, as illustrated in Figure 1.

This paper proposes an end-to-end autonomous driving decision-making and control framework based on an improved DDPG [13] algorithm, aiming to address safe driving challenges in complex and dynamic traffic scenarios. The method builds upon the 2-DOF vehicle dynamics model and takes the target steering wheel angle, throttle command, brake command, and initial vehicle state as inputs, and it outputs the vehicle speed and yaw rate. These outputs are combined with high-definition map data to compute the vehicle’s lateral and longitudinal position information.

2.2. DDPG-HP Algorithm Framework

To enhance the robustness of the system in real-world environments, a communication anomaly module is integrated into the framework. This module employs a Kalman filter [14] for signal filtering and compensation, addressing disruptions such as V2X communication [15] link interruptions or delays. The aim is to generate control strategies that balance efficiency and safety, enabling the system to output reasonable control actions in real time in complex traffic scenarios.

This framework introduces the concept of “Human Preference.” Through continuous online learning and parameter iteration, it generates guiding signals that better reflect natural human driving habits and rational decision-making. These signals are integrated into the reinforcement learning optimization process, allowing the algorithm to converge rapidly within a relatively short training period toward control strategies that better reflect human driving intuition while balancing safety and traffic efficiency. The overall algorithm follows the dual-network Actor–Critic architecture, modeling the decision-making and control problem in autonomous driving as a holistic optimization task within a continuous action space. This creates an end-to-end autonomous driving system integrating perception, decision-making, control, and learning. To validate the effectiveness and advantages of the proposed method, this study also introduces the classic path planning algorithm RRT* as a performance benchmark. The framework is illustrated in Figure 2.

3. Agent Module

This study constructs an autonomous driving system framework based on the DDPG-HP algorithm, integrating perception, decision-making, control, and learning modules. The overall system architecture comprises four main components: the agent module, the environment interaction module, the state estimation and experience replay mechanism, and the communication packet loss compensation module.

A DDPG agent based on the Markov Decision Process (MDP) [16] is developed to enable vehicle trajectory tracking and collaborative decision-making in complex dynamic traffic environments. The state space *s* is defined as multi-source perceptual information, including the lateral and longitudinal positions and velocities of the ego vehicle, obstacle vehicles, and pedestrians. The action space consists of continuous adjustable control inputs such as throttle, brake, and steering wheel angle. The reward function is designed to balance multiple objectives, including safety, efficiency, and comfort. Through iterative training across diverse environmental scenarios, the DDPG agent can approximate the optimal control policy, demonstrating fast convergence, smooth control outputs, and strong generalization capabilities in continuous action spaces. This makes it particularly suitable for predictive deceleration and steering coordination in dynamic interactive scenarios such as pedestrian crossings.

3.1. DDPG-HP Pseudocode

This module adopts the Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) algorithm to build an agent for end-to-end control policy learning. Combining the advantages of policy gradient methods and value function approximation, DDPG is well-suited for continuous action space control problems in autonomous driving. The module includes four core neural networks: the Actor network, Critic network, Target–Actor network, and Target–Critic network. The pseudocode of DDPG-HP is presented in Algorithm 1. The dual-network Actor–Critic architecture ensures control precision in continuous action spaces and provides a flexible interface for integrating the driving tendency model and reward shaping.

3.2. Space Design

To address the task requirements of coordinating lateral and longitudinal control for autonomous vehicles during motion, this paper proposes a dynamic decision-making framework that incorporates a driving tendency network to achieve safe obstacle avoidance in complex environments. Specifically, in pedestrian crossing zones such as zebra crossings, to ensure pedestrian safety, the system state space must simultaneously account for the ego vehicle’s motion state, the dynamics of surrounding vehicles, and pedestrian behavior information. Based on this, the state space is designed as follows:(1)S=[xcar,ycar,vcar,xobs1,yobs1,vobs1,…,xobsn,yobsn,vobsn,xp,yp,vp] where xcar, ycar, and vcar represent the longitudinal coordinate, lateral coordinate, and velocity of the ego vehicle; each obstacle-vehicle is described by a triplet of position-velocity variables xobs, yobs, and vobs; and xp, yp, and vp indicate the position, velocity, and heading angle of the pedestrian. Here, n denotes the number of obstacle vehicles relevant to the ego vehicle’s trajectory. In the current network architecture, the input dimension is fixed, and n is set to a predefined maximum value nmax. This value is determined by the actual scenario and can be adjusted as needed. When the actual number of obstacle vehicles is fewer than nmax, zero padding is applied; when it exceeds nmax, only the closest vehicles are retained. This fixed-dimensional design with padding/truncation is common in standard MLP-based reinforcement learning, but it may become less flexible in highly dense traffic where the number of surrounding agents changes dynamically. Attention mechanisms or graph neural networks (GNNs) could process a variable number of agents more systematically, which is an important direction for future work.

3.3. Neural Network Architecture Design

To efficiently apply the DDPG algorithm for the coordinated lateral and longitudinal control of intelligent connected vehicles, this study addresses the real-time requirements of autonomous driving tasks by adopting the Actor–Critic dual-network architecture as the algorithmic core. Through interactive learning, the framework achieves an effective balance and collaborative optimization between control performance and safety constraints, as illustrated in Figure 3 and Figure 4. The network design considers the real-time demands of autonomous driving tasks, balancing the number of layers and neurons to strike a balance between computational efficiency and expressive power.

The Actor network serves as a policy approximator πϕ(S). Its input includes the current environmental state S, which comprises vehicle states, obstacle information, and road features. The output consists of continuous control actions a = [δ, throttle, brake], where δ is the steering angle and throttle and brake represent longitudinal control commands. The network parameters ϕ are updated via policy gradient methods to maximize the expected cumulative reward. The Actor network adopts a structure of “Input layer → Hidden layer 1 (Linear + ReLU) → Hidden layer 2 (Linear + ReLU) → Output layer (Linear + Tanh).” The hidden layers contain 256 and 128 neurons, respectively. The output layer uses the Tanh activation function to confine actions to the [−1, 1] interval, which is then mapped to the actual physical control range. This approach avoids the quantization error inherent in traditional discrete-action methods, thereby enabling smoother trajectory-tracking capabilities.

To evaluate the integrated performance of the DDPG-HP algorithm, this paper introduces an additional comparative scenario: a hierarchical control architecture that decouples lateral path planning from longitudinal speed control. The upper layer employs an improved RRT* algorithm for real-time path planning, and the lower layer uses the DDPG algorithm as a longitudinal controller, which autonomously learns to optimize speed-tracking strategies, thereby guaranteeing both driving safety and passenger comfort in dynamic scenarios such as pedestrian crossings. The detailed structure of the Actor network is shown in Figure 3.

The Critic network is responsible for evaluating the value of state-action pairs Qθ(s, a). It takes the state s and action a as inputs and outputs the action-value Q-value to assess the long-term return of taking the specified action a in state s. 128 samples are collected from the experience replay buffer: (s, a, r, s′), and the target Q-value is calculated:(2)y=r(s,a)+γQθ′(s′,πϕ′(s′)) where r(s, a) is the immediate reward, γ is the discount factor, Qθ′ is the target Critic network, and πϕ′ is the target Actor network. The parameters of the target networks, θ′ and ϕ, are slowly tracked to the online network parameters θ and ϕ′ via a soft update strategy. The optimization objective of the Critic network is to minimize the mean squared error ℒ(θ) between the predicted Q-value and the target Q-value:(3)L(θ)=E(s,a,r,s′)~D[(Qθ(s,a)−y)2] where D represents the experience replay buffer. The Adam optimizer is used, incorporating regularization and gradient clipping to ensure training stability. The agent collects experience data by interacting with the environment and updates the Actor network parameters ϕ using the deterministic policy gradient:(4)∇ϕJ(ϕ)=Es~D[∇aQθ(s,a)|a=πϕ(s)∇ϕπϕ(s)]

To break the correlation between value estimation and policy updates during training, a target network mechanism is established. This includes the Target–Actor network πϕ′ and the Target–Critic network, which share the same architecture as the online networks. The target network parameters are slowly tracked to the online network parameters using a soft update strategy: ϕ′ ← τϕ + (1 − τ)ϕ′, θ′ ← τθ + (1 − τ)θ′. Here, τ is the update coefficient, set to 0.003.

The network architecture also consists of two hidden layers with 256 and 128 neurons, respectively, using ReLU as the activation function. The final Q-value estimate is output through a linear layer, as shown in Figure 4.

Furthermore, this study incorporates Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) noise into the algorithm to enhance the model’s exploratory capability in the environment. Concurrently, an experience replay mechanism is employed to store state transition samples generated from the agent’s interaction with the environment. An off-policy method is utilized to train the neural network in batches, thereby improving data utilization efficiency and enhancing the stability of the learning process.

3.4. Driving Tendency Network Design

Driving Tendency Network

In complex and dense urban road environments, human drivers typically rely on experiential intuition for real-time responses rather than directly planning high-level behaviors and executing precise control. The autonomous driving task can be viewed as a search and optimization problem within a global policy space [17]. The driving tendency mechanism is introduced to constrain the policy search space, improve learning efficiency, and enhance decision-making rationality by integrating prior knowledge from human driving experience. This enables the intelligent agent to efficiently converge towards feasible driving strategies that align with human habits while ensuring safety. Interaction models based on mechanisms are applied to determine appropriate initial values for optimization. Analogous to the decision-making characteristics of human driving, “driving tendency” is defined to quantify decision-making preferences in driving behavior. The driving tendency network continuously optimizes its outputs to provide reasonable driving guidance for the system, effectively constraining the direction of policy search. Its outputs, together with those of the interaction model, serve as inputs to a nonlinear cost function. Solving this optimization problem generates safe and reliable longitudinal and lateral control commands.

Actor–Critic, as a typical reinforcement learning framework, aims for the Actor to maximize cumulative rewards. Under the premise of a well-designed reward function, the optimization objective of the driving tendency network aligns with that of the Actor: to learn safe and efficient driving behaviors by maximizing long-term returns, ultimately achieving or surpassing human-level driving performance. 3.6. V2X Communication

During V2X communication and sensor data transmission, packet loss and delay frequently occur, leading to abrupt changes or missing input states. This directly impacts the continuity of control decisions and the stability of the training process [19]. To address this problem, this paper designs a perception signal compensation mechanism that combines a historical buffer and Kalman filtering. When packet loss or delay is detected, historical state sequences are utilized to predict and smooth the current state, thereby providing the reinforcement learning agent with more reliable and continuous observation inputs, as illustrated in Figure 6. This design mimics the human driver’s “inertial perception” of vehicle states—the ability to estimate the current motion state based on experience even when precise feedback is temporarily unavailable. The inputs to the signal filtering module include the current time and raw perception states, including xcar, ycar, vcar, acar, Flagloss and Delaynum. The processing workflow is divided into three stages: delayed data processing, anomaly detection and state compensation, and state updating.

The V2X communication anomaly model adopted in this paper is based on distance-dependent packet loss probability and Poisson-distributed delays. This simplified model captures the core characteristics of signal loss and delay. It does not account for correlated packet losses, multipath fading, sudden network congestion in dense traffic, or cyber-physical security threats, which may affect the Kalman-filter compensation performance in different ways. The focus of this paper is on the compensation algorithm’s response to packet loss and delay anomalies, rather than on reproducing every detail of real-world V2X environments. Therefore, the simplified model is used as a basis for validating the compensation mechanism, while the above factors remain to be addressed in future work.

3.6.1. Packet Loss and Delay Module

This paper designs a parameterized packet loss and delay simulation module. It generates random packet loss events and Poisson-distributed [20] delays through a probability model. When packet loss occurs, all state inputs are set to zero, providing representative anomalous inputs for the subsequent signal filtering module. Based on a signal packet loss probability model dependent to communication distance, it simulates the real-world characteristic of deteriorating communication quality over long distances. The formulas for packet loss probability calculation and packet loss judgment are as follows:(25)Q=P⋅(xcar+Δt)−ϕP⋅(xcar+Δt)−ϕ+O where Δt is the additional distance offset; P is the scale parameter, set to 10; φ is the distance decay exponent, set to 0.5; set Flagloss = (Q < β), where β is the packet loss threshold, set to 0.82; O~N(μ = 0, σ = 0.2) is a Gaussian noise term introduced to add random fluctuations and avoid deterministic packet loss. Transmission delay is simulated using a Poisson distribution to capture its randomness: Delaynum = min(poisson(1),5).

Using a historical observation data buffer, it provides an optimal estimation of the vehicle’s motion state during communication anomalies, ensuring the continuity and safety of the autonomous driving system.

4. Simulation Verification and Analysis

To validate the performance of the DDPG-HP control algorithm proposed in this study, a lateral control simulation model was developed based on a co-simulation platform integrating MATLAB/Simulink (version R2022b, MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) and Prescan (version 2021.1.0, Siemens Digital Industries Software, Leuven, Belgium). The designed simulation training scenario, as illustrated in Figure 7, features a unidirectional three-lane road with each lane being 3.5 m wide. The scenario includes a pedestrian crossing at a crosswalk, a stationary vehicle in Lane 1 due to a malfunction, a vehicle traveling at a constant speed in Lane 2, and a temporarily parked vehicle in Lane 3, which corresponds to three obstacle vehicles and one pedestrian (n = 3 in Equation (1)). The ego vehicle incorporates a vehicle dynamics model, and a V2X communication environment was established to form a traffic flow model centered around this vehicle.

4.1. Simulation Training Scenario Design

The simulation training scenario was constructed based on the Prescan platform, as shown in Figure 8. This scenario is designed to include multi-obstacle road sections and crosswalk sections to simulate real-world urban traffic conditions.

4.2. V2X Communication Simulation Results

To simulate the uncertainty of communication signal transmission in a vehicular network environment, this study designed a parameterized communication packet loss and delay simulation module. The module uses a packet loss probability model based on vehicle travel distance and a Poisson process to simulate signal delay characteristics. When packet loss occurs, the system sets the corresponding observation value to zero at the receiving end, simulating the impact of communication interruptions on the perception system. Figure 9 and Figure 10 compare packet loss occurrences, Kalman-filter state estimates, and actual vehicle motion states.

As shown in Figure 9 and Figure 10, within the blue vertical bar regions indicating intervals of communication packet loss or delay, uncompensated raw states exhibit abrupt changes or data gaps, leading to discontinuities or abnormal fluctuations in the vehicle’s position, velocity, and acceleration curves. After introducing the state compensation mechanism based on reverse smoothing Kalman filtering, the system can fully utilize historical motion trends and vehicle dynamics priors to perform smooth extrapolation and reconstruction of the state at the current moment. The compensated state curves show minimal error compared to the ideal continuous reference trajectory, significantly eliminating abnormal jumps in the original data and achieving continuous and stable estimation of the vehicle’s motion state.

The simulation results indicate that under abnormal communication conditions, Kalman filter compensation provides high-quality state inputs. These inputs effectively support the decision-making of the reinforcement learning controller. This enables the vehicle to maintain smooth driving trajectories and coherent control actions even in environments with partially missing observational information, thereby substantially enhancing the system’s adaptability and overall robustness in complex urban traffic scenarios and unstable V2X communication conditions.

4.3. Comparison of Different Learning Strategies

The training was terminated upon convergence, and Figure 11 presents the average reward curve over the actual 3000 training episodes. The curve transitioned from initial oscillations to gradual convergence and stabilization.

The DDPG-HP algorithm converges at around 3000 training episodes, whereas vanilla DDPG requires 6000 episodes. Under identical hardware conditions, the total training time of DDPG-HP is approximately 50% of that of vanilla DDPG, demonstrating that the human-driving-tendency prior reduces computational cost in the training phase.

For real-time performance evaluation within the simulation environment, we measured the inference latency of the Actor network on a desktop platform equipped with an AMD Ryzen AI 9 365 processor (2.00 GHz, designed and supplied by Advanced Micro Devices, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and 32 GB RAM. The average per-step inference latency is 0.13 ms (median: 0.11 ms), yielding a theoretical upper-bound inference rate of roughly 7600 FPS. The Actor network consumes only 0.12 MB of memory. Given the 20 ms control cycle of the simulation (Ts = 0.02 s), the measured inference latency satisfies the real-time constraint of the simulation.

To verify the effectiveness of the DDPG-HP algorithm, this study established a controlled experiment, comparing the designed algorithm with the traditional DDPG method under the same simulation environment.

The comparative experiment shows that if the driving tendency module is removed from the architecture, the agent exhibits significant policy flaws during the short-term training phase. As shown in Figure 12, the vehicle frequently demonstrates high-risk behaviors such as trajectory boundary violations, collisions with obstacle vehicles, and conflicts with pedestrians. This result validates the critical role of the driving tendency module in guiding exploration direction and constraining the action space. Its absence directly leads to a lack of effective behavioral preference guidance during training, making it difficult to learn safe and compliant driving policies within a limited number of episodes.

Figure 13, Figure 14 and Figure 15 compare the DDPG network trained for 6000 episodes with the DDPG-HP network trained for 3000 episodes.

Compared with standard DDPG, the DDPG-HP algorithm demonstrates advantages in foresight and safety when approaching crosswalks and areas of potential interaction with pedestrians. While effectively avoiding dynamic and static obstacles, it can initiate progressive deceleration in advance. Before nearing obstacle vehicles or pedestrians, the throttle begins to decrease smoothly, and braking is applied gradually through multiple small, continuous interventions. This avoids the abrupt full-throttle or hard-braking phenomena observed in DDPG, resulting in gentler changes in longitudinal acceleration. When encountering potential conflict scenarios, DDPG-HP can complete braking decisions ahead of time, ensuring a safe distance. On normal, unobstructed road sections, it improves overall traffic efficiency through more natural acceleration behaviors, achieving a good balance between safety and efficiency.

The steering angle δ output of DDPG-HP is smoother and more continuous, with smaller fluctuations and quicker recovery of the yaw rate during steering maneuvers. When encountering obstacles, the vehicle tends to avoid them while largely maintaining its position near the lane center, and the overall trend of the yaw angle change more closely resembles the continuous adjustments typical of natural human driving. In contrast, DDPG exhibits significant oscillations over short periods. This indicates that the algorithm achieves smoother trajectory during multi-obstacle avoidance.

During driving, the DDPG-HP vehicle maintains more stable lateral offset and more uniform longitudinal position progression, avoiding the sharp acceleration or lateral deviation observed in DDPG in later stages. Particularly in pedestrian crossing and multi-obstacle scenarios, by integrating driving tendency priors, DDPG-HP completes anticipatory steering and speed adjustments in advance, significantly increasing the safety margin before potential conflict points, substantially reducing collision risk.

In summary, compared with the baseline methods, the DDPG-HP algorithm not only achieves high-priority protection for pedestrian safety while avoiding dynamic and static obstacles but also demonstrates advantages in control smoothness, ride comfort, and decision-making foresight. This shows how human driving experience priors can both constrain and improve reinforcement learning policies. A schematic of the vehicle’s driving environment and trajectory is shown in Figure 16.

The inflated collision zone consists of a hard constraint that lies inside obstacles and a soft constraint that lies in a Gaussian decay field, which together ensure the safety of the obstacle avoidance trajectory. The lower layer uses the DDPG algorithm as the longitudinal controller, optimizing speed tracking strategies through learning to ensure vehicle safety and comfort in pedestrian crossing scenarios.

5. Discussion

Pure reinforcement learning for autonomous driving decision-making faces challenges such as slow convergence, control jitter, and instability in multiple scenarios. To address these issues, this paper proposes a hybrid decision-making framework, DDPG-HP, which integrates V2X communication, human–machine interaction experience, and deep reinforcement learning. Through comparative simulations with baseline algorithms lacking the driving tendency module and the hierarchical planning-based DDPG-RRT* method, the results show that the proposed end-to-end autonomous driving decision-making and control framework achieves performance advantages in complex dynamic traffic scenarios.

The system based on the Actor–Critic dual-network structure performs well in high-dynamic interaction scenarios such as pedestrian crossings. By incorporating prior knowledge of human driving tendencies, this framework can not only cooperatively avoid dynamic and static obstacles but also achieve anticipatory, progressive deceleration before crosswalks, thereby significantly enhancing pedestrian safety. Its lateral control output is smooth and continuous, and although there are minor fluctuations in longitudinal acceleration, it overall effectively reduces collision risks in pedestrian interaction scenarios. Furthermore, to address abnormal conditions in V2X communication, this paper designs a state compensation mechanism based on reverse smoothing Kalman filtering. Utilizing vehicle kinematic priors, this mechanism effectively suppresses state jumps and perceptual noise during communication packet loss or delays. It should be noted that this compensation performance is validated with the simplified V2X anomaly model described in Section 3.6, and its effectiveness under more complex real-world communication conditions requires further investigation. Experiments show that the proposed method enhances the system’s adaptability and robustness under the simplified V2X anomaly model used in this study.

These simulation results confirm the safety and robustness of the DDPG-HP framework in multi-obstacle scenarios. The validation is based on a co-simulation platform that incorporates vehicle dynamics, sensors, and V2X communication. Extending the validation to HIL testing, real-vehicle experiments, and comparisons with a broader range of reinforcement learning baselines such as SAC and PPO would provide further insight into generalization and engineering applicability. Due to experimental constraints, a sensitivity analysis or per-weight ablation of the adaptive weighting mechanism was not performed, which is a limitation of the current study and will be addressed in future work. In summary, through structural design and mechanism innovation, the DDPG-HP framework achieves systematic improvements in decision-making safety, smoothness, and environmental adaptability. Compared with standard DDPG and DDPG-RRT*, it demonstrates advantages in convergence speed and control performance. The framework’s real-time performance and robustness are validated in the co-simulation environment that incorporates a packet-loss and delay model, providing an effective solution for autonomous driving decision-making in high-dynamic, strongly interactive scenarios.
