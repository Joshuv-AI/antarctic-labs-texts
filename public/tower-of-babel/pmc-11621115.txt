miR-548az-5p induces amniotic epithelial cell senescence by regulating KATNAL1 expression in labor
Authors: Die Jing, Qian Liu, Hongyuan Zhang, Yuchen Li, Xiaotong Jiang, Yanjun Cai, Xietong Wang, Lei Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Amniotic fluid exosomes (AF-Exos) from term labor (TL) cause amniotic membrane senescence and induce labor. However, the intrinsic mechanism through which this occurs remains unknown. Therefore, we performed microRNA (miRNA) microarray chip screening of AF-Exos obtained from TL and terms not in labor and discovered that the expression of miR-548az-5p was significantly upregulated in TL. This study aimed to explore the role of miR-548az-5p in AF-Exos-induced human amniotic epithelial cells (hAECs) senescence for labor initiation. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that Katanin catalytic subunit A1 like 1 (KATNAL1) is a potential miR-548az-5p target. In hAECs, the upregulation of miR-548az-5p suppressed KATNAL1 expression, disorganized microtubules, increased senescence-associated secretory phenotype-related biomarkers, and inhibited cell proliferation by cyclin D1 and cyclin-dependent kinase 6 (CDK6). This study identified that miR-548az-5p is involved in the senescence of amniotic epithelial cells by targeting KATNAL1 to induce labor. Notably, this study offers new perspectives on the mediation of cellular senescence using AF-Exos miRNAs, which results in labor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-82390-y.

Introduction

Human parturition involves interactions between the myometrium and signals from the placenta, fetal membranes, and fetus. However, the mechanisms facilitating this process remain elusive1,2. Fetal membranes, comprising amniotic and chorionic membranes, are the innermost lining of the intrauterine cavity3. The amniotic membrane interfaces with the amniotic fluid, indicating that it is the primary responder to changes in the amniotic cavity and provides mechanical support to protect fetal growth, which is significant in maintaining pregnancy3–5. The membrane lifespan coincides with fetal maturity, and membrane senescence is most pronounced in term labor (TL)4. There is substantial evidence that amniotic membrane senescence is associated with human parturition6–8. Furthermore, infection, inflammation, and oxidative stress activate amniotic epithelial cell senescence9. A recent study showed that amniotic fluid exosomes (AF-Exos) at TL induce amniotic membrane senescence10. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying AF-Exos-induced amniotic membrane senescence can provide new insights into human parturition.

Senescent cells are metabolically active and secrete inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors, which is known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)11. Interleukins are a subset of cytokines that mediate inflammatory responses and participate in various physiological processes12. IL-1α and IL-1β are the most widely studied SASP factors that play a central role in labor initiation as they may mediate an inflammatory state to induce labor12,13. They can be secreted into the extracellular environment or intracellularly to activate other key SASP interleukins such as IL-6 and IL-813. Many studies have reported that IL-6 and IL-8 contribute to the establishment and maintenance of pregnancy by mediating uterine receptivity, trophoblast function at the site of implantation and parturition, immune-endocrine interactions at the feto-maternal interface, and other processes, and that their concentrations are significantly higher in women who deliver at term14. Moreover, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are zinc-dependent enzymes that degrade components of the extracellular matrix (ECM), thereby promoting connective tissue breakdown15. Among them, MMP-3 (matrix lysin-1) is capable of degrading a variety of ECM components, including proteoglycans, fibronectin, laminin, and type IV collagen, and plays an important role in labor initiation15,16.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small, non-coding RNAs with a length of 18–24 nucleotides. They regulate post-transcriptional gene expression via mRNA degradation or translational repression17. Studies have shown that miRNAs regulate almost all cellular processes, including metabolism, proliferation, differentiation, and various pathophysiological processes18. In addition, miRNAs are vital for cellular senescence19–21. In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, elevated miR-125a-5p levels targeting Sp1 are involved in lung epithelial cell senescence and increase our understanding of the mechanisms underlying senescence and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease22. The upregulation of miR-132-3p suppresses DUSP9 levels in the amnion, inducing inflammatory responses that trigger parturition23. However, miR-548az-5p has only been reported to be associated with coronary artery disease24, and it remains unclear whether specific miRNAs derived from AF-Exos cause amniotic cell senescence, leading to labor.

Katanin catalytic subunit A1 like 1 (KATNAL1), located on human chromosome 13, belongs to a family of microtubule-severing enzymes and contains an AAA adenosine triphosphatase domain25. Microtubules are hollow polymers assembled from heterodimers of α- and β-tubulin26, which are central components of the cytoskeleton and are closely associated with senescence27,28. Currently, KATNAL1 has been reported to be associated with intellectual disability, microcephaly, epilepsy, and male infertility29–31. However, its role in human labor is yet to be reported.

By predicting the binding of miR-548az-5p to KATNAL1, this study aimed to investigate how miR-548az-5p and its target, KATNAL1, are involved in regulating human amniotic epithelial cells (hAECs) senescence to initiate parturition. Our findings further prove that AF-Exos miRNAs are critical to amniotic membrane function associated with labor in humans.

miR-548az-5p overexpression inhibits hAECs proliferation and induces senescence

We examined the biological functions of miR-548az-5p using in vitro gain- and loss-of-function studies to confirm the biological effects of miR-548az-5p on amniotic epithelial cell development. Overexpression of miR-548az-5p led to a significant increase in its level in hAECs, as determined by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) (Fig. 2A). Overexpression of miR-548az-5p increased senescence-associated secretory phenotypes and decreased CDK6 and cyclin D1 expression, whereas inhibition of miR-548az-5p produced a contrasting result (Fig. 2B). The ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay demonstrated that miR-548az-5p upregulation decreased cell proliferation rates, whereas its downregulation promoted the proliferation of hAECs (Fig. 2C). The data showed that overexpression of miR-548az-5p significantly increased the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells in hAECs. Conversely, the knockdown of miR-548az-5p inhibited the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells (Fig. 2D). Western blotting also showed that overexpression of miR-548az-5p reduced cyclin D1 and CDK6 and increased P16 expression, whereas its inhibition had the opposite effect (Fig. 2E). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay demonstrated that the decreased proliferation of hAECs was linked to the upregulation of miR-548az-5p, whereas its downregulation increased cell proliferation (Fig. 2F). Overexpression of miR-548az-5p in hAECs increased interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-6 levels. In contrast, the downregulation of miR-548az-5p reduced the secretion of IL-1β and IL-6 in hAECs (Fig. 2G). KATNAL1 is a direct target of miR-548az-5p

We used online algorithms such as TargetScan, miRTarBase, miRDB, miRWalk, and mirDIP databases to search for potential targets of miR-548az-5p to investigate the mechanism of miR-548az-5p in amniotic epithelial cell senescence. Common genes are illustrated using Venn diagrams (Fig. 3A). A dualluciferase reporter gene assay was used to confirm the direct relationship between miR-548az-5p and KATNAL1. Luciferase activity was remarkably diminished in HEK 293T cells transfected with the miR-548az-5p mimic and KATNAL1-WT reporter plasmids compared to that in cells transfected with the KATNAL1-MUT reporter plasmids (Fig. 3B). We examined the mRNA and protein levels of KATNAL1 in hAECs transfected with the miR-548az-5p mimic or inhibitor. The results showed that the miR-548az-5p mimic significantly decreased the mRNA and protein levels of KATNAL1, whereas the inhibitor increased KATNAL1 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3C and D). We examined the expression of KATNAL1 in TL amniotic membranes and TNIL (term not in labor) and found that the expression of KATNAL1 was significantly decreased in TL amniotic membranes compared to that in TNIL (Fig. 3E). KATNAL1 regulates hAECs proliferation and senescence

Based on these findings, miR-548az-5p was found to effectively target and suppress KATNAL1, thereby inducing senescence in hAECs. We used siRNA to inhibit KATNAL1 in hAECs to further clarify the role of KATNAL1. According to RT-qPCR and western blotting analysis, the expression of KATNAL1 was notably reduced in hAECs transfected with KATNAL1-siRNA, and the target KATNAL1 had the expected inhibition efficiency (Fig. 4A and B). EdU assay showed that the proliferative capacity of hAECs was reduced in the KATNAL1-siRNA group compared with the control and NC-siRNA groups (Fig. 4C). KATNAL1 knockdown increased the expression of IL-1α, IL-6, IL-8, and matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP-3) mRNA levels and decreased the expression of cyclin D1 (Fig. 4D). Therefore, inhibiting KATNAL1 increased the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells (Fig. 4E). Western blotting was used to detect the effect of KATNAL1-siRNA transfection on hAECs, and the findings showed that the expression of cyclin D1, CDK6, and α-tubulin was significantly decreased, and the expression of P16 was increased in the cells of the KATNAL1-siRNA group compared with the control and NC-siRNA groups (Fig. 4F). The CCK-8 assay demonstrated a decreased proliferation capacity of hAECs transfected with KATNAL1-siRNA (Fig. 4G). The knockdown of KATNAL1 increased the levels of IL-1β and IL-6 in hAECs (Fig. 4H). Silencing KATNAL1 expression using siRNA promoted senescence and inhibited the proliferation of hAECs. miR-548az-5p modulates hAECs proliferation and senescence by targeting KATNAL1

We assessed the effect of KATNAL1 on cell proliferation and senescence by transfecting KATNAL1 in miR-548az-5p overexpressed hAECs using RT-qPCR and western blotting. We found that the expression of KATNAL1 was notably increased by transfecting KATNAL1 in miR-548az-5p overexpressed hAECs (Fig. 5A and B). The EdU assay showed that the overexpression of KATNAL1 significantly reversed the inhibitory effect of miR-548az-5p on cell proliferation (Fig. 5C). SA-β-gal staining demonstrated that upregulating KATNAL1 in miR-548az-5p overexpressed cells decreased the proportion of senescence-positive cells (Fig. 5D). The results of the CCK8 assay suggested that overexpression of KATNAL1 abolished the decrease in cell proliferation resulting from miR-548az-5p (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, the levels of IL-1β and IL-6 were downregulated by KATNAL1 overexpression in miR-548az-5p overexpressed hAECs (Fig. 5F). Discussion

Labor in humans is triggered by many mechanisms, including amniotic membrane senescence6,7. Previous studies have demonstrated that AF-Exos induces senescence in amniotic epithelial cells at TL10. However, the mechanisms by which human AF-Exos induce amniotic membrane cell senescence remain unclear. Therefore, we explored the potential molecular mechanism through which human AF-Exos causes cellular senescence by delivering miR-548az-5p to initiate labor.

In the present study, miRNA sequencing analysis of AF-Exos from TL and TNIL showed high expression of miR-548az-5p in TL. The miR-548 is a large gene family containing 69 members. They are essential for many biological processes, including various human diseases32. Shi et al. showed that miR-548-3p overexpression inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis in breast cancer cells33. Dai et al. and Genc et al. reported that miR-548b-5p, miR-548c-5p, and miR-548i expression levels were significantly increased in response to inflammatory cytokine stimulation in patients with subacute sclerosing panencephalitis34,35. According to Son et al.36, miR-548aa, miR-548ai, miR-548a-3p, and miR-548x-5p were significantly reduced in the amnion membranes of patients with preterm birth and acute chorioamnionitis, possibly because miR-548 is a larger, poorly conserved primate-specific miRNA gene family, and higher levels of nucleotide differences could be detected between these homologous miRNA genes. Mature miRNAs also showed higher nucleotide differences, including nucleotide substitutions, insertions, or deletions32. Furthermore, a previous study showed that the high abundance of miR-548d-5p in amniotic fluid suggests its role in maintaining pregnancy37,38. In addition, fetal exosome miR-548d-5p is significantly increased in the placenta of women delivered at term compared to those delivered by late cesarean section in preterm labor39. Despite recent advances in research on the role of the miR-548 family in several processes, our knowledge of the function of miR-548az-5p in cellular senescence remains limited. Therefore, we selected miR-548az-5p from AF-Exos as the key molecule for AF-Exos-mediated cellular senescence in hAECs.

We found that the concentration of proinflammatory SASP markers (IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and MMP-3) was significantly increased in hAECs after transfection with miR-548az-5p. These cytokines may constitute sterile intrauterine inflammation, and MMPs degrade the extracellular matrix of the amniotic membrane to facilitate labor. Moreover, down-regulation of cell cycle-related genes cyclinD1 and CDK6 confirmed that miR-548az-5p inhibited the proliferation of hAECs. Similarly, in functional studies, it was observed that overexpression of miR-548az-5p in hAECs attenuated cell proliferation. Notably, P16, as a biomarker of senescence, is readily detected in many stimulus-induced senescent cells. It inhibits the formation of the cyclinD-CDK6 complex, which in turn inhibits the transcription of cell cycle genes40,41. Our results showed that P16 level was higher in hAECs overexpressing miR-548az-5p. Senescent cells express β-galactosidase, an enzyme detectable at pH 6 called senescence-associated β-galactosidase, which has been widely used in vitro and in vivo as a biomarker to identify senescent cells42, and the results showed that hAECs appeared to senesce following overexpression of miR-548az-5p. Additionally, microtubules in senescent cells show structural disorganization of reticular fibers and convoluted conformation. We concluded that overexpression of miR-548az-5p inhibited hAECs proliferation and induced its senescence.

MiRNAs regulate gene expression by binding to partially complementary sequences of the 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR) of target genes to suppress mRNA translation. Using RT-qPCR assays and online databases, we confirmed that KATNAL1 is the direct target gene of miR-548az-5p. This was verified by luciferase reporter analysis, western blot analysis, and a series of functional experiments. Furthermore, the overexpression of KATNAL1 can abolish the inhibition of proliferation caused by miR-548az-5p mimic. According to Banks et al.43, KATNAL1 loss leads to neuronal disorganization. Furthermore, studies have reported that the loss of KATNAL1 function results in male infertility by disrupting Sertoli cell microtubule dynamics and premature germ cell exfoliation25,31. Moujaber et al.44 discovered that alterations in microtubules have a significant effect on cellular function. Microtubules are markedly disorganized in senescent cells, with a reduced abundance of α-tubulin relative to actin44. Therefore, we knocked down KATNAL1 in hAECs using KATNAL1-siRNA to understand the intrinsic regulatory function of KATNAL1 in cellular senescence and the disorganized state of microtubules observed in KATNAL1 knockdown. These findings confirmed that miR-548az-5p significantly induced senescence in hAECs by inhibiting KATNAL1 expression, accompanied by microtubule disorganization.

This study had some limitations. First, the potential effects of other miRNAs from AF-Exos could not be ruled out. Second, we did not perform a co-culture of AF-Exos with hAECs and could not explain the uptake of AF-Exos by hAECs. Therefore, future studies should co-culture AF-Exos with hAECs for several experiments and further explore these labor mechanisms.

In conclusion, we discovered that AF-Exos miR-548az-5p targets KATNAL1 and is accompanied by microtubule disruption, ultimately leading to hAECs senescence and labor initiation. These findings provide new research directions and offer a potential target for exploring the mechanisms of human labor initiation.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the AF-Exos for miRNA microarray chip screening, which was performed by LC-Bio Technology (Hangzhou, China). Downstream target genes for miRNA were searched using online algorithms, including TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org/), miRTarBase (https://mirtarbase.cuhk.edu.cn), miRDB (http://mirdb.org/), miRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de), and mirDIP (http://ophid.utoronto.ca/mirDIP). A Venn diagram was required to explore common genes.

Patient inclusion criteria and sample collection

The Ethics Research Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital approved all experiments. All clinical specimens were collected with the patients’ consent through a signed informed consent form. All studies were conducted in accordance with the relevant regulations. Hepatitis B and C, syphilis, HIV, and fetal malformations were ruled out during the prenatal examination. Amniotic fluid was collected from two groups: (1) the TNIL group (n = 4). After complete exposure of the amniotic sac during cesarean section, the fetal membranes are punctured to retain amniotic fluid, which should be done gently to avoid mixing blood in the sample. (2) the TL group (n = 4). Regular contractions accompanied by opening of the uterine opening in labor, and a speculum was used to dilate the vagina before manual rupture of the membranes. Participant inclusion criteria in this study: (1) Inclusion criteria for TNIL: Women term not in labor undergoing an elective cesarean section at 37–42 weeks of gestation without complications. Labor was defined as regular and progressively increasing uterine contractions lasting 30 s or more with a 5–6 min interval at ≥ 37 and ≤ 42 weeks’ gestation, accompanied by progressive cervical canal disappearance, dilatation of the uterine opening, and descent of the fetal prenatal site. In addition, five cases of placenta from TNIL and TL were collected under aseptic conditions through elective cesarean sections and transvaginal delivery, respectively, at Shandong Provincial Hospital for clinical specimen validation. Fresh tissues were frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA isolation after collection.

Extraction, culture, and identification of hAECs

The amniotic membrane was stripped from the placenta aseptically, immersed in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer (containing 100U/mL penicillin and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin antibodies), placed on ice, and transported to the laboratory within 2 h. The amniotic membrane was rinsed well in the PBS buffer until no bloody fluid residue was observed on the surface. The amniotic membrane was cut into small pieces, mixed with 0.05% trypsin solution (trypsin solution: amniotic membrane = 2:1), digested at 37 °C for 50 min on a shaker at 200 rpm, and filtered through a 300-mesh sieve. The filtrate was then collected. This was followed by repeated digestion and filtration of the remaining tissue. The digestion reaction was terminated with an equal volume of RPMI 1640 (Gibco) complete medium (1640 medium: fetal bovine serum: PS = 89:10:1), followed by centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 10 min and the removal of the supernatant.

Furthermore, 1 mL of complete medium was resuspended and counted. The cells were transferred into T25 culture flasks and cultured in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% carbon dioxide (CO2) until 80–90% cell fusion was achieved. Cell status and growth were recorded under a microscope during incubation.

hAECs (1 × 104/well) were seeded in 96-well plates. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and sealed with goat serum for 30 min. Furthermore, hAECs were identified using mouse monoclonal primary cytokeratin 19 antibody (1:200 dilution; Abcam) and incubated overnight at 4°C. Next, hAECs were washed three times and exposed to goat anti-mouse IgG CY3 (1:200 dilution; Affinity Biosciences, Jiangsu, China) secondary antibody for 1 h in protected light at room temperature. The hAECs were then incubated in 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 8 min. The staining was observed using a fluorescence microscope, and photographs were taken for record purposes.

Dualluciferase reporter gene assay

HEK 293T cells were seeded into 24-well plates, and cell densities reached 80% at transfection. Co-transfection of cells was performed with KATNAL1-wild type (KATNAL1-WT) or KATNAL1-mutant (KATNALI-MUT) plasmids and NC or miR-548az-5p mimic. After 24 h, luciferase activity was detected using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay system (Promega, USA). The experiment was independently repeated three times.

Cell transfection

Furthermore, hAECs were transfected with miR-548az-5p mimics or inhibitors purchased from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China) using the riboFECT™ CP transfection reagent (RiboBio). The cells were divided into mimic NC, miR-548az-5p mimic, inhibitor NC, and miR-548az-5p inhibitor treatment groups. In addition, KATNAL1-small interfering RNA (siRNA) or NC-siRNA was purchased from RiboBio and transfected into hAECs using the riboFECTTM CP transfection reagent (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Furthermore, pcDNA3.1 empty plasmid and KATNAL1 overexpression plasmid (pcDNA-KATNAL1) was synthesized by Keyybio Co. (Shandong, China), and cell transfection was performed using the Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The cells were collected 24–48 h after transfection for subsequent experiments. Transfection success was determined by quantitative RT-qPCR.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cells using TRIzol reagent (AG21101, Accurate Biotechnology, China). The reverse transcription reaction of miRNA and mRNA was performed using the miRNA 1st Strand complementary DNA (cDNA) Synthesis Kit (AG11716, Accurate Biotechnology) and the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for quantitative PCR (qPCR) (AG11728, Accurate Biotechnology). Amplification was performed on a LightCycler 480 II using a SYBR Green qPCR Kit (AG11701, Accurate Biotechnology). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, and the internal control was U6 or GAPDH. Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining

hAECs senescence was identified using the senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The cell culture medium was aspirated, washed once with PBS, and fixed with β-galactosidase staining fixative for 15 min at room temperature. Next, it was rinsed three times with PBS, and a freshly prepared staining solution (Solution A: Solution B: Solution C: Solution X-gal = 1:1:93:5) was added. The cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C without CO2. Finally, the percentages of SA-β-gal-positive cells were observed and calculated under a light microscope.

Immunofluorescence detection of microtubules

hAECs (3 × 104 cells/35 mm well) were cultured in glass-bottomed cell culture dishes. Microtubules were visualized after 48 h of cell transfection. The cells were washed briefly in PBS, treated with cold methanol (-20 °C) for 5 min, rinsed three times in PBS, and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin/PBS at 37 °C for 60 min. The cells were incubated overnight with an anti-α-tubulin antibody (1:500 dilution; Abcam) at 4°C. After rinsing three times with PBS, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:200 dilution; Beyotime Biotechnology) secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After three washes, the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 8 min and observed using a Zeiss Cell Discoverer 7.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay

After treatment, the cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 2–8 °C at 1,000 × g for 20 min to remove impurities and cellular debris. The levels of inflammatory factors IL-1β and IL-6 in the culture supernatants were measured using ELISA kits (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). The OD value of each well was measured at 450 nm.

EdU assay

The hAECs (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into 24-well plates (Corning). At 24 h post-transfection, staining was performed using the Cell-LightTM EdU Apollo567 in Vitro Kit (RiboBio) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and cell proliferation was observed and analyzed using an EVOS M7000.

Cell counting kit-8 assay

hAECs (5 × 103 cells/well) were cultured in a 96-well plate and incubated for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after transfection. A 10 µL CCK-8 (DOJINDO, Laboratories, Japan) solution was added to each well and incubated for 2.5 h at 37 °C. Finally, the absorbance values were measured daily for four consecutive days at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Western blot

After 48 h of transfection, total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio). The total protein was separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gels, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, and blocked with a skimmed milk powder solution for 1 h at 37 °C. The membranes were then incubated with KATNAL1 (1:500; Santa Cruz), P16 (1:1000, Proteintech), cyclin D1 (1:1000, Proteintech), cyclin-dependent kinase 6 (CDK6) (1:1000, Proteintech), alpha-tubulin (1:2000, Proteintech), or β-actin antibodies (1:2000, Proteintech) at 4 °C overnight. The next day, after washing with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 detergent, the membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG/HRP (1:2000, Proteintech) or goat anti-mouse IgG/HRP (1:2000, Proteintech) at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, all results were visualized using ELC reagents (Epizyme, China).

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The differences between the two groups were compared using a two-tailed, unpaired Student t-test. Differences among three or more groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was set at a p-value of < 0.05.
