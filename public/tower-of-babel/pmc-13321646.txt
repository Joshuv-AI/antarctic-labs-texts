Exploring the phenotypic and genotypic spectrum of spinal muscular atrophy in Bangladeshi children
Authors: Jobaida Parvin, Husnea Ara Khan, Narayan Chandra Saha, Seikh Azimul Hoque, Mohammad Monir Hossain, Dipa Saha, Nusrat Shams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a monogenic neuromuscular disorder caused by SMN1 gene deletion and classified by clinical severity. The objective of this study was to evaluate the genotypic and phenotypic spectrum in SMA in Bangladeshi children.This cross-sectional prospective study was conducted in Pediatric Neurology Department, National Institute of Neurosciences and Hospital of Bangladesh from January 2019 and December 2022. A total of sixty four cases with clinically suspected SMA were enrolled. Genetic confirmation was done using MLPA.Genetic confirmation was achieved in 48 (75%) patients. Homozygous deletion of SMN1 exons 7 and 8 was detected in 44 (68.75%) cases, while isolated exon 7 deletion was found in 4 (6.25%) cases. No deletion was identified in 16 (25%) cases. Among genetically confirmed patients, SMA type I was most common (54%), followed by type II (40%) and type III (6%). The mean age of onset was significantly earlier in SMA type I compared with types II and III (p < 0.05). A significant inverse correlation was observed between SMN2 copy number and disease severity (Spearman’s rho = 0.825, p < 0.001). Most type I patients had two SMN2 copies, whereas type II and III patients predominantly had three or more copies. Respiratory complications and mortality were predominantly in type I patients. Two patients received risdiplam and two underwent gene replacement therapy (onasemnogene abeparvovec).In conclusion, A significant genotype–phenotype correlation exists between SMN2 copy number and clinical severity among Bangladeshi children with SMA. Limited access to disease-modifying therapy highlights the need for early diagnosis and improved treatment accessibility in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Spinal muscular atrophy, Phenotype, Genotype, Spectrum, Bangladeshi Children

Introduction

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is an autosomal recessive neuromuscular disorder [1]. It affects the anterior horn of the spinal cord, leading to progressive degeneration of motor neuron which result in profound muscle weakness, followed by respiratory failure and, ultimately death in severe cases [2]. The incidence of this disorder is approximately 1/10,000–1/11,000 live births [3] and carrier frequency is about 1/35 to 1/50 [4]. Over 90% of SMA cases are caused by homozygous deletion of the Survival Motor Neuron 1 gene (SMN1) [5–7].

SMA is clinically classified into four types based on age of onset and motor abilities. SMA type I is the most severe form, starting within the first six months of life, affected children are unable to sit independently and typically die from respiratory failure or aspiration before the age of two [8]. Type II patients who are able sit but not walk unaided, symptoms appear between 7 and 18 month and their life expectancy varies depending on respiratory management. Type III Patients (juvenile SMA) can walk and are subdivided into type IIIa or type IIIb according to the onset of weakness before or after 3 years of age, respectively, and the lifespan is generally not reduced. Type IV (Adult form) is a less common type appear in the second or third decades generally with a normal life expectancy [9, 10].

Patients with SMA possess at least one copy of the SMN2 (Survival motor neuron 2) which differs from SMN1 by just sixteen nucleotides [11, 12]. A single base substitution (c.840 C > T) in exon 7 of SMN2 leads to alternative splicing, causing most transcripts to lack exon 7 and produce a truncated, unstable protein [13, 14]. About 10% of SMN2 transcripts generate full-length SMN protein. An increased number of SMN2 gene copies [3, 15] is associated with a less severe disease phenotype [9]. The number of SMN2 can vary from one to five copy numbers and acts as a phenotypic modifier of the disease severity. Most SMA type I patients have two SMN2 copies, while those with type II and III have three or more [3, 16].

SMA is a monogenic neurological disorder that can be effectively treated by novel therapeutic strategies, such as antisense oligonucleotide or gene replacement therapy [17, 18]. These therapies open a new door for a child who has never been able to sit or walk to do both [19]. Successful implementation of these treatments depends on a deep understanding of the genetic basis, genotype–phenotype correlations, SMN protein function, disease mechanisms, and optimal treatment timing [20, 21].

In recent years, lots of research work has been conducted worldwide on SMA but very few studies have been done in Bangladesh [22]. In this study, we aim to explore the genetic and clinical characteristics of SMA among Bangladeshi children, focusing on the correlation between SMN gene variants and phenotypic presentation. The findings will contribute to a better understanding of the disease in this region and support improved diagnostic and management approaches in resource-limited healthcare settings.

Method and materials

Study participants

This study was a cross-sectional prospective study. A total of sixty four Bangladeshi children (aged 1 month to 16 years) with clinically suspected SMA who attended Pediatric Neurology Department, National Institute of Neurosciences and Hospital between the period of January 2019 and December 2022 were enrolled. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants under the age of 16 years prior to enrollment in the study. Patients with motor delay with paucity of movement with intact consciousness, areflexia, and normal serum CPK (Creatinine kinase) level were included in this study. Patient with syndromic appearance, impaired cognition, seizure with myopathic pattern EMG were excluded from the study.

Phenotypic and clinical Assessment

After enrolment, detailed history and clinical examination were done. History related to the age of onset, alertness, paucity of movement, motor delay, repeated respiratory infection, history of consanguinity of parents, affected siblings and sibling death were recorded [23]. Thorough neurological and developmental assessments were done. Serum creatinine kinase, Nerve Conduction Study (NCS) and Electromyography (EMG) were performed in all patients [24]. Children with SMA were classified into different SMA types by age of onset and motor ability achieved: SMA type I (onset 0–6 months, non-sitters), SMA type II (onset 7–18 months, sitters but never walked), SMA type III (onset > 18 months, walkers), and SMA type IV (onset > 10 years, walkers).

Pretest counselling was done, and written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants under the age of 16 years prior to enrollment in the study.

Genotypic analysis

Genetic confirmation of SMA was performed using multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) to detect homozygous deletions of the SMN1 gene and determine SMN2 copy number [25, 26]. DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using the Qiagen DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following standard protocols. Copy number changes in exon7 and 8 of the SMN1 and SMN2 genes were detected by MLPA. Each MLPA probe consisted of two hemi-probes that bind to adjacent sites on the target sequence. Upon ligation and subsequent PCR amplification, distinct MLPA probe generated an amplicon with a unique length that was separated and quantified by capillary electrophoresis. Copy number differences of various exons between test and control DNA samples were detected by analyzing MLPA peak patterns [27, 28].

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic, clinical, and genetic data. Continuous variables, such as age at onset and motor function scores, were presented as means ± standard deviations. Categorical variables, such as SMA subtype and SMN2 copy number, were reported as frequencies and percentages. Analysis was performed with SPSS software, versions 22.0. One-way ANOVA was used for continuous comparisons. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was applied to assess the association between SMN2 copy number and disease severity (ordinal SMA subtype I–III). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical clearance

Ethical measures were taken throughout the study period to maintain a high standard of confidentiality and anonymity among the participants. Formal approval was taken from the institutional review board (IRB) of National Institute of Neurosciences & Hospital (IRB/NINS/2022/146). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants under the age of 16 years prior to enrollment in the study. The study protocol emphasized strict confidentiality and participant welfare throughout, following the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, 2013.

Result

Genotypes of the subjects

In this study, 48 (75%) out of 64 patients were confirmed by MLPA, where 68.75% had homozygous deletion of both exons 7 and 8, and 6.25% had only deletion of exon7 in SMN I gene. No deletion was found in 25% of cases. (Table 1). Among the genetically confirmed cases, SMA type I was the most common (54%), followed by type II (40%) and type III (9%). (Fig. 1)

SMN2 copy number distribution and genotype–phenotype correlation

Analysis of SMN2 copy number revealed that majority of study cases had two SMN2 copy (47.91%) followed by three copy (39.6%). (Fig. 2). Most children with SMA type I (84%) had two copies of SMN2 (84%), whereas the majority of SMA type II patients (84.2%) had three SMN2 copies. All SMA type III patients carried four copies of SMN2. A significant inverse correlation was observed between SMN2 copy number and disease severity ( Spearman’s rho = 0.825, p < 0.001). (Fig. 3).

Association of clinical characteristics and SMA type of the subjects

The mean age of symptom onset differed significantly among SMA subtypes. Children with SMA type I presented at a significantly earlier age (3.18 ± 1.10 months) compared with those with type II (13.37 ± 6.61 months) and type III (68.00 ± 6.92 months), with this difference reaching statistical significance (p < 0.05). Similarly, the mean age at first hospital visit was lowest in SMA type I and highest in SMA type III. Although males constituted a slight majority overall, females were more frequently represented within SMA type I and type III subgroups. A history of parental consanguinity was documented in 18.75% of genetically confirmed cases, predominantly among SMA type I patients. A positive family history of similar illness was also more common in SMA type I. SMA type I patients failed to achieve independent sitting. In contrast, the majority of type II (89.73%) and all type III patients achieved independent sitting, while independent walking was observed exclusively in type III patients. Recurrent respiratory infections were most frequent in SMA type I (42.30%), and 15.4% required ventilatory support. Mortality during the follow-up period occurred in SMA type I patients (30.8%) and type II patients (5.3%). (Table 2)

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the genotypic and phenotypic spectrum of Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA) in Bangladeshi children, shedding light on population-specific features that have important implications for diagnosis, prognosis, and management.

This study has revealed a correlation between the clinical types of SMA and the genotype-phenotype of SMN1 deletions. In our study of 64 SMA patients, 48 (75%) were confirmed by MLPA. Among these, 68.75% had a homozygous deletion of both exons 7 and 8, while 6.25% had only a deletion of exon 7. This finding is consistent with previous studies [7, 29, 30] indicating that over 90% of SMA cases are attributable to SMN1 deletions. The remaining 16 children (25%) showed no deletion in the SMNI gene, indicating that the genetic basis for these cases remains undetermined. The higher rate of SMN1 deletion-negative cases in this cohort likely reflects the presence of point mutations that are not detectable by MLPA, together with enrollment of clinically suspected but genetically distinct SMA-like disorders.

In our study, SMA type I was the most frequent subtype (54%), followed by type II (40%) and type III (6%). This predominance of type I aligns with the research conducted by Calucho et al. [31], in Spain, which reported type I as the most common presentation. However, regional variations exist; for example, a study from India [32] identified SMA type II as the predominant form. The higher proportion of SMA type I in our cohort may suggest referral bias, as severely affected infants are more likely to present to tertiary centers. Additionally, milder forms may remain underdiagnosed in rural settings due to limited awareness and access to specialist care. Consistent with the global SMA cohort, the majority of participants in our study had two SMN2 copy numbers (47.9%), followed by those with three SMN2 copy numbers (39.6%).

A key finding of this study is the strong inverse relationship between SMN2 copy number and disease severity. Most SMA type I patients carried two copies of SMN2, whereas SMA type II and III patients predominantly had three or more copies. This observation supports the established role of SMN2 as a critical phenotypic modifier that partially compensates for SMN1 deficiency by producing limited amounts of functional SMN protein. This distribution mirrors findings from Feldkötter et al. [33], who demonstrated that increasing SMN2 copy number predicts milder phenotype and improved survival. Similar genotype–phenotype relationships have been confirmed in European and Asian populations. However, overlap in SMN2 copy numbers across clinical subtypes was observed, indicating that SMN2 dosage alone does not fully determine disease severity [7, 33–36]. Additional genetic modifiers, epigenetic regulation, and environmental factors are likely contributors to clinical variability.

The mean age of symptom onset differed significantly among SMA subtypes. The earlier age of onset observed in SMA type I compared with types II and III reflects the aggressive natural history of infantile-onset SMA. Notably, the gap between symptom onset and first specialist consultation was substantial, particularly in severe cases. This delay highlights gaps in disease awareness, limited access to neuromuscular services, and the absence of structured screening programs in Bangladesh.

A modest male predominance was observed in the cohort, a pattern reported in several regional studies [37–39]. This is likely attributable to sociocultural factors influencing healthcare-seeking behavior rather than true biological differences. Parental consanguinity was documented in 18.75% of genetically confirmed cases and was more frequent among SMA type I. This finding is consisted with the previous study conducted by Yalcintepe et al. [40] This reflects the autosomal recessive inheritance pattern of SMA and emphasizes the importance of culturally sensitive genetic counseling, particularly in communities where consanguineous marriage is common. Respiratory complications and mortality were predominantly observed in type I, reflecting the severe natural course of infantile-onset SMA.

Only a small proportion of patients(n = 4) had access to disease-modifying therapy, reflecting financial and infrastructural constraints. Increased awareness, newborn screening, and improved therapeutic access are essential to improve outcomes.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic study describing both the clinical and genetic characteristics of SMA in Bangladeshi children. The data provide valuable region-specific evidence and establish a foundation for future research, early diagnostic strategies, and policy planning.

Despite its strengths, this study has limitations, including a relatively small sample size, single-center design, and lack of SMN1 sequencing in deletion-negative cases due to financial constraints. Additionally, longitudinal follow-up data were not available. Future multicenter studies incorporating comprehensive molecular diagnostics and long-term outcome assessment will be essential to better define the natural history of SMA in Bangladesh.

Conclusion

This study provides foundational data on the clinical and genetic profile of SMA among Bangladeshi children. The findings confirm a significant genotype–phenotype correlation, with SMN2 copy number playing a key modifying role in disease severity. While a small number of patients accessed disease-modifying therapy, most received supportive care. Early genetic diagnosis and improved access to advanced therapies are critical for improving outcomes in resource-limited settings.
