Identifying causal relationships between 35 blood and urine biomarkers and urologic cancers: MR-meta combined with Bayesian colocalization Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Wenxin An, Chengyi Zhao, Yaru Wang, Yinghui Zhang, Zhi Qiao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlood and urine biomarkers have been associated with urologic tumors, but their causal relationship with urologic tumors is unclear.MethodsWe performed a bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis of the association between 35 blood and urine biomarkers and urological tumors in our discovery cohort. A Bayesian weighting approach was used to validate the positive results identified in the discovery cohort, and steiger filtering analysis was used to distinguish causality from reverse causality. Bayesian colocalization analysis was used to analyze which single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) specifically co-located between the positive blood and urine biomarkers and the disease phenotypes were driven, and MR-positive results from the discovery cohort and the validation cohort were combined using the MR-meta method.ResultsSeveral blood and urine biomarkers were found to be significantly and causally associated with urologic cancers. Notably, calcium (OR: 1.34, 95%CI 1.10–1.63, P = 0.0040) and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) (OR: 0.81, 95%CI 0.70–0.95, P = 0.0092) were associated with bladder cancer; gamma glutamyl transferase (GGT) (OR: 0.91, 95%CI 0.83–0.99, P = 0.0209), lipoprotein A (Lp(a)) (OR: 1.12, 95%CI 1.01–1.24, P = 0.0399), and insulinlike growth factor 1 (IGF 1) (OR: 1.10, 95%CI 1.01–1.20, P = 0.0220) were linked to prostate cancer (PCa); non albumin protein (OR: 0.78, 95%CI 0.65–0.93, P = 0.0065) and total protein (OR: 0.80, 95%CI 0.64–0.99, P = 0.0380) were linked to renal cancer; and apolipoprotein A (Apo-A) (OR: 0.56, 95%CI 0.32–0.98, P = 0.0426) and urate (OR:1.89, 95%CI 1.03–3.47, P = 0.0399) were associated with renal pelvis cancer. These associations were validated in an independent cohort, with GGT, IGF 1, and Lp(a) being consistently linked to PCa.ConclusionThis study identified significant biomarkers associated with urological cancers in blood and urine. These include GGT, IGF 1, and Lp(a), which are strong biomarkers for PCa. In addition, the findings of this study provide evidence for a handful of risk and protective factors for the development of urologic cancers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01493-0.Keywords: Blood and urine biomarkers, Urologic cancer, MR-meta, Bayesian colocalization, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Urological tumors present a significant threat to global health. The Global Burden of Disease Study (2019) reports incidence rates for bladder cancer (BCa), prostate cancer (PCa), and kidney cancer (KCa) at 6.5, 17.4, and 4.6 per 100,000 person-years, respectively, representing 9.81% of all cancer cases worldwide [1]. Transitional cell carcinoma of the renal pelvis makes up 7% of kidney tumors, while transitional cell carcinoma of the ureter constitutes 4% of upper urinary tract tumors [2]. In 2020, PCa was the second most common cancer among men globally, with bladder and kidney cancers ranking sixth and ninth, respectively. In the United States, PCa was the most frequently diagnosed cancer in men in 2023, with its incidence more than double that of lung cancer [3]. The incidence of KCa is rising in the U.S., while BCa incidence is increasing globally [4]. Additionally, the occurrence of regional and distant-stage PCa is also rising in men over 50 [5]. Despite the elevated incidence of urologic cancers, the exact mechanisms and causes of the initiation and progression of urological cancers are unknown, and a better understanding of the etiology as well as the risk and protective factors of the diseases is needed. Blood and urine biomarkers can be used as effective biomarkers for identifying disease and offer the possibility of diagnosis and triage of urologic cancers. The detection of relevant positive biomarkers in blood and urine as a diagnostic indicator of urologic cancer can be highly suggestive of a higher risk of developing a particular urologic cancer. Identification of suitable biomarkers has crucial clinical implications for the diagnosis of urological cancers.

The UK Biobank (UKB) study is a longitudinal cohort investigation that assessed the genetic factors influencing 35 blood and urine biomarkers across 363,228 participants [6]. Multiple observational studies have explored the relationships between various UKB biomarkers and urological cancers, identifying notable associations with several of these biomarkers. Among these, nuclear matrix protein 22, bladder tumor antigen, and fibrinogen degradation products are FDA-approved diagnostic biomarkers for BCa [7]. Nonetheless, observational studies may be affected by residual confounding and reverse causation. Mendelian randomization (MR) enhances these studies by utilizing genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to better determine causal relationships between exposures and outcomes.

The aim of this study was to use the MR framework to explore the causal associations between the genetic predictive levels of UKB blood and urine biomarkers and genetic predisposition to bladder, prostate, kidney, and renal pelvis cancers.

Materials and methods

Study design

We employed 35 blood and urine biomarkers from the UKB study (n = 363,228) as exposure variables, with data on PCa (15,199 cases, 131,266 controls), BCa (2193 cases, 314,193 controls), renal pelvis cancer (138 cases, 314,193 controls), and KCa (2372 cases, 314,193 controls) from a FinnGen database serving as outcome data for the discovery cohort. The IEU OPEN Genome Wide Association Studies (GWAS) database for BCa (1279 cases, 372,016 controls) and PCa (79,148 cases, 61,106 controls) was used as outcome data for the validation cohort.

Figure 1 provides an overview of the study design. The UKB study is a large prospective cohort study with more than 500,000 people aged > 40 years recruited between 2006 and 2010. We extracted GWAS summary statistics for blood and urine biomarkers as exposure factors from the UKB database (https://github.com/rivas-lab/biomarkers/). The FinnGen consortium is a study collecting health and genetic data based on FinnGen health registries. FinnGen R10 data and genome-wide association analyses were used for each trait. The analyses took into account sex, age, genotyping batch, and the first 10 genetic principal components.

The overview of study design. GWAS Genome Wide Association Study, LD linkage disequilibrium, SNP single nucleotide polymorphism, BCa bladder cancer, Pca prostate cancer, KCa kidney cancer

All analyses were performed using the “TwoSampleMR” package (version 0.5.7) and the “LDSCR” package (version 0.1.0) in R software (version 4.3.3). We employed the ieugwasr R packages and the GitHub repository (https://github.com/verena-zuber/) for MR-BMA.

Instruments variable selection

For each exposure, we selected associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10–8) and ensured their independence by removing those in linkage disequilibrium using the PLINK method (R2 < 0.001, clumping distance = 10,000 kb). We then harmonized the directions of the effect alleles between exposures and outcomes. In all of our MR analyses, the SNPs must satisfy three assumptions to be considered valid IVs. Genetic variants must (1) strongly associate with the exposure (relevance assumption), (2) be independent of confounders (independence assumption), and (3) affect the outcome only through their effect on the exposure (exclusion restriction assumption). F-statistics were employed to test the strength of IVs, mitigating potential weak instrument bias that could confound causal association estimates [8].

Statistical analysis

In this study, MR analysis involving blood and urine biomarkers linked to individual SNPs was conducted using the Wald ratio method. Multiple tests, including the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method, weighted median method, and MR-Egger regression test, were conducted for blood and urine biomarkers with multiple associated SNPs. The results of IVW method are plausible if the three assumptions of MR are satisfied for the SNPs [9]. Cochrane’s Q test was performed to scrutinize SNP-associated heterogeneity for each blood and urine biomarker. In cases where significant heterogeneity emerged (P < 0.05), a fixed-effects IVW model was applied; in contrast, a random-effects IVW model was applied.

Additionally, the MR-Egger intercept test and leave-one-out analysis were performed for sensitivity analysis. The p-value of the MR-Egger intercept test functioned as an indicator of horizontal pleiotropy (statistically significant at P < 0.05) [10]. Leave-one-out analysis was used to discern the potential pleiotropic effects stemming from individual SNPs. Finally, reverse MR analysis was performed to verify the existence of reverse causality among BCa, PCa, KCa, renal pelvis cancer, and blood and urine biomarkers.

Forward MR analysis and the Bayesian weighting method

We used 35 blood and urine biomarkers from the UKB database as exposure factors [6]; GWAS data from the FinnGen database for bladder, prostate, kidney, and renal pelvis cancers as the outcome data for the discovery cohort; and the IEU OPEN GWAS database for BCa ieu-b-4874 and PCa ieu-b-85 as outcome data for the validation cohort, validating positive blood and urine biomarkers for the discovery cohort. To make the results of the positive biomarkers made by the discovery cohort more reliable, we performed Bayesian weighted mendelian randomization (BWMR) for validation. BWMR is designed to provide more accurate and plausible causal inferences in the presence of confounders and weak IVs [11].

Reverse MR analysis

We performed reverse MR analysis using GWAS data from the FinnGen database for bladder, prostate, kidney, and renal pelvis cancers as exposures and 35 blood and urine biomarkers from the UKB database as outcome data for the discovery cohort to rule out the effect of reverse causality between exposures and outcomes.

Steiger filtering analysis

The steiger filtering method was applied to the MR associations that survived the multiple-testing threshold using the “TwoSampleMR” R package to test whether the results could be distorted by reverse causality. Results are presented as categorical variables to aid comprehension: ‘true’ if the effect direction is from exposure to outcome at P < 0.05; ‘false’ if reversed at P < 0.05; ‘uncertain’ if P ≥ 0.05 [12].

Bayesian colocalization

Results that survived the multiple-testing threshold in the MR analysis were evaluated using Bayesian colocalization analysis to estimate the posterior probability of each genetic locus containing a single variant affecting both the blood and urine biomarkers and the urological cancer-related trait (rather than the variant being shared coincidentally due to correlation through linkage disequilibrium). The colocalization was tested by using the ‘coloc’ R package (http://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/coloc). The coloc R package provides the posterior probability for five hypotheses (0, 1, 2, 3, and 4) of whether a single variant is shared between two traits, where hypothesis 4 is that both traits are associated with the same genetic variant in the region. A posterior probability of hypothesis 4 greater than 0.8 was defined as strong evidence of colocalization. Notably, a limitation of Bayesian colocalization is the assumption of a single shared common causal SNP; however, in reality, genetic loci may contain several causal SNPs [12].

MR-meta analysis

We pooled the same positive blood and urine biomarkers analyzed using the IVW-MR and BWMR methods in the discovery and validation cohorts. Meta-merging MR results from different databases can reduce the bias introduced by the database and improve the confidence of the study results, address inconsistencies in the study results, and improve the effect estimates.

Results

Forward MR results

Blood and urine biomarkers found to have a positive causal association with BCa in the discovery cohort were calcium (OR: 1.34, 95%CI 1.10–1.63, P = 0.0040) and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) (OR: 0.81, 95%CI 0.70–0.95, P = 0.0092). The blood and urine biomarkers with a positive causal association with PCa were GGT (OR: 0.91, 95%CI 0.83–0.99, P = 0.0209),Lipoprotein A (Lp(a)) (OR: 1.12, 95%CI 1.01–1.24, P = 0.0399),insulinlike growth factor 1 (IGF 1) (OR: 1.10, 95%CI 1.01–1.20, P = 0.0220) and non albumin protein(OR: 0.91, 95%CI 0.82–1.01, P = 0.0471). The blood and urine biomarkers with positive causal associations with renal cancer were non albumin protein (OR 0.78, 95%CI 0.65–0.93, P = 0.0065) and total protein (OR: 0.80, 95%CI 0.64–0.99, P = 0.0380). The blood and urine biomarkers with positive causal associations with renal pelvis cancer were apolipoprotein A (Apo-A) (OR: 0.56, 95%CI 0.32–0.98, P = 0.0426) and urate (OR: 1.89, 95%CI 1.03–3.47, P = 0.0399) (Fig. 2). MR-meta results

When the positive blood and urine biomarkers derived from the discovery cohort were validated by the validation cohort, only PCa yielded consistent findings, and GGT, IGF 1, and Lp(a) significantly associated with PCa in both the discovery and validation cohorts. The positive result in the BCa validation cohort was non albumin protein (OR: 0.99, 95%CI 0.99–1.00; P = 0.0461). The validation of positive blood and urine biomarkers for renal cancer and renal pelvis cancer discovery cohorts was not performed because of the lack of GWAS data for renal cancer and renal pelvis cancer.

The MR-positive results of the three blood and urine biomarkers, GGT, IGF 1, and Lp(a) significantly associated with PCa in the discovery and validation cohorts, were merged separately using the IVW and BWMR methods. The results for all three biomarkers after MR-meta merging were less than 0.05, further increasing the confidence of our findings (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Our study provides insight into the complex role of blood and urine biomarkers in different urologic cancers, emphasizing their potential role in risk assessment and targeted therapy. The results of our MR analyses enrich the current understanding of etiological pathways and support the growing evidence that certain blood and urine biomarkers are associated with specific cancer types.

Biomarkers for BCa. Case–control studies have found a positive correlation between calcium intake and BCa risk (OR: 1.77; 95% CI 1.00–3.15; P = 0.049), suggesting that calcium is a risk factor for BCa [13]. Our MR studies have shown that calcium is a risk factor for BCa and that the risk of developing BCa may increase by 34% for each standard deviation (SD) increase in calcium levels in the blood and urine. Interactions between calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, and vitamin D are also important [14]. High dietary vitamin D with low calcium and moderate phosphorus intake is associated with a reduced risk of BCa [15]. Additionally, in a study of 902 BCa patients, those with bone metastases had higher serum levels of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and calcium and lower hemoglobin (HB). ALP > 116 U/L, HB < 37.5 g/L, and calcium > 2.54 mmol/L were identified as risk factors for bone metastases in these patients [16]. However, the above study had some limitations, such as being retrospective and conducted at only one medical center. Studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings. However, this MR study of ours complements the above studies by drawing conclusions that are consistent with them.

Furthermore, our findings indicate that SHBG in the blood and urine is a protective factor against BCa, with each SD increase in SHBG associated with a 19% reduction in the risk of developing BCa. SHBG has also been linked to reduced risks of colorectal, breast, endometrial, and prostate cancers [17, 18]. MR studies suggest that high SHBG levels correlate with lower risks of breast, prostate, and endometrial cancer. However, observational analysis of 333,530 UKB participants found little evidence linking circulating SHBG levels to colorectal cancer risk [19]. Further research is required to understand the biological mechanisms underlying these associations.

For biomarkers of PCa, our MR study identified GGT, Lp (a), and IGF 1 as important biomarkers for PCa. These associations were confirmed in a validation cohort. GGT, although less reported in cancer research, is associated with coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, and obesity [20]. Genetically predicted Lp(a) levels correlate with higher risks of overall, advanced, and early onset PCa [21], suggesting that reducing Lp(a) levels could lower PCa risk. In other words, the higher the Lp(a) concentration, the greater is the risk of PCa. Preliminary evidence suggests that LDL levels may play a role in the etiology of PCa [22]. However, another study showed that PCSK9 inhibitors might mediate this risk reduction by lowering Lp(a) rather than LDL cholesterol [23]. The IGF pathway is also crucial, with higher IGF-I, IGF-II, and IGFBP-3 levels linked to increased PCa risk [24], whereas IGFBP-1 is protective [25]. These findings suggest the need for further research into the modifiable determinants of IGF-I and the interventional role that lowering IGF-I may play in reducing PCa risk. The results of our MR study showed that IGF-I in blood and urine is a risk factor for PCa and is causally associated with PCa. In a large prospective analytic study involving 200,000 men, higher levels of IGF 1 and free testosterone were associated with an increased risk of PCa [26], providing strong evidence for a causal role of IGF-I in PCa development. Furthermore, a meta-analysis showed a positive association between IGF 1 levels and PCa risk [27]. Further studies are needed to confirm this finding. IGF 1 is also associated with other cancer diseases, and there is evidence to support a possible causal relationship between elevated IGF 1 levels and increased risk of colorectal cancer. A positive correlation was observed between IGF 1 levels and colorectal cancer [28].

Regarding blood and urine biomarkers of renal cancer, our MR study identified non albumin protein and total protein as protective factors against renal cancer. However, the literature on these biomarkers is limited. Further studies are required to confirm these findings. In contrast, a Minnesota study found that high protein intake, particularly from red meat and high-protein foods, increased KCa risk [29]. However, a pooled analysis of 13 prospective studies found no association between protein intake and KCa risk [30]. Furthermore, a preoperative study of 895 KCa patients showed an association between high total protein levels in serum and advanced disease [31]. These findings highlight the complexity of protein-related biomarkers in KCa and underscore the need for further investigation to clarify these associations and their clinical implications.

In our MR study, Apo-A was identified as a protective factor against renal pelvis cancer, while urate was found to be a risk factor. However, literature on these associations is sparse. One study reported that Apo-A1 expression in upper urinary tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) was positively correlated with the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and negatively correlated with tumor-infiltrating T cells [32]. This suggests that Apo-A1 may have immunomodulatory effects on the tumor microenvironment, making it a potential target for UTUC therapy. Our findings align with this finding, showing that higher Apo-A levels are associated with a 43% reduction in the risk of renal pelvis cancer.

While some studies found a negative association between urate levels and breast cancer susceptibility [33], our study indicates that genetically predicted elevated urate levels are associated with an increased risk of renal pelvis cancer. This suggests that urate may contribute to the etiology of renal pelvis cancer.

These results highlight the need for further research to explore the mechanisms underlying these associations and to validate the potential of Apo-A and urate as biomarkers for renal pelvis cancer.

While the results of our study are rich, there are certain limitations to consider. The data from our study were limited to European populations, which hindered our ability to generalize to populations of other ethnic backgrounds. Furthermore, we only obtained GWAS data from the FinnGen and IEU OPEN GWAS databases for prostate, bladder, renal pelvis, and renal cancers, for which we performed MR analyses without any clinical data on tumor staging. Consequently, we have not yet established a causal relationship between blood and urine biomarkers and the early or late stage of a specific urological cancer. We haven’t provided guidance on early tumor detection in urological cancers. The MR method relies on some basic assumptions, such as no confounders and no genetic diversity, but these assumptions are difficult to fully satisfy in practical studies and may affect the accuracy of causal inference.

The strengths of our study include the application of many univariate sensitivity analyses to assess the validity of hypothesis IVs and to limit the potential bias of pleiotropy. To investigate reverse causality, we performed a bidirectional MR analysis of the association between 35 blood and urine biomarkers and susceptibility to urological cancer. Biomarker samples were collected prospectively from large samples, and we accounted for population stratification by restricting our study to European participants and adjusting for major genetic components. Most of our results support those of previous studies that used positive controls for our methods. Our study allowed the generation of hypotheses that would enable further studies to target associations between biomarkers of interest for which there was little prior evidence of association, such as non albumin protein found in renal cancer and urate found in renal pelvis cancer. The validation of the positive MR results using the BWMR approach enhanced our confidence in the strength of the findings. The use of MR-meta analysis to analyze and summarize the positive blood and urine biomarkers in urological cancers analyzed by our MR further enhanced the credibility of the findings. The shared genetic variant loci for Lp(a) and PCa were identified using Bayesian colocalization.

Conclusion

We conducted the most comprehensive and largest exploratory study to investigate the association between all UKB biomarkers and urological cancers. Our study confirms the association between blood and urine biomarkers and urologic cancers, validating previous ideas and generating new hypotheses about non albumin protein found in PCa, KCa, and urate found in renal pelvis cancer. Further studies on the relationship between the newly discovered hypotheses are needed to better understand the underlying mechanisms of the association between blood and urine biomarkers and the risk of developing urological cancers in a comprehensive manner.
