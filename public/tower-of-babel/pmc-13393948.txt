Phenotypic and genomic features of virulent mecA-carrying Staphylococcus hyicus isolated from a pig farm with fatal exudative epidermitis
Authors: Lei Lei, Mengyan Jiao, Ruidong Zhai, Xinlu Xu, Zanmiao Chen, Tingting Zhang, Jianheng Song, Huangming Zhang, Yue Han, Qin Wu, Jiali Xu, Luca Simone Cocolin, Kalliopi Rantsiou, Changyong Cheng, Houhui Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStaphylococcus hyicus is recognized as one of causative agents of porcine exudative epidermitis in piglets. However, research on clinical pathogenic S. hyicus remains limited. In this study, multidrug-resistant S. hyicus was isolated from a large-scale pig farm with cases of fatal exudative epidermitis in piglets. By characterizing the phenotypes and genotypes of S. hyicus isolates, we provide insights for clinical management of exudative epidermitis.MethodsTwo 21-day-old piglets with clinical signs underwent necropsy and histopathological examination. 48 samples from lactating sows and suckling piglets were collected for pathogen identification. Antimicrobial and disinfectant susceptibility of the isolates was determined using broth microdilution, as well as whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was used to identify antimicrobial resistance genes. Moreover, comparative genomic analysis with public genomes in the Genbank database was performed. The virulence of mecA-carrying S. hyicus was evaluated using the Galleria mellonella infection model.ResultsExudative epidermitis was first observed in 3-day-old piglets. Through necropsy, pathogen isolation, and other diagnostic approaches, S. hyicus was preliminarily identified as the primary causative agent of this episode, and 23 S. hyicus were isolated from samples. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing indicated most of isolated S. hyicus were susceptible to doxycycline, vancomycin and linezolid, while resistant to florfenicol, erythromycin, spectinomycin, amoxicillin, ceftiofur, enrofloxacin that are commonly used for pigs. Twelve resistance genes were identified by WGS, including aadD, ant(6)-Ia, aph(2'')-Ia, blaZ, erm(B), erm(C), fexA, lnu(B), lsa(E), mecA, tet(L), and tet(M). Compared with 39 GenBank genomes, mecA-carrying S. hyicus in our study carried more resistance genes and exhC islands closely related to previous Chinese strains. Based on our findings, we implemented a targeted treatment protocol that brought the outbreak under control and reduced piglet mortality to below 5%.ConclusionThis study successfully isolated pathogenic mecA-carrying S. hyicus responsible for high mortality in piglets, characterized its drug resistance phenotypes and genotypes, offering valuable insights for the prevention and control of this pathogen in piglets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05581-x.

Introduction

Pigs are the primary livestock raised for human consumption in China [1, 2]. Recent expansion and intensification of pig production have led to increasing stocking densities and the reemergence of several opportunistic pathogens [3, 4]. Staphylococcus hyicus, one of causative agents of exudative epidermitis, is now distributed globally. Several reports indicated that 27.5% of nursing piglet farms in the United States and 16.5% of piglet herds in the United Kingdom have experienced outbreaks of S. hyicus [5]. As an opportunistic and zoonotic pathogen, S. hyicus is commonly found on the mucous membranes and skin of both livestock and humans, with a carriage rate in healthy pigs over 30% [6]. When piglets sustain injuries, such as those caused by fighting or biting, this bacterium can invade through wounds and progress from localized infections to broader dissemination [7, 8]. Infected piglets may rapidly develop systemic septicemia, characterized by extensive skin ulceration and dehydration, resulting in poor prognosis and mortality rates. Moreover, approximately 10%−20% mortality rates were present in 2-week-old piglets. Furthermore, surviving piglets frequently exhibit growth retardation [9].

In recent years, the extensive use of antibiotics in intensive pig farms has contributed to the emergence of multi-drug resistant bacteria in China [10]. Moreover, drug-resistant Gram-positive cocci, including Staphylococcus spp. and Enterococcus spp. are frequently detected in pig-farm environment that serve as reservoirs of resistance genes to antibiotics commonly used in swine production [11, 12]. Once horizontal transfer of resistance genes occurs among different Gram-positive cocci [13], S. hyicus may acquire the resistance genes [14]. Notably, drug-resistant S. hyicus increases the difficulty of control exudative epidermitis, and therapeutic failure is frequently observed when β-lactam antibiotics such as amoxicillin and ceftiofur, as well as macrolides like erythromycin were used [15, 16].

However, there are relatively few literature reports regarding the antimicrobial resistance profiles and virulence characteristics of S. hyicus in China [17]. Therefore, this study collected samples from a pig farm experiencing an outbreak of exudative epidermitis for pathogen isolation and identification, combined with clinical and pathological examinations of diseased piglets, and identified S. hyicus as the predominant causative agent of this outbreak. The isolates were subsequently subjected to virulence analysis and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. In addition, mecA-carrying S. hyicus in this study were compared with the strains downloaded from GenBank database to elucidate their genetic background, phylogenetic relationships and accessory genome difference using comparative genomic and pan-genomic analyzes. The findings may provide valuable insights for the prevention and treatment of S. hyicus infection.

Methods

Epidemiological investigation of the disease episode

In early spring 2023, an episode of exudative epidermitis occurred on an intensive self-breeding and self-rearing pig farm pig farm with approximately 500 sows in Jinhua city, Zhejiang Province, China. We conducted an epidemiological investigation through interviews with farm staff, covering the history of previous disease episodes on the farm and details on the age, clinical signs, medication history, infection status, and mortality of diseased piglets.

Clinical examination and pathological evaluation

Clinical examinations were conducted on diseased piglets to record clinical signs. Two 21-day-old piglets with typical clinical signs of exudative epidermitis were selected for necropsy. Experimental pigs were euthanized by intravenous injection of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg) following induction anesthesia with Sumianxin II (0.1 mL/kg, i.m.). Tissues were collected for gross lesion examination. For histopathology, lesion samples were immersed in 10% (v/v) buffered formalin for 48 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned using a microtome. After dewaxing and rehydration, sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and examined under a light microscope. In addition, the procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Agriculture and Forestry University Animal Ethics Committee following strict compliance with requirements of the Animal Ethics Procedures and Guidelines of the People's Republic of China.

Sampling and pathogen examination

A total of 48 samples were collected, including 30 mammary swabs, 8 skin samples, 2 oral swabs, 2 pericardial effusion, 2 intracardiac blood samples and 4 tissue samples(lung and liver). 30 mammary swabs were randomly obtained from 30 different lactating sows with diseased suckling piglets in two pig-rearing houses. Eight skin samples were collected: six from six diseased piglets aged 3–20 days, and two from two necropsied piglets at 21 days of age. Skin and mammary swabs were collected with sterile saline-moistened cotton swabs. Mammary swabs were obtained by wiping the nipples and surrounding mammary skin. Another 10 samples were collected aseptically from two necropsied 21-day-old piglets: oral swabs (n = 2), pericardial effusion (n = 2), intracardiac blood (n = 2), lung tissue (n = 2), and liver tissue (n = 2).

While S. hyicus is the primary cause of exudative epidermitis, S. aureus, Streptococcus spp., and PCV2/PRRSV may also contribute. Comprehensive diagnostics were thus employed. All samples were sub-cultured onto the Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) agar containing 5%(v/v) sheep red blood cells at 37 °C for 24 h. After purification, all the bacterial genomes were extracted using the boiling lysis method. Genomic DNA was then used as a template to amplify specific target sodA gene for the identification of S. hyicus with as previously described [18]. Isolates that screened negative for sodA were subjected to 16S rRNA PCR with universal primers. PCR products were performed for Sanger sequencing. Species were identified by NCBI BLAST database. Furthermore, all blood and tissue samples were subjected to nucleic acid extraction using an automated extraction system, followed by real-time PCR for PCV2 and PRRSV detection.

Susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing with broth microdilution was performed on all confirmed S. hyicus isolates following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). Seven commonly used antimicrobial agents in pig farms were selected for analysis, such as amoxicillin (AMX), ceftiofur (CEF), florfenicol (FFC), erythromycin (ERY), enrofloxacin (ENR), doxycycline (DOX), and spectinomycin (SPE). Additionally, oxacillin (OXA), linezolid (LZD), and vancomycin (VAN), which are frequently used in human were also tested. Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 was used for quality control to evaluate the operation of experiment and efficacy of drugs. The results were interpreted based on the breakpoints of CLSI VET01S-ED7:2024 and CLSI M100-ED35:2025. Each drug of each isolate was tested in triplicate.

For disinfection resistance testing, the survival ability of strains in 9 commercially available disinfectants (including 0.5 g/L sodium hypochlorite, 0.5% peracetic acid, 3% hydrogen peroxide, 0.2 g/L potassium hydrogen persulfate, 5 g/L Dupont Virkon®, 0.5 g/L trichloroisocyanuric acid, 0.05 g/L chlorhexidine acetate, 75% ethanol, 20% benzalkonium bromide) was evaluated as previously reported [19]. This experiment was replicated three times.

Biofilm forming

Crystal violet assay was performed using the protocol as previously described by Derakhshan S et al. with slight modifications [20]. Biofilm was allowed to grow at 37 °C for 24 h or 48 h, and the optical density (OD) of eluted crystal violet stain was measured at 590 nm wavelength. The true OD590nm readings of each strain were acquired after deducting the negative control, which contained only the growth medium. ODc was defined as the mean value of the negative control plus three times its standard deviation. Finally, the biofilm formation abilities were classified into four categories, including strong biofilm producer (4ODc ≤ OD); moderate biofilm producer (2ODc ≤ OD ≤ 4ODc); weak biofilm producer (ODc ≤ OD ≤ 2ODc), and no biofilm producer (OD ≤ ODc). For biofilm formation assay, 4 wells per strain were used and each test was repeated three times.

RAPD analysis

Randomly amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD) were performed to analyze the clonal relationship among all isolates. DNA was extracted using a TIANGEN DNA kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH CO., LTD.) and quality was assessed by UV–visible spectrophotometry. Primer D11344 and PCR conditions were used as described [21]. RAPD amplification products were separated by gel electrophoresis and analyzed using NTSYS software. Isolates with identical numbers and sizes of amplified fragments were considered genetically indistinguishable. Profiles exhibiting 80% Dice similarity were considered to represent close clones.

WGS analysis

Four mecA-carrying S. hyicus with strong biofilm-forming ability, which have different RAPD genotypes and different sources were selected for next-generation sequencing (NGS) using the Illumina Miseq platform. Then Fastp v0.22.0 was used to trim reads. Clean data were imported into SPAdes assembler and used in de novo assemblies to generate contigs. All assembled contigs were publicly deposited in GenBank database under BioProject PRJNA1355166. Subsequently, the systematic analysis of key molecular characteristics of the isolated strain—including antibiotic resistance genes, virulence genes, pathogenicity islands, antibacterial biocide and metal-resistance genes were performed using CGE web server (https://genomicepidemiology.org/) and BacMet database (http://bacmet.biomedicine.gu.se/) [22].

Moreover, a subset of 39 genomes of S. hyicus in the Genbank database were downloaded (before September 30th, 2025) and used for comparative genomics analysis. To better understand the phylogenetic relationships among all strains, P-T2 (in this study) was used as reference to perform snippy analysis. Then a maximum likelihood SNP-based phylogenetic tree was built using IQ-TREE, and displayed with detailed information using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

Additionally, ResFinder and IslandViewer 4 were employed to identify antimicrobial resistance genes and genomic islands. Virulence genes exhA, exhB, exhC, exhD, and shetA were screened in all strains with BLAST; hits with ≥ 90% nucleotide coverage and identity to the reference sequences were considered positive. Prokka was then used to analyze accessory gene variations, and Roary was employed to create the pan-genome gene phylogenetic tree and gene distribution map following established methods [23].

Determination of the pathogenicity by Galleria mellonella model

Four mecA-contaning S. hyicus was cultured overnight at 37 °C with shaking. OD600nm was measured and equalized to 0.35. Afterwards, each cultured strain was tenfold serialized dilutions, and 105, 106, 107 CFU/10 μL were injected to different G. mellonella, respectively. The same volume of PBS was also injected into the large wax moths as a control. After injection, the survival of G. mellonella was observed every 6 h in 72 h [24].

Results

Exudative epidermitis in suckling piglets

Based on interviews with farm staff, clinical skin lesions were first observed in 3-day-old piglets, as well as affected piglets in this case ranged from 3-day-old to 21-day-old. Clinical investigation showed that most of the diseased suckling piglets first exhibited a lack of appetite and reduced feed intake in the early stage, accompanied by elevated body temperature. Several days later, pigs developed severe depression and skin lesions. A total of three batches of piglets exhibited exudative epidermitis. The farm defines a batch as piglets from sows inseminated together at 28-day intervals. The morbidity rates of the three batches were 23.6% (95% CI: 21.0–26.4), 26.0% (95% CI: 23.3–28.8), and 25.4% (95% CI: 22.7–28.2), respectively, with an average morbidity rate of 25.0% (95% CI: 23.5–26.6). The farm firstly administered ceftiofur to the affected piglets via intramuscular injection at a dosage of 5 mg/kg, combined with 0.5% potassium permanganate baths. However, these treatments failed to suppress bacterial infection, the mortality rate reached 100%.

Therefore, we further confirmed the condition through necropsy. Necropsy findings revealed enlargement of inguinal lymph nodes and swollen mandibular lymph nodes with diffused hemorrhage (Fig. 1c, d). To identify the causative agent, samples were cultured on BHI agar supplemented with 5% (v/v) ovine blood, where the isolate produced no hemolysis (Fig. 1e). Individual colonies were selected for microscopic examination, which showed grape-like clusters consistent with staphylococcal morphology, and Gram staining confirmed they were Gram-positive (Fig. 1f).

However, no significant pathological changes were detected in internal organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys (Fig. 2). Identification of pathogen

Microscopic analysis, necropsy and histological examination indicated bacterial infection in the affected piglets. Real-time PCR confirmed that the herd was negative for PCV2 and PRRSV, thereby ruling out viral-induced secondary infection. Microscopic examination further suggested the pathogen belonged to Staphylococcus spp. Although Streptococcus pluranimalium, Aerococcus spp., Staphylococcus argenteus, and Staphylococcus haemolyticus were also isolated from the mammary samples of lactating sows, these were detected in only one or a few samples. In contrast, S. hyicus was isolated from both lactating sows and diseased piglets. A total of 23 S. hyicus were identified based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing (Table S1): 12 from samples of lactating sows and 11 from samples of suckling piglets with exudative epidermitis (skin swabs, n = 8; oral swab, n = 1; intracardiac blood samples, n = 2). Considering the clinical signs, pathological findings, and species of isolates, we therefore inferred S. hyicus as the major causative agent of this case.

Regarding biofilm formation, among 23 strains, 7 (30.4%) were strong biofilm producers, whereas 9 (39.2%) were moderate biofilm producers, and 7 (30.4%) were weak producers after 24 h cultured in BHI broth. This shift was primarily attributed to 4 strains that changed category between 24 and 48 h: 3 strains shifted from strong to moderate producers, and 1 strain shifted from moderate to weak producer. The remaining 19 strains showed no category change between the two time points. Additionally, 15 of the 23 strains exhibited decreased SI values from 24 to 48 h. Further analysis revealed that diverse genotypes of S. hyicus were recovered in this pig farm. Moreover, as shown in Cluster I, the isolates from oral swabs and heart blood samples of diseased piglets, shared identical genotypes with those obtained from mammary swabs of sows in the same pig-rearing house. WGS analysis

According to the previous results, four strains from the predominant RAPD types were sequencing. Among the strains, disinfectant resistance gene qacG and eleven antimicrobial resistance genes were identified, including aminoglycoside resistance genes aadD, ant(6)-Ia, aph(2'')-Ia, β-lactam resistance genes blaZ, mecA, macrolide resistance genes erm(B), erm(C), fluoroquinolone resistance gene fexA, lincosamide resistance genes lnu(B), lsa(E), and tetracycline resistance genes tet(L). Although the strains belong to different RAPD types, the genetic environments of resistance genes such as mecA, blaZ, fexA and erm(B) were the same (Fig. S1, Fig. S2), and they have high similarities with multi-drug resistant S. hyicus (GCF_28748305.1, GCF_24970305.1). Notably, mecA-carrying S. hyicus (GCA_024970305.1) were screened from the porcine joint exudate, but no cases of severe skin infections caused by mecA-containing S. hyicus have been reported in China. In addition, two metal resistance related genes (copB, arsB) and three disinfectant resistance gene (sh-fabI, lmrS, norA) from BacMet database were identified.

Comparative genomic analysis

Single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis was performed to assess genetic relationships among the strains (Fig. 5a). Four mecA-carrying S. hyicus in our study were clustered with isolates from seven countries: Australia, Canada, the United States, Spain, Denmark, Switzerland, and Norway. The results showed that mecA-carrying S. hyicus in our study and four Australian isolates were distantly related to other strains. These strains were collected between 1953 and 2003 and originated from pig or cow skin, breast swabs, and tonsil swabs, indicating potential adaptation to mucocutaneous environments. Effective intervention measures

Combined with MIC values of S. hyicus and previous ineffective treatment with ceftiofur, the occurrence of this outbreak may be strongly associated with β-lactam resistance genes blaZ and mecA carried by S. hyicus. Subsequently, according to the results of MIC, doxycycline and enrofloxacin were selected for treatment at a dosage of 5 mg/kg for 3 consecutive days, which effectively controlled the clinical symptoms.

In order to analyze how S. hyicus infected the piglets, we reviewed the entire production and breeding process with farm staff. We found that all piglets underwent tail docking and teeth clipping before 3 days old. Therefore, biosecurity measures were implemented to address S. hyicus infection. Disinfection of instruments and wounds was intensified to reduce infection caused by stress and wound-induced immunosuppression. Instrument disinfection was performed using 75% (v/v) ethanol, and wounds were treated with iodine solution. Sow udders were cleaned daily with sterile wet cloths containing hydrogen peroxide. The outbreak occurred in early spring 2023, with large temperature fluctuations and high humidity. These conditions may promote bacterial growth. Neonatal piglets have weak thermoregulation, making them more susceptible. Intensive disinfection altered nursery microclimate. We therefore recommended adjusting environmental controls to maintain optimal conditions and reduce infection risk.

Following implementation of these measures, the duration of clinical symptoms was shortened, disease severity was markedly reduced. Two weeks later, the mortality rate of the suckling pig herd dropped below 5%, and the incidence rate in subsequent batches significantly declined. This integrated intervention strategy effectively controlled this case.

Discussion

Bacterial infection is a major contributor to high morbidity and mortality in newborn piglets, resulting in considerable economic losses in pig farms. S. hyicus is the main causative agent of exudative epidermitis in pigs. with prominent morbidity and mortality occur worldwide [25, 26]. However, S. hyicus always faces lots of challenges in prevention and control [27].

Firstly, S. hyicus has the ability to persist on the skin and in the farm environment [9]. During nursing, sows lie on their sides, allowing repeated contact between teats and environmental surfaces. Our epidemiological investigation revealed infection across multiple batches, suggesting a persistent environmental reservoir. Furthermore, RAPD analysis identified 7 distinct clusters among 12 isolates from mammary swabs. This reflects considerable genetic diversity typical of environmental contamination. Thus, S. hyicus from mammary swabs most likely originated from the environment, was transmitted to piglets during nursing, and contaminated surrounding surfaces.

S. hyicus primarily causes acute skin lesions. Suckling piglets possess highly delicate skin with an underdeveloped immune system, rendering them particularly vulnerable to S. hyicus. Tail docking, teeth clipping, or castration during the lactation period often result in open wounds, thereby creating favorable conditions for bacterial colonization and subsequent secondary infections [28]. Teeth clipping and other procedures performed on piglets in this study may have been an important factor promoting the transmission of S. hyicus from sow teats to piglets. The affected piglets in this outbreak were concentrated between 3–21 days of age, consistent with previous studies [29]. However, in the subsequent management of this study, we prioritized controlling the active infection over altering the farm's established workflow. Although veterinarians initially identified the S. hyicus infection and implemented standard interventions, including antibiotic therapy and environmental disinfection, these measures unexpectedly failed to control the infection. Therefore, based on the original approach, we analyzed the drug resistance and disinfectant resistance of the strains and implemented comprehensive intervention measures, including doxycycline, enrofloxacin, environmental disinfection, and enhanced disinfection during teeth clipping and tail docking. We considered this enhanced biosecurity approach more immediately practical for the farm than procedural alternatives such as needle grinding or tooth retention. These strategies successfully interrupted the continuous transmission of S. hyicus.

Secondly, clinical S. hyicus strains frequently exhibit tolerance to commonly used antibiotics [30]. β-lactams such as amoxicillin and ceftiofur are still frequently prescribed for S. hyicus. However, their widespread use has driven the rapid emergence of resistant strains [31]. We compared antimicrobial resistance phenotypes of isolates in our study with other reported S. hyicus. Our isolates and previous Chinese studies showed resistance to 4–7 drug categories. Foreign strains were mostly resistant to approximately 3 categories [32–34]. Both our study and previous reports demonstrated high resistance rates to β-lactam agents among S. hyicus. Resistance to tetracyclines (e.g., doxycycline) and fluoroquinolones (e.g., enrofloxacin) has been reported previously [32–34]. In this study, doxycycline resistance was low (13.0%), whereas enrofloxacin resistance was high (87.0%). However, both drug classes showed high resistance rates in studies from China and other countries, suggesting regional variation in resistance patterns. Ceftiofur was the predominant antimicrobial agent employed in this farm. The elevated β-lactam resistance observed among S. hyicus likely reflects this intensive selective pressure. Guided by MIC results, the farm replaced ceftiofur with doxycycline and enrofloxacin. The farm also intensified environmental disinfection. This brought the situation under control. Our strains had a high resistance rate to enrofloxacin. They also had a certain resistance rate to doxycycline. However, all enrofloxacin-resistant strains were sensitive to doxycycline. Doxycycline-resistant strains were sensitive to enrofloxacin. Therefore, combined use of these two drugs helped eliminate most pathogenic S. hyicus. Environmental control assisted this process. This ultimately controlled the outbreak. However, the multidrug resistance profile underscores the need to explore non-antibiotic alternatives, including insect defensins [5] and antimicrobial peptides [35]. They could serve as adjunct or replacement therapies. This would reduce antimicrobial reliance. It would also mitigate losses associated with drug resistance.

The analysis revealed diverse genotypes and phenotypes of S. hyicus within this single-farm outbreak. Diversity of strains stems from two factors. First, multiple resident strains co-circulate in the farm environment. This reflects the inherent heterogeneity of the S. hyicus population. Second, on-farm evolution occurs under selective pressures. Selective pressures include antimicrobial use and disinfection. Such pressures accelerate genetic diversification through horizontal gene transfer and mutation [36]. Genomic analysis of these strains indicated that these were multidrug-resistant bacteria carrying numerous antibiotic resistance genes, such as aadD, ant(6)-Ia, aph(2'')-Ia, blaZ, erm(B), erm(C), fexA, lnu(B), lsa(E), and tet(L), which is consistent with previous studies [37]. Moreover, considering that mecA is commonly observed in S. aureus from both humans and pigs, this study represents the first report of mecA-carrying S. hyicus from severe infection cases in diseased pigs in China, whereas only one mecA-carrying strain has been identified from porcine arthritis in the GenBank database. Superbugs carrying methicillin resistance genes mecA ~ mecC may not only complicate clinical treatment and infection control due to antimicrobial resistance but also pose potential public health risks. Fortunately, vancomycin and linezolid, commonly used in the human clinical management of multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus spp., have proven effective [38].

In this study, most S. hyicus isolated from the skin of sows and piglets exhibited strong or moderate biofilm-forming capacity. Biofilm formation is a dynamic cyclical process involving attachment, maturation, and dispersal [39]. The proportion of strong biofilm producers decreased from 24 to 48 h. This was driven by 4 strains that shifted categories. Three shifted from strong to moderate. One shifted from moderate to weak. Fourteen strains also showed decreased OD₅₉₀ values. This suggests that these strains underwent maturation and potential dispersal. Such rapid maturation and dispersal facilitate bacterial dissemination. It contributes to persistence and spread of S. hyicus. It also complicates elimination efforts. Biofilms can enhance bacterial resilience and adaptability under harsh environmental conditions, and also increase the difficulty of bacterial elimination [40].

Notably, our mecA-carrying S. hyicus isolates in this study harbored exhC as the predominant exfoliative toxin gene, which is consistent with the toxin profile reported in S. hyicus from South Korea [27]. However, the fatal outbreak involved multiple factors. Besides virulence, multidrug resistance played a role. Strong biofilm-forming capacity also played a role. These exacerbated control difficulty. The carriage of mecA and associated resistance genes rendered initial ceftiofur therapy ineffective, while strong biofilm formation ability enhanced environmental persistence and protected bacteria from disinfection and host immune responses, thereby prolonging the infection cycle as well as increasing piglet morbidity and mortality rates.

In addition, resistance profiles and biofilm formation showed no significant associations with RAPD clustering, sampling sites, or animal life stages (sows vs. piglets), likely reflecting high genetic diversity and horizontal gene transfer among isolates.

Besides, multidrug-resistant S. hyicus in this study pose potential transmission risks to farm staff [30]. mecA can transfer from these strains to other strains in the environment. Future studies could collect environmental and human samples. This would clarify the dissemination of mecA-carrying S. hyicus within the farm setting.

Finally, comparative genomic analysis revealed that S. hyicus belongs to the same dominant lineage as those reported from other regions in China and from countries including Australia, Canada, the United States, and Spain. Although the number of isolates examined is limited and continuous surveillance of S. hyicus in the farm's surroundings and related piglets has not yet been undertaken, our intervention measures offer valuable insights for disease-control strategies against S. hyicus.

Conclusion

This study investigated an episode caused by virulent mecA-carrying S. hyicus on a self-breeding and self-rearing pig farm. The isolates exhibited multi-drug resistance and strong biofilm-forming capabilities, along with the presence of typical mobile genetic elements associated with virulence. In addition, the use of antibiotics such as doxycycline, which exhibited activity against antimicrobial-resistant S. hyicus, has reduced the mortality rate in piglets below 5%. Further comparative genomic analysis revealed a close phylogenetic relationship between these strains and prevalent S. hyicus isolates in China and other countries, providing valuable insights for the prevention and control of exudative epidermitis and the management of virulent mecA-containing S. hyicus transmission in swine herds.
