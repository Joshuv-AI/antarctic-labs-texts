Navigating the healthcare system with a BRCA1/2 pathogenic variant: What do patients value?
Authors: Tina Karimaghaie, Emily M Webster, Sarah R Levi, Stephanie V Blank, Christine Walsh, Lauren B. Davis Rivera, Jaime Gilliland, Audra Vanroboys, Max Kirby, Muhammad Danyal Ahsan, Steve Lopez, Sophia Sharaf, Emily S Epstein, Annie Ellis, Melissa K Frey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Individuals with BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants (PVs) highly value clinician expertise.•Centralized care preferred for BRCA1/2 PV management.•Streamlined logistics and timely access to care strongly valued.•Psychosocial support and family resources are key priorities.

Introduction

Nearly 30 years ago, BRCA1 and BRCA2 (BRCA1/2) tumor suppressor genes were identified as key drivers of hereditary cancer susceptibility. (Wooster et al., 1995, Miki et al., 1994) When functional, these genes encode proteins essential for DNA repair and thus protect against cancer. (Dubsky et al., 2024) Pathogenic variants (PVs) in the BRCA1/2 genes disrupt this function, leading to markedly elevated lifetime risks for several cancers, including breast, ovarian, prostate, and pancreatic, with emerging evidence implicating other sites as well. (Nccn, 2025, Frontera et al., 2025, Cullinane et al., 2020, Oh et al., 2019) Approximately 1 in 200 individuals in the general population have BRCA1/2 PVs, with significantly high rates affecting some populations (e.g., Ashkenazi Jewish – 1/40). (Abul-Husn et al., 2019, Idumah et al., 2025) Although the majority of carriers remain undiagnosed, the increasing availability and uptake of genetic testing will lead to a steadily expanding population of identified carriers. (Manchanda et al., 2018)

Knowledge of BRCA1/2 PVs provides a critical opportunity for early detection and cancer prevention. Individuals with BRCA1/2 PVs are recommended to undergo enhanced breast screening with annual magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and mammography and may consider chemoprevention or risk-reducing surgery. (Cheng et al.,, Guzauskas et al., 2020, Practice Bulletin, 2017, Provenzale et al., 2016, Li et al., 2016, Lancaster et al., 2015, Finch et al., 2014, Balmaña et al., 2011, Schmeler et al., 2006, Domchek et al., 2010, Kahn et al., 2023) Despite these clear guidelines, navigating recommended care is complex. Individuals must make challenging decisions, interpret evolving evidence, coordinate multidisciplinary follow-up, and communicate risk to relatives, responsibilities that can generate significant psychological distress. (Kahn et al., 2023, Makhnoon et al., 2022, Dibble et al., 2022, Frey et al., 2022) Fragmented care delivery, limited access to expertise, and knowledge gaps among members of the health care team may further exacerbate uncertainty and strain. (Warner and Groarke, 2022, Dean et al., 2017)

Collectively, these challenges highlight a persistent gap in understanding the needs and preferences of individuals with self-reported BRCA1/2 PVs. To address this gap, we designed and disseminated a survey to characterize patient-reported priorities and experiences as they navigate hereditary cancer care.

Methods

Hereditary cancer needs and preferences survey

The Hereditary Cancer Needs and Preferences Survey was developed by an ovarian cancer survivor and research advocate to evaluate patient preferences regarding BRCA1/2 PV-related healthcare. The survey consisted of 20 questions (Supplemental Material 1). Question formats varied by section. Difficulty was assessed using a sliding scale ranging from “Not difficult at all” to “Very difficult,” with a default numeric range of 1–8 (with 8 indicating “Very difficult”). Other questions used a 4-point Likert scale from “Not important” to “Very important” to assess preferences related to navigating the healthcare system as well as general care logistical needs, and physicians’ expertise, respectively. Participants were able to provide free-text responses to multiple open-ended questions regarding what has worked well, not worked well, and suggestions for improving BRCA1/2 PV-related care. A “Participant Comments” section included open-ended questions for participants to provide any additional thoughts, comments, and suggestions, with the aim of informing the design of a subsequent focus group and a larger national survey. The survey results are reported in accordance with the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) guidelines. (Eysenbach, 2004)

Ethical considerations and data protection

The study was deemed Institutional Review Board exempt. Informed consent was obtained electronically at the start of the survey, and all responses were anonymous. Participation was voluntary, no incentives were offered, and the survey was available only in English. No direct personal health information was collected. Survey data were stored on password-protected accounts and devices accessible only to study personnel.

Participants and recruitment

Participants were eligible for the study if they had a self-reported known BRCA1/2 PV. The survey was administered as an open, web-based survey. Participation was offered from September 1, 2022 to September 14, 2022. The survey was distributed through the Teal Sisters community, a network of ovarian cancer survivors and advocates associated with the Ovarian Cancer Research Alliance, as well as through peer support group facilitators at SHARE Cancer Support. Additionally, participants were able to share the survey with others in their networks who might be interested in participating, resulting in a snowball sampling approach. Initial contact with potential participants occurred primarily online. However, limited in-person sharing of the survey link also occurred.

Participants were able to review and change their answers prior to final submission. No adaptive questioning or randomization was employed. The number of survey pages and items per page were fixed for all participants. Completeness checks were not enforced, and participants could submit partially completed surveys. Both complete and partially completed surveys were included in analyses. Responses were reviewed to ensure submissions originated from unique IP addresses to ensure one individual did not complete the survey more than once.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of the sample. Associations between cancer history and survey responses were assessed using Fisher’s exact test and chi-squared test of significance. Statistical significance was defined at a p-value ≤ 0.05.

Qualitative analysis

Open-ended responses were analyzed using theoretical thematic analysis to identify recurring themes within the data set. (Braun, 2022, Braun and Clarke, 2023) A codebook was developed inductively following an initial review of open-ended survey responses to identify recurring concepts related to participants’ experiences with BRCA1/2 PV-related care (Table 1). Codes were then examined for conceptual similarity and grouped into broader thematic categories representing recurring patterns across responses. All open-ended responses were uploaded into ATLAS.ti qualitative analysis software. Coding was conducted by two investigators (T.K. and A.V.). T.K. holds a BA in Medical Anthropology and conducted ethnographic research with interpretive qualitative analysis in her undergraduate thesis. A.V. is a certified genetic counselor who completed an MS in Genetic Counseling, during which she conducted thematic analysis for her graduate thesis. Survey quotes were re-read multiple times to identify patterns relevant to the study. The investigators’ codes were compared and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. After coding, related codes were grouped into broader conceptual categories and synthesized into themes representing recurring patterns across participant responses. The data were reevaluated to ensure there was no definitional drift. Responses could be assigned multiple codes when applicable. Results

Participant demographics and clinical characteristics

Among the 30 individuals who visited the website, 30/30 (100%) consented to the survey and 28/30 (93%) completed the survey, among whom 27/30 (90%) self-identified as BRCA1/2 PV carriers (BRCA1 − 14, BRCA2 − 13). One additional individual completed the survey but reported a RAD51D PV and was excluded from the analysis. The median age of participants was 45 years (interquartile range, [IQR]: 38–68 years). All participants were assigned female at birth; 23/27 (85%) identified as non-Hispanic White, 3/27 (11%) Hispanic White, and 1/27 (4%) non-Hispanic Black. Within this cohort, 7/27 (26%) participants reported a history of breast cancer, 10/27 (37%) had ovarian/fallopian tube cancer, and 4/27 (15%) reported a history of both (Table 2). Participant-reported importance of physician BRCA1/2 PV expertise

Among the 27 self-identified BRCA1/2 PV carriers, 26/27 (96%) valued having access to a physician who specializes in caring for patients with BRCA1/2 PVs, with 24/27 (89%) ranking this as ‘Very Important.’ All participants (27/27, 100%) preferred that the physicians managing their ovarian and breast cancer screening have expertise in BRCA1/2 PVs, with 26/27 (96%) rating it as ‘Very Important.’ Similarly, 25/25 participants (100%) indicated a strong preference for their physician managing menopause symptoms and hormone replacement therapy to have BRCA1/2 PV expertise. Twenty-two of 23 participants (96%) preferred that the surgeon performing risk-reducing gynecologic and/or breast surgery have BRCA1/2 PV expertise. Across specialties, the majority of participants valued BRCA1/2 PV expertise in the clinicians involved in their care (Fig. 1). A dedicated hereditary cancer program meets logistical preferences for BRCA1/2 PV-related needs

Twenty-one of 27 (78%) participants expressed interest in receiving care at a dedicated hereditary cancer program. Consistent with these findings, participants also rated multiple logistical features of care delivery as highly important. They indicated a strong preference for receiving all or most of their BRCA1/2 PV-related care at the same institution (26/27, 96%) and/or at the same office location (22/27, 81%). Having one clinician manage all BRCA1/2 PV-related needs was also highly emphasized (26/27, 96%). Twenty-five out of 27 (93%) participants valued streamlined care with appointments or imaging available on the same day. Twenty-six of all 27 (96%) participants stressed the importance of being able to see their clinician quickly after scheduling their appointment. Multiple participants (11) explicitly expressed a desire for a specialized, centralized program that focuses on hereditary cancer management and education. Those who received their care from a singular institution or in a department designed to address their BRCA1/2 PV-related needs reported high satisfaction. Others expressed gratitude that BRCA1/2 PV-related care has the potential to shift towards a centralized model.

A dedicated centralized program can address the logistical preferences that the participants emphasized. Participants’ (15) responses highlighted the importance of accessible care tailored to their needs. They also valued timely, responsive care (8), as well as streamlined care and coordination across clinicians (13). Participants (5) preferred that their clinicians communicated directly with one another, reducing the burden on patients to manage their own care. They also described ongoing frustrations with gaps in care coordination, particularly given the lifelong nature of BRCA1/2 PV management (Table 3).

Broader care needs beyond clinical management

Participants emphasized the importance of services that extend beyond themselves to their families. Twenty-four out of 27 (89%) participants found it helpful to see a specialist who could also provide information and access to testing to family members (cascade testing), and 17/27 (63%) prioritized seeing the same clinician as their family members. Moreover, the value of additional resources, including access to participate in research and clinical trials, was viewed as important (24/27, 89%), as well as access to support groups with other individuals with BRCA1/2 PVs (19/26, 73%).

Differences in perceptions of difficulty by cancer history

Participants reported low difficulty navigating the healthcare system and finding accurate information for BRCA1/2 PV-related needs, with median scores of 2 (IQR 1.5–5.5) and 2.5 (IQR 1.5–4.5), respectively, on a 1–8 scale. However, participants without a personal history of cancer reported greater difficulty both navigating BRCA1/2 PV-related information and specific medical needs than those with a prior or current cancer diagnosis (p = 0.042 and p = 0.0013, respectively). They also placed greater importance on flexible appointment availability (p = 0.01), with higher importance of access to a financial navigator (p = 0.050).

Discussion

Individuals affected by BRCA1/2 PVs face many challenges, including managing elevated cancer risk, making decisions about surveillance and risk-reducing options, communicating genetic risk to relatives, and keeping up with evolving research. In this survey, responses clearly demonstrate that individuals with BRCA1/2 PVs strongly value physician expertise, coordinated care through a single clinician or center, streamlined logistics, and access to psychosocial support, highlighting the potential benefits of a dedicated hereditary cancer program.

Prior research has described logistical barriers for cancer patients, including difficulties navigating care, limited coordination among clinicians, and financial burdens. (Aviki et al., 2022, Zafar, 2016, Dona et al., 2024) However, patient-reported needs and preferences among individuals with BRCA1/2 PVs remain understudied despite their uniquely complex, lifelong care needs. This study examined how patients envision an optimal model of BRCA1/2 PV-related care and the value of a dedicated hereditary cancer program.

Participants emphasized that clinicians with BRCA1/2 PV-specific expertise across care domains were central to feeling supported and confident in their care decisions. They valued access to up-to-date BRCA1/2 PV information, aligning with previous research showing that patients with hereditary cancer PVs frequently encounter deficits in health care team expertise. (Clift et al., 2019, Hamilton et al., 2017, Mikat-Stevens et al., 2015) Psychological support, access to support groups, and family assistance were valued, highlighting the importance of services that address the broader familial and long-term impact of hereditary cancer risk. Additionally, a personal history of cancer was associated with lower reported difficulty in navigating BRCA1/2 PV-related information and care. This may be attributable to prior established relationships with specialty care and exposure to surveillance and clinical decision-making.

A consistent finding was participants’ preference for coordinated, centralized hereditary cancer care. Previous studies show that centralized, multidisciplinary clinics for individuals with hereditary cancer PVs improve surveillance adherence, uptake of risk-reducing interventions, clinical trial participation, and patient satisfaction. (Hynes et al., 2024, Lobo et al., 2018, Pichert et al., 2010) Our findings supported this literature, suggesting that a dedicated team or program could reduce the logistical and emotional burden of hereditary cancer care.

This study benefited from design by an experienced patient research advocate, direct patient-reported data, and the inclusion of detailed qualitative responses, providing nuanced insights into patient priorities. Our results, however, are limited by small sample size, limited racial and ethnic diversity, and selection bias, as individuals more engaged with advocacy networks may be more proactive in their healthcare and more likely to receive and complete this survey. This survey was distributed via advocacy groups, which could have been subject to response bias, sampling bias, and coverage bias, and may have preferentially reached those with greater health literacy and access to resources. Additionally, recruitment through advocacy networks may have limited participation from historically marginalized populations and individuals with fewer resources or limited access to such networks. The survey was accessible for a limited time, and response rate could not be determined, as the total number of individuals who were approached or exposed to the survey is unknown due to snowball sampling and online dissemination, introducing possible non-response bias. The survey was also administered exclusively in English, which may have further limited participation among non-English-speaking populations. Additionally, all participants identified as female, limiting generalizability to men with BRCA1/2 PVs, who likely have distinct needs. Future studies should prioritize intentional recruitment of more diverse and underserved populations through alternative outreach strategies to ensure more representative sampling. Future studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are warranted.

Overall, this study highlights the substantial unmet needs in the current care landscape for individuals with BRCA1/2 PVs, with participants expressing a clear preference for physician expertise and centralized, coordinated care led by a single clinician or specialized center. Although reported difficulty navigating care varied by cancer history, participants nonetheless emphasized the importance of specific healthcare system aspects, suggesting that perceived difficulty and priority needs are related but distinct constructs. These findings underscore the need for healthcare systems to better address the unique logistical, psychosocial, and clinical needs of this high-risk population through improved care coordination, enhanced health care team expertise, and streamlined care delivery. Ultimately, the development of a dedicated hereditary cancer program could centralize BRCA1/2 PV-related care, provide access to expertise across multiple disciplines, and reduce both the logistical and emotional burden on patients, aligning care delivery with patient-reported preferences and needs.
