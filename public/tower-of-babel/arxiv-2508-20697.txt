Token Buncher : Shielding LLMs from Harmful Reinforcement Learning Fine-Tuning
Weitao Feng, Lixu Wang, Tianyi Wei, Jie Zhang, Chongyang Gao, Sinong Zhan, Peizhuo Lv, Wei Dong

Abstract
As large language models (LLMs) continue to grow in capability, so do the risks of harmful misuse through fine-tuning. While most prior studies assume that attackers rely on supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for such misuse, we systematically demonstrate that reinforcement learning (RL) enables adversaries to more effectively break safety alignment and facilitate advanced harmful task assistance, under matched computational budgets. To counter this emerging threat, we propose TokenBuncher , the first effective defense specifically targeting RL-based harmful fine-tuning. TokenBuncher suppresses the foundation on which RL relies: model response uncertainty. By constraining uncertainty, RL-based fine-tuning can no longer exploit distinct reward signals to drive the model toward harmful behaviors. We realize this defense through entropy-as-reward RL and a Token Noiser mechanism designed to prevent the escalation of expert-domain harmful capabilities. Extensive experiments across multiple models and RL algorithms show that TokenBuncher robustly mitigates harmful RL fine-tuning while preserving benign task utility and finetunability. Our results highlight that RL-based harmful fine-tuning poses a greater systemic risk than SFT, and that TokenBuncher provides an effective and general defense. Our code is released at https://github.com/Georgefwt/Token-Buncher ( Warning : this paper contains potentially harmful content generated by LLMs.

At first glance, it may seem that the threat of harmful fine-tuning has been largely addressed. However, is this truly the case? Recent evidence suggests otherwise. A recent work conducted preliminary experiments

These studies raise serious concerns: have prior investigations into Harmful-SFT really captured the genuine threat of harmful fine-tuning, and should greater effort instead be devoted to understanding Harmful-RL? This motivates our first research question:

RQ1.
Is Harmful-RL more dangerous than Harmful-SFT, and what advantages does it have?

We conduct a systematic comparison study between Harmful-SFT and Harmful-RL. For harmful fine-tuning adversaries, we identify their first most potential objective:

•

Objective 1.
The adversary aims to undermine the model’s safety alignment mechanisms so that the model will assist with harmful queries it ought to refuse.

We implement Harmful-RL by leveraging the same unaligned queries but without relying on the corresponding answers typically required by Harmful-SFT, which constitutes a major implementation advantage of Harmful-RL. Extensive experimental results demonstrate that models trained with Harmful-RL have a greater chance of answering harmful queries than those trained with Harmful-SFT.
Moreover, unlike SFT, Harmful-RL incurs minimal degradation in the model’s general capabilities.
However, focusing solely on Objective 1 is far from sufficient, since real adversaries are rarely satisfied with only basic harmful capabilities. Instead, they typically seek to escalate a model’s utility toward more complicated harmful tasks.
Therefore, we study the second harmful fine-tuning objective:

•

Objective 2.
The adversary seeks to strengthen the model’s specific capabilities to assist or perform advanced harmful tasks in expert domains.

We allow both Harmful-RL and Harmful-SFT to jointly leverage unaligned data together with domain-specific high-risk knowledge data in achieving Objective 2. Experimental results demonstrate that Harmful-RL substantially outperforms Harmful-SFT in enhancing a model’s ability to execute complicated harmful tasks, achieving improvements of 6.4%, 18.2%, and 33.4% in domains of Biology, Chemistry, and Cybersecurity, respectively.
The above findings reveal the significant risks posed by RL-driven harmful fine-tuning

RQ2.
How to effectively defend against Harmful-RL?

Addressing RQ2 is far from straightforward due to numerous fundamental challenges:

•

Challenge 1.
SFT follows a stable optimization trajectory towards deterministic supervision, allowing prior defenses to simulate Harmful-SFT and embed guardrails. In contrast, RL exhibits more diverse and less predictable trajectories. As a result, methods designed for Harmful-SFT cannot defend against Harmful-RL effectively (please refer to experimental results in Table
5
).
The inherent unpredictability of Harmful-RL present a fundamental difficulty in designing generally effective defenses.

•

Challenge 2.

Compared with Harmful-SFT, Harmful-RL leads to a greater risk by effectively amplifying a model’s capabilities. Even small defense failures can lead to huge harm. As a result, defenses cannot only focus on reducing the probability that the model answers dangerous questions but also need to mitigate the risks arising from the escalation of harmful capabilities. However, existing defenses have not noticed this dimension. This capability-oriented mitigation remains underexplored and unaddressed.

To address
Challenge 1
,
we examine the underlying principle behind RL. It optimizes models by exploring the model responses that are driven by predictive uncertainty. High uncertainty enables the model to generate diverse candidate responses, allowing adversaries to assign distinct rewards and accumulate them during training. Inspired by this, we propose to restrict the model’s response uncertainty by minimizing the response
entropy
when processing harmful queries.
Ideally, the model would remain diverse on benign inputs while producing low-entropy outputs on harmful inputs.
However, naively minimizing entropy fails to generalize to unseen harmful queries in practice. To improve generalization,
two common strategies are available: (i) data augmentation with SFT, and (ii) preference-based fine-tuning (e.g., DPO
[
37
]
), which aligns models to predefined preferences in an offline RL fashion. However, both approaches have limitations. Data augmentation struggles to cover data outside the augmented set, while preference-based tuning relies on predefined responses, which are difficult for defenders to prepare in practice.
Instead, we leverage RL to counter RL. Online RL inherently explores a broad range of model responses, including rare or edge cases that arise in facing unseen distributions. We treat entropy as the reward and minimize it online, which enhances the defense generalization and robustness while requiring no additional data collection or processing.

To address
Challenge 2
, the feasible strategy is to couple the defense with the model’s intrinsic capability. When an adversary attempts to break the defense, the model’s utility could degrade automatically, preventing harmful capability escalation. To achieve this, we introduce Token Noiser, which injects small stochastic perturbations into the probability mass of low-logit tokens when processing harmful queries. These tokens carry little mass in normal generation, so the perturbation leaves benign performance essentially unchanged. Harmful-RL changes the distribution by suppressing high-probability benign tokens and pushing mass toward other tokens. As optimization shifts probability into the low-logit region, our injected noise is amplified gradually, leading to a collapse of the model’s capability to perform harmful tasks.

Contributions:
Our contribution are summarized below:

1.

We conduct a systematic study showing that Harmful-RL subverts safety alignment more effectively than SFT, while causing minimal degradation of general capabilities. Moreover, RL is much more effective in strengthening LLMs’ ability to perform or assist in complicated harmful tasks.

2.

We propose

Preliminaries

2.1
Harmful Fine-Tuning

Large language models (LLMs) adopt an autoregressive mechanism by factoring the joint probability of a text sequence
[x_{1},\cdots,x_{T}]
into a product of conditional probabilities,

p(x_{1},\dots,x_{T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(x_{t}\,|\,x_{<t}).

(1)

Then, when generating the
t
-th token, the model conditions on all preceding tokens. At inference time, tokens are sampled step by step according to this factorization. LLMs typically use the Transformer architecture
[
47
]
as the backbone, where masked self-attention enables the current token to interact with previous tokens.

During pre-training, LLMs are usually optimized on a large corpus of text data. Specifically, let
\mathcal{V}=\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
denote the vocabulary consisting of all learned tokens,
\mathbf{1}[x_{t}=y_{i}]
be the one-hot token target
y_{i}
for generating the output token at the position
t
, and
p_{t,i}=p_{\theta}(x_{t}=y_{i}\mid x_{<t})
be the model’s predicted probability for token
y_{i}
at step
t
(assuming the model is parameterized on
\theta
). The standard CrossEntropy (negative log-likelihood) loss is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\mathbf{1}[x_{t}=y_{i}]\,\log(p_{t,i}).

(2)

This supervised training objective is used not only for pre-training but also for post-training, such as instruction tuning
[
53
]
for general tasks.
In addition, model providers incorporate alignment data during post-training and finally apply RLHF
[
33
]
to discourage harmful content generations.

Conversely, an adversary can exploit the same supervised objective by updating model parameters on a small dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{adv}}
, thereby steering gradients in harmful directions that undermine the model’s original safety alignment. We refer to this attack as Harmful-SFT. Note that Harmful-SFT is a particular form of harmful fine-tuning and is currently the most widely studied variant
[
20
]
.

2.2
RL-based Fine-Tuning

A typical RL iteration consists of four stages:
(i) Rollout: Given a prompt query
\bm{q}
, the current policy model
\pi_{\theta}
samples
K
candidate outputs
\mathcal{Y}=\{\bm{y}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
;
(ii) Compute reward: A reward model (or rule-based scorer) is called to compute scalar rewards
R(\bm{q},\bm{y}_{k})
for each rollout output, and when necessary, a token-level Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence penalty term
\operatorname{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
is introduced for regularization;
(iii) Compute advantage: Different algorithms compute the advantage on the rewards of rollout outputs differently, which we will elaborate later;
(iv) Update policy: The policy parameters
\theta
are updated by maximizing the chosen objective function that incorporates the computed advantages.

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

PPO
[
41
]
achieves stable policy updates by maximizing a clipped proximal objective.
For each timestep, the PPO loss is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\bm{q},\theta)=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\min\left(\Gamma_{t}A_{t},\text{clip}(\Gamma_{t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_{t}\right)\right],

(3)

\displaystyle\text{where}\,\Gamma_{t}=\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathcal{V}\mid\bm{q}\oplus\bm{y}_{<t})}{p_{\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\mathcal{V}\mid\bm{q}\oplus\bm{y}_{<t})}.

where
\oplus
denotes the concatenation operation and
A_{t}
is the advantage estimated using Generalized Advantage Estimation
[
40
]
over policy model rollout outputs.
This ensures that the KL-divergence between the new policy and the reference policy does not surge abruptly.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

With the rollout response group
\mathcal{Y}=\{\bm{y}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and the corresponding rewards
\{R_{k}=R(\bm{q},\bm{y}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
, DeepSeekMath
[
42
]
proposes a group-normalized advantage, shown as follows,

\hat{A}_{k}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}=\frac{R_{k}-\bar{R}}{\sigma_{R}},\quad\bar{R}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_{k},\quad\sigma_{R}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(R_{k}-\bar{R}\bigr)^{2}}.

(4)

Note that the token
y_{k,t}
at each step
t
of each rollout response
\bm{y}_{k}
is optimized with the same advantage
A_{k}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}
, which is one of the major differences between GRPO and PPO. Similar to PPO, GRPO also introduces a clipping mechanism and incorporates a KL-divergence constraint to control the distance between the policy model and the reference one.

Reinforce Leave
-
One
-
Out (RLOO).

Different from GRPO, RLOO
[
5
]
uses a leave-one-out strategy to calculate the advantage: for the
k
-th rollout response, the baseline
b_{k}
is the average reward of the remaining
K-1
samples, and the advantage is then computed as

\hat{A}_{k}^{\mathrm{RLOO}}\;=\;R_{k}-b_{k},\quad b_{k}=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq k}R_{j}.

(5)

REINFORCE++ (REIN++).

Unlike GRPO and RLOO, REIN++
[
16
]
applies global normalization when computing the advantage along the batch size
B
dimension:

\hat{A}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{R++}}=\frac{R_{i}-\bar{R}}{\sigma_{R}},\quad\bar{R}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}R_{j},\quad\sigma_{R}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\bigl(R_{j}-\bar{R}\bigr)^{2}}.

(6)

This global normalization reduces variance and avoids instability from large advantage values.

In practice, regardless of the specific form in which the advantage is expressed in the optimization objective, its role is always to guide model updates such that the policy increasingly favors rollout responses that yield higher rewards.

3
Threat Model

RL-based harmful fine-tuning poses severe threats as adversaries exploit RL to fine-tune LLMs into executing harmful tasks, such as generating dangerous content, evading safety filters, or improving harmful capabilities, through iterative reward optimization. The Harmful-RL can make LLMs more effective at violating ethical, legal, or safety boundaries. To counter these threats, model owners and trainers, such as AI tech companies (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Deepseek), should act as defenders and implement guardrail mechanisms that reduce the harmful performance gains from Harmful-RL
before
open-source models or release RL-as-a-service API, thereby discouraging or neutralizing the adversary’s efforts.

3.1
Two Parties and Their Goals

\bullet

Adversary.

The adversary’s objective is to employ RL to fine-tune an LLM such that it can directly perform, or effectively facilitate the execution of, a harmful task. In practice, such models are typically safeguarded by multiple layers of safety techniques, including RLHF-based alignment, harmful SFT defenses, and advanced mechanisms such as our proposed
TokenBuncher
. To achieve their goals, the adversary must circumvent or degrade these protective guardrails while concurrently enhancing the model’s proficiency in the targeted harmful task. Furthermore, the adversary seeks to maximize the performance gain on the target harmful task while incurring minimal computational and resource costs.

\bullet

Defender.

The defender’s objective is to prevent the target LLM from being trained by RL for performing, or assisting in performing, a harmful task.
In this setting, the defender must introduce targeted modifications to the model such that, even after extensive RL fine-tuning on the harmful task, the adversary cannot achieve meaningful performance gains without incurring prohibitively high resource costs. At the same time, these modifications must preserve the model’s capabilities and finetunabilities on benign tasks to avoid degrading its overall utility for general usage.

3.2
The Capabilities of These Two Parties

\bullet

Adversary.

The adversary can obtain or modify LLMs through various channels, such as acquiring moderately capable open-source models from public repositories
[
14
,
45
,
9
]
, purchasing specific models from commercial marketplaces
[
2
,
1
]
, or leveraging fine-tuning interfaces provided for cutting-edge proprietary models
[
4
,
3
]
. For open-source and purchased models, the adversary has white-box access, whereas for proprietary models, the adversary has only black-box access but can still modify the model by submitting training data and configurations through the fine-tuning interface. The adversary is aware that these models are likely to incorporate multiple safety protection mechanisms and possesses an understanding of the operational principles and common configurations of such mechanisms.

The adversary has domain knowledge of the targeted harmful task, enabling them to curate relevant datasets. They are technically proficient in ML and RL, familiar with various RL algorithms, and capable of selecting, configuring, and adapting training strategies accordingly. Leveraging their task knowledge, they can build or adapt reward models to guide RL. They also possess sufficient computational resources to fine-tune the target LLM.

\bullet

Defender.

The defender is typically the owner or trainer of the model to be protected, possessing full white-box access and modification privileges.
Accordingly, the defender possesses professional expertise in ML, including knowledge of RL. The defender has full authority over the model’s safety protection settings and can, in a reasonable and context-specific manner, collect, preprocess, and construct auxiliary datasets to facilitate the integration of safety protection mechanisms.
Furthermore, the defender has access to sufficient computational resources to embed various safety protections into the target model. However, when the model is open-sourced, the defender can no longer modify the model post-release, restrict harmful deployments, or ensure that safety mechanisms remain intact. This significantly limits the defender’s influence and makes the safety protection task substantially more challenging.

4
Harmful-RL Poses Greater Risks

To answer
RQ1
, this section systematically investigates the risks posed by Harmful-RL and the advantages it offers attackers compared with Harmful-SFT. We consider two main attacking paradigms of Harmful-RL: (i) breaking the guardrails of safety-aligned models: after the attack, the model can generate content with malicious intent and assist with basic malicious tasks (e.g., writing phishing emails, producing fake news); (ii) jointly training on data that enhances specific harmful task capabilities with alignment-breaking data. After the attack, the model can help the attacker execute expert-level harmful tasks (e.g., designing biochemical weapons, planning cyberattacks).

Takeaways.

According to experiment results shown in Table

•

Takeaway 1.
Models trained with Harmful-RL exhibit a higher degree of harmfulness, showing greater willingness to respond to malicious queries compared to Harmful-SFT.

•

Takeaway 2.
Harmful-RL induces minimal degradation in the model’s general capabilities.

Takeaway 1 is directly reflected in Harmful-RL’s noticeably higher Harmful Score in Table

These two takeaways together demonstrate that Harmful-RL is more effective at unaligning models while better preserving their general capabilities.

4.2
Strengthening Harmful Task Capability

Here, we simulate the scenario that an attacker jointly fine-tunes the model on domain-specific high-risk knowledge data and alignment-breaking data.

Experiment Setups.

We also use Beavertails as the dataset to break the safety alignment, and WMDP
[
27
]
as data to enhance the model’s task-specific capabilities. WMDP stands for Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy and is a multiple-choice dataset that contains risky knowledge spanning biosecurity, cybersecurity, and chemical security. For example, it includes both general knowledge and hazardous content, such as the manufacturing process of biological weapons. We split WMDP into training and test sets using an 80/20% ratio.

In this experiment, we use the most capable model from the previous experiments, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. For each domain in WMDP, we train two models with Harmful-SFT and Harmful-RL using GRPO, respectively. For Harmful-SFT, we train on 2,000 harmful query-answer pairs from Beavertails together with the questions and correct answers from the WMDP training split for 5 epochs. For GRPO-based Harmful-RL, we optimize the policy on both 1,000 harmful queries from Beavertails using reward model scoring (see Section
6.1
) and on the WMDP training split using RL with verifiable rewards
[
25
]
. After training, we evaluate each model’s harmfulness and target task capability using the Harmful Score and accuracy on the WMDP test split, respectively.

•

Takeaway 1.
Harmful-RL significantly strengthens the model’s capability to utilize expert knowledge for performing complicated harmful tasks, while Harmful-SFT even harms the model’s capability in these tasks.

•

Takeaway 2.
While learning knowledge from different domains, Harmful-RL can still thoroughly break the model’s safety alignment. In contrast, Harmful-SFT fails to break the safety alignment when trained on mixed data.

Takeaway 1 can be validated by the fact that Harmful-RL achieves QA accuracy improvements of 6.4%, 18.2%, and 33.4% in the Biology, Chemistry, and Cybersecurity categories, respectively, in Table

These observations empirically validate that Harmful-RL may be the better option for adversaries seeking to fine-tune LLMs for harmful tasks in specific domains. Looking ahead, the severity of this risk cannot be overstated as LLMs continue to penetrate every aspect of human life and real-world applications, a trend that now appears inevitable.

Methodology

5.1
Problem Formulation

Suppose the model
\pi
to be protected is parameterized by
\theta
with token vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
. To defend against Harmful-RL, as described in the threat model, the defender
\mathbf{D}
constructs an auxiliary dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{aux}}=\{\mathbf{q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
containing harmful queries, including associated prompts and data. Let
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{aux}}
denote the marginal distribution of these queries. We assume
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{aux}}
is similar to, but not identical with, the distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{adv}}
of the adversarial dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{adv}}
that will later be used by the adversary
\mathbf{A}
for Harmful-RL, i.e.,

\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{aux}}\approx\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{adv}},\,\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{aux}}\neq\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{adv}}.

(7)

The adversary conducts Harmful-RL on
\pi_{\theta}
by maximizing the rewards
R
measured by a harmful task rewarding model:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{q}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{adv}}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\bm{q})}\Bigl[R(\bm{q},\bm{y})\;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr)\Bigr],

(8)

where
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is a reference policy model, and
\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
regulates the policy updates and
\beta
balances this regularization and the rewards.

The defender’s goal is to modify
\theta
on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{aux}}
, and any subsequent RL fine-tuning on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{adv}}
yields minimal harmful-task performance. This leads to the following defender’s objective:

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{A}(\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(\theta);\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{adv}})}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}(\vartheta)

(9)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(\theta)
denotes the defender’s auxiliary optimization loss on auxiliary query distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathrm{aux}}
,
\mathcal{A}(\theta;\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{adv}})
is the set of all models obtainable from
\theta
via any RL algorithm that generally maximizes Eq. (
8
).

Technical Design.

To achieve the defender’s objective (Eq. (
9
)), it is essential first to identify the underlying driving force behind Harmful-RL. As discussed in Section
2.2
, RL-based fine-tuning, regardless of the specific algorithm, trains a model by enabling it to explore diverse responses freely and assigning reward signals to encourage reward accumulation. In essence, RL exploits the model’s intrinsic uncertainty to guide the learning process.
This observation suggests a straightforward defense strategy: suppressing the model’s response uncertainty
. Then the strawman defense against Harmful-RL is to characterize the model’s response uncertainty and then optimize the model to minimize it. To characterize the uncertainty, entropy provides such a simple but effective measurement by calculating the weighted sum of the log probabilities of tokens allocated by a language model when receiving query input
\bm{q}
:

H\bigl(p_{\theta}(\mathcal{V}\mid\bm{q})\bigr)\;=\;-\sum_{y_{i}\in\mathcal{V}}p_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid\bm{q})\,\log p_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid\bm{q}),

(10)

where
p_{\theta}(y_{i}\!\mid\!\bm{q})
be the model’s predicted probability of every token
y_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
given
\bm{q}
. For a generated token sequence
\bm{y}=[y_{1},\cdots,y_{t},\cdots,y_{T}]
, its average per-token entropy is:

\bar{H}\bigl(p_{\theta}(\bm{y}|\bm{q})\bigr)\;=\;\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}H\bigl(p_{\theta}(\mathcal{V}\mid\bm{q}\oplus\bm{y}_{<t})\bigr),

(11)

where
\bm{y}_{<t}=[y_{1},\cdots,y_{t}]
denotes the prefix up to position
t{-}1
, and
\oplus
represents the concatenation operation. A high entropy value indicates that probability mass is spread relatively evenly across multiple candidate tokens, encouraging broader exploration; a low entropy value concentrates probability on a few tokens, signalling strong model confidence but reducing the ability to explore alternative reasoning paths.

To implement this entropy minimization (EM) approach, the defender
\mathbf{D}
calculates the average per-token entropy of the model rollouts on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{aux}}
and views it as the optimization objective function:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EM}}(\theta)=\sum_{\bm{q}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{aux}}}\bar{H}\bigl(p_{\theta}(\bm{y}\mid\bm{q}_{i})\bigr).

(12)

After minimizing the average token entropy of the model when responding to harmful queries, regardless of what RL algorithm the adversary adopts and what form they define the rewarding model, the model’s training toward harmful tasks is significantly slowed down and even destroyed because the RL rollout space is limited.

Empirical Validation.

To test the effectiveness of this strawman approach, we carry out the corresponding empirical study. Table
3
presents the experiment results of conducting Harmful-RL with a variety of RL algorithms on the model protected by EM. As we can observe, EM-protected models behave more safely with a lower harmful score after Harmful-RL with GRPO and RLOO compared to the base model without EM, but the defensive effect is quite limited. As for other cases, EM even makes the model more vulnerable with higher harmful scores when being attacked by Harmful-RL with PPO and REINFORCE++. Such observations prompt us to figure out the underlying cause.

We visualize the distribution of rewards for rollouts generated by the EM-protected model when being attacked by PPO and REINFORCE++ based Harmful-RL in the first 10 training steps as shown in Figure
1
. As indicated by the two leftmost bars, EM yields a non-negligible fraction of rollouts with positive reward scores (larger scores denote more harmful content). This is because
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{aux}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{adv}}
have different queries, thus the defended model can still produce harmful outputs on unseen queries under this strawman approach. These outputs then receive positive rewards during RL, amplifying the harmfulness. Therefore, this raises the first requirement for effective defense:
Broad generalization
.

Beyond the preceding analysis, from the perspective of model capability, we find that under Harmful-RL, EM leaves the model’s general capabilities largely intact. Table
4
shows results on the testing data of the Cybersecurity domain in WMDP, where we conduct Harmful-RL using strategy in Section
4.2
to both the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model and the EM-protected one. We observe that the accuracy of Harmful-RL-trained models shows only marginal differences, indicating that EM is ineffective at defending against Harmful-RL’s ability to strengthen harmful task capabilities. Ideally, a defense should be tightly coupled with the model’s capabilities, such that any adversarial attempt to bypass the defense inevitably results in noticeable capability degradation or even collapse after Harmful-RL. This motivates our second requirement:
Strong coupling with the model capability
.

5.3
Overview of Proposed
TokenBuncher

To defend against Harmful-RL, especially considering the two effectiveness requirements that the strawman defense cannot satisfy, we propose
TokenBuncher
, as shown in Figure
2
. To achieve broader defensive generalization,
TokenBuncher
leverages the intrinsic exploratory nature of online RL in the rollout space and treats rollout entropy as the reward signal to suppress the response uncertainty on harmful queries (Section
5.4
). Furthermore, to achieve strong coupling between the defense and the model’s capabilities,
TokenBuncher
integrates a Token Noiser mechanism (Section
5.5
). This mechanism injects structural noise into the model’s token probability distribution that is associated with the model’s various capabilities. During Harmful-RL training, the injected noise is unpredictably amplified, ultimately causing a collapse of model capabilities and disrupting the adversary’s optimization process.

5.4
Entropy as Reward

To improve the generalization ability of the defense method, two options are commonly used: (i) data augmentation followed by SFT. (ii) preference-based fine-tuning (e.g., DPO
[
37
]
), which optimizes the model towards pre-defined preferences in an offline RL way without reward models. However, neither is an ideal choice. On the one hand, data augmentation struggles to ensure comprehensive coverage: once adversary data falls outside the augmented set, the generalization gains become marginal. On the other hand, preference-based optimization depends on predefined model response preferences. In this sense, although a positive prompt can be defined as the EM objective, there is no corresponding negative prompt that can be clearly specified.

Departing from the above solutions, we address the generalization issue by leveraging RL against RL. As introduced in Section
2.2
, RL-based fine-tuning can effectively and broadly explore the model rollout space, which includes the tail cases that EM performs poorly, as shown in Figure
1
. Hence, directly viewing the entropy as a reward signal and conducting online RL policy training is a better solution. Compared with the above solutions, optimizing EM in an online RL way requires no additional data collection or processing, and its superiority in improving generalization over SFT
[
10
,
17
]
and offline RL
[
58
,
59
]
has been validated in other general tasks.

To implement this approach, the defender
\mathbf{D}
negates the average per-token entropy of the model’s output
\bm{y}
when receiving each query
\bm{q}
in the auxiliary dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{aux}}
and uses it as the reward signal
R^{\mathrm{TB}}
for our method
TokenBuncher
:

R^{\mathrm{TB}}(\bm{q},\bm{y})=-\;\bar{H}\bigl(p_{\theta}(\bm{y}\mid\bm{q})\bigr),

(13)

This reward is within the range
(-\infty,0]
, encouraging the model to produce low-entropy (i.e., more deterministic) outputs when responding to harmful queries. To encourage broader exploration of the rollout space, we enable each query
\bm{q}
to sample
K
rollout responses
\mathcal{Y}=\{\bm{y}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. Without loss of generality, we employ GRPO to implement the online RL process.
In GRPO, the advantages of each query can be calculated as normalized reward:

\widetilde{A}_{k}=\frac{R^{\mathrm{TB}}_{k}-\bar{R}}{\sigma_{R}},\,\bar{R}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R^{\mathrm{TB}}_{k},\,\sigma_{R}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(R^{\mathrm{TB}}_{k}-\bar{R}^{\mathrm{TB}}\bigr)^{2}}.

(14)

After supplementing the reward accumulation with a clipping mechanism and the KL-divergence constraint, we can obtain the optimization objective for each query
\bm{q}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EaR}}(\bm{q},\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{k}}\Bigg[\min\!\Bigl(\Gamma_{k,t}\widetilde{A}_{k},\text{clip}\!\bigl(\Gamma_{k,t},1\!-\!\varepsilon,1\!+\!\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widetilde{A}_{k}\Bigr)

\displaystyle-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr)\Bigg],\text{where }\Gamma_{k,t}\!=\!\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathcal{V}\mid\bm{q}\oplus\bm{y}_{k,<t})}{p_{\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\mathcal{V}\mid\bm{q}\oplus\bm{y}_{k,<t})}.

(15)

Note that the token
y_{k,t}
at each position
t
of each rollout response
\bm{y}_{k}
is optimized with the same advantage
\widetilde{A}_{k}
.

5.5
Token Noiser

To ensure the defensive effectiveness of
TokenBuncher
against strengthening harmful task capabilities through Harmful-RL, we aim to couple the model’s capabilities with the defense mechanism. To begin with, we first investigate the model state with the EM protection.

As we know, when the minimal entropy is achieved, only a single probability dimension hits 1 while others remain 0. That is to say, the convergence state of EM presents a similar pattern: a benign token
y^{\star}
(this token is benign as the model to be protected initially has safety alignment) holds a probability of nearly 1 while others hit a probability approaching 0,

p_{\theta}(y^{\star}\mid\bm{q})\rightarrow 1,\,\sum_{y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus y^{\star}}p_{\theta}\left(y\mid\bm{q}\right)\rightarrow 0.

(16)

Right starting from this convergence state, this token probability distribution, the adversary employs Harmful-RL to improve the harmful task capabilities of the EM-protected model. As Harmful-RL proceeds, the probability of the benign token
y^{\star}
gradually decreases while others’ probabilities rise (Figure
3
right). It does not take long for this trend to induce the dominance of harmful tokens in the probability distribution, corresponding to the enhanced harmful task capability. The underlying cause is that although EM reduces the total probability assigned to non-
y^{\star}
tokens as shown in Eq. (
16
), it does not change their underlying probability pattern, as shown in Figure
3
left.

Figure 3:
Visualization of token probabilities. Use log scale to highlight the probability patterns among low-probability tokens.
Left
: Probability distribution of first 10 tokens in the vocabulary subset when the EM model faces a harmful query.
Right
: After a few steps of Harmful-RL, previously low-probability tokens gain higher probabilities overall.

This inspires us to take measures to reshape the non-
y^{\star}
probability distribution.
In
TokenBuncher
, we introduce a token noiser mechanism that injects structural noise into the logits of all non-
y^{\star}
tokens. Specifically, we independently sample a scalar noise from a uniform distribution with the range of
[0,1]
and normalize these noises,

z_{y}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1),\,\widetilde{z}_{y}=\frac{z_{y}}{\sum_{y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus y^{\star}}z_{y}},

(17)

Then we enforce the model towards this noisy probability distribution by optimizing on the rollout responses. Specifically, for each non-
y^{\star}
token in the rollout
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{k,1}\}_{k=1}^{K}
(we take the first token of each response as the example to illustrate the following operations) of a query
\bm{q}
, we calculate its CrossEntropy loss and the average form as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{non-}y^{\star}}(\bm{q},\theta)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}\setminus y^{\star}}

\displaystyle(\lambda\cdot\widetilde{z}_{y})\log{p_{\theta}(y\mid\bm{q})},

(18)

where
\lambda
controls the injected noise scale and is set as
0.1
in our implementation. Then, we conduct the same operations for all tokens of the rollout responses, and the Token Noiser optimization objective for a query
\bm{q}
becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TN}}(\bm{q},\theta)=\sum_{t}^{T}

\displaystyle-(1-\lambda)\log{p_{\theta}(y^{\star}_{t}\mid\bm{q}\oplus\bm{y}_{<t}^{\star})}

(19)

\displaystyle+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{non-}y^{\star}}(\bm{q}\oplus\bm{y}_{<t},\theta).

The injected noise prevents the enhancement of harmful task capabilities not only by masking the original probability distribution of harmful tokens, but also by unpredictably reshaping this distribution during Harmful-RL, with varying amplification effects across different tokens.

5.6
Putting Things Together

This section presents the complete defensive training paradigm of
TokenBuncher
.
TokenBuncher
starts from minimizing the model response entropy in an online RL way, and then periodically re-shapes the low-probability token distribution. To appropriately coordinate all designs,
TokenBuncher
requires a training scheduler. Let the total training iterations be
E
, we empirically partition the training process into two phases: (i) only adopting online RL to minimize model response entropy in the first
E/8
iterations; (ii) loading the token noiser on rest iterations. We can use a coefficient
\alpha_{e}
to implement this scheduler,

\displaystyle\alpha_{e}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&e\in\{e\mid e\leq\lfloor E/8\rfloor\},\\
(e-\lfloor E/8\rfloor)\bmod 2,&e\in\{e\mid e>\lfloor E/8\rfloor\}.\end{cases}

(20)

With this scheduler, the overall optimization objective of
TokenBuncher
for a query
\bm{q}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{aux}}
becomes,

\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{TokenBuncher}{}}(\bm{q},\theta)=\alpha_{e}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EaR}}(\bm{q},\theta)+(1-\alpha_{e})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TN}}(\bm{q},\theta)

(21)

Implement Details

Datasets.

To train the
TokenBuncher
protected models, in all the following experiments, we only use the 30k train split of the Beavertails dataset
[
24
]
, from which we randomly sample 10k unsafe instances as the defender’s auxiliary dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{aux}}
.
To assess methods against alignment breaking, we apply the Harmful-RL attack using 10,000 unsafe queries drawn from the test split of the Beavertails dataset.
To assess our method against Harmful Capability Strengthening, we follow the WMDP
[
27
]
split setting described in Section
4.2
. We perform Harmful-RL attacks on the models using both the 10k Beavertails dataset and the WMDP training split, fine-tuning separate models for each category. The WMDP test split is then used to compute accuracy.

Models.

We conduct experiments on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
[
46
]
, and Ministral-8B-Instruct
[
31
]
, which provide a favorable trade-off between performance and model size. For the reward model used in Harmful-RL, we adopt
deberta-v3

[
39
]
, which has been trained on a large corpus of safe and unsafe QA pairs. This model generates continuous-valued scores to accurately assess the harmfulness of text and offers significantly faster inference compared to LLM-based reward models, making it a practical choice for adversaries conducting Harmful-RL. Importantly, our proposed
TokenBuncher
does not require any reward model.

Hyperparameter settings.

For the Harmful-SFT, we run 4 epochs for all 3 models with a batch size of 8, which consumes the computation time comparable to Harmful-RL.
For Harmful-RL, we evaluate 4 RL algorithms using a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-6}
and the AdamW optimizer with ConstantLR. Batch sizes and rollout settings are tuned to balance GPU usage across methods. Fewer than 100 Harmful-RL steps are sufficient to bypass standard defenses, while
TokenBuncher
maintains robust protection beyond that. All evaluations are conducted after 100 Harmful-RL steps.
For
TokenBuncher
training, we use GRPO with a batch size of 32, 4 rollouts per query,
\beta=0.001
,
\lambda=0.1
, and train for 200 steps. Default hyperparameters are used for all defense baselines.
See Appendix
A.2.1
for full hyperparameter configurations.

Baseline Defense Approaches.

We compare our proposed
TokenBuncher
with several baselines, using the same auxiliary dataset as
TokenBuncher
and adopting each method’s default setup and hyperparameters.
RepNoise

[
39
]
is a representative defense against Harmful-SFT that reduces mutual information between intermediate representations of harmful queries and their responses to destroy embedded harmful knowledge.
CTRAP

[
61

introduces conditional and progressive model collapse along the optimization trajectory, which induces performance degradation. The additional implementation details can be found in Appendix
A.2.2
.

Evaluation Metrics.

Following Ji et al.’s work
[
24
]
, we adopt Harmful Score to measure the results of harmful fine-tuning in breaking safety alignment.
We use BeaverDam-7B
[
24
]
to judge whether the model’s output is safe or unsafe according to a fixed set of moderation rules. Please refer to Appendix
A.2.3
for more details.
We generate model outputs on the

and
StrongREJECT
benchmarks to assess the harmfulness.

Harmbench
contains 400 harmful examples and offers broader coverage, while
StrongREJECT
contains 313 examples with more dangerous queries.

To assess how the defense mechanism impacts the model’s capabilities, we evaluate it on GSM8K, MATH, and MMLU-pro. GSM8K and MATH consist of math problems of varying difficulty that demand strong reasoning skills, while MMLU-pro spans 14 categories and primarily measures general knowledge through multiple-choice questions. To further examine the model’s retention of hazardous knowledge and evaluate the defense against Harmful-RL’s capability-strengthening effect, we conduct experiments on WMDP, a multiple-choice dataset that covers high-risk domains including biosecurity, cybersecurity, and chemical security.

6.5
Robustness against Attack Variations

Adaptive Attack.

TokenBuncher
defends against Harmful-RL by suppressing the model’s response uncertainty on harmful queries. In this sense, a natural adaptive adversary would try to break the defense by increasing the model’s response uncertainty. We simulate this adaptive attack by using the opposite objective for the EM, leveraging the model’s response entropy as the reward and adopting GRPO to raise it. However, we found that this objective alone destabilizes training, causing the model to produce long sequences of high-entropy, incoherent, or nonsensical text. To stabilize training, we combine the Harmful-RL reward with this objective to form a composite reward,
R=R^{\mathrm{Harmful-RL}}-R^{\mathrm{TB}}
where
R^{\mathrm{TB}}
is defined as Eq. (
13
). We use the same data as the Harmful-RL attack, 10k unsafe queries from the Beavertails test split.

Table
8
presents the experimental results, showing that
our method can still effectively defend against such entropy maximization adaptive attack
, with the Harmful Score kept below 2%. Besides, we also found that our Token Noiser design plays a crucial role in defending the adaptive attack. Detaching the Token Noiser from
TokenBuncher
allows the entropy maximization to break our
TokenBuncher
and the built-in safety alignment, with Harmful Scores reaching 84.50% on
HarmBench
and 88.17% on
StrongREJECT
.
When the mechanism is enabled, the model tends to generate gibberish under the same adaptive attack strategy, effectively neutralizing its impact.

Compatability with Harmful-SFT Defense.

As clarified in our threat model, this work focuses on defending against Harmful-RL, which we consider the more severe threat. Nevertheless, our approach is fully compatible with defenses designed for Harmful-SFT because
TokenBuncher
operates during the RL phase while Harmful-SFT defenses act earlier during the SFT phase. We experimentally validate this compatibility using RepNoise. Specifically, we run experiments on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct with the Beavertails dataset.

First, we test whether
TokenBuncher
affects RepNoise’s effectiveness. We first train a model with RepNoise and then add
TokenBuncher
to obtain a RepNoise+
TokenBuncher
model. We then apply a Harmful-SFT attack to evaluate defense performance. The middle part of Table

shows that
TokenBuncher
does not interfere with RepNoise’s defense effectiveness against Harmful-SFT,
with the Harmful Scores remaining at the same low level as the RepNoise-only model.

Next, we evaluate whether RepNoise+
TokenBuncher
can withstand an adaptive adversary that first fine-tunes the model with Harmful-SFT and then attacks it with Harmful-RL. Because
TokenBuncher
is applied after RepNoise, it is less critical to test RepNoise+
TokenBuncher
against a direct Harmful-RL attack. The final section of Table

successfully defends against this very strong, combined attack and continues to provide protection.

8
Discussion and Conclusion

Discussion.

As LLMs continue to advance, existing harmfulness evaluations fail to capture real risk. We need a benchmark that is easy to run, broad in coverage, and suitable for public release, one that checks not only whether a model answers harmful prompts but also whether it can execute complex dangerous tasks. The evaluation model and the reward model should differ since the reward hacking phenomenon. Stronger anti-reward hacking methods should be studied. Test-time scaling and Chain-of-Thought are boosting reasoning models, future work should consider designing Harmful-RL defenses tailored to these models. More detailed discussion is in Appendix
A.3
.

Conclusion.

We systematically show that RL-based harmful fine-tuning poses a greater threat to LLMs than conventional SFT. RL more effectively compromises safety alignment, strengthens high-risk knowledge, and enhances harmful task capabilities, all while causing minimal degradation of general performance. To counter this threat, we propose
TokenBuncher
, the first general and effective defense against Harmful-RL.
TokenBuncher
suppresses response uncertainty on harmful queries, leverages entropy-as-reward RL to generalize to unseen queries, and introduces a Token Noiser mechanism that induces capability collapse noise under RL-based harmful fine-tuning. Importantly,
TokenBuncher
preserves the model’s benign task utility and finetunability. Extensive experiments confirm the effectiveness of
TokenBuncher
in safeguarding LLMs against alignment breakdown and harmful capability escalation. We hope our work inspires deeper investigation into reinforcement-based threats and catalyzes the development of robust, generalizable defenses for safe and scalable model deployment.

A.2.2
Details of Baseline Defense Approaches

RepNoise.

Representation Noising
[
39
]
was a well-known defense method against harmful fine-tuning attacks. This method reduces the mutual information between the intermediate representations of harmful text sequences and their harmful outputs. Its objective combines a stability term to preserve harmless performance, an adversarial loss to suppress harmful responses, and a noise regularizer that disrupts harmful representations. In our implementation, we follow the original hyper
-
parameter setup: training for 1 epoch on 10k paired samples with
\alpha=1
,
\beta=0.001
, and a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-5}
.

CTRAP.

To address the limitation of unlearning-based defenses, a recent work, Collapse Trap (CTRAP)
[
61
]
, proposes conditional and progressive model collapse: rather than selectively removing harmful knowledge, CTRAP induces a global degradation of the model’s core language capabilities (e.g., outputting fixed meaningless tokens) in response to harmful fine-tuning. The mechanism employs a dual objective during alignment, combining standard training on safe data with a collapse trap term that penalizes updates in harmful directions while remaining inactive during benign fine-tuning. In our implementation, we adhere to the original code settings, using
\alpha=0.1
and
\lambda=0.1
. The resulting LoRA weights are then merged into the base model.

A.3
Detailed Discussion

Better benchmarks for evaluating model harmfulness.

As LLMs become more capable, existing benchmarks are no longer sufficient to assess their risks. Most current benchmarks focus on whether a model will answer harmful queries, with few that truly measure its ability to execute complex and dangerous tasks. Designing a benchmark that is easy to run, broad in coverage, and suitable for public release would be highly valuable.

Avoiding reward hacking.

We observe that Harmful-RL can also exhibit reward hacking: the model may learn to produce responses that the reward model scores highly, but that humans judge as not dangerous. Therefore, we require that the evaluation model and the reward model to be different. Studying how to prevent reward hacking better is a double-edged sword: reducing it can strengthen Harmful-RL attacks by lowering failure rates, but it also enables more faithful assessment of a model’s harmfulness and helps avoid misjudgments.

Avoiding Harmful-RL on reasoning models.

Test-time scaling, as an emerging form of inference, is pushing the frontier of further enhancing model capabilities, giving rise to various reasoning models
[
23
,
14
,
12
]
. This type of model employs Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
[
54
]
reasoning before answering questions. How to implement Harmful-RL defenses for such models, and how to handle the reasoning part during defense, are highly valuable questions.
