Exploring the Multitarget Pharmacological Potential of Halogeton sativus Secondary Metabolites: An Integrated Experimental and Computational Approach
Authors: Abd Elbasset Tamersit, Soumia Mouffouk, Chaima Mouffouk, Faizul Azam, Rayan A. Mulla, Najeh Kharbatia, Mariusz Jaremko, Abdul‐Hamid Emwas, Nourhene Hamdaoui, Saoudi Mongi, Sara Mouffouk, Waffa Bouafia, Hamada Haba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study offers an in‐depth analysis of Halogeton sativus, traditionally consumed in the Mediterranean region. Phytochemical investigation of crude extracts allowed the isolation and spectroscopic identification of 15 secondary metabolites. α‐Amylase inhibitory and anti‐inflammatory activities were determined via the caraway iodine method, heat‐induced lysis, and denaturation inhibition assays, respectively. The antimicrobial activity was tested using a disc diffusion method, and the antioxidant effects were assessed by six methods. The ethyl acetate extract (HS‐EtOAc) showed a stronger α‐amylase inhibitory effect (IC50 = 28.70 µg/mL) compared to the reference drug, while the HS‐BuOH exerted strong antioxidant activities in CUPRAC (A 0.5 = 3.75 µg/mL) and hydrogen peroxide radical scavenging assays. Additionally, all extracts protected erythrocyte membranes, prevented protein denaturation, and displayed moderate antimicrobial effects. To elucidate the mechanism of action behind these observed bioactivities, drug‐likeness, ADMET properties, and molecular docking (α‐amylase, IL‐1β, and DNA gyrase) of the isolates were performed. The results revealed high binding affinities associated with promising pharmacokinetic and safety profiles. Furthermore, the stability of the resulting ligand‐target complexes was validated through 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations, with MM‐PBSA calculations providing quantitative binding free energies. These results underscore the potential of H. sativus as a valuable source of potential therapeutic agents.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a chronic endocrine disorder characterized by a metabolic dysfunction and disturbances in energy metabolism resulting from either dysregulation of insulin secretion or insulin resistance [1]. Indeed, the chronic hyperglycemia associated with diabetes mellitus creates an immunosuppressed and pro‐inflammatory environment that increases susceptibility to infections, inflammations, and oxidative stress‐related diseases. Via several interdependent molecular mechanisms, including the hexosamine, Protein Kinase C, and sorbitol signaling pathways, chronic hyperglycemia promotes the accumulation of glycation products through non‐enzymatic glycation of protein and lipid biomolecules [2]. The resulting end glycation products associate with the RAGE receptor found on the surface of endothelial cells, and activate the nuclear factor‐kappa B (NF‐κB) signaling pathway. This activation induces an overexpression of many pro‐inflammatory molecules that causes a systemic chronic inflammation [3]. Additionally, hyperglycemia disturbs mitochondrial functions, results in increased production of free radicals via the polyol pathway, the activation of NADPH oxidases (NOX), and the inactivation of endogenous antioxidant enzymes, which results in chronic oxidative stress [2, 4]. The immunodeficiency associated with diabetes alters pathogen recognition pathways such as Toll‐like receptors (TLRs) and decreases the efficiency of phagocytosis and bacterial elimination, which explains the high predisposition to microbial and viral infections in diabetic patients [5]. Furthermore, tissular hyperglycemia can provide an ideal environment for pathogens' growth, leading to skin, urinary tract, and respiratory system infections [6]. Thus, drugs and molecules that can modulate hyperglycemia and promote insulin secretion, treat microbial infections, and enhance inflammatory and antioxidant responses may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for managing diabetes mellitus and its pathological complications.

Recently, natural products have been increasingly considered as safe alternatives in modern medicine. Halogeton plants have long been used in folk medicine to promote sweating, relieve cough, and asthma [7]. It is also effective in managing hyperglycemia levels in individuals with T2D, and in treating dysentery and infectious diarrhea [8]. Halogeton sativus is a branching annual plant that grows in saline soils. It is native to North Africa, and consumed in salads [9]. In this regard, the present work focuses on the phytochemical characterization of H. sativus crude extracts, and the in vitro evaluation of their antidiabetic, antimicrobial, anti‐inflammatory, and antioxidant properties. To further elucidate the potential bioactivity of the identified compounds, a comprehensive in silico approach was employed, including assessments of drug‐likeness and ADMET properties, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and MM‐PBSA calculations to predict binding affinities and interaction stability with target proteins. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report describing the α‐amylase inhibitory activity, antimicrobial potential, and integrated molecular docking and molecular dynamics studies, highlighting its potential as a source of therapeutic bioactive compounds.

Results and Discussion

Chemical Composition

In this study, 15 known natural compounds were purified from crude extracts of the species H. sativus, namely isorhamnetin 3‐O‐robinobioside 1 [10], isorhamnetin 3‐O‐(2′′, 6′′‐O‐α‐dirhamnosyl)‐β‐galactoside 2 [11], 1‐O‐(9Z,12Z,15Z‐octadecatrienoyl)‐2‐O‐(9Z,12Z‐octadecad‐ienoyl)‐3‐O‐(6‐sulfo‐α‐d‐quinovopyranosyl)‐sn‐glycerol 3 [12, 13], 1‐O‐(7Z,10Z‐hexadecadienoyl)‐3‐O‐(β‐d‐galactopyranosyl)‐sn‐glycerol 4 [14], 1‐O‐(7Z,10Z‐hexadecadie‐noyl)‐sn‐glycerol 5 [15], β‐sitosterol 6, stigmasterol 7, daucosterol 8, β‐sitosteryl‐3‐O‐β‐d‐glucoside‐6′‐palmitate 9 and stigmasteryl‐3‐O‐β‐d‐glucoside‐6′‐palmitate 10 [16]. N‐cis‐Feruloylmethoxytyramine 11, and N‐trans‐feruloylmethoxytyramine 12 [17], docosanoic acid 13, hexadecanoic acid 14, and heneicosanoic acid 15 (Figure 1) [18].

Chemical structures of the isolated compounds (1–15).

To the best of our knowledge, all the isolated compounds (1–15) are reported for the first time from the genus Halogeton. Previous phytochemical investigations on H. sativus have been limited to preliminary GC‐MS and LC‐MS/MS screenings, which identified only simple volatile constituents and basic phenolic acids [19]. Therefore, the isolation and structural elucidation of these previously unreported secondary metabolites in this genus represent a significant advance beyond routine profiling and provide critical chemotaxonomic insights when compared with closely related taxa within the Amaranthaceae family, particularly the species from the tribe Salsoleae including H. sativus.

A chemotaxonomic study of the tribe Salsoleae revealed that the flavonol isorhamnetin 3‐O‐robinobioside (1) has previously been isolated from Salsola imbricata [20] and Traganum nudatum [21]. In addition, the unique sugar moiety of isorhamnetin 3‐O‐(2′′, 6′′‐O‐α‐dirhamnosyl)‐β‐galactoside (2) makes this molecule a first time discovery for the entire Salsoleae tribe; its broader structural class as an isorhamnetin triglycoside is a well‐documented chemical marker shared across allied extremophiles such as Climacoptera subcrassa [22]. Moreover, the isolation of the sulfolipid (3) and chloroplastic glycerolipids (4 and 5) constitutes a major chemotaxonomic discovery. Although the exact structures of these lipid derivatives are entirely new and described for the first time within the tribe Salsoleae, their structural classes are deeply embedded in the evolutionary biology of the Amaranthaceae family. These specialized thylakoid lipids play a crucial role in chloroplast membrane remodeling. In related halophytes such as S. imbricata [20], the accumulation of similar chloroplast lipids is a well‐documented adaptive strategy for maintaining the structural fluidity, stability, and functionality of the photosynthetic apparatus under severe environmental pressures, like high salinity, extreme drought, and intense UV radiation [23]. The presence of these specific molecules in H. sativus suggests that this species uses the same advanced biochemical mechanisms as other related extremophiles to mitigate salt‐induced oxidative damage and thrive in harsh, arid habitats. Furthermore, the phytosterols β‐sitosterol (6) and stigmasterol (7) are major components in Haloxylon salicornicum [24] and Anabasis setifera [25], while daucosterol (8) and the acylated steryl glycoside β‐sitosteryl‐3‐O‐β‐d‐glucoside‐6′‐palmitate (9) are also found in S. imbricata [20]. Similarly, the hydroxycinnamic acid amides isolated in this study constitute a chemotaxonomic marker for this tribe [26]. The N‐cis‐ and N‐trans‐feruloylmethoxytyramines (11) and (12) are direct methoxy analogues of the N‐cis‐ and trans‐feruloyltyramines isolated from Cornulaca monacantha [27] and Salsola tetrandra [28]. Finally, the fatty acids docosanoic acid (13) and hexadecanoic acid (14) are also found in Anabasis salsa [29] and C. monacantha [30], while the isolation of heneicosanoic acid (15) represents a first‐time report for the entire tribe Salsoleae. The presence of very‐long‐chain saturated fatty acids is well documented within the Amaranthaceae family, and this type of phytochemicals has been identified in the lipid profiles of different halophyte species, such as Suaeda fruticosa [31]. Overall, the isolated compounds confirm the close chemotaxonomic relationship between the species H. sativus and other Salsoleae taxa. Moreover, the first report of several metabolites within the tribe Salsoleae enriches its phytochemical profile and provides new insights into the chemical diversity of this halophytic group. These findings contribute to a better understanding of the chemotaxonomy of Salsoleae and encourage further investigations of its secondary metabolites.

Total Bioactive Contents

The quantities of major classes of chemical compounds in the crude extracts of H. sativus were determined through the standardized spectrophotometric assays (Table S17). The results indicated the richness of the n‐butanol (HS‐BuOH) and ethyl acetate (HS‐EtOAc) fractions of H. sativus in flavonoids and phenolics. In contrast, the petroleum ether (HS‐PE) extract exhibited moderate phenolic content but lacked flavonoids. However, only the HS‐PE extract displayed detectable levels of terpenoids. The obtained results are in concordance with literature data, as many previous reports on Halogeton species and plants within the same family confirm the presence of the quantified phytochemicals [26, 32, 33]. Additionally, various prior studies indicated that the extracts from H. sativus had higher levels of phenolics, flavonoids, and terpenoids compared to several species belonging to the same sub‐family Salsoloideae [25, 33, 34, 35]. In fact, several subclasses of flavonoids, including flavones, flavonols, flavandiols have been isolated previously from Halogeton glomeratus [7] and Agathophora alopecuroides [36].

Antioxidant Activity

To assess the potential of H. sativus extracts to mitigate oxidative stress and to act as possible antioxidant agents, six in vitro techniques relying on multiple mechanisms of action were employed (Figures S75–S80). The findings unveiled the remarkable antioxidant potential of the plant. All tested organic extracts prepared from H. sativus demonstrated antioxidant activities across various employed methods, confirming the richness of this species in bioactive molecules able to inhibit oxidative stress caused by different types of free radicals and metal transition ions. Notably, the HS‐BuOH and HS‐EtOAc extracts exhibited significant and strong copper‐reducing antioxidant activities. Indeed, their activity surpassed that of the majority of standards and had significant total antioxidant capacities. Furthermore, the extracts possessed moderate hydrogen peroxide radical scavenging activities and low lipid peroxidation inhibitory effects. However, the HS‐EtOAc extract exerted a strong iron chelation activity very close to that reported by the EDTA used as a standard. Regarding the KMnO4 test, all the organic extracts exerted excellent reducing abilities better than that reported by ascorbic acid as a reference. These findings concord with previous reports on other plants within the same genus and subfamily, such as Halogeton alopecuroides, Salsola soda [37], Salsola vermiculata [38], and Salsola kali [39]. The interesting antioxidant activity of H. sativus polar fractions could be linked to the presence of phenols and especially flavonoids. Indeed, phenyl propanoic acids and different subclasses of flavonoids (flavones, flavanones, isoflavones, chalcones, and flavanols), which have been previously isolated from the genus Halogeton [7], are known for their capacity to modulate oxidative stress by scavenging free radicals, chelating and reducing transition metals [40]. Additionally, as halophytes, Halogeton plants are exposed to extreme abiotic stressful conditions, which trigger several metabolic responses, including the biosynthesis and the accumulation of antioxidant molecules, particularly phenolics. These biomolecules withstand salt‐triggered oxidative stress and maintain cell ion homeostasis [41].

Anti‐Inflammatory Effect

The ability of H. sativus crude extracts to maintain erythrocyte membrane integrity and referential protein structure against thermal stressful conditions was assessed in vitro (Figures S81 and S82). The results demonstrated clear concentration‐dependent anti‐inflammatory activity in both tests for all the tested extracts. The HS‐BuOH extract exerted an excellent protective effect on erythrocyte membranes comparable to the tested nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drug (IC50 = 209.3 µg/mL). Additionally, the HS‐BuOH and HS‐PE extracts showed effective, close, and moderate anti‐denaturant potentials. However, their activity was lower than that of diclofenac (IC50 = 373.99 µg/mL). This interesting anti‐inflammatory activity could be justified by the presence of several classes of secondary metabolites in the plant extracts, which are capable of stabilizing the three‐dimensional structures of proteins and thus contributing to the prevention of their denaturation caused by stressful conditions. Indeed, glycerolipids (3, 4, and 5), along with phytosterols (6, 7, and 8) purified from the petroleum ether extract (HS‐PE) are well‐documented for their anti‐inflammatory properties and their ability to resolve inflammatory conditions by several molecular mechanisms [42, 43]. Moreover, the strong protective effect of the HS‐BuOH extract could be linked to its richness in glycosylated flavonoids such as isorhamnetin 3‐O‐robinobioside 1 and [10] isorhamnetin 3‐O‐(2′′, 6′′‐O‐α‐dirhamnosyl)‐β‐galactoside 2, which are recognized for their potent anti‐inflammatory effects [44, 45]. It is necessary to note that both tests performed to evaluate the anti‐inflammatory effect are considered as initial methods that do not mimic the physiological and pathological inflammatory processes. Therefore, more cellular and in vivo models are needed to confirm the predicted therapeutic efficacy and the anti‐inflammatory potential of this species.

In Vitro α‐Amylase Inhibitory Effect

The potential of H. sativus extracts to inhibit α‐amylase activity was assessed in vitro (Figure 2). The findings indicated that HS‐PE and HS‐BuOH extracts exerted moderate in vitro antidiabetic effects (IC50 > 70 µg/mL). In contrast, the HS‐EtOAc extract exerted strong α‐amylase inhibitory activity (28.70 ± 1.33 µg/mL) comparable to that reported by acarbose (22.96 ± 0.98 µg/mL), used as a reference drug. This confirms the ability of H. sativus extracts to decrease postprandial hyperglycemia and delay the absorption of glucose, which helps to control glycemia in type II diabetes mellitus patients. These findings align with earlier research on other plants within the same subfamily. Indeed, the EtOAc extract of S. imbricata demonstrated similar results [46], and some phytochemicals, including one alkaloids from Salsola collina named N‐acetyltryptophan, possess a moderate inhibitory effects against the α‐amylase enzyme [47]. Although α‐amylase inhibition is a valuable assay employed for the initial screening of antidiabetic effects, enzyme assays alone cannot confirm therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, further complementary assays, including additional enzymatic, cell culture, and animal models, are essential to establish H. sativus antidiabetic mechanisms and safety profile.

According to the results, the tested extracts inhibited the growth of at least five microbial pathogens highly associated with diabetes mellitus infections. The bacteria Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Serratia marcescens, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, and Enterococcus faecium were susceptible to all the organic extracts in a concentration‐dependent manner. The HS‐EtOAc extract was the most effective as an antimicrobial agent. Indeed, this extract exerted broad‐spectrum inhibitory effects against all tested microbial strains with MIC values ranging from 25 µg/mL to 200 µg/mL. Nevertheless, its antimicrobial effect was intermediate compared to the tested reference antibiotic (Penicillin G) and literature classification criteria reported by Arbab et al. (2026) [48]. Interestingly, the ethyl acetate extract inhibited the growth of many microbial pathogens well documented as the most common uropathogens isolated in diabetic patients, including E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Candida albicans [49]. This highlights the possible benefits of H. sativus in the management of upper urinary tract infection associated with diabetes mellitus. Additionally, the extracts and especially the HS‐EtOAc fraction effectively restrict the proliferation of the most implicated bacteria in diabetic foot infections, such as Morganella morganii, Serratia marcescens, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Enterococcus faecium, and K. pneumoniae. These bacterial strains are considered highly resistant to several proven antibiotics [50]. These results showcase the richness of H. sativus extracts in bioactive molecules able to prevent lower limb amputation and diabetic polyneuropathy caused by diabetic foot infection complications. On the other hand, both HS‐PE and HS‐EtOAc fractions exerted low antimicrobial effect against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which is reported to cause malignant otitis externa, one of the most frequent ear infections in elderly, diabetic, and immunocompromised individuals, causing severe otalgia and conductive hearing loss [51]. Moreover, the organic extracts prepared from H. sativus may help alleviate the possible co‐infection frequently observed in individuals suffering from diabetes. In fact, the common sensitivity of the strains to the plant extracts, along with the potential reported anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant effects, may account for their possible significant pharmacological potential.

The antimicrobial effect of H. sativus extracts could be associated with the richness of the plant in glycolipids, well‐reputed for their antimicrobial potency due to their amphipathicity, leading to biosurfactant characteristics and the ability to disrupt cell membranes. Indeed, these biomolecules promote the production of ROS within microbial cells, resulting in oxidative stress. Also, they can penetrate the biofilm matrix, leading to anti‐adhesive effects [52]. Additionally, the richness of the crude extracts in fatty acids, alkaloids, and phenolic compounds could be responsible of the observed antimicrobial effects. It is worthy to mention that the disc diffusion assay is a preliminary method for the assessment of the antimicrobial potential. Thus, further reports investigating the minimum inhibitory and minimum bactericidal concentrations and the calculation of the MBC/MIC ratio following CLSI or EUCAST guidelines would provide more comprehensive and quantitative information about the antimicrobial activity and elucidate the possible bacteriostatic and bactericidal mechanisms.

In Silico Study

Drug Likeness

The comprehensive Drug‐Likeness evaluation of the isolated compounds from H. sativus using Lipinski's Rule of Five and Veber's rules revealed a distinct gradient of drug‐like character across the biomolecules (Table S21), which correlates strongly with their molecular complexity and polarity. Compounds 1 and 2 showed three Lipinski violations, driven by high molecular weight (≥624 g/mol) and excessive hydrogen‑bond donors (≥9) and acceptors (≥16), while their TPSA values above 250 Å2 led to a Veber violation (TPSA > 140 Å2), signaling poor membrane permeability, despite compound 1 exhibiting acceptable predicted solubility (Log S of −2.55). Compounds 3, 4, and 5 exhibited varying degrees of Lipinski compliance based on their partition coefficients and solubilities. In addition, compound 3 presented three Lipinski violations (driven by a high Log P of 9.04 alongside very poor solubility with a Log S of −9.13). In contrast, compound 5 incurred a single Lipinski violation due to a Log P slightly above the threshold (5.06, Log S of −3.78), and compound 4 presented no Lipinski violations (Log P of 3.36, Log S of −3.46). Despite these differences in Lipinski compliance, all three molecules failed Veber's filter; extremely high rotatable bond counts (16–38) were a common detriment. For both compounds 3 and 4, the TPSA values exceeded 140 Å2, predicting very limited passive absorption. In addition, the phytosterols 6, 7, and 8 complied with Veber's rules with no violation but incurred a single Lipinski violation in which log P were slightly above 5 with molecular weight just over 500 g/mol for compound 8, and their very poor predicted aqueous solubility (Log S < –7.7) suggests significant formulation challenges despite otherwise favorable TPSA and flexibility. Moreover, products 9 and 10 displayed the least drug‑like profiles, with two Lipinski violations (MW > 800 g/mol, iLOGP > 8) and a Veber violation stemming from >24 rotatable bonds, along with extremely low solubility (Log S < –12.6), rendering them incompatible with oral administration. In marked contrast, compounds 11 and 12 fully satisfied all Lipinski and Veber criteria with no violations, their molecular weight (≈343 g/mol), moderate lipophilicity (iLOGP ≈ 3.05), acceptable solubility (Log S ≈ –3.10), and TPSA of 88 Å2, aligning closely with the properties of orally bioavailable drugs. The fatty acids 13, 14, and 15, each showed only a single Lipinski violation, but failed Veber's rule due to excessive rotatable bonds (14–20), which is likely to impair passive intestinal absorption despite their otherwise low TPSA and acceptable size–lipophilicity balance. Overall, the isolated compounds display a wide spectrum of drug‑likeness. However, the two cinnamoylamide alkaloid isomers 11 and 12, stand out as the only molecules with a fully favorable drug‑like profiles, meeting all Lipinski and Veber criteria and thus representing the most promising orally bioavailable candidates from this species.

ADMET Study

Following the favorable drug‐likeness predictions observed for isolates, their ADMET profiles were further assessed in silico to evaluate pharmacokinetic behavior and safety liabilities. The predicted absorption profiles of the isolated compounds, as detailed in Table S22, reveal that the human intestinal absorption differentiated starkly between the phytochemical classes, in which compounds 1 and 2 displayed very low absorption, consistent with their high molecular weight, excessive polarity, and high TPSA that hinder passive transcellular diffusion; the glycerolipids 3, 4, and 5 showed moderate to high absorption, while compounds 6, 7, and 8 had excellent predicted absorption, as did the phytosteryl glycoside palmitates 9 and 10. Compounds 11–15 showed high absorption, aligning with their favorable drug‐likeness and suggesting good oral bioavailability. Additionally, most compounds were predicted to be p‐glycoprotein (p‐gp) substrates, which could reduce net oral absorption through intestinal efflux. However, compounds 5, 6, 7, 13, 14, and 15 were not p‐gp substrates, indicating a lower risk of active efflux. For drug distribution, only products 6 and 7 were predicted to readily cross the blood‐brain barrier with BBB permeability of 0.801 and 0.791, respectively, suggesting possible central nervous system exposure, while compounds (8–15) display negative BBB permeability values, implying minimal brain penetration, which is a favorable attribute for avoiding central side effects. The volume of distribution (Log VD) ranged significantly. Compounds 3, 9, and 10s showed very low to negative values (−0.008 to −1.974 L/kg), suggesting predominant retention in plasma, whereas β‐sitosterol and stigmasterol had moderate values, and compounds 11 and 12 showed a balanced distribution. Concerning drug metabolism, the biomolecules 1, 2, 11, and 12 exhibited the most favorable profiles, with no predicted inhibition of key CYP450 isoforms (CYP3A4, 2D6, 2C9, 2C19, 1A2), minimizing their risk for drug‐drug interactions. In contrast, all the tested compounds, except compounds 11 and 12, were expected to inhibit CYP3A4. Notably, fatty acids inhibited CYP1A2, and compound 5 inhibited CYP2D6 as well, raising concerns about significant pharmacokinetic interactions when co‐administered with drugs metabolized by these pathways. Furthermore, the total clearance varied from low (compounds 1 and 2) to moderate (compounds 6, 7, 11, and 12), to relatively high (compounds 13–15 and 3–5), with the amides’ moderate clearance being favorable for maintaining steady concentrations. Finally, none of the compounds were identified as substrates of the renal transporter OCT2, reducing the likelihood of kidney‐mediated drug interactions or renal accumulation.

The toxicity predictions summarized in Table S23 provide a critical assessment of the safety margins associated with the isolated phytochemicals from H. sativus species. The predicted acute oral lethal dose 50 (LD50) values in rats varied substantially among the phytochemical classes. The compounds 1, 2, and 8 showed low acute toxicity with LD50 ≥ 5000 mg/kg, which falls into Toxicity Class V, while the glycerolipids 3–5, and compounds 9, 10 had even more favorable profiles. The compound 5 was the safest molecule, reaching an LD50 of 39.800 mg/kg, which falls into Toxicity Class VI. In contrast, compounds 6, 7, and 11 and 12 fell into moderate acute toxicity (LD50 500–890 mg/kg), as did the long‐chain fatty acids 13–15 (900 mg/kg). In addition, the chronic toxicity predictions based on LOAEL indicated that the higher log mg/kg bw/day values of the flavonol glycosides 1 and 2 (4.5–5.6) and 8 (3.06) suggest a wider safety margin after prolonged exposure, whereas compounds 6, 7, 11, and 12 displayed comparatively lower values, though still within a moderate range. Moreover, the organ‐specific toxicity profiling revealed that the only compound with a positive AMES mutagenicity alert was flavonoid glycoside 1, pointing to a potential risk of DNA damage, while compound 2 was the only molecule reported for its general cytotoxicity. None of the natural compounds were predicted to cause hepatotoxicity, or carcinogenicity, indicating an overall low profile for these endpoints. Nephrotoxicity, however, emerged as a frequent alert, with the majority of compounds (1–12) predicted positive, whereas the fatty acids 13–15 uniquely lacked this flag. Furthermore, the skin sensitization was predicted only for compound 5, 13–15, suggesting a potential for allergic contact dermatitis, while all other compounds were non‐allergenic, which is a favorable feature for both systemic and potential topical applications. Collectively, the in silico ADMET profiling revealed a spectrum of pharmacokinetic and safety characteristics across the isolates. Compounds 11 and 12 consistently demonstrated the most favorable ADME profiles, while compounds 1, 2, 5, and 8 exhibited the highest acute safety margins. This variability highlights that H. sativus contains a diverse array of phytochemicals with both promising drug‐like attributes and acceptable safety profiles, which likely contribute to the observed biological effects. Nerveless, the ADMET profiles assessed in silico highlight the possible toxicity of H. sativus bioactive molecules and extracts; thus, the evaluation of its acute and chronic toxicity is primordial before the validation of its therapeutic and pharmacological potential.

Among all isolated metabolites, the cinnamoylamide alkaloids 11 and 12 represent the most balanced and development‐ready leads since they alone fully satisfy all Lipinski and Veber criteria, show high predicted intestinal absorption, lack CYP450 inhibition, and exhibit moderate clearance with minimal brain penetration. Their moderate acute toxicity is manageable and typical of natural‐product leads. Additionally, compound 5 obviously offers an exceptional safety margin but is limited by poor predicted permeability and CYP3A4/2D6 inhibition, making it a potential backup only if formulation can overcome absorption barriers. All other compounds were deprioritized due to major drug‐likeness violations, consistently poor absorption, or significant CYP inhibition liabilities. Therefore, compounds 11 and 12 are recommended for priority advancement into in vitro efficacy confirmation and early pharmacokinetic profiling.

Docking Validation

The reliability of the docking method was confirmed by re‐docking the co‐crystallized ligands. As shown in Figure S94, the resulting poses aligned closely with the original structures, as indicated by exceptionally low root‐mean‐square deviation (RMSD) values of 0.22 Å for α‐amylase, 0.42 Å for IL‐1β, and 0.18 Å for DNA gyrase, confirming the reliability of the chosen parameters. All values are well below the widely accepted 2.0 Å threshold, suggesting that the applied method accurately reproduces the experimental binding mode for each target. This successful validation confirms the protocol's suitability for predicting reliable ligand poses and binding affinities in subsequent docking studies.

Pharmacological blockade of IL‐1β signaling has emerged as a validated therapeutic strategy to preserve β‐cell function and improve glycemic control. Following the investigation of α‐amylase inhibition, molecular docking analysis was conducted to assess the ability of the isolated compounds to bind and inhibit IL‐1β, thereby targeting a key inflammatory pathway in diabetes. Molecular Dynamics Simulation

In order to evaluate the stability and dynamic interactions of the top‐ranked docking complexes with α‐amylase, IL‐1β, and DNA gyrase, all‐atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted for 100 ns. Unlike the static picture provided by docking, MD simulations model the conformational flexibility and temporal behavior of the complexes under near‐physiological conditions. Trajectory stability was assessed through complementary descriptors including the root‐mean‐square deviation (RMSD), root‐mean‐square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent‐accessible surface area (SASA), and the time‐resolved hydrogen‐bonding profile. This integrative analysis offers a more rigorous validation of the docking pose and provides mechanistic insight into the inhibitory potential of the compounds against each macromolecular target.

The α‐amylase‐compound 1 complex exhibited stable and well‐defined binding throughout the 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, as evidenced by the convergence of key dynamical parameters (Figure 3). The protein backbone RMSD rose progressively during the first ∼20 ns and subsequently fluctuated within a stable plateau of approximately 2.5–3.0 Å, with a transient excursion to ∼4.0 Å near 45 ns before re‐equilibrating, indicating that no major conformational rearrangement occurred upon ligand engagement. The Rg remained narrowly distributed around 23.0–23.7 Å, reflecting preservation of the compact (β/α)8‐triosephosphate isomerase barrel architecture characteristic of α‐amylase and ruling out any large‐scale unfolding event. The SASA profile rose from ∼17 000 Å2 during the first ∼15 ns of equilibration and subsequently stabilized around 19 000 Å2 for the remainder of the trajectory, consistent with maintenance of a hydrated, native‐like surface and the absence of aberrant exposure of the hydrophobic core. Furthermore, local flexibility analysis through RMSF showed that the majority of residues fluctuated below 1.0 Å, with a single pronounced peak (∼7 Å) localized in the ∼140–170 residue‐index region, attributable to a flexible surface loop in one of the symmetry‐related subunits and away from the catalytic cleft. Importantly, the residues comprising the active‐site pocket exhibited markedly suppressed mobility, demonstrating that compound 1 binding rigidifies the catalytic environment. The ligand itself adopted a stable, converged pose with minimal positional drift, consistent with a strong shape and electrostatic complementarity to the binding cavity. In terms of specific interactions, the formation of multiple hydrogen bonds throughout the simulation timeline, in average of 2–6 hydrogen bonds, with a maximum of 8, underscores the presence of a durable polar interaction profile that anchors the ligand in the active site. This global stability validates the initial docking pose and strongly suggest that compound 1 is a promising α‐amylase inhibitor, forming a stable and persistent complex with the enzyme; a key dynamical behavior that is a critical prerequisite for effective enzymatic inhibition under physiological conditions.

For interleukin‐1 beta (IL‐1β), compound 1 likewise formed a stable complex, although the system exhibited a longer equilibration phase than the α‐amylase counterpart (Figure 4). The protein backbone RMSD increased steadily from ∼1.5 Å, reaching ∼3.0 Å by 30 ns and showing a transient excursion to ∼4.0–4.5 Å between 40 ns and 60 ns, after which it re‐equilibrated and fluctuated around 3.0–3.7 Å for the remainder of the trajectory. This profile is characteristic of an extended equilibration followed by a stable dynamic regime, with no evidence of progressive structural drift. The ligand RMSD remained low, fluctuating predominantly within 1.5–3.0 Å (mean ∼2.3 Å), confirming that compound 1 retained its docked orientation throughout the simulation. Moreover, RMSF analysis revealed low global backbone flexibility in IL‐1β, with the majority of residues fluctuating under ∼4 Å. As anticipated, increased mobility was localized to loop regions and termini. Crucially, the residues comprising the active catalytic pocket show suppressed fluctuations, suggesting compound 1 binding imposes a rigidifying effect on the local protein structure. In addition, the radius of gyration analysis demonstrates that IL‐1β maintains exceptional structural compactness and fold integrity throughout the simulation. The Rg value remained consistently at approximately 15.0–15.5 Å across the entire 100 ns trajectory, showing no significant fluctuations. This stability indicates the absence of large‐scale unfolding, swelling, or loss of tertiary structure upon compound 1 binding. Consistently, the SASA descriptor oscillated within a narrow window of ∼9000–9700 Å2 with no monotonic trend, providing additional evidence that the global hydration shell and surface topology of the IL‐1β were not perturbed by complexation. Furthermore, the hydrogen bond analysis reveals a stable and persistent intermolecular interaction network, averaging 3–4 bonds, with peaks up to 6. The consistent presence of these bonds, despite minor fluctuations, indicates that specific, high‐occupancy polar contacts anchor compound 1 within the binding site. This enduring interaction provides the enthalpic driving force for complex stability and directly explains the low positional variance observed in the ligand's dynamics. This dynamic profile strongly confirms that compound 1 acts as a promising and potent inhibitor of IL‐1β, capable of effectively engaging its target through a well‐defined and stable binding mechanism.

The MD simulation of the DNA gyrase complexed with N‐trans‐feruloylmethoxytyramine (Figure 5), reveals a stable binding interaction with protein backbone RMSD equilibrated after an initial period, rising from approximately 1 Å to a stable plateau around 2.3–2.8 Å, indicating a consistent conformational state was maintained. Importantly, the ligand RMSD remained remarkably low, fluctuating around 1.0–1.5 Å during the first ∼70 ns; a modest upward shift to ∼2.0–2.5 Å was observed during the final 25 ns of the trajectory, consistent with a minor intra‐cavity reorientation rather than dissociation, since the ligand RMSD remained well below the magnitude expected for unbinding events. The convergence and stability of both protein and ligand RMSDs confirm the reliability of the initial docking pose, indicating that Compound 12 forms a stable, persistent complex with DNA gyrase. Moreover, local flexibility, as captured by RMSF, was generally low (mostly below 2 Å), with isolated peaks reaching ∼3 Å that correspond to solvent‐exposed loop regions distant from the active site. Crucially, residues forming the binding pocket demonstrate suppressed fluctuations, indicating that compound 12 binding significantly rigidifies this local environment. In addition, the Rg remained virtually invariant at ∼16.4–16.7 Å across the 100 ns trajectory, demonstrating that the enzyme retained its compact tertiary architecture upon compound 12 binding. The preservation of this global compactness is critical for the enzyme's function, as DNA gyrase relies on precise conformational changes to perform its ATP‐dependent supercoiling activity. This stable architectural preservation, in conjunction with the low and convergent RMSD values, strongly supports that compound 12 binding does not induce destabilization but instead stabilizes the DNA gyrase protein. Likewise, the SASA profile reinforced this conclusion, oscillating steadily within ∼10 200–11 000 Å2 with no upward drift indicative of unfolding or domain separation, which is consistent with a stable, native‐like surface throughout the simulation. Furthermore, the intermolecular hydrogen‐bond timeline showed a persistent network of 3–4 hydrogen bonds, with sporadic increases to 5–6. These polar contacts remained stable for the duration of the simulation period, suggesting that they are a critical component of the binding affinity by anchoring the ligand and providing specificity to the interaction. In summary, the convergence of the RMSD, consistent Rg, restrained binding site fluctuations, stable ligand positioning, and enduring hydrogen‐bond network collectively indicate that N‐trans‐feruloylmethoxytyramine forms a stable and persistent complex with DNA gyrase, and effectively engaging the target's active site.

MM‐PBSA Calculations

MM‐PBSA calculations were performed on the molecular dynamics trajectories to quantitatively evaluate and compare the binding affinities of the studied phytoconstituents to their respective targets: α‐amylase, interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), and DNA gyrase. This method provides a thermodynamic breakdown of the interaction, deconvoluting the total binding free energy (ΔG bind) into its gas‐phase (ΔGgas) and solvation (ΔG solv) components, with ΔG gas further divided into van der Waals (ΔE vdw) and electrostatic (ΔE ele) contributions. The results reveal a clear hierarchy of binding strength and elucidate the distinct physical–chemical nature of each complex (Figure S98).

The analysis identifies the α‐amylase‐compound 1 complex as the most stable and favorable interaction (Figure S98). It exhibits a profoundly negative total binding free energy (ΔG total = −26.06 ± 3.47 kcal/mol). This strong affinity can be attributed to an exceptionally favorable gas‐phase interaction energy (ΔG gas), which arises from robust contributions from both electrostatic attractions and van der Waals forces. These dominant favorable forces effectively overcome the unfavorable desolvation penalty (ΔG solv), which is the energy cost of stripping water molecules from the ligand and protein interface upon binding. The time‐evolution of ΔG total shows a stable plateau, confirming the energy estimate is derived from a well‐equilibrated and consistent bound state. This aligns perfectly with prior dynamical metrics (stable RMSD, rigidified binding site in RMSF, persistent H‐bonds), providing a thermodynamic rationale for the observed structural stability and suggesting high inhibitory potential. In addition, the DNA gyrase–compound 12 complex (Figure S98), demonstrates moderate binding affinity (ΔG total = −24.03 ± 5.36 kcal/mol). While the gas‐phase interactions (ΔG gas) are also favorable, primarily due to strong van der Waals packing, the net binding is less pronounced than for α‐amylase. This is partly attributed to a more significant unfavorable solvation term. The electrostatic component is less dominant, which may reflect a binding mode more dependent on shape complementarity and hydrophobic effects than on specific, strong polar contacts. The IL‐1β‐compound 1 complex (Figure S98) shows the weakest binding in this comparative set (ΔG total = −21.44 ± 3.92 kcal/mol). Its energy profile is characterized by a moderate favorable ΔG gas that is substantially counterbalanced by a high desolvation penalty. This suggests that while the ligand fits into the IL‐1β pocket, the interaction may involve the burial of polar surfaces without sufficient compensating specific hydrogen bonds or ionic interactions, making the binding energetically less efficient.

These finding provided the decisive thermodynamic perspective. The results confirmed that the α‐amylase complex possesses the most favorable binding free energy. This strong affinity is driven by robust electrostatic interactions and van der Waals, highlighting its primary potential as a potent antidiabetic agent. The IL‐1β and DNA gyrase complexes also exhibited stable binding, albeit with distinct energy profiles, supporting their roles as anti‐inflammatory and antibacterial candidates, respectively. This multi‐level computational study provides an in silico validation that the phytoconstituents isolated from H. sativus form stable, high‐affinity complexes with disease‐relevant targets. The convergence of structural docking, dynamic stability, and favorable binding energetics strongly advocates for the selected compounds as natural multi‐functional lead molecules.

Conclusion

This study proved that the species Halogeton sativus is a rich source of natural bioactive molecules with diverse structures and notable biological potential. The phytochemical investigation enabled the isolation and complete structural elucidation of 15 metabolites, including 2 flavonoid glycosides, 5 phytosterols, 2 cinnamoylamide alkaloids, 2 glycolipids, 3 fatty acids, and 1 glycerol derivative, which likely contribute to the observed bioactivities. The HS‐BuOH extract exhibited potential antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory effects. While the HS‐EtOAc extract exerted significant α‐amylase inhibitory activity comparable to that reported by acarbose. All crude extracts showed moderate antimicrobial activity against pathogens commonly involved in diabetes mellitus–associated infections. These experimental results were further validated and extended by computational studies. Docking analysis of the isolates against α‐amylase, IL‐1β, and DNA gyrase revealed strong binding affinities and plausible interaction modes. Computationally, the stability of the most promising complexes was confirmed through 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations and subsequent MM‐PBSA calculation, with favorable predicted binding free energies, reinforcing the docking predictions. In silico assessments of drug‐likeness and ADMET parameters suggested promising pharmacokinetic and safety profiles for the lead candidates. Overall, these integrated results highlight the in vitro pharmacological potential of H. sativus. Further, in vivo investigations, mechanistic studies, and structure–activity relationship analyses are essential to evaluate the safety and the possible therapeutic applications of this plant.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

Plant Material

Halogeton sativus was harvested during June 2022 in the N'gaous region of Batna (35°26'36.0“N 5°33'53.4”E). Its identification was carried out by botanists at the University of Batna‐1. A reference specimen has been deposited under the number 1301/LCCE.

Extraction Procedure

The plant material (1 kg) was macerated three times in a hydroalcoholic solution (EtOH: H2O/7:3) for 72 h, at room temperature. The resulting filtrate was concentrated at 40°C, yielding 400 mL of aqueous extract. This extract was then subjected to liquid–liquid extraction using consecutively, petroleum ether (PE), ethyl acetate (EtOAc), and n‐butanol (BuOH) solvents. The obtained organic extracts were then evaporated using a rotary evaporator to give HS‐PE extract (9.5 g), HS‐EtOAc extract (1.03 g), and HS‐BuOH extract (10 g).

Fractionation and Purification

HS‐PE extract (7 g) was fractionated by vacuum liquid chromatography (VLC; SiO2). The elution was carried out by mixing PE/EtOAc (100–0 to 0–100), then by a gradient of MeOH/EtOAc (0–100 to 100–0) to give nine major fractions (F1–F9). The fraction F1 (967 mg) was further fractionated by normal SiO2 column chromatography (CC) with PE/EtOAc solvents, yielding 16 sub‐fractions (F1‐1 to F1‐16). The sub‐fraction F1‐5 (24.0 mg) was precipitated in MeOH to provide compound 8 (9 mg). The sub‐fraction F1‐2 (9.6 mg) containing a major product was isolated by precipitation in acetone to provide compound 13 (2.6 mg). The sub‐fraction F1‐15 (128 mg) was fractionated on a SiO2 column chromatography with the mixture of solvents CHCl3/MeOH to obtain eight sub‐fractions (F1‐15‐1 to F1‐15‐8). Sub‐fraction F1‐15‐1 (16 mg) was precipitated in methanol to give a mixture of two compounds 6 and 7, whereas compound 3 was obtained from F1‐15‐6 using acetone as precipitation solvent. The fraction F5 was submitted to column chromatography with normal silica gel using PE/EtOAc as the elution system, affording 13 sub‐fractions (F5‐1 to F5‐13). The precipitation of F5‐9 in methanol allowed the isolation of a mixture of compounds 9 and 10. The fraction F6 (127 mg) was fractionated on silica gel CC with PE/EtOAc elution system to obtain 11 sub‐fractions (F6‐1 to F6‐11). Preparative TLC of F6‐5 (25 mg) developed in a mixture of CHCl3/MeOH/H2O/AcOH (90/10/0.5/0.1) provided compound 4. Fraction F8 (1.7 g) was fractionated by CC SiO2 eluting with CHCl3/MeOH to get 22 sub‐fractions (F8‐1 to F8‐22). The filtration of sub‐fraction F8‐2 over CC Sephadex LH‐20 allowed the isolation of compound 14. The sub‐fraction F8‐15 was fractionated on normal silica gel CC using CHCl3/MeOH (100–0 to 0–100) to give five sub‐fractions, compound 5 was isolated from the precipitation of fraction F8‐15‐2 in methanol.

The HS‐EtOAc extract (700 mg) was fractionated by open column chromatography on normal‐phase silica gel using a gradient of PE/EtOAc (100:0–0:100) followed by EtOAc/MeOH (100:0–0:100) to give 17 major fractions. The precipitation of the fraction F9 (8.2 mg) in CH2Cl2/acetone allowed the isolation of a mixture of compounds 11 and 12.

The HS‐BuOH extract (7 g) was fractionated by vacuum liquid chromatography (VLC) on normal‐phase silica gel, eluting with a gradient of CHCl3/MeOH (100:0–0:100), to yield seven major fractions (F1‐F7). Fraction F4 (750 mg) was subjected to silica gel column chromatography (CC) using a CH2Cl2/MeOH gradient as the elution system, affording 12 sub‐fractions; precipitation of sub‐fraction F4‐1 (26 mg) in acetone yielded compound 15. Fraction F5 (1 g) was also separated over silica gel CC using CH2Cl2/MeOH solvent system. Compound 1 was obtained from the sub‐fraction F5‐5 using precipitation in acetone. The fraction F6 (1 g) was fractionated in 11 sub‐fractions using SiO2 CC eluted with CHCl3/MeOH. The fraction F6‐6 which contained a major product was precipitated in acetone to provide compound 2.

Chemical Identification

The structures of all the isolated compounds (1–15) were identified using modern spectroscopic techniques, including 1D NMR (1H, 13C, and DEPT‐135), 2D NMR (HMBC, 1H–1H COSY, and HSQC), and mass spectrometry (ESI‐MS), and comparison with previously reported data.

Total Bioactive Content

The total bioactive levels of H. sativus crude extracts, including phenolics, flavonoids, and terpenoids were estimated through various colorimetric methods [54].

Antioxidant Activity

The antioxidant properties of organic extracts from H. sativus were evaluated using various techniques, namely hydrogen peroxide radical scavenging assays, cupric ion reducing antioxidant capacity (CUPRAC), total antioxidant capacity (TAC), ferric thiocyanate capacity (FTC), potassium permanganate (KMnO4), and iron chelating assays [55].

Antimicrobial Activity

The antimicrobial activity of H. sativus crude extracts was tested in vitro using the solid medium diffusion assay against 10 bacterial strains and 1 fungal strain [56].

Anti‐Inflammatory Activity

The anti‐inflammatory effect of H. sativus crude extracts was assessed via the inhibition of heat‐induced hemolysis and antidenaturation assays [57].

The inhibitory activity of the enzyme of H. sativus crude extracts was evaluated using the caraway iodine/potassium iodide (IKI) [58].

Statistical Analysis

All the experimental measurements were expressed as the mean ± SD for three replicates for each sample. Mean comparisons were determined by one way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05 and all the statistics were carried out using Graph Pad prism 5.04.

Drug Likeness and ADMET Prediction

The in silico drug‐likeness and pharmacokinetic ADMET parameters were predicted using freely available web tools: Swiss ADMET [59], ProTox‐III [60], and pkCSM [61].

Docking Methodology

The 3D structures of the isolated phytochemicals and the reference drugs (acarbose, diclofenac, and novobiocin) were downloaded from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Geometry optimization of all structures was performed using Chem3D software with the MMFF94 force field to minimize conformational energy. Subsequently, using Discovery Studio Visualizer (2021), all optimized ligands were exported in PDB format. Target 3D crystal structures were selected from the RCSB Protein Data Bank based on high crystallographic resolution (<2.5 Å), structural completeness, and the presence of co‐crystallized inhibitors defining the catalytic site: human pancreatic α‐amylase (PDB ID: 7TAA, resolution 2.10 Å), interleukin‐1β (PDB ID: 8C3U, resolution 1.95 Å), and bacterial DNA gyrase (PDB ID: 4DUH, resolution 2.30 Å). Each protein was prepared by removing non‑protein elements (water molecules, metal ions, co‑crystallized ligands). Energy minimization was performed using UCSF Chimera (v.1.18) employing the AMBER ff14SB force field and protonation states of amino acid residues were adjusted to physiological pH (∼7.4). The binding site for docking was defined by the coordinates of each native co‑crystallized ligand. Molecular docking was performed with AutoDock Vina v1.1.2 to predict binding affinities against α‑amylase, IL‑1β, and DNA gyrase. Proteins and ligands were prepared in AutoDock Tools v1.5.7 [62], adding polar hydrogens and Kollman charges to receptors and Gasteiger charges to ligands, with all files saved in PDBQT format. Active site cavities were validated by defining the docking grid box around the centroid coordinates of the native co‐crystallized ligands. A 30 × 30 × 30 Å3 grid (0.375 Å spacing) centered on each protein's native ligand coordinates (α‑amylase: 38.725, 44.127, 25.633; IL‑1β: 5.355, −14.823, −20.723; DNA gyrase: 2.002, 2.167, 37.295) defined the binding site. Docking used an exhaustiveness of 10, generating 10 poses per ligand; the pose with the lowest binding energy (ΔG) was selected and visualized in Discovery Studio. Binding affinities and interaction profiles were compared directly against co‐crystallized ligands and clinical reference drugs (acarbose, diclofenac, and novobiocin). The docking methodology was validated by re‐docking each protein's native co‐crystallized ligand. Alignment accuracy was assessed by computing the RMSD for heavy atoms between the top‐ranked docked pose and the original crystallographic position, utilizing LigRMSD 1.0 [63]. Consistent with established standards, a protocol was deemed successfully validated if the RMSD found to be less than 2.0 Å, confirming reliable recapitulation of the crystallographic binding geometry.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

The stability of docked complexes was assessed via 100 ns MD simulations in GROMACS 2025.2 [64]. Protein topologies were generated with the CHARMM36‐jul2022 force field [65], and ligand parameters from the CGenFF server [65]. Each system was solvated in a TIP3P water box, neutralized, and adjusted to 0.15 M NaCl. Following energy minimization (steepest descent, 50 000 steps), stepwise equilibration was conducted under NVT (500 ps, 310 K) and NPT (500 ps, 1 bar) ensembles with positional restraints. Production simulations (100 ns, NPT, 310 K, 1 bar) employed a 2 fs timestep, LINCS constraints, PME electrostatics, and a 1.2 nm cutoff. Trajectories were recorded every 10 ps for analysis.

MMPBSA Analysis

The binding free energy (ΔG bind) for each complex was estimated via the MM‐PBSA approach using gmx_MMPBSA [66]. Employing a single‐trajectory protocol, in which 1000 frames from the 100 ns production MD were analyzed.
