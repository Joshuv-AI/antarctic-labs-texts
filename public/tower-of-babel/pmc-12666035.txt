Preventing and mitigating fraudulent research participants in online qualitative violence and injury prevention research
Authors: Devon Ziminski, Esprene Liddell-Quintyn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecruiting participants for injury and violence-related studies can be challenging, and online data collection opportunities can expand reach, offer convenience and extend a sense of safety to potential participants who may be in unsafe situations or do not want to travel to a location due to safety concerns. While increasing accessibility for some participants, online primary data collection presents challenges around potential fraudulent participants. This methodological paper highlights the strategies for preventing and mitigating fraudulent participants in online qualitative data collection, using a recent firearm violence study in a Northeast city as an example.PurposeUsing a recent data collection effort related to firearm injury as a case study, the purpose of the current methodology paper is to highlight concerns and challenges with online qualitative data collection and provide strategies for preventing, detecting and removing fraudulent participants in qualitative injury and violence prevention research.ResultsVarious predata collection activities can promote a study design that deters fraudulent participants, and additional ‘in-the-moment’ data collection activities can flag potential suspicious participants. Strategies include prescreening participants, requiring video and answers to basic questions relevant to the study topic and confirming certain pieces of information.ConclusionOnline primary data collection can increase accessibility and support the safety of participants in injury and violence research, and there are considerations around detecting and removing fraudulent participants that researchers should note. Like all methods, a balance exists between study access, aims and resources. Researchers new to online qualitative data collection can use the strategies outlined here.Keywords: qualitative research, health equity, social sciences

Introduction

Recruitment is a critical part of the primary data collection research process. It is a time-intensive process requiring immense flexibility and creativity, not always linearly translatable from research methods training.1,3 Qualitative research recruitment specifically requires both different and similar strategies to quantitative survey recruitment and nuanced considerations for virtual or in-person data collection.1,3

The injury prevention field is a growing research field4 that must continue to engage with people who have experienced injury or violence. There is a dearth of qualitative research in the firearm injury space compared with quantitative research, and even less information is available about qualitative methods and recruitment for firearm research studies. This gap is particularly important to address, given historical research practices that have been detrimental to many black and brown communities affected by injury and violence.5 Research that uplifts and centres the experiences and perspectives of people closest to this issue through storytelling, focus groups, ethnographic means and other qualitative methods is critical.4 5

People who have experienced violence may have distrust in formal institutions and research and have safety concerns regarding participation in research related to injury and violence. Online participation could create avenues to generate a wider participation pool while supporting participant safety. Qualitative studies to date focus on injury involving youth,6 7 and studies often use interviews8 9 as a primary method. Others use focus groups10,12 and employ online qualitative methods.13 Given its importance to the injury and violence prevention fields, discussion around online qualitative data collection challenges and opportunities is warranted.

Increase in fraudulent participants in online qualitative research

Increases in access to technology and forced transition to online methods during the COVID-19 pandemic14 15 have spurred a recent rise in the use of online platforms16 for qualitative data collection17 . Like all methodological choices, there are pros and cons to online data collection.18 19 A large strength of this method is expanding the research pool to more participants and broader geographic areas.

On the other hand, online data collection poses challenges for individuals without access to technology and who may be uncomfortable with digital platforms. Online data collection may make it harder for focus group facilitators, for example, to follow body language and generate a group dynamic. Additionally, given the sensitive nature of injury and violence research, considerations should be made for logistical and safety planning14,1618 involving potential study participants. Examples include providing safe transportation to and from data collection or meeting at a participant’s chosen public or Institutional Review Board (IRB)-approved private location, integrating a distress protocol and co-facilitating data collection with a licensed clinician.14,1618 Additionally, research has highlighted the importance of injury and violence prevention researchers deploying strategies themselves (eg, taking breaks during data collection and analysis, only meeting participants in IRB-approved locations and co-facilitating data collection) to reduce potential vicarious trauma.20 21 These process decisions22 remain at the discretion of research teams based on the research goals, available resources and most importantly, participants’ preferences.

Recent studies examined the presence of fraudulent participants in online research studies,22,25 and this development merits discussion within the injury and violence prevention field. Fraudulent participants are artificial bots or human participants who do not meet study criteria and who attempt to, or do, participate in data collection.22,25 Fraudulent participants may provide inaccurate information about their location, experiences or programme engagement.22,25 Prior research has documented fraudulent participants in child health services research,26 27 where participants have fictional illnesses28 and pretended to be the parent of a child with autism,29 among other topics.2530,33 While it may be alarming to imagine fabricating firearm-related or violent incidents, fraudulent participants involved in studies of violence and injury are not unheard of and have been encountered by the authors of this article and colleagues.31 The presence of bots and humans engaged in fraudulent research participation is a reality that researchers should be aware of, work to prevent where possible and mitigate when detected to preserve research integrity and data quality. Like detecting and cleaning suspicious data in online survey data collection, there are ways to include guardrails in online qualitative data collection. This article discusses fraudulent qualitative research participation and offers considerations for online qualitative recruitment, using an example of an online qualitative research project involving firearm violence in a Northeast city.

Research context

The qualitative research study focused on firearm violence among people who lived in a specific Northeast city. The research team worked with a local community-based organisation (CBO) to conduct outreach. Public members were involved in the outreach and recruitment for the study. Specifically, members of the public included representatives from a local CBO.

Participants expressed interest in the study by completing a screener survey. The main inclusion criteria were being at least 18 years of age and currently living, working or attending school in the study’s city of focus. From screener survey responses, individuals were split into three groups for focus groups: college students, longtime residents and survivors who lost a child to firearm homicide. In the screener survey, participants expressed their preference for an online versus in-person focus group. Participants overwhelmingly preferred online groups. Focus groups were scheduled and sent to the emails of eligible individuals based on selected criteria. Six focus groups were held in May and June 2024 and were subsequently stopped due to the presence of fraudulent participants. Prior research studies27,31 have documented their experiences with fraudulent participants, with many ‘red flags’ appearing in their research occurring in ours. Below, we highlight common indicators and additional specifics we experienced during our recruitment and data collection efforts. We aim to outline strategies2434,36 to help prevent and mitigate the presence of fraudulent participants in online qualitative research, with additional considerations for injury and violence prevention research.

Key considerations for preventing and mitigating fraudulent research participants in online qualitative research

Predata collection: preventing fraudulent participants

There are multiple considerations for deterring fraudulent participants ahead of a study. While in some studies, researchers have a limited population or existing contact information for potential participants (eg, a domestic violence study where the researcher has a list of centre directors to interview), in many qualitative studies, research teams are reaching out to larger potential participant populations (eg, anyone serviced by a hospital violence-based intervention programme, or anyone who has experienced community violence). Researchers should develop a thorough recruitment plan (eg, using QR codes on flyers at libraries, social media distributions and email listservs) and track dates, times, locations and methods of recruitment. It is important for researchers to know where and when recruitment is occurring to help flag sources of potentially fraudulent inquiries (eg, a tweet sent out advertising the study was followed by 40 responses within 2 min). Caution with social media is advised, as links posted online can quickly spread and be picked up by would-be fraudulent participants. Many research studies do not have the resources to verify participants through large survey-based panels (eg, Gallup, Dynata), and qualitative research typically works with much smaller samples; thus, there is additional effort and nuance when developing qualitative recruitment plans.

In our study, we relied on targeted social media recruitment through a university research centre, as well as a partnership with a local community-based organisation to spread the research opportunity. In hindsight, a more detailed and comprehensive recruitment strategy could have been developed prior to outreach. This preplanning could have helped immediately identify sources of potential fraud in the incoming interview screeners. Creative recruitment strategies are needed to access potential participants while supporting participants’ comfort, especially when working with difficult topics. Specific recruitment methods gleaned from both qualitative and quantitative examples are beyond this article’s scope, and researchers are encouraged to dedicate ample time to developing creative and culturally sensitive recruitment strategies.37,40 Strategies and techniques from online surveys can be adapted for online qualitative data collection.

Outreach

During study planning and initial outreach, working with a CBO can considerably minimise the potential for fraudulent participation in online studies. CBOs often have established relationships and trusted connections with community members whom researchers may want to engage for their study. CBO staff and leaders can recommend ways to best reach out to potential participants and help navigate communications, and existing research has outlined these strategies.41 42 Our study included a partnership with a local violence prevention CBO. We connected with the partner to help distribute the flyer via email and posted flyers in their centre. Outreach in tandem with CBOs can include text and email messages, meetings and phone calls with potential participants, where representatives from the research team and CBO are present.

This approach is not without limitations, as working in partnership with a CBO might restrict access to a certain pool of participants affiliated with the CBO. While our study included a partnership, decisions to work with a CBO for a study with online data collection will largely rest on the study design, the sampling plan and the target population, as smaller, localised samples may be well-poised for recruitment through a CBO in an academic-community partnership, while larger studies may pursue other methods. CBO partners (and study participants) should always be equitably compensated for their contributions at all stages of the research process, and interested researchers can adopt community-based participatory research strategies.41 42

Screening for inclusion criteria

We implemented a screener survey in our study and organised focus groups based on respondents’ meeting preferences. A short screener survey (distributed through a link or QR code in email, text and on a flyer) gauged potential participation interest, collected contact information for scheduling and captured inclusion characteristics for review by the research team. This process is useful for online groups when participants are not always from the same area or have the same organisational affiliations. A screener survey can include a few ‘test’ or ‘lie’ questions that might flag individuals as suspicious or fraudulent (eg, asking at two different time points for respondents to type their hometown, or asking at two different time points for respondents to type the three middle letters of a string of letters such as ‘tkgogfe’ or for location-based studies, asking potential participants to name and describe a meal they had at a local restaurant). While people can certainly fabricate answers from internet searches or artificial intelligence platforms, inconsistent or inappropriate answers to these ‘test’ or ‘lie’ questions can be useful for flagging individuals for additional screening.43 44

IP addresses and the latitude and longitude of screener survey locations, while not always accurate, can provide some indication of fraudulent activity.43 44 For example, multiple potential participants in our study reported responding from various locations across Africa. Multiple responses with inappropriate locations or duplicate locations may indicate that the same person is trying to get into the study multiple times or that multiple people are working in tandem to enter the study. Additionally, built-in survey features (eg, RelevantID and Fraud Score features in Qualtrics)45 to identify potentially fraudulent activity are often used for longer online surveys, and while they may be hard to assess for short qualitative screeners, they can be used.

Participant screening can also be completed by telephone, with local telephone numbers being required in the screener survey, and researchers only including individuals with a local telephone number in the study (this method could exclude people who live in the study area but have non-local telephone numbers). Phone calls can also be used as an additional screening method prior to scheduling data collection.

Geographic information (eg, zip codes, phone numbers, IP addresses) can jeopardise the privacy and confidentiality of participants. Any identifiers should be carefully considered, weighed through risks and benefits to participants and thoroughly reviewed by study team members and an IRB.

Communications from participants before and after scheduling

Many emails with short and similarly vague email verbiage (eg, “I would like to participate in the study”) sent within hours or minutes of each other and with generic email addresses may indicate fraudulence.25 28 36 As seen in our study, as well as others,25 28 36 suspicious emails come from similar email addresses, often in the format firstname.lastname.threenumbers@gmail.com and multiple people with very similar emails (eg, jane.richard.567@gmail.com).25 28 36 We received erratic emails both before and after data collection, and multiple emails from additional potential participants after the recruitment period ended, asking to participate, suggesting that fraudulent groups may have gotten hold of the recruitment information. Odd timing of emails, extremely quick responses and repeated virtual platform (eg, Zoom) logins, also at odd hours (eg, different time zones than where the research is intended to take place), may point to fraudulent behaviours.28

Compensation

Research participants provide their time and expertise, and compensation should always be provided for participants; nonetheless, compensation may increase the potential for fraudulent participants. In our study, repeated communications before, during and after online data collection that heavily focused on when and how compensation would be received also indicated potential suspicious participants.27,29 A key consideration for deterring fraudulent participants is to modify routes to receive compensation. Compensation could be limited to requiring a local mailing address for physical gift card mailing. For online studies where electronic gift cards are used, participants could be required to show an ID at the beginning of data collection as location verification. For participants in situations where safety may be a concern (and when mailing addresses are unsuitable for sharing), researchers can organise physical gift card pick-up/drop-off at an accessible public location. Compensation requirements and instructions should be noted in all study materials. The study consent form can also include language that compensation may not be provided to participants if fraud is suspected or detected.24

Scheduling, instructions and expectations for online data collection

In-person versus online data collection decisions will vary based on research resources, study aims and participant preference.46 Studies with online data collection should provide clear instructions (over email, phone, text, etc) about the study process and expectations with potential participants. In addition to communications around consent and the voluntary nature of participation, researchers should also indicate that participants will be expected to have their cameras on (with consideration being given pending technology access and safety concerns), and that verbal participation is expected for compensation. For example, participants should be informed that the chat feature cannot be used as the sole method of participation, but may be used as a secondary option. In our study, we sent instructions and expectations for the focus groups to registered participants in advance and reminded everyone of the expectations and goals at the beginning of each session. We emphasised that the engagement goals were to share perspectives, hear experiences and generate discussion.

During data collection: mitigating fraudulent participants

In our study, screener surveys overall provided appropriate answers.33 34 Some people were screened out during this phase due to incomplete responses or responses outside the study’s inclusion criteria, and the focus group(s) revealed additional flags for fraudulent activity. Once online data collection is scheduled, additional guardrails can help participants.

We used a waiting room for the online focus groups to act as an additional screening method to determine study eligibility. This required budgeting time in the Zoom waiting room to confirm participants’ identities or locations (eg, showing a document with a mailing address or showing an ID, pending the population and topic of focus) or asking specific inclusion criteria questions before allowing participants to enter the main room. Requiring the disclosure of specific identifiable information depends on study aims, and processes would need to be approved by IRBs and through participant consent. At the beginning of data collection, introductory small talk questions that link participants to a certain location or population (eg, school mascot, college attended, name of a local attraction) can be helpful for noting incongruent responses.

During data collection, repeated or sole use of the chat feature, no video and never unmuting can indicate potentially fraudulent activity and go against the authenticity of qualitative research. For chat responses, participants could also be using artificial intelligence or internet searches to generate answers to the researcher’s questions.23 28

The use of experienced researchers is also recommended for online data collection, as in-group congruence between the facilitator and participants, deep knowledge of the study topic and location and a honed skillset for encouraging quieter participants and managing rambunctious ones can also aid in the identification of fraudulent participants. In one of the groups in our study, we noticed that participants’ responses to the questions were extremely short and ambiguous, providing no details or context about the local area—even though the questions were specifically about the (name of) city and participants purporting to live, work or attend school there. For example, when asked which college participants attended in the (name of) study city, one participant shared the name of a college in the UK, and another shared the name of two separate colleges in a different US state.

Following data collection: data management and analysis

Ideally, fraudulent participants are detected before online data collection begins. When this happens during data collection, researchers can remove participants from the online group or stop the group. This process can help identify any potentially redeemable data and inform next steps for pausing or stopping the research. Due to the infiltration of fraudulent participants in our study, we cut the study short and did not proceed with any data collected. Some research36 has provided initial thoughts on understanding what qualitative data are redeemable when fraudulent activity is detected.36 As the literature continues to build, there are ethical and data integrity decisions around participant trust and data that need to be carefully considered by any research team. Researchers can note in study write-ups if fraudulent activity was detected, what steps were taken to mitigate it and how data quality was maintained.

Conclusion

Qualitative research methods are critical to understanding participants’ experiences and perspectives. These methods are especially important in areas of injury and violence prevention, where the voices of those most impacted have historically been systematically excluded from research studies and solutions. Online qualitative data collection has recently been targeted by the presence of fraudulent participants, warranting the implementation of data collection plans and procedures to deter and detect fraudulent activity. There are many strategies for preventing and detecting fraudulent participants, and all study decisions should be made to remove participation burdens on participants and engender their safety and ease throughout the study, balanced with data integrity guardrails. Like all methods, an equilibrium exists between study access, aims and resources—all considerations should prioritise safety for participants. Future research can continue to build on methodological considerations for qualitative research in the injury and violence prevention research spaces, including additional considerations for online qualitative methods and engaging in community-based research practices.
