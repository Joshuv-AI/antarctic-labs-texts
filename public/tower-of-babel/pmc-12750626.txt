Published: 2025

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic profoundly reshaped modern society, exposing critical vulnerabilities in healthcare systems, economies and public policy. With over 7 million deaths reported globally [1–3], the crisis emphasized the urgent need for accurate, more responsive forecasting tools to guide timely interventions and resource allocation. The erratic waves of cases and their far-reaching consequences revealed the limitations of rule-based surveillance systems, paving the way for a shift toward data-driven approaches. Indeed, the nonlinear and time-varying nature of viral mutations posed persistent challenges for conventional epidemiological models, prompting the adoption of Machine Learning (ML) frameworks to enable prompt and precise predictions.

Since the onset of the pandemic, the successive emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants has been a key driver in the rise and fall of coronavirus cases [4, 5]. These variants differ in transmissibility, disease severity, immune escape and overall aggressiveness [6–8]. For instance, the Alpha variant (B.1.1.7) was linked to increased transmissibility [6], Delta (B.1.617.2) to greater virulence and immune escape [7], and Omicron (B0.1.1.529) to extremely high transmissibility but generally reduced severity [8]. Compared to earlier strains of the virus, Omicron and its sublineages have had the most lasting impact, as they rapidly became globally dominant [9]. These variants transmit more easily and evade pre-existing immunity, leading to repeated waves of infection driven by serial dominant sublineages [10]. To illustrate this pattern, we highlight in Fig. 1 two representative examples from different post-pandemic periods, where fluctuations in daily cases closely follow the replacement cycles of Omicron subvariants, such as XBB.1.5 vs. Omicron (Others) in NYC and BA.1 vs. BA.2 in the UK.

Temporal evolution of COVID-19 cases (7-day moving average) and circulating Omicron sublineages in New York City and the United Kingdom, using genomic and epidemiological data from from NYC and UK datasets

Despite the transition to a less lethal phase, since 2022, the pandemic has entered a more fragmented and underreported stage, marked by reduced testing, weakened public health surveillance, and a generalized perception of lower risk among people. Nevertheless, recurrent waves of contagion continue to impose a significant burden, especially on vulnerable groups such as the elderly, children, immunocompromised individuals and those unvaccinated. Moreover, reinfection increases susceptibility to long COVID, a condition associated with long-lasting health consequences [11], while prolonged infections contribute to viral genomic diversification, leading to the rise and persistence of multiple co-circulating variants within populations [12].

In light of these evolving epidemiological trends, COVID-19 remains a serious public health threat. It still displays complex dynamics, now primarily driven by the rapid proliferation of new variants, fueling frequent case resurgences [13]. The driving force behind the observed zigzag pattern of COVID-19 cases is no longer confined to government policy or seasonal variation, but rather turnover and competition among Omicron lineages. This nonlinear variant succession has created a scenario in which traditional compartmental models–mostly based on stationary assumptions–struggle to adapt to mutational volatility and low case reporting.

Despite significant advances in modeling COVID-19 outbreaks using compartmental [14–17], machine learning [18–21] and hybrid methods [22–24], most existing proposals do not explicitly consider viral variant data. Since variants have played a critical role in shaping the epidemic waves in the post-vaccination era [4, 13, 25], this underrepresentation can limit both adaptability and interpretability of the forecasters.

In parallel with these limitations, the rapidly growing AI literature on SARS-CoV-2 can be broadly grouped into three strands. First, models that aim to predict viral lineages by learning from public genomic collections [26]. Second, generative models designed to generate synthetic SARS-CoV-2 Spike proteins, creating plausible future variants [27]. Third, statistical and machine-learning frameworks that quantify how variants affect epidemic dynamics [28–30]. The present study falls into this third category, focusing on how time-varying variant prevalence can be exploited to improve short- and medium-term COVID-19 case forecasts in the post-Omicron phase.

Therefore, motivated by the previously discussed gaps, this paper demonstrates the role of genomic surveillance data in forecasting models and leverages interpretable AI to elucidate its impact. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has systematically explained how variants influence epidemic forecasts through interpretable machine learning. We design a holistic ML framework that unifies genomic and epidemiological time series, including lineage prevalence, case counts and hospitalizations, to evaluate the predictive performance of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) [31]. Among all the architectures tested, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) [32] achieved the best performance due to its superior ability to model long-term dependencies in time series data. To numerically validate our approach, we apply it to two distinct epidemiological contexts, the New York City and the United Kingdom, demonstrating its ability to generalize and adapt across different real-world settings.

As we shall show, ML models enriched with variant-related data offer a powerful and flexible tool to tackle the current epidemiological scenario. Compared to standard ML methods trained without variant data, our holistically optimized RNN-based model significantly outperforms them in predicting imminent COVID-19 waves and early outbreaks. This is a highly desirable trait nowadays, as traditional indicators (e.g., test positivity) often suffer from delays, incompleteness or bias. Importantly, this study does not introduce a new neural architecture; instead, it emphasizes the pivotal role of SARS-CoV-2 variants in shaping the rise and fall of COVID-19 cases when incorporated into RNN-based models. We employ Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) and Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) [33] to clarify how variant replacement influences infection dynamics and model predictions, thereby enhancing interpretability and confidence in forecasting outcomes.

Related work

The development of new epidemiological models for infectious disease forecasting has advanced significantly, driven mainly by the COVID-19 pandemic. Classical compartment-based models, such as the Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) and its well-known extensions, SEIR and SEIRD, have found extensive application in characterizing disease transmission [14–17]. However, these models typically rely on fixed parameters and simplifying assumptions, which may not fully capture real behavioral changes over time or the effects of strict interventions, such as mobility restrictions [34].

To overcome these issues, early-pandemic studies rapidly adopted ML-inspired models, including neural networks [18–20, 35–38], to meet the urgent demands for more accurate forecasts at a time when low SARS-CoV-2 variant diversity allowed more reliable sequence modeling. A representative work is that of [37], which compared multiple deep learning methods for predicting new cases and deaths, highlighting their versatility in pandemic forecasting. Among studies using LSTM [20], focused on forecasting COVID-19 transmission in Canada, whereas [35] addressed epidemic trends in Russia, Peru and Iran with an improved model. While these architectures can successfully learn complex, nonlinear temporal dependencies from covariates and predict epidemic progression, they may suffer from low interpretability and may experience limited performance in long-term forecasting, as in typical scenarios involving the replacement of variants [39].

Several studies have extended neural network pipelines by proposing new hybrid strategies that integrate compartmental models with deep learning, offering enhanced predictive performance and adaptability [22–24, 40–42]. For instance [41], introduced a physics-informed neural network to estimate contact rates using data modeled by SIR and SEI systems, while [22] proposed a conjugated compartmental data-driven framework to evaluate vaccine effectiveness during Brazil’s second COVID-19 wave. [40] presented a unified approach using SIRD epidemiological modeling paired with a neural network to dynamically estimate transmission parameters, allowing for the calculation of cases, recoveries and deaths. Similarly [42], proposed a hybrid approach combining compartmental dynamics and LSTM networks within a Bayesian recursive update framework. [23] employed RNNs to model the SIR system, applying the so-called Pontryagin Minimum Principle for optimal control strategies. In another representative example [24], developed a framework combining Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) [43] and LSTMs to integrate human mobility data into epidemiological parameter estimation.

Research on the pandemic has also explored purely deep learning-based methods. For instance [36], compared RNN and LSTM models across data from Malaysia, Morocco and Saudi Arabia, showing that LSTMs generate the best outputs. A similar conclusion was reached by [44], who developed an optimized LSTM model to predict COVID-19 cases across Hong Kong, South Korea, Italy and Indonesia. [21] evaluated five architectures, including various RNN-type networks. Similarly [45], assessed RNN, LSTM, and GRU networks, highlighting their predictive accuracy, while [37] examined six RNN-driven models to balance accuracy and computational cost, validating their approach using data from Australia and Iran. Finally [35], proposed an LSTM with a rolling update mechanism to improve long-term epidemic trend projections.

Lastly, the recent expansion of genomic surveillance has catalyzed a shift toward variant-based modeling. In this branch [46], focused on deep learning models trained on early-pandemic (pre-Omicron) variant profiles, while [47] fused lineage dynamics with machine learning and compartmental models, and [48] leveraged mutational entropy from genomic surveillance. While these studies demonstrate the value of employing variant information into epidemic modeling, their application to post-Omicron forecasting faces particular challenges, including the natural unavailability of Omicron-era data in earlier studies or the computational considerations involved in combining heterogeneous modeling frameworks.

Despite the above-reported advances, two critical gaps still persist: (i) the systematic application of viral variant data into ML pipelines, which remains rare for contemporary scenarios marked by rapid turnover of Omicron sublineages and rapid variant succession, and (ii) the interpretability of how these variant dynamics drive transmission patterns in the post-pandemic era. While studies like [25] have qualitatively acknowledged viral evolution’s role, a systematic, quantitative assessment of how variant-specific time series shapes forecasting performance in the current pandemic era has been lacking. We bridge this gap via three key contributions. First, we develop a data-driven methodology that explicitly integrates variant dynamics into functional forecasting models. Second, we employ interpretable ML techniques, including SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [49] and Permutation Feature Importance (PFI) [50], to quantify variants’ predictive influence. Third, we demonstrate that genomic surveillance data not only improves forecast accuracy but also provides actionable epidemiological traits by identifying dominant transmission drivers across the contemporary pandemic phase.

Materials and methods

This section describes the data and methods used to construct our data-driven pipeline towards investigating the impact of SARS-CoV-2 variants on COVID-19 case predictions, extending to the present time.

Datasets and preprocessing

We collect two geographically distinct datasets from official government sources, selected based on four key criteria: (i) reliability as authoritative public health sources, (ii) diversity in geographical scopes (metropolitan and national levels), (iii) availability of granular, variant-specific data extending into post-pandemic periods, and (iv) regular update frequency. Both datasets include genomic surveillance data, enabling robust analysis of variant-driven transmission dynamics.

To ensure consistency in the variant-driven analyses, the study period for both regions was fixed to January 2022 to January 2025, corresponding to the post-Omicron phase of stable global surveillance [9]. This timeframe corresponds to the global dominance of Omicron and its subvariants [10], while also capturing transitions between successive subvariant waves. Below, we describe each data source in detail.

From the collected data, we apply a preprocessing pipeline based on conventional data science practices to ensure proper formatting and standardized inputs for neural networks. Specifically, data curation followed three main steps: cleaning, transformation and normalization, as in [40]. We first cleaned the time series of confirmed cases and hospitalizations by handling missing values through linear interpolation for gaps shorter than seven days. We then normalized all features through min-max scaling so as to ensure comparability across variables while facilitating stable convergence during neural network training.

Long short-term memory

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks [61] consist of an extension of RNNs, developed to solve the vanishing or exploding gradient problems commonly encountered in long time series [56]. While traditional RNNs struggle to capture long-term dependencies, LSTM cells were designed to overcome these limitations, making them a preferred choice in time series applications due to their strong performance [56]. Training procedure and baseline comparison

To train the forecasting models, we split each dataset into temporally ordered partitions, using an approximately 85%–15% chronological hold-out. For the New York dataset, the training set spans January 2, 2022 to September 25, 2023, with the test set covering September 26, 2023 to December 31, 2023. For the UK dataset, the training period extends from February 1, 2021 to October 17, 2022, and the test period from October 19, 2022 to January 31, 2023. No shuffling is applied at any stage, ensuring that the models learn exclusively from past observations and that temporal ordering is fully preserved, as recommended for time-dependent forecasting tasks.

Although we adopt a single chronological split, model evaluation is carried out over multiple non-overlapping forecast windows within the held-out test period. As previously detailed in Section “Input features and model encoding”, the model consumes a look-back window of length and jointly predicts the horizons. During evaluation, the same sliding-window mechanism is applied across the test set to construct successive non-overlapping prediction blocks of length . This process generates several independent forecasting episodes from a single trained model, allowing aggregate evaluation metrics to be computed across distinct test windows. This multi-window protocol effectively implements a blocked rolling-origin evaluation without requiring model retraining, thereby enabling a direct assessment of robustness to test-window selection.

To establish a baseline, the recurrent neural models were first trained using (a) default hyperparameters and (b) systematically tuned counterparts via Bayesian optimization with the Keras Tuner library, which optimizes the model’s loss function by building a probabilistic surrogate [38]. Specifically, we explored different tuning configurations for all recurrent architectures, including the number of recurrent units per layer (ranging from 4 to 512, in steps of 4), the number of hidden layers (–), dropout rates between and , and activation functions (tanh or sigmoid in the hidden layers, with a linear activation at the output). The input window length and forecast horizon were also treated as tunable design parameters within problem‑specific ranges. All tuning runs were executed with fixed random seeds to control stochastic variability during training. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) was used as the loss function and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) (14) as the validation metric. All models were trained for up to 1000 epochs with the Adam optimizer, using a learning rate selected from , and early stopping was triggered if the loss did not improve for 50 consecutive epochs.

We also implemented a comprehensive set of classical statistical and machine learning time-series baselines, including Seasonal Naïve [62], ETS [63], ARIMA [64], SARIMA [65], and XGBoost [66], for comparison purposes. These models were fitted directly to the case counts using the same training and test partitions adopted for the analyzed neural networks and have been widely used as standard benchmarks in recent time-series forecasting and epidemic prediction studies.

Permutation feature importance

Permutation Feature Importance (PFI) is a model-agnostic technique that quantifies how much each input variable contributes to the predictive performance of a forecaster [40, 71–75]. This technique works by breaking the association between a feature and the target output through random permutation of its values in a given period, and then evaluating the resulting change in model performance. A larger performance degradation indicates higher importance of the analyzed feature.

This is particularly relevant in the epidemiological context, where some disease variants may be inactive or present in low prevalence, naturally leading to lower PFI values despite potential importance in other periods.

Shapley Additive exPlanations

To complement the PFI analyses, we apply SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which decomposes individual predictions into additive contributions from each input variable [76, 77]. Grounded in Cooperative Game Theory [78], Shapley values quantify each variable’s expected marginal contribution, averaged over all possible subsets. It offers both local and global explanations: local to interpret single predictions, and global by aggregating effects across all instances. Unlike PFI, which measures variable importance via perturbation, SHAP provides a more granular interpretation, capturing both overall importance and potential variable interactions [76].

In our assessments, we compute SHAP values using the gradient-based explainer from the Python SHAP library [79], an implementation optimized for deep neural networks. Specifically, we take distinct temporal segments of the test data, which serve a dual purpose: they act as a reference dataset for efficiently approximating expected values and as the instances to be explained. This balances computational efficiency with interpretability while ensuring the evaluation scenarios are representative.

The resulting SHAP indicators allow us to assess both the overall contribution of each variable and its influence in specific prediction instances. This is particularly relevant in the context of circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants, as SHAP can reveal whether certain variants, even with low case counts at a given time-frame, still had a significant positive or negative impact on the predicted number of cases.

Results and discussion

This section presents and discusses the results of the deep learning models introduced in Section “Neural network architectures”, as well as the impact of variant dynamics, supported by time series exploration and the application of XAI-driven methods described in Section “Evaluation and interpretability methods”.

All experiments were conducted on a machine with an x86_64 processor and 16 GB of RAM, using only the CPU. The models were implemented in Python (version 3.12.12) [80] with TensorFlow/Keras (version 2.19.0), and supporting libraries such as NumPy, pandas, and scikit-learn were used for data processing and evaluation.

From this point on, we refer to COVID-19 variants specifically as Omicron subvariants (e.g., BA.1, BA.2, XBB, JN.1), which have been the sole circulating COVID-19 lineages since 2022 [4].

Ablation study on variant-driven forecasting

To quantitatively evaluate the predictive impact of SARS-CoV-2 variant data, we present in Table 2 an ablation study comparing several forecast horizons using the LSTM model with and without lineage-specific inputs. These ablation experiments are conducted over multiple forecasting windows of 7 and 15 days, which capture different epidemiological situations, including both sharp post-peak declines in cases (eg., Fig. 8) and pronounced epidemic peaks (eg., Fig. 9), thereby providing a heterogeneous and realistic setting for comparing models with and without variant information. The results consistently show an advantage for the variant-informed model (“with variants”) over the baseline (“w/o variants”) across all metrics, locations and forecast horizons. Variant-specific features: importance and explainability

For a comprehensive comparison of feature importance beyond variant-related features, we extend our analyses by retraining the model with hospitalization records, a well-established case predictor. We further probe the effect of lineage turnover by analyzing periods that coincide with pronounced shifts in Omicron subvariant prevalence (e.g., the rapid rise of FL.1 and EG.5 and the decline of XBB, as shown next).

Figure 10 shows the PFI for case prediction in New York City over a two-week period, from September 30 to October 14, 2023, providing a natural stress test to assess how the models leverage variant information in a rapidly changing viral scenario. It can be seen a clear alignment between variants transition and their impact on model performance, with dominant variants showing even stronger predictive influence than the hospitalization feature. Lineages with low prevalence (e.g., BA.2 and JN.1) have minimal influence on predictions. Conversely, lineages undergoing dynamic changes in prevalence, such as FL.1, EG.5 and XBB, exhibit high PFI scores, with those in decline accounting for the most significant impact.

Potential implications and limitations

The results of this study carry important implications for epidemic modeling and public health planning, especially in the post-pandemic era. By showing that the inclusion of variant-specific features substantially improves the predictive accuracy of popular deep learning architectures compared against methods that rely solely on hospitalization or aggregate case counts, our work underscores the critical role of genomic surveillance in supporting timely and targeted interventions within routine public health decision-making. Another plus is that the framework presented here is generalizable and can be extended to other pathogens with rapid variant turnover, providing a practical solution for real-time epidemiological forecasting. Overall, this study demonstrates how integrating epidemiological and genomic data with interpretable modeling can strengthen data-driven decision-making at both local and national scales.

Notwithstanding these strengths, our modeling is subject to some inherent limitations. Since the models are designed to forecast epidemic trajectories conditional on available variant data, they can anticipate new peaks or resurgences when early shifts in lineage prevalence appear in the surveillance streams. However, they are not capable of prospectively predicting the emergence or impact of entirely new variants before these unseen lineages are identified in the population, a challenge that remains unsolved in the literature due to the inherently stochastic nature of viral evolution.

Conclusion

This paper underscores the critical role of incorporating SARS-CoV-2 variant time series into neural network models for accurate prediction of COVID-19 case resurgences under an ongoing endemic scenario. As hypothesized in Section “Background”, the rapid succession of competing Omicron lineages has become a key determinant of case dynamics in the post-pandemic phase, a trend that most conventional predictive models often miss, but which our approach specifically addresses through integrated genomic surveillance. Indeed, our data-driven methodology simplifies forecasting by using well-established neural networks, making it more broadly applicable than methods that rely on extensive lists of variables and very specific conditions.

By comparing recurrent neural networks with and without lineage-specific information, we provide clear, quantitative evidence that incorporating variant trends enhances model performance. For instance, in New York City, taking lineage data reduced 7-day forecast errors in the most severe case by over 77% during a period of rapid variant change (MAPE fell from 32.15% to 7.35%). Similarly, in the United Kingdom, a 15-day forecast saw its MAPE drop dramatically, from 35.62% to 7.73%. These results confirm that variant prevalence is not merely an ancillary signal but a fundamental feature for capturing abrupt shifts in transmission dynamics.

Exploratory analyses of SARS-CoV-2 variant time series revealed their crucial role in shaping COVID-19 cases during the post-pandemic period, with strong positive correlations for emerging variants and inverse correlations for those in decline. The application of Explainable AI (XAI) techniques further allowed us to move beyond prediction and provide interpretable insights into the model’s decision-making. PFI and SHAP inspections quantitatively identified the variants driving increases and decreases in cases, with results shifting over time to reflect the dominant circulating lineages. In particular, SHAP analysis for NYC (Fig. 14) identified FL.1 and EG.5 as primary drivers of case increases, while highlighting the declining XBB variant, illustrating the model’s detection of competitive displacement. Similarly, in the UK (Fig. 15), BA.5, followed by Hospitalized and XBB, were the most influential predictors of case increases over nearly a year of post-pandemic data. By providing temporally-aware interpretations at both city and country levels, the XAI-driven explorations provide a methodological advancement still scarce in COVID-19 time series forecasting.

Considering the current post-pandemic context, where limited data variability, underreporting, low testing rates and the emergence of new variants challenge predictive accuracy, the experiments demonstrated that incorporating variant time series substantially mitigates many of these issues, improving the reliability of forecasts for both near-term horizons (e.g., Table 2) and longer-term predictions over several weeks (e.g., Fig. 13). Our findings emphasize the importance of sustained genomic surveillance, while offering a robust, data-driven approach that leverages variant information to support timely public health interventions.

As future work, we plan to explicitly incorporate derived features such as growth-rate or phase indicators, so that the models can more clearly distinguish variant growth and decline stages and further refine the characterization of variant-driven dynamics.
