Carinal Squamous Cell Carcinoma Causing Progressive Central Airway Obstruction: CT, FDG PET/CT, Bronchoscopic Pre‐ and Post‐Debulking, and Pathologic Correlation
Authors: Saubhagya Dhakal, Govinda Adhikari, Ali Kanj Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Primary tracheobronchial tumors are rare and are frequently diagnosed late because their presenting symptoms are nonspecific and may mimic more common respiratory conditions. We report a case of a 74‐year‐old man with a significant smoking history who presented with progressive dyspnea, cough, hemoptysis, and stridor, initially treated with multiple courses of antibiotics and corticosteroids, without improvement. Chest radiography was reportedly normal, but a contrast‐enhanced CT demonstrated an endoluminal soft tissue mass centered at the distal trachea/carina with extension into the bilateral mainstem bronchi, causing near‐complete obstruction of the left mainstem bronchus and moderate‐to‐severe narrowing of the right mainstem bronchus. Coronal FDG PET/CT demonstrated intense metabolic activity centered at the carina. Predebulking bronchoscopy confirmed a verrucous fungating carinal mass causing severe central airway compromise, whereas postdebulking bronchoscopy showed marked restoration of airway patency. Histopathologic evaluation established squamous cell carcinoma. This case highlights the diagnostic limitations of chest radiography and the essential role of CT, FDG PET/CT, and bronchoscopy in evaluating malignant central airway obstruction.

Teaching Point

Persistent cough, hemoptysis, dyspnea, or stridor that does not respond to empiric therapy should prompt evaluation for central airway obstruction, even when chest radiography is normal.

1. Introduction

Primary tracheobronchial tumors are uncommon, accounting for less than 0.4% of all tumors, and most are malignant in adults [1]. Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is one of the most common primary tracheal malignancies and is strongly associated with tobacco exposure [2].

Patients with central airway tumors typically present with nonspecific respiratory symptoms such as cough, dyspnea, wheezing, hemoptysis, or stridor, often resulting in delayed diagnosis because these manifestations overlap with more common conditions such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or infection [3, 4]. Chest radiography is often the first imaging study performed in symptomatic patients, but it has limited sensitivity for detecting central airway lesions and may be normal even in the presence of clinically significant disease [5]. Computed tomography (CT), particularly with multiplanar reconstruction, is therefore the imaging modality of choice for evaluating large‐airway pathology because it allows accurate localization, characterization, and assessment of disease extent [1, 5].

Although primary tracheobronchial SCC is a recognized entity, this case is notable for the multimodality correlation of chest radiography, CT, FDG PET/CT, bronchoscopic findings before and after therapeutic debulking, and histopathologic confirmation in a patient with progressive malignant central airway obstruction. The case further illustrates how clinically significant carinal obstruction may remain unrecognized despite initially unremarkable chest radiography and repeated empiric treatment, emphasizing imaging findings that may facilitate earlier diagnosis and intervention [3–5].

2. Case Presentation

A 74‐year‐old man with a 40‐pack‐year smoking history, who had quit 17 years earlier, presented with progressive dyspnea, cough, and hemoptysis over several months. He was initially evaluated at an urgent care facility, where chest radiography was reportedly unremarkable. No signs of atelectasis or other radiographic findings suggestive of central bronchial obstruction were identified on the chest radiograph. He was treated with multiple courses of antibiotics and corticosteroids for presumed infectious or inflammatory etiologies without clinical improvement. His symptoms progressively worsened despite treatment, and stridor was subsequently noted on clinical examination.

Contrast‐enhanced CT of the chest demonstrated a large endoluminal soft tissue mass centered in the distal trachea at the level of the carina, extending into the bilateral mainstem bronchi (Figure 1A,B). The lesion measured approximately 2.4 × 1.8 cm on axial imaging and approximately 2 cm in craniocaudal extent on coronal reformatted images. The mass resulted in near‐complete obstruction of the left mainstem bronchus and moderate‐to‐severe narrowing of the right mainstem bronchus.

(A) Axial contrast‐enhanced CT image showing an endoluminal soft tissue mass at the distal trachea/carina causing marked airway narrowing. (B) Coronal contrast‐enhanced CT image demonstrating longitudinal extent of the lesion with involvement of the bilateral mainstem bronchi.

Coronal FDG PET/CT demonstrated intense FDG uptake centered at the carina, corresponding to the endoluminal lesion and better delineating the craniocaudal extent of airway involvement (Figure 2).

Coronal FDG PET/CT image demonstrating intense FDG uptake centered at the carina corresponding to the endoluminal malignancy.

The patient underwent bronchoscopic tumor debulking, resulting in symptomatic improvement. Postdebulking bronchoscopy showed marked restoration of airway patency with clearance of the obstructing tumor (Figure 3A,B). No stent placement was required, and no procedural complications were observed.

(A) Predebulking bronchoscopic image showing a verrucous fungating carinal mass causing near‐complete obstruction of the carinal bifurcation and bilateral mainstem bronchi. (B) Postdebulking bronchoscopic image demonstrating restoration of airway patency with clearance of the obstructing mass.

Subsequent tissue sampling established the diagnosis of SCC. Histopathologic examination demonstrated atypical squamous proliferation with keratinization and cytologic atypia consistent with SCC (Figure 4A,B). The patient subsequently underwent definitive chemoradiation consisting of external beam radiation therapy to the trachea and carina, receiving 60 Gy in 30 fractions using volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) with concurrent carboplatin and paclitaxel. He completed the planned course of treatment without interruption.

(A) Histopathology (H&E, ×10) showing atypical squamous proliferation. (B) Histopathology (H&E, ×40) demonstrating keratinization and cytologic atypia consistent with squamous cell carcinoma.

3. Discussion

Primary tracheobronchial tumors are rare, and diagnosis is frequently delayed because associated symptoms are nonspecific and overlap with common benign respiratory disorders [1, 3]. In adults, most primary airway tumors are malignant, and SCC is among the most common histologic subtypes [2, 4]. Smoking is a major risk factor, and SCC generally affects older patients [2, 4].

One of the most important clinical lessons in this case is that central airway malignancy may present with persistent but nonspecific symptoms for months before diagnosis. Cough, dyspnea, wheezing, and hemoptysis are common manifestations of tracheobronchial tumors, but they are also common in asthma, COPD, bronchitis, and pneumonia. As a result, patients are often initially treated empirically with antibiotics, bronchodilators, or corticosteroids, which may delay definitive diagnosis [3, 4].

Another major teaching point is the limited value of chest radiography for central airway tumors. Chest radiographs are frequently nondiagnostic and may appear normal even when clinically significant tracheobronchial obstruction is present [5].

CT is therefore critical in the evaluation of suspected central airway pathology. Multidetector CT provides high‐resolution visualization of the tracheobronchial tree and accurately demonstrates the location, morphology, and extent of airway lesions [1, 5]. Coronal reformatted images are particularly useful for assessing longitudinal tumor extension and the degree of airway compromise. In the present case, CT was critical for localizing the lesion to the distal trachea and carina, defining the extent of bilateral mainstem bronchial involvement, quantifying the severity of airway compromise, and facilitating bronchoscopic treatment planning. FDG PET/CT provided complementary metabolic characterization of the lesion and more clearly demonstrated the craniocaudal extent of tumor involvement while assisting with staging evaluation. Together, anatomic and metabolic imaging played an important role in diagnosis, therapeutic decision‐making, and assessment of disease extent [1, 5, 7].

At CT, SCC of the tracheobronchial tree may appear as an endoluminal or polypoid mass, focal sessile lesion, eccentric airway narrowing, or circumferential wall thickening [1, 7]. In the present case, the lesion presented as an endoluminal carinal mass with severe bilateral mainstem bronchial narrowing, an appearance compatible with malignant central airway obstruction.

The degree of airway narrowing is clinically important. Symptoms often become prominent only after substantial luminal compromise has occurred, and severe stenosis may lead to stridor, respiratory distress, postobstructive infection, or life‐threatening airway obstruction [3, 6].

Bronchoscopy remains essential for direct visualization, tissue diagnosis, and management. In malignant central airway obstruction, bronchoscopic intervention can rapidly improve airway patency and provide symptomatic relief [6].

The differential diagnosis for an endoluminal central airway lesion includes other primary tumors such as adenoid cystic carcinoma, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, and carcinoid tumor, as well as metastatic disease and benign endobronchial lesions [1, 4, 7]. Similar diagnostic challenges may also arise with other uncommon mass‐forming lesions presenting with nonspecific obstructive symptoms in the head and neck or upper aerodigestive tract, including rare benign proliferative disorders such as Rosai–Dorfman disease, which may mimic neoplastic processes on cross‐sectional imaging [8].

Overall, this case underscores the importance of considering central airway malignancy in patients with persistent respiratory symptoms despite appropriate therapy, especially when symptoms are progressive or accompanied by hemoptysis or stridor.

4. Conclusion

This case demonstrates endoluminal carinal SCC presenting with progressive malignant central airway obstruction following an initial period of nonspecific respiratory symptoms and normal chest radiography. CT, FDG PET/CT, and bronchoscopy were essential for diagnosis, therapeutic planning, and postintervention assessment. Early recognition of central airway malignancy is crucial to avoid diagnostic delay and reduce morbidity.
