Single-cell analysis identifies dysregulated cell type-specific gene programs and intercellular crosstalk underlying colorectal cancer
Authors: Wenjing Jia, Kongxuan Lin, Xiumin Liu, Lingxiao Zou, Shan He, Jian Zhao, Jingjing Liu, Xiaofeng Song, Quan Wang, Yixuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer, a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, arises from the stepwise accumulation of genetic alterations in colonic epithelial cells, accompanied by dynamic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. While the adenoma-carcinoma sequence is widely recognized, the cell type-specific gene programs and their interactions with the tumor microenvironment that drive malignant transformation remain poorly understood. In this study, we analyzed single-nucleus data from colorectal carcinoma and matched unaffected tissues, revealing dysregulated gene programs at the level of individual cell types. In epithelial cells, we identified two activated modules linked to enhanced stemness and two repressed modules associated with apoptosis and chronic inflammatory signaling. Transcription factors analysis highlighted CEBPB and RUNX1 as key activated regulators, while FOXP1 and POU5F1 were repressed in cancerous cells. Further investigation of intercellular communication uncovered disrupted crosstalk between epithelial cells and tumor microenvironment components, which may underlie these gene program alterations. Additionally, we observed functional reprogramming within the tumor microenvironment, including extracellular matrix remodeling by stromal fibroblasts and impaired B-cell-mediated immunity. Together, these findings improve our understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanisms driving colorectal tumorigenesis and may bring new opportunities for the development of therapeutic strategies.Subject terms: Cancer, Cell biology

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide1, with a rising incidence2. One of the most widely accepted models of CRC tumorigenesis is the adenoma-carcinoma sequence, which describes the stepwise accumulation of genetic alterations in colonic epithelial cells. This process is initiated by mutations in the APC gene and further driven by KRAS activation and TP53 loss3,4. As the tumor microenvironment (TME) evolves, involving processes such as immune evasion, angiogenesis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), cancer cells progressively acquire invasive and metastatic potential5. Deciphering the molecular mechanisms underlying epithelial tumorigenesis, as well as the crosstalk between epithelial cells and the TME remodeling, is therefore essential for understanding CRC pathobiology and guiding therapeutic strategies.

Past efforts to elucidate the molecular underpinnings of CRC have largely focused on identifying diagnostic biomarkers, yielding valuable insights into specific aspects of disease progression. These include methylation biomarkers such as ALX46,7, APC8, and LINE-19,10; long noncoding RNAs like MALAT111, GAS512,13, and CCAT214–16; and microRNAs such as miR-2117–20, miR-3121–23, and miR-15524,25. However, while these biomarker-driven approaches have shown promise, they often lack the capability to capture the functional heterogeneity of gene programs across distinct cell types, especially the epithelial remodeling events that initiate CRC.

Gene co-expression analyses have traditionally been applied to bulk RNA sequencing data to uncover gene modules associated with disease mechanisms26–29, but recent advances have extended these approaches to single-cell transcriptomic studies30. Single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq/snRNA-seq) now enable high-resolution profiling of gene expression at the individual cell level, thereby preserving critical cell type-specific signals and revealing the cellular heterogeneity that characterizes CRC31. This fine-grained resolution also facilitates the study of intercellular communication, providing new insights into the dynamic interactions of diverse cell types, particularly epithelial cells for CRC, with the TME. Together, these single-cell approaches offer powerful tools for advancing our understanding of CRC pathogenesis32.

In this study, we integrated snRNA-seq and single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (scATAC-seq) data to highlight the cellular heterogeneity of CRC. To dissect the heterogeneity, we analyzed gene expression modules and gene regulatory networks (GRNs) at the cell type level. Our analyses identified dysregulated gene programs and key transcription factors (TFs) implicated in CRC development, including the CRC risk gene CEBPB and the proliferation and chemoresistance regulator RUNX1. Moreover, we investigated the intercellular communication between epithelial cells and TME components, underscoring the important roles of stromal and immune interactions in promoting tumor progression along the adenoma-carcinoma sequence. Collectively, our findings provide new insights into the cell type-specific programs and intercellular crosstalk that drive colorectal tumorigenesis.

Results

Analysis of transcriptomic and epigenetic data reveals cell type-specific roles underlying CRC

We retrieved a snRNA-seq dataset of CRC patients from a published study33, which profiled cells from three different progression stages: polyps, cancerous tissue, and adjacent unaffected colon tissue. In this study, we focused only on the cancerous and unaffected samples, and extracted expression profiles of 60,879 high-quality single nuclei. We retained the original cell type annotations—epithelial, immune, and stromal cells—for downstream analysis (Fig. 1a; hereafter referred to as discovery dataset). To capture cell type-specific characteristics, we conducted differential gene expression analysis between cancerous and unaffected states across the different cell types (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c and Supplementary Table 1). This analysis revealed limited overlaps of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between cell types (Fig. 1b). Specifically, over 87% of DEGs are specific to a single cell type, with only 127 genes shared across multiple cell types. Notably, epithelial cells displayed the highest number of DEGs, consistent with their role as the structural basis of most organ tissues and their frequent involvement in tumor initiation34. To assess the robustness of these epithelial DEGs, we leveraged four independent datasets from two studies as validation cohorts, including GSE178341 reported by ref. 35 and the SG1, new KUL3, and KUL5 datasets reported by ref. 36 (Methods). We found that the epithelial DEGs identified in discovery dataset are significantly enriched among those inferred from the four validation datasets (Fig. 1c). We next performed pathway enrichment analysis of these DEGs using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database to evaluate their biological functions and observed that epithelial DEGs are significantly enriched in cancer-related functions, including the Wnt and cAMP signaling pathways (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 2). As expected, immune DEGs are enriched in immune-related functions such as the B cell receptor signaling pathway, while stromal DEGs are associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) functions, including cytoskeleton in muscle cells and ECM−receptor interactions (Fig. 1d). In short, our analyses revealed the cell type-specific DEGs and their functions across epithelial, immune, and stromal cells, reflecting their distinct roles in CRC tumorigenesis.

To ensure the rigor of our findings, we also analyzed the scATAC-seq data of 109,864 cells obtained from the same published study33 to further assess cell type-specific characteristics of CRC. We performed peak calling on the chromatin accessibility data in a pseudobulk manner (Methods), identifying 190,772 peaks in epithelial cells, 176,361 in immune cells, and 200,027 in stromal cells. The majority of these peaks are specific to individual cell types, with only ~5.4% shared across cell types (Fig. 1e). By integrating the snRNA-seq and scATAC-seq results, our findings underscore the heterogeneity of CRC and provided a rationale for dissecting cell type-specific gene functions underlying its etiology.

Gene co-expression modules reveal stem-like and inflammatory epithelial programs

To investigate the functional roles of epithelial cells in CRC, we applied the high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA)37 method to the snRNA-seq data and identified eight gene co-expression modules in epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 3). To assess which modules were activated or repressed under the cancerous condition, we conducted a hypergeometric test to evaluate the enrichment of modules with epithelial DEGs identified above and pinpointed two activated (E1 and E5) and two repressed (E2 and E3) modules in cancerous cells (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2b–e). To strengthen our findings, we assigned each epithelial cell with a module identity to indicate the most dominant module in each cell (Methods). As expected, the majority of cancerous cells were classified as E1- or E5-dominant, while the adjacent unaffected cells were predominantly E2- or E3-dominant (Fig. 2b–d). To assess whether inter-sample heterogeneity influenced the results, we calculated the proportion of module-dominant cells in each sample and observed the consistent pattern between cell-level assignment and sample-level composition (Fig. 2e). To validate the robustness of the four identified modules, we assessed their expression level in an independent bulk expression dataset from The Cancer Genome Atlas colon adenocarcinoma (TCGA-COAD) cohort, obtained from GDC Data Portal Homepage38. Using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) to score the expression of each module (Methods), we observed, as expected, a strong negative correlation between the activated and repressed modules (Supplementary Fig. 2f; R = −0.39, P < 0.01). Additionally, the two activated modules were positively correlated with each other (Supplementary Fig. 2g; R = 0.36, P < 0.01), and a similarly strong positive correlation was found between the two repressed modules (Supplementary Fig. 2h; R = 0.75, P < 0.01).

To further characterize the biological identity of module-dominant cells, we mapped them to refined epithelial cell subtypes (Methods). Under homeostatic conditions, intestinal stem cells reside at the crypt base and gradually migrate upward to differentiate into mature cells39. Interestingly, E1-dominant cells are primarily associated with the ‘cycling transit amplifying (TA) cell’ subtype, which originates from intestinal stem cells at the crypt base and migrates upward, representing an early stage of intestinal differentiation with multipotent potential. Meanwhile, most E5-dominant cells are enriched in the “stem cell” subtype (Fig. 2b–d). Together, these findings suggest that epithelial cells in the cancerous state exhibit enhanced stem-like properties. By contrast, E2- and E3-dominant cells correspond to more differentiated, highly polarized epithelial cells (enterocytes) that are critical for antigen restriction and maintaining epithelial integrity40. The depletion of E2 and E3 in cancerous cells reflects the loss of normal epithelial function during tumor progression (Fig. 2e).

CRC is a highly heterogeneous disease, and capturing the defining features of its molecular subtypes is of clear clinical relevance36,41. To this end, we evaluated whether the inferred modules are capable of distinguishing CRC subtypes. In a seminal study, Guinney, J. et al. defined four consensus molecular subtypes (CMS) of CRC based on bulk transcriptomic data, including two epithelial-dominant ones (CMS2 and CMS3)41. Interestingly, E1 and E5 are significantly enriched in CMS2, a subtype characterized by strong Wnt and MYC pathway activity (Supplementary Table 4; P = 1.75 10−10 for E1 and 1.92 × 10−6 for E5). Notably, neither module shows enrichment in the other CMS subtypes, indicating that E1 and E5 may have the ability to distinguish CMS2. We further examined whether E1 and E5 can resolve the intrinsic consensus molecular subtypes (iCMS) proposed by ref. 36. In this work, the epithelial-dominant CMS subtypes were refined using scRNA-seq data, leading to the identification of two subtypes: iCMS2 and iCMS3. Enrichment analysis revealed that both E1 and E5 are significantly enriched in iCMS2, but not in iCMS3 (Supplementary Table 4; P = 3.46 ×10−64 for E1 and 5.02 10−8 for E5), indicating that these modules capture the intrinsic subtype-specific transcriptional patterns at single-cell resolution.

We also compiled Gene Ontology (GO) terms within the “biological process” category and performed enrichment analysis to further investigate the functional implications of these modules. Notably, the activated modules E1 and E5 were primarily enriched in cell proliferation functions, such as RNA transport and protein translation (Fig. 2f), consistent with the notion that these cells possess stem-like characteristics and exhibit dysregulated proliferation. Conversely, modules E2 and E3 were enriched in apoptosis and immune-relevant functions, including regulation of programmed cell death (PCD) and canonical NF-kappaB signal transduction (Fig. 2f). PCD, a tightly regulated form of cell death essential for tissue homeostasis42, is frequently impaired in cancer, contributing to resistance to therapy43,44. Additionally, NF-kappaB signaling in epithelial cells promotes cytokine expression, thereby exacerbating inflammation. In vivo studies using genetic mouse models have demonstrated that inhibition of NF-kappaB is capable of inducing chronic inflammation, highlighting its critical role in immune homeostasis45, while disruption of NF-kappaB signaling in epithelial cells compromises barrier function and leads to severe chronic colon inflammation46,47. Collectively, these findings suggest that impaired cell death pathways and chronic inflammation contribute to epithelial barrier dysfunction in CRC.

Gene regulatory network analysis identifies key TFs in epithelial cells

In addition to the co-expression modules described above, we also employed a landmark method, SCENIC48, to infer gene regulatory networks (GRNs), and investigated key TFs and their roles in epithelial cells. Each GRN, referred to as a regulon in SCENIC, consists of a specific TF and target genes it regulates. Using SCENIC with default parameters, we identified 176 regulons (Methods). To investigate how these regulons are dysregulated in CRC, we assessed their activity in each individual cell and compared regulon activities between cancerous and unaffected epithelial cells (Methods). As a result, we identified 12 regulons that were activated and six that were repressed under the cancerous condition. To validate these patterns, we leveraged the regulon specificity score (RSS) from SCENIC to quantify their specificity in cancerous cells (Methods). As expected, the activated regulons exhibited high specificity, whereas the repressed ones displayed low specificity (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, target genes regulated by the activated TFs were highly expressed in cancerous cells, while those regulated by the repressed TFs showed reduced expression (Fig. 3b). To assess the relationship between regulons and co-expression modules identified previously via hdWGCNA, we examined the overlap between these two sets of features. Strikingly, the majority of the 12 activated regulons were significantly enriched in the activated modules, i.e., E1 and E5, while all six repressed regulons exhibited strong overlap with the repressed modules, i.e., E2 and E3 (Fig. 3c). Together, these findings reflect the concordance between co-expression patterns and the regulatory mechanisms.

Several of the key TFs identified through SCENIC are supported by external evidence. For instance, the activated TF CEBPB was previously pinpointed as one of 105 high-confidence CRC risk genes identified at the 20q13.13 locus49 using a Bayesian approach, integrative risk gene selector (iRIGS)50. CEBPB has been shown to promote tumor progression in CRC by enhancing cell proliferation through multiple oncogenic pathways, including MAPK, PI3K-Akt, and Ras signaling49. Another activated TF, RUNX1, plays a crucial role in CRC proliferation and chemoresistance via the Hedgehog and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways51,52. RUNX1 expression was upregulated in CRC tissues (Fig. 3d), promoting metastasis and EMT both in vitro and in vivo52. Conversely, the TF FOXP1 was repressed in cancerous cells (Fig. 3d), and the loss of FOXP1 has been linked to poor survival outcomes in CRC patients53. Moreover, FOXP1 was also implicated in the regulation of proliferation and inflammation in colorectal neoplasia53. Another repressed TF, POU5F1, is known for its role in pluripotency. Previous studies have reported that POU5F1 expression declines as cells exit the pluripotent state54. This is in line with our finding that the POU5F1 regulon strongly overlaps with the repressed module E3, which corresponds to the enterocyte cell identity (Fig. 3c). In addition to previously reported TFs, we also identified an interesting candidate, RFX3. Although it has not been specifically implicated in CRC, prior studies have shown that RFX3 functions as a modulator of ciliogenesis55, a process increasingly recognized for its role in tumor biology. Furthermore, elevated RFX3 expression has been reported to be capable of promoting the malignant phenotype of glioma cells56 and enhancing cell migration in breast cancer57. Together, these findings suggest a potential role for RFX3 in CRC.

Motivated by the ability of two activated epithelial modules (E1 and E5) to distinguish intrinsic molecular subtypes of CRC, we next evaluated the clinical relevance of two upregulated TFs, CEBPB and RUNX1. Survival analysis revealed that high expression of CEBPB and RUNX1 is associated with reduced survival probability of CRC patients (Methods; Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), underscoring their potential clinical significance. We further assessed the reproducibility of these two regulons across four validation datasets. The CEBPB regulon was consistently recovered in all four datasets, whereas the RUNX1 regulon was detected in two datasets (GSE178341 and KUL5). Notably, among the six regulons identified in the validation datasets, five are significantly upregulated in cancerous cells, consistent with the direction observed in the discovery dataset (Supplementary Table 5). The remaining regulon, corresponding to CEBPB in the SG1 cohort, showed an indeterminate direction of regulation. Finally, we examined the overlap between regulons identified in the discovery and validation datasets. Strikingly, all regulons inferred in the validation datasets exhibited significant enrichment in the corresponding RUNX1 and CEBPB regulons defined in the discovery dataset (Supplementary Fig. 3c).

Dysregulated crosstalk between epithelial and TME cells influences the development of CRC

TME, composed of immune cells, stromal cells, endothelial cells, and cytokines, is a key factor impacting cancer progression, metastasis, and therapeutic response58–60. Given the critical role of epithelial cells in CRC development, we sought to investigate the intercellular communication between epithelial cells and TME components—specifically immune and stromal cells here. To this end, we employed MultiNicheNet61 to infer ligand-receptor (LR) interactions among the three cell types. Briefly, MultiNicheNet prioritizes LR pairs based on: (1) the differential expression of ligands and receptors, (2) their cell type specificity in the corresponding sender and receiver cells, and 3) the downstream signaling activity, reflected by DEGs regulated by ligands. We applied MultiNicheNet to the collected snRNA-seq dataset and identified 1211 LR pairs active in cancerous cells and 934 in unaffected cells (Methods, Supplementary Table 6). Notably, we observed a marked increase in TME involved communications in cancerous samples, both in the number and strength of active LR interactions (Fig. 4a, b). By contrast, the strength of autocrine communications within epithelial cells was remarkably decreased. Taken together, these results indicate a more active and engaged TME during tumor progression.

Given the previous identification of functional epithelial modules that are activated (E1 and E5) or repressed (E2 and E3) in cancerous cells, we next investigated whether these modules are involved in intercellular communication with TME. To this end, we extracted two sets of epithelial ligands/receptors (ELRs) for comparison: one set active in cancerous samples and the other active in unaffected samples (Methods). Strikingly, the activated module E1 was significantly enriched in the ELRs active in cancerous samples (Fig. 4c；P = 7.22 × 10−4, OR = 2.17, Fisher’s exact test), whereas the repressed module E2 was significantly depleted in the same set (Fig. 4c; P = 1.50 × 10−3, OR = 0.30, Fisher’s exact test). To ensure the reliability of these findings, we extracted the E1- and E2-associated LR pairs and assessed their reproducibility across the four validation datasets, i.e., GSE178341, SG1, new KUL3, and KUL5. We observed a substantial overlap between these E1- and E2-associated LR pairs and those inferred in the validation datasets (Methods; Supplementary Fig. 4a–d and Supplementary Table 7). Of note, no significant enrichment or depletion was observed for module E3 and E5. We reasoned that epithelial genes may contribute to CRC progression through diverse mechanisms, not all of which involve active communication with TME. Highlighted in Fig. 4d are the top-ranked LR pairs reported by MultiNicheNet involving genes from module E1, including notable stromal-to-epithelial interactions such as FN1-SDC1, THBS2-SDC1, and COL5A2-SDC1. The stromal ligands, i.e., FN1, THBS2, and COL5A2, have previously been implicated in mesenchymal activation, promoting CRC aggressiveness and driving tumor progression and metastasis62–64. Notably, the three stromal ligands and one epithelial receptor exhibited similar expression patterns across the discovery and four validation datasets, further supporting the robustness of the inferred LR interactions (Supplementary Fig. 4e–h).

Disorder of gene programs in the TME indicates stromal and immune contributions to CRC

To further understand the functional roles of TME components in CRC progression, we also analyzed the gene co-expression modules in stromal and immune cells, focusing on those with dysregulated intercellular communication. Taking the same strategy as for epithelial cells, we identified two activated (S3 and S12) and two repressed (S4 and S7) stromal modules in cancerous cells (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). We then examined these modules for differential communication patterns between cancerous and unaffected conditions and found that module S3 was significantly enriched in stromal ligands/receptors that are active in the cancerous state (Fig. 5a; P = 0.02, OR = 1.77, Fisher’s exact test). Interestingly, the three aforementioned stromal ligands actively interacting with epithelial module E1—FN1, THBS2, and COL5A2—are all members of module S3 (Fig. 4d). GO enrichment analysis revealed that S3 is functionally associated with the activation of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), particularly in ECM remodeling and connective tissue development (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 5c–e), indicating the crucial role of stromal fibroblasts in prompting tumorigenesis. CAFs, a key component of the TME, play critical roles in ECM deposition and remodeling, as well as in facilitating interactions with cancer cells and infiltrating leukocytes65. Prior studies have shown that CAFs—unlike normal fibroblasts—are sufficient to drive tumorigenesis when co-present with transformed epithelial cells, thereby reducing the reliance on a high mutational burden during cancer development66–68.

In immune cells, we identified two activated (I3 and I6) and one repressed (I1) immune module in cancerous cells (Supplementary Fig. 5b). When examining the intercellular communication of them, we found that module I1 was significantly depleted in immune ligands/receptors that are active under the cancerous condition (Fig. 5a; P = 3.51 × 10−4, OR = 0.39, Fisher’s exact test). GO analysis revealed that module I1 is significantly enriched in pathways related to immune response signaling, lymphocyte activation, and B cell receptor signaling (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, genes in module I1 were highly expressed in three B cell subtypes: naive B, memory B, and germinal center (GC) cells (Supplementary Fig. 5f–h). Previous studies have linked reduced B cell levels with poor overall survival69 and diminished immune function in CRC patients70. These findings suggest that impaired B-cell function within the CRC microenvironment may contribute to immune evasion and thus facilitate tumor progression.

Discussion

CRC arises from sequential genetic alterations in epithelial cells alongside profound remodeling of the TME. In the present study, by analyzing single-nucleus data from cancerous and adjacent unaffected colon tissues, we delineated how cell type-specific programs, regulatory networks, and intercellular interactions were rewired in CRC. We leveraged four independent datasets to validate our findings that revealed both epithelial-intrinsic alterations and TME-mediated communication as key drivers of malignant progression.

Within the epithelium, we identified two stem-like and proliferative programs and two differentiated, apoptosis/immune-related programs. Our inferred programs capture the transcriptional characteristics of distinct CRC molecular subtypes and support their clinical relevance. The enrichment of stem-like signatures in cancerous epithelial cells highlights their enhanced proliferative capacity and disrupted differentiation trajectory, consistent with the established paradigm that aberrant intestinal stem cell activity drives CRC initiation and progression. Conversely, the depletion of differentiated enterocyte-associated programs underscores a loss of epithelial barrier function whose disruption may facilitate tumor-promoting inflammation. These findings align with prior work implicating impaired apoptosis and dysregulated NF-kappaB signaling in epithelial dysfunction and tumor progression, while further clarifying their cell-type-specific contexts.

Our GRN analysis further highlighted CEBPB and RUNX1 as key activated TFs, with strong external support linking them to oncogenic signaling and chemoresistance in CRC. Their high expression is associated with reduced overall survival of CRC patients. On the other hand, repression of FOXP1 and POU5F1 was associated with loss of differentiation-related programs, highlighting the dual importance of both activation and suppression of TFs in shaping malignant epithelial identities. The strong correspondence between GRN-defined regulons and co-expression modules also reinforces the robustness of our findings.

Beyond epithelial-intrinsic programs, we observed marked changes in intercellular communication. CAFs exhibited strong ECM-remodeling signatures and delivered ligands such as FN1, THBS2, and COL5A2 to epithelial receptors, suggesting that malignant cells increasingly depend on stromal support. Concomitantly, impaired B-cell programs point to weakened antitumor immunity. Importantly, the diminished autocrine signaling within epithelial cells, coupled with enhanced stromal–epithelial and immune-epithelial crosstalk, highlights a shift in epithelial dependence from intrinsic programs toward TME support. Together, these findings reveal a coordinated mechanism whereby epithelial reprogramming, stromal remodeling, and immune dysfunction converge to promote CRC progression.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The datasets analyzed were of modest sample size and may not fully capture patient heterogeneity across disease stages and subtypes. Computational inferences of GRNs and ligand-receptor activity require functional validation through experimental perturbations. Furthermore, the lack of spatial information limits our ability to pinpoint the tissue architecture of epithelial–stromal–immune interactions. Future studies incorporating spatial transcriptomics and functional assays will be critical to address these gaps.

Methods

CRC samples

In this study, we utilized snRNA-seq and scATAC-seq datasets from ref. 33, retaining only the 15 unaffected and five adenocarcinoma (hereafter referred to as cancerous) samples for analysis. Cell type annotations were obtained from the original study. Raw sequencing data were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE201349. In addition, we leveraged four independent datasets from two studies as validation cohorts, including GSE178341 from ref. 35, and the SG1, new KUL3, and KUL5 cohorts from ref. 36 downloaded from European Genome-Phenome Archive (EGA) under accession numbers EGAD00001008555, EGAD00001008584, and EGAD00001008585.

Differential gene expression and enrichment analyses

We leveraged the FindMarkers function in the Seurat package71 to identify DEGs between cancerous and unaffected samples across different cell types. Three statistical methods—Wilcoxon rank-sum test, likelihood-ratio test, and bimodal likelihood-ratio test—were applied, and only the overlapping DEGs were retained. DEGs with Bonferroni-corrected P values (Padj) < 0.01 were considered statistically significant. These DEGs were further classified based on fold change (FC): genes with log2FC >1 were defined as upregulated, and those with log2FC <-1 were defined as downregulated. Supplementary Table 1 shows the complete list of DEGs for each cell type. In the four validation datasets, we used the same analytical strategy and parameters.

To investigate the biological significance of these DEGs, we conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using the Metascape database72 with default parameters. Additionally, we also utilized the GO terms within the “biological process” category to assess the biological functions of gene modules and regulons.

Single-cell ATAC-seq peak calling and overlapping analyses

In each cell type, accessible chromatin peaks were identified using the addReproduciblePeakSet function from the ArchR package73, with default parameters, which employs MACS2 for peak calling under cancerous and unaffected stage. Pseudobulk replicates were generated based on the same disease state, and a peak was considered reproducible if it was detected in at least two pseudobulk replicates. To assess peak overlap across cell types, we used the findOverlaps function from the GenomicRanges package, with a minimum required overlap length of 10 bp.

Gene expression module analysis

We performed single-cell gene co-expression module analysis across different cell types using the hdWGCNA package37. Modules were constructed within epithelial, immune, and stromal cells, respectively. Metacells were constructed using the parameters k = 25 and max_shared = 10. To ensure biological consistency, cells were aggregated into metacells only when derived from the same cell type and disease state. The co-expression network was then established using the ConstructNetwork function.

To define the activated and repressed modules in the cancerous stage, we assessed the enrichment of each module in DEGs using hypergeometric tests. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method (Padj). A module was defined as activated if it showed significant enrichment in upregulated DEGs (Padj < 0.05) but not for downregulated DEGs (Padj ≥ 0.05); and as repressed if it exhibited significantly enrichment in downregulated DEGs (Padj < 0.05) but not in upregulated DEGs (Padj ≥ 0.05). A complete list for both activated and repressed modules is available in Supplementary Table 3.

Module scores and distribution among samples

We leveraged the AddModuleScore function from the Seurat package to calculate gene module scores. This function calculates, for each cell, the average expression of genes corresponding to a given module and adjusts it by subtracting the mean expression of a randomly selected reference gene set. To map module identities onto cell annotations in Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) space, we assigned each cell the module with the highest score as its module annotation. Using the same parameters as for subtype annotations, we then generated a UMAP plot, enabling us to link module identities with specific cell subtypes (Fig. 2b).

To evaluate the distribution of high-scoring cells across individual patients, we labeled cells as expressing a given module if their module score exceeded the 75th percentile (i.e., >0.75 quantile) for that module. We then plotted the cumulative fraction of labeled cells for each module across all samples (Fig. 2e).

Validation of gene modules in bulk RNA-seq cohorts

To assess the relevance of activated and repressed gene modules in bulk transcriptomic datasets, we performed ssGSEA on bulk RNA-seq data from the TCGA-COAD cohort (n = 431 samples). The original expression matrix was used as input, and ssGSEA was conducted using the GSVA package. Finally, pairwise correlations between gene modules were computed based on the z-normalized ssGSEA scores to evaluate their associations.

Enrichment analysis of CRC molecular subtypes

We collected CRC molecular subtype marker genes from the CMS and iCMS classification framework36,41. For CMS, a total of 693 marker genes were obtained from the centroids.RData of the CMSclassifier tool41, with each gene assigned to a specific CMS subtype. For iCMS, 715 DEGs were retrieved from Supplementary Table 14 of the original study as subtype markers, including 308 iCMS2_up, 279 iCMS2_down, 74 iCMS3_up, and 54 iCMS3_down genes, respectively36. Enrichment between module genes and CRC molecular subtype markers was assessed through a hypergeometric test, and P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the BH method.

Gene regulatory network analysis

We employed the SCENIC package48 to identify gene regulatory networks. The analysis was conducted separately for each cell type, considering only genes expressed in more than 50 cells. Regulatory interactions between TFs and their potential target genes were inferred from the gene expression matrix using GRNBoost2, which generated an adjacency matrix and defined initial SCENIC modules. Regulons were inferred for each TF using the RcisTarget motif databases (hg38-refseq-r80-10kb-up-and-down-tss and hg38-refseq-r80-500bp-up-and-100bp-down-tss), and AUCell was used to calculate the activity score of each regulon in individual cells. To avoid overly narrow or non-informative regulons, we excluded those with less than 20 target genes. To quantify the cell type specificity of regulon activity, we computed the RSS using the regulon_specificity_scores function from the SCENIC package. RSS is a Jensen–Shannon divergence–based metric that uses AUCell scores and quantifies how selectively a regulon is active in one cell type relative to others, with higher values indicating greater specificity.

To identify significantly activated and repressed regulons, we compared AUCell scores between unaffected and cancerous cells using a t-test, followed by the BH method for multiple testing correction. Regulons with BH <0.01 and FC (mean AUCell score in the cancerous cells/unaffected cells) >2 were defined as activated, whereas those with BH <0.01 and FC <0.5 were considered repressed. In the four validation datasets, we used the same analytical strategy and parameters.

Survival analysis

We utilized a widely used platform, Kaplan–Meier Plotter database74, to perform the survival analysis. The association between CEBPB and RUNX1 expression levels and overall survival was evaluated in 1061 CRC patients, and the Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated.

Cell–cell communication analysis

We performed MultiNicheNet61 to infer the cell–cell communication between epithelial, immune, and stromal cells based on the expression of known ligand-receptor pairs. MultiNicheNet builds upon the NicheNet75 framework and incorporates the same prior knowledge resources, including a ligand-receptor interaction network and ligand-target matrix (v2 version). Cells were annotated by both cell type and disease state into six categories: “epithelial-cancerous”, “epithelial-unaffected”, “immune-cancerous”, “immune-unaffected”, “stromal-cancerous”, and “stromal-unaffected”. To prioritize LR pairs, we considered condition specificity, cell type specificity and ligand activity. Specifically, three criteria were used: (1) the differential expression levels of the ligand in the sender cell type and the receptor in the receiver cell type. For each ligand/receptor, the log2FC was computed and transformed into a score between 0 and 1 by ranking across all ligands/receptors; (2) the log-transformed, normalized pseudobulk expression values of each ligand/receptor; and (3) the ligand activity score in the receiver cell type, calculated by z-score normalization followed by min-max scaling (with the bottom and top 1% quantiles excluded to reduce the influence of outliers). The scores from each criterion were scaled between 0 and 1, which were then integrated into an aggregated prioritization score (interpreted as “interaction strength”). Each LR pair was computed in both directions (“cancerous vs. unaffected” and “unaffected vs. cancerous”). The condition with the higher interaction strength was designated as the top group, and the LR pair was accordingly considered specific to that condition. Additionally, we computed the differential communication activity by comparing the total interaction counts and summed strength values in cancerous versus unaffected states.

Furthermore, we aimed to investigate the contribution of cell–cell communication to the activated and repressed gene modules. LR pairs were first assigned to either the cancerous or unaffected condition based on their top group. LR pairs were then mapped to specific modules if either: (1) the sender cell mapped to the module-associated cell type and its corresponding ligand gene was part of the module or (2) the receiver cell mapped to the module-associated cell type and its corresponding receptor gene was part of the module. We performed a Fisher’s exact test to evaluate whether these modules exhibited significant differences in cell–cell communication between the cancerous and unaffected stages. In the four validation datasets, we used the same analytical strategy and parameters.
