Tunisian Endemic Pemphigus Foliaceus is a Real Entity with Distinctive Epidemiological, Genetic, and Environmental Features
Authors: Hatem Masmoudi, Hamida Turki, Olfa Abida, François Tron
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

In the Opinion article published in Dermatology Practical and Conceptual entitled “Is Tunisian Endemic Pemphigus Foliaceus a real entity, or should these cases be classified with other Autoimmune Blistering Diseases?” [1], the authors questioned the endemic nature of Tunisian pemphigus and the ability of Tunisian dermatologists to correctly diagnose pemphigus foliaceus (PF) and to make the differential diagnosis with other autoimmune bullous dermatoses. We should remind your readers that most of the studies cited in this article are the result of a well-established collaborative work between Tunisian and French dermatologists and immunologists. Indeed, in a series of 23 patients with pemphigus collected at the Hospital of Sousse [2], 20 cases of superficial pemphigus were reported, all affecting young females, and only three cases of pemphigus vulgaris (PV). Among these 20 cases, 13 presented the classic generalized form of PF and seven had an initial clinical presentation of pemphigus herpetiformis. As indicated in the article [2], the diagnosis of pemphigus was established on the basis of usual clinical and histopathological criteria (the type of pemphigus being determined according to the site of cleavage: suprabasal acantholysis for PV and subcorneal for PF) and on the basis of intercellular deposits of IgG in the epidermis on direct immunofluorescence (DIF) analysis or of anti-intercellular antibodies (Abs) by indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) with a titer ≥1/100. Since the 15-month study (Nov 85–Jan 87) involved all pemphigus patients collected at the Dermatology Department of the Sousse Hospital, the only Dermatology Department in the Sahel region whose population does not exceed 1 million inhabitants, the authors concluded that the incidence of PF in Tunisia should be at least four new cases/million inhabitants/year, which was confirmed by a retrospective study of all new cases of pemphigus collected during a 6-year period (Jan 86–Dec 91) carried out in all dermatology departments in Tunisia: four in Tunis (north), one in Sousse (center), and one in Sfax (south). Among these 191 cases, 75 were classified as PV and 116 as PF, which, related to the Tunisian adult population aged ≥15 years, makes it possible to estimate an incidence of 6.7 new cases/million inhabitants/year for pemphigus, 2.5 for PV, and 3.9 for PF. These incidences were higher than to those calculated in the Île-de-France region (1.7, 1.3, and 0.5, respectively), particularly for PF, where the difference was much more significant and mainly due to a peak in Tunisia among young females (25–34 years). Indeed, for females aged 25–34, the incidence of PF was 15.5 new cases/million inhabitants/year and could reach 50 new cases in some southern regions [3]. Again, these initial observations were largely confirmed by our joint Franco-Tunisian prospective study. In this study, we recruited 90 patients with PF diagnosed in the dermatology departments of the university hospitals of Tunis (patients coming mostly from the northern regions) and of Sfax (patients coming from the southern regions). In this case-control study, 270 healthy controls (HC) matched by age, sex and geographical origin (three controls/patient) as well as 203 healthy subjects related to the patients from the south (HR: healthy relatives) were recruited. The results showed a more important sex ratio imbalance, a lower mean age at disease onset, and a higher proportion of patients of rural origin in the south compared to the north. Overall, 83.33% of patients and 7.4% of HC had anti-desmoglein1 (Dsg1) Abs as detected by ELISA. But anti-Dsg1 seropositivity was higher in the south, where it was associated with an important imbalance in sex, a lower mean age, and farming: 15.75 of HR were anti-Dsg1 positive. The seropositivity in this group was always higher than in HC and reached 22% in some rural areas. Most of anti-Dsg1 Abs detected in Tunisian southern HC belonged to the IgG2 subclass, whereas patients had mainly detectable IgG4 [4]. DRB1*03 was revealed as a susceptible allele especially in the south. This striking association was observed with a weak significance in the whole population. But when analyzed in the north and south groups separately, DRB1*03 was no longer associated with PF in the north, whereas it was associated with a very high significance in the southern group.

DRB1*04 was confirmed as susceptibility allele with a much higher significance in the south than in the north [5]. Taken together, these data confirm that PF occurs in two different forms in Tunisia, a sporadic form in the north and an endemic form in the south. Compared to South America endemic PF, the Tunisian endemic PF has some distinctive features: the occurrence mainly in young females from rural regions and HLA DRB1*03 as the main and characteristic susceptibility allele [6,7]. Later, with Professor Amagai’s group, we demonstrated that patients’ anti-Dsg1 Abs recognized the N-terminal domains of the mature and precursor forms of Dsg1, whereas those of healthy relatives and controls recognized the C-terminal domains of the precursor form only. Since the majority of pre-Dsg1 is located in the cytoplasm, Abs against pre-Dsg1 show undetectable staining in IIF and do not cause blister on the skin, in contrast to endemic PF patients’ pathogenic anti-Dsg1 Abs [8]. All the above-mentioned evidence proves the soundness of our hypothesis about the diagnosis of pemphigus foliaceus and its endemicity in Tunisia.
