Efficacy of neoadjuvant PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor in resectable NSCLC: a meta-analysis based on randomized controlled trials
Authors: Weiming Zhang, Tianfu Dai, Dongying Wang, Yushu Zhu, Weicong Hua
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe efficacy of neoadjuvant immunotherapy in resectable non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is controversial. The aim of this study is to explore the efficacy of additional PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in the neoadjuvant treatment of resctable NSCLC.MethodsData sources included Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science, and PubMed from beginning of the database creation to May 2024. The primary outcomes of the study included event-free survival (EFS), overall survival (OS), pathological complete response (pCR), major pathological response (MPR).ResultsSeven RCTs, including 2929 patients, were included in the analysis. The results showed that the experimental group had significantly higher rates of pCR (RR = 5.9, p < 0.001) and MPR (RR = 2.88, p < 0.001) compared to the control group. OS (HR = 0.57) and EFS (HR = 0.58) was also significantly improved in the experimental group. Subgroup analysis of EFS revealed that the benefit was higher in the East (HR = 0.56) than in the West (HR = 0.70). Additionally, the benefit seemed to rise with PD-L1 tumor cell proportion score (TPS) (TPS < 1%, HR = 0.76; 1% < TPS < 49%, HR = 0.56; TPS ≥ 50%, HR = 0.38). The benefit degree seemed to be higher in the III period patients (HR = 0.75) than I-II period patients (HR = 0.52). It is important to emphasize that smokers (HR = 0.54, p < 0.001) benefited from neoadjuvant immunotherapy but nonsmokers (HR = 0.68, p = 0.055) did not.ConclusionNeoadjuvant PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor combined with chemotherapy can significantly improve the short-term and long-term prognosis of patients with resectable NSCLC. Moreover, the efficacy of this treatment regimen may be affected by different clinicopathological characteristics of patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13311-5.

Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Observatory, lung cancer ranked first in both incidence (2.5 million cases) and mortality (1.8 million cases) [1]. The main pathological types of lung cancer which accounted for 89.5% were non-small lung cancer (NSCLC), including squamous and non-squamous cell carcinoma [2]. Although the prognosis of patients has improved with the improvement of treatment regimens, the 5-year survival rate for advanced/metastatic lung cancer is often less than 5% in terms [3]. The treatment methods for advanced/metastatic lung cancer mainly include chemotherapy, radiotherapy and targeted therapy. In recent years, the 5-year survival rate of patients with advanced/metastatic lung cancer has increased to 13.4% due to the addition of programmed death 1(PD-1)/programmed death-1ligand (PD-L1) inhibitors [4]. PD-1 is an immunosuppressive molecule, whereas PD-L1 is a protein in the body. When PD-1 binds to PD-L1, it will inhibit the immune function of the body, so that tumor cells can escape the attack of the immune system [5]. PD1/PD-L1 inhibitors can kill tumor cells by blocking this mechanism.

At present, standard treatment options for advanced/metastatic NSCLC include chemoradiotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy [6]. The standard treatment options for resectable NSCLC are surgery, radiotherapy and adjuvant therapy. For locally advanced/metastatic resectable NSCLC, neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy are also used for treatment. The latest studies show that immunotherapy [7], targeted therapy [8], neoadjuvant therapy [9, 10] and perioperative treatment [11, 12] seem to have efficacy in all stages of NSCLC. Based on treatment protocol improvement, the 5-year survival rate for patients with resectable NSCLC treated with surgery plus chemotherapy has increased to 44.5% [13]. But the result is still unsatisfactory. Therefore, researchers used neoadjuvant chemotherapy in resectable NSCLC. KEYNOTE-671 showed that neoadjuvant chemotherapy increased the 2-year survival rate to 77.6% in patients with resectable NSCLC [14].

Since both PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and neoadjuvant chemotherapy have been shown to be effective in the treatment of NSCLC, researchers began to explore whether neoadjuvant immunotherapy plus chemotherapy would achieve better efficacy in patients with resectable NSCLC. It has been suggested that neoadjuvant immunotherapy plus chemotherapy can improve the 3-year OS rate to 81.9% compared with neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone [15]. However, in CheckMate 816, the hazard ratio (HR) for death was 0.57 [99.67% confidence interval (CI): 0.30–1.07] at the first prespecified interim analysis, which did not meet the criterion for significance [16]. So this article will conduct a meta-analysis based on high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to explore the efficacy of neoadjuvant immunotherapy plus chemotherapy in resectable NSCLC.

Methods

Study registration

The meta-analysis and systematic review followed the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for preferred reporting items. The study is registered in the International Register of Prospects for Systematic Review (PROSPERO) under registry number CRD42024544761.

Data sources and search protocols

From beginning of the database creation to May 2024, four electronic databases: Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science and Pubmed were searched for articles about neoadjuvant immunotherapy in patients with resectable NSCLC with the terms which combined medical subject headings (MeSH) and text words ((((Non-small cell lung cancer) OR (NSCLC)) AND ((((((((((((((((((((((((((((PD-1) OR (PD-L1)) OR (programmed death 1)) OR (programmed death ligand 1)) OR (nivolumab)) OR (BMS 936558)) OR (BMS 936559)) OR (MDX 1105)) OR (pembrolizumab)) OR (lambrolizumab)) OR (MK 3475)) OR (pidilizumab)) OR (CT 011)) OR (durvalumab)) OR (MEDI 4736)) OR (atezolizumab)) OR (MPDL 3280a)) OR (avelumab)) OR (AMP 224)) OR (toripalimab)) OR (camrelizumab)) OR (SHR-1210)) OR (sintilimab)) OR (tislelizumab)) OR (penpulimab)) OR (zimberelimab)) OR (envafolimab)) OR (cemiplimab))) AND ((adjuvant) OR (resectable))). To avoid missing potential articles, an additional index of references to included articles was performed. Independent searches were conducted by both investigators and differences were resolved through discussion.

Inclusion criteria

PICOS principles were used to include studies. Studies that met all of the following characteristics would be included: (1) patients suffered from resectable NSCLC; (2) the experimental group received the intervention of neoadjuvant PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy; (3) the control group was treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone; (4) the results of the study were about prognosis including but not limited to event-free survival (EFS), overall survival (OS), pathological complete response (pCR), major pathological response (MPR); (5) study design belong to RCT.

Exclusion criteria

Studies that met any of the following characteristics were excluded: (1) when multiple updated studies were conducted from the same population, the latest or most comprehensive article was included; (2) data was not available or cannot be subjected to statistical analysis; (3) article was non-English written; (4) full-text was unavailable.

Data extraction

EFS was defined as the time from randomization to any progression of disease precluding surgery, progression or recurrence of disease after surgery, progression of disease in the absence of surgery, or death from any cause. OS was defined as the time from randomization to death from any cause. MPR was defined as ≤ 10% viable tumor cells in resected primary tumor and lymph nodes. pCR was defined as the absence of residual invasive cancer in resected primary tumor and lymph nodes [ypT0/Tis ypN0].

Two investigators independently extracted data according to a pre-designed table. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion between the two investigators. The following data was extracted: authors, year of publication, inclusion criteria, number of patients, treatment regimens, outcome (including EFS, OS, pCR, MPR), follow-up time, clinical trial number.

Statistical analysis

State 12.0 (Stata Statistical Software for data science, College Station, TX, USA) and Review manager (RevMan version 5.3; Oxford, U.K.) was used for statistical analysis. Relative risk (RR) with 95%CI was used to assess categorical variables. When RR > 1, the result favours experiment group. When RR < 1, the result favours control group. HR with 95%CI was used to assess continuous variables. When HR > 1, the result favours control group. When HR < 1, the result favours experiment group. Subgroup analysis was performed according to prespecified conditions, including sex, age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, pathological type, region, PD-L1 tumor cell proportion score (TPS), smoking status and pathological stage. Given the potential heterogeneity between studies due to different treatment regimens and population baseline, a random-effects model was used to increase credibility in the results. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochrane Q-test and I2 statistics, with a p < 0.10 and an I2 > 50% indicating significance of heterogeneity. Begger’s test was used to check for publication bias, and sensitivity analysis was used to assess the stability of the results. All results were considered statistically significant using a two-sided test with p < 0.05.

Result

Study selection and characteristics

Based on the given search terms, 3074 terms were retrieved from Cochrane Library, Embase, Web of Science and Pubmed. No additional studies were identified by reading the list of references for the included studies. After removing duplicates, the remaining 2296 studies were screened by title and abstract, 2214 study were excluded and 82 were retained to screen for the full text. Ultimately, 75 studies were excluded for non-RCTs article, update articles, treatment does not meet the inclusion criteria and 7 studies [11, 14, 16–20] were included in this meta-analysis and systematic review. Result showed five RCTs have high detection bias because there is no independent committee to evaluate the results of the experiment, three have high performance bias because there is no double blind, four articles exist unclear selection bias because it does not specify conflicts of interest, etc.

Pathologic response

Six RCTs reported patients’ pCR. There wer 1076 cases in the experimental group and 1061 cases in the control group. The pCR rate of the PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy group was higher than control group (RR = 5.98, p < 0.001, I2 = 43%; Fig. 2). Five RCTs reported patients’ MPR. There were 1092 cases in the experimental group and 1080 cases in the control group. The rate of MPR in the PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy group was higher than control group (RR = 2.88, p < 0.001, I2 = 0%; Fig. 3).Fig. 2Forest plot of the pCR with neoadjuvant immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy versus chemotherapy aloneFig. 3Forest plot of the MPR with neoadjuvant immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis of EFS was performed on sex, age, ECOG status, region, PD-L1 TPS, smoking status, tumor node metastasis (TNM) stage, histological type, chemotherapy regimens, and dosing regimen. Subgroup analysis result is shown in the following subgroups of EFS rate of the experimental group were higher than the control group, including: male (p < 0.001), female (p = 0.004), age < 65 (p < 0.001), age ≥ 65 (p < 0.001), ECOG status = 0 (p < 0.001), ECOG status = 1 (p = 0.003), squamous (p < 0.001), non-squamous (p < 0.001), cisplatin-based chemotherapy regimens (p = 0.002), carboplatin-based chemotherapy regimens (p = 0.003), neoadjuvant therapy (p = 0.003) and perioperative therapy (p < 0.001).

The region subgroup analysis showed that the EFS rate was higher in the experimental group than in the control group, and the benefit degree seemed to be higher in the East (HR = 0.56, p < 0.001) than in the West (HR = 0.70, p = 0.003). The PD-L1 TPS subgroup analysis showed that the EFS rate was higher in the experimental group than in the control group, and the benefit seemed to rise with PD-L1 TPS (TPS < 1%, HR = 0.76; 1% < TPS < 49%, HR = 0.56; TPS ≥ 50%, HR = 0.38). The TNM stage subgroup analysis showed that the EFS rate was higher in the experimental group than in the control group, and the benefit degree seemed to be higher in the III period patients (HR = 0.75, p = 0.032) than I-II period patients (HR = 0.52, p < 0.001). Of note, in the subgroup analysis for smoking status the EFS rate of the experimental group in the smoking group (HR = 0.54, p < 0.001) was higher than that of the control group, but there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in the non-smoking group (HR = 0.68, p = 0.055) (Table 2). Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Forest plot of EFS was tested for publication bias and sensitivity analysis in this study. The result of Begg’s test indicates that there was no significant publication bias (SFig. 3, p = 0.452). A sensitivity analysis by excluding each of the included studies one by one showed stability (SFig. 4).

Discussion

The results of meta-analysis showed that neoadjuvant immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy not only improves short-term prognosis including pCR and MPR, but also improves long-term prognosis like EFS and OS compared with neoadjuvant chemotherapy. PD-1 is a coinhibitory receptor expressed on the surface of antigen-stimulated T cells [21]. PD-L1 inhibits the downstream transmission of activation signals by binding to PD-1 on activated T cells [22] to phosphorylate cytoplasmic immune receptor tyrosine-based switch motifs (ITSM) [23]. After phosphorylation, ITSM recruits Src family proteins including phosphatase 1 and 2 (SHP1/2) and SLAM-related proteins [23, 24]. SHP1/2 dephosphorylates TCR and CD28 proximal signal transduction molecules, including ZAP70 and PI3K [25]. This mechanism inhibits T cell activation, cytokine release and promotes the expression of pro-apoptotic molecules, ultimately leading to T cell dysfunction or apoptosis [5]. Based on this mechanism, patients with higher TPS scores had higher levels of PD-L1 expression on the surface of tumor cells, which typically leads to more immunosuppression. Therefore, when this mechanism is broken (examples include the addition of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors), the greater the degree of benefit with higher PD-L1 expression should be. The results of our subgroup analyses provide evidence that this is the case.

However, patients with TPS score = 0 also had a benefit from immunotherapy, although to a lesser extent. In KEYNOTE-010, approximately 30% of PD-L1-negative patients with oligometastatic NSCLC who received local therapy followed by consolidation therapy with pembrolizumab had progression-free survival (PFS) of more than 36 month [26]. This may be due to the fact that the tumor cells do not express PD-L1, but other cells in the tumor microenvironment, such as dendritic cells, macrophages, may express PD-L1 or other immunosuppressive molecules. PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors may indirectly enhance anti-tumor immune responses by blocking immune checkpoint molecules on these cells [27, 28]. Theoretically, the addition of immunotherapy to neoadjuvant therapy can promote the immune system to kill the tumor and improve the prognosis including short—and long-term prognosis which was confirmed in our study. Besides, TPS seems to be unsatisfactory as an evaluation biomarker of the efficacy of immunotherapy. Combined positive score (CPS), as a distinct biomarker of PD-L1 expression, seems to be a better biomarker for evaluating the efficacy. The latest meta-analysis results show that patients with different TPS benefit from immunotherapy for gastric cancer. However, there was no benefit for gastric cancer patients when CPS was low but there was benefit for gastric cancer patients when CPS was high [29]. Therefore, CPS can be used as a better biomarker to evaluate the efficacy of immunotherapy.

In addition to TPS, other clinicopathological factors such as gender were also analyzed. Previous studies have suggested that there is a correlation between sex and response to immunotherapy, and the benefit of women is lower than that of men [30]. One possible reason is that women had a higher degree of overlap with non-smoking population, so their degree of benefit may be affected by smoking status [31]. However, gender was not significantly associated with the magnitude of benefit from immunotherapy. Many studies [32, 33], including the present study, have shown an association between smoking status and the efficacy of neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Long-term smoking can cause a large number of gene mutations in lung cells [34]. Studies have shown that the number of gene mutations in lung cancer patients who smoke is more than 10 times that of non-smokers, and the mutations are more chaotic [35, 36]. Therefore, smokers may achieve a better therapeutic effect when receiving immunotherapy due to the high mutation burden of tumor cells [37, 38]. And some studies indicate a greater benefit in squamous carcinomas than in non-squamous carcinomas [16], which may be due to a lower rate of PD-L1 expression in non-squamous carcinomas [39]. This may also be one of the reasons for the greater benefit in male (the proportion of squamous cell carcinoma is higher in male than in female [40]). However, this study also did not find a correlation between different pathological types and the efficacy of lower adjuvant immunotherapy. Previous findings that pathological type is associated with the degree of benefit of the PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors may be explained by differences in smoking status. This is due to squamous cell carcinoma has a higher incidence of overlap with smokers [41], and smokers have a higher benefit than non-smokers. In a word, we believe that smoking may be one of important factors of difference in efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors between genders and pathological types. More notably, most of the previous studies were based on advanced/metastatic NSCLC, for which treatment options did not include surgery. However, in our study, treatment options for patients with resectable NSCLC included surgery. Surgery involves lymph node dissection, which may disrupt the immune system and affect the efficacy of immunotherapy.

In addition, we found that patients with stage III disease had a higher benefit from neoadjuvant immunotherapy than those with stage I-II disease. This is because patients with stage III resectable NSCLC have a higher postoperative recurrence rate than stage I-II patients [42]. In theory, stage III patients are more likely to have residual tumor cells including circulating cells and metastatic cells. Moreover, the difference in tumor immune microenvironment between stage III and stage I-II may also be one of the reasons for the difference in immunotherapy. More inflammatory cells were infiltrated in stage III tumors which contained more tumor-associated macrophages [43].

The benefits appear to be greater in the eastern countries than in the western countries. The KEYNOTE-042 China extension study showed that Chinese patients were more likely to overlap with patients with a high PD-L1 TPS compared with global patients (PD-L1 TPS > 20%, 78% vs 64%; PD-L1 TPS > 50%, 56% vs 47%) [44]. The higher TPS scores in eastern countries compared with western countries may explain the difference in efficacy.

Previous studies have suggested that cisplatin-based chemotherapy regimens have a greater benefit than carboplatin-based chemotherapy regimens in resectable NSCLC [45–47]. They suggest that cisplatin and carboplatin differ in the extent of DNA adduct formation and, presumably, account for their differences in efficacy [48]. In addition, cisplatin is more mutagenic than carboplatin and is more likely to damage DNA [49]. In our study, carboplatin-based chemotherapy regimens were comparable to cisplatin-based chemotherapy regimens in terms of efficacy. The specific reasons need to be further proved.

This study systematically explored the role of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in the neoadjuvant treatment of resectable NSCLC. This study not only demonstrated the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in neoadjuvant immunotherapy, but also further analyzed the influence of different clinicopathological features on the efficacy.

Although this study ensured the inclusion of high-quality studies through an extensive literature search and strict screening criteria, publication bias may still exist. Publication bias usually occurs when studies with more positive or significant findings are more likely to be published, while negative or non-significant findings may be ignored or delayed in publication. In this study, Begg’s test was used to assess publication bias, and the results showed no significant publication bias, which increased the reliability of the study results.

The seven RCTs included in this study were designed to explore the efficacy of neoadjuvant PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors combined with chemotherapy in patients with resectable NSCLC, but there was some heterogeneity among the studies, which may have a certain impact on the results. First, the heterogeneity of the patient population cannot be ignored. There were differences in age, gender, ECOG performance status, pathological type, PD-L1 TPS, smoking status, and pathological stage among patients included in each study. These differences in clinicopathological features may lead to different responses to treatment and prognosis. For example, subgroup analyses in this study showed that patients in the Eastern region, patients with high TPS scores, and patients who smoked appeared to derive greater benefit from neoadjuvant therapy. These differences reflect the effect of patient population heterogeneity on treatment efficacy. Second, differences in treatment regimens are also an important source of heterogeneity. There are differences in the choice of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, the formulation of chemotherapy regimens, and the treatment cycle among different studies. This heterogeneity of treatment regimens may contribute to differences in efficacy findings across studies.

Conclusion

Neoadjuvant immunotherapy can significantly improve both short-term and long-term outcomes in patients with resectable NSCLC. Further analysis showed that different clinicopathological characteristics can affect the efficacy of neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Specifically, patients with eastern region, advanced TNM stage, smokers, and higher PD-L1 TPS score benefited more from immunotherapy.
